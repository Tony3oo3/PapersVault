## Problem

Statistical learning theory (SLT) proofs have grown substantially longer and more intricate, relying on advanced tools like concentration inequalities, covering numbers, and chaining arguments that lack structured, machine-readable formal libraries in Lean 4. Prior Lean formalizations of generalization bounds (e.g., Sonoda et al. 2025) are limited to simple Rademacher complexity settings over entire function classes, missing the sharper localized empirical process framework that underpins modern minimax-rate analyses. This paper addresses the gap by building the complete formal infrastructure for SLT from scratch, including the full Gaussian analysis pipeline and Dudley's entropy integral, which had no prior Lean 4 implementation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes natural-language theorems from SLT textbooks (Wainwright 2019, Boucheron et al. 2013, Vershynin 2018) along with existing Mathlib infrastructure and human-designed proof strategies encoded in structured TASK.md specifications (target Lean signature, infrastructure pointers, tactic-level proof plan, and hard boundaries). The output is approximately 30,000 lines of verified Lean 4 code containing over 1,000 theorems and lemmas with zero `sorry` or custom axioms, organized as a reusable formal library. A key byproduct is the explicit resolution of implicit assumptions (measurability, integrability, topological conditions, non-emptiness of empirical spheres) that standard textbook treatments suppress, making the formal statements strictly stronger than their natural-language counterparts.

## Method

The formalization is executed through a human-AI collaborative workflow using Claude Code (Opus-4.5), where humans design proof strategies and decompose theorems into ≤300-line leaf lemmas while the AI agent executes tactical proof construction. The mathematical pipeline builds Gaussian Lipschitz concentration from a chain of five components (Efron-Stein → Gaussian Poincaré → density/mollification arguments → Gaussian logarithmic Sobolev inequality via tensorization and Bernoulli LSI → Herbst argument), and Dudley's entropy integral via a three-stage chaining proof (finite dyadic nets with recursive projections → countable dense sequence via Fatou's lemma with a shift trick → uncountable set via path continuity). The application layer formalizes the localized empirical process framework for least-squares regression, deriving sharp minimax rates for linear regression (rank-dependent) and ℓ₁-constrained regression (via Maurey's empirical method for covering ℓ₁-convex hulls). The collaboration protocol uses iterative specification evolution (typically 3–4 TASK.md revisions per nontrivial lemma), an anti-rewrite instruction to prevent proof abandonment, and a quantitative intervention breakdown showing ~40% of declarations require full human-specified proofs while only ~15% are fully autonomous.

## Results

The paper formalizes approximately 30,000 lines of Lean 4 code containing over 1,000 theorems and lemmas, all compiling without `sorry` or custom axioms. The key mathematical results include the first complete formalization of Gaussian Lipschitz concentration (via Efron-Stein → Gaussian Poincaré → density arguments → Gaussian LSI → Herbst argument) and Dudley's entropy integral bound for sub-Gaussian processes. As applications, the framework yields the minimax rate P(‖f̂−f*‖²ₙ ≤ C₁σ²r/n) ≥ 1−exp(−C₂r) for linear regression and the O(R√(log d/n)) rate for ℓ₁-constrained (Lasso) regression. The human-AI collaboration required ~500 hours of supervised development with ~60 preserved task specifications, with ~45% of declarations proven autonomously or with only sketch-level guidance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The only prior Lean 4 formalization of statistical learning theory is Sonoda et al. (2025), which formalizes generalization bounds via Rademacher complexity using McDiarmid's inequality and Hoeffding's lemma. The present work surpasses this in two fundamental ways: (1) it replaces the bounded-difference (McDiarmid) concentration with the substantially more advanced Gaussian Lipschitz concentration, requiring a full chain of functional-analytic results (Efron-Stein, Gaussian Poincaré, LSI, Herbst argument) that were absent from Lean; and (2) it implements the localized empirical process framework with Dudley's entropy integral, yielding sharp minimax rates rather than the looser global-class bounds of Sonoda et al. The paper does not run head-to-head numerical comparisons (as is standard for formalization work), but positions its contribution as strictly extending the scope and tightness of the prior Lean SLT library. No other baselines are compared, as the paper's contribution is the creation of new formal infrastructure rather than a competitive algorithmic comparison.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the nascent line of Lean 4 formalization of machine learning theory, most directly building on Sonoda et al. (2025), who formalized generalization bounds via Rademacher complexity using McDiarmid's inequality and Hoeffding's lemma over the entire function class. The key departure is the shift to the localized empirical process framework, which requires substantially more advanced machinery: Gaussian Lipschitz concentration (via Efron-Stein, Gaussian Poincaré, density arguments, and the Gaussian logarithmic Sobolev inequality) and Dudley's entropy integral with full chaining arguments. The paper also connects to the broader Lean 4 formalization ecosystem (Mathlib) and to parallel efforts in formalizing optimization (Li et al., 2024–2025) and reinforcement learning theory (Zhang, 2025), but is the first to develop the complete Gaussian analysis and metric entropy infrastructure needed for sharp minimax rates in regression.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work establishes a reusable, machine-checkable foundation for statistical learning theory that was previously entirely absent from theorem provers, enabling future formal verification of generalization results with sharp rates. The formalization process itself exposes and resolves implicit measurability, integrability, and topological assumptions that standard textbooks (Boucheron et al., 2013; Wainwright, 2019) routinely suppress, providing a more rigorous reference. The human-AI collaborative workflow (~500 hours, structured TASK.md specifications, iterative spec evolution) offers a transferable methodology for large-scale formalization projects, though the approach is currently validated only on this single domain. Open questions include whether the framework extends to non-Gaussian noise, kernel methods, or deep network architectures, and whether the formalization can be automated further to reduce the ~40% 'specified' tier where humans must still articulate full proof plans.
