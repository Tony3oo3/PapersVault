## Problem

Reconstructing full spatiotemporal fields from extremely sparse sensor observations (here, 64 sensors covering only 1.56% of the spatial domain) remains difficult when the deployment domain exhibits distributional shifts absent from training data. Existing deep learning approaches require GPU clusters and dense supervision, while geostatistical baselines fail to preserve topological structure such as field boundaries and land-cover discontinuities. The paper tackles the combined challenge of hyper-sparse sensing, sim-to-real domain adaptation, and multi-scale structural fidelity in a single lightweight framework.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 64 randomly placed sensor readings (1.56% of a 64×64 = 4096-pixel grid) together with a 5-lag temporal history at each sensor, and produces a full 64×64 spatial field (e.g., NDVI) plus a set of hierarchically decomposed high-frequency correction layers. Critically, the full ground-truth field is never observed during training—only sparse sensor measurements from the target (ground-truth) season are available, while the 'simulation' season provides full-field supervision for the base encoder. The output corrections are spectrally separated by design, with each peeling layer capturing a distinct frequency band excluded from subsequent layers.

## Method

SENDAI decomposes reconstruction into a low-frequency pathway and a hierarchical high-frequency peeling (HFP) pathway. The LF pathway uses a 2-layer LSTM encoder (SHRED) trained on simulation data, followed by a residual GAN generator that adversarially aligns simulation-derived latents to ground-truth sensor-induced latents, and an MLP decoder whose output is explicitly low-pass filtered in Fourier space. The HF pathway consists of N sequential peeling layers, each computing the sensor residual after all prior corrections, encoding it to a compact latent code, and decoding via a coordinate-based implicit neural representation (INR) with Fourier positional encoding to produce spatially coherent corrections. Each peeling layer is regularized with a bandlimited L1/L2 sparsity term, an exclusion penalty that suppresses energy near frequencies already captured by earlier layers, and an adaptive top-k mode selection determined by correlation clustering and energy-concentration thresholds on the input residual. Training proceeds in three stages—SHRED pretraining, adversarial latent alignment, then sequential HF peeling with warmup schedules and post-training frequency identification—enabling the entire pipeline to train on standard CPU hardware in minutes.

## Results

SENDAI reconstructs full 64×64 NDVI fields from only 64 sensors (1.56% spatial coverage) across six globally distributed sites. On the three sites requiring the full hierarchical architecture, SENDAI achieves SSIM of 0.467 (Imperial Valley), 0.478 (Tarim Basin), and 0.335 (Riverina), representing up to 185% SSIM improvement over the best traditional geostatistical baseline (SG+IDW) and 36% improvement over the recent INR-based MMGN method. The simplified SENDAI Jr. variant (latent alignment only) already achieves +120% to +185% SSIM gains over baselines on smoother landscapes, while the full pipeline adds 9–36% further improvement where high-frequency corrections are needed. Training completes in ~32 minutes on a single CPU (Apple M4) with 1.5M parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SENDAI decisively outperforms all three geostatistical baselines (SG+IDW, HANTS+IDW, Kriging) by 3–10× in SSIM across every site, confirming that interpolation-based methods fundamentally fail to preserve topological structure under extreme sparsity. The recent INR-based MMGN method, despite strong performance on climate/oceanographic data at 5–50% sensor coverage, degrades to SSIM 0.078–0.123 under the 1.56% coverage and heterogeneous agricultural landscapes tested here, yielding a 36% SSIM gap versus full SENDAI on Tarim Basin. Among deep learning baselines, masked FNO (SSIM 0.345), adapted DeepONet (0.401), and adapted Senseiver (0.408) all fail to bridge the sim2real seasonal domain shift without SENDAI's latent-space alignment. When augmented with SENDAI's hierarchical multi-scale pipeline, DeepONet-SENDAI reaches SSIM 0.477 and Senseiver-SENDAI reaches 0.467, approaching but still below full SENDAI (0.489), confirming that SENDAI's contributions—latent GAN alignment and hierarchical frequency peeling—are the active ingredients rather than the specific spatial decoder choice.

## Relation to Prior Work

SENDAI extends the DA-SHRED and Cheap2Rich data assimilation frameworks (Bao & Kutz, 2025a; Bao et al., 2026) from physics-simulation contexts to Earth observation, where no true governing PDE exists and seasonal periods serve as a proxy for sim2real domain shift. Two key methodological departures are introduced: (i) a hierarchical frequency peeling strategy that sequentially extracts spectrally distinct correction layers with explicit exclusion constraints, going beyond the single-stage high-frequency correction in Cheap2Rich; and (ii) a coordinate-based implicit neural representation (INR) decoder with Fourier positional encoding for the high-frequency pathway, replacing the direct MLP decoder to produce spatially coherent fields. The latent-space GAN alignment mechanism is inherited from DA-SHRED, while the INR component draws on the SIRE/NeRF lineage (Sitzmann et al., 2020; Tancik et al., 2020).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework demonstrates that full 64×64 field reconstruction from only 64 sensors (1.56% coverage) is achievable on standard CPU hardware in minutes, making it operationally viable for resource-constrained deployment where GPU clusters are unavailable. The hierarchical peeling produces physically interpretable correction layers (phenological offsets, hydrological gradients, edaphic heterogeneity), enabling downstream inference of indirectly observed variables—a capability that entangled latent representations in prior methods lack. Key limitations include the assumption of stationary spatial structure between simulation and ground truth periods (breaking under land cover change), random sensor placement without information-theoretic optimization, and unaddressed temporal sparsity from satellite revisit constraints. The demonstrated transferability to LST, LSWI, and seismic waveforms suggests a general template, but validation remains limited to six sites and three additional variables.
