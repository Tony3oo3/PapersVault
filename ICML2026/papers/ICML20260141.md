## Problem

Standard video tokenizers represent all videos as fixed-size 3D spatiotemporal grids, forcing downstream generative models to predict every low-level pixel detail regardless of the video's inherent complexity. This uniform token budget wastes compute on redundant detail and prevents adapting representation compactness to downstream needs, making long-video generation prohibitively expensive.

## Input / Output

The tokenizer encodes video clips (17-frame 4-second at 128×128 for Kinetics-600, or 81-frame 10-second at 256×256 for Panda-70M) into a 2D token grid of shape T×K (time × coarse-to-fine depth, K up to 256), where any prefix of the K dimension yields a valid, decodable representation. Downstream, a text prompt (encoded by T5) or class label conditions an autoregressive Transformer that predicts tokens in time-first order, and the flow decoder reconstructs a full-resolution video from as few as 1–2 tokens per latent frame (e.g., 672 total tokens for a 10-second video).

## Method

VideoFlexTok is an autoencoder operating in VidTok VAE latent space. The encoder interleaves VAE latents with learnable register tokens along the temporal axis and processes them through a Transformer with time-causal attention; nested dropout randomly truncates the register dimension to induce an emergent coarse-to-fine hierarchy, and FSQ quantization discretizes the output. The decoder is a DiT-based rectified-flow generative model that takes any number of tokens plus noised VAE latents and denoises them into clean latents, trained with a REPA loss that distills DINOv2 features into early decoder layers to bias the first tokens toward semantic and motion information. A key design choice is the time-causal attention in both encoder and decoder, which creates a predictive self-supervised objective (each token must encode information for all future frames) and enables streaming-compatible tokenization. For long videos, the tokenizer splits into overlapping chunks and conditions the flow decoder on previously generated frames to maintain temporal consistency, allowing a 3.2B AR model to generate 10-second videos from only 672 tokens—8× fewer than a comparable 3D grid tokenizer.

## Results

On Kinetics-600 class-to-video generation with a 2.2B AR model, VideoFlexTok using only 160 tokens (6–8× fewer than baselines) achieves gFVD of 80.0 and a classification alignment score of 0.833, outperforming VidTok FSQ (gFVD 131.7, Cls. 0.799), Cosmos-DV (gFVD 187.6, Cls. 0.825), and LARP (gFVD 87.5, Cls. 0.739). On the text-to-video task (Panda-70M), VideoFlexTok achieves comparable gFVD and ViCLIP scores to a 3D grid tokenizer with a 5× smaller model (1.1B vs. 5.2B parameters) and an order of magnitude less compute. For long video generation, a 3.2B model trained on 10-second 81-frame videos uses only 672 tokens per video, 8× fewer than the 5376 required by a comparable 3D grid tokenizer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VideoFlexTok with only 160 tokens (6–8× fewer than the 1280 used by most baselines) achieves the best generation fidelity (gFVD 80.0) among all compared tokenizers, beating VidTok FSQ (131.7), Cosmos-DV (187.6), Omnitokenizer (102.6), and LARP (87.5). On class-video alignment, VideoFlexTok (0.833) outperforms VidTok FSQ (0.799), Cosmos-DV (0.825), and LARP (0.739), but is slightly behind Omnitokenizer (0.858), which is the only baseline it does not surpass on alignment. On reconstruction fidelity (rFVD), VideoFlexTok (48.7) is competitive with LARP (42.1) and substantially better than VidTok FSQ (84.1) and Cosmos-DV (220.5), though Omnitokenizer (63.6) remains stronger. The key efficiency advantage is that VideoFlexTok achieves these results with a fraction of the token budget, enabling 5–10× smaller AR models or 5–10× fewer training tokens for comparable performance, and 8× fewer tokens for 10-second video generation (672 vs. 5376).

## Relation to Prior Work

VideoFlexTok extends FlexTok's (Bachmann et al., 2025) 1D flexible-length image tokenization to video by introducing a 2D time-causal register structure and a generative rectified-flow decoder, departing from the de facto standard of fixed-size spatiotemporal 3D grid tokenizers (VidTok, Cosmos-DV, Omnitokenizer). It combines nested dropout (Matryoshka-style) to induce a coarse-to-fine hierarchy with a REPA semantic bias loss distilling DINOv2 features, and FSQ quantization for autoregressive compatibility. Unlike ElasticTok (Yan et al., 2025), which focuses on adaptive compression, VideoFlexTok emphasizes the downstream generative benefit of the hierarchical structure, showing that the first tokens emergently capture motion and semantics without explicit per-level supervision.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 5–10× training compute reduction and the ability to generate 10-second videos with only 672 tokens (8× fewer than 3D grid baselines) directly lower the barrier to video generative research, making it accessible to smaller labs. Open questions include the optimal compute allocation between the AR model and the flow decoder at inference, whether the coarse-to-fine structure transfers to video understanding/reasoning tasks, and how to further reduce flow-decoder denoising steps via distillation. The paper also notes that pixel-level reconstruction (MSE, LPIPS) lags behind grid tokenizers, suggesting the generative decoder has room for improvement.
