## Problem

SciML models (PINNs, neural operators, NODEs) exhibit training and generalization failures that are highly sensitive to physical parameters and optimization choices, yet prior work has characterized these failures only in isolation per model family or per PDE system. There is no unified, task-oblivious diagnostic framework that reveals when a method is reliable, why it fails, and which interventions most effectively improve robustness across the full landscape of SciML configurations. Additionally, standard loss-landscape metrics inherited from CV/NLP can misrepresent the underlying optimization behavior in SciML, making conventional flatness/sharpness intuitions unreliable.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes trained SciML model weights (PINN MLPs with 4×50 hidden layers, FNOs with ~1M parameters, NODEs with 3×16 hidden layers) paired with a PDE system specification (governing equation, physical coefficient, domain) and a training-data configuration (number of collocation points N_f or training samples). It produces 2D regime maps where the x-axis is a problem-specific physical parameter (e.g., convection coefficient β ∈ [5,70]) and the y-axis is data quantity, with regime boundaries extracted automatically via training-loss and test-error thresholds (perturbed ±20% for robustness). Hessian metrics are computed using the full set of residual collocation points rather than mini-batches, and spectral densities are estimated via Stochastic Lanczos Quadrature on a symmetric log scale to capture eigenvalues spanning multiple orders of magnitude.

## Method

The paper develops a regime-aware diagnostic framework that jointly analyzes performance, training dynamics, and loss-landscape geometry across five SciML model families, four PDE systems, and six optimization/training strategies (Adam, L-BFGS, NNCG, ALM, curriculum learning, RoPINN). It identifies a consistent three-regime structure (Well-Trained, Under-Trained, Over-Trained) from the joint behavior of training and test errors over the 2D configuration space, showing that optimization interventions reshape regime boundaries rather than eliminate the underlying structure. For loss-landscape analysis, the framework computes Hessian top eigenvalues (power iteration), trace (Hutchinson's method), and full spectral density (Stochastic Lanczos Quadrature) at converged models, revealing two SciML-specific pathologies: deceptive sharpness (Hessian eigenvalues grow as loss approaches zero, indicating funnel-shaped minima) and deceptive flatness (small Hessian eigenvalues coexist with elevated training loss, indicating vanishing gradient signal). The paper further tests and rules out three standard CV/NLP failure mechanisms—basin jumping, loss barriers, and landscape aging—as explanations for SciML optimization difficulty, attributing the breakdown of the Hessian-loss correlation to the intrinsic nonconvexity and anisotropy of physics-constrained loss surfaces.

## Results

The paper identifies a consistent three-regime structure (Well-Trained, Under-Trained, Over-Trained) across five SciML model families (PINN, FNO, PINO, NODE, PINODE) and four PDE systems, with regime boundaries extracted from training/test-error thresholds. Key quantitative findings include: NNCG fine-tuning reduces test error by ~50% in data-limited Regime I and improves training loss by ~60% in well-trained scenarios, but becomes numerically unstable in high-β Regime II/III; ALM shifts the PINN trainability boundary on 1D convection from β≈25 to β≈50; and CL achieves comparable accuracy with N_f=1000 collocation points where ALM requires N_f≥5000. Hessian analysis reveals SciML landscapes have outlier eigenvalues exceeding 10³–10⁴ (vs. ~10 for ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10), with a decoupling of the standard Hessian-loss correlation that produces 'deceptive sharpness' and 'deceptive flatness' phenomena.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares six optimization/training strategies (L-BFGS, Adam, ALM, NNCG, RoPINN, CL) across five SciML models, finding that no single method dominates all regimes. ALM (hard-constrained augmented Lagrangian) wins on trainability in challenging physical regimes, shifting the PINN 1D convection boundary from β≈25 to β≈50, but does not eliminate Regime III (over-training) and requires 5× more collocation points than CL for comparable fidelity at β=30. CL (curriculum learning) outperforms ALM in Regime III by significantly reducing the generalization gap at low data (N_f=1000 vs. N_f≥5000) and produces smoother regime transitions, but does not match ALM's trainability boundary shift. NNCG (Nyström Newton-CG) delivers the largest gains in Regime I (~50% test error reduction, ~60% training loss improvement) by exploiting curvature information, but suffers numerical instability and divergence in high-β Regime II/III where the Hessian is extremely ill-conditioned. RoPINN improves trainability with a sharper boundary shift than CL but does not fully eliminate generalization failure in low-collocation settings. Adam (used for FNO, NODE, PINO, PINODE) produces smoother, more diffuse regime transitions compared to L-BFGS-based PINN training, with less clearly separated trainability and generalization regions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the regime-aware loss landscape diagnostic framework from Yang et al. (2021) and Zhou et al. (2023b), originally developed for CV/NLP models, to the SciML setting by systematically applying it across five model families (PINNs, FNOs, PINOs, NODEs, PINODEs) and multiple PDE systems. It builds on prior SciML failure-mode analyses (Krishnapriyan et al., 2021; Rathore et al., 2024; Krishnapriyan et al., 2023) but departs from them by providing a unified, cross-model taxonomy rather than model-specific diagnostics, and by explicitly testing whether standard CV/NLP landscape intuitions (flatness, connectivity, aging) explain SciML optimization difficulty. The paper also incorporates recent optimization methods (NNCG, RoPINN, ALM) as interventions to probe how regime boundaries shift under different training strategies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of a consistent three-regime structure across diverse SciML models provides a practical diagnostic vocabulary for practitioners to locate where their training is failing (optimization vs. generalization) and which interventions are most likely to help in each regime. The counterintuitive findings—deceptive sharpness (Hessian eigenvalues growing as loss decreases) and deceptive flatness (flat Hessian regions corresponding to undertraining rather than robust solutions)—challenge the standard Hessian-loss correlation that underpins much of CV/NLP optimization theory, suggesting that SciML-specific optimization methods and diagnostics are needed. Limitations include the empirical (non-theoretical) nature of the analysis, the use of relatively small PDE benchmarks, and the somewhat arbitrary threshold-based regime boundary extraction; open questions include whether the three-regime structure persists for higher-dimensional PDEs, more complex physics, or larger model scales, and whether the identified failure modes can be addressed by principled architectural changes rather than training tricks.
