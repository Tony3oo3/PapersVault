## Problem

Data owners of sensitive or copyrighted texts lack a reliable, text-preserving method to verify whether their documents were used to fine-tune a deployed LLM under black-box access. Existing approaches either modify visible text (homoglyphs, canaries), lack statistically grounded false-positive control (membership inference attacks, copyright traps), or fail under multi-watermark settings, leaving no practical tool for post-hoc provenance auditing with provable guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a set of K candidate watermarks (one committed for embedding, K−1 reserved as counterfactuals) and a suspicious model accessible only via prompt–response API calls. At audit time, prompts are constructed by concatenating a cue chunk with the first t·(1+step) elements of the following reply chunk to complete the cue; the model is queried λ times per chunk, and detection reduces to extracting all invisible Unicode characters from the output and checking whether the flattened reply appears as a contiguous subsequence. The output is a binary membership decision: the committed watermark's score is ranked against all K−1 counterfactual scores, and membership is claimed if its rank exceeds a threshold k, yielding FPR ≤ k/K.

## Method

Watermarks are built from an alphabet of 121 invisible Unicode characters grouped into syllables of m=4 characters, forming a cue (j=5 syllables) and a reply (n−j=3 syllables) with global uniqueness and non-containment constraints enforced by a trusted third party. During embedding, each document is split into δ-word chunks; odd-indexed chunks receive the cue minus its last t=1 syllable, while even-indexed chunks receive that tail syllable plus the full reply, all inserted cyclically every `step` words so the visible text remains byte-identical. At detection, the model is prompted with completed cues and the reply is searched for among the invisible characters in the generated output. The decision procedure ranks the committed watermark's aggregate chunk-level score against K−1 counterfactual watermarks evaluated in the identical context, providing a provable FPR bound of k/K without requiring access to model weights, logits, or token probabilities. This combination of invisible-Unicode canaries, cue–reply elicitation, and ranking-based hypothesis testing is, to the authors' knowledge, the first text-preserving DPA scheme that simultaneously supports black-box operation, multi-watermark attribution, and statistically sound false-positive control.

## Results

The proposed method achieves a true positive rate of 96.7% at 0% false positive rate (k=1) using only 40 watermarked documents (as little as 0.25% of the total fine-tuning corpus). No spurious regurgitation of counterfactual watermarks is detected over more than 10⁵ challenges, while legitimate watermarks yield average scores of 27–142 across models and datasets. Collection-level miss probability p_FN drops as low as 10⁻⁵⁹ (LLaMA on News, 30 docs) and 10⁻¹⁹⁹ (LLaMA on News, 50 docs), confirming near-certain detection at moderate collection sizes. Regurgitation rates exceed 28% per document and saturate between 0.75 and 0.98 as the watermarked collection grows.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary quantitative baseline is the homoglyph watermarking scheme of Wei et al. (2024), the only prior text-preserving DPA technique with statistically grounded proofs. Under a challenging multi-watermark setting (50 unique watermarks, 5,000 documents), the homoglyph baseline produces Z-scores concentrated around −0.1 to −0.6, corresponding to p > 0.2 and thus statistically non-significant detection across all datasets and models. In contrast, the proposed invisible-Unicode cue-reply method achieves regurgitation rates above 0.8 under the identical setting, yielding reliable membership signals. Qualitatively (Table 3), the proposed method is the only DPA technique simultaneously satisfying text authenticity, black-box operability, and statistical soundness; the homoglyph baseline requires word-level token probabilities (not black-box) and does not support multiple concurrent watermarks, while other approaches (MIAs, copyright traps, fictitious knowledge) fail on one or more of these three properties.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of dataset provenance auditing (DPA) and text watermarking for LLMs, building directly on the statistical framework of Zhang et al. (2025), who formalized conditions under which a ranking test yields a provable FPR bound for membership proofs. It departs from the closest prior text-preserving method—homoglyph watermarking by Wei et al. (2024)—by replacing visually distinguishable character substitutions with invisible Unicode sequences, eliminating the need for word-level token probabilities, and supporting concurrent multi-watermark attribution. More broadly, it contrasts with non-intrusive MIAs (which lack soundness guarantees for free-text generation) and intrusive canary methods like fictitious knowledge or copyright traps (which alter visible text and lack statistical grounding).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first method that simultaneously preserves visible text, operates under black-box access, and delivers a provable FPR bound for fine-tuning data provenance—addressing a gap that legal instruments alone cannot fill, as highlighted by the EU AI Act's transparency obligations and ongoing copyright litigation. Key limitations include vulnerability to targeted adversarial removal (stripping zero-width characters), dependence on a trusted third party for watermark allocation, and scope restricted to supervised fine-tuning rather than pretraining or RLHF. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to larger-scale or full-parameter fine-tuning, how to harden against adaptive adversaries, and whether watermark-based audit results will be admissible in legal proceedings.
