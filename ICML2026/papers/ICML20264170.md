## Problem

Progressive layer stacking (gradually adding layers during training) has been shown to underperform full-capacity training on knowledge-intensive tasks, and curriculum learning remains largely unexplored in modern LM pretraining. The paper asks whether synchronizing model depth growth with a structured data curriculum of increasing complexity can unlock the compute-efficiency benefits of progressive stacking that prior work has failed to realize.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264170/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a text corpus pre-stratified into difficulty tiers (e.g., TinyStories/BookCorpus/DCLM at GPT-2 scale, or high-school/undergraduate/graduate splits of DCLM at 1B scale) and a target transformer architecture, producing a full-depth pretrained language model. Data is consumed in staged mixtures with prescribed per-stage weights (e.g., 70/10/20 → 15/25/60 → 5/5/90 for the small-scale curriculum), and the model starts at half the final depth (N/2 layers) before being expanded to the target depth across 3–4 stages.

## Method

CGLS jointly scales model depth and data difficulty across training stages. At each stage, newly added transformer layers are randomly initialized and first trained in isolation with all prior layers frozen (an initialization phase consuming ~20% of the stage budget at a higher learning rate), then the entire model is unfrozen and fine-tuned on a progressively harder data mixture. The data curriculum shifts from simple, structured text (synthetic stories or general web content) toward complex, specialized content (academic papers, code) as depth increases. A default configuration initializes at N/2 layers, allocates compute as 20/20/20/40% across four stages, and uses a DistilBERT-based classifier (trained on GPT-4o-labeled samples) to stratify DCLM into three complexity tiers. The key insight is that model expansion alone is insufficient; the synchronization between capacity growth and data difficulty is what preserves early representations and enables better generalization on reasoning tasks.

## Results

At the GPT-2-Small scale (2.5B tokens), CGLS achieves 36.61% average zero-shot accuracy across PIQA, ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, and LAMBADA, outperforming the best baseline (MIDAS) by 1.05 points and the randomized baseline by 1.48 points, with the largest gain on ARC-Easy (43.01% vs. 38.85%). At the Llama-3.2-1B scale (2.5B tokens), CGLS reaches 39.08% average accuracy, exceeding the randomized baseline (37.38%) by 1.7 points and MIDAS (33.55%) by 5.5 points, with ARC-Easy improving from 36.36% to 41.37%. Scaling to the Chinchilla-optimal 20B-token budget, CGLS widens its lead to 42.30% average vs. 39.72% for the curricularized baseline and 38.41% for the randomized baseline, with gains of +4.4 points on PIQA and +5.2 points on ARC-Easy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary external baseline is MIDAS (Saunshi et al., 2024), which progressively stacks layers but does not synchronize data difficulty with model growth. CGLS outperforms MIDAS on average accuracy at both scales: 36.61% vs. 35.56% at GPT-2-Small and 39.08% vs. 33.55% at Llama-3.2-1B, with the gap widening substantially at the larger scale (5.5 points). On ARC-Easy specifically, CGLS beats MIDAS by 5.4 points (GPT-2) and 8.8 points (Llama-3.2-1B), while MIDAS retains a small edge on LAMBADA at the GPT-2 scale (28.29% vs. 28.08%). Internally, CGLS also surpasses the curricularized baseline (which uses the same data schedule but a fixed-depth model) by 1.32 points at GPT-2-Small and 1.75 points at Llama-3.2-1B, and decisively outperforms the layer-scaling-only baseline (which grows depth without a curriculum) by 1.67 and 4.84 points respectively, confirming that the joint synchronization of data complexity and model depth is what drives the gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

CGLS unifies two previously separate threads in efficient pretraining: curriculum learning (Bengio et al., 2009), which orders data from easy to hard, and progressive layer stacking (Gong et al., 2019; Saunshi et al., 2024), which grows model depth in stages. The freeze-then-unfreeze two-phase initialization for newly added layers follows Kumar et al. (2022) to protect previously learned representations. The key departure from prior progressive stacking work (e.g., MIDAS) is the hypothesis that stacking alone is insufficient for knowledge-intensive tasks and must be synchronized with a structured data curriculum—a coupling that neither the curriculum-learning nor the model-growth literature had explored for LM pretraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides evidence that progressive stacking, previously shown to underperform on memorization and reasoning tasks, can be made effective when paired with a data curriculum, offering a simple recipe for compute-efficient pretraining. However, the evidence is limited to ≤1.2B parameters and ≤20B tokens, perplexity does not consistently improve at smaller budgets, and gains on some tasks (HellaSwag, LAMBADA) are negligible. Open questions include whether the synchronization benefit persists at 7B+ scale, whether better difficulty classifiers or alternative curriculum signals (syntactic diversity, factuality) further strengthen the approach, and whether the method extends to multimodal pretraining.
