## Problem

Existing Concept Bottleneck Models constrain the task predictor to a single expression whose functional form is fixed a priori (e.g., linear or Boolean), limiting both predictive accuracy and adaptability to diverse user interpretability needs. The two-dimensional design space defined by the number of expert expressions and their functional form remains largely unexplored, with no principled framework to navigate the accuracy-interpretability tradeoff across these axes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw RGB images (preprocessed via DINOv2 or ViT embeddings) or text (via Flan-T5 embeddings) and produces a task prediction (class label or regression value) together with the selected expression tree that generated it. The task predictor operates exclusively on predicted concept values (not raw inputs), ensuring semantic transparency; the finite set of M expression trees is fully inspectable by a user, and the selector p(m|x) routes each input to one expert. For Sym-M-CBE, the operator vocabulary W is user-specified, enabling post-hoc adaptation of expression complexity without retraining the encoder or selector.

## Method

M-CBEs models the task predictor as a mixture of M expression trees, each defined by a tuple of operators, edges, parameters, and input variables, with a learned selector p(m|x) (implemented as an MLP with Gumbel-Softmax sampling) routing inputs to experts. Two instantiations explore the design space: Linear M-CBE fixes the tree structure to a parametric linear form and learns only weights/biases per expert, while Symbolic M-CBE uses PySR's evolutionary algorithm to discover expert functions from a user-defined operator vocabulary. Sym-M-CBE training is three-stage: (i) jointly train the concept encoder, selector, and placeholder MLP predictors; (ii) apply symbolic regression to each expert's data subset to recover expression trees; (iii) fine-tune the discovered symbolic parameters end-to-end. The framework provides universal approximation guarantees (Proposition 1) and explicit error bounds for polynomial experts (Proposition 2), and subsumes existing CBMs (CBM, CMR, DCR, LICEM) as special cases.

## Results

On regression tasks, Sym-M-CBE achieves MAE of 0.33±0.12 on Pendulum and 1.33±0.18 on MAWPS, approaching the black-box upper bound (0.14 and 0.79 respectively) while remaining fully interpretable. Sym-M-CBE recovers the exact data-generating expression (Tree Edit Distance ≈ 0) on Pendulum, MNIST-Arith, and MAWPS, whereas Lin-M-CBE and MLP-M-CBE achieve TED of 5.71 and 43.63 on Pendulum. On high-dimensional classification (CUB-200, CIFAR-10 with >100 concepts), Boolean-based baselines (CMR, DCR) degrade to near-100% error, while Lin-M-CBE and Sym-M-CBE maintain robust performance, with algebraic forms outperforming Boolean logic by up to 65% in accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CBM (Koh et al., 2020), which is equivalent to Lin-M-CBE with M=1, the multi-expert variant recovers predictive performance on incomplete concept sets (AWA2-Incomplete, CUB-200-Incomplete) where the single-expert baseline degrades. Compared to CEM (Espinosa Zarlenga et al., 2022), which uses a non-interpretable MLP predictor, Sym-M-CBE achieves comparable or better MAE on Pendulum (0.33 vs. 0.60) and MAWPS (1.33 vs. 0.73) while providing fully inspectable symbolic expressions; CEM's black-box predictor is generally Pareto-dominated by structured M-CBE instantiations. Against DCR (Barbiero et al., 2023) and CMR (Debot et al., 2024), both of which restrict the task predictor to Boolean expressions, M-CBEs demonstrate a critical scalability advantage: Boolean forms reach near-100% error on CUB-200 and CIFAR-10 (>100 concepts) due to the brittleness of conjunctive rules, while Lin-M-CBE and Sym-M-CBE maintain robust accuracy. Relative to LICEM (De Santis et al., 2025a), which produces a distinct expression per input (infinite model complexity), M-CBEs learn a finite set of expressions enabling global inspection and verification, and exhibit superior intervention robustness under OOD inputs (LICEM shows MAE ≈ 100 without interventions and fails to recover even with all concepts corrected). Sym-M-CBE also outperforms the KAN-based alternative (Kan-M-CBE) in expression compactness (10.6 vs. 101.6 nodes on Pendulum) and ground-truth alignment (TED 7.02 vs. not reported, but KAN expressions are substantially larger per Table 4).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies and extends three research threads: Concept Bottleneck Models (Koh et al., 2020), which decompose prediction into concept encoding and a fixed-form task predictor; Mixture of Experts (Jacobs et al., 1991; Shazeer et al., 2017), which routes inputs to specialized sub-networks; and Symbolic Regression (Cranmer, 2023), which discovers interpretable mathematical expressions from data. Prior concept-based methods (CMR, DCR, LICEM, CEM) all fix the task predictor's functional form a priori—either a single linear function or Boolean conjunctive rules—leaving the joint design space of functional form × expert count largely unexplored. M-CBEs positions itself as exposing this two-dimensional space, showing that existing CBMs are special cases (M=1, fixed form) while proposing two novel instantiations (Linear M-CBE, Symbolic M-CBE) that occupy previously undefined regions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides practitioners with a principled, user-controllable dial for the accuracy-interpretability tradeoff: choosing algebraic over Boolean forms avoids the near-total failure of conjunctive rules at >100 concepts (up to +65% accuracy gain), and multiple experts compensate for incomplete concept sets or heterogeneous task logic. Sym-M-CBE's ability to recover ground-truth expressions (TED ≈ 0) and support post-hoc operator-vocabulary changes without retraining makes it practically attractive for domain-specific deployment. Key open questions remain: the selector p(m|x) is still a black-box, limiting full transparency; symbolic regression is computationally expensive for large vocabularies; and the number of experts M must be set manually. Whether the selector itself can be made interpretable or M can be learned automatically would be natural next steps.
