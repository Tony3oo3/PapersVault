## Problem

Existing LLM inference schedulers (e.g., SSJF, LTR) predict a single point estimate for each request's output length to enable shortest-job-first scheduling, but this is fundamentally mismatched with the stochastic decoding process where the same prompt can yield different output lengths depending on when the EOS token is sampled. This mismatch causes mispredicted long requests to block subsequent shorter ones (HOL blocking), degrading both per-token latency and throughput. The paper argues that output length should be modeled as a distribution rather than a single value to properly account for this inherent uncertainty.

## Input / Output

The method takes a raw text prompt as input, which is encoded by a fine-tuned DeBERTa-v3-base (86M parameters) into contextualized embeddings. A multi-pooling strategy (CLS, mean-pool, max-pool) feeds two separate MLP heads that predict the log-t distribution parameters μ and σ (with ν fixed at 3.5). The output is a single scalar scheduling score computed as E[X̃] + β·CVaR_α[X̃], where X̃ is the log-t distribution censored at max_tokens, α=0.9, and β is adaptively set between 0.1 and 0.5 based on current queue length relative to batch size. Prediction runs asynchronously in batches (up to 32 requests, 3 ms timeout) so it never blocks the main scheduling thread.

## Method

The paper first proves (Theorem 3.2) that under a mild assumption on termination-rate distributions, LLM output length exhibits power-law tail decay, justifying the use of heavy-tailed distributions. Empirically, the log-t distribution with fixed ν=3.5 achieves a 90.6% KS-test pass rate across 1,000 prompts, outperforming log-normal (60.3%), gamma (32.0%), and other candidates. A DeBERTa-v3-base encoder with multi-pooling fusion and separate MLP heads predicts (μ, σ) via supervised regression (R²=0.82 for μ, 0.76 for σ), trained on 45K prompts × 20 generations each. The scheduling score combines the censored expectation (consistency) with CVaR at α=0.9 (robustness), weighted by an adaptive β that increases with queue pressure, formalizing a consistency-robustness trade-off from learning-augmented algorithms. An exponential waiting-time decay (γ=0.9, τ=30s) prevents starvation of long requests under sustained high load.

## Results

On the LMSYS-Chat-1M dataset with the 8B model at 100 RPS, TIE achieves an average per-token latency of 0.57 s/token, reducing it by 4.73× over FCFS (1.66 s), 2.91× over SSJF (0.95 s), and 2.31× over LTR (0.86 s). For offline synthetic data generation on Alpaca (8B model), TIE completes 3K samples in 98.12 s (2.34× faster than FCFS, 1.42× faster than LTR) and achieves a throughput of 4,762 samples within 3 minutes (1.42× over LTR's 3,672). On the 70B model, TIE achieves a 3.77× per-token latency speedup over FCFS (2.41 s vs. 9.08 s) on LMSYS-Chat-1M, and generalizes well to ShareGPT and Alpaca datasets without retraining.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TIE consistently outperforms all three non-preemptive baselines (FCFS, SSJF, LTR) across every dataset, model size, and evaluation metric. Against FCFS (vLLM's default), TIE achieves 2.91–4.73× per-token latency reductions and 1.4–1.6× TTFT reductions. Against SSJF (proxy-model length prediction, ISCA 2024), TIE wins by 1.67–2.91× on per-token latency, with the largest gap on the 70B model where distribution modeling captures tail uncertainty that point estimates miss. Against LTR (learning-to-rank, NeurIPS 2024), TIE achieves 1.25–2.31× per-token latency improvements, with the advantage growing under high load (at 100 RPS, TIE's latency increase from 30→100 RPS is only 3.68× vs. 6.17× for LTR). In the offline SDG setting, TIE improves throughput by 1.42× over LTR and 1.37× over SSJF. Against the preemptive baseline TRAIL (ICLR 2025), TIE matches or exceeds performance at low-to-moderate RPS (≤40) and pulls ahead significantly at higher loads (0.67 vs. 1.41 s/token at 100 RPS), while avoiding the overhead of iterative prediction and preemption.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant point-estimate paradigm in LLM inference scheduling (SSJF, LTR) by arguing that predicting a single output length is fundamentally misaligned with the stochastic token-sampling process of autoregressive decoding. It draws on classical stochastic scheduling theory (SEPT, Weber 1983) and risk-management tools (CVaR) to construct a distribution-aware scheduling score, while adapting these ideas to the practical constraints of LLM serving (prompt-conditioned prediction, continuous batching, max_tokens censoring). The log-t distribution choice is grounded in both empirical fitting (93.1% KS pass rate) and a theoretical result showing power-law tail decay under a mild assumption on termination-rate heterogeneity. Unlike preemptive methods (TRAIL, ELIS) that re-predict and preempt during generation, TIE makes a single pre-decoding prediction and handles uncertainty through the score itself, avoiding preemption overhead.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 2.31× per-token latency reduction and 1.42× throughput gain over the best non-preemptive baseline are substantial and validated across 9 models, 3 datasets, and both online/offline scenarios, making this a practically deployable improvement for LLM serving infrastructure. The distribution-modeling perspective extends beyond scheduling to KV cache pre-allocation and API cost estimation, broadening its impact. Key limitations include the training-data requirement (multiple generations per prompt to fit distributions, unlike point-estimate predictors that use single production logs) and the fixed parametric form, which may not capture all workload irregularities. Open directions include dynamic distribution updates during decoding, non-parametric or hybrid distributional predictors, and online adaptation to reduce the multi-generation training burden.
