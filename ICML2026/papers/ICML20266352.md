## Problem

Current LLM safety evaluation pipelines are unreliable due to intertwined sources of noise—small and inconsistently subsampled datasets, undocumented implementation details, fragmented judge ecosystems, and unrealistic evaluation setups—that make it impossible to fairly compare attacks and defenses. This position paper argues that these issues, analogous to the reproducibility crises that previously stalled adversarial robustness research in computer vision, are now more severe in the LLM setting due to higher compute costs and a more complex, multi-stage evaluation pipeline.

## Input / Output

The paper analyzes the full LLM safety evaluation pipeline, consuming the standard components of the field (safety and overrefusal datasets of 100–500 prompts, adversarial attack algorithms such as GCG and PAIR, victim LLMs, and automated judge models) and producing a stage-by-stage diagnosis of where bias and noise enter, supported by empirical case studies. Outputs include Clopper-Pearson confidence intervals quantifying statistical uncertainty, controlled ASR comparisons under implementation variations, z-tests on published results, and a 5.2-million-generation cross-judge comparison, culminating in a set of concrete guidelines for future evaluations.

## Method

The core contribution is a systematic decomposition of the LLM safety evaluation pipeline into three stages—dataset curation, attack optimization, and response evaluation—with targeted empirical case studies quantifying the practical impact of each identified issue. Key experiments include: (1) showing that common datasets of 100–500 prompts yield 95% confidence intervals too wide for reliable model comparisons, (2) demonstrating that minor implementation details (whitespace token alignment, chat template choice, int8 vs. BF16 quantization, token filtering) cause up to 28% ASR differences in GCG against Llama models, (3) running 5,269,564 generations across 10 attack algorithms and 25 models to reveal up to 25% attack-specific and 24% model-specific biases between the HarmBench Llama-2-13B and StrongREJECT Gemma-2B judges, and (4) performing two-proportion z-tests on published results from Guan et al. (2024) to show that their Pareto-improvement claim would be statistically inconclusive on public datasets alone. The paper closes with concrete recommendations: tiered dev/full evaluation splits, standardized implementation documentation, budget-controlled attack comparisons, distributional (non-greedy) generation, and mandatory joint reporting of safety and overrefusal metrics.

## Results

The paper's case studies reveal that minor implementation choices (chat template, token filtering, quantization, system prompt) cause GCG's many-trial ASR against Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct to range from 0.47 to 0.85, a 38-point spread among all reasonable configurations. Incorrectly handling the SentencePiece whitespace token reduces GCG's ASR against Llama-2-7B-chat-hf by 28% (91% → 63%). A large-scale comparison of 5,269,564 generations across 10 attacks and 25 models shows up to 25% attack-specific and 24% model-specific ASR discrepancies between the StrongREJECT Gemma 2B and HarmBench Llama-2 13B judges. Statistical re-analysis of Guan et al. (2024) shows the claimed Pareto improvement of o1 over Claude 3.5 Sonnet is not significant on XSTest (p≈0.12), and human-verified results (p≈0.38) contradict the automated judge's highly significant finding (p≈0.004).

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it compares evaluation configurations to expose noise and bias. Among GCG implementation variants, the HuggingFace default template with strict filtering (ASR 0.85) outperforms the Meta-recommended template (0.77) by 8 points, while int8 quantization drops ASR by a further 6 points (0.71), and omitting the system prompt causes a dramatic 38-point collapse to 0.47. Between the two most widely used automated judges, StrongREJECT's Gemma 2B consistently reports lower ASR than HarmBench's Llama-2 13B, but the gap is highly non-uniform: it reaches 25% for specific attacks and 24% across model families, meaning the relative ranking of attacks or models can flip depending on which judge is used. In the statistical case study, the automated judge declares o1 significantly more robust than Claude 3.5 Sonnet (p≈0.004), yet human verification on the same StrongREJECT prompts yields p≈0.38, rendering the difference non-significant and undermining the Pareto-improvement claim. These comparisons collectively demonstrate that no single 'standard' configuration exists, and that results are highly sensitive to choices that are often undocumented or inconsistent across papers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper draws a direct parallel to the adversarial robustness literature in computer vision, where Athalye et al. (2018) and Carlini (2024) showed that unreliable evaluations produced misleading feedback and slowed progress for a decade. It consolidates and extends scattered observations from the LLM safety evaluation literature—implementation discrepancies (Biderman et al., 2024), statistical concerns (Miller, 2024), judge biases (Hughes et al., 2025), and dataset limitations (Souly et al., 2024; Xhonneux et al., 2024)—into a unified pipeline analysis spanning datasets, optimization algorithms, and response evaluation. Rather than proposing a new attack or defense, the paper repurposes the CV robustness community's hard-won lessons about standardization (RobustBench, CleverHans) as a template for what the LLM safety field still needs to achieve.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's case studies provide concrete, quantified evidence that current evaluation practices can produce misleading conclusions: a 28% ASR drop from a single whitespace token, 14% shifts from chat template choices, 25% disagreement between two widely-used judges across 5.2M generations, and a demonstration that a major paper's Pareto-improvement claim is statistically inconclusive on public data alone. These findings matter because the field is at a critical juncture where safety evaluations inform regulatory decisions and model deployment, yet the evaluation pipeline introduces enough noise to obscure which attacks and defenses are genuinely effective. Open questions include how to balance statistical rigor with compute constraints in academia, how to standardize multilingual evaluation, and whether the community can adopt tiered evaluation protocols without further fragmenting the field.
