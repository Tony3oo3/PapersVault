## Problem

In semi/self-supervised learning, the benefit of unlabeled data hinges on whether the prior assumption underlying a pretext task is compatible with the target scenario, yet no method exists to assess this compatibility before committing to expensive full-scale training and validation. Existing work treats pretext task selection as a heuristic trial-and-error process, requiring scarce labeled data for validation and incurring substantial computational cost for each candidate task.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261890/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes only 5 labeled samples per class and 50 unlabeled samples per class (e.g., 500 unlabeled total for CIFAR-10 versus 49,950 used in actual training), along with a pretext task specification defined by an augmentation type (rotation, crop, color jitter, flip, etc.) and a discrete strength parameter. It outputs three scalar scores—learnability, reliability, and completeness—combined into a single estimated target performance value via a closed-form product formula, which is then compared against actual performance obtained after full semi- or self-supervised training on the complete test set.

## Method

The paper derives a theoretical upper bound on target generalization error decomposed into three multiplicative factors: assumption learnability (the model's residual error on the pretext task), assumption reliability (the probability that the assumption holds for the true data distribution), and assumption completeness (the residual error after both learnability and reliability are satisfied). Empirically, learnability is estimated by training a pretext model on the small unlabeled subset and measuring its consistency violation rate; reliability is estimated by training a supervised target model on the small labeled subset as a surrogate oracle and checking whether its pseudo-labels violate the assumption on the learnable subset; completeness is estimated by measuring prediction disagreement between the pretext and target models on the reliable subset, using class-prototype alignment to map pretext embeddings to target labels. The three estimates are combined as R̂_target = 1 − (1−R̂_unlearnable)(1−R̂_unreliable)(1−R̂_incomplete), yielding a low-cost performance prediction that correlates strongly (Pearson r up to 0.95) with actual performance across 115 pretext tasks, multiple datasets, and diverse SSL algorithms.

## Results

The paper validates its three-factor estimation method (learnability, reliability, completeness) by measuring Pearson correlation between estimated and actual target performance across 115 pretext tasks. On CIFAR-10, correlations reach 0.785 (semi-supervised) and 0.820 (self-supervised); on CIFAR-100, they rise to 0.905 and 0.950; on ImageNet-200, they are 0.675 and 0.678. The estimation requires only 5 labeled and 50 unlabeled samples per class, consuming ~29.6 seconds on a single A800 GPU versus ~1547–1652 seconds for full training and validation (a ~50× speedup). Ablation confirms all three indicators contribute: removing any one drops correlation by 0.05–0.60.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing estimation methods; its validation is against ground-truth performance obtained via full semi/self-supervised training and test-set evaluation. The key finding is that the three-factor estimate (learnability × reliability × completeness) tracks actual performance with strong correlation (0.67–0.95) across all tested datasets, backbones, algorithms, and hyperparameter settings. Among the 115 pretext tasks, the estimation correctly ranks them in near-synchronized fashion for both semi-supervised and self-supervised training, indicating that pretext-task benefit is largely independent of the training strategy. The ablation (Tables 2–3) shows that reliability and completeness individually achieve correlations of ~0.70–0.73, while learnability alone is weak (0.19–0.22); combining all three yields the best correlation (0.785/0.820 on CIFAR-10). The method's main limitation is that correlation drops for ResNet-50 (0.478 semi-supervised) and for some self-supervised algorithms (MoCo at 0.453), suggesting the estimation is most reliable for ViT backbones and consistency-based semi-supervised methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate theoretical traditions: neuro-symbolic (NeSy) learning, where prior knowledge is assumed fully reliable, and semi/self-supervised learning (SSL), where pretext tasks encode probabilistic assumptions about data invariance. By extending the NeSy generalization framework to handle knowledge that holds only with certain probability, the authors derive a three-factor decomposition (learnability, reliability, completeness) that unifies both settings. Conceptually, the work departs from prior SSL robustness/safety analyses (which diagnose *why* unlabeled data can hurt) by providing a *quantitative, pre-training* estimation of target performance, and from prior pretext-task misalignment studies (Kundu et al., Kornblith et al.) by grounding the evaluation in a formal error decomposition rather than empirical probing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: the proposed estimation requires only 5 labeled and 50 unlabeled samples per class and ~30 seconds of compute, versus ~1500+ seconds and 10,000 validation labels for full training-and-validation, enabling rapid screening of pretext tasks in resource-constrained settings. The theoretical unification of NeSy and SSL under a probabilistic-knowledge framework opens a path for importing other NeSy tools (e.g., knowledge completeness verification, conflict detection) into SSL. Key limitations include reliance on a surrogate oracle model trained on a tiny labeled set (introducing approximation error in the reliability estimate), restriction to consistency/contrastive-style pretext tasks, and the fact that correlation, while strong, is not perfect (ranging from 0.45 to 0.95 across settings), leaving room for improved estimators.
