## Problem

Spatial causal inference faces two intertwined challenges—unmeasured spatial confounders (e.g., weather, pollution transport) and interference from neighboring treatments—that existing methods address separately but rarely jointly. Interference-based estimators assume all confounders are observed, while spatial-confounding methods assume away spillovers or absorb them implicitly. The paper shows these challenges are deeply connected: localized interference reveals structure in the latent confounder, enabling nonparametric identification of both direct and spillover effects without multiple treatment types or a known latent-field model.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a rectangular spatial lattice (e.g., 0.25°×0.25° grid cells) where each site provides local and neighborhood covariates, a binary treatment assignment, and a continuous outcome. The encoder consumes the joint vector of own and neighbor treatments together with local and neighborhood covariates as a 'multi-cause' signal, while the grid Laplacian and neighborhood radius r parameterize the spatial prior. Outputs are a smooth substitute confounder Z_s (a piecewise constant function of neighborhood exposure and covariates), a direct effect estimate τ_dir, and a spillover effect estimate τ_spill, with uncertainty bands obtainable by drawing multiple Z_s from the posterior.

## Method

The Spatial Deconfounder is a two-stage framework. Stage 1 trains a conditional VAE whose encoder maps (A_s, A_Ns, X_s, X_Ns) to a latent embedding Z_s, with a decoder predicting A_s given covariates and Z_s, and a Gaussian-Markov random-field prior N(0, τ⁻¹(L+εI)⁻¹) on Z enforcing spatial smoothness via the grid Laplacian; training minimizes a reconstruction loss plus KL divergence with warm-up. Stage 2 feeds the point estimate Ẑ_s into a flexible spatial outcome model (e.g., U-Net or GNN) trained by supervised regression on (A_s, A_Ns, X_s, X_Ns, Ẑ_s) → Y_s, then estimates effects via plug-in contrasts that vary own or neighbor treatments. The key insight is that interference itself supplies the multi-cause structure deconfounders require: own and neighboring treatments are jointly shaped by the same latent spatial field, so fitting a factor model to their joint distribution recovers a substitute confounder that restores ignorability. The paper proves identification of direct and spillover effects under localized interference, a latent-field sufficiency assumption, and separable outcome structure, without requiring a parametric latent-field model.

## Results

Under local confounding (Table 1), C-VAE-SPATIAL+(R=1) achieves the lowest spillover bias (SPILL = 0.02) across both PM2.5→mortality environments and ties for the best direct-effect bias (DIR = 0.02) in the PM2.5→m setting, while also reporting predictive p-values near 0.5 (0.51–0.53). Under spatial confounding (Table 2), C-VAE-UNET(R=2) achieves DIR = 0.04 and SPILL = 0.07 on PM2.5→mortality (ρpop), improving over the unconfounded UNET baseline (DIR = 0.06, SPILL = 0.17) and matching or exceeding S2SLS-LAG1 and DURBIN on direct effects. Across all environments, the method is the only baseline that simultaneously estimates both direct and spillover effects while adjusting for hidden spatial confounding.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The Spatial Deconfounder (C-VAE-SPATIAL+ / C-VAE-UNET) is compared against 11 baselines spanning spatial econometrics (S2SLS-LAG1, DURBIN, GMERROR), spline/residualization (SPATIAL, SPATIAL+), exposure-mapping variants (E-MAP-SPATIAL, E-MAP-SPATIAL+), graph neural networks (GCNN), proximity matching (DAPSM), and a U-Net without deconfounding. On direct effects under local confounding, the method ties SPATIAL and E-MAP-SPATIAL at DIR = 0.02 and beats DAPSM (0.25) and GCNN (0.36) by an order of magnitude. On spillover effects, C-VAE-SPATIAL+(R=1) achieves SPILL = 0.02, substantially outperforming DURBIN and S2SLS-LAG1 (0.07) and E-MAP variants (0.07). Under spatial confounding, C-VAE-UNET(R=2) improves over the plain UNET baseline (DIR 0.06→0.04, SPILL 0.17→0.07) and matches or beats S2SLS-LAG1 on direct effects, though DURBIN achieves a lower SPILL (0.01 vs. 0.07) in the SO4→PM2.5 setting. DAPSM and GCNN are consistently the weakest baselines (DIR up to 1.57), confirming that neither matching nor raw graph aggregation handles hidden spatial confounding well.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the deconfounder framework (Wang & Blei, 2019), which recovers latent confounders from the joint distribution of multiple simultaneous treatments, by showing that spatial interference itself generates the required multi-cause structure: a unit's own treatment plus its neighbors' treatments are all shaped by the same latent spatial field. The Stage 1 architecture is a C-VAE (Sohn et al., 2015) augmented with a Gaussian-Markov random-field prior on the latent code, enforcing spatial smoothness without specifying a parametric latent-field model. The paper departs from spatial interference methods (Hudgens & Halloran, 2008; Forastiere et al., 2021) that assume all confounders are observed, and from spatial confounding methods (Dupont et al., 2022; Papadogeorgou et al., 2019) that assume away interference, by addressing both simultaneously in a single nonparametric framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The joint treatment of interference and unobserved spatial confounding fills a genuine gap: prior methods handle one while assuming away the other, and the paper demonstrates that ignoring either leads to biased estimates in realistic environmental-health settings. Key limitations include the separability assumption on the outcome model, the latent-field sufficiency condition (no purely local unobserved confounders), and reliance on C-VAE consistency that is assumed rather than proven in full generality. Open questions include extensions to spatiotemporal settings, continuous treatments, and richer uncertainty quantification beyond the plug-in posterior draws. The stress tests honestly reveal that the method degrades under highly localized confounding or weak overlap, delineating its practical applicability.
