## Problem

Momentum SGD uses a single fixed momentum coefficient across all parameters, which fails to handle the highly anisotropic curvature of modern neural network loss landscapes, creating a persistent performance gap with Adam on transformer architectures. The paper asks whether this gap can be closed by adapting the damping (friction) mechanism on a per-parameter basis, without resorting to Adam's explicit per-parameter learning rates.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262057/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The optimizers consume the standard loss gradient and current model parameters at each step, producing updated parameters alongside optimizer state vectors. iKFAD maintains a per-parameter adaptive friction vector ξ (an exponentially weighted average of past kinetic energies) in addition to position and momentum, while CD adds only a scalar cubic damping coefficient c to the standard mSGD state. CADAM retains Adam's second-moment estimate ζ and adds the cubic damping term, so its memory footprint matches Adam's 3N states. Discretization is performed via first-order Lie-Trotter operator splitting with closed-form sub-step updates rather than a single Euler step.

## Method

The paper reframes momentum-based optimization as a dissipative Hamiltonian system where the friction coefficient γ controls kinetic energy, then replaces the constant γ with a coordinate-wise adaptive term. iKFAD introduces a per-parameter friction ξ that tracks an exponentially weighted average of past kinetic energies [p]², automatically increasing damping when momenta grow large and relaxing it when they shrink. In the near-equilibrium regime (ξ̇≈0), iKFAD reduces to a cubic damping law, motivating the simpler CD method that directly adds a −c[p]³ term to the momentum equation. CADAM augments the continuous-time Adam dynamics with the same cubic damping. All three schemes are discretized via operator splitting into transport, gradient, damping, and adaptive sub-steps with analytical updates, and the paper proves exponential convergence for iKFAD and CD under strong convexity, both in continuous time and for sufficiently small discrete step sizes.

## Results

Across six transformer-based benchmarks (TinyViT/CIFAR-10, DistilBERT/SST-2, DistilBERT/QNLI, GPT2-Nano/Shakespeare, GPT2-XS/OpenWebText, GPT2-S/OpenWebText), iKFAD and CD achieve test losses competitive with or slightly better than Adam while substantially closing the Adam–mSGD gap. On DistilBERT SST-2, CD (0.268) and iKFAD (0.276) outperform Adam (0.299) by 3–10%; on GPT2-S (123M), both CD (3.274) and iKFAD (3.263) beat Adam (3.301). mSGD consistently lags Adam by 4–12% in test loss on transformer tasks, whereas iKFAD and CD reduce this gap to near zero or reverse it.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against **Adam** (Kingma & Ba, 2015), iKFAD and CD match or slightly outperform on most transformer benchmarks: CD beats Adam by 3.1% on DistilBERT SST-2 (0.268 vs 0.299) and by 0.8% on GPT2-S (3.274 vs 3.301), while iKFAD edges out Adam on TinyViT (0.612 vs 0.613) and GPT2-Nano (1.641 vs 1.647). On GPT2-XS, CD (3.691) and iKFAD (3.693) both beat Adam (3.710). Against **mSGD** (Poljak, 1964), the improvement is dramatic on transformer tasks: mSGD trails Adam by 4–12% in test loss (e.g., 1.784 vs 1.647 on GPT2-Nano), while iKFAD and CD close this gap entirely. Against the predecessor **KFAD** (Karoni et al., 2023), which uses a single global adaptive friction coefficient, iKFAD achieves substantially lower test loss (0.612 vs 0.642 on TinyViT; 1.641 vs 1.736 on GPT2-Nano), confirming that per-parameter friction adaptation is critical for transformer architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Friction-Adaptive Descent (FAD) framework of Karoni et al. (2023) from a single global adaptive friction coefficient to per-parameter individual friction coefficients (iKFAD), and introduces a cubic damping term into both mSGD (CD) and Adam (CADAM) continuous-time dynamics. The key conceptual bridge is the demonstration that iKFAD's adaptive friction, in the near-equilibrium regime, reduces to a cubic damping term — linking a thermostat-inspired optimization mechanism to a well-studied vibration-suppression technique from structural dynamics. The paper also draws on the LDHD (Linearly Dissipative Hamiltonian Dynamics) framework to formalize the equivalence between discrete momentum and continuous-time friction, and positions its methods against momentum restart schemes (O'Donoghue & Candès) and adaptive Langevin thermostats (Ding et al., Shang et al.) as a continuous, per-coordinate alternative to discrete resets or global thermostats.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in CD (with γ=0) matching or exceeding Adam on transformer benchmarks while retaining the same 2N memory footprint and hyperparameter count as standard mSGD — a potentially attractive drop-in replacement for settings where Adam's 3N state and per-parameter learning rates are undesirable. However, the performance margins over Adam are modest (often within one standard deviation), the largest model tested is only 123M parameters, and the cubic damping coefficient c and iKFAD's ρ vary by several orders of magnitude across tasks, indicating unresolved sensitivity to gradient scale. Open questions include whether these methods scale to billion-parameter LLMs, whether gradient normalization can eliminate the hyperparameter sensitivity, and whether the lack of a convergence proof for CADAM reflects a fundamental limitation of combining cubic damping with Adam's adaptive learning rates.
