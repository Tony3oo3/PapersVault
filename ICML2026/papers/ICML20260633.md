## Problem

State-of-the-art MLLMs achieve strong scores on knowledge-intensive benchmarks (e.g., MMMU, HLE) yet consistently fail on basic visual tasks—such as tracing curves through intersections, counting 3D blocks under occlusion, or detecting subtle shape differences—that 3-year-old children solve effortlessly. No existing benchmark systematically isolates these pre-linguistic visual primitives from linguistic or domain knowledge, leaving a blind spot in how we measure foundational visual competence.

## Input / Output

BabyVision presents 388 image-question pairs (135 multiple-choice, 253 fill-in-the-blank) where questions average 25.9 words and are tightly grounded in visual content to minimize language-based shortcuts; models output a text answer scored by an LLM-as-judge (Qwen3-Max) for semantic equivalence. BabyVision-GEN adapts 280 of these questions for generation models, which receive a 22.9-word prompt and must produce an annotated image (circles, lines, arrows, or text overlaid on the original) rather than a verbal answer, evaluated by Gemini-3-Flash comparing the generated annotation against a human-annotated ground-truth solution image.

## Method

The benchmark is constructed through a three-stage curation pipeline grounded in developmental psychology: (1) defining four categories and 22 subtypes from children's visual development literature and selecting ~100 seed images; (2) expanding to ~4,000 candidates via reverse image search and keyword retrieval with strict copyright and content filtering; (3) manual annotation with double-blind expert review ensuring answers are derivable from visual analysis rather than language. The paper's central analytical contribution is identifying the 'verbalization bottleneck'—MLLMs compress visual input into linguistic tokens before reasoning, discarding fine-grained geometric and topological information—and validating it causally via a CoT ablation (removing chain-of-thought yields +1.3% overall accuracy and a 5.28× increase in image-token attention) and an RLVR experiment (GRPO fine-tuning of Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking on 1,400 examples yields +4.8% overall but degrades Visual Tracking, confirming that language-mediated reasoning cannot recover continuous perceptual tracking). BabyVision-GEN further tests whether visual generation models can bypass this bottleneck by reasoning directly in pixel space, with an automatic evaluation toolkit achieving 96.1% agreement with human judgments.

## Results

On BabyVision (388 questions, 22 subtypes across 4 visual domains), the best proprietary model Gemini3-Pro-Preview achieves 49.7% Avg@3 accuracy, falling 44.4 points below the adult human baseline of 94.1%. GPT-5.2 scores 34.4%, Doubao-1.8 scores 30.2%, while Qwen3-VL-Plus (19.2%), Grok-4 (16.2%), and Claude-4.5-Opus (14.2%) trail further behind. The largest deficits appear in Visual Tracking (best: 43.4%) and Spatial Perception (best: 53.7%), where models exhibit systematic failures such as losing track of curves through intersections and inability to perform 3D mental transformations. On the generative extension BabyVision-GEN (280 questions), the best image generator NanoBanana-Pro reaches only 18.3% overall accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gemini3-Pro-Preview leads all proprietary models at 49.7% overall but remains 44.4 points below the 94.1% human baseline, and approximately 20 points below typical 6-year-old children on the BabyVision-Mini subset. GPT-5.2 (34.4%) and Doubao-1.8 (30.2%) form a second tier, with GPT-5.2 notably strong on Visual Pattern Recognition (54.9%, matching Gemini3-Pro-Preview) but weak on Visual Tracking (34.9%). Among open-source models, Kimi-K2.5 achieves the best score at 32.1%, still 17.6 points behind Gemini3-Pro-Preview, while Qwen3VL-235B-Thinking reaches 22.2% and InternVL3.5-241B reaches 19.2%. A striking finding is that most frontier MLLMs perform at or below the level of 3-year-old children (40.0% on BabyVision-Mini), despite achieving PhD-level scores on knowledge-intensive benchmarks like MMMU (where Gemini 3 Pro attains 92.8%). The RLVR fine-tuning experiment shows that GRPO training on Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking yields only +4.8 points overall and actually degrades Visual Tracking (−1.2 points), confirming that language-mediated reasoning cannot resolve the core perceptual deficit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by grounding a visual-reasoning benchmark in developmental psychology (core knowledge hypothesis, infant perception literature) rather than in expert-level academic tasks. It explicitly departs from the dominant MLLM benchmark lineage (MMMU, HLE, MathVista, BLINK, MMVP, SpatialViz-Bench), which the authors argue tests knowledge-intensive or semantic-recognition skills while leaving pre-linguistic visual primitives unmeasured. The BABYVISION-GEN extension repurposes the visual generation literature (Bagel, Sora 2, Veo 3) as an alternative reasoning pathway, and the RLVR experiment applies GRPO-based reinforcement learning—previously successful for language reasoning—to probe whether language-mediated training can close the perceptual gap.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 44.4% human–model gap and the finding that frontier MLLMs perform at or below 3-year-old level on basic visual tasks provide a sharp diagnostic that existing knowledge-heavy benchmarks mask. The verbalization bottleneck hypothesis, supported by CoT ablation (+1.3% without CoT) and attention probing (5.28× vision-token attention increase), suggests that the LLM paradigm's reliance on linguistic intermediates is a structural limitation for continuous perceptual reasoning, not merely a data or scale issue. Open questions include whether unified generation architectures that preserve visual representations throughout reasoning can close this gap, whether the bottleneck is fundamental to autoregressive token prediction, and whether the 388-question benchmark is sufficiently diverse to capture the full space of early-vision competence. The generation-based evaluation (BABYVISION-GEN) remains at very low accuracy (18.3% best), indicating that 'thinking in pixels' is still far from reliable.
