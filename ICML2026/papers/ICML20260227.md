## Problem

Existing memorisation detection in LLMs relies on string-matching metrics (Levenshtein, Indel) that severely undercount the amount of proprietary alignment data a model can regurgitate, by at least an order of magnitude. This paper asks whether open-weight models leak their post-training (SFT and RL) data in a way that is semantically meaningful even when not verbatim, and whether such extracted data can be repurposed to retrain other models, thereby eroding the competitive advantage of the original data owner.

## Input / Output

The method requires an open-weight LLM whose post-training dataset is publicly available (OLMo 2 for SFT, Open-Reasoner-Zero for RL), the model's chat template prefix tokens (e.g., `<|user|>\n`), and the full post-training dataset to build a vector search index. It produces a set of synthetic generations scored by embedding similarity against the training set, a per-sample memorisation verdict at a 0.95 embedding threshold, and a filtered retraining dataset (~930k SFT samples or ~57k RL samples) that can directly post-train a base model. Unlike prior extraction work that outputs a boolean extractability flag, the output here is a continuous semantic similarity score per generation paired with its nearest training neighbour.

## Method

The core mechanism exploits the fact that chat template special tokens are introduced exclusively during post-training, so conditioning on them steers the model toward the alignment data distribution. The pipeline embeds the entire post-training set with gemini-embedding-001 to form a vector search index, then repeatedly prompts the model with the chat template prefix (temperature 1) to generate samples; each generation is embedded and matched against the index via a single matrix multiplication (dot product on unit-norm vectors). The key methodological contribution is replacing string-matching thresholds (0.90 Levenshtein/Indel) with a 0.95 embedding-similarity threshold, which the authors show recovers roughly 10× more memorised samples and correctly flags cases where trivial numerical or formatting differences deflate string scores. The paper further demonstrates that the extracted synthetic data, after basic Gemini 2.5 filtering, can SFT or RL-train a base model to recover most of the original post-trained model's benchmark performance, and that RL (PPO) training increases the likelihood of training prompts by up to six orders of magnitude despite the objective not explicitly maximising sequence likelihood.

## Results

On SFT (Table 2), the model trained on extracted synthetic data achieves comparable or better performance than the original OLMo 2 SFT baseline on 5 of 8 benchmarks: BBH (0.516 vs 0.495), GSM8K (0.785 vs 0.741), POPQA (0.249 vs 0.236), TQA (0.553 vs 0.486), and DROP (0.592 vs 0.596), while underperforming on MATH (0.171 vs 0.207) and notably on IFEval (0.503 vs 0.656). On RL (Figure 8), the model post-trained with Dr. GRPO on the extracted 57k synthetic samples recovers most of the performance of the original ORZ-trained baseline across AMC, MATH500, Minerva Math, and OlympiadBench. A key methodological finding is that string matching (Levenshtein/Indel at 0.9 threshold) undercounts memorisation by at least 10× compared to gemini-embedding-001 scores (0.95 threshold), with embedding scores revealing a substantial fraction of the 1M generations as semantically memorised.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the OLMo 2 SFT baseline (OLMo et al., 2024), the extracted-data model wins on BBH (+0.021), GSM8K (+0.044), POPQA (+0.013), and TQA (+0.067), ties on DROP (−0.004), and loses on MMLU (−0.008), MATH (−0.037), and IFEval (−0.153). The IFEval gap is attributed to the extraction pipeline generating too few instruction-following-specific examples. Against the Open-Reasoner-Zero RL baseline (Hu et al., 2025), the model trained on the extracted 57k synthetic RL samples achieves broadly comparable scores on all four math benchmarks (AMC, MATH500, Minerva Math, OlympiadBench), demonstrating that a simple chat-template prompting attack on a 7B RL-trained model can recover a usable RL training set. The paper also shows that conditioning on the full chat template (⟨|endoftext|⟩⟨|user|⟩\n) yields a mean best embedding score of 0.892 versus 0.857 for the bare end-of-text token, confirming the template is a more effective extraction prefix.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the training-data-extraction line of work (Carlini et al., 2021; Nasr et al., 2025) by replacing string-matching metrics with neural-embedding similarity to capture approximate *semantic* memorisation, building directly on the Magpie observation (Xu et al., 2024) that chat-template prefixes elicit alignment-like generations. It departs from prior memorisation studies that treat verbatim or near-verbatim reproduction as the unit of analysis, arguing instead that semantically equivalent samples should count as memorised for the purpose of measuring data leakage. The RL extraction component extends the scope beyond SFT, where memorisation is well understood, into PPO/GRPO-trained models where the objective does not explicitly maximise sequence likelihood.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that string matching undercounts memorisation by at least an order of magnitude has immediate implications for how model developers assess and report data-leakage risk, and for the legal/competitive landscape around open-weight releases. The demonstration that extracted data can retrain a base model to recover meaningful performance reframes distillation as a form of indirect training-data exfiltration, raising licensing and fair-use questions. Key limitations include the requirement for open-weight access (user-controlled tokenisation) and access to the original training set for verification; the RL memorisation mechanism remains unexplained, and the threat to closed models (where chat templates are enforced server-side) is left as future work.
