## Problem

Sequential inference on conditional mean functions (CMF) and CATE in nonparametric settings with general covariate spaces remains largely unaddressed. Existing anytime-valid methods either require finite context sets, fixed sample sizes, or parametric (linear) projections of the CMF, failing to provide high-confidence conclusions during continuous monitoring when covariates are continuous or high-dimensional. This paper fills that gap by constructing the first fully nonparametric asymptotic anytime-valid tests for global null hypotheses on the CMF/CATE that accommodate arbitrary covariate spaces without discretization.

## Input / Output

The method consumes i.i.d. observations arriving sequentially: either (X_i, Y_i) pairs for CMF testing or (X_i, A_i, Y_i) triples with known propensity scores π for CATE testing, along with a prespecified null function f and hyperparameters (α, ρ, t₀, ε_t, l). At each time t ≥ t₀ it produces a binary rejection decision; the first rejection time N_f characterizes sample complexity. By inverting the family of tests over f ∈ L_∞(B), the method yields function-valued asymptotic confidence sequences C_t, though in continuous/high-dimensional settings these are typically evaluated at prespecified candidate functions rather than tracked explicitly. The one-sided design (tracking only lower bounds) is a structural consequence of the predictable weight construction.

## Method

The core mechanism is a weighted martingale ψ_t(f) = Σ w_i(X_i,f)(φ_i − f(X_i)), where φ_i are conditionally unbiased pseudo-outcomes for the CMF/CATE and the predictable weights w_i = sign(τ̂_i − f)·max{ε_i, |τ̂_i − f|/v̂_i} use online F_{i-1}-measurable regressors τ̂_i and v̂_i for the CMF and conditional variance. The sign and inverse-variance structure of the weights guarantees nonnegative conditional drift under the alternative (τ ≠ f), justifying one-sided lower-bound tracking via a Gaussian mixture martingale bound L_t(f,α,ρ) derived from the Strassen invariance principle. Asymptotic anytime-valid error control holds under mere boundedness of nuisances (no convergence required), power one follows from a mild positive-covariance condition on the limiting weight function, and under full consistency of the sequential regressors the rejection time achieves the optimal Γ^{-1}(τ,f)·log(1/α) sample complexity matching the parametric Gaussian shift lower bound. Flexible nonparametric regressors (neural networks, k-NN, random forests) can be plugged in as the online estimators, and the framework extends to CATE via inverse-propensity-scored pseudo-outcomes.

## Results

Across four synthetic DGP1 setups (Null, Step, Bump, Sine) and a real-world DGP2 setup (French Jobs RCT, 33,797 observations), GAAVI maintains type-I error at the nominal α=0.1 level under continuous monitoring with burn-in times as small as t₀=250 (synthetic) and t₀=2000 (real-world). Under alternatives, GAAVI achieves either the highest or second-highest rejection probability by T=10,000 across all examples uniformly over t≥3000. The F-test baseline reaches near-certain rejection by t=1000 on the Step example but drops below 0.2 rejection probability by T=10,000 on Bump and Sine, while the binning baseline fails to reject entirely in some settings (e.g., Step with 8 bins in Jobs) due to within-bin signal cancellation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares GAAVI against two baselines: (1) the asymptotic anytime-valid F-test of Lindon et al. (2025), which tests linear regression coefficients, and (2) Bonferroni-corrected asymptotic AV tests across binned conditional means (Waudby-Smith et al., 2024; Chugg et al., 2025a). GAAVI wins decisively on non-linear alternatives: on the Bump and Sine synthetic examples, the F-test's rejection probability falls below 0.2 by T=10,000 because linear projections of X cannot capture the non-linear shape of τ, whereas GAAVI (using a 3-layer neural network for τ̂_t) adapts to the function's shape and rejects with probability near 1. On the Step example and the French Jobs dataset, where linear structure is present, the F-test is competitive (rejection near 1 by t=1000 on Step), but GAAVI still matches or slightly exceeds it. The binning baseline outperforms all methods only when bins align perfectly with homogeneous regions of τ(x)−f(x) (e.g., Bin(8) on Sine), but suffers severe power loss from multiple-testing corrections at higher bin counts (Bin(16) on Jobs) and from signal cancellation when bins straddle regions where τ(x)−f(x) changes sign (Step, Bin(8) on Jobs). GAAVI avoids both issues by tracking a single weighted test statistic with adaptively estimated weights, requiring no discretization or multiple-testing correction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the asymptotic anytime-valid inference framework of Waudby-Smith et al. (2024) and Bibaut et al. (2024), which previously focused on point null tests for univariate mean parameters, to the fundamentally different problem of testing global null hypotheses on entire conditional mean functions. It departs from prior global null tests (Fisher's F-tests, higher criticism, max tests) by being fully nonparametric, sequential, and applicable to general covariate spaces without discretization or parametric projection. The closest prior work—Lindon et al. (2025) for continuous contexts and Chugg et al. (2025b) for finite contexts—either restricts to linear regression coefficients or requires finite conditioning sets, leaving the nonparametric sequential global null problem for general covariates unaddressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GAAVI enables practitioners to continuously monitor conditional mean functions (e.g., CATE in clinical trials, fairness metrics in algorithmic auditing) and halt experiments with high confidence at any time beyond a burn-in, without pre-specifying sample sizes or discretizing covariate spaces. The theoretical result that nonparametric sequential testing achieves the same asymptotic sample complexity as parametric Gaussian testing with known variance is a clean optimality statement that simplifies the design space for sequential experiments. Key limitations include the bounded-outcome assumption, the i.i.d. requirement (no nonstationarity), and the need for a burn-in period; open directions include extending to time-varying distributions, composite nulls, and relaxing boundedness to moment conditions.
