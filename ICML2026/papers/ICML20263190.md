## Problem

The Bertrand paradox asks why real markets sustain prices well above marginal cost when classical one-shot analysis predicts prices collapse to cost. While prior work showed a high-utility coarse correlated equilibrium (CCE) exists for linear demand in the continuous setting, it remained open whether such equilibria exist for arbitrary non-increasing demand functions in the discrete price setting, how transaction prices scale with the number of firms, and whether a single no-swap-regret learner suffices to drive prices down against a no-external-regret opponent.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a discrete price grid {1/k, 2/k, …, 1}, an arbitrary non-increasing demand function f: P → [0,1], per-player marginal costs c_i, and a deviation class Φ_i (constant maps for CCE, all maps for CE, or mixed for Φ-CE). The output is a set of equilibrium existence and impossibility theorems: explicit utility lower bounds (e.g., ≥ 1/(4e²) of monopoly utility under CCE), upper bounds on transaction prices (≤ c + 2/k under CE), and an exponential decay rate e^{1−n/2} on total utility as the number of firms n grows. Experiments compute exact best-CCE utilities via numerical optimization over the k-dimensional price grid for k up to 100 and n up to 10.

## Method

The paper introduces a unified Φ-correlated equilibrium framework that interpolates between CCE (constant deviation maps) and CE (all deviation maps), then characterizes attainable equilibria under each. For the CCE lower bound (Theorem 2.1), they construct a symmetric equilibrium where both players draw the same price from a distribution whose tail probabilities τ_i = B/S_i are tied to the running maximum S_i of per-unit margins, using harmonic-number inequalities to show expected utility is at least 1/(4e²) of monopoly optimum. For the CE upper bound (Theorem 2.2), a key swap-deviation lemma (Lemma A.1) shows any price above c + 2/k can be profitably swapped downward, forcing all CE support within two price steps of marginal cost. For the mixed Φ-CE construction (Theorem 2.3), they design a specific marginal distribution p_j = M/(j(j+1)) for the no-swap-regret player and a conditional response rule for the no-external-regret player, verifying both regret constraints via telescoping sums and harmonic number lower bounds. The n-firm exponential decay (Theorem 2.5) follows from averaging the CCE no-deviation constraint over all players to bound Pr(min X_j > c+a)·f(c+a) ≤ min{f(c+a), W/(na)}, then optimizing the resulting integral bound over W/n.

## Results

In the symmetric-cost duopoly, Theorem 2.1 shows that for any non-increasing demand function, a CCE exists where each player achieves at least (1/(4e²)) ≈ 5.4% of the maximum monopoly utility, meaning no-external-regret learners can sustain high transaction prices. In contrast, Theorem 2.2 proves that under any correlated equilibrium (i.e., no-swap-regret learning), each player's utility is bounded by f(c+1/k)/k, which vanishes as the price grid k grows. Theorem 2.5 establishes that the total utility across all n players in any CCE decays exponentially as n(1−c)f(c+1/k)·e^(1−n/2), becoming negligible beyond ~5 firms. Numerical experiments confirm the duopoly CCE-to-monopoly utility ratio converges to approximately 1/e ≈ 0.368 as k increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without direct algorithmic baselines; the central comparison is between equilibrium notions induced by different regret guarantees. The key finding is a sharp dichotomy: no-external-regret learners (converging to CCE) can sustain high transaction prices for any non-increasing demand function (Theorem 2.1), while no-swap-regret learners (converging to CE) always drive prices to marginal cost (Theorem 2.2). A surprising intermediate result (Theorem 2.3) shows that a single no-swap-regret learner is *not* sufficient to drive prices down when facing a no-external-regret opponent—high prices can still be sustained under a mixed Φ-correlated equilibrium. In the n≥3 oligopoly, however, just two no-swap-regret learners are sufficient to drive all players' utilities to zero (Theorem 2.4). The exponential decay result (Theorem 2.5) extends prior observations by Collina et al. (2025) and Arunachaleswaran et al. (2024), who studied only constant demand with zero costs, to arbitrary non-increasing demand functions and non-zero marginal costs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on equilibrium analysis in Bertrand pricing games under no-regret learning from the continuous-price, specific-demand-function setting (Nadav & Piliouras 2010; Jann & Schottmuller 2015) to the discrete-price, arbitrary non-increasing demand function setting with general marginal costs. It builds most directly on Arunachaleswaran et al. (2024), who studied the discrete Bertrand duopoly with constant demand and zero costs, by removing both restrictions and introducing a unified Φ-correlated equilibrium framework that interpolates between CCE and CE. The paper also connects to the broader algorithmic collusion literature (Calvano et al. 2020; Collina et al. 2025) by providing theoretical guarantees on when no-regret learners can sustain supra-competitive prices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean separation of concerns: external regret permits high-price equilibria for any demand function, while swap regret eliminates them, yet a single swap-regret learner is not always sufficient to drive prices down (Theorem 2.3). The exponential decay of achievable utility with the number of firms (Theorem 2.5) has direct implications for market design and antitrust policy in algorithmic pricing. Key open questions remain: which specific no-regret learning dynamics (beyond the regret guarantee) determine whether high prices are sustained, and why do standard no-swap-regret learners in experiments converge to equilibria where transaction prices fall below the higher-cost firm's marginal cost, a phenomenon the theory does not yet explain.
