## Problem

Prevailing best-of-N ensemble methods for LLM reasoning rely on external signals such as token probabilities, entropy, or self-evaluations to score candidate responses, yet these signals are poorly calibrated after post-training and require comparable textual outputs. The paper asks whether the model's internal neuron activations encode richer, more reliable correctness signals that can guide response selection without ground truth or text-level matching.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260612/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Given a text prompt, the method generates n=64 sampled reasoning trajectories (temperature 0.6, top-p 0.9) and computes per-trajectory activated neuron sets from SwiGLU FFN contributions using a per-token top-64 threshold (k=500 across layers). The output is a single selected response, and under the early-stopping variant, the selection is made after only the first B=32 tokens, terminating all other trajectories and resuming generation from the chosen one. Unlike text-matching baselines, NAD operates entirely on internal activation vectors, making it applicable to open-ended code generation where majority voting is infeasible.

## Method

Neuron Agreement Decoding (NAD) exploits two empirical findings: correct responses activate substantially fewer unique neurons than incorrect ones, and correct responses exhibit stronger cross-sample agreement in their activated neuron sets. For each sampled trajectory, the method computes a set of (neuron, layer) pairs whose SwiGLU contribution exceeds a threshold, then builds a pairwise Jaccard similarity matrix over the n trajectories. Selection is performed via one of four structure-discovery strategies—kNN-agreement (top-k similarity sum), global medoid (maximizing total similarity), DBSCAN (largest cluster medoid), or MinAct (fewest activated neurons)—optionally preceded by a DeepConf tail-token confidence filter that prunes the bottom 30% of trajectories. The early-stopping variant applies the same selection after the first 32-token chunk, reducing token consumption by roughly two orders of magnitude while maintaining or improving accuracy over full-sequence baselines.

## Results

On Qwen3-4B-Think, NAD-kNN† achieves 85.0% on AIME24+25 and 71.7% on GPQA, with an overall average of 80.5% across all five benchmarks, surpassing the Avg@64 baseline (76.7%) by 3.8 points. On R1-Qwen3-8B, NAD-Medoid† reaches 85.0% on AIME24+25 and 68.7% on GPQA (78.8% average), outperforming Avg@64 (73.0%) by 5.8 points. On code generation benchmarks where majority voting is inapplicable, NAD variants consistently beat Avg@64 by 2–4 points (e.g., NAD-Medoid† achieves 95.7% HumanEval and 59.3% LCBv5 vs. 92.8% and 57.7% for Avg@64 on R1-Qwen3-8B). With early stopping at 32 tokens, NAD reduces token consumption by ~98% (e.g., from 55.2M to 1.3M on AIME24+25) while maintaining or improving accuracy over random sampling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Self-Consistency (Cons@64), NAD-kNN† is competitive on AIME24+25 (85.0 vs. 86.7 for Qwen3-4B-Think) but uniquely extends to code generation benchmarks where majority voting is inapplicable, achieving 97.0% HumanEval and 62.3% LCBv5. Compared to DeepConf, NAD-kNN† matches or exceeds it on GPQA (71.7 vs. 70.7 for Qwen3-4B-Think; 68.7 vs. 67.7 for R1-Qwen3-8B) while also covering code tasks. Against Self-Certainty, NAD variants show clear advantages (e.g., 85.0 vs. 76.7 on AIME for Qwen3-4B-Think; 66.2 vs. 61.1 on GPQA for Qwen3-4B-Instruct). Versus Short-1@64, NAD methods outperform on most benchmarks (e.g., 80.5 vs. 79.1 average for Qwen3-4B-Think; 78.8 vs. 76.0 for R1-Qwen3-8B). In the TTT comparison (Appendix Table 6), NAD-kNN† achieves 66.7% on AIME24 and 50.0% on AIME25 versus TTRL's 58.9% and 37.8%, showing NAD generalizes better out-of-domain while incurring no training cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the neuron-activation formalism from Cao et al. (2025) and the tail-token confidence filtering from DeepConf (Fu et al., 2025), but departs fundamentally from the entire sample-evaluate-ensemble literature (self-consistency, self-certainty, DeepConf, Short-1) by replacing external output signals (token probabilities, entropy) with internal neuron-activation patterns as the selection criterion. The key conceptual move is demonstrating that scalar confidence metrics are low-dimensional projections of high-dimensional activation states, and that the residual information in those states—specifically sparsity and cross-sample Jaccard agreement—carries discriminative signal for correctness that external metrics miss. The method is thus positioned as a mechanistic-interpretability-informed alternative to confidence-based best-of-N selection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that correct reasoning trajectories activate substantially fewer unique neurons and exhibit stronger cross-sample agreement offers a new, label-free quality signal that enables ~99% token reduction via early stopping at 32 tokens while maintaining competitive accuracy. However, the accuracy gains over strong baselines like DeepConf are modest or even negative on several benchmarks (e.g., 85.0 vs. 88.3 on AIME24+25), and the method is restricted to SwiGLU-based architectures. Open questions include the causal mechanism linking sparsity to correctness, the interaction between the two complementary signals (sparsity vs. agreement), storage overhead for activation caches, and whether the pattern generalizes beyond the tested model families.
