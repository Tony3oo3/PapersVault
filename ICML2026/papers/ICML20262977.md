## Problem

Existing multimodal time-series forecasting benchmarks suffer from three critical flaws: they rely on small-scale or fully synthetic data that does not generalize to real-world performance, they offer a narrow and inconsistent set of textual context types, and they cannot prevent data leakage as pretrained models continuously update their training corpora. These limitations lead to misleading conclusions—for example, synthetic benchmarks like CiK severely overestimate LLM advantages over time-series foundation models, while others understate the value of textual context.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance provides a 96-step historical window of a univariate time series (daily or weekly frequency) alongside four aligned textual context channels: static metadata, calendar-derived holiday annotations, natural-language covariate summaries (mean, median, extrema, trend of related series in the same domain), and up to K=10 time-stamped events whose announcement dates strictly precede the 12-step prediction horizon. The benchmark spans 190 variables across 19 domains and multiple continents, with a rolling stride of 4 (weekly) or 12 (daily) steps yielding ~2.5K samples; outputs are point forecasts scored by geometric-mean normalized MASE and CRPS for uncertainty, with all evaluation restricted to horizons after the knowledge cutoff of every model tested.

## Method

TimesX's core technical contribution is an automated, refreshable multi-agent dataset construction pipeline (Hypothesizer–Verifier–Enricher–Synthesizer) that produces fact-checked, timestamp-aligned event contexts while enforcing strict temporal isolation to prevent leakage. The Hypothesizer iteratively proposes events to cover detected peaks in the target series using time-bounded web search; the Verifier adversarially crawls and cross-checks each claim against source URLs, discarding hallucinations and unresolved contradictions; the Enricher fills remaining information gaps under hard ±k-day search constraints; and the Synthesizer performs final adjudication, rejecting ~32% of unverified candidates. For empirical evaluation, the paper benchmarks zero-shot TFMs (TimesFM-2.5, Moirai-2.0, Sundial), LLMs (Gemini-2.0-Flash, GPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3), and four composed solutions (AVGENS, TEXTREV, CODEREV, FUNCREV) over 312K+ LLM inferences, revealing that simple average ensembles of a TFM and an LLM outperform all agentic revision methods, and that the full set of context types yields ~16% MASE improvement over metadata alone.

## Results

On TimesX (190 variables, 19 domains, evaluated after July 2024 to avoid data leakage), the best-performing method is a simple average ensemble of TimesFM-2.5 and Gemini-2.0-Flash (AVGENS), achieving a geometric-mean MASE of 0.619 and average MASE rank of 4.249. The second-best is AVGENS with GPT-4o (MASE 0.627, rank 4.735), followed by GPT-4o alone (MASE 0.643, rank 5.466). Notably, complex agentic revision methods (CODEREV, FUNCREV) perform worse than their individual components, with CODEREV achieving MASE 0.713 versus 0.645 for TimesFM-2.5 alone. The evaluation involves over 312,000 independent LLM inferences across 10 repeated runs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 13 methods across three categories: unimodal TFMs (TimesFM-2.5, Moirai-2.0, Sundial), multimodal LLMs (Gemini-2.0-Flash, GPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3), and composed agentic/ensemble solutions. A key finding is that on TimesX, the performance gap between LLMs and TFMs is far smaller than on synthetic benchmarks like CiK, where Gemini-2.0-Flash had a large lead over TimesFM-2.5; on TimesX, TimesFM-2.5 (MASE 0.645) actually outperforms Gemini-2.0-Flash (MASE 0.650) in zero-shot unimodal vs. multimodal comparison. The simple average ensemble (AVGENS) of TimesFM-2.5 and Gemini-2.0-Flash achieves the best overall MASE (0.619), outperforming both its individual components and all agentic revision methods (CODEREV at 0.713, FUNCREV at 0.720), which paradoxically degrade performance below their TFM component alone. This contrasts sharply with CiK, where CODEREV significantly outperformed both components, demonstrating that synthetic benchmarks overestimate the value of instruction-following and code-generation approaches for real-world forecasting. The paper also shows that adding all four context types (metadata, calendar, covariates, events) to Gemini-2.0-Flash reduces MASE from 0.787 (metadata only) to 0.650 (all contexts), a 16% improvement, confirming that comprehensive textual context matters for real-world forecasting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in multimodal time-series forecasting evaluation by introducing TimesX, a real-world, leakage-free benchmark that departs from prior efforts (CiK, Time-MMD, MT-Bench, MoTime) on three axes: using exclusively real data rather than synthetic series or contexts, enforcing strict temporal isolation to prevent pretraining contamination, and providing a comprehensive union of four context types (metadata, calendar, covariates, events) with a multi-agent Hypothesizer-Verifier-Enricher-Synthesizer pipeline for event construction. Conceptually, it builds on the time-series foundation model literature (TimesFM, Moirai, Sundial) and LLM-based forecasting, but its primary contribution is an evaluation framework rather than a new forecasting method. The paper explicitly positions itself as a corrective to the field's reliance on synthetic benchmarks, showing that conclusions drawn on CiK do not transfer to real-world settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that method rankings completely flip between synthetic (CiK) and real (TimesX) benchmarks is a significant warning for the field: prior conclusions about LLM superiority in context-enriched forecasting may be artifacts of synthetic data design. The practical impact is substantial — TimesX is the first refreshable, leakage-free multimodal TSF benchmark, enabling fair evaluation as model knowledge cutoffs advance. Open questions include whether the ensemble-over-agentic finding generalizes with instruction fine-tuning, whether the ICL results (Table 7) will scale to full fine-tuning, and how the benchmark performs on multivariate or lower-frequency settings. The paper's focus on zero-shot methods and univariate forecasting leaves training-based multimodal fusion as a major future direction.
