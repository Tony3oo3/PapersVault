## Problem

While studies of LLM internals show that networks parse text and build syntax-invariant semantic representations, the specific data statistics that enable parsing and the sample complexity required remain unknown. Prior theoretical work on PCFG learnability either characterized post-hoc what trained networks implement or restricted to fixed-tree grammars where parsing is unnecessary, leaving open how varying tree topology and local ambiguity affect learnability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263606/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The learner receives flat sequences of terminal symbols (sentences) of variable length between 2^L and 3^L, paired with a single root nonterminal label, with no structural annotations. The method must recover the full hierarchical parse tree (a chart of nonterminal indicators over all spans) and the production rules that generated the sentence, ultimately predicting the root class. Sentences are drawn from a Varying-Tree RHM where branching ratios of 2 or 3 at each node create both local ambiguity (overlapping rules) and, for large rule fractions, global ambiguity (multiple valid parse trees with different root labels).

## Method

The paper introduces a method-of-moments algorithm that iteratively infers production rules layer-by-layer from bottom to top using only root-to-pair and root-to-triple covariances. At each level, it thresholds the pair covariance to isolate valid binary sibling pairs, clusters their normalized vectors to recover parent groupings, then subtracts spurious binary contributions from the triple covariance to identify ternary rules. The key theoretical result is that in the asymptotic vocabulary limit, binary sibling contributions dominate the pair covariance and spurious contributions from local ambiguity vanish, enabling provable rule recovery. The sample complexity scales as (p₂²/2)^(1−L) · v · m₃ · m₂^(L−1), determined by the weakest correlations along the 'easiest' (all-binary) branches. Predictions are validated empirically on hierarchical CNNs, a vanilla transformer, and an Inside Neural Network (INN) that mirrors the parsing chart structure, all showing the predicted power-law scaling in vocabulary size.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative prediction is that the sample complexity of root classification on the Varying-Tree RHM scales as P* ∝ (p₂²/2)^(1−L) · v · m₃ · m₂^(L−1). In the low-ambiguity regime (f = 1/v), this reduces to P* ∼ v², and rescaling training curves by this prediction yields a remarkable collapse across vocabulary sizes for CNNs. In the intermediate-ambiguity regime (f = 1/4, L = 3), the predicted P* ∼ v⁵ scaling is likewise confirmed. In the high-ambiguity regime (f = 0.6, 0.8), the cross-entropy loss saturates at the analytically computed Bayes-optimal value (finite class entropy), while still following the predicted sample-size scaling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three architectures—hierarchical CNN, vanilla encoder-only Transformer, and an Inside Neural Network (INN) with explicit binary/ternary filters—on the same root-classification task with f = 1/v so that P* ∼ v². All three architectures exhibit the same predicted scaling (confirmed by collapse of rescaled learning curves for L = 2 and L = 3), but differ substantially in pre-factors: the INN, which encodes prior knowledge of the branching structure, achieves a massive gain in sample efficiency over the Transformer for L = 3, while the CNN sits in between. This confirms that the inductive bias toward the underlying tree topology (as in the INN) can dramatically reduce the constant in the sample complexity, even though the asymptotic scaling exponent is architecture-independent. No architecture fails to reach Bayes-optimal performance given sufficient data, validating the conjecture that depth ≥ L is the key requirement rather than a specific architectural choice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Random Hierarchy Model (RHM) line of work (Cagnetta et al., 2024; Cagnetta & Wyart, 2024) from fixed-tree PCFGs—where parsing is unnecessary—to varying-tree PCFGs where the learner must simultaneously infer both the production rules and the correct span assignments. The learning mechanism builds directly on the iterative covariance-clustering framework of Malach & Shalev-Shwartz (2018), adapted here to handle the two new challenges introduced by variable tree topology: unknown span boundaries and local ambiguity from overlapping binary/ternary rules. Unlike prior work that either characterized post-hoc what trained transformers implement (inside algorithm) or studied PCFG learnability under fixed syntax, this paper provides a forward-looking algorithm with provable correctness and a quantitative sample complexity prediction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first quantitative theory connecting the sample complexity of learning to parse context-free languages to specific data statistics (vocabulary size, rule density, branching probabilities), and the prediction is confirmed across CNNs, transformers, and a purpose-built Inside Neural Network. The finding that the 'easiest' tree branches (minimal ternary productions) govern the overall sample complexity provides a concrete, testable mechanism for why depth is essential. Key limitations include the uniform-depth assumption, the supervised (root-classification) framing, and the gap between the proposed method-of-moments algorithm and actual gradient descent dynamics. Extending to non-uniform depth, next-token prediction, and more structured grammars (dependency, attribute) are identified as important next steps.
