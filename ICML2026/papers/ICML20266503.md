## Problem

Commercial Opaque LLM Services (COLS) bill users for hidden reasoning tokens, agent calls, and tool invocations that are entirely invisible and unverifiable from the user's side, creating a fundamental accountability gap with no existing technical or regulatory mechanism to detect quantity inflation or silent quality downgrades. This paper formalizes the threat model and argues that auditing hidden operations must be treated as a core system-design requirement rather than an afterthought.

## Input / Output

Auditing operates under strict asymmetric information: the user observes only the prompt, final output, and provider-reported usage metadata, while the provider retains full access to internal reasoning traces and agent workflows. The three-layer architecture transforms raw execution into verifiable commitments (hashed traces, encrypted call logs, semantic embeddings) in Layer 2, which Layer 3 auditors then verify against user-side expectations. Outputs are structured audit reports containing inflation detection flags, quality downgrade indicators, and interpretable confidence metrics rather than binary pass/fail verdicts, enabling dispute resolution without exposing proprietary internals.

## Method

The paper proposes a modular three-layer auditing architecture: Layer 1 (COLS service execution), Layer 2 (secure commitment and recording via cryptographic hashes, Merkle-tree proofs, and blockchain-anchored multi-agent commitments), and Layer 3 (auditing and user verification). Within Layer 3, four complementary strategies are specified: commitment-based auditing (exemplified by CoIn, using Merkle-tree proofs to verify token counts without exposing traces), predictive auditing (exemplified by PALACE, using learned estimators to predict expected hidden token or call usage from prompt-answer pairs), behavioral and signature-based auditing (detecting model or tool substitution via calibrated prompts, response fingerprints, and timing signals), and TEE-based auditing (hardware-attested execution for high-stakes deployments). The paper formalizes a profit-driven threat model quantifying the provider's extra profit as P = (T̂_Q·T_q + Ĉ_Q·C_q) − (T_Q·Ť_q + C_Q·Ĉ_q), capturing both quantity inflation and quality downgrade incentives. Empirically, the authors demonstrate that predicting hidden reasoning token counts from observable inputs is intrinsically difficult (all classification and regression accuracies below 50% across four DeepSeek-R1 datasets), motivating the need for multi-strategy verification rather than any single auditing method.

## Results

The paper's key empirical finding is that hidden reasoning tokens in current reasoning LLM APIs exceed visible answer tokens by 25–46× (e.g., o3-mini: 46.33×, o1: 38.71×), meaning over 90% of billed tokens are never exposed to users. A proof-of-difficulty experiment using two-layer neural networks to predict reasoning token length from prompt/answer length across four DeepSeek-R1 datasets (Math, Coding, Medical, General) yields classification accuracies of 22–44% and regression accuracies (within 25% error) of 20–30%, all well below 50%, demonstrating that hidden token counts are intrinsically hard to predict from limited observable information.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper and does not propose a single method to benchmark against competing baselines; the compares_to list is empty. The empirical component serves as a proof-of-difficulty rather than a method comparison: the two-layer neural network baselines (classification and regression) are shown to fail at predicting hidden reasoning token counts, with all accuracies below 50%, motivating the need for the multi-strategy auditing framework (commitment-based, predictive, behavioral, signature-based, TEE-based) proposed in the paper. The paper positions its proposed auditing taxonomy against the status quo of no auditing, arguing that market competition, outcome-based pricing, and institutional governance alone are insufficient to address the information asymmetry between COLS providers and users.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper carves new ground by unifying several previously separate threads—commitment-based verification (CoIn, Sun et al. 2025a), predictive usage estimation (PALACE, Wang et al. 2025b), model substitution detection (Cai et al. 2025), watermarking (Kirchenbauer et al. 2023; Zhao et al. 2023), and TEE-based attestation (Schnabl et al. 2025)—into a single formalized auditing problem for Commercial Opaque LLM Services. Rather than proposing a new algorithm, it introduces the COLS abstraction, a quantitative threat model (Eq. 1), and a three-layer architectural blueprint that positions these techniques as complementary modules. The paper departs from prior work by explicitly treating both quantity inflation and quality downgrade as first-class billing risks and by arguing that no single mechanism suffices, thereby motivating a multi-strategy auditing framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper quantifies a concrete and growing transparency gap: in current reasoning LLM APIs, 96–98% of billed tokens are invisible to users (Table 2), and even supervised models trained on large-scale reasoning data cannot reliably predict hidden token counts (Table 4, all accuracies <50%). This frames auditing not as a nice-to-have but as a structural requirement for fair commercial LLM markets. Key open questions remain: how to detect adaptive inflation that is structurally valid but semantically vacuous, how to balance IP protection against verifiability in agentic multi-agent settings, and whether institutional governance (SLAs, regulatory reporting) can substitute for or must complement cryptographic and behavioral auditing. As a position paper, its impact lies in problem formalization and research agenda-setting rather than in validated solutions.
