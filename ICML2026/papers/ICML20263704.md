## Problem

Few-shot in-context learning performance depends critically on both which demonstrations are selected and their ordering, yet existing methods either score exemplars independently (ignoring compositional and ordering effects) or require intractable exhaustive enumeration over the factorial space of permutations. This paper addresses the gap by formulating demonstration selection as a sequential decision problem that jointly optimizes content and order in a tractable, query-adaptive manner.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263704/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an input query and a pool of N candidate exemplars (query-answer pairs), each represented as a 2D matrix embedding (padded token length × embedding dimension, e.g., 320 tokens × GPT-2 embedding dim) to preserve token-level sequential structure. It produces an ordered sequence of unique exemplars of variable length (up to a maximum T, default 4), where the sequence terminates adaptively when the policy selects a learned End-of-Sequence (EOS) signal rather than a candidate exemplar. The EOS embedding is initialized as the mean exemplar embedding plus a small Gaussian perturbation (σ=0.01).

## Method

AUTOSELECT formulates demonstration selection as an autoregressive sequential decision process: at each step, a ViT-based policy encodes the concatenated matrix embeddings of the query and previously selected exemplars into a contextual representation z_t, then selects the next exemplar (or EOS) via softmax over squared Frobenius-norm distances to all remaining candidates. Training uses a KL-regularized RL objective whose closed-form optimal policy induces a Plackett-Luce ranking over trajectories; the paper proves (Theorem 4.2) that minimizing a tractable policy-level cross-entropy loss over a finite trajectory collection provably upper-bounds the worst-case PL ranking discrepancy from the optimal policy. The training procedure collects variable-length trajectories (including early-terminated sub-trajectories via EOS) with sequence-level rewards computed by evaluating the downstream LLM on a small validation batch, and updates the policy using both instant trajectories and a replay buffer across 400 episodes.

## Results

Across nine diverse tasks (classification, reasoning, and math), AUTOSELECT achieves an average rank of 1.1 out of 9 methods, consistently outperforming all baselines. The headline improvement is up to 11.2% over the strongest learning-based baseline (CEIL) on Winowhy (0.657 vs. 0.591), with substantial gains on other challenging tasks: +10.1% on Epistemic_reasoning, +8.7% on Hyperbaton, +5.6% on AQuA, and +4.8% on Trec. On the saturated AGNews task, AUTOSELECT (0.845) is within 0.4% of the computationally expensive greedy-oracle (0.848) while using far fewer LLM calls. AUTOSELECT also outperforms retrieval-based and order-independent methods (BM25, Contriever, top-k, EXPLORA, CASE) by 3–11% on four reasoning tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AUTOSELECT outperforms all ten compared baselines on the majority of tasks. Against the re-ordering baseline (Lu et al., 2022), which only permutes a fixed random set, AUTOSELECT wins on all nine tasks by 3–11%, confirming that learned query-aware selection far exceeds static reordering. Versus the greedy-oracle (Zhang et al., 2022), which exhaustively evaluates every candidate at each step at enormous computational cost, AUTOSELECT matches or exceeds it on 8 of 9 tasks (e.g., 0.657 vs. 0.589 on Winowhy, 0.395 vs. 0.374 on AQuA) while being orders of magnitude cheaper. Against the three learning-based methods—ActRL (Zhang et al., 2022), CEIL (Ye et al., 2023), and EASE (Wu et al., 2024)—AUTOSELECT wins on all nine tasks, with the largest margins on reasoning tasks (e.g., +11.2% over CEIL on Winowhy, +8.7% over CEIL on Hyperbaton). In the complementary order-independent comparison (Table 2), AUTOSELECT beats BM25 (Robertson et al., 2009), Contriever (Izacard et al., 2022), top-k retrieval (Margatina et al., 2023), EXPLORA (Purohit et al., 2024), and CASE (Purohit et al., 2025) by 3–11% on four reasoning tasks, demonstrating that modeling exemplar interactions and ordering is critical. Finally, against RDES (Wang et al., 2025), a concurrent RL-based method, AUTOSELECT achieves higher accuracy on all four evaluated tasks (e.g., 0.657 vs. 0.577 on Winowhy, 0.395 vs. 0.375 on AQuA).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the KL-regularized RL framework of Peters & Schaal (2007), whose closed-form optimal policy (proportional to exp(reward/β)) directly motivates the Plackett-Luce ranking formulation used here. It departs from prior demonstration-selection methods—retrieval-based (BM25, Contriever, top-k), subset-selection (CEIL with DPPs), and MDP-based (ActRL, EASE)—by reframing the problem as an autoregressive sequential decision process analogous to LLM token generation, using a ViT-based policy over 2D matrix embeddings that preserve token-level structure. The key conceptual departure is the theoretical bridge (Theorem 4.2) showing that minimizing a tractable policy-level CE loss provably bounds the worst-case PL ranking discrepancy, giving a principled training objective where prior methods relied on heuristic rewards or exemplar-level supervision.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AUTOSELECT provides a theoretically grounded, computationally tractable alternative to exhaustive enumeration for a problem that reduces to the NP-hard Linear Ordering Problem, and its transferability results (50–100 adaptation episodes matching task-specific optimization) suggest the learned selection policy encodes generalizable reasoning patterns rather than task-specific memorization. Limitations include evaluation primarily on a 3B-parameter LLM with a fixed pool of 100 exemplars and maximum sequence length 4, leaving open questions about scaling to larger pools, longer sequences, and multi-task or cross-domain generalization. The autoregressive framing also raises the question of whether the sequential dependency structure is truly necessary or whether a well-designed parallel scoring function could capture similar compositional effects at lower inference cost.
