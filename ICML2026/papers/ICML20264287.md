## Problem

MoE architectures exhibit 'Safety Sparsity,' where safety capabilities concentrate in a small subset of experts, creating a single-point-of-failure attack surface that adversaries can exploit by steering routing away from safety-critical experts. Conventional alignment methods (SFT, DPO, RL) designed for dense models, when naively applied to MoEs, either overwrite expert specialization and degrade general utility or disrupt the learned routing topology, failing to address the structural vulnerability at its root.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained MoE LLM (e.g., DeepSeek-V2-Lite, Qwen3-30B-A3B), a 30K mixed safety/helpfulness dataset (SafeRLHF + UltraFeedback), and a 1,000-sample calibration set used to compute empirical expert activation frequencies. It produces an aligned MoE LLM in which only a selected subset of experts is fine-tuned with safety data and the router is refined via an OT-based loss, leaving all other experts and the general routing topology intact. The expert selection is not random or heuristic but is computed as a transport plan over the activation-frequency manifold, and the router loss is stream-conditioned: safety inputs receive a rectified OT target while general inputs are anchored to the frozen baseline distribution.

## Method

MESA reframes MoE safety alignment as a resource-allocation problem solved via KL-regularized Optimal Transport. First, Expert Capacity Reallocation constructs a Beta-rational cost function C(f) = 1/[(f+α_shift)(100−f)²] over percentile ranks, derived from two theorems: a routing-inertia lower bound (‖Δφ‖ ≥ Ω(p_i^{-1/2})) showing tail experts require prohibitively large router updates, and a Hessian-fragility bound (R_i = p̄_i·Λ_i → ∞ as p̄_i → 0) showing tail experts reside in sharp minima. A Sinkhorn-Knopp solver then computes the optimal transport plan π* between the empirical activation distribution and the cost matrix, selecting the top-w fraction of experts for safety fine-tuning. Second, Dynamic Routing Refinement introduces an online OT constraint during training: for safety inputs, the router is trained to match a rectified target that shifts mass toward the adapted experts; for general inputs, the cost is zeroed so the target degenerates to the frozen baseline, enforcing topological preservation. The total loss jointly updates the selected experts (via SFT) and the router (via the stream-conditioned OT loss), achieving decentralized safety coverage without global parameter perturbation.

## Results

On DeepSeek-V2-Lite, MESA achieves 95.00% on Strata and 90.90% on WildJailbreak for safety, while retaining 66.11% GSM8K and 42.07% HumanEval for utility—substantially outperforming SFT (77.70% Strata, 16.15% GSM8K) and SafeX (64.00% Strata, 63.46% GSM8K). On Qwen3-30B-A3B, MESA reaches 99.00% Strata and 97.65% WildJailbreak while maintaining 96.44% GSM8K and 94.51% HumanEval, surpassing all baselines on the safety-utility Pareto front. Against the F-SOUR routing-exploitation attack on JailbreakBench, MESA achieves 0.00% attack success rate, matching full-parameter SFT and Stair-DPO, while SafeX suffers 15.38% and GRPO 22.73%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MESA dominates all four baselines on the safety-utility trade-off. Against SFT, MESA improves Strata by 3.00 points (95.00 vs 92.00) on DeepSeek while recovering GSM8K from 16.15% to 66.11%—a 50-point gap that reveals SFT's catastrophic alignment tax on MoE architectures. Against Stair-DPO (the strongest content-level safety method), MESA gains 2.00 points on Strata (95.00 vs 93.00) on DeepSeek and 11.50 points on Qwen (99.00 vs 87.50), while GSM8K jumps from 15.54% to 66.11% on DeepSeek. Against SafeX, the only other MoE-specific method, MESA achieves a 14-point Strata advantage on DeepSeek (95.00 vs 81.00) and 8 points on Qwen (99.00 vs 91.00), confirming that topological expansion outperforms SafeX's localized additive merging. GRPO, while competitive on utility (59.06% GSM8K on DeepSeek), is the weakest on safety (64.00% Strata), suggesting RL-based alignment relies on shallow rejection heuristics that fail under adversarial pressure.

## Relation to Prior Work

MESA departs from the dominant paradigm of content-level safety alignment (SFT, DPO, GRPO) that treats all parameters uniformly, and from SafeX (Lai et al., 2025), the first MoE-specific safety method that patches existing safety-critical experts via additive weight merging without expanding the routing topology. Conceptually, the paper reframes MoE safety alignment as a resource allocation problem and imports KL-regularized Optimal Transport (Cuturi, 2013) as the selection mechanism, using a Beta-rational cost function derived from Fisher-information and Hessian-curvature analyses to identify 'shoulder' experts. This positions MESA as a structural, architecture-aware alternative to both dense-model alignment methods and the topologically static patching strategy of SafeX.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that naively applying standard alignment to MoE architectures causes catastrophic utility collapse (e.g., GSM8K dropping from 55.95% to 15.54% on DeepSeek), establishing that MoE safety alignment is not a drop-in extension of dense-model techniques. The OT-based expert reallocation offers a principled, mathematically grounded path to decentralizing safety without destroying expert specialization, and the 0% attack success rate against routing-exploitation attacks (F-SOUR) suggests topological expansion is more durable than localized patching. Key open questions include whether the theoretical Hessian-fragility bounds and OT selection strategy scale to trillion-parameter MoEs where exact curvature computation becomes intractable, and whether the shoulder-expert heuristic generalizes across architectures with fundamentally different routing mechanisms.
