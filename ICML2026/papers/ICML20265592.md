## Problem

While scaling laws have been well-characterized for language and vision models, no systematic cross-model scaling analysis exists for data-driven weather forecasting. Weather models face unique challenges—chaotic dynamics, hundreds of correlated physical variables, and heterogeneous architectures (GNNs, Transformers, Fourier operators)—that may produce scaling behaviors fundamentally different from NLP. This paper provides the first unified empirical study of how validation loss scales with model size, dataset volume, and compute budget across five state-of-the-art global weather models.

## Input / Output

All five models consume ERA5 reanalysis data at 0.25° global resolution with 6-hour timesteps (00:00, 06:00, 12:00, 18:00 UTC), using a maximal common set of surface and upper-air variables (geopotential, temperature, u/v wind, specific humidity at multiple pressure levels). They produce 6-hour forecasts evaluated on ERA5 2021 via a unified area-weighted, pressure-level-weighted MSE loss that normalizes each variable by its standard deviation. The dataset size D is measured as cumulative training samples ingested (not a fixed dataset), following the standard scaling-law convention, and the total compute exceeds 430,000 GPU hours across all experiments.

## Method

The paper trains GraphCast (GNN), AIFS (Graph Transformer), Aurora (Swin Transformer), Pangu (Swin Transformer), and SFNO (Spherical Fourier Operator) under strictly unified conditions—same data, resolution, learning rate schedules, and validation protocol—then systematically varies parameter count N, cumulative training data D, compute budget C, and model shape (width vs. depth at fixed N). Power-law fits of the form L(D)=αD⁻ᵝ, L(N)=γN⁻ᵈ, and L(C)=λC⁻ᵉ are extracted via log-log linear regression, and compute-optimal frontiers are identified by fitting parabolas to loss-vs-D curves at fixed C. Key empirical findings include: Aurora achieves the steepest data-scaling exponent (β≈0.51), GraphCast shows the best parameter efficiency but only 1.03% H100 GPU utilization versus Aurora's 37.2%, and—contrary to NLP—wider configurations consistently outperform deeper ones at matched parameter counts across all five models. Variable-specific RMSE analysis (e.g., 10m wind vs. 2m temperature) reveals heterogeneous scaling, and CRPS evaluation with 10-member ensembles confirms that probabilistic skill tracks deterministic scaling trends within 2–5%.

## Results

Across five state-of-the-art weather models trained on ERA5, Aurora achieves the strongest data-scaling behavior with β≈0.51 (a 10× increase in training data reduces validation loss by up to 3.2×), compared to AIFS (β≈0.46), Pangu (β≈0.43), GraphCast (β≈0.36), and SFNO (β≈0.34). GraphCast demonstrates the highest parameter efficiency, achieving the lowest validation loss at equivalent or smaller N, yet suffers from only 1.03% H100 GPU utilization versus Aurora's 37.2% (~36× gap). Critically, wider model configurations consistently outperform narrower ones at fixed parameter counts across all five models, a finding that contrasts with NLP scaling laws where shape matters little. Compute-optimal analysis confirms that under fixed compute budgets, allocating resources to more training data yields greater performance gains than increasing model size.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Aurora dominates on data-scaling efficiency (β=0.51 vs. 0.30–0.46 for others) and hardware utilization (37.2% H100 peak, ~36× GraphCast), and achieves the lowest RMSE for 10m wind (10U) at all dataset sizes. GraphCast is the most parameter-efficient model, reaching the lowest validation loss at equivalent or smaller N, and surpasses all other models on 2m temperature (2T) RMSE, but its GNN message-passing architecture limits it to 1.03% GPU utilization, making it impractical at scale. AIFS ranks second in data-scaling (β≈0.46) but shows only the left half of compute-optimal parabolas, indicating it is bottlenecked by insufficient training data rather than model capacity. Pangu exhibits a notable threshold effect: its parameter-scaling exponent δ is low at 15 TB but increases at 30 TB, revealing that its capacity to leverage additional parameters is gated by data volume. SFNO performs competitively even at depth=1, consistent with the broader finding that weather forecasting benefits more from width (representational capacity) than depth (nonlinear transformations), and its compute-optimal behavior mirrors Aurora's with parabolas concentrated in the left half.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the power-law scaling framework established by Kaplan et al. (2020) and Hoffmann et al. (2022) for language models into the weather forecasting domain, providing the first systematic cross-model scaling analysis across five state-of-the-art architectures (GraphCast, AIFS, Aurora, Pangu, SFNO). It departs from prior scaling law work in two key ways: it shows that the standard C ≈ 6ND compute assumption breaks down for GNN-based models operating on resolution-independent latent graphs, and it reveals that model shape (width vs. depth) has a much stronger effect on performance than in NLP, where shape is nearly irrelevant at fixed N. The paper also extends the analysis beyond aggregate loss to variable-specific scaling, a dimension absent from prior scaling law studies in any domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that weather models consistently favor width over depth directly contradicts NLP scaling law results and provides concrete architectural guidance for the next generation of weather models. The 36× hardware utilization gap between Aurora (37.2%) and GraphCast (1.03%) exposes a critical blind spot in classical scaling law analyses that treat compute as a static resource, highlighting that theoretical parameter efficiency does not translate to practical deployment efficiency. Open questions include whether the width preference persists at kilometer-scale resolutions, whether the compute-optimal frontier shifts as models approach the physical predictability limit of the atmosphere, and how variable-specific scaling heterogeneity should inform hybrid or multi-task model designs.
