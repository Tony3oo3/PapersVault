## Problem

RLVR with verifiable rewards is widely used to improve LLM mathematical reasoning, but the mechanisms behind these gains remain poorly understood. This paper asks a provocative question: can RLVR produce substantial math performance improvements even when the reward signal carries no task-relevant information (e.g., random or deliberately incorrect rewards)? It further asks why such spurious rewards work for some model families (Qwen2.5-Math) but fail for others (Llama3, OLMo2), and what pre-existing model behaviors enable or prevent this effect.

## Input / Output

The training pipeline takes math problem text from the DeepScaleR dataset as input, paired with a binary 0-1 reward signal that ranges from ground-truth correctness to completely random Bernoulli(γ) assignments or even rewards for verifiably incorrect answers. The output is a fine-tuned language model evaluated on MATH-500 (pass@1), AMC (avg@8), and AIME 2024/2025 (avg@8). A key unusual aspect is that the reward is assigned independently of the model's actual answer quality in the spurious settings, yet the model still improves on certain architectures. Training uses GRPO with 16 rollouts per prompt, 64 prompts per rollout batch, and 128 mini-batch size on 8 A100 GPUs (~24 hours per run).

## Method

The paper designs a controlled progression of five reward functions (ground truth, majority vote, format-only, random, and majority-voted incorrect) and trains Qwen2.5-Math, Qwen2.5, Llama3, and OLMo2 models with GRPO under each. The core mechanistic insight is that GRPO's PPO-style clipping term introduces a 'clipping bias': for high-probability tokens (e.g., π_old > 0.85), the upper clipping threshold exceeds 1.0 and is never triggered, yielding a net positive gradient that reinforces the token; for low-probability tokens, the threshold is easily exceeded, yielding a net negative gradient that suppresses them. This asymmetry means random rewards still produce a non-zero expected gradient that systematically amplifies the base model's pre-existing high-prior behaviors. The paper validates this by showing that removing clipping (via three independent ablation variants) eliminates all random-reward gains. As a case study, it identifies 'code reasoning'—generating Python code in reasoning chains without execution—as a pre-existing behavior in Qwen2.5-Math-7B (65% frequency, 60.9% accuracy with code vs. 28.0% without) that gets amplified to >90% frequency under spurious rewards, and confirms causality by showing that explicitly inducing or suppressing code reasoning via prompting or RL-based rewards correspondingly increases or decreases performance.

## Results

On MATH-500 (pass@1), RLVR with random rewards improves Qwen2.5-Math-7B by 21.4% absolute accuracy, while ground-truth rewards yield 29.1%; incorrect-label rewards achieve 24.1% and format rewards approach ground-truth levels. On AMC (avg@8), random, incorrect, and format rewards yield 21.4%, 24.1%, and 13.8% gains respectively, compared to ~27–29% from ground-truth labels. Code reasoning frequency in Qwen2.5-Math-7B rises from 65% to over 90% under spurious rewards, and answers containing code achieve 60.9% accuracy versus 28.0% without code. Critically, these spurious-reward gains do not transfer: Llama3.1-8B-Instruct and OLMo2-7B show minimal or negative improvements under the same reward signals.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates two existing weak-supervision RLVR methods—TTRL (Zuo et al., 2025) and One-Shot RL (Wang et al., 2025b)—across multiple model families to test whether their gains generalize beyond Qwen. Both methods reproduce the same Qwen-centric pattern: they match or approach ground-truth reward performance on Qwen2.5-Math-7B and Qwen2.5-7B, but fail to yield meaningful gains on Llama3.1-8B-Instruct, Llama3.2-3B-Instruct, or OLMo2-7B(-SFT) (Appendix E, Figure 15). This mirrors the paper's own spurious-reward findings, suggesting that the apparent effectiveness of these methods on Qwen models may partly reflect amplification of pre-existing code-reasoning priors via GRPO's clipping bias rather than genuine learning from the training signal. The paper's key contribution over these baselines is the mechanistic explanation (clipping bias in GRPO, Eq. 2) and the demonstration that removing clipping eliminates the spurious-reward gains entirely (Figure 4), a diagnostic neither TTRL nor One-Shot RL performs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) as its RL algorithm and on the broader RLVR literature (Lambert et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025) that has shown reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards improves mathematical reasoning. It extends the nascent hypothesis that RLVR elicits rather than teaches reasoning (Wang et al., 2025b; Liu et al., 2025; Yue et al., 2025) to its logical extreme by demonstrating that even information-free random rewards or actively incorrect rewards produce substantial gains. The paper also connects to Yu et al. (2025) on clipping bias reducing exploration, but isolates the clipping mechanism as the sole source of training signal under random rewards through gradient derivation and ablation. Unlike prior work that uses weak supervision (majority vote, format) as a practical compromise, this paper uses spurious rewards purely as an analytical probe to understand what RLVR is actually optimizing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that Qwen-centric RLVR research may be overestimating the importance of reward design has immediate methodological implications: the paper recommends using spurious rewards as dummy baselines and validating across model families before drawing conclusions about RL techniques. A key open question is what determines whether a model's pre-existing behaviors are 'amplifiable' by the clipping bias—why Qwen2.5-Math has effective code reasoning priors while Llama and OLMo2 do not remains partially unexplained. The paper also raises the broader question of whether RLVR at current compute scales is genuinely learning new capabilities or merely reshuffling probability mass toward pre-existing patterns, with implications for how the field interprets reported improvements.
