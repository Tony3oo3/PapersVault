## Problem

Reinforcement learning for LLM post-training typically demands large labeled datasets, carefully engineered reward functions, and thousands of gradient steps, yet prior work has only superficially noted that entropy minimization alone can improve reasoning. This paper asks whether a fully unsupervised, single-example, ten-step procedure can match RL's reasoning gains, and provides the first rigorous theoretical and empirical analysis of *why* entropy minimization works as a post-training signal.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single unlabeled text prompt (e.g., a math word problem) selected via a variance-based criterion that maximizes pass@k variance across k sampled completions, ensuring the model sits on the capability boundary. No ground-truth answers, reward labels, or chain-of-thought annotations are required. The output is a set of updated model parameters after 10 gradient steps; at inference the model produces standard autoregressive reasoning responses, and the paper shows greedy decoding becomes the optimal sampling strategy post-EM (in contrast to RL-trained models that benefit from higher temperatures).

## Method

One-shot Entropy Minimization (EM) computes the token-level conditional entropy H_t = −Σ_v p_θ(v|y_{<t},x) log p_θ(v|y_{<t},x) over generated positions only, averages it into a single loss, and minimizes it with plain gradient descent for 10 steps at a fixed learning rate of 2×10⁻⁵. The gradient admits a closed-form score-function expression with a self-induced reward r(v) = −(log p_θ(v)+1), requiring no external reward model or value baseline. The paper proves that EM induces a right-skewed logit shift—high-probability tokens receive positive updates scaled by p_i while the low-probability tail is compressed—formalized via a thresholded update rule (Δz_i ≥ 0 iff p_i ≥ e^{−H}) and a logit-gap amplification proposition. Empirically, this distribution-shaping effect saturates around 10 steps; further training causes 'over-confidence' and performance decline, confirming EM reshapes the output distribution rather than injecting new knowledge. To validate results free of data contamination, the authors pretrain a 7B model (Deconta-7B) from scratch on a strictly n-gram-decontaminated corpus and show consistent gains across math, logic, and code benchmarks.

## Results

One-shot EM (1 unlabeled example, 10 gradient steps) yields an average score of 51.1 on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, a +24.9-point gain over the base model (29.2), with individual improvements of +25.8 on MATH500, +24.3 on MinervaMath, +22.5 on OlympiadBench, and +26.2 on AMC23. On the fully decontaminated Deconta-7B-RL model, EM adds +10.2 to the average score (59.5→62.3), and on Deconta-7B-SFT it adds +4.5 (52.1→56.6). Across all tested base models (Qwen3-8B-Base, Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Llama-3.1-Nemotron-Nano-8B-v1), EM consistently improves every benchmark, with the largest gains observed on mathematically oriented bases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, 1-shot EM (1 data point, 10 steps) achieves an average score of 51.1, matching OpenReasoner-Zero-7B (51.1, 129k data, 600+ steps) while using ~129,000× less data and 60× fewer steps. It outperforms SimpleRL-Zoo (49.3, 24K data, 4000 steps) by +1.8, Prime-Zero-7B (44.8, 230K data, 240 steps) by +6.3, Oat-Zero-7B (45.8, 12K data, 300 steps) by +5.3, and RLVR (47.2, 1.2K data, 1000 steps) by +3.9. Notably, even RLVR with 1-shot data but 1000 steps (47.7) falls short of 1-shot EM's 51.1, highlighting that the entropy-minimization objective itself—rather than the number of optimization steps or data volume—drives the gain. On the decontaminated Deconta-7B-RL model, 1-shot EM pushes the average from 59.5 to 62.3 (+10.2), demonstrating that EM remains effective as a complementary test-time technique on top of strong RL-trained checkpoints.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the entropy-minimization observation made by Wang et al. (2025) and Agarwal et al. (2025), who noted that optimizing an entropy loss during post-training improves reasoning but left the mechanism unexplained. Here, the authors push the idea to its extreme—literally one unlabeled example and ten gradient steps—and provide a theoretical account (right-skewed logit shift, thresholded token updates) that reframes EM as a distribution-shaping mechanism rather than a conventional learning process. The paper also positions EM in direct geometric contrast to RL: both perform 'token reranking' to unlock latent capabilities, but EM stretches the high-probability tail while RL compresses it, yielding opposite logit-skewness signatures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a single unlabeled example and ten gradient steps can yield double-digit reasoning gains (up to +24.9 average on Qwen2.5-Math-7B) challenges the assumption that post-training requires large labeled datasets or elaborate reward engineering, and the decontaminated 7B model validation addresses a real credibility concern in the field. However, the method exhibits substantial seed-to-seed variance (up to 2× in average score across 16 seeds), and the 'over-confidence' phenomenon—where continued loss reduction degrades performance—limits straightforward scaling of training steps. Open questions include whether the stochasticity can be reduced, whether the gains persist at larger model scales, and how to best combine EM with RL in a principled pipeline (the paper shows EM-before-RL works but EM-after-RL harms performance).
