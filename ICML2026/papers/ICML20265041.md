## Problem

Standard LRM evaluation assumes a static 'frozen world' where the problem and environment remain fixed during the model's reasoning, yet real-world deployments require models to handle mid-inference interruptions (users requesting early answers) and dynamic context changes (problem specifications updating during reasoning). No prior work systematically measures how large reasoning models degrade under these dynamic conditions or characterizes the specific failure modes that emerge.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265041/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation protocol takes a math or coding problem, generates a full reasoning trace, then truncates it at a relative position X ∈ {0.1, …, 0.9} of the trace length and injects an interruption signal (a hard-stop token, a speedup directive, or an update modifying the problem). The model must then produce a final answer conditioned on its partial trace plus the interruption, and the system reports both accuracy (agreement with ground truth) and post-interruption output length as a proxy for compute cost. For update-driven interruptions, the augmented problem p′ is presented first and the update u is injected mid-reasoning, with correctness evaluated against the original problem's answer.

## Method

The paper introduces a standardized evaluation suite rather than a new model architecture. It defines two interruption classes—time-constrained (hard interrupts that force immediate answers via ⟨end-thinking⟩ or ⟨force-answer⟩ tokens, and soft interrupts that request speedup while allowing continued reasoning) and update-driven (new information that modifies the task specification mid-reasoning)—and constructs human-verified augmented datasets by altering variable values, constraints, or starter code in GSM-8K, MATH-500, AIME-24/25, and LiveCodeBench-v6 problems. Across five open-weight model families (Qwen3, GPT-OSS, Magistral, DeepSeek-R1, Nemotron-3-Nano), the protocol reveals three recurring pathologies: reasoning leakage (models continue thinking inside the answer segment after hard stops, inflating output up to 10×), panic (models abandon reasoning entirely under speedup pressure, causing up to 30% accuracy drops), and self-doubt (models second-guess and ignore valid updates, accounting for ~80% of update-driven errors). A training-free prompt-guidance postfix appended after the update tag partially mitigates self-doubt on easier tasks, and ablations confirm the findings are robust to interruption granularity, model scale, and prompt paraphrasing.

## Results

Across five open-weight LRM families (Qwen3-8B, GPT-OSS-20B, Magistral-Small-1.2, GPT-OSS-120B, DeepSeek-R1) on GSM-8K, MATH-500, AIME-24/25, and LiveCodeBench-v6, static evaluations consistently overestimate robustness under dynamic conditions. Under hard interrupts, models exhibit anytime behavior (accuracy improves with more reasoning budget) but suffer reasoning leakage, with early interruptions producing final answers up to 10× longer than full-thinking outputs. Under soft interrupts (speedup), models on harder tasks (AIME, LiveCodeBench) show panic—prematurely terminating reasoning—causing up to 30% accuracy drops, with >90% of new errors attributable to this behavior. Under update-driven interruptions, accuracy drops by up to 60% when updates arrive late in the trace, with ~80% of errors stemming from self-doubt (models second-guess the update and revert to original reasoning).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline comparison is against Chain of Draft (Xu et al., 2025), a compact-reasoning inference-control method, evaluated under all three interruption types with Qwen3-8B. Chain of Draft reduces output length in some settings but fails to resolve any of the three identified pathologies. Under hard interruption at position 0.3, answer lengths remain inflated by 1.38× on AIME and 6.27× on LiveCodeBench-v6 relative to no-interruption baselines, confirming persistent reasoning leakage. Under soft interruption on AIME at position 0.3, the Chain-of-Draft variant exhibits a higher panic rate (13.1%) than the standard assistant-turn interrupt (3.8%), and its accuracy degrades more than the regular reasoning baseline. Under update-driven interruptions, Chain of Draft still exhibits self-doubt on AIME and LiveCodeBench-v6; prompt guidance partially mitigates the behavior but accuracy remains slightly lower across all interruption positions. The paper also notes that NoThinking (Ma et al., 2025) and Budget Forcing (Muennighoff et al., 2025) operate under a static 'frozen world' assumption and do not address the dynamic, externally-imposed interruption setting studied here.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the efficient-reasoning literature (NoThinking, Budget Forcing, Chain of Draft, TALE, etc.), which operates under a static 'frozen world' assumption where the problem is fixed and the goal is to shorten or bypass the thinking phase. It also extends Fan et al.'s observation that missing premises cause overthinking and self-doubt loops, by studying what happens when the missing information is actually delivered mid-inference as an update. The key conceptual shift is from controlling reasoning length to evaluating reasoning resilience: the paper treats mid-inference interruption as a first-class evaluation dimension rather than an implementation detail, building on Ma et al.'s NoThinking idea of probing models at different reasoning points but reframing it as a robustness question under externally imposed, unpredictable cutoffs and context changes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that static accuracy consistently overestimates real-world robustness—up to 60% drops under late-stage updates—challenges a core assumption in how LRMs are benchmarked and deployed, and the three identified failure modes (reasoning leakage, panic, self-doubt) provide a concrete error taxonomy for practitioners building interactive systems. The public benchmark and the demonstration that a simple prompt-guidance postfix can fully resolve self-doubt on easier tasks but not AIME suggest that interruptibility is an improvable but unsolved capability, pointing toward interruption-aware post-training as a natural next step. Open questions include whether findings generalize to multi-turn agentic workloads, adversarial or noisy updates, and closed-source models where mid-reasoning intervention is not natively supported.
