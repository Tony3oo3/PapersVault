## Problem

High-quality LLM agent trajectory datasets are expensive to produce (e.g., ~$100 per SWE-bench task, 1000+ hours for Mind2Web 2), yet existing watermarking methods for text or standalone code cannot protect them. Agent trajectories have an interleaved action-observation structure where only action tokens are trainable, and datasets are small (1–2K samples), making token-level or syntactic watermarks both inapplicable and unlearnable at practical watermark ratios.

## Input / Output

For injection, ACTHOOK takes a collected trajectory dataset of (action, observation) pairs, a watermark ratio R, a watermark scheme W defining CHECK/INJECT/DETECT predicates, and a secret activation key k; it outputs a watermarked dataset where hook action-observation pairs are inserted at action boundaries and k is appended to user prompts. For detection, ACTHOOK takes a black-box target agent and a set of test prompts, queries the agent with and without k (N prompts × Q queries each), and produces a hook activation score Δq (the difference in hook action emergence rates) along with a binary verdict from a paired one-sided t-test against a sham key.

## Method

ACTHOOK embeds watermarks at the behavioral level by inserting auxiliary 'hook actions'—drawn from the dataset's existing action distribution—into trajectories at action boundaries, where token entropy peaks and the model faces maximum uncertainty about which action to take. Two watermark types are supported: standalone hooks (e.g., a pwd command) that are semantically independent, and contextual hooks (e.g., a file-existence check after creation) conditioned on specific trajectory elements, with an auxiliary LLM generating diverse surface forms for stealth. Detection is statistical rather than deterministic: the hook activation score Δq = q_k − q_c measures how much the activation key increases the probability of hook action occurrence, and a paired t-test against a sham key provides a calibrated p-value. Unlike standard backdoors that substitute the output, ACTHOOK is additive (all original actions are preserved), task-preserving (Pass@1 with key ≈ Pass@1 without), and verified via hypothesis testing, which explains why direct adaptations of CodeMark, AutoPoison, and similar backdoor-based watermarks fail on small agent-trajectory datasets (AUC ≤ 0.57 vs. > 0.96 for ACTHOOK).

## Results

On Qwen-2.5-Coder-7B with N=1 prompt and Q=8 queries, ACTHOOK (Standalone) achieves AUC of 99.5 on MATH, 99.6 on SimpleQA, and 88.3 on SWE-Smith, while ACTHOOK (Contextual) reaches 96.3, 87.9, and 94.2 respectively. Averaged across all three datasets, Standalone and Contextual variants yield 97.8 and 90.8 AUC, compared to CodeMark's 55.5. Watermarking introduces negligible Pass@1 degradation (e.g., 75.3% for both watermarked and non-watermarked fine-tuned models on MATH/SimpleQA at 7B scale), and detection remains robust (AUC > 85) under DeCoMa filtering, paraphrasing, and action summarization attacks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ACTHOOK decisively outperforms all four baselines across every dataset. Against CodeMark (the primary baseline adapted for agent trajectories), ACTHOOK (Standalone) achieves AUC 99.5 vs. 56.7 on MATH and 99.6 vs. 52.6 on SimpleQA, while CodeMark's TPR@1%FPR remains below 0.07, indicating near-random detection. The three backdoor-based baselines—AutoPoison, DeadCode, and StyleTransfer—all fail to learn on small (1–2K) agent-trajectory datasets, with AUC below 0.73 and TPR@1%FPR below 0.16 on MATH and SimpleQA. On SWE-Smith, where trajectories are longer and more complex, ACTHOOK (Contextual) achieves the highest AUC (94.2) among all methods, while CodeMark drops to 53.1. The paper attributes the baselines' failure to their token-level or syntactic-level perturbations conflicting with the low-entropy interior of agent actions, whereas ACTHOOK's behavior-level hook actions exploit high-entropy action boundaries where the model is most receptive to learning new patterns.

## Relation to Prior Work

ACTHOOK repurposes the software-engineering concept of hooks (sys.settrace, Git pre-commit hooks) as a watermarking mechanism for LLM agent trajectories, a data format that prior dataset-watermarking methods (CodeMark, CoProtector, TabularMark) and backdoor-based provenance tools (AutoPoison, DeadCode, StyleTransfer) do not address. The key conceptual departure is operating at the behavioral level—inserting in-distribution hook actions at high-entropy action boundaries—rather than at the token or syntactic level, which the paper shows is incompatible with the interleaved action-observation structure and small (1–2K) scale of agent trajectory datasets. The detection procedure borrows the statistical hypothesis-testing framework from radioactivity work (Sablayrolles et al., 2020) but applies it to a trigger-conditioned behavioral signal rather than generic output-distribution shifts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

High-quality agent trajectory datasets cost hundreds of dollars per task or thousands of human hours to produce, yet no prior mechanism exists to trace unauthorized reuse once released; ACTHOOK fills this gap with a practical, low-ratio (5%) watermark that survives paraphrasing, summarization, and DeCoMa filtering. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to non-code agents (e.g., GUI or robotics trajectories), whether larger-scale fine-tuning or multi-epoch training further dilutes the signal, and whether adaptive attackers who observe the detection protocol can develop targeted removal strategies beyond the tested attacks. The finding that 3B models struggle to absorb the watermark on complex SWE-Smith trajectories also raises the question of minimum model capacity for reliable watermarking.
