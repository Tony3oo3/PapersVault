## Problem

Existing automated agentic system design methods optimize task performance but treat robustness as a post-hoc concern, leaving systems vulnerable to adversarial attacks and internal execution failures. The core difficulty is threefold: structural topology and behavioral actions must be co-designed (entanglement), failure sources are open-ended and evolving (unpredictability), and many distinct designs achieve similar performance, creating ambiguous optimization signals (equifinality). No prior framework systematically embeds robustness into the design-and-optimization loop itself.

## Input / Output

The method takes a task query and a set of textual robustness signals (diagnostics distilled from prior execution traces) as conditioning inputs, and produces a primitive sequence of length at most L=16 drawn from a finite alphabet of 17 structural and behavioral tokens. This sequence is deterministically compiled via a stack machine into an executable multi-agent workflow, which is then run to yield a final answer along with a structured execution trace. The trace feeds back numeric rewards (accuracy, cost, external/internal robustness scores) and natural-language safety signals that condition the next round of sequence generation, closing the design–execution–feedback loop.

## Method

AutoRAS represents an agentic system as a sequence over a unified primitive alphabet that jointly encodes structural connectivity (sequential, parallel, branching composition) and behavioral actions (reasoning, ensembling, safety filtering, tool guarding), compiled into an executable workflow by a deterministic RPN-style stack machine that also emits legality masks to constrain generation. The generative policy is a lightweight encoder-decoder (MiniLM embeddings + cross-attention + autoregressive decoder) that scores candidate primitives at each step, trained with a GFlowNet Trajectory Balance (TB) loss that matches forward log-probabilities against reward-scaled backward flows, enabling trajectory-level credit assignment and naturally absorbing equifinality by sharing reward mass across behaviorally equivalent designs. The reward function R(X) = αu + ρr_ext + ηr_int − βc directly embeds external robustness, internal reliability, and cost into the design objective. In addition to the TB gradient, a textual gradient is computed by an LLM-based analyzer that distills execution traces into structured prompt edits for action primitives, refining their natural-language instructions without retraining the underlying LLMs. This dual signal—probabilistic flow optimization plus textual prompt refinement—iteratively evolves designs that are both accurate and robust.

## Results

AutoRAS achieves the highest average vanilla accuracy (74.27%) across MMLU, MSMARCO, MATH, and ProgramDev, and the smallest average performance drop under adversarial attacks (2.13%), compared to 5.47–18.43% for other automated designers. On MMLU, it reaches 83.01% vanilla / 82.35% under attack; on MSMARCO, 90.00% / 88.75%; on MATH, 57.41% / 54.94%; and on ProgramDev, 66.67% / 62.50%. The method also demonstrates stable cross-model transferability, maintaining the smallest attack drop (1.31% average) across GPT-4o-mini, DeepSeek-V3.1, Claude-3.5-Haiku, and Gemini-2.0-Flash backbones.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AutoRAS outperforms all 11 baselines on average vanilla accuracy (74.27% vs. 71.51% for the next-best AFlow) and achieves the smallest average attack degradation (2.13% vs. 5.47% for GPTSwarm, 14.26% for AFlow, and 18.43% for MaAS). Among manually designed methods, SC(CoT) is the strongest single-agent baseline (60.57% avg vanilla) but still trails AutoRAS by 13.7 points; G-Safeguard, despite its explicit security focus, suffers the largest attack drop among manual methods (17.23%). Among automated designers, AFlow leads on ProgramDev vanilla (70.83% vs. 66.67%) but degrades sharply under attack (↓19.46 on MATH, ↓17.50 on MSMARCO), while AutoRAS maintains ≤4.17% drop on every dataset. AgentPrune and G-Designer are the closest automated competitors on MMLU (81.70% and 82.35% vanilla respectively) but still fall 0.66–1.31 points behind AutoRAS and show 5–9× larger attack drops. MaAS, despite its supernet-based search, is the most attack-vulnerable automated method (18.43% avg drop), confirming that architecture search alone does not yield robustness without explicit safety objectives in the reward.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on two distinct research threads: (1) GFlowNets and Trajectory Balance (Bengio et al., 2021; Malkin et al., 2022), which provide the flow-conservation-based optimization machinery for learning stochastic policies over discrete objects, and (2) automated agentic system design (AFlow, G-Designer, MaAS, GPTSwarm), which has focused on optimizing task accuracy through workflow search. The key departure is twofold: prior automated designers treat robustness as an afterthought or omit it entirely, while prior robustness work (G-Safeguard, PeerGuard) operates reactively at execution time. AutoRAS unifies structural topology and behavioral actions into a single primitive-sequence representation and embeds robustness directly into the flow-based design objective, closing the loop between design, execution, and safety feedback in a way neither thread had attempted.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The primitive-sequence abstraction decouples agentic system logic from specific backbone models, enabling transferability across GPT-4o-mini, DeepSeek, Claude, and Gemini without retraining—a property that prior graph-based or prompt-based designs lack. The finding that design-time structural choices (safeguard placement, guarded tool paths, agreement merges) yield a 2.13% average attack degradation versus 14–27% for competing automated designers suggests that robustness is better achieved through architecture than through post-hoc patching. Open questions include whether the single-round (K=1) setting generalizes to multi-round interactions, whether the fixed 17-primitive vocabulary suffices for open-ended or long-horizon tasks, and whether the LLM-based monitor's 94.89% accuracy is adequate for high-stakes deployments where missed failures compound.
