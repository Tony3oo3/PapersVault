## Problem

Extreme data sparsity in recommendation systems—where users interact with only a tiny fraction of available items—produces rugged optimization landscapes and poor generalization. Existing remedies (curated side information, external teacher models, heuristic augmentations) are expensive, introduce distributional mismatches, or merely re-shuffle existing data without generating novel, high-fidelity interaction sequences. The paper asks whether a model can bootstrap its own performance through recursive self-generation without any external data source or teacher.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265924/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes chronologically ordered user interaction sequences (item IDs from a global vocabulary) and produces two outputs: (1) improved next-item rankings evaluated via NDCG@K and Recall@K, and (2) synthetic interaction sequences that are merged into the training set for the next iteration. Generation is seeded with a random prefix of the user's true history and extended autoregressively; each candidate item is accepted only if it passes a rank-threshold fidelity check against the remaining held-out items in the user's original sequence, and sequences shorter than two items or exact duplicates are discarded.

## Method

RSIR operates as a closed loop over K iterations: train a backbone model (SASRec, CL4SRec, or HSTU) on the current dataset, use it to autoregressively generate m synthetic trajectories per user via a hybrid candidate pool (with probability p draw from the user's own history for exploitation, otherwise from the global item set for exploration), filter each generated step through a fidelity-based quality control that terminates the trajectory if no remaining true item is ranked within threshold τ, and retrain a successor on the augmented dataset. Theoretically, the paper proves that this process induces a Manifold Tangential Gradient Penalty—an implicit regularizer that smooths the loss landscape specifically along the tangent space of the user preference manifold—and derives a recursive error bound showing convergence is guaranteed only when the fidelity leakage rate stays below a critical breakdown point. Empirically, RSIR yields cumulative gains across four benchmarks and three architectures, with even weak models generating effective training curricula for stronger ones.

## Results

RSIR delivers consistent, significant improvements over all baselines across three backbone architectures (SASRec, CL4SRec, HSTU) and four datasets. Headline gains include up to 10.97% NDCG@10 improvement (CL4SRec on Amazon-Beauty) and up to 10.00% Recall@10 improvement (CL4SRec on Amazon-Beauty). For the strongest backbone HSTU, RSIR achieves 6.25% NDCG@10 gain on Amazon-Toys and 6.48% Recall@10 gain on Yelp. Gains are cumulative over multiple iterations, with HSTU on Amazon-Sport extending from 8.02% to 13.92% Recall@10 after three iterations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RSIR outperforms all five baselines on every dataset and backbone combination. The strongest baseline is DR4SR (dataset regeneration via a separate generative model), which RSIR surpasses by 1.80–6.25% NDCG@10 and 3.43–6.48% Recall@10 depending on backbone and dataset. The heuristic baselines Reordering and Insertion provide only marginal or inconsistent gains (e.g., Reordering actually degrades HSTU on Toys from 0.0512 to 0.0497 NDCG@10), while RSIR consistently improves. Among learnable generation baselines, ASReP and DiffuASR show limited or negative effects on several backbones (e.g., DiffuASR drops HSTU on Toys to 0.0462 NDCG@10), whereas RSIR never degrades performance. The largest absolute gap appears with CL4SRec on Amazon-Beauty, where RSIR achieves 0.0318 NDCG@10 versus DR4SR's 0.0310 and the base model's 0.0307, a 10.97% relative improvement over the best baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

RSIR carves new ground by transplanting the self-improvement loop popularized in LLM research (STaR, REST, self-rewarding) into sequential recommendation, but with a critical departure: instead of relying on a large teacher or external generative model, the recommendation model itself serves as the data generator. This distinguishes it from prior data-centric methods (Reordering, Insertion, ASReP, DiffuASR, DR4SR) that either apply heuristic perturbations or train separate generative models. The fidelity-based quality control mechanism is the paper's key technical novelty, directly addressing the model-collapse failure mode identified by Shumailov et al. (2024) and Alemohammad et al. (2024) in self-consuming generative systems, and the theoretical reframing of the loop as a manifold tangential gradient penalty connects it to classical manifold regularization (Belkin et al., 2006) in a way prior recommendation work has not.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even weak models can produce effective training curricula for stronger ones reframes self-improvement from a capability of large models to a general regularization benefit accessible to any architecture, with direct practical implications for resource-constrained deployment. The demonstrated catastrophic collapse without fidelity control (NDCG@10 dropping from 0.0293 to 0.0119 by iteration 3) underscores that naive recursive self-training is dangerous, making the quality-control mechanism essential rather than optional. Open questions include whether the observed performance plateau after 3–4 iterations can be overcome with adaptive exploration policies, how the approach scales to industrial-scale vocabularies beyond the clustering-based approximation tested here, and whether the implicit regularization interpretation holds under distributional shift in production settings.
