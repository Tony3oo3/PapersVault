## Problem

Existing LLM-based rule learning methods either iteratively optimize a single rule or generate multiple rules without modeling their global interdependence, leaving the challenge of learning a compact, coordinated rule set unresolved. Additionally, whether LLMs can reliably apply fine-grained rule importance scores for calibrated probabilistic aggregation in neuro-symbolic reasoning remains unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265441/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes natural-language text (or pairs of texts for pairwise tasks such as Retweets and Headlines) and produces a weighted set of up to H=10 natural-language rules with Elastic Net-learned logistic regression coefficients, yielding a calibrated binary probability score and class label. Each rule's applicability to an instance is a ternary judgment (+1, −1, or 0 for abstention), which explicitly models rule coverage and enables sparse aggregation rather than forcing every rule to fire on every input.

## Method

RLIE is a four-stage neuro-symbolic pipeline that separates LLM-based semantic induction from classical probabilistic aggregation. In Rule Generation, an LLM proposes candidate natural-language rules from sampled examples, which are filtered by a minimum training coverage threshold. In Logistic Regression, an Elastic Net-regularized logistic model is fitted over the ternary rule-judgment vectors to learn sparse, calibrated weights that act as global importance scores for rule selection. In Iterative Refinement, the top-k hardest misclassified examples are fed back to the LLM alongside the current rule bank to trigger reflective revision or targeted extension, with capacity-constrained pruning by validation F1. The Evaluation stage compares direct combiner inference against four LLM prompting variants (rules only, rules+weights, rules+weights+prediction), empirically demonstrating that LLMs struggle to exploit numeric weight signals and that the external logistic combiner is the most stable and accurate inference protocol.

## Results

On the six HypoBench tasks, RLIE with DeepSeek-V3.2 achieves the best or near-best Accuracy/Macro-F1 across all datasets: 70.9/70.7 (Reviews), 82.3/82.3 (Dreaddit), 67.0/67.0 (Headlines), 64.6/63.0 (Citations), 90.7/90.7 (LLM Detect), and 65.7/65.6 (Retweets). The strongest backbone (Qwen3-235B) pushes LLM Detect to 88.3/88.3 and Retweets to 66.5/66.5. RLIE also achieves significantly lower Expected Calibration Error (ECE ≈ 0.11) than rule-injection prompting (ECE > 0.45) on most tasks, confirming the logistic combiner's calibrated outputs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the strongest rule-learning baseline HypoGeniC (Zhou et al., 2024), RLIE with the same DeepSeek-V3.2 backbone wins on all six tasks, with the largest margins on Citations (64.6 vs. 46.9 accuracy, +17.7) and Headlines (67.0 vs. 59.9, +7.1), and more modest gains on Dreaddit (82.3 vs. 80.5) and Retweets (65.7 vs. 61.9). Compared to IO Refinement (Qiu et al., 2023), which iteratively refines a single rule, RLIE outperforms it on every dataset, most notably on Citations (64.6 vs. 54.2) and LLM Detect (90.7 vs. 83.6), confirming that multi-rule global aggregation is essential. Against Zhong et al. (2024), which learns natural-language predicates with a logistic readout, RLIE dominates on Headlines (67.0 vs. 48.2), Citations (64.6 vs. 58.1), and Retweets (65.7 vs. 50.0), while Zhong et al. remains competitive only on LLM Detect (84.9 vs. 90.7). The paper further shows that re-injecting the learned rules and weights back into LLM prompting (E2–E4) consistently degrades or destabilizes performance relative to the simple logistic combiner (E1), underscoring that LLMs are unreliable at controlled probabilistic aggregation.

## Relation to Prior Work

RLIE sits at the intersection of classical logic regression (Ruczinski et al., 2003), which learns weighted combinations of rule indicators via logistic regression, and recent LLM-based hypothesis generation frameworks (HypoGeniC, Zhou et al. 2024; IO Refinement, Qiu et al. 2023), which iteratively propose and refine natural-language rules. The key departure is a principled division of labor: rather than asking the LLM to both generate rules and aggregate them (as in prompt-based inference), RLIE delegates global weighted aggregation to an explicit Elastic-Net logistic combiner, while the LLM handles only local semantic tasks (rule proposal, per-instance applicability judgment). The hard-example-driven iterative refinement loop extends the single-rule refinement of IO Refinement to a multi-rule setting with capacity-constrained pruning, and the systematic comparison of four inference paradigms (combiner vs. rule-only vs. rule+weight vs. rule+weight+prediction prompting) is novel in explicitly testing whether LLMs can substitute for a calibrated probabilistic aggregator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical takeaway—that LLMs are unreliable at exploiting numeric rule weights and reference predictions under prompting, and that a simple logistic combiner consistently outperforms or matches all prompt-based variants—provides a clear design principle for neuro-symbolic systems: separate semantic induction from probabilistic aggregation. This is reinforced by calibration analysis showing the combiner achieves substantially lower ECE than LLM prompting on 5 of 6 datasets. Open questions include whether the linear combiner is sufficient or whether richer aggregation (e.g., tree-based, attention-based) would help, whether the framework extends naturally to multi-class or structured prediction, and whether the hard-example refinement loop can be made more sample-efficient for very large rule spaces. The approach also raises the question of whether the observed LLM aggregation failure is a prompt-engineering artifact or a fundamental limitation of current architectures.
