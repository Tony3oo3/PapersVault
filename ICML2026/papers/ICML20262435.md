## Problem

Existing neural operators and Transformer-based PDE surrogates suffer from two recurring limitations: their latent states are opaque and not aligned with explicit geometric or modal structures, and global self-attention exhibits a low-frequency bias that makes sharp fronts and vortical filaments harder to capture while incurring O(N²) spatial cost. The paper asks whether a learned, particleized Gaussian basis can serve as a directly visualizable intermediate representation that simultaneously improves interpretability, spectral retention, and computational efficiency.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262435/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a PDE field sampled at N spatial locations (2D or 3D, regular grids or irregular/masked domains) together with query coordinates, and produces a predicted target field at those queries. Unusually, the intermediate state is not a fixed-size latent vector but a per-site coefficient matrix Z ∈ R^{N×G} (G ≪ N) derived from learned anisotropic Gaussian particles (centers μ, axis-aligned scales σ, mixture weights w), which are themselves inspectable geometric objects. The operator is resolution-agnostic: changing the query set or grid resolution only requires re-evaluating the Gaussian basis at new locations while reusing the same modal operator and decoder.

## Method

GPO has two coupled components. First, a Gaussian Particle Representation: an encoder with Fourier-feature coordinate embeddings predicts per-site anisotropic Gaussians (μ, σ, w), and evaluating these kernels at query points yields a compact, locally supported basis Z that acts as a mollified, data-adaptive expansion of the field. Second, a Petrov-Galerkin Gaussian Attention layer operates in modal space: learned Gaussian modal windows perform a PG-style measurement aggregating N spatial sites into G modal tokens (N→G), a standard multi-head scaled dot-product attention parameterizes the G×G modal coupling kernel, and the coupled modes are scattered back to spatial sites (G→N) with a convex residual update and row-wise mass renormalization. This measure→couple→scatter pipeline yields a basis-to-basis operator whose dominant cost is linear in N (with G fixed), whose intermediate quantities (particle geometry, modal windows, attention weights) are directly visualizable, and which extends naturally to irregular geometries and 3D domains without architectural changes.

## Results

On one-step prediction (relative L2 error), GPO achieves the best or near-best result across all 8 benchmarks: 3.02×10⁻² on NS2D, 3.44×10⁻¹ on NS3D, 2.26×10⁻³ on ERA5-temp, 6.68×10⁻² on ERA5-wind-u, 2.97×10⁻¹ on CARRA, 1.12×10⁻³ on Airfoil, 3.95×10⁻¹ on Turbulent, and 8.01×10⁻² on PlanetSWE. GPO is the lowest-error method on 7 of 8 tasks; on NS2D, M2NO (2.76×10⁻²) edges out GPO by ~9% relative. On real reanalysis data (ERA5, CARRA), GPO's margins over the next-best baselines are larger (e.g., 32% lower than M2NO on ERA5-temp, 7% lower than AMG on CARRA), consistent with the mesh/mask-agnostic Gaussian basis.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPO outperforms all seven baselines on 7 of 8 tasks, with the sole exception being M2NO on NS2D (2.76×10⁻² vs. GPO's 3.02×10⁻², a ~9% relative gap), where spectral inductive biases favor the multi-resolution operator. Against FNO, GPO improves by 7–65% relative L2 across tasks, with the largest gains on Turbulent (3.95 vs. 4.82×10⁻¹) and CARRA (2.97 vs. 3.50×10⁻¹). Against LSM and AMG (MgNO), GPO leads on every task, most notably on NS3D (3.44 vs. 3.80×10⁻¹) and Airfoil (1.12 vs. 3.09×10⁻³). Among Transformer-based operators, GPO dominates: it beats Transolver by 10–25% relative on most tasks, Galerkin Transformer by 65–70%, and GNOT by a wide margin (GNOT's errors are 10–20× higher, suggesting training instability in the authors' setup). A capacity-fair FNO (matched to ~1.6M parameters) on Turbulent still trails GPO (4.63×10⁻¹ vs. 3.95×10⁻¹), indicating the gain is architectural rather than purely parametric.

## Relation to Prior Work

GPO sits at the intersection of three lineages: (i) Petrov-Galerkin finite-element theory, where trial and test spaces are distinct function spaces and residuals are enforced orthogonally; (ii) radial-basis-function / Gaussian mixture approximation, which provides the density guarantee for the learned particle basis; and (iii) neural-operator and Transformer-operator architectures (FNO, GNOT, Transolver, Galerkin Transformer), which learn resolution-agnostic maps between function spaces but typically through opaque latent states or fixed spectral bases. The key departure is repurposing anisotropic Gaussians—previously used in graphics (3DGS) for rendering or in RBF theory for static approximation—as the *input-adaptive trial space* of a neural operator, with PG-style attention parameterizing the modal coupling kernel rather than image-space compositing. This 'basis-to-basis' framing (measure N→G, couple G×G, scatter G→N) is, to the authors' knowledge, unexplored in the operator-learning literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GPO offers a practical middle ground between the interpretability of explicit particle representations and the accuracy of learned operators: the Gaussian parameters (centers, anisotropic scales, weights) and modal couplings are directly visualizable, enabling representation-level diagnostics that black-box FNO or GNOT cannot provide. However, the paper is candid that accuracy gains over the strongest baselines are moderate and task-dependent, and the method does not enforce physical invariants (mass/energy conservation, divergence-free constraints). Open questions include whether adaptive or hierarchical modal budgets can close the remaining gap on spectral-favorable tasks, whether sparse or low-rank modal attention can reduce the G² cost at extreme resolutions, and whether tighter physics coupling can elevate the particles from representation-level to mechanistic interpretability.
