## Problem

Prior LLM-based robotic planning approaches treat automatically generated PDDL planning domains as one-shot search utilities, overlooking their potential as scalable sources of verified reasoning supervision and structured reward signals. Meanwhile, the SFT+RL paradigm that has proven effective for general LLM reasoning is difficult to apply to robotic planning because high-quality CoT traces are expensive to curate manually and long-horizon combinatorial tasks lack structured, dense reward functions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261708/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference time the model receives only a natural-language planning problem specifying the initial state, goal state, available predicates, and action schema (no additional skills or reasoning traces), and must output a valid action sequence enclosed in <FINAL> tags. During training, each example is a triple pairing the problem, its symbolic plan, and an LLM-generated CoT trace that includes plan justification, state-transition verification, alternative-action exploration, and failure backtracking. For real-robot deployment, a VLM or rule-based classifier provides the logical state estimates from visual input, and the model plans over potentially noisy or incomplete predicate sets.

## Method

SELF-CRITEACH reinterprets LLM self-generated PDDL domains as a dual-purpose training resource rather than a planning tool. In the self-teaching stage, the base LLM generates and iteratively repairs a PDDL domain (via feedback prompting and hill-climbing pruning), a symbolic solver produces diverse problem-plan pairs, and the same base LLM converts each symbolic state-action transition into a structured CoT trace through four elicitation steps (plan explanation, state-transition checking, alternative exploration, failure backtracking); these traces are then used for SFT. In the self-critiquing stage, the identical self-written domain is reused as a structured reward function within Constrained Policy Optimization, where the reward measures goal-predicate satisfaction at each step and the constraint penalizes precondition violations and inconsistent state transitions, providing dense step-level feedback without manual reward engineering. The key design insight is that the symbolic-to-CoT transformation is essential: training directly on raw symbolic plans leads to solution memorization and poor generalization, whereas requiring the model to structurally explain each transition before SFT aligns symbolic planning semantics with the model's latent reasoning space.

## Results

SCT-4B achieves an overall planning success rate of 0.46 and progress score of 0.76, representing a 20% absolute gain over its base model Qwen3-4B (0.26 / 0.59). On the long-horizon BW Hard benchmark, SCT-4B reaches 0.45 success rate versus 0.24 for the base model (+21%). Across all six task types (three seen, three unseen), SCT-4B outperforms every compared model of similar or larger scale, including Qwen3-8B (0.35), Mistral-24B (0.21), and GPT-4o (0.19). In real-robot experiments, SCT-4B achieves 0.70–0.90 task success under noisy perception, compared to 0.20–0.70 for a PDDL solver.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SCT-4B (4B params) outperforms all compared models on overall success rate: Qwen3-8B (0.35, −0.11), Qwen3-4B base (0.26, −0.20), Mistral-24B (0.21, −0.25), GPT-4o (0.19, −0.27), Gemma3-12B (0.08, −0.38), and Gemma3-4B (0.01, −0.45). Against reasoning-enhancement baselines sharing the Qwen3-4B backbone, SCT-4B beats 30B-Distill (0.36), Majority Vote (0.32), Self-Distill (0.28), and Prompt-CoT (0.27) by 0.10–0.19 in overall success rate, while also using fewer inference tokens (7,543 vs. 9,555 for 30B-Distill and 29,864 for Majority Vote). On the unseen Reorganize Room task, SCT-4B (0.18) is the only model above 0.15, with Qwen3-8B (0.19) being the sole exception that slightly edges it. In the real-robot experiment, SCT-4B (0.70–0.90) substantially outperforms the Fast-Forward PDDL solver (0.20–0.70) under both VLM-based and rule-based perception, demonstrating robustness to imperfect logical states that the symbolic solver lacks. Notably, Qwen3-30B (0.72 overall) still outperforms SCT-4B, confirming that scale retains an advantage, but SCT-4B narrows the gap to within 0.26 while using ~30% fewer tokens.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the line of LLM-based PDDL domain generation (Huang et al. 2025b, Oswald et al. 2024, Han et al. 2024a) but fundamentally reinterprets the generated domains: rather than treating them as one-shot planning utilities for symbolic search, it repurposes them as dual-purpose training infrastructure—both as scalable data generators for CoT supervision and as structured reward functions for RL. It also draws on the SFT+RL post-training paradigm established for reasoning LLMs (Wei et al. 2022, Shao et al. 2024, Rafailov et al. 2023) and applies it to robotic planning, where prior work has relied on manually curated reasoning traces or sparse success-based rewards. The symbolic-to-CoT transformation step departs from prior work that either trains directly on raw symbolic traces (which the paper shows leads to memorization) or uses CoT distilled from larger models (which is costly and token-inefficient).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a self-contained, annotation-free pipeline for improving LLM planning capability, which is practically significant given the cost of collecting high-quality robotic reasoning traces. The real-robot experiments demonstrating robustness to VLM perception noise (where PDDL solvers fail) highlight a concrete deployment advantage of internalized planning over external symbolic search. Key limitations include the assumption that tasks can be cleanly abstracted into PDDL (excluding continuous, deformable, or contact-rich manipulation), the dependence on a base LLM with sufficient reasoning ability to generate valid domains, and the evaluation scope being limited to a single action-space family. Open questions include whether the approach scales to more complex multi-robot or continuous-control settings, and how it integrates with learned low-level controllers like VLA models.
