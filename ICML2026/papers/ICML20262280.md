## Problem

Existing fMRI foundation models rely on either lossy parcellation representations (reducing dimensionality ~100×) or computationally expensive full 4D volumes, with no systematic comparison of intermediate representations. Additionally, the field lacks a reproducible, standardized benchmark suite, making cross-model comparisons unreliable due to inconsistent preprocessing and evaluation protocols.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262280/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes 16-frame fMRI clips (16 s duration) in one of three representations: 224×560 cortical flat map images (77K valid pixels), 400-parcel Schaefer time series, or sparse cortical volumes restricted to ~132K gray-matter voxels (vs. 900K for full MRI). After pretraining, the ViT-B encoder produces patch-level embeddings that are probed with logistic regression (trait prediction, 100 random splits) or attentive probes (state decoding, single fixed split) to yield classification labels for 21 cognitive tasks or 24 object categories.

## Method

CortexMAE adapts the spatiotemporal masked autoencoder (MAE-st) to fMRI by projecting 3D volumes onto a 2D cortical flat map via pycortex, making the data directly compatible with standard ViT spacetime patch embeddings (p_t×16×16). The encoder processes a sparse subset of observed patches while the decoder reconstructs masked ones using tube masking (0.9 ratio) to prevent temporal interpolation, trained with MSE loss only on valid non-background pixels. The key insight is that flat maps occupy a 'goldilocks zone'—preserving full cortical signal like volumes while injecting geometric inductive bias and reducing sequence length like parcellation. The authors train three variants (flat, parcel, volume) on 2.1K hours of HCP-YA data, establish the first power-law scaling analysis for fMRI, and release BRAINMARKS, a reproducible benchmark suite covering seven datasets and six prior foundation models.

## Results

On dynamic cognitive state decoding, CortexMAE-F (flat map) achieves 98.9% balanced accuracy on HCP-YA Task21 and 31.0% on NSD COCO24, outperforming all prior fMRI foundation models by a large margin (next best: BrainHarmonix-F at 94.4% and 23.8%). On subject-level trait prediction, the paper reports a challenging null result: no single model reliably outperforms a simple functional connectivity (FC) baseline across the four clinical diagnosis datasets (ABIDE, ADHD-200, ADNI, PPMI). CortexMAE-V (volume) leads on HCP-A age prediction (53.4% vs. 56.6% for NeuroSTORM), while CortexMAE-F leads on sex prediction (87.4%). Notably, a pretrained MAE-st that has never seen fMRI data (95.8% Task21, 24.3% COCO24) outperforms all prior fMRI-specific foundation models on state decoding, approaching CortexMAE's performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On dynamic state decoding, CortexMAE-F dominates all baselines: it beats the best prior parcellation model (BrainHarmonix-F) by 4.5 points on Task21 (98.9% vs. 94.4%) and by 7.2 points on COCO24 (31.0% vs. 23.8%), and outperforms volume-based models NeuroSTORM (70.5%/12.2%) and SwiFT (63.6%/7.7%) by even larger margins. The FC connectome baseline (82.4% Task21, 7.4% COCO24) is clearly surpassed by all foundation models on state decoding, confirming that learned representations capture dynamic information beyond static connectivity. On trait prediction, results are inconsistent: NeuroSTORM leads on ADNI (66.8%) and HCP-A Age (56.6%), while CortexMAE-P leads on ABIDE (62.0%) and PPMI (61.4%), but no model reliably beats the FC baseline across all four clinical datasets. A striking finding is that a generic MAE-st pretrained on natural video (never exposed to fMRI) achieves 95.8% on Task21 and 24.3% on COCO24, outperforming all prior fMRI-specific foundation models on state decoding and approaching CortexMAE's performance, suggesting that much of the benefit may come from the ViT architecture and flat map representation rather than fMRI-specific pretraining. The paper attributes the poor state decoding of SwiFT, Brain-JEPA, and NeuroSTORM to their apparent lack of coordinate normalization during pretraining, which renders them effectively blind to the functional (dynamic) component of the BOLD signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the spatiotemporal masked autoencoder (MAE-st) framework to fMRI by introducing a cortical flat map projection that converts 3D volumes into 2D images directly compatible with standard Vision Transformers. It departs from prior fMRI foundation models (BrainLM, Brain-JEPA, NeuroSTORM, SwiFT, Brain-Semantoks), which all use either parcellation or volume representations, by proposing flat maps as an intermediate 'goldilocks' representation that preserves cortical signal while injecting geometric inductive bias. The paper also introduces BRAINMARKS, the first open, reproducible benchmark suite for fMRI foundation models, addressing the reproducibility gap identified in prior evaluations where results were difficult to compare due to inconsistent preprocessing and evaluation protocols.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The most consequential finding is a well-evidenced null result: no fMRI foundation model reliably outperforms a simple functional connectivity baseline on standard trait prediction benchmarks, challenging the field's implicit assumption that large-scale self-supervised pretraining yields clinically useful representations. On dynamic state decoding, where sample sizes are larger, CortexMAE's flat map variant does achieve clear SOTA, and a generic MAE-st pretrained on natural images (never seeing fMRI) approaches its performance, raising questions about how much domain-specific pretraining is truly necessary. Open questions include whether multi-source pretraining (beyond HCP-YA) can break the observed scaling saturation at ~37M parameters, whether better intermediate representations exist, and how to translate dynamic state representations into clinical applications. The UKBB result—where 10× more data underperforms HCP-YA—suggests data quality and diversity may matter more than raw volume.
