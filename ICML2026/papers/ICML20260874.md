## Problem

Large reasoning models generate excessively long chain-of-thought traces, inflating inference cost and latency. Existing compression methods rely on external teacher models, handcrafted pruning heuristics, or reinforcement learning pipelines, all of which add substantial training complexity and cost. The paper asks whether LRMs can naturally produce shorter reasoning traces under certain prompting conditions, and whether that behavior can be internalized without any of these external dependencies.

## Input / Output

At inference time, the fine-tuned model takes a single text question and produces a compressed reasoning trace (within explicit think tags) followed by a boxed final answer. During data construction, the model instead receives a packed prompt containing N independent questions (N=3 by default) separated by neutral delimiters, and generates per-question reasoning traces and answers in a single response. The 32k context window and temperature-0.6 sampling ensure full multi-question rollouts without truncation, and only traces whose extracted answers match ground truth are retained as training targets.

## Method

ConPress exploits a reproducible inference-time phenomenon called self-compression: when N independent questions share a single prompt, the model spontaneously shortens each per-question reasoning trace due to multi-question contextual pressure, with compression scaling monotonically from N=2 to N=8. The pipeline has three stages: (1) multi-question sampling, where N questions are packed into one prompt and the model generates all per-question traces; (2) compressed trace extraction, where per-question traces are parsed via question-identifier anchors and filtered by answer correctness (rejection sampling); (3) single-question transfer, where the filtered compressed traces serve as SFT targets under standard token-level negative log-likelihood, teaching the model to produce concise reasoning when given a single question. The entire supervision is self-generated—no external teacher, no manual rewriting, no RL—requiring only ~8k fine-tuning examples and ~84 A100 GPU-hours for the full pipeline.

## Results

ConPress reduces average reasoning token usage by 48.7% on Qwen3-4B-Thinking, 33.9% on R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, and 40.2% on R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, while maintaining accuracy within 0.6 points of the original models. On MATH500, ConPress cuts tokens from 6634 to 2661 (Qwen3-4B) and from 3136 to 1720 (R1-7B) with accuracy preserved or slightly improved. On the harder AIME25 benchmark, token usage drops from 21442 to 14258 (Qwen3-4B) and from 10643 to 8698 (R1-7B), with accuracy drops of 2.4 and 0.4 points respectively. The full pipeline requires only ~8k fine-tuning examples and ~84 A100 GPU hours for the 7B model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ConPress is compared against five baselines: RFT_shortest (rejection fine-tuning on shortest correct trace), DPO_shortest (preference optimization between shortest and longest correct traces), and three RL-based methods (LC-R1, ThinkPrune, AdaptThink). RFT_shortest provides negligible compression (only +0.5% token change on R1-7B), while DPO_shortest achieves moderate compression (−31.4% on R1-7B) but at a notable accuracy cost (−2.3 points). Among RL-based methods, LC-R1 achieves the strongest compression (−45.4% on R1-7B) but suffers the largest accuracy drop (−3.1 points); AdaptThink achieves −36.3% tokens with −1.2 accuracy. ConPress achieves comparable compression to RL methods (−33.9% on R1-7B, −40.2% on R1-1.5B) while preserving accuracy (ΔAcc = +0.0 and −0.2 respectively), outperforming all baselines on the accuracy–efficiency trade-off. On Qwen3-4B-Thinking, ConPress achieves a 48.7% token reduction with only −0.6 accuracy, far exceeding DPO_shortest (−13.1%) and RFT_shortest (−5.9%) in compression while maintaining accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from both dominant paradigms for reducing LRM reasoning length—SFT with external teacher models or curated pruning pipelines, and RL with token-level penalties or budget constraints—by identifying an emergent inference-time phenomenon (self-compression under multi-question contexts) and repurposing it as a self-supervised training signal. Conceptually, it connects to the multi-question/batch prompting literature (REST, MathFusion, batch prompting), which has treated multi-question inputs primarily as an evaluation or efficiency setting, but here the multi-question prompt is used as a data-generation mechanism rather than an inference-time trick. The method is closest in spirit to rejection fine-tuning (RFT) in that it filters for correctness, but the supervision source is the model's own contextually-compressed outputs rather than multiple single-question samples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of self-compression as a reproducible, model-agnostic, and structurally-driven phenomenon is the paper's most valuable contribution: it reveals that reasoning verbosity is not solely a training objective but is also modulated by prompt structure, opening a new axis for controlling inference cost without RL or external teachers. Practically, achieving 30–60% token reduction with only 8k SFT examples and no RL pipeline is attractive for deployment, though the residual accuracy-efficiency trade-off (especially on harder benchmarks like AIME25) and the limited evaluation to math-adjacent domains temper the generality claim. Open questions include whether the phenomenon has a mechanistic explanation in attention or internal representations, whether it extends to non-math reasoning at scale, and whether the compression can be made difficulty-adaptive rather than uniformly aggressive on easy problems.
