## Problem

While chain-of-thought reasoning substantially improves LLM performance on complex tasks, the precise mechanisms by which different reasoning strategies facilitate generalization remain poorly understood. Existing explanations are either qualitative (e.g., 'structure' or 'relevance' of reasoning chains) and thus not quantifiable, or quantitative but associated with conflicting evidence (e.g., trajectory length shows inconsistent correlations with effectiveness). The paper asks: is there a principled, quantitative measure that consistently predicts which reasoning strategies will generalize well?

## Input / Output

The method takes a fixed pretrained LLM (Gemma-3 1B or 4B), a task dataset (GSM8K training split), and a set of reasoning strategies (14 variants ranging from No CoT to Executed PoT, Critical CoT, etc.) as input. For each strategy, it produces an intrinsic dimensionality score — the minimum number of LoRA trainable parameters needed to reach a common accuracy threshold — which then serves as a predictor of both in-distribution and out-of-distribution generalization performance. The threshold τ is set uniformly across all strategies (90% of the maximum full-capacity training accuracy after one epoch), ensuring comparability even when strategies differ in maximum attainable performance.

## Method

The paper inverts the standard intrinsic dimensionality setup: rather than varying the model to study a fixed task, it fixes the model architecture and varies the training data by changing the reasoning strategy used to generate solutions. It uses LoRA as a structured low-dimensional projection, sweeping across configurations that vary both the rank r and which weight matrices receive adaptation (attention Q/V, all attention, MLP, or all layers), with parameter counts uniformly distributed in log scale from ~1M to full capacity. For each configuration, the model is fine-tuned and training accuracy is recorded; the intrinsic dimension d_int is the minimum parameter count at which accuracy crosses the common threshold τ. The key finding is a strong inverse Spearman correlation (0.93 for Gemma-3 4B, 0.75 for 1B) between d_int and generalization performance across 14 reasoning strategies, substantially outperforming baseline metrics like trajectory length (0.31), KL divergence (−0.17), and token perplexity (0.82). This holds across model scales, threshold selections (70%–90%), and diverse reasoning domains (mathematical, algorithmic, and cognitive tasks from Reasoning Gym).

## Results

On Gemma-3 4B, intrinsic dimensionality achieves a Spearman rank correlation of 0.93 with overall generalization accuracy across 14 reasoning strategies, substantially outperforming token perplexity (0.82), trajectory length (0.31), and KL divergence (−0.17). On Gemma-3 1B, the correlation remains strong at 0.75. Executed PoT achieves the lowest intrinsic dimensionality (1.49M trainable parameters) and the best OOD accuracy (43.40%), while No CoT requires the most capacity (5246M) and performs worst (7.18% overall). The finding is robust to threshold selection, with correlations ranging from 0.72 to 0.94 across 70%–90% training accuracy thresholds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Intrinsic dimensionality (Spearman ρ = 0.93 on 4B, 0.75 on 1B) decisively outperforms all alternative metrics for predicting reasoning strategy effectiveness. Token perplexity is the closest competitor at ρ = 0.82 (4B) and 0.63 (1B), but still falls short; the authors note the two metrics are interrelated since low-surprisal chains are also more compressible. Trajectory length shows only weak correlation (ρ = 0.31 on 4B, 0.24 on 1B), consistent with conflicting prior evidence about optimal chain length. Sequence KL divergence performs worst (ρ = −0.17 on 4B), as it is unduly influenced by sequence length and thus cannot fairly compare strategies of different lengths. LongPPL (Fang et al., 2025), a length-aware perplexity variant, achieves ρ = 0.60–0.62 on 4B, well below both token perplexity (0.82) and intrinsic dimensionality (0.93), confirming that the additional long-context correction does not help in this setting where CoT strategies fall within the short-context window.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the intrinsic dimensionality framework from Li et al. (2018) and Aghajanyan et al. (2021)—originally used to characterize how different models compress a fixed task—by inverting the experimental setup: the model is held fixed while the task formulation is varied through different chain-of-thought reasoning strategies. LoRA (Hu et al., 2022), itself motivated by intrinsic dimension findings, serves as the practical mechanism for sweeping parameter budgets. The conceptual grounding draws on the Minimum Description Length principle (Rissanen, 1978; Grünwald, 2007), positioning effective reasoning as a form of task compression rather than merely increased test-time computation or structural guidance, which are the dominant but non-quantifiable explanations in prior CoT analysis work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled, quantitative predictor of reasoning-strategy effectiveness that outperforms length, KL divergence, and perplexity baselines, with practical implications for how to annotate reasoning data, align strategies to student models, and design regularizers. However, the measurement protocol (sweeping 20–30 LoRA configurations per strategy) is computationally expensive, limiting direct optimization; the authors acknowledge that cheaper proxies remain an open question. Generalization beyond mathematical reasoning is only partially tested (Reasoning Gym covers algorithmic and cognitive tasks but not broader domains), and the framework has not yet been applied to RL-based post-training settings where reasoning chains are generated by the model itself rather than a teacher.
