## Problem

Transformer-based neural operators use dense dot-product attention that scores all token pairs uniformly, ignoring the near-far structure of PDE kernels where far-field interactions are numerically compressible. Classical fast solvers exploit this via hierarchical trees, but embedding such exponentially branching structures in Euclidean space incurs inherent geometric distortion. The paper addresses this mismatch by grounding the attention kernel in hyperbolic geometry, which naturally accommodates exponential volume growth.

## Input / Output

The method takes a PDE input field (coefficient or forcing) defined on an arbitrary domain geometry—point clouds, structured or unstructured meshes, or regular grids—and produces the full solution field on the same domain. The input field conditions the kernel, making the interaction metric data-adaptive rather than fixed to physical coordinates. The operator is resolution-agnostic: the same parameter set applies across discretization resolutions, and for large-scale meshes (~32k nodes) the integral is approximated via Nyström quadrature with M≪N representative tokens (patch tokens on grids/meshes, learned latent tokens on point clouds).

## Method

HNO replaces the standard Euclidean dot-product attention logit with a Gibbs kernel over stabilized hyperbolic geodesic distance on the Lorentz hyperboloid: query and key vectors are lifted via Π(z)=(√(1+‖z‖²), z), and attention weights are exp(−d_ε/τ) where d_ε = arcosh(max{1+ε, −⟨u,v⟩_M}). The key mechanism is radius-angle coupling: the hyperbolic law of cosines shows that bounded distance increasingly constrains angular separation as radii grow (exponentially in r_u+r_v), so tokens at large learned radii attend only within exponentially narrow cones (local/near-field) while small-radius tokens remain broadly connected (global/far-field). This yields a learnable, data-adaptive near-far routing within a single attention mechanism without explicit FMM trees or multipole expansions. For scalability, the dense N×N score matrix is avoided by routing interactions through M≪N representative tokens, reducing per-layer cost from O(N²) to O(NM+M²). The full operator follows the standard lift–process–project neural operator architecture with the hyperbolic kernel as the sole nonlocal coupling term.

## Results

HNO achieves the lowest mean relative ℓ2 error on all six standard PDE benchmarks, with relative improvements of +42.2% (Elasticity), +24.2% (Navier-Stokes), +16.7% (Darcy), +25.0% (Plasticity), +9.4% (Airfoil), and +35.7% (Pipe) over the second-best method. On the two large-scale ~32k-node unstructured CFD tasks (ShapeNet Car and AirfRANS), HNO also produces lower drag/lift coefficient errors and cleaner residual maps than Transolver++ and RNO. HNO uses only 0.82M parameters versus 2.83M for Transolver/Transolver++/RNO, while consuming 0.227 GB VRAM (vs. 6.07 GB for Transolver++) and 4.47 ms/batch inference (vs. 74.77 ms for Transolver++).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HNO outperforms all 19 baselines across all six standard benchmarks. The closest competitors are Transolver++ (best transformer) and RNO (best frequency-domain method): on Elasticity, HNO achieves 0.0037 vs. 0.0064 (Transolver++) and 0.0078 (RNO); on Navier-Stokes, 0.0676 vs. 0.1010 and 0.0894; on Pipe, 0.0027 vs. 0.0042 and 0.0044. Among classic CNN surrogates, U-Net is the strongest (e.g., 0.0235 on Elasticity), still 6.4× worse than HNO. On the two large-scale ~32k-node unstructured CFD tasks (ShapeNet Car, AirfRANS), HNO—using the Transolver++ backbone with only the attention logits swapped to hyperbolic-distance logits—produces lower per-sample lift/drag coefficient errors and visibly cleaner residual maps than both Transolver++ and RNO. In ablations, replacing hyperbolic distance with Euclidean dot-product logits raises Darcy error by 10.5%, confirming the gain is geometric rather than architectural. HNO also uses ~3.5× fewer parameters (0.82M vs. 2.83M) and ~27× less VRAM than Transolver++ (0.227 GB vs. 6.07 GB) while achieving lower error.

## Relation to Prior Work

HNO extends the kernel-integral neural operator framework (Kovachki et al., 2023) by replacing the standard Euclidean dot-product attention kernel with a Gibbs kernel defined on stabilized hyperbolic geodesic distances in the Lorentz model (Nickel & Kiela, 2018). Conceptually, it bridges classical fast-solver theory—where near-far decomposition and hierarchical matrix compression exploit the low-rank structure of well-separated kernel blocks (Greengard & Rokhlin, 1987; Hackbusch, 2015)—with modern transformer-based operator learning, but without implementing explicit multipole expansions or tree data structures. The key departure from prior transformer-based operators (Transolver, Transolver++, GNOT) is the geometric inductive bias: hyperbolic space's exponential volume growth naturally accommodates the exponentially branching hierarchy of near-far interactions, whereas Euclidean dot-product attention allocates pairwise scoring uniformly regardless of spatial separation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HNO provides a principled, geometry-driven mechanism for multi-scale attention in neural operators that is both more accurate and substantially more memory-efficient than dense dot-product transformer baselines (0.23 GB vs. 2.2–6.1 GB VRAM on Darcy), making it practical for large-scale unstructured CFD problems. The matched Euclidean routing controls (temperature gates, radial-basis gates) and causal radius interventions strongly support that the hyperbolic geometry itself—not merely a learnable scale variable—is the active ingredient, which is a useful negative result for the broader attention-design literature. Open questions include whether the advantage persists at even larger token counts or in 3D+time settings, whether the token-compression bottleneck (M ≪ N) can be made input-adaptive, and whether the approach extends to problems where far-field interactions are not asymptotically smooth (e.g., strongly nonlinear or discontinuous kernels).
