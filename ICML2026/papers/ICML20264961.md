## Problem

Multi-group mean estimation requires allocating a finite sampling budget across K groups to obtain uniformly accurate mean estimates, but existing UCB-based approaches (Aznag et al., 2023) impose an unnecessary exploration phase. The paper shows that because the optimal allocation inherently requires Ω(T) samples per arm, explicit exploration is redundant, and it further extends the framework to contextual bandits—an underexplored setting where UCB-type methods provably fail due to the data-dependent norm structure of the estimation error.

## Input / Output

The method takes a time horizon T, K arms with σ-subgaussian reward distributions, and optionally a variance lower bound σ² (or context vectors c_t ∈ ℝ^d in the contextual setting) as input. It produces per-group mean estimates μ̂_k(T) (or ridge-regression parameter estimates β̂_k in the contextual case) along with the allocation scheme n_k. The objective is a p-norm aggregation of per-group MSEs, R_p(n) = ‖{σ_k²/n_k}‖_p, which differs fundamentally from regret minimization in classical bandits since every arm must receive Θ(T) samples rather than concentrating on the best arm.

## Method

The core insight is that the optimal Neyman-type allocation n_k* ∝ σ_k^q (where q = 2p/(p+1)) already prescribes Ω(T) plays per arm, making a dedicated exploration phase unnecessary. The non-adaptive algorithm (Algorithm 1) uses a uniform round-robin initial phase of length τ to estimate variances, then allocates remaining budget proportionally to estimated σ̂_k^q; the adaptive algorithm (Algorithm 2) replaces the known variance lower bound with data-driven LCB/UCB confidence intervals and an arm-elimination loop that adaptively adjusts allocation weights. For the contextual extension (Algorithm 3), the paper leverages Tropp's matrix concentration inequality to bound the ridge-regression estimation error, and critically, the allocation decision is made before observing the context to preserve independence of context vectors across arms. The analysis is sharpened by a refined Hanson-Wright-based variance concentration inequality (Lemma 2.4) that removes a √log(1/δ) factor present in prior bounds, and by identifying strictly subgaussian distributions (Gaussian, Rademacher, symmetric Beta) that yield constants matching the ideal Gaussian case.

## Results

At T=10⁶ with K=4 Gaussian arms, the proposed exploration-free algorithm achieves numerical regret of 3.62×10⁻¹⁰ (GSG) and 4.15×10⁻¹¹ (SSG) for p=1, compared to 5.77×10⁻⁸ for the UCB baseline from Aznag et al. (2023) — a roughly 160× improvement. Theoretically, the optimality gap scales as T^{-3/2}√(log T) for p=∞ and T^{-2} log T for finite p, matching known minimax lower bounds up to logarithmic factors while improving on the prior log T dependence. In the contextual bandit setting (K∈{5,10,20}, d=4), the empirical regret exhibits a slope close to −2, consistent with the predicted T^{-2} log T rate from Theorem 5.5.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the UCB-based algorithm from Aznag et al. (2023), which uses a truncation-based concentration inequality (Carpentier et al., 2011) and an explicit exploration phase. The proposed exploration-free algorithm outperforms this baseline by approximately two orders of magnitude in numerical regret at T=10⁶ across all p values (e.g., 4.15×10⁻¹¹ vs. 5.77×10⁻⁸ for p=1). Even the UCB-refined variant — which applies the paper's improved Hanson-Wright-based concentration inequality to the original UCB algorithm — still lags behind the exploration-free method by roughly 27× for p=1, confirming that the algorithmic design (removing UCB exploration) contributes independently of the tighter concentration bound. Theoretically, the paper removes one factor of √(log T) (for p=∞) or one factor of log T (for finite p) relative to Aznag et al., while also eliminating the K^{3/2} or K³ dependence present in their bounds. In the strictly subgaussian (SSG) setting, the first-phase length reduces to a constant independent of σ, enabling faster convergence in practice, as confirmed by the Rademacher-and-Gaussian experiment where SSG achieves the theoretical guarantees at much smaller T than GSG.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the multi-group mean estimation framework of Aznag et al. (2023), replacing their UCB-based active learning algorithm with exploration-free non-adaptive and adaptive allocation schemes that achieve tighter regret bounds. The concentration inequality improvement builds on the Hanson-Wright inequality (Rudelson & Vershynin, 2013; Epperly, 2022) to sharpen the variance estimation bounds of Carpentier et al. (2011), removing a √log(1/δ) factor. The contextual extension adopts the linear contextual bandit model of Slivkins (2011; 2019) and connects to the Neyman allocation literature (Zhao, 2024; Li et al., 2024) for treatment-effect estimation, while the adaptive arm-elimination mechanism draws on best-arm identification techniques (Karnin et al., 2013; Cai et al., 2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that explicit UCB-style exploration is structurally unnecessary for multi-group mean estimation because the optimal allocation already requires Ω(T) samples per arm, yielding simpler algorithms with tighter theoretical guarantees. The demonstration that UCB-type methods completely fail in the contextual setting (the data-dependent norm cannot be converted to the Euclidean norm needed for the objective) is an important negative result that redirects algorithm design. Open questions include developing adaptive algorithms that exploit partially known distributional structure (e.g., strictly subgaussian families with unknown variances) and extending the framework to non-stochastic or adversarial context distributions, which the current analysis does not cover.
