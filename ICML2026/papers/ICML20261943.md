## Problem

Current LLM pretraining entangles the acquisition of semantic knowledge with the development of abstract reasoning skills, causing models to rely on surface-level heuristics rather than systematic procedures. This paper investigates whether a lightweight 'warm-up' stage on abstract, non-semantic procedural data can build algorithmic scaffolding that accelerates and improves subsequent standard pretraining on semantically-rich corpora.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261943/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The two-stage pipeline first consumes procedurally-generated sequences (balanced brackets, cellular automaton states, sequence transformations, stack operations) of up to 128 tokens, where loss is computed only on output tokens for input/output pair tasks. It then consumes standard semantic data (natural language, code, or informal mathematics) in the usual next-token prediction format. The final model produces next-token predictions evaluated via perplexity on semantic corpora and accuracy on algorithmic diagnostic tasks (needle-in-a-haystack, arithmetic, sorting) and downstream benchmarks.

## Method

The core procedure is a two-phase pretraining: a short 'procedural pretraining' stage (as little as 0.1–0.3% of total tokens) on data generated by simple algorithms—formal languages (k-Dyck, k-Dyck Shuffle), cellular automata (ECA Rule 110), sequence transformations (SET, UNION, SORT, REVERSE, IDENTITY, DELETE), and memory operations (STACK)—followed by standard pretraining on semantic data, with all weights trained in both phases. A key mechanistic finding is that useful pretrained information is localized: attention layers carry the transferable structure for structured domains (code, math) while MLP layers benefit natural language, and selectively transferring only the relevant component can outperform full-model transfer. The paper further shows that benefits from different procedural data types are additive and can be combined either by mixing data sources during pretraining or by surgically assembling attention layers from one pretrained model with MLP layers from another. Across scales up to 1.3B parameters and 10.5B tokens, procedural pretraining enables reaching equivalent loss with 55–86% of the original semantic data, and gains persist after downstream fine-tuning.

## Results

Pretraining on Dyck sequences (balanced brackets) boosts needle-in-a-haystack accuracy from 11.3% to 98.3%, while ECA Rule 110 improves reversed addition from 76.4% to 91.1%. In the substitutive setting, as little as 2.1M procedural tokens (0.1–0.3% of the semantic corpus) enables models to match baseline perplexity using only 55% of C4 tokens, 67% of CODEPARROT tokens, and 86% of DEEPMIND-MATH tokens. At 1.3B scale, procedural pretraining (UNION) reduces C4 perplexity from 28.8 to 27.3 and CODEPARROT perplexity from 3.45 to 3.36, and a 124M model with procedural warm-up achieves non-random MBPP accuracy where the baseline scores zero across 80 attempts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is standard pretraining with no procedural warm-up (T₁=0), and the paper consistently shows procedural pretraining outperforms it across all domains and scales. In the additive setting, 2–4M procedural tokens (0.1–0.3% of the semantic corpus) yield clear perplexity gains over the baseline on C4, CODEPARROT, and DEEPMIND-MATH, with additional procedural tokens beyond ~4M providing diminishing returns. In the substitutive setting, procedural tokens act as an efficient substitute: 2.1M procedural tokens save ~45% of C4 semantic tokens, 2.1M save ~33% of CODEPARROT tokens, and 10.4M save ~14% of DEEPMIND-MATH tokens. Selective transfer experiments reveal that attention-only transfer dominates for pure code (JavaCorpus) while MLP-only transfer dominates for natural language (WikiText, C4), and full-model transfer is best for mixed domains (documented code, informal math). The paper also rules out simpler explanations: shuffling procedural sequences eliminates all gains, layer-wise weight shuffling collapses performance to baseline, and explicit attention-entropy regularisation fails to replicate the benefits, confirming that precise structural information in the pretrained weights is essential.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on pretraining with synthetic/procedural data (Wu et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2025) by repositioning it from a drop-in substitute or linguistic imitation tool to an explicit complementary 'warming-up' stage before standard semantic pretraining. It departs from prior work by adopting a broader algorithmic perspective—systematically mapping which procedural data types improve which specific skills—and by localizing the transferable information to distinct architectural components (attention vs. MLP layers), a dimension prior studies did not investigate. The motivation draws on the knowledge-reasoning entanglement critique (Han et al., 2025; Kumar et al., 2025), proposing procedural data as a data-driven path to inject an 'algorithmic reasoning prior' before world-knowledge acquisition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: as little as 0.1–0.3% procedural tokens (2–4M out of 655M–1.6B) consistently outperforms standard pretraining across language, code, and math, and enables reaching equivalent loss with 55–86% of the original semantic data—a meaningful FLOP reduction for large-scale training. The localization finding (attention for structured domains, MLPs for natural language) is practically actionable and conceptually intriguing given that MLPs are thought to store factual knowledge yet are improved by purely abstract data. Key limitations include the modest model scale (up to 1.3B parameters) and proof-of-concept status of multi-source combination; open questions include whether gains persist at frontier scale, a first-principles explanation for why procedural data helps, and optimal mixture strategies across many procedural sources.
