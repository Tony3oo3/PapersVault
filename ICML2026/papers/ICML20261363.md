## Problem

Chain-of-thought (CoT) monitoring is a promising oversight channel for increasingly capable AI agents, but 'monitorability'—how well a monitor can actually detect safety-relevant properties from available observations—remains poorly characterized. There is no standardized metric invariant to class imbalance, no broad evaluation suite spanning different behavioral regimes, and no understanding of how monitorability scales with model size, reasoning effort, or monitor compute. This paper addresses these gaps to enable reliable measurement, regression detection, and targeted improvement of CoT monitorability.

## Input / Output

The monitor (an LLM judge, typically GPT-5 Thinking at high reasoning effort) receives agent trajectories at one of three scopes: CoT-only (intermediate reasoning text), action-only (tool calls and final answer), or all-messages (full trajectory). For intervention evaluations, the monitor is additionally given the task prompt and a description of the hypothesized intervention, and must output a binary judgment of whether the intervention meaningfully influenced the observed behavior. Per-trajectory judgments are aggregated into a g-mean² score (TPR × TNR), which is invariant to base-rate shifts; for intervention settings, TPR is replaced by a 'minimal criterion TPR' that scores against the total-effect lower bound on causal attribution rather than requiring per-sample causal labels.

## Method

The paper introduces a formal two-sided definition of monitorability (agent output informativeness × monitor extraction ability) and a g-mean² metric with an intervention-specific variant that uses total effect to lower-bound the fraction of positives attributable to the intervention, avoiding the need for unobservable per-sample causal labels. It organizes evaluations into three archetypes—intervention (controlled perturbation, detect *why* behavior changed), process (constrained solution paths, detect valid intermediate reasoning given a correct answer), and outcome-property (unambiguously labelable output property, predict it from observation)—and builds a broad suite spanning sycophancy, bias, misalignment, and neutral regimes. Empirically, the paper studies scaling along pretraining compute and inference-time compute, identifies a 'monitorability tax' (capability loss from switching to a smaller model at higher reasoning effort for greater monitorability), and shows that asking follow-up questions and providing the follow-up CoT to the monitor substantially improves monitorability, especially for intervention evaluations.

## Results

The paper reports g-mean² (TPR × TNR) as its primary monitorability metric, where a random monitor scores 0.25. Across frontier models, CoT-only monitoring substantially outperforms action-only monitoring (which sees only tool calls and final answers), with all-messages monitors performing best overall. GPT-5 Thinking and o4-mini achieve the highest aggregate monitorability, while Claude 3.7 Sonnet has the lowest CoT monitorability despite relatively strong action-only scores. Scaling the monitor's test-time compute (o4-mini) raises g-mean² by ~12% with CoT access versus only ~4% with action-only observations, and upgrading from o3-mini to GPT-5 as monitor yields nearly +40% improvement across reasoning efforts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare a single proposed method against named external baselines; instead, it compares three monitor scopes (CoT-only, action-only, all-messages) and multiple frontier reasoning models (GPT-5 Thinking, o3, o3-mini, o4-mini, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, DeepSeek R1-0528-Qwen3-8B, Kimi K2 Thinking) on a unified evaluation suite. The central comparison is between monitoring signals: CoT-only monitors consistently and substantially outperform action-only monitors across all models and reasoning efforts, while all-messages monitors (seeing the full trajectory) perform best. Among models, GPT-5 and o4-mini lead in aggregate monitorability; o3-mini sits on the Pareto frontier of monitorability versus CoT length; and Claude 3.7 Sonnet has the weakest CoT monitorability. The paper also identifies a 'monitorability tax' tradeoff: at matched capability, a smaller model at higher reasoning effort can be substantially more monitorable than a larger model at lower effort, at the cost of additional inference compute. Follow-up questions (giving the monitor access to a follow-up CoT) further improve monitorability, approaching the effect of tailored follow-ups when multiple completions are sampled.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CoT monitorability framework formalized by Baker et al. (2025) and the CoT-necessity insights of Emmons et al. (2025) from narrow, single-setting evaluations (e.g., hinted GPQA, reward-hacking detection) into a systematic multi-archetype evaluation suite spanning intervention, process, and outcome-property designs. The intervention evaluation methodology builds on the controlled-perturbation approach of Chen et al. (2025) and Turpin et al. (2023), but introduces a novel minimal-criterion TPR grounded in causal total-effect lower bounds to handle stochastic agents. The g-mean² metric is adapted from the imbalanced-classification literature (Kubat & Matwin, 1997) to address class-prevalence invariance, and the outcome-property environments repurpose existing misalignment benchmarks (ShadeArena, anti-scheming, WMDP) under a unified monitorability framing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As reasoning models become central to deployment, CoT monitoring is a key ingredient in scalable oversight, yet its reliability under scaling, different training procedures, and architectural changes was previously unmeasured. The paper's actionable finding—that a smaller model at higher reasoning effort can be substantially more monitorable than a larger model at lower effort (the 'monitorability tax')—gives deployers a concrete lever, while the follow-up elicitation technique offers a low-cost way to boost monitorability post-hoc. Key open questions remain: whether monitorability degrades under continued pretraining scale or RL pressure (the paper's single-seed pretraining experiments show high variance), whether the evaluation suite captures all relevant failure modes (e.g., novel reasoning strategies that break process-eval ontologies), and how to handle the fundamental tension between CoT faithfulness and practical monitorability.
