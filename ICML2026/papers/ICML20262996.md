## Problem

Existing dataset auditing methods for deep learning are either intrusive—requiring watermarks or perturbations injected into datasets before release, which degrade model performance and introduce security risks—or they rely on empirical behavioral signals (accuracy gaps, confidence differences) that lack a principled mathematical connection to the training process and are highly sensitive to training randomness. A non-intrusive, theoretically grounded auditing method that produces statistically defensible evidence of dataset usage without modifying any data has been missing.

## Input / Output

DataGuard takes a black-box suspect model (queried only via its prediction API to obtain probability vectors), a protected target dataset, and a collection of auxiliary non-training datasets drawn from the same raw data pool as the target. The model's full or Top-N probability vectors are used to compute mutual information metrics; no access to model weights, hyperparameters, or training logs is required. The output is a single p-value (auditing score) derived from a Hotelling's T² test, where a small p-value indicates statistically significant evidence that the suspect model was trained on the target dataset, and a p-value of 1.0 indicates no evidence of usage.

## Method

DataGuard integrates three components: (1) differential comparison, where the target dataset's information-plane metrics are contrasted against auxiliary non-training baselines to isolate the training-specific behavioral gap; (2) information forensics, which establishes formal inequalities on the Information Plane—specifically, I(T;Y) is provably higher and I(X;T) provably lower for training data than for non-training data (Theorems 3.1 and 3.3), grounded in cross-entropy minimization and Fokker-Planck diffusion dynamics of SGD; and (3) a Hotelling's T² multivariate test that jointly models the two-dimensional vector (I(X;T), I(T;Y)) and its covariance across auxiliary datasets, yielding an asymptotic χ² distribution under the null hypothesis without requiring parametric assumptions on the underlying metrics. Mutual information is estimated via binning of model confidence scores into 200 equal intervals, following the Information Plane framework. The approach is entirely non-intrusive: no dataset modification, no watermark injection, and no model internals are needed, making it applicable to already-released datasets and black-box model APIs.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and Tiny-ImageNet with VGG-16, ResNet-18, and DeiT, DataGuard achieves p-values below machine precision (<10⁻³⁰⁸) for all positive cases (suspect model trained on target dataset) and p-values of exactly 1.0 for all negative cases, yielding a 0% False Detection Rate across 100 independent runs. Correct Detection Rate ranges from 92% (VGG-16 on Tiny-ImageNet) to 100% (all models on CIFAR-10). In the head-to-head comparison on CIFAR-10, DataGuard incurs 0% Degradation of Benign Accuracy (DBA) and 0% Harmful Degree (H), whereas all eight watermarking baselines incur 0.92–9.59% DBA and 10–100% H.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All nine baselines (eight watermarking methods plus MemAud) correctly detect dataset usage, matching DataGuard's detection capability. However, DataGuard is the only method that incurs zero collateral damage: 0% DBA and 0% Harmful Degree across all three model architectures, while every watermarking baseline degrades benign accuracy by 0.92–9.59% (worst: Label-Consistent at 9.59% for RN-CF10) and misclassifies 10–100% of watermarked samples (worst: BadNets, Blended, and Label-Consistent at 100% H). Even the least intrusive watermark, Domain Watermarking (Guo et al., 2023), still misclassifies over 10% of watermarked data. MemAud (Huang et al., 2024) avoids backdoor triggers but still perturbs the dataset, causing 0.79–1.92% DBA. DataGuard's advantage is thus not in detection sensitivity (all methods detect) but in complete non-invasiveness: it requires no dataset modification, introduces no security vulnerability, and produces a statistically interpretable p-value rather than a binary watermark-trigger response.

## Relation to Prior Work

DataGuard repurposes the Information Plane framework (Shwartz-Ziv & Tishby, 2017) — originally a visualization tool for understanding training dynamics — into a formal auditing mechanism by establishing provable mutual-information inequalities that separate training from non-training data. It departs from the dominant watermarking-based auditing line (BadNets, WaNet, Domain Watermarking, etc.), which requires intrusive dataset modifications, and from dataset-inference methods (Maini et al., 2021; Xu & Li, 2022) that rely on unstable empirical signals like accuracy or confidence gaps. The statistical layer (Hotelling's T² with asymptotic χ² distribution) is borrowed from classical multivariate hypothesis testing, applied here for the first time to information-plane metrics in a provenance-verification context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DataGuard offers a legally defensible, zero-impact auditing tool at a time when high-profile lawsuits (NYT v. OpenAI, artists v. Midjourney) demand verifiable evidence of dataset misuse without the security and performance costs of watermarking. Key open questions include: extending the framework to LLMs where discrete token sequences break standard differential-entropy MI estimators; developing defenses against adversarial strategies that deliberately obscure information-plane structure; and handling settings where no auxiliary raw-data pool is available. The requirement for representative non-training baselines from the same distribution remains a practical constraint that limits immediate deployment in all real-world pipelines.
