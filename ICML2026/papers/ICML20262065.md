## Problem

The standard Transformer attention mechanism uses three separate learned projections (Query, Key, Value), but the individual contribution of each projection and the consequences of sharing or omitting them remain poorly understood. This gap is practically significant because the KV cache—built from the K and V projections—dominates inference memory in production LLM serving, yet no systematic study has characterized which projection-sharing constraints preserve quality while reducing that cache.

## Input / Output

The method is evaluated across four task families: (1) language modeling on 10B SlimPajama tokens with GPT-2 tokenization (vocab 50,304, max sequence 2048) producing next-token predictions; (2) image classification on 32×32 (CIFAR) and 224×224 (TinyImageNet, patch size 16) inputs producing class labels; (3) synthetic sequence tasks on integer sequences of length 16–128 producing transformed sequences; and (4) set anomaly detection on 10 frozen 512-dim ResNet-34 feature vectors producing the anomaly index. In all cases the only architectural change is the attention projection mechanism—embedding, MLP, normalization, and positional encoding remain identical to the QKV baseline.

## Method

The paper proposes three progressive projection-sharing constraints on self-attention: Q=K≠V (symmetric attention, no cache benefit), Q≠K=V (asymmetric attention with K reused as V, yielding 50% KV cache reduction), and Q=K=V (single projection, 50% cache but severe quality loss). For symmetric variants, a 2D sinusoidal positional encoding is injected via a 1×1 convolution over the attention map to restore directional asymmetry. The key empirical finding is that Q≠K=V preserves quality (3.1% perplexity degradation at 300M, 2.48% at 1.2B) because trained K and V matrices exhibit high cosine similarity (0.73) and similar effective rank, meaning they occupy overlapping representational space, whereas Q=K forces symmetric attention that breaks causal directionality. Crucially, projection sharing is orthogonal to head sharing: combining Q≠K=V with GQA-4 or MQA yields compound cache reductions of 87.5% and 96.9% respectively, validated at 1.2B scale with downstream task evaluations confirming that perplexity gaps do not translate to proportional capability losses.

## Results

On 300M-parameter language models trained on 10B SlimPajama tokens, Q≠K=V projection sharing achieves 50% KV cache reduction with only 3.1% validation perplexity degradation (5.27 vs. 5.11 for QKV), while Q=K≠V incurs 4.9% degradation with zero cache benefit and Q=K=V suffers catastrophic 25.4% degradation. At 1.2B scale, Q≠K=V degradation shrinks to 2.48%, and downstream 5-shot accuracy drops only 0.41% on average (35.99% vs. 36.40%). Combining Q≠K=V with GQA-4 yields 87.5% cache reduction (+3.9% PPL), and with MQA yields 96.9% reduction (+4.8% PPL), approaching the theoretical limit for cache-based Transformers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the standard QKV Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017), Q≠K=V trades 3.1% perplexity for a 50% KV cache reduction, making it the only two-projection variant with a practical deployment advantage; Q=K≠V performs comparably on vision tasks (0.766 avg accuracy vs. 0.758 for QKV) but offers zero inference memory savings since both K and V must still be cached separately. Against GQA-4 (Ainslie et al., 2023), Q≠K=V has worse perplexity (+3.1% vs. +0.7%) but the two are strictly complementary: combining them (Q-GQA-4) achieves 87.5% cache reduction with only 3.9% degradation, a point neither method reaches alone. Against MQA (Shazeer, 2019), Q≠K=V again shows higher perplexity cost (+3.1% vs. +1.5%), yet Q-MQA pushes cache reduction to 96.9% with 4.8% degradation—nearly the theoretical maximum while preserving asymmetric attention. On downstream 5-shot benchmarks at 1.2B scale, Q≠K=V (35.99% avg) is statistically indistinguishable from GQA-8 (35.86%) and within 0.41% of QKV (36.40%), confirming that the perplexity gap does not translate to meaningful task-level loss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work questioning the necessity of three separate QKV projections, building on Borji (2023) and Kowsher et al. (2025), but provides the first systematic multi-task, multi-scale evaluation of all three sharing constraints (Q=K≠V, Q≠K=V, Q=K=V) under identical training protocols. It positions projection sharing as orthogonal to the now-standard head-sharing methods (GQA, MQA) and demonstrates their multiplicative combination, distinguishing itself from DeepSeek-V2's MLA which achieves a similar K-V unification through learned compression rather than a hard equality constraint. The theoretical appendix further connects QKV collapse to state-space models via a recurrent outer-product formulation, situating the architectural simplification within the broader attention-to-SSM continuum.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is concrete: Q≠K=V halves the KV cache with ~3% perplexity cost, and stacking with GQA-4 or MQA pushes cache reduction to 87.5–96.9%, directly addressing the dominant memory bottleneck in long-context LLM serving and enabling on-device inference. The counterintuitive finding that K=V works while Q=K fails (despite Q and K being the 'matching' pair) provides a useful architectural principle for future design. Key open questions include whether the quality gap continues to shrink beyond 1.2B parameters (the authors note 2.48% at 1.2B vs 3.1% at 300M), whether K=V remains optimal under different training regimes or architectures, and whether the QKV-collapse-to-SSM connection can be exploited for further architectural unification.
