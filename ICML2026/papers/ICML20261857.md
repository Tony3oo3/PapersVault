## Problem

Orthogonality constraints are essential in modern ML (orthogonal attention, recurrent networks, CNN filters, probabilistic circuits), yet existing optimizers fail to simultaneously satisfy three desiderata: feasibility (staying on the Stiefel manifold), scalability (handling thousands of matrices with minimal overhead), and competitiveness (matching unconstrained adaptive optimizers in downstream performance). Retraction-based Riemannian methods are accurate but slow; the faster Landing algorithm relaxes orthogonality during training and is limited to SGD-like updates, leaving a persistent performance gap versus unconstrained baselines.

## Input / Output

POGO takes a wide parameter matrix X ∈ R^{p×n} (p ≤ n) on or near the Stiefel manifold and its Euclidean gradient ∇f(X), and produces an updated matrix X_{t+1} that is provably o(ξ^7)-close to the manifold (where ξ = ηL < 1). The method operates on batches of up to tens of thousands of matrices simultaneously (e.g., 218,624 orthogonal 3×3 kernels in a CNN), and supports both real and complex Stiefel manifolds. The entire update requires only 5 matrix multiplications of cost O(p²n) when using the fixed λ = 1/2 approximation, making it fully GPU-resident without SVD or QR decompositions.

## Method

POGO (Proximal One-step Geometric Orthoptimizer) reformulates the Landing update into two decoupled steps: a tangent-space leap M = X − ηXS (where S = Skew(XᵀG) is the relative gradient from a pluggable linear base optimizer like VAdam), followed by a normal-direction landing X_{t+1} = M + λ(I − MMᵀ)M. The key theoretical contribution is showing that the squared manifold distance after the landing step is a quartic polynomial P(λ) in λ (the 'landing polynomial'), whose root gives the exact optimal step size; remarkably, under the mild condition ξ = ηL < 1, the fixed value λ = 1/2 yields P(1/2) = o(ξ⁷), meaning all iterates remain extremely close to the manifold without solving the polynomial. This λ = 1/2 choice coincides with a first-order Taylor approximation of the polar retraction, connecting POGO to both Landing and RGD with polar retractions. The method reduces hyperparameters to just the learning rate, base optimizer choice, and a binary flag for exact vs. approximate λ, while enabling adaptive optimizers through the linearity/equivariance property of the relative gradient operator.

## Results

On the CIFAR-10 CNN benchmark with 218,624 orthogonal 3×3 kernels, POGO trains in 3 minutes versus 17 hours for RSDM — a ~340× speedup — while matching the accuracy of an unconstrained Adam baseline. On the O-ViT (18 orthogonal 1024×1024 matrices) CIFAR-10 task, all methods reach similar test accuracy, but POGO completes 10 epochs 5 minutes faster than RSDM and twice as fast as RGD, while remaining on the Stiefel manifold throughout training. On squared unitary probabilistic circuits (1,048 complex matrices) fitted to MNIST, POGO achieves the same bits-per-dimension as RGD (1.5 h) in half the time, and outperforms Landing which gets stuck at ε=0.5 manifold distance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

POGO dominates RSDM (Han et al., 2025) on every benchmark: on the 218,624-kernel CNN it is ~340× faster (3 min vs 17 h), and RSDM consistently drifts away from the manifold (3 orders of magnitude further than RGD on O-ViT). Against RGD with QR retraction (Absil et al., 2008), POGO is 2× faster on O-ViT and squared unitary PCs while maintaining equal or tighter manifold distance, and matches downstream accuracy. Compared to the original Landing (Ablin & Peyré, 2022), POGO stays on the manifold at every iteration (Landing's distance is uncertain and can grow when multiple matrices are optimized), and POGO supports adaptive base optimizers like VAdam that Landing's SGD-only formulation cannot. Against LandingPC (Loconte et al., 2026), POGO converges faster on both the Procrustes problem and squared unitary PCs, and avoids LandingPC's initial manifold-distance increase. Versus SLPG (Liu et al., 2024), POGO matches performance in the orthogonal-filter CNN case but clearly outperforms it on orthogonal kernels, where SLPG required an extremely low learning rate (0.1 vs POGO's 0.5) to avoid numerical divergence.

## Relation to Prior Work

POGO directly extends the Landing algorithm (Ablin & Peyré, 2022), which replaced costly Riemannian retractions with a retraction-free 'landing field' combining tangent and normal gradient steps. The key conceptual departure is the two-step 'leap, land, repeat' reformulation that disentangles the tangent step (M = X − ηXS) from the normal correction (X_{t+1} = M + λ(I − MMᵀ)M), enabling exact computation of the optimal λ via a quartic polynomial and the surprising result that λ = 1/2 suffices under mild conditions. POGO also connects to SLPG (Liu et al., 2024), whose approximate polar retraction coincides with POGO's normal step at λ = 1/2, and departs from RSDM (Han et al., 2025) by avoiding random submanifold sampling entirely. Unlike Landing, POGO can wrap modern adaptive base optimizers (e.g., VAdam) while preserving the equivariance of the relative gradient.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper closes a long-standing practical gap where orthogonality constraints—despite their theoretical benefits for training stability and robustness—were never competitive with unconstrained Adam due to the computational cost of retraction-based methods. The 5-orders-of-magnitude speedup (3 minutes vs. 17 hours for RSDM on 218,624 orthogonal kernels) makes large-scale orthogonality constraints practically viable for the first time. Open questions include whether the o(ξ⁷) manifold-distance guarantee holds robustly in very deep or wide networks, whether POGO extends cleanly to other manifolds (beyond Stiefel and complex Stiefel), and whether the linear-optimizer requirement (Def. 1) can be relaxed to accommodate more aggressive adaptive methods like Muon.
