## Problem

Conventional PINNs suffer from two structural pathologies—gradient shattering, where input-output Jacobians decorrelate exponentially and their norms vanish or explode with depth, and flow mismatch, where layerwise updates drift away from true descent directions—causing solutions to satisfy PDE residuals locally while failing to propagate initial and boundary conditions globally. Prior work attributed improvements in time-dependent PINNs to sophisticated sequence modules (attention, state-space operators), but the paper shows these gains actually stem from residual pathways rather than explicit sequence modeling.

## Input / Output

The network takes spatio-temporal coordinates (x, t) as input, encoded into a latent state via a linear encoder, and produces the PDE solution u(x, t) through a linear decoder. Training is entirely self-supervised via weighted mean-squared residuals of the PDE interior operator, initial condition, and boundary condition evaluated at collocation points on a 101×101 space-time grid, requiring no labeled solution data. The residual flow is realized as K=10 stacked blocks of width 64 (three fully connected layers each with a skip connection), or as a continuous-depth Neural ODE integrated with a 4th-order Runge-Kutta solver.

## Method

ResPINNs reformulate PINNs as residual flows where each block applies a small corrective update around the identity (h_{k+1} = h_k + f(h_k; θ_k)), directly connecting the architecture to classical predictor-corrector iterative solvers. The theoretical analysis shows this design mitigates both failure modes: near-identity per-step Jacobians (singular values bounded in [1−α_k, 1+α_k] via Weyl's inequality) prevent gradient shattering, while small update magnitudes keep each step aligned with the local loss gradient descent direction (Lemma 3.2). A controlled ablation replacing attention and state-space modules in PINNsFormer and PINNMamba with simple linear or MLP projections (parameter-matched within ±10%) demonstrates that residual pathways—not sequence modules—are the mechanism behind prior performance gains. Three instantiations are provided: ResPINN (discrete residual stack), O-PINN (continuous-depth via RK4 integration of a shared residual block), and Progressive Residual Flow (curriculum-style depth growth with frozen earlier blocks), all trained with L-BFGS and wavelet activations.

## Results

On four canonical PDE benchmarks (Wave, Reaction, Convection, Heat), ResPINN achieves the lowest rMAE and rRMSE across all problems, with headline values of 0.0130/0.0154 (Wave), 0.0047/0.0075 (Reaction), 0.0028/0.0046 (Convection), and 0.0035/0.0048 (Heat). These represent order-of-magnitude improvements over vanilla PINNs (e.g., 0.8514 rMAE on Convection) and consistent gains over the strongest sequence-based baselines PINNMamba and PINN_SOAP. ResPINN achieves this with only 53k parameters and 1.26× per-epoch time overhead relative to a vanilla PINN, compared to 6–9× overhead for transformer- and state-space-based models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ResPINN outperforms all nine baselines on three of four PDEs (Wave, Reaction, Convection), with the closest competitor being PINNMamba, which ResPINN beats by roughly 2× on rMAE (e.g., 0.0028 vs 0.0188 on Convection, 0.0047 vs 0.0094 on Reaction). On the Heat equation, PirateNet achieves a lower rMAE (0.0005 vs 0.0035), though ResPINN still substantially outperforms all other baselines on that problem. Compared to the original PINN formulation (Raissi et al., 2019), ResPINN reduces rMAE by 1–3 orders of magnitude (e.g., 0.8514 → 0.0028 on Convection). Against optimization-focused methods like PINN_SOAP and RoPINNs, ResPINN wins on all four PDEs, with particularly large margins on Convection (0.0028 vs 0.0340 and 0.6350). The ablation study (Table 1) further shows that replacing attention or state-space modules in PINNsFormer/PINNMamba with simple linear or MLP projections preserves or improves accuracy, supporting the paper's claim that residual pathways—not sequence modules—are the key stabilizing mechanism.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reformulates PINNs (Raissi et al., 2019) as residual flows, drawing on the skip-connection principle from ResNets (He et al., 2016) and the continuous-depth limit of Neural ODEs (Chen et al., 2018), but positions the contribution as a diagnostic and stabilization framework rather than a new sequence architecture. It explicitly departs from the narrative in PINNsFormer (Zhao et al., 2024) and PINNMamba (Xu et al., 2025a) that attention or state-space recurrence is the mechanism fixing condition-propagation failures, arguing instead—via controlled ablations—that residual pathways are the common stabilizing factor. The closest prior work, PirateNet (Wang et al., 2024), also uses residual connections but couples them with gating, adaptive loss weighting, and physics-informed initialization without isolating the residual mechanism or connecting it to gradient shattering and flow mismatch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: ResPINN achieves state-of-the-art accuracy on canonical PDEs with ~10× fewer parameters than standard PINNs and ~8× fewer than PINNsFormer, while incurring only 1.26× wall-clock overhead and no memory increase, making it a lightweight drop-in replacement. The theoretical framing (mean-field Jacobian decorrelation, local Jacobian neutrality via Weyl's inequality) gives a principled explanation for *why* residual flows help, which should guide future architecture design beyond PINNs. Open questions include whether continuous-depth (O-PINN) formulations outperform discrete stacks in very deep regimes, how different ODE solvers (beyond Euler/RK4) shape refinement behavior, and whether the approach extends to inverse problems or data-scarce settings where collocation is limited.
