## Problem

Text-to-image generation models like FLUX.1 [Dev] produce high-quality images, yet fine-grained color control remains difficult because the encoding of color in the VAE latent space is poorly understood. Existing color-control methods (ColorPeel, ReNO, IP-Adapter) add training overhead or inference cost without revealing the underlying mechanism, making the system opaque and hard to trust. This paper asks: can we identify an interpretable color structure in the latent space and use it for both observation and intervention without any additional training?

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard text prompt (with no color words), a target HSL color specification, and optionally a segmentation mask (from Seg4Diff cross-attention at transformer layer 18) to localize the edit to a specific object. It produces a color-controlled image where the target object (or entire image) shifts to the specified hue, saturation, and lightness, while preserving structural content. A secondary output is a patch-level color observation grid (O_t) that visualizes the predicted final colors directly from latent coordinates at any intermediate timestep, without invoking the 50M-parameter VAE decoder.

## Method

The core finding is that color in FLUX's VAE latent space is confined to a 3D subspace (the Latent Color Subspace, LCS) whose geometry mirrors HSL: lightness along one axis, hue as an angle in a perpendicular plane, and saturation as radial distance, forming a bicone. The authors construct a closed-form bijection between LCS coordinates and HSL using eight anchor points (six canonical hues plus black and white), with lightness from projection onto the achromatic axis, hue from angular interpolation on the anchor circle, and saturation from distance-to-axis normalized by a bicone radius that shrinks linearly toward the poles. Because flow matching traverses the latent space over timesteps, they introduce time-dependent shift (α_t) and per-axis scale (β_t) statistics to normalize intermediate latents to a reference timestep before decoding or intervening. Intervention uses two strategies—Type I (uniform translation in LCS) and Type II (decode to HSL, shift, re-encode)—interpolated with a timestep-dependent coefficient (Type ★), applied at t≈9 to balance color integration with texture preservation. The entire pipeline is training-free and operates purely through closed-form latent-space projection and manipulation.

## Results

On the PRECISE (NATURAL, SMALL) benchmark, the proposed LCS(★,local) intervention achieves a CIEDE2000 color error of ΔE₀₀ = 10, substantially outperforming prompt-based color injection (ΔE₀₀ = 27), best-of-50 sampling (ΔE₀₀ = 19), ColorPeel (ΔE₀₀ = 29), ReNO (ΔE₀₀ = 23), and IP-Adapter (ΔE₀₀ = 40). On GenEval's color task, the method raises color accuracy from 9% (no color in prompt) to 72% (global) / 68% (local) without modifying the prompt, approaching the 79% achievable with explicit color prompts. Structure preservation is also superior: LCS(★,local) achieves SSIM = 0.62 and LPIPS = 0.32, compared to 0.48/0.48 for prompt-based changes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The LCS method decisively outperforms all three external baselines on every metric. Against ColorPeel (Butt et al., 2024), LCS achieves ΔE₀₀ = 10 vs. 29 (2.9× lower color error) and SSIM = 0.62 vs. 0.22, while also being training-free (ColorPeel requires per-color optimization). Against ReNO (Eyring et al., 2024), LCS achieves ΔE₀₀ = 10 vs. 23 and LPIPS = 0.32 vs. 0.61, without incurring ReNO's per-image test-time optimization cost. Against IP-Adapter (Ye et al., 2023), the gap is largest: ΔE₀₀ = 10 vs. 40 (4× lower) and SSIM = 0.62 vs. 0.29, with no additional adapter model required. LCS also beats the simple prompt baseline (ΔE₀₀ = 10 vs. 27) and best-of-50 sampling (ΔE₀₀ = 10 vs. 19) while preserving structure far better (SSIM 0.62 vs. 0.48 and 0.32 respectively). Notably, LCS is the only method that improves interpretability of the underlying model rather than adding uninterpretable optimization or auxiliary networks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of color control in diffusion models, which typically adds external modules (IP-Adapter, ColorPeel) or optimization loops (ReNO, best-of-N) to steer generation. Instead, it draws on the interpretability literature for diffusion transformers (sparse autoencoders, attention-based analysis) but applies a simpler tool—PCA on solid-color encodings—to reveal a 3D HSL-like bicone in FLUX's VAE latent space. Concurrent work by Arias et al. (2025) also identifies color structure in VAE space but lacks the temporal flow-matching analysis, the bidirectional HSL↔LCS mapping, and the intervention component that this paper provides.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that color is confined to a clean 3D geometric subspace in an otherwise high-dimensional, uninterpretable VAE latent space offers a rare mechanistic window into how these models encode a fundamental visual property, and the cross-architecture validation (SD3.5, FLUX.2, Qwen-Image) suggests this is not an artifact of one model. Practically, the training-free intervention outperforms trained baselines (ColorPeel, ReNO) on color accuracy while better preserving structure, making it an attractive lightweight tool for production pipelines. Open questions include whether analogous low-dimensional subspaces exist for other image attributes (texture, shape, spatial layout), whether the bicone approximation degrades for non-solid or multi-material surfaces, and whether the approach transfers to video or 3D generation.
