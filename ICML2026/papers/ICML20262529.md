## Problem

Despite the empirical success of transformer-based diffusion models, no prior work provides a convergence analysis for training such models via gradient descent on the DDPM loss. Specifically, it remains unexplained why a nonlinear transformer can match the score function and why gradient-based optimization converges to the optimal denoiser despite the non-convex loss landscape.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262529/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a noisy multi-token sequence X^t ∈ R^{d×P} (P tokens of dimension d) at a given diffusion time step t, where the clean data X^0 follows a Multi-Token Gaussian Mixture distribution with K out of M orthogonal Gaussian components. It outputs a per-token noise prediction f(Ψ; X^t, t) ∈ R^{d×P} that approximates the injected Gaussian noise E^t, from which the score function s_θ(X^t, t) = −f(Ψ; X^t, t)/√(1−ᾱ_t) is directly constructed. The analysis requires P to scale as Ω(ν_min^{-1}(ρ²+1)ε^{-1} log d) tokens per data point for near-optimal denoising.

## Method

The paper analyzes a one-layer single-head transformer with softmax attention and a residual connection, trained by gradient descent on the population DDPM noise-prediction loss. The key technical contribution is proving that the trained self-attention implements a 'mean denoising mechanism': query-key inner products grow large for tokens sharing the same Gaussian pattern while remaining small for different patterns, causing softmax attention weights to concentrate uniformly among same-pattern tokens and produce a minimum-variance unbiased estimator of the mean pattern. This mechanism allows the transformer to approximate the oracle MMSE estimator (computed with known component means), and the paper further shows the oracle denoising risk is within O(ε) of the true Bayes risk when P is sufficiently large, establishing global convergence to near-optimal denoising. The convergence rate depends polynomially on the pattern imbalance ratio, the number of distinct patterns K, and the inverse time-averaged SNR, with K=1 (single pattern per data) yielding the fastest convergence.

## Results

The paper's primary results are theoretical: Theorem 1 proves that a one-layer single-head transformer trained by gradient descent on the population DDPM loss achieves L(Ψ^(S)) ≤ R_Bayes + O(ε) under conditions on dimension d, number of tokens P, and iterations S. Theorem 2 further shows the trained model can be converted into a score network with matching error E(θ) ≤ ε·(SNR+1). Empirically, synthetic experiments (d=64, M=8, P=256, T=50) confirm that the Bayes and oracle denoising risks are nearly identical, the DDPM loss converges to these values, and attention weights on same-pattern keys concentrate to ~1. On MNIST with a 6-layer 4-head DiT, the minority digit '2' (trained on only 30% of its data) exhibits a measurably slower FID decrease than digits 0, 1, and 3, consistent with the theory that rarer patterns are learned more slowly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is primarily a theoretical contribution and does not run head-to-head empirical comparisons against other diffusion model architectures. Instead, it positions itself against prior theoretical analyses via Table 1. The key gap it fills is that no prior work simultaneously provides (a) a global-optimum loss landscape characterization, (b) a convergence guarantee for gradient-based training, and (c) an explicit denoising mechanism for transformer-based diffusion models. Wang et al. (2025) identify the global optimum (PCA) for U-Nets but offer no convergence proof; Han et al. (2024) and Wang et al. (2024) prove convergence for fully-connected or linearized models but do not analyze transformers or identify a denoising mechanism; Han et al. (2025) show balanced feature learning for CNNs but only at stationary points without convergence guarantees. The present work is the first to establish all three properties for a nonlinear transformer, and its empirical MNIST experiment (minority digit '2' learning more slowly) provides qualitative validation consistent with the theoretical prediction that pattern frequency affects learning speed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the feature-learning analysis framework developed for transformer convergence in classification and regression tasks (Li et al., 2023a; 2024a; Jiang et al., 2024) to the denoising objective of DDPM (Ho et al., 2020), which is the first such extension to diffusion model training. It departs from prior diffusion theory in two key ways: unlike Wang et al. (2025) and Li et al. (2024e), which characterize optimal denoisers via loss-landscape analysis without proving training algorithms reach them, this paper establishes gradient-descent convergence; and unlike Han et al. (2024; 2025) and Wang et al. (2024), which rely on linearization (NTK, random features) or simpler architectures (fully-connected, convolutional), this paper preserves the nonlinearity of a one-layer single-head transformer. The proof technique adapts attention-concentration arguments from in-context learning to the denoising setting, introducing the 'mean denoising mechanism' as the learned behavior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical result showing that gradient descent on the DDPM loss converges a nonlinear transformer to near-optimal denoising, addressing a fundamental gap in understanding why transformer-based diffusion models (e.g., DiT) work. The identified mean denoising mechanism provides an interpretable explanation for self-attention's role in diffusion and suggests why the model generalizes to shifted mixture proportions. However, the analysis is restricted to a one-layer single-head transformer, orthogonal Gaussian patterns, and population loss, leaving open whether the mechanism and convergence guarantees extend to multi-head architectures, empirical losses, non-orthogonal data structures, and realistic image distributions. Future work on multi-head attention with multiple internal token relationships and generalization gaps between empirical and population loss are natural next steps.
