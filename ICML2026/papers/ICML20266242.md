## Problem

Existing video-based humanoid control methods treat video as passive kinematic priors, relying on curated motion datasets and ignoring the dynamic humanoid-environment interactions (e.g., adapting gait to terrain geometry and contact forces) that govern robust locomotion. A fundamental technical bottleneck is that environmental context is inherently unobserved in monocular video, making it unclear how to ground video-derived motion strategies in novel physical environments where explicit terrain observations are available.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266242/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

In the video domain, the method consumes only reconstructed proprioceptive motion sequences (SMPL parameters from TRAM on 30 fps gameplay footage), with no access to terrain geometry or contact forces. In the target domain, the policy receives the proprioceptive state s_t, a rasterized 32×32×3 local heightmap covering a 2×2 m² square, and a task-specific goal (trajectory waypoints, target speed, or 3D reach point). The output is a two-part action: a discrete primitive index sampled from a 256-entry codebook (selecting a short-horizon interaction pattern) and a continuous residual vector, both fed into a pretrained PULSE action decoder to produce the final control action at 30 Hz.

## Method

TRIP decouples *what* interaction to perform from *when* to invoke it via three coupled modules. First, a VQ-VAE discretizes concatenated PULSE proprioceptive priors and kinematic latent sequences (over a τ=2 sliding window) into a 256-entry codebook of ungrounded interaction primitives, each encoding a relative short-horizon motion strategy. Second, a shared 128-dim physical context latent c_t is learned by aligning two asymmetric inference pathways: a context encoder p_ψ(c_t|h_t, s_t) that reads explicit heightmaps in the target domain, and a posterior q_φ(c_t|s_t, a_t, s_{t+1}) that recovers context purely from motion dynamics in the video domain, with KL alignment plus transition and reward model regularization to ensure c_t encodes physically predictive factors. Third, a hierarchical RL policy (PPO) selects a primitive from the codebook conditioned on c_t, decodes a short-horizon latent plan via a pretrained decoder, and applies a continuous residual for fine-grained adaptation; a value-difference-weighted bidirectional KL regularizer selectively distills a parallel video-domain behavior-cloning policy into the RL policy only when the video policy is predicted to yield higher one-step returns, preventing divergence while preserving task-specific adaptation.

## Results

On the 1.5× scaled terrain evaluation, TRIP achieves 197±3.6 (Trajectory), 216±3.4 (Speed), and 123±8.2 (Reach), yielding a 17.2% average improvement over the strongest baseline. On unseen wave-shaped terrain, TRIP reaches 223±11.9, 232±7.2, and 183±1.8, substantially outperforming all baselines. Under external disturbances, TRIP scores 203±8.3, 206±7.2, and 157±5.9, maintaining the largest margins on the Reach task where baselines collapse to single-digit scores.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TRIP outperforms all four baselines across every task and every generalization condition. Against PULSE (the strongest baseline), TRIP gains approximately 25 points on Trajectory, 20 on Speed, and 26 on Reach under 1.5× terrain scaling, and extends the lead to 43, 18, and 69 points on unseen wave terrain. AMP and PULSE+AMP fail catastrophically on the Reach task (scores of 5–6), indicating they cannot maintain balance on irregular terrain while tracking a target, whereas TRIP consistently achieves 123–183. PPO is included only in the training-curve comparison (Figure 4) and converges slower with lower final performance than TRIP. The advantage is most pronounced under distribution shift: on unseen wave terrain, TRIP's 183 on Reach exceeds PULSE's 114 by 61%, and under external disturbances TRIP's 157 on Reach surpasses PULSE's 134 by 17%.

## Relation to Prior Work

TRIP builds directly on PULSE (Luo et al., 2024a), reusing its pretrained proprioceptive prior, variational encoder, and action decoder as the motion-semantics backbone, but departs fundamentally by replacing PULSE's continuous, proprioception-only latent conditioning with a discrete VQ-VAE codebook of interaction primitives and a cross-domain physical context latent. Prior video-based humanoid methods (OKAMI, GenMimic) treat video as a source of kinematic trajectories to imitate, while motion-prior methods (AMP, PULSE+AMP) assume static environments; TRIP instead explicitly models the humanoid-environment interaction as a first-class object, grounding discrete motor strategies in a shared context space that is inferred from motion dynamics in the video domain and from explicit terrain observations in the target domain. The cross-domain alignment via a KL-regularized shared latent, combined with a value-difference-weighted bidirectional policy regularizer, has no direct analogue in prior humanoid control literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TRIP offers a structured, scalable pathway for transferring interaction knowledge from passive (even AI-generated) videos to physically grounded humanoid control, reducing reliance on curated motion-capture datasets and paired demonstrations. The demonstration that text-to-video models (Seedance, Kling) can substitute for game footage suggests a practical route to scaling interaction primitive learning without any real-world data collection. Key limitations include dependence on motion reconstruction quality (TRAM), restriction to terrain-centric locomotion (no object manipulation or tool use), and the absence of real-world deployment or sim-to-real validation. Open questions include whether the discrete primitive abstraction generalizes to richer interaction categories and how the framework adapts when the target domain's observation modality (e.g., egocentric vision) differs substantially from the video domain.
