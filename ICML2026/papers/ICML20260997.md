## Problem

Clustering benchmarks collapse performance into single aggregated leaderboards that obscure why methods succeed or fail, which data properties drive failure, and how conclusions shift under representation changes. No existing framework attributes clustering difficulty to interpretable structural mechanisms (separability, scale heterogeneity, topology) or explains regime-dependent ranking reversals across algorithm families.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260997/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

CHB takes a point-cloud dataset D with reference labels y (used only for diagnosis, never to fit clustering) and optionally a learned embedding representation. It produces an 8-dimensional hardness fingerprint h(D)=[S1,S2,S3; C1,C2; T1,T2,T3] computed entirely descriptor-only (no clustering method is run), a deterministic regime assignment (A/B/C) via a separability gate and a blob-calibrated topology threshold, regime-conditioned NMI rankings across method families, and axis deltas Δh for representation auditing. The topology cutoff τ_TOP=15.0 is anchored to a Gaussian-blob null (95th percentile), making regime assignment suite-independent.

## Method

CHB embeds each dataset-representation pair into an interpretable hardness space via three mechanism-aligned descriptor blocks: Separation (cross-label kNN overlap S1, hubness infiltration S2, normalized margin thickness S3), Cohesion (multi-scale density heterogeneity C1, linear elongation C2 via covariance spectrum), and Topology (resampled persistent-homology summaries T1–T3 for PH0/PH1/PH2). Regime construction uses a separability-first hierarchy: a 2-of-3 gate on (S1>0.5, S2>0.33, S3<1.0) declares Regime A (separability collapse); gate-passing datasets are split by a blob-calibrated topology evidence statistic T_evid against τ_TOP=15.0 into Regime B (topology mismatch) or Regime C (scale heterogeneity). The key insight is that separability acts as a necessary gate for recoverability, while conditional on it, topology drives family-specific breakdowns (e.g., density methods collapse in B) and cohesion heterogeneity systematically penalizes single-resolution methods through merge-split instability. Representation learning is then interpreted as movement in CHB space, with gains attributed to separability rescue (S1,S2↓) or mismatch correction (T1–T3↓) rather than opaque metric deltas.

## Results

CHB reveals a consistent global clusterability ordering across three structural regimes: Regime A (separability collapse) yields near-universal failure with median NMI ≈ 0.067, Regime B (topology mismatch) reaches ≈ 0.529, and Regime C (scale heterogeneity) peaks at ≈ 0.704. Within regimes, the best-performing method family shifts: Spectral leads in A (≈0.115) and C (≈0.782), while deep-pipeline (DDC) dominates in B (≈0.695). Representation learning produces the largest gains when datasets transition from Regime B to C (e.g., MNIST/KMNIST/FMNIST), whereas datasets remaining in Regime A (CIFAR-10/100, SVHN) show only modest, family-conditional improvements.

## Baselines & Comparisons

No single clustering method dominates across all regimes; rankings invert depending on the active hardness mechanism. Spectral clustering (Von Luxburg, 2007) is the strongest in Regimes A and C (0.115 and 0.782) but is outperformed by DDC (Ren et al., 2020) in Regime B (0.631 vs. 0.695). K-means (Lloyd, 1982) and GMM (Dempster et al., 1977) as classical-center methods achieve moderate scores (≈0.10 in A, ≈0.49 in B, ≈0.70 in C), consistently trailing the regime-optimal family. DBSCAN (Ester et al., 1996) and HDBSCAN (Campello et al., 2013) collapse in Regime B (≈0.05) and remain far below the regime ceiling in C (≈0.45), confirming their sensitivity to topology mismatch and scale heterogeneity. Ward (Ward Jr, 1963) and average linkage (Lance & Williams, 1967) are competitive in C (≈0.728) but middling in B (≈0.57). Deep-k-center methods IDEC (Guo et al., 2017) and DCN (Yang et al., 2017) track linkage closely in B (≈0.58) and C (≈0.71). SHADE (Beer et al., 2024) as the deep-density method underperforms in B (≈0.35) and C (≈0.45), while DDC as the deep-pipeline representative is the clear winner in B and competitive in C (≈0.727).

## Relation to Prior Work

CHB departs from standard clustering benchmarks (Wiwie et al., 2015; Gagolewski, 2022; Lu et al., 2024) that report aggregate ARI/NMI leaderboards without explaining *why* methods fail, and from meta-learning/algorithm-selection lines (ISA, De Souto et al., 2008) that use correlational features for black-box selection. It repurposes persistent homology (Ghrist, 2008; Edelsbrunner & Harer, 2010) not as a clustering objective but as a lightweight diagnostic covariate, and combines it with kNN-based separation and covariance-based cohesion descriptors into a unified 8-dimensional hardness fingerprint. The separability-first hierarchy draws on margin/stability conditions from clustering theory (Balcan et al., 2009; Kumar & Kannan, 2010; Awasthi et al., 2012), but no prior benchmark operationalizes these conditions as a performance-free regime taxonomy that conditions method comparisons.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CHB provides a reusable, open diagnostic framework that turns clustering leaderboards into mechanism-level explanations, which is practically valuable for anyone selecting or comparing clustering methods under realistic tuning budgets. The representation-auditing capability (attributing embedding gains to measurable shifts in separability vs. topology) is a new tool for the growing field of representation learning evaluated via clustering. Key limitations include restriction to metric point-cloud data (no graph-structured data), dependence on reference labels for diagnosis, and the fact that PH3+ features are not explored; extending to unlabeled settings via stability-based surrogates and to richer geometries are natural next steps.
