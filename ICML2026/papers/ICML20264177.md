## Problem

Multi-objective combinatorial optimization requires finding Pareto-optimal tradeoffs over exponentially large discrete spaces where each evaluation is expensive (hours of simulation) and no gradients are available. Existing approaches force a tradeoff: surrogate-based Bayesian optimization scales cubically and struggles with discrete kernels, evolutionary heuristics lack finite-time guarantees, and neural methods demand millions of training samples. No prior method simultaneously provides domain generality, sample efficiency under expensive black-box evaluations, and formal regret guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a combinatorial decision space (permutations, binary vectors, or mixed-integer assignments), a set of m black-box objective functions, a metric-based decomposition into K overlapping subproblems of size d = O(n/K), a set of scalarization weight vectors, and a total iteration budget T. It produces a Pareto archive of non-dominated solutions with their objective vectors, built by running the algorithm once per scalarization weight. Critically, the feedback model is full-bandit: each evaluation reveals only the aggregate per-objective values of the complete solution, with no per-position or per-component reward decomposition, making it the most challenging feedback setting for combinatorial optimization.

## Method

D&L reformulates MOCO as an online learning problem over a decomposed decision space, reducing the effective action space from O(2^n) to O(K·2^(n/K)) by partitioning variables into K overlapping subproblems. Within each subproblem, a multi-expert bandit ensemble (UCB for optimistic exploration, EXP3 for adversarial robustness via clipped importance weighting, and FTRL for regularized stability on the probability simplex) stochastically selects actions at each position, with all experts sharing position-action statistics globally to enable information transfer across overlapping variables. Cross-subproblem consistency is enforced through Lagrangian relaxation: dual multipliers updated via accelerated mirror descent penalize a soft violation measure that captures disagreement potential from value-estimate variance and visit-count confidence, naturally vanishing as estimates converge. Between expert-driven phases, zeroth-order perturbations perform gradient-free local refinement within subproblems. The resulting regret bound is O(d√T log T), depending only on subproblem size d rather than the combinatorial space size |X| ∈ {2^n, n!}, yielding an exponential-to-polynomial reduction over standard combinatorial bandits.

## Results

On MOCO benchmarks (Bi-TSP, Bi-KP, Tri-TSP, Bi-CVRP up to 100 nodes), D&L-TS achieves the highest hypervolume ratio among general-purpose methods, reaching 80–98% of problem-specific solvers (WS-LKH/DP, PPLS/D-C) while outperforming Bayesian optimization baselines by 20–90% in HV. On the hardware-software co-design task (4 objectives, 150-evaluation budget), D&L-TS achieves HV 0.372±0.006, improving ~22% over the best baseline (MOBO-qParEGO at 0.34±0.012). D&L reduces computational cost by 90–99% (FLOPs) compared to Bayesian baselines, yielding 10–30× wall-clock speedups, and discovers 2–3× more non-dominated solutions in tri-objective settings where BO methods collapse.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against problem-specific solvers WS-LKH/DP and PPLS/D-C, D&L-TS achieves 80–98% of their hypervolume without any domain-specific operators (e.g., 0.600 vs. 0.625 on Bi-TSP-20; 0.470 vs. 0.690 on Bi-TSP-100), with the gap widening at larger scales. Against the pretrained neural method PMOCO* (matched compute budget), D&L exceeds it by ~30% on average across bi-objective benchmarks, with the largest margin (~70%) on Bi-TSP, and remains competitive on Tri-TSP where PMOCO* degrades. Against NSGA-II, D&L outperforms substantially at scale (e.g., 0.470 vs. 0.104 on Bi-TSP-100; 0.234 vs. 0.030 on Tri-TSP-100), as NSGA-II's problem-specific crossover/mutation operators do not transfer well without tuning. Against all Bayesian optimization baselines (qNEHVI, qParEGO, BOPR), D&L improves hypervolume by 20–90% while using 90–99% fewer FLOPs (e.g., 0.470 vs. 0.104 on Bi-TSP-100; 0.372 vs. 0.34 on HW-SF), with the advantage growing as problem size and objective count increase. On the hardware-software co-design task, D&L-TS (0.372) outperforms MOBO-qParEGO (0.34), MOBO-qNEHVI (0.287), and NSGA-II (0.291) under a fixed 150-evaluation budget, confirming sample-efficiency gains in the expensive-evaluation regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

D&L synthesizes three previously separate threads: (1) no-regret bandit algorithms (UCB, EXP3, FTRL) applied at the position-action level rather than the full-solution level, (2) decision-space decomposition with Lagrangian coordination (distinct from MOEA/D's objective-space decomposition), and (3) zeroth-order local refinement for within-subproblem interactions. The key departure from prior combinatorial bandit work (Chen et al. 2013; Kveton et al. 2015) is the feedback model: D&L operates under full-bandit feedback (one aggregate scalar per objective) rather than semi-bandit per-arm observations, and handles nonlinear scalarized rewards rather than linear additive models. The paper explicitly notes that no prior work provides regret-theoretic results for black-box MOCO under this full-bandit setting, positioning D&L as the first to bridge this gap through structured decomposition that reduces the effective dimensionality from |X| (exponential) to d (subproblem size, polynomial).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for settings where each evaluation costs hours of simulation (hardware co-design, protein engineering, drug discovery): D&L achieves 80–98% of specialized solvers' hypervolume without domain-specific operators or pretraining, while using 90–99% less compute than Bayesian optimization. The O(d√T log T) regret bound—depending on subproblem size rather than combinatorial space cardinality—provides the first formal convergence guarantee for this problem class under realistic feedback. Key limitations include the bounded-coupling assumption (requiring locality in the problem structure), fixed expert mixture weights, and bounded rewards; open questions include adaptive expert selection, sub-Gaussian reward extensions, and applicability to problems with dense global interactions where decomposition may be less effective.
