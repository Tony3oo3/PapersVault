## Problem

Existing LLM safety defenses rely on static, pre-collected adversarial distributions and thus lag behind evolving attack strategies, leading to recurring jailbreak failures. Two key obstacles remain unresolved: (1) the scarcity of high-quality Chain-of-Thought data for offensive reasoning prevents attackers from discovering novel, non-templated attack patterns, and (2) shared-parameter self-play methods impose opposing objectives on the same parameter space, causing gradient conflicts that hinder stable co-evolution.

## Input / Output

The attacker receives a seed query (harmful or benign) and outputs a structured response containing hidden reasoning in  tags followed by the adversarial prompt in <answer> tags; the format is enforced via a dedicated reward to prevent the defender from observing the attacker's strategy. The defender receives the adversarial prompt and produces a natural-language response (safe refusal or helpful answer). The RL training dataset contains an equal 50/50 mix of 15,000 harmful and 15,000 benign vanilla prompts from WildJailBreak, which the attacker rewrites online during training.

## Method

MAGIC formulates LLM safety alignment as an asymmetric two-player sequential game and approximates the Subgame Perfect Nash Equilibrium via alternating GRPO updates. Phase 1 initializes the attacker via SFT on an Attack Pool Benchmark (SorryBench enriched with Gemini-2.5-Pro-generated CoT traces across 20 rewriting strategies) to overcome the cold-start problem. Phase 2 alternates between defender optimization (freeze attacker; defender samples G responses and maximizes a composite reward of harmfulness + refusal signals from Qwen3Guard) and attacker optimization (freeze defender; attacker samples G adversarial rewrites and maximizes negative defender reward plus a format reward). The SPNE formulation yields a per-sample safety guarantee—every response in the defender's support is safe—stronger than the expectation-level guarantee of prior normal-form game approaches. The two-timescale update schedule (comparable T_D and T_A steps) balances defender adaptation against attacker exploration to prevent overfitting to the current attack distribution.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, MAGIC reduces the WildGuardTest adversarial-harm ASR from 36.5% (base) to 2.3%, and WildJailBreak adversarial-harm ASR from 70.1% to 19.8%, while maintaining XSTest benign compliance at 94.5% (vs. 94.0% base). On Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, MAGIC achieves near-zero ASR on WildGuardTest (0.9%) and WildJailBreak (7.6%), and on Llama3.1-8B-Instruct it drops WildGuardTest ASR from 18.7% to 1.2%. General capabilities (IFEval, MMLU, ARC-C, GPQA, AlpacaEval 2) are largely preserved, with differences from the base model typically within 1–5 percentage points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MAGIC consistently outperforms Self-RedTeam (the primary adversarial co-training baseline) across all three model families on every harmful-refusal benchmark: e.g., on Qwen2.5-7B, MAGIC achieves 2.3% vs. 25.5% WildGuardTest ASR and 19.8% vs. 44.2% WildJailBreak ASR. Against the inference-time defenses SmoothLLM and Self-Eval (evaluated on HarmBench with diverse attackers), MAGIC achieves the lowest ASR across GCG, PAIR, TAP, and AutoDAN (e.g., 11.25% GCG ASR vs. 20.00% SmoothLLM and 22.50% Self-RedTeam on Qwen2.5-7B), though AutoDAN-turbo remains the hardest attack for all methods. On benign compliance, MAGIC matches or slightly exceeds the base model (94.5% vs. 94.0% XSTest Comply on Qwen2.5-7B), whereas Self-RedTeam degrades compliance to 90.4%, indicating that MAGIC's asymmetric game formulation avoids the over-refusal collapse seen in shared-parameter self-play. On general capabilities (IFEval, MMLU, ARC-C, GPQA, AlpacaEval 2), MAGIC stays within ~1–5 points of the base model, while Self-RedTeam shows larger drops (e.g., AlpacaEval 2 drops from 33.7% to 34.3% for Self-RedTeam but 33.2% for MAGIC on Qwen2.5-7B; on Llama3.1-8B, Self-RedTeam drops AlpacaEval from 24.2% to 21.4% while MAGIC recovers to 24.1%).

## Relation to Prior Work

MAGIC extends the adversarial co-evolution paradigm for LLM safety (Self-RedTeam, Liu et al. 2025) by reformulating the interaction as an asymmetric sequential game with Subgame Perfect Nash Equilibrium rather than a symmetric normal-form game, and by decoupling attacker and defender into separate models to avoid gradient conflicts inherent in shared-parameter self-play. It draws on bilevel optimization theory (Hong et al., 2023) for the alternating update scheme and leverages the ReMA multi-agent RL infrastructure (Wan et al., 2025) for training. A key practical departure is the construction of a CoT-enriched Attack Pool Benchmark (from SorryBench + Gemini-2.5-Pro) to solve the cold-start problem of giving the attacker initial offensive reasoning, which prior methods addressed only through static red-teaming datasets or heuristic mutations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The SPNE formulation yields a stronger safety guarantee than prior Nash-equilibrium-based approaches: safety is ensured per-sample (every response in the defender's support is safe) rather than only in expectation, which matters for safety-critical deployment. The observation that iterative RL causes the attacker to autonomously discover novel combinatorial strategies (e.g., role-play + translation) suggests that co-evolutionary training can uncover long-tail vulnerabilities invisible to static red-teaming, shifting safety alignment from reactive patching to proactive discovery. Limitations include computational overhead of dual-model training, sensitivity to the attacker's SFT initialization quality, and the fact that the framework is validated primarily on 7B–14B models; open questions include extension to multimodal and tool-use settings, scalability to larger models, and whether the discovered combinatorial attacks transfer across model families.
