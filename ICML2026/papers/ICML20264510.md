## Problem

Prover-Verifier Games (PVGs) such as Merlin-Arthur Classifiers provide formal interpretability guarantees but fail to scale to high-dimensional inputs like images, where pixel-level explanations are computationally expensive and semantically opaque. Meanwhile, Concept Bottleneck Models handle high-dimensional data through interpretable concept encodings but rely on linear classifiers that cannot capture nonlinear concept interactions (e.g., XOR, counting). The paper addresses the gap between these two paradigms by asking whether PVGs can be shifted to concept space to yield both scalability and expressivity.

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images (128×128 for CLEVR-Hans, 32×32 for CIFAR-100, 224×224 for ImageNet-1k) and first passes them through a frozen concept extractor to produce a dense concept encoding vector of dimension C. The provers then select a sparse subset of m concepts (m=12 for NCB-based, m=32 for CLIP-based instantiations) via binary masks, and the verifier predicts from only those masked concepts. The output is a class label from K+1 classes, where the extra class is a rejection/abstention option (⊥) that allows the verifier to refuse a decision when uncertain, which is essential for enforcing soundness guarantees.

## Method

NCV decomposes classification into three jointly trained modules operating in concept space: a frozen concept extractor (NCB or CLIP-based SpLiCE) producing dense encodings, two provers (cooperative Merlin and adversarial Morgana) implemented as Set Transformers or MLPs that output sparse binary masks selecting m concepts, and a nonlinear verifier (Arthur) that predicts from only the masked concepts. Training follows a two-phase min-max scheme: in the first phase, Merlin minimizes cross-entropy on its selected subset while Morgana maximizes a modified loss that penalizes Arthur for being confidently wrong (with abstention counted as sound); in the second phase, Arthur minimizes a γ-weighted combination of both losses. Sparsity is enforced by discretizing continuous mask scores via a top-m operator before Arthur's update, while gradients flow through soft masks during prover updates. The key architectural insight is that shifting the prover-verifier interaction from raw pixels to a compact, semantically grounded concept space simultaneously resolves the dimensionality bottleneck of pixel-based PVGs and the expressivity limitation of linear CBMs, while the adversarial Morgana prover intrinsically mitigates shortcut learning by forcing the verifier to rely on robust, task-relevant concepts.

## Results

NCV achieves the highest completeness (accuracy) on 4 of 5 datasets: 98.92% on CLEVR-Hans3, 97.89% on CLEVR-Hans7, 83.32% on CIFAR-100, and 75.42% on COCOLogic, while attaining near-perfect soundness (≥97.87%) across all benchmarks. On CIFAR-100 and COCOLogic, NCV surpasses the opaque ResNet-50 baseline (83.32% vs. 81.45% and 75.42% vs. 65.80% respectively). Pixel-MAC, the pixel-space PVG baseline, collapses on real-world data (15.27% completeness on CIFAR-100, 35.06% on ImageNet-1k), whereas NCV scales gracefully. The sole exception is ImageNet-1k, where NCV (67.04%) trails ResNet-50 (76.01%) and the linear CBM (68.59%) due to the added training complexity of the three-module game.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ResNet-18/50 (opaque black-box), NCV wins on CLEVR-Hans3 (98.92% vs. 97.87%), CIFAR-100 (83.32% vs. 81.45%), and COCOLogic (75.42% vs. 65.80%), ties on CLEVR-Hans7 (97.89% vs. 98.71%), but loses on ImageNet-1k (67.04% vs. 76.01%). Against Pixel-MAC (pixel-space PVG), NCV dominates on every dataset: it matches or exceeds completeness while achieving perfect soundness, whereas Pixel-MAC collapses to 15.27% completeness on CIFAR-100 and 35.06% on ImageNet-1k. Against the linear CBM, NCV closes the interpretability-accuracy gap, improving by +3.5 points on CLEVR-Hans3, +8.8 on CLEVR-Hans7, +7.9 on CIFAR-100, and +16.6 on COCOLogic. Against DCR (neural-symbolic concept reasoning), NCV achieves 83.32% vs. 51.76% on CIFAR-100 and 75.42% vs. 38.61% on COCOLogic, while DCR times out on ImageNet-1k (>3 days/epoch). Against Variational Information Pursuit (V-IP), NCV outperforms on CIFAR-100 in the supplementary comparison, benefiting from its fixed-vocabulary, per-sample sparse selection rather than sequential query selection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of work: Prover-Verifier Games (PVGs) / Merlin-Arthur Classifiers (Anil et al., 2021; Waldchen et al., 2024), which provide information-theoretic interpretability guarantees but are limited to low-dimensional inputs, and Concept Bottleneck Models (Koh et al., 2020; Stammer et al., 2024b; Bhalla et al., 2024), which offer interpretable concept-based predictions but typically rely on linear classifiers that fail on nonlinear tasks. NCV's key architectural move is to shift the prover-verifier interaction from raw pixel space into a compact concept encoding space, thereby inheriting the formal guarantees of MAC while gaining the scalability and human interpretability of concept-based models. The framework is agnostic to the concept extractor (NCB for synthetic, CLIP/SpLiCE for natural images) and delegates sparsity to the adversarial prover-verifier game rather than to the concept space itself, distinguishing it from prior sparse-concept approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NCV addresses a concrete scalability bottleneck: pixel-based MAC essentially collapses on real-world datasets (15% completeness on CIFAR-100) while NCV achieves 83% with near-perfect soundness, demonstrating that concept-space PVGs are a viable path to verifiable classification at scale. The framework also narrows the interpretability-accuracy gap of linear CBMs and shows a modest but consistent reduction in shortcut-learning vulnerability, making it relevant for high-stakes deployment. Key limitations include dependence on concept-extractor quality, inherited CLIP biases, increased training complexity from the three-module min-max game, and the fact that the information-theoretic guarantees remain idealized (not directly estimable in high-dimensional, multi-class settings). Open questions include whether the approach extends to NLP/structured data, whether more stable discrete-mask training is possible, and how to handle concept spaces that diverge from expert semantics.
