## Problem

Nonlinear causal discovery in time series learns state-dependent functions that map past system configurations to future outcomes, yet the resulting causal relationships are almost universally summarized by scalar edge scores. The paper argues this practice constitutes a severe information bottleneck: scalar scores conflate between-state variation in the learned causal function with within-state residual noise, making qualitatively distinct mechanisms (thresholded, saturating, sign-changing) indistinguishable under standard ranking or thresholding.

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained additive, contribution-decomposable autoregressive model (e.g., NAVAR with K lags) and its original multivariate time series as input, operating on the learned contribution tensor rather than modifying the model architecture. It produces, for each directed edge i→j, a continuous causal response function g_ij(x) mapping source variable values to expected prediction changes, along with regime-conditional variants that partition the response by tertiles of the target's lagged value. The output is a set of curves (evaluated on a grid spanning the empirical range of the source variable) rather than a single scalar per edge.

## Method

The paper formalizes function-valued causal influence as the conditional expectation of a source variable's contribution given its state, and proves via the law of total variance that the standard scalar score (standard deviation of contributions) upper-bounds the variance of this function, with the gap equal to expected within-state residual variance. For estimation, it adapts Individual Conditional Expectation (ICE) as an intervention-style operator: for each grid point x, all lags of the source variable are simultaneously replaced with x (lag-aggregated ICE), the model prediction is recomputed, and the change relative to the original prediction is averaged over all valid time windows. Regime-conditional ICE further partitions windows by discrete bins of a regime variable (e.g., tertiles of the target's lagged value) to recover state-specific activation patterns. The framework is architecture-agnostic, requiring only that the model admit an additive decomposition of per-variable contributions, and is validated on controlled synthetic systems with four distinct nonlinear mechanisms and a 139-country democratic development panel from V-Dem.

## Results

In synthetic experiments, four qualitatively distinct causal mechanisms (linear, threshold, saturating, sign-changing) produce nearly identical NAVAR scalar causal scores (mean 0.626–0.650, overlapping ranges across 15 runs), making them indistinguishable under score-based ranking. Lag-aggregated ICE recovers the true functional form with Pearson r ≥ 0.968 for all mechanisms (linear and saturating: r = 0.996; sign-changing: r = 0.977; threshold: r = 0.968). In the V-Dem democracy panel (139 countries, 35 years, 16 indicators), 98% of 50 edges above the score threshold exhibit regime reversal, 32% show saturation, and 10% show threshold activation—patterns entirely invisible to the scalar scores that treat all edges as broadly comparable.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing causal discovery methods; instead, its central comparison is representational: standard scalar causal scores (standard deviation of contribution time series) versus the proposed ICE-based function-valued analysis. Scalar scores fail to distinguish among linear, threshold, saturating, and sign-changing mechanisms, with all four occupying a narrow band (0.626–0.650) and overlapping min/max ranges. ICE-based function-valued analysis recovers the correct qualitative functional form in every case (Pearson r ≥ 0.968), revealing threshold activation, saturation, and sign reversal that scalar summaries collapse. In the democracy application, edges with similar scalar magnitudes (~0.1 for judicial constraints and freedom of association; ~0.2 for freedom of expression and legislative constraints) exhibit fundamentally different regime-conditional behaviors—flat-then-activating, smooth gradual, asymmetric with saturation—that are invisible in the scalar graph. The paper also shows robustness: alternative scalar metrics (mean absolute contribution, max, variance) and varied noise/jump parameters all fail to separate the mechanisms, confirming the limitation is dimensional rather than metric-specific.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Neural Additive Vector Autoregression (NAVAR) architecture (Bussmann et al., 2021) and the broader class of additive, contribution-decomposable autoregressive models (Li & Genton, 2009), which learn per-variable nonlinear contribution functions. Its key methodological tool is Individual Conditional Expectation (ICE; Goldstein et al., 2015), repurposed from a model-interpretation technique into an intervention-style estimator for causal response functions in time series. The paper's conceptual departure from prior nonlinear Granger-causal discovery work (e.g., Zhou et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2020) is a reframing: rather than treating the output of causal discovery as a scalar edge score matrix, it formalizes the learned object as a function-valued causal influence and shows that standard scalar summaries constitute a provable information bottleneck via the law of total variance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that qualitatively distinct causal mechanisms (threshold, saturation, sign-reversal) produce nearly indistinguishable scalar scores (range 0.626–0.650) has direct consequences for any domain where causal interpretation drives decisions, particularly the social sciences and policy analysis. The democracy panel application showing 98% of edges exhibit regime reversal—entirely invisible to scalar scores—demonstrates that the representational collapse is not merely theoretical but systematically misleading in realistic settings. Key limitations include restriction to additive architectures (non-additive models would require Shapley interaction indices), Granger-style (predictive) rather than structural causality, and potential ICE bias in sparsely covered regions of the state space. Extending function-valued analysis to non-additive architectures and connecting it to structural causal inference remain important open directions.
