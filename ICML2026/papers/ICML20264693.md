## Problem

Loss spikes in large-scale LLM pretraining arise from diverse and often subtle causes—data outliers, hardware faults, numerical precision issues, and optimizer hyperparameter sensitivity—yet all converge at the optimizer level as abnormal gradients contaminating first- and second-moment states. Existing mitigations like GlobalGC use a single fixed global threshold that suffers from both temporal mismatch (the optimal threshold drifts over training) and spatial mismatch (gradient statistics vary asynchronously across tensors), leaving localized outliers undetected. The paper asks whether a unified, gradient-centric intervention at the point of moment accumulation can robustly suppress spikes regardless of their upstream origin.

## Input / Output

At each training step, AdaGC consumes the per-tensor gradient vectors (one per parameter tensor, e.g., each attention weight, FFN layer, or embedding) along with a single scalar EMA state γ_{t,i} per tensor tracking its historical clipped gradient norm. It outputs a scaled (clipped) version of each tensor's gradient—unchanged if its ℓ₂ norm is within λ_rel·γ_{t-1,i}, otherwise rescaled to exactly that bound—and an updated EMA value computed from the clipped norm rather than the raw one. During the first T_start steps (default 100), the method falls back to standard GlobalGC while tracking the minimum per-tensor norm to seed the EMA, avoiding early overestimation from large initial gradients.

## Method

AdaGC replaces the single global clipping threshold with an independent adaptive threshold per parameter tensor, computed as an exponential moving average (β=0.99) of that tensor's own historical clipped gradient norms. When a tensor's current gradient norm exceeds λ_rel (default 1.04) times its EMA, the gradient is rescaled to the threshold before entering the optimizer's moment accumulators, preventing outlier contamination of both first- and second-moment estimates. The method is optimizer-agnostic (validated with AdamW, Lion, and Muon) and reduces communication overhead in hybrid-parallel training because per-tensor norms only require all-reduce within tensor-parallel groups, unlike GlobalGC which aggregates across all DP, TP, and PP groups. A convergence analysis (Theorem G.1) shows that Adam with AdaGC retains the standard O(1/√T) convergence rate under mild assumptions, and ablations confirm that both the per-tensor locality and the EMA adaptivity are necessary—global adaptive clipping or fixed per-tensor thresholds each underperform.

## Results

AdaGC reduces spike scores to zero across all tested models (Llama-2 7B, Mixtral 8×1B, ERNIE 10B-A1.4B) and improves zero-shot average accuracy over GlobalGC by +1.32% (Llama-2 7B: 51.01% vs 49.69%), +1.27% (Mixtral 8×1B: 49.01% vs 47.74%), and +2.48% (ERNIE 10B-A1.4B: 34.73% vs 32.25% at 1T tokens). On Qwen3-1.7B, AdaGC achieves 50.37% zero-shot accuracy vs 48.42% for GlobalGC and 48.42% for ZClip, while reducing the spike score from 0.2842% to 0.0053%. End-to-end GPU hours are reduced by up to 4.48% (Llama-2 7B) compared to GlobalGC due to lower communication overhead in hybrid-parallel training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GlobalGC (the standard norm-based clipping baseline), AdaGC wins on every model: +1.32% zero-shot on Llama-2 7B (51.01% vs 49.69%), +1.95% on Qwen3-1.7B (50.37% vs 48.42%), +1.27% on Mixtral 8×1B (49.01% vs 47.74%), and +2.48% on ERNIE 10B-A1.4B at 1T tokens, while reducing spike scores from 0.0333%–0.2842% to 0%–0.0053%. Against update-clipping methods AGC and Clippy, AdaGC achieves 51.01% vs 49.34% and 49.39% respectively on Llama-2 7B zero-shot, confirming that gradient-level intervention outperforms update-level control. Against the most recent adaptive clipping method ZClip, AdaGC improves zero-shot accuracy by +1.95% on Qwen3-1.7B (50.37% vs 48.42%) and reduces spike score from 0.0421% to 0.0053%. Against non-clipping stabilization methods (SPAM, ScaledEmbed, WeSaR), AdaGC leads on Llama-2 7B zero-shot (51.01% vs 48.85%/49.42%/49.59%) and achieves zero spike scores where SPAM (0.0333%) and ScaledEmbed (0.0889%) still exhibit spikes. AdaGC also reduces end-to-end GPU hours by 0.27%–4.48% compared to GlobalGC, an additional efficiency gain from avoiding cross-DP/PP all-reduce for the global norm.

## Relation to Prior Work

AdaGC extends the norm-based gradient clipping lineage initiated by GlobalGC (Pascanu et al., 2013) by replacing the single fixed global threshold with a per-tensor adaptive threshold derived from an EMA of each tensor's historical clipped gradient norm. Conceptually, it departs from prior work that either targets specific root causes of loss spikes (data quality, hardware faults, numerical precision) or clips parameter updates (AGC, Clippy) by adopting a unified gradient-centric perspective: all spike sources converge at the optimizer level, so intervention should occur at the gradient level before moment accumulation. Compared to the closest prior method ZClip (Kumar et al., 2025), which tracks a global gradient norm with z-score-based anomaly detection, AdaGC operates at tensor granularity with a simpler EMA mechanism, and unlike AGC/Clippy it clips gradients rather than updates, directly protecting first- and second-moment states.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Loss spikes remain a major source of wasted compute in large-scale LLM pretraining, and AdaGC offers a simple, optimizer-agnostic, low-overhead drop-in that consistently eliminates spikes across dense and MoE architectures from 1.3B to 10B parameters while also reducing communication costs in hybrid-parallel settings. The ability to stabilize training under aggressive hyperparameter choices (e.g., ε=1e-15 in AdamW, β₂=0.999) has practical value for practitioners seeking to exploit adaptive learning rates more fully. However, the paper explicitly acknowledges that AdaGC cannot address cumulative numerical drift from low-precision implementations or silent infrastructure bugs, and the theoretical analysis provides only a convergence rate guarantee rather than a spike-elimination proof. Open questions include principled hyperparameter selection rules and diagnostics to distinguish optimizer-mitigable gradient anomalies from failures requiring root-cause intervention.
