## Problem

Existing evaluation metrics for discrete diffusion language models (dLLMs) conflate denoiser approximation error with sampler-induced error from the sampling dynamics, such as conditional independence assumptions in parallel decoding and discretization from finite time steps. Unlike autoregressive models where ancestral sampling exactly reflects the learned distribution, dLLM samplers introduce systematic bias independent of model quality, yet no existing metric can attribute generation error to either source. This paper asks whether few-step dLLM samplers are actually distributionally correct when denoiser error is removed.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a ground-truth sparse Markov transition kernel (constructed from bigram statistics on OpenWebText with ByteBPE-4096 vocabulary or character-level Text8) and a masked sequence state z_t, computing exact posterior marginals via HMM forward-backward inference to serve as oracle denoisers. Each sampler (SEDD, MDLM, LLaDA, ReMDM) consumes these oracle marginals while preserving its original update logic, producing generated sequences of length T=1024 across 8–1024 diffusion steps. Evaluation outputs transition-level correctness metrics (KL, NLL, TV, entropy), diversity diagnostics (n-gram diversity, duplication rate), coverage metrics (other-mass, support fraction), and external metrics (GenPPL via GPT-2 Large, MAUVE).

## Method

The core contribution is a sampler-centric oracle evaluation framework that eliminates model approximation error by construction. The data distribution is defined as a discrete Markov chain with a sparse transition kernel (top-K outgoing transitions per state plus a small teleport component for ergodicity), and the forward masking process induces an HMM for which exact posteriors under partial observations are computed via forward-backward smoothing in the log domain. Each dLLM sampler is instantiated by replacing its learned denoiser with this exact oracle posterior while preserving its original sampling dynamics: score-based τ-leaping for SEDD, parallel masked-position decoding for MDLM, confidence-based remasking for LLaDA, and re-masking schedules (confidence-based and loop-based) for ReMDM. This isolates sampling dynamics as the sole source of distributional deviation, enabling controlled comparison across sampler designs. The key empirical finding is that few-step samplers exhibit substantial transition-level mismatch even under an exact oracle denoiser, with error vanishing only as diffusion steps approach the sequence length, and that commonly used metrics (GenPPL, MAUVE) can remain favorable or even improve despite this mismatch.

## Results

Under an exact HMM oracle denoiser on OpenWebText (BPE-4096, T=1024), all four discrete diffusion samplers (SEDD, MDLM, ReMDM, LLaDA) exhibit substantial transition-level mismatch at few steps: at 8 steps, transition KL is ~1.07–1.32 versus the AR baseline's 0.2454, and NLL is ~5.25–5.27 versus 4.1179. SEDD, MDLM, and ReMDM converge to the AR baseline only as steps approach T (at 1024 steps, KL ≈ 0.24–0.26, NLL ≈ 4.11–4.13), while LLaDA never converges: at 1024 steps its KL remains 1.33 (5.4× the AR value) and its 3-gram diversity collapses to 0.35 (vs. 0.9975 for AR). In a controlled AR sanity check, local temperature sharpening (β from 1 to 25) monotonically reduces GenPPL from 688.4 to 1.27 while destroying n-gram diversity, demonstrating that GenPPL can be gamed without faithful sampling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The AR baseline (exact sampling from the ground-truth Markov chain) serves as the correctness reference with NLL=4.1179 and KL=0.2454. SEDD, MDLM, and ReMDM all converge to the AR baseline as diffusion steps approach T=1024 (KL within 0.01 of AR), but at practical few-step settings (8–32 steps) they remain 4–5× worse in KL, confirming that few-step discrete diffusion is not distributionally correct even with a perfect denoiser. LLaDA is the clear outlier: it never converges, maintaining KL≈1.33 (5.4× AR) at all step counts, while its entropy rate collapses from 2.98 to 1.26 and 3-gram diversity drops to 0.35, revealing a sampler–denoiser confidence coupling that the oracle setting exposes. Among the three converging samplers, MDLM and SEDD track each other closely (nearly identical KL at every step), while ReMDM is marginally slower to converge (KL=0.2564 vs. 0.2446/0.2466 at 1024 steps). The paper further shows that GenPPL and MAUVE—commonly used external metrics—fail to detect these transition-level errors: GenPPL improves under temperature sharpening that destroys correctness, and MAUVE remains uniformly high across ReMDM variants despite substantial KL differences.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the discrete diffusion language model (dLLM) lineage—SEDD, MDLM, LLaDA, and ReMDM—by preserving each sampler's original update logic while replacing its learned denoiser with an exact HMM forward-backward posterior computed from a ground-truth Markov chain. Conceptually, it departs from prior dLLM evaluation, which assesses final generated samples through task-based or likelihood metrics that conflate denoiser approximation error with sampler-induced error. The oracle framework repurposes classical HMM smoothing as a controlled diagnostic tool, and the transition-level correctness metrics (KL, NLL, entropy against the oracle kernel) are introduced specifically to measure what existing surface-level metrics (GenPPL, MAUVE) cannot capture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that few-step dLLM samplers remain distributionally incorrect even under a perfect oracle denoiser, with transition-level mismatch vanishing only as steps approach sequence length—reveals a structural limitation of parallel masked decoding that the field has not previously isolated. The demonstration that GenPPL can be monotonically improved by local sharpening (even in a trivial AR bigram model) while destroying transition faithfulness, and that MAUVE remains insensitive to substantial sampler error, has direct practical implications for how dLLM papers report and compare results. Key limitations include the first-order Markov oracle (which cannot capture higher-order linguistic dependencies) and the absence of a proposed corrective sampler; open questions include whether the mismatch persists under richer ground-truth distributions and whether any few-step sampler design can achieve transition-level correctness without sacrificing parallelism.
