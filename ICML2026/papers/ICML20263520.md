## Problem

Search-based reinforcement learning methods like AlphaZero achieve superhuman performance in board games but demand enormous computational resources (over 10 GPU-years), hindering reproducibility and practical use. Model-free RL, while efficient in other domains, has not been fully explored in two-player board games due to perceived instability in self-play settings. This paper asks whether a pure model-free algorithm can achieve stable, competitive learning with dramatically fewer training resources by properly combining regularization techniques.

## Input / Output

The method takes grid-based board game state observations of varying shapes (e.g., (9,9,17) for 9×9 Go, (11,11,4) for Hex, (8,8,2) for Othello) and produces a discrete action distribution over the legal move set (65–1225 actions depending on the game) along with per-action value estimates. The policy network and action-value network share a 6-block ResNet backbone (128 channels, ~2M parameters) with separate heads; the final action distribution is not the raw network output but is computed analytically from the Q-values and the current policy via a closed-form expression, making the network a parameterization rather than a direct policy head.

## Method

KLENT solves a regularized policy optimization problem that combines reverse-KL divergence (for gradual policy updates addressing non-stationarity in self-play) and entropy regularization (for sustained exploration addressing train-test distribution shift). The key insight is that this specific combination admits a closed-form optimal policy π'(a|s) ∝ exp((Q(s,a) + β log π(a|s))/(α+β)), which is used as the training target for the policy network via cross-entropy loss, completely eliminating the need for MCTS. The action-value function is trained with λ-returns (λ = e^{-1/8}) rather than Monte Carlo returns to balance bias and variance. Training alternates between a self-play phase (collecting on-policy data using the analytical policy) and a fitting phase (minimizing a joint loss over policy cross-entropy and Q-value MSE). Theoretically, the paper proves local linear convergence of the update rule in normal-form games under the condition α(α+2β) > ‖R‖²₂/4 (broader than prior work) and convergence to the entropy-regularized optimal policy in finite-length games via backward induction.

## Results

KLENT achieves up to 4× higher training efficiency than Gumbel AlphaZero (the strongest search-based baseline): Gumbel AlphaZero requires 300M simulator evaluations to reach 50% average win rate across five board games, while KLENT reaches the same level in only 75M. In head-to-head matches with test-time MCTS (800 rollouts) after 800M training evaluations, KLENT attains a 77.2% average win rate against the Pgx baseline, compared to 53.6% for Gumbel AlphaZero and 32.2% for AlphaZero. In 9×9 Go specifically, KLENT achieves 89% win rate with MCTS versus 37% for Gumbel AlphaZero and 7% for AlphaZero. Even in the large-scale 19×19 Go environment, KLENT maintains competitive learning efficiency against AlphaZero.

## Baselines & Comparisons

KLENT decisively outperforms all search-based baselines in high-branching-factor environments: in 9×9 Go (branching factor 42.3) and Hex (90.6), KLENT achieves 89% and 98% win rates with MCTS versus 37%/47% for Gumbel AlphaZero and 7%/8% for AlphaZero. In lower-branching-factor games (Animal Shogi, Gardner Chess), KLENT is competitive with Gumbel AlphaZero (63% vs 67% in Animal Shogi) but clearly beats AlphaZero. Among model-free baselines, KLENT dominates: PPO and DQN (shown in Figure 6) trail significantly in learning speed, while Discrete SAC and V-MPO fail to exceed 1% win rate after 800M evaluations in 9×9 Go. Recent model-free approaches Munchausen DQN (2%) and GRPO (3%) also fall far short of KLENT's 89% at 800M evaluations. The ablation study confirms that removing any of the three key components (reverse-KL, entropy, λ-returns) degrades performance, with entropy removal causing the most severe instability (policy entropy collapsing to near zero).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the theoretical framework of Sokota et al. (2022), who analyzed the combination of KL and entropy regularization in two-player zero-sum games, and on Grill et al. (2020), who showed that AlphaZero implicitly solves a KL-regularized policy optimization problem. The key departure is twofold: (1) the authors derive a broader convergence condition (α(α+2β) > ‖R‖²₂/4 vs. the prior αβ > ‖R‖²₂) for the policy update rule in normal-form games, and (2) they demonstrate that this regularized policy optimization, combined with λ-returns, yields a fully model-free algorithm (KLENT) that eliminates MCTS entirely during training. The authors explicitly note their contribution is not new algorithmic ingredients but new theoretical and empirical characterizations of this specific regularization combination in the two-player game setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: KLENT achieves up to 4× training efficiency over Gumbel AlphaZero across five board games without any look-ahead search, which could make competitive game-playing agents accessible in resource-constrained settings and accelerate research in adjacent domains (discrete optimization, algorithmic discovery, mathematical proving) where AlphaZero-style methods are currently standard. A key limitation acknowledged by the authors is that efficiency gains do not imply superior asymptotic performance under unlimited compute, so search-based methods may still dominate at scale. Open questions include whether the convergence guarantees extend to infinite-horizon or stochastic environments, and whether the model-free approach can match search-based methods in very high-branching-factor games (e.g., full 19×19 Go) given sufficient training budget.
