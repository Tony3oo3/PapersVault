## Problem

Existing LoRA fine-tuning pipelines execute each adapter configuration in isolation, yet individual LoRA jobs are inherently lightweight (small batch sizes, low-rank matrix ops, tiny parameter updates), resulting in severe GPU underutilization with SM occupancy as low as 16.7% and memory utilization below 55%. When practical workflows require training many heterogeneous adapters for hyperparameter search, multi-task adaptation, or domain specialization, this per-job inefficiency compounds into long makespans despite abundant hardware. Prior systems (SLoRA, Punica, DLoRA) address concurrent LoRA *serving* at inference time but leave the training-side scheduling and kernel optimization problem unexplored.

## Input / Output

PLoRA takes a frozen base LLM (Qwen-2.5 3B–32B, LLaMA-3.2-3B, LLaMA-3.1-8B), a heterogeneous set of LoRA configurations (each specifying learning rate, batch size, rank 8–128, and scaling factor α), a pool of GPUs (e.g., 8×A100 40 GB or 8×A10 24 GB), and per-task training datasets, and produces fine-tuned LoRA adapters stored in a checkpoint pool along with a complete job scheduling policy. The scheduling policy specifies which configurations are packed into each job, the tensor-parallelism degree (a power of two) assigned to each job, and the temporal ordering of jobs. A cost model estimated from the first ~10 iterations drives both the offline planner and the online resource monitor.

## Method

PLoRA employs a two-stage design: an offline packing planner and an online execution engine. The planner formulates joint configuration packing and GPU allocation as an NP-hard variant of 0-1 knapsack, solved by a Decomposed Throughput Maximization (DTM) algorithm that enumerates power-of-two parallelism degrees, invokes a Gurobi ILP solver for each degree to select the optimal subset of configurations maximizing instantaneous throughput under memory constraints, and recursively packs remaining configurations; a job planner then sequences the resulting packs over time using predicted completion events. The execution engine deploys packed jobs with tensor parallelism and monitors resource availability to launch new jobs as GPUs free up. To make packed execution efficient, PLoRA implements custom CUDA kernels (built on CUTLASS) that concatenate LoRA A and B matrices across adapters and tile along sequence and hidden dimensions—deliberately avoiding tiling along the small rank dimension—to achieve near-linear scaling up to 32 concurrent adapters. A theorem bounds the tail effect of the greedy scheduling, yielding an approximation ratio between 1.05 and 1.14 in practice.

## Results

PLoRA reduces end-to-end fine-tuning makespan by up to 7.52× (LLaMA-3.2-3B) and 7.08× (Qwen-2.5-3B) over the Min GPU baseline, and improves job-level throughput by up to 12.8× at batch size 1 across Qwen-2.5 model sizes (3B–32B). The speedup decomposes into ~1.8× from the packing planner alone and up to 3.93× additional from optimized packed LoRA CUDA kernels. On model quality, the best adapter found by searching 120 configurations with PLoRA outperforms the default Unsloth configuration by up to 23.4% (CoLA on Qwen-2.5-3B), with consistent gains of 2.9%–11.8% across all four tasks and model families.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PLoRA is compared against two sequential baselines: Min GPU (allocates the minimum GPUs per job and launches parallel jobs to fill the pool) and Max GPU (dedicates all 8 GPUs to a single job at a time). PLoRA dominates both on makespan: it achieves 6.33×–7.52× speedups over Min GPU across Qwen-2.5 (3B–32B) and LLaMA-3 (3B, 8B) models, while Max GPU is consistently the worst performer due to even lower hardware utilization. On job-level throughput at batch size 1, PLoRA reaches up to 12.8× over Min GPU; the gap narrows at larger batch sizes (e.g., ~4× at BS=4) because Min GPU can better saturate the GPU with larger batches. The ablation (Figure 6) shows the packing planner alone yields ~1.8× makespan reduction, while the custom packed LoRA kernels contribute an additional up to 3.93×, confirming both components are necessary for the full speedup. On model quality, PLoRA's search finds adapters that beat the default Unsloth configuration on every task–model pair, with the largest gain of +23.4% on CoLA (Qwen-2.5-3B).

## Relation to Prior Work

PLoRA builds directly on LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) as the underlying parameter-efficient fine-tuning technique, but departs from the dominant line of LoRA research that focuses on inference-time serving of already-trained adapters (SLoRA, Punica, DLoRA). It also differs from mLoRA, which scales a single LoRA training run via pipeline parallelism, by targeting the orthogonal problem of concurrently training many heterogeneous LoRA adapters. The scheduling component draws on cluster job-scheduling literature (Gandiva, Tiresias, Themis) but introduces the novel joint optimization of adapter packing and parallelism degree, formulated as a knapsack variant with a provably near-optimal DTM algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For enterprises offering fine-tuning-as-a-service (e.g., Apple Intelligence, LoRA Land), the ability to train dozens of LoRA adapters concurrently on shared hardware is a direct operational bottleneck; PLoRA's 12.8× throughput and 7.52× makespan reductions address this with a generalizable system that extends to QLoRA, DoRA, and AdaLoRA. Open questions include whether the cost model and packing strategy remain accurate for much larger models (70B+) or for training paradigms beyond supervised fine-tuning (e.g., RLHF, multi-turn dialogue), and whether the near-linear kernel scaling holds beyond 32 adapters on next-generation hardware. The paper's evaluation is limited to 3B–32B models and four GLUE/GSM8K tasks, leaving scalability to frontier-scale models untested.
