## Problem

Despite Mamba's empirical success in in-context learning, no prior work provided a theoretical analysis of its training dynamics or provable ICL generalization guarantees, largely due to the nonlinearity of its sigmoid-based gating mechanism. This paper addresses three open questions: how Mamba learns ICL, how robust it is to outliers in context examples, and under what conditions it provably outperforms Transformers for ICL in the presence of distribution-shifted additive outliers.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a prompt matrix of shape (d+1) × (l+1), where the first d rows hold input vectors for l labeled context examples and one query, and the (d+1)-th row holds their binary labels (the query's entry is 0). Context examples may be corrupted by additive outlier patterns that deterministically flip, target, or randomize their labels, modeling data-poisoning attacks. The model outputs a scalar score F(Ψ; P) whose sign yields the predicted binary label for the query.

## Method

The one-layer Mamba is decomposed into a linear attention component (key/query matrices W_B, W_C) followed by a nonlinear sigmoid gating layer (parameter vector w), and is trained via SGD with hinge loss on prompts from a set of binary classification tasks. The key mechanistic insight is that the linear attention concentrates weight on context examples sharing the same relevant pattern as the query, while the nonlinear gating suppresses outlier-containing examples (driving their gate values to near zero) and applies exponential decay in importance based on index distance from the query. The central theoretical result shows that Mamba maintains accurate ICL generalization even when the test-prompt outlier fraction α approaches 1, whereas a linear Transformer (gating fixed to 1) provably fails once α exceeds 1/2. The nonlinear gating thus trades a harder training landscape (requiring Θ(l_tr) more iterations and a larger batch size) for substantially superior robustness at inference time.

## Results

On synthetic binary classification tasks, the trained one-layer Mamba maintains classification error below 0.01 even when the outlier fraction α approaches 0.8, while the one-layer single-head linear Transformer exhibits large error as soon as α exceeds 1/2. On the SST-2 sentiment dataset (3-layer, 2-head models, α=0.75), Mamba achieves 74.10% ICL accuracy when outliers are placed farthest from the query (FQ) and 73.86% at random positions (R), compared to 70.18% and 69.98% for linear attention. Theoretically, Mamba requires Θ(l_tr) more training iterations and a larger batch size than linear Transformers to converge, but in exchange it generalizes accurately when the outlier fraction α approaches 1, whereas linear Transformers are limited to α < 1/2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is the one-layer single-head linear Transformer, obtained by setting the nonlinear gating function G_{i,l+1}(w)=1 in the Mamba formulation. On synthetic data, Mamba clearly wins on robustness: it sustains <1% classification error at outlier fractions up to α≈0.8, while the linear Transformer's error spikes sharply once α>1/2, confirming the theoretical threshold in Theorem 4. On SST-2, Mamba outperforms linear attention by roughly 4 percentage points in the FQ and R settings (74.10% vs 70.18%, 73.86% vs 69.98%), but the gap narrows in the CQ setting (71.95% vs 69.82%) where Mamba's exponential gating decay penalizes clean examples pushed farther from the query. The trade-off is in training cost: Theorem 1 shows Mamba needs Θ(l_tr) more iterations and a batch size scaled by β^{-4}V²κ_a^{-2}(1-p_a)^{-2} compared to the linear Transformer's simpler requirements in Theorem 3. The authors note that multi-head or softmax-attention Transformers can partially close the robustness gap (Appendix C.1), but the one-layer single-head linear comparison isolates the specific contribution of Mamba's nonlinear gating mechanism.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical ICL framework developed for Transformers (Zhang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024a; Huang et al., 2023) to the Mamba architecture, providing the first provable training-dynamics analysis of a one-layer Mamba model. It builds directly on the Mamba formulation of Gu & Dao (2024), decomposing it into a linear attention component (analogous to Transformer keys/queries) and a nonlinear sigmoid gating layer, and shows that the gating is the sole structural difference that enables outlier robustness. The data formulation with relevant/irrelevant orthogonal patterns and additive outliers follows the sparse-representation setup standard in ICL theory, while the outlier-robustness angle connects to empirical data-poisoning studies (Wan et al., 2023; He et al., 2025b) and the empirical Mamba-vs-Transformer comparisons of Park et al. (2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes the first theoretical guarantees that Mamba can maintain accurate ICL even when the outlier fraction in the test prompt approaches 1, whereas linear Transformers are provably limited to below 1/2 — a clean, well-evidenced separation that explains Mamba's empirical advantage in noisy-prompt settings. The mechanism analysis (attention selects same-pattern examples; gating suppresses outliers and induces exponential local bias) offers interpretable insight into why Mamba's harder-to-optimize gating layer is precisely what buys robustness. Key limitations include the one-layer, orthogonal-pattern, binary-classification setting, and the requirement that test-time outliers be positive linear combinations of training-time outliers; extending to multi-layer Mamba, incoherence conditions, and other SSM variants remains open.
