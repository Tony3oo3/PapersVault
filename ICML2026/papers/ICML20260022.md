## Problem

Activation steering methods for LLM adaptation remain largely heuristic-driven, with critical design choices such as intervention location and parameterization determined through empirical trial-and-error rather than principled analysis. There is no formal equivalence mapping between weight-space fine-tuning and activation-space interventions, and no comparative framework explaining why certain intervention sites outperform others, leaving steering design as a black-box process.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260022/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard text prompts for NLP tasks (commonsense reasoning, math, long-range dependency) and produces text responses. The intervention is applied at the post-block residual stream (after the skip connection is added to the MLP output) at every transformer layer, where a bottleneck adapter takes the d-dimensional hidden state h and produces a low-rank shift W₂φ(W₁h) of rank r (typically r=8), adding it back to the residual stream. This means the trainable parameters scale only with model dimension d rather than MLP hidden dimension, yielding ~0.04% trainable parameters.

## Method

The paper establishes a first-order Taylor equivalence between weight-space fine-tuning and activation-space steering for GLU MLPs, showing that post-MLP steering can replicate fine-tuning updates that pre-MLP steering cannot (accounting for the ΔW_d·m term). Building on this, it identifies the post-block output—where the skip connection is added back to the MLP output—as a theoretically grounded, highly expressive intervention site that modulates the full residual stream, with Theorem 3.1 bounding the approximation error via principal angles between subspaces. The practical method applies a bottleneck adapter (W₂φ(W₁h)) globally at every layer, and introduces joint adaptation that simultaneously trains LoRA-style weight updates and activation adapters with an orthogonality constraint (projecting W₂ onto the orthogonal complement of the LoRA B matrix's column space) to prevent both components from collapsing into the same learned subspace, enabling them to capture complementary functional roles.

## Results

Post-block steering achieves an average performance gap of only 0.2%–0.9% from full-parameter SFT across four models (Llama-3.2-1B, Gemma-3-1B, Qwen-3-4B, Llama-3.1-8B) and six reasoning tasks, while training just 0.04% of model parameters. On the ultra-efficient setting (0.001%–0.005% params), the method outperforms LoFiT and JoLA by 2–8 percentage points in average accuracy, with the gap widening to ~8% at the 8B scale where baselines collapse. In instruction tuning (AlpacaEval 2.0), the nonlinear adapter reaches 11.34% LC win rate vs. 11.49% for SFT, and in RL (GSM8K Pass@1) it achieves 84.7% vs. 81.5% for LoRA using 13× fewer parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LoRA (0.45% params), the proposed post-block steering (0.04% params) matches or exceeds accuracy on 1B-scale models (e.g., +1.9 on WinoG, +0.3 on AQuA for Llama-3.2-1B) while training 11× fewer parameters; at 8B scale, Ours achieves +0.5 avg Δ vs. LoRA's +0.1. Against ReFT at the same 0.04% budget, the gap is dramatic: Ours limits the average deficit to −0.2% to −0.9% while ReFT suffers −2.8% to −10.9%, with the largest divergence on ListOps (−0.8 vs. −16.9 on Llama-3.2-1B) and ARC-Challenge (−20.4 for ReFT on Gemma-3-1B). In the ultra-efficient regime (Table 2), Ours at 0.003%–0.005% params outperforms both LoFiT (0.003%) and JoLA (0.007%) by 2–8 points in average accuracy, with the advantage growing at scale (8.0% over the best baseline on Llama-3.1-8B). In instruction tuning, the nonlinear adapter (11.34% LC win rate) beats LoRA r=16 (9.59%) and ReFT r=16 (10.96%) while using 10× fewer parameters than LoRA. In RL, Ours achieves 84.7% Pass@1 on GSM8K vs. LoRA's 81.5%, a 3.2-point gain at 13× lower parameter cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the activation steering line of work (ReFT, LoFiT, JoLA) and PEFT methods (LoRA, adapters), but departs from them by replacing empirical trial-and-error for intervention-site selection with a first-order equivalence framework between weight-space updates and activation-space interventions. It identifies the post-block output (after the skip connection) as a theoretically grounded intervention locus, contrasting with prior methods that operate on isolated sub-layer pathways. The joint adaptation component extends the PEFT/steering dichotomy by showing that weight and activation updates are functionally complementary rather than redundant, a distinction not previously formalized in the literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first principled theoretical foundation for a field that has been largely heuristic-driven, giving practitioners clear guidance on where and how to intervene in the residual stream. The practical impact is significant: 0.04% trainable parameters achieving within 0.2–0.9% of full SFT across multiple models and tasks, with the joint adaptation paradigm potentially unlocking further gains. Key limitations include the first-order nature of the analysis (which may break for large perturbations), the evaluation being limited to moderate-scale models (1B–8B) and a moderate task set, and the inability to fully disentangle the contribution of the post-block locus from the global (all-layers) intervention policy. Open questions include whether the framework scales to much larger models, whether the orthogonality constraint generalizes beyond the MSE-loss setting, and whether the linear-adapter sufficiency finding holds under more aggressive adaptation regimes.
