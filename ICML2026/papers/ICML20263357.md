## Problem

Event-based spiking neural networks face two fundamental barriers to practical training on modern hardware: the sequential charge-fire-reset cycle prevents parallelization along the time dimension, and precise spike times require solving continuous-time equations without discrete-time bins. Prior parallel methods either remove hard resets, use soft resets, or relax spike dynamics into continuous surrogates, while prior continuous-time methods impose restrictive analytical assumptions on neuron parameters (e.g., τm = 2τs). No prior work achieves parallel acceleration of exact hard-reset dynamics with machine-precision spike times.

## Input / Output

The system consumes continuous-time spike trains (precise timestamps, no time binning) as input, with each neuron receiving a sorted event queue of incoming spikes weighted by learned synaptic weights and continuous delays. Output is produced as class logits via exponentially weighted leaky integrators that accumulate incoming spikes over a prespecified window, where earlier spikes receive higher weight. The key constraint is that all spike times—both input and output—are preserved at full continuous-time resolution, and compute/memory scale with the number of spikes N rather than the number of time steps T.

## Method

The paper introduces two complementary mechanisms. First, sub-threshold LIF dynamics are expressed as affine maps (M, b) that admit an associative Combine operator, enabling parallel processing of chunks of K input spikes via jax.lax.associative_scan with O(log K) depth; speculative execution processes each chunk without knowing whether a spike occurs, then lightweight analytical checks (evaluating V at the analytically computed t_Vmax) identify the first within-chunk spike, after which post-spike work is discarded and the next chunk resumes from the reset state. Second, Newton-Raphson (14 iterations) and Bisection (20 iterations) solvers recover output spike times to float32 machine precision without discrete-time grids or restrictive time-constant relationships, with gradients propagated via the Implicit Function Theorem (∂t*/∂p = -(∂R/∂t)⁻¹ · ∂R/∂p). Together these yield up to 44× speedups over sequential event-based simulation while retaining exact hard-reset semantics, continuous delays, and multiple spikes per neuron.

## Results

On MNIST (latency-encoded), the method achieves 98.04% accuracy with 1 feedforward layer of 350 hidden units, outperforming prior exact-gradient methods (97.20% and 97.60%). On SHD, it reaches 94.96% (2F512HD) and on SSC 77.79% (2F512HD), both with exact gradients and continuous spike times. The parallel associative scan implementation achieves up to 44× speedup over a sequential event-based baseline with exact hard-reset dynamics, with compute depth O(C log K) versus O(N) or O(T) for prior methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On MNIST, the method outperforms both prior exact-gradient baselines: Goltz et al. (2021) at 97.20% (restricted to τm=2τs) and Wunderlich & Pehle (2021) at 97.60%, achieving 98.04% while also being parallelized. On SHD, the method (94.96%) slightly trails Hammouamri et al. (2024) at 95.07% but substantially outperforms Mészáros et al. (2025) at 93.10%; the paper notes Hammouamri's advantage likely stems from surrogate gradients favoring rate-coding on this benchmark. On SSC, the method (77.79%) beats Mészáros et al. (76.10%) but falls short of Hammouamri et al. (80.69%). Across all comparisons, the method is the only one combining exact gradients, continuous spike times, and parallelized computation (O(C log K) depth), and it additionally supports continuous synaptic delays and multiple spikes per neuron—capabilities absent in the discrete-time baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the event-based exact-gradient framework of Wunderlich & Pehle (2021) and Mostafa (2018), which use the Implicit Function Theorem to backpropagate through spike times, and on the classical parallel prefix-sum (associative scan) technique from Blelloch (1990). It departs from prior parallel SNN methods (PSN, PSU, SPikE-SSM, FPT) that all modify the neuron model—removing resets, using soft resets, or relaxing spike generation into sigmoids—to achieve parallelism. The key conceptual departure is showing that exact hard-reset dynamics admit an associative structure (affine maps between spikes) that can be scanned in parallel without any approximation, and that numerical root solvers (Newton-Raphson, Bisection) can replace the restrictive analytical spike-time solutions that prior continuous-time methods require (e.g., τm = 2τs).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper removes two practical barriers to event-native SNN training on GPUs: the sequential bottleneck of charge-fire-reset dynamics (solved via speculative chunked associative scans) and the need for analytical spike-time closed forms (solved via differentiable numerical solvers). This opens the door to training SNNs with heterogeneous time constants, arbitrary neuron models, and machine-precision spike timing—properties needed for compatibility with event-based sensors and neuromorphic hardware. Limitations include evaluation only on feedforward fully-connected networks (no convolutional or recurrent architectures), a fixed compute budget required for JIT compilation, and accuracy that is competitive but not dominant over discrete-time baselines on SSC. Open questions include extending the method to recurrent and convolutional SNNs, adaptive chunk sizing, and deployment to actual neuromorphic processors.
