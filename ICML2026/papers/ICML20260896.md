## Problem

Dominant PDE surrogate models are trained with deterministic losses (MSE/MAE), producing single point predictions that fail to capture the inherent uncertainty of chaotic, partially observed physical systems. While probabilistic alternatives like diffusion models exist, they require expensive from-scratch training and iterative inference, making them impractical for repurposing the large deterministic backbones the community has already invested in. The paper asks whether existing deterministic checkpoints can be efficiently converted into probabilistic emulators with modest additional compute.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a history of k discretised PDE state snapshots (e.g., 6 steps for Walrus at 32×32 token resolution, 1 step for Poseidon at 128×128, or 4 latent states for Lola at 1/32 spatial resolution) along with an internally sampled low-dimensional global noise vector ε ∈ R^{d_noise} (d_noise=32). At inference, drawing M independent noise vectors (typically M=16) produces an ensemble of M future state predictions (or temporal residuals), enabling users to trade off predictive accuracy against inference cost by varying M. The noise is shared across all spatial dimensions, acting as a global inductive bias for spatially coherent variability.

## Method

The retrofitting procedure adds a lightweight stochastic branch to a pre-trained deterministic transformer: a global noise vector is projected through a two-layer MLP into noise embeddings, which then modulate hidden representations in every (or encoder-only) transformer block via conditional layer normalisation (AdaLN) with learned scale (γ), shift (β), gate (α), and optional skip (δ) parameters. Training uses the unbiased (fair) CRPS loss, which simultaneously rewards individual ensemble member accuracy and inter-member diversity, preventing mode collapse. The key insight is that CRPS is structurally close to the MAE/MSE objectives used in pre-training, so the deterministic backbone provides a strong initialisation—unlike diffusion retrofitting, which requires learning a fundamentally different denoising task. Practical training uses differential learning rates (smaller for the frozen-in-spirit backbone, larger for the new noise branch) and near-zero initialisation of the AdaLN head so the model starts as an approximate identity and gradually learns to incorporate stochasticity. The approach is architecture-agnostic, validated across pixel-space (Walrus, Poseidon) and latent-space (Lola) transformers on five physical systems, achieving 20–54% CRPS reductions and up to 30% VRMSE improvements over compute-matched deterministic fine-tuning.

## Results

Across three architectures (Walrus, Lola, Poseidon) and five PDE systems, CRPS retrofitting achieves 20–54% reductions in rollout fCRPS and up to 30% improvements in VRMSE relative to compute-matched deterministic fine-tuning. For the 640M-parameter HalfWalrus foundation model, the same procedure yields 34–40% CRPS reductions and >13% VRMSE gains on Rayleigh-Bénard and TRL. The method also exhibits test-time scaling: rollout VRMSE decreases monotonically with ensemble size (up to 16 members), providing a controllable accuracy–compute trade-off at inference.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is compute-matched deterministic fine-tuning (same number of training steps and samples, but optimising MAE/MSE instead of CRPS). CRPS retrofitting wins on rollout fCRPS in every single configuration tested (20–54% reduction), confirming that the probabilistic objective consistently improves calibration. On VRMSE, the gains are largest for Walrus (up to 29.6% on Euler) and Lola (up to 21.5% on RB), where the deterministic baselines have already plateaued; for Poseidon and Walrus on Shear Flow, VRMSE improvements are smaller or statistically indistinguishable from zero because those deterministic backbones were still improving at the comparison point. For the HalfWalrus foundation model, CRPS retrofitting outperforms deterministic fine-tuning by 34–40% in CRPS and >13% in VRMSE on RB and TRL, though on the non-chaotic Viscoelastic steady/transition regimes the VRMSE slightly degrades (while CRPS still improves). The paper also ablates against deep ensembles (four independently fine-tuned deterministic copies) and diffusion-based retrofitting, confirming that the gains are driven by the CRPS objective itself rather than merely the presence of an ensemble, and that CRPS retrofitting leverages pre-trained representations more efficiently than converting to a diffusion task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the CRPS-based probabilistic retrofitting strategy from weather forecasting (Alet et al., 2025; Lang et al., 2024b) to the broader PDE simulation domain, demonstrating that the same global-noise-injection-via-AdaLN recipe transfers across three architecturally distinct transformer families (pixel-space, latent-space, and multiscale operator). It departs from the dominant probabilistic PDE paradigm—diffusion and flow-based generative models (Lippe et al., 2024; Rozet et al., 2025; Shysheya et al., 2024)—by arguing that the CRPS objective's proximity to the MAE/MSE losses used in deterministic pre-training makes retrofitting far more efficient than retraining a denoising model from scratch. The AdaLN modulation mechanism is borrowed directly from DiT (Peebles & Xie, 2023), and the fair CRPS estimator follows Alet et al. (2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the PDE modelling community invests heavily in large deterministic foundation models (Walrus, Poseidon, DPOT, PhysicsX), this work offers a practical path to unlock probabilistic capabilities without discarding those checkpoints, which is increasingly important as training costs become prohibitive. Key limitations include inheritance of backbone instabilities (e.g., over-smoothing in Poseidon, under-dispersion in latent-space Lola) and the univariate nature of CRPS, which does not guarantee spatial consistency. Open questions include whether multi-step rollout fine-tuning or auxiliary spectral losses can address these architectural biases, and whether the approach scales to 3D or higher-dimensional PDEs where the compute savings would be most impactful.
