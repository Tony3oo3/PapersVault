## Problem

While reasoning models have been extensively studied in math and coding, it remains unclear how specific dataset design choices influence the evolution of reasoning quality, faithfulness, and training stability across the SFT-to-RL pipeline. This paper isolates these effects in chess—a verifiable, sequential decision domain with an efficient engine oracle—where the influence of training interventions can be measured in a controlled setting without the confounds of heavily pre-trained domains.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260928/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a chess board rendered in a visual ASCII grid (chosen over FEN due to tokenizer artifacts that combine characters unevenly across rows) along with a task-specific prompt, and produces outputs ranging from a single UCI-notation move (e.g., e1e2) to a 4–6 ply optimal line terminated by a centipawn delta, a full list of legal moves for a given piece, or a selection from five candidate moves. The use of UCI rather than Standard Algebraic Notation avoids disambiguation errors, and the board omits move-repetition history, making it a Markov-complete state for all evaluation tasks.

## Method

Starting from Qwen2.5 7B-Instruct, the authors construct six theoretically-motivated datasets—spanning direct policy learning (Best Move), n-step bootstrapping with valuations (Best Line), verbalized tree search (Verbalized Alpha-Beta Pruning), factual board QA, rejection-sampled teacher outputs, and guided synthetic explanations—then systematically compare them through SFT (LlamaFactory, full fine-tuning) followed by RL (veRL with Dr. GRPO, Clip-Higher strategy, no KL penalty). The key finding is that training on multi-move trajectories (Best Line) yields comparable downstream performance to single-move prediction (Best Move) but with faithful reasoning and more stable RL dynamics, whereas Best Move produces unfaithful reasoning where the final answer is disconnected from the reasoning trace. They further introduce a 'chess information density' framework combining predictive complexity (fraction of tokens with probability >0.995 after SFT on a 0.5B probe model), accuracy, and chess-token density to explain why dense, high-complexity datasets outperform prose-heavy alternatives.

## Results

The best model (SFT8+SFT9XL, 120M SFT tokens + 16k RL samples) achieves 98% legal move rate and 0.83 average move rank on the Predict Move task, substantially outperforming the base Qwen2.5 7B-Instruct (8% legal, 0.56 rank) and Llama 4 Maverick (42% legal, 0.62 rank). On the Best Move selection task it reaches 62% accuracy (vs. 57% for gpt-oss-120b), and referenced move accuracy (a hallucination proxy) reaches 90% (vs. 70% for gpt-oss-120b). However, it trails gpt-oss-120b on Legal Moves IoU (0.87 vs. 0.96), Worst Move accuracy (64% vs. 79%), and out-of-distribution mate finding (14.8% vs. 78.3%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against gpt-oss-120b (Medium), the 7B model wins on Predict Move average rank (0.83 vs. 0.66), Best Move accuracy (62% vs. 57%), and referenced move accuracy (90% vs. 70%), but loses on Legal Moves IoU (0.87 vs. 0.96), Worst Move accuracy (64% vs. 79%), Factual Board Answering (82.3% vs. 99.5%), and out-of-distribution mate finding (14.8% vs. 78.3%). Against Llama 4 Maverick, the model dominates across nearly all metrics: 98% vs. 42% legal moves, 0.83 vs. 0.62 average rank, 0.87 vs. 0.43 Legal Moves IoU, and 62% vs. 27% Best Move accuracy. Against the base Qwen2.5 7B-Instruct, improvements are dramatic on every axis (e.g., 98% vs. 8% legal moves, 0.87 vs. 0.26 Legal Moves IoU, 90% vs. 12% referenced move accuracy). The paper notes that the gpt-oss-120b advantage on Legal Moves and OOD mates likely reflects its much larger parameter count and broader pretraining, while the 7B model's strength in move quality and hallucination resistance stems from its targeted chess SFT+RL recipe.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of the RL-for-reasoning paradigm (initiated by o1, DeepSeek-R1, Kimi k1.5) and the long-standing observation that LLMs struggle with chess. It adopts Dr. GRPO and DAPO's Clip-Higher strategy as its RL backbone but departs from prior reasoning research by using chess—a verifiable, episodic MDP with an efficient oracle—as a controlled testbed to isolate how SFT data design (single-move vs. multi-move trajectories) shapes reasoning faithfulness through RL. The 'chess information density' framework and the MDP-based dataset taxonomy (policy function vs. world model learning) are novel analytical lenses not present in prior reasoning-model training studies, which have largely focused on math and coding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that direct best-move SFT produces strong but unfaithful reasoning after RL—while multi-move trajectory SFT yields comparable performance with faithful reasoning—offers a practical design principle for reasoning-model training: the structure of SFT data determines whether RL elicits genuine reasoning or post-hoc rationalization. The demonstration that SFT-checkpoint qualitative metrics (hallucination rate, reasoning quality) predict final RL performance provides a useful early-stopping or data-selection signal. However, the study is confined to a single 7B base model and one domain, and the final model still underperforms o3 in full-game play, leaving open whether the faithfulness and information-density findings generalize to larger models or other sequential decision domains.
