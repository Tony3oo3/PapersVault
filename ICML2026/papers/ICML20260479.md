## Problem

Existing AI Scientist benchmarks evaluate LLM agents on research workflows, data analysis, and interactive discovery, but none explicitly incorporate the observational pitfalls—selection bias, hidden confounders, and noisy measurements—that make naive statistical reasoning systematically misleading in real-world scientific discovery. This paper asks whether frontier LLM agents can perform causal thinking: distinguishing genuine causal mechanisms from spurious correlations and actively designing experiments to recover hidden structure.

## Input / Output

The agent receives historical flight records of surviving drones only (introducing survivorship bias), partial environment observations (weather, altitude, hit counts), and a budget of 200 drones across up to 10 deployment calls in Stage 1. It must output a single final DEF allocation across seven drone components (engine, wing, body, cockpit, antenna, camera, gun) plus a natural-language explanation report. Evaluation is two-pronged: a quantitative survival rate on a 1,000-drone fleet in Stage 2 (win threshold ~75%), and a fine-grained rubric score (16 points total) assessed by an LLM judge across causal reasoning, experimental design, reflection quality, and data usage.

## Method

CausalGame instantiates 14 game scenarios as structural causal models (SCMs) where the agent must recover the true data-generating mechanism through active experimentation. Three causal challenges are embedded via the SCM design: selection bias (only surviving drones are observable, creating spurious correlations like the Antenna Trap where protecting the antenna increases detection), hidden confounders (latent variables like EMI level or mission zone jointly drive multiple observed variables, as in the Deployment Zone Trap inspired by Farr's Cholera Paradox), and measurement noise (observation error varying with weather conditions). The agent operates in two execution modes—single-turn Prompting (code execution) and multi-turn Agentic (ReAct with structured tool calling and an exploration guard)—and is evaluated on both survival rate and a rubric-based LLM-as-a-judge assessment. A judge-independent configuration-path analysis complements the rubric by directly examining the sequence of design vectors deployed across rounds to identify behavioral failure patterns such as component lock-in and trap entrapment.

## Results

Across 29 frontier LLMs evaluated on 14 CausalGame scenarios, all models remain significantly below the 75% win threshold and the ~80% optimal survival rate. The best-performing agents (GPT-5.5, GPT-5.5-High) achieve only 68.4% mean survival in Agent mode, while the LLM range spans 57.5%–70.9%. Non-LLM baselines (Default, Random, Uniform High, No-Explore LLM) achieve 49.0%–57.5%, and the OpenCode coding-agent framework improves over ReAct by an average of +6.9% yet still falls far below the win threshold. Removing selection bias yields a +9.5% (Agent) / +7.8% (Prompt) survival gain, confirming that survivorship bias is a primary source of failure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against the OpenCode coding-agent framework, which outperforms the ReAct-based Agentic mode by an average of +6.9% survival rate across 5 models × 14 scenarios × 3 trials, confirming that more capable agentic scaffolding helps but does not close the gap to the 75% win threshold. Against non-LLM baselines, full LLM agents (57.5%–70.9%) outperform Default (49.0%), Random (52.0%), and Uniform High (52.7%), but the No-Explore LLM baseline (52%–63%) can match or exceed several full-agent models on bias-heavy scenarios, underscoring that causal reasoning—not search heuristics—is the bottleneck. Within LLMs, increased reasoning compute does not reliably help: GPT-5.5-XHigh (65.1%) underperforms GPT-5.5 (68.4%), and DeepSeek-V3.2-Think (59.9%) trails its non-thinking counterpart (61.7%). Agentic mode generally benefits top models (e.g., DeepSeek-V4-Pro gains +5.7% over Prompting), though Grok-4.1-Fast is a near-tie (−1.2%). Overall, no evaluated method—LLM or non-LLM—reaches the 75% win threshold, with the best result (68.4%) still 6.6 percentage points short.

## Relation to Prior Work

CausalGame synthesizes ideas from three distinct research threads: (1) AI Scientist benchmarks that evaluate scientific workflow execution (ResearchBench, MLE-Bench, PaperBench) but treat data as given; (2) interactive discovery environments (DiscoveryWorld, BoxingGym, NewtonBench) that let agents design experiments but do not explicitly encode observational pitfalls; and (3) causal inference benchmarks (CausCIBench, Keshmirian et al.) that test causal reasoning from a data-science perspective without replicating the iterative, hypothesis-driven nature of real discovery. The paper's key departure is treating a structural causal model as the game engine and deliberately injecting selection bias, hidden confounders, and measurement noise so that naive correlational strategies are systematically misleading—something no prior benchmark combines with multi-turn interactive experimentation and rubric-based explanation evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides the first controlled, automated testbed for evaluating whether LLM agents can perform active causal discovery under realistic observational imperfections, filling a gap the authors argue has been 'surprisingly neglected' in AI Scientist development. The consistent failure of all 29 frontier models—particularly the finding that 68% of agent sessions show no causal engagement and that increased reasoning compute does not reliably improve performance—sets a clear baseline and motivates future work on causal agent architectures. Limitations include simplified scenarios relative to open-ended discovery, non-controlled cross-mode comparisons, and reliance on LLM-as-a-judge for fine-grained rubric scoring. A promising open direction the authors highlight is using the procedurally generated scenarios for training causal agents, with progress measured jointly on survival rate and causal-reasoning rubric scores.
