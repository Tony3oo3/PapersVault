## Problem

Prevalent multimodal self-supervised learning methods assume substantial task-relevant overlap across modalities (the redundancy assumption), which breaks down for heterogeneous, variable-length real-world data such as healthcare signals. Enforcing strict cross-modal alignment in such settings discards modality-unique information and can inadvertently amplify demographic bias, yet it remains unclear which properties of SSL objectives actually drive fairness improvements under heterogeneity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260700/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

FairSSL takes variable-length multimodal inputs from the same subject (e.g., audio + video vlogs, EEG + audio, chest X-ray + radiology text, or tabular clinical data split column-wise), preprocesses each modality into fixed-length segments, and encodes each segment independently with a modality-specific encoder. The output is a set of per-segment embeddings per modality, from which a single pooled representation is derived for linear probing to produce a binary class prediction (e.g., depressed vs. non-depressed). The segment-based design is what makes the method applicable to modalities with arbitrary temporal length, unlike standard contrastive methods that require fixed-size paired views.

## Method

FairSSL builds on VICReg's variance-invariance-covariance regularization but introduces two key modifications for heterogeneous multimodal data. First, a segment-based pooling strategy splits each variable-length modality into fixed-length segments encoded independently, then applies average pooling to one modality's segment embeddings to produce a single pooled vector that is aligned against each individual segment of the other modality in the invariance term, enabling the model to identify which segments most strongly drive alignment. Second, the invariance term is made subject-aware through four variants (M1–M4): intra-subject alignment (M1), inter-subject alignment across all pairs in a batch (M2), class-conditioned inter-subject alignment (M3), and alternating M2/M3 across epochs (M4) to avoid training bias from same-class batches. The variance and covariance terms are applied per-modality without modification, and theoretical analysis shows the combined objective bounds the statistical parity difference between protected groups via the group mean score disparity and within-group score deviations.

## Results

Across four heterogeneous multimodal datasets, FairSSL consistently achieves the best aggregated fairness score (AGG_F) while maintaining competitive predictive performance. On D-Vlog, FairSSL (M2) reaches AGG_F = 0.86 (vs. 0.73 for the best supervised baseline X-concat and 0.52 for the best contrastive SSL method FACTORCL); on MODMA, FairSSL (M1) achieves AGG_F = 0.90 (vs. 0.76 for EMO-GCN and 0.58 for FACTORCL); on MIMIC-CXR, FairSSL (M3) attains AGG_F = 0.98 with F1 = 0.81; and on MIMIC-III, FairSSL (M2) reaches AGG_F = 0.95 with F1 = 0.27. The key finding is that FairSSL improves fairness by 13–36 percentage points in AGG_F over the strongest baselines without sacrificing accuracy, and in several cases (e.g., D-Vlog M1: F1 = 0.73) even improves predictive performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FairSSL (M2) achieves the best AGG_F of 0.86 on D-Vlog, outperforming the strongest supervised baseline (X-concat, 0.73) by 13 points and the best contrastive SSL method (FACTORCL, 0.52) by 34 points; the VICReg baseline it modifies scores only 0.04, showing the subject-aware invariance term is critical. On MODMA, FairSSL (M1) reaches AGG_F = 0.90, beating the best supervised method (EMO-GCN, 0.76) and the best SSL method (FACTORCL, 0.58), while also matching the best supervised accuracy (MultiDepr, 0.79) with FairSSL (M2) at 0.67. On MIMIC-CXR, FairSSL (M3) achieves AGG_F = 0.98 with F1 = 0.81, marginally surpassing CoMM/CLIP (0.96) and the VICReg baseline (0.94) while also attaining the highest F1. On MIMIC-III, FairSSL (M2) leads with AGG_F = 0.95 and F1 = 0.27, edging out SimCLR (0.94, F1 = 0.18) and the VICReg baseline (0.88, F1 = 0.11). Across all datasets, contrastive SSL methods (CoMM, FOCAL, QUEST, CLIP) consistently underperform on fairness (AGG_F ≤ 0.52 on D-Vlog, ≤ 0.58 on MODMA), confirming the paper's hypothesis that strict cross-modal alignment amplifies bias in heterogeneous settings. The redundancy-reduction baselines (DeCUR, VICReg) show intermediate fairness but still trail FairSSL by 10–30+ AGG_F points, indicating that subject-aware pooling and intra/inter-subject regularization provide a distinct advantage over generic modality-decoupling strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

FairSSL builds directly on VICReg's variance-invariance-covariance regularization objective, modifying the invariance term to be subject-aware and introducing segment-based pooling to handle variable-length modalities. It departs from the dominant contrastive multimodal SSL paradigm (CLIP, CoMM, QUEST, FACTORCL) that assumes substantial cross-modal redundancy, and from redundancy-reduction methods (DeCUR, FOCAL) that decompose shared vs. unique components but do not explicitly target fairness. The paper also extends the line of fair SSL work (Yfantidou et al., 2024; Barker et al., 2024) from unimodal or tabular settings to heterogeneous, non-tabular, variable-length multimodal data, and provides theoretical bounds linking the regularization terms to group fairness measures under a linear-probe setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and empirically confirms a non-trivial gap: multimodal SSL does not automatically improve fairness over supervised multimodal learning, and in class-balanced settings can actually worsen it. This reframes how practitioners should think about the performance-fairness trade-off in multimodal SSL and motivates explicit fairness-aware objectives rather than relying on alignment alone. Open questions include whether the theoretical bounds (scoped to linear probing) hold under non-linear fine-tuning, whether the subject-aware strategy generalizes beyond two modalities, and whether the modest fairness gains on large-scale datasets like MIMIC-CXR translate into clinically meaningful improvements. The authors also note that fairness improvements may not generalize across institutions or demographic settings, underscoring the need for external validation before deployment in high-stakes domains.
