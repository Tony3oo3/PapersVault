## Problem

Differentiable logic gate networks map directly to gate-level circuits for ultra-low-latency FPGA inference, but their logic footprint often exceeds target device capacity, and no post-training mechanism exists to trade complexity for accuracy in this setting. Naively extending classical pruning saliency criteria (Taylor-based, Hessian-based) to unit tying is unreliable because tying enforces a near-discrete transition from an input-dependent operator to a constant, violating the small-perturbation assumption that underlies local approximations.

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained convolutional differentiable logic gate network (LogicTreeNet variants for CIFAR-10 and MNIST) and a small calibration set (16 samples for screening, 80 for refinement). It produces a simplified network where a fixed fraction of units per layer are tied to constant-0 or constant-1, enabling constant propagation and downstream logic elimination during synthesis. The final output is a Verilog netlist whose post-synthesis LUT count fits within a target FPGA budget, with the tied ratio serving as the control knob between accuracy and area.

## Method

The paper proposes a two-stage unit tying algorithm under a teacher-referenced logit-distortion objective (MSE between tied-network and original-network logits). Stage-A performs fast Gauss-Newton screening: it approximates the distortion induced by tying each unit via a Hessian-free quadratic form computed from Jacobian-vector products, then selects an overshoot set of size T+k with the lowest scores. Stage-B refines this set by repeatedly applying a Binary Split procedure (inspired by group testing) that isolates the single most harmful unit using only O(log|S|) finite-difference distortion evaluations per removal, correcting both approximation errors and non-additive interaction effects among simultaneously tied units. The key insight is that the discrete nature of tying makes per-unit Taylor saliency unreliable, so the method combines a cheap approximate screen with a small number of exact subset-level evaluations to achieve better accuracy-area trade-offs than either approach alone.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 (M) (original accuracy 71.57%), the proposed two-stage method achieves 61.44% top-1 accuracy at 50% tied ratio, outperforming FD-DL (56.05%), Fisher-TL (53.97%), and ConstProb (24.73%). On MNIST (M) (original 98.91%), the method reaches 96.20% at 50% tied, versus 92.18% (FD-DL), 84.69% (Fisher-TL), and 31.18% (ConstProb). With a single post-tying fine-tuning run, the method recovers to 69.22% on CIFAR-10 (M) and 98.74% on MNIST (M) at 50% tied. Hardware synthesis shows up to 48.33% LUT reduction on CIFAR-10 (M) and 42.88% on MNIST (M) at 50% tied, enabling deployment on FPGAs where the original model is out-of-capacity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three baselines: ConstProb (magnitude-analogue), Fisher-TL (empirical-Fisher second-order proxy from Theis et al., 2018), and FD-DL (per-unit finite-difference distortion evaluation). The proposed two-stage method consistently outperforms all three baselines without fine-tuning across all tied ratios on both datasets. At the most aggressive 50% tied setting, the gap is largest: on CIFAR-10 (M), Ours achieves 61.44% vs. 56.05% (FD-DL), 53.97% (Fisher-TL), and 24.73% (ConstProb); on MNIST (M), 96.20% vs. 92.18%, 84.69%, and 31.18% respectively. The Fisher-TL baseline (Theis et al., 2018) degrades substantially at higher tying ratios, confirming that task-loss-based second-order saliency is less reliable for discrete tying than the teacher-referenced distortion objective. With fine-tuning, the method also leads: at 50% tied on CIFAR-10 (M), Ours w/FT (69.22%) edges out Fisher-TL w/FT (68.92%) and ConstProb w/FT (66.78%), while on MNIST (M) it reaches 98.74% vs. 98.54% and 95.12%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the second-order saliency framework from classical pruning (Optimal Brain Damage/Surgeon) and the function-preservation distortion objective from channel pruning (He et al., 2017; Frantar & Alistarh, 2023) to a fundamentally different structural primitive: unit tying in differentiable logic gate networks (Petersen et al., 2022, 2024). The key conceptual departure is recognizing that tying a gate to a constant is a near-discrete intervention that violates the small-perturbation assumption underlying Taylor-based saliency, motivating a two-stage design that combines a fast Gauss-Newton screen (Hessian-free, via Jacobian-vector products) with a binary-split refinement using exact finite-difference evaluations. The Fisher-pruning baseline (Theis et al., 2018) is repurposed as a task-loss-based comparison point, highlighting that the teacher-referenced distortion objective is more reliable than task-loss saliency for this discrete setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic study of post-training complexity control for differentiable logic gate networks, a model class whose direct FPGA mapping makes capacity overruns a hard feasibility constraint rather than a soft performance penalty. The demonstrated 48% LUT reduction (CIFAR-10) and 43% (MNIST) with modest accuracy loss provides a practical knob for meeting diverse FPGA budgets, and the finding that standard second-order pruning criteria are unreliable under tying has broader implications for any compression method involving discrete structural edits. Limitations include evaluation only on CIFAR-10 and MNIST with small-to-medium models, and the approach is specific to the logic-gate-network architecture; open questions include scalability to deeper/wider logic networks, applicability to other discrete-architecture model families, and whether the two-stage design generalizes to other near-discrete compression primitives.
