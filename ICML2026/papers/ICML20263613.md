## Problem

Current safety alignment methods for language models rely on sequential adversarial training—alternating between training an Attacker to generate jailbreaks and fine-tuning a Defender to resist them—which suffers from the inefficiencies and instabilities inherent in alternating optimization. Moreover, existing game-theoretic approaches either impose a zero-sum structure that misrepresents the asymmetric objectives of attacker and defender, or use self-play with shared parameters that entangles gradients and encourages degenerate solutions. This paper asks whether a joint, non-cooperative, non-zero-sum game formulation with preference-based supervision can produce a Defender that is simultaneously more helpful and more robust.

## Input / Output

The Attacker receives a seed query s together with its class label c ∈ {harmful, benign} and outputs a rephrased adversarial prompt x that preserves the original intent while attempting to elicit an undesired response. The Defender receives only the adversarial prompt x (without the class label or seed) and must produce a safe response—either a deflection (safe semantically adjacent information) for harmful queries or a compliant, helpful answer for benign ones. A separate faithfulness judge verifies that the Attacker's prompt has not drifted from the seed's meaning, and pairwise preference judges (compliance or deflection) produce binary winner/loser decisions rather than scalar scores, which the paper argues is more robust to reward hacking.

## Method

AdvGame jointly trains a separate Attacker LM ρ and Defender LM π (no parameter sharing) as a non-zero-sum, non-cooperative game, where each agent optimizes its own KL-regularized objective against the other's evolving policy. The key design choice is a reward-swapping scheme: on benign seeds the Attacker is rewarded for eliciting deflection while the Defender is rewarded for compliance, and vice versa on harmful seeds—this prevents the degenerate zero-sum solution where the Attacker simply produces gibberish. Training uses online DPO or IPO losses derived from the closed-form KL-regularized optimum, with a Bradley-Terry preference model instantiated by an LLM judge that compares two responses (or two prompt-response pairs) and returns a binary preference. For stability, data generation uses exponential moving average (EMA) models as off-policy samplers rather than the current on-policy weights, and the Attacker is trained concurrently rather than kept fixed, ensuring the Defender faces an increasingly diverse and challenging attack distribution throughout training.

## Results

On Qwen2.5-7B, AdvGame-DPO-MD reduces HarmBench adversarial ASR from 31.6% (Original) to 4.7% and WildJailbreak ASR from 69.6% to 8.5%, while preserving MMLU at 71.8 and actually improving ArenaHard from 55.5 to 61.3. On Llama3.1-8B, it cuts HarmBench ASR from 25.0% to 7.4% and WJB ASR from 58.6% to 6.4%, with MMLU preserved at 69.1 and ArenaHard improved from 33.6 to 41.0. Against adaptive attacks (PAIR, TAP, GCG), AdvGame-DPO-MD achieves ASR of 7.2/10.0/25.3 on Qwen and 9.1/12.8/24.4 on Llama, substantially outperforming Self-RedTeam (37.2/40.3/23.4 and 23.7/34.5/25.3 respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Self-RedTeam (Liu et al., 2025a), the primary baseline, AdvGame-DPO-MD achieves substantially lower ASR on nearly all safety benchmarks: on Qwen, 4.7% vs. 16.8% on HarmBench and 8.5% vs. 41.1% on WJB; on Llama, 7.4% vs. 14.9% on HarmBench and 6.4% vs. 11.5% on WJB. Critically, AdvGame preserves or improves utility (e.g., ArenaHard 61.3 vs. 52.2 on Qwen; 41.0 vs. 13.6 on Llama), whereas Self-RedTeam causes significant utility drops (Llama MMLU 64.8 vs. 69.3 Original; AlpacaEval2 13.1 vs. 29.9). Against adaptive attacks, AdvGame-DPO-MD is far more robust: PAIR ASR drops to 7.2% (Qwen) and 9.1% (Llama) versus 37.2% and 23.7% for Self-RedTeam. The paper also compares against LifelongSA (Wang et al., 2025), Circuit Breakers/RR (Zou et al., 2024), and LAT (Sheshadri et al., 2024) in the appendix (Table 9), where AdvGame-DPO-MD achieves the best safety-utility tradeoff, with LifelongSA showing the strongest safety but the largest utility degradation. The trained Attacker also matches or exceeds dedicated adaptive attack methods (PAIR, TAP, GCG) as a red-teaming tool, achieving 55.6% ASR on Qwen without any iterative search.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adversarial safety-alignment line initiated by Self-RedTeam (Liu et al., 2025a) by replacing its zero-sum, parameter-shared self-play formulation with a non-zero-sum, non-cooperative game between two independently trained LMs. The training objectives are derived from the DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) and IPO/IPO-MD (Azar et al., 2024; Calandriello et al., 2024) preference-optimization frameworks, with the EMA-based off-policy generation borrowed from Nash-MD (Munos et al., 2024). The key conceptual departure is the reward-swapping mechanism for the Attacker (rewarding deflection on benign queries and compliance on harmful ones), which avoids the degenerate gibberish equilibrium of a naive zero-sum inversion, and the use of pairwise LLM judges in place of point-wise scalar rewards to mitigate reward hacking.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AdvGame shifts safety post-training from a brittle sequential cat-and-mouse loop to a joint online game, yielding a Defender that is substantially more robust to adaptive black-box attacks (PAIR, TAP) while preserving utility, and a reusable Attacker that matches dedicated red-teaming methods without any special iterative search. The ablation showing that pairwise judges outperform point-wise judges on actual safety (despite worse validation reward curves) highlights a practical reward-hacking vulnerability in scalar-reward RLHF that the field has underappreciated. Limitations include the exclusion of non-readable attack sequences (e.g., GCG), a significant compliance drop for Llama on WJB-benign (98.8→69.9), and reliance on a large judge model; open questions include whether better reward-model design can close the GRPO gap and whether the game formulation extends to multi-turn or agentic attack settings.
