## Problem

In online fair division with indivisible goods, strong impossibility results rule out finite approximations to EF1 and MMS, but the approximability of the weaker proportionality-up-to-one-good (PROP1) benchmark remained unresolved. The paper asks whether any nontrivial PROP1 approximation is achievable under irrevocable online decisions with only weak predictive side-information (maximum item value predictions), and characterizes the boundary between what is and is not achievable under adaptive versus non-adaptive adversaries.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives indivisible goods one at a time in an unknown-length sequence, with each agent's additive valuation for the current good revealed upon arrival; the total number of goods m is not known in advance. Optionally, the algorithm is given per-agent MIV predictions (a single scalar per agent estimating the maximum value that agent assigns to any good in the sequence), with one-sided error bounded by a factor of 1/(1−ε). The output is an immediate, irrevocable assignment of each arriving good to exactly one agent, accompanied by a worst-case or high-probability PROP1 approximation guarantee.

## Method

The paper establishes three complementary results. First, it proves that three natural greedy allocation rules (max-value, min-ratio, and min-PROP1-gap) all fail to guarantee any positive PROP1 approximation against an adaptive adversary, via explicit counterexample constructions. Second, it shows that uniform random allocation achieves Θ(1/log(n/δ))-PROP1 with probability at least 1−δ against a non-adaptive adversary, using Bernstein's inequality on the sum of independent bounded terms, with a matching lower bound; in the 'small items' regime (each good's value ≤ 3ε²/(8 log(n/δ)) · v_i(G)/n), the guarantee improves to (1−ε)-PROP1. Third, it designs a learning-augmented online algorithm that, given MIV predictions, achieves 1/n-PROP1 even against adaptive adversaries by maintaining a per-agent potential function that tracks proximity to the 1/n-PROP1 violation threshold and allocating each good to the agent whose assignment minimizes the global potential; a graceful degradation theorem then converts any α-PROP1 algorithm for perfect predictions into one achieving α(1−ε)/(1−αε/n)-PROP1 under one-sided error ε.

## Results

Theoretically, Algorithm 1 (with MIV predictions) guarantees 1/n-PROP1 against adaptive adversaries, and degrades gracefully to β = (1−ε)/(n − ε/n)-PROP1 under one-sided prediction error ε. Against non-adaptive adversaries, uniform random allocation achieves α = Θ(1/log(n/δ))-PROP1 with probability ≥ 1−δ, and (1−ε)-PROP1 in the small-items regime. Empirically (n=8, m=40, 500 trials), Algorithm 1 achieves PROP1 ratio 1.0 on all four random instance families with normalized welfare 0.781–0.960, while Greedy-1 attains higher welfare (0.946–0.967) but drops to PROP1 ratios as low as 0.423 on dense instances. On the adversarial counterexamples from Section 3, Algorithm 1 maintains PROP1 ratios of 0.920–1.000, whereas the targeted greedy baselines collapse to 0.008–0.385.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Algorithm 1 (MIV-prediction-based) against three natural greedy baselines: Greedy-1 (allocate to highest-valuing agent), Greedy-2 (allocate to lowest current share), and Greedy-3 (allocate to minimize PROP1 violation). On benign random instances, Algorithm 1 achieves perfect PROP1 (ratio 1.0) across all four families, while Greedy-1 trades fairness for welfare—reaching 0.946–0.967 welfare but dropping to PROP1 ratios of 0.423–0.775 on competitive (uniform, dense, correlated) instances. Greedy-2 and Greedy-3 maintain near-PROP1 fairness on random instances but suffer substantially lower welfare (0.300–0.770 vs. 0.781–0.960 for Algorithm 1). On the explicit adversarial counterexamples constructed in Section 3, the targeted greedy strategies collapse to PROP1 ratios of 0.008 (Greedy-1, Greedy-2) and 0.385 (Greedy-3), while Algorithm 1 retains ratios of 0.920–1.000, confirming the theoretical impossibility results for greedy methods against adaptive adversaries.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the online fair division model studied by Neoh et al. (2025) and Zhou et al. (2023), which assumed stronger predictive information (normalization/total-value knowledge), by showing that much weaker MIV predictions suffice for a finite PROP1 approximation. It also builds on the learning-augmented algorithm framework (Lykouris & Vassilvitskii, 2021) and the offline analysis of random allocation by Lipton et al. (2004), but is the first to establish multiplicative PROP1 guarantees in the online setting without structural assumptions. The paper departs from prior work by demonstrating that standard greedy heuristics—pervasive in both theory and deployed allocation systems—fundamentally fail for PROP1 under adaptive adversaries, a gap not addressed in earlier impossibility results that focused on EF1 and MMS.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work resolves a key open question in online fair division by identifying PROP1 as the strongest proportionality-type notion that remains approximable under weak predictive information, while EF1, MMS, and PROPX are shown to be inapproximable even with perfect MIV predictions. The 1/n-PROP1 guarantee via a potential-function algorithm with graceful degradation under one-sided prediction error provides a practical template for ML systems that must make irrevocable allocation decisions with noisy forecasts. A central open question remains: whether any nontrivial PROP1 approximation is achievable without any predictive or structural assumptions, which would determine whether fairness in online allocation is fundamentally prediction-dependent.
