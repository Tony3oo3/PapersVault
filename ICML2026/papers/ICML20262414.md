## Problem

In RL fine-tuning of LLMs with GRPO, homogeneous rollout allocation (e.g., N=8 for every prompt) wastes compute: easy prompts produce all-positive groups with zero gradient, while hard prompts produce all-negative groups that also yield zero gradient. The paper asks how to distribute a fixed global rollout budget across prompts to maximize the fraction of rollouts that produce useful learning signal, a resource-allocation question that is complementary to and distinct from prompt selection or filtering.

## Input / Output

The method takes a mini-batch of M prompts, a fixed total rollout budget N_total = M×N, and per-prompt success-rate estimates p̂_i (bootstrapped from the first homogeneous epoch, then updated online with a 16-sample buffer for smoothing). It outputs heterogeneous integer rollout budgets {N_1,…,N_M} bounded by N_low and N_up, solved by a greedy priority-queue knapsack solver in under 1 second. The rest of the pipeline—rollout generation, binary reward evaluation, and GRPO policy update—remains unchanged, so the only modification is the number of generation requests dispatched per prompt.

## Method

Knapsack RL formulates rollout allocation as a discrete knapsack problem where each prompt-budget pair is an item with unit cost and a learning value V(N,p) = (1 − p^N − (1−p)^N)·p(1−p)^2. The first factor is the probability that N samples yield a mixed-outcome (non-zero-gradient) group; the second is a first-order Taylor approximation of the expected success-rate increase (InfoGain), which is asymmetric and assigns higher value to harder prompts (p < 0.5). Proposition 3 proves V is discrete-concave in N with a hard-task bias, so Proposition 4 shows a greedy water-filling algorithm—repeatedly assigning the next rollout to the prompt with the largest marginal value ΔV—is globally optimal for the unit-cost case. A fallback strategy redistributes surplus budget from saturated prompts (p̂=1) to unsolved prompts (p̂=0) to prevent degenerate allocations, and the entire allocation step adds negligible overhead (<1 s) to the per-iteration wall-clock time.

## Results

Under the same total rollout budget (N_total = 2048 per iteration), Knapsack-GRPO improves the average benchmark score by 2–4 points across all four model settings: +3.8 for DPSK-R1-Distill-1.5B (45.9→49.7), +1.9 for Qwen3-4B-Base (43.2→45.1), +2.7 for Qwen3-4B-Instruct (59.2→61.9), and +2.3 for Qwen2.5-Math-7B (45.2→47.5). Peak per-benchmark gains reach 9.1 points (AMC, Qwen3-4B-Base) and 6.4 points (AIME, DPSK-R1-Distill-1.5B). The effective-gradient ratio (EGR) increases by up to 40%, and achieving comparable performance with homogeneous allocation requires roughly 2× more rollout compute.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is homogeneous GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), which assigns a fixed N=8 rollouts to every prompt. Knapsack-GRPO wins on the average score in all four model settings under the identical total rollout budget: +3.8 (DPSK-R1-Distill-1.5B), +1.9 (Qwen3-4B-Base), +2.7 (Qwen3-4B-Instruct), and +2.3 (Qwen2.5-Math-7B). The largest single-benchmark gain is +9.1 on AMC for Qwen3-4B-Base (56.9→66.0), and the method also improves GPQA (out-of-domain) by up to +5.5 points (Qwen3-4B-Instruct: 54.4→59.9). The second baseline is REINFORCE++ (Hu et al., 2025); applying the same knapsack allocation to REINFORCE++ raises the average from 39.3 to 41.1 (+1.8), with notable gains on MINERVA (+6.6) and OLYMPIAD (+4.5), though AIME drops slightly (−1.3). Overall, the paper demonstrates that the allocation strategy is algorithm-agnostic and consistently outperforms homogeneous allocation without increasing total compute.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) as its base policy optimizer, extending its fixed-group-size estimator to support variable group sizes. It positions itself as complementary to the growing line of prompt-selection and difficulty-filtering methods (DAPO, dynamic sampling, curriculum learning) by addressing a different axis: not which prompts to train on, but how many rollouts each selected prompt should receive. The core technical contribution repurposes classical knapsack/resource-allocation optimization (Hurwicz, 1973; Pisinger & Toth, 1998) for the LLM RL setting, where rollout generation is the constrained resource, and proves greedy optimality under the proposed value function.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that homogeneous GRPO wastes over 40% of rollout compute on zero-gradient groups, and that a simple knapsack reallocation recovers this waste to yield 2–4 point average gains (up to 9 points) with the same budget, is practically significant for anyone scaling RL fine-tuning. The 2× compute-saving claim means practitioners can either halve their rollout budget or double their effective training signal. Open questions include: whether the binary-reward assumption generalizes to continuous-reward RLHF, whether the epoch-level success-rate estimation remains accurate under rapid policy shifts, and how to handle the ~20% of prompts that remain extremely hard and may require trajectory replay or tree-based exploration rather than more independent samples.
