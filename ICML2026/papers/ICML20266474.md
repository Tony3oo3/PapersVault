## Problem

Contemporary AI paradigms—Human-Centered AI, Responsible AI, AI for Social Good, and AI safety—remain fundamentally human-centered and insufficient for addressing planetary-scale challenges characterized by systemic risk, non-stationarity, and deep uncertainty. The paper diagnoses five recurring failure mechanisms (technical misalignment with wicked problems, narrow value framing, impact blind spots in entangled systems, risk amplification via rebound effects, and absence of shared global direction) that arise when AI is deployed in coupled socio-ecological contexts without explicit attention to system-level dynamics.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, PCAI does not process data inputs but reorients the AI lifecycle itself. It takes as analytical input the full AI pipeline (problem formulation, model design, evaluation, deployment) situated within coupled socio-ecological systems, and produces a design philosophy with a two-dimensional wickedness diagnostic (knowledge certainty × value consensus), trajectory-oriented evaluation protocols, and concrete operational recommendations spanning researchers, institutions, funders, conferences, and governance bodies.

## Method

PCAI is a design philosophy grounded in systems thinking that reshapes each stage of the AI lifecycle. At problem-setting, it mandates system mapping and explicit theories of change as design preconditions, requiring researchers to document causal accounts of how model outputs influence system dynamics and identify leverage points before modeling begins. At model design, it prioritizes robustness under deep uncertainty, non-stationarity, and regime shifts, reframing AI's role from prediction and control toward foresight through simulation, exploratory modeling, and scenario analysis (e.g., interpretable multi-agent RL and mixture-of-experts couplings with Earth-system models). At evaluation, it introduces trajectory-oriented evaluation using Pareto frontiers to surface trade-offs across efficiency, equity, resilience, and environmental impact, supplemented by systemic risk probes for rebound effects and counterfactual baselines against no-AI alternatives. At deployment, it reframes operation as a sustained, monitorable, and revisable intervention with predefined escalation, modification, and rollback pathways triggered by system-level risk indicators. The paper advances a falsifiable claim: in domains governed by wicked dynamics, AI systems optimized for narrow objectives without explicit consideration of systemic feedbacks are more likely to exacerbate than mitigate systemic instability.

## Results

This is a position paper with no empirical experiments or quantitative benchmarks. Its key quantitative evidence is drawn from cited literature: only 16–26% of AI ethics guidelines explicitly address non-human life or ecological systems; autonomous electric vehicles may emit ~8% more lifecycle GHG than non-autonomous counterparts despite a 21% per-mile operational reduction; induced travel demand from autonomy ranges from 2% to 47% increases in household vehicle miles; and a sensitivity analysis of the Earth4All model shows that just 6 of 21 proposed global policies account for most system-level improvement. The paper's central contribution is a falsifiable hypothesis: AI systems optimized for narrow objectives without explicit systemic feedback consideration are more likely to exacerbate than mitigate systemic instability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark PCAI against competing methods experimentally; instead, it positions PCAI against four alternative framings in Section 4. Against Human-Centered AI (HCAI), PCAI argues that anthropocentric framing treats environmental and systemic risks only as indirect proxies for human harm, delaying risk recognition; it cites Vinuesa et al. (2020) showing AI may inhibit 35% of SDG targets while enabling 79%, with target interactions poorly characterised. Against AI Safety/Alignment, PCAI concedes the importance of internal alignment but argues it targets a different risk class: well-aligned systems deployed in socio-ecological contexts can still amplify crises through rebound effects and feedback loops. Against the view that systemic reasoning is outside AI's scope, PCAI points to field evidence (Adams et al., 2026) where 25 GenAI deployments in LMICs converged on socio-technical, system-mapping approaches organically. Against technological progress as primary solution, PCAI cites Toyama (2010, 2011) showing technology acts as a multiplier on pre-existing conditions, with the US poverty rate unchanged since 1970 despite four decades of digital innovation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes and reorients several existing intellectual threads—systems thinking (Meadows), the wicked/super-wicked problem distinction (Rittel & Webber; Levin et al.), Human-Centered AI (Shneiderman), AI Safety (Russell), and Integrated Assessment Models—into a unified design philosophy called Planet-Centered AI (PCAI). It departs from HCAI by shifting the unit of analysis from individual users and communities to coupled socio-ecological systems and their long-run trajectories, and from AI Safety by reframing the central question from 'does the system behave as intended?' to 'does the intended behavior, enacted at scale, contribute to stable planetary trajectories?' The paper explicitly positions PCAI as complementary to rather than replacing existing frameworks, while arguing that the systems perspective is currently 'absent from AI frameworks' and that this absence becomes consequential under polycrisis conditions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central contribution is a falsifiable claim—that AI systems optimized for narrow objectives without explicit systemic feedback consideration are more likely to exacerbate than mitigate systemic instability—which, if empirically validated, would fundamentally restructure evaluation and deployment practices across the AI lifecycle. Key open questions include whether the proposed diagnostics (wickedness classification, system mapping, theories of change) can be operationalized at scale, whether trajectory-oriented evaluation and Pareto-front assessment are feasible in practice, and how PCAI principles interact with the pace and incentives of current AI research. As a position paper, it offers no empirical validation of its core claim, and the operationalization gap between the proposed lifecycle requirements and current research practice remains substantial.
