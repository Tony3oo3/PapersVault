## Problem

A counterintuitive 'small-vs-large gap' has been observed where repeating on fewer samples leads to faster convergence than using a larger dataset under a fixed compute budget, yet prior theories (CSQ-SQ separation, gradient variance reduction, biased input distributions) fail to explain the phenomenon in key settings such as full-batch gradient descent and discrete tasks. This paper identifies the underlying mechanism and provides both a theoretical characterization and broad empirical validation.

## Input / Output

The method takes synthetic tasks (sparse parity, single-index model, in-context linear regression, modular addition) with a tunable dataset size N as the key variable, and trains 2-layer MLPs (ReLU, SGD) or Transformers (AdamW, optional QK-norm) under mini-batch or full-batch updates. The primary output is a convergence rate bound showing total steps scale as (Nd)^{1/4}·log(d/ε) under a 2-phase schedule, plus empirical measurements of layer-wise norm ratios ‖a‖₂/‖W‖_F that reveal the mechanism. A notable feature is that the speedup persists even when Phase 1 uses random labels, isolating sampling bias from task signal.

## Method

The core insight is that smaller datasets induce stronger sampling biases in the empirical moment matrix M̂ (deviating from the population M by O(N^{-1/2}) rather than O(1/d)), which accelerates growth of the output-layer weight a relative to the input-layer weight w, effectively acting as an implicit layer-wise learning-rate adjustment that speeds up feature learning. Theoretically, a 2-phase analysis on a quadratic neuron for 2-sparse parity shows Phase 1 (small dataset) grows |a| at rate N^{-1/2} in O(√N) steps, while Phase 2 (full population) converges w via power iteration in O(1/a*) steps, yielding total O((Nd)^{1/4} log(d/ε)) steps that decrease with N. Empirically, the paper confirms the mechanism through interventions: training Phase 1 on random labels reproduces the speedup, layer-wise learning-rate or initialization-scale adjustments close the gap, and QK normalization in Transformers has nuanced effects that partially replicate or disrupt the bias-driven acceleration.

## Results

For (20,6)-sparse parity with full-batch GD on a 2-layer MLP, training on N=2^14 samples converges in ~1500 steps while the full population (N=2^20) requires >2000 steps, yielding a ~100× compute speedup (accounting for per-step cost). Theorem 1 establishes that 2-phase training on a small set of size N achieves total convergence in O((Nd)^{1/4} log(d/ε) log^{1/2}(d/δ)) steps, which decreases as N shrinks. The small-vs-large gap persists even when Phase 1 uses random labels (Corollary 2), confirming that sampling bias—not task signal—drives the acceleration. For Transformers, increasing the QK learning rate to 3.6× the global LR (large-set) or 2.5× (small-set 100-epoch) substantially narrows the gap, and QK normalization nearly closes it for parity and SIM but hurts ICL and modular addition.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between training on a small repeated subset versus the full population under matched compute (batch size × steps). Small-set training consistently outperforms full-population training across all four tasks (sparse parity, SIM, ICL linear regression, modular addition), both architectures (MLP, Transformer), and both update modes (mini-batch SGD/AdamW, full-batch GD). The gap is not explained by prior theories: it persists under full-batch GD (ruling out CSQ-SQ and gradient-variance explanations) and survives removal of input-distribution biases (ruling out biased-distribution accounts). Training with random labels in Phase 1 reproduces the speedup, isolating sampling bias as the mechanism. Layer-wise interventions—scaling initialization (e.g., 8× QK scale for large-set Transformers) or using per-layer learning rates (η₁ ≫ η₂ for MLPs)—can close the gap entirely, confirming that the small-set advantage is an implicit norm-growth preconditioner. The gap widens with model depth (exponential percolation) and task complexity, but narrows with model width (approaching the kernel regime), suggesting it is specific to the feature-learning regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on the 'small-vs-large gap' observed in multi-epoch training [7, 9, 3, 19, 16], where repeating on fewer samples accelerates learning. Prior explanations invoked the CSQ-SQ statistical query gap for single-index models, gradient variance reduction, or biased input distributions, but the authors show these are insufficient—particularly for full-batch gradient descent and discrete tasks like sparse parity. The paper's key departure is identifying dataset sampling bias as the primary mechanism, which adjusts relative layer norms and effectively acts as an implicit layer-wise learning rate, connecting to the broader literature on μP parameterization and feature learning [29, 30, 31].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the speedup persists even with random labels is a strong mechanistic result: it isolates the optimization-dynamics explanation (layer norm growth) from any task-relevant signal, suggesting that data repetition can serve as an implicit 'optimization-shaper' independent of data quality. This motivates training pipelines that explicitly separate an early small-data 'preconditioning' phase from a later full-data 'coverage' phase, and raises open questions about whether the effect transfers to real-world LLM training (the authors note it is unlikely for free-form text but potentially relevant for structured reasoning tasks). Limitations include the theoretical analysis being restricted to 2-sparse parity with quadratic activation, and the nuanced role of QK normalization remaining unexplained.
