## Problem

While scaling laws provide clear compute-allocation prescriptions for LLM pre-training, no analogous guidance exists for RL post-training, where exploration and optimization are tightly coupled. Given a base model, a problem distribution, and a fixed sampling compute budget, practitioners lack a concrete recipe for how to split compute across parallel rollouts per problem, problems per batch, and sequential update steps to maximize downstream performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260693/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a base LLM, a fixed prompt dataset with binary (0/1) outcome rewards, and a total sampling compute budget C measured in rollouts (C = B_p · n · M). It produces compute-optimal allocation rules — specifically a sigmoidal fit for the optimal number of parallel rollouts n*(C) as a function of budget, along with recommended ranges for B_p and M. Compute is measured in rollouts rather than tokens, since response length is not known a priori; the paper verifies that token-based compute yields the same qualitative scaling trend.

## Method

The paper first establishes a 'healthy' RL recipe (GRPO with difficulty-dependent KL/entropy regularization and √B learning-rate scaling) to ensure stable training dynamics across compute budgets, then exhaustively sweeps (B_p, n, M) configurations under fixed total compute to extract compute-optimal frontiers via record-breaking validation reward points. The key finding is that the optimal n follows a sigmoidal scaling law with compute: it increases predictably with budget and then saturates, driven by solution sharpening on easy problems and coverage expansion on hard problems. The paper further shows that scaling n (rather than M) is preferred due to cross-problem interference — more rollouts per problem yield more uniform gradient updates and mitigate the rich-gets-richer dynamics that degrade previously solvable problems. These allocation rules are validated across three base models (Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen3-4B, Llama 3.1-8B), multiple problem distributions, and alternative RL algorithms (PPO, CISPO), with the saturation point shown to depend on model capacity, dataset size, and problem difficulty.

## Results

The compute-optimal number of parallel rollouts n* follows a sigmoidal scaling law with total sampling compute C, saturating at n≈512 on easy problems (avg@16 ∈ [0.3, 0.6]) and at smaller values on hard problems (avg@16 ∈ [0, 0.0625]). On the easy set, varying n produces a 9.2% reward range versus only 1.9% for varying Bp, confirming n as the dominant performance lever; on the hard set, sensitivity to Bp (2.2%) becomes comparable to n (3.1%). Generalization across models and domains confirms the trend: Qwen2.5-7B on Guru-Code improves from 68.7% (n=8) to 73.9% (n=128), and Qwen3-32B on SWE-agent improves from 35.9% (n=8) to 43.5% (n=64).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external methods; instead, it studies how different compute allocations (varying n, Bp, M) of the same on-policy RL algorithm (GRPO, PPO, CISPO) affect performance. The central comparison is between small n (e.g., n=8) and large n (e.g., n=128, 256) under matched total compute: larger n consistently outperforms at high compute budgets across all three base models (Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen3-4B, Llama 3.1-8B) and both easy and hard problem sets. On the easy set, the advantage of larger n is driven by sharpening (worst@4 improves), while on the hard set it is driven by coverage (best@4 improves). Varying Bp within a moderate range (32–1024) has only marginal impact on the easy set (1.9% reward range) but becomes more consequential on the hard set (2.2%), where the optimal Bp–n trade-off is non-monotonic in M. The trend also holds across RL algorithms: PPO on easy problems improves from 58.8% (n=32) to 64.8% (n=128), and CISPO improves from 61.3% (n=32) to 69.4% (n=256), confirming the allocation rule is not specific to GRPO.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of LLM RL scaling studies (Khatri et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2025; BroRL; KnapSackRL) that have each examined a single axis—training duration, model size, or rollout width—in isolation. It departs from this single-axis tradition by formulating compute allocation as a joint constrained optimization over three coupled resources (parallel rollouts n, problem batch Bp, and sequential steps M), building on GRPO's group-normalized advantage mechanism (Shao et al., 2024) and drawing an explicit analogy to Chinchilla-style pre-training scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022). The theoretical grounding in a single-problem bandit analysis (Mei et al., 2023) provides a clean baseline prediction that the empirical results then deliberately contradict, motivating the interference-based explanation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to provide prescriptive, difficulty-aware allocation rules for LLM RL practitioners facing a fixed compute budget, moving the field from descriptive scaling curves to actionable recipes. The finding that the optimal n saturates—and that the saturation point is governed by the interaction between base model capacity, dataset size, and problem difficulty—sets a clear boundary on how much parallel sampling is useful. Key open questions remain: modeling cross-problem interference to predict saturation points a priori, understanding behavior at extremely large batch sizes where both Bp and n are simultaneously large, and extending the framework to continuous-reward or multi-step RL settings beyond binary outcome rewards.
