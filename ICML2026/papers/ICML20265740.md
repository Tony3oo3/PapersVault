## Problem

Extracting executable, interpretable programs from trained Transformer weights is hindered by representation entanglement (superposition), where features occupy overlapping, non-orthogonal directions rather than isolated neurons. Prior algorithm-extraction work (MIPS) was limited to RNNs and relied on post-hoc integer autoencoders to approximate discreteness, restricting it to finite-state, integer-valued tasks. The paper asks whether a Transformer architecture can be designed so that its learned computation graph is natively discrete and directly decomposable into symbolic expressions.

## Input / Output

The model consumes fixed-length sequences of numerical scalars (length 10 by default), spanning both discrete (binary, small integers) and continuous-valued (floating-point physics) tasks. During training it produces a scalar prediction per position via MSE loss; after extraction, the output is a concise, executable Python program that reproduces the model's algorithmic behavior. The residual stream is not a dense vector but an explicit collection of named scalar variables, and the extracted program operates on these variables as named array positions (V0, V1, …) rather than on opaque latent embeddings.

## Method

The Discrete Transformer enforces strict functional disentanglement: Numerical Attention acts solely as a deterministic pointer (routing values across positions via hard attention with Piecewise Linear Encoding of scalar queries/keys), while Numerical MLP sub-modules perform bounded-fan-in (k=2) element-wise arithmetic. A concatenation-based numerical residual stream preserves the full computational history as disentangled coordinates, and a temperature-annealed sampling mechanism (interpolating from Gumbel-Softmax to Sparsemax) gradually crystallizes all selection matrices into one-hot discrete pointers. Extraction proceeds in three stages: (1) hypothesis testing over attention matrices to classify each head as Fixed Offset or Windowed Extrema routing, (2) PySR symbolic regression on each sub-MLP's input-output pairs to recover closed-form arithmetic expressions, and (3) backward traversal from the sparsified linear output head to assemble the full program. This architecture natively handles continuous-valued intermediate computations, extending mechanistic-interpretability-based synthesis beyond the finite-state scope of MIPS.

## Results

On the algorithmic benchmark, the Discrete Transformer achieves 1.00 extraction accuracy on 20 out of 24 discrete tasks, matching the MIPS baseline, with RMSE values typically below 10⁻⁴ (e.g., 8.22×10⁻⁵ on sum_last2, 1.21×10⁻⁶ on parity_last2). Crucially, the Discrete Transformer extends to five continuous-dynamics tasks (exponential_moving_average, free_fall_height, linear_drop, quadratic_drop, damped_harmonic_oscillator) where MIPS is inapplicable, achieving RMSE as low as 4.04×10⁻⁷. The model's training MSE approaches zero on most tasks (e.g., 8.82×10⁻⁹ on sum_last2, 1.62×10⁻¹³ on exponential_moving_average), confirming near-perfect predictive fit before extraction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is MIPS (Michaud et al., 2024), an RNN-based mechanistic-interpretability program synthesis method. On the 24 shared discrete tasks, the Discrete Transformer matches MIPS's 1.00 accuracy on 20 tasks, with the remaining four showing minor gaps: bit_addition (0.99 vs. 1.00), balanced_parenthesis (0.98 vs. †/inapplicable), div_3 (0.67 vs. †), and magnetic (0.02 vs. †). The decisive advantage is scope: MIPS relies on post-hoc integer autoencoders to quantize continuous latent states into finite states, making it fundamentally inapplicable to the five continuous-dynamics tasks (exponential_moving_average, free_fall_height, linear_drop, quadratic_drop, damped_harmonic_oscillator), whereas the Discrete Transformer natively processes real-valued variables and extracts executable programs with RMSE as low as 4.04×10⁻⁷. Thus, the Discrete Transformer ties MIPS on shared discrete tasks while substantially broadening the class of extractable algorithms to include floating-point physical dynamics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the algorithm extraction paradigm established by MIPS (Michaud et al., 2024) from RNNs to Transformers, while departing from the Transformer Programs / Adaptive Transformer Programs line of work (Friedman et al., 2023; Lai-Dang et al., 2025) in both representation and objective: rather than faithful forward decompilation into bounded categorical formalisms, it recovers compact executable algebraic programs via symbolic regression over continuous scalar variables. Architecturally, it builds on the RASP abstraction and Tracr's compiled-Transformer paradigm but introduces a purpose-built Discrete Transformer with a scalar residual stream, temperature-annealed Gumbel-Sparsemax sampling, and strict functional disentanglement between routing (Numerical Attention) and arithmetic (Numerical MLP), replacing MIPS's post-hoc integer autoencoders with native discrete optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Discrete Transformer provides the first principled bridge between continuous Transformer representations and discrete symbolic logic for algorithm extraction, and its ability to handle continuous-valued intermediate computations (e.g., damped harmonic oscillator, exponential moving average) substantially broadens the scope beyond MIPS's finite-state constraint. The controllability demonstrations—steering the model toward alternative but equivalent algorithms by removing MLPs or masking history—position the architecture as a testbed for interpretable algorithm discovery rather than a one-shot extraction tool. Key limitations include the intentional trade-off of expressivity for interpretability (bounded fan-in, router-only attention), evaluation at small benchmark scale, and the open question of whether the approach scales to deeper networks, richer program classes, or tasks requiring broad aggregation over long sequences.
