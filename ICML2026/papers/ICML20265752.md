## Problem

Mainstream Direct-Mapping time series forecasting models learn a unified value-level mapping from history to future, which exhibits systematic response lag at regime switches and trend turning points: predictions continue extrapolating the pre-switch level and trend for several steps, causing error accumulation within the switching window. The root cause is that these models lack an explicit signal to distinguish whether the system is still following the current regime or about to switch, so they cannot update their forecasting basis in a timely manner.

## Input / Output

The model takes a multivariate time series with V feature channels over a lookback window of T timesteps (T=96 for long-term, T=168 for short-term forecasting) and outputs predicted values for H future steps (H ∈ {96, 192, 336, 720} or H=24). The input is first normalized to zero mean and unit variance; the normalized sequence is then split into two parallel branches—the original sequence and a derived L-Context—before being jointly fed into the predictor. The final output is inverse-normalized back to the original scale.

## Method

L-Drive consists of two modules. The L-Context Generator normalizes the input, applies a 1D convolution along time to align cross-channel change delays, computes first-order differences, and passes them through a sigmoid-gated MLP that adaptively retains meaningful increments while suppressing high-frequency noise; the gated difference sequence is then integrated by a GRU to produce a time-evolving L-Context that encodes the current change pattern. The Struct-Aided Predictor concatenates L-Context with the preprocessed sequence, partitions the fused sequence into non-overlapping patches, and introduces patch-shared learnable relative positional basis functions (K bases of length P) to model intra-patch structure without absolute-position memorization; an MLP maps each variable's combined patch-and-position representation, and all patches are merged and projected to the H-step forecast. Theoretically, L-Context acts as an increment-related correction term in the model's local EWMA-like recursion, shifting the residual lag bound from being governed by the model's inertial coefficient ρ to being governed by the increment estimation error ε, with an explicit upper bound of ρ/(1−ρ)·ε̄.

## Results

L-Drive achieves the best MSE/MAE in 12 out of 16 evaluated cases (8 datasets × 2 metrics) for long-term and short-term forecasting. Headline numbers include MSE 0.424 / MAE 0.430 on ETTh1, MSE 0.169 / MAE 0.266 on ECL, and MSE 0.168 / MAE 0.207 on EPF (short-term). The method also demonstrates a favorable accuracy–efficiency trade-off, sitting closest to the lower-left corner of the MSE-vs-training-time bubble chart on Weather.

## Baselines & Comparisons

L-Drive outperforms all nine baselines on the majority of dataset–metric pairs (12/16 best, 2/16 second-best). Against the strongest MLP baseline CrossLinear, it reduces MSE by up to 6.9% and MAE by 5.3% on ECL; against DLinear the gap is dramatic, with up to 49.3% lower MSE and 33.0% lower MAE on ETTh2. Among Transformer-based methods, L-Drive achieves up to 13.7% lower MSE than iTransformer on Weather and 16.2% lower MSE / 16.8% lower MAE than PatchTST on Solar. It also beats the CNN-based TimesNet by up to 17.1% MSE on ETTh2 and the time-frequency FilterTS by up to 15.9% MAE on ETTm2. The only dataset where L-Drive does not take the top MSE spot is Solar, where TimeBridge edges it out (0.227 vs 0.230), though L-Drive still matches the best MAE (0.262, tied with PatchMLP). Overall, the gains are most pronounced on datasets with pronounced regime shifts (ETTh2, EPF) and diminish slightly on smoother series (ETTm1, ETTm2), consistent with the method's focus on change-aware adaptation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant Direct-Mapping paradigm (iTransformer, PatchTST, TimesNet, DLinear, CrossLinear) by arguing that a unified observation-space mapping inherently produces response lag at regime switches, and introduces L-Context—a GRU-based latent change representation with gated first-order differencing—as a conditioning signal to accelerate adaptation. It explicitly distinguishes itself from non-stationarity treatments (RevIN, TimeBridge, Koopa) that address statistical mismatch or long-term distributional drift, positioning response lag around local switching points as a more specific temporal phenomenon. The patch-shared relative positional basis functions extend the patch-based design of PatchTST/PatchMLP by replacing absolute positional encodings with learnable intra-patch relative templates to reduce position-specific overfitting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical decomposition (EWMA-like inertia bound) that formalizes why Direct-Mapping models lag at regime switches, and shows that L-Context shifts the residual lag from being governed by the model's inertial structure to being governed by the increment estimation error, with an explicit upper bound. The portability experiments (injecting L-Context into DLinear, iTransformer, PatchTST) suggest it is a model-agnostic signal rather than an architecture-specific trick, which could make it a useful plug-in component. However, the practical gains on real datasets are modest (typically 1–3% MSE), the theoretical analysis relies on local linearization and contraction assumptions that may not hold in all regimes, and the synthetic switching experiment, while illustrative, is a simplified proxy for real-world regime changes. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to high-frequency financial data with rapid, overlapping regime shifts, and whether the GRU-based context can be replaced by lighter-weight alternatives without losing the bounded-error guarantee.
