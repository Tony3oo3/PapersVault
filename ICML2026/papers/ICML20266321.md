## Problem

Long-form video understanding requires capturing information scattered across thousands of frames, yet computational budgets limit how many frames a VLM can process. Existing strategies either sample uniformly (risking missed events) or select keyframes in a single pass with no recovery mechanism, while prior agent-based methods route sampling decisions through a text-only LLM that never directly perceives video content, creating an information bottleneck. Additionally, agentic VLMs trained with RL suffer from reward hacking, where models invoke sampling tools indiscriminately to maximize rewards even when initial frames already suffice.

## Input / Output

The model receives 16 uniformly sampled initial frames from a video (which may span up to 209,000+ frames) along with a text question. At each reasoning step, the model outputs structured text: a <thinking> block followed by either a tool call (specifying which agent to invoke with frame-range and optional prompt parameters) or a final <answer>. Each agent call returns a small batch of additional frames (4 for CLIP retrieval, 8 for uniform sampling) that are appended to the context for the next reasoning round, with up to 5 interaction rounds total.

## Method

VideoBrain is an end-to-end framework built on Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct that employs two complementary sampling agents: a CLIP-based agent (using SigLIP2-B/16) for semantic retrieval of visually relevant frames across the entire video, and a Uniform agent for dense temporal sampling within a specified interval. The VLM directly perceives frames and reasons about information sufficiency, deciding when and which agent to invoke, eliminating the vision-to-text bottleneck of pipeline approaches. Training uses a two-stage pipeline: SFT on Teacher-Model-generated trajectories (classified into Direct, Adaptive, and Active categories via dual-model evaluation) to teach basic reasoning and tool usage, followed by GRPO reinforcement learning with a behavior-aware reward function that assigns +0.5 for answering Direct questions without agents, +0.5 for using agents on Active questions, and a partial +0.2 for agent invocation on Active questions even when the answer is wrong, explicitly preventing reward hacking by making unnecessary agent calls unrewarded.

## Results

VideoBrain (8B) achieves consistent improvements over the Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct baseline across all four long video benchmarks: +8.1% on LongVideoBench (53.3% vs 45.2%), +9.0% on LVBench (41.3% vs 32.3%), +4.2% on Video-MME Long (49.8% vs 45.6%), and +3.5% on MLVU Test (46.9% vs 43.4%). Notably, these gains are achieved while using 30–40% fewer frames on average (22.3, 21.6, 19.1, and 20.0 frames respectively vs. 32 for the baseline). On short video benchmarks, VideoBrain generalizes without fine-tuning, achieving +5.1% on DREAM-1K and +2.4% on Video-MME Short while using 46–47% fewer frames.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the direct baseline Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, VideoBrain wins on all four long video benchmarks by +3.5% to +9.0% while using 30–40% fewer frames. Compared to the closest open-source competitor FrameThinker (7B, also agentic RL), VideoBrain achieves +4.7% on LVBench (41.3% vs 36.6%) and +2.2% on Video-MME Long (49.8% vs 47.6%) with fewer frames, attributed to the dual-agent design and behavior-aware reward. VideoBrain also outperforms Video-R1 (7B) on all benchmarks (e.g., +5.0% on LVBench, +1.6% on Video-MME) and Qwen2.5-VL-Instruct (7B) by large margins (e.g., +9.7% on LVBench). Against proprietary models, VideoBrain trails GPT-4o on all benchmarks (e.g., 53.3% vs 58.5% on LongVideoBench) and Gemini-1.5-Pro on Video-MME Long (49.8% vs 67.4%), but surpasses Gemini-1.5-Pro on LVBench (41.3% vs 33.1%) while using far fewer frames (21.6 vs 3600). The paper also demonstrates zero-shot generalization to short videos, where VideoBrain improves over the baseline by +5.1% on DREAM-1K and +2.4% on Video-MME Short with 46–47% fewer frames.

## Relation to Prior Work

VideoBrain sits at the intersection of three research threads: adaptive frame sampling for long video understanding, agent-based video QA (VideoAgent, VideoAgent2), and agentic reinforcement learning for VLMs (FrameThinker, FrameMind, DeepSport). It departs from prior agent-based video methods in two key ways: (1) replacing the decoupled text-only-LLM-orchestrates-tools architecture with an end-to-end VLM that directly perceives frames and makes sampling decisions, and (2) introducing a dual-agent design (CLIP semantic retrieval + uniform temporal sampling) where prior agentic video methods rely solely on uniform sampling. The behavior-aware reward function and Direct/Adaptive/Active data classification pipeline are novel contributions that address reward hacking, a problem identified but not systematically solved in prior agentic RL work for VLMs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in achieving consistent accuracy gains (+3.5% to +9.0%) while using 30–40% fewer frames, which directly addresses the cost-accuracy tradeoff that limits deployment of long video understanding in real systems. The behavior-aware reward design offers a generalizable recipe for training agentic VLMs without reward hacking, applicable beyond video to any setting where a model must decide whether to invoke tools. However, the error analysis reveals that 54.3% of failures stem from imprecise agent sampling (wrong frame ranges or vague CLIP prompts) and 17.1% from premature termination, indicating the learned policy is still far from optimal. Open questions include whether the dual-model classification pipeline scales to new domains without re-annotation, how the approach behaves at larger model scales (tested only at 8B), and whether the 5-round interaction budget is sufficient for the hardest multi-hop temporal reasoning tasks.
