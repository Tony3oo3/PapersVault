## Problem

Existing hallucination detection treats each prompt-response pair in isolation, attributing errors to missing knowledge. This paper shows that the same small LLM can produce both correct and incorrect answers to the same prompt under repeated sampling, indicating that hallucinations arise from retrieval instability rather than knowledge absence. No prior work has characterised the geometric structure of repeated responses in embedding space to exploit this distinction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263872/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 150 repeated responses per prompt from a base small LLM (7B–32B), embedded via SBERT all-MiniLM-L6-v2 into R^384, along with a small labelled subset of 30–50 responses tagged Genuine or Hallucinated by Claude Sonnet 4.5. It outputs a per-response G/H class label for all unlabelled responses in the same prompt-model set. Unusually, classification is not a pointwise distance-to-boundary decision but a distributional comparison: each response's point-to-set distance distributions in Fisher space are compared against reference intra-class distributions via Wasserstein distance, and the Fisher direction itself is prompt-specific (cross-prompt cosine similarity ≈ 0.058), so no global classifier is shared across prompts.

## Method

APORIA first computes three empirical distance distributions in SBERT space — intra-genuine (D_GG), intra-hallucinated (D_HH), and inter-class (D_GH) — and shows via 1D Wasserstein distance (validated against label-permutation null) that D_GG is systematically tighter than D_HH, revealing reduced semantic cohesion in hallucinated responses. A regularised Fisher Discriminant Analysis (λ≈1.2, shared across all models) then projects responses onto a single discriminant direction, amplifying the inter-to-intra-class distance ratio from ~1.13 to ~7.26 on average. APORIA-LP exploits this projection for label propagation: each unlabelled response is mapped to its Fisher coordinate z, and is classified as Hallucinated if W(Δ_GG, Δ_G(z)) > W(Δ_HH, Δ_H(z)), i.e., its distance distribution to the genuine cluster is less consistent with the genuine intra-class structure than its distance distribution to the hallucinated cluster is with the hallucinated intra-class structure. This distributional-consistency rule, rather than nearest-centroid assignment, is what makes the classifier geometry-aware and robust to class imbalance, achieving F1 > 90% with only 30–50 labelled examples per prompt-model pair.

## Results

APORIA-LP achieves an average accuracy of 87.2% and F1 of 90.7% across ten small-sized LLMs (7B–32B) on SOCRATES-300K, with per-model F1 ranging from 84.0% (Phi-4-14B) to 95.3% (Yi-1.5-9B). The structural analysis shows that Fisher projection amplifies the inter-to-intra-class distance ratio from 1.13× in the original SBERT embedding space to 7.26× on average, confirming that hallucination-related variance concentrates along a single discriminant direction. Performance saturates with as few as 30–50 labelled responses per prompt, and a single regularisation value (λ≈1.2) is optimal across all architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

APORIA-LP outperforms all three external baselines on both accuracy and F1. SelfCheckGPT (NLI) is the closest competitor, achieving 89.8% F1 versus APORIA-LP's 90.7%, a gap of roughly 1 point; however, SelfCheckGPT has notably lower variance (std 3.8 vs. 7.1). SemanticEntropy trails by about 4.4 F1 points (86.3% vs. 90.7%), and INSIDE lags substantially at 74.9% F1, suffering particularly from the repeated-answer framing where genuine and hallucinated responses coexist for the same prompt. The authors caution that the comparison is not strictly apples-to-apples: APORIA-LP performs label propagation from a small annotated subset, whereas the baselines perform per-response or per-prompt hallucination detection, so the stronger APORIA-LP numbers partly reflect the different task formulation. Despite this caveat, the consistent ordering across all three baselines supports the claim that the Fisher-space geometric signal exploited by APORIA-LP is more discriminative than NLI-based consistency or internal-state scoring.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant knowledge-gap framing of hallucination detection by arguing that, when factual knowledge is demonstrably present, hallucinations arise from retrieval instability rather than missing information. Methodologically, it repurposes Fisher Discriminant Analysis (Fisher, 1936) as a projection tool to expose a geometric separation between genuine and hallucinated responses in SBERT embedding space, combined with Wasserstein distance for distributional comparison—a combination not previously applied to hallucination analysis. It positions itself against both internal-state-based detectors (INSIDE, ACT-ViT) and semantic-consistency methods (SemanticEntropy, SelfCheckGPT), which it benchmarks against, while introducing a label-propagation classifier (APORIA-LP) that exploits the Fisher-projected geometry to classify new responses from as few as 30–50 annotations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The reframing of hallucination as a geometric retrieval-instability phenomenon shifts the mitigation focus from parameter scaling or knowledge injection toward decoding, conditioning, and retrieval guidance, with practical implications for deploying small open-weight models in agentic pipelines. The release of SOCRATES-300K (300K labelled repeated responses across 10 models) fills a gap left by prior detection papers that did not release datasets. Key open questions include whether the prompt-local Fisher directions transfer across prompts or domains, whether the geometric signature holds for non-factual hallucination types, and whether the findings generalise beyond the SBERT embedding family and time-sensitive factual queries used here.
