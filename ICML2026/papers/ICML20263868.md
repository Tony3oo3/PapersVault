## Problem

Static rubrics used as reward signals in RL-based LLM post-training are vulnerable to reward-hacking and fail to capture emergent desiderata that arise as the policy evolves during training. Offline rubrics, whether human-written or synthetic, cannot realistically cover every unexpected pattern (e.g., self-praising language that fools LLM verifiers), and correct traits in new generations go unrewarded if not already encoded in the fixed criterion set.

## Input / Output

At each training step, the method consumes a prompt, M pairs of responses (one from the current policy π_θ and one from a control policy π_control), and the existing prompt-specific rubric C_i. The LLM extractor produces a set of new weighted binary-checkable criteria grounded in the observed differences between the two responses, which are deduplicated and appended to C_i for that step only (elicited criteria are ephemeral and not carried forward). The final trained artifact is a policy model whose reward at each step is computed over the augmented criterion set C_i ∪ C_i^e.

## Method

OnlineRubrics integrates an online rubric elicitation loop into the GRPO training procedure: at each step, M pairs of rollouts from the current and control policies are compared by an LLM extractor that identifies discriminative differences not already covered by the existing rubric, converts them into weighted criteria, and deduplicates the result. The key insight is that pairwise contrast is more reliable for LLMs than pointwise criterion generation, since differences between two concrete responses surface discriminative properties (including reward-hacking patterns) that a single response cannot reveal. A formal proposition bounds the gradient estimation error by the L1 norm of unmodeled (implicit) criterion weights, motivating that tightening this bound via online elicitation improves training stability. Two control-policy variants are explored (reference policy vs. previous-step policy), and the elicited criteria augment rather than replace the offline rubric, enabling seamless integration with any existing rubric-based scoring mechanism.

## Results

On the Generalist domain, OnlineRubrics-π_old achieves 55.0% win rate on AlpacaEval (vs. 46.4% for Offline Rubrics Human) and 56.5% on Arena-Hard (vs. 52.4%), with a Generalist Rubrics score of 63.2. On the Expert domain, OnlineRubrics-π_old reaches 38.1% on GPQA-Diamond and 80.5% on GSM8K, outperforming the human-written rubric baseline (36.2% and 79.9%). A blind human validation study confirms the gains: annotators preferred OnlineRubrics responses in 70 out of 100 cases (p < 0.001).

## Baselines & Comparisons

OnlineRubrics consistently outperforms the Universal Requirements baseline (Viswanathan et al., 2025) across all metrics: on AlpacaEval, OnlineRubrics-π_old achieves 55.0% WR vs. 44.4% for Universal Requirements (+10.6 pp), and on Arena-Hard 56.5% vs. 53.8% (+2.7 pp), demonstrating that sample-grounded online elicitation is more effective than fixed catch-all criteria. Against the strongest static baseline, Offline Rubrics (Human), OnlineRubrics-π_old gains +8.6 pp on AlpacaEval WR (55.0 vs. 46.4) and +4.1 pp on Arena-Hard (56.5 vs. 52.4). Pointwise Extraction, which derives criteria from a single response rather than a pairwise contrast, improves over offline rubrics but is still surpassed by OnlineRubrics (48.1 vs. 55.0 on AlpacaEval), confirming that pairwise comparison is the critical mechanism. Across three independent training seeds, OnlineRubrics significantly outperforms both Offline Rubrics (mean +2.68 pp, p = 0.015) and Pointwise Extraction (mean +2.31 pp, p = 0.049) after Bonferroni correction, while Pointwise Extraction does not significantly differ from Offline Rubrics (p = 0.672). On the Expert domain (Table 3), OnlineRubrics-π_old leads on GPQA-Diamond (38.1% vs. 36.2%) and GSM8K (80.5% vs. 79.9%) over Offline Rubrics (Human), with the largest gain on Expert Rubrics win rate (56.5% vs. 51.8%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the rubric-based reward framework for LLM reinforcement learning (Gunjal et al., 2025; Viswanathan et al., 2025) by making criterion elicitation dynamic and online rather than static. It builds on the GRPO training loop (Shao et al., 2024) and the preference-learning literature on pairwise comparisons (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022), repurposing pairwise contrast not to train a reward model but to generate new evaluation criteria grounded in actual policy rollouts. The key departure from concurrent work (Dr Tulu, Liu et al. 2025a) is that criteria are elicited from pairwise differences between current and control policies rather than from external knowledge or offline synthetic generation, making the rubric adapt to emergent training behaviors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical failure mode in rubric-based RL—reward hacking and missed emergent desiderata—that static rubrics cannot capture, and provides a clean formal motivation (Proposition 1) bounding the gradient error from unmodeled criteria. The consistent 4–8 pp gains across multiple benchmarks, model families (Qwen, Llama), and sizes (3B–14B), confirmed by blind human validation, make the technique practically adoptable. Open questions include the semantic drift when using π_old as control, the ceiling imposed by rollout quality in expert domains, and the need for fully open-source extractors and graders to ensure reproducibility.
