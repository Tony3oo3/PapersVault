## Problem

Modern VLMs expose both a direct Instruct mode and an explicit-reasoning Thinking mode, yet practitioners lack principled, instance-level criteria for deciding when reasoning actually improves accuracy and how much test-time compute to allocate. This paper asks whether VLMs internally encode meta-cognitive signals—reasoning helpfulness and desired generation length—that could support adaptive inference, and whether those signals are localized to identifiable, causally manipulable circuits.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260280/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes an image-question pair (I, x) with multiple-choice options and a gold answer, running K=10 stochastic generations in both Instruct and Thinking variants to derive oracle labels (reasoning helpfulness r_help ∈ [-1,1] and desired length l_opt in tokens). Probes operate on the final-layer last-token hidden state extracted from a single prefill pass, making them usable before committing to a long generation. Interventions modify multi-head attention outputs at specific (layer, head) positions during decoding, producing steered answers with controlled length or shifted perception-reasoning balance across five benchmarks.

## Method

The paper trains three independent linear probes (regression for r_help and l_opt, classification for difficulty d) on final-layer last-token embeddings from both Instruct and Thinking variants of InternVL and Qwen3-VL, finding that Thinking models encode these signals with 29% lower MSE for r_help and 18% lower MSE for l_opt. Head-wise attribution via ablation (scaling each head's output to zero and measuring probe prediction change) localizes two partially disentangled circuits: length-control heads concentrated in middle-to-lower layers (mean layer ~15) and reasoning/difficulty heads in higher layers (mean layer ~27) with 0.68–0.76 Jaccard overlap. Causal steering is then achieved by scaling selected head outputs during generation: length-head scaling controls token budget by ±30% with <1.5% accuracy loss, while depth-controlled reasoning-head scaling (low-layer amplification for perception-dominant examples, high-layer for reasoning-dominant) yields up to +5.3% accuracy gains without retraining.

## Results

Thinking-model probes achieve 29% lower MSE for reasoning helpfulness (r_help) and 18% lower MSE for desired length (l_opt) compared to Instruct-model probes on MMMU, with the gap widening at 8B and 32B scale. Head attribution localizes length-control heads to middle-to-lower layers (mean layer 15) and reasoning/difficulty heads to higher layers (mean layer 27), with 0.68–0.76 Jaccard overlap between reasoning and difficulty head sets. Causal head-scaling interventions control generation length by ±30% with <1.5% accuracy loss, while depth-controlled reasoning-head scaling yields a perception–reasoning trade-off: low-layer amplification improves perception-dominant examples by +4.1% and high-layer amplification improves reasoning-dominant examples by +5.3% on MMMU (InternVL-8B-T). The same qualitative pattern generalizes across MathVista, ScienceQA, ChartQA, and A-OKVQA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, it performs controlled internal comparisons. The primary contrast is between Instruct and Thinking variants of the same model family: Thinking representations consistently yield lower probe error (e.g., Qwen3-VL-8B Thinking-probe MSE 0.126 vs. Instruct-probe MSE 0.173 for r_help; MAE 36.8 vs. 42.9 for l_opt), confirming that reasoning-oriented training reorganizes internal representations. For causal steering, the paper compares three intervention conditions—length-head scaling, reasoning-increase (push-pull), and depth-controlled reasoning steering—and finds that the depth-controlled condition produces the largest cohort-specific gains (+4.1%/+5.3%) while length-head scaling achieves the largest length control (±30%) with minimal accuracy degradation (<1.5%). Across the five evaluation benchmarks, the same directional pattern holds: low-layer reasoning-head amplification benefits perception-dominant examples and high-layer amplification benefits reasoning-dominant examples, with effect sizes scaling with model capacity (4B < 8B < 32B for probe quality).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Lee et al. (2025), who showed that problem difficulty is linearly decodable from final-token representations in LLMs and can be causally manipulated via head-output scaling, by transferring that framework to vision-language models and expanding the probed signals from a single difficulty label to two metacognitive targets: reasoning helpfulness and desired generation length. It also situates itself within the test-time compute allocation literature (Snell et al. 2025, Fu et al. 2025, Ni et al. 2026) and the overthinking/underthinking line (Su et al. 2025a, Cuadron et al. 2025), but departs from those works by providing a mechanistic, head-level account of *where* in the network the perception-versus-reasoning decision is computed, rather than relying on external confidence scores or learned verifier heads. The comparison between Instruct and Thinking variants within the same model family (InternVL, Qwen3-VL) is a controlled design choice not present in prior probing work, which typically examines a single model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a practical, retraining-free mechanism for adaptive test-time compute allocation in VLMs: a single prefill pass yields probe predictions that can gate or steer generation, and targeted head scaling shifts the perception-reasoning balance with modest accuracy gains (up to +5.3%) and controllable length changes (±30%). The depth-dependent finding—that lower-layer reasoning heads govern perceptual grounding while higher-layer heads govern abstract reasoning—provides a mechanistic explanation for the overthinking phenomenon and a concrete intervention point. Key limitations temper the impact: oracle labels are model- and protocol-specific rather than intrinsic question properties, the approach is strictly white-box (requiring internal tensor access), gains are modest and measured on only two model families, and robustness to adversarial inputs or distribution shift is untested. Open questions include whether these circuits generalize to architectures without explicit thinking/instruct variants, whether the interventions compose with other test-time scaling strategies (self-consistency, tree search), and whether the metacognitive signals can be distilled into a lightweight external controller that works across model families.
