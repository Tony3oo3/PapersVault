## Problem

Reverse-mode automatic differentiation never stores per-example gradients, making nonlinear statistics of the mini-batch gradient distribution (e.g., per-example variance, sign, clipping) inaccessible to optimizers and researchers. This has been viewed as prohibitively expensive or requiring complex custom implementations, leaving a large portion of the training algorithm design space unexplored, particularly for modern optimizers and distributed training settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263893/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a mini-batch of B text sequences and model parameters θ, and produces per-example (or per-token) gradient statistics such as the average elementwise squared gradient ν_micro = (1/B)Σg_i² or the sign of individual gradients, which are then fed into optimizer update rules to produce updated parameters. A key structural detail is that for sequence-level architectures like transformers, the per-token variant avoids materializing a full B×D×F tensor of per-example gradients by exploiting the rank-one structure of dense-layer gradients, keeping peak memory unchanged relative to standard ADAM. The method also outputs batch-size scaling behavior (universal vs. non-universal learning curves) as a diagnostic for optimizer design.

## Method

The paper introduces two complementary implementation strategies for computing per-example gradient statistics with negligible overhead: (1) JAX's vmap for rapid prototyping across workloads, and (2) computational graph surgery that parses the jaxpr of the backward pass to inject a nonlinear function φ just before the final sum-reduction over the batch, exploiting the fact that per-example gradients are rank-one vectors in dense layers (so φ(Σ h_b λ_bᵀ) can be computed as Σ φ(h_b)φ(λ_b)ᵀ for factorable φ). The paper then applies this machinery to two optimizer analyses: for SignSGD, a signal-to-noise-ratio argument shows the sign operation should be applied as late as possible (SIGNEMA > SIGNSGD > MICROSIGNSGD), and for ADAM, the authors derive unbiased estimators of μ² and σ² from ν_adam and ν_micro, revealing that the preconditioner is better dominated by mean-squared rather than variance information, leading to MICROADAMMSQ which slightly outperforms standard ADAM while exhibiting linear (rather than square-root) batch-size scaling.

## Results

On a 151M decoder-only transformer trained on C4 (batch size 64), the final eval losses are: ADAM 3.06, MICROADAM 3.10, MICROADAMMSQ 3.05, and MICROADAMVAR 3.20. For SIGNSGD variants, SIGNEMA (sign applied after EMA) achieves the best training, followed by standard SIGNSGD, with MICROSIGNSGD (sign applied per-example before averaging) performing worst. MICROADAM and MICROADAMMSQ both exhibit universal batch-size scaling with η ∝ B across B ∈ [8, 64], whereas ADAM requires η ∝ √B. The per-example gradient computation incurs negligible peak memory overhead on transformers (Figure 1) and modest time/memory increases on transformer workloads in the AlgoPerf benchmark (Figure 2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the original SignSGD (Bernstein et al., 2018), the paper shows that the placement of the sign operation is critical: applying sign per-example before averaging (MICROSIGNSGD) performs worst, while applying it after averaging and EMA (SIGNEMA) performs best, explained by a signal-to-noise ratio argument showing sign reduces SNR for low-SNR inputs. Against Zhao et al. (2025), who proposed SIGNEMA as competitive with ADAM for large transformers, the paper confirms SIGNEMA's superiority among SIGNSGD variants and provides a mechanistic SNR-based explanation for why the sign must be applied as late as possible. Against Wang & Aitchison (2024), who proposed MICROADAM with a true per-example second-moment preconditioner, the paper confirms their predicted η ∝ B scaling rule holds for a true implementation, but finds MICROADAM actually trains worse than ADAM (3.10 vs 3.06 eval loss) due to variance-dominated preconditioning. The paper's new variant MICROADAMMSQ, which emphasizes the mean-squared component, slightly outperforms ADAM (3.05 vs 3.06), suggesting that μ² information in the preconditioner is more valuable than σ², contrary to the conventional variance-dominated interpretation underlying the square-root scaling rule.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the per-example gradient computation framework of Dangel et al. (2019, Backpack) from dense MLP layers to sequence-level architectures like transformers, and provides the first true (non-approximated) large-scale implementation of the MICROADAM preconditioner proposed by Wang & Aitchison (2024). Rather than relying on per-device micro-batches to approximate per-example statistics, the authors show that JAX's vmap and a novel computational graph surgery technique can compute these statistics with negligible overhead on transformer workloads. The paper also introduces new optimizer variants (MICROSIGNSGD, MICROADAMMSQ, MICROADAMVAR) and a signal-to-noise-ratio analysis for sign placement that go beyond what either prior work addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The most impactful finding is conceptual: the ADAM preconditioner is empirically better when dominated by the mean-squared gradient (μ²) rather than the variance (σ²), directly contradicting the variance-dominance assumption underlying the widely-used square-root learning-rate scaling rule. This creates an unresolved tension—μ² dominates yet the square-root rule still works—whose resolution could reshape how practitioners think about adaptive optimizers and batch-size scaling. Limitations include evaluation on a single 151M decoder-only transformer on C4, and non-negligible overhead for architectures with standard dense layers. Open questions include whether the μ²-dominance phenomenon generalizes across architectures and scales, and whether the computational graph surgery approach can extend to second-order statistics like Hessian-vector products.
