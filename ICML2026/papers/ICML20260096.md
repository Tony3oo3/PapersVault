## Problem

Classical high-dimensional data assimilation methods (EnKF, 4DVar) rely on Gaussian approximations that break down under complex nonlinear dynamics and observation operators, while existing flow/diffusion-based DA approaches either require retraining the generative model at every time step, depend on specialized dynamical models, or are memory-intensive smoothing methods that scale poorly. The paper addresses the need for a scalable filtering algorithm that leverages a stationary, pre-trained generative prior to perform flexible probabilistic inference without per-step retraining, while still incorporating forecast information from arbitrary dynamics models.

## Input / Output

At each assimilation step, DAISI consumes a forecast ensemble of J particles (produced by any numerical or ML-based dynamics model), a sparse noisy observation y_n, and a pre-trained flow-based generative prior (a U-Net with 3.5M parameters for 64×64 or 256×256 spatial fields, or a small MLP for low-dimensional systems) that maps N(0,I) to the invariant measure of the system. The output is an ensemble of J updated particles approximating the filtering distribution p(x_n | y_{1:n}), from which point estimates, credible intervals, and probabilistic scores (CRPS, SSR) can be derived. The prior is trained once on long trajectory samples and never retrained during assimilation.

## Method

DAISI's core mechanism is an inverse-sampling step that transfers forecast information into the latent space of a stationary flow-based generative prior. Given forecast particles, the backward SDE of the stochastic interpolant is integrated from t=1 down to a tunable t_min, mapping each forecast state to a latent variable that encodes dynamical information in 'noise space.' These latents then serve as initial conditions for a guided forward SDE (integrated from t_min to 1) that conditions on the current observation via a guidance term (MMPS or DPS), producing posterior samples. The hyperparameter t_min controls the trade-off between retaining forecast information (t_min→1) and matching the observation-conditioned prior (t_min→0), while ε (the SDE noise level) prevents ensemble collapse by allowing stochastic resampling. This design decouples the generative prior from the forecast model, enabling zero-shot compatibility with any dynamics model and any gradient-based guidance method without retraining.

## Results

On the SQG benchmark (Table 3), DAISI achieves CRPS of 1.32±0.13 (Noisy), 1.73±0.18 (Sparse), 1.81±0.44 (Multimodal), 1.54±0.09 (Saturating), and 0.016±0.01 (SEVIR), consistently outperforming or matching all baselines except SDA-smoothing on linear settings. On the L63 system (Table 1), DAISI with tuned hyperparameters achieves RMSE 2.03±1.01 and CRPS 2.61±1.38, approaching the asymptotically exact Bootstrap Particle Filter (RMSE 1.18±0.50, CRPS 1.46±0.49) while maintaining well-calibrated uncertainty (SSR ≈ 1.08 vs. BPF's 2.57). DAISI-ML, which replaces the numerical forecast model with the learned FlowDAS model, shows no performance degradation, confirming that FlowDAS's failures stem from its filtering scheme rather than its forecast model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LETKF, DAISI matches on the Noisy setting (CRPS 1.32 vs. 1.34) but clearly outperforms it under Sparse (1.73 vs. 2.35), Multimodal (1.81 vs. 1.97), and Saturating observations (1.54 vs. 5.24), where LETKF diverges due to its Gaussian assumptions. Against FlowDAS, DAISI wins decisively across all five settings (e.g., 1.32 vs. 2.81 on Noisy, 0.016 vs. 0.045 on SEVIR), which the authors attribute to FlowDAS sampling from the local conditional p(x_{n+1}|x_n, y_{n+1}) rather than the full filtering distribution p(x_{n+1}|y_{1:n+1}). Against EnSF, DAISI dominates on Noisy (1.32 vs. 5.15), Sparse (1.73 vs. 4.20), Multimodal (1.81 vs. 6.38), and SEVIR (0.016 vs. 0.075), but EnSF edges out DAISI on the Saturating arctan observations (1.33 vs. 1.54), where its analytical score approximation handles the nonlinearity well. Against SDA-filtering, DAISI wins on Sparse (1.73 vs. 2.22), Multimodal (1.81 vs. 3.88), Saturating (1.54 vs. 4.20), and SEVIR (0.016 vs. 0.018), but loses on Noisy (1.32 vs. 1.13). SDA-smoothing outperforms DAISI on the linear problems (Noisy, Sparse, SEVIR) but degrades severely on nonlinear settings (Multimodal 3.82, Saturating 3.37), and it solves a fundamentally different (smoothing) problem, so the comparison is not directly apples-to-apples.

## Relation to Prior Work

DAISI builds on the stochastic interpolant framework (Albergo et al., 2023) for flow-based generative modeling and adapts it to sequential filtering via a novel inverse-sampling step that runs the backward SDE from forecast ensemble members into latent space before guided forward sampling. Conceptually, it extends the hybrid EnVar philosophy—blending a static background with an error-of-the-day ensemble—but replaces the Gaussian background covariance with a full learned generative measure P_∞. It departs from FlowDAS (Chen et al., 2025), which targets the local conditional p(x_{n+1}|x_n, y_{n+1}) rather than the true filtering distribution, and from SDA (Rozet & Louppe, 2023), which is a memory-intensive smoothing method, by using a stationary pre-trained prior that requires no retraining at each assimilation step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DAISI provides a practical path to non-Gaussian, high-dimensional filtering that avoids the curse of dimensionality of particle filters and the Gaussian assumptions of EnKF/4DVar, while remaining compatible with arbitrary (numerical or ML-based) forecast models without retraining. Key open questions include developing principled debiasing for the inherent approximation (the t_min/ε trade-off), reducing the high SDE-integration cost for operational use, and replacing the biased MMPS guidance with more accurate estimators. Scaling to realistic large-scale weather forecasting with complex observation operators is identified as the natural next step, and the method's success on SEVIR radar nowcasting suggests practical relevance for operational meteorology.
