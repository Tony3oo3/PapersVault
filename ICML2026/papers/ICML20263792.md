## Problem

Low training error in ML models applied to PDEs does not guarantee convergence to the correct physics or generalization beyond the training function space, yet the field lacks rigorous a priori bounds characterizing when and why this failure occurs. This paper identifies that the function space of the training data—not merely its quantity or grid discretization—is the critical determinant of generalization, and provides both theoretical proofs and a practical benchmark to measure it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263792/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Models receive discrete pairs (f(x_i), u(x_i)) sampled on a uniform grid of N_grid points, where f is drawn from a specified function space F(type, p) with p basis terms (polynomial, sine, cosine, or piecewise-linear/FEM). The key experimental protocol is a 25×25 cross-evaluation: each model is trained on one of 25 datasets (one per function-space/type/degree combination, 10,000 samples each) and then evaluated on all 24 others, producing log-scale MSE heatmaps that reveal sharp generalization boundaries (transitions of ~10^10). Outputs range from a scalar material parameter w, a full N_grid × N_grid operator matrix W, to extracted Green's function representations via one-hot impulse evaluations.

## Method

The paper combines two theoretical results with a new interpretability lens and a cross-validation benchmark. Theorem 3.1 proves that finite-difference parameter estimation has irreducible error O(Δx^q) set by stencil order q, and that enriching training data beyond degree q worsens the bias. Theorem 3.2 shows that gradient descent on a linear model u = Wf converges to the projection of the true Green's-function operator A onto the training-data subspace (W* = AUU^T + W^0(I−UU^T)), meaning full operator recovery requires the training subspace to span the full grid dimension. For interpretability, the authors evaluate trained black-box models on one-hot impulse inputs e_j to extract discrete Green's function rows, revealing whether learned weights encode the true solution operator or merely fit the training subspace. Empirically, eight model families (finite difference, linear, deep linear, MLP, DeepONet, Fourier Neural Operator, PINN, Physics-Informed DeepONet) are trained and cross-evaluated on 25 datasets spanning 1D Poisson, 1D biharmonic, and 2D Poisson equations, demonstrating that physics-informed losses do not mitigate the subspace limitation and that different model classes can exhibit opposing generalization behaviors.

## Results

The paper proves two theoretical bounds: (1) finite-difference parameter error scales as O(Δx^q) where q is the stencil order, and increases with training polynomial degree p (Theorem 3.1); (2) a linear model's learned weights converge to the projection of the true Green's-function operator onto the training data's function subspace, W* = A·UUᵀ + W⁰(I−UUᵀ) (Theorem 3.2). Empirically, out-of-distribution error can exceed training error by more than 10⁸×, and the FEM (piecewise linear) dataset is the only one that recovers the full solution operator. Physics-informed losses (PINN, PI-DeepONet) do not mitigate the subspace limitation: the PI-DeepONet achieves only ~10⁻⁶ baseline MSE versus ~10⁻²⁰ for the linear model, and noise raises the error floor from ~10⁻²⁰ to ~10⁻⁹ without altering the generalization boundary structure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All four compared model families exhibit the same fundamental subspace generalization failure, but with distinct nuances. DeepONet (Lu et al., 2021) shows block lower-triangular structure in its 25×25 cross-evaluation heatmap, indicating partial subspace generalization, but its diagonal (training distribution) entries are lower than off-diagonal entries, revealing slight overfitting that violates strict subspace generalization. The Fourier Neural Operator (Kovachki et al., 2023) displays a similar pattern but fails to achieve low training MSE on all function classes, making its generalization picture less clean. The Physics-Informed DeepONet (Wang et al., 2021) inherits the DeepONet's subspace pattern but with a substantially higher error floor (~10⁻⁶ vs. ~10⁻²⁰), demonstrating that incorporating the PDE as a loss term does not overcome the data-subspace limitation. PINNs (Raissi et al., 2019), used here for inverse parameter estimation, show parameter error growing with polynomial degree in exact agreement with Theorem 3.1, confirming that the PDE-loss mechanism absorbs truncation error into the learned parameter identically to explicit finite differences. The overarching finding is that no model class—including those explicitly designed for PDE learning or augmented with physics priors—escapes the function-space generalization barrier identified by the theory.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes two previously separate threads: Green's function-based data-requirement bounds for elliptic PDEs (Gin et al., 2021; Boullé et al., 2022, 2023) and mechanistic interpretability of trained network weights (Nanda et al., 2023), repurposing the Green's function as both a theoretical anchor for generalization bounds and a visual interpretability lens on black-box model parameters. It extends the line of work identifying generalization failures in SciML (Krishnapriyan et al., 2021; Sakarvadia et al., 2025; Queiruga et al., 2020) by providing the first rigorous a priori error bounds for ML models on linear PDEs and by showing that the function space of training data—not merely grid resolution or data quantity—is the governing factor. The paper departs from prior resolution-focused analyses (ReNO, RINO) by studying generalization when the test function class differs from the training class, a distinct and previously uncharacterized failure mode.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that physics-informed losses (PINNs, Physics-Informed DeepONets) do not mitigate the subspace generalization limitation directly challenges a core assumption in the SciML community that incorporating PDE structure into the loss improves out-of-distribution performance. The proposed cross-validation methodology—training on one function subspace and evaluating on another—provides a lightweight, practical benchmark that can be applied before committing to expensive training runs, and the Green's function extraction technique offers a new diagnostic for whether a model has genuinely learned the underlying operator. Key open questions include extending the theory to nonlinear PDEs, identifying data collection strategies that span the necessary function space, and developing remedies for the identified subspace limitation, which the authors explicitly do not address.
