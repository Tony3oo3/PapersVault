## Problem

Prior work established similar optimization guarantees for Byzantine failures and data poisoning in robust distributed learning, yet experiments consistently showed worse generalization under Byzantine attacks. Whether this gap reflects an inherent difference in adversarial power or merely the suboptimality of practical attacks remained an open question. This paper provides the first theoretical analysis proving a fundamental generalization gap between the two threat models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260323/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis considers n workers with f misbehaving (f < n/2), each honest worker holding m i.i.d. samples, under C-Lipschitz L-smooth convex or strongly convex losses, with SMEA as the robust aggregation rule. The method produces tight uniform stability bounds showing an additive degradation of Θ(f/(n−f)) under data poisoning versus Ω(√(f/(n−2f))) under Byzantine failures for (S)GD. A key result (Lemma 4.1) establishes a direct proportionality between stability and generalization error, converting the stability gap into a generalization gap of Ω((n−f)/√(f(n−2f))).

## Method

The core technique is uniform algorithmic stability analysis of robust distributed (S)GD with SMEA aggregation. For Byzantine failures, the proof introduces an intermediate comparison between the robust update and honest-worker averaging, since Byzantine vectors cannot be assumed to satisfy loss regularity, yielding an additive √κ term. For data poisoning, the proof exploits the fact that poisoned updates remain gradients of the smooth loss, decomposing the update by the intersection of SMEA-selected subsets across neighboring datasets, which tightens the bound to f/(n−f). Lower bounds are constructed by maximizing honest gradient variance and showing Byzantine workers can adaptively trigger SMEA to discard one honest subgroup, while poisoned workers—constrained to fixed datasets—cannot replicate this. Lemma 4.1 then establishes a direct equality between generalization error and stability for a specific distribution, proving the stability gap translates into a fundamental generalization gap.

## Results

For convex losses with f misbehaving out of n workers, the paper proves that uniform algorithmic stability degrades by an additive factor of Θ(f/(n−f)) under data poisoning, but by Ω(√(f/(n−2f))) under Byzantine failures (for f ≥ n/3). This yields a fundamental generalization gap: the ratio of Byzantine to poisoning generalization error is Ω((n−f)/√(f(n−2f))), which grows from 2 at f = n/3 to exceeding √n/2 as f approaches the breakdown point n/2. Numerical experiments with n=15, T=5 epochs confirm that the theoretical upper bound under data poisoning closely matches empirically measured generalization error, while the tailored Byzantine attack produces significantly larger error across all f values.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that does not compare algorithms against empirical baselines; instead, it compares two threat models (Byzantine failures vs. data poisoning) under the same robust distributed learning setup (SMEA aggregation, GD/SGD). The central finding is that Byzantine failures are strictly more harmful to generalization than data poisoning: the stability degradation under Byzantine failures scales as √(f/(n−2f)) while under data poisoning it scales only as f/(n−f), an order-of-magnitude gap. Theorem 4.2 formalizes this by showing the generalization error ratio E_gen^byz / E_gen^pois ∈ Ω((n−f)/√(f(n−2f))), proving the gap is not merely a constant factor but grows with n. The paper contrasts with prior work (Farhadkhani et al., 2024b) that showed equivalent optimization error guarantees for both threat models, explaining that the gap emerges specifically in the generalization (stability) analysis because Byzantine workers can send arbitrary, adaptive updates that exploit the aggregation rule's selection mechanism, whereas poisoned workers remain constrained by the loss function's regularity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the algorithmic stability framework (Bousquet & Elisseeff, 2002; Hardt et al., 2016) to the robust distributed learning setting, building on the (f,κ)-robustness definition and SMEA aggregation rule from Allouah et al. (2023a,b). While Farhadkhani et al. (2024b) showed that Byzantine failures and data poisoning yield comparable *optimization* error guarantees, this paper is the first to analyze *generalization* error via uniform stability, revealing that the two threat models are fundamentally separated in this dimension. The key methodological departure is that stability analysis exploits the regularity of poisoned gradients (which remain gradients of a smooth loss) in a way that optimization analysis does not, making the threat model distinction visible only through the stability lens.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first theoretical explanation for a long-observed empirical phenomenon: Byzantine attacks degrade generalization more than data poisoning, even when both are optimally designed. The gap is not a constant factor but an order-of-magnitude separation (√(f/(n-2f)) vs. f/(n-f)) that widens as f approaches the breakdown point n/2, with practical implications for choosing between cryptographic verification (ZKPs) and robust aggregation. Key open questions include whether a robust aggregation rule can preserve co-coercivity (which would eliminate the data-poisoning stability overhead), whether the gap persists in the f < n/3 regime at the order-of-magnitude level, and how these results extend to non-worst-case, data-dependent settings.
