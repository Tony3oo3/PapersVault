## Problem

Despite neural scaling laws empirically guiding large-scale LLM training, no existing theory can quantitatively predict the data-limited scaling exponent for any modern LLM trained on any natural language dataset. Kernel-based theories assume fixed features and do not apply to feature-learning transformers, while prior toy-model approaches to hierarchical language structure never provided a direct, parameter-free link between measurable language statistics and the observed loss-learning-curve exponent.

## Input / Output

The theory requires only two dataset-level statistics measured from the raw text corpus: the conditional-entropy decay exponent γ (from the power-law decay of next-token entropy H_n with context length n) and the token-token correlation decay exponent β (from the power-law decay of the operator norm of the lag-n covariance matrix). Given these two numbers and a training token count P with maximal context length T, the method produces a parameter-free prediction of the data-limited scaling exponent α_D = γ/(2β), a predicted collapse of all n-gram learning curves under the rescaling L_n → n^γ L_n and P → P/n^(2β), and the expected power-law decay of the full autoregressive loss. No synthetic data model or fitted hyperparameters are needed.

## Method

The theory decomposes the autoregressive loss into a boundary term (the conditional entropy at a data-dependent prediction time horizon n*(P)) and a sum of excess losses from suboptimal use of tokens within that horizon. The horizon n*(P) is derived from a signal-to-noise argument: the model can beneficially exploit tokens at lag n only when the strongest token-token correlation ||C(n)||_op exceeds the O(P^{-1/2}) sampling-noise floor, yielding n*(P) ∝ P^{1/(2β)} under the assumed power-law correlation decay. Combined with the power-law entropy decay H_n − H_∞ ∝ n^{−γ}, this gives the data-limited exponent α_D = γ/(2β) in the regime where within-horizon excess losses decay faster than the boundary term. A second, more general prediction is that all n-gram learning curves L_n(P) collapse onto a single master curve when plotted in rescaled variables (n^γ L_n vs. P/n^{2β}), which is validated empirically on GPT-2, RoPE-GPT-2, LLaMA, and Mamba architectures trained from scratch on TinyStories and WikiText-103.

## Results

The theory predicts the data-limited scaling exponent as α_D = γ/(2β) with no free parameters. For TinyStories (γ = 0.325 ± 0.003, β = 0.88 ± 0.06), the predicted exponent is α_D = 0.185 ± 0.013; for WikiText-103 (γ = 0.265 ± 0.016, β = 0.94 ± 0.16), it is α_D = 0.141 ± 0.025. Empirical learning curves from GPT-2 and LLaMA-style transformers trained from scratch on both datasets match these predictions: bootstrap 95% confidence intervals of the fitted empirical exponents overlap the predicted range for up to 10 of 12 points on TinyStories and all 8 points on WikiText. Additionally, n-gram loss curves for all time horizons n collapse onto a single master curve under the rescaling P → P/n^{2β} and L_n → n^γ L_n, providing a stringent multi-curve validation of the scaling ansatz.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing scaling-law theories or alternative architectures in a head-to-head benchmark; instead, it validates its parameter-free prediction against empirical learning curves across multiple model families and datasets. The predicted exponent α_D = γ/(2β) is consistent with empirical power-law fits for GPT-2 (absolute positional embeddings), GPT-2 (RoPE), and LLaMA-style transformers on both TinyStories and WikiText-103, with no architecture-specific fitting. The n-gram collapse under the rescaling P/n^{2β} and n^γ L_n holds across all tested architectures, confirming that γ and β are dataset-level properties rather than model artifacts. A minor exception is the LLaMA model on TinyStories, where the sharpest visual collapse occurs at β ≈ 0.65 (below the independently measured β = 0.88 ± 0.06), though the resulting autoregressive scaling exponent remains compatible with the predicted α_D. The theory's fast-learning assumption (within-horizon excess losses decay faster than P^{-γ/2β}) is directly verified: for n ≤ 12 on TinyStories, the measured decay exponents δ_n all exceed γ/(2β), confirming the horizon-limited regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical framework of Cagnetta & Wyart (2024) and Cagnetta et al. (2024), which established that increasing training data enables models to exploit progressively longer contexts in toy hierarchical language models, by deriving the first parameter-free prediction of data-limited scaling exponents from measurable statistics of natural language. It departs from kernel-method approaches (Caponnetto & De Vito, Spigler et al., Bordelon et al.) that predict learning-curve exponents from fixed feature spectra, arguing these are inapplicable to feature-learning LLMs, and from synthetic-data solvable models (context-free grammars, Markov chains) by working directly with empirical language statistics. The power-law entropy decay hypothesis (Eq. 6) is a differential form of Hilberg's (1990) hypothesis, while the correlation decay (Eq. 7) connects to the hierarchical structure of language identified by Lin & Tegmark (2017).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theory that quantitatively predicts data-limited neural scaling exponents for LLMs from two independently measurable language statistics (γ and β) with no free parameters, providing a direct mechanistic link between the statistical structure of language and the shape of scaling laws. The n-gram loss collapse under the predicted rescaling offers a stringent, multi-parameter validation that is harder to fake than a single exponent match. Key open questions include whether the horizon-limited regime persists at industrial scale (trillion-token datasets, 10⁵+ context windows), whether a broader 'universality class' of deep architectures (state-space models, CNNs) shares the same exponents, and whether the fast-learning-within-horizon assumption that underpins the prediction holds universally or is specific to transformers.
