## Problem

Existing VLA models lack tactile modality entirely, making them unable to perform force-sensitive tasks like gentle object handling, while the few VTLA efforts that incorporate touch face two compounding barriers: scarce aligned vision-tactile-language data (real tactile collection is expensive and low-throughput) and the absence of any closed-loop force feedback mechanism or standardized evaluation that measures interaction gentleness beyond binary task success.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265080/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy consumes synchronized 20 Hz streams of RGB-D from a wrist camera and a third-person camera, an 8-frame history of an 11×9 tactile marker displacement field (2D planar coordinates per marker), a language instruction optionally containing force-modifying adverbs (e.g., 'gently', 'firmly'), and robot proprioception. It outputs a continuous action vector (13 semantic dimensions, padded to 32) comprising a predicted end-effector pose in SE(3), a predicted gripper width, and 6D fingertip force setpoints; these are tracked in real time by a fixed decoupled admittance controller that separately regulates applied translational force and grip force.

## Method

Tabero-VTLA is built on the Pi0 flow-matching backbone and introduces three coupled components. First, a data pipeline replays open-source MuJoCo trajectories in Isaac Lab with a tactile-equipped Franka gripper, modulating gripper impedance gains (Kp, Kd) to synthesize diverse force distributions and augmenting language with force adverbs. Second, a lightweight TCN encodes the spatiotemporal marker displacement field into conditional tokens injected as a prefix into the transformer, while a weighted flow-matching loss upweights force dimensions by λ_force to prioritize accurate force prediction. Third, a decoupled force-position hybrid controller separates grip force (regulated via a feedforward-plus-admittance loop on gripper width with a deadzone) from applied translational force (regulated via an admittance position correction in the base frame), so the high-level policy only needs to predict appropriate force setpoints while the fixed low-level controller ensures compliant tracking.

## Results

On the Tabero benchmark (Dataset B, 10% force regime), the best configuration (FIELD+FS: marker displacement field with force supervision) achieves 86% success under firm instructions and 52% under gentle instructions, while reducing average grip force from 32.4 N to 3.7 N (≈89% reduction). Without any tactile input, the policy fails completely (0% success in both regimes). The model also generalizes to unseen adverbs: 'lightly' (OOD) yields 14.5 N average grip force and 'forcefully' (OOD) yields 18.1 N, showing intermediate force modulation between the trained 'gently' (3.7 N) and 'firmly' (32.4 N) conditions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external VTLA baselines on a shared benchmark; instead, it ablates internal design choices. The most critical comparison is between the no-tactile baseline (NONE) and any tactile-augmented variant: without tactile input, the policy achieves 0% success in both firm and gentle regimes, confirming that gentle manipulation is impossible without force awareness. Among tactile-only variants (IMG, FIELD, FORCE E), gentle success is near zero (≤0.01), showing that passive tactile perception alone is insufficient for force modulation. Adding explicit force supervision (FS) is the single largest improvement, jumping gentle success from ~0.01 to 0.45–0.52. The best configuration (FIELD+FS) edges out IMG+FS (0.52 vs. 0.48 gentle SR) and FORCE E+FS (0.52 vs. 0.49), indicating that the marker displacement field provides richer contact geometry than raw images or scalar force vectors. In the cross-dataset comparison (Table 2), the binary-gripper baseline (Dataset C) shows no force modulation (AG = 34.6 N under 'gentle' vs. 35.8 N under 'firm'), whereas the tactile-equipped policy (Dataset B) achieves an 89% grip-force reduction under gentle instructions.

## Relation to Prior Work

Tabero-VTLA builds directly on the Pi0 flow-matching VLA infrastructure for continuous action prediction, extending it to jointly output force setpoints alongside pose commands. The tactile simulation stack composes TacEx (Isaac Sim integration), Taxim (photometric-stereo GelSight rendering), and FOTS (marker displacement fields), while the decoupled force-position controller extends the admittance-based design from Tactile-VLA (Huang et al., 2025) with an explicit grip-force feedback loop. Conceptually, the paper departs from prior VTLA work (VTLA, TA-VLA, OmniVTLA, VLA-Touch) by shifting the focus from contact-rich task success to language-conditioned force modulation, and by repurposing existing MuJoCo-based trajectories (LIBERO) through tactile simulation rather than collecting new tactile data from scratch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Tabero benchmark addresses a genuine bottleneck in tactile robotics: the cost and complexity of collecting large-scale vision-tactile-language data. By replaying open-source trajectories in a high-fidelity tactile simulator and augmenting them with force-diversity via impedance parameter modulation, the pipeline offers a scalable path to training data that was previously infeasible. The process-aware metrics (MG, AG, MA, AA) move evaluation beyond binary success toward physically meaningful interaction quality, a gap the authors correctly identify in current VLA benchmarks. Key open questions include sim-to-real transfer of the learned force policies, whether the 70% force reduction generalizes to deformable or fragile objects beyond the rigid LIBERO suite, and how to jointly optimize gentleness and reliability (the paper acknowledges an inherent trade-off in ultra-gentle regimes that RL might resolve).
