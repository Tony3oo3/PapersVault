## Problem

Steering large language models via activation vectors is widely used but produces inconsistent, sample-specific outcomes that existing theory cannot explain. The dominant Linear Representation Hypothesis assumes concepts can be orthogonalized for lossless control, yet this idealization is geometrically impossible when the number of concepts exceeds the representation dimension, and linear separability metrics do not reliably predict actual steering success. The paper asks: what geometric structure in representation space accounts for the observed variability in steering effectiveness across samples?

## Input / Output

The framework takes a text prompt, a target concept (a directed attribute pair like male→female), and a steering vector constructed from positive/negative representation differences, and produces steered text output along with a steerability score (success-rate slope per unit vector norm). For probing, the method optimizes steering vectors under multiple norm budgets (0.1× to 2.0× the difference-vector norm) and applies PCA to the resulting vector set to extract a sample-specific cylindrical coordinate system (axis + 2D normal plane), over which loss landscapes and generation outputs are mapped at 30 phases × 5 radii per axial position.

## Method

The Cylindrical Representation Hypothesis (CRH) extends LRH by allowing concept directions to be non-orthogonal, so that a sample-level difference vector decomposes into a central axis (the primary semantic transition) and a sample-specific normal plane spanned by the orthogonal components of the target concept and the dominant direction of all other concepts. Within this plane, the phase of the steering vector's projection determines whether it falls in a high-sensitivity sector (target concept dominates) or a low-sensitivity sector (non-target concepts dominate), explaining why angularly similar steering vectors can produce opposite outcomes. The paper proves two key predictability properties: the normal-plane magnitude is inferable from the axis decomposition (Theorem 4.1), but the sensitive sector is fundamentally non-predictable because the mapping from latent concept compositions to the observable difference vector is non-injective when concepts exceed dimension (Theorem 4.3). Empirical validation spans 100 concepts across Gemma-2B-IT and LLaMA2-7B-Chat using four steering methods (DiffMean, PCA, Mean-Centering, Probe-based), testing three observable implications: the axis/plane trade-off in concept activation vs. output corruption, a unimodal correlation signature confirming plane determinability from the axis, and the absence of similarity transfer between difference vectors and steering outcomes.

## Results

The paper validates CRH's cylindrical structure through probing experiments showing that the top-3 PCA components of optimized steering vectors explain 95.14% (±1.52%) of variance across 2,451 cylinders and 99 concepts on Gemma-2B-IT (layer 9). Verification of the three observable implications confirms: (1) penalizing the normal-plane component produces a clear trade-off between earlier concept activation and earlier output corruption; (2) the normal plane is determinable from the axis, evidenced by a unimodal correlation peak in the mixed-power angle test; and (3) sensitive sectors are non-determinable, with no correlation between difference-vector similarity and steering outcomes (Pearson = −0.034, p > 0.05). The LLM-as-a-judge evaluation achieves 94% weighted F1 validated by human annotation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new steering method to outperform existing ones; instead, it uses four standard steering methods—DiffMean (Rimsky et al., 2024), PCA-based steering (Zou et al., 2023), Mean-Centering (Jorgensen et al., 2023), and Probe-based steering (Li et al., 2023)—as test cases to verify that CRH's geometric predictions hold universally. All four methods exhibit the same qualitative patterns: unimodal target-activation curves followed by monotonic corruption, consistent phase-dependent sensitivity in the normal plane, and the predicted trade-off between concept emergence speed and output stability when the orthogonal component is penalized. The key comparison is conceptual: LRH predicts that steering vectors with similar angular alignment to the target should yield comparable success, but CRH explains why this fails in practice—vectors with similar axis alignment can project into different sensitive sectors within the sample-specific normal plane. The non-determinability result (Pearson = −0.034) specifically refutes the LRH-based assumption that representation separability or difference-vector similarity can predict per-sample steering outcomes, a limitation previously noted by Bas & Novak (2025) and Braun et al. (2025) but not geometrically explained.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Linear Representation Hypothesis (LRH) of Park et al. (ICML 2024) by relaxing its orthogonality assumption, proposing instead that overlapping concept contributions induce a sample-specific cylindrical geometry (axis, normal plane, sensitive sectors) that governs steering effectiveness. It positions itself against the broader activation-steering literature (Rimsky et al. 2024, Zou et al. 2023, Jorgensen et al. 2023, Li et al. 2023a) not by proposing a new steering method, but by providing a geometric account of *why* existing methods exhibit sample-dependent instability. The work also connects to geometric representation studies (Engels et al. 2025, Nanda et al. 2023, Modell et al. 2025) but focuses specifically on how local representation geometry shapes intervention outcomes rather than characterizing global concept geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reframes a long-standing practical puzzle—why steering outcomes vary across samples even with well-aligned vectors—as an intrinsic geometric property rather than a vector-quality problem, which shifts the research agenda from finding better steering directions to understanding and potentially exploiting sample-specific geometry. The predictability asymmetry (normal-plane magnitude is inferable, sensitive sectors are not) provides a principled explanation for the limits of steering controllability. Key limitations include the framework being conceptual rather than directly verifiable, the analysis focusing on vector-level interactions without fine-grained activation dynamics, and evaluation limited to 100 concepts in text/code/math domains. Open questions include whether the cylindrical structure persists in multi-step reasoning or agent-style settings, and how to incorporate sample-specific geometry into steering algorithms for more reliable control.
