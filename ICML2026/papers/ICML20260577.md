## Problem

Existing longitudinal causal inference methods estimate each counterfactual treatment policy in isolation, fitting separate sequences of nuisance models for every policy. The paper proves that this separate estimation paradigm induces a structurally uncontrolled second-order bias in CATE estimation that does not cancel even when two policies are identical, inflating finite-sample variance even after standard LTMLE debiasing. The gap is the lack of a principled mechanism to share information across counterfactual trajectories while preserving identification.

## Input / Output

The method consumes n i.i.d. longitudinal trajectories of horizon τ (each containing time-varying covariates L_t, binary treatments A_t, and a terminal outcome Y) together with a set of K counterfactual dynamic treatment policies. Before training, pairwise MMD distances between policies are computed at each time step and reduced to fixed-dimensional vectors via metric MDS, which serve as structured policy embeddings fed into the network. The output is a per-policy CAPO estimate (with influence-function-based confidence intervals) for each of the K policies, from which CATE contrasts are obtained by differencing.

## Method

PEQ-Net reparameterizes the ICE Q-functions to explicitly condition on the future policy tail π_{t+1:τ}, shifting the policy dependency from implicit (separate model per policy) to explicit (single shared model with policy as input). A policy-tail encoder (RNN) aggregates per-time-step MMD-based embeddings into a fixed-dimensional representation that is concatenated with the Transformer backbone output and passed to the Q-head, enabling one set of outcome and propensity models to serve all K policies simultaneously. Training uses a lagged target network with Polyak averaging to stabilize the recursive ICE G-computation targets, and all K policies are optimized jointly in a single loss. After training, a standard LTMLE targeting step debiases the plug-in estimator. The key theoretical result (Theorem 4.2) proves that under Lipschitz regularity, the CATE second-order remainder is bounded by a constant times the trajectory-level MMD distance between the two policies, providing a structural smoothness guarantee absent in the separate estimation paradigm.

## Results

On semi-synthetic MIMIC-III-derived data with dynamic treatment policies (Table 2), PEQ-Net achieves RMSE of 0.0026 and 0.0003 for the 'policies differ only in first two steps' setting (Limited and Expanded DGPs, respectively), compared to 0.1255 and 0.1302 for DeepLTMLE — a 48× and 434× reduction. For the harder 'policies differ in all steps' setting, PEQ-Net still halves the RMSE (0.1220 vs 0.2725 Limited; 0.1420 vs 0.3692 Expanded). On deterministic-sequence counterfactuals (Table 1), PEQ-Net achieves the lowest RMSE across all six settings, e.g., 0.27 vs 1.33 (DeepLTMLE) for CF 3a under Limited confounding.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PEQ-Net outperforms all four baselines across every counterfactual setting and both DGPs. Against the strongest baseline, DeepLTMLE (which shares the same Transformer backbone), the gains are most dramatic for dynamic policies with structural overlap: RMSE drops from 0.1255 to 0.0026 (Limited) and 0.1302 to 0.0003 (Expanded) when policies differ only in the first two steps, confirming that the policy-aware reparameterization and shared encoder exploit tail-identity to eliminate spurious variance. For fully divergent policies (CF 1c/2c), PEQ-Net still achieves roughly 2–2.6× lower RMSE than DeepLTMLE. Against the classical baselines (ICE G-computation and LTMLE with Super Learner, and DeepACE), PEQ-Net's advantage is even larger — e.g., on deterministic CF 3a under Limited confounding, RMSE is 0.27 vs 1.94 (G-comp), 2.47 (LTMLE), and 2.12 (DeepACE). The ablation (Table 3) further shows that even when jointly training with structurally dissimilar extreme policies (always-treat, never-treat), PEQ-Net maintains its advantage over DeepLTMLE, indicating robustness to policy-set heterogeneity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ICE G-computation framework (Snowden et al., 2011) and the DeepLTMLE Transformer architecture (Shirakawa et al., 2024) by introducing a policy-aware reparameterization of the recursive Q-functions that conditions them explicitly on the future policy tail, enabling a single shared model to serve multiple counterfactual policies. The key departure from prior work (DeepACE, DeepLTMLE) is the rejection of the standard separate-estimation paradigm, where each policy's nuisance models are fitted independently; instead, the paper uses RKHS-based MMD embeddings to encode policy dissimilarity and a shared policy encoder to condition the outcome regression, with a theoretical guarantee (Lipschitz control of the second-order remainder) that this shared structure stabilizes CATE estimation. The LTMLE debiasing step is retained from the existing doubly-robust literature but applied to the jointly-learned initial estimates rather than per-policy ones.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the standard separate-estimation paradigm induces a non-cancelling second-order remainder even for identical policies (Lemma 3.1) reframes a long-standing practical observation—variance inflation in multi-policy evaluation—as a structural defect rather than an unavoidable cost. The practical impact is most relevant to retrospective clinical policy comparison (e.g., vasopressor weaning thresholds in sepsis), where candidate policies are typically conservative and closely related, making the shared-representation benefit strongest. Limitations include the quadratic scaling of MMD computation in both sample size and number of policies, the semi-synthetic nature of most experiments (N=1000, τ=15), and the observation that gains diminish when policies are structurally dissimilar. Open questions include adaptive information sharing across heterogeneous policy sets, scaling to very large K via kernel approximations, and validation on larger real-world cohorts.
