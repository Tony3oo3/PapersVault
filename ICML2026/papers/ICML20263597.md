## Problem

Existing results on the minimum depth and width of neural networks for universal approximation and memorization are derived under exact arithmetic and cannot be directly applied to real implementations that use finite floating-point sets with inexact operations and round-off errors. This paper asks: what are the exact minimum depth and width of floating-point ReLU networks (with floating-point parameters and IEEE-754 operations) needed to represent all functions from F^d to F, and how do these bounds differ from the exact-arithmetic case?

## Input / Output

The network takes a d-dimensional vector of finite floating-point numbers (from F^d, where F is defined by mantissa bits p and exponent bits q per IEEE-754) and produces a single finite floating-point number. All internal computations—matrix-vector products, additions, and ReLU activations—use floating-point arithmetic with round-to-nearest-even rounding, meaning intermediate values can overflow to ±∞ or produce NaN. The domain and codomain can be restricted (e.g., to non-negative floats [0,∞)_F or bounded intervals like [-1,1]_F), and these restrictions materially change the required depth and width in ways that have no analogue under exact arithmetic.

## Method

The paper establishes that the minimum depth is exactly three: a three-layer construction (Theorem 1) builds indicator functions 1_{x=z_i} for every point z_i in F^d using two-layer sub-networks that detect coordinate-wise equality via ReLU thresholds, then a final layer sums weighted indicators to reproduce any target function. The tightness is shown (Theorem 2) by a counterexample f* with f*(0)=-Ω and f*(±ω)=Ω, where any two-layer network must overflow at x=±1 due to the interplay between the wide dynamic range of F and the monotonicity constraints on ReLU neurons. For width, the paper proves 2d+4 is sufficient and 2d is necessary in general (Theorems 5–6), but reduces to d+4 sufficient and d necessary when inputs are non-negative (Theorems 7–8), and further to ⌈d/(p+1)⌉+4 when inputs are restricted to {0,ω}^d (Theorem 9). The key mechanism distinguishing floating-point from exact arithmetic is that nontrivial additions are not injective due to round-off, and overflow constraints force additional layers or neurons that are unnecessary under exact arithmetic.

## Results

The paper establishes that the minimum depth of floating-point ReLU networks to represent all functions from F^d to F is exactly 3 (Theorem 1: upper bound; Theorem 2: lower bound), improving upon the prior four-layer construction of Park et al. (2024). For width, the minimum lies between 2d and 2d+4 in general (Theorems 5–6), and between d and d+4 when the domain is restricted to non-negative floats (Theorems 7–8). For the tiny domain {0,ω}^d, width ⌈d/(p+1)⌉+4 suffices (Theorem 9), where p is the mantissa bit-width. These bounds are strictly larger than their exact-arithmetic counterparts (depth 2, width d+1 or 2), demonstrating that floating-point round-off and overflow fundamentally alter network expressiveness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to Park et al. (2024), which established a four-layer upper bound for floating-point function representation, this paper tightens the depth to exactly three layers (Theorem 1), and proves tightness via a counterexample (Theorem 2). Against the classical exact-arithmetic results of Pinkus (1999) and Bubeck et al. (2020), which show depth 2 suffices for universal approximation and memorization respectively, the floating-point setting strictly requires depth 3 — a gap caused by overflow and round-off errors that prevent the standard two-layer constructions from working. On width, the paper shows a fundamental departure from Hanin & Sellke (2017) and Park et al. (2021): while exact arithmetic requires only width d+1 (universal approximation) or width 2 (memorization), floating-point arithmetic demands width at least 2d (Theorem 6) because nontrivial addition is not injective under round-off, making the standard input-preservation and projection tricks infeasible. The paper further reveals a domain-dependent width phenomenon absent in exact arithmetic: restricting to non-negative inputs reduces the width requirement from 2d to d (Theorems 7–8), and restricting to {0,ω}^d reduces it further to ⌈d/(p+1)⌉+4 (Theorem 9), showing that the minimum width is not invariant under domain transformations in the floating-point setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the floating-point expressiveness framework initiated by Park et al. (2024), who showed that four-layer floating-point ReLU networks can represent all floating-point functions. The key departure from the classical literature (Cybenko, Hornik, Pinkus for universal approximation; Baum, Bubeck for memorization) is that all prior minimum depth/width results assume exact arithmetic, where two layers and width d+1 (or 2 for memorization) suffice. By working under IEEE-754 semantics with round-off and overflow, this paper shows the landscape shifts fundamentally: depth becomes 3 and width roughly doubles to ~2d, and the answer now depends on the specific domain/codomain in ways that have no analogue under exact arithmetic.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The results establish a clean, provable gap between what neural networks can do in theory (exact arithmetic) and in practice (floating-point), which has been an implicit but unquantified concern in the field. The tight depth bound of exactly 3 and the width bounds of [2d, 2d+4] give practitioners concrete architectural constraints: no two-layer floating-point ReLU network can represent all floating-point functions, and width below 2d is insufficient in general. Open questions include closing the gap between the 2d lower and 2d+4 upper width bounds, extending results to other activation functions or mixed-precision settings, and understanding whether the domain-dependent depth/width phenomena (e.g., two layers suffice for non-negative inputs) have practical implications for network design in low-precision training.
