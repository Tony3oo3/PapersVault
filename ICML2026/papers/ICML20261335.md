## Problem

Multi-pretext graph self-supervised learning typically combines objectives via per-step gradient mixing (weighted sums, MGDA, PCGrad), forcing every parameter update into an instantaneous compromise that produces three failure modes: Disagreement (conflict-induced negative transfer), Drift (nonstationary objective utility), and Drought (hidden starvation of underserved objectives). The paper argues that coordination is fundamentally a temporal allocation problem—deciding *when* each objective receives optimization budget—rather than a per-step weighting problem, and that even unplanned temporal separation (uniform random single-task scheduling) already outperforms sophisticated per-step mixing methods.

## Input / Output

ControlG consumes an attributed graph G=(V,E,X) and a fixed pool of K=5 pretext objectives (link prediction, masked feature reconstruction, node-subgraph mutual-information contrast, representation decorrelation, and METIS partition prediction), processing them in blocks of B_block mini-batches with full-graph state estimation every u blocks. It outputs pretrained node embeddings from a GCN/GAT encoder plus an auditable temporal allocation schedule that records which objective was active in each block, making the training process interpretable: one can inspect which objectives drove learning, when the planner reallocated budget, and whether any objective was starved.

## Method

ControlG replaces per-step gradient blending with feedback-controlled temporal allocation through three coupled loops at different timescales. At the sensing timescale (every u blocks), it computes per-objective spectral demand (the Rayleigh quotient of the representation-gradient field H_k on the normalized Laplacian, capturing how high-frequency the learning signal is) and interference (MGDA-weighted pairwise negative cosine between unit-normalized parameter gradients, identifying Pareto-relevant conflicts), combining them into a bounded difficulty state D_k. At the planning timescale (per epoch), log-hypervolume sensitivities w_k^HV = 1/(r_k − L̃_k) are tempered by difficulty to yield an allocation target f(t) ∈ Δ^K via a proportional-fair concave program. At the execution timescale (per block), a PID controller tracks cumulative allocation deficits e_k = N_k^ref − N_k, producing logits mapped through softmax with ε-greedy exploration to stochastically select which single objective trains in the next block, with a geometric drought-tail bound guaranteeing no objective is starved for more than O(K/ε) blocks in expectation.

## Results

On node classification across 9 datasets, ControlG achieves the best average rank (1.4) with particularly strong gains on homophilic graphs: Cora 81.92% (+1.5% over CAGrad), PubMed 84.24% (+1.1%), and Co-CS 96.14% (+1.8%). On the large-scale OGBN-Arxiv, ControlG reaches 72.86%, a 1.2% improvement over the strongest baseline CAGrad (71.62%). ControlG also achieves the best average rank on link prediction (1.9) and node clustering (1.8), and is 5–15× faster than per-step mixing methods like AutoSSL and ParetoGNN.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ControlG outperforms all nine baselines on average rank. Against the strongest per-step mixing baselines, CAGrad (avg rank 5.2) and PCGrad (5.8), ControlG wins on 8 of 9 datasets for node classification, with the largest margins on CiteSeer (+3.8% over CAGrad) and Squirrel (+2.8%). Against multi-pretext methods, ControlG substantially outperforms AutoSSL (avg rank 6.4) on PubMed (+3.4%) and Co-CS (+2.5%), and dominates WAS (12.9) and ParetoGNN (10.8) across nearly all datasets. Against single-pretext SSL methods (BGRL, DGI, GRACE, MVGRL), ControlG consistently leads, with the gap most pronounced on CiteSeer where BGRL drops to 35.28% versus ControlG's 66.48%. Notably, even the simple Random scheduler (avg rank 9.4) and Round-Robin (7.4) outperform several sophisticated per-step mixing methods, underscoring that temporal separation itself is a strong inductive bias; ControlG's feedback-controlled scheduling then adds a further 2–4% over these naive temporal baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

ControlG departs from the dominant per-step gradient-mixing paradigm in multi-task graph SSL (ParetoGNN, PCGrad, CAGrad, AutoSSL, WAS) by reframing coordination as a temporal allocation problem: rather than blending all objectives into a single update direction, it trains one objective per block and uses feedback control to decide which. It repurposes MGDA (Sener & Koltun, 2018) not as an update rule but as a sensing oracle for pairwise interference, borrows the log-hypervolume indicator from multi-objective optimization (Guerreiro et al., 2020) as a Pareto-compliant planning signal, and adapts deficit round-robin scheduling (Shreedhar & Varghese, 1996) into a PID controller for discrete task selection. The spectral demand signal draws on the low-pass-filter interpretation of GNN message passing (Nt & Maehara, 2019), connecting graph structure to optimization difficulty in a way prior multi-pretext methods do not exploit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even a uniformly random single-task scheduler outperforms all per-step mixing baselines on downstream clustering challenges a core assumption in multi-task learning—that instantaneous gradient compromise is necessary—and suggests that temporal separation is a strong inductive bias that prior methods were inadvertently fighting against. ControlG's auditable schedule (which objective was active when, and why) offers interpretability that per-step scalarization methods cannot provide, which is valuable for debugging and trust in self-supervised pipelines. Open questions include whether the framework extends beyond 5 pretext tasks or to non-graph domains, how sensitive performance is to the sensing period u and block size, and whether the low-pass assumption underlying spectral demand holds for all GNN architectures (e.g., attention-based or heterophilic GNNs).
