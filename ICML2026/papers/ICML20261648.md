## Problem

Query-time adaptations in RAG (e.g., query expansion) are inherently stateless: gains are recomputed per query and discarded, preventing cumulative learning and incurring repeated inference cost. Index-side persistence (key expansion, memory modules) exists but is applied offline or with weak supervision, causing semantic drift and misalignment with downstream task utility. The paper addresses this structural disconnect by asking how transient, query-validated expansion signals can be selectively and stably converted into persistent retrieval index improvements without retraining.

## Input / Output

ERM consumes a user query, a document corpus with existing retrieval keys (built from titles, keywords, abstracts, or full text), query expansion units produced by any QE method (HyDE, Diver, decomposition, etc.), and binary correctness feedback from either a retrieval verifier (recall@K, DPR match) or a generation verifier (ROUGE, task loss, LLM-as-judge). The output is a set of evolved document keys in the retriever's embedding space, augmented additively with top-scoring expansion embeddings; at inference time, queries are executed directly against these evolved keys with zero additional expansion overhead, yielding retrieved documents and a generated response at native retrieval latency (~150–280 ms).

## Method

ERM is a training-free, three-stage framework that converts validated query expansions into persistent key updates. (1) A Correctness-Gated Feedback Verifier accepts an expanded query only if either retrieval or generation correctness holds, preventing noisy updates. (2) Selective Expansion Attribution decomposes the accepted expansion into atomic units and computes a marginal similarity gain Δ_{i,j} for each unit–document pair, retaining only units with positive gain relative to the original query. (3) Progressive Key Evolution accumulates softmax-normalized attribution scores in a per-document expansion memory, then augments each key additively with the top-x highest-scoring expansion embeddings under a norm-bounded, batched update rule with patience-based saturation stopping. The paper proves query-key equivalence under bilinear similarity, almost-sure convergence of attribution scores via the strong law of large numbers, stability of the resulting key sequences, and sublinear Θ(T^{1/α}) amortized expansion cost under Zipf-like query distributions.

## Results

ERM achieves +46% average nDCG@1 improvement over BM25 (26.3→38.5) and +11–15% over dense retrievers (BGE-Large 48.6→55.7, GTE-Base 49.9→56.4, Cohere 48.7→55.2, Voyage 50.8→56.3) across 13 BEIR and BRIGHT domains. Gains are most pronounced on reasoning-intensive tasks: AoPS (+115–2200%), TheoremQA (+75–378%), Pony (+64–103%), and LeetCode (+19–44%). Downstream generation improves by +2–6% (BM25: 72.6→76.6, GTE-Base: 77.4→79.0), and ERM operates at native retrieval speed (150–280 ms) versus 7–15 s for HyDE, a 50–100× latency reduction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against BM25 (Robertson et al., 1995), ERM delivers the largest absolute gains (+46% avg nDCG@1), with particularly dramatic improvements on reasoning-intensive datasets (AoPS +2200%, TheoremQA +378%), confirming that key evolution compensates for the lexical-matching limitations of sparse retrieval. Against dense retrievers BGE-Large/Base (Xiao et al., 2024) and GTE-Base (Li et al., 2023), ERM yields +13–15% average nDCG@1, with the strongest gains concentrated on coding and math domains (LeetCode +40–44%, Pony +28–103%, AoPS +131–508%) where surface-level embedding similarity is weakest. Against BGE-M3 (Chen et al., 2024), ERM similarly improves retrieval, though the paper reports BGE-M3 primarily as a dense baseline without a dedicated ERM row in the main table. Against HyDE (Gao et al., 2023) as an inference-time query expansion baseline, ERM matches or exceeds HyDE's nDCG@10 while reducing latency from 7–15 s to 150–280 ms (50–100× speedup), demonstrating that persisting validated expansion signals into keys amortizes the cost. ERM also outperforms proprietary APIs (Cohere, Voyage) by +11–13% average, showing that key evolution complements rather than duplicates strong pre-trained embeddings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of query expansion (QE), key expansion (KE), and memory-augmented RAG, three lines of work that have been treated as structurally separate. Prior QE methods (HyDE, Diver, BGE-Rewriter) adapt at inference time but discard their signals; prior KE and memory-augmented approaches persist information but operate offline or with weak task-level supervision. ERM repurposes the QE signal itself as the update mechanism for the index, introducing correctness-gated feedback and selective attribution as the bridge, and proves a formal equivalence between query-side and key-side expansion under bilinear similarity that unifies the two perspectives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ERM offers a practically attractive recipe: amortize the 50–100× inference cost of LLM-based query expansion into a one-time index update while retaining (and often exceeding) the retrieval quality, with no model retraining. The convergence guarantees and sublinear amortized cost under Zipf-like query distributions provide theoretical grounding that prior heuristic memory-update schemes lack. Key open questions include how the correctness-gated signal degrades when no ground truth is available (relying on LLM-as-judge), whether the positive feedback loop amplifies retrieval bias in long-running deployments, and how ERM interacts with periodic re-embedding or corpus updates in production systems.
