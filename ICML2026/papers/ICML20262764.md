## Problem

Script-based trajectory verification for computer-using agents (CUAs) is prohibitively expensive to scale and cannot provide step-wise assessment, motivating VLM-based reward models as an alternative. However, the effectiveness of existing CUA reward models remains largely unverified: most lack open-source implementations or detailed methodology, and no standardized benchmark exists for systematically evaluating both outcome reward models (ORM) and process reward models (PRM) on CUA trajectories.

## Input / Output

The reward model receives a trajectory consisting of a text task instruction, a sequence of single-channel RGB screenshots (one per step, no DOM or accessibility metadata), and the agent's reasoning and action at each step. It produces a binary trajectory success/failure label (ORM) and per-step correctness labels (PRM), where step correctness is defined probabilistically as whether the action increases the probability of eventual task success. Notably, only 'key' actions (those with maximal impact on success probability) are annotated rather than every step, and the benchmark deliberately includes bad actions within successful trajectories and vice versa to decouple trajectory-level and step-level evaluation.

## Method

CUARewardBench comprises 272 trajectory-level and 346 step-level human annotations across 10 software categories and 7 agent architectures (25.9%–50.8% success rates), curated with difficulty controls and validated through a dual-phase independent-annotation-plus-consensus protocol. The paper evaluates 12 vision-language/reward models (general VLMs, visual reasoning models, specialized CUA models, and specialized CUA reward models) under 3 prompt templates (ZeroGUI, OpenCUA reflector, SE-WSM), revealing that visual reasoning capability dominates over CUA-specific training and that prompt templates primarily shift precision–NPV trade-offs rather than overall accuracy. Building on these findings, the paper proposes Unanimous Prompt Ensemble (UPE), which combines strict unanimous voting (a sample is classified only when all ensemble members agree, otherwise it is abstained) with heterogeneous prompt-template configurations that exploit complementary P-NPV characteristics, achieving 88.0% precision / 95.3% NPV for ORM and 83.1% precision / 86.2% NPV for PRM.

## Results

On CUARewardBench, the proposed UPE (Unanimous Prompt Ensemble) achieves 88.0% precision and 95.3% NPV for ORM, and 83.1% precision and 86.2% NPV for PRM, substantially outperforming all single VLMs and majority-voting ensembles on the critical NPV metric. The best single VLM for ORM is GPT-5 with the SE-WSM prompt (89.0% P, 86.6% NPV), while for PRM it is GPT-5 with SE-WSM (83.7% P, 68.8% NPV). UPE's key advantage is its NPV: for PRM, UPE (86.2% NPV) exceeds GPT-5 (68.8% NPV) by 17.4 percentage points, demonstrating that strict unanimous voting with diverse prompts dramatically improves negative-prediction reliability. Downstream validation shows that reward-model-filtered trajectories improve Qwen3VL-7B's LibreOffice Impress success rate from 34.68% to 46.10% (33% relative gain).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among single VLMs, GPT-5 with the SE-WSM prompt achieves the highest ORM precision (89.0%) and competitive NPV (86.6%), while Qwen3VL-235B-T with ZeroGUI leads on NPV (90.6%) at slightly lower precision (82.6%). Specialized CUA models (GUI-OWL-32B: 69.0% P, 73.7% NPV) and the dedicated CUA reward model SE-WSM-7B (70.0% P, 52.2% NPV) both underperform general VLMs, confirming that visual reasoning capability dominates over CUA-specific training for reward evaluation. For PRM, GPT-5 with SE-WSM achieves the best single-model precision (83.7%) but only 68.8% NPV, whereas UPE (Q3-32-s + Q3-235-o) matches GPT-5's precision (83.1%) while boosting NPV to 86.2%—a 17.4-point improvement. Majority-voting ensembles (e.g., Q32-s + G106-s) improve precision over single models but sacrifice NPV (68.5% vs. 90.4% for GLM-4.5V alone), whereas UPE's strict unanimous voting recovers NPV to 93.3% at a modest precision cost. The end-to-end SFT validation confirms that UPE-identified reward models translate to practical gains: filtering 4,370 rollouts to 1,532 trajectories yields a 33% relative improvement in agent success rate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the reward model evaluation lineage (RewardBench, AgentRewardBench, Agent-RewardBench) to the specific domain of computer-using agents operating desktop software, introducing the first benchmark that jointly evaluates both outcome reward models (ORM) and process reward models (PRM) across 10 software categories. It builds directly on OSWorld as the trajectory source and adopts prompt templates from three prior CUA reward model works (ZeroGUI, OpenCUA reflector, SE-WSM), but departs from them by providing a standardized, multi-model, multi-prompt evaluation protocol rather than evaluating a single proposed system. The UPE ensemble method extends classical unanimous voting (Kuncheva, 2014) by combining strict consensus on both positive and negative predictions with strategic prompt-template diversity, a combination not previously explored in the reward model literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CUARewardBench fills a critical gap: as CUA training pipelines increasingly rely on VLM-based reward models for trajectory filtering and RL signal provision, there was no standardized way to assess whether these reward models are actually reliable. The finding that general visual reasoning capability dominates over CUA-specific training for reward evaluation (with GUI-OWL-32B underperforming its base Qwen2.5VL-32B) challenges the assumption that domain specialization always transfers to evaluation tasks, and the 33% relative downstream agent improvement from reward-filtered data validates the benchmark's practical utility. Key open questions remain: PRM performance lags substantially behind ORM (suggesting step-level verification needs dedicated architectures), purely vision-based reward models face a theoretical ceiling due to partial state observability (motivating hybrid verification with script-based checkers), and deployment efficiency for large-scale reward inference is unaddressed.
