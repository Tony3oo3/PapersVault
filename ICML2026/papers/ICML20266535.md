## Problem

The ML community increasingly frames endangered language revitalization as a scaling problem—more data, larger models, broader benchmark coverage—yet the paper argues this is a false promise. The actual limiting constraint is not data volume or model capacity but a methodological and evaluative misalignment: benchmark-driven pipelines privilege abstract accuracy metrics (BLEU, perplexity, F1) over cultural fidelity, community trust, and situated use, producing brittle or culturally hollow outputs that fail to support real revitalization.

## Input / Output

The paper examines text-based ML pipelines applied to endangered languages, where inputs are typically web-scraped corpora, archival documents, or LLM-generated synthetic data, and outputs are language identifications, OCR transcriptions, machine translations, or NER annotations. The critical finding is that these outputs degrade in cultural and contextual appropriateness as scale increases: LID systems misclassify phonemized Navajo as Icelandic or Lingala, OCR flattens interlinear glosses and idiosyncratic typefaces in community documents, and synthetic augmentation yields diminishing or negative returns beyond small curated thresholds.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is the 'epistemic integration' framework, which reorients ML practice for endangered languages from benchmark optimization toward culturally grounded design and evaluation. It is supported by three empirical cases—synthetic data diminishing returns in NER and NMT, LID brittleness across endangered languages, and OCR failure on nonstandard orthographies—that collectively demonstrate scaling produces diminishing returns and amplified misalignment. The proposed reorientation includes culturally contextual ML design (matching paradigms to oral vs. textual traditions, ceremonial registers, and data sovereignty norms), plural evaluation that adapts to linguistic structure (e.g., morpheme-level metrics for polysynthetic languages like Yup'ik), participatory authorship with community members, and a required framing review to prevent extractive or savior narratives in publications.

## Results

This is a position paper without a single quantitative results table. The empirical evidence is drawn from three cases: (1) synthetic data augmentation for NER across 11 low-resource languages shows negligible improvement for several languages, with small curated datasets consistently outperforming large synthetic volumes (Kamath & Vajjala, 2025); (2) Google's LID system misclassifies phonemized Navajo as typologically unrelated languages (e.g., Icelandic, Lingala) despite Navajo having 170,000+ speakers; (3) general-purpose OCR models fail on endangered language materials with nonstandard orthographies, handwritten marginalia, and interlinear glosses, even as model size increases. The paper's central claim is that performance plateaus quickly and further scaling amplifies cultural misalignment rather than closing gaps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines. Instead, it critiques the prevailing scaling paradigm using three empirical cases as evidence. In synthetic data generation, the paper contrasts quantity-driven augmentation (large LLM-generated corpora) against small curated datasets, finding the latter consistently superior for NER and NMT tasks (Bartelds et al., 2023; Kamath & Vajjala, 2025). In LID, it contrasts high-coverage systems (Google LangID ~200 languages, WiLI-2018 235 languages) against actual reliability for endangered languages, showing that increased nominal coverage does not translate to correct identification (Navajo → Icelandic/Lingala misclassification). In OCR, it contrasts scaling general-purpose models against the need for script-specific, context-aware systems, arguing that quality over quantity is the appropriate strategy. The paper positions 'epistemic integration' (community validation, cultural fidelity, participatory design) as the alternative to benchmark-driven scaling, but does not provide a head-to-head quantitative comparison between the two paradigms.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper departs from the dominant ML paradigm for endangered languages—scaling data volume, expanding benchmark coverage, and relying on transfer learning or synthetic augmentation—by synthesizing existing empirical failures (LID misclassification, OCR brittleness, diminishing returns from synthetic data) into a unified critique. It draws on critical ML scholarship (Bender & Friedman, Bird, Caswell et al.) and sociolinguistic literature (Fishman, Hale, Roche) to argue that the field's evaluation and modeling assumptions lack epistemic fit with culturally embedded languages. Rather than proposing a new architecture or dataset, the paper repurposes findings from LID, OCR, NMT, and NER to advocate for 'epistemic integration': community-defined evaluation, culturally contextual design, and participatory authorship as methodological requirements rather than downstream concerns.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a deeply entrenched assumption in multilingual ML—that insufficient data is the primary bottleneck for endangered languages—and redirects attention to evaluation design, cultural fidelity, and community trust as the actual limiting constraints. Open questions include how to operationalize plural, community-defined evaluation in a way that remains reproducible and comparable, how to fund and structure long-term collaborative research that resists the publication-cycle incentive structure, and whether regionally organized forums can sustainably replace monolithic global benchmarks. As a position paper, it offers no new empirical results or methods, so its impact depends on whether the field adopts its framing; the concrete recommendations (framing reviews, shared authorship norms, community-led evaluation) remain to be tested in practice.
