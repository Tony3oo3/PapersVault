## Problem

Multi-Agent Debate (MAD) improves final-answer accuracy, yet it remains unclear whether iterative debate genuinely improves individual agents' beliefs or merely benefits from aggregation. Prior work shows debate can cause correct answers to flip to incorrect ones and that homogeneous agents stagnate, but no principled framework explains when peer information exchange yields net benefit versus net harm. This paper asks: what uncertainty dynamics govern the trade-off between epistemic information gain and aleatoric reasoning instability during debate?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263733/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each agent receives a math problem x and a debate context c_{i,t} built by concatenating all peer agents' previous-round responses (reasoning steps plus final answers) with role instructions via a uniform mapping function Φ. The paper deliberately removes the final aggregation stage (majority voting, LLM-as-a-Judge) so evaluation reflects each agent's individual post-debate response y_{i,t}, which contains intermediate reasoning steps and a final answer extracted via special tokens and verified by exact string match. Uncertainty is estimated by sampling G=16 rollouts per prompt per turn and constructing answer-level frequency distributions over a common support set across all agents.

## Method

The paper introduces a Bayesian uncertainty decomposition that splits answer-level total uncertainty into system epistemic uncertainty (cross-agent disagreement, measured by generalized Jensen-Shannon divergence) and system aleatoric uncertainty (within-agent response instability, measured by average per-agent entropy), showing that successful debate requires high epistemic gain under controlled aleatoric cost. Building on this, UMAD extends GRPO into a multi-agent Dec-POMDP with two uncertainty-guided mechanisms: (1) an Aleatoric Uncertainty-Aware Advantage that modulates the standard group-normalized advantage by a weight W(U)=exp(−α_au·U) computed from token-level negative log-probability, down-weighting unstable generations; and (2) an Epistemic Influence Intrinsic Reward r^{eu}_{i,t} that credits an agent for the average correctness improvement it induces in peers' next-round rollouts. Trained with only 2-round debates on MATH, UMAD generalizes to 5-round inference, with the weaker agent in heterogeneous pairs (Qwen2.5-3B + Qwen3-4B) gaining the largest improvements, confirming that the method teaches stable absorption of complementary peer evidence rather than mere conformity.

## Results

In heterogeneous MAD (Qwen2.5-3B + Qwen3-4B) at T=5, UMAD raises the weaker agent's average pass@1 accuracy from 54.8% (Zero-Shot) to 66.5%, a +11.7 pp gain, while the stronger agent reaches 65.8%. In homogeneous MAD (two Qwen2.5-3B) at T=5, UMAD achieves 42.0% and 43.1% average accuracy for the two agents, outperforming Zero-Shot (39.8%, 40.6%) and IPPO (41.6%, 41.9%). UMAD is trained on only 2 debate rounds (T_train=2) yet generalizes to T=5 inference, with the weaker heterogeneous agent improving further from 57.2% at T=2 to 66.5% at T=5.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UMAD is compared against two baselines: Zero-Shot MAD (frozen models, no training) and Standard IPPO (independent multi-agent RL without uncertainty guidance). In heterogeneous MAD at T=5, UMAD decisively outperforms both baselines on the weaker agent (A0): 66.5% vs. 60.2% (IPPO, +6.3 pp) and 54.8% (Zero-Shot, +11.7 pp). On the stronger agent (A1), UMAD (65.8%) slightly trails IPPO (67.4%) by 1.6 pp but matches Zero-Shot (65.7%), indicating the gains are asymmetric and concentrated in the weaker model's peer-information utilization. In homogeneous MAD at T=5, UMAD wins on both agents (42.0%, 43.1%) over IPPO (41.6%, 41.9%) and Zero-Shot (39.8%, 40.6%), though margins are modest (1–2.5 pp), consistent with limited epistemic diversity in same-model debates. An additional Single-GRPO Pair baseline (independently fine-tuned agents, 15 epochs) reaches 60.2% on A0 at T=5, still below UMAD's 66.5%, confirming that debate co-training with uncertainty guidance yields gains beyond standalone RL.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) from single-agent RLVR to a multi-agent debate setting formulated as a Dec-POMDP, building on the IPPO-style independent learning paradigm (De Witt et al., 2020) and the uncertainty-aware advantage shaping mechanism from UCAS (Xie et al., 2025). It departs from the dominant MAD literature that focuses on inference-time prompting, aggregation rules, or communication topology by introducing a Bayesian uncertainty decomposition (Jensen-Shannon-based epistemic/aleatoric split at the answer level) as both an analytical diagnostic and a source of trainable reward signals. Unlike concurrent MAD diversity studies that propose inference-time protocols or anonymization, this work connects the uncertainty trade-off directly to policy optimization, making the epistemic-gain-versus-aleatoric-cost balance an explicit training objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The epistemic-gain/aleatoric-cost decomposition provides a principled, operational lens for diagnosing why debates succeed or fail, moving beyond post-hoc accuracy metrics to explain the mechanism of belief change during debate. The finding that uncertainty-guided training yields asymmetric gains primarily for the weaker agent in heterogeneous pairs suggests a practical pathway for leveraging model diversity without requiring all agents to be equally strong. Limitations include evaluation on only two agents, small-scale models (3B–4B), and math reasoning alone; whether the framework generalizes to larger agent counts, non-numeric domains, or frontier-scale models remains open. The answer-level (rather than token-level or reasoning-step-level) uncertainty proxy also limits granularity of the diagnostic.
