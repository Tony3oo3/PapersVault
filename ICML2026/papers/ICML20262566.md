## Problem

Existing chart editing benchmarks rely on intermediate code representations (e.g., matplotlib scripts or Vega-Lite specs), evaluating whether models generate correct code rather than whether they can directly transform a chart image into an edited output. This code-mediated paradigm decouples visual perception from editing execution and leaves the true end-to-end image-to-image chart editing capability of multimodal models largely unexplored and insufficiently evaluated.

## Input / Output

The benchmark provides triplets of (original chart image, natural-language editing instruction, reference edited chart image), where images average ~1584×1161 pixels and span 10 chart types. Editing instructions are concise (average 22 words) and are deliberately agnostic to code syntax, describing edits as a user would through a GUI. The 12 task types split into 8 local edits (numeric, scale, color, title, font, legend, axis, visual elements) and 4 global edits (conversion, order, filtering, reference elements), with 565 local and 423 global samples across 808 unique images.

## Method

ChartE³ is constructed via a five-stage pipeline: (1) collecting ~10K candidate charts from four sources (ChartBench, ChartX, ChartM³, Chart2Code), (2) diversity filtering via CLIP embeddings and k-means clustering to retain ~100 representative images per chart type, (3) chart-code pair generation using Gemini-2.5-Pro with a reflection loop (render → CLIP-similarity check → revise, up to 3 iterations) to ensure >99% code fidelity, (4) instruction generation where an LLM samples an editing primitive, produces edited code, renders it, and verbalizes the code-to-code diff as a natural-language instruction, and (5) manual filtering by three human experts to remove invalid or ambiguous samples. The code serves solely as an annotation aid; models are evaluated in a pure image-to-image setting. Evaluation combines five objective metrics (SSIM, PSNR, CLIP similarity, DINO similarity, LPIPS) with two GPT-5.1-based subjective scores (Correctness and Consistency on a 1–5 scale), whose alignment with human judgments is validated via pairwise accuracy (0.79/0.87) and NDCG@7 (0.92/0.97).

## Results

On ChartE³, Nano Banana (closed-source) achieves the best overall performance with SSIM 0.83, PSNR 17.53, CLIP 0.96, DINO 0.96, LPIPS 0.23, and GPT-judged Correctness/Consistency of 4.18/4.65. GPT-Image-1.5 follows closely (SSIM 0.79, PSNR 15.05, Correctness 3.79, Consistency 4.28). Among open-source models, Qwen-Image-Edit leads (SSIM 0.83, PSNR 16.83) but trails closed-source models substantially on subjective scores (Correctness 2.58 vs. 4.18). Global editing tasks (Order, Filtering) show the largest performance gaps, with even Nano Banana dropping to Correctness 3.42 and 3.59 respectively, while most open-source models fall below 2.0.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Nano Banana dominates all seven baselines across every objective and subjective metric, achieving the highest PSNR (17.53 vs. 15.05 for GPT-Image-1.5) and the lowest LPIPS (0.23 vs. 0.36), while also leading in GPT-judged Correctness (4.18) and Consistency (4.65). GPT-Image-1.5 is the strongest runner-up, matching Nano Banana on CLIP (0.96) but trailing on PSNR by 2.48 dB and LPIPS by 0.13. Among open-source models, Qwen-Image-Edit matches Nano Banana on SSIM (0.83) and approaches it on PSNR (16.83 vs. 17.53), yet its Correctness score (2.58) is only 62% of Nano Banana's, exposing a large instruction-following gap. BAGEL achieves competitive SSIM (0.82) and PSNR (16.28) but drops sharply on subjective scores (Correctness 1.84), indicating it preserves visual structure without faithfully executing edits. Step1X-Edit, InstructPix2Pix, and OmniGen2 all score below 2.0 on Correctness, with OmniGen2 being the weakest overall (SSIM 0.55, PSNR 8.70, LPIPS 0.52). A code-mediated GPT-5.1 baseline (93.14% execution success) still underperforms Nano Banana on SSIM (0.77 vs. 0.83), PSNR (13.59 vs. 17.53), and LPIPS (0.42 vs. 0.23), suggesting that end-to-end image editing can surpass code-generation pipelines in perceptual fidelity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant code-mediated chart editing paradigm (ChartM3, ChartX, ChartEdit, Plot2Code), where models generate or modify rendering code that is then executed by an engine, and instead formulates chart editing as a direct image-to-image transformation task. It also distinguishes itself from the concurrent FigEdit benchmark by covering both local (appearance-level) and global (data-centric) editing with reference target images, and from chart understanding benchmarks (ChartQA, MMC) by targeting generation rather than comprehension. The data construction pipeline repurposes existing chart datasets (ChartBench, ChartX, ChartM3, Chart2Code) and leverages CLIP-based k-means clustering for diversity sampling, while using Gemini-2.5-Pro for chart-code pair synthesis and GPT-5.1 for instruction verbalization and evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ChartE³ is the first comprehensive benchmark to systematically evaluate end-to-end chart image editing, filling a gap where prior work either assessed code correctness or chart comprehension but not direct visual transformation fidelity. The finding that even the strongest model (Nano Banana) drops from ~4.8 correctness on appearance edits to ~3.4 on data-centric global edits, and that open-source models achieve high SSIM yet low instruction-following scores, reveals that perceptual similarity metrics are insufficient proxies for editing quality. Open questions include whether the gap between local and global editing can be closed with better training signals, whether code-mediated and end-to-end paradigms can be unified, and how the benchmark extends to interactive or 3D visualizations.
