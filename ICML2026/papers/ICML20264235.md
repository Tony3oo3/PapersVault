## Problem

Agentic theorem provers combine LLM policies, retrieval, search, and proof assistant verifiers, yet it remains unclear which components actually improve finite-budget proof success and why they help on real mathematical workloads. Classical proof theory characterizes whether a proof exists, and complexity theory characterizes worst-case hardness, but neither explains the probability that a specific algorithm generates a verified proof within a fixed compute budget on the biased problem distributions it actually receives.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a formal theorem statement (encoded as an initial proof state with open goals and local context), a verifier-call budget B, and depth-wise learned action-value functions as input. It produces either a verified formal proof (when the greedy prover succeeds within budget) or a statistical provability bound quantifying the gap between the learned prover's success probability and the optimal prover's. The bound is structured as an occupancy-weighted sum of per-depth action-value errors, with the leading multiplier being the learned prover's average truncated proof length—a quantity directly readable from proof traces.

## Method

The paper formalizes proof search as a finite-horizon reachability MDP where states are proof obligations with verifier feedback, actions are prover moves (tactics, lemma invocations), and transitions are the verifier's deterministic response, showing that under a faithful state abstraction, optimal MDP success probability coincides exactly with syntactic provability within the same budget. The analyzed pipeline performs depth-wise offline action-value regression: for each remaining horizon t, it learns Q_t*(x,a) = V_{t-1}*(F(x,a)) by minimizing worst-case deviation from verifier-backed binary rollout labels, then proves greedily at test time by selecting argmax actions. The main theorem bounds the provability gap by an occupancy-weighted sum of uniform action-value errors, where each error decomposes into approximation error, a geometric coverage term depending on effective local dimension and Lipschitz constants, and Monte Carlo label noise. Under an action-gap margin condition, the linear dependence on error improves to a fast rate with exponent β+1. The key interpretability result is that the leading complexity multiplier is the learned prover's average truncated proof length, so any mechanism that shortens proofs, improves margins, or localizes the learning problem to better-covered regions directly reduces end-to-end loss.

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical experiments or quantitative benchmark results. The main result (Theorem 3.1) establishes that the statistical provability gap between a learned greedy prover and the optimal finite-budget prover is bounded by 2·Len_B^π̂(q₀)·ε̄_B, where Len_B^π̂(q₀) is the learned prover's average truncated proof length and ε̄_B is a uniform bound on depth-wise action-value estimation error. The error ε_t decomposes into three terms: approximation error (inf over hypothesis class), a coverage/geometry term scaling as (L_{H,t}+L_{Q,t})·c_t^{-1/d_t}·(log(4N_t/δ_t)/N_t)^{1/d_t}, and rollout noise scaling as 2√(log(4N_t/δ_t)/(2m_t)). Under an action-gap margin condition with exponent β, the bound improves to a fast rate with (2ε_t)^{β+1} replacing the linear ε_t dependence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is a theoretical analysis and does not run empirical comparisons against baseline systems. The 'comparisons' are between different design choices interpreted through the lens of the provability bound: (1) proof-shortening mechanisms (decomposition, lemma introduction) reduce the occupancy-weighted sum by making P(T_G ≥ s) decay faster, directly shrinking the end-to-end loss multiplier; (2) retrieval and representation learning help by reducing the effective local dimension d_t, increasing the coverage constant c_t, or making Q_t^* smoother (lower L_{Q,t}); (3) verifier feedback improves both the rollout labels (reducing the noise term) and the action-gap margins (enabling the fast-rate regime); (4) the theory explicitly does not contradict worst-case hardness — it explains why agentic provers succeed on biased theorem distributions q_0 (library idioms, recurring patterns) while adversarial or uniform distributions over hard instances remain intractable. The paper positions itself as complementary to Sonoda et al. (2026), which proves an exponential sample-complexity separation between flat and hierarchical provers under cut-aware structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the finite-horizon reachability MDP and offline action-value regression machinery from reinforcement learning and statistical learning theory to analyze agentic theorem provers, a setting where prior work (LeanDojo, DeepSeek-Prover, TacticToe, GPT-f) has been almost entirely empirical. It explicitly distinguishes itself from Bourbaki's MDP-for-proving approach by using the MDP as an analytical lens rather than an implemented search framework, and from classical proof theory by replacing the binary existence question with a finite-budget success probability on a biased instance stream. The connection to interactive learning theory (Angluin's L*) and test-time scaling literature is drawn as a conceptual bridge, but the paper's core contribution is a new statistical provability bound whose leading multiplier is the learned prover's average truncated proof length.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The bound provides the first component-sensitive theoretical account of why retrieval, verifier feedback, representation geometry, and proof-shortening mechanisms improve finite-budget proof success on realistic (biased) theorem workloads, without contradicting worst-case hardness. The average truncated proof length as the main multiplier is directly measurable from proof traces, making the theory actionable for diagnosing prover failures. Key limitations include the restriction to greedy proving after offline regression (no beam search, backtracking, or decomposition policies), idealized oracle rollout labels, and compact concentration assumptions that may not hold globally. Open questions include extending the Bellman-certificate analysis to richer planners and empirically estimating the bound's quantities (effective dimension, action-gap tails, coverage constants) from real prover traces.
