## Problem

Standard attribution methods produce overcomplete saliency maps that highlight both relevant and irrelevant features, failing to distinguish class-discriminative evidence from loosely associated regions. The paper argues this lack of focus stems not from the attribution methods themselves but from the common practice of computing attributions on a single target logit, which discards the model's inherent softmax-based discriminative mechanism and ignores competing classes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264566/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image, a pre-trained classifier (CNN or ViT), and a set of comparison classes C' (e.g., top-k most probable classes or predefined quadrant classes). It first computes standard per-class attribution maps for each class in C', then applies a spatial softmax across classes at every pixel to produce a local class-probability distribution. The final output for a target class c is the original attribution map element-wise multiplied by its local class probability and masked to retain only pixels where c exceeds chance level (1/|C'|), yielding a refined, class-specific saliency map of the same spatial dimensions as the input.

## Method

The Attribution Lens (AL) is a training-free, plug-and-play post-processing refinement that reintroduces the competitive nature of the softmax into attribution maps without suffering from gradient vanishing. For a chosen set of classes C', AL computes per-class attribution maps using any existing method, applies a spatial softmax over classes at each pixel (with temperature t) to obtain local class dominance probabilities, scales the target class attribution by its local probability, and thresholds out pixels where the target class is at or below chance. To increase robustness, AL averages over multiple temperatures (1/t ∈ {1, 5, 100}). The key theoretical insight is that the softmax gradient vanishes for confident predictions (making direct softmax attribution ineffective), so AL moves the contrastive mechanism from the saturated output layer directly into the attribution maps, where it can selectively discount evidence shared with competing classes while preserving class-specific signal.

## Results

Attribution Lens (AL) consistently improves all 18 tested attribution methods across 7 architectures, with average gains of up to ~2× on localization metrics. On the Quad-ImageNet grid-pointing game, ResNet-50 methods see average improvements of +53% RA, +37% IoU, and +24% F1, while ViT-base-16 methods see +29% RA, +103% IoU, and +62% F1. On the more challenging Part-Quad-ImageNet benchmark, ViT IoU improvements reach +182% on average (e.g., T-attn goes from 0.07 to 0.29 IoU, a 321% gain). Insertion AUC improves by 8–11% for CNNs and ~2% for ViTs, and randomization sanity checks show AL-enhanced attributions degrade more appropriately after layer randomization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AL is a plug-and-post-processing refinement, so it does not compete against baselines but enhances them; every one of the 18 tested attribution methods improves or stays on par after AL is applied. The largest relative gains appear for methods that produce the most diffuse maps: Guided Backpropagation (GBP) on ResNet-50 sees RA more than double (+144% on Quad-ImageNet), and LRP gains +97% IoU. For ViT-based methods, attention-rollout approaches (Bi-attn, T-attn, InFlow) benefit most dramatically, with IoU improvements of +180% to +321% on Part-Quad-ImageNet, because AL filters the uniformly high saliency that characterizes transformer attributions. Gradient-based methods that are already relatively focused (e.g., raw Gradient saliency on ViT) show only marginal gains (+2–11%), confirming AL's effect is to sharpen rather than create signal. On insertion tests, CNN methods gain 8–11% AUC on average (e.g., GBP on ResNet-50: 0.52→0.65), while ViT methods gain only ~2%, consistent with their already tighter baseline maps. Perturbation-based methods (Shapley Values, LIME, Occlusion) in Table 4/5 show similar consistent improvements of +20–60% on RA/IoU for the grid-pointing benchmarks, confirming AL's architecture- and method-agnostic nature.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard attribution pipeline, which computes saliency maps with respect to a single target-class logit, by arguing that this discards the model's inherent contrastive mechanism (the softmax). Conceptually, it draws on the multi-concept normalization idea from ConceptAttention (Helbling et al., 2025) for diffusion models, but repurposes it as a post-hoc, pixel-wise softmax over per-class attribution maps. Rather than proposing a new attribution method, it introduces Attribution Lens (AL) as a model- and method-agnostic refinement that can be plugged into any existing gradient- or perturbation-based attribution technique, positioning itself as a correction to how attributions are consumed rather than a new way to compute them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: AL is training-free, architecture-agnostic, and requires only computing a few extra attribution maps, making it immediately adoptable for any existing explainability pipeline. The finding that class-discriminative structure was already latent in standard single-logit attributions reframes a long-standing criticism of attribution methods (overcompleteness, lack of specificity) as a pipeline issue rather than a methodological one. Open questions include whether the modest gains on natural multi-object scenes (COCO) reflect a fundamental limit of pixel-wise contrast or a need for richer class-set selection, and whether the approach extends cleanly to non-visual modalities (language, audio) where the 'spatial location' structure differs.
