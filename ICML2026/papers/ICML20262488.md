## Problem

Evolutionary multi-view classification (EMVC) assumes training labels are noise-free, yet real-world annotation errors introduce label noise that distorts fitness landscapes and causes fitness evaluation bias (FEB), trapping evolutionary search in local optima. While FEB was previously studied from the view-imbalance perspective, the more fundamental data-level cause—harmful supervision from mislabeled samples—remained unexplored. Moreover, existing label-noise methods adopt a 'detect-and-remove' strategy, implicitly assuming boundary-near mislabeled samples are irrecoverable, which the paper shows is not always true.

## Input / Output

The method takes multi-view training data (pre-extracted features such as audio spectrograms, video frame embeddings, multilingual text vectors, or image features) with potentially noisy labels, along with a clean test set. It first produces a purified training dataset D* where detected noisy samples have their labels corrected via prototype matching, then drives evolutionary search over binary-tree-encoded multi-view fusion structures to output a final multi-view classifier that predicts class labels. For image-based experiments (CIFAR-10N/100N), the anchor model is ResNet-34 operating on 32×32 RGB images; for feature-based multi-view datasets, the anchor model uses a fixed full-view additive fusion architecture with per-view encoders mapping to a unified 128-dim space.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage 'detect-then-calibrate' data purification framework applied before evolutionary search begins. Stage 1 (gradient space detection): an anchor model with fixed full-view additive fusion is trained on the noisy dataset, per-sample gradients of the last linear layer are extracted to form a high-dimensional gradient space, and Isolation Forest identifies outliers (at a configurable ratio κ%) as potential noisy samples. Stage 2 (feature space prototype calibration): the anchor model is retrained on the trusted (non-outlier) subset, fused feature vectors are extracted, class prototypes are computed as per-class feature means, and each noisy sample is reassigned to the class whose prototype has maximum cosine similarity to its feature vector. The key insight motivating calibration over removal is the experimentally demonstrated discrepancy between human and model decision boundaries: samples that are confusable to human annotators (e.g., Deer/Horse, Frog/Ship) remain separable in model representation space, so their labels can be reliably corrected rather than discarded. The resulting purified dataset D* then drives the standard EMVC loop—population initialization over view/fusion-operator binary trees, fitness evaluation via test accuracy, and iterative selection/crossover/mutation—yielding more accurate fitness landscapes and better-converged multi-view models.

## Results

On the label noise learning benchmarks, GFD-EMVC achieves 92.08% on CIFAR-10N (Aggregate), 90.68% on CIFAR-10N (Random), 84.46% on CIFAR-10N (Worst, 40.21% noise), and 61.47% on CIFAR-100N (40.20% noise), outperforming the strongest baseline OGA by 0.74% and 0.48% under the two most severe noise settings. On the five multi-view datasets, applying the proposed purification consistently improves all tested methods: for example, EFB-EMVC on YouTubeFace rises from 81.26% to 82.05%, and the fixed-fusion Mul method on Reuters5 jumps from 28.15% to 69.46%. The purification also drives evolutionary search to converge to better solutions, as verified by retraining the best evolved individuals on both original and purified data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the label noise learning benchmarks (Table 1), GFD-EMVC outperforms all 11 baselines across all four noise settings. The closest competitor is OGA (Chhabra et al., ICML 2025), which uses the same iForest gradient outlier detection but discards detected samples; GFD-EMVC's calibrate-instead-of-remove strategy yields +0.74% on CIFAR-10N Worst (84.46 vs 83.72) and +0.48% on CIFAR-100N (61.47 vs 60.99). Influence-function-based methods (GradientTracing, LiSSA, DataInf, Self-LiSSA, Self-DataInf) and noisy-label correction methods (Normalized Margin, Self-Confidence, ConfidenceEntropy) all trail by 0.5–2.3% under high-noise settings. On the multi-view experiments (Table 4), the comparison is structured as each method trained on original vs. purified data rather than GFD-EMVC vs. a single competitor; all 16 method–dataset combinations improve, with the largest gains on weaker baselines (e.g., Mul on Reuters5: 28.15→69.46; RCML on YouTubeFace: 34.48→45.33) and more modest but consistent gains on strong EMVC methods (e.g., EFB-EMVC on YouTubeFace: 81.26→82.05; EDF on MVoxCeleb: 70.93→77.26). The purification also changes the evolutionary convergence direction: under original data, EDF on MVoxCeleb converges to Avg-dominated individuals, while under purified data it converges to Add-dominated individuals, confirming that label noise distorts the fitness landscape.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the EMVC (Evolutionary Multi-View Classification) line of work by Liang et al. (2021, 2024, 2025c), specifically addressing Fitness Evaluation Bias (FEB) from a data-level perspective, complementing EFB-EMVC which attributed FEB to view imbalance. For the noisy-sample detection stage, it directly adapts OGA (Chhabra et al., ICML 2025), which uses Isolation Forest on gradient-space outliers, but departs from OGA's 'detect-and-delete' strategy by replacing removal with feature-space prototype calibration. The calibration step draws on the broader label-noise-learning literature (influence functions, gradient tracing) but repurposes it within the evolutionary search context, where preserving cross-view complementary information from 'correctable' mislabeled samples is particularly valuable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first evidence that label noise distorts fitness landscapes in EMVC, opening a new axis for FEB research beyond view imbalance. The practical 'detect-then-calibrate' framework is simple and plug-and-play across multi-view paradigms (fixed fusion, trusted MVC, EMVC), making it broadly applicable. However, a key limitation is that the method only handles 'correctable mislabels'—samples near the model's own decision boundary remain uncalibratable—and the multi-view experiments rely on synthetic random-flip noise rather than real human annotation errors, weakening the practical grounding. Open questions include whether more sophisticated calibration (e.g., iterative re-estimation, view-aware prototypes) can handle model-boundary samples, and what other data-level or structural factors induce FEB.
