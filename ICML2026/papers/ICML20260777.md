## Problem

Most genome language models are pretrained with sequence reconstruction objectives (next-token prediction or masked language modeling) borrowed from NLP, yet recent work shows these models often fail to capture meaningful biological signal and can underperform simple baselines. Although evolutionary rate is one of the most powerful predictors of genome function, it has not been systematically investigated as a direct pretraining target for gLMs, leaving open whether it can improve learned representations.

## Input / Output

The model consumes single genomic DNA sequences at single-nucleotide resolution (2 kb context windows from hg38, with repetitive and low-quality regions excluded). During training, PhyloP scores from the Zoonomia 241-mammalian alignment serve as supervision targets; at inference, only the sequence is needed. The evolutionary rate head outputs a full Gaussian distribution (predicted mean and log variance) per position rather than a point estimate, while the sequence head outputs 4-class nucleotide logits. Downstream evaluations use the final hidden-layer representations pooled over regions of interest.

## Method

The paper introduces two evolutionary-rate pretraining tasks that parallel standard sequence reconstruction: Current Evolution Prediction (CEP), which autoregressively predicts the PhyloP score at position i from the sequence up to that position, and Masked Evolution Modeling (MEM), which predicts PhyloP scores at all positions from a sequence with 15% of positions masked. These tasks are composable with NTP and MLM respectively, enabling controlled ablations across three training regimes (sequence-only, evolution-only, both) using identical architectures and data. The Gamba model family instantiates this with ArGamba (~66M params, Jamba-based hybrid Transformer-Mamba with MoE) for autoregressive tasks and Bi-Gamba (~4M params, Caduceus-based bidirectional Mamba) for masked tasks. The evolutionary rate loss is a Gaussian negative log-likelihood, combined with cross-entropy for sequence reconstruction by simple addition, and the models are trained for ~7 epochs on ~1 billion bp of filtered human genome.

## Results

On the functional region separation benchmark (zero-shot 1-NN), the best Gamba model (Bi-Gamba MEM-only) achieves 72.9% balanced accuracy on the stringent upstream-localization task, outperforming all sequence-only Gamba variants (65.9%) and matching Caduceus (70.0%) while using fewer parameters. On the ATG codon context classification task, ArGamba NTP+CEP reaches 53.5% balanced accuracy versus 44.6% for NTP-only, and Bi-Gamba MEM-only reaches 56.2% versus 46.9% for MLM-only. On variant effect prediction (ClinVar missense AUROC), Bi-Gamba MEM-only achieves 0.641 versus 0.476 for MLM-only, and ArGamba NTP+CEP achieves 0.595 versus 0.528 for NTP-only. Across all three evaluation suites, evolutionary-rate-pretrained Gamba models (4M–66M params) achieve 95.5–97.7% of Evo2's (7B params) performance on functional region tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Evo2 (7B params), the strongest external baseline, Gamba models (4M–66M params) achieve 95.5–97.7% of Evo2's balanced accuracy on functional region tasks, and on VEP, Bi-Gamba MEM-only (0.641 AUROC on ClinVar) and ArGamba NTP+CEP (0.595) match or exceed Evo2's likelihood-based scores (0.856 on ClinVar) when evaluated via predicted evolutionary rate on non-coding and GWAS fine-mapped tasks. Against Nucleotide Transformer multi-species (498M params), Gamba is competitive on upstream localization (72.9% vs 72.1%) but trails on ATG classification (56.2% vs 80.1%), where NT's 6-mer tokenization provides additional context. Against PhyloGPN (83M params), the closest methodological competitor (also predicting evolutionary rate), Gamba outperforms it on all four functional region tasks (e.g., 72.9% vs 66.4% on upstream localization) but underperforms on ATG classification (56.2% vs 74.2%). Against Caduceus (7.7M params), the architecture Bi-Gamba is based on, evolutionary-rate pretraining yields a clear 2.9–7.0 percentage point gain (72.9% vs 70.0% on upstream localization). HyenaDNA (6.6M params) performs worst among pretrained gLMs, with its pretrained model actually scoring below its random initialization on multiclass region classification (34.4% vs 38.6%), suggesting its pretraining captures little biological signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Jamba hybrid Transformer-Mamba architecture (for autoregressive ArGamba) and the Caduceus bi-directional Mamba SSM (for Bi-Gamba), repurposing both as backbones for a new pretraining objective. It departs from the dominant gLM pretraining paradigm of sequence reconstruction (NTP/MLM) by introducing evolutionary rate prediction (PhyloP scores) as a composable pretraining signal, and contrasts with MSA-based approaches (GPN-MSA, GPN-STAR) that require multi-species input by operating on single sequences. The closest prior work, PhyloGPN, also predicts evolutionary rate but only at a single central nucleotide per input and does not test composability with sequence reconstruction; this paper predicts rates at every position and enables controlled ablations across sequence-only, evolution-only, and joint training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that evolutionary rate prediction is a powerful and composable pretraining signal for gLMs, with small models (4M–66M parameters) matching or outperforming 7B-parameter models on several variant effect prediction tasks when scored by predicted evolutionary rate. The new zero-shot benchmarks (functional region separation with multiple negative sets, ATG codon context classification) address known evaluation confounders and show that prior claims of gLMs failing to beat random initialization may reflect evaluation design rather than model failure. Key open questions include whether these gains persist at larger scale (the authors note their models are underparametrized), whether alternative evolutionary scores (PhastCons, different MSAs) or richer evolutionary signals (indels, rate heterogeneity) further improve representations, and how evolutionary rate pretraining interacts with multi-species training strategies.
