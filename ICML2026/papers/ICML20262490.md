## Problem

Molecular GNNs achieve strong in-distribution performance but fail under OOD shifts (scaffold, size, assay) because they entangle stable chemical semantics with environment-dependent structural variations, leading to spurious correlations. Existing invariant learning methods either require predefined environment partitions or enforce invariance directly on entangled representations, which is brittle under complex shifts. The paper asks: can we explicitly separate invariant chemical semantics from shift-sensitive nuisance factors to achieve robust OOD generalization?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262490/cropped_page2_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 2D molecular graphs with atom-level features (type, valence, aromaticity) and bond connectivity, encoded by a GNN into a fixed-dimensional entangled vector u_G. The key intermediate output is a semantic invariant representation z_G, defined as a probability-weighted convex combination of K learnable prototypes, alongside an environment-dependent residual e_G = u_G − z_G. Downstream, z_G feeds a prediction head for classification (ROC-AUC) or regression (MAE), and in the conformation-prediction setting the same prototype bottleneck is plugged into a graph transformer to output 3D atomic coordinates.

## Method

MoSIR introduces a learnable semantic prototype dictionary {μ_k}_{k=1}^K that projects the entangled GNN embedding u_G into an explicit semantic space via softmax assignment, yielding an invariant component z_G (a convex combination of prototypes) and a residual e_G. Semantic consistency constraints (intra-molecular reconstruction and inter-molecular smoothness) regularize the prototype space to prevent degenerate solutions. The key mechanism is a bi-level min-max objective: adversarial perturbations δ (bounded by ε) are applied exclusively to the residual e_G with stop-gradient on z_G, simulating plausible environment shifts while preserving the semantic anchor; the inner maximization searches for worst-case δ that maximally disrupts semantic invariance, and the outer minimization updates the encoder, prototypes, and predictor to maintain both accuracy and semantic stability. Theoretically, the prototype bottleneck restricts the predictor to operate within the convex hull of K prototypes, yielding a Rademacher complexity bound of O(LM/√n · √log K) that tightens the OOD generalization gap.

## Results

On the GOOD benchmark, MoSIR achieves the best performance across all 12 OOD settings: on GOOD-HIV (ROC-AUC↑), it reaches 77.13–81.15, surpassing the strongest baseline CFD (76.42–79.28) by 0.7–1.9 points; on GOOD-ZINC (MAE↓), it attains 0.0553–0.1317, outperforming CFD (0.0765–0.1421) by up to 21%; on GOOD-PCBA (AP↑), it scores 20.32–26.83 vs. CFD's 19.18–25.64. On DrugOOD (ROC-AUC↑), MoSIR leads all six settings, e.g., 74.65 on IC50-assay and 79.52 on EC50-assay, exceeding CFD by 0.8–1.2 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoSIR consistently outperforms all 22 baselines across every OOD setting on both GOOD and DrugOOD. The closest competitor is CFD (Wu & Deng, ICLR 2025), which MoSIR surpasses by 0.7–1.9 ROC-AUC points on GOOD-HIV, by 0.011–0.021 MAE on GOOD-ZINC (a 19–21% relative reduction), and by 0.8–1.2 ROC-AUC points on DrugOOD. The second-strongest baseline iMoLD (Zhuang et al., NeurIPS 2023) trails MoSIR by 4.2–6.2 ROC-AUC on GOOD-HIV and 0.046–0.086 MAE on GOOD-ZINC, indicating that latent discrete-space invariance is less effective than explicit prototype-based semantic decomposition. General-purpose OOD methods (ERM, IRM, VREx, GroupDRO, Coral, DANN) and graph-specific methods (MoleOOD, CIGA, CAL, DisC) are substantially outperformed, with gaps of 5–20+ points on ROC-AUC or AP, confirming that generic invariance or substructure identification alone is insufficient for molecular OOD. On DrugOOD, MoSIR also beats MILI (Wang et al., KDD 2024) by 1.0–2.1 ROC-AUC, showing that privileged substructure identification is less robust than prototype-constrained semantic invariance under assay and scaffold shifts.

## Relation to Prior Work

MoSIR builds directly on the iMoLD implementation (Zhuang et al., 2023) but departs from the broader invariant-representation-learning lineage (IRM, VREx, GroupDRO, MoleOOD, CIGA) by introducing a learnable prototype dictionary that explicitly decomposes entangled molecular embeddings into a semantic invariant component and an environment-dependent residual. Rather than enforcing invariance on the full representation or relying on predefined environment partitions, MoSIR applies adversarial perturbations exclusively in the residual space and formulates learning as a bi-level min-max objective, which is a distinct formulation from prior contrastive or causal-disentanglement approaches. The theoretical contribution connects the prototype bottleneck to Rademacher complexity reduction, providing a formal justification absent in most prior molecular OOD methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The prototype-based semantic decomposition offers a principled and theoretically grounded mechanism for molecular OOD generalization that does not require explicit environment annotations or high-quality concept signals, addressing a practical limitation of methods like CFD and iMoLD. The consistent improvements across GOOD and DrugOOD under both covariate and concept shifts, combined with the conformation-prediction results, suggest the learned semantic space is broadly useful beyond a single task. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very large chemical spaces with thousands of distinct semantic concepts, whether the end-to-end learned prototypes can be further aligned with known chemical ontologies for interpretability, and whether the residual-only perturbation strategy remains effective under more complex, multi-factorial distribution shifts.
