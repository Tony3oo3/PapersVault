## Problem

Existing Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL) methods generate pseudo-labels via majority voting, assuming the most frequent answer among sampled trajectories is correct. However, on complex reasoning tasks, correct solutions frequently constitute a 'logical minority'—statistically outvoted by systematic errors—creating a performance ceiling (Maj@N far below Pass@N) and a scalability paradox where increasing sample count N fails to improve pseudo-label quality. The paper asks whether statistical consensus can be replaced by logical consensus, where the model itself evaluates the quality of different reasoning paths.

## Input / Output

The method takes a math problem and N sampled reasoning trajectories (up to 8,192 tokens each) from the current policy model. Trajectories are first grouped by extracted final answer, one representative per group is selected, and each is condensed into a concise 'reasoning skeleton' via a summarization prompt that preserves key derivations while stripping conversational fillers and self-corrected errors. The output is a single pseudo-label (the final answer of the self-reflectively selected trajectory) used to compute binary rewards for all N trajectories, which then drive a GRPO policy update. Notably, SR-TTRL achieves comparable performance to standard TTRL with only N=8 samples versus N=64, an 8× reduction in sampling cost.

## Method

SR-TTRL replaces majority voting with a three-step self-reflective pseudo-labeling mechanism: (1) Candidate Trajectory Pool Construction groups N sampled trajectories by final answer and selects one representative per distinct answer to form a compact, diverse candidate pool; (2) Trajectory Summarization condenses each representative into a logically complete 'reasoning skeleton' via prompt engineering, preserving critical derivations and decision points while removing redundancy to fit the context window; (3) Self-Reflective Verification feeds all summarized candidates side-by-side into the same policy model under a structured reflection prompt, instructing it to cross-compare, verify each derivation step for mathematical accuracy, and select the single most logically sound trajectory. The selected trajectory's final answer becomes the pseudo-label, and all N original trajectories receive binary rewards based on consistency with this label. Policy optimization uses GRPO with group-relative advantages, enabling the model to receive positive reinforcement for correct trajectories even when they belong to a statistical minority. The key distinction from standard TTRL is the shift from a quantity-driven mechanism (frequency counting) to a quality-driven one (qualitative logical verification), which recovers correct solutions that majority voting systematically discards.

## Results

SR-TTRL achieves an average Pass@1 of 68.9 on Qwen3-8B across AIME24, AIME25, AMC23, and MATH-500, improving from the 52.0 static baseline by +16.9. On the most challenging benchmark AIME24, Qwen3-8B reaches 55.8 Pass@1 (vs. 29.1 baseline and 46.7 for standard TTRL). Across all five models tested (Qwen3-1.7B/4B/8B, LLaMA3.2-3B, LLaMA3.1-8B), SR-TTRL consistently outperforms the majority-voting TTRL baseline by an average margin of +7.9 to +11.2 points. Notably, SR-TTRL with only N=8 samples matches TTRL with N=64, demonstrating an 8× reduction in sampling cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is standard TTRL (Zuo et al., 2025), which uses majority voting over sampled trajectories to generate pseudo-labels. SR-TTRL wins on every model–benchmark combination in Table 1, with average gains over TTRL ranging from +7.9 (Qwen3-8B) to +11.2 (LLaMA3.1-8B-Instruct). The largest individual margins appear on the hardest benchmarks: +14.2 on AIME24 for LLaMA3.1-8B-Instruct and +19.2 on AMC23 for the same model. On easier benchmarks like MATH-500, the gap narrows to +2.6–+8.1, consistent with the paper's analysis that majority voting already works reasonably well when the correct answer dominates the sampling distribution. In an oracle comparison (Table 3), SR-TTRL narrows the gap to ground-truth labels from 18.7 points (TTRL) to 9.6 points on AIME24 with Qwen3-8B, recovering nearly half the 'lost potential' that majority voting discards. The speed overhead is modest: SR-TTRL takes 205 s/step vs. 160 s/step for TTRL (+28%), a cost the authors argue is justified by the +9.1 Pass@1 gain.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends TTRL (Zuo et al., 2025), which formulates test-time inference as a local RL problem using majority voting over sampled trajectories to derive pseudo-labels. The key departure is replacing the statistical-consensus pseudo-labeling mechanism with a three-step self-reflective verification pipeline (candidate pool construction, trajectory summarization, and cross-comparative logical evaluation), while retaining GRPO-style policy optimization for the update step. It also situates itself within the broader RL fine-tuning literature (DAPO, REINFORCE++, DeepSeek-R1) and the test-time training lineage (TTT, TTT-LLM), but its core contribution is orthogonal to those algorithmic improvements, targeting the reward-signal quality bottleneck specifically.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the correct answer is a statistical minority in roughly half of solvable problems on competition-level math (AIME24/25) challenges the foundational 'wisdom of the crowd' assumption underpinning majority-voting TTRL, and the proposed self-reflective mechanism recovers nearly half the gap to oracle pseudo-labels at only 28% additional compute. However, the evaluation is confined to mathematical reasoning across five models of two families, with no validation on agentic, multimodal, or non-math domains, leaving open whether self-reflective verification generalizes beyond structured symbolic tasks where logical correctness is well-defined. A key open question is whether the self-reflection quality itself scales with model capability or plateaus, and whether the approach remains effective when the model's own reasoning is systematically biased (e.g., consistent logical traps that the model cannot detect in self-evaluation).
