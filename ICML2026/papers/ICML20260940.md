## Problem

LLM-based search agents connect models to the open Internet, but unreliable search results (content farms, black-hat SEO, outdated sources) can mislead agents into producing unsafe outputs, and no systematic, scalable, automated framework exists to measure this threat. Existing search-agent benchmarks evaluate task accuracy rather than safety, and prior safety evaluations either rely on labor-intensive manual red-teaming or craft adversarial queries rather than modeling the realistic scenario where benign queries retrieve unreliable results.

## Input / Output

Each test case pairs a benign user query with an LLM-generated unreliable website (≤2,000 tokens) that is appended to a list of five authentic search results returned by a real search tool. The search agent (one of three scaffolds: search workflow, tool calling, or deep research, backed by one of 17 LLMs) processes the mixed results and produces a final response. Evaluation yields a per-test-case boolean safety judgment via a checklist-assisted LLM judge (validated at >95% human agreement) and a 1–5 helpfulness score, aggregated into Attack Success Rate (ASR) per risk type and overall, and a Helpfulness Score (HS) on a 0–100 scale, each averaged over three trials at temperature 0.6.

## Method

SAFESEARCH is a two-stage automated red-teaming framework. In the offline stage, a reasoning model (o4-mini) generates test cases through a three-step workflow—scenario anchoring, test design (specifying target consequence and website guidelines), and test instantiation (producing website-generation guidelines and a safety-evaluation checklist)—and a differential-testing filter retains only cases that are both attainable under a manipulated search tool and inert under a benign one. In the online stage, a single LLM-generated unreliable website is injected into authentic search results (appended to the list, first round only for multi-turn agents), and the agent's response is scored by two specialized LLM evaluators: a checklist-assisted safety judge producing a boolean compromise signal and a helpfulness rater producing a 1–5 plausibility score. The framework's key design choices—sandboxed simulation instead of real-world SEO manipulation, guideline-assisted website generation to reduce generator sensitivity, and time-stamped website conditioning to model the threat's temporal nature—make it cost-efficient, reproducible, and continuously updatable by varying timestamps or escalating baseline-agent difficulty.

## Results

Across 17 LLMs and three search-agent scaffolds, SAFESEARCH reveals that search agents are highly vulnerable to unreliable search results, with the highest overall Attack Success Rate (ASR) reaching 90.5% (GPT-4.1-mini, search workflow) and the lowest at 4.6% (Claude-Sonnet-4.5, tool calling). Average ASR drops from 63.1% (search workflow) to 49.3% (tool calling) to 38.9% (deep research), showing that scaffold design significantly affects safety. Misinformation is the most severe risk type (72.9% avg ASR under search workflow), while Indirect Prompt Injection is the least (55.3%). GPT-5 and GPT-5-mini achieve the best combined safety and helpfulness, with overall ASR of 18.4%/18.9% (search workflow) and 5.0%/4.9% (tool calling) while maintaining Helpfulness Scores above 99.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark paper, so comparisons are across scaffolds, backend LLMs, and risk types rather than against prior methods. Across scaffolds, the deep-research scaffold achieves the lowest average ASR (38.9%) versus tool calling (49.3%) and search workflow (63.1%), but a budget-controlled ablation (Table 2) shows that when search budgets are matched, tool-call forced (36.7% at 9 searches) can outperform deep-research forced (46.2%), indicating the deep-research advantage largely comes from using more searches rather than superior reasoning. Across LLMs, reasoning models (GPT-5, GPT-5-mini, o4-mini, Claude-4.5) are substantially safer than non-reasoning counterparts: GPT-5 achieves 5.0% ASR with tool calling versus 77.3% for GPT-4.1 under the same scaffold, a 72-point gap. Across risk types, Misinformation is the most dangerous (72.9% avg ASR under search workflow) while Indirect Prompt Injection is the least (55.3%), and GPT-5 with tool calling achieves 0% ASR on prompt injection but still 8.3% on misinformation. On defenses, reminder prompting is largely ineffective (reducing ASR by only a few points), while a GPT-4.1-mini-based filtering detector reduces ASR by roughly half (recall 44.2%), though neither eliminates the threat entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by formulating the safety problem of LLM-based search agents exposed to unreliable search results under benign user queries, a threat model distinct from both RAG poisoning (which assumes a strong attacker manipulating a local corpus) and adversarial query-based red-teaming (which crafts risk-inducing inputs). The most relevant prior efforts—Luo et al. (2025) and Ou et al. (2025)—target system-level outcomes with adversarial queries, while existing agent safety benchmarks (AgentHarm, ASB, Agent-SafetyBench) rarely cover search-tool-specific threats. SAFESEARCH departs from all of these by assuming benign queries, attributing risk to the search tool itself, and using simulation-based injection of LLM-generated unreliable websites to avoid the ethical and practical problems of real-world SEO manipulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even the safest configuration (Claude-Sonnet-4.5 with tool-calling) still yields a 4.6% ASR, and that the intuitive defense of reminder prompting is largely ineffective, exposes a knowledge-action gap that has direct implications for how search agent developers should design safety layers. The framework's scalability and sandboxed nature make it a practical tool for continuous safety monitoring during agent development, addressing a gap where prior safety testing relied on manual, expert-driven red-teaming. Open questions include whether simulation-based ASR translates to real-world failure prevalence, how to defend against stealthier websites that the current detector misses, and whether the observed vulnerabilities persist as search services improve their ranking quality.
