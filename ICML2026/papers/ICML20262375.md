## Problem

Despite widespread use of LLMs to simulate human behavior in social science, gaming, and recommendation, the extent to which their outputs genuinely resemble real human actions remains unquantified. The Turing test is limited to short conversations with adversarial bias, and existing behavioral benchmarks reduce human behavior to single-step, objective-answer tasks, failing to capture the sequential, subjective, and distributional nature of real human decision-making.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes three types of input: (1) a user's historical behavior sequence (item IDs for shopping, question-answer pairs for Q&A, or check-in attributes for mobility), which is split in half to form a personal profile; (2) scenario-specific context such as a candidate item set, a question to answer, or candidate venues; and (3) large-scale real user behavior data serving as the population reference. The LLM simulator produces a multi-turn sequence of simulated actions, which the user encoder maps into high-dimensional vectors. The evaluator then outputs four scalar scores per model: Distinguishability (classifier error rate), Predictability (mean cosine similarity between evolving user embedding and generated items), Consistency (same-user classifier accuracy on split sequences), and Diversity (area under the user-similarity curve comparing simulated vs. real population distributions).

## Method

The framework has four components: a behavior dataset, an LLM simulator, a user encoder, and a multi-metric evaluator. The LLM simulator uses a carefully engineered multi-turn prompt (instruction + personal profile + context) to generate a sequence of actions, with each generated action appended to the profile for subsequent turns. The user encoder is domain-specific: SASRec (unidirectional transformer over item-ID embeddings) for shopping, e5-base-v2 text embeddings with a bidirectional transformer for Q&A, and e5-base-v2 with a unidirectional transformer for mobility; all are trained via contrastive next-item or behavior-to-user embedding alignment. The evaluator operationalizes three principles: Rationality is split into Distinguishability (a binary classifier trained on 1:1 real/simulated sequences, where higher error = more human-like) and Predictability (cosine similarity between the evolving user embedding and each generated item); Consistency uses a classifier to judge whether two halves of a simulated sequence belong to the same user; Diversity computes nearest-neighbor cosine similarities between every real user and the closest simulated user, integrating the resulting curve. The key design choice is evaluating at the distribution level over sequences rather than against a single ground-truth label, enabling population-level, sequence-aware, and domain-agnostic assessment.

## Results

GPT-4o achieves the best overall human-likeness scores among all evaluated LLMs, with average scores of 0.5052 (Movies&TV), 0.6247 (Worldbuilding), and 0.7153 (Foursquare), yet still trails real users by approximately 10%, 17%, and 10% respectively. Among open-source models, Llama-3.1-405B (0.4560/0.6105/0.7080) and Qwen2.5-72B (0.4501/0.5909/0.7188) are the strongest, with Llama-3.1 leading in consistency (0.8540) and Qwen2.5 leading in distinguishability (0.2380) and predictability (0.1370) on Movies&TV. Small models (<10B parameters) such as Phi-3-mini and Qwen2.5-3B score below 0.26 average on Movies&TV, indicating severe deficiencies in all four evaluation dimensions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o outperforms all open-source models on the average score across all three datasets, leading by 4.9% over Llama-3.1-405B on Movies&TV (0.5052 vs. 0.4560) and by 1.5% on Foursquare (0.7153 vs. 0.7080). Among open-source models, Llama-3.1-405B achieves the highest consistency (0.8540) and competitive diversity (0.6035) on Movies&TV, while Qwen2.5-72B leads in distinguishability (0.2380) and predictability (0.1370), suggesting Qwen2.5 better mimics instant human-like behavior and Llama-3.1 offers more stable long-term simulations. Mistral-large (123B) and Qwen3-32B occupy the mid-range, with Mistral-large scoring 0.4385 on Movies&TV and Qwen3-32B at 0.3880. Small models (Phi-3-mini, Qwen2.5-3B, Qwen3-4B, Llama-3.1-8B) consistently score below 0.33 average on Movies&TV, with Qwen2.5-3B even producing a negative predictability score (−0.0012), indicating its simulated behaviors are anti-correlated with the user's actual preferences. Notably, even GPT-4o's distinguishability score (0.3800) remains far below the real-user noise baseline (0.4820), confirming that current LLMs are still readily distinguishable from humans in open-ended behavior simulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior human-likeness evaluation approaches—conversational Turing tests (Jones et al., 2025; Jannai et al., 2023) and single-step psychological/economic game benchmarks (Mei et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2026)—by shifting to distribution-based, sequence-aware evaluation over large-scale open-ended web behavior data. Rather than treating human behavior as deterministic with a single correct answer, it models the stochastic, heterogeneous nature of real user populations using trained encoders (SASRec for sequential data, e5-base-v2 for text) and multi-principle metrics. The LLM simulation protocol draws on prompt-based persona simulation (Tseng et al., 2024; Park et al., 2023), but the evaluation framework itself is novel in combining distinguishability, predictability, consistency, and population-level diversity into a unified, fully automated pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even GPT-4o trails real users by 10–17% across three diverse domains underscores that current LLMs remain unreliable as human surrogates in social simulation, raising concerns for downstream applications in social science and behavioral research. The multi-dimensional breakdown revealing that different model families excel in different aspects (Llama in consistency/diversity, Qwen in predictability/distinguishability) provides actionable guidance for targeted improvements. Open questions include whether the gap can be closed through better training objectives or persona conditioning, whether the framework's reliance on trained encoders introduces domain-specific biases, and how to extend evaluation to interactive multi-agent settings where behaviors are co-determined rather than independently generated.
