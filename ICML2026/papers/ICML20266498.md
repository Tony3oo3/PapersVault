## Problem

Sampling-based inference (SAI) for Bayesian neural networks has reached computational parity with optimization-based approximate inference, yet its practical adoption remains stunted by persistent misconceptions about its feasibility and a field-wide misalignment of research priorities. The paper argues that the community overemphasizes narrow algorithmic refinements and restrictive posterior approximations while underinvesting in two critical bottlenecks: scalable exploration of multimodal posteriors and the underexplored problem of efficiently storing, distilling, and reusing posterior samples for practical downstream inference.

## Input / Output

The SAI workflow consumes a supervised dataset and a neural network architecture, producing a collection of full network weight vectors (posterior samples) rather than a single point estimate. These samples are combined via Bayesian model averaging to yield the posterior predictive density, providing both point predictions and calibrated epistemic uncertainty. A central practical concern is that test-time inference cost scales linearly with the number of stored samples—up to 1000× a single network even after parallelization—making sample distillation and efficient storage a first-order design consideration rather than an afterthought.

## Method

As a position paper, the core contribution is a three-pillar research agenda supported by a meta-study of recent empirical results. First, it systematically dispels misconceptions about SAI's time complexity (showing sampling costs are now comparable to a single optimization run), scalability to millions of parameters, the adequacy of simple isotropic Gaussian priors, and the limited practical benefit of Metropolis-Hastings adjustment in high-dimensional BNNs. Second, it advocates for parallelized, optimization-informed exploration where deep ensembles provide warm-starts and multiple parallel chains reduce sensitivity to local approximation errors, arguing the main bottleneck is exploration strategy rather than sampler design. Third, it identifies posterior sample distillation—compressing large sample sets into smaller, more informative collections via sequential testing, learned generative surrogates, or importance sampling—as the critical underexplored stage for practical deployment, and calls for standardized benchmark collections of posterior samples plus dedicated end-to-end software tooling. The meta-study (Table 1) demonstrates SAI consistently outperforms deep ensembles, SWA, Laplace approximation, and variational inference across mid-scale architectures (ResNet-18, ViT, Wide CNN, Llama 3 adapters), positioning this regime as the sweet spot for scalable yet flexible Bayesian inference.

## Results

Across a meta-study spanning 11M to 218M parameters, SAI consistently matches or outperforms strong optimization-based baselines. On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18 (11.2M params), pSMILE achieves LPPD −0.26 and 91% accuracy vs. DE's −0.30 and 90%. On a 73M-parameter Wide CNN, SMS-UBU reduces NLL from 0.56 (SWA) to 0.31 and calibration error from 0.014 to 0.005. On the airfoil regression task, a single BDE chain (1000 samples) surpasses a 35-member deep ensemble in LPPD while requiring less wall-clock time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against deep ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017), SAI wins decisively on LPPD: pSMILE achieves −0.26 vs. −0.30 on CIFAR-10 ResNet-18 and −0.77 vs. −0.99 on Imagenette ViT, while matching accuracy. On the airfoil task, a single BDE chain outperforms a 35-member DE in both LPPD and runtime. Against Laplace approximation (Daxberger et al., 2021) and mean-field variational inference (Ranganath et al., 2014), SAI (BDE) achieves higher LPPD on the airfoil regression benchmark, with the gap widening as the competing methods are granted equal or greater computational budgets. Against stochastic weight averaging (Izmailov et al., 2018), SMS-UBU reduces NLL by 45% (0.31 vs. 0.56) and calibration error by 64% (0.005 vs. 0.014) on the 73M-parameter Wide CNN. The one case where SAI does not strictly dominate is LLM fine-tuning, where SGHMC achieves higher AUROC (0.95 vs. 0.80) but slightly worse in-distribution loss (4.40 vs. 4.20) compared to plain SGD.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not introduce a new method but repositions the entire Bayesian deep learning research agenda by arguing that sampling-based inference (SAI) has reached computational parity with optimization-based approximate inference (variational inference, Laplace approximation, deep ensembles, SWA). It synthesizes recent SAI advances—pSMILE, SMS-UBU, SGHMC, Bayesian deep ensembles—and repurposes classical MCMC concepts (HMC, parallel tempering, microcanonical Langevin dynamics) for the high-dimensional BNN setting, while explicitly departing from the optimization-centric ABI paradigm that has dominated the field. A notable conceptual contribution is framing 'posterior distillation' as a distinct, underexplored research problem, separating the sampling phase from the inference/serving phase in a way that prior BDL literature has not systematically addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a genuine practical bottleneck that the BDL community has underappreciated: even if sampling becomes as cheap as optimization, inference-time cost (forward passes over all posterior samples) can be up to 1000× that of a single network, making sample management and distillation the critical unsolved problem. Two open challenges are clearly articulated—scalable posterior exploration via parallelized, optimization-informed strategies, and principled distillation of posterior samples for efficient downstream inference—but neither has a mature solution yet. The 'store first, improve later' philosophy and the call for standardized posterior sample benchmarks are practical proposals that could accelerate the field, but the paper's impact ultimately depends on whether the community adopts this reframing over the more familiar optimization-based workflow.
