## Problem

Neural scaling laws relate loss to total model size, but the specific quantitative contribution of depth versus width remains unclear. Prior empirical studies showed LLMs use depth inefficiently (redundant or incremental layers) but lacked a quantitative framework explaining why this inefficiency exists and what scaling law depth follows. This paper asks: what is the precise depth-loss relationship in LLMs, and which mechanistic regime—compositional assembly, procedural assembly, or ensemble averaging—governs it?

## Input / Output

For LLM analysis, the method takes pretrained model weights (Pythia, Qwen-2.5, OPT) and streams ~2M tokens from FineWeb, computing per-token angular changes θ(h_l, h_l+1) between consecutive hidden states at each layer position, exploiting layer norms to make angles the natural geometric measure. For scaling law fitting, it consumes ~200 (width, depth, dataset size, loss) tuples reconstructed from Chinchilla and GPT-3 model configurations. The toy model takes i.i.d. Gaussian inputs x ∈ ℝ^m and produces logits y ∈ ℝ^n through ℓ residual MLP blocks, trained via KL divergence against a deeper teacher. Outputs include the depth scaling exponent α_ℓ, PCA cluster assignments of hidden-state trajectories, inter-layer update correlations, and the full seven-parameter loss decomposition.

## Method

The paper combines three complementary analyses. First, it probes LLM hidden states by measuring angular updates across layers, performing PCA on per-token trajectory vectors to identify behavioral clusters, and computing correlations between neighboring layer updates θ(Δh_l, Δh_{l+1}) to distinguish smooth dynamics (procedural assembly) from independent updates (ensemble averaging). Second, it proposes a decomposed scaling law L = c_m/m^α_m + c_ℓ/ℓ^α_ℓ + c_D/D^α_D + L_0 and fits it via nonlinear regression in log-space to Chinchilla data, yielding α_ℓ ≈ 1.2 ± 0.3 (or 1.1 ± 0.2 when using ℓ−2 to exclude non-ensemble layers). Third, it designs controlled toy residual-network experiments with tied (ρ=1, inducing smooth ODE-like dynamics) versus independent (ρ=0, inducing random-walk dynamics) teacher weights, showing that tied weights produce procedural assembly with converged α_ℓ=3 while independent weights produce ensemble averaging with α_ℓ≈1. The theoretical analysis shows that under ensemble averaging, each layer contributes an O(1/ℓ) error that accumulates diffusively via the central limit theorem, yielding typical loss scaling L ~ 1/ℓ. The LLM hidden-state signatures (uniform middle-layer updates, weak inter-layer correlations, inverse-depth update magnitude) match the ensemble averaging toy model, leading to the conclusion that inverse depth scaling in LLMs is driven by most layers acting as a noisy ensemble.

## Results

Fitting the decomposed scaling law L = c_m/m^α_m + c_ℓ/ℓ^α_ℓ + c_D/D^α_D + L_0 to ~200 Chinchilla data points yields α_m = 0.98 ± 0.08, α_ℓ = 1.2 ± 0.3, and α_D = 0.30 ± 0.01, with a mean relative error of 0.4% between empirical and predicted loss. Hidden-state analysis on Pythia-410m (2M FineWeb tokens) shows that 99.6% of tokens are updated evenly across middle layers while only 0.4% (mostly first tokens of documents) exhibit early stopping. In controlled toy-model experiments, independent teacher weights (ρ=0) robustly produce α_ℓ ≈ 1 (ensemble averaging), while tied weights (ρ=1) yield α_ℓ increasing from 1 to 3 as training converges (procedural assembly), confirming that the LLM-observed inverse scaling matches the ensemble-averaging prediction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new architecture or training method to benchmark against, but instead tests three competing theoretical regimes (compositional assembly, procedural assembly, ensemble averaging) against LLM measurements. Compositional assembly is rejected: only 0.4% of tokens show early stopping, and the first/last layers (which may be depth-independent) are excluded from the dominant scaling. Procedural assembly is also unlikely to dominate in LLMs because it predicts a converged depth exponent α_ℓ = 3 (from discretization error of a first-order ODE integrator), whereas the fitted LLM exponent is α_ℓ ≈ 1. Ensemble averaging, which predicts α_ℓ = 1 from central-limit-theorem error cancellation, matches both the LLM fit and the toy-model hidden-state signatures (uniform per-layer updates, inverse scaling of update magnitude with depth, and near-orthogonal neighboring updates θ(Δh_l, Δh_{l+1}) ≈ π/2). The findings are consistent with prior empirical observations of layer redundancy (Gromov et al. 2024; Sanyal et al. 2024; Men et al. 2025) and robustness to layer deletion/permutation (Lad et al. 2024), but provide the first quantitative inverse-depth scaling law and a mechanistic explanation. Cross-model-family validation on Qwen-2.5 and OPT confirms the qualitative picture, though OPT shows slightly different last-layer behavior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neural scaling law framework (Hoffmann et al., 2022; Kaplan et al., 2020) by decomposing the model-size term into separate width and depth contributions, following theoretical suggestions from Liu et al. (2025a) and Bordelon et al. (2025b). It builds on the ensemble interpretation of residual networks (Veit et al., 2016; Lu et al., 2020) and the neural ODE / procedural assembly perspective (Chen et al., 2018; Sander et al., 2022; Chizat, 2025), but is the first to quantitatively connect these theoretical regimes to measured LLM hidden-state dynamics and loss scaling. The paper also extends a large body of empirical work showing LLM layer redundancy (Gromov et al., 2024; Sanyal et al., 2024; Lad et al., 2024; Csordas et al., 2025) by providing a specific quantitative law (inverse depth scaling) and a mechanistic explanation (ensemble averaging) rather than merely documenting inefficiency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LLM depth loss scales inversely with depth (α_ℓ ≈ 1) due to ensemble averaging of similar layers has direct implications for architecture design: improving depth efficiency requires encouraging compositional use of depth rather than simply adding more similar layers. The predicted optimal width-depth relationship (m ∝ ℓ) and the resulting N^{-1/3} scaling exponent provide a concrete target for model scaling strategies. Key limitations include the loss decomposition being proposed rather than derived from first principles, and the inability to rigorously exclude alternative mechanisms producing the same scaling. Open questions include why ensemble averaging emerges as the dominant regime (architectural bias of residual connections? incompatibility of next-token prediction with smooth dynamics?) and whether recurrent or other architectural innovations can shift LLMs toward compositional depth utilization.
