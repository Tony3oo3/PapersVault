## Problem

Existing time-series forecasting methods either predict each system independently (repetitive, costly, and ignoring inter-system interactions) or treat multiple targets as multivariate series of a single system, which conflates fundamentally different predictive goals. As the number of interacting systems grows (e.g., 320 regions in epidemic modeling), per-system prediction becomes computationally prohibitive while multivariate approaches lose accuracy and scalability. The paper asks whether all systems in an ensemble can be forecast simultaneously in a single, efficient pass that explicitly models their mutual influence through shared attributes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265254/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes p historical target values for each of n homogeneous systems (e.g., daily exchange rates or new cases) plus m attribute values per system (e.g., GDP, population, band), all aligned at the same time points. Unlike standard time-series models, attribute data is essential—without it the equilibrium estimation fails to converge. The output is a vector of n simultaneous forecasts at horizon h, structured as the product of an aggregate trend (from an internal AR model or an external predictor) and the estimated equilibrium proportions, so the internal distribution is decoupled from the overall level.

## Method

ESE decomposes multi-system forecasting into two stages: (1) an Equilibrium State Estimator that normalizes all attributes, computes an initial equilibrium via MLE-estimated attribute influence coefficients (Eq. 5), then iteratively refines it with a damped correction vector (λ=0.5) until a cointegration test between the estimated equilibrium and recent history yields p < 0.05; (2) a Predictor that multiplies the aggregate trend θ_{t+h}·MS_t (estimated by log-likelihood of a linear AR model, or supplied by any external model) by the equilibrium proportions to yield per-system forecasts. The key insight is that the proportional distribution across systems is stable over short horizons and is determined by attribute patterns rather than target magnitudes, making the problem separable into 'how much total' and 'how to split it.' This yields linear complexity in n, m, and p, and allows seamless integration with any existing predictor (LSTM, SCINet, Informer, etc.) that only needs to forecast the single aggregate series, delivering 10–70× speedups while matching or exceeding SOTA accuracy.

## Results

On G20 exchange rate prediction (16 currencies, 100 input steps, 1-step horizon), ESE alone achieves RMSE* 1.183 and MAE* 0.600 in 0.22 min, matching or exceeding the best standalone baselines (SCINet: RMSE* 1.184; TimeLLM: RMSE* 1.182). When combined with SOTA predictors, ESE consistently improves accuracy (e.g., TimeLLM+ESE reaches RMSE* 1.167, DLinear+ESE reaches MAE* 0.586) while reducing runtime by 10–11× (e.g., SCINet from 8.82 to 0.77 min). On the 320-region COVID-19 dataset, ESE alone (RMSE 4.83) outperforms all standalone baselines, and ESE integration yields up to 70× speedup (SCINet: 206.06 → 2.94 min; FiLM: 181.06 → 2.84 min).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ESE alone is competitive with the strongest standalone baselines: on exchange rates it matches SCINet (RMSE* 1.183 vs. 1.184) and TimeLLM (1.183 vs. 1.182) while being 20–40× faster; on the 320-region COVID-19 task it clearly outperforms all standalone methods (RMSE 4.83 vs. best baseline PatchTST 5.11). When integrated, ESE improves every SOTA predictor on exchange rates (e.g., DLinear RMSE* drops from 1.212 to 1.174; TimeLLM from 1.182 to 1.167) and on 320-region COVID-19 (e.g., FiLM RMSE drops from 5.74 to 4.36; SCINet from 5.94 to 4.46). The only case where ESE does not improve a baseline is KVAE on 20-region COVID-19 (RMSE 70.85 → 71.94), a minor regression. Computationally, ESE integration reduces runtime by 2–10× on exchange rates and up to 70× on the 320-region COVID-19 dataset (SCINet: 206→2.94 min; FiLM: 181→2.84 min), whereas ARIMA is the only method faster than ESE on small synthetic data due to its training-free nature. VAR, which requires manual parameter tuning, is excluded from cost comparisons but is outperformed in accuracy by ESE on both real-world datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

ESE draws conceptually from Nash equilibrium (Kreps, 1989) to frame multi-system prediction as an equilibrium estimation problem, and from econometric cointegration theory as its convergence criterion. It explicitly departs from multi-variate time series (VAR, SEM), multi-target prediction, multi-compartment models, and hierarchical forecasting, arguing that all of these treat either a single system with multiple variables or heterogeneous components, whereas ESE models an ensemble of homogeneous interacting systems with a shared attribute set. The method also borrows the deep equilibrium model's iterative fixed-point idea (Bai et al., 2019) but applies it to a statistical equilibrium over system proportions rather than network depth.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 10–70× speedup with linear complexity makes simultaneous multi-system forecasting practical at scale (e.g., 320 regions), a regime where transformer-based SOTA methods become prohibitively expensive. ESE's plug-in design—where any existing predictor handles the aggregate trend while ESE allocates proportions—lowers the barrier to adoption. Key limitations remain: the method requires attribute data, assumes homogeneous systems with a fixed membership (no additions/removals), and performs worse on short input sequences. Open questions include whether the equilibrium assumption holds for truly heterogeneous or adversarial systems, and whether the cointegration-based convergence is the optimal stopping criterion versus learned alternatives.
