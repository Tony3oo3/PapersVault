## Problem

Most UAV autopilot platforms deploy navigation and flight control on separate hardware (companion computer + dedicated FCU), which fragments the taskset across different clocks, OS kernels, and hardware, preventing system-wide observability and holistic scheduling. Furthermore, scheduling decisions affect flight performance through implicit, delayed, and cross-coupled interactions across estimation, planning, control, and computational resources, making traditional model-based or heuristic scheduling approaches ineffective.

## Input / Output

The policy consumes a 10-step sliding-window buffer of runtime observations (CPU time for flight-control and navigation tasks, current execution frequencies, trajectory deviation, vehicle velocity, and distance to nearest obstacle) sampled at a fixed 10 Hz decision rate. It outputs a discrete scheduling configuration selecting one flight-control frequency from {100, 200, 300, 400} Hz and one frequency per navigation group (localization, planning, offboard control) from {10, 20, 30, 40, 50} Hz, yielding 500 total configurations. The policy is deliberately restricted to bounded frequency-level decisions and does not modify thread priorities or kernel scheduling policies.

## Method

UAV² integrates the full autopilot pipeline (FAST-LIO2, EGO-Planner, PX4-Autopilot, offboard control) onto a single Rockchip RK3588S platform, modifying PX4's WorkQueue and ROS1's CallbackQueue so that each of 25 threads becomes an independently schedulable unit grouped into four functional categories. Scheduling is formulated as a POMDP where the latent state encompasses the joint physical and computational state of the vehicle, and a Recurrent PPO policy with separate actor/critic LSTMs (256 hidden units, 1 layer) learns history-dependent scheduling strategies from execution feedback. A scheduling middleware enforces decisions by pinning flight-control threads at SCHED_FIFO with adjustable activation rates and driving navigation threads through a user-space dispatcher that delays trigger messages to control wake-up timing. Training is conducted in a hardware-in-the-loop loop (Gazebo Classic simulator + real embedded hardware over 1 Gb/s Ethernet) with a penalty-based reward combining trajectory tracking deviation, estimation error, CPU overload, and collision, and the learned policy is evaluated against four fixed-rate baselines across simulation and real-flight scenarios.

## Results

In the hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) simulation on the Pillars environment, the learned UAV² policy achieves an 86% mission success rate, outperforming all four fixed-rate baselines (100/200/300/400 Hz flight-control loops), while maintaining a mean trajectory tracking deviation of ~0.15 m and mean CPU time of ~74 ms. In a real-world forest flight experiment (10 trials per strategy), UAV² achieves 100% success with 0.17 m mean deviation, compared to 40%, 80%, 90%, and 70% success for baselines A–D respectively. The learned policy operates at moderate CPU utilization, well below the 95% saturation threshold, while higher-rate baselines (especially D) show substantially larger deviation variance and more outlier episodes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UAV² is compared against four fixed-rate scheduling baselines that pair flight-control loop rates (100–400 Hz) with matched navigation group rates (20–50 Hz), reflecting the static-frequency strategy common in real UAV deployments. In the HIL simulation, UAV² achieves the highest success rate (86%) while all four baselines fall below this threshold; the highest-rate baseline D achieves the smallest median trajectory deviation but with substantially larger inter-episode variance and more outliers, indicating less consistent tracking. In the real-world experiment the gap widens: UAV² completes all 10 flights successfully (100%) versus 40% (A), 80% (B), 90% (C), and 70% (D), while its 0.17 m mean deviation is within 0.02 m of the best fixed-rate baselines C and D. On CPU usage, UAV² operates at a moderate level (~74 ms in HIL), below the highest-rate baselines, demonstrating that adaptive frequency selection allocates computation more efficiently than uniformly raising all task rates. The key finding is that trajectory accuracy alone does not capture mission robustness: strategies with similar median deviation (e.g., C and D) still exhibit markedly lower success rates, and the learned policy's advantage is primarily in reducing failure cases caused by delayed estimation errors and transient computational overloads.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on RL-based real-time scheduling (Glaubius et al., 2010; Demirel et al., 2018; Mao et al., 2016) to the specific domain of UAV autopilot systems, where scheduling decisions are tightly coupled with physical flight dynamics through delayed, cross-coupled interactions. It departs from the dominant split-architecture paradigm (companion computer + dedicated FCU) by unifying navigation and flight control on a single embedded platform, which is a prerequisite for system-wide observability and holistic scheduling. The learning algorithm itself is standard Recurrent PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) applied to a POMDP formulation; the novelty lies in the system architecture, the POMDP problem formulation capturing delayed scheduling effects, and the hardware-in-the-loop training setup rather than in the RL method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap in UAV autonomy: fixed-rate scheduling is the default in most deployments, yet it cannot adapt to the implicit, delayed coupling between computational resource allocation and flight performance. The unified architecture + RL scheduling approach offers a path toward performance-aware, resource-adaptive autopilot systems on embedded platforms. Key limitations include the absence of formal safety guarantees (the collision penalty is a training-time safeguard only), preliminary real-world validation with only 10 trials, and limited cross-hardware transfer (zero-shot transfer drops success to 46%). Open questions include whether the learned policy generalizes to significantly different hardware, whether safety can be formally certified, and whether the approach scales to multi-UAV or more complex autonomy stacks.
