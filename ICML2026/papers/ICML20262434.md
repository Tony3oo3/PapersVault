## Problem

GRPO's std-based group normalization becomes unstable when response groups contain extreme reward patterns (e.g., 7/8 correct or 7/8 wrong), producing near-zero variance that over-amplifies a few samples while neglecting balanced ones. This issue is structurally amplified in multimodal settings, where perceptual complexity and reasoning uncertainty jointly increase the frequency of such extreme groups—empirically, 31–44% of effective samples exhibit 7:1 reward patterns throughout training. The paper identifies that the root cause is not the std term itself but the fixed per-sample grouping, which makes extreme cases inevitable at small group sizes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an RGB image and a text question, and the model produces a reasoning chain enclosed in  tags followed by a final answer in \boxed{}. Training uses a binary verifiable reward (0/1) on the final answer, combined with a small format reward (weight 0.1 vs. 0.9 for accuracy). The difficulty metrics operate at the batch level: perceptual entropy is pre-computed offline from frozen visual-encoder patch features, while reasoning confidence is derived from the average sequence-level log-probability across G=8 rollouts already generated during training, so no extra forward passes are required.

## Method

Durian replaces GRPO's per-sample std with a difficulty-aware shared std by re-grouping samples across the batch along two axes. For perceptual difficulty, patch-level features from the Qwen2.5-VL visual encoder are used to build a P×P covariance matrix; the Shannon entropy of its normalized eigenvalue distribution scores visual complexity, and samples are partitioned into three groups at the 25th/75th percentiles, each sharing one std. For reasoning difficulty, the average sequence-level log-probability over G rollouts serves as a confidence score, and samples are binned into b quantile groups, each sharing its own std. The final advantage is an element-wise weighted sum of the original GRPO advantage, the perceptual-group-normalized advantage, and the reasoning-group-normalized advantage, preserving intra-group distinctions while preventing extreme samples from distorting the normalization scale. The approach is plug-and-play over both GRPO and DAPO, adds no trainable parameters, and incurs only ~15 minutes of offline pre-computation plus a sub-second binning step per training iteration.

## Results

Durian achieves an average accuracy of 59.3% across five multimodal reasoning benchmarks (MathVerse, MathVision, MathVista, WeMath, HallusionBench) when built on DAPO with only 2.1K training samples (Geometry3K), and 60.1% with 39K samples (ViRL39K). This represents an 11.3% relative improvement over the Qwen2.5-VL-7B base model (53.3% average) and a 2.0–2.4% absolute gain over vanilla DAPO (57.3%) and vanilla GRPO (56.9%). The largest single-benchmark gain is on MathVision, where Durian (DAPO, 2.1K) reaches 29.0% versus 25.0% for the base model (>16% relative improvement).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Durian (DAPO, 2.1K) achieves 59.3% average, matching NoisyRollout (59.2%, also trained on 2.1K) while outperforming ThinkLite-7B-VL (58.1%, 11K data) and Vision-R1-7B (57.0%, 200K+10K data) by 1.2% and 2.3% respectively, using far less training data. Against the base Qwen2.5-VL-7B (53.3%), Durian gains +6.0% absolute (11.3% relative) on average, with the largest margin on MathVision (+4.0% absolute, >16% relative). Durian (GRPO, 2.1K) at 59.2% also matches NoisyRollout and surpasses R1-VL-7B (56.2%, 260K+10K data) by 3.0%. Scaling to 39K samples (ViRL39K) pushes the average to 60.1%, the best among all 7B RLVR-based methods in the table. On HallusionBench (perception), Durian (DAPO, 2.1K) reaches 71.4%, exceeding NoisyRollout (72.1% is slightly higher) but well above the base model (64.6%) and R1-VL-7B (57.2%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and its DAPO variant (Yu et al., 2025b), identifying a specific weakness in their std-based group normalization: extreme reward groups (nearly all-correct or all-wrong) produce near-zero variance that over-amplifies those samples during optimization. The proposed Durian strategy departs from both data-centric GRPO extensions (which expand candidate sets or augment inputs) and algorithm-centric refinements (which modify clipping or reward shaping) by intervening at the grouping level itself—re-partitioning samples by perceptual difficulty (eigenvalue entropy of patch covariances) and reasoning difficulty (sequence-level log-probability confidence) before computing the shared standard deviation. This positions the work as a targeted normalization fix rather than a new RL objective or reward design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is clear: an 11.3% average gain over the base model with negligible computational overhead (pre-computed entropy, sub-second binning per step) makes this an attractive drop-in modification for anyone training multimodal models with GRPO/DAPO. However, the approach introduces several hyperparameters (number of difficulty groups, three weighting coefficients) and relies on a fixed 3-way perceptual split, leaving open whether adaptive or learned grouping boundaries would further improve stability. The broader implication—that normalization design in RLVR matters as much as reward design for multimodal models—could influence how the community thinks about post-training robustness, but the current formulation is specific to binary-reward settings and may not transfer directly to continuous or multi-step reward structures.
