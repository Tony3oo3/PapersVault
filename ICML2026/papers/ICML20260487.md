## Problem

Supervised Causal Learning (SCL) methods for interventional data face two critical gaps: they are typically restricted to narrow intervention settings (e.g., hard interventions with known targets), limiting versatility, and they suffer from distribution shift between synthetic training data and real-world test data, limiting generalizability. No existing SCL method handles the general case of unknown, potentially soft, multi-variable interventions while maintaining strong empirical performance across diverse causal graph scales.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260487/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a collection of discrete tabular samples D = {D_0, ..., D_K} generated under an unknown intervention family (soft or hard, single or multi-variable, targets unknown) and produces two outputs: the I-CPDAG (a partial DAG encoding all causally identifiable edges under the given interventions) and the set of intervention targets for each experiment. The input is first converted into an augmented dataset by stacking all samples and appending binary environment-indicator columns, transforming the interventional problem into an observational one over system and environment nodes.

## Method

TICL combines three components: (1) JCI pooling converts the interventional data collection into a single augmented observational dataset with binary environment variables; (2) a self-augmentation strategy samples causal graphs from the posterior P(G_I|D_I) via an optimized Interventional Structural-MCMC (IS-MCMC) that enforces system-environment constraints (no sys→env or env→env edges), re-estimates CPTs via MLE with parameter reuse across iterations, and forward-samples compatible datasets to create free training pairs ⟨G_i, D_i⟩; (3) a two-phase supervised learning scheme inspired by the PC algorithm, where Phase 1 trains an XGBoost classifier on k-order conditional dependence and local structural features to predict the skeleton, and Phase 2 trains a second classifier on separating-set features to identify v-structures, with Meek rules then orienting remaining edges. The key insight is that test-time self-augmentation from the posterior avoids distribution shift, while the two-phase design ensures the model predicts only identifiable structures (I-CPDAG), preserving asymptotic correctness.

## Results

On 14 bnlearn benchmark causal graphs (ranging from 8 to 100+ nodes), TICL achieves an average F1 rank of 1.36±0.37 for I-CPDAG discovery and a perfect 1.00±0.00 for intervention target detection, outperforming all 9 baselines. TICL improves the F1 score by 13.62% over the best baseline (JCI-BLIP) for causal discovery and by 50.21% over the second-best method for intervention target detection. Notably, TICL is the only method that completes all 14 datasets without crashing or timing out, while methods like AVICI, ENCO, and IGSP fail on graphs with more than ~50 nodes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TICL outperforms all 9 baselines on the majority of datasets for both I-CPDAG discovery and intervention target detection. The strongest baseline is JCI-BLIP (rank 4.64±5.37 on F1 for causal discovery), which TICL surpasses by 13.62% in average F1; JCI-BLIP is the only method that completes all 14 datasets alongside TICL. For intervention target detection, TICL achieves a perfect F1 rank of 1.00±0.00, beating the next-best JCI-BLIP (2.43±0.67) by 50.21% in average F1. Score-based methods (GIES, IGSP, UT-IGSP) and gradient-based methods (AVICI, ENCO) degrade sharply or crash on larger graphs (>50 nodes), while TICL maintains consistent performance even on PATHFINDER (100+ nodes). JCI-PC and JCI-HC, though constraint/score-based, show moderate performance on small graphs but fail or timeout on larger ones. The paper notes that ENCO on PATHFINDER is the sole exception where a baseline (with known intervention targets) achieves comparable performance to TICL.

## Relation to Prior Work

TICL sits at the intersection of three research threads: the Joint Causal Inference (JCI) framework (Mooij et al., 2020), which unifies diverse interventional settings into an augmented observational problem; the PC algorithm's skeleton-then-orientation structure (Spirtes & Glymour, 1991), which is reinterpreted as a two-phase supervised classification task; and the Test-Time Training paradigm (Sun, 2023), which is repurposed here to generate instance-specific training data via posterior sampling (IS-MCMC) and forward sampling. Unlike prior SCL methods for interventions (AVICI, CSIvA, SDI) that are limited to specific settings like known hard interventions, TICL leverages JCI to handle unknown, soft, and multi-variable interventions uniformly. The self-augmentation strategy—sampling graphs from the posterior P(G|D) and generating compatible datasets—departs from standard SCL's reliance on pre-generated synthetic training data, instead producing in-domain training instances at test time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the generalization bottleneck in supervised causal learning for interventional data can be substantially mitigated by test-time self-augmentation, achieving consistent SOTA across 14 real-world-inspired benchmarks including graphs with over 100 nodes where many baselines fail or time out. The 50% F1 improvement in intervention target detection and the efficiency parity with non-SCL methods (while outperforming all SCL baselines) make this practically relevant for high-stakes causal discovery where intervention targets are unknown. Key limitations include restriction to discrete data, reliance on faithfulness and causal sufficiency assumptions, and the need for a proxy algorithm for good MCMC initialization. Open questions include extension to continuous data, relaxation of assumptions, and whether the TTT+JCI paradigm can scale toward a causal foundation model.
