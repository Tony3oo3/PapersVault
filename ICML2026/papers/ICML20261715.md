## Problem

Standard RLHF relies on transitive scalar rewards (Bradley-Terry), which fundamentally cannot represent the intransitive, cyclic patterns present in real human preferences. While the General Preference Model (GPM) captures cyclicity via skew-symmetric bilinear forms, the paper identifies a critical theoretical limitation: GPM entangles transitive hierarchy with cyclic dynamics, and for any fixed embedding dimension, there exist cyclic configurations where no dominant candidate can be represented (Theorem 4.7). This structural brittleness means GPM cannot simultaneously preserve global quality rankings and model local intransitive nuances.

## Input / Output

The HRC model takes a prompt x and a response y, producing two outputs from a shared language model backbone: a clipped scalar reward r(y|x) ∈ [-δ, δ] (transitive head) and a unit-norm embedding v(y|x) ∈ R^{2d} (cyclic head). The pairwise preference score is the sum of the scalar difference and a context-gated skew-symmetric bilinear term v_w^T D(x) R^≻ D(x) v_l, where D(x) is a non-negative diagonal matrix computed from the prompt. For downstream alignment, DSPPO consumes this decomposed score and dynamically reweights the transitive and cyclic components over T iterations via the schedule s_t = (1+λ/√t)·s_T + (1-λ/√t)·s_C, ultimately producing an aligned policy π that approximates the Nash equilibrium of the full preference game.

## Method

The HRC model explicitly decomposes the preference score into an orthogonal transitive component (BT scalar reward difference) and a cyclic component (GPM skew-symmetric bilinear form), grounded in the game-theoretic decomposition theorem of Balduzzi et al. (2019) for symmetric zero-sum functional-form games. This decoupling resolves GPM's structural limitation: by assigning a sufficiently large scalar reward to a dominant candidate, HRC can mathematically override the bounded residuals of the cyclic component, guaranteeing dominant-solution preservation regardless of cycle complexity. Complementing this, DSPPO reformulates alignment as a time-varying game where the preference signal transitions from transitive-dominant (establishing a global quality baseline) to cyclic-dominant (refining local intransitive nuances), following a curriculum-learning-inspired schedule. The paper proves an O(1/√T) convergence rate for the mixture policy toward the Nash equilibrium under boundedness and convergence assumptions on the time-varying scores, and validates the framework empirically on RewardBench 2, AlpacaEval 2.0, Arena-Hard-v0.1, and MT-Bench.

## Results

On RewardBench 2, HRC (dim=2+1) achieves 57.63% average accuracy with Gemma-2B-it, surpassing the best baseline GPM (dim=4) at 56.40% by +1.23% and BT at 55.93%. With Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, HRC (dim=2+1) reaches 70.95% average, outperforming BT (70.10%) by +0.85%. In downstream alignment, HRC+DSPPO achieves a peak length-controlled win rate of 44.75% on AlpacaEval 2.0 and 46.8% on Arena-Hard-v0.1 (Gemma-2B-it), significantly exceeding BT+SPPO (40.9%) and GPM+SPPO (42.1%) on Arena-Hard.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Bradley-Terry (BT) scalar model, HRC wins on average accuracy at both scales: +1.70% on Gemma-2B-it (57.63% vs 55.93%) and +0.85% on Llama-3.1-8B (70.95% vs 70.10%), with the largest gains in the Ties domain (39.22% vs 37.25% for Gemma; 74.51% vs 73.53% for Llama). Against GPM, HRC also consistently outperforms: on Gemma-2B-it, HRC (dim=2+1) beats GPM (dim=4) by +1.23% average, while on Llama-3.1-8B, HRC (dim=2+1) beats GPM (dim=2) by +0.87% and GPM (dim=4) by +1.26%. In downstream alignment, HRC+DSPPO surpasses both BT+SPPO and GPM+SPPO on Arena-Hard-v0.1 (46.8% vs 40.9% and 42.1% respectively, a +3.2% margin over the best baseline) and on AlpacaEval 2.0 LC win rate (44.75% vs lower baselines). The Ties domain results are particularly notable, as they validate HRC's ability to handle non-strict preferences where both BT's forced transitivity and GPM's purely cyclic structure struggle.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of two recent threads in LLM alignment: the General Preference Model (GPM) for capturing intransitive preferences via skew-symmetric bilinear forms, and game-theoretic self-play optimization (SPPO, INPO, NLHF) for finding Nash equilibria of preference games. It departs from GPM by identifying a structural limitation (Theorem 4.7) — that fixed-dimensional GPM cannot guarantee dominant solutions in arbitrary cyclic contexts — and resolves this by explicitly decomposing preferences into a transitive scalar (BT) component and a cyclic vector (GPM) component, grounded in the FFG decomposition of Balduzzi et al. (2019). The alignment algorithm DSPPO extends SPPO's static multiplicative-weight update to a time-varying game, borrowing curriculum-learning principles to schedule the transition from transitive to cyclic signals.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The explicit transitive-cyclic decomposition provides a clean theoretical guarantee (Dominant Arbitrariness) that pure GPM lacks, and the DSPPO curriculum schedule offers a practical recipe for stabilizing training in multi-dimensional preference landscapes. However, the empirical gains are modest (+1.23% on RewardBench 2, +1.75% LC.WR on AlpacaEval), and the paper acknowledges that the zero-mean condition underlying the decomposition is violated in practice (E[v] ≈ (0.13, −0.51)), leaving open whether the theoretical orthogonality holds in trained models. A notable open question is whether the cyclic component provides meaningful benefit for larger backbones, since on Llama-3.1-8B the scalar BT model nearly matches GPM, suggesting the intransitive structure may be less critical at scale.
