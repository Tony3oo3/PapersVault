## Problem

Traditional competitive equilibrium pricing, where supply meets demand, does not extend to data markets because data is non-rivalrous: the same dataset can be sold to multiple buyers simultaneously at near-zero marginal cost, so there is no natural supply constraint to equate. This paper asks whether a Nash-equilibrium-based pricing notion can restore the stability, welfare, and revenue guarantees that make competitive equilibrium attractive in classical markets, and under what utility structures such equilibria exist and can be computed efficiently.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input the number of sellers m and buyers n, each buyer's budget b_i and minimum utility threshold τ_i (below which the buyer exits the market), and a common valuation weight vector w = (w_j) across homogeneous buyers. It produces an equilibrium price vector p = (p_j) and the induced allocation matrix x = (x_ij), with the key output being the CONSTPROD price vector at the optimal sink state, which simultaneously maximizes total seller revenue, buyer welfare, buyer participation, and seller fairness. The utility functions are thresholded (zero below τ_i, concave above), introducing discontinuities that rule out standard fixed-point existence proofs.

## Method

The paper defines a Nash equilibrium for data markets where each seller sets a price and each buyer demands the utility-maximizing affordable bundle, with no supply-side constraint since data is freely replicable. The central technical tool is the graph-of-deviations: the strategy space is partitioned into at most n 'states' (each corresponding to a buyer-activity threshold b_i/τ_i), a canonical CONSTPROD price vector is identified per state, and a directed edge i→k is drawn if a seller profitably deviates from CONSTPROD prices in state i to state k. The key proof shows this graph is acyclic by ruling out 2-cycles (via a sign-contradiction argument on revenue ratios) and then showing any longer cycle can be shortened, guaranteeing a sink state whose CONSTPROD prices form a Nash equilibrium. For Leontief (complementary) utilities this yields existence, polynomial-time computation, and a unique optimal equilibrium at the lowest sink state; for linear (substitutable) utilities, equilibrium fails to exist with multiple buyers but is restored under price discrimination (single-buyer decomposition). Best-response dynamics among sellers are shown to converge to a (1+ε)-approximate equilibrium in O(L/ε) steps, though experiments indicate they may not reach the globally optimal equilibrium.

## Results

For Leontief (complementary) data markets with homogeneous buyers (100 sellers, 100 buyers, 1000 random instances), best-response dynamics (BRD) always converges to an equilibrium, but the quality varies significantly with initialization: mean welfare ratio (BR/maxW) is 0.768 and mean revenue ratio (BR/maxR) is 0.770, with worst-case ratios as low as 0.071 and 0.075. On the real-world FedHeart Disease dataset (4 buyers, 2 sellers), the gap widens substantially, with mean BR/maxW of 0.239 and mean BR/maxR of 0.238, indicating that decentralized price adjustment frequently lands far from the optimal equilibrium. The paper proves that a platform-mediated CONSTPROD pricing at the lowest sink state simultaneously maximizes total seller revenue, buyer welfare, buyer participation, and seller fairness, and is computable in polynomial time via the graph-of-deviations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a primarily theoretical paper without direct comparisons to prior pricing methods; the empirical evaluation instead contrasts decentralized best-response dynamics (BRD) against the platform-computed optimal equilibrium (CONSTPROD at the lowest sink state). BRD always converges to *some* equilibrium in all tested instances, but the resulting welfare and revenue can be far below optimal: on synthetic homogeneous markets the mean gap is ~23%, while on the real-world FedHeart dataset the mean gap reaches ~76%. The paper's key practical finding is that buyers and sellers cannot discover the optimal equilibrium through intuitive price-adjustment dynamics, motivating platform mediation. For linear (substitutable) utilities, the paper shows no equilibrium exists with multiple buyers (even approximate ones), whereas a single-buyer or price-discrimination setting always admits a revenue- and welfare-optimal equilibrium computable in polynomial time. No prior data-pricing method is benchmarked head-to-head; the contribution is the equilibrium framework itself rather than an algorithmic improvement over existing baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the classical competitive equilibrium tradition (Arrow-Debreu, Fisher markets) that underpins most prior data pricing work, arguing that supply-demand balance is ill-defined for non-rivalrous, freely replicable data. It also distinguishes itself from concurrent and prior equilibrium data pricing studies (Jain & Vazirani 2010a; Chaudhury et al. 2026) that enforce full-purchase constraints, by adopting a Nash equilibrium formulation where sellers strategically set prices and buyers respond. The graph-of-deviations proof technique and the CONSTPROD pricing concept are novel contributions not present in the pricing-game or market-equilibrium literature, and the paper positions itself as complementary to (rather than subsumed by) the simultaneous work of Chaudhury et al., which addresses approximate equilibria under non-uniform pricing for substitutable markets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled, existence-guaranteed framework for Nash-equilibrium data pricing, with the strong result that for complementary (Leontief) data a platform can compute a single price vector that simultaneously maximizes seller revenue, buyer welfare, and seller fairness in polynomial time. The non-existence result for linear utilities—even with just two sellers—sharply delineates when decentralized pricing fails and motivates platform-mediated price discrimination, a strategy already observed in practice. Key open questions include extending the framework to hybrid (CES-type) utilities that lie between pure complementarity and substitutability, handling heterogeneous buyer valuations, and understanding whether the graph-of-deviations technique generalizes to other non-convex valuation classes.
