## Problem

In binary-reward correctness-based tasks (math reasoning, program synthesis, navigation), reinforcement learning is used as a workaround for non-differentiable sampling, yet it optimizes only the expected pass rate rather than the principled maximum likelihood objective. The paper proves that standard expected-reward RL is merely a first-order approximation of the log-likelihood, and that this mismatch causes suboptimal scaling with compute and data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264348/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on correctness-based problems where the model generates a latent trajectory z (e.g., a chain-of-thought or navigation path) from a discrete policy m_θ(z|x), followed by a deterministic decoder f that maps z to a final answer y. Correctness is evaluated only on the decoded output via a binary indicator r = I{f(z) = y*(x)}. The key practical detail is that N rollouts are sampled per input, and the estimator uses only the K successful rollouts (K ≤ N) to compute the gradient, making the effective learning signal depend on the pass rate in a way that standard RL does not.

## Method

The paper derives a Maclaurin expansion of the log-likelihood in terms of fail@k events, showing that the ML gradient is an infinite harmonic mixture of pass@k gradients while standard RL retains only the k=1 term. MAXRL truncates this expansion at order T, yielding a compute-indexed family of objectives where T=1 recovers RL and T→∞ recovers exact ML. The practical estimator is a one-line modification to REINFORCE: instead of normalizing score functions by total samples N (as in RLOO) or by reward standard deviation (as in GRPO), MAXRL normalizes by the number of successful samples K, making it an unbiased estimator for the truncated ML gradient of order T=N. A zero-mean control variate (the unconditional average score) is subtracted for variance reduction. Increasing rollout budget N thus improves the objective itself (higher-order approximation to ML) rather than merely reducing variance of a fixed objective.

## Results

On Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B trained on POLARIS-53K (16 rollouts, 1000 RL steps), MAXRL Pareto-dominates GRPO across all four math benchmarks (AIME 2025, BeyondAIME, MATH-500, Minerva), achieving higher pass@1 while simultaneously improving pass@k in 7 of 8 evaluation settings. With a perfect verifier, MAXRL yields up to 20× test-time scaling efficiency gains over GRPO. In the maze-navigation setting (1M unique mazes, 3M-param transformer), MAXRL at only 4 training rollouts outperforms RLOO at 128 rollouts, and MAXRL at 16 rollouts outperforms GRPO at 128 rollouts across all pass@k metrics. On GSM8K (SmolLM2-360M, 50 epochs), MAXRL overtakes both baselines at ~30 epochs and continues improving while RLOO and GRPO plateau, with substantially less pass@k degradation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against RLOO (Ahmadian et al., 2024), MAXRL wins in every tested setting: in the maze environment, MAXRL with only 4 training rollouts surpasses RLOO at 128 rollouts on all pass@k metrics; on GSM8K, RLOO peaks around 10 epochs and then degrades in pass@k, while MAXRL continues improving past 30 epochs; on Qwen3 math reasoning, MAXRL achieves higher pass@1 and better pass@k than RLOO. Against GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), MAXRL similarly Pareto-dominates: in the maze setting, MAXRL at 16 rollouts beats GRPO at 128 rollouts; on GSM8K, GRPO exhibits massive pass@k degradation with extended training while MAXRL maintains healthy diversity; on Qwen3-1.7B/4B, MAXRL outperforms GRPO on all four benchmarks (AIME 2025, BeyondAIME, MATH-500, Minerva) and delivers up to 20× test-time scaling efficiency gains when a perfect verifier is available. The paper also notes that GRPO's population-level weighting (1/√(p(1−p))) inverts at high pass rates, assigning increased weight to very easy inputs, whereas MAXRL's weighting (1−(1−p)^T)/p monotonically approaches the 1/p likelihood weighting as T grows.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Davis & Recht (2025), who showed that the maximum likelihood gradient admits a conditional-expectation form over successful trajectories, and on the classical REINFORCE policy gradient (Williams, 1992). The key conceptual departure is the Maclaurin expansion of log p in terms of fail@k events, which reveals that standard expected-reward RL optimizes only the first-order term of the ML objective. Unlike Xiong et al. (2025b), which treats non-linear pass-rate weighting as part of an adaptive-sampling algorithmic design space, MaxRL derives a fixed on-policy estimator whose truncation order is determined solely by the rollout budget N, yielding an exact finite-sample estimator-objective equivalence. The practical algorithm is a single-line modification to the advantage normalization (dividing by the number of successful rollouts K rather than total rollouts N), positioning it as a drop-in replacement for RLOO or GRPO.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a one-line normalization change Pareto-dominates both RLOO and GRPO across image classification, maze navigation, and billion-parameter LLM math reasoning is practically significant for the active LLM-RL community, and the up-to-20× test-time scaling efficiency gain over GRPO with a perfect verifier has direct inference-cost implications. The theoretical reframing—RL as a first-order Taylor approximation of ML—provides a principled explanation for why RL underweights hard, low-pass-rate prompts and why additional rollouts improve the objective itself rather than merely reducing variance. Key limitations include the restriction to binary rewards, on-policy training, and the assumption of a deterministic decoding function; extending MaxRL to continuous rewards, multi-turn RL, and off-policy (PPO-style) settings are explicitly identified as open problems.
