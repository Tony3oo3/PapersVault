## Problem

Current proactive AI agents operationalize proactivity as action selection within assumed task frames, collapsing all ignorance into uncertainty over already-parameterized variables. This leaves unrecognized forms of ignorance—unknown unknowns, error-as-knowledge, denial, taboo—unrepresented, so agents cannot detect when their task formulation itself is incomplete or when commitment outpaces warranted understanding. The paper argues that proactivity must be grounded both epistemically (what the agent can legitimately claim to understand) and behaviorally (how strongly and when it intervenes), rather than treated as a uniform capacity to act earlier or more autonomously.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the framework takes as its analytical objects the three dominant paradigms of proactive agent design (anticipatory, autonomous/planning-based, and mixed-initiative systems) and the philosophical literature on ignorance (Kerwin, 1993) and organizational behavior (Parker et al., 2010). It produces a two-dimensional coupling model, four minimal behavioral requirements for proactive systems, and a five-question research agenda, without prescribing specific architectures, training procedures, or algorithms. The output is a diagnostic and normative lens for evaluating when proactive intervention is justified, risky, or inappropriate under epistemic incompleteness.

## Method

The core contribution is epistemic-behavioral coupling: a joint two-dimensional space where one axis is behavioral commitment (degree of intervention, irreversibility, and world-shaping) and the other is epistemic legitimacy (whether the agent is justified in intervening given what it can legitimately claim to understand). The paper draws on Kerwin's philosophy of ignorance to distinguish uncertainty from structurally different epistemic failures (error, tacit knowing, taboo, denial, unknown unknowns), and on the inverted doughnut model from organizational behavior to show that initiative is bounded by role scope and recoverability. By mapping four prominent failure modes—epistemic overreach, suppressed epistemic signals, runaway commitment under false certainty, and premature lock-in—onto mis-couplings in this joint space, the framework unifies diverse failures under a single structural cause: commitment is rewarded without regard for epistemic justification. The paper then derives four minimal behavioral requirements (interruptibility, recoverability-scaled commitment, signal preservation, and active modulation of initiative by uncertainty) and identifies commitment, not autonomy, as the critical control variable that existing training objectives systematically underconstrain.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not empirically compare methods against baselines or report win/lose outcomes on benchmarks. Instead, it provides a qualitative taxonomy (Table 1) showing that all six surveyed categories of proactive systems share a structural ceiling: none fully engages with unknown unknowns (UU). The paper's argument is that this gap is not a matter of incremental improvement within existing paradigms (anticipatory, autonomous, or mixed-initiative) but a structural limitation requiring a new coupling between epistemic legitimacy and behavioral commitment. The closest 'comparison' is conceptual: the authors contrast their proposed epistemic-behavioral coupling against (a) action-centric proactivity that treats initiative as the sole control variable, and (b) behavioral accounts (e.g., the inverted doughnut model) that constrain initiative without modeling epistemic validity, arguing that both are necessary but individually insufficient.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes three otherwise disconnected literatures—Kerwin's philosophy of ignorance, the inverted doughnut model from organizational behavior (Parker et al., 2010), and the three dominant AI proactivity paradigms (anticipatory, autonomous, mixed-initiative)—to argue that all existing approaches share a structural blind spot: they treat proactivity as action selection within a pre-specified task frame, externalizing epistemic uncertainty rather than representing it. Rather than building on a single prior method, the paper repurposes the inverted doughnut's behavioral constraints and Kerwin's taxonomy of ignorance (error, tacit knowing, taboo, denial) to define a joint epistemic-behavioral coupling space that no prior AI proactivity framework explicitly models. It departs from the dominant 'more autonomy = better proactivity' framing by identifying commitment, not autonomy, as the critical control variable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reframes the central design question for proactive agents from 'how much initiative should the agent have?' to 'how strongly may it act under incomplete understanding?', which has direct implications for training objectives (current benchmarks reward momentum over justified restraint) and evaluation protocols (success should be assessed at the time of action, not in hindsight). Its five open research questions—particularly how to represent epistemic legitimacy without collapsing it into a single confidence score, and how to evaluate whether commitment was warranted at the time of action—delineate a concrete agenda that current agent benchmarks (AgentBench, GAIA, SWE-bench) do not address. As a position paper with no empirical validation, its impact depends on whether the coupling framework proves generative enough to motivate new architectures, training signals, or evaluation metrics; the risk is that the epistemic-behavioral joint space remains a useful metaphor rather than an operationalizable design constraint.
