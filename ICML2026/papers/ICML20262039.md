## Problem

Noise correlation-based credit assignment methods estimate gradients by injecting perturbations and correlating them with output changes, but they scale poorly because the number of perturbations needed to accurately estimate the Jacobian grows with network size. Additionally, the standard isotropic noise assumption conflicts with neurobiological evidence that neural activity lies on low-dimensional manifolds. The paper asks whether the structure of the neural manifold can be exploited to make noise correlation both scalable and biologically plausible.

## Input / Output

For image classification, the method operates on standard RGB images (32×32 CIFAR-10, 224×224 ImageNet) and produces class labels, with the output layer always trained via exact cross-entropy gradients. For the RNN experiments, inputs are sequences of integers (symbols 0–4) of length T=15, and outputs are the reproduced symbol sequences after a go-cue. The key operational detail is that perturbations are injected into hidden-layer activations (after ReLU) rather than at the input, and the 'output' of the credit-assignment step is a pseudo-gradient at each layer computed from learned feedback weights and the global output error.

## Method

NMNC estimates a low-dimensional activity manifold at each layer online via incremental PCA, then samples noise in the d_l-dimensional manifold space and projects it to the full n_l-dimensional activation space via the learned basis U_l. A noisy forward pass yields an output change Δy, which is correlated with the manifold-restricted perturbation ξ_l to update feedback weights B_l via an exponential moving average. The learned B_l then converts the global output error into layer-wise pseudo-gradients for local Hebbian weight updates. The core theoretical insight is that the Jacobian row space aligns with the neural manifold in trained networks (because gradient descent shapes downstream weights to be spanned by presynaptic activations), so restricting perturbations to the manifold captures most functionally relevant gradient information while reducing the estimation dimensionality from n_l to d_l ≪ n_l. For RNNs, the principle is adapted as rank-1 weight perturbation whose factors are sampled from the hidden-state manifold rather than i.i.d., avoiding the need for explicit temporal feedback pathways.

## Results

On ImageNet (AlexNet), NMNC achieves 39.0±0.3% top-1 test accuracy, substantially outperforming VNC (35.0±0.2%) while a gap remains versus backpropagation. NMNC also yields higher Brain Scores than VNC across all three benchmarks: V4 (0.519 vs. 0.475), IT (0.457 vs. 0.423), and Behavior (0.169 vs. 0.127), and this advantage persists even when NMNC checkpoints are performance-matched to VNC. On CIFAR-10, NMNC approaches backpropagation accuracy and substantially outperforms both VNC and direct feedback alignment under a fixed perturbation budget. In the RNN sequential memory task, rank-1 manifold weight perturbation achieves the best performance among all weight-perturbation variants.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NMNC consistently outperforms vanilla noise correlation (VNC) across all three experimental regimes: on CIFAR-10 it achieves higher test accuracy at every feedback-update frequency (Figure 3B–C), on ImageNet it gains ~4 percentage points in top-1 accuracy (39.0% vs. 35.0%), and in the RNN setting rank-1 manifold WP beats both full-rank WP and rank-1 i.i.d. WP on the sequential memory task. Against direct feedback alignment (DFA, Nøkland 2016), NMNC achieves substantially higher CIFAR-10 accuracy, closing much of the gap to backpropagation. Compared to backpropagation (Rumelhart 1986), NMNC approaches but does not match performance: on CIFAR-10 the gap is small, while on ImageNet a several-point gap remains. In the RNN experiments, standard full-rank weight perturbation (Jabri & Flower 1992) is the weakest baseline, and even rank-1 i.i.d. perturbations underperform the manifold-restricted variant, confirming that the benefit comes from aligning perturbations with the neural manifold rather than merely reducing rank.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends noise correlation-based credit assignment (Williams, 1992; Werfel et al., 2003) by restricting perturbations to the low-dimensional neural activity manifold rather than the full activation space, drawing on the neuroscience literature showing that population activity lies on low-dimensional manifolds (Cunningham & Yu, 2014; Huang et al., 2019). It connects this to the Weight Mirror layerwise feedback scheme (Akrout et al., 2019) for ImageNet-scale experiments and to weight perturbation (Jabri & Flower, 1992) for recurrent networks, while positioning itself against feedback alignment methods (Lillicrap et al., 2016; Nøkland, 2016) that use fixed random feedback. The key conceptual departure is the theoretical and empirical demonstration that the Jacobian row space aligns with the neural manifold in trained networks, providing a principled reason why manifold-restricted perturbations should be more sample-efficient than isotropic ones.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NMNC offers a concrete mechanistic hypothesis for how biological circuits could perform credit assignment by exploiting the low-dimensional structure of population activity, potentially eliminating the need for independent perturbations across all neurons. The finding that manifold dimensionality scales slowly with network width and can even decrease with depth in later layers suggests that the effective dimensionality of credit assignment may remain tractable even in very large networks. Key limitations include the reliance on linear PCA (potentially missing nonlinear manifold structure), the computational cost of online PCA for very large activation tensors, and a persistent performance gap to backpropagation especially at ImageNet scale. Open questions include whether biologically plausible online PCA implementations (Oja, 1982; Pehlevan et al., 2015) can replace the current incremental PCA, whether nonlinear manifold models further improve performance, and how to close the remaining gap to backpropagation through complementary local learning signals.
