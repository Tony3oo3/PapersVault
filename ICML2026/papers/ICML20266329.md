## Problem

Existing agentic RL approaches for LLM tool use assume a fixed, predefined inventory of tools, causing agents to overfit to closed toolsets and fail when new or unseen tools are introduced at inference time. The paper asks: how can LLM agents dynamically select the appropriate tool from a large, evolving toolset during multi-step reasoning trajectories, rather than relying on memorized tool identities?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266329/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent receives a text or image question (e.g., a math problem rendered as an image, a multi-hop QA prompt) together with an evolving toolset of up to 1,346 tools, each annotated with a feature description specifying name, functionality, input-output schema, and usage constraints. The output is a full reasoning trajectory that interleaves three step types—internal CoT reasoning, tool-selection (a rationale followed by an embedding-anchored tool pick), and tool-integration (execution and feedback ingestion)—culminating in a boxed final answer. During training the candidate pool is fixed at 460 tools; at inference it expands to the full 1,346-tool library, of which 886 tools are entirely unseen during training.

## Method

AutoTool employs a dual-phase optimization pipeline. Phase I stabilizes trajectory generation via SFT followed by RL (GRPO), teaching the agent to produce coherent long CoT sequences that correctly interleave reasoning, tool-selection, and tool-integration steps. Phase II refines tool selection specifically by masking out reasoning and integration tokens and optimizing a KL-regularized objective under a Plackett-Luce (PL) ranking formulation: N trajectory rollouts per query are ranked by a masked tool-selection reward (combining a process reward model score and final-answer accuracy), and the policy is trained with a tractable cross-entropy loss derived from the closed-form KL-regularized optimal policy (Theorem 2.1 establishes equivalence between matching the optimal policy and matching its induced PL ranking distribution). Tool selection itself is performed via embedding-anchored matching: each tool is embedded in the LLM's hidden-state space, and at each selection step the model generates a rationale plus a predicted anchor embedding, then samples a tool from a softmax-normalized Frobenius-distance distribution over candidate tool embeddings—avoiding direct name generation and enabling generalization to unseen tools. The framework is trained on a 200k curated dataset spanning 1,000+ tools and 120 task types across math, science, code, and multimodal domains, with explicit tool-selection rationales inserted into trajectories.

## Results

AutoTool (Qwen3-8B) achieves 68.8% on AIME24, 51.2% on AIME25, 45.1% on HotpotQA, 48.8% on 2WikiMultiHopQA, 24.7% on V-Chart, 53.0% on V-Math, and 56.1% on V-Code, consistently outperforming both larger LLM agents (e.g., GPT-4o, QwQ-32B) and domain-specific tool-integration methods (e.g., ReTool, Search-R1) with only 8B parameters. Compared to the strongest agentic training baselines (GRPO, ARPO), AutoTool delivers average gains of 6.4% in math & science, 4.5% in search, 7.7% in code generation, and 6.9% in multimodal understanding. Under a fully unseen-tool setting (886 tools never seen in training), AutoTool retains performance within 2% of its full-toolset results and still outperforms ReTool and Search-R1 on AIME24, HotpotQA, LiveCodeBench, and MedQA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against large LLM agents, AutoTool (Qwen3-8B) substantially outperforms GPT-4o on math (68.8 vs 18.2 on AIME24) and search (45.1 vs 38.7 on HotpotQA), while GPT-4o retains a slight edge on V-Chart (27.3 vs 24.7). AutoTool also surpasses QwQ-32B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-14B on all shared benchmarks, e.g., 68.8 vs 47.3 (QwQ) and 68.8 vs 65.9 (DeepSeek) on AIME24. Among fixed tool-integration methods, AutoTool dominates Search-R1 (7B) on math (68.8 vs 12.1 on AIME24) and ReTool (32B) on search (45.1 vs 21.4 on HotpotQA), though ReTool edges out AutoTool by 1.3 points on AIME24 (70.1 vs 68.8). Against agentic training baselines, AutoTool (Qwen3-8B) improves over GRPO by 10 points on AIME24 (66.7 vs 56.7) and 6.5 points on HotpotQA (45.1 vs 38.6), and over ARPO by 3.3 points on AIME25 (53.3 vs 50.0) and 2.3 points on Bamboogle (56.8 vs 54.5). The 7B multimodal variant (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) similarly outperforms v-ToolRL (2B) on V-Math (44.3 vs 19.5) and V-Code (52.5 vs 13.3), demonstrating that dynamic tool selection generalizes across model scales and modalities.

## Relation to Prior Work

AutoTool extends the agentic RL line (GRPO, ARPO) by adding an explicit tool-selection optimization phase on top of standard trajectory-level policy optimization. Conceptually, it repurposes the Plackett-Luce ranking model (Cheng et al., 2010) as a tractable surrogate for KL-regularized policy optimization, deriving a cross-entropy loss from the closed-form optimal policy of DPO-style RL (Rafailov et al., 2023). The key departure from prior tool-integration work (Toolformer, ReTool, Search-R1) is the shift from fixed, domain-specific tool inventories to embedding-anchored selection over an evolving toolset, where the LLM predicts a hidden-state anchor and matches it against pre-computed tool embeddings rather than generating tool names autoregressively.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap that becomes increasingly relevant as tool ecosystems grow: agents trained on a fixed tool inventory fail to generalize when new tools appear at inference. The dual-phase recipe (stabilize trajectories, then refine tool selection via PL ranking) is a clean, reproducible training pipeline, and the 200k dataset with 1,346 tools provides a useful resource for the community. Open questions include whether the embedding-anchored mechanism scales to truly massive toolsets (10k+), how it handles multi-tool compositions or tool creation (not just selection), and whether the PL-ranking formulation offers advantages over simpler preference-based objectives at larger scale. The evaluation is limited to 7–8B backbones, leaving open whether the approach transfers to frontier-scale models.
