## Problem

Long video understanding faces a fundamental trade-off: cloud LMMs provide superior reasoning but incur prohibitive bandwidth costs and latency, while edge-only solutions sacrifice accuracy. Existing edge-cloud collaborative methods rely on flat semantic similarity to select keyframes, which causes 'Semantic Submergence'—dominant visual features drown out subtle but logically critical cues in complex multi-hop queries, breaking the evidence chain needed for correct reasoning.

## Input / Output

The system takes a long video (sampled at 1 FPS, dominant resolution 1280×720) and a natural-language text query as input, operating under a strict frame transmission budget (16, 32, or 64 frames). The edge node produces a deduplicated, temporally-sorted keyframe set that is uploaded to the cloud, where a large VLM generates the final text answer. The cloud planner outputs a structured DAG of atomic sub-queries with discrete importance scores (1–10), which serves as the intermediate 'observation plan' guiding edge-side retrieval.

## Method

SCOPE shifts the cloud from a passive receiver to an active planner: a cloud LLM decomposes the query via Chain-of-Thought into a Directed Acyclic Graph of atomic, visually verifiable sub-queries, each assigned a semantic importance score. The edge node then allocates its frame budget across sub-queries using the Largest Remainder (Hamilton) Method, ensuring integer frame counts proportional to importance while guaranteeing a minimum per sub-query. Retrieval is executed as a single parallel matrix multiplication (frame embeddings × sub-query embeddings) followed by Maximal Marginal Relevance selection per sub-query to balance relevance and visual diversity. Selected frames are deduplicated, temporally sorted to reconstruct narrative order, and uploaded to the cloud VLM for final reasoning. This structured, importance-weighted multi-sub-query retrieval replaces holistic single-vector similarity matching, directly addressing semantic submergence while keeping edge latency within ~1 second of flat baselines.

## Results

On Video-MME, SCOPE achieves consistent accuracy leadership across all model scales (3B–72B) and frame budgets (K=16/32/64). With the 72B model at K=16, SCOPE reaches 66.0% average accuracy, surpassing the SOTA baseline AKS (64.2%) by 1.8% and the Uniform baseline (62.6%) by 3.4%. On LongVideoBench, the advantage grows with video duration: at K=64 with the 72B model, SCOPE achieves 66.5% average accuracy versus 63.2% for AKS and 64.8% for Top-K, with the largest gains in the 900+ second interval (62.8% vs. 57.3% for AKS). Edge-side latency overhead is minimal—SCOPE adds only ~0.7–1.0 s over Top-K/AKS while delivering the highest accuracy at every budget.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SCOPE is compared against three baselines: Uniform (context-agnostic equal-interval sampling), Top-K (cosine-similarity-based keyframe selection via CLIP/BLIP), and AKS (Tang et al., CVPR 2025, the SOTA adaptive keyframe sampling method). Against AKS, SCOPE wins on nearly all settings: at 72B/K=16 on Video-MME it leads by 1.8% (66.0 vs. 64.2), at 32B/K=16 by 2.5% (63.0 vs. 60.5), and on LongVideoBench at 72B/K=64 by 3.3% (66.5 vs. 63.2). The gap widens for longer videos and tighter budgets, confirming that structured DAG decomposition is most beneficial when evidence is sparse and temporally distributed. Against Top-K, SCOPE's advantage is even larger (up to 3.4% at 72B/K=16 on Video-MME), directly validating the paper's 'semantic submergence' hypothesis that flat similarity matching fails on compositional queries. Uniform sampling, while having negligible edge latency (~0.3 s), consistently trails SCOPE by 3–4% at K=16, showing that query-aware selection is essential. In terms of latency, SCOPE incurs only ~0.7–1.0 s additional edge processing over Top-K/AKS (e.g., 15.26 s vs. 14.26 s at K=16), making the accuracy gains essentially free in the end-to-end pipeline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant 'video-centric' paradigm in edge-cloud collaborative video understanding, where prior methods (cascaded inference, RL-based schedulers, and similarity-based keyframe selectors like AKS) treat the query as a flat semantic vector for holistic frame matching. SCOPE repurposes the cloud LLM's reasoning capacity as an upstream planner that decomposes queries into DAG-structured sub-goals, shifting the edge from a blind compressor to a purposeful executor. The budget allocation mechanism borrows the classical Largest Remainder (Hamilton) apportionment method and combines it with MMR-based diversity-aware retrieval, synthesizing ideas from structured reasoning (CoT/DAG planning) and combinatorial optimization in a way not previously applied to video keyframe selection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically critical deployment bottleneck—bandwidth cost and latency in long-video VQA—and provides a clean architectural answer (query-driven planning at the cloud, evidence retrieval at the edge) that achieves cloud-level accuracy with ~85% latency reduction on real heterogeneous hardware. However, the accuracy gains over the strongest baseline (AKS) are modest (1–2.5%), and the system is limited to temporal keyframe selection without spatial downsampling or video compression, leaving the full bandwidth optimization problem open. The 'semantic submergence' diagnosis is a useful conceptual tool, but its generalization to open-ended or non-decomposable queries remains untested, as does robustness on substantially weaker edge hardware.
