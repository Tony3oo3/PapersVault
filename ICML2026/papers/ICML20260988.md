## Problem

Accurate inversion of rectified-flow models (e.g., FLUX) is bottlenecked by discretization errors in standard Euclidean solvers, which accumulate across timesteps and degrade reconstruction fidelity. Existing corrections like RF-Solver treat the observed trajectory curvature as a numerical artifact to be compensated with high-order Taylor expansions, but the paper argues this curvature is a geometric necessity—a centripetal force that constrains the flow to the data manifold—and that Euclidean solvers introduce a systematic spurious centrifugal drift (O(h²) per step) by linearizing angular motion.

## Input / Output

For inversion, the method takes a real image encoded in FLUX.1-dev's latent space and produces the corresponding latent noise representation via backward ODE integration over 15 steps (32 NFE for the full invert-then-denoise editing pipeline). For editing, it additionally accepts a text prompt that guides the denoising trajectory while the inversion trajectory preserves source structure. The method is entirely training-free and zero-shot, requiring no additional model evaluations beyond the standard per-step velocity query.

## Method

SlerpFlow reinterprets each integration step through intrinsic spherical geometry: it decomposes the latent state Z_t into a radial component ρ_t and a unit direction d_t, then applies Spherical Linear Interpolation (Slerp) to the angular component instead of Euclidean addition, eliminating the O(h²) spurious centrifugal drift that causes trajectories to spiral off the manifold shell. Concretely, a Predictor-Corrector structure (analogous to Heun's method) first estimates a target direction via Slerp between the current and predicted directions, linearly updates the radius, and synthesizes a chordal velocity vector connecting the current state to the geometrically corrected target point. By caching the corrected velocity across steps, SlerpFlow achieves second-order global convergence (O(h²)) while maintaining the computational cost of a first-order Euler solver. The key theoretical contribution is proving that the chordal update matches the exact ODE solution up to the h² term, yielding O(h³) local truncation error, and that the Slerp operator gracefully degrades to linear interpolation when the angular separation approaches zero.

## Results

On PIE-Bench with FLUX.1-dev, SlerpFlow achieves the best reconstruction fidelity across all metrics: 20.73 PSNR / 75.91 SSIM / 30.56 LPIPS (unconditional) and 21.19 PSNR / 79.35 SSIM / 26.01 LPIPS (conditional), outperforming the closest competitor FireFlow (20.19/74.47/31.09 unconditional) at the same 15-step budget. For text-driven editing, SlerpFlow attains the highest CLIP-Whole (26.79) and CLIP-Edited (26.38) scores among all methods, indicating the strongest semantic alignment with the target prompt, while maintaining 32 NFE (matching FireFlow). The method also transfers to SD3, achieving 24.21 PSNR / 0.8241 SSIM / 0.2121 LPIPS at 30 steps, surpassing FireFlow (24.01/0.8024/0.2336).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SlerpFlow outperforms all four reconstruction baselines (Euler, Heun, RF-Solver, FireFlow) on every metric in both unconditional and conditional settings, with the largest gains over RF-Solver (+3.76 PSNR unconditional) and the most marginal but consistent edge over FireFlow (+0.54 PSNR, +1.44 SSIM unconditional). In the editing comparison (Table 2), SlerpFlow achieves the highest CLIP-Whole (26.79) and CLIP-Edited (26.38) scores, beating FireFlow (26.02/25.96) and RF-Solver (26.00/22.88) on semantic alignment while using the same 32 NFE as FireFlow and half the NFE of RF-Solver (60). However, SlerpFlow trails FireFlow slightly on background preservation (PSNR 21.14 vs. 22.47, SSIM 88.21 vs. 89.53), which the authors attribute to the global angular correction perturbing unedited regions. Against diffusion-based editing methods (P2P, PnP, PnP-Inv, MasaCtrl, InfEdit), SlerpFlow achieves the best CLIP scores but higher Structure Distance than InfEdit (23.35 vs. 13.78), reflecting a trade-off between semantic editability and structural preservation. Overall, SlerpFlow is positioned as a geometric regularizer that improves reconstruction fidelity and prompt alignment at matched computational cost, rather than an extreme few-step accelerator.

## Relation to Prior Work

SlerpFlow departs from the dominant line of rectified-flow inversion solvers (RF-Solver, FireFlow) that treat observed trajectory curvature as a numerical artifact to be compensated via higher-order Taylor expansions or gradient-based corrections. Instead, it reframes this curvature through the Manifold Hypothesis as a geometrically necessary centripetal force that constrains flow to the data manifold, and replaces Euclidean polynomial corrections with Spherical Linear Interpolation (Slerp) on the latent hypersphere. The method inherits the Predictor-Corrector structure of Heun's method but operates in decoupled radial-angular coordinates, and its velocity-caching strategy keeps NFE parity with FireFlow. Conceptually, it provides a geometric explanation for why second-order solvers empirically outperform Euler in rectified-flow inversion: the second-order term approximates the intrinsic curvature of the data manifold.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The geometric reinterpretation of trajectory curvature as manifold-constraining rather than solver-error offers a principled lens for designing future flow-based solvers, potentially guiding adaptive or curvature-aware integration strategies. However, the hyperspherical assumption is a simplification: the paper acknowledges over-correction risk in anisotropic or locally flat regions, and the editing experiments reveal a trade-off where stronger semantic alignment (CLIP) comes at the cost of slightly worse background preservation. Open questions include whether adaptive curvature estimation or Lie-group-based integrators can capture the full geometric structure without the uniform-shell assumption, and whether the approach generalizes to non-rectified flow formulations or 3D generation.
