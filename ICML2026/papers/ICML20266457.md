## Problem

Benchmark-centered evaluation has been extraordinarily effective for capability research (Q1: can machines exhibit intelligent behavior?), but the field is now shifting toward Q2 questions (can machines do so while aligned, interpretable, and safe?) where success criteria must be constructed through deliberation rather than read off from tasks. The paper argues that importing Q1's benchmark-driven selection regime into Q2 without validating that it transfers imposes an 'exaptation tax'—systematically filtering out exploratory work whose value is latent and only revealed under future problem regimes, potentially foreclosing the very discoveries Q2 requires.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, this work takes the current institutional structure of AI research evaluation—benchmark culture, peer review norms, funding mechanisms, and venue practices—as its analytical subject, drawing on historical cases from AI, biology, and the science of science. It produces a three-part diagnostic (community composition, research agenda, evaluation limits) and a set of institutional proposals for restoring exaptive capacity without abandoning benchmarking, including plural evaluation regimes, protected venues for exploratory work, and reflexive governance of selection criteria.

## Method

The paper's core analytical tool is exaptation—a biological concept for traits evolved for one function that later become decisive for another—applied to research ecosystems to argue that ideas with latent potential must survive periods of apparent uncompetitiveness to later realize that potential. It traces AI's evaluation history from the pre-benchmark pluralistic era through the Common Task Framework to the current saturation regime, using documented cases (GPUs, U-Net, GANs, backpropagation, LSTM) to illustrate how selection regimes determine which ideas persist long enough to be repurposed. The paper then develops the Q1→Q2 distinction as a kind-level rather than degree-level shift, arguing that such-that problems lack the world-side ground truth that made benchmarking effective for capability research, and that premature convergence on current proxies may foreclose discovery of what we actually need to measure. Finally, it proposes three institutional mechanisms: plural evaluation regimes with controlled randomness and disagreement-as-signal, protected venues with time-limited runway and ex-post tracking, and reflexive governance that treats critique of evaluation criteria as a citable core research activity.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experimental results or main results table. The paper's central empirical claims are historical and structural: it cites a systematic study of 65 million papers and patents (Wu et al., 2019) showing small teams more often 'disrupt' existing trajectories, an analysis of 41 million papers (Hao et al., 2026) confirming AI tools narrow thematic focus, and a study of 60 LLM benchmarks (Akhtar et al., 2026) finding saturation is now the norm. The paper's 'results' are the three-part diagnosis (community composition narrowing, research agenda fixation on hill-climbable problems, and evaluation limits under Knightian uncertainty) and four institutional proposals (plural evaluation regimes, protected exploratory venues, long-horizon funding, and reflexive governance of evaluation criteria).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not compare methods or baselines in the empirical sense; `compares_to` is empty. Instead, it engages with five alternative views (objections) in Section 6: the Success Objection (benchmark culture produced AlphaFold, GPT-4, etc.), the Invisibility Objection (lost innovations are unfalsifiable), the Protection Objection (protected venues could shelter unproductive work), the Industry Lab Objection (elite labs already escape benchmark pressure), and the Measurement Objection (such-that problems need more rigor, not less). The authors concede the specific counterfactual of lost innovations is unavailable but argue structural narrowing is observable (thematic diversity stagnation per Klinger et al., 2020) and that the asymmetry of error costs favors the proposed hedges: if wrong, the cost is some inefficiency; if right, the field may be filtering out what it most needs to find.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes concepts from evolutionary biology (exaptation, Gould & Vrba), philosophy of science (Kuhn's normal/revolutionary science), sociology of science (Bourdieu's field theory), and the history of AI evaluation (Koch & Peterson's Common Task Framework) to articulate a new argument about the structural costs of benchmark-driven research selection. It builds on prior critiques of benchmarking (Raji et al., Koch & Peterson) but departs from them by reframing the issue not as a measurement-validity problem but as an evolutionary-ecological one: benchmarks impose a selection pressure that taxes the persistence of ideas whose value is latent or only revealed under future problem regimes. The Q1→Q2 distinction (capability vs. such-that problems) is the paper's key conceptual contribution, drawing on Gabriel's alignment analysis, Knightian uncertainty, and the science-of-science literature on combinatorial innovation (Wu et al., Uzzi et al.).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that the evaluation regime optimized for capability research may actively foreclose the exploratory work needed for alignment, interpretability, and safety—poses a genuine strategic question for the field at a moment when leading benchmarks are saturating. Its proposals (plural evaluation regimes, protected venues, reflexive governance of criteria) are institutional rather than technical, limiting immediate practical impact for individual researchers but making them relevant to venue organizers, funders, and AI policy makers. Open questions include whether the Q1→Q2 distinction is as sharp as claimed, whether the exaptation tax is measurable or merely unfalsifiable (as the authors concede in their Invisibility Objection), and whether the proposed mechanisms can be implemented without creating the 'protection objection' they themselves raise.
