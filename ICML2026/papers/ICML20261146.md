## Problem

Despite widespread adoption of graph mixup as a data augmentation technique for graph classification, no independent empirical evaluation has been conducted under a unified, statistically rigorous protocol, and no prior work has analyzed whether the generated mixup graphs actually interpolate between their inputs. This paper addresses both gaps: it questions whether existing graph mixup methods provide any real benefit and investigates the relationship between interpolation quality and empirical performance.

## Input / Output

Each mixup method takes a pair of input graphs (with node/edge features) and a mixup ratio λ sampled from Beta(α, α), producing a synthetic mixup graph and a corresponding convex-combination class label. The evaluation framework uses GCN and GIN as backbone classifiers on six TUDataset benchmarks (MUTAG, ENZYMES, IMDB-BINARY, PROTEINS, IMDB-MULTI, NCI1) and OGBG-MOLHIV, with 5-fold nested cross-validation and three repeated runs per split. The interpolation analysis additionally computes exact graph edit distances (GED) between input and mixup graphs to quantify how well each method satisfies the interpolation criteria.

## Method

The paper introduces a formal interpolation framework defining two criteria (IC1, IC2) that a mixup graph must satisfy to be a valid interpolation at ratio λ, along with absolute interpolation error (AIE) and normalized mixup interpolation error (IE) measured via GED. To establish an optimal reference, the authors propose GED-Mixup, which computes an exact minimal edit set using A* search (AStar-BMao) and applies a λ-fraction of edit operations in a valid topological order, achieving near-zero interpolation error by construction (Proposition 2). The empirical study evaluates seven existing mixup methods plus GED-Mixup under a rigorous unified protocol with Bayesian hyperparameter tuning and Welch's t-test, and performs pooled analyses under three statistical assumptions of increasing strength. The key findings are that no method yields statistically significant improvement over the no-mixup baseline, most methods produce graphs with high interpolation error (mean IE > 1, with S-Mixup, GeoMix, and FGW-Mixup exceeding 2), and while poor interpolation correlates with degraded performance, even optimal interpolation via GED-Mixup does not produce significant gains—suggesting interpolation quality is necessary but not sufficient for mixup to be beneficial.

## Results

Across six TUDataset benchmarks (MUTAG, ENZYMES, IMDB-BINARY, PROTEINS, IMDB-MULTI, NCI1) with GCN and GIN backbones, none of the seven evaluated graph mixup methods achieved a statistically significant improvement over the no-mixup baseline under any of three pooled statistical assumptions (A1–A3). Pooled average accuracy was 70.29% for the baseline versus at most 70.69% (SubMix, p=0.79) and 70.54% (GED-Mixup, p=0.86) under the weakest assumption A1. Under the strongest assumption A3, S-Mixup (68.11%, p<0.01) and GeoMix (69.11%, p<0.01) were significantly worse than baseline. Interpolation analysis revealed that most methods (S-Mixup, GeoMix, FGW-Mixup) produced mixup graphs with mean interpolation error (mIE) ≥ 1.6, far from the near-zero error of GED-Mixup (0.02) or SubMix (0.40).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates seven prior graph mixup methods against a no-mixup baseline under a unified nested cross-validation protocol. None of the seven methods—Emb-Mixup (Wang et al., 2021), FGW-Mixup (Ma et al., 2023), G-Mixup (Han et al., 2022), GeoMix (Zeng et al., 2024), If-Mixup (Guo & Mao, 2023), S-Mixup (Ling et al., 2022), or SubMix (Yoo et al., 2022)—achieved a statistically significant improvement over the baseline in the pooled analysis under the weakest assumption (A1). Under stronger assumptions (A2, A3), S-Mixup (p<0.01) and GeoMix (p<0.01) were significantly worse than baseline, and G-Mixup was also significantly worse under A3 (p=0.03). The authors' own GED-Mixup baseline, which uses optimal graph-edit-distance-based interpolation (mIE≈0.02), also failed to produce a significant gain (pooled 70.54% vs. 70.29%, p=0.86), indicating that even perfect interpolation is insufficient to improve performance. SubMix (mIE≈0.40) and If-Mixup (mIE≈0.91) interpolated moderately well but still showed no significant benefit. The key takeaway is that the previously reported improvements in the literature do not survive rigorous independent evaluation with proper significance testing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as the first independent evaluation study of graph mixup, departing from the prior literature that primarily proposed new mixup methods (If-Mixup, S-Mixup, SubMix, G-Mixup, FGW-Mixup, GeoMix, Embedding Mixup) without rigorous cross-method comparison or significance testing. It builds on the original mixup framework (Zhang et al., 2018), the graph edit distance literature (Sanfeliu & Fu, 1983; Chang et al., 2023), and optimal transport theory (Villani, 2008) to formalize interpolation criteria and error metrics that no prior graph mixup paper had introduced. The GED-Mixup baseline is a simplified variant of EPIC (Heo et al., 2024), repurposed as an analysis tool rather than a competitive method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that no graph mixup method yields statistically significant gains—and that even provably optimal interpolation (GED-Mixup) fails to improve performance—challenges the core assumption that mixup's benefits in vision/NLP transfer to graphs. This opens a fundamental question: what additional factors beyond correct interpolation are needed for mixup to be beneficial in the graph domain? The paper also highlights methodological gaps in prior evaluations (lack of significance testing, non-independent reproduction, use of validation rather than test performance), setting a higher bar for future graph augmentation research. Limitations include evaluation on relatively small TUDataset benchmarks and two backbone architectures, leaving open whether results generalize to larger-scale or more complex graph tasks.
