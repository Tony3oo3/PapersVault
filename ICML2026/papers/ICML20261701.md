## Problem

Standard stationary GP kernels (RBF, Matérn, RQ) impose a fixed smoothness assumption, and spectral mixture kernels built from Gaussian components are light-tailed in frequency, requiring many components to capture heavy-tailed or rough spectral structures. This paper asks whether the spectral tail exponent itself can be made a learnable parameter, so that a single kernel family adapts its effective regularity from data without manual kernel-class selection.

## Input / Output

The method consumes noisy black-box evaluations y_i = f(x_i) + ε over a compact search space X ⊂ R^d (d ranging from 3 to 30 in experiments) under a fixed evaluation budget T, with initial design points of size n_0. It outputs the best observed point x_best, the full GP posterior (mean and variance) for acquisition-function optimization, and the learned stability parameters — a single shared α for ALAS or per-dimension α_j values for ALAS-Sep. The α parameters are constrained to (0, 2] via a smooth bounded transform during optimization, and the kernel hyperparameters (weights, modulation frequencies γ, spectral scales δ) are re-estimated by exact marginal-likelihood maximization at every BO iteration.

## Method

ALAS constructs a stationary GP kernel whose spectral density is a symmetric α-stable (SαS) distribution, making the stability index α ∈ (0, 2] a learnable hyperparameter that directly controls spectral tail heaviness: α = 2 recovers the Gaussian (RBF) case, while α < 2 yields polynomial tails p(ω) ∝ |ω|^{-(1+α)} that capture rougher structure. The 1D component is a powered-exponential envelope exp(−|τ/ℓ|^α) modulated by cos(2πγτ), where the modulation frequency γ shifts spectral mass to capture oscillatory correlations without altering the tail exponent. Two parameterizations are offered: ALAS uses a single d-dimensional component with ARD scales and one shared α, while ALAS-Sep sums independent 1D ALAS components so each dimension learns its own α_j, improving robustness on approximately decomposable objectives. On the theory side, the paper proves that the SαS spectral tail implies Mercer eigenvalue decay λ_h = Θ(h^{-(1+α)}), yielding explicit information-gain bounds γ_T = Õ(T^{1/(1+α)}) and regret bounds R_T = Õ(T^{1/2 + 1/(2(1+α))}), with the additive variant giving a linear-in-d information-gain bound governed by α_min. Training uses exact GP inference with L-BFGS on the marginal log-likelihood, initialized via an FFT-based empirical-spectrum heuristic for γ and δ, and α initialized near 2 so that marginal likelihood moves it toward rougher regimes only when supported by data.

## Results

On 1D predictive-fit diagnostics (Table 3), ALAS achieves the best RMSE on Weierstrass (0.26±0.09 vs. RBF 0.31±0.11) and Rastrigin (0.01±0.00 vs. RBF 0.02±0.01), with the highest PLL on Rastrigin (4.33±0.34 vs. RBF 3.18±0.11) while matching RBF on smooth Branin (RMSE 0.27 vs. 0.27). In full BO optimization (Figure 3, d=3–30), ALAS is a robust default on interaction-heavy tasks (Rosenbrock-10D, Robot Pushing, Portfolio) and ALAS-Sep shows clear gains on approximately decomposable or oscillatory landscapes (Levy-20D, Rastrigin-30D, Weierstrass-3D). The learned stability parameter α adapts from ~1.96 on smooth Exponential-5D down to ~1.56 on rough Weierstrass-3D, confirming data-driven smoothness selection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against standard stationary kernels (RBF, Matérn-5/2, RQ), ALAS wins on rough or oscillatory objectives: on 1D Weierstrass it reduces RMSE from 0.31 (RBF) to 0.26 and lifts PLL from −2.86 to 0.01 by learning α≈1.24; on 1D Rastrigin it halves RMSE (0.01 vs. 0.02) and boosts PLL by 36% (4.33 vs. 3.18). On smooth Branin, ALAS ties RBF (RMSE 0.27 vs. 0.27) and slightly exceeds it in PLL (0.18 vs. 0.06), confirming no overfitting penalty. Against spectral baselines (Sinc, SDK), ALAS and ALAS-Sep are competitive across all nine BO benchmarks (d=3–30); ALAS is particularly strong on interaction-heavy tasks (Rosenbrock-10D, Robot Pushing, Portfolio) where a single d-dimensional component captures cross-dimensional dependencies, while ALAS-Sep dominates on approximately decomposable objectives (Levy-20D, Rastrigin-30D) by learning per-dimension α_j. The paper notes a mild 'warm-up' effect where ALAS lags fixed-smoothness baselines in the first few BO iterations before overtaking them as α stabilizes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the spectral kernel learning line initiated by Wilson & Adams (2013), which approximates the spectral density with Gaussian mixtures, by replacing Gaussian components with symmetric α-stable distributions whose stability parameter α is learned via marginal likelihood. The base envelope is the classical powered-exponential covariance (Diggle et al., 1998), augmented with a learnable cosine modulation to capture oscillatory structure. For the separable variant ALAS-Sep, the paper adopts the additive GP decomposition framework of Kandasamy et al. (2015) to enable dimension-wise tail adaptivity while retaining exact inference. The key conceptual departure from prior spectral kernels is that α directly and continuously controls spectral tail heaviness, interpolating from Gaussian (α=2) to Cauchy (α=1) behavior within a single kernel family, rather than requiring many Gaussian mixture components to approximate heavy-tailed spectra.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ALAS offers a principled, data-driven alternative to manual kernel selection in BO: the learned α adapts effective regularity to the objective's unknown smoothness, with explicit regret bounds showing how α governs sample complexity. The practical limitation is that exact GP inference still scales cubically, and the single-component design (one α-stable component per dimension) may be less expressive than multi-component spectral mixtures for highly complex landscapes. Open questions include extending to scalable GP approximations, exploring intermediate structures between the shared-α and fully per-dimension parameterizations, and whether multi-component α-stable mixtures would further improve performance on highly non-stationary or multi-scale objectives.
