## Problem

Existing LVLM robustness benchmarks focus on hallucination or misleading textual inputs, but misleading visual inputs that naturally arise in real-world scenes (e.g., mirror reflections, occlusions, visual illusions) remain substantially underexplored. This gap is critical because, unlike humans who can correct initial misperceptions through contextual reasoning, LVLMs often rely on superficial visual patterns and struggle to disambiguate naturally occurring misleading cues.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260579/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance consists of a single RGB image (either normal or its misleading counterpart) paired with a four-option multiple-choice question (A–D). The paired design is central: both images in a pair share nearly identical semantic content and the same MCQ, differing only by subtle misleading visual cues, with at least one distractor crafted to appear plausible under the misleading condition. The model outputs a single letter answer, and evaluation yields per-category and overall accuracy on normal (Acc_n) and misleading (Acc_m) images, plus the MVI-Sensitivity score computed as |Acc_n − Acc_m| / Acc_n.

## Method

MVI-Bench organizes misleading visual inputs into a three-level taxonomy grounded in fundamental visual primitives: Visual Concept (Resemblance, Representation Confusion), Visual Attribute (Material Confusion), and Visual Relationship (Occlusion, Mirror Reflection, Visual Illusion). The benchmark comprises 624 expertly annotated VQA pairs (1,248 instances) sourced from natural images, Seedream-generated synthetic images, and human-edited images, with data filtering via two strong LVLMs (InternVL3-2B, Qwen2.5-VL-3B) to remove low-difficulty samples where both models answer correctly on both images. The key evaluation metric, MVI-Sensitivity, quantifies relative performance degradation from normal to misleading inputs, enabling fine-grained robustness comparison. The paper further introduces controlled experiments—caption-assisted inference to isolate perceptual contributions and long-form chain-of-thought ablations to isolate reasoning contributions—along with attention-guided masking to identify spurious correlations in counterintuitive cases where models answer misleading images correctly but fail on normal ones.

## Results

Across 18 state-of-the-art LVLMs evaluated on MVI-Bench (1,248 VQA instances), the best-performing model GPT-5-Chat achieves 63.78% accuracy on misleading images (Acc_m) with 23.02% MVI-Sensitivity, while GPT-4.1 attains the lowest sensitivity at 20.80% (Acc_m = 62.82%). Open-source models are substantially more vulnerable: the strongest, Qwen2.5-VL-72B, reaches only 57.21% Acc_m (32.38% Sens), and Molmo-7B performs worst at 37.66% Acc_m with 48.69% Sens. Humans achieve 98.24% Acc_m with just 1.63% Sensitivity, confirming that the misleading scenarios are easily resolvable by humans but remain highly challenging for all current LVLMs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among closed-source models, GPT-5-Chat leads with 63.78% Acc_m, narrowly ahead of GPT-4.1 (62.82%) and Gemini-2.5-Pro (62.50%), while Claude-3.7-Sonnet performs worst in this group at 42.13% Acc_m despite its strength on reasoning tasks. Among open-source models, Qwen2.5-VL-72B and Qwen2-VL-72B are the strongest (57.21% and 58.17% Acc_m respectively), but both trail the best closed-source models by roughly 6 percentage points; Molmo-7B is the weakest at 37.66% Acc_m. Spatial-reasoning categories (Mirror Reflection, Occlusion) are the most challenging for all models: even GPT-5-Chat drops to 70.19% on Mirror and 51.43% on Occlusion, while smaller open-source models (InternVL3-2B, Qwen2.5-VL-3B) fall below 30% on Mirror. Compared to prior misleading-visual benchmarks (IllusionVQA, IllusionBench+, O-Bench), MVI-Bench uniquely covers all six misleading categories with a paired normal/misleading design, enabling the MVI-Sensitivity metric that prior benchmarks (which rely on single-image accuracy) cannot provide.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by shifting the robustness evaluation focus from misleading textual inputs (MAD-Bench, Dang et al.) and single-phenomenon visual benchmarks (IllusionVQA, O-Bench) to a comprehensive, taxonomy-driven evaluation of misleading visual inputs across six categories. It departs from prior work in two key ways: (1) grounding the taxonomy in fundamental visual primitives (concept, attribute, relationship) rather than ad-hoc phenomenon lists, and (2) introducing a paired normal-vs-misleading design with the MVI-Sensitivity metric for fine-grained degradation measurement, which prior benchmarks relying on raw accuracy lack. The paper does not build on a specific method but repurposes the VQA evaluation paradigm to isolate the effect of visual misleading cues through controlled image pairs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even frontier models (GPT-5-Chat, Gemini-2.5-Pro) suffer 20–40% accuracy drops under misleading visual inputs, and that open-source models are substantially more vulnerable, providing a concrete gap for the community to close. The finding that long-form chain-of-thought reasoning can degrade performance on perception-heavy categories (visual resemblance, representation confusion) challenges the prevailing assumption that more reasoning always helps, and the spurious-correlation cases (~4%) expose a shortcut-learning problem in current VQA training. Limitations include the relatively small scale (1,248 instances), reliance on the paired design for the spurious-correlation analysis (limiting generalizability), and the fact that the taxonomy, while principled, may not capture all real-world misleading phenomena. Open questions include whether targeted data augmentation for underrepresented categories (visual illusion, occlusion) can close the gap, and whether training objectives that enforce causal grounding over shortcut cues can mitigate the observed vulnerabilities.
