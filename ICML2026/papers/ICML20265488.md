## Problem

Large reasoning models trained with RL produce chain-of-thought traces that frequently contain structural redundancy (repetitive restatements, trivial deductions), inflating computational cost without improving correctness. Existing mitigation strategies rely on token-uniform length penalties that penalize all tokens equally, making them unable to distinguish necessary reasoning from redundant exploration and risking suppression of essential intermediate steps. The paper asks whether a finer-grained, segment-level criterion can identify and suppress only the truly redundant portions of a CoT trace.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt (a math problem or reasoning question) and produces a structured output o = [z || a], where z is the CoT reasoning trace and a is the final answer. During training, the sliding-window detector operates on the token sequence z under the old policy π_old, computing the joint probability of w consecutive tokens at each position. The trimming reward is gated on correctness: it is applied only to rollouts judged correct by a rule-based verifier, preventing the efficiency signal from encouraging degenerate shortcuts on incorrect trajectories.

## Method

SLAT derives a sufficient condition (Proposition 3.1) under which allocating probability mass to a segment z₁ is suboptimal under a correctness-length Lagrangian objective: the condition is easier to satisfy when the segment is long and generated with high probability, identifying long near-deterministic stretches as natural candidates for suppression. To operationalize this, SLAT uses a sliding window of size w over the CoT tokens, computing the joint probability of each window under π_old and counting windows whose probability exceeds a threshold τ; this count C(z) grows roughly linearly with the length of a redundant high-probability stretch, serving as a computable proxy for the theoretical condition. The trimming reward r_ER(z) = −C(z) is added to the original correctness reward (r = r_ori + λ·r_ER) and integrated into GRPO training as reward shaping, with λ set small (0.001) so the trimming term acts as a gentle regularizer. The approach is correctness-gated (applied only to correct rollouts) and requires only three hyperparameters (w, τ, λ) that jointly control trimming aggressiveness.

## Results

On DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, SLAT achieves the best average accuracy (66.4%) across MATH500, OlympiadBench, AMC23, AIME24, and AIME25 while reducing average CoT length from 8,404 to 4,176 tokens (−50%) relative to the original model. On Qwen2.5-Math-7B trained from scratch with GRPO, SLAT (w=7, λ=0.001) achieves 46.2% average accuracy at 566 tokens, compared to 48.8% at 1,054 tokens for vanilla GRPO and 45.7% at 617 tokens for Length-MaxMin. On Qwen3-14B, SLAT reduces average CoT length from 5,024 to 2,677 tokens (−47%) with only a −0.5% average accuracy drop, and even improves AMC23 accuracy by +3.4% with a −61% length reduction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SLAT achieves a Pareto improvement over 6 of 7 baselines in Table 1, simultaneously attaining higher average accuracy and shorter CoT length. Compared to LC-R1 (64.0% acc, 4,676 tokens), SLAT improves accuracy by +2.4 points while further reducing length by 10.5%. Against AdaptThink (65.5%, 6,272 tokens), SLAT gains +0.9 accuracy at 33% shorter traces. DAST is the closest competitor in accuracy (66.1% vs 66.4%) but retains 45% longer CoTs (7,664 vs 4,176 tokens). The aggressive compression baseline L1-Max achieves the shortest traces (3,144 tokens) but sacrifices 5.2 accuracy points, whereas SLAT recovers that accuracy at only a 33% length increase. In the base-model training setting (Table 5), SLAT (w=7, λ=0.001) matches or exceeds Length-MaxMin and Length-Mean on harder benchmarks (AIME24: 30.4% vs 28.5%/27.9%) while Truncation underperforms across all datasets, confirming that token-uniform penalties disrupt long-horizon reasoning dependencies that segment-aware trimming preserves.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the GRPO training framework (Shao et al., 2024) and the R1-zero recipe from DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025) as its RL backbone, but departs from the dominant line of efficient-reasoning work that applies token-uniform length penalties (ThinkPrune, LC-R1, L1, TLMRE, Kimi-k1.5). The key conceptual departure is a segment-level perspective: rather than penalizing all tokens equally, the paper identifies high-probability, low-information segments as the primary source of redundancy and derives a sufficient condition (Proposition 3.1) under which generating such a segment is suboptimal under a correctness-length Lagrangian. This theoretical characterization then motivates a simple sliding-window reward-shaping rule that operationalizes the condition in practice, positioning SLAT as a structure-aware alternative to both sequence-level length control and uncertainty-based halting heuristics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 50% reduction in average CoT length while maintaining competitive accuracy across five math benchmarks and two model scales (7B and 14B) is practically significant for deployment cost, and the method's simplicity (a sliding window with a fixed probability threshold) makes it easy to adopt. However, the approach remains tied to a fixed window size and a single probability threshold, and the paper acknowledges that adaptive segment identification, scaling to 100B+ models, and safety implications of training-time compression (adversarial robustness, information leakage) are open questions. The theoretical condition, while clean, relies on a conservative upper bound (P(A|x,z₁) ≤ P(A|x)/π(z₁|x)), so the practical proxy may be looser than the theory suggests, and the correctness-gating (penalizing only correct rollouts) limits the trimming signal's coverage during early training.
