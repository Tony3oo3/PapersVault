## Problem

AI research predominantly treats models as static artifacts, responding to undesirable behaviors with post-hoc interventions (RLHF, input/output filtering) rather than understanding why those behaviors emerge during training. This 'fix it in post' mentality produces brittle, non-generalizing solutions and prevents the field from developing predictive, causal theories of how training dynamics shape model behavior.

## Input / Output

The paper takes as its subject the existing body of work across mechanistic interpretability, fairness, memorization, and simplicity bias research, evaluating each area against criteria drawn from the philosophy of science. It produces a three-tiered capability framework (predict, intervene, design) as a progress metric, a set of concrete open problems per domain, and a diagnosis of where current research practice diverges from scientific norms.

## Method

The paper grounds its argument in Kuhn's six criteria for good scientific practice (empirical accuracy, internal/external consistency, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness) and uses them to diagnose gaps in current AI research. It then proposes three progressively stronger capabilities—predicting model properties from early training signals, intervening when trajectories go wrong, and designing training procedures that reliably produce desired properties—as operational measures of progress toward a science of AI. The framework is applied to four case studies (mechanistic interpretability, fairness/bias, memorization, and simplicity bias), where the authors identify which areas have begun building predictive or causal theories and which remain purely descriptive. A key conceptual move is reframing models as snapshots of time-evolving dynamical systems rather than fixed objects, so that explanations target the training process rather than a single set of weights.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experimental results or main results tables. Its central argument is that AI research should shift from post-hoc analysis to studying training dynamics, framed through three progressive capabilities: predicting outcomes from early training signals, intervening when trajectories go wrong, and designing training procedures that reliably produce desired properties. The paper identifies scaling laws for loss as the one area where prediction is already routine, and highlights memorization dynamics (e.g., models are most 'absorbent' around 10–20% into training) and simplicity bias (progressive learning of higher-order statistics) as the most promising nascent theories. It surveys progress across mechanistic interpretability, fairness, memorization, and simplicity bias, concluding that progress is real but uneven.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not experimentally compare methods against baselines. Instead, it contrasts the proposed 'science of AI' framework against four alternative viewpoints: (1) Engineering Sufficiency, which the authors acknowledge has produced real but fragile advances; (2) Anti-Theory Skepticism, which they rebut by pointing to scaling laws and memorization dynamics as evidence that partial theories yield predictive insight; (3) Automation as Scientific Progress, which they argue conflates optimization with understanding; and (4) Safety Pragmatism, which they accept as necessary in the short term but argue is insufficient for design-level solutions. The paper's implicit 'baseline' is the dominant 'fix it in post' paradigm (RLHF, filtering, post-training interventions), which it characterizes as brittle and non-generalizing, citing examples such as safety fine-tuning being undone by benign fine-tuning (Qi et al., 2024) or past-tense jailbreaks (Andriushchenko & Flammarion, 2025).

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not introduce a new method but reframes existing research threads—scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022), mechanistic interpretability (Olsson et al., 2022; Tigges et al., 2024), memorization dynamics (Biderman et al., 2023a; Huang et al., 2024), and simplicity bias (Refinetti et al., 2023; Belrose et al., 2024)—under a unified 'science of AI' program centered on training dynamics rather than static model analysis. It draws on Kuhn's (1977) criteria for scientific practice to argue that the field's dominant 'fix it in post' paradigm (RLHF, filtering, post-hoc debiasing) is insufficient, and that the object of study should be the time-evolving training process. The paper carves new conceptual ground by proposing a three-tiered progress metric (predict → intervene → design) that no prior work has articulated as a unified framework, and by explicitly identifying which existing results satisfy or fail to satisfy each tier.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's main value is diagnostic: it identifies concrete, actionable gaps (predicting memorization from early checkpoints, quantitatively mapping data composition to bias amplification, designing training for target demographic frequencies) that the field has been working around without a unifying theory. Its limitation is that, as a position paper, it offers no new empirical results or methods—its contribution is entirely in framing and synthesis. The open problems it identifies (e.g., the non-monotonic memorization window at 10–20% training, the lack of causal tracing through multi-stage training pipelines for bias) are genuinely underexplored and could redirect significant research effort. Whether the field will adopt this 'training dynamics as primary object' framing over the entrenched engineering paradigm remains an open sociological question the paper itself acknowledges.
