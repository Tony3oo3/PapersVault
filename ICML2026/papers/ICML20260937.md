## Problem

Predicting the next state of an unknown, marginally stable nonlinear dynam system from partial observations is a fundamental problem in science and engineering, yet existing approaches either require explicit (nonconvex) system identification that fails at marginal stability, or rely on black-box deep architectures lacking formal performance guarantees. This paper introduces a third paradigm—improper learning via online convex optimization—that provides provable prediction guarantees for any nonexpansive nonlinear system with finitely many marginally stable modes, without ever recovering the true dynamics.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a streaming sequence of scalar observations y_1, …, y_t (and optionally past inputs u_1, …, u_t) and produces a single next-step prediction ŷ_{t+1} at each time step. Internally, it constructs a (T−1)-dimensional Hankel matrix from the observation history to extract spectral components, then combines these with a fixed number of autoregressive terms and regression terms. The algorithm is fully online: parameters are updated via projected gradient steps (or the VAW forecaster) after each observation, with no batch training phase and no dependence on the hidden state dimension of the underlying system.

## Method

The core algorithm, Observation Spectral Filtering (OSF), predicts by filtering past observations through the top eigenvectors of a Hankel matrix (weighted by σ_i^{1/4}), adding autoregressive components on recent inputs and observations, and optimizing the combined predictor via online convex optimization. The key theoretical insight is that this improper learner provably matches the best high-dimensional Luenberger observer system in hindsight, whose complexity is captured by Q⋆—the minimal spectral condition number over all observer gains that place eigenvalues in a desirable set [0,1−ρ]∪{1}∪D_{1−γ}. For nonlinear systems, a global linearization via state-space discretization (a finite Markov chain approximating the Perron-Frobenius operator) reduces the problem to the linear case, and the resulting loss bound scales as Õ(R² log³(Q⋆T)/γ + R² log(Q⋆)/ρ), with no dependence on the hidden dimension. This generalizes prior spectral filtering to asymmetric dynamics and adversarial noise, and the Luenberger program provides a new control-theoretic measure of learnability that quantifies how many marginally stable complex eigenvalues a system inherently requires.

## Results

The main theoretical guarantee (Theorem 4.1) shows that Algorithm 1 (Observation Spectral Filtering) achieves cumulative squared prediction error O(R² log₊(R Q⋆ T/γ)³/γ + R² log₊(Q⋆)/ρ) for any nonexpansive nonlinear system, where Q⋆ is the Luenberger complexity measuring the non-normality of the best linear observer. Informally, if N is the number of marginally-stable complex eigenvalues in a linearization, then Õ(N²) parameters suffice to achieve loss Õ(R² N³). On the Lorenz system (σ=10, ρ=28, β=8/3), SFeDMD (spectral filtering over nonlinearly lifted observables) achieves the lowest instantaneous prediction loss in both fully and partially observed settings, outperforming eDMD with fewer parameters, while direct LDS observer learning fails due to nonconvexity and instability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four algorithms on the Lorenz system: direct LDS observer parameterization (LDS), spectral filtering over raw observations (SF), classical eDMD Koopman operator learning, and spectral filtering over lifted observables (SFeDMD). SFeDMD achieves the lowest prediction loss in the fully observed setting, outperforming eDMD despite using fewer parameters, confirming that the filtering-based predictor more effectively exploits the nonlinear lifting. In the partially observed setting (y_t = Cx_t), both SF and SFeDMD learn successfully while eDMD cannot handle partial observations naturally, as filtering aggregates information across time. Direct LDS observer learning (LDS) fails entirely due to instability and nonconvexity of the optimization landscape. In autoregressive rollouts of length 512 from unseen initial conditions, SFeDMD maintains sharp predictions longer than eDMD, and avoids qualitatively wrong trajectories that eDMD produces due to error compounding with the system's chaotic dynamics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the spectral filtering framework of Hazan et al. (2017) from symmetric, noise-free linear systems to general asymmetric linear systems with adversarial noise, and then lifts the guarantee to nonlinear dynamics via a discretized Markov chain linearization. The key conceptual departure is the 'improper learning' paradigm: rather than explicitly recovering the Koopman operator or a DMD-style linearization (as in Brunton et al., 2021), the algorithm competes in hindsight against a class of Luenberger observer systems, repurposing classical control-theoretic tools (pole placement, observer design) as a theoretical comparator class rather than a design target. This sidesteps the nonconvexity and convergence difficulties of explicit system identification while retaining strong guarantees, and it generalizes the partial asymmetric extension of Marsden & Hazan (2025) to fully general eigenvalue configurations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Q_star measure provides the first quantitative bridge between control-theoretic observability and machine-learning learnability, giving practitioners a principled a priori criterion for whether a given nonlinear system is efficiently predictable by spectral filtering. The main limitations are that the guarantee covers only single-step prediction (not autoregressive rollout or multi-step forecasting), requires the dynamics to be nonexpansive and bounded, and the 1/√T spectral-gap requirement in the observer construction may be restrictive. Open questions include extending the framework to open-loop inputs, adversarial disturbances in the nonlinear setting, and whether the improper-learning paradigm transfers to the autoregressive deployment regime where next-token accuracy does not guarantee rollout fidelity.
