## Problem

Post-hoc pixel-space watermarking of AI-generated images incurs substantial computational overhead (operating on 512×512×3 representations) and provides no security for open-source deployments where users can trivially bypass watermarking. Existing in-model watermarking methods like Stable Signature are architecture-specific and do not generalize across both continuous latent diffusion models and discrete autoregressive models. The paper asks whether watermarking can be performed directly in the compressed latent space of generative models and whether such latent watermarkers can be distilled into model weights to enable secure, zero-overhead in-model watermarking.

## Input / Output

The post-hoc latent embedder takes a latent representation (e.g., 8×8×128 continuous latents for DCAE or 16×16×256 token embeddings for RAR-XL) and a 64-bit binary message, producing a watermarked latent via z_w = z + ε·W_θ(z, m); for autoregressive models, this can be applied before quantization (yielding a new discrete token sequence) or after quantization (modifying token embeddings). The extractor takes augmented watermarked images (after JPEG, crop, rotation, brightness, etc.) and predicts the 64-bit message. After distillation, the generative model takes only a class label or text prompt and directly outputs watermarked images with no additional inference step, while the latent decoder takes non-watermarked latents and produces watermarked images.

## Method

DISTSEAL is a two-stage framework. Stage 1 trains a post-hoc embedder-extractor pair in latent space by adapting VideoSeal's UNet embedder (removing all downsampling/upsampling layers to match the lower spatial resolution of latents) and training with a combination of binary cross-entropy extraction loss and an adversarial discriminator loss in pixel space, without reconstruction or perceptual losses. Stage 2 optionally distills the trained watermarker into either the generative model (by fine-tuning on watermarked latents for 10k–100k steps) or the latent decoder (by training it to reconstruct watermarked images from non-watermarked latents using a reconstruction loss plus an extractor-guided loss). The key insight is that latent watermarkers introduce more structured, lower-frequency perturbations that are easier for the decoder to learn than the high-frequency pixel-space perturbations, making latent distillation significantly more effective—distilled latent watermarkers achieve 96.77% average bit accuracy versus only 51.38% for distilled pixel watermarkers using reconstruction loss alone. The framework achieves up to 20× inference speedup over pixel-space watermarking by operating on compressed representations.

## Results

On DCAE (512×512), post-hoc latent watermarking achieves 95.18% average bit accuracy across transformations (vs. 97.78% for pixel watermarking) with a 20× inference speedup (3 ms vs. 63 ms per image on CPU). After distilling into the latent decoder with extractor loss (λ_w=0.1), the in-model watermarker reaches 96.77% average bit accuracy—surpassing the teacher—while maintaining 11.48 FID. On RAR-XL (256×256), post-hoc latent watermarking after quantization achieves 93.96% average accuracy, and distillation into the autoregressive model yields 89.13% with 3.32 FID (better than the teacher's 3.56 FID). Critically, distilling latent watermarkers into the decoder substantially outperforms distilling pixel watermarkers (96.77% vs. 92.95% avg accuracy, with FID of 11.48 vs. 17.08).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the pixel-space VideoSeal baseline, latent watermarking trades a modest 2.6-point drop in average bit accuracy on DCAE (95.18% vs. 97.78%) for a 20× speedup and negligible FID increase (+0.76 vs. +0.18). Against Stable Signature (Fernandez et al., 2023), DISTSEAL's decoder distillation achieves 91.34% combined-attack accuracy versus 77.65% for Stable Signature at λ_w=0.5, while maintaining comparable FID (11.48 vs. 12.09). Against RoSteALS (coverless), DISTSEAL improves average accuracy from 94.59% to 96.77% and combined accuracy from 73.24% to 91.34%. Against Tree-Ring (zero-bit), DISTSEAL achieves 96.77% average bit accuracy versus 76% TPR@FPR=0.01, with a particularly large gap on combined attacks (91.34% vs. 27%). The key finding is that distilling latent watermarkers into the decoder dramatically outperforms distilling pixel watermarkers: 96.77% avg accuracy at FID 11.48 versus 92.95% at FID 17.08 for pixel distillation, confirming that latent-space perturbations are more structured and easier for the decoder to learn.

## Relation to Prior Work

DISTSEAL adapts the VideoSeal post-hoc watermarking architecture (UNet embedder + ConvNeXt-tiny extractor) from pixel space to the latent space of generative models, and departs from Stable Signature's in-model distillation by retaining the teacher embedder during distillation rather than relying solely on the extractor as a guidance signal. The key conceptual move is unifying latent watermarking across both continuous (diffusion) and discrete (autoregressive) latent spaces, and showing that the resulting latent watermarkers can be distilled into either the generative model or the latent decoder. This extends the in-model watermarking line of work (Stable Signature, WouAF, AquaLoRA) from diffusion-only to a general framework covering both model families.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for open-source model deployment: in-model watermarking via distillation eliminates the trivial bypass of removing a single line of code, while adding zero inference overhead. The finding that latent watermarks are easier to distill than pixel-space ones (despite being slightly less robust as post-hoc methods) provides a clear design principle for practitioners. Limitations include susceptibility to watermark forgetting under LoRA fine-tuning (for generative-model distillation), bounded performance by the teacher watermarker, and insufficient robustness against strong geometric attacks. The authors identify video generation as a natural next step where the speedup advantage would be most pronounced.
