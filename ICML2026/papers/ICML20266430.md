## Problem

Medical AI models are trained and evaluated primarily on human opinions and textual syntheses—clinical guidelines, FDA product labels, and clinician consensus—rather than actual longitudinal treatment outcome data. This means models lack genuine causal understanding of treatment effects, and current benchmarks are structurally unable to detect or reward such understanding. The paper argues this methodology contradicts both the evidence-based medicine tradition and the ambition for AI to exceed any human expert's performance.

## Input / Output

The paper advocates shifting from the current paradigm—where models consume patient case descriptions and free-form clinical questions to produce treatment recommendations validated against guideline text or clinician opinion—to one where models consume longitudinal patient records (EHR event sequences, insurance claims) and produce treatment effect estimates or recommendations validated against randomized trial outcomes. Evaluation should use target trial emulations derived from RCTs rather than multiple-choice questions sourced from medical exams or guideline passages, with held-out observational data generating causal estimates when experimental data are unavailable.

## Method

This position paper builds its case through empirical evidence rather than proposing a new architecture. The authors survey training corpora of 19+ medical foundation models (Table 1), showing that only small EHR-specific models (MOTOR-T, EHRMamba, Manba-CLMBR) train on real treatment outcomes, while all large LLMs rely on publications, guidelines, and web text. They demonstrate concrete failures: Gemini passes both automated RxQA verification checks with an incorrect answer about ivacaftor for CFTR-related pancreatitis, and OpenEvidence's explicitly guideline-grounded response misses a prescribing cascade in a real cardiac case. Analysis of HealthBench's meta-evaluation reveals that 'clinician-level agreement' is unmoored from ground truth when 91% of labels come from exactly 2 clinicians per example, and a systematic review of 4,609 clinical LLM evaluations finds only 16 reusable benchmarks involving real treatment outcomes. The paper recommends training on longitudinal EHR data (e.g., the CRITICAL dataset of ~400,000 critical care patients) and evaluating via target trial emulations from RCTs, with intermediate predictive tasks still pursued but their contribution to outcomes quantified.

## Results

Across 19 modern medical AI models surveyed in Table 1, only 4 small EHR foundation models (MOTOR-T, EHRMamba, Manba-CLMBR, Curiosity-L) train on real treatment outcomes; all large LLMs (Med-Gemini, Med-PaLM2, AMIE, etc.) rely on publications, guidelines, and web text. In a systematic review of 4,609 clinical LLM evaluations, only 180 (~3.9%) are potentially-reusable benchmarks focused on treatment, and 88.9% (702/789) of treatment-related evaluations use guidelines or clinician opinion rather than real patient outcomes as ground truth. The HealthBench meta-evaluation reveals that 91.2% of labels come from examples scored by exactly 2 clinicians, and the authors construct a scenario where GPT 4.1 could be 100% accurate while clinicians are only 78.5% accurate, yet both achieve 'clinician-level agreement.' In JAMA Clinical Challenge, only 18.1% of verifiable questions actually involve assessment or modification of treatment, with the majority asking for diagnosis or further workup.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it audits existing training pipelines and evaluation practices. The central comparison is across 19 models in Table 1, revealing a stark divide: no large LLM (≥7B) trains on real treatment outcomes, while all four small EHR sequence models do. The paper also demonstrates that RxQA's automated verification (using FDA product labels) can be bypassed: an incorrect answer (D: 'Ineffective') for an ivacaftor-pancreatitis question passes both of Palepu et al.'s LLM-based correctness checks, while the correct answer (C: 'Effective, with low certainty') is rejected. In the prescribing-cascade example (Ha et al., 2021), OpenEvidence's 'guideline-directed' response increases furosemide dosage rather than recognizing the cascade, and ChatGPT for Clinicians/Healthcare similarly fail to recommend dose reduction. The HealthBench Professional analysis shows ~86% of clinician-authored grading criteria are directly grounded in guideline passages, far exceeding the 55–60% real-world guideline concordance observed in practice, suggesting benchmark input diverges from actual clinical behavior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper repurposes the evidence-based medicine framework (Guyatt et al., 1992; Sackett et al., 1996) for the AI context, arguing that medical LLMs should be trained and evaluated on real treatment outcome data (observational databases, RCTs) rather than textual syntheses like guidelines and drug labels. It is conceptually adjacent to Joshi et al. (2025), which also calls for outcomes-focused medical AI but from a deployment-validation perspective, and to the causal medical AI literature (Feuerriegel et al., 2024; Prosperi et al., 2020), but departs from both by targeting the training and benchmarking methodology of language models specifically. The paper does not propose a new architecture or algorithm; its contribution is a diagnostic of the field's data and evaluation choices, supported by concrete counterexamples (the ivacaftor/RxQA bypass, the prescribing-cascade/OpenEvidence failure, the HealthBench agreement analysis).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a load-bearing assumption in medical AI benchmarking—that FDA labels, clinical guidelines, and clinician consensus are adequate ground truth for treatment reasoning—and provides concrete, reproducible demonstrations that this assumption fails in practice. If the field accepts the recommendation to shift training toward longitudinal EHR data and evaluation toward target-trial-emulation benchmarks, it would fundamentally restructure how medical LLMs are developed and validated, with downstream implications for regulatory acceptance and clinical deployment. Key open questions include the practical feasibility of large-scale EHR training pipelines (the authors concede most databases are not yet ready), the privacy and access barriers, and whether the proposed evaluation shift can be implemented without the mature annotation infrastructure that text-based benchmarks currently enjoy.
