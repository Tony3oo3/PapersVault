## Problem

Existing active causal learning methods focus on binary treatments and global effect estimation, leaving continuous individualized dosing policy optimization underexplored. The key gap is that estimation-oriented sampling criteria waste budget on suboptimal dose regions, whereas the downstream objective is to minimize decision regret at the optimal dose. This paper bridges this gap by showing that policy regret is governed by gradient posterior variance at the estimated optimum, not by global predictive uncertainty.

## Input / Output

The method takes a finite pool of unlabeled individuals (each with a d-dimensional covariate vector), a continuous dose space T=[0,1], and a GP posterior over the dose-response function. At each round it outputs a batch of B intervention queries (individual, dose) pairs under a one-shot constraint—once an individual is queried, it is permanently removed from the pool. Feedback is observed only after the full batch executes (batched setting), and the final output is an individualized dosing policy π̂: X→T mapping covariates to optimal doses.

## Method

GVALID exploits a theoretical bound (Theorem 4.1) showing that Bayes policy regret is upper-bounded by the expected posterior variance of the dose-response gradient evaluated at the current policy's recommended doses. To make this tractable, it approximates the gradient via symmetric finite-difference proxies at each target dose, yielding a trace-based uncertainty objective over a finite set of representative contexts. Batch selection then greedily maximizes the one-step trace reduction of this gradient covariance, with a closed-form Schur-complement score (Theorem 5.1) enabling efficient rank-one covariance updates after each selection without recomputing the full posterior. This policy-oriented criterion concentrates experimental budget on reducing stationarity uncertainty near the estimated optimum rather than indiscriminately improving global dose-response fit.

## Results

The primary metric is policy suboptimality R (lower is better), evaluated at budgets N=150, 250, and 350 across four synthetic datasets (HNL, CSC, SWV, STS) and the semi-synthetic News benchmark. At N=350, GVALID achieves the lowest suboptimality on HNL (0.12±0.04 vs. 0.17 for ABC3), CSC (0.40±0.26 vs. 0.51 for PG), SWV (0.29±0.10 vs. 0.34 for PLAS), and STS (0.17±0.36, tied with ABC3 at 0.17±0.40). On the high-dimensional News dataset, GVALID also leads with the fastest convergence in both policy suboptimality and absolute dosage error. The advantage is most pronounced at intermediate budgets (N=250), where GVALID reduces HNL suboptimality to 0.18 versus 0.24–0.25 for the next-best baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GVALID outperforms all nine baselines on HNL at every budget, with the gap widening as budget increases (0.12 vs. 0.17 for ABC3 at N=350, a ~30% relative improvement). On CSC, GVALID beats the strongest baselines (PG at 0.51, ABC3 at 0.55) by a clear margin, while bandit methods (TS, GP-UCB, EI) and Soft Top-K degrade substantially due to over-smoothing or outlier sensitivity. On SWV, where the unimodality assumption is violated, PLAS (0.34) and CATS (0.38) are the closest competitors, but GVALID still leads at 0.29 by leveraging GP nonparametric flexibility to escape local optima. On STS (plateau-shaped responses), GVALID ties ABC3 (both 0.17) but with lower variance, while PG degrades severely (10.6). On the 500-dimensional News dataset, GVALID achieves the fastest convergence in both policy suboptimality and absolute dosage error, outperforming all bandit and active-learning baselines that struggle with high-dimensional sparse covariates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of active causal inference, batched contextual bandits, and best-arm identification by reframing the sampling criterion from estimation error reduction to direct policy value maximization. While prior active causal learning (Puha et al., Cha & Lee, Razzak et al.) minimizes posterior variance of treatment effects, and batched bandits (Kalkanli & Ozgur, Li & Scarlett) operate under passive context arrivals, GVALID combines active context selection with a gradient-variance objective derived from a regret bound (Theorem 4.1) that is specific to continuous dosing. The Schur-complement-based greedy batch selection repurposes standard GP posterior update machinery, but applies it to finite-difference gradient proxies rather than function values, a non-trivial adaptation that enables closed-form marginal gain scores.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The conceptual shift from estimation-oriented to policy-oriented sampling is practically significant for budget-constrained clinical trials, where allocating experiments to suboptimal dose regions wastes scarce resources. The theoretical bound (regret ≤ C_g × expected gradient variance) provides a clean, actionable acquisition criterion that avoids the cumulative errors of intermediate estimation steps. Key limitations include reliance on smoothness and unimodality assumptions (Assumption 4.1), GP scalability to very high-dimensional covariates, and the one-shot constraint that precludes repeated experimentation on the same individual. Open questions include extending the framework to multi-objective dosing (balancing efficacy and toxicity), replacing GP surrogates with scalable deep Bayesian models, and characterizing the fundamental sample complexity of continuous-dose BAI.
