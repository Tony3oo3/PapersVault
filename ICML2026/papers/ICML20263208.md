## Problem

Standard Bayesian Optimization treats each experiment as returning only a scalar reward, ignoring the rich auxiliary information (sensor time series, learning curves) that real-world trials naturally produce. While composite BO assumes a restrictive f(x)=g(h(x)) structure and single-task setting, no prior method learns to generalizably exploit high-dimensional auxiliary information h(x) across a history of related tasks to accelerate optimization of new tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263208/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a few-shot context of N_C evaluated designs, each providing the design vector x, scalar reward f(x), and potentially high-dimensional auxiliary observations h(x) (e.g., 16×16 tactile maps over time in gripper design, or 50×5 per-epoch learning curves in HPO), plus a target set of N_T unobserved designs specified only by x. For each target design, it outputs a univariate normal N(μ̂, σ̂) over the reward f(x), which feeds into a Probability of Improvement acquisition function to select the next design. Notably, h(x) is consumed only as conditioning in the context encoder and is never predicted as a target, keeping the learning objective focused on reward prediction.

## Method

The method is a transformer-based neural process trained via meta-learning on a history of design tasks. Each context point is encoded by a dedicated encoder E_θ^c that processes h(x) through a sequence transformer (with a [CLS] token and a special (x, f(x)) input token that modulates attention over the sequence), then adds the resulting sequence embedding to an embedding of (x, f(x)); target points use a simpler MLP encoder E_θ^t(x). The transformer uses an asymmetric attention structure where context tokens attend to each other and target tokens attend only to context, with no positional encodings. Training samples a task, then disjoint context and target subsets, and minimizes the negative log-likelihood of the predicted reward distributions over target points. At test time the model is frozen and deployed as a surrogate in a standard discrete BayesOpt loop, avoiding iterative re-training and enabling efficient acquisition over large design sets.

## Results

On the Gripper Design Benchmark (997 tasks, 4.28M evaluations), after 30 optimization trials, the proposed method reaches ~90% of maximum achievable reward on average, solves 35.7% of test tasks to maximum reward, and achieves regret ≤ 0.5 on 68.5% of tasks. On LCBench (35 HP tuning tasks), the method solves 89.7% of tasks (regret ≤ 0.01) after 30 trials. For few-shot prediction on the gripper task with only 5 context observations, the method achieves MSE of 170.9 versus 201.6 for the reward-only ablation (Ours w/o h), demonstrating the value of auxiliary tactile information.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all baselines on both domains across both prediction and optimization metrics. Against DGP (Wistuba & Grabocka, 2021), the state-of-the-art deep kernel transfer BO method that uses only scalar reward f(x), the proposed method achieves significantly lower prediction MSE and higher optimization reward on both the gripper and LCBench benchmarks, confirming that auxiliary information h(x) provides measurable gains beyond what transfer learning on f(x) alone can achieve. Against STGP (single-task GP via BoTorch), which fits a GP online to test-time data without any task history, the proposed method shows consistent improvement, validating the benefit of multi-task pre-training. The critical ablation 'Ours w/o h' (same architecture but without auxiliary information) is the closest competitor: on the gripper task, the full method solves 68.5% of tasks to regret ≤ 0.5 versus 59.3% for the ablation, and on LCBench achieves 89.7% versus 82.1% solved. The GP-H baseline, which uses a multi-output GP to jointly model h(x) and f(x), performs notably worse than the proposed neural approach, suggesting that learning effective representations of high-dimensional auxiliary information is highly nontrivial and benefits from the flexible transformer-based architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes two previously separate lines of Bayesian optimization research—composite BO (which models f(x)=g(h(x)) in a single-task setting, e.g., Astudillo & Frazier 2019) and transfer/multi-task BO (which leverages task history but ignores auxiliary signals, e.g., Wistuba & Grabocka 2021, Wang et al. 2024)—into a unified setting that requires both. The method is architecturally grounded in Transformer Neural Processes (Nguyen & Grover, 2022), adopting their context-attends-to-context, target-attends-to-context attention structure and the broader Neural Process training paradigm of sampling context/target splits from a task distribution. The key departure from prior work is that h(x) is not assumed to arise from a composite function structure and is instead treated as a general, potentially high-dimensional observation whose representation must be learned end-to-end across tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper introduces a practically motivated problem formulation (auxiliary sensor/curve data + task history) that mirrors real experimental workflows in robotics, drug design, and HPO, and contributes a 4.28M-evaluation gripper benchmark that is substantially larger than existing multi-task BO benchmarks. A notable open question is how the method handles out-of-distribution test tasks, which the authors acknowledge but leave for future work. The finding that a GP-based approach to incorporating h(x) (GP-H) underperforms the neural process method suggests that learning effective representations of high-dimensional auxiliary signals for optimization is nontrivial and may not be well-served by kernel-based surrogates. Long-horizon acquisition strategies that exploit the richer per-trial information from h(x) remain unexplored.
