## Problem

Reinforcement learning agents are increasingly deployed in high-stakes, real-world systems, yet no mathematical framework exists for quantifying how rational an agent behaves in deployment relative to its training. While regret and value-alignment metrics capture cumulative suboptimality in reward, they do not measure whether an agent actually takes the actions that maximise its learned value function at each step. This paper fills that gap by introducing a suite of rationality measures and proving the first theoretical bounds on the rational risk gap for RL agents.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes an episodic MDP specification (state space, finite action space, horizon H, reward functions, transition kernels, and initial state distributions for both training and deployment environments) together with T training trajectories and the learned policy's Q-value estimates. It produces two scalar outputs: the rational risk gap |R(π) − R̂(π)|, which measures deployment rationality given observable training behaviour, and a practical rational value metric R̂_ALG(π) that replaces the intractable optimal Q* with the algorithm's approximate Q̂^T, adding a 2Hε approximation term to the bound. The measure is 'local and immediate'—each action is judged rational if it is individually optimal given all information at that time step, rather than requiring global or long-term optimality.

## Method

The paper defines a perfectly rational action as one that maximises the true (possibly unknown) action-value function in the steepest direction, then defines rational value loss as the gap between the rational action's value and the taken action's value. Cumulating this loss over a trajectory yields the expected rational value risk (in deployment) and its empirical counterpart (in training); their difference, the rational risk gap, is decomposed via a triangle-inequality argument into an extrinsic component (environment shift between training and deployment) and an intrinsic component (algorithm generalisability in a dynamic, online setting). The extrinsic gap is upper-bounded by L_s·H·W₁(p₀†,p₀) + H²L_s(L_p+1)·W₁(p†,p) using Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality and a backward-induction Bellman recursion, while the intrinsic gap is bounded by L_Π·H·√(2 log|A|) + 2ΣR̂_h(Q_Π) + 3H²√(log(4H/δ)/2T) via policy-drift analysis (Pinsker's inequality on KL-constrained policy updates) and a symmetrisation + McDiarmid concentration argument on the empirical Rademacher complexity. Experiments with DQN on Taxi-v3 and Cliff Walking validate three derived hypotheses: regularisers (layer norm, ℓ₂, weight norm) reduce the intrinsic gap, domain randomisation reduces the extrinsic gap, and larger environment shifts monotonically increase the rational risk gap.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative result is the rational risk gap (lower is better) measured on Taxi-v3 and Cliff Walking. With L2 regularisation at strength 10⁻³, the rational risk gap drops from 35.34±22.91 (vanilla DQN) to 15.07±5.71 on Taxi-v3, and from 206.67±26.50 to 150.13±18.08 on Cliff Walking. Domain randomisation further reduces the rational risk gap in both environments, with the largest improvement observed in Cliff Walking. Rational risk gap increases monotonically with the environment shift level (0%→70% action randomisation), confirming that larger training-to-deployment distribution shifts degrade rationality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing RL algorithms; instead, it benchmarks vanilla DQN against regularised and augmented variants to verify theoretical predictions. Among regularisers, ℓ₂ at 10⁻³ consistently yields the lowest rational risk gap in both environments (15.07 on Taxi-v3, 150.13 on Cliff Walking), outperforming vanilla DQN by ~57% and ~27% respectively. Layer normalisation provides the strongest control in Taxi-v3, while weight normalisation is more effective in Cliff Walking compared to vanilla DQN. Domain randomisation reduces the rational risk gap relative to the DQN baseline in both environments, with the most pronounced benefit in Cliff Walking. Across environment shift levels (0%–70% action randomisation), the rational risk gap increases monotonically, confirming the theoretical prediction that larger Wasserstein distances between training and deployment transition kernels degrade rationality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the first mathematical framework for measuring the rationality of RL agents, building conceptually on bounded rationality from economics (Simon, Sen) and prior formalizations by Valiant (1995), Abel (2019), and Sunehag & Hutter (2015), which the authors note lack a unified theoretical treatment. Technically, it repurposes standard tools—1-Wasserstein distance for environment shifts (connecting to sim-to-real transfer literature) and empirical Rademacher complexity for hypothesis complexity (from PAC learning and deep RL generalization theory, e.g., Liu et al. 2022a, Wang et al. 2019)—into a novel decomposition of the 'rational risk gap' into extrinsic and intrinsic components. The 'local and immediate' action-level definition of rationality departs from trajectory-level regret analyses that dominate the RL literature, aligning instead with the economic tradition of evaluating individual decisions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled lens for understanding why standard techniques (regularization, domain randomization) improve RL agent behavior, and quantifies the cost of environment shifts in deployment—directly relevant to the sim-to-real gap and to deploying RL in high-stakes domains like finance and autonomous driving. However, the experimental validation is limited to two toy environments (Taxi-v3, Cliff Walking) with a single algorithm (DQN), leaving open whether the theoretical predictions hold for modern deep RL at scale. The authors outline promising but unexplored directions: multi-agent system stability analysis, socioeconomic simulation with imperfectly rational agents, and mechanism design under bounded rationality, which could significantly broaden the framework's impact.
