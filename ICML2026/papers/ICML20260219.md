## Problem

The classifier two-sample test (C2ST) is a widely used tool for comparing two distributions, but its reliability hinges on access to a near-Bayes-optimal classifier—a requirement rarely met in practice, especially in settings like Neural Posterior Estimation where classifiers are trained on limited data. This creates an ambiguity: a 'pass' could mean the distributions truly match, or simply that the classifier is too weak to detect a difference. The paper asks whether a weak, biased, or overfit classifier can still yield a valid and powerful two-sample test.

## Input / Output

The method takes i.i.d. samples from a true distribution p and an approximate distribution q (e.g., true vs. learned posterior in NPE), along with a pre-trained binary classifier that assigns scores to each sample. The classifier is trained in a standard supervised fashion (label 1 for p, label 0 for q) but need not be well-calibrated or accurate. The output is a hypothesis test decision at a chosen significance level α: the uniform test produces per-point conformal p-values aggregated via a Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic, while the multiple test produces a single normalized U-statistic. The uniform test requires m fresh calibration draws from p per test point (cheap in NPE since p is the simulator), whereas the multiple test reuses one shared calibration set, exactly matching C2ST's sampling budget.

## Method

The core mechanism replaces C2ST's hard thresholding at 0.5 with conformal p-value calibration: for each test point from q, its classifier score is ranked against m calibration scores from p, yielding a p-value that measures how extreme the test point is relative to the reference distribution. Two variants are provided: (i) the uniform test, which draws independent calibration sets per test point and aggregates the resulting p-values via a one-sample KS test, giving exact finite-sample Type-I error control for any deterministic scoring function; and (ii) the multiple test, which uses a single shared calibration set and applies the U-statistic normalization from Hu & Lei (2024), matching C2ST's computational budget while retaining robustness. The key theoretical insight is that power is governed by the classifier's ranking ability (AUC) rather than its classification accuracy, and the expected p-value under the alternative degrades only as O(ε^{2/3}) in the L2 estimation error of the density ratio, meaning even a weak classifier that preserves approximate ordering yields a powerful test. The classifier itself is a standard neural network trained with binary cross-entropy, and no retraining or calibration of the classifier is needed—only its raw scores are consumed by the conformal procedure.

## Results

On the gravitational-lensing benchmark (16×16 source images, 2000 samples per distribution), the Conformal Uniform test (m=50) achieves 66% power at bias level γ=0.60, compared to 28% for C2ST, 6% for SBC, and 6% for TARP. At γ=0.70, Conformal Multiple reaches 92% and Conformal Uniform(50) reaches 98%, versus 76% for C2ST. Under classifier degradation (β=0.80, γ=1.0), Conformal Multiple retains 74% power while C2ST drops to 40% and AutoML to 44%. DC is shown to be an invalid test, inflating Type-I error to 10% at γ=0 (nominal 5%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against C2ST, both conformal variants win decisively on both sensitivity and robustness: at γ=0.60, CU(50) achieves 66% power versus C2ST's 28%, and under classifier degradation at β=0.80, CM retains 74% power while C2ST collapses to 40%. Against SBC and TARP, the conformal methods are dramatically superior — SBC and TARP remain near the 5% noise floor across nearly all perturbation levels (2–10% power even at γ=1.0), while conformal tests reach 98–100% by γ=0.70. Against DC, the conformal methods are not only more powerful but also valid: DC inflates Type-I error to 10% at γ=0, making it an unreliable test, and its power peaks at only 46–52% in the mid-range (γ=0.60–0.70) where conformal methods already reach 50–98%. Against AutoML, conformal methods pull ahead under classifier degradation: at β=0.80, CM achieves 74% versus AutoML's 44%, and at β=0.90, CM still reaches 30% while AutoML drops to 10%. Against MMDFuse (kernel-based), the gap is the widest: at γ=0.70, CM achieves 92% power while MMDFuse reaches only 18%, and MMDFuse never exceeds 28% across the entire bias range.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the conformal two-sample test framework of Hu & Lei (2024), which uses oracle density ratios to construct valid tests for conditional distributions, and on the classical C2ST of Lopez-Paz & Oquab (2016). The key departure is that Hu & Lei assume access to a strong classifier to model density ratios, while this paper provides robustness theory showing that conformal calibration preserves validity and non-trivial power even when the plug-in score comes from a weak, biased, or overfit classifier. In the NPE validation context, it positions itself against SBC, TARP, Discriminative Calibration, and AutoML, showing that conformal post-processing of classifier ranks outperforms these baselines in both power and robustness to classifier degradation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical message—that a near-Bayes-optimal classifier is not required for reliable two-sample testing, only a weak but informative ranking—removes a major practical bottleneck in NPE validation and generative model evaluation, where perfectly trained classifiers are rarely available. The O(ε^{2/3}) degradation rate provides a clean theoretical guarantee that power degrades gently with classifier error, which is reassuring for practitioners. Key limitations include the test being global (no localization of where q deviates from p), the M-closed scope (does not address model misspecification), and the fact that the budget-matched Multiple test is only asymptotically valid. Open questions include extending the framework to localize discrepancies, handling the M-open setting, and tightening the conservative bounding arguments.
