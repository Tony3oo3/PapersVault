## Problem

Byte-level tokenization enables language models to handle arbitrary Unicode input, but models can generate structurally invalid UTF-8 sequences, particularly for rare or unseen characters. Prior work has not systematically studied when byte-level models acquire reliable UTF-8 generation, and standard perplexity metrics do not capture this capability: the paper shows UTF-8 validity convergence lags perplexity convergence by roughly a factor of two, meaning a model that appears well-trained by perplexity may still emit undecodable byte streams.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263348/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At Level 0, the model receives a 2-byte UTF-8 prefix of an OOV character and must generate the remaining continuation bytes; at Level 1, it receives a full sentence context (synthesized by Gemini 3 Pro) plus a short byte prefix of the target character and must emit the exact remaining suffix. Outputs are scored by a DFA-based partial-credit validity metric (fractional progress through multi-byte sequences), a binary Term Match indicator (exact byte completion returning to a UTF-8 boundary), and a teacher-forced likelihood difference Δ_LL that diagnoses whether failures stem from missing knowledge or decoding/calibration. Evaluation spans 420 training checkpoints with 256 samples per language per level.

## Method

The paper trains a 355M-parameter GPT-2-style decoder (RMSNorm, RoPE, GQA, GeGLU) with an 8,000-token BPE vocabulary and byte-fallback on 80B tokens from a balanced multilingual corpus (10% English, 30% each Japanese/Korean/Chinese) assembled via a Weighted Dynamic sampling algorithm that preserves sentence boundaries while converging to target language proportions. The core contribution is a three-axis evaluation framework—structural validity (G1, via a UTF-8 DFA with partial-credit scoring), semantic correctness (G2, via Term Match), and probabilistic preference (G3, via Δ_LL likelihood comparison)—instantiated at two levels: context-free byte completion (Level 0) and context-guided byte retrieval (Level 1). Key findings include that byte-length exposure dominates over character frequency as an axis of difficulty (a control experiment with unseen 3-byte vs. 4-byte characters shows a 48.2 pp gap at prefix=1), that structural validity exceeds semantic correctness (96.21% validity vs. 60.30% Term Match), and that the structural-semantic gap persists across 10 open-weight models from 1B to 9B parameters.

## Results

The 355M-parameter baseline achieves 96.21% partial-credit validity on Common-tier characters and 86.97% on Unseen 4-byte characters at the final checkpoint (80B tokens), but strict validity drops to 50.47% (Common) and 33.33% (Unseen). UTF-8 validity convergence lags perplexity by roughly a factor of two: perplexity stabilizes after ~2.1B tokens while validity requires ~4.2B tokens. In the context-guided Level 1 setting, Term Match Rate reaches only 60.30%, revealing a stark structural-semantic gap. Cross-model evaluation across 10 open-weight models (1B–9B) confirms the V_p − V_s gap persists universally, ranging from 9.9 pp (Qwen-3.5 9B) to 65.4 pp (OLMo-2 7B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is a cross-model evaluation of 10 open-weight models (Gemma-3, Llama-3.2, Llama-2, Mistral, OLMo-2, Qwen-3.5) against the authors' 355M baseline using an identical Level 0/1 protocol. The headline finding is that the structural-semantic gap (V_p − V_s) is universal: it ranges from 9.9 pp for Qwen-3.5 9B to 65.4 pp for OLMo-2 7B, and does not close with scale within a family (OLMo-2 shows 59.5→65.4 pp from 1B to 7B). On Level 1 term match, the baseline (47.8%) dramatically outperforms all GPT-2-style BPE models (Qwen-3.5 9B: 0.5%, Gemma-3 4B: 0.0%) and even Llama-3.2 (0.8–1.0%), while SentencePiece byte-fallback models like Llama-2 (33.1%) and Mistral (23.3%) are intermediate. The Gemma-3 1B model is a notable outlier with only 33.6% partial-credit validity and 0.9% strict validity, suggesting its tokenizer or training regime severely limits byte-level generation. Overall, tokenizer design (byte-fallback frequency during training) predicts semantic byte completion more strongly than model size.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of byte-level tokenization research (ByT5, Byte Latent Transformer) and the growing literature on tokenization vulnerabilities in LLMs (Geh et al. 2025, Li et al. 2024, Land & Bartolo 2024). While prior work addresses invalid byte generation at decode time via constrained decoding (Willard & Louf 2023, Koo et al. 2024, Cognetta & Okazaki 2025), this paper is the first to systematically measure *when* during training byte-level models acquire UTF-8 competence, explicitly separating structural validity from semantic correctness. It departs from the scaling-laws tradition (Kaplan et al. 2020, Hoffmann et al. 2022) by showing that perplexity convergence does not predict UTF-8 validity convergence, and from the multilingual tokenization literature (Rust et al. 2021, Singh et al. 2024) by identifying byte-length exposure as a distinct difficulty axis beyond character frequency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that UTF-8 validity convergence lags perplexity by a factor of two provides a concrete, actionable threshold for practitioners deploying byte-level models: a model that appears well-trained by standard metrics may still emit undecodable byte streams. The persistent structural-semantic gap across 10 open-weight models (valid UTF-8 but wrong character) suggests that constrained decoding alone is insufficient and that semantic byte retrieval requires additional training or architectural changes. Open questions include whether the convergence lag is specific to byte-level generation or a general rare-token phenomenon, whether explicit byte-position encodings or hierarchical representations can close the gap, and whether beam search or temperature sampling can recover the Δ_LL > 0 semantic failures without retraining.
