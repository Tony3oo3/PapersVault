## Problem

Deploying LLMs as domain-specific agents in agentic workflows requires a gating mechanism to reject or reroute out-of-scope requests, yet existing OOD detection methods either treat the model as a black box, require fine-tuning or labeled OOD data, or offer limited interpretability. This paper asks whether the internal layer-to-layer dynamics of an LLM, when projected into a sparse interpretable feature space, carry a domain-specific signature that can be exploited for lightweight, ID-only scope gating.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a text request (up to 512 tokens) and extracts residual-stream activations from a contiguous block of deeper transformer layers (e.g., layers 16–24 for Gemma-2-2B). These activations are encoded by pretrained 16k-dimensional SAEs, mean-pooled over the token axis, filtered by global activation density, and binarized via Top-k (k=10) selection to yield one binary sparse distributed representation (SDR) per layer. The output is a scalar anomaly score S(x) computed as the mean per-layer transition anomaly across depth, which can be thresholded to produce an in-scope or out-of-scope decision.

## Method

The core pipeline converts each layer's residual-stream activation into a sparse binary SDR via a pretrained SAE encoder, token-axis pooling, density-based feature masking, and Top-k binarization, producing a depthwise sequence of k-hot binary vectors. A lightweight sequential scorer—by default a first-order Markov transition model trained on in-domain data only—learns adjacent-layer co-activation statistics and scores test inputs by the negative mean log-likelihood of observed active feature-pair transitions across consecutive layers. The key insight is that in-domain inputs exhibit stable, low-entropy depthwise transition regularities in SAE feature space, while out-of-domain inputs deviate from this learned transition structure. Ablations confirm that the SAE decomposition is essential (raw activations yield AUROC 0.753 vs. 0.919), that first-order Markov transitions capture most of the discriminative signal (matching or exceeding HTM and RNN backends), and that transition modeling adds signal beyond static per-layer feature geometry. The approach requires no fine-tuning of the base LLM, no labeled OOD data, and provides interpretable explanations by decoding the SAE feature indices responsible for anomalous transitions.

## Results

In the far-OOD setting (ID: 20 Newsgroups), the SAE+Markov method achieves AUROC 0.92–0.99 but is consistently outperformed by the likelihood-ratio (LR) baseline, which reaches AUROC ≥0.994 across all OOD sources (SST-2, MNLI, RTE, IMDB). In the near-OOD setting, the method is competitive or superior: on AGNews (4 one-vs-all splits), Gemma2-2B SAE+Markov achieves AUROC 0.88–0.98 versus 0.73–0.82 for the LR baseline, and on SNIPS it reaches AUROC 0.970 (2B) vs. 0.975 (LR). Performance degrades sharply on CLINC150 (AUROC 0.739 for 2B vs. 0.866 for LR), which the authors attribute to representational aliasing when the intent taxonomy is finer-grained than SAE feature resolution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the likelihood-ratio (LR) criterion between a pretrained LLM and an in-scope fine-tuned variant (Zhang et al., 2025), which requires fine-tuning the base model. In the far-OOD regime, the LR baseline dominates decisively: it achieves AUROC ≥0.994 and FPR95 ≤0.017 across all four OOD sources, while the SAE+Markov method lags by 1–8 AUROC points (e.g., 0.917 vs. 0.998 on IMDB-2B). In the near-OOD regime the picture is more nuanced: on AGNews the SAE+Markov method clearly wins, reaching AUROC 0.90–0.98 (2B) versus 0.73–0.82 for the LR baseline, with FPR95 as low as 0.03 on one split. On SNIPS and ROSTD the two methods are roughly competitive (within ~1–2 AUROC points), with the LR baseline holding a slight edge on SNIPS-9B (0.980 vs. 0.900). On CLINC150 the SAE method loses substantially (AUROC 0.739 vs. 0.866 for 2B), which the authors attribute to SAE feature resolution being too coarse for 150 fine-grained intents. The paper's stated advantage over the LR baseline is not raw detection accuracy but interpretability (decoded SAE feature transitions explain each decision) and the absence of any base-model fine-tuning requirement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the SAE interpretability line (Cunningham et al., 2023) by repurposing pretrained sparse autoencoder features not for mechanistic analysis but as a discrete state space for sequential anomaly scoring. It departs from prior OOD detection work (Hendrycks & Gimpel, 2018; Podolskiy et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2022) that typically operates on raw activations, output confidence, or fitted density models, by instead modeling depthwise transitions between Top-k binarized SAE features. The closest comparator is the likelihood-ratio criterion of Zhang et al. (2025), which also requires no OOD labels but relies on fine-tuning a second model; this paper replaces that with a lightweight Markov/HTM/RNN scorer trained on ID-only internal dynamics, trading some accuracy for interpretability and deployment simplicity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides an interpretable, fine-tuning-free scope-gating mechanism for agentic LLM deployments, where decoded SAE feature transitions offer a white-box explanation for rejection decisions—a property absent from output-confidence or likelihood-ratio baselines. A key open limitation is representational aliasing: when the task taxonomy is finer-grained than SAE feature resolution (e.g., CLINC150's 150 intents), distinct intents collapse onto overlapping feature supports and discrimination degrades sharply, suggesting that larger dictionaries (65k+) or activation-magnitude signals are needed. Further open questions include whether the method generalizes beyond the tested Gemma-2 family, how it behaves under long-context or localized out-of-scope triggers, and whether the first-order Markov sufficiency finding holds under different SAE sparsity/reconstruction tradeoffs.
