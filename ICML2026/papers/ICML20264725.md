## Problem

No comprehensive, cryptography-specific benchmark for evaluating LLMs exists. Prior cybersecurity benchmarks include only a small fraction of crypto challenges with inconsistent quality, and the interdisciplinary nature of cryptography—spanning theoretical foundations, heavy large-number computation, and practical vulnerability exploitation—makes it difficult to assess LLM capabilities in a unified framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264725/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes three input formats: (1) 135 rewritten multiple-choice questions on cryptographic concepts, (2) 150 CTF challenges providing source code plus ciphertext (or a running server for dynamic challenges), with helper scripts to load truncated outputs exceeding 4096 characters, and (3) 30 proof problems drawn from unpublished course exams and assignments. Outputs are a per-task score—MCQ accuracy, CTF pass@3 success rate over 100-turn agent episodes, and best-of-three proof scoring rate—combined into a 300-point composite score. Proof grading is fully automated using two LLM graders (gpt-5.1 and gemini-3-pro-preview), each scoring three times per answer, with the six scores averaged and validated against human expert ratings (Pearson r = 0.90).

## Method

AICrypto is built through 546+ person-hours of expert collaboration, with all tasks curated, rewritten, and verified by a panel including a cryptography professor, four Ph.D. students, and two undergraduates. For CTF challenges, the LLM operates as a ReAct-style agent in a sandboxed Ubuntu environment (Python 3.10, SageMath 10.5, yafu), issuing one action per turn (command, file creation, verification, or give-up) over up to 100 rounds, with pass@3 as the metric. Proof problems are graded automatically via detailed per-problem scoring rubrics and reference solutions, with two strong LLMs independently scoring each answer three times; the simple average of all six scores achieves the highest correlation with human experts. MCQs are evaluated in a single turn with a structured output format. The paper evaluates 17 state-of-the-art LLMs and establishes human expert baselines for all three task types, while conducting contamination analysis via n-gram retrieval and official-solution availability comparisons.

## Results

On AICrypto (composite score out of 300), gemini-2.5-pro achieves the highest score of 234.8, followed by o3-high and o3 at approximately 230, while human experts score 267.0. On MCQs, o3 reaches 97.8% accuracy (only 3 errors out of 135), surpassing the best human expert at 94.1%. On CTF challenges, a persistent 25–30 percentage-point gap remains: human experts achieve 81.2% success rate versus 56.0% for gemini-2.5-pro and 54.0% for o3-high. On proof problems, human experts lead at 94.0% scoring rate, with gemini-2.5-pro at 85.4% and o3-high at 82.9%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AICrypto is the most comprehensive cryptography-specific LLM benchmark to date, containing 315 total crypto tasks (150 CTF + 135 MCQ + 30 proof) compared to CyBench's 16, NYU CTF's 62, Shao et al.'s 4, and InterCode-CTF's 15. It also evaluates 17 LLMs, substantially more than any prior benchmark (2–8 models). Unlike prior benchmarks that include only CTF challenges, AICrypto adds MCQs for knowledge assessment and proof problems for formal reasoning, providing a multi-faceted evaluation. The paper does not directly compare model scores across benchmarks (since tasks differ), but positions AICrypto as filling a gap: no prior work systematically evaluates LLMs across the full spectrum of cryptographic skills from memorization to vulnerability exploitation to research-level proof construction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of LLM cybersecurity evaluation (CyBench, NYU CTF Bench, InterCode-CTF) by isolating cryptography as a first-class evaluation domain rather than a minor component. It departs from CipherBank (Li et al., 2025), which studies LLM reasoning through decryption tasks limited to classical cryptography, by covering the full spectrum from conceptual knowledge to vulnerability exploitation to formal proof construction. The agent-based CTF evaluation framework builds directly on ReAct-style interaction loops used in prior CTF benchmarks, while the automated proof grading adapts the multi-grader averaging strategy from Luong et al. (2025) to a cryptography-specific rubric-based setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AICrypto provides the first comprehensive, expert-verified benchmark for assessing LLM cryptographic capability, filling a gap the authors identify as stemming from the field's interdisciplinary complexity. The clear performance tiering—surpassing humans on MCQs, approaching them on proofs, but lagging by 25–30 points on CTF—pinpoints that LLMs' weakness is not knowledge but dynamic numerical reasoning and multi-step exploitation planning. Open questions include whether more sophisticated agent frameworks (multi-agent, tool-augmented) can close the CTF gap, and whether the MCQ saturation signals a need for fundamentally harder conceptual benchmarks. The high-correlation automated grading pipeline (Pearson 0.90) is itself a reusable resource for future crypto-proof evaluation.
