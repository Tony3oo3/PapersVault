## Problem

Transcendental functions like the exponential are approximated by methods (Taylor series, Chebyshev, Padé) developed to attain arbitrary accuracy, yet modern hardware uses finite-precision types (e.g., float32) where accuracy beyond the type's precision is discarded. The paper asks whether explicitly targeting high-but-finite accuracy can yield approximations that are significantly more efficient (fewer operations) than these established methods, and whether an automated, zero-knowledge search can discover such approximations without any prior mathematical knowledge.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263834/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a target function (e.g., 2^x) restricted to a bounded interval (e.g., [0,1], with range reduction extending to ℝ), a set of allowed operations (default {+,-,×,÷}, extensible to √· or hardware-native instructions), and two optimization objectives (accuracy and operation count or CPU throughput). It produces imperative computer programs written as sequences of arithmetic instructions that can reuse intermediate values—a structural advantage over closed-form expressions—and, for the most salient results, mathematically proven error bounds via interval arithmetic or the Gappa prover.

## Method

AutoNumerics-Zero is a zero-knowledge symbolic regression that evolves programs from empty code using a nested optimization loop. The outer loop is a distributed variant of NSGA-II (dNSGA-II) that maintains a population of compute-graph programs and selects parents near the accuracy-vs-operations Pareto front; mutations randomly insert, delete, or rewire vertices/edges in the compute graph, introducing no mathematical knowledge. The inner loop uses CMA-ES to optimize the program's floating-point coefficients against a sampled loss. The key insight is that representing functions as programs (rather than formulas) enables intermediate-value reuse and arbitrary operation combinations, while the evolutionary search can optimize non-differentiable objectives like operation count or actual CPU throughput—enabling, for example, the discovery of a 10-operation exponential approximation with 14 significant figures and hardware-aware programs that exploit compiler heuristics for >3× speedups.

## Results

The headline result is a 10-operation program approximating 2^x with a mathematically proven maximum relative error below 5.4×10⁻¹⁵ (14 significant figures), exceeding previously known approximations of the same operation count by more than 6 orders of magnitude. In the hardware-aware setting, the top evolved float32 program is more than 3× faster than the best baseline while maintaining a proven error bound under 1 ULP. For the Airy function, a 19-operation evolved program achieves accuracy 4.2, reducing maximum error by two orders of magnitude compared to the best 20-operation baseline. For the Bessel function, the 8-operation evolved program (accuracy 8.1) outperforms a carefully hand-designed 9-operation Chebyshev-based baseline (accuracy 7.8).

## Baselines & Comparisons

For the exponential function, evolved programs surpass every baseline (Taylor/Horner, Padé, Chebyshev, Remez minimax, and continued fractions of Euler, Gauss, and Macon) at every operation count, with the 10-operation program exceeding the best same-size baseline by over 6 orders of magnitude in accuracy. For cosine, evolved programs compare favorably to the Chebyshev approximation, which is the strongest baseline for this periodic function. For the Airy function, the 19-operation evolved program reduces maximum error by two orders of magnitude versus the best 20-operation baseline. For the Bessel function, evolved programs outperform the Frobenius method baseline; a tailored hand-designed Chebyshev baseline (9 ops, accuracy 7.8) is slightly worse than the 8-operation evolved program (accuracy 8.1). For the error function, the comparison is mixed: evolved programs find more concise approximations at lower accuracy targets, but Padé approximants win at higher accuracies due to fortuitous term cancellation. In the hardware-aware exponential setting, evolved programs are >3× faster than all baselines (minimax with lattice reduction, Taylor, Padé) while maintaining <1 ULP error, a speedup that transfers across 8 years of Intel and AMD architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper combines three established threads—genetic programming (Koza, 1992), multi-objective evolutionary selection via NSGA-II (Deb et al., 2002), and the symbolic regression paradigm (Schmidt & Lipson, 2009)—into a 'zero-knowledge' pipeline that searches over arbitrary compute graphs of {+,-,×,÷} starting from empty code. It departs from prior symbolic regression work, which has focused on recovering known physics equations from noisy data, by targeting in-silico function approximation where ground truth is exact and the search space is all programs rather than a fixed expression family. It also contrasts with superoptimization (which requires a correct starting program) and with RL-based algorithm discovery (Fawzi et al., 2022; Mankowitz et al., 2023), which searches at small input sizes and generalizes analytically, whereas AutoNumerics-Zero searches directly at practical 32-bit floating-point scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates, with formally proven error bounds, that evolutionary search over unconstrained program structures can outperform centuries of hand-designed numerical methods (Taylor, Chebyshev, Padé, Remez, continued fractions) by more than 6 orders of magnitude in accuracy at the same operation count for the exponential function. The hardware-aware variant showing a 3× speedup by exploiting compiler heuristics suggests a broader principle: black-box evolutionary search can optimize objectives (throughput, ULP error) that are intractable for traditional analytical methods. Key limitations include the reliance on range reduction (shared with all baselines), substantial upfront compute (6k–10k core-days), and an interpretability-quality tradeoff; open questions include incorporating range reduction into the search, adding non-floating-point operations, and exploring backward stability as an objective.
