## Problem

Asynchronous Pipeline Parallelism (Async PP) eliminates pipeline bubbles and maximizes GPU throughput, but its adoption for LLM pretraining has been limited by the widespread belief that gradient staleness causes fundamental optimization instability. Prior work (Ajanthan et al., 2025) used the original PipeDream schedule with variable delays across stages, reporting severe degradation at 16 stages, leaving open whether the issue is the schedule, the optimizer, or both. This paper asks whether a constant one-step delay (as in PipeDream-2BW) is truly a barrier or merely an optimizer-dependent artifact.

## Input / Output

The method consumes text tokens from FineWeb-Edu (and FineWeb at 10B scale) as the training corpus, along with model parameters and one-step-delayed gradients g_{t-1} computed at the previous pipeline stage. The Error Feedback variant additionally requires storing one extra model-sized buffer of the previous optimizer update u_{t-2}(g_{t-2}). Output is the updated parameter matrix X_{t+1} per pipeline stage, with the full system producing a trained decoder-only LLM (dense SmolLM2 at 135M/360M, or MoE at 2B/10B) whose final validation loss is compared against a synchronous baseline trained with identical hyperparameters.

## Method

The paper combines three key components. First, it adopts the PipeDream-2BW schedule, which accumulates gradients over M micro-batches before updating, guaranteeing a uniform one-step delay across all pipeline stages (unlike original PipeDream's variable delays). Second, it identifies through systematic benchmarking of 10+ optimizers that staleness robustness is optimizer-dependent: AdamW and MARS degrade severely (+0.28–0.47 loss) while Muon, Adan, SOAP, and others remain within 0.03, with momentum coefficient being the critical hyperparameter. Third, it proposes an optimizer-agnostic Error Feedback correction that modifies the update rule to x_{t+1} = x_t − 2·u_{t-1}(g_{t-1}) + u_{t-2}(g_{t-2}), effectively adding the difference between the previously applied update and the current delayed update to compensate for staleness. This correction recovers 50–90% of the sync-async gap across optimizers. The paper further provides the first convergence analysis of LMO-based methods (specifically Muon) with Error Feedback under fixed gradient delay, and validates the full pipeline at 10B MoE scale where Async PP + EF matches the synchronous baseline exactly (1.906 vs 1.906 final loss).

## Results

On the 10B-parameter MoE model trained for 200B FineWeb-Edu tokens, Async PP with Error Feedback achieves a final validation loss of 1.906, exactly matching the synchronous baseline (1.906), while standard Async PP alone incurs only a +0.005 gap (1.911). On the 360M dense model, Muon under one-step delay shows a mere +0.012 validation loss increase versus +0.278 for AdamW, and Error Feedback recovers 71% of the Muon gap and 90% of the AdamW gap. Across 10 optimizers benchmarked on 135M and 360M models, only AdamW and MARS suffer severe degradation (>0.25 loss increase), while Muon, Adan, AdaMuon, SOAP, and Lion all remain within +0.03 of their synchronous baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 10 optimizers under one-step gradient delay. Muon (Jordan et al., 2024) is the standout: it achieves the best synchronous performance among robust optimizers (2.578 on 360M) while maintaining the smallest practical sync-async gap (+0.012), and with Error Feedback its 10B-scale loss matches the synchronous baseline exactly (1.906 vs. 1.906). Adan (Xie et al., 2024) shows the absolute smallest gap (+0.006/+0.010) but is less established in practice. SOAP (Vyas et al., 2024) and AdaMuon (Si et al., 2025) are also robust (+0.017 to +0.027), while Lion (Chen et al., 2023) and NorMuon (Li et al., 2025c) show moderate degradation (+0.030 to +0.046). AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017) and MARS (Yuan et al., 2024) suffer catastrophic degradation (+0.278 to +0.469), confirming that the historically dominant optimizer is fundamentally incompatible with one-step delay. Against the original PipeDream variable-delay schedule (Narayanan et al., 2019), PipeDream-2BW's constant delay is essential: at P=16 stages, even Muon with μ=0.99 and EF loses >0.03, whereas the constant-delay schedule maintains a stable +0.012 gap regardless of depth. SAPipe-style weight prediction (Chen et al., 2022) performs on par with the proposed Error Feedback correction on the 135M model (2.845 vs. 2.845 for Muon), confirming both are effective mitigation strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the PipeDream-2BW constant-delay schedule (Narayanan et al., 2021a) and adapts the Error Feedback concept (Seide et al., 2014) from gradient compression to the delayed-update setting, while extending the LMO convergence framework of Kovalev (2025) to handle arbitrary gradient delays. It departs from the only prior Async PP study for LLM pretraining (Ajanthan et al., 2025), which used the original PipeDream schedule with variable delays and concluded that staleness causes severe degradation; this paper reframes the problem as a constant one-step delay and shows the degradation is optimizer-specific rather than intrinsic. The theoretical contribution is the first convergence analysis of LMO-based methods (specifically Muon) with Error Feedback under fixed gradient delay.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work removes a major practical barrier to adopting asynchronous pipeline parallelism at scale: by showing that Muon + Error Feedback achieves exact parity with synchronous training on a 10B MoE model, it makes the significant throughput gains from eliminating pipeline bubbles accessible without quality loss. The finding that staleness robustness is governed by first-moment dynamics (momentum coefficient) rather than being a fundamental optimization limitation redirects the field's attention from schedule design to optimizer selection. Open questions include whether the sync-async gap remains bounded at trillion-token training horizons, whether the results generalize to non-MoE architectures at this scale, and whether the EF correction's single extra model-sized buffer becomes prohibitive at extreme parameter counts.
