## Problem

Verifying whether a language model is genuinely reasoning or merely pattern-matching remains an open problem: formal proof assistants conflate logical validity with syntactic acceptability (rejecting correct proofs for timeouts or missing imports), while learned verifiers require substantial labeled data and may not generalize across architectures. This paper asks whether the internal geometry of transformer attention itself carries a measurable, training-free signal of reasoning validity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263475/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a formal proof (Lean 4 text) and a pre-trained transformer model, running the proof through the model to extract post-softmax attention matrices and hidden states at every layer and head. It produces four per-layer spectral diagnostics (Fiedler value, HFER, spectral entropy, smoothness) and a binary validity prediction via a single calibrated threshold on one chosen metric at one chosen layer. No learned parameters are involved; the only free parameter is the threshold τ, which requires roughly 50 labeled examples to calibrate for a new model.

## Method

The core mechanism treats each post-softmax attention matrix as a directed weighted token graph, symmetrizes it, aggregates across heads via attention-mass weighting, and constructs the combinatorial graph Laplacian. Hidden states at each layer are then projected onto the Laplacian eigenbasis (Graph Fourier Transform), yielding four training-free diagnostics: the Fiedler value (algebraic connectivity), High-Frequency Energy Ratio (fraction of signal energy above the median eigenvalue), spectral entropy (energy spread across modes), and normalized smoothness (1 − Dirichlet energy / max energy). Valid proofs consistently produce lower HFER, higher Fiedler value, and higher smoothness, reflecting smoother and more globally connected attention graphs; a single-threshold rule on the best metric-layer pair achieves 85–96% accuracy across seven models from four architectural families. Notably, Sliding Window Attention shifts the discriminative feature from HFER to smoothness, demonstrating that attention architecture governs which spectral channel encodes reasoning quality.

## Results

Across seven models from four architectural families (Llama, Qwen, Phi, Mistral), the spectral signature is universally significant with p_MW < 10⁻⁴⁷ and p_t < 10⁻⁷⁵ in every case. Effect sizes range from Cohen's d = 2.09 (Mistral-7B, Sliding Window Attention) to d = 3.30 (Phi-3.5-mini), far exceeding the conventional 'large' threshold of 0.8. Single-threshold classification achieves 85.9–95.6% calibrated accuracy (82.8–85.9% under nested cross-validation) versus a ~57% majority-class baseline. In Best-of-16 proof search, HFER reranking improves Pass@1 by +4.4–6.6% over log-probability, and on informal MATH chain-of-thought the signal attenuates to d = 0.78 (p < 10⁻³) while still outperforming a supervised Random Forest.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the supervised hallucination probe of Obeso et al. (2026) (AUC 0.981, trained on millions of labels), the training-free HFER method achieves 98% of that AUC (0.962) using only 50 calibration labels, and 0.923 AUC with zero labels. In Best-of-16 proof search on Llama-3.1-8B, HFER reranking reaches 34.2% Pass@1, outperforming log-probability (29.8%, +4.4 pp) and token entropy (30.4%, +3.8 pp) despite a slightly lower AUC-ROC (0.962 vs. 0.979), because HFER penalises structurally incoherent but fluent hallucinations that log-probability misses. On the informal MATH dataset, the spectral threshold (77.1% full / 68.4% balanced) outperforms a supervised Random Forest (74.5%) and the majority-class baseline (76.6% full / 50% balanced), demonstrating an 'inverse overfitting' effect where the parameter-free method generalises better than the learned one. A linear probe trained on 363 labels achieves AUC 0.949, still below the 50-label HFER calibration (0.962). The ensemble of log-probability and HFER (ZLP-ZHFER) achieves the best overall Pass@1 of 37.1% and AUC 0.988, confirming the two signals are orthogonally complementary.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the attention-graph framework of El et al. (2025), which established mathematical equivalences between self-attention and message passing on weighted graphs, by applying classical spectral graph theory (Laplacian eigenvalues, Graph Fourier Transforms) to extract validity-relevant diagnostics. It departs from the dominant probing paradigm (Azaria & Mitchell, Marks & Tegmark, Lightman et al.) that trains linear or nonlinear classifiers on hidden states, instead requiring zero learned parameters and operating purely on attention geometry. The graph signal processing formalism of Shuman et al. (2013) and Ortega et al. (2018) provides the mathematical scaffolding for treating hidden states as signals on the attention graph, but no prior work has connected spectral properties of attention to reasoning validity classification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The training-free nature (single threshold, no labels) makes this immediately deployable in proof-search pipelines where formal verification is too slow, and the Best-of-N reranking gains (+4.4–6.6% Pass@1) demonstrate practical utility. The 'Platonic validity' finding—that the spectral signal tracks logical coherence rather than compiler acceptance—is conceptually important, suggesting transformer attention geometry encodes something closer to mathematical truth than syntactic well-formedness. However, the method remains correlation-based rather than causal, the signal attenuates substantially on informal chain-of-thought (d=0.78 vs. d>2.09 for formal proofs), and the theoretical explanation for *why* valid reasoning induces smoother attention graphs is absent. Open questions include whether the signature generalizes beyond Lean-formalized mathematics, whether it can be made causal (intervention on specific attention heads), and whether the architectural determinism finding (SWA shifting the discriminative channel) extends to other sparse-attention variants.
