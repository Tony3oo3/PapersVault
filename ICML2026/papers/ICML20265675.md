## Problem

While transformers are known to learn in stages, it remains unclear how individual attention heads coordinate during this process: do they immediately specialize in different patterns, or do they first compete for the same statistically dominant signal before differentiating? This paper addresses this gap by studying sparse attention formation in a controlled setting where the next token depends on multiple groups of past positions with differing statistical importance.

## Input / Output

The model takes a sequence of w past tokens (each a one-hot vector in R^d, augmented with positional one-hot encodings) and produces a probability distribution over the next token via softmax of aggregated feature-matrix outputs. The context is partitioned into h disjoint groups I(1),...,I(h) of positions, each associated with a feature matrix A_k^* whose Frobenius norm determines its statistical importance. The architecture is a single-block decoder transformer with h attention heads, where each head is expected to learn a sparse attention pattern copying from its assigned group of positions.

## Method

The paper introduces a high-order Markov-chain task with an importance hierarchy over positional groups and shows that single-layer multi-head transformers learn it incrementally: all heads first compete on the most important positions (competitive phase), then one head offshoots to the next pattern while remaining heads compensate (cooperative phase). To analyze this, the authors derive a simplified regression variant where the population gradient flow reduces to a nonlinear tensor factorization problem. Theoretical results (Theorems 1–4) prove that near-symmetric initialization induces coupled dynamics converging to the dominant pattern, and that an offshooting head can subsequently converge to the next pattern while non-offshooting heads adjust their value matrices to cancel cross-terms. The analysis connects the observed head-level specialization to saddle-to-saddle dynamics, and experiments show that dataset size controls how many stages are completed, linking incremental learning to implicit complexity selection via early stopping.

## Results

The paper's main empirical finding is that single-block decoder transformers with h=3 heads learn a high-order Markov-chain task in exactly three discrete stages, each corresponding to the acquisition of one sparse attention pattern (for groups I(1), I(2), I(3) ordered by feature-matrix norm). The dynamics transition from a competitive phase (all heads attend to I(1)) to a cooperative phase (heads specialize sequentially), with the offshooting head's residual attention compensated by the remaining heads' value matrices. Theoretically, Theorems 1–4 prove stagewise convergence of the population gradient flow on a regression surrogate: near-symmetric initialization drives all heads to the dominant pattern (Theorem 1), small deviations remain bounded for a controlled time (Theorems 2–3), and an offshooting head converges to the next feature (Theorem 4). Reducing the dataset size below a threshold decreases the number of completed stages, with early-stopped models approaching simpler predictors that use only the top-k most important positions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare its method against external baselines; it is a mechanistic and theoretical study of transformer training dynamics on a synthetic task. The empirical 'comparisons' are ablations across controlled variations: (i) initialization scale u — smaller u slows learning, and u=0 yields exactly symmetric heads that learn only the first pattern; (ii) importance multiplicative constant m — at m=1 (no hierarchy) the three stages collapse to two, while m=1.7 yields three clearly separated stages; (iii) dataset size — below ~2000 samples the number of observed stages drops from three to two or one; (iv) architecture (full vs. minimal), optimizer (AdamW vs. SGD), and interval structure (disjoint vs. overlapping) all preserve the competitive-to-cooperative transition. The regression gradient-flow simulation (Figure 19) is compared qualitatively against the minimal transformer trained on the classification task (Figure 21), confirming that the theoretical predictions capture the same structural signatures (competitive phase, sequential offshoot order, compensatory value-matrix dips) despite differences in loss, parameterization, and timescale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on staged/incremental learning in neural networks (Saxe et al., 2019; Arora et al., 2019; Gissin et al., 2020) and transformer attention dynamics (Jelassi et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2023; Zucchet et al., 2026) by introducing a high-order Markov-chain task with an explicit importance hierarchy over positional groups. It builds on Edelman et al. (2024), who observed sub-n-gram stages in in-context Markov-chain learning, but adds the multi-head coordination question: how do heads divide labor? The theoretical analysis repurposes the tensor-factorization framework from implicit regularization (Razin et al., 2021; Jin et al., 2023) to characterize the competitive-to-cooperative transition in multi-head attention, a setting where prior analyses have been largely single-head or have frozen parameter blocks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical account of how head specialization in multi-head attention emerges from standard training dynamics without an explicit curriculum, connecting the observed competitive-to-cooperative transition to saddle-to-saddle dynamics in tensor factorization. The finding that dataset size controls the number of stages reached (fewer stages with less data) suggests a principled link between early stopping and effective model complexity in transformers. Key limitations include the single-layer architecture, the regression surrogate for analysis, and the near-symmetric initialization assumption; extending the theory to deeper stacks, more realistic data distributions, and non-symmetric initializations remains open. The framework could inform curriculum design and interpretability tools that track head specialization during training.
