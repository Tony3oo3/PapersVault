## Problem

Determining where a mobile robot should position itself to execute a manipulation policy is typically treated as a geometric reachability search, which is insufficient for modern learning-based policies that are extremely sensitive to initial base poses. The only existing policy-aware alternative (Mobi-π) relies on pre-built 3DGS scene reconstruction and iterative Bayesian optimization, yielding prohibitively slow inference (~274s) and incompatibility with policies trained on large or continuously updated datasets. This paper reformulates base positioning as a pose-preference prediction problem learned directly from policy rollouts, treating the manipulation policy as a black box.

## Input / Output

The network takes a single ego-centric RGB point cloud (downsampled to 8,192 points) captured by an onboard RGB-D camera at the navigation end pose, requiring no global map or historical information. It outputs the parameters of a Gaussian Mixture Model (K=1 or 2 kernels) over the robot's initial pose in SE(2) or SE(3) space (x, y, θ, and optionally torso height h), from which a collision-free pose is sampled via a 2D occupancy map. The GMM formulation explicitly handles the multi-modality of preferable poses, e.g., a drawer-closing policy that works from both sides.

## Method

N2M encodes the point cloud with a fine-tuned Point-BERT into a fixed-length latent vector, then passes it through an MLP to predict GMM parameters (kernel weights, means, and covariances). Training is supervised: labels are poses at which the manipulation policy successfully completed a task, and the loss is the negative log-likelihood of the GMM augmented with three regularization terms (weight entropy, inter-mode Mahalanobis distance, and weighted mode entropy) to prevent mode collapse and overfitting. The key technical contribution is a viewpoint augmentation strategy: for each collected scene-pose pair, M viewpoints are uniformly sampled within the task area, point clouds are rendered from each via camera projection, and all rendered observations share the same pose label, enabling the model to learn viewpoint-invariant preferences with remarkable data efficiency (matching oracle performance with as few as 10 rollouts). This design yields real-time inference (~0.07s per prediction) and plug-and-play applicability across diverse policies, tasks, and robot hardware without requiring access to the policy's training data.

## Results

On the CloseDrawer benchmark (Table 1), N2M achieves 55% success rate under Diffusion Policy and 56% under BC Transformer, with a single-inference time of 0.07 s. This substantially outperforms the non-policy-aware Reachability baseline (16%/15%) and the policy-aware Mobi-π (24%/9%), while approaching the Oracle upper bound (93%/97%). In broader RoboCasa simulation experiments across four tasks and three policy architectures, N2M trained with as few as 10 rollouts matches or even surpasses the Oracle baseline, and generalizes zero-shot to unseen scenes with only 10–50 rollouts across 1–5 training scenes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

N2M decisively outperforms the Reachability baseline (non-policy-aware, used by MoManipVLA, MoTo, and IRM) by roughly 40 percentage points in success rate on both policies, confirming that geometric reachability alone is insufficient for learning-based manipulation policies. Against Mobi-π, the only prior policy-aware method, N2M achieves more than 2× higher success rate (55% vs. 24% under DP; 56% vs. 9% under BC) while being ~3,900× faster at inference (0.07 s vs. 273.52 s), since Mobi-π requires iterative Bayesian optimization over a pre-built 3DGS scene. N2M also requires far less human labor per environmental change (one-time 20.7 min rollout collection vs. n × 5 min scene reconstruction for Mobi-π). In the broader RoboCasa simulation (Fig. 5), N2M matches or occasionally exceeds the Oracle baseline across diverse tasks and policy architectures (BC Transformer, Diffusion Policy, ACT), a result the authors attribute to the policy's true preference diverging from the fixed training-data pose. In real-world sequential tasks (Fig. 8), N2M substantially improves both subtask and overall sequence completion rates compared to both Reachability and a Human-Intuition baseline, demonstrating compounding benefits in long-horizon mobile manipulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant geometric-search paradigm for mobile manipulation base positioning (IRM, MoManipVLA, MoTo), which only ensures physical reachability, and from the sole prior policy-aware method Mobi-π, which relies on 3DGS scene reconstruction and iterative Bayesian optimization. Rather than building on either lineage, N2M repurposes a pre-trained Point-BERT encoder with a GMM head to predict pose preferences directly from policy rollouts, treating the manipulation policy as a black box. The conceptual shift is from 'find a reachable pose' to 'learn where the policy actually succeeds,' which the authors frame as a systematic reformulation of the base positioning problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that N2M can outperform the oracle baseline (the fixed pose used during policy training) in certain task-policy combinations reveals that policy preferences are not simply recoverable from training data distribution—a conceptual insight that challenges the assumption underlying similarity-based in-distribution methods. Practically, the 0.07s single-forward-pass inference versus Mobi-π's 273s iterative search makes policy-aware positioning viable in dynamic, non-static environments for the first time. Open questions include how N2M adapts when the underlying manipulation policy is continuously updated (e.g., via RL fine-tuning), whether the GMM formulation scales to higher-dimensional pose spaces (e.g., full 6-DoF with joint configuration), and how to handle the observed sensitivity to small objects and far distances in real-world deployment.
