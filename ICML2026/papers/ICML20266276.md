## Problem

Existing scientific safety benchmarks suffer from four critical limitations: they assess either safety knowledge or refusal behavior but rarely both; they test superficial ethical scenarios rather than deep domain expertise requiring intricate scientific reasoning; they rely on LLM-as-a-Judge introducing inherent evaluation bias; and they are vulnerable to data contamination since frontier models are trained on the same public databases (PubChem, ChEMBL) from which questions are drawn. SafeSci addresses all four gaps simultaneously with a unified framework.

## Input / Output

SafeSciBench consumes 250K test queries in five deterministic formats (MCQ, TFQ, fill-in-the-blank, QA, and structured generation) spanning 125 tasks across seven fields. For generation tasks, inputs are partial property descriptions or edit instructions, and outputs are SMILES/SELFIES strings, amino acid sequences, or nucleotide sequences scored via fingerprint Tanimoto similarity, BLOSUM45 alignment, and BLAST-based coverage respectively. Safety risk queries produce a binary refusal determination, and all evaluations are repeated five times with random 3,000-question subsamples to report mean±std scores.

## Method

SafeSci is built on four design principles: an explicit dichotomy between safety knowledge (model must answer correctly) and safety risk (model must refuse), a focus on professional domain expertise over generic malicious intent, objective deterministic-answer evaluation to eliminate judge bias, and data contamination mitigation via cross-database interaction and 125 diverse task formulations. Questions are constructed primarily (>90%) through a template-based pipeline where 15,000+ LLM-generated templates with placeholder slots are filled with structured metadata from sources like PubChem, UniProt, DrugBank, and GenBank; a smaller fraction (<10%) uses a dual-agent Generator-Validator system for unstructured literature. For safety enhancement, SafeSciTrain (1.5M instruction samples, non-overlapping with the benchmark) is used for LoRA fine-tuning (rank 64, alpha 128, 1 epoch, lr 1e-4) on open-source models, demonstrating that refusal rates nearly double (e.g., Qwen3-8B from 0.31 to 0.64) while general capabilities remain largely preserved.

## Results

On SafeSciBench safety knowledge (MCQ/TFQ accuracy), Claude-Sonnet-4.5 achieves the highest overall score of 0.80, followed by Intern-S1 (0.76) and Gemini-3-Pro (0.74), while Grok-4.1-reasoning scores the lowest at 0.32. On safety risk (refusal rate), Grok-4.1-reasoning achieves the highest overall safety rate of 0.65, followed by Gemini-3-Pro (0.61) and Claude-Sonnet-4.5 (0.59), while Llama-4-Scout-Instruct is the worst at 0.16. After LoRA fine-tuning on SafeSciTrain, Qwen3-8B's safety risk rate nearly doubles from 0.31 to 0.64 (+0.33), and its knowledge accuracy improves from 0.59 to 0.70 (+0.11), with negligible impact on general capabilities (MMLU, HumanEval).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 24 LLMs (proprietary, open-source, and scientific-specialized) on SafeSciBench rather than comparing a single method against baselines. A key finding is the decoupling of safety knowledge and safety risk: Claude-Sonnet-4.5 leads in knowledge (0.80) but trails Grok-4.1-reasoning in risk refusal (0.59 vs. 0.65), while Grok-4.1-reasoning has the worst knowledge accuracy (0.32). Closed-source models do not uniformly outperform open-source ones; Intern-S1 (a scientific LLM) achieves 0.76 knowledge accuracy, surpassing GPT-5.2 (0.68) and matching Gemini-3-Pro (0.74). Open-source models show particularly weak risk refusal in engineering and psychology (e.g., Qwen3-8B: 0.16 and 0.14), whereas closed-source models like Gemini-3-Pro achieve 0.92–0.96 in chemistry and biology risk. Fine-tuning on SafeSciTrain yields the largest gains for smaller open-source models: Qwen3-8B's risk rate jumps from 0.31 to 0.64, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct's from 0.41 to 0.58, while general capabilities (MMLU, HumanEval) remain essentially unchanged.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by unifying two previously separate evaluation dimensions—safety knowledge accuracy and safety risk refusal—into a single multi-disciplinary framework, whereas prior benchmarks (SciKnowEval, SOSBench, SciSafeEval, WMDP) each addressed only one dimension or a narrow subset of fields. It departs from the prevailing 'LLM-as-a-Judge' paradigm by restricting evaluation to deterministic-answer tasks (MCQ, TFQ, structured generation), and mitigates data contamination through cross-database interaction and task diversification rather than simple retrieval from public corpora. The inclusion of a 1.5M-sample fine-tuning dataset (SafeSciTrain) alongside the benchmark extends the scope from pure evaluation to active safety enhancement, a component absent from all prior scientific safety benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that safety-risk refusal capability does not improve with model scale challenges the implicit assumption that larger models are inherently safer, pointing to the need for targeted alignment interventions. The observed over-refusal problem—particularly acute in biology—highlights a practical tension between safety and utility that current models fail to resolve, and the paper's fine-tuning results (e.g., Qwen3-8B refusal rate doubling from 0.31 to 0.64) demonstrate a viable path forward while simultaneously exposing a trade-off where biology knowledge accuracy drops. Open questions include developing context-aware refusal that avoids blanket rejections, building dynamic evaluation systems resistant to benchmark gaming, and extending the framework to multimodal scientific inputs.
