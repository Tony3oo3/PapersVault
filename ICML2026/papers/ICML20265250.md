## Problem

While prior work established that looped Transformers subsume deterministic CoT and exhibit a strict separation at logarithmic iterations, two fundamental questions remained open: whether the separation extends beyond the logarithmic regime, and whether latent thought is universally more expressive than CoT. This paper resolves both by showing that latent thought gains an efficiency advantage in the polylogarithmic regime through parallelizability, while CoT retains a unique capability for stochastic approximate counting that latent thought cannot match.

## Input / Output

The formal framework treats reasoning as evaluation of directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) over a finite alphabet, where inputs are token sequences encoding problem instances (e.g., generator sequences for group word problems, adjacency structures for connectivity, arithmetic expressions, string pairs, DNF formulas, or graphs for coloring). CoT consumes inputs autoregressively, appending one discrete token per step, while latent thought (Coconut or looped TF) iterates over continuous hidden states of dimension O(poly(n)) without emitting intermediate tokens. In the probabilistic setting, CoT stochastically samples both intermediate reasoning tokens and output tokens, whereas latent thought performs only deterministic transformations in latent space and samples only at the final output position.

## Method

The paper establishes a formal complexity-theoretic separation by relating each reasoning paradigm to Boolean circuit classes. For decision problems, they show latent thought with log^k n iterations exactly captures TC^k (or AC^k under constant precision) by simulating circuit depth layer-by-layer in parallel, while CoT with log^k n steps is upper-bounded by TC^{k-1} due to its inherently sequential token-by-token computation, yielding a strict separation under standard circuit complexity assumptions. For approximate counting, they leverage the self-reducibility structure of counting problems to show that CoT's stochastic decoding emulates Monte Carlo FPRAS algorithms, while latent thought's deterministic latent-space computation cannot achieve the same approximation guarantee (assuming FPTAS ⊊ FPRAS). This separation extends to approximate sampling via the classical Jerrum–Valiant–Vazirani inter-reducibility between approximate counting and sampling for self-reducible relations. Empirically, they validate both separations: looped TFs solve parallelizable tasks (NC^1-complete word problems, TC^1 connectivity, arithmetic, edit distance) with O(log n) loops versus O(n) CoT steps, and CoT produces more uniform empirical distributions over valid graph colorings than looped TFs.

## Results

On parallelizable tasks, Looped Transformer (latent thought) achieves comparable or superior accuracy to CoT with dramatically fewer iterations: on Arithmetic Evaluation (n=32/16), Looped TF reaches 99.4% at 4 loops while CoT plateaus at 82.5% even at 64 steps; on Graph Connectivity (n=32), Looped TF hits 99.0% at 6 loops versus CoT's 88.2% at 32 steps. On approximate counting and sampling of graph colorings, CoT produces empirical distributions closer to the target uniform distribution (lower TV distance), while the looped model concentrates probability mass on a smaller subset of valid colorings. These empirical results validate the theoretical separations: latent thought excels at parallel computation in the polylogarithmic regime, while CoT's stochastic decoding enables approximate counting and sampling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Looped Transformer (as the latent thought instantiation) against Chain of Thought on four parallelizable tasks and approximate counting/sampling. On parallelizable tasks, Looped TF decisively outperforms CoT in iteration efficiency: on Arithmetic Evaluation, Looped TF reaches 99.4% at just 4 loops while CoT only achieves 82.5% at 64 steps (an 8x iteration gap with a 17-point accuracy gap); on Graph Connectivity, Looped TF reaches 99.0% at 6 loops versus CoT's 88.2% at 32 steps. On Edit Distance, CoT with 64 steps (94.8%) slightly edges out Looped TF at 8 loops (90.7%), though Looped TF at 6 loops (86.2%) is already competitive with CoT at 32 steps (87.5%). On approximate counting and sampling of graph colorings, the comparison reverses: CoT produces a distribution closer to the target uniform distribution over valid colorings (lower TV distance), while the looped model concentrates probability mass on a smaller subset, confirming the theoretical separation in favor of CoT for stochastic tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the complexity-theoretic analysis of Transformer reasoning initiated by Merrill & Sabharwal (2023, 2024) and Li et al. (2024), which characterized CoT's power via Boolean circuit classes, and the subsequent work on looped Transformers and Coconut (Saunshi et al. 2025; Merrill & Sabharwal 2025a) that established a strict separation at logarithmic depth. The key departures are twofold: (1) extending the latent-thought-over-CoT separation from the logarithmic to the full polylogarithmic regime (log^k n for arbitrary k), and (2) introducing the first formal separation in the opposite direction, showing that stochastic decoding gives CoT an irreducible advantage for approximate counting and sampling that deterministic latent thought cannot replicate. The probabilistic model definitions build on Nowak et al. (2024) and the self-reducibility/FPRAS framework from classical complexity (Jerrum et al. 1986; Karp et al. 1989).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dual separation provides a principled, complexity-theoretic criterion for selecting between reasoning paradigms: latent thought (looped TF, Coconut) is fundamentally more efficient for parallelizable computation, while CoT is the only paradigm that can express randomized approximate counting and uniform sampling over solution sets. This reframes the 'latent thought vs. CoT' debate from a one-directional hierarchy into a complementary picture, which is practically relevant for designing hybrid architectures. Open questions include whether distillation or other techniques can close the iteration gap without sacrificing computational power, whether the analysis extends to diffusion language models (which possess both parallelizability and stochasticity), and whether the separations hold under more realistic training and evaluation settings beyond the non-uniform, toy-task regime studied here.
