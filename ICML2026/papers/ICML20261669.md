## Problem

Existing refusal-detection methods (RepE-style probes, gradient-based detectors) rely on static readouts—terminal token states or sequence-averaged representations—to identify jailbreak attempts. The paper shows via causal tracing that refusal is actually a sparse, dynamic process localized to specific upstream layer-token positions, and that terminal representations carry attenuated signals that optimization-based attacks (e.g., GCG) can suppress. This static assumption leaves a gap: detectors built on terminal or pooled readouts collapse under attacks that specifically target the final output while leaving the upstream refusal trajectory intact.

## Input / Output

SALO consumes the raw residual-stream hidden states from a selected intermediate layer window (e.g., layers 10–20 for Qwen2.5-7B, layers 5–15 for Llama-3.1-8B), stacked into a 3D tensor M ∈ R^{d×|W|×T} that preserves full layer-depth and token-position geometry. It is not a steering direction, PCA component, or mean-centered vector—the detector learns directly from the activation volume. The output is a single sigmoid detection score (malicious vs. benign), trained with binary cross-entropy on ~5,500 prompts from PKU-SafeRLHF and Toxic-Chat after filtering out explicit jailbreak examples and sequences exceeding 400 tokens, ensuring the model never sees the attack families it must detect at test time.

## Method

SALO (Sparse Activation Localization Operator) is a lightweight white-box detector grounded in causal-tracing findings that refusal manifests as a sparse trajectory across upstream layers rather than a static terminal direction. It applies a bank of 2D convolutional kernels with fixed height 3 (matching the empirically identified causal-tracing layer window) and varying temporal widths {2, 3, 5} in parallel branches, capturing both sharply localized refusal onsets and broader distributed contextual evidence. Each branch applies Conv2d → BatchNorm → ReLU → masked Global Max-Pooling (rather than mean pooling, to preserve the sparse peak activations that constitute the refusal signal), and the pooled features are concatenated, dropped out, and passed through a linear classifier. The theoretical guarantee for attack generalization comes from causal attention: adversarial suffixes appended after the instruction cannot retroactively alter hidden states formed during earlier token processing, so the upstream refusal trajectory persists even when the terminal refusal signal is suppressed. Trained exclusively on standard safety-alignment data with jailbreak examples removed, SALO achieves >85% detection on GCG and >97% on AutoDAN across Qwen, Llama, and Mistral models without ever being exposed to those attack families.

## Results

SALO achieves Detection Success Rates (DSR) exceeding 85% across all three model families (Qwen2.5-7B, Mistral-7B, Llama-3.1-8B) and all four attack types (Direct, Prefilling, GCG, AutoDAN) under a fixed 10% FPR threshold calibrated on XSTest. Headline numbers include 99.4% DSR on Prefilling (Qwen), 98.8% on AutoDAN (Qwen), and 100% on AutoDAN (Llama), with XSTest AUROC of 94.6, 93.2, and 94.8 respectively. In contrast, GradSafe collapses to 0% DSR on Prefilling (Qwen) and 2.0% on AutoDAN (Mistral), while PPL Filter drops to 0% on Prefilling (Mistral) and AutoDAN (Mistral). SALO requires no gradient backpropagation and adds fewer than 20M parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SALO outperforms all five baselines on the majority of model–attack combinations. Against PPL Filter (Jain et al., 2023), SALO wins decisively on natural-language attacks: PPL Filter scores 0% DSR on Prefilling (Mistral) and AutoDAN (Mistral), while SALO achieves 99.4% and 98.8% respectively; PPL Filter's only advantage is 100% GCG detection via suffix perplexity. Against GradSafe (Xie et al., 2024), SALO recovers detection where GradSafe collapses to near-zero (0% Prefilling on Qwen, 2.0% AutoDAN on Mistral, 17.7% GCG on Qwen), while GradSafe retains a slight edge on Direct prompts for Llama (100% vs. 98.3%). Against Linear Probe (Alain & Bengio, 2017), SALO leads on GCG by up to 75 percentage points (96.5% vs. 71.0% on Mistral) and on Prefilling by 31 points (99.4% vs. 68.1% on Mistral). Against SmoothLLM (Robey et al., 2025), SALO dominates across nearly all settings, e.g., 99.4% vs. 57.9% on Qwen-Prefilling and 98.8% vs. 1.6% on Mistral-AutoDAN. Against static RepE-style refusal-direction readouts (Zou et al., 2025), SALO substantially outperforms: RePE-Terminal drops to 0% DSR on GCG (Qwen) and 0.38% (Llama), while RePE-Mean achieves near-random XSTest AUROC (47–49%), confirming that collapsing the layer-token structure into a single direction loses the sparse refusal signal SALO exploits.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant Representation Engineering (RepE) paradigm, which characterizes refusal as a static direction extracted from terminal or sequence-averaged hidden states (Arditi et al., 2024; Wollschläger et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2025). By applying causal tracing (Meng et al., 2022) to minimal malicious-benign prompt pairs, the authors reframe refusal as a sparse, dynamic spatiotemporal trajectory anchored at the token immediately following the semantic concept, rather than a single readout vector. The resulting detector, SALO, is a multi-scale CNN over raw hidden-state volumes from a mid-layer window, contrasting with prior white-box defenses (GradSafe, HiddenDetect) that rely on gradients or linear probes over pooled representations. The paper explicitly positions itself as diagnostic rather than steering-oriented: it uses causal intervention to localize where refusal is constructed, then tests whether that localized structure serves as a robust detection signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the strongest causal refusal signal lives at the 'Refusal Onset' (the token after the harmful concept) rather than at the terminal position challenges a widely adopted assumption in both safety probing and RepE-based steering, and the semantic-collapse result under adaptive GCG (ASR drops to 7% when the adversary suppresses SALO's score) provides strong evidence that the refusal trajectory is inextricably linked to malicious intent. However, SALO is strictly white-box, requires access to intermediate hidden states, and its ROI selection has not been validated on 70B+ models; encoded or cipher-like inputs that the model fails to semantically decode may not trigger the trajectory at all. Open questions include whether the trajectory structure persists at scale, whether individual attention heads or MLP features can be identified as the necessary circuit components, and how to calibrate thresholds for deployment-specific traffic distributions beyond the fixed 10% FPR on XSTest.
