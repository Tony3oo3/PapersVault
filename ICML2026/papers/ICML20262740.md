## Problem

LLMs are extensively trained on symbolic representations such as code and graphs, yet real-world user tasks are specified in natural language, making it critical to understand whether procedures learned in one representation transfer to another. Existing work conflates surface representational factors with deeper structural ones, providing limited insight into why symbolic training helps in some cases but not others. This paper isolates pure procedure generalization by studying isomorphic tasks across three representations and shows that standard post-training methods (SFT, distillation, STaR, GRPO) fail to achieve reliable cross-representation transfer.

## Input / Output

Each asynchronous planning instance is presented in one of three isomorphic formats: a natural language description of steps with durations and ordering constraints, a graph adjacency list paired with a separate time dictionary, or a Python function implementing DAG longest-path search with a weighted adjacency list and randomly reindexed nodes. All three share identical underlying DAG structures, so the only variable is surface representation. The model must produce chain-of-thought reasoning followed by the critical path duration encoded in a structured <answer> tag.

## Method

The paper proposes a two-stage GRPO curriculum: Stage 1 trains on symbolic data (Graph) for 40 steps to induce abstract procedures, then Stage 2 continues on natural language for 40 steps to make those procedures usable in NL. With the same total token budget, this substantially outperforms NL-only training (0.782 vs. 0.698 test accuracy on NL for Qwen-1.5B), and the 1.5B curriculum-trained model matches zero-shot GPT-4o on the planning task. The curriculum order is critical—reversing to NL→Graph degrades performance below NL-only—and the approach is specific to RL rather than SFT, where the same curriculum yields no benefit. Analysis via structure-mapping theory shows the curriculum encourages generative analogy: test performance correlates more strongly with structural similarity to training items than with frequency of similar items, and NL test accuracy after curriculum training correlates more with Graph training than with NL training, indicating that symbolic warm-up specifically encourages learning of transferable procedures.

## Results

The two-stage GRPO curriculum (Graph 40 steps → NL 40 steps) achieves 0.782 test accuracy on NL with Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, matching zero-shot GPT-4o (0.782) and substantially outperforming NL-only 80-step training (0.698) under the same token budget. The curriculum also outperforms the 7B Qwen variant trained on NL alone (0.698) and GPT-4o-mini zero-shot (0.440). On the dialect-robustness test (NL-AAVE), the curriculum reaches 0.573 vs. 0.507 for NL-only, without any explicit dialect exposure during training. Across model families, the same curriculum lifts Olmo-2-7B from 0.613 to 0.631 on NL and from 0.431 to 0.569 on NL-AAVE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks four post-training methods (vanilla SFT, distillation via MiniLLM-style teacher-student [Gu et al., 2024], STaR self-bootstrapping [Zelikman et al., 2022], and GRPO) across three representations and three model families. Distillation achieves the strongest within-representation SFT performance, approaching GRPO with far less compute, but it sometimes *exacerbates* cross-representation degradation (e.g., Code-trained Qwen-7B drops on Graph test). STaR shows modest generalization over vanilla SFT but remains weaker than distillation and far below the proposed curriculum. Critically, replacing the curriculum's stage-2 GRPO with NL distillation collapses performance from 0.782 to 0.462 on NL test, and applying the same two-stage order under SFT yields only 0.249 (vs. 0.244 for NL-only 4 epochs), confirming that RL is substantially more effective than SFT for cross-representation transfer. The GRPO-based curriculum thus wins decisively over both SFT baselines on the primary NL test metric while also improving dialect robustness (NL-AAVE) without any dialect-specific training data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions cross-representation procedure generalization as a form of generative analogy from cognitive science (Gentner's structure-mapping theory), deliberately distinguishing it from the proportional-analogy work that dominates LLM analogy literature (e.g., king:man = queen:woman). It builds on the GRPO reinforcement learning framework from DeepSeek-R1 and the AsyncHow planning benchmark, but departs from prior symbolic-training studies (code pretraining, graph training) by using isomorphic data across three representations to isolate structural procedure transfer from surface-form confounds. The two-stage curriculum connects to curriculum learning in multi-task settings (Pentina et al., Standley et al.) but applies it specifically to the symbolic-to-natural-language transition with RL rather than SFT.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The well-evidenced negative result—that none of four popular post-training methods reliably transfer procedures from symbolic to natural language across model families and scales—challenges the growing assumption that code/graph training should improve natural-language reasoning. The practical two-stage RL curriculum (symbolic warm-up then NL adaptation) offers a concrete recipe that enables a 1.5B model to match zero-shot GPT-4o on planning, with the generative-analogy interpretation providing a theoretical lens for understanding when and why transfer succeeds. Open questions include whether scaling the number of training representations helps, whether the NL-AAVE performance gap can be closed, and how to bridge the large gap between LLMs' need for extended in-representation training and humans' few-shot analogical generalization.
