## Problem

Prior studies of many-shot ICL scaling behavior have focused almost exclusively on non-reasoning tasks, where demonstration order sensitivity diminishes with scale and similarity-based retrieval explains gains. It remains unknown whether these established rules extend to many-shot chain-of-thought in-context learning (CoT-ICL) for reasoning tasks, or whether the mechanism is merely scaled pattern matching or a form of genuine in-context learning.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pool of CoT demonstrations (each a question + reasoning chain + final answer triple) and a test query, and produces an ordered demonstration sequence that is prepended to the query in the prompt. Because CoT demonstrations are roughly 30× longer than standard ICL examples, the scaling analysis is constrained to n ≤ 128 demonstrations. Each demonstration is embedded as a single concatenated string (question + CoT + answer) using Qwen3-Embedding-4B, and the LLM is prompted to generate both an intermediate reasoning chain and a final answer conditioned on the ordered sequence.

## Method

The paper reframes many-shot CoT-ICL as in-context test-time learning rather than pattern matching, supported by three empirical findings: (1) scaling is unstable for non-reasoning LLMs on reasoning tasks but positive for reasoning-oriented LLMs, (2) similarity-based retrieval fails on reasoning because question similarity does not ensure procedural compatibility, and (3) performance variance grows with the number of CoT demonstrations. Guided by two principles—ease of understanding (demonstrations should align with the model's own generation distribution) and smoothness of knowledge progression (consecutive demonstrations should have gradual conceptual transitions)—the authors propose Curvilinear Demonstration Selection (CDS), which orders demonstrations to minimize total curvature of their embedding trajectory. CDS formulates the ordering as a TSP-like problem with a pairwise edge cost combining normalized Euclidean distance and a local curvature proxy, solved via nearest-neighbor initialization followed by 2-opt local search, taking under one minute for n ≤ 128. This yields up to a 5.42 percentage-point gain on a math task with 64 demonstrations and an average 3.81% improvement across math and narrative reasoning tasks.

## Results

CDS (Curvilinear Demonstration Selection) achieves up to a 5.22 percentage-point gain over the original ordering on geometry with Qwen3-14B at n=64 demonstrations (70.36% vs. 65.14%), and consistent gains across all three reasoning tasks (geometry, number_theory, DetectiveQA) and both target LLMs (gpt-5.2, Qwen3-14B). The paper reports an average of 3.81% performance gains across math and narrative reasoning tasks. Ordering curvature is strongly negatively correlated with accuracy (overall r = -0.547), confirming that smoother demonstration trajectories yield better in-context learning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CDS is compared against the original (unretrieved) demonstration ordering and a variant using bge-m3 embeddings (CDS_bge). On geometry, CDS_bge outperforms origin by +5.22 pp at n=64 (Qwen3-14B) and +5.22 pp at n=16 (gpt-5.2), while CDS with the original embedding model gains +3.75 pp and +5.22 pp respectively. On DetectiveQA, both CDS and CDS_bge consistently beat origin, with the largest gap of +3.90 pp (CDS_bge, Qwen3-14B, n=128). On number_theory, gains are smaller (≤1.5 pp) because baseline accuracies are already high (88–92%), leaving limited room for improvement. The paper also shows that similarity-based retrieval (most-similar demonstrations) consistently underperforms both the original and most-dissimilar sets on all three reasoning tasks, confirming that surface-level question similarity is a poor proxy for procedural compatibility in CoT-ICL.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three established threads: many-shot ICL (Agarwal et al., 2024; Bertsch et al., 2025), which demonstrated reliable scaling on non-reasoning tasks; CoT-ICL (Dr.ICL, Auto-CoT), which studied chain-of-thought demonstrations in few-shot settings; and test-time scaling (Snell et al., 2025b), which frames inference-time computation as a form of adaptation. The key departure is twofold: it shows that the 'robustness with scale' and 'similarity retrieval works' findings from non-reasoning many-shot ICL do not transfer to reasoning tasks, and it reframes many-shot CoT-ICL as in-context test-time learning governed by pedagogical principles (ease of understanding, smooth knowledge progression) rather than as scaled pattern matching. The proposed CDS method repurposes TSP heuristics (nearest-neighbor + 2-opt) from combinatorial optimization into a demonstration-ordering tool, a novel application of that machinery to prompting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that similarity-based retrieval actively hurts reasoning performance (most-similar sets underperform most-dissimilar sets) challenges the dominant retrieval paradigm for demonstration selection and has direct practical implications for anyone deploying many-shot CoT-ICL with reasoning LLMs. The order-variance-grows-with-scale result means that naive random ordering becomes increasingly unreliable as context windows expand, motivating principled sequencing. Open questions include whether the curvature principle generalizes to non-mathematical reasoning domains, whether CDS scales beyond n=128, and whether the 'ease of understanding' principle can be operationalized as an automatic selection criterion rather than relying on self-generated demonstrations. The moderate CDS gains (~3.8% average) suggest the ordering signal is real but not yet the dominant bottleneck compared to demonstration quality and model capability.
