## Problem

Ensembles of neural networks outperform individual models but double inference cost and memory, while weight aggregation (e.g., OT Fusion) keeps a single model's footprint at the expense of accuracy. No existing method provides a continuous, principled interpolation between these two extremes, leaving practitioners with a binary choice between full ensembles and fully merged models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263355/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two (or more) trained feedforward networks of potentially different architectures and layer widths, along with neuron feature vectors (activation vectors computed on a small data sample, or outgoing weight matrices) to define similarity. It produces a single fused network whose hidden layers have a three-part block structure—isolated neurons from model A, weight-interpolated neurons in model B's coordinate system, and isolated neurons from model B—with zero off-diagonal blocks frozen during any subsequent fine-tuning. For the generalized pruning variant, the input is a single network (or an ensemble) and the output is a compressed network where neurons are either deleted, kept isolated, or linearly combined via cluster assignments.

## Method

Partial fusion extends OT-based model merging by solving a partial optimal transport problem that matches only a (1−α) fraction of the mass between two layers' neuron distributions, leaving the remaining neurons isolated rather than force-matched. Matched neurons are weight-averaged using the standard interpolation formula, while isolated neurons retain their original weights, yielding a fused layer with (1+α)× the neurons of a single model. A fixed-point iteration across layers handles the mutual dependence of weight-based features on neighboring-layer alignments. The paper additionally reframes the problem as generalized pruning of an ensemble, where a clustering objective (solved via stochastic Ward's hierarchical clustering) determines which neurons to merge, delete, or keep isolated—offering more degrees of freedom than partial fusion's pairwise-matching inductive bias. The key insight is that neuron-level similarity differences (how unique a neuron's functional role is) are exploitable: isolating the least-similar neurons raises the average similarity of the fused subset, making the size–accuracy tradeoff more efficient than either pure aggregation or pure ensembling.

## Results

On split MNIST with MLPs, partial OT fusion with fine-tuning on 1% of data achieves 95.1–96.5% accuracy across α ∈ [0, 1], surpassing both individual models (93.8% and 87.8%). On split CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18, fine-tuning on 5% of data yields 85.3–91.8% accuracy, consistently exceeding both individual models (79.8% and 76.7%) for all α ≥ 0.2. A mere 20–40% increase in neurons per layer (α = 0.2–0.4) already recovers most of the gap between full OT fusion and the ensemble, demonstrating that the parameter/accuracy tradeoff is highly efficient. For single-model compression, generalized pruning via clustering outperforms both unstructured pruning and OT-fusion post-processing on both MLPs and CNNs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against OT Fusion (Singh & Jaggi, 2020), which corresponds to α = 0, partial OT fusion monotonically improves accuracy as α increases: on split MNIST MLPs, going from α = 0 to α = 0.4 (a 40% neuron increase) lifts fusion accuracy from 84.1% to 87.4%, and fine-tuned accuracy from 95.1% to 96.1%; on ResNet-18/CIFAR-10 the same range lifts fine-tuned accuracy from 85.3% to 90.0%. Against ZipIt! (Stoica et al., 2024), which is shown in Figure 15 as a particular clustering algorithm within the generalized pruning framework, the paper's stochastic Ward hierarchical clustering achieves comparable but slightly higher accuracy at λ = 0.5 on MNIST MLPs. Against Net2WiderNet (Chen et al., 2016) as a capacity-matched baseline (Table 3), partial fusion clearly wins: on ResNet-18/CIFAR-10, N2W plateaus at ~86.6% regardless of α while partial fusion reaches 91.7% at α = 1.0; on MNIST MLPs, N2W stays at ~94.5–95.0% while partial fusion reaches 96.5%. For single-model pruning (Section 3.4), generalized pruning via clustering outperforms both unstructured pruning and OT-fusion post-processing, with the advantage being most pronounced for MLPs where it can flexibly trade off neuron deletion versus linear combination.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends OT Fusion (Singh & Jaggi, 2020) by replacing full optimal transport couplings with partial couplings, so that only the most similar neurons are matched while the rest remain isolated, yielding a continuous interpolation between weight aggregation and ensembles. It incorporates the fixed-point iteration from Git Re-Basin (Ainsworth et al., 2023) to optimize neuron alignments globally across layers rather than independently per layer. A key conceptual departure is the dual perspective: partial fusion is shown to be a special case of 'generalized pruning' of an ensemble, where neurons may be linearly combined (not just deleted), unifying pruning, clustering, and model fusion under a single framework. The partial optimal transport formulation follows Figalli (2010), and the clustering-based generalized pruning uses hierarchical (Ward's) methods rather than standard K-means, motivated by the regime of many cluster centers relative to points.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practitioners with a principled, tunable knob (the parameter α) to navigate the cost-performance tradeoff between ensembles and weight aggregation, with experiments showing that modest increases in neuron count (20–40%) often yield disproportionate accuracy gains over full fusion. The generalized pruning perspective is a useful unification that extends naturally to single-model compression, where clustering-based pruning outperforms both standard pruning and OT Fusion post-processing. However, experiments are limited to small architectures (MLPs, VGG11, ResNet18) on MNIST and CIFAR-10, and the authors explicitly flag scaling to larger models, richer similarity notions (e.g., CCA-based), and principled layer-dependent treatment as open questions. The finding that partial fusion's inductive bias (combining at most two neurons from different networks) benefits CNNs more than MLPs, while clustering generalizes better for MLPs, suggests the right method may depend on architecture in ways not yet fully understood.
