## Problem

Existing grid-based diffusion PDE solvers (e.g., DiffusionPDE) apply PDE-residual guidance only near the end of the reverse diffusion process because Gaussian noise in physical space converges to spatial white noise, making differential operators ill-defined at high noise levels. This paper asks whether a spectral-space formulation can preserve function regularity throughout the entire diffusion process, enabling physics-informed constraints to be enforced at every step and yielding lower PDE residuals and faster inference for forward, inverse, and joint PDE reconstruction under sparse observations.

## Input / Output

The method consumes sparse pointwise observations of the PDE solution u, the PDE coefficient a, or both (e.g., 500 or 1000 points on a 128×128 grid), and for Navier-Stokes, sparse-in-time vorticity snapshots at selected time steps. It outputs the full reconstructed solution field, coefficient field, or the complete 10-step spatio-temporal vorticity trajectory. All operations occur in a truncated spectral latent space (44×44 sine modes for Poisson/Helmholtz, 32×32 Fourier modes per time step for Navier-Stokes), reducing the effective dimensionality from 128×128 and enabling a 3–15× inference speedup over grid-based baselines.

## Method

PISD represents functions via data-scaled spectral coefficients: each Fourier or sine mode is divided by its empirical standard deviation from the training set, so that unit-variance Gaussian noise in the latent space maps to a random function with the same Sobolev regularity as the data (Lemma 3.2). A Vision Transformer denoiser (2M parameters) is trained in EDM style on these scaled coefficients, and at inference the reverse-time ODE is discretized with DPS-style guidance terms for the PDE residual and measurement mismatch. The key distinction from standard DPS is replacing plain gradient-descent updates at each diffusion step with a frequency-aware Adam optimizer that applies larger effective learning rates to low-frequency modes and smaller rates to high-frequency modes, yielding substantially lower PDE residuals and more stable convergence, especially for inverse problems. The spectral formulation also allows PDE operators (Laplacian, advection) to be evaluated exactly in coefficient space rather than via finite differences, further improving residual accuracy.

## Results

On the forward Poisson problem with 500 sparse observations, PISD achieves 3.08±1.71% relative error with a PDE residual of 0.87, compared to 4.06±1.51% (residual 237.49) for DiffusionPDE and 2.23±1.50% (residual 4619.34) for FunDPS. On the inverse Poisson problem (500 obs), PISD achieves 13.81±3.11% relative error, substantially outperforming DiffusionPDE (22.17±6.61%) and FunDPS (21.09±7.10%). Inference time is 52 s per solution on an RTX 2080 Ti, a ~15× speedup over DiffusionPDE (802 s) and ~3× over FunDPS (153 s), enabled by reducing the latent dimension from 128×128 grid points to 44×44 spectral modes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DiffusionPDE, PISD achieves substantially lower relative error on the inverse problem (13.81% vs 22.17% for Poisson with 500 obs; 12.76% vs 19.33% for Helmholtz) and dramatically lower PDE residuals (0.87 vs 237.49 for forward Poisson; 0.45 vs 178.46 for inverse Poisson), reflecting the ability to enforce PDE constraints throughout the entire diffusion process rather than only in the final 10% of reverse steps. Against FunDPS, PISD wins decisively on the inverse problem (13.81% vs 21.09% for Poisson) and on PDE residuals (0.87 vs 4619.34), but FunDPS achieves slightly lower relative error on the forward Poisson problem (2.23% vs 3.08% at 500 obs). On the Helmholtz forward problem, PISD outperforms both baselines in relative error (3.47% vs 9.55% and 2.08%). In all settings, PISD is 3–15× faster at inference due to the reduced spectral latent dimensionality (44×44 modes vs 128×128 grid). The PDE residual advantage is the most consistent and largest gap across all problem types, suggesting PISD's remaining error is dominated by the inherent ill-posedness of sparse-observation problems rather than PDE inconsistency.

## Relation to Prior Work

PISD sits at the intersection of latent diffusion models (Rombach et al., 2022; Karras et al., 2022), diffusion posterior sampling for constraint enforcement (Chung et al., 2023a), and physics-informed PDE solvers (DiffusionPDE, Huang et al., 2024; FunDPS, Yao et al., 2025). The key departure from prior physics-informed diffusion methods is the move from grid-based physical-space diffusion to a data-scaled spectral latent space, where Gaussian noise corresponds to functions with controlled Sobolev regularity rather than converging to spatial white noise. This spectral formulation, combined with replacing GD by a frequency-aware Adam optimizer in the DPS guidance loop, is the paper's central methodological contribution. The theoretical guarantee (Lemma 3.2) that the forward diffusion process preserves Sobolev regularity distinguishes PISD from earlier spectral diffusion work (Phillips et al., 2022) that lacked data-dependent scaling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ability to enforce PDE constraints throughout the entire reverse diffusion process (rather than only in the final 10% as required by grid-based methods) yields PDE residuals that are orders of magnitude lower, suggesting the remaining reconstruction error is dominated by the inherent ill-posedness of sparse-observation problems rather than by PDE inconsistency. The 3–15× inference speedup from spectral dimensionality reduction makes full-trajectory generation (e.g., all 10 Navier-Stokes time steps) feasible where baselines can only produce initial and terminal states. Key limitations include the requirement for hand-crafted spectral encodings suited to regular domains with periodic or homogeneous Dirichlet/Neumann boundary conditions, the need for a training dataset of PDE solutions, and the restriction to problems where differential operators are naturally expressed in the spectral basis. Extending to irregular geometries, learned encoders, and low-data regimes are identified as important open directions.
