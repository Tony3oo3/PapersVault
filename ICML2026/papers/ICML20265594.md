## Problem

Existing approaches for building LLM workflows rely on human-crafted pipelines and prompts, creating a substantial bottleneck for scaling across tasks and domains. While recent prompt optimization methods can improve individual components, they assume the overall workflow structure is fixed. This paper asks how to automatically induce both the workflow structure and the individual LLM call specifications in a fully data-driven way.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265594/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a batch of input-output pairs (x, y) from a task distribution as training signal, where x is a task question or problem and y is the ground-truth answer. At inference time, the induced workflow processes a single query through a chain of LLM calls (some augmented with retrieval tools like Wikipedia search) and produces a final answer text. The workflow itself is parameterized entirely in text space: each step is a high-level description paired with a detailed prompt specifying instructions and tool access, rather than being represented as executable code or a program graph.

## Method

FLOWBOT formulates workflow induction as a bilevel optimization problem with two nested loops, both optimized via textual gradients (natural-language feedback generated by an LLM). The inner loop treats the workflow as a stack of modular layers and backpropagates textual gradients layer-by-layer from the loss backward, updating each step's prompt while conditioning on downstream errors—directly analogous to gradient-based parameter optimization within a fixed architecture. The outer loop uses textual gradients to refine the workflow sketch itself, adding, removing, or reordering LLM calls—mirroring architecture-level search in neural architecture search. Initialization is obtained by running a single gradient step on a trivial workflow, and the full procedure requires only one epoch of training with batch size 5 and two bilevel optimization loops per batch. Ablations confirm that both the bilevel formulation and layer-wise (rather than one-shot) gradient backpropagation are essential for performance.

## Results

On the six AFlow benchmarks (HotpotQA2, DROP, HumanEval, MBPP, GSM8K, MATH), FLOWBOT achieves an average of 84.80, outperforming the reimplemented AFlow baseline (80.45) by 4.35 points. On the four GEPA benchmarks (HotpotQA1, IFBench, HoVer, PUPA), FLOWBOT scores 69.80 on average versus GEPA's 66.97 and GEPA+Merge's 68.69, with the largest gains on retrieval/verification-heavy tasks (HotpotQA1: 72.80 vs. 69.00; HoVer: 63.20 vs. 51.67). FLOWBOT also requires substantially fewer API calls than AFlow (54.7K vs. 338K total) at lower cost ($47.67 vs. $130.95).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the main prompt-optimization baseline GEPA (which starts from hand-crafted workflows), FLOWBOT wins on average (69.80 vs. 66.97) and on retrieval-heavy tasks—HotpotQA1 (72.80 vs. 69.00) and HoVer (63.20 vs. 51.67)—but trails on PUPA (90.67 vs. 94.47) and is essentially tied on IFBench (52.51 vs. 52.72). Against the main workflow-generation baseline AFlow (reimplemented with the same GPT-4.1 mini model), FLOWBOT wins on 4 of 6 benchmarks: HotpotQA2 (80.19 vs. 75.41), DROP (92.28 vs. 79.69), GSM8K (96.01 vs. 86.16), and MATH (72.71 vs. 72.01), while narrowly losing on HumanEval (93.74 vs. 94.65) and MBPP (73.84 vs. 74.78). FLOWBOT also outperforms all prompting-only baselines (CoT, Self-Consistency, MedPrompt, MultiPersona, Self-Refine) by a wide margin on the AFlow benchmarks, with the largest gap on MATH (72.71 vs. 50.40 best prompting baseline). Against ADAS, FLOWBOT dominates across all six benchmarks (e.g., MATH: 72.71 vs. 35.40). Notably, FLOWBOT achieves these results while discovering workflows from scratch, whereas GEPA and its variants begin from human-designed pipeline structures.

## Relation to Prior Work

FLOWBOT sits at the intersection of two research threads: textual gradient optimization (TextGrad, ProTeGi) for updating prompts via natural-language feedback, and neural architecture search (NAS) for jointly optimizing structure and parameters. Prior prompt optimization methods (GEPA, MIPROv2, Trace) assume a fixed workflow topology and only tune individual prompts, while prior workflow generation methods (AFlow, ADAS) search over structures in program space but lack modular, layer-wise component optimization. FLOWBOT's key departure is formulating workflow induction as a bilevel problem—outer loop edits the workflow sketch, inner loop backpropagates textual gradients layer-by-layer—thereby coupling architecture-level search with parameter-level optimization in a single framework, directly importing the NAS analogy into the LLM systems setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a fully automated, data-driven pipeline can induce both workflow structure and component prompts from scratch, removing the human-engineering bottleneck that currently limits deployment of compound AI systems. The bilevel formulation with layer-wise textual backpropagation provides a clean, interpretable mechanism for diagnosing and fixing errors at specific workflow stages, as illustrated by the HotpotQA hallucination case. Key limitations include rapid convergence (one epoch suffices, suggesting the search space is small or the optimization landscape is easy), restriction to feedforward chains (no complex control flow beyond simple loops/conditionals), and nontrivial optimization-time compute. Open questions include whether more flexible learning mechanisms can better leverage data, how the approach scales to deeper or more branching workflows, and whether the unified multi-task workflow extension can close the gap with task-specific designs.
