## Problem

Test-time scaling methods like majority vote and iterative refinement depend critically on how errors are distributed across samples, not just on raw accuracy. When model errors concentrate on a small set of shared mistakes, majority vote selects the dominant error and refinement trajectories collapse onto it. Prior work treats the error distribution as fixed and optimizes correctness alone, leaving this concentration unaddressed.

## Input / Output

For distributional reflection, the model receives the task prompt plus a compact context summarizing the empirical answer distribution from previous samples (counts per option and one representative full response per answer choice). For anti-votes, each sampled response additionally includes a predicted most-common-mistake label. The final output is an aggregated answer via reweighted majority vote (S_j = p̃_j − η q̃_j) or a refined response conditioned on the distribution, with the diverse-failure reward applied only when the model's answer is incorrect and differs from the most frequent incorrect answer in the reference set.

## Method

D-FUSEr combines two complementary mechanisms under a distributional reflection paradigm where self-refinement is conditioned on the full empirical answer distribution rather than a single prior response. The diverse failure reward r_D-FUSEr = 1{Z ∉ {y, Z_err}} · 1{Z_err defined} incentivizes the model to be wrong in ways that differ from the most common mistake, and the paper proves (Theorem 4.6) that for m=3, optimizing this reward is equivalent to optimizing expected majority-vote accuracy at fixed single-sample accuracy. Anti-votes provide an inference-time complement: the model predicts the dominant error alongside its solution, and a reweighted score S_j = p̃_j − η q̃_j down-weights concentrated mistakes during aggregation, with a theoretically derived optimal η. Training uses Dr. GRPO with the composite reward (correctness + ½·diverse-failure), and the framework is validated across Qwen-2.5, Llama-3.1, and Gemma-2 models on four reasoning benchmarks.

## Results

D-FUSEr achieves the best majority-vote accuracy across all three model families and four OOD benchmarks. On Qwen-2.5-14B, D-FUSEr reaches 91.9% on BBH, 48.9% on GPQA, 73.5% on MMLU-Pro, and 65.6% on RBENCH, outperforming the strongest baseline (Dist-Reflect) by 3.7, 0.3, 3.4, and 4.7 points respectively. On Llama-3.1-8B, D-FUSEr gains up to 14.4 points over GRPO on RBENCH (54.9% vs 46.0%), and on Gemma-2-2B it improves BBH by 4.3 points over Self-Reflect (51.2% vs 46.8%). D-FUSEr also improves average single-sample accuracy (Table 2), confirming that error-distribution shaping does not sacrifice correctness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

D-FUSEr consistently outperforms all three external baselines across model families. Against Self-Reflect (Madaan et al., 2023), D-FUSEr gains 2.7 points on BBH and 6.7 on RBENCH for Qwen-14B, and 4.4 points on BBH for Gemma-2B. Against GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), which optimizes accuracy only, D-FUSEr achieves substantially larger margins on harder benchmarks: +13.9 on RBENCH and +9.1 on GPQA for Llama-8B, confirming that accuracy-only RL training worsens error concentration. Against Reflect-GRPO (Li et al., 2025), D-FUSEr wins on all four OOD benchmarks for Llama-8B (e.g., 45.8 vs 38.2 on GPQA) and Gemma-2B (e.g., 37.7 vs 31.9 on GPQA). D-FUSEr also beats its own ablation baseline Dist-Reflect (distributional reflection trained with correctness-only reward) on most settings, with the largest gap on RBENCH (+4.7 for Qwen-14B, +17.0 for Llama-8B), isolating the contribution of the diverse failure reward. Notably, standard RL training (GRPO, Reflect-GRPO) often degrades majority-vote accuracy relative to the untrained model, while D-FUSEr consistently improves it.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the self-reflection paradigm (Madaan et al., 2023) by introducing distributional reflection, which conditions refinement on the empirical distribution of sampled answers rather than a single prior response. It departs from prior inference-time scaling work (majority vote, self-consistency, iterative refinement) by arguing that the *shape* of the error distribution—not just single-sample accuracy—determines aggregation quality, and proposes a theoretically grounded diverse failure reward to explicitly reshape that distribution during RL training (using GRPO). The anti-vote mechanism is a novel inference-time complement that has no direct analogue in prior aggregation literature, and the majorization-based theoretical framework connecting error entropy to majority-vote accuracy is new.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core insight—that concentrated errors cause majority vote and iterative refinement to fail in ways that accuracy-only training cannot fix—reframes how practitioners should think about test-time scaling and provides a principled training signal (diverse failure reward) with clean theoretical guarantees. Limitations include restriction to discrete answer spaces with well-defined extraction, the m=3 assumption in key theorems (though argued to extend approximately), and the need for a sufficiently accurate anti-vote predictor that is left as future work. Open questions include whether the framework extends to open-ended generation (via clustering or LLM-based categorization), whether dedicated anti-vote predictors can be trained effectively, and whether the monotone-updating assumption holds more broadly across model families and refinement strategies.
