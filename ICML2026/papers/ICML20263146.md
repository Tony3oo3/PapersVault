## Problem

Existing membership inference attacks (MIAs) treat multimodal federated learning (MFL) parameters as a monolithic entity, overlooking the heterogeneous optimization dynamics across modalities. Modality imbalance—where a dominant modality converges faster and overfits more—introduces a new attack surface that makes MFL inherently more vulnerable to MIAs, yet this vulnerability and its exploitation remain largely unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263146/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The attacker consumes per-modality MIA scores (loss values, gradient norms, cosine similarities, and LiRA likelihood ratios) computed from intercepted FL model parameters of the target client, along with cross-modal performance gaps as discriminative features. The output is a single calibrated membership score per sample, from which a binary member/non-member decision is made via a threshold chosen on the FPR–TPR trade-off curve. The attack model itself is a lightweight MLP (Gating or Cross-Attention head) trained on a small constructed dataset of a few thousand samples, requiring under 10 seconds to train on a single GPU.

## Method

The paper first provides a theoretical analysis showing that the generalization bound for late-fusion multimodal models contains an additional covariance term between fusion weights and modality-specific losses, and that the empirical loss convergence rate is proportional to the fusion weight—proving that dominant modalities overfit faster and are more vulnerable to MIAs. Building on this, the proposed modality-aware attack framework consists of two modules: a weight allocation module (Gating or Cross-Attention) that maps cross-modal performance gaps to adaptive weights over modality-specific MIA scores, and a joint scoring module that fuses the weighted scores into a final calibrated membership prediction. The attack model is trained using the attacker's own FL local data as positive (member) samples and non-overlapping data from colluding clients as negative (non-member) samples, eliminating the need for auxiliary datasets. The key design insight is that samples with different modality preferences exhibit distinct MIA score distributions, so the model adaptively selects which modality's attack signal is most informative for each sample and recalibrates the score accordingly.

## Results

The proposed modality-aware MIA framework (best variant: ASL-CrossAtt) achieves AUC of 0.7804 on CREMA-D-6, 0.7736 on CREMA-D-10, 0.7544 on Balanced-20, and 0.7187 on AVE-6, consistently outperforming all baselines across every dataset and metric. At TPR@1%FPR, the best variant (BCE-CrossAtt) reaches 16.67% on CREMA-D-6 and 15.23% on Balanced-20, compared to the strongest baseline (Grad-Cosine on audio) at 12.90% and 12.01% respectively. The method also attains the highest Balanced Accuracy (71.04% on CREMA-D-6) and TPR@0.1%FPR (7.08% on CREMA-D-6) among all approaches tested.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against FedMIA (Zhu et al., 2025), the strongest baseline Cosine-LiRA achieves 0.7551 AUC on CREMA-D-6 (audio target), while the proposed ASL-CrossAtt reaches 0.7804 (+2.5 AUC) and BCE-CrossAtt achieves 16.67% TPR@1%FPR versus Cosine-LiRA's 11.47%. Loss-LiRA from the same paper is substantially weaker (0.6989 AUC), confirming that likelihood-based comparison alone is suboptimal in the multimodal setting. Against Li et al. (2023), Grad-Cosine (audio) reaches 0.7366 AUC on CREMA-D-6, outperformed by the proposed method by ~4.4 AUC; Grad-Diff and Grad-Norm from the same paper are near-random (≈0.54 AUC) in the multimodal setting. Against Yeom et al. (2018), Loss-Based MIA achieves only 0.6629 AUC on CREMA-D-6, a gap of over 11 AUC points. Against Nasr et al. (2018), Grad-Norm is essentially ineffective (0.5414 AUC), far below even the weakest proposed variant. The proposed method wins on every metric across all four datasets, with the largest margins observed at low FPR thresholds (TPR@0.1%FPR) where baselines often drop to 0–2%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the membership inference attack (MIA) literature from unimodal federated learning to the multimodal setting, building directly on the LiRA likelihood-based comparison framework from FedMIA (Zhu et al., CVPR 2025) and gradient-based attacks from Li et al. (ICLR 2023). Its key conceptual departure is repurposing the difficulty calibration paradigm (Sablayrolles et al., 2019) from 'learning difficulty' to 'modality preference,' using cross-modal performance gaps as the calibration signal rather than per-sample loss. The theoretical analysis draws on generalization bound techniques from multimodal learning (Zhang et al., 2023a; Cao et al., 2024) to show that modality imbalance—an established performance problem in MFL—induces an unavoidable overfitting gap that existing MIA methods fail to exploit because they treat all modality parameters as a monolithic entity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic study connecting modality imbalance (a well-known MFL performance issue) to a concrete privacy vulnerability, which reframes how practitioners should think about the security implications of multimodal federated deployments. The finding that modality-balancing methods (OGM, unimodal loss regularization)—designed purely for performance—also reduce MIA susceptibility suggests a practical, low-cost defense that avoids the utility degradation of differential privacy. Open questions include whether the results generalize beyond audio-visual late-fusion architectures, whether the collusion assumption can be relaxed, and whether the modality-aware calibration idea transfers to other federated privacy threats (e.g., model inversion, data reconstruction).
