## Problem

Conventional label distribution learning (LDL) loss functions such as KL divergence and MSE optimize global distributional proximity but are inherently inconsistent with divisiveness — the propensity of a label distribution to exhibit dissension between semantically opposing labels. This inconsistency means that minimizing standard LDL losses does not guarantee preservation of polarization structure, which is a critical risk indicator in decision-making scenarios like crowd opinion prediction and urban governance.

## Input / Output

The method takes a d-dimensional feature vector x (extracted from images, audio, or text depending on the dataset) and outputs an m-dimensional label distribution y in the probability simplex Δ^m. Supervision requires ground-truth label distributions (e.g., normalized crowdsourced voting proportions) plus user-defined polarity vectors ρ and η that encode which labels are semantically positive, negative, or neutral. The divisiveness setting is dataset-specific: ordinal datasets use a staircase scheme while categorical datasets partition labels by semantic polarity.

## Method

The paper formalizes a divisiveness measure ψ(y;ρ,η) = min{⟨ρ,y⟩, ⟨η,y⟩} and proves it satisfies an axiomatic property called polarity monotonicity, which no conventional LDL metric or alternative divisiveness definition preserves. It then proves a divisiveness error bound showing that small KL or L2 distances can still correspond to large divisiveness errors, establishing the necessity of a dedicated loss term. To avoid adversarial gradients from directly minimizing the absolute divisiveness error (which yields identical gradient directions for all semantically parallel labels), the authors propose a pairwise divisiveness loss that decomposes the error over all label pairs, constituting an unbiased second-order U-statistic estimator of the original divisiveness error. A smooth softmin approximation φ replaces the non-differentiable min operator, and the final training objective combines standard KL divergence with the pairwise divisiveness loss weighted by a trade-off hyperparameter α.

## Results

LDL-DVS achieves the best DVSE (divisiveness error) on 6 out of 7 datasets, with notable improvements on RAF-ML (0.0764 vs. 0.0829 for the next-best δ-LDL) and Music (0.0571 vs. 0.0573). It also attains the best KLD on 6/7 datasets, e.g., 0.1873 on RAF-ML and 0.1052 on Music, confirming that divisiveness preservation and global distributional proximity are complementary objectives. The only exception is the Painting dataset, where several baselines (AA-kNN, DF-LDL) achieve lower DVSE, attributed to their tendency to produce more uniform predictions that coincidentally reduce divisiveness error at the cost of overall accuracy (best μ metric across all methods is only 23.14%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LDL-DVS outperforms all nine baseline families on both DVSE and KLD across 6 of 7 datasets. The closest competitor is δ-LDL (Li et al., ICML 2025), which achieves the second-best DVSE on every dataset (e.g., 0.0829 vs. 0.0764 on RAF-ML; 0.0573 vs. 0.0571 on Music) and is the only method that also achieves competitive KLD without the instability seen in LDLSF. Among the earlier LDL methods, SA-BFGS, LDLLC, LCLR, DF-LDL, LRR, and DPA (Geng 2016; Jia 2018; Ren 2019; González 2021; Jia 2023) cluster tightly on KLD (~0.195 on RAF-ML) but are consistently 15–30% worse on DVSE, confirming the paper's central claim that global distributional proximity does not guarantee divisiveness preservation. RG4LDL (Tan et al., 2025) shows the largest DVSE gap on RAF-ML (0.1118 vs. 0.0764) but is competitive on Music (0.0674). The sole exception is the Painting dataset, where AA-kNN (0.1416) and DF-LDL (0.1445) achieve lower DVSE than LDL-DVS (0.1523), which the authors attribute to these methods producing more uniform predictions that coincidentally reduce divisiveness error while degrading overall accuracy (μ metric drops to ~17–20% vs. 23.14% for LDL-DVS). LDLSF (Ren et al., 2019a) exhibits severe KLD instability (e.g., 4.11 on Painting, 0.83 on RAF-ML) due to its label-specific feature design, making it unreliable as a baseline despite competitive DVSE on some datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operates within the well-established Label Distribution Learning (LDL) framework (Geng, 2016) and its rich lineage of methods capturing label correlations, specialized loss functions, and practical decision-making alignment. It departs from prior LDL work by identifying a previously unaddressed property—divisiveness, the dissension between semantically opposing labels—and formalizing it through an axiomatic property (polarity monotonicity) that no conventional LDL metric satisfies. The pairwise divisiveness loss, structured as a fully enumerated second-order U-statistic, draws on statistical estimation theory to resolve the adversarial gradient problem that arises from directly minimizing divisiveness error, a technique not previously applied in the LDL literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical demonstration that KL divergence and MSE are fundamentally inconsistent with divisiveness error provides a principled justification for why standard LDL training can produce predictions that are globally accurate yet decisionally misleading (e.g., a bell-shaped prediction for a U-shaped ground truth). The divisiveness measure and DVSE metric offer a new lens for evaluating risk-sensitive applications like crowd-sourced opinion prediction and urban governance. Key limitations include the requirement for semantically opposing labels (rendering the measure vacuous for non-polarized label spaces) and the weaker approximation quality of the pairwise surrogate for very small label cardinalities, both of which the authors flag as open problems for future work.
