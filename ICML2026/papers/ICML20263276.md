## Problem

Current physics-based motion imitation metrics (e.g., MPJPE) only quantify final tracking error, conflating two distinct failure sources: policy limitations versus the intrinsic learning difficulty of the target motion. Without a policy-agnostic measure of how hard a motion is to learn, researchers cannot reliably attribute errors, compare policies fairly, or identify which motions are fundamentally harder to imitate.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a 100-frame motion sequence (joint Euler angles, global positions, and their time derivatives) paired with an SMPL-based humanoid model (24 joints, standard mass distribution) and produces a single scalar TVS score per clip. Computation is performed via the CPU-based RBDL dynamics library, taking roughly 1–2 minutes per clip. Beyond the scalar score, the pipeline yields a difficulty-annotated AMASS dataset (30,000+ clips), a Maximum Imitable Difficulty (MID) threshold per policy, Difficulty-Stratified Joint Error (DSJE) values at fixed TVS cutoffs, and per-segment anomaly flags for flawed mocap detection.

## Method

TVS is derived from the rigid-body equation of motion M(q)q̈ + h(q,q̇) = τ + f_ext by measuring how much joint torques vary under small bounded pose perturbations, using the Jacobian dF of the torque mapping. The score aggregates three complementary terms: Spectral Diversity (log-volume of the induced torque space via singular values of the per-frame Jacobian), Variance Diversity (geometric mean of per-joint Jacobian variance capturing cross-joint spread), and Segment Diversity (temporal partitioning into K=4 segments whose equal-volume maximization reflects learning-facilitating structure). The key theoretical insight is that high-TV motions produce flat, Dirac-delta-like reward landscapes where action noise yields negligible state deviation, starving the policy of informative gradients—empirically confirmed by showing reward plateauing under perturbation for high-TVS motions. TVS is entirely policy-agnostic, characterizing the motion's optimization landscape rather than any controller's performance, and is validated by strong Spearman correlations (0.66–0.82) with MPJPE across UHC and PHC+ on 3,000–10,000+ AMASS clips.

## Results

TVS shows strong positive correlation with imitation error (MPJPE-G) across two state-of-the-art policies: Spearman r = 0.6586 (UHC) and r = 0.8203 (PHC+), both well above the 0.50 threshold for 'large/strong' effect size. The Maximum Imitable Difficulty (MID) threshold is 308.22 for UHC and 320.50 for PHC+, quantitatively confirming PHC+'s superior robustness boundary. Empirically, high-TVS motions (>350) exhibit reward plateauing (ΔReward ≈ 0 across perturbation levels), while low-TVS motions (<150) show steep, informative reward gradients (ΔReward up to −22.71), validating the flat-landscape hypothesis.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a competing imitation policy; instead, it introduces TVS as a policy-agnostic diagnostic metric and validates it against two existing methods (UHC and PHC+) as test subjects. TVS achieves stronger correlation with PHC+ (Spearman 0.8203) than UHC (0.6586), suggesting the metric captures difficulty more cleanly when the policy itself is less of a bottleneck. The ablation (Table 2) confirms all three TVS components are necessary: removing Spectral Diversity drops UHC Spearman from 0.6586 to 0.5847, and removing Variance Diversity drops PHC+ from 0.8203 to 0.7963. A notable diagnostic finding emerges from DSJE: PHC+ actually underperforms UHC on the easiest motions (DSJE-200: 30.38 vs. 27.79 mm) despite outperforming on moderate and high-difficulty subsets, a counter-intuitive result invisible under aggregate evaluation. The TVS-guided curriculum learning experiment (Appendix C.1) shows a 2.86 mm improvement in overall MPJPE-G (45.22 vs. 48.08) over random-sampling baseline, with the largest gains on easy motions (5.22 mm improvement at TVS<200).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new diagnostic ground within the well-established physics-based motion imitation literature (UHC, PHC+, DeepMimic, etc.) by introducing a policy-agnostic difficulty metric derived from rigid-body dynamics. Rather than extending any specific imitation method, it repurposes Jacobian and spectral analysis from Featherstone's rigid-body formulation to quantify how a motion's dynamical properties shape the RL optimization landscape. The 'flat reward landscape' / vanishing-gradient connection bridges dynamics and optimization theory in a way prior imitation work (which focused almost exclusively on similarity-to-reference rewards) did not explore. The curriculum learning application connects to Bengio et al. (2009) but is novel in using a physics-derived difficulty ordering rather than heuristic or loss-based scheduling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TVS provides the first principled mechanism to attribute imitation errors to policy limitations versus motion-inherent difficulty, enabling fairer comparisons and targeted improvements in humanoid control. The DSJE finding that PHC+ underperforms UHC on the easiest motions—despite superior overall performance—reveals a counter-pattern invisible to aggregate metrics, suggesting that difficulty-aware evaluation should become standard practice. Key limitations include CPU-bound computation (~1–2 min per 100-frame clip via RBDL) and idealized dynamics that ignore sensor noise and actuator saturation; GPU-accelerated solvers and incorporation of practical constraints are natural next steps. Open questions include whether TVS generalizes to non-SMPL humanoid morphologies and whether the difficulty ordering holds under different reward formulations or policy architectures beyond UHC/PHC+.
