## Problem

Existing AI-generated video detectors operate on short, independent clips under the assumption that each clip is entirely real or fake, making them unable to handle the realistic scenario where AI-generated content is sparsely embedded within otherwise authentic long-form videos. This paper formulates the task of Temporal AI-Generated Segment Localization and Explanation (TASLE), targeting authenticity detection, precise temporal localization, and interpretable forensic analysis of manipulated segments in untrimmed long videos.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261104/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a long untrimmed video (2–124 s, standardized to 832×480 at 15 FPS) and produces a set of manipulated segments, each annotated with start/end temporal boundaries and a natural-language forensic rationale. Rationales are structured at two granularities: boundary-level explanations describing inconsistencies at real-to-fake and fake-to-real transitions, and object/segment-level explanations identifying specific visual anomalies (e.g., unnatural motion, inconsistent occlusion) within the manipulated content. The proposal stage consumes the video via non-overlapping 2-second sliding windows of 8 uniformly sampled frames, while the refinement stage adaptively samples dense frames in boundary regions and sparse frames in event interiors.

## Method

MSLoc is a coarse-to-fine two-stage framework. The first stage (MSLoc-PG) adapts the DeMamba Mamba backbone with a sliding-window strategy and reformulates detection as a four-class boundary-aware classification (real, fake, real-to-fake transition, fake-to-real transition) rather than binary real/fake, enabling the model to capture subtle temporal discrepancies at manipulation boundaries and generate coarse proposals. The second stage (MSLoc-PR) refines these proposals using a Trace-based MLLM with three key mechanisms: (1) adaptive sampling that densely samples boundary regions (2×N_b frames) and sparsely samples event interiors (8 frames); (2) a Difference-Aware Modeling module using a Q-Former to compress boundary tokens and derive inter-frame variant/invariant representations that preserve discriminative transition cues; and (3) an Anomaly-Aware Loss with dedicated tokens that predict anomaly categories to stimulate the model's perception of manipulation cues. The two stages are trained sequentially under supervised learning: MSLoc-PG with cross-entropy on the four-class labels, and MSLoc-PR fine-tuned for 2 epochs (batch 16, lr 3×10⁻⁵) on the TASLE dataset.

## Results

On the TASLE test set (seen AIGC types), the full MSLoc framework achieves 70.1 F1Det, 72.2 F1Loc, and 4.05 Rationale Quality (RQ), substantially outperforming the end-to-end Trace baseline (42.0 F1Det, 41.4 F1Loc, 3.32 RQ) and the binary-classification DeMamba detector (54.9 F1Det, 54.0 F1Loc). On unseen AIGC types, MSLoc reaches 67.0 F1Det and 62.8 F1Loc, and on out-of-domain mixed data it achieves 55.0 F1Det and 56.3 F1Loc. The MLLM-based refinement stage (MSLoc-PR) contributes +7.8 F1Det and +17.6 F1Loc over the proposal generator alone on out-of-domain data, indicating that semantic reasoning generalizes better than the Mamba-based detector.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MSLoc-PG (the boundary-aware proposal generator) outperforms the binary-classification DeMamba* by +12.6 F1Det and +10.8 F1Loc on seen data, demonstrating that the four-class boundary formulation (real, fake, real-to-fake, fake-to-real) captures transition cues that binary detection misses. The full MSLoc framework surpasses the end-to-end Trace* localization model by +28.1 F1Det and +30.8 F1Loc on seen data, with the gap widening to +23.2 F1Det on out-of-domain mixed data, confirming that the coarse-to-fine design effectively filters irrelevant authentic content before MLLM reasoning. Among detection-only baselines, D3 (training-free, F1Det 34.6) and fine-tuned BusterX++ (F1Det 33.6) and Qwen3-VL-8B (F1Det 27.6) all fall well below MSLoc-PG, highlighting the difficulty of transferring short-clip detectors to long-video mixed contexts. Notably, feeding MSLoc-PG proposals into Trace* lifts its F1Det from 42.0 to 69.0, showing that the bottleneck for MLLM-based localization is interference from irrelevant segments rather than model capacity. On rationale quality, MSLoc achieves 4.05 RQ versus 3.32 for Trace*, indicating that boundary-focused attention and anomaly-aware tokens improve explanation fidelity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines: (1) AI-generated video detection, which has progressed from Mamba/Transformer-based short-clip classifiers (DeMamba, D3) to MLLM-powered explainable detectors (BusterX, FakeShield), and (2) MLLM-based temporal video grounding (TimeChat, Trace, VTimeCoT), which localizes semantic events in long videos. The key departure is reframing detection from a binary real/fake clip-level problem to a four-class boundary-aware classification (real, fake, real-to-fake, fake-to-real) applied via sliding windows, and then coupling a lightweight Mamba proposal generator with a Trace-based MLLM refinement stage that jointly performs precise boundary localization and rationale generation. The dataset construction repurposes existing controllable generation tools (Wan2.1, Kling, VACE) and SAM2 segmentation to create realistic sparse manipulations within long untrimmed videos, a setting absent from prior benchmarks like GenVideo or GenVidBench.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TASLE is the first benchmark to combine long-video sparse segment localization with segment-level and boundary-level explainability annotations, filling a clear gap between short-clip detection benchmarks and the realistic scenario where AI-generated content is embedded in otherwise authentic footage. The cascaded coarse-to-fine design is a practical recipe for long-video forensics, but its recall is fundamentally bounded by the first-stage proposal generator, and the entire approach relies on visible visual artifacts that will diminish as generation quality improves. Open questions include whether end-to-end joint training can eliminate error propagation, whether physics-aware or audio-visual cues can detect the increasingly subtle forgeries from next-generation models, and whether the boundary-aware classification formulation generalizes beyond the specific manipulation types (FLF2V, TI2V, MV2V) used here.
