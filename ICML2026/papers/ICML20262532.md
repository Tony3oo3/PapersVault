## Problem

Multi-agent LLM systems are increasingly deployed for collaborative decision-making, yet no existing evaluation cleanly isolates failures in collective information integration from limitations in individual reasoning. Without controlled information distribution and verifiable ground truth, it is impossible to determine whether poor group performance stems from weak individual inference or from a breakdown in coordinating distributed knowledge. This paper addresses that gap by adapting the Hidden Profile paradigm from social psychology into a reproducible benchmark for LLM agents.

## Input / Output

Each agent receives a natural-language scenario with a set of decision options (≥3), a pool of shared facts available to all agents, and exactly one unique unshared fact; agents are explicitly not told their information differs from others'. The output is a post-discussion decision (option label + rationale in JSON) aggregated via an average rule (proportion correct) or majority rule, reported as accuracy and as the gap Y^post − Y^full relative to the Full Profile ceiling. The benchmark comprises 65 tasks across diverse domains (healthcare, organizational planning, cultural preservation), each validated to require ≥80% Full Profile accuracy and ≤20% Hidden Profile pre-discussion accuracy, ensuring success is only achievable through information pooling.

## Method

HIDDENBENCH is constructed through three sources: 3 manually designed tasks, 5 adapted from prior Hidden Profile studies, and 57 auto-generated via a GPT-4.1 pipeline that produces candidate tasks, executes them under both Full and Hidden Profile conditions (10 sessions each), and retains only those meeting validation thresholds. The core design ensures shared information systematically favors a decoy option while the union of unshared facts uniquely identifies the correct answer, and no single agent's local information suffices. The paper evaluates 15 frontier LLMs (GPT, Gemini, Qwen, Llama families) under controlled multi-agent communication, then runs targeted ablations—varying communication depth, prompting strategy, group size, and adversarial composition—to isolate the failure mode. The key diagnostic finding is that forced full disclosure (Reveal-All) nearly eliminates the accuracy gap, proving the bottleneck is information surfacing rather than integration. A lightweight two-stage Exchange-then-Decide protocol (agents share 1–2 facts and one counter-argument, then summarize and vote) substantially closes the gap across model families without revealing the asymmetry.

## Results

Across 15 frontier LLMs on HIDDENBENCH (65 tasks), multi-agent systems achieve only 30.1% post-discussion accuracy under the Hidden Profile (distributed information) condition, compared to 80.7% for single agents under the Full Profile (complete information) condition. Pre-discussion accuracy under Hidden Profile ranges from 0.082 to 0.217, confirming no agent can solve tasks from local information alone, while Full Profile pre-discussion accuracy ranges from 0.435 to 0.981. The best-performing model, Gemini-2.5-Pro, reaches 0.671 post-discussion accuracy but still lags its own Full Profile performance by 0.310; the weakest model (GPT-5 Minimal Reasoning) shows a gap of −0.750. A lightweight structured communication protocol (Exchange-then-Decide) improves accuracy from 0.037→0.800 (GPT-4.1), 0.173→0.727 (Gemini-2.5-Flash), and 0.043→0.743 (Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite) on 18 tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against prior multi-agent benchmarks or methods; instead, it benchmarks 15 frontier LLMs (GPT-4.1, GPT-4.1-Mini, GPT-5 variants, Gemini-2.5-Flash/Pro/Flash-Lite, Qwen, Llama) against two internal reference conditions: the Full Profile upper bound (all information given to each agent) and the Hidden Profile pre-discussion lower bound. The Gemini family consistently outperforms other families in collective settings—Gemini-2.5-Pro achieves the highest post-discussion accuracy (0.671) and smallest gap to Full Profile (−0.310), while Gemini-2.5-Flash (0.550) and Flash-Lite (0.394) also lead. In contrast, GPT-5 variants, despite stronger individual reasoning under Full Profile, fail to substantially outperform smaller models like GPT-4.1-Mini in multi-agent settings, and GPT-5-Nano shows no improvement from communication (−0.004). Neither model scale nor individual reasoning accuracy reliably predicts collective performance, indicating the failure is structural (latent information asymmetry) rather than a matter of raw capability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the Hidden Profile paradigm from social psychology (Stasser & Titus, 1985; Schulz-Hardt & Mojzisch, 2012)—a canonical diagnostic for group information-sharing failures—into a controlled, reproducible benchmark for multi-agent LLM systems. While prior multi-agent LLM evaluations (CAMEL, MetaGPT, AutoGen, AgentVerse) assess task completion or coordination quality, they conflate individual reasoning ability with collective information integration; HIDDENBENCH isolates the latter by construction, ensuring each task is individually solvable under full information. The paper also draws on the broader literature on collective reasoning failures in human groups (shared information bias, groupthink, conformity) to frame the specific failure mode it diagnoses: agents converge on shared evidence without actively probing for unobserved knowledge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that multi-agent LLMs achieve only 30.1% accuracy under distributed information versus 80.7% with full information—and that this gap persists across 15 frontier models, prompting strategies, and communication depths—provides a clear, theory-grounded diagnosis of a fundamental coordination bottleneck. The counterintuitive result that adding more agents worsens performance (improvement drops from +0.348 at N=3 to +0.006 at N=7) challenges the common assumption that scaling agent count pools more useful information. The demonstration that a lightweight structured communication protocol (Exchange-then-Decide) recovers most of the gap (e.g., 0.037→0.800 for GPT-4.1) suggests the bottleneck is in communication structure rather than model capability, pointing toward protocol design as a practical lever. Open questions include whether the protocol generalizes to open-ended domains, whether training objectives can internalize epistemic exploration, and whether the 65-task benchmark captures the full diversity of real-world information asymmetry.
