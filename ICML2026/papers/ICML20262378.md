## Problem

In the learnware paradigm, a released model is paired with a public specification (e.g., RKME) that enables model identification without raw-data exchange. Prior privacy analyses either studied the specification in isolation or focused on model-side leakage, leaving the incremental risk introduced by attaching the specification to an already-released model unaddressed. This paper asks: how much additional inference power does the specification contribute beyond the model-only observation, and can this be controlled for a concrete specification mechanism?

## Input / Output

The framework takes a released model f, its RKME specification (a weighted reduced set of m atoms in the feature space), the underlying training dataset D of size n, and a bounded twice-continuously-differentiable kernel k with k(x,x)≤κ². It produces a chain of theoretical certificates: an exact Bayes-value characterization of the incremental risk, a conditional influence upper bound, an RKHS-smoothed total-variation bridge, and a record-level stability bound of the form C_stab(m)/n. The final design output is a principled trade-off: choosing m=√n for Gaussian RKME yields record-level risk O(m^{-ξ}) while preserving identification accuracy at rate O(m^{-α}).

## Method

The paper builds a four-layer analytical chain. First, learnware-inversion is formalized as a family of statistical decision games where the specification risk is the incremental Bayes value from the model-only observation O₀=(f,ν) to the full-learnware observation O₁=(f,R_f,ν). Second, a conditional influence certificate (Theorem 3.4) upper-bounds this risk by the conditional total-variation distinguishability of the specification after the model-only view is fixed, separating model-side and specification-side contributions. Third, an RKHS-smoothed total-variation bridge (Theorem 4.5) converts this distinguishability into RKHS-norm perturbations of the released mean element, restricted to the kernel-score interface through which RKME is actually used in learnware search. Fourth, RKME is analyzed as a local metric projection of the empirical kernel mean embedding onto the reduced-set family in RKHS; a curvature-residual margin χ_D = s_D² − ρ_D M_D certifies regularity, and the implicit function theorem yields a projection stability factor C_stab(D,m) = ‖DΦ_m‖²_op / χ_D that controls how much one training record can perturb the released specification. Under polynomial nondegeneracy of the tangent Gram matrix, C_stab(m) grows subquadratically, so the record-level risk at m=√n vanishes as O(m^{-ξ}).

## Results

The paper establishes that the incremental specification risk for RKME under RKHS-compatible attacks is bounded by Δ_G^{H,Λ} ≤ C_G · Λκ · C_stab(m)/n + o(n^{-1}), where C_stab(m) is the local projection-stability factor of the RKME reduced-set generator. For Gaussian RKME with polynomially nondegenerate solutions, C_stab(m) ≤ C_0 · m^{2−ξ} for some ξ > 0, yielding a record-level risk of O(m_*^{−ξ}) at the square-root size m_* = ⌊√n⌋. Simultaneously, the RKME approximation error (governing learnware identification utility) is O(m^{−α}) + O_p(m^{−1}), so the square-root size balances identification accuracy against privacy risk. In the well-conditioned case (uniformly nondegenerate tangent Gram matrix), the incremental risk reduces to O(n^{−1/2}).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or comparison tables. The framework is positioned relative to prior privacy analyses in three ways: (1) Unlike differential privacy (Dwork, 2006), which requires randomized noise injection and provides worst-case guarantees, this paper analyzes the deterministic RKME specification through a model-aware incremental lens, measuring only the additional risk beyond the already-released model. (2) Unlike Lei et al. (2024), which studied RKME privacy in the specification-alone setting (adversary sees only R_f), this paper formalizes the full-learnware setting where the adversary observes both the model f and the specification R_f, and isolates the specification's marginal contribution via the conditional influence certificate. (3) Unlike classical membership inference or model inversion analyses (Shokri et al., 2017; Fredrikson et al., 2014), which treat the released model as the sole leakage surface, this paper decomposes risk into a model-side baseline O_0 = (f, ν) and a specification-side increment, showing that the specification's contribution is governed by RKME projection stability rather than model overfitting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the privacy analysis of RKME specifications from Lei et al. (2024), which studied the specification in isolation, to the full-learnware setting where the adversary observes both the released model and its specification simultaneously. The key conceptual departure is framing the privacy question as an *incremental* one: rather than asking what the specification reveals in isolation, the paper asks how much additional Bayes value the specification contributes beyond the model-only baseline. The analytical toolkit draws on classical statistical decision theory (Blackwell's comparison of experiments, Bayes decision values) and RKHS projection geometry (implicit function theorem, influence functions), neither of which had been applied to learnware specification privacy before. The RKME reduced-set structure from Wu et al. (2023) is treated as a local metric projection in RKHS, enabling curvature-based stability certificates that were not available in prior specification-only analyses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical framework that separates model-side leakage from specification-side risk in the learnware paradigm, providing a principled design rule (square-root specification size m = √n) that balances identification utility against incremental privacy risk. The main limitation is that all guarantees are for RKHS-compatible attack interfaces and local regularity conditions; the bounds control record-level incremental risk, not total leakage, and no empirical validation is provided. Open questions include whether the framework extends to adaptive multi-query attacks, white-box model interfaces, and specification mechanisms beyond RKME (e.g., PAVE-style parameter-vector specifications). For the learnware community, this establishes the theoretical vocabulary and the key quantity (conditional influence) that future specification designs should target.
