## Problem

Existing LLM schedulers approximate SJF/SRPT using predicted decode lengths or rank, optimizing mean-centric metrics (TTFT/TBT) while offering limited control over tail latency (P90–P99) that dominates user experience. These prediction-driven policies are fragile under distribution shifts, bursty arrivals, and GPU memory pressure, and even with perfect length knowledge they can be arbitrarily bad for tail sojourn time under light-tailed or capped workloads. The paper asks whether schedulers need explicit length predictions at all to optimize tail latency in online LLM serving.

## Input / Output

The scheduler consumes each request's known prefill token count and arrival time, plus lightweight runtime signals: the number of decoded tokens produced so far (attained service) and a rolling window of observed TTFT/TTLT/TBT quantiles. It does not require any predicted output length or rank. Outputs are (i) a per-microbatch composition and priority ordering across both prefill and decode phases, (ii) preemption/eviction decisions gated by KV-cache capacity and a geometric quantization threshold, and (iii) a self-tuned γ parameter updated via EMA from the empirical P95–P99 tail slope, which continuously reshapes the fairness–responsiveness tradeoff without operator intervention.

## Method

UNIBOOST is a four-phase, prediction-free scheduling framework built on the γ-Boost priority function b_γ(w) = (1/γ)·log(1/(1−e^{−γw})), where each request's priority is its arrival time minus a boost computed from attained service. Phase 1 (DISTBOOST) treats prefill and decode as separate queues; Phase 2 (UNIBOOST-BASE) unifies them into a single priority space with a hysteresis threshold δ to prevent micro-preemption. Phase 3 (MEMGUARD) discretizes priority updates via geometric quantization (bins at k·2^n), bounding each request to at most 1+⌊log₂(S/k)⌋ preemption opportunities and amortizing KV-cache swap costs. Phase 4 (γ-Ada) estimates γ online by fitting a log-linear slope over the [P95, P99] latency band, adapting the boost strength to the current tail shape. The co-design of soft priority shaping with cache-aware eviction is the key differentiator: it interpolates smoothly between FCFS (protecting long jobs from starvation) and SJF (reducing HoL blocking) while respecting the memory-coupled decode dynamics that make naive preemption expensive in LLM serving.

## Results

On a 70% reasoning + 30% Azure trace workload with Llama-3-8B, UniBoost achieves P99 TTLT 35.1% lower and P99 TTFT 34.0% lower than SRPT with perfect decode-length prediction (TRAIL+), while also improving P95 TTFT by 97.4% and P99 TBT by 33.8%, all without sacrificing throughput (+1.2% tok/s). Across workloads (reasoning-heavy, chat-heavy, mixed), the paper reports P99 TTLT reductions of 35–50% and TTFT reductions of 34–47% relative to the oracle SRPT baseline. Under bursty load, UniBoost shifts the load-latency knee right by 4–6% and reduces P90/P99 latency by up to 10× near saturation compared to MLFQ+.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the oracle SRPT baseline (TRAIL+ with perfect decode-length prediction), UniBoost wins decisively on all tail metrics: P99 TTLT +35.1%, P99 TTFT +34.0%, P99 TBT +33.8%, and P95 TTFT +97.4%, while incurring a 19.1% mean E2E latency penalty. Against SJF (LTR+ with perfect prediction), UniBoost dominates on every tail metric (P99 TTLT +35.1% vs. +11.2%; P99 TTFT +34.0% vs. −9.7%) and throughput (+1.2% vs. −7.9%), showing that prediction-free boosting outperforms even clairvoyant size-based scheduling on tails. Against Sarathi (the vLLM/SGLang default with chunked-prefill and decode-priority), UniBoost improves P99 TTLT by 42.7 percentage points (from −7.6% to +35.1% relative to TRAIL+) and P99 TTFT by 42.1 points, while also recovering throughput (−10.9% → +1.2%). Against LAS/MLFQ+ (FastServe's skip-join), the ablation in Fig. 7 shows UniBoost shifts the saturation knee right by 4–6% and cuts P90/P99 latency by up to 10× near ρ≈0.99. The one consistent trade-off is mean TBT (−25.3% vs. TRAIL+), reflecting UniBoost's deliberate smoothing of decode interleaving to protect tails at the cost of average per-token speed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two research threads: (1) queueing-theoretic tail-optimal scheduling, specifically the γ-Boost framework (Yu & Scully, 2024) and its extension to unknown job sizes via Gittins-type policies (Harlev et al., 2025), and (2) LLM inference schedulers that approximate SJF/SRPT using predicted decode lengths (LTR/Fu et al. 2024, TRAIL/Shahout et al. 2024). The key departure is replacing explicit length prediction with a distribution-aware, prediction-free boosting policy, and co-designing it with KV-cache eviction (MEMGUARD) to handle the memory-coupled, multi-phase execution that distinguishes LLM serving from classical queues. Unlike prior LLM schedulers that optimize mean TTFT/TBT, this work explicitly targets P95/P99 TTLT and shows that mean-optimal policies can be arbitrarily bad for tail latency under light-tailed or capped distributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLM serving becomes SLO-constrained with tail latency as the binding constraint, this work demonstrates that a lightweight, prediction-free scheduler can outperform even oracle SRPT on tail metrics (35–50% lower P99 TTLT), reducing the operational burden of maintaining and updating length predictors under distribution shifts. The co-design with KV-cache eviction (MEMGUARD) addresses a practical gap that pure queueing-theoretic policies ignore, and the adaptive γ mechanism makes the system robust to workload mix changes without retraining. Open questions include extending formal guarantees to M/G/k with preemption costs, handling multi-model/multi-replica deployments with joint routing, and characterizing behavior under truly heavy-tailed reasoning workloads where the light-tailed tail-optimality conditions may not hold.
