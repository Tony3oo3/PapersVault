## Problem

ML-based weather prediction models fail to faithfully reproduce the spectral signature of atmospheric data at fine scales, limiting their ability to resolve sharp phenomena like frontal zones and tropical cyclones. The paper identifies three distinct failure modes: spectral damping from deterministic training, high-frequency aliasing from compressive encoding onto coarser latent grids, and residual high-frequency leakage from predicting tendencies rather than states directly. MOSAIC addresses all three simultaneously through probabilistic training, native-resolution spatial interactions, and direct next-state prediction.

## Input / Output

The model consumes 82 dynamic channels (4 surface variables plus 6 pressure-level variables at 13 levels) on a 240×121 equiangular lat-lon grid at 1.5° resolution, concatenated with 3 static geographic fields, Cartesian unit-sphere coordinates, and sinusoidal time encodings, over a history of 2–4 timesteps. Internally, data is interpolated onto a HEALPix mesh (49,152 pixels at N_side=64) via learned cross-attention, and the output is a 48-member ensemble of 82 atmospheric variables at the next timestep, denormalized back to physical units. The model is trained with a latitude-weighted, variable-weighted fair CRPS loss in standardized space, producing well-calibrated probabilistic forecasts rather than a single deterministic prediction.

## Method

MOSAIC is a 214M-parameter U-Net that processes weather data at progressively coarser HEALPix resolutions, with the critical design choice that the first encoder stage computes spatial interactions via block-sparse attention at native resolution before any coarsening occurs. Block-sparse attention groups spatially adjacent query tokens into blocks that jointly select which key-value blocks to attend to across three branches (compression, selection, local), achieving linear cost in sequence length while capturing long-range dependencies; the HEALPix NESTED indexing ensures contiguous memory access for these spatial blocks. For uncertainty quantification, a 32-dimensional noise vector is injected as an additive bias inside every SwiGLU gate (cSwiGLU), producing globally consistent ensemble members with input-dependent covariance. The model predicts the next state directly rather than the residual tendency, avoiding high-frequency error accumulation in autoregressive rollouts. Training uses the Muon optimizer in fp16 on 8×H100 GPUs with progressively longer autoregressive rollouts during finetuning, and the block-sparse attention kernel is implemented in Triton following FlashAttention's memory-efficient tiling strategy.

## Results

At 1.5° resolution (240 h lead time, 2020 test year), MOSAIC achieves the best Q700 RMSE (1358.1) among all 1.5° models and second-best U850 (4.880) and SP (576.44), while narrowing the Z500 gap to the top probabilistic baselines (624.08 vs. NeuralGCM 606.84, ArchesWeatherGen 610.36). In the cross-resolution 0.25° benchmark, MOSAIC at 1.5° outperforms IFS-ENS, Aurora, Pangu, and GraphCast on Z500 (610 vs. 622/668/800/748 m²/s²) and matches IFS-ENS on T2M (1.99 K). A 24-member, 10-day ensemble forecast completes in 11.6 s on a single H100 GPU (0.048 s/member/step), making MOSAIC 20× faster than ArchesWeatherGen and 150× faster than NeuralGCM.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the two deterministic 1.5° baselines (Stormer, ArchesWeather-Mx4), MOSAIC wins on every variable by a wide margin (e.g., Z500: 624 vs. 666/694; Q700: 1358 vs. 1445/1541). Against the two strongest probabilistic 1.5° baselines (ArchesWeatherGen, NeuralGCM-ENS), MOSAIC achieves the best Q700 (1358.1 vs. 1373.7/1374.2) but trails on Z500 (624.08 vs. 610.36/606.84) and T850 (2.778 vs. 2.755/2.756), while being 20× and 150× faster respectively. In the 0.25° cross-resolution benchmark at 240 h, MOSAIC (trained at 1.5°) outperforms all deterministic 0.25° models—Pangu (Z500: 610 vs. 800), GraphCast (610 vs. 748), and Aurora (610 vs. 668)—as well as IFS-ENS on Z500, Q700, U850, V850, SP, U10M, and V10M. MOSAIC trails the two strongest probabilistic 0.25° baselines: GenCast is marginally better on Z500 (607.5 vs. 610.4) and T850 (2.693 vs. 2.703), while FGN leads on all variables (e.g., Z500: 583.1, CRPS: 242 vs. MOSAIC's 258). On ensemble calibration (spread-to-skill ratio), MOSAIC achieves 0.83–0.95, closely matching FGN (0.89–0.97) and substantially outperforming GenCast (1.03–1.24, persistent underconfidence).

## Relation to Prior Work

MOSAIC extends Native Sparse Attention (NSA) from token-level to block-level sparsity, exploiting the spatial locality of physical data on the HEALPix mesh so that spatially adjacent queries jointly select key-value blocks at linear cost. For probabilistic forecasting, it adopts the learned functional perturbation framework of FGN (Alet et al., 2025) but relocates noise injection from layer normalization to SwiGLU gates, which the authors found empirically superior. Architecturally, MOSAIC departs from the dominant compressive-encoding paradigm of GraphCast, Pangu, and Aurora by computing spatial interactions at native resolution before any coarsening, and from residual-prediction models (FourCastNet, Pangu) by predicting the next state directly to avoid high-frequency error accumulation in autoregressive rollouts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean taxonomy of three distinct spectral degradation failure modes (statistical damping, architectural aliasing, parametric residual leakage) and shows that addressing all three simultaneously yields near-perfect spectral alignment, which translates into skillful extreme-event forecasts (Hurricane Ian case study). The practical impact is significant: a 24-member, 10-day probabilistic forecast in under 12 s on a single H100 at 1.5° resolution matches or outperforms models trained on 6× finer grids, making high-quality ensemble weather forecasting accessible on commodity hardware. Open questions include whether block-sparse attention generalizes to other physical simulation domains, whether the HEALPix requirement limits adoption in communities standardized on lat-lon grids, and whether the spectral fidelity advantage persists at even higher resolutions where the native grid exceeds hundreds of thousands of tokens.
