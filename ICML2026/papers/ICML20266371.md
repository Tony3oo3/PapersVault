## Problem

The ML community has focused on reducing numerical precision (FP16, quantization) for computational efficiency, yet scientific ML inherits the precision sensitivity of traditional scientific computing where even small numerical deviations can invalidate physical conclusions. This position paper argues that the FP32-versus-FP64 distinction is a critical, underreported factor in scientific ML: models trained or evaluated in single precision can yield different scientific conclusions than their double-precision counterparts, a discrepancy that is currently unreported in the literature.

## Input / Output

The empirical studies feed identical scientific inputs—atomic coordinates for DFT and ML potential calculations, electromagnetic wave parameters (wavelength, bandwidth, nonlinear susceptibility) for FDTD simulations, and PDE coefficients for PINNs—through the same codebases under both FP32 and FP64 precision to isolate the effect of numerical format. The outputs of interest are not only direct model predictions (energy, force, transmittance) but also derived quantities such as vibrational mode frequencies and their ordering, geometry-optimized structures, and weak-signal spectral features, where precision effects become most visible and scientifically consequential.

## Method

The paper conducts controlled experiments across three scientific computing domains to empirically demonstrate precision sensitivity. For DFT, geometry optimization of water is run in PySCF with both HF and B3LYP, showing FP32 fails to converge (gradients stagnate or diverge) while FP64 converges within three steps. For FDTD, nonlinear Kerr-media simulations in Meep reveal that FP32 cannot resolve second-harmonic and third-harmonic generation below a χ² threshold of ~10⁻⁶ m/V, whereas FP64 maintains accuracy down to ~10⁻⁸ m/V. For ML potentials, MACE-OFF23-medium is trained from scratch in both precisions on the SPICE dataset; while energy and force MAEs appear comparable, PES scans show discrepancies up to 1 eV at larger displacements, and a systematic analysis of 49,804 molecules finds 85% exhibit at least one vibrational mode misalignment between FP32 and FP64 predictions. The paper supplements these experiments with a literature survey of PINN precision failures and a forward-looking discussion of LLM inference nondeterminism, then proposes concrete recommendations including mandatory FP32/FP64 comparison reporting, standardized precision-sensitivity benchmarks, and mixed high-precision training strategies.

## Results

On the MACE-OFF23 test set (49,804 molecules), 85.15% (42,406) exhibit at least one vibrational mode misalignment between FP32 and FP64 models, averaging 6.03 misalignments per affected molecule, with the affected fraction rising from 33.6% (1–10 atoms) to 100% (51+ atoms). Selected vibrational frequencies differ by up to 5.2 cm⁻¹ between precisions, which falls within the resolution of modern Raman/IR spectroscopy and can reorder peak assignments. On the oseltamivir PES scan, energy and force discrepancies grow to 1 eV and 10 eV/Å at large displacements, while near the equilibrium structure the differences are 41 meV and 6.6 meV/Å. The computational overhead of FP64 training is modest: 1.26× training time and 1.43× energy/CO₂ on an NVIDIA H200 (1.32× and 1.46× on B200), well below the theoretical 2× FLOP ratio.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is FP32 versus FP64 training of the same MACE-OFF23-medium architecture. On standard MAE metrics (energy and force), the two precisions are nearly indistinguishable: differences are ≤0.5 meV/atom for energy and ≤0.5 meV/Å for force across all splits (Train, Validation, DES370K, Dipeptides, PubChem, QMugs, Solvated Amino Acids, Water) on both H200 and B200 GPUs. However, this surface-level parity masks significant downstream divergence: FP32-trained models produce vibrational frequencies that shift by 1–5.2 cm⁻¹ relative to FP64, causing mode-ordering changes that alter spectroscopic peak assignments in 85% of the test set. In traditional scientific computing, the contrast is even starker: FP32 DFT/HF geometry optimization of water fails to converge entirely (gradients stagnate at 0.2–0.4 Ha/Bohr vs. a 10⁻⁶ threshold), while FP64 converges in three steps; FP32 FDTD simulations of Kerr media become unreliable below χ² = 10⁻⁶ m/V, whereas FP64 remains accurate down to ~10⁻⁸ m/V. The practical cost of adopting FP64 is a 1.26–1.32× increase in training time and 1.43–1.46× increase in energy and CO₂ emissions, which the authors argue is negligible relative to the risk of drawing incorrect scientific conclusions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes three previously separate threads: the long-standing FP64 standard in traditional scientific computing (Quantum ESPRESSO, PySCF, Meep), the ML community's widespread adoption of reduced-precision training for efficiency (mixed-precision, quantization), and isolated prior observations of precision failures in PINNs (Nakamura et al., 2022; Sharma & Shankar, 2022). Rather than building on a single methodological lineage, it carves new ground by systematically connecting these threads and providing controlled experiments on ML potentials (MACE) and FDTD simulations to demonstrate that the ML community's default to FP32 is incompatible with the precision requirements inherited from the scientific domains it serves. The LLM reproducibility work of Yuan et al. (2025) on floating-point non-associativity is cited as a forward-looking parallel, but the paper's core empirical contribution is its own MACE-OFF23 and DFT/FDTD benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 85% of molecules in the MACE-OFF23 test set exhibit at least one vibrational mode misalignment between FP32 and FP64 models—and that these misalignments fall within the resolution of modern Raman/IR spectroscopy—raises a practical reliability concern for any research pipeline that uses ML potentials for spectral assignment. The paper's actionable recommendations (report FP32 vs. FP64 comparisons, release FP64 models, develop precision-sensitive benchmarks) are low-cost to adopt and could prevent underreported errors in published scientific conclusions. Key open questions remain: what systematic criteria identify precision-critical tasks (the paper tentatively suggests high-order derivatives, strong nonlinearity, and derived properties), how to implement mixed high-precision training efficiently, and whether the LLM quantization trend will further erode precision in scientific applications.
