## Problem

Existing inference-time safety alignment methods for LLMs assume access to model internals (weights or architecture), making them inapplicable to proprietary black-box API models used by third-party stakeholders. This paper addresses the fundamental safety-helpfulness trade-off—balancing informative but potentially risky responses against safe but uninformative ones—without requiring retraining or access to the underlying model, targeting the dominant deployment pattern of selecting among a finite candidate pool.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt, a finite candidate set of responses (MCQ options or k sampled completions), a designated safe fallback with zero risk, and a scalar safety budget T. Each candidate is scored via self-probing (normalized log-probabilities of YES/NO tokens from the base model) to produce helpfulness and risk scores, from which fallback-relative margins M_i and Δ_i are computed. The output is a single selected response drawn from the LP-optimal mixture over candidates, or the safe fallback r_s if the risk constraint is infeasible.

## Method

The paper formulates the safety-helpfulness trade-off as a one-shot imperfect-information game between the LLM and an uncertain user intent, whose minimax equilibrium yields a constrained linear program: maximize expected helpfulness lift over the safe fallback subject to a bound on expected extra risk. Two non-obvious design choices emerge from the adaptation-safety framework—fallback-relative margins (measuring improvement over the safe default rather than raw scores) and an aggregate risk constraint over the mixture (rather than per-candidate thresholds)—which together enable the LP to recover borderline-but-safe candidates that no threshold filter can. A robustness guarantee (Theorem 3.1) shows that tightening the budget by 2ε_s under bounded scorer error preserves true feasibility, and a sigmoid relaxation handles noisy probe scores. The decision layer is scorer-agnostic: any black-box scoring mechanism (self-probes, external classifiers like Llama-Guard-3-8B, or reward models) can be plugged in, and the LP is solved per prompt at inference time with no training or model modification.

## Results

Across 20 model-benchmark settings (5 models × 4 benchmarks), Safety Game (SG) achieves the best safety-helpfulness trade-off in 14 cases. On SafetyBench (N=11,435), SG delivers the largest and most consistent gains, e.g., 54.79% accuracy for LLaMA-2-7B vs. 45.71% for the best baseline (G), with pairwise McNemar p<10⁻⁴ for nearly all model-baseline pairs. On SORRY-Bench, SG achieves the lowest expected human fulfillment rate (HFR) on 3 of 5 models, with relative HFR reductions of 45–81% over the strongest baseline (e.g., 0.051 vs. 0.272 for LLaMA-2-7B). On TruthfulQA, SG is best on 4/5 models (e.g., 54.7% BLEU-Acc for GPT-OSS-20B vs. 52.1% for ER-D), and on HHH it matches or exceeds the strongest baseline on 3/5 models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All baselines (G, D, MI, SC, ER-G, ER-D) are candidate-ranking rules from the Consensus Game framework (Jacob et al., 2024) that operate on the same fixed candidate set. On SafetyBench, SG outperforms every baseline on 4 of 5 models by 5–18 percentage points (e.g., 54.79% vs. 45.71% for G on LLaMA-2-7B; 60.13% vs. 57.40% for D on GPT-OSS-20B), with the sole exception being Llama-3.2-1B where SG (39.00%) is marginally below MI (44.61%). On SORRY-Bench, SG achieves the lowest HFR on LLaMA-2-7B (0.051 vs. 0.272 for SC), LLaMA-2-13B (0.142 vs. 0.295 for SC), and Llama-3.1-8B (0.167 vs. 0.304 for ER-G), but loses to G/MI on GPT-OSS-20B (0.223 vs. 0.009) where those baselines collapse to near-stonewall refusals, and to ER-G on Llama-3.2-1B (0.415 vs. 0.220) in a calibration-limited regime. On TruthfulQA, SG leads on 4/5 models (e.g., 50.4% vs. 50.1% for G on LLaMA-2-7B; 54.7% vs. 52.1% for ER-D on GPT-OSS-20B). On HHH, SG ties the best baseline on GPT-OSS-20B (71.5%) and LLaMA-2-7B (72.9%), wins on Llama-3.1-8B (67.9% vs. 65.6% for MI), but trails D on LLaMA-2-13B (69.2% vs. 75.1%) and Llama-3.2-1B (54.3% vs. 65.2% for MI). The paper further isolates the decision layer's contribution by showing that, with Llama-Guard-3-8B scores held fixed, the LP recovers 81 more human-safe responses than the best threshold filter at any cutoff (419 vs. 338 on SORRY-Bench), confirming the gain is intrinsic to the aggregate-risk constraint rather than scorer quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the adaptation-safety framework from imperfect-information game solving (Ge et al., 2024) into a decoding-time candidate selection rule, translating the 'bound the refined strategy's exploitability relative to a blueprint' principle into a fallback-relative, aggregate-risk LP constraint. It departs from prior inference-time alignment methods (InferenceGuard, InferAligner, MAVIS) that require access to model internals or auxiliary teachers, operating instead as a purely black-box selector over a finite candidate set. The main experimental baselines come from the Consensus Game line (Jacob et al., 2024), which optimizes coherence/agreement via equilibrium search; Safety Game replaces that objective with an explicit bi-objective (helpfulness minus risk) under a hard safety cap, and adds a principled infeasibility fallback that the consensus-game selectors lack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that safety alignment becomes accessible to stakeholders (SMEs, public-sector organizations, resource-constrained entities) who consume LLMs via API and cannot retrain or modify model internals. The cleanest empirical finding is that the decision layer itself—not the scorer—drives the safety gain: with Llama-Guard-3-8B scores held fixed, the aggregate-risk LP recovers 81 more human-safe responses than any per-candidate threshold at any cutoff, isolating the structural advantage of the constrained formulation. Key limitations include the scorer-calibration bottleneck (Llama-3.2-1B fails under self-probing but recovers with an external scorer), per-task tuning of the safety budget T, and the restriction to single-turn selection. Open directions include multi-turn dialogue with sequential dependencies, multi-player safety games with distinct stakeholder utilities, and calibration-free budget selection.
