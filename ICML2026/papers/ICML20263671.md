## Problem

Protein language models (PLMs) have demonstrated strong ability to identify and complete repeating sequences in masked-token prediction, but their internal mechanisms for doing so remain uncharacterized. This paper reverse-engineers how PLMs detect both exact and approximate repeats, asking whether the same circuitry handles both cases and what biological knowledge is integrated with general pattern-matching mechanisms.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263671/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a protein sequence tokenized into individual amino acid tokens containing two repeat occurrences (identical or differing by up to 50% substitutions), with a single masked position in one occurrence. For approximate repeats, masking is restricted to positions identical to their aligned counterparts but adjacent to a substitution site, ensuring local similarity is disrupted while the repeat relationship remains detectable. Beyond the predicted amino acid, the analysis produces circuit-level identifications (minimal subsets of attention heads and MLPs), attention head functional clusters, neuron-to-biological-concept matches, and a three-phase mechanistic description of how components interact across layers.

## Method

The paper uses attribution patching with integrated gradients (AP-IG) to discover minimal circuits of attention heads and MLPs that explain repeat-completion performance, with counterfactuals constructed by replacing amino acids in the non-masked repeat with BLOSUM62 substitutions. Cross-task comparison reveals that the approximate-repeat circuit functionally subsumes the identical and synthetic circuits, indicating a shared core mechanism with additional logic for handling divergence. Attention heads are clustered into three functional groups—relative-position heads, induction heads attending to aligned repeat tokens, and amino-acid-biased heads—while MLP neurons are matched to biological concepts including individual amino acids, BLOSUM62 substitution groups, IMGT physicochemical classes, and secondary-structure propensities. A logit-lens and EAP-IG interaction analysis reveals a three-phase mechanism: early layers build contextual representations via relative-position heads and biochemical neurons, middle layers use induction heads to copy aligned residues while repeat neurons inhibit, and late MLPs refine the output distribution. Comparing ESM-3 and ESM-C shows both share this mechanism, with ESM-3 additionally engaging secondary-structure-sensitive neurons due to its multi-modal training on structure and functional annotations.

## Results

Both ESM-3 and ESM-C achieve near-perfect accuracy on synthetic (99.8%/99.9%) and identical natural repeats (99.0%/99.4%), but drop to ~79% on approximate repeats with up to 50% substitution rate. Circuit discovery shows that ~14% of ESM-3 components suffice for synthetic/identical repeats, while ~17% are needed for approximate repeats; the approximate-repeat circuit is the only one that functionally generalizes to the other tasks (cross-task faithfulness > 1.0). At the neuron level, retaining just ~3% of MLP neurons per layer in ESM-3 (and ~7% in ESM-C) recovers most circuit faithfulness, indicating high sparsity. The mechanism operates in three phases: early-layer relative-position heads and biochemical-similarity neurons build context, mid-layer induction heads copy aligned repeat tokens, and late-layer MLPs refine the output distribution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two protein language models, ESM-3 (1.4B, multi-modal training) and ESM-C (600M, sequence-only training), on identical repeat-completion tasks. On raw accuracy, the two models are nearly indistinguishable: ESM-C edges out ESM-3 by 0.1–0.4 points on synthetic and identical repeats, and by 0.7 points on approximate repeats (79.7% vs. 79.0%). Mechanistically, however, they differ in a notable way: ESM-3 engages neurons sensitive to protein secondary structure (e.g., helix-breaking amino acids like glycine and proline) for repeat identification, while ESM-C shows no such neurons, likely reflecting ESM-3's additional structural and functional training data. Circuit compactness also differs: ESM-3 requires ~14–17% of components for 85% faithfulness depending on task, whereas ESM-C requires ~25% across all tasks. Both models share the same three-phase induction-based mechanism, and the approximate-repeat circuit in each model is the only one that functionally generalizes to the other task variants.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies the mechanistic interpretability toolkit developed for LLMs—circuit discovery via attribution patching (AP-IG/EAP-IG), induction head analysis, logit lens, and neuron-level concept matching—to protein language models for the first time in the context of repeat detection. It builds directly on the transformer circuits framework (Elhage et al., 2021), induction head theory (Olsson et al., 2022), and recent PLM interpretability work using sparse autoencoders (Simon & Zou, 2025; Nainani et al., 2025), but departs from the SAE-based approach by showing that individual MLP neurons are often interpretable without decomposition. The biological side draws on classical repeat-detection algorithms (RADAR) and substitution matrices (BLOSUM62), positioning PLM-internal similarity as a learned analogue of these hand-designed tools.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first mechanistic characterization of how PLMs detect and complete protein sequence repeats, revealing a clean three-phase circuit (context building → induction-based copying → output refinement) that integrates general language-modeling mechanisms with biology-specific components. The finding that ESM-3 engages secondary-structure neurons while ESM-C does not, tied to differences in training modalities, offers a concrete example of how training data shapes internal representations. Key limitations include restriction to exactly two repeat occurrences, sequence-level (not structural) repeats, up to 50% substitution, and manually defined concepts for neuron matching; extending to multi-occurrence, structural, and indel-containing repeats, as well as automated concept discovery, are natural next steps.
