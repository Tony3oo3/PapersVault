## Problem

VLA models frequently fail in robotic manipulation, yet fault diagnosis remains expert-dependent, subjective, and lacks standardized evaluation criteria. No existing benchmark jointly assesses VLMs on static fault identification, temporal rollback localization, geometric correction, and real-robot recovery, and current VLM-VLA interactions are instruction-based without a unified closed-loop recovery framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265342/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For static evaluation, the model receives a stage-wise sequence of video frames (sampled at fixed intervals to enable image-only models) plus a text instruction, and outputs a diagnostic tuple of fault type (from 6 categories), severity level (0–3), and per-subtask binary success labels. For dynamic evaluation, the model additionally produces a stop timestamp, a rollback timestamp, and a 3D correction vector with discrete per-axis values in {−2,…,2}, forming an intentionally minimal interface that requires no model-specific output heads. On real robots, the VLM inspects 1-second, 20-FPS video segments at 1 Hz in a sparse pause-and-inspect loop rather than every control step.

## Method

The paper introduces VLA-FixBench (6,034 episodes across 40 simulated and 2 real-robot environments) with a two-level hierarchical failure taxonomy (perception/planning/control × four severity levels) and multi-dimensional annotations spanning temporal onset, spatial deviation, and semantic fault type. FaultEval benchmarks 20 VLMs on a dual-track protocol: a static track scoring fault-type accuracy, level-specific severity accuracy, and subtask success accuracy, and a dynamic track measuring Geometric Correction Accuracy (axis alignment, direction consistency, magnitude accuracy), Temporal Localization Accuracy (fault-onset and rollback-initiation timing within asymmetric tolerance windows), and Simulation Success Rate. The VLM-VLA collaboration mechanism implements a detect-rollback-correct chain: upon detecting state s_{t_stop} ∈ C_fail, a rollback operator reverts the robot to a safe historical state s_{t_rb}, and the VLA policy's action is offset by a 3D correction vector (a'_{t_rb} = π(s_{t_rb}) + δ·v). This minimal interface is designed so that general black-box VLMs can participate without adapters, and ablations show that precise temporal rollback outperforms coarse task-level restarts by 8.3% in simulation, while human-expert upper-bound intervention yields 13% and 35% success-rate gains on LIBERO and real robots respectively.

## Results

On the dynamic (closed-loop) evaluation, the human upper-bound achieves 100% on all GCA and TLA sub-metrics, while the best VLM (GPT-5) reaches only 68.6% Axis Alignment on the X-axis and 20% Magnitude Accuracy overall, exposing a large reasoning-to-execution gap. In simulation, human-in-the-loop correction improves task success by 13 percentage points over open-loop execution, and by 35 points on real-robot tasks (Franka arm). On real-robot evaluation, GPT-5.2 achieves the highest fault-detection recall (0.857) and F2-Score (0.714) but a 0% success rate due to a 0.757 false-positive rate, whereas the human baseline reaches 65% success rate. No single VLM dominates both static and dynamic dimensions (Spearman ρ = 0.43, Kendall τ = 0.33), confirming that semantic diagnostic ability does not transfer to closed-loop physical recovery.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 18 VLMs (plus a VLA-only baseline) across static, dynamic, and real-robot tracks. On static fault-type classification, Gemini-3-Flash leads in perception-centric faults (VDF: 0.907) and GPT-5.2 leads in long-horizon reasoning (MLT: 0.706), while open-source Qwen2.5-VL-72B outperforms all closed-source models on spatial object errors (SOE: 0.560). On dynamic geometric correction, GPT-5 and GPT-5.2 achieve the highest Axis Alignment (68.6% and 45.4% on X) but their Magnitude Accuracy remains below 20%, far from the human 100%. A striking 'correction paradox' emerges: aggressive correctors (GPT series) produce lower SSR than the OpenVLA-7B no-intervention baseline (69.8% vs. 84.9%), because imprecise adjustments trigger secondary failures, while conservative models like Qwen3-VL-235B maintain high SSR (83.3%) by rarely intervening. On real-robot tasks, GPT-5.2's high recall (0.857) is negated by a 75.7% false-positive rate yielding 0% success, whereas Qwen3-VL-235B's low FPR (5.9%) gives 15% success—still well below the 65% human baseline. The overall finding is that no off-the-shelf VLM reliably bridges the gap between static diagnostic accuracy and physically effective closed-loop recovery.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of three previously separate threads: low-level robotic anomaly detection (joint torque, force-torque deviations), static VLM evaluation benchmarks (spatial reasoning, VQA), and VLA manipulation benchmarks (LIBERO's binary success metrics). Rather than building on a single prior method, it repurposes the VLM-as-judge paradigm for a new closed-loop role—diagnosing, localizing, and repairing VLA execution failures—where no prior work had defined a unified evaluation protocol spanning static diagnosis, temporal rollback, geometric correction, and real-robot recovery. The hierarchical fault taxonomy (perception/planning/control × severity levels) departs from both the fragmented failure analyses in prior robotics work and the coarse binary success/failure framing of existing VLA benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a critical 'reasoning-execution gap': VLMs that score well on static fault identification (e.g., GPT-5.2, Gemini-3-Flash) do not reliably translate that into effective closed-loop recovery, and the correction paradox—where aggressive diagnostic models actually reduce task success by triggering imprecise interventions—challenges the assumption that better diagnosis automatically yields better recovery. The 35% real-robot improvement from human-expert upper-bound intervention versus the limited automated gains clearly delineates what current VLMs can and cannot do, providing a concrete target for future work on confidence-calibrated intervention and spatio-temporal grounding. Open questions include whether finer-grained supervision, richer correction interfaces, or dedicated VLM fine-tuning can close the gap between semantic diagnosis and physically executable recovery.
