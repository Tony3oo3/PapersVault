## Problem

Standard phylogenetic models like TKF92 enforce unrealistic assumptions—fixed sequence length, no indel context dependence, and uniform selection pressure—while unconstrained neural seq2seq models lack the inductive bias of molecular evolutionary theory. This paper asks whether extending TKF92 with hierarchical latent-state mixtures, or embedding TKF92 structure inside neural architectures, can match or exceed the fit of large neural networks to real protein alignments.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262284/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each model receives an ancestral protein sequence X, an autoregressively generated descendant sequence Y, a lapsed evolutionary time t (Pfam branch length), and the gap profile of the previous alignment column. The alignment Z is treated as a latent variable and marginalized out via the Forward algorithm (for HMM-based models) or learned implicitly (for neural models). The output is the autoregressive per-column conditional probability T^{Y|X}(b|a,i,j,X,Y_{...j},t); the Neural TKF model additionally produces site-specific TKF92+F81 parameters (λ, μ, r, π) at every alignment position. All models are evaluated on 1,201,564 pairwise alignments extracted from Pfam 36.0 seed alignments and phylogenetic trees, with sequences longer than 512 columns removed.

## Method

The paper introduces three model families. First, hierarchical mixtures of TKF92 processes: MixFrag draws each fragment from a categorical mixture of fragment processes (each with its own substitution mixture), and MixDom nests a mixture of TKF92 models inside a TKF91 outer birth-death process, coordinating fragment-level indel rates and substitution parameters within latent domain classes—yielding the first HMM-based indel model where indel rates depend on local sequence context. Second, a Basic neural transducer that autoregressively generates alignment columns using sequence embeddings (Residual CNN, LSTM, or Transformer with RoPE) and a feedforward prediction head, with no evolutionary constraints. Third, a Neural TKF hybrid that uses the same sequence embedders to generate per-position TKF92+F81 parameters (via sigbound-constrained rates and softmax equilibrium distributions), which then populate the standard TKF92 transition and emission matrices. All models satisfy the alignment-Markovian property, enabling alignment marginalization. Training minimizes conditional NLL (or jointly-normalized NLL for mixtures) with Adam; the best Neural TKF (6-block Transformer, 43.55M params) achieves ECE 5.52, while the 10-component MixDom (~29K params) reaches ECE 5.70—competitive with neural models three orders of magnitude larger.

## Results

On the Pfam pairwise alignment benchmark (1,201,564 alignments, 70:10:20 split), the best model—Neural TKF with a 6-block Transformer embedder (43.55M parameters)—achieves an exponentiated cross-entropy (ECE, per-column perplexity) of 5.5169 and total test NLL of 61.72×10⁶. Remarkably, the 10-component MixDom hierarchical mixture (only 29.23K parameters, ~1,500× fewer than the neural models) achieves ECE 5.7013, ranking third overall and outperforming all Basic Neural variants. The simplest TKF92+F81 model (11 parameters) reaches ECE 7.1398, while the naive conditional column frequency baseline (0 parameters) scores 7.2257.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The Neural TKF hybrid consistently outperforms the Basic Neural model across all three sequence embedding architectures: with the 6-block Transformer, ECE drops from 5.6992 (Basic) to 5.5169 (Neural TKF); with LSTM, from 5.6877 to 5.5340; with Residual CNN, from 5.7455 to 5.6258. The 10-component MixDom model (29.23K parameters) outperforms every Basic Neural variant and even beats the Neural TKF 1-block Transformer (5.7013 vs. 5.6983 ECE, essentially tied), while using ~1,000× fewer parameters. Among simple indel models, TKF92 (ECE 7.1398) edges out H20 (7.1530) on real Pfam alignments despite H20 being superior on simulated GGI gap profiles, and both substantially outperform LG05 (7.5423), RS07 (7.5434), and TKF91 (7.7942). The 30-component MixFrag (26.16K params, ECE 5.9602) also outperforms the Basic Neural 1-block Transformer (5.8817). Stratified analysis (Appendix F) reveals that MixDom generalizes better than Neural TKF on the most distant sequence pairs and gappiest alignments, where it outperforms all neural models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the TKF92 birth-death indel model (Thorne et al., 1992) through hierarchical mixtures of fragment and domain classes, creating the first HMM-based indel model where indel rates depend on local sequence context via coordinated mixture parameters. It also introduces a hybrid Neural TKF architecture that uses neural sequence embeddings (Transformer, LSTM, CNN) to generate site-specific TKF92+F81 parameters, contrasting this with a 'Basic' neural transducer that lacks evolutionary constraints. The work positions itself against both the line of GGI-approximating indel models (LG05, RS07, H20) and recent neural approaches to sequence evolution (EvoLSTM, EvoFlows, protein language models), showing that the alignment-Markovian property shared by all proposed models enables exact marginalization of the alignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a 30K-parameter MixDom model is competitive with 30–43M-parameter neural networks—and outperforms them on the most evolutionarily distant pairs—challenges the assumption that capturing complex evolutionary dynamics requires massive neural capacity. This has practical implications for phylogenetics, where computationally tractable, interpretable models are preferred. Open questions include whether pre-trained protein language model embeddings can further boost the hybrid approach, how to incorporate co-evolutionary (Potts model) signals into the HMM framework, and whether variational methods can bring TKF-like structure to neural likelihoods. A key limitation is the time-reversibility assumption, which may not hold biologically but is standard across phylogenetics.
