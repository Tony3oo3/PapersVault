## Problem

In LLM pretraining systems with 100k+ GPUs, node failures occur every few minutes and global restarts take tens of minutes, pushing the system into a restart-dominant regime where restart downtime exceeds useful work time. Existing fault-tolerance approaches are unprepared: checkpointing reduces rework but not restart frequency, and traditional replication masks failures at a linearly inflating r× computation cost that becomes prohibitive at high redundancy. The paper asks whether one can mask as many failures as replication while keeping computation overhead near-constant.

## Input / Output

The method operates on N data-parallel groups (each a model-parallel group of M GPUs) with N training data shards, a chosen redundancy degree r, and a stream of node failure events. Data shards are pre-distributed across groups using a cyclic Golomb ruler so that no two shard types co-occur in more than one group. The system outputs a committed all-reduce stack (the minimal number of stacks to compute before triggering gradient synchronization), reordered per-group shard stacks after each failure, the synchronized full gradient, and a wipe-out signal when any shard type is fully depleted, triggering global restart.

## Method

SPARe replicates individual data shards (not entire groups) and stacks them into r layers across parallelism groups via a cyclic Golomb ruler distribution, ensuring any two shard types share at most one host group. At each training step, all-reduce is triggered as soon as all N shard types are collectible among surviving groups—typically after only 2–3 stacks rather than all r—minimizing redundant computation. Upon node failure, the RECTLR controller runs in three phases: Hopcroft-Karp matching on fixed stacks to check if reordering is needed (Phase 0), incremental HK matching with free permutation to find the minimal feasible all-reduce stack (Phase 1), and min-cost max-flow to reorder stacks with minimal movement (Phase 2). The paper derives closed-form expressions for the average endurable failure count μ(N,r) ≈ Γ(1/r)/r · N^{1−1/r} (matching replication's asymptotic) and the average computation overhead S̄(N,r) ≈ 2–3× (vs. r× for replication), and jointly optimizes redundancy with checkpointing to yield r* ≈ ⌊log₂N + 0.833⌋ for minimal time-to-train.

## Results

Under a harsh restart-dominant 600k H100 cluster setting (MTBF = 5 min, global restart = 60 min), SPARe+CKPT achieves 40–50% lower time-to-train than the best Rep+CKPT configuration: 2.92×T₀ vs. 6.07×T₀ at N=200 (51.9% gain), 2.49×T₀ vs. 4.27×T₀ at N=600 (41.7% gain), and 2.34×T₀ vs. 3.88×T₀ at N=1000 (39.6% gain). SPARe+CKPT reaches 87–97% system availability at optimal redundancy r*≈8–9, while maintaining near-constant computation overhead of 2.3–2.9× even at r=20, compared to the linear r× overhead of traditional replication. CKPT-only fails to complete training under these conditions, proceeding only a few steps before being overwhelmed by frequent global restarts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPARe+CKPT decisively outperforms Rep+CKPT (traditional replication with checkpointing, following Ferreira et al. 2011 and Elliott et al. 2012) across all tested data-parallel degrees, achieving 39.6–51.9% lower time-to-train while reaching 87–97% availability versus 62–84% for the best replication configuration. The key advantage is that SPARe's computation overhead stays near-constant (2.3–2.9×) even at high redundancy (r=8–9), whereas Rep+CKPT's overhead scales linearly with r, making higher redundancies prohibitively expensive. Against CKPT-only (standard data-parallel training with optimal checkpointing per Saxena et al. 2024), SPARe+CKPT is the only viable scheme: CKPT-only cannot complete training under the 5-min MTBF / 60-min restart regime, as global restarts dominate wall-clock time. Notably, SPARe+CKPT underperforms its theoretical prediction at low redundancy (r=2), even losing to Rep+CKPT at that point, due to the Weibull(k<1) failure distribution causing faster early failure accumulation than the exponential model assumes; this gap closes and reverses at the optimal r*≈8–9.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPARe builds on the replication-based fault-tolerance line (Ferreira et al., 2011; Benoit et al., 2019) and the checkpointing literature (Young & Daly, 2006; Saxena et al., 2024), but departs fundamentally from the assumption that masking k failures requires k× redundant computation. Instead of fully replicating model-parallel groups, it introduces a combinatorial shard-stacking scheme (via cyclic Golomb rulers) combined with online adaptive reordering (Hopcroft-Karp matching + min-cost max-flow) at the data-parallel layer. This positions SPARe as orthogonal to inner-topology recovery systems (Bamboo, ReCycle, FT-HSDP) and complementary to storage-assisted checkpointing (GEMINI, DataStates-LLM), operating at a higher abstraction that only requires all partial-gradient types to remain collectible across surviving groups.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLM pretraining scales toward 100k–600k GPUs, the projected 5-minute MTBF and 60-minute global restart latency create a restart-dominant regime where existing checkpointing-only approaches become impractical. SPARe's 40–50% time-to-train reduction over replication, backed by closed-form theoretical guarantees, offers a concrete path to >90% availability without the prohibitive linear overhead. Key limitations include simulation-only validation (no real 600k-GPU cluster), the assumption of independent failures in the theory (Weibull k<1 in simulation causes underperformance at low r), and operation restricted to the data-parallel layer. Open questions include robustness under correlated/heterogeneous failure domains, integration with real NCCL communicator-shrinking latency, and whether the optimal r* formula holds under dynamic workload conditions.
