## Problem

Standard MoE aggregates selected expert outputs via a permutation-invariant weighted sum, meaning the final representation depends only on the multiset of chosen experts with no notion of ordering or interaction among them. Prior scaling efforts have focused on routing algorithms, sparsity, and expert granularity, but have overlooked this third critical component—how expert outputs are composed—leaving a fundamental expressiveness bottleneck unaddressed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261119/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Given a text token embedding x ∈ R^d, the router selects top-K experts whose gated outputs form the initial node set at depth 0. The DAG learning module then operates in a reduced hidden dimension d_g (set independently of both model hidden size d and expert hidden size d_r), iteratively refining node representations over L iterations (typically 1–2). The final output is the sum of all depth-L node representations, which is added to the residual stream and passed to the next transformer layer for causal next-token prediction.

## Method

DAG-MoE replaces the standard weighted-sum aggregation with a learned directed acyclic graph (DAG) over the K selected experts, where each expert occupies a distinct structural role. A lightweight DAG learning module (parameterized by W_down, W_edge, W_node, W_up) runs for L iterations: at each iteration, node representations are projected to a lower-dimensional space, pairwise edge gates are computed by concatenating source and target projections and passing through a gating network, and gated information is aggregated with residual connections back to the original dimension. Each node at depth l aggregates only from depth l−1 (earlier depths are injected via residuals), keeping the graph sparse and the search space tractable. Theoretically, the paper proves that DAG-style MoE is strictly more expressive than standard MoE (Theorem 3.2) and that a single DAG-style MoE layer can simulate a full pass of dynamic programming (Theorem 3.3), enabling multi-step compositional reasoning within one forward pass without additional router-side complexity.

## Results

On the 40B-token pretraining run (699M params), DAG-MoE-l reduces perplexity to 10.27 on Pile vs. 10.51 for the parameter-matched MoE-l baseline, with larger gains on out-of-domain corpora (C4: 34.21 vs. 35.21; FineWeb-Edu: 24.69 vs. 25.38). After instruction fine-tuning, DAG-MoE-l achieves an average downstream accuracy of 26.13% vs. 24.06% for MoE-l, with the largest improvements on multi-step reasoning tasks: +6.06 on GPQA, +3.46 on Lambada, and +3.15 on PIQA. In a controlled comparison at matched added-parameter budget (393K), DAG-MoE reduces perplexity by 0.587 over standard MoE, outperforming Chain-of-Experts (0.480) and a shared expert (0.433).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline throughout the paper is a standard MoE with identical expert count, hidden size, top-K router, and training recipe, augmented with a shared expert to match DAG-MoE's total parameter count. DAG-MoE consistently outperforms this baseline on perplexity across all model scales (small, medium, large) and both coarse-grained (top-4) and fine-grained (top-8) routing settings. Against Chain-of-Experts (CoE), the closest architectural competitor that also iteratively refines expert outputs within a single MoE layer, DAG-MoE achieves a perplexity reduction of 0.587 versus CoE's 0.480 at a matched 393K added-parameter budget, indicating that the gain comes from the DAG structural form of aggregation rather than iterative refinement alone. A naive MLP mixing ablation (concatenating expert outputs into a single MLP) actually degrades performance below standard MoE, confirming that the DAG inductive bias is essential. On downstream tasks, DAG-MoE-l wins on 6 of 7 benchmarks, with gains concentrated on compositional reasoning (GPQA, Lambada, PIQA, BBH) while remaining within noise on pattern-matching tasks (HellaSwag, MMLU).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as introducing a third axis for MoE scaling—structural aggregation of expert outputs—complementary to the well-studied axes of routing mechanism and expert granularity. It builds directly on the D-VAE framework (Zhang et al., 2019) for its expressiveness proofs and on Feng et al. (2023) for the dynamic-programming simulation theorem, while departing from the closest prior structural MoE work (S'MoRE, which fixes a tree topology and serves as a fine-tuning adapter) by learning a token-adaptive DAG end-to-end as a standalone MoE backbone. The architecture is grounded in standard Switch-Transformer-style routing and Llama-3.1 design, with the DAG learning module as the sole novel component.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper opens a principled, theoretically grounded direction for improving MoE expressiveness without touching the router or expert configurations, and the lightweight DAG module (≈1.5–4.5% throughput overhead) makes it practically attractive. However, all evidence comes from small-scale models (≤699M parameters, ≤40B tokens), so it remains unclear whether the structural-aggregation advantage persists at billion-parameter, trillion-token scale or whether the restricted DAG class (fixed nodes per depth, depth-adjacent connections only) is sufficient. Open questions include how to learn the optimal DAG structure more effectively, whether the DP-simulation capacity translates to broader compositional reasoning at scale, and how the approach interacts with other MoE scaling strategies (finer granularity, alternative routing).
