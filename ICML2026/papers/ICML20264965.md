## Problem

In Interactive Task Learning (ITL) systems, rule preconditions must be induced from small, heavily imbalanced sets of human-graded examples, yet standard classifiers (decision trees, random forests, XGBoost) perform poorly under these conditions and provide no self-aware signal of learning progress. A critical practical gap is that existing methods suffer from error reoccurrence—fixing one misclassified example can reintroduce previously corrected errors—making the training experience inconsistent for human instructors who need to know when training is complete and which edge cases still need attention.

## Input / Output

STAND consumes labeled feature vectors representing candidate rule applications in an environment state, where features are strictly categorical (e.g., object positions, game states, arithmetic step attributes) and often high-dimensional with many distractor features. Rather than outputting a single prediction, STAND produces a compact lattice of consistent classifiers (general set G) paired with per-leaf specific extensions (set S), from which it derives unnormalized certainty scores Cert(y=c|x) ∈ [0,1] that sum to ≤1 across classes. These scores serve triple duty: as prediction probabilities for the prevailing class, as active-learning signals identifying the most informative next training example, and as training-completion indicators when they approach 1.0.

## Method

STAND constructs its general set G by expanding multiple near-best feature splits at each node (all literals within acceptance rate α of maximum gain) rather than selecting a single split, then avoids combinatorial explosion via node caching—any two edges selecting the same subset of training samples are routed to a shared node, forming a lattice. The specific set S stores per-leaf invariant feature values (or ranges for continuous features), providing additional criteria beyond top-down filtering. Certainty is computed as the product of a specific contribution (weighted overlap of the query with leaf extensions, regularized via hierarchical shrinkage over ancestor nodes) and a general agreement term (fraction of upstream path segments the query satisfies, measuring how much G must restructure). Hierarchical shrinkage is applied to joint class-feature probabilities for impurity calculation, to node weights, and to extension weights, using separate regularization parameters (λ_p, λ_n, λ_s) that downweight deep nodes with few samples and literals admitted only due to permissive α thresholds.

## Results

On the synthetic precondition induction task (100 training examples, 400 features, 2000 holdout), STAND with hierarchical shrinkage achieves 92.8% holdout accuracy with 2.2% false-positive and 0.8% false-negative error reoccurrence, outperforming Decision Trees (88.6% acc, 7.3% FP reocc.), XGBoost (84.8% acc), Random Forests (56.2% acc), and VSSM (55.5% acc). On real ITL tasks, STAND(h.s.) reaches 99.4–99.9% final holdout accuracy (Dice Adventure: 99.51%, Fractions: 99.88%, MC Addition: 99.43%), with 100% accuracy in 39/40, 35/40, and 16/40 repetitions respectively. STAND also shows the best calibration (ECE 0.123, Brier 0.0029) and productive monotonicity (60–70% vs. ~50% for alternatives), enabling reliable self-assessment of training progress.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Decision Trees, STAND wins on both accuracy (90.0% vs. 88.6%) and error reoccurrence (2.6% FP vs. 7.3% FP; 1.2% FN vs. 3.8% FN), with the gap widening under hierarchical shrinkage (92.8% vs. 87.81% accuracy). Against XGBoost, STAND achieves 5.2 points higher accuracy (90.0% vs. 84.8%) and lower FP reoccurrence (2.6% vs. 3.4%), though XGBoost matches STAND on FN reoccurrence (0.8%). Against Random Forests and VSSM, STAND's advantage is overwhelming on accuracy (90.0% vs. 56.2% and 55.5%), though both baselines show lower reoccurrence rates—VSSM's near-zero reoccurrence is meaningless given its impractically low accuracy. On the non-synthetic ITL tasks, STAND(h.s.) leads the next-best Decision Tree by 0.7–4.0 points (e.g., 99.43% vs. 95.4% on MC Addition). On larger, noisier UCI datasets (Appendix E), STAND(h.s.) is comparable to tree baselines (89.18% mean accuracy vs. 89.12% Decision Tree, 89.67% Random Forest, 89.51% XGBoost), confirming it does not collapse under noise unlike classic version spaces.

## Relation to Prior Work

STAND synthesizes ideas from greedy divide-and-conquer decision trees (Breiman et al., 2017), sequential covering (Quinlan, 1996), and version space learning (Mitchell, 1982) into a unified framework that maintains a compact lattice of all near-optimal classifiers via node caching, rather than enumerating them individually as option trees (Kohavi & Kunz, 1997) or Rashomon-set methods (TreeFARMS, SPLIT) do. It adapts hierarchical shrinkage (Agarwal et al., 2022) from regression to classification probability estimates, node weights, and specific-extension weights, which is a non-trivial repurposing. The key conceptual departure from prior version space work is avoiding the intractability of candidate elimination over disjunctive concepts (Hirsh, 1992) by using a greedy approximation that still captures the full space of consistent classifiers in a shared data structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

STAND addresses a practically important gap in interactive task learning systems where human instructors need reliable signals about training progress, error reoccurrence, and when to stop training—capabilities that standard ML methods (ensembles, neural networks) provide poorly in small-data, structured, categorical settings. The dramatically better calibration (ECE 0.123 vs. 0.219–0.423 for baselines) and productive monotonicity (60–70% vs. ~50%) suggest that explicit ambiguity modeling via a classifier space is more informative than statistical averaging when data is scarce. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to richer rule structures (beyond binary preconditions), how it scales to domains with continuous or relational features without relative featurization, and whether the node-caching trick remains tractable as the number of near-optimal splits grows in high-dimensional settings.
