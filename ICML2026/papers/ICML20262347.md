## Problem

Vision tokens in VLMs outnumber text tokens by orders of magnitude, causing quadratic attention cost and KV-cache bloat. Existing token-dropping strategies face a fundamental trade-off: salience-based methods rely on dense attention maps (incompatible with FlashAttention and prone to position bias), while diversity-based methods select redundant pivots that cause region-level imbalance and are sensitive to hyperparameters. No prior approach jointly guarantees both importance-aware selection and local spatial coverage under a fixed token budget.

## Input / Output

SPLIT operates on the ViT-side hidden states of an image (or video frames) already encoded into patch tokens (e.g., 576 tokens for 336×336 LLaVA-1.5, 2880 for 672×672 LLaVA-Next), alongside a text prompt. The image is partitioned into a fixed spatial grid (default 4×4 = 16 regions), and the method outputs a reduced vision token set of size B (e.g., 192, 128, or 64 tokens) that is concatenated with text tokens and fed to the LLM for standard autoregressive generation. No retraining or architectural modification is required; the method is plug-and-play at inference time.

## Method

SPLIT combines three mechanisms: (1) a temporal-shift importance score that measures the normalized L2 change of each token's hidden state across consecutive ViT layers, Δ_ℓ(x) = ‖h_ℓ(x) − h_{ℓ−1}(x)‖₂ / ‖h_ℓ(x)‖₂, averaged over a layer set, providing an attention-free salience signal; (2) an adaptive region-level budget allocation that partitions the image into K spatial regions and assigns each region a budget B̃_k = B/K + B·I(X_k)/Σ_j I(X_j), blending a uniform floor with importance-proportional allocation to guarantee no region is empty; (3) a diversity score D(i) = λσ_i − μ_i computed from the self-similarity matrix (where μ_i is mean similarity and σ_i is standard deviation of token i's row), used to select the top-B̃_k most distinctive, non-redundant tokens within each region. The theoretical analysis proves that adaptive budgets yield a tighter Hausdorff-distance coverage bound than uniform allocation, and that even a minimum budget of 1 per region eliminates catastrophic coverage failure.

## Results

On LLaVA-1.5-7B across 10 image understanding benchmarks, SPLIT preserves 99.3% of vanilla accuracy with 192 vision tokens (↓66.7%), 97.8% with 128 tokens (↓77.8%), and 92.8% with only 64 tokens (↓88.9%). Under the most aggressive 64-token budget, SPLIT outperforms the next-best method DivPrune by 1.1% in average accuracy. On video understanding (LLaVA-Video-7B, 64×16 tokens, ↓90.5%), SPLIT retains 87.7% of vanilla performance, surpassing DivPrune (+0.8%) and DART (+0.4%). Inference efficiency analysis shows SPLIT achieves 1.80× total speedup and 2.76× prefilling speedup with minimal FLOPs (19.8%) and KV-cache (192 MB) overhead.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPLIT consistently outperforms all 11 baselines across every token budget and model family tested. At 192 tokens on LLaVA-1.5-7B, SPLIT (99.3%) edges out the closest competitor DART (99.0%) by 0.3%, while salience-based methods like FastV (96.1%) and HiRED (95.7%) trail by 3+ points. Under the extreme 64-token budget, the gap widens: SPLIT (92.8%) surpasses DivPrune (91.7%) by 1.1% and DART (91.4%) by 1.4%, whereas attention-based methods collapse to 86.6–86.8%. On Qwen2-VL-7B at 88.9% reduction, SPLIT (85.6%) leads DivPrune (84.9%) and DART (82.4%), with salience-based methods (FastV 77.6%, HiRED 77.0%) falling 8+ points behind. On video (LLaVA-Video-7B, 64×16 tokens), SPLIT (87.7%) beats DART (87.3%) and DivPrune (86.9%), confirming the advantage extends to temporal redundancy. In the region-budget ablation (Table 6), applying SPLIT's allocation constraint to VisPruner, VisionZip, and CDPruner improves each by 0.3–0.6% average, validating that the budget mechanism is method-agnostic.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPLIT sits at the intersection of two previously separate token-dropping paradigms in VLMs: salience-based methods (FastV, HiRED, SparseVLM) that use attention scores to rank tokens, and diversity-based methods (DART, DivPrune, GreedyPrune) that suppress redundancy via similarity thresholds. It builds directly on DART's theoretical framework—adopting its Hausdorff-distance bounds and Lipschitz-continuity assumptions as the foundation for proving that duplicate removal is safe—while addressing DART's key weakness of redundant pivot selection. Structurally, it adapts HiRED's region-level budget allocation idea but replaces the CLS-attention signal with a temporal-shift metric (normalized layer-to-layer hidden-state change), thereby decoupling importance estimation from dense attention computation and making the method compatible with FlashAttention kernels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for deployment: SPLIT achieves 1.80× total and 2.76× prefilling speedup on LLaVA-Next-7B at 88.9% token compression while retaining ~93% accuracy, and the temporal-shift signal avoids the attention-map computation that limits FlashAttention compatibility in prior salience-based methods. The theoretical contribution (Theorem 2) cleanly shows that adaptive region budgets yield tighter coverage guarantees than uniform allocation, giving practitioners a principled justification for the design. Open questions include whether the fixed 16-region grid generalizes to dynamic-resolution models (e.g., Qwen-VL's variable token counts), whether the LLM-side cross-modal extension (SPLIT-LLM-B) can be made latency-neutral, and whether the method extends to audio or 3D modalities where the spatial partitioning assumption may not hold.
