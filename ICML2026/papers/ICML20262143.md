## Problem

Inferring synaptic connectivity from neural population recordings is fundamentally degenerate: many distinct connectivity structures produce identical dynamics, yet standard methods (e.g., LINT) return a single point estimate whose spurious features depend on initialization and training hyperparameters. The paper asks how to infer a *distribution* over connectivity that is maximally unbiased over unidentifiable components while still matching the observed dynamics, rather than committing to one arbitrary solution.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262143/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method requires a pre-trained lrRNN (via LINT or variational sequential Monte Carlo) as a prerequisite, consuming its learned loadings M, B, d, the estimated N, and latent trajectories z alongside the original neural firing rates and external inputs. It outputs a full generative model p(m,n,b,d) from which arbitrarily many new networks can be sampled, each reproducing the same mean-field dynamics. The conditional covariance S of p(n|m,b,d) is a free hyperparameter that parameterizes the residual degeneracy; different S values yield different admissible connectivity distributions with identical dynamics.

## Method

Connector decomposes the connectivity distribution into p(m,b,d) and p(n|m,b,d) and learns each separately. For p(m,b,d), it trains a continuous normalizing flow via flow matching on the rows of the trained lrRNN's M, B, d matrices, yielding a flexible density estimator that can capture multimodality and heavy tails. For p(n|m,b,d), it exploits the key theoretical result that mean-field dynamics depend only on the first moment μ(m,b,d)=E[n|m,b,d], estimated via ℓ₂-regularized linear regression of latent updates on population activity; the maximum entropy distribution with this fixed mean and a chosen covariance S is then Gaussian N(μ,S). The framework is grounded in a characterization of three identifiability sources (linear ambiguity of z, rank conditions on p(m,b,d), and first-moment-only identifiability of p(n|m,b,d)), and introduces a distribution-level dissimilarity measure combining Sinkhorn optimal transport over (m,b,d) with a conditional-mean term over n to compare networks of unequal size.

## Results

On synthetic datasets (quadstable attractors, bistable attractors, limit cycles, ring attractors), Connector accurately recovers the ground-truth connectivity distribution p(m,n) when the conditional covariance S matches the true value, and correctly identifies cell types via clustering (k-means and GMM). Under in silico perturbation (silencing one cell type), Connector-inferred cell types reproduce the ground-truth perturbed dynamics, whereas LINT-based cell types do not. On rat frontal cortex recordings (K_obs=240), Connector identifies at least 2 computational cell types whose relative contributions to the flow field vary across state space, with changes coinciding with decision-commitment turning points. The proposed dissimilarity measure (Eq. 11) correctly assigns the lowest D(True, Inferred) on the diagonal across all four dynamical systems, while the Valente et al. (2022) measure does not.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is LINT (Valente et al., 2022), which infers a single connectivity matrix from trained lrRNN parameters. On the quadstable attractor task, LINT correctly recovers p(m) but its inferred p(n|m) deviates substantially from ground truth and depends on initialization and training hyperparameters (Figure S5), whereas Connector recovers the full p(m,n) distribution when S is set correctly. Under perturbation (silencing one of four cell types), Connector-inferred clusters produce dynamics matching the ground-truth perturbed network, while LINT-based clusters do not (Figure 3). On the rat frontal cortex dataset, clustering the raw LINT connectivity matrix fails to reveal interpretable cell types, while Connector's distribution-based clustering identifies at least 2 clusters with spatially varying functional roles (Figure 5). For the dissimilarity comparison (Figure 4), the paper's measure (Eq. 11, combining Sinkhorn divergence and conditional-mean difference) correctly ranks the true-inferred pair as most similar across all four dynamical systems, whereas the Valente et al. effective-connectivity correlation measure does not, particularly for nonlinear activation functions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the lrRNN framework (Mastrogiuseppe & Ostojic, 2018) and its data-driven training via LINT (Valente et al., 2022) by shifting the inference target from a single connectivity matrix to a full distribution over connectivity, p(m,n,b,d). It draws on continuous normalizing flows (Chen et al., 2018) trained via flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023) as the flexible density estimator, and on classical mean-field theory (Beiran et al., 2021; Sompolinsky et al., 1988) to formalize which aspects of the distribution are actually constrained by observed dynamics. The key conceptual departure is the maximum-entropy selection principle (Jaynes, 1957; Schneidman et al., 2006), which resolves the degeneracy among admissible solutions by choosing the least-assuming distribution, rather than accepting whatever a particular optimization run produces.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled answer to a long-standing degeneracy problem in circuit inference: multiple connectivity structures can generate identical population dynamics, and standard point-estimate methods silently pick one arbitrary solution. By characterizing three sources of non-identifiability and selecting the maximum-entropy distribution, Connector distinguishes computationally necessary structure from optimization artifacts, which is directly relevant for generating testable perturbation hypotheses. Key limitations include the conditional covariance S being a free hyperparameter (not identifiable from data alone), the assumption of time-invariant connectivity, and the lack of Dale's-law constraints. Open questions include extending to time-varying connectivity, E/I-balanced networks, and more expressive LVMs beyond lrRNNs, where identifiability degrades further.
