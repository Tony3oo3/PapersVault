## Problem

Neural-based MILP branching methods treat all B&B nodes uniformly, ignoring systematic distributional shifts between upstream and downstream nodes that degrade branching accuracy. Additionally, the inherent imbalance of the B&B tree means upstream nodes—where decisions most impact solving efficiency—are severely underrepresented in training data, and collecting strong branching labels for them is computationally prohibitive (e.g., ~68 hours for the equivalent of 30% additional samples).

## Input / Output

Each B&B node is encoded as a bipartite graph with constraint vertices (5 features: obj_cos_sim, bias, is_tight, dualsol_val, age), variable vertices (13 features: type, coef, bounds, solution status, reduced cost, etc.), and edge features (normalized coefficients), all extracted from SCIP 7.0.1. The GCNN encoder produces a graph-level embedding via mean pooling over variable embeddings, which is scored against each candidate variable to yield a branching score. At upstream nodes (identified by candidate-set size relative to root), a hybrid strategy selects top-k=5 candidates via SC-MILP and applies strong branching among them; downstream nodes use SC-MILP directly.

## Method

SC-MILP first partitions B&B nodes into m strata via K-means clustering on 7 node-level features (depth, lower bound, fractional variable count, etc.), ordered by representative depth. To address upstream scarcity, it generates augmented training data through two theoretically equivalent derivations (diagonal sign-flip linear transformation LT-MILP and redundant-constraint addition RC-MILP, both proven to preserve feasible regions and strong branching outcomes) and three lightweight perturbations (Gaussian noise on objective, constraints, or dual variables), applied at 35% and 10% probability respectively. The core training loss combines supervised cross-entropy (imitating strong branching) with a dynamic stratified contrastive loss: nodes in the same stratum serve as positives while cross-stratum nodes are negatives, with a learnable weight w(g(i),g(j)) = σ(Σ_{l=1}^{|g(i)−g(j)|} log(1+exp(θ_l))) that monotonically increases repulsion strength with stratum distance, enforcing smooth separation between adjacent strata and stronger discrimination across distant ones. The balancing coefficient λ is learnable and set to zero at inference.

## Results

On four generated MILP benchmarks (Set Covering, Combinatorial Auction, Capacitated Facility Location, Maximum Independent Set) at three difficulty levels, SC-MILP achieves the shortest solving time and most wins (77.08% overall: 66% easy, 75.25% medium, 87% hard), reducing average solving time by 12.36% over the SOTA MILP-Evolve and 26.48% over GCNN. On branching accuracy, SC-MILP attains the best acc@1 across all benchmarks (68.9% Set Covering, 66.4% Combinatorial Auction, 71.3% Capacitated Facility Location, 65.3% Maximum Independent Set), outperforming the strongest baseline TGAT by 0.4% on average. On six real-world MILP benchmarks (MIK, CORLAT, MIPLIB neos/supportcase, Load Balancing, Anonymous), SC-MILP achieves an average solving-time reduction of 5.15% over MILP-Evolve and 22.83% over full strong branching.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SC-MILP outperforms all 15 baselines on solving time across every dataset and difficulty level. Against the strongest neural baseline MILP-Evolve (Li et al., 2025), SC-MILP reduces solving time by 12.36% on average (21.25% easy, 20.45% medium, 11.25% hard) and achieves 77.08% wins versus MILP-Evolve's single-digit win rates. Against GCNN (Gasse et al., 2019), the improvement is 26.48% in solving time. On branching accuracy (Table 2), SC-MILP beats TGAT (Seyfi et al., 2023) by 0.4% acc@1, 5.1% acc@3, 4.0% acc@5, and 0.7% acc@10 on average, despite TGAT using a substantially more complex temporo-attentional architecture. On real-world benchmarks (Table 5), SC-MILP achieves the best solving time on all six datasets, reducing time by 5.15% over MILP-Evolve, 9.86% over GCNN, and 22.83% over full strong branching (FSB). Traditional methods (FSB, RPB) are consistently outperformed by all neural methods, confirming the efficacy of learned branching; among RL-based methods (Symb4CO, GS4CO, DQN-BBMDP), SC-MILP achieves substantially higher win rates (e.g., 74/100 vs. 4/100 on Set Covering Medium). The ablation (Table 3) confirms that removing upstream-augmented MILP derivation increases solving time by 14.7% and removing dynamic stratified contrastive learning increases it by 18.9%, with the largest upstream acc@1 drops (3.1% and 0.3% additional) when these components are removed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GCNN-based bipartite graph paradigm for MILP branching (Gasse et al., 2019) by introducing a dynamic stratified contrastive training objective that modifies Supervised Contrastive Learning (Khosla et al., 2020) with distance-aware weights between feature-clustered node groups. It departs from CAMBranch (Lin et al., 2024), which also applies contrastive learning to branching but defines positive pairs narrowly as original-augmentation pairs, whereas SC-MILP structures contrast through stratified groups with progressive separation. The upstream-augmented MILP derivation (equivalent linear transformations and redundant constraints with theoretical guarantees, plus lightweight perturbations) addresses data scarcity in a way that is structurally grounded in MILP algebra rather than generic augmentation, distinguishing it from prior data-centric approaches like LLM-generated MILPs (Li et al., 2025).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper tackles a practically critical but under-addressed problem: upstream B&B nodes disproportionately determine solving time yet are severely underrepresented in training data, and existing methods treat all depths uniformly. The theoretical guarantees for equivalent MILP derivation (preserving both feasible regions and strong branching outcomes) provide a principled foundation for augmentation that avoids the distributional distortion of naively regenerating expert samples. Open questions include whether the static K-means stratification adapts well to heterogeneous problem classes, whether the approach generalizes across solvers beyond SCIP, and whether the hybrid branching strategy (neural + strong branching at upstream nodes) can be fully replaced by a purely learned policy as model quality improves.
