## Problem

Existing multi-scale time-series forecasting methods rely on fixed, discrete scaling parameters applied uniformly across all timesteps, which cannot represent temporal dynamics whose characteristic scales are real-valued and vary over time. Moreover, no principled mathematical foundation has been established to unify the diverse scaling operators (pooling, subsampling, wavelets, etc.) used across the literature, leaving their structural limitations implicit.

## Input / Output

The model takes a multivariate time series of fixed observation length L=96 and produces a T-step forecast with T ∈ {96, 192, 336, 720} for long-term tasks, or frequency-specific prediction lengths for the M4 short-term benchmark. Each variable is processed independently (channel independence) after reversible instance normalization, and the learnable scale parameters are optimized as dataset-level parameters during training and held fixed at inference, rather than being predicted per-sample.

## Method

SIGMA introduces a learnable discrete Gaussian (LDG) kernel where each temporal position i receives its own continuous scale parameter s_i, making smoothing distance-aware and position-dependent via the modified Bessel function I_{|i-j|}(s_i). This kernel is proven to be the unique symmetric operator satisfying the discrete scale-space axioms, and it generalizes all classical discrete scaling operators (pooling, moving average, subsampling) as special cases at integer scales. The forecasting module is a lightweight MLP that concatenates the smoothed component K(s)X with its residual (I−K(s))X—mirroring a trend-seasonal decomposition—then applies a single MLP with a skip connection and projection heads to produce the forecast. Because the LDG kernel already encodes coherent cross-scale structure at the representation level, no additional multi-scale mixing or aggregation modules are needed, yielding up to 5.3× faster training and 3.8× lower memory than competing multi-scale architectures.

## Results

On long-term forecasting (Table 2), SIGMA achieves the best MSE in 13 out of 16 dataset-horizon settings, with particularly strong gains on high-dimensional datasets: 5.4% lower MSE on Electricity (0.175 vs. 0.185 for TimeMixer) and 7.8% lower MSE on Traffic (0.458 vs. 0.501 for TimeMixer). On the M4 short-term benchmark, SIGMA attains the best weighted-average SMAPE (11.840), MASE (1.585), and OWA (0.868), winning 11 of 15 individual category-metric combinations. Beyond accuracy, SIGMA reduces memory consumption by up to 3.8× and improves training speed by up to 5.3× compared to the strongest baseline (AMD).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SIGMA outperforms all eight multi-scale baselines on 13 of 16 long-term evaluation settings. The largest margins appear on high-dimensional datasets: on Traffic, SIGMA's MSE of 0.458 beats the next-best TimeMixer (0.501) by 7.8%, and on Electricity, 0.175 beats TimeMixer (0.185) by 5.4%. On Weather, WPMixer edges out SIGMA on MSE (0.245 vs. 0.247), though SIGMA matches it on MAE (0.273); the paper attributes this to Weather's low dimensionality and high intrinsic forecastability, where discrete scaling suffices. On the M4 short-term benchmark, SIGMA leads in 11 of 15 category-metric cases, with the clearest wins on Quarterly (OWA 0.886 vs. 0.892 for MultiPatch.) and Monthly (OWA 0.882 vs. 0.887 for MultiPatch.), while losing narrowly on the small 'Others' category to TimeMixer. AMD (Hu et al., 2025a) is the strongest overall competitor but is consistently outperformed by SIGMA on both accuracy and efficiency (3.8× less memory, 5.3× faster training). Pyraformer (Liu et al., 2022b) is the weakest baseline across nearly all settings, with MSE values 2–10× higher than SIGMA on ETT and Traffic datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the discrete scale-space theory of Lindeberg (2002), which establishes the discrete Gaussian as the unique smoothing kernel satisfying scale-space axioms, and repurposes it for time-series forecasting with learnable, position-dependent scale parameters. It departs from the multi-scale forecasting literature (TimeMixer, AMD, TimesNet, Pyraformer, etc.) by formalizing their disparate scaling operations—pooling, subsampling, wavelet decomposition, moving averages—under a single 'scaling operator family' definition, then showing that all existing methods are restricted to discrete, uniform scales. The key architectural departure is replacing complex multi-scale mixing modules with a single LDG kernel followed by a lightweight MLP, arguing that coherent cross-scale structure is already preserved at the representation level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled theoretical foundation for multi-scale time-series modeling, clarifying why discrete scaling is suboptimal (Theorem 4.2's quantization gap) and why the Gaussian kernel is the natural continuous generalization. The practical impact is significant: 5.3× training speedup and 3.8× memory reduction over the strongest baseline while improving accuracy, making multi-scale modeling accessible at scale. Key limitations include dataset-level (not sample-level) scale parameters, O(L²) kernel computation (mitigable via FFT), and weaker gains on low-dimensional, high-forecastability datasets like Weather. Open questions include whether sample-specific dynamic scaling can be stabilized and whether the framework extends naturally to multivariate cross-channel scaling.
