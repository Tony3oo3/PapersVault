## Problem

Masked self-supervised learning (SSL) has become a foundational training paradigm for transformers, yet fundamental questions remain open: how much data is needed for good generalization, how performance depends on the correlation structure of the data, and what the learned predictor's spectrum reveals about the underlying geometry. Unlike standard regression, masked SSL produces a joint matrix-valued predictor whose coordinates are strongly statistically dependent, making existing random matrix theory results for individual ridge estimators insufficient.

## Input / Output

The method takes n real-valued sequences of length d (forming a data matrix X ∈ R^{n×d}) and a ridge regularization parameter λ > 0, and produces a d×d matrix-valued predictor Â (the SSR matrix) with zero diagonal, where each column â_k is the ridge-regression solution for predicting coordinate k from all others under the constraint a_k = 0. The theoretical outputs are closed-form asymptotic expressions for the generalization and training errors (depending only on the population covariance Σ, the ratio α = n/d, and λ) and a deterministic equivalent for the resolvent of Â that characterizes its full spectral distribution. The analysis operates in the proportional high-dimensional regime where n, d → ∞ with n/d → α.

## Method

The key structural insight is that the aggregate predictor admits the closed form Â = I_d − Q(λ)[diag(Q(λ))]^{−1}, where Q(λ) = (Σ̂ + λI_d)^{−1} is the resolvent of the sample covariance, reducing the problem to analyzing a product of the resolvent with a diagonal matrix that is itself a nonlinear function of the resolvent. To handle this self-referential structure, the authors first establish sharp concentration of the diagonal entries of Q(λ) around their deterministic limit, then apply a linearization trick to a 2d×2d block matrix and use a leave-one-out / Sherman-Morrison argument to derive a self-consistent equation for the deterministic equivalent of the resolvent of Λ^{1/2}Q(λ)Λ^{1/2}. This yields explicit asymptotic formulas for the generalization and training errors (Theorems 3.5 and 3.7) and the full spectral density of Â (Theorem 3.8). For spiked covariance models, the analysis reveals a BBP-type phase transition in the spectrum of Â at the same critical SNR threshold as the sample covariance matrix, while for AR(1) Toeplitz covariance, SSR provably outperforms PCA unless the number of PCA directions is extensive in d.

## Results

The paper derives a closed-form asymptotic limit for the generalization error of the self-supervised ridge (SSR) predictor (Theorem 3.5), showing it depends only on the population covariance Σ, the sample ratio α = n/d, and the regularization λ. For the spectral structure, Theorem 3.8 provides a deterministic equivalent for the resolvent of the SSR matrix, and Corollary 3.9 establishes that in the ridgeless limit with diagonal Σ, the spectral density is universal (independent of Σ) with support [−2/(√α−1), 2/(√α+1)]. For the spiked covariance model, Proposition 4.2 shows the SSR matrix undergoes a BBP-type phase transition at the same critical SNR θ* = 1/√α as the sample covariance matrix. For the AR(1) Toeplitz model, Proposition 4.4 gives an explicit threshold γ*(ρ) on the fraction of PCA directions needed for PCA to match SSR performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is PCA (following Reiss & Wahl, 2020), compared against SSR in the population (infinite-data) limit for two structured covariance models. For the spiked covariance model Σ = I_d + θvv^⊤, PCA strictly outperforms SSR for any number of directions p ≥ 1 and any SNR θ (Lemma 4.1); even the SSR approximation error is not better than L = 1 (no reconstruction), because the spike introduces only vanishingly weak correlations in high dimensions. In sharp contrast, for the AR(1) Toeplitz covariance Σ_{jk} = ρ^{|j−k|}, SSR strictly outperforms PCA unless the fraction of PCA directions γ = p/d exceeds an explicit threshold γ*(ρ) that increases with ρ (Proposition 4.4). Concretely, for moderate ρ (e.g., ρ = 0.5), PCA needs γ ≈ 0.3 or more directions to match SSR, while for strong temporal correlation (ρ → 1), PCA must use γ → 1 (i.e., nearly all d directions) to compete. This demonstrates that masked self-supervised learning provably exploits sequential structure in a way that classical spectral methods cannot match without extensive dimensionality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the random matrix theory (RMT) toolkit developed for individual high-dimensional ridge regression (Dobriban & Wager, 2018; Wu & Xu, 2020; Hastie et al., 2022) to the fundamentally different problem of analyzing an *aggregate* matrix-valued predictor, where all coordinate-wise estimators are trained on the same data matrix and thus exhibit strong cross-coordinate dependencies. The technical machinery draws on deterministic equivalents for sample covariance resolvents (Couillet & Liao, 2022) and the double-trace formula of Bach (2024), but the key departure is that the SSR matrix involves a nonlinear product Q(λ)[diag(Q(λ))]⁻¹ that does not belong to any standard random matrix ensemble, requiring a new linearization and leave-one-out argument. The paper also connects to the BBP phase transition literature (Baik et al., 2005) by showing the SSR matrix undergoes the same spectral transition as the sample covariance matrix, and positions itself relative to the growing body of RMT analyses for kernels, random features, and in-context learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first precise high-dimensional asymptotic analysis of a masked self-supervised learning objective, providing closed-form expressions for generalization error and spectral structure that depend only on the population covariance. The clean separation between the spiked covariance case (where PCA strictly dominates SSR) and the AR(1) case (where SSR can beat PCA unless p = Θ(d)) gives practitioners a principled understanding of when the sequential inductive bias of masked modeling is genuinely advantageous over classical spectral methods. The main limitation is that the analysis is restricted to linear ridge regression; extending these tools to genuinely nonlinear masked models (e.g., transformer-based MLM or MAE) remains an open and important direction. The universality result—that the ridgeless-limit spectrum is independent of the covariance structure for diagonal Σ—suggests a robustness property that may generalize to more complex settings.
