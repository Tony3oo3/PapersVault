## Problem

Existing prompt-optimization pipelines treat instruction optimization (IO) and in-context learning (ICL) demonstration selection as separate problems, optimizing each independently and combining them post hoc under the implicit assumption that a 'best' instruction and a 'best' demonstration set compose well. In practice, instructions and demonstrations interact strongly: a demonstration set optimal for one instruction may underperform for another, making decoupled pipelines brittle. The paper identifies that ICL performance exhibits consistent diminishing returns across diverse instructions and leverages this structural regularity to enable principled, query-adaptive joint selection of the instruction-demonstration pair.

## Input / Output

At inference, SMILE takes a test query, a pool of N labeled demonstrations, a candidate instruction set (typically |T|=10 generated by an upstream proposer), and an ICL budget K (e.g., 10), and outputs the best instruction-demonstration pair to condition the LLM for final generation. The surrogate is trained offline using a fixed budget B of LLM reward evaluations (normalized teacher-forced log-likelihood) on a held-out development set, learning only non-negative calibration weights over fixed modular channels (embeddings, ROUGE-L, information gain). At test time, no additional LLM calls are required beyond the final generation—selection is performed entirely via the learned surrogate and precomputed per-example signals, with optional prefiltering of the instruction pool by a cheap query-instruction score to reduce cost when |T| is large.

## Method

SMILE instantiates an Extended Deep Submodular Function (EDSF) as an instruction-conditioned surrogate f(S; q, T) that scores demonstration sets, combining three concave-over-modular experts via a hard min (robust conjunction): a sample-sample expert (k-means bucket coverage with soft assignment weights), a sample-query expert (cosine similarity + ROUGE-L F1), and a sample-instruction expert (per-example information gain relative to a default instruction + softmax-weighted prototype similarity). The surrogate is trained with a logistic pairwise ranking loss over LLM-feedback-derived preferences (within-instruction and cross-instruction), using a fixed-temperature softmin relaxation for gradient stability during training while reverting to hard min at inference to preserve the bottleneck semantics. A separate query-instruction scorer s_QI(q, T) captures query-dependent instruction preference, and the final pair is selected by maximizing s_QI(q,T) + f(S_T; q, T) over candidate instructions, with S_T obtained by greedy submodular maximization. The paper provides a high-probability guarantee (Theorem 2.5) showing greedy selection achieves a near-(1−1/e) approximation ratio even when exact submodularity does not strictly hold, under a mild per-step good-step condition that holds with probability at least 1−ε at each iteration.

## Results

On Qwen3-4B-Instruct, SMILE achieves an average score of 63.0 across six datasets, outperforming the strongest joint baseline APE+R.S. (59.8) by 3.2 points and MIPRO (59.5) by 3.5 points. On Llama3.1-8B-Instruct, SMILE reaches 60.7 average, beating APE+R.S. (57.4) by 3.3 and MIPRO (56.7) by 4.0. Gains are most pronounced on reasoning tasks: SMILE improves Date from 56.4 to 63.2 and Salient from 66.4 to 70.0 on Qwen3-4B, and from 59.2 to 66.0 and 58.4 to 67.2 on Llama3.1-8B, respectively. SMILE also transfers to black-box APIs, with SMILE-L achieving 84.9 average on GPT-5.2 versus 83.1 for APE+R.S.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SMILE consistently outperforms all seven baselines on average across both LLM backbones. Against IO-only methods, SMILE beats APE (54.0/54.4 avg) by 9.0/6.3 points and OPRO (56.8/55.1) by 6.2/5.6 points on Qwen3-4B/Llama3.1-8B, indicating that instruction optimization alone is insufficient without query-adaptive demonstration selection. Against ICL-only methods, SMILE surpasses Nearest (59.0/55.2) by 4.0/5.5 points, with the largest gap on GPQA (38.9 vs. 36.9 on Qwen3-4B; 30.3 vs. 23.2 on Llama3.1-8B), suggesting that instruction-demonstration compatibility matters most for scientific reasoning. Against the strongest post-hoc joint baseline APE+R.S. (59.8/57.4), SMILE gains 3.2/3.3 points, with the advantage concentrated on reasoning tasks (Date: +6.8/+6.8; Salient: +3.6/+8.8), supporting the paper's claim that query-adaptive joint selection outperforms fixed instruction-demonstration pairing. Against MIPRO (59.5/56.7), SMILE leads by 3.5/4.0 points, again with the largest margins on Date and Salient. On XSum (summarization), SMILE matches or slightly trails MIPRO (18.5 vs. 18.6 on Qwen3-4B), suggesting the gains are task-dependent and most relevant for heterogeneous reasoning workloads.

## Relation to Prior Work

SMILE builds on the Extended Deep Submodular Function (EDSF) framework of Hosseini et al. (2024) and the concave-over-modular construction of Bilmes & Bai (2017), repurposing them as a learned surrogate for ICL utility rather than as hand-crafted objectives. It departs from prior submodular ICL methods (Liu et al., 2022; Kumari et al., 2024) that use fixed proxies like embedding similarity or facility-location coverage, by calibrating the surrogate to actual LLM feedback via pairwise ranking. Relative to joint IO+ICL baselines (MIPRO, APE+R.S., Wan et al., 2024), SMILE's key conceptual shift is query-adaptive selection: the optimal instruction-demonstration pair is chosen per query rather than fixed globally, and the EDSF explicitly models sample-instruction interactions that prior post-hoc combinations ignore.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, theoretically grounded framework for joint prompt optimization that respects the empirically observed diminishing-returns structure of ICL, offering a clean alternative to ad-hoc 'optimize-then-compose' pipelines. The high-probability greedy guarantee under approximate submodularity (Theorem 2.5) is a useful theoretical contribution for practitioners working with learned surrogates that are not exactly submodular. Practical impact is reinforced by demonstrated transfer to black-box APIs (GPT-5.2, Gemini 2.5-Flash) without access to internal signals. Open questions include behavior when diminishing returns break down (e.g., multi-step reasoning requiring complementary examples), scalability to more radical prompt restructuring beyond template selection, and online adaptation under distribution shift or model updates.
