## Problem

Large vision-language models hallucinate by letting linguistic priors override visual evidence at the token-decision level, yet existing training-free mitigations either act as external decoding policies or rely on attention-mass proxies (e.g., VAR) that are correlational rather than decision-aligned. Coarse head-rescaling interventions suppress both visual and textual routes indiscriminately, harming genuinely grounded reasoning. The paper asks whether one can identify and suppress only the prior-dominant textual pathway within individual attention heads while preserving visual grounding.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a pretrained LVLM (e.g., LLaVA-1.5-7B, Qwen-VL-Chat, Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct) that receives an RGB image plus a text prompt. At each decoding step it consumes internal activations—specifically the per-head attention weights, route-specific value outputs, and a single reverse-mode gradient of a task-dependent scalar score (log-probability of the target token for generation, or Yes/No logit margin for binary QA)—to compute per-head route effects. The output is the standard model output (generated caption tokens or a Yes/No decision), but with selectively gated text-route contributions in a small subset of attention heads. The method requires white-box access to activations and gradients, so it does not apply to closed-source APIs.

## Method

CRG decomposes each multi-head attention output into an exact visual-route component and text-route component via diagonal selection matrices on the value matrix, then defines do-style causal route effects (CRE) that measure how toggling each route changes a task-specific decision score. A one-forward/one-gradient first-order approximation (inner product of the score gradient with the route-specific head output) estimates these effects at negligible extra cost per decoded token. A Visual Reliance Index (VRI) normalizes the relative magnitude of visual versus textual effects to rank heads, and a sign-based taxonomy identifies two conflict regimes: Conflict-A (visual supports, text opposes the decision) and Conflict-B (visual opposes, text supports—the hallucination hallmark). At decoding time, CRG selects the top-k most prior-dominant heads within each conflict set by smallest VRI and applies a smooth rank-based gate schedule that suppresses only the text route (mildly for Conflict-A, strongly for Conflict-B) while leaving the visual route untouched, incurring roughly 2.3× latency with no additional GPU memory.

## Results

On POPE (the primary discriminative benchmark), CRG achieves the best accuracy across all three models and all three settings (Random, Popular, Adversarial), improving over Regular decoding by 5.5–7.0% accuracy and 4.2–8.2% F1. On CHAIR (caption-level hallucination), CRG reduces subject hallucination C_S from 52.8 to 34.2 and object hallucination C_I from 15.9 to 11.2 on LLaVA-1.5-7B, while maintaining recall (77.8 vs. 77.3). On AMBER, CRG lowers CHAIR from 8.3 to 4.6 and raises F1 from 73.7 to 77.5, yielding a +3.75 AMBER Score gain. On MME, CRG consistently improves over Regular decoding across nearly all categories, with particularly strong gains on grounding-sensitive skills (Existence, Count, Position, Color).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CRG outperforms all four training-free baselines (VCD, OPERA, PAI, VTI) on POPE across every model and setting. Against the strongest baseline VTI, CRG gains +0.8% accuracy on LLaVA-1.5-7B (Random), +1.8% on Qwen-VL-Chat (Random), and +1.0% on Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct (Random); the gap widens under Adversarial settings (e.g., +1.9% on LLaVA-1.5-7B). On CHAIR, CRG achieves the lowest hallucination counts (C_S = 34.2, C_I = 11.2 on LLaVA-1.5-7B) compared to VTI (37.6/12.9), OPERA (44.6/12.8), and VCD (51.6/14.9), while preserving or improving recall. On AMBER, CRG improves the composite AMBER Score by +3.75 over Regular (82.70 → 86.45), driven by both reduced CHAIR (8.3 → 4.6) and higher F1 (73.7 → 77.5). In terms of efficiency, CRG incurs 2.27× latency overhead with essentially zero additional GPU memory (×1.00), comparable to VCD (2.01×) and far cheaper than OPERA (7.12×, 1.52× memory).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the causal intervention lineage from Causal Head Gating (Nam et al., NeurIPS 2025) by decomposing each attention head into a visual route and a text route, enabling route-specific do-interventions rather than treating heads as atomic units. It departs from VAR-style attention-mass proxies (Jiang et al., 2025; Zheng & Zhang, 2025) by showing formally that attention allocation is not decision-aligned, and from output-level decoding controls (VCD, OPERA) by intervening on internal computation with a one-forward/one-gradient estimator. The conflict-aware gating strategy—suppressing only prior-dominant text routes while preserving visual routes—addresses a limitation of coarse head rescaling methods that indiscriminately suppress both routes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The non-identifiability theorem (Theorem A.6) provides a clean formal argument that attention-mass proxies cannot identify the sign of decision-aligned visual influence, which should shift the field away from VAR-based head selection toward decision-aligned causal measures. Practically, CRG offers a training-free, near-zero-memory-overhead intervention (2.27× latency, ×1.00 memory) that consistently reduces hallucination across five benchmarks and three model families, making it deployable in settings where retraining is infeasible. Key open questions include whether the first-order approximation remains reliable under strong cross-layer interactions or ambiguous visual evidence, whether the method extends to closed-source APIs, and how to balance faithfulness against descriptive richness when visual evidence is genuinely weak.
