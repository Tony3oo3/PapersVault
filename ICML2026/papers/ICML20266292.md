## Problem

While AI hallucination research has focused on model-side detection and behavioral user studies, the neurocognitive mechanisms by which human brains process hallucinated versus non-hallucinated MLLM-generated content remain unexplored. Specifically, it is unknown at which temporal processing stage (perceptual, semantic, inferential) hallucination detection succeeds or fails, and whether misjudged hallucinations engage the same neural pathways as correctly detected ones.

## Input / Output

Participants view a 6000 ms image followed by a sentence presented word-by-word at 750 ms per word, generated by Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct from 60 controlled AMBER/MSCOCO image-response pairs spanning entity, attribute, and relation hallucination types. EEG is recorded at 1000 Hz from 64 channels (Scan NuAmps Express, Quik-Cap), yielding 760-dimensional feature vectors per word combining differential entropy across five frequency bands in four ROIs plus ERP-window features. Outputs include ERP component amplitudes (N100, P200, N400, P600) across seven brain regions, binary hallucination predictions at word and sentence level, and behavioral recognition accuracy.

## Method

The study combines two complementary analyses. First, averaged ERP analysis partitions the 50–750 ms post-stimulus window into four time windows and applies repeated-measures ANOVA with FDR-corrected post-hoc comparisons across three conditions (HalluCorrect, NoHallu, HalluWrong) in seven brain regions, revealing that enhanced N100, P200, N400, and P600 amplitudes for hallucinated words appear only when participants correctly detect the hallucination, while HalluWrong vs. NoHallu shows no significant difference at any component or region. Second, supervised prediction experiments train SVM, GBDT, and a 2-layer Transformer Encoder (128-dim embeddings, 8 attention heads, 0.5 dropout) on the 760-dim EEG features for word-level and sentence-level binary classification, achieving AUC ≈ 0.94 for HalluCorrect vs. NoHallu but dropping to chance for HalluWrong vs. NoHallu, confirming that EEG carries discriminative hallucination signal only when the participant's conscious recognition is engaged.

## Results

For the core EEG prediction task (HalluCorrect vs. NoHallu), the SVM achieves the best word-level AUC of 0.9393 (within-subject) and 0.8631 (cross-subject), while the attention-based model achieves the best sentence-level AUC of 0.9647 (within-subject) and 0.9846 (cross-subject). Critically, when comparing HalluWrong vs. NoHallu, all models drop to near-chance AUC (0.49–0.57), confirming that EEG signals only carry discriminative information when participants correctly recognize the hallucination. In the direct comparison against AI-based hallucination detection methods (Table 9), the EEG-based cross-subject attention model achieves AUC 0.931, outperforming all model-internal baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The EEG-based cross-subject attention model (AUC 0.931) outperforms all four AI-based hallucination detection baselines on the same constructed dataset. It beats the uncertainty-based TokenConfidence method [Su et al., 2024] by 3.9 AUC points (0.931 vs. 0.892), the confidence-based SelfCheckGPT consistency approach [Manakul et al., 2023] by 5.0 points (0.931 vs. 0.881), the semantic-entropy/PPL uncertainty method [Farquhar et al., 2024] by 5.5 points (0.931 vs. 0.876), and the self-verification prompt approach [Su et al., 2024] by 8.0 points (0.931 vs. 0.851). The authors attribute this advantage to two factors: (1) the EEG signals are derived from averaged ERP responses across 27 participants, yielding a more stable representation, and (2) neural signals provide an intrinsic process-level measurement rather than relying on post-hoc behavioral outputs or heuristic model-internal statistics. Notably, the EEG-based approach's advantage is conditional: it only works when participants correctly recognize the hallucination, whereas the model-internal baselines operate independently of human perception.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of AI hallucination research and cognitive neuroscience. On the AI side, it situates itself alongside model-centric hallucination detection work (SelfCheckGPT, semantic entropy, retrieval-augmented generation) but shifts the lens to the human perceiver. On the neuroscience side, it repurposes standard ERP methodology (N100, P200, N400, P600) well-established in language comprehension and anomaly-detection literature (Ye et al. 2022; Pinkosova et al. 2022) to the entirely new domain of MLLM-generated hallucination verification. No prior work, to the authors' knowledge, has recorded EEG while participants judge AI-generated hallucinated content, making this the first neuroimaging study of that specific cognitive process.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that misjudged hallucinations produce no significant ERP difference from non-hallucinated content—suggests that fluent, contextually plausible AI hallucinations can bypass the brain's standard fact-verification circuitry entirely, with implications for AI safety in high-stakes domains. The demonstration that EEG carries discriminative signal only when humans correctly recognize hallucinations opens a path toward neural-based implicit feedback for real-time hallucination warning systems. Key limitations include the small sample (n=27), laboratory-only setting, and limited hallucination-type granularity; open questions include how domain expertise modulates these neural signatures, whether effects generalize to naturalistic reading, and whether online EEG decoding can feed adaptive user interfaces.
