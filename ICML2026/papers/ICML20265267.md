## Problem

Lifelong multimodal knowledge editing requires storing many sequential updates without catastrophic forgetting, and modular parameter isolation (e.g., LoRA-based MoE editors) is the current solution. However, this strategy suffers from a scalability bottleneck: dense or low-rank expert matrices cause linear memory growth, and managing the growing repository requires a separate, decoupled routing module (external retriever), introducing architectural redundancy and additional inference overhead.

## Input / Output

Each edit instance is a triplet (image, text prompt, target output), and the method injects a residual perturbation at a fixed intermediate layer (default layer 21) of a frozen VLM backbone (BLIP-2 or LLaVA-v1.5). At inference, the hidden state at that layer is layer-normalized, reshaped into a 2D matrix (s₁×s₂ where s₁·s₂ = H), and passed through the active experts to produce an edited hidden state that propagates to the final text output. The system maintains a persistent expert repository that grows with each new edit, with no modification to the base model weights.

## Method

TIME parameterizes each knowledge edit as a rank-R CP decomposition of a 4-way mapping tensor (s₁×s₂×s₁×s₂), reducing per-expert parameters from O(rH) (LoRA) to O(R√H). The key insight is intrinsic routing: the input-side CP factors (U^in, V^in) naturally define an activation subspace, so expert relevance is determined by projecting the input onto these factors—eliminating any external retriever. A structure-aware contrastive alignment loss explicitly trains the input factors to be discriminative, maximizing the target expert's projection score relative to negative experts. At inference, experts exceeding a threshold are sparsely selected and their residuals are combined via a softmax-weighted convex mixture, with theoretical guarantees of boundedness and Lipschitz stability for the combined operator.

## Results

On lifelong editing (Gap=1000) with BLIP2, TIME achieves an average score of 94.15 on E-VQA and 82.53 on E-IC, outperforming the strongest baseline LiveEdit (93.69 and 82.22 respectively). On LLaVA-v1.5 lifelong editing, TIME reaches 93.14 (E-VQA) and 81.12 (E-IC), again surpassing LiveEdit (92.76 and 79.92). In terms of efficiency, TIME reduces parameter storage by ~33% (523 MB vs. 668 MB on BLIP2; 786 MB vs. 1178 MB on LLaVA) and cuts editing latency by ~32% for single edits and ~37% for lifelong edits compared to LiveEdit.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TIME outperforms the strongest modular baseline LiveEdit on lifelong editing across all four dataset-backbone combinations: +0.46 avg on E-VQA/BLIP2 (94.15 vs. 93.69), +0.31 on E-IC/BLIP2 (82.53 vs. 82.22), +0.38 on E-VQA/LLaVA (93.14 vs. 92.76), and +1.20 on E-IC/LLaVA (81.12 vs. 79.92). Traditional hypernetwork editors collapse catastrophically in lifelong settings: MEND drops to 0.06 average on LLaVA E-VQA (Gap=1000), and LEMoE falls to 31.67 on BLIP2 E-VQA, confirming that non-modular or loosely-coupled approaches cannot isolate sequential updates. RECIPE (85.16 avg on E-VQA/BLIP2) and SERAC (63.05) maintain moderate reliability but suffer significant locality degradation (I-Loc drops to 87.73 and 13.13 respectively), indicating interference with unedited knowledge. In single-edit settings (Gap=1), TIME is competitive but slightly trails LiveEdit on E-VQA/BLIP2 (96.70 vs. 96.94 avg), while matching or exceeding it on E-IC (87.69 vs. 87.52). The key differentiator is efficiency: TIME achieves these gains with 33% fewer parameters and 32–37% lower editing latency by eliminating the external routing module that LiveEdit requires.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the modular expert-repository paradigm for lifelong knowledge editing (LiveEdit, LEMoE, RECIPE) by replacing LoRA-based experts with CP-decomposed tensors and, more critically, by eliminating the decoupled external retriever that all prior modular methods require. The key conceptual departure is 'intrinsic routing': the input-side factors of the CP decomposition simultaneously define the editing subspace and the routing criterion, so the expert parameters serve as their own router. This draws on the tensor decomposition literature (Kolda & Bader, 2009) and the Khatri-Rao product structure, but repurposes them for a self-contained routing mechanism rather than for pure compression. The paper also builds on the broader MoE routing literature (Mixtral, DeepSeekMoE) but inverts the standard design: instead of a learned gating network selecting among experts, the experts' own geometry determines activation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The intrinsic routing idea addresses a real scalability bottleneck: as lifelong editing repositories grow to thousands of experts, the auxiliary routing infrastructure (stored keys, feature extractors) becomes a non-trivial memory and latency cost that the paper quantifies at ~33% of total parameters. The cross-architecture ablation (Table 5) provides a useful negative result: CP tensors and intrinsic routing are mathematically coupled and cannot be grafted onto decoupled architectures, suggesting that future editing systems should co-design storage format and selection logic. Limitations include the fundamental expressivity trade-off of low-rank CP (acknowledged by the authors), the reliance on a fixed target layer, and the fact that performance gains over LiveEdit are modest (~0.5 points average) despite the architectural simplification. Open questions include whether dynamic rank adaptation can close the expressivity gap and whether intrinsic routing generalizes to non-CP parameterizations.
