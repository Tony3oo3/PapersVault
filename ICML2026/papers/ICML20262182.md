## Problem

Existing graph-based offline goal-conditioned navigation methods rely on dense keypoint coverage of the dataset support, making planning computationally expensive (O(|Z|³) for Floyd-Warshall), and they lack any mechanism to adapt to topological changes such as new obstacles at test time. Additionally, contrastive and representation-learning approaches produce uncalibrated similarities rather than true time-to-reach distances, limiting their utility as geometric priors for principled navigation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an offline dataset of trajectories (state-action sequences) and, at inference, takes the agent's current state (position, orientation, velocity), an 11×11 depth raycast observation, and a goal position. It produces a learned time-to-reach quasimetric d_θ, a goal-conditioned policy π_ψ, and a sparse directed keypoint graph G=(Z,E) with edge weights given by the quasimetric. At test time, the system outputs a planned path through keypoints and, upon detecting budget overruns, dynamically prunes unreliable edges and replans a new route without retraining.

## Method

AQM unifies three components: (1) a time-to-reach quasimetric learned via Interval Quasimetric Embeddings (IQE) within the QRL framework, calibrated by a local one-step consistency loss (L_close) and a global separation loss (L_push) with an adaptive Lagrange multiplier; (2) a sparse topological graph built by Iterative Wavefront Expansion (IWE), a greedy heuristic for the maximal δ-separated set problem that grows keypoints outward like a wavefront to enforce both separation and connectivity, with formal covering and strong-connectivity guarantees under density assumptions; and (3) a goal-conditioned policy trained via Policy Bootstrapping, where a target policy masters micro-behaviors to near-future subgoals via Quasimetric Advantage Weighted Regression (Q-AWR) and a main policy distills these skills toward long-horizon goals via Quasimetric Subgoal Advantage Weighted (Q-SAW) KL-divergence minimization. At test time, AQM monitors a time-to-reach budget B(z_i, z_{i+1}) = (1+κ)·d_θ(z_i, z_{i+1}) on each edge; if elapsed traversal time exceeds this budget, the edge is marked untraversable and the agent performs zero-shot replanning via Floyd-Warshall on the pruned graph.

## Results

On the OGBench AntMaze suite, AQM achieves state-of-the-art success rates across all eight navigation tasks, reaching 88% on antmaze-giant-navigate and 90% on antmaze-giant-stitch, where the next-best graph-based method GAS achieves 78% and 88% respectively. Critically, AQM attains these results with up to 100× fewer keypoints (e.g., 158 nodes vs. 4,286 for GAS on giant-navigate), while maintaining competitive performance on medium and large mazes (98% and 94% respectively). On Continual NavBench test-time adaptation, AQM achieves an average diagonal success of 98% and demonstrates robust zero-shot replanning when previously open gates are closed, outperforming SAW (avg. 88%), HIQL (avg. 80%), and GCBC (avg. 53%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AQM outperforms all flat baselines (GCBC, GCIQL, QRL, CRL, SAW) by a wide margin on long-horizon tasks: on antmaze-giant-navigate, AQM reaches 88% versus 70% for SAW, 14% for QRL, and 0% for GCBC/GCIQL. Against the hierarchical method HIQL, AQM gains 21 points on giant-navigate (88% vs. 67%) and 89 points on giant-stitch (90% vs. 1%), where HIQL essentially fails. The closest competitor is the graph-based GAS (Baek et al., 2025), which AQM matches or exceeds on all tasks (e.g., 88% vs. 78% on giant-navigate, 90% vs. 88% on giant-stitch) while using 27–93× fewer graph nodes (158 vs. 4,286 on giant-navigate; 163 vs. 12,901 on giant-stitch). HILP (Park et al., 2024b) is competitive on medium tasks (96% vs. 98%) but collapses to 53% on giant-navigate and 0% on giant-stitch. On Continual NavBench test-time adaptation, AQM's budget-based edge invalidation enables successful zero-shot replanning when gates close (O→X), a capability none of the other baselines possess; GCBC and HIQL show near-total failure on off-diagonal (distribution-shift) evaluations.

## Relation to Prior Work

AQM builds directly on the Quasimetric RL (QRL) framework and Interval Quasimetric Embeddings (IQE) for learning calibrated time-to-reach distances, and on the Policy Bootstrapping / SAW paradigm for hierarchical policy distillation. Its closest conceptual predecessor is Graph-Assisted Stitching (GAS), which also combines a learned temporal geometry with graph-based planning and a low-level controller; AQM departs from GAS by replacing dense temporal-distance clustering with a principled greedy dominating-set approximation (Iterative Wavefront Expansion) grounded in covering theory, and by introducing a budget-based edge-invalidation mechanism for zero-shot test-time replanning. The Floyd-Warshall shortest-path structure is repurposed not just for planning but as the theoretical backbone connecting the quasimetric's triangle inequality to graph connectivity guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 100× reduction in keypoint count while matching or exceeding SOTA performance addresses a real deployment bottleneck: polynomial pathfinding complexity and nearest-keypoint lookup make dense graphs prohibitive in resource-constrained settings like live video games. The test-time adaptation capability (pruning unreliable edges and replanning without retraining) is practically important for Games-as-a-Service environments where map layouts change frequently. However, adaptation is strictly bounded by the behavioral coverage of the training data — when required recovery skills (e.g., long detours around non-jumpable walls) are absent from the dataset, replanning fails, and the authors acknowledge that stochastic environments and probabilistic graph formulations remain open.
