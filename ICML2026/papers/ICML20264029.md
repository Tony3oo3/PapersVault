## Problem

Policies for controlling spreading processes on networks (epidemics, information cascades, product adoption) suffer catastrophic performance degradation when transferred from simulation to deployment, with mean-field ODE-trained policies achieving negative utility at deployment. The paper traces this sim-to-real gap to three theoretical pathologies—Optimism Bias (Jensen's inequality underestimating variance), Hub Blindness (global aggregation obscuring super-spreader dynamics on scale-free networks), and the Valley of Death (mean-value critics failing on bimodal extinction-vs-viral outcomes)—and demonstrates that neither a scalable state representation nor a high-fidelity simulator alone suffices; both are required synergistically.

## Input / Output

The agent consumes a fixed-dimensional stratified observation (K×|S| ≈ 60 values) encoding per-tier state densities and mean holding times, which is independent of network size N and depends only on degree distribution statistics, augmented with tier-wise mean ages for non-Markovian dynamics. It produces a 2D continuous action vector—intervention intensity q_t ∈ [0,1] and friction parameter p_t ∈ [0.5, 2.0]—sampled from Beta distributions to respect control bounds. The fixed observation dimensionality is the key design choice enabling zero-shot transfer from N=10⁵ training to N=10⁶ deployment without retraining or architectural changes.

## Method

The approach pairs a Stratified Mean-Field Observer, which partitions nodes into K logarithmic degree tiers and produces topology-agnostic observations via O(N) parallel reduction, with FLASHSPREAD, a dual-engine GPU simulator supporting both Markovian and non-Markovian renewal dynamics through binomial tau-leaping and dual CSR memory layouts that eliminate atomic contention. The policy is learned with Truncated Quantile Critics (TQC), which model the full return distribution and apply pessimistic truncation (dropping top quantiles) to navigate the bimodal cascade outcome space. The actor processes stratified observations through 1D convolution over the tier dimension followed by an MLP, discovering a 'hub-sniper' strategy that applies protective actions to high-degree tiers while using moderate actions peripherally. The entire training loop executes in GPU VRAM (zero-copy architecture), with the simulator sustaining >2×10⁷ events/second for Markovian and >4×10⁴ events/second for non-Markovian dynamics at N=10⁶, reducing training from weeks on CPU to hours.

## Results

On the non-Markovian renewal environment (N=10⁵, 50 episodes), TQC trained on Renewal dynamics achieves 43,340 mean profit with ±498 std, outperforming TD3 on the same environment (41,030, ±723) by 5.6% in mean and 31% in variance. The 10th percentile of TQC (42,551) exceeds the mean of TD3 (41,030), confirming risk-aware superiority. For zero-shot transfer to real-world SNAP networks, the policy achieves 100% hub retention and positive profit on Facebook (+6,700), Twitter (+133,100), and YouTube (+1.76M), while all baselines yield negative profit. Training on non-Markovian dynamics yields a 59× improvement over the best Markovian baseline (43,340 vs 730).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares six configurations on a common non-Markovian evaluation environment. TQC trained on Renewal dynamics (43,340) dominates all baselines: it outperforms TD3 on the same Renewal environment (41,030) by 5.6% in mean profit and 31% in variance, and its worst-case 10th percentile (42,551) exceeds TD3's mean. Against Markovian-trained baselines, the improvement is 59× over TQC-Markovian (730) and the method is the only one that avoids catastrophic negative returns (TD3-Markovian: −65,477; MF-ODE: −238,731). On zero-shot transfer to real-world networks (Facebook, Twitter, YouTube), TQC-Renewal is the sole method achieving positive profit and 100% hub retention, while Random, MF-ODE, and TD3-Markovian all produce negative profit (e.g., −4.20M on YouTube for MF-ODE). The ablation (Table 8) confirms training environment is the dominant factor (−98.3% when switching Renewal→Markov), followed by observation representation (−11.2% Stratified→Global) and algorithm choice (−5.3% TQC→TD3).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of distributional RL (Bellemare et al., 2017; Kuznetsov et al., 2020) and network spreading dynamics (Barabási & Albert, 1999; Pastor-Satorras & Vespignani, 2001), but departs from the dominant GNN-based graph RL paradigm (Meirom et al., 2021; Almasan et al., 2022) by replacing node-level message passing with a fixed-dimensional stratified degree-tier observation. It extends the sim-to-real gap literature from robotics (Salvato et al., 2021; Muratore et al., 2022) to graph-based RL, formalizing three pathologies (Jensen-gap optimism bias, hub blindness, bimodal valley of death) that prior network-control work (Ohi et al., 2020; Kompella et al., 2020) addressed only implicitly. The GPU simulator adapts binomial tau-leaping (Chatterjee et al., 2005; Tian & Burrage, 2004) from chemical kinetics to non-Markovian network renewal processes, a domain where prior GPU epidemic simulators (Zou et al., 2013; Komarov & D'Souza, 2012) were limited to Markovian dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, open-source GPU simulator (FLASHSPREAD) that makes non-Markovian network RL training tractable at 10^5–10^6 nodes, removing a major computational barrier for the community. The zero-shot transfer to real SNAP networks (Facebook, Twitter, YouTube) with 100% hub retention demonstrates that degree-tier statistics are a sufficient topology summary for scale-free networks, potentially simplifying deployment pipelines. Open questions remain: the exchangeability assumption within degree bins may fail on networks with strong community structure, temporal/dynamic topologies are unaddressed, and partial observability (hidden states, missing edges) would require recurrent or belief-state policies. The dual-use implications for public health, information ecosystems, and algorithmic marketing are explicitly flagged but left to practitioners to govern.
