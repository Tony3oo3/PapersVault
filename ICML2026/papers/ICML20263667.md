## Problem

Existing evaluations of multi-agent LLM debates rely on outcome-based proxies such as consensus, majority vote, or LLM-as-judge scores, which cannot distinguish genuine deliberation from sycophancy, agent domination, or premature convergence. In real-world deliberative domains without ground truth (city planning, politics, stakeholder decision-making), this reliance is especially problematic because a flawed process produces unreliable outcomes regardless of how coherent the final answer appears. The paper asks: how can we evaluate the deliberative process itself, not just the outcome it produces?

## Input / Output

The framework consumes full multi-agent debate transcripts containing per-round stance trajectories (five-point Likert for subjective settings, categorical multiple-choice labels for objective settings), natural-language explanations, and confidence scores from each agent. Reasoning quality scores q_a^t ∈ [0,1] are computed via an LLM-as-judge protocol that scores each message's justification given the full prior debate context. The output is a set of six interpretable diagnostic scores—four process-level (engagement, responsiveness, influence asymmetry, balance) and two outcome-level (stability, group welfare)—each targeting a specific interaction failure mode rather than producing a single global quality score.

## Method

The paper formalizes eight deliberative axioms drawn from deliberative democracy, social choice, and game theory (monotonicity, responsiveness, non-domination, pluralism, openness, conditional convergence, stability, agent utility), then maps them via a many-to-one reduction to six operationalizable metrics computed directly from debate transcripts. Process-level metrics include: engagement (average stance-change magnitude weighted by reasoning quality), responsiveness (indicator of whether an agent moves closer to the group mean, weighted by justification quality), influence asymmetry (normalized entropy of a per-agent influence distribution estimated from how often others shift toward each agent's prior stance), and balance (a product of anti-collapse ratio and anti-oscillation ratio based on temporal stance-dispersion dynamics). Outcome-level metrics operationalize Nash-equilibrium stability and average utility change. The framework is validated through three complementary experiments: controlled axiom-violation sanity checks confirming metric specificity, pairwise human-preference alignment on subjective datasets, and Spearman correlation with correctness on MMLU-Pro as an objective case study.

## Results

On subjective tasks (City Planning and Politics), process-level diagnostics achieve 0.70–0.75 pairwise agreement with human preferences, substantially outperforming surface signals (0.48–0.57, near chance) and LLM-as-Judge (0.56–0.63). On the objective MMLU-Pro validation, average process metrics reach Spearman ρ = 0.52–0.57 with final-answer correctness, compared to ρ = 0.35–0.39 for LLM-as-Judge and ρ = 0.10–0.20 for surface signals. In recovery cases where the initial majority answer is wrong, high-process debates recover the correct answer in 74% of cases versus 40% for low-process debates. Sanity tests confirm metric specificity: each diagnostic degrades most sharply under its targeted violation (e.g., engagement drops 0.81 under dogmatism, balance drops 0.76 under premature convergence).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its process-level diagnostics against four categories of alternative evaluation signals: surface features (token count, round count, consensus), LLM-as-Judge scoring, and outcome-level game-theoretic metrics (Nash-Stability, Agent Utility). Process metrics consistently outperform all alternatives across both subjective datasets and all three model families. Surface signals perform at or near chance (0.48–0.57), confirming that debate length or whether consensus is reached carries almost no information about deliberative quality. LLM-as-Judge improves to 0.56–0.63 but remains 8–14 points below the best process metrics. Outcome-level metrics (Nash-Stability, Agent Utility) land in between (0.59–0.66), suggesting they capture some deliberative structure but miss interaction-level failures like domination or non-responsiveness. On the objective MMLU-Pro validation, the same ordering holds: process metrics (ρ = 0.52–0.57) beat LLM-as-Judge (ρ = 0.35–0.39), outcome metrics (ρ = 0.38–0.45), and surface signals (ρ = 0.10–0.20), with the gap widening in recovery cases (74% vs. 40% correct-answer recovery).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant evaluation paradigm for multi-agent LLM debates, which relies on outcome-based proxies (consensus, majority vote, LLM-as-judge scores) by importing axiomatic evaluation traditions from deliberative democracy, social choice theory, and explainability. Rather than building on a specific prior multi-agent method, it repurposes the diagnostic/property-based evaluation approach (analogous to how Arrow's axioms or faithfulness criteria are used in their respective fields) to define a minimal set of deliberative axioms and operationalize them as computable interaction-level metrics. The many-to-one mapping from axioms to four process metrics (engagement, responsiveness, influence asymmetry, balance) plus two outcome metrics (stability, welfare) is explicitly framed as a novel diagnostic lens rather than an extension of any single prior evaluation pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practical gap: in domains like city planning and political simulation where ground truth is unavailable, practitioners currently have no principled way to distinguish healthy deliberation from sycophantic or dominated consensus. The finding that consensus and surface signals are near-chance predictors of human preference (0.48–0.57) while process metrics reach 0.70–0.75 is actionable for anyone deploying multi-agent LLM simulations. Limitations include reliance on LLM-as-judge for reasoning quality scores (introducing a circularity concern), testing only three models under a fixed five-round protocol, and the assumption of flat (non-hierarchical) agent structures. Open questions include whether the axioms generalize to adversarial or competitive debate settings, how metrics behave with more than five rounds or larger agent groups, and whether the framework can be extended to detect persona drift or strategic manipulation beyond the pathologies tested.
