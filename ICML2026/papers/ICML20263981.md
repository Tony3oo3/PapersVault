## Problem

While prior work has studied how trained language models represent CFG structure, the *dynamics* of how models acquire language remain largely unexplored, and no prior CFG-learning study has accounted for the fact that CFGs decompose into subgrammars. This paper asks: how does language-modeling loss interact with the subgrammar hierarchy of a CFG, and do models learn subgrammars in parallel or sequentially as children do?

## Input / Output

The method consumes token sequences sampled from hand-designed, unambiguous PCFGs (ranging from 2-rule Dyck grammars to a ~50-rule Python-like grammar) and feeds them to small decoder-only transformers (1–4 layers, 6–32 hidden dims, vocab ≤ 100). Beyond the standard next-token distribution, the key analytical outputs are: a per-subgrammar decomposition of the KL-divergence loss, cross-seed CKA representational similarity scores computed on subgrammar-only vs. full-grammar evaluation subsets, and prediction error as a function of recursion depth (up to ~200 levels) for generalization probes.

## Method

The paper introduces two formal notions of subgrammar (inner, corresponding to derivation subtrees; outer, corresponding to rule subsets) and proves that the language-modeling KL-divergence recurses linearly over top-level subgrammars (Theorem 4.3), reducing under a context-insensitivity assumption to a weighted sum of per-subgrammar divergences (Corollary 4.5) with a recursion-dependent blow-up factor 1/(1−E[R]) (Theorem 4.6). Empirically, scaled-down nanoGPT transformers are trained via standard maximum-likelihood on these PCFGs, and the authors verify the loss decomposition visually, demonstrate that all subgrammars are learned in parallel (contrasting with child-like staged acquisition), and show that subgrammar pretraining acts as an inductive bias that improves final loss for tiny models and produces more seed-aligned internal representations (measured via linear CKA), while depth of recursion—not sequence length—remains the primary generalization bottleneck.

## Results

The paper's central theoretical result (Theorems 4.3, 4.5, 4.6) proves that the KL-divergence of a language model from a PCFG decomposes linearly over the grammar's subgrammars, with an additional blow-up factor of 1/(1−E[R]) for recursive grammars. Empirically, small transformers (2-layer, d=20) trained on synthetic PCFGs learn all subgrammars in parallel rather than sequentially, and subgrammar pretraining increases cross-seed CKA alignment in attention layers from 0.258 to 0.303 (2-layer, 20-epoch pretraining) and from 0.249 to 0.323 (4-layer, 10-epoch pretraining) on PythonPCFG. On the Nested Parentheses grammar, prediction error remains low for longer contexts at fixed depth but grows approximately as an inverse-log curve with increasing recursion depth, a failure mode also observed in GPT-5.1 Instant (5/5 correct on non-deep arithmetic chains vs. only 2/5 on a single depth-7 expression).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods or architectures; its primary comparison is between training from scratch and training with subgrammar pretraining (a curriculum-learning baseline). For 2-layer transformers on PythonPCFG, 20 epochs of subgrammar pretraining increases attention-layer CKA by +21.7% (0.249→0.303) and subgrammar-sequence CKA by +20.8% (0.288→0.348), while MLP-layer CKA changes minimally (−0.2% to −4.7%), indicating the alignment effect is concentrated in attention. The benefit diminishes for 4-layer models (+8.3% attention CKA) and can even slightly hurt final loss for larger models, suggesting the inductive bias is most useful when model capacity is tight relative to grammar complexity. On the small ABC Grammar (Table 4), 4 epochs of pretraining yields +10.7% attention CKA for full-grammar sequences, confirming the effect generalizes across grammars. The parallel-learning finding (all subgrammars reduce error simultaneously) contrasts with the staged, discontinuous learning reported by Cagnetta & Wyart (2024), which studied non-recursive PCFGs with final-token-only prediction; the authors attribute the difference to autoregressive modeling and the presence of recursion.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on neural language modeling of context-free grammars (Allen-Zhu & Li, 2023; Cagnetta & Wyart, 2024) by introducing a formal subgrammar decomposition framework and proving that KL-divergence recurses linearly over subgrammar structure. Unlike Cagnetta & Wyart, whose PCFGs produce finite-support languages and show learning in discontinuous stages, this paper handles recursive grammars and demonstrates parallel subgrammar learning in autoregressive models. The subgrammar definitions (inner and outer) are novel, though related to classical CFG algebra (Gruska, 1971), and the curriculum-learning experiments connect to the broader literature on inductive biases and staged training (Bengio et al., 2009; Papadimitriou & Jurafsky, 2020).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The fundamental theorems linking language-modeling loss to subgrammar structure provide a new mathematical lens for understanding how compositional structure is acquired during training, with potential implications for curriculum design and interpretability. However, the empirical evidence is limited to small transformers on synthetic, unambiguous PCFGs, leaving open whether parallel subgrammar learning persists at scale, with ambiguity, or in other architectures (RNNs, LSTMs). The depth-generalization finding (models handle length but fail at recursion depth) is consistent with prior literature but is reinforced here with a clean controlled setting. A key open question is whether the 'context-insensitivity' assumption needed for the cleanest theorems holds in practice for larger models, and whether subgrammar-aware training strategies can be made to scale beyond tiny models.
