## Problem

During autoregressive decoding at moderate batch sizes, MoE models are memory-bound because the number of unique activated experts (the union of all tokens' top-k choices) grows rapidly with batch size, making latency linear in that count rather than in total computation. No existing inference-time method reduces this union while guaranteeing a batch-independent per-token quality floor, leaving a gap between static expert pruning (which permanently loses capacity) and batch-level expert selection (which can drop experts critical to individual tokens).

## Input / Output

The method consumes, at each decode step, the batch of B token embeddings, their router score vectors R(x_i) ∈ Δ^N, and the sorted expert indices e_{i,j} per token, along with a single hyperparameter k_0 (the per-token baseline expert count). It produces re-routed expert sets S_i ⊆ {1,…,N} for each token such that |∪_i S_i| is minimized while each S_i always contains its top-k_0 experts. The routing is applied only during the decode phase (not prefill) and is integrated into the SGLang serving framework, where batch size varies dynamically as requests complete or are enqueued.

## Method

OEA is a two-phase, retraining-free routing algorithm that minimizes the number of unique activated experts T per decode batch. Phase 1 establishes a batch-independent quality floor by retaining each token's top-k_0 highest-scoring experts, forming a per-token baseline set S_i^base. Phase 2 (piggybacking) then traverses each token's remaining experts in descending score order and adds an expert to S_i only if it already belongs to the global union S^base = ∪_i S_i^base, thereby recovering quality without increasing T. The paper formalizes a roofline latency model showing total MoE latency equals b·T + a·Bk, confirming that in the memory-bound regime (typical for moderate batches) reducing T yields near-proportional latency gains. Ablations on cross-entropy and downstream benchmarks show that the simplified variant (Algorithm 2, with only k_0 as a free parameter and k^max = k) matches the full algorithm's performance, achieving 39% MoE-layer latency reduction on Qwen3-30B and 15% on Qwen3-235B at batch size 16 with minimal accuracy degradation.

## Results

At batch size 16, OEA reduces MoE-layer decode latency by 39% on Qwen3-30B-A3B (k₀=3) and 23% (k₀=5), while on Qwen3-235B-A22B it achieves a 15% speedup at k₀=5. On Qwen3-30B, OEA at k₀=5 matches or exceeds vanilla top-8 accuracy on all four benchmarks (e.g., GPQA 61.1 vs. 60.2, AIME24 81.5 vs. 80.4) with standard-error-adjusted equivalence. The piggybacking phase is critical: at k₀=3 on Qwen3-30B, pure pruning drops AIME24 to 51.2% and LiveCodeBench to 37.4%, while OEA recovers them to 80.0% and 61.2% respectively, at no additional latency cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external routing methods; instead it isolates the contribution of its two phases by comparing three internal configurations: Vanilla (top-8), Pruned (top-k₀ only, Phase 1), and OEA (top-k₀ + piggybacking, Phase 1+2). The central finding is that piggybacking recovers most of the quality lost by aggressive pruning at zero additional latency cost, since it only routes tokens to experts already loaded for other tokens. On Qwen3-30B, OEA at k₀=5 is standard-error-adjusted equivalent to or better than vanilla on all four benchmarks, while activating only 35.1 vs. 48.8 average experts (a 28% reduction). On Qwen3-235B, OEA at k₀=5 retains 97–98% of vanilla accuracy (e.g., GPQA 67.5 vs. 68.4, LiveCodeBench 66.1 vs. 68.5) with a 15% latency reduction. The gap between Pruned and OEA is largest at low k₀: on Qwen3-235B at k₀=3, LiveCodeBench jumps from 5.7% (pruned) to 63.4% (OEA), demonstrating that the piggybacking mechanism is essential for maintaining quality under aggressive expert reduction.

## Relation to Prior Work

OEA sits at the intersection of dynamic MoE routing and batch-aware inference optimization. It generalizes the top-p cumulative-score threshold from Huang et al. (2024) by capping it with a fixed k_0, and departs from token-centric expert-skipping methods (Lu et al., 2024; ODP) by explicitly exploiting cross-token expert sharing within a decode batch. Compared to the closest concurrent work—Lynx (Gupta et al., 2024), which prunes from the batch-level union, and SERE (Wu et al., 2026), which uses expert-similarity reconstruction—OEA's distinguishing design choice is a batch-independent per-token quality floor (top-k_0) combined with an additive piggybacking step that routes tokens to already-active experts in router-score order, guaranteeing consistent per-token computation regardless of batch composition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is direct: a 39% MoE-layer decode latency reduction on Qwen3-30B and 15% on Qwen3-235B at batch size 16, with no retraining and a single tunable hyperparameter (k_0) in the simplified version, making it easy to integrate into existing serving stacks like SGLang. The clean linear roofline model (R² > 0.99) gives practitioners a principled way to predict latency gains from expert-count reductions. Limitations include the method's diminishing returns at large batch sizes (where the expert pool saturates), sensitivity to batch composition (homogeneous batches reduce piggybacking opportunities), and a more visible quality gap on the larger 235B model. Open questions include interaction with expert-parallel serving strategies (METRO), co-optimization with KV-cache management, and whether the piggybacking heuristic generalizes to architectures with shared experts or different sparsity ratios.
