## Problem

Most spatial intelligence benchmarks for VLMs evaluate unconstrained everyday scenes where a single image admits multiple plausible 3D interpretations, making strict relational queries underdetermined and solvable via 2D shortcuts or appearance priors. This paper identifies the missing evaluation regime of constraint-governed spaces—real-world 3D structures whose components are restricted by geometric regularity, topological connectivity, and physical feasibility—and asks whether current models can recover a constraint-consistent 3D state and reason over it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265394/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each question presents one or two high-resolution photographs of a real-world structure (roofs, bridges, towers, trusses, railings), resized to a 512-pixel longer side, alongside a natural-language ranking prompt specifying a geometric or topological criterion. The model must output a parsable Python list encoding a permutation of 3 or 4 candidate indices (e.g., [1,3,2,4]); for Multi-View questions, two separately annotated images are provided—one highlighting a reference member (Member 0) and the other highlighting target members—to test cross-view structural correspondence. To reduce occlusion and color-induced bias, each answer option is rendered as its own annotated image rather than marking all candidates in a single frame.

## Method

SSI-Bench formalizes Structure-Centric Spatial Reasoning (SCSR) as inferring relations consistent with a constrained feasible set defined by equality constraints (geometric compatibility, connectivity) and inequality constraints (non-intersection, support, physics-based feasibility), then instantiates this as 1,000 multiple-choice ranking questions across seven geometric sub-tasks (Ground Height, Ground Angle, Dimension, Relative Distance, Area, Volume, Multi-View) and three topological sub-tasks (Hop Distance, Cycle Length, Multi-View). The benchmark is built through a fully human-centered pipeline: ten researchers spend over 400 hours curating ~20,000 images down to 2,000+ candidates, annotating rankings and localizations in Label Studio, and independently reviewing every question for unambiguity and challenge. The ranking formulation is deliberately stricter than binary or standard multiple-choice: a correct answer requires resolving all pairwise 3D relations among candidates simultaneously, so partial credit is captured only via a supplementary Pairwise Accuracy metric. Evaluation of 31 VLMs (10 proprietary, 21 open-source) uses exact-match Taskwise Accuracy at temperature 0, revealing a large human-AI gap (91.6% vs. 33.6% best model) and four dominant failure modes—member-extent misestimation, object-recognition errors, comparison-logic errors, and 3D spatial-logic errors—that point to limitations in structural grounding and constraint-consistent 3D reconstruction rather than in language understanding.

## Results

On SSI-Bench (1,000 ranking questions over geometric and topological criteria), the best proprietary model Gemini-3-Flash achieves 33.60% Taskwise Accuracy, while the best open-source model GLM-4.6V reaches 22.20%. Human evaluators score 91.60%, yielding a 58.0-point human-AI gap. The random-ranking baseline is 12.85%, and several smaller models (e.g., InternVL3.5-2B at 11.10%) perform at or below chance, indicating that SCSR cannot be solved by weak 2D heuristics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 31 VLMs (10 proprietary, 21 open-source) on SSI-Bench. Proprietary models consistently outperform open-source counterparts: Gemini-3-Flash (33.60%) leads all models, followed by Gemini-3-Pro (29.50%) and GPT-5.2 (29.10%), while the best open-source model GLM-4.6V trails at 22.20% and Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B at 21.90%. All models fall far short of the 91.60% human score, with a 58-point gap that dwarfs the ~21–33-point gaps reported on prior unconstrained spatial benchmarks. Within model families, generational progress is incremental (e.g., Gemini-2.5-Flash 22.30% → Gemini-3-Flash 33.60%; GPT-4.1 22.40% → GPT-5.2 29.10%), and scaling within a family yields inconsistent gains (GLM-4.5V 21.40% vs. GLM-4.6V 22.20%). Chain-of-thought reasoning provides only marginal improvements (Gemini-3-Pro: 27.1% → 29.5%; Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B: 20.6% → 22.5%), and error analysis attributes the dominant failures to member-extent misestimation, object-recognition errors, incorrect comparison logic, and globally inconsistent 3D spatial reasoning rather than to insufficient deliberation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new evaluation regime—Structure-Centric Spatial Reasoning (SCSR) in constraint-governed spaces—that departs from the dominant scene-centric spatial benchmarks (SpatialRGPT, VSI-Bench, MMSI-Bench, etc.) where 3D configurations are weakly constrained and often underdetermined from a single view. Rather than building on a specific prior method, it repurposes the general spatial-reasoning task by restricting scenes to real-world 3D structures (roofs, bridges, towers, trusses) whose geometric, topological, and physical constraints make component relations more determinate, and by adopting a ranking-based evaluation format (permutation of 3–4 candidates) that requires resolving all pairwise 3D relations simultaneously. The fully human-centered construction pipeline (400+ researcher-hours, Label Studio annotation, independent quality control) contrasts with the auto-generation pipelines common in prior spatial benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 58-point human–AI gap (91.6% vs. 33.6%) and the finding that chain-of-thought reasoning yields only ~2-point gains expose a fundamental limitation: current VLMs lack constraint-consistent 3D structural reconstruction, not just more deliberation. The error taxonomy (member-extent, object-recognition, comparison-logic, and 3D spatial-logic failures) provides actionable diagnostic directions for improving structural grounding. Open questions include whether structure-aware training signals (component-level annotations, connectivity graphs) can close the gap, whether the semi-automatic expansion pipeline can scale the benchmark to thousands of questions, and whether the constraint-governed regime generalizes to embodied or robotic settings where physical feasibility is even more tightly enforced.
