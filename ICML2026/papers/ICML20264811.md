## Problem

While REPA demonstrated that aligning diffusion model hidden states with external pretrained encoders (e.g., DINOv2) accelerates training and improves generation quality, this approach depends on large foundation models that are unavailable for many modalities such as 3D point clouds, proteins, and graphs. The intrinsic theoretical connection between diffusion generative modeling and self-supervised representation learning remains insufficiently understood, leaving open whether a fully self-supervised alignment strategy can capture the same benefits without external teacher signals.

## Input / Output

The method takes Gaussian noise as input and generates images at resolutions from 32×32 (CIFAR-10, CelebA) to 256×256 (ImageNet, in latent space via Stable Diffusion VAE), 3D point clouds of 2048 points (ShapeNet), and protein/RNA torsion angles on 2D/7D tori. A key training-time requirement is that for each clean sample x₀, two independently perturbed views x_t and x_t' are drawn from the same diffusion perturbation kernel p_{0t}, so the spectral learner receives a paired noisy input in addition to the single noisy input consumed by the denoiser. The spectral learner and diffusion denoiser share the same DiT backbone, with a time-conditioned two-layer MLP projection head probing an intermediate layer for alignment.

## Method

The paper unifies diffusion models and spectral representation learning (SRL) under a shared perturbation-kernel perspective: the diffusion forward process defines a time-varying Gaussian kernel p_{0t}, from which a kernel integral operator K_t is constructed whose eigenfunctions encode the local geometry of the data manifold at each noise level. A self-supervised spectral regularizer (derived from Neural Eigenmap/NeuralEF) is added to the standard diffusion score-matching loss, training a spectral learner ψ_θ(x_t, t) to approximate the eigenfunctions of K_t by aligning two noisy views of the same clean sample while separating views from different samples via a stop-gradient upper-triangular penalty. Theoretically, the paper proves that optimizing this spectral objective is gradient-equivalent to minimizing a KL divergence between a negative-sample distribution and a positive-sample distribution in representation space—i.e., diffusion score distillation—yielding mode-seeking dynamics that tighten clusters of similar samples. Empirically, the spectral regularizer is integrated into DiT-based rectified flow training across pixel-space and latent-space image generation, 3D point cloud generation (DiT-3D), and Riemannian flow matching for protein/RNA torsion angles, consistently improving FID, 1-NNA, and NLL over matched baselines without requiring any external pretrained encoder.

## Results

On image generation, the spectral regularizer consistently reduces FID across all four datasets: 11.588→8.742 on CIFAR-10 (25% relative), 28.806→25.678 on CelebA (11%), 13.766→13.074 on FFHQ (5%), and 9.441→7.994 on ImageNet-64 (15%). On ImageNet-256 latent diffusion, FID drops from 2.508 to 2.298 (8%). On 3D point cloud generation (ShapeNet), the method achieves 31% lower 1-NNA (CD) and 65% higher COV (CD) on Chair at just 5K iterations, with further gains at 10K iterations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole external-teacher comparison is REPA (Yu et al., 2024), which aligns denoiser features to DINOv2 embeddings. On ImageNet-256 latent diffusion, REPA achieves FID 1.745 versus the proposed method's 2.298, so the spectral regularizer loses to REPA by 0.55 FID in this setting. However, the proposed method's key advantage is that it requires no external pretrained encoder, making it applicable to modalities (3D point clouds, proteins) where such encoders are unavailable. Against the vanilla DiT baseline (same architecture, same training schedule, no representation alignment), the spectral regularizer wins on every dataset: FID improves by 1.5 on ImageNet-64, 0.2 on ImageNet-256, 2.8 on CIFAR-10, 3.1 on CelebA, and 0.7 on FFHQ. On ShapeNet point clouds, the method substantially outperforms the DiT-3D baseline at both 5K and 10K iterations across all three categories (Chair, Airplane, Car) on both 1-NNA and COV metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies diffusion models and self-supervised spectral representation learning (SCL, Neural Eigenmap) through a shared perturbation-kernel formulation, then uses the spectral objective as a self-supervised regularizer for diffusion training. It departs from REPA (Yu et al., 2024), which aligns denoiser hidden states to external pretrained encoders like DINOv2, by replacing the external teacher with a fully self-supervised spectral loss derived from the same Gaussian perturbation kernel used in the diffusion process. The implementation architecture (shared backbone, projection head, time conditioning) follows REPA, while the loss formulation draws on Neural Eigenmap/NeuralEF and the kernel construction follows SCL's population-level augmentation graph. A key theoretical contribution is showing that the spectral alignment gradient is equivalent to diffusion score distillation in representation space, bridging the two objectives via the dimension-contrastive/sample-contrastive duality of Garrido et al. (2022).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The main practical value is eliminating the dependency on large pretrained encoders (DINOv2, CLIP), which are unavailable or impractical for modalities like 3D point clouds, proteins, and graphs—settings where the paper shows the largest relative gains (e.g., 31–123% improvement on ShapeNet at 5K iterations). However, on the primary large-scale benchmark (ImageNet-256 latent), the method still trails REPA, and the added training overhead (10–70%) limits its appeal for compute-constrained settings. Open questions include whether the spectral regularizer can close the gap to external-teacher methods at scale, whether the time-varying geometric interpretation transfers to video or other sequential modalities, and whether the score-distillation equivalence can be exploited for more efficient sampling or distillation procedures.
