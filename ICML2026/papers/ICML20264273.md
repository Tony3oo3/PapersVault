## Problem

Training LLMs on non-verifiable tasks (creative writing, ethical reasoning, open-ended dialogue) is difficult because ground-truth labels are absent, and existing LLM-as-Judge approaches lack meta-evaluation—the ability to assess and improve the evaluator itself. Without this, self-rewarding models develop uncorrected biases (e.g., favoring verbosity over quality) and performance is capped at the model's initial evaluation quality. The paper asks whether evaluation skills can be trained without ground truth or external judges.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264273/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Given a text query, N agents (default N=4) sharing a single policy π_θ but assigned distinct strategic personas (e.g., 'Rigorous Formalist,' 'Adversarial Skeptic') engage in a structured four-round conversation producing initial solutions, blind pairwise rankings, targeted critiques, revised solutions, and final rankings. Pairwise comparisons are aggregated via the Bradley-Terry model into latent quality scores V^init and V^final, from which three reward signals are derived per agent. The trained policy outputs both solutions and calibrated evaluations, with segment-level credit assignment ensuring each token receives the reward corresponding to its conversational role (solution, critique, or ranking).

## Method

CoNL unifies generation and meta-evaluation through multi-agent self-play, where the key insight is that critique quality is measurable by whether it enables other agents' solutions to improve (ΔV_k = V_k^final − V_k^init > 0). The diagnostic reward r_diag sums positive score changes for all agents critiqued by a given agent, providing explicit supervision for meta-evaluation without external judges or ground truth. Two complementary rewards—solution quality (r_sol = V_i^final) and majority alignment (r_meta)—train generation and calibrated judgment respectively, with r_diag weighted highest (w_2=2.0). Training uses importance-sampled policy gradients with token-level credit assignment, where initial ranking tokens receive zero reward to prevent agents from strategically deflating the baseline. The framework bootstraps from capable instruction-tuned models and maintains diversity through distinct personas, while adversarial revision dynamics (agents can defend against invalid critiques) prevent false diagnostic rewards.

## Results

On non-verifiable benchmarks, CoNL (Qwen3-8B) achieves 59.2 on HealthBench, 27.9 on Research-Plan-Gen, and 64.1 on WildBench, outperforming the strongest label-free baseline SRT-M by 4.1, 5.2, and 3.8 points respectively. On verifiable reasoning benchmarks, CoNL reaches 76.5 Pass@1 on AIME 2024, 73.5 on AIME 2025, 79.2 on GPQA Diamond, and 87.1 on DeepMath, exceeding SRT-M by 6.7–8.3 points. The trained policy also improves judge quality on RewardBench-2 by +6.3 points overall (52.3→58.6), confirming that meta-evaluation is learned alongside generation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CoNL consistently outperforms all four baselines across every benchmark and model size tested. Against inference-only methods, the margins are large: on AIME 2024 (Qwen3-8B), CoNL scores 76.5 versus 61.8 (Self-Consistency), 63.2 (Self-Refine), and 64.5 (Multi-Agent Debate). The most competitive comparison is against the training-based Self-Rewarding baselines (Shafayat et al., 2025), which use the same four-round conversation structure but rely on majority voting as the reward signal. CoNL beats SRT-M (Multi-Agent) by 6.7 points on AIME 2024 (76.5 vs. 69.8), 8.3 points on DeepMath (87.1 vs. 78.8), and 3.8 points on WildBench (64.1 vs. 60.3), with lower training variance (e.g., ±0.9 vs. ±1.1 on USACO). The paper attributes this gap to the diagnostic reward r_diag, which explicitly trains meta-evaluation by rewarding critiques that enable measurable solution improvement, rather than relying on potentially incorrect majority consensus.

## Relation to Prior Work

CoNL extends the self-rewarding LLM paradigm (Yuan et al. 2024; Shafayat et al. 2025) by replacing majority-voting rewards with a diagnostic reward that measures whether a critique enables measurable improvement in the critiqued agent's solution. It repurposes multi-agent debate (Du et al. 2024) from a purely inference-time strategy into a training signal generator, and introduces 'peer-supervision' as a third paradigm for training evaluators—distinct from self-improvement (circular reasoning) and verifiable RL (domain-restricted). The Bradley-Terry aggregation of pairwise comparisons and the segment-level credit assignment follow standard RL practice, but the specific reward decomposition (solution quality, diagnostic effectiveness, consensus alignment) and the zero-reward masking of initial rankings are novel design choices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The diagnostic reward provides a concrete, implementable mechanism for training meta-evaluation—the ability to assess one's own judgments—without ground truth, addressing a gap the paper identifies as fundamental to all current LLM-as-Judge systems. The consistent 2.7–8.3 pp gains over the strongest label-free baseline (SRT-M) across four model sizes and eight benchmarks, combined with training dynamics that match ground-truth RL, suggest the approach is robust rather than artifact-driven. Key limitations include reliance on bootstrapping from already-capable instruction-tuned models (the method may fail from weak initializations), the fact that peer consensus can still diverge from objective quality in adversarial or low-diversity settings, and that the non-verifiable benchmark results (HealthBench, WildBench) depend on external judges for evaluation even though training is label-free. Open questions include scalability to 70B+ models, applicability to truly open-ended creative tasks where 'improvement' is harder to measure via peer ranking, and whether the four-round protocol is optimal or whether longer conversations yield diminishing returns.
