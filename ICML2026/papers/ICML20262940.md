## Problem

Training LLMs to generate high-performance GPU kernels via RL is hampered by two failure modes—reward hacking (exploiting measurement loopholes for spurious speedups) and lazy optimization (producing trivially correct kernels that miss true bottlenecks)—and by a biased policy-gradient estimator in GRPO that shrinks updates in multi-turn settings. Prior work either ignores speedup entirely, relies on imprecise LLM-as-a-judge verification, or trains on tiny datasets for only a handful of steps, leaving the full potential of multi-turn RL for kernel generation unexplored.

## Input / Output

The model receives a Torch reference implementation and a kernel task specification as the first-turn prompt; in subsequent turns it also receives structured environment feedback (correctness status, error diagnostics, runtime profiling summaries, and hacking-check verdicts) appended to the prompt. The output is a multi-turn trajectory of Triton kernel implementations, where each turn produces a revised kernel conditioned on the full interaction history. Evaluation uses KernelBench's Fast@p metric (fraction of samples that are both correct and achieve ≥p× speedup over the Torch reference), with the paper's stricter protocol additionally rejecting any kernel that fails the execution-based hacking check.

## Method

The paper introduces KERNELGYM, a distributed server-worker GPU execution environment with per-task subprocess isolation, elastic worker scaling, execution-based hacking checks (instrumenting Triton's launch path to verify kernels are actually called), and a profiler that exposes kernel-level runtime breakdowns. For multi-turn RL, the authors identify that GRPO's in-group mean baseline suffers from self-inclusion, causing a systematic (1−1/N) shrinkage of the policy gradient; they fix this with TRLOO (Turn-level REINFORCE Leave-One-Out), which excludes the sample's own return from the baseline to yield an unbiased advantage estimator. To combat lazy optimization, they add Profiling-based Rewards (PR), which augment the per-turn reward with the fraction of total CUDA runtime attributable to the generated kernels, and Profiling-based Rejection Sampling (PRS), which probabilistically discards low-coverage samples during training. Mismatch Rejection Sampling (MRS) is incorporated to stabilize training against rollout–training engine drift. At inference, Sequential Test-Time Scaling (STTS) with a context-management strategy (retaining only the top-w rewarded turns in the prompt) extends refinement beyond the 3-turn training horizon, yielding up to 47.8% Fast@1.2 on KernelBench Level-2.

## Results

On KernelBench Level 2, DR. KERNEL-14B achieves 49.2% Fast@1 and 25.6% Fast@1.2, competitive with Claude-4.5-Sonnet (50.0/26.7) and GPT-5 (46.7/28.6). With sequential test-time scaling (STTS), DR. KERNEL-14B reaches 59.8% Fast@1 and 31.6% Fast@1.2, surpassing both GPT-5 (28.6%) and Claude-4.5-Sonnet (26.7%) on the stricter Fast@1.2 metric. Selecting the best candidate across all turns (best-of-history) further lifts Fast@1.2 to 47.8% and Fast@1 to 80.9%. On Level 1, DR. KERNEL-14B achieves 20.3% Fast@1 and 16.9% Fast@1.2, outperforming all open-source baselines and approaching GPT-5 (19.5/16.5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against AutoTriton, the most directly comparable Triton-based RL baseline, DR. KERNEL-14B achieves substantially higher performance on Level 2: 49.2% vs. 30.6% Fast@1 and 25.6% vs. 9.2% Fast@1.2, demonstrating that multi-turn RL with hacking checks and profiling-based rewards far exceeds single-turn correctness-focused training. Against open-source Qwen3 models (8B, 32B, Coder-A30BA3), DR. KERNEL-14B dominates across all levels, e.g., 49.2% vs. 13–14% Fast@1 on Level 2. Against frontier proprietary models, DR. KERNEL-14B is competitive but slightly behind GPT-5 on Fast@1.2 (25.6 vs. 28.6) and Claude-4.5-Sonnet on Fast@1 (49.2 vs. 50.0) at Level 2; however, with STTS, DR. KERNEL-14B surpasses both (31.6% vs. 28.6%/26.7% Fast@1.2). The gap on Level 3 remains significant for all models, with DR. KERNEL-14B achieving only 8.8% Fast@1 versus 21.0% for GPT-5 and Claude-4.5-Sonnet, indicating that harder compositional tasks still require further scaling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of RL-based kernel generation work (AutoTriton, TritonRL, Kevin) by systematically addressing two failure modes—reward hacking and lazy optimization—that prior methods only partially mitigated. Methodologically, it builds on GRPO's in-group mean baseline (Ahmadian et al., 2024) and the Leave-One-Out estimator (Kool et al., 2019), identifying a self-inclusion bias in GRPO's advantage estimation for multi-turn settings and proposing TRLOO as an unbiased correction. For training stability, it adopts geometric Mismatch Rejection Sampling (Liu et al., 2025), and introduces profiling-based rewards and rejection sampling as a novel bottleneck-aware objective alignment mechanism. The KERNELGYM environment generalizes prior ad-hoc execution setups into a distributed, fault-isolated serving system with execution-based hacking checks and structured profiling feedback.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first complete, open-sourced pipeline for multi-turn RL training of LLMs on performance-oriented kernel generation, with the 14B model reaching competitive performance with frontier proprietary models (Claude-4.5-Sonnet, GPT-5) on KernelBench Level 2. The TRLOO correction is a general contribution to multi-turn RL beyond kernel generation, and the staged diagnosis—showing that training stability (MRS) is necessary but insufficient without objective alignment (PR/PRS)—offers a useful template for other RL tasks with sparse, multi-level rewards. Key limitations include the narrow scope to Triton (not CUDA or other DSLs), modest model sizes (8B/14B), and still-limited Level 3 performance, suggesting that scaling data and model capacity remains an open direction. Whether the lazy optimization phenomenon is specific to kernel generation or reflects a broader tendency of LLMs to exploit low-hanging-fruit rewards in any performance-optimized RL task is an open question.
