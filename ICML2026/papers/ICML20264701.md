## Problem

No single action space (language skills, motion primitives, grounding actions, latent codes) is universally optimal for agentic models—the best choice is highly task-dependent, which blocks the goal of building one generalist agent. Additionally, hierarchical agents (with separate high-level planner and low-level decoder) and end-to-end VLA models have remained separate paradigms, with no unified framework that lets abstracted actions serve as intermediate reasoning within a single autoregressive model.

## Input / Output

The model consumes RGB screenshots (Minecraft observations), a text instruction, and up to 15 steps of action history as working memory. It produces a two-stage autoregressive output: first an abstracted action (e.g., 'Grounding: Approach(object=sheep, point=[200,300])' or 'Motion: move forward, turn left'), then the corresponding low-level environmental action serialized as text function calls (e.g., 'move(4,-1) and press(w)'). At inference, a system prompt selects the output format, and the same model supports both a Fast decoupled mode (abstract action decoded by a lightweight policy) and a Slow unified autoregressive mode (full VLM generates both levels).

## Method

The Chain-of-Action (CoA) framework reframes action generation as a single autoregressive process where the abstracted action A is an intermediate 'thought' rather than a command to a separate module, factorizing the joint probability as P(A,a|ins,obs) = P(a|ins,obs,A)·P(A|ins,obs) within one Qwen2-VL-7B model trained end-to-end via imitation learning. The All-in-One training strategy then mixes trajectories annotated with different action spaces (Motion, Grounding, Text) into a single dataset, using the shared primitive action as a 'common currency' that grounds all abstractions and enables the model to learn functional equivalences across representations. Training follows a two-stage curriculum: pretraining on D_A ∪ D_a to familiarize the model with both abstract and primitive vocabularies, then fine-tuning on D_CoA triplets (observation, abstract action, primitive action). A single trained model supports both Fast (decoupled, ~5.6 FPS) and Slow (unified, ~1.2 FPS) inference modes, and an emergent Auto mode lets the agent dynamically allocate compute per step, achieving near-Slow performance at ~5 FPS.

## Results

On the OpenHA benchmark of 800+ hand-verified Minecraft tasks, OpenHA (trained on mixed action spaces with CoA) achieves the best overall performance across all three task categories simultaneously: 30.1% ASR on Embodied tasks, 32.5% on GUI tasks, and 31.9% on Combat tasks. No single specialist model dominates all categories—GroundingHA leads on Embodied (37.1%) but collapses on GUI (6.7%), while MotionHA achieves 0.0% on GUI. OpenHA also achieves the best Bo10 success rate on Embodied (75.8%) and Combat (90.2%), and the fewest average steps on Embodied (287) and GUI (314) among all end-to-end models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OpenHA outperforms all end-to-end VLA baselines: it beats JARVIS-VLA by +7.4% on GUI (32.5% vs 25.1%) and +13.4% on Combat (31.9% vs 18.5%) while matching it on Embodied (30.1% vs 30.0%), and surpasses TextVLA by +3.1% on Embodied, +6.7% on GUI, and +21.9% on Combat. Against hierarchical agents, OpenHA wins decisively on GUI (32.5% vs 6.7% for GroundingHA, 0.0% for MotionHA) and Combat (31.9% vs 26.5% for GroundingHA, 4.3% for MotionHA), though GroundingHA retains a 7.0% edge on Embodied (37.1% vs 30.1%). OpenHA also substantially outperforms the older policy baselines—VPT (6.0% Embodied), STEVE-1 (8.0%), and SkillHA (11.3%)—by 19–24 points on Embodied ASR. The key finding is that no single specialist action space wins everywhere: GroundingHA is best on Embodied, ROCKET-1 is competitive on Combat (27.9%), but only OpenHA, trained on a mixture of action spaces, achieves strong performance across all three categories simultaneously, validating the All-in-One training strategy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes two major paradigms in agentic AI: hierarchical agents (RT-H, SayCan, MolmoAct) that use a VLM to generate abstracted actions decoded by a separate low-level policy, and end-to-end VLA models (OpenVLA, JARVIS-VLA, STEVE-1) that directly predict primitive actions. The Chain-of-Action (CoA) framework departs from both by treating the abstracted action as an intermediate autoregressive 'thought' within a single unified model, borrowing the conceptual structure of Chain-of-Thought reasoning but grounding it in executable action tokens rather than free-form language. The All-in-One training strategy extends multi-task learning principles to the action-space dimension, showing that a single model trained on heterogeneous action representations outperforms specialists—a finding that parallels multi-task benefits in NLP but is novel in the embodied control setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The systematic demonstration across 800+ hand-verified tasks that no single action space is universally optimal provides much-needed empirical grounding for a design choice that has been largely treated as a matter of architectural preference. The CoA framework offers a practical, end-to-end trainable path to hierarchical reasoning without the multi-stage training and performance bottlenecks of classic HA architectures, and the emergent Auto mode (where the model dynamically allocates reasoning depth) hints at a new axis of inference-time compute scaling for agents. Key limitations include reliance on imitation learning from VPT data (no RL), Minecraft-specific evaluation, and moderate absolute success rates (~30-35% ASR) that leave substantial room for improvement. Open questions include whether CoA and All-in-One training generalize to continuous control (robotics), whether RL can further exploit the hierarchical structure, and whether the task-dependent action-space finding holds in more diverse or real-world environments.
