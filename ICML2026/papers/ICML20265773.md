## Problem

Existing attention-based time series imputation methods re-discover feature relationships at every layer without persistent anchors, causing cross-feature attention to collapse under heavy missingness. Graph-based alternatives impose predefined topologies that assume homogeneous node structure, failing when features mix spatial locations with semantic variables or when spatial priors are unavailable. The paper asks whether learnable, data-independent feature identities can serve as stable semantic anchors that enable robust cross-feature reasoning regardless of observed values.

## Input / Output

The model receives a zero-filled incomplete multivariate time series X̃ ∈ R^{T×F} (where missing entries are set to 0) and a binary mask M ∈ {0,1}^{T×F} indicating observed positions, and outputs the fully imputed series X̂ ∈ R^{T×F}. It is evaluated under five missingness protocols (Point-10/50/90%, Block-50%, Subseq-50%) across datasets ranging from 7 to 862 features and 12 to 48 time steps, with training using a combined ORT (observed reconstruction) + MIT (masked imputation) L1 loss on artificially masked observed entries.

## Method

HELIX introduces a Feature Identity Embedding (FeatID): a learnable matrix F_id ∈ R^{F×d_f} where each row f_i is a persistent per-feature vector concatenated with the observed value, sinusoidal positional encoding, and mask to form a dual-anchored token representation. The attention score between features decomposes into an identity prior (f_i^T A_ff f_j), identity-context cross-terms, and a dynamic context term; under severe missingness the dynamic term degenerates while the identity prior remains fully informative, preventing cross-feature attention collapse. The backbone uses a Double-Helix hybrid encoding: Stage 1 applies temporal and cross-feature multi-head attention in parallel, Stage 2 applies serial cross-dimensional attention (feature-attention on temporal output and vice versa), and the four branch outputs are averaged per layer. A multi-level fusion aggregates all intermediate outputs via simple averaging (empirically outperforming learnable gating), followed by LayerNorm and a linear projection to produce the imputation. All inter-feature dependencies are learned end-to-end from temporal co-variation without any graph structure or spatial prior.

## Results

HELIX achieves an average rank of 1.00 across all 21 experimental settings (5 datasets × 5 missing patterns), outperforming all 16 baselines. On ETT-h1 Point-50%, HELIX attains MAE 0.189±0.012, improving over ImputeFormer (0.296) by 36% and SAITS (0.208) by 9%, while using only 803K parameters versus SAITS's 88.2M. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (25 seeds) confirm statistically significant superiority over all baselines (p<0.001). HELIX also achieves the best average rank on MSE (1.38) and MRE (1.00), confirming metric-agnostic dominance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HELIX wins on all 21 settings against every baseline. Against ImputeFormer (KDD'24), the closest competitor, HELIX improves MAE by 36% on ETT-h1 Point-50% (0.189 vs. 0.296) and by 68% on BeijingAir Subseq-50% (0.166 vs. 0.322). Against SAITS (ESWA'23), HELIX achieves a 9% MAE reduction on ETT-h1 Point-50% (0.189 vs. 0.208) while using 110× fewer parameters (803K vs. 88.2M). Against iTransformer (ICLR'24), HELIX improves by 44% on ETT-h1 Point-50% (0.189 vs. 0.339) and by 74% on BeijingAir Subseq-50% (0.166 vs. 0.626), where iTransformer's variate-as-token design struggles with long sequential gaps. Against TimeMixer (ICLR'24) and MOMENT (ICML'24), HELIX achieves 73% and 75% MAE reductions respectively on ETT-h1 Point-50%, with MOMENT additionally incurring 64× slower inference (3.82s vs. 0.06s). Notably, even the naive Linear Interpolation baseline (rank 5) outperforms several deep methods, yet HELIX still improves over it by 37.7% on average, underscoring the value of learned cross-feature structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

HELIX carves new ground at the intersection of three existing lines: attention-based imputation (SAITS, ImputeFormer), graph-based cross-feature modeling (GRIN, SPIN), and hybrid temporal-feature encoding (Crossformer, iTransformer). Its core departure is the Feature Identity Embedding—a learnable per-feature vector inspired by soft prompting in NLP—that provides a persistent, data-independent semantic anchor for cross-feature attention, eliminating the need for predefined graph topology or homogeneous node assumptions. The double-helix architecture (parallel temporal/feature attention followed by serial cross-attention) extends the two-stage attention pattern of Crossformer by adding explicit cross-dimensional information exchange and multi-level fusion, while the embedding design (concatenating value, sinusoidal PE, feature identity, and mask) addresses a gap the authors identify: prior methods anchor tokens along only one axis, leaving cross-dimensional attention without a persistent reference on the complementary axis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's most transferable insight is that embedding quality—specifically, equipping every token with sufficient identity information independent of observed values—matters more than architectural complexity for cross-variate reasoning, a principle the authors argue generalizes beyond imputation to forecasting, anomaly detection, and sensor fusion. The emergent recovery of geographic and clinical structure from purely temporal co-variation, without any spatial or domain supervision, suggests the learned identities capture genuine latent semantics. Key open questions include cross-dataset transfer of feature identities (currently per-dataset), scaling beyond F=1000, and whether the simple-averaging fusion strategy (which outperforms gated fusion) reflects a broader principle about unconditional information preservation in heterogeneous missingness scenarios.
