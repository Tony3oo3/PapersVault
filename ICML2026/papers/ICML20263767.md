## Problem

Scaling test-time compute via multi-path chain-of-thought improves LLM reasoning accuracy, but existing methods handle the exploration–exploitation trade-off in rigid ways: tree-based search hard-codes branching schedules that disrupt post-trained reasoning, while parallel reasoning over-explores redundant paths and relies on weak answer selection. The paper identifies that the optimal balance is phase-dependent (early exploration vs. late exploitation) and that correct vs. incorrect paths often diverge only at late stages, yet no prior method dynamically reallocates compute across these phases under a fixed budget.

## Input / Output

HyPER takes a text reasoning prompt and an MoE LLM as input, consuming them through an SC-style multi-path decoding backend with a warm-up phase that estimates a sliding-window confidence threshold from the top-10 initial traces. The output is a single selected final answer produced by a two-stage length- and confidence-aware voting rule over all surviving and pruned paths, where candidate answers are first truncated to the top-K_a by majority count and then re-ranked by a linearized proxy importance-sampling score combining normalized path length and global average token confidence. Throughout decoding, the method maintains a pool of up to S_max=80 surviving hypothesis paths, each annotated with per-token confidence, entropy, and expert-routing states.

## Method

HyPER reconceptualizes test-time scaling as a closed-loop expand-reduce control problem over a pool of hypothesis paths, using three coupled components. First, a lightweight online controller evaluates confidence signals (mean token confidence, top-k entropy, top-1 consensus) and diversity signals (Jensen-Shannon divergence plus normalized Levenshtein edit distance) every T decoding steps, then selects among four actions—NONE, SINGLETOKEN, MULTITOKEN, and BRANCH—via simple averaging scores that shift from exploration to exploitation as the pool matures. Second, the SINGLETOKEN action exploits MoE routing diversity by perturbing router logits with Gumbel noise to generate K parallel expert-routed proposals per token, applying a two-pass sampling scheme with a column-wise prefix-accumulation penalty to enforce intra-token expert diversity, and aggregating the resulting logits via confidence-weighted combination while sharing KV caches across proposals to avoid O(K) memory growth. Third, a length- and confidence-aware voting rule bridges the existence-selection gap by exploiting the structural asymmetry that confidence pruning makes surviving correct paths systematically longer than incorrect ones, combining this length signal with path confidence in a linearized proxy importance-sampling framework that admits a Bayes-optimal interpretation under a simple generative model.

## Results

Across four MoE models (Qwen3-30B, Qwen3-Next-80B, OLMoE, DeepSeek-V2) and eight reasoning benchmarks, HyPER achieves the best accuracy on every model–dataset pair while using fewer tokens than SC and RoE. On the hardest benchmark (HLE), HyPER reaches 15.5% with Qwen3-Next (0.76× tokens) versus 7.0% for SC and 6.5% for RoE (2.98× tokens). On AIME25, HyPER attains 95.3% (Qwen3-30B) and 96.0% (Qwen3-Next) at 0.71× and 0.59× normalized token cost, respectively, outperforming the strongest pruning baseline DeepConf by 4.6 and 2.0 points. Overall, HyPER improves accuracy by 8–10 percentage points over prior test-time scaling baselines while reducing token consumption by 25–40%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HyPER outperforms all four baselines on every model–dataset combination. Against Self-Consistency (SC), HyPER gains 5–10+ points on hard reasoning (e.g., 95.3% vs. 86.0% on AIME25 with Qwen3-30B) and 2–5 points on light reasoning (e.g., 86.5% vs. 80.1% on ARC-E with OLMoE), while using 0.36×–0.94× the tokens. Against Self-Certainty, HyPER wins by a wide margin (often 5–13 points), as Self-Certainty's selection strategy is consistently the weakest baseline. Against DeepConf (the strongest pruning-only baseline), HyPER improves accuracy by 1.3–4.6 points on hard benchmarks (e.g., 94.0% vs. 92.7% on AIME24; 15.5% vs. 11.0% on HLE with Qwen3-Next) at a modest token-cost increase (0.54×–0.94× vs. 0.46×–0.91×), demonstrating that the saved budget is productively reallocated into exploitation rather than simply discarded. Against RoE (always-on token-level refinement), HyPER dominates on both axes: higher accuracy (e.g., 95.3% vs. 84.7% on AIME25) and substantially lower effective token cost (0.71× vs. 1.64×), confirming that adaptive, phase-dependent control is far more efficient than uniform token-level expansion.

## Relation to Prior Work

HyPER unifies three previously separate threads in test-time scaling: DeepConf's confidence-guided pruning for reducing wasted exploration, RoE's Gumbel-noise expert routing for token-level MoE refinement, and Self-Consistency's multi-path CoT decoding as the base paradigm. It departs from tree-based search (ToT, MCTS, ETS) by arguing that rigid step-level branching disrupts post-trained reasoning models, and from pure pruning methods by explicitly reallocating saved budget into exploitation (single-token refinement, short-horizon expansion) rather than merely discarding low-quality paths. The key conceptual move is framing the entire process as a closed-loop expand-reduce control problem with a lightweight online policy, rather than a fixed schedule or a learned reward model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As a training-free method that plugs into any MoE reasoning model, HyPER offers a practical path to better accuracy-compute trade-offs without additional training infrastructure, and the 8–10% accuracy gain at 25–40% lower token cost is meaningful for deployment. The observation that confidence pruning inverts the typical length bias (surviving correct paths become systematically longer) provides a simple, interpretable signal for answer selection that could inform other multi-path aggregation schemes. Open questions include whether the controller's simple averaging rules remain optimal at larger scales, how the method interacts with learned Process Reward Models, and whether the MoE-specific SINGLETOKEN primitive can be replaced by architecture-agnostic token-level refinement for dense models (the preliminary dense-model experiment shows smaller but positive gains).
