## Problem

Standard coresets preserve clustering objective values under small geometric perturbations but offer no guarantee that point-to-center assignments remain stable, meaning different representations of the same data can yield entirely different clusterings. This paper introduces γ-resilient coresets that simultaneously provide (1+ε)-approximation and enforce that at most a γ(ξ) fraction of assignments change between ξ-close datasets, and proves a lower bound showing that achieving tight approximation under resilience constraints is inherently harder than standard clustering.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes a point set P ⊆ [Δ]^d of size n (with Δ = n^{O(1)}), a cluster count k, an accuracy parameter ε, and the optimal k-median cost OPT (recoverable via O(ε^{-1} log n) doubling search). It outputs a weighted coreset S of size O(ε^{-d}k log Δ + d^{d/2}ε^{-2d-2}log²Δ) together with a bijection φ: P → S assigning each input point to its coreset representative. The critical additional requirement is that when the same shared random bits are used on two ξ-close point sets, the induced assignments differ on at most O(√d(1+ξ)/log ε^{-1}) fraction of points in expectation.

## Method

The construction is a randomized grid-based algorithm with four steps: a uniform random shift, a hierarchical family of grids where a random subset forms 'pivot grids' in an arithmetic progression with stride y = log(a/ε), a quadtree partition classifying cells as heavy or light, and a coreset assembly that assigns points from light cells to representatives sampled from their ancestor cells in the nearest pivot grid rather than from within the cell itself. This ancestor-pivot assignment is the key design choice that decouples assignment stability from local cell boundaries, enabling the resiliency guarantee. The analysis combines a balls-in-bins argument (each point falls into each residue class modulo y with probability 1/y) with an exponential decay bound on the probability that a perturbed point set crosses a coarser grid boundary, yielding the O(√d(1+ξ)/log ε^{-1}) resiliency factor. The hardness result constructs a chain of ξ-close 1D instances on A+1 disjoint paths where any α-approximate algorithm opening βk centers must reassign Ω(|P|/A) points, and applies Yao's minimax principle to extend the deterministic lower bound to randomized algorithms.

## Results

The main positive result (Theorem 3.1) constructs a γ-resilient (k,ε)-coreset of size O(ε^{-d}k log Δ + d^{d/2}ε^{-2d-2}log²Δ) with resiliency γ = O(√d(1+ξ)/log ε^{-1}) for k-median and k-means in constant dimension. The hardness results (Theorems 4.1–4.3) show that any α-approximation algorithm opening at most βk centers must have γ ≥ Ω(ln(1+ξ)/(ξ√α·β ln(αβ))) for k-means, and γ ≥ Ω(ln(1+ξ)/(ξαβ ln(αβ))) for k-median and k-center, even in 1D. The coreset achieves a (1+ε)-approximation with O(log n(log n+k)·poly(1/ε)) centers and O(ln(1+ξ)) resiliency, demonstrating a favorable bi-criteria trade-off compared to the lower bound.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or experimental comparisons. The key comparison is between the paper's positive coreset construction and its own hardness lower bound. The lower bound shows that any constant-approximation algorithm (even one permitted to open βk centers for any constant β) cannot achieve resiliency better than Ω(ln(1+ξ)/(ξ√α·β ln(αβ))) for k-means. In contrast, the paper's coreset achieves a (1+ε)-approximation (arbitrarily close to 1) while maintaining O(ln(1+ξ)) resiliency, at the cost of opening O(log n(log n+k)·poly(1/ε)) centers rather than just k. This demonstrates that the bi-criteria approach (trading extra centers for better approximation and resiliency) is not only useful but essentially necessary, as the lower bound rules out achieving both tight approximation and strong resiliency with only O(k) centers. The construction builds on the grid-based framework of Frahling & Sohler (2005) but requires fundamentally different analysis due to the resiliency constraints, and extends the resilience framework of Ahmadian et al. (2024) from algorithms to coresets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the γ-resilient algorithm framework of Ahmadian et al. (2024) from individual clustering algorithms to a new object—γ-resilient coresets—making stability a property of the data summary itself rather than of any particular solver. The construction adapts the grid-based coreset framework of Frahling & Sohler (2005) (hierarchical grids, heavy/light cell classification, quadtree partitioning) but departs substantially in design: instead of assigning points to representatives within their own cell, points in light cells are assigned to representatives sampled from ancestor cells in randomly selected 'pivot grids,' a mechanism specifically engineered to control assignment drift under near-isometric perturbations. The paper also answers an open question from Ahmadian et al. by proving a lower bound showing that resilience fundamentally increases the hardness of clustering, even in one dimension and even under bi-criteria (extra centers) relaxations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The introduction of resilient coresets decouples stability guarantees from algorithmic choices, enabling any downstream solver to inherit the resilience property simply by operating on the coreset—a modular design principle with potential utility in streaming, distributed, and privacy-preserving pipelines where data representations are routinely perturbed. The lower bound (Ω(ln(1+ξ)/ξ) resiliency even with 100k centers in 1D) establishes that resilience is not a free lunch: it fundamentally changes the complexity landscape of geometric clustering, and the coreset's O(log(1+ξ)) guarantee is near-optimal up to constants. Open questions include extending the construction to higher dimensions (current bounds are constant-dimension only), tightening the gap between the coreset's resiliency and the lower bound, and exploring whether the pivot-grid mechanism generalizes to other geometric optimization problems beyond clustering.
