## Problem

Existing stopping rules for Bayesian optimization are either heuristic (fixed iterations, convergence checks) or theoretically grounded but designed to minimize simple regret without accounting for evaluation costs, providing no protection against unnecessary evaluations when costs are high. No principled stopping rule existed that explicitly handles varying per-point evaluation costs and offers a theoretical guarantee on the cost-adjusted simple regret (simple regret plus cumulative cost).

## Input / Output

At each iteration the rule consumes the GP posterior (mean and variance) over the search space, the current best observed value, and the evaluation cost function c(x) (which may be known deterministically or modeled stochastically via a GP on log-cost, with E[c(x)] = exp(μ_ln c(x) + σ²_ln c(x)/2) substituted in place of c(x)). It outputs a binary stop/continue decision: stop when the minimum PBGI acquisition value across unevaluated points exceeds the current best, equivalently when the maximum LogEIPC value drops to zero or below. In practice, a stabilization window (first W=20 iterations) and a moving-average smoothing over W iterations are applied to prevent spurious early stops from GP parameter fluctuation or imperfect acquisition optimization.

## Method

The paper derives a unified stopping rule from the Pandora's Box Gittins Index (PBGI) framework: stop when min_x α_t^PBGI(x) ≥ y*_{1:t}, i.e., when no unevaluated point's 'fair value' (the threshold g satisfying EI(x;g)=c(x)) falls below the current best. The key insight is that this condition is algebraically equivalent to max_x LogEIPC(x) ≤ 0, meaning no point's expected improvement over the current best exceeds its evaluation cost, thereby unifying the stopping criterion for both PBGI and LogEIPC acquisition functions. The rule uses after-posterior-update acquisition values (rather than before-update as in prior correlated Pandora's Box work), which the authors show yields empirical gains. Theorem 3.2 proves that when paired with either PBGI or LogEIPC, the expected cost-adjusted simple regret is bounded above by U + C (the cost-adjusted regret of stopping immediately after the initial evaluation), which is the first such guarantee for any adaptive stopping rule in general correlated Bayesian optimization. A corollary provides a principled choice of the cost-scaling factor λ = U/(B−C) to ensure expected budget compliance in the budgeted formulation.

## Results

On LCBench (35 datasets), the PBGI/LogEIPC stopping rule paired with PBGI achieves Top-1 rank on 20–50% of large datasets (>10K instances) and Top-3 rank on 80–90% of large datasets across cost-scaling factors λ ∈ {10⁻³, 10⁻⁴, 10⁻⁵}. In 8D Bayesian regret experiments, the method nearly matches the hindsight-optimal stopping across all three cost regimes (uniform, linear, periodic). On NATS-Bench, the PBGI/LogEIPC pair closely approaches hindsight optimal on cifar10-valid, while UCB-LCB and SRGap-med fail to trigger within the 200-iteration cap in 50/50 trials on all three NATS datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The PBGI/LogEIPC stopping rule outperforms UCB-LCB (Makarova et al., 2022) and SRGap-med (Ishibashi et al., 2023) most dramatically on NATS-Bench, where both baselines fail to stop within the 200-iteration cap in 50/50 trials on all three datasets, while the proposed rule reliably terminates early. In 1D Bayesian regret, the proposed rule is particularly strong under high cost-scaling (λ=0.1), where cost-unaware baselines incur excessive evaluation costs and achieve higher cost-adjusted regret. LogEIPC-med (Ishibashi et al., 2023) is the closest competitor in 8D uniform-cost settings but still trails the proposed rule, which nearly matches hindsight optimal. PRB (Wilson, 2024) is competitive in regret but significantly more computationally expensive (optimizing over up to 1000 posterior samples per iteration) and also fails to stop early on NATS-Bench in 26/50 trials on Cifar10. The GSS heuristic (Bakshy et al., 2018) and Convergence baseline are simple but lack cost-awareness; the proposed rule matches or beats them in most settings while providing the first theoretical guarantee (no worse than immediate stopping) that none of the baselines offer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Pandora's Box Gittins Index (PBGI) acquisition function of Xie et al. (2024) from a fixed-budget setting to adaptive stopping, and shows that the resulting stopping condition is mathematically equivalent to a principled thresholding rule for LogEIPC and, under uniform costs, to the EI-thresholding stopping rule of Nguyen et al. (2017). It departs from Gergatsouli & Tzamos (2023), who derived a correlated Pandora's Box stopping rule using before-posterior-update acquisition values, by instead using after-posterior-update values, which the authors argue more faithfully captures the 'fair value' intuition and yields better empirical performance. The theoretical guarantee (no worse than immediate stopping in cost-adjusted regret) is the first of its kind for adaptive stopping in general correlated Bayesian optimization, going beyond the independent/discrete Pandora's Box setting of Weitzman (1979).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical guarantee bounding expected cost-adjusted simple regret for any adaptive stopping rule in Bayesian optimization, which is a clean and interpretable safety net (the algorithm never does worse than stopping immediately). Practically, this addresses a real gap: existing stopping rules (UCB-LCB, PRB, GSS) are designed for simple regret and can incur excessive evaluation costs when costs are high, while the proposed rule adapts naturally to varying costs without heuristic tuning. Open questions include extension to noisy, multi-fidelity, or batched evaluations, and the guarantee is tight in the worst case (immediate stopping is optimal when costs dominate), so it serves as a lower-bound safety net rather than a performance guarantee. Under model misspecification (as in empirical benchmarks), the method remains competitive but can underperform on small datasets where validation-test rank correlation is poor.
