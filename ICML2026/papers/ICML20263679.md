## Problem

Strong coresets for k-means must preserve costs for every possible solution, yet in practice coresets are only queried on solutions produced by approximation algorithms, making the uniform guarantee unnecessarily pessimistic. This paper asks whether substantially smaller summaries suffice if one only requires preserving the approximation guarantees of clustering algorithms rather than exact costs for all solutions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263679/cropped_page8_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a point set P in a metric space (Euclidean or arbitrary finite metric), a number of clusters k, and an accuracy parameter ε, and produces a weighted coreset Ω of size Õ(k/ε³) in Euclidean space or Õ(k/ε²) in arbitrary metrics. Given any α-approximate k-means solution computed on Ω, a polynomial-time lifting procedure returns an α(1+ε)-approximate solution for P in the Euclidean case or an α(4+ε)-approximate solution in the general metric case. The coreset is composable: APCs for disjoint subsets can be merged into an APC for their union, supporting streaming and distributed settings.

## Method

The key insight is the notion of a witness set: a finite family of structured k-center solutions whose costs, if preserved by the coreset, suffice to convert any approximate solution on the coreset into a good approximate solution for the full input. For Euclidean k-means, the witness family consists of all k-tuples of means of O(1/ε)-subsets of a strong coreset, and the lifting procedure performs a Lloyd-style re-centering step followed by snapping each center to a structured representative mean. For arbitrary finite metrics, the construction iteratively applies sensitivity sampling to eliminate the log n dependence, and lifting simply maps each center to its nearest coreset point (incurring a factor-4 loss). The technical backbone is a new concentration bound for sensitivity sampling (Theorem 2.2) that provides uniform (1±ε) cost preservation over an arbitrary finite family of N solutions with sample size O(ε⁻²(k log k + log N)), which is shown to be optimal for finite metrics.

## Results

Across Adult, MNIST, and CoverType datasets, the cost ratio (coreset solution cost / full-dataset solution cost) approaches 1 as m/k increases, with sampling 10k points yielding only a 1.2× approximation loss compared to running the algorithm on the full dataset. Speedups of roughly 20×–80× are observed for LOCAL-SEARCH and LOCAL-SEARCH++ when using coresets instead of the full input. The cost-ratio curves are visually invariant to the choice of k, confirming that the APC guarantee is effectively universal across cluster counts. Theoretically, the paper achieves (1+ε)-APCs of size Õ(k/ε³) for Euclidean k-means and (4+ε)-APCs of size Õ(k/ε²) for arbitrary metrics, both smaller than the corresponding strong coreset bounds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing coreset constructions head-to-head; instead, the implicit baseline is running each approximation algorithm directly on the full dataset, and the APC is evaluated by the cost ratio between the coreset-derived solution and the full-data solution. All three tested algorithms (K-MEANS++, LOCAL-SEARCH, LOCAL-SEARCH++) exhibit similar cost-ratio behavior, confirming the APC's algorithm-agnostic guarantee: even when LOCAL-SEARCH produces a slightly better full-data solution than K-MEANS++ (e.g., 3.438e+05 vs. 3.536e+05 at k=10 on Adult), the corresponding coreset solutions remain comparably close to their respective full-data baselines. The theoretical comparison is against strong coresets: the Euclidean APC of size Õ(k/ε³) is smaller than the optimal strong coreset of size Õ(kε⁻²min{√k, ε⁻²}) when k ≫ ε, and the metric-space APC of size Õ(k/ε²) removes the log n dependence entirely. A lower bound shows that any β-APC with β < 4 in arbitrary metrics must have size Ω(log n / log log n), establishing that the factor-4 approximation is essentially tight for n-independent coresets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces approximation-preserving coresets (APCs), a new guarantee notion positioned between strong coresets (preserving costs for all solutions) and weak coresets (preserving costs only for the optimum). The constructions build directly on sensitivity sampling (Feldman & Langberg 2011; Bansal et al. 2024) as the sampling primitive, on the subset-mean approximation lemma of Inaba et al. (1994) for the Euclidean lifting step, and on the iterative size-reduction idea of Braverman et al. (2021) for the finite-metric construction. The key departure from prior work is the use of 'witness sets'—finite families of structured solutions that suffice to preserve approximation guarantees—rather than ε-nets over the full solution space, which is what drives the smaller coreset sizes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled explanation for the well-known gap between worst-case strong coreset bounds and the much smaller sizes that work in practice: one only needs to preserve costs for solutions that approximation algorithms actually produce. The (4+ε)-APC for arbitrary metrics yields an FPT approximation algorithm with a factor close to the best known (≈3.94), and the matching lower bound for β<4 shows the constant is essentially tight. The new concentration bound (Theorem 2.2) is of independent interest, giving the first optimal-size strong coreset analysis for finite metrics. Open questions include whether the factor of 4 can be improved in general metrics, whether the Euclidean bound of Õ(k/ε³) is tight, and whether the witness-set framework extends to other clustering objectives beyond k-means.
