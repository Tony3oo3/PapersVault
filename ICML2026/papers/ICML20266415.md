## Problem

The field increasingly treats RL as the default mechanism for creating reasoning capability in foundation models, conflating behavioral sharpening with capability creation and propagating unnecessarily costly, less reproducible recipes. This position paper argues that RL is most reliably a high-cost, high-leverage post-training operator that reallocates probability mass toward behaviors a model can already express, and should not be abused as a default recipe for early-stage capability creation when meaningful supervised or scaffolded signals are available.

## Input / Output

The proposed framework takes a partially capable foundation model (post-pretraining and cold-start supervision) as input and produces a refined model with improved correctness, consistency, and constraint satisfaction. The critical entry constraint is that RL should only be invoked when the target behavior is already elicitable in some conditions, failures are diagnosable, and success is auditable via verifiers, tests, or externally checkable criteria. For RL-first exception regimes, the output must additionally include compute-accountable reporting of sampling budgets, verifier robustness, stabilizer sensitivity, and comparisons against minimal scaffolded baselines.

## Method

The paper proposes a four-pillar adjustment framework: (1) RL adjusts after pretraining when a model is partially capable but inconsistent, (2) meaningful supervised or scaffolded signals should precede RL when available, (3) RL gains must be disentangled from representational scaffolding provided by pretraining and supervision, and (4) rewards should be auditable, verifiable, and minimally composed rather than opaque reward stacks. Three lightweight empirical observations support the framework: RL gains track cold-start quality (staged SFT-then-RL outperforms RL-only), verifiable rewards improve reliability but reveal a verifier bottleneck that motivates stress testing, and cross-domain R1-style pipelines (3D understanding, navigation, VLA, motion generation, robotics) consistently converge on supervised scaffolds followed by RL refinement. A practical decision framework specifies entry criteria, stage discipline with explicit exceptions for RL-first regimes, reward minimalism, and compute-accountable reporting. Self-supervised RL is discussed only as a boundary case for structured interaction settings where verification is strong and self-generated experience is externally auditable.

## Results

This is a position paper and does not present original quantitative experiments or a main results table. Its empirical support consists of three qualitative observations drawn from cited works: (1) RL gains strongly track cold-start SFT quality (Wei et al., 2025), (2) verifiable rewards improve reliability but are limited by verifier coverage and exploitability (Cai et al., 2025; Tu et al., 2025), and (3) cross-domain R1-style pipelines (3D understanding, navigation, VLA, motion generation, mobile robotics) consistently adopt a staged SFT-then-RL structure rather than RL-first training. No headline metrics or benchmark scores are reported.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not benchmark a proposed method against baselines. Instead, it steelmans two alternative views and argues against them on principled grounds. Against Alternative View A (RL-zero / RL creates reasoning, exemplified by DeepSeek-R1-Zero, Guo et al. 2025), the authors note that RL-zero results are consistent with selection and amplification of pretrained structure, that the original R1-Zero pipeline exhibited repetition and readability issues requiring later cold-start data, and that controlled comparisons (Wei et al., 2025) show staged SFT-then-RL outperforms RL-only when supervision is available. Against Alternative View B (RL-first is most scalable when supervision is costly), the authors concede the regime is real (theorem discovery, self-play, open-ended strategy search) but argue it should be treated as an exception requiring stronger evidence—sampling budgets, verifier stress tests, stabilizer disclosure, and comparisons against minimal scaffolded baselines—rather than normalized as the default post-training recipe. The paper's 'comparison' is thus conceptual: staged adjustment vs. RL-first capability creation, with the former favored under current foundation-model practice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not introduce a new method but reframes the interpretive and practical role of RL in post-training by synthesizing evidence from RLHF/RLAIF pipelines, preference optimization (DPO, ORPO, KTO), RLVR, and cross-domain R1-style systems. It departs from the 'RL-zero' narrative popularized by DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025) by arguing that controlled comparisons (Wei et al., 2025) and cross-domain convergence on staged SFT-then-RL pipelines indicate RL is most reliably an adjustment operator rather than a capability-creation mechanism. The paper builds conceptually on LeCun's cake analogy and on reward-modeling/verifier literature (Lambert et al., 2025; Skalse et al., 2022) to advocate for reward minimalism and compute-accountable reporting as field norms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a growing confusion in the field where 'more RL' is conflated with 'more capability,' risking wasted compute, poor reproducibility, and incorrect causal attribution of reasoning gains. Its practical contribution is a decision framework (entry criteria, stage discipline, reward minimalism, compute-accountable reporting) that could become a reporting standard for RL post-training papers. Open questions include: how to operationalize 'meaningful supervision is unavailable' as a falsifiable criterion, what benchmarks adequately measure verifier robustness and proxy gaming, and whether the staged-training regularity holds as models scale or as RL algorithms improve. As a position paper, its impact depends on community adoption of its reporting norms rather than on a specific technique.
