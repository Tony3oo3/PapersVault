## Problem

Synthetic data is a central ingredient in modern LLM training pipelines, yet its effectiveness is highly inconsistent: some pipelines produce dramatic capability gains while iterative self-training leads to model collapse. Despite numerous empirical mitigation techniques, a principled account of *when* and *why* synthetic data helps or hurts has been missing. This paper asks: under what structural conditions does a synthetic-data pipeline sustain improvement, and among effective pipelines, what determines their sample efficiency and generalization?

## Input / Output

The framework takes as input the structural description of a training pipeline: the current model state Z_t, the synthetic data D generated from it, and any external signal S (verifier, rubric, environment, fixed reference) that shapes which outputs are retained or rewarded. It produces two formal outputs: (1) an information-openness criterion stating that sustained improvement requires I(X; S | D) > 0, i.e., the external signal must carry task-relevant information beyond what the model already encodes; and (2) a decomposition of any supervision signal's information into a task-relevant component (resolving which equivalence class the output belongs to) and a within-class component (resolving which specific element within that class), yielding a scalar efficiency η_π(S) ∈ [0,1].

## Method

The paper anchors its analysis in the Data Processing Inequality (DPI), formalizing self-training as a Markov chain X → Z_t → D_t^syn → Z_{t+1} and proving that information-closed loops (no external signal) must monotonically lose task-relevant information, making model collapse a predicted rather than anomalous outcome. It then defines information-openness: a pipeline is effective when an external signal S satisfies I(X; S | D) > 0, and derives a per-iteration bound I(X; Z_{t+1}) ≤ I(X; Z_t) + I(X; S | Z_t) that quantifies how much new information each iteration can inject. The key conceptual contribution is the *meta-level* of supervision: a signal operating at a coarser granularity (e.g., binary correctness) treats all acceptable outputs as equivalent, so each labeled example constrains a larger region of the output space. The paper proves a decomposition theorem (Theorem 4.2) showing that any supervision signal's information splits into task-relevant gain I([Y]_π; S | Q) and within-class gain I(Y; S | [Y]_π, Q), and that π-measurable signals (like binary verifiers) achieve perfect efficiency η_π = 1. The guiding thesis—that gradient-based learning preferentially converges to the most information-efficient signal component—unifies the explanation for both rapid generalization under high meta-level supervision and reward hacking when a spurious coarse pattern is easier to exploit than the intended criterion.

## Results

This is a theoretical paper without a standard benchmark results table. Its main quantitative contributions are: (1) a DPI-based monotonicity bound showing I(X; Z_{t+1}) ≤ I(X; Z_t) for closed-loop self-training, predicting model collapse; (2) Theorem 4.2 decomposing supervision information into task-relevant and within-class components, with efficiency η_π(S) = 1 for any π-measurable (high meta-level) signal; and (3) empirical validations including a judge model whose acceptance rate decreased monotonically across self-training iterations (closed-loop collapse), and a rubric-based RL setup where only 24% sample-level consistency across 16 independent scorings still yielded strong out-of-distribution generalization due to the π-measurable reward structure. The paper also reports that JudgeRLVR (binary correctness only) transfers across domains (math → code, logic, general reasoning) without further supervision, and that CoT compression trained on math alone generalizes to code, instruction following, and agentic settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not perform standard method-vs-method benchmark comparisons; it is a theoretical analysis with supporting case studies. The closest to 'comparisons' are conceptual contrasts between supervision paradigms: SFT (low meta-level, instance-level target) vs. RLVR (high meta-level, binary correctness), where the paper argues RLVR's π-measurable signal achieves η_π = 1 while SFT wastes log M bits on within-class identification. Empirically, the paper contrasts closed-loop self-training (monotonic degradation, as in the judge model example) against information-open pipelines with fixed external signals (sustained improvement, as in Anthropic's generator-evaluator harness). The reward-hacking case study shows that a spurious length/style signal (coarser partition) outcompetes the intended correctness signal, and that decorrelating the spurious pattern immediately restores accuracy growth—validating the thesis that learning converges to the most information-efficient available signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Data Processing Inequality—a standard tool from information theory—to create a unified analytical framework for synthetic data pipelines in LLM training, rather than building on a specific prior method. It positions itself against the model-collapse literature (Shumailov et al., 2024; Alemohammad et al., 2024), which documents the failure mode empirically, by providing the formal mechanism (information-closed Markov chains) that makes collapse a predicted rather than anomalous outcome. The novel conceptual contribution is the 'meta-level of supervision' and the accompanying decomposition theorem (Theorem 4.2), which formalize why coarse signals like binary correctness outperform instance-level targets in both efficiency and generalization, connecting RLVR-style training to a clean information-theoretic advantage over SFT. The reward-hacking analysis extends this by showing that spurious patterns at coarser granularity are preferentially learned, reframing hacking as efficient learning of the wrong signal rather than a failure of optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides practitioners with a principled design criterion (information-openness) and a concrete efficiency metric (task-relevant partition coverage) for building synthetic data pipelines, with the practical implication that prompt diversity should be prioritized over volume and that verification capacity—not data or compute—is the true scaling bottleneck. The paper is explicitly qualitative and does not predict sample complexity or quantify gains; it takes the external signal as given and leaves open how verifier errors propagate through iterative training and how to design verifiers that remain reliable as models improve. The connection to Sutton's Bitter Lesson—meta-level signals as the synthetic-data analogue of 'investing in methods that find structure'—suggests a broader research direction toward expanding the frontier of what can be reliably verified in open-ended domains.
