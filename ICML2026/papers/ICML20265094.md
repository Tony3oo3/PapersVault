## Problem

LLMs deployed with user-provided context (in-context demonstrations or documents) can be significantly degraded by incorrect or adversarial inputs, a phenomenon called 'overthinking' where performance peaks at intermediate layers and collapses in deeper layers. No principled framework existed to guarantee that harmful context cannot push performance below a safe baseline while still allowing the model to benefit from helpful context, and prior risk-control approaches for early-exiting (CALM, Jazbec et al.) could not handle the negative losses that arise when context is helpful.

## Input / Output

The method takes an input text or question x together with user-provided context c (a set of in-context demonstrations for classification tasks, or a context passage for open-ended QA) and produces either a class label from a finite set or a multi-token answer, along with a per-sample decision of which intermediate layer to exit from. The model must make this exit decision without knowing a priori whether the context is helpful or harmful. For classification, early-exit is restricted to the second half of layers (e.g., layers 16–32 for a 32-layer model) because early layers are empirically overconfident in incorrect predictions; if no exit layer exceeds the confidence threshold, the model defaults to the zero-shot prediction.

## Method

The paper introduces three coupled components: (1) a safe context-aware early-exit predictor that returns an intermediate-layer prediction if confidence exceeds a threshold λ, otherwise falls back to the zero-shot model's final-layer prediction, thereby bounding the damage from harmful context; (2) a context-aware loss ℓ_c that measures the prediction error of the early-exit model with context relative to the zero-shot model, yielding positive values for harmful context and negative values for helpful context; and (3) a domain-preserving risk transformation that linearly rescales the bounded loss from [a,b] to [0,1] so the Learn-then-Test (LTT) framework can be applied without clipping negative losses to zero. Because the resulting risk is non-monotonic in λ, LTT is the only viable risk-control method. The transformation preserves the full loss distribution, enabling the calibrated threshold to simultaneously control overthinking risk on harmful inputs and exploit efficiency/performance gains on helpful inputs, achieving >50% fewer layers evaluated on average compared to loss-clipping baselines.

## Results

Across 9 tasks (8 ICL classification + SQuAD v2.0) and 5 models (LLaMA-3-8B, LLaMA2-7B, LayerSkip variants, T5-Large), the proposed risk transformation approach satisfies the DFRC guarantee (empirical risk ≤ ε) at all tested risk levels, regardless of the proportion of helpful vs. harmful context. In terms of efficiency, the method achieves an average of 53% fewer layers evaluated on ICL tasks at ε=0.05 and up to 81.8% fewer layers on SQuAD v2.0, compared to the loss-clipping baseline. The zero-shot fallback mechanism activates 3.3× more frequently on unanswerable (harmful) questions than answerable ones (3.0% vs. 0.9%), and when it does trigger on harmful context, zero-shot outperforms the early-exit prediction on 71% of questions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two prior early-exit + risk-control baselines that both use loss-clipping (setting negative losses to zero): CALM (Schuster et al., 2022) and Fast Yet Safe (Jazbec et al., 2024). The proposed domain-preserving risk transformation strictly outperforms both on efficiency: at ε=0.05 on ICL tasks, it saves 53% of layers on average versus the clipping approach, and on SQuAD v2.0 it saves up to 81.8% of layers. This is because clipping discards the negative losses that correspond to helpful context, forcing the risk-control procedure to be overly conservative and exit earlier than necessary. On risk control itself, both approaches satisfy the DFRC guarantee (empirical risk ≤ ε), but the transformation approach is consistently less conservative and better matches the user-specified ε (Fig. 11). Additionally, unlike CALM which always uses the full-model prediction after processing all layers, the proposed method includes a zero-shot fallback that selectively intervenes on harmful inputs, providing a safety mechanism CALM lacks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) distribution-free risk control via the Learn-then-Test (LTT) framework (Angelopoulos et al., 2022; Bates et al., 2021), which provides finite-sample guarantees for threshold selection without monotonicity assumptions; (2) confidence-based early-exiting for efficient inference (CALM, Schuster et al., 2022; Jazbec et al., 2024), which the authors repurpose from an efficiency tool into a safety mechanism; and (3) the 'overthinking' phenomenon in LLMs (Halawi et al., 2024), where deeper layers degrade performance on harmful in-context demonstrations. The key departure from prior early-exit work is the introduction of a zero-shot baseline as the safety anchor and a context-aware loss that can take negative values (capturing helpful context), requiring a novel risk transformation to make LTT applicable. Unlike Halawi et al.'s static attention-head pruning, this approach uses dynamic per-sample early exiting with distribution-free guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first principled framework that simultaneously provides distribution-free safety guarantees against harmful user-provided context and achieves substantial computational efficiency gains (50–82% fewer layers evaluated) on helpful context, addressing a practical deployment concern that prior work treated separately. Key limitations include the absence of worst-case guarantees for individual prompts, the lack of class-conditional risk control (the marginal guarantee does not extend to the harmful-context subgroup alone), and design choices (half-layer exit cutoff, per-token confidence thresholding) that are empirically validated but may not transfer to longer-generation or differently-architected models. Open questions include robustness to calibration/test distribution mismatch, handling rare high-stakes safety violations, and extending the framework to settings where helpful and harmful context co-occur within a single prompt.
