## Problem

RLHF pipelines assume annotation responses reflect stable, genuine human preferences, yet six decades of behavioral science show people routinely produce answers without holding opinions (non-attitudes), construct preferences on the spot from contextual cues, and interpret identical questions differently. The field has invested heavily in downstream aggregation and reward modeling while leaving the logically prior question unaddressed: whether the responses being aggregated are preferences at all. This paper argues that measurement validity must precede preference aggregation, and that current practice models noise as signal and elicitation artifacts as human values.

## Input / Output

The method consumes existing RLHF annotation datasets (PRISM and PluriHarms) containing per-annotator preference ratings (0–100 scale) on prompt-response pairs. It identifies semantically similar prompt pairs via cosine similarity over all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence embeddings (threshold ≥ 0.70) and defines inconsistency as a score divergence of ≥ 15 points. Outputs include per-annotation validity classifications (non-attitude, constructed preference, measurement artifact, genuine preference), per-annotator consistency scores across four diagnostic dimensions, and filtered or reweighted annotation sets suitable for downstream reward model training.

## Method

The paper proposes a four-category taxonomy of annotation responses mapped to distinct diagnostic signatures and handling strategies, formalized through a latent preference framework where θ ∈ {0,1,∅} captures whether a genuine preference exists. Four consistency diagnostics—temporal (repeated identical items), framing (semantically equivalent prompts with varied wording), order (reversed presentation), and cross-item (correlation across items tapping the same value dimension)—locate each annotation on a signal-strength spectrum from no signal to full signal. Empirically, they apply temporal consistency to 44 identical prompt-response-model triples in PRISM and framing consistency to 673 observations across 7 semantically similar prompt pairs in PluriHarms, classifying 57% of PRISM inconsistencies as non-attitudes and 32% as constructed preferences. They further show that inconsistency is directionally biased (not symmetric noise): annotators below the median inconsistency ratio assign significantly higher harm ratings (t=5.31, p<.001), and filtering high-inconsistency annotators flips majority harm classifications for 18.6% of prompts while shifting mean ratings by 13.2 points on a 100-point scale.

## Results

Across two widely used RLHF datasets, preference inconsistencies are prevalent and systematic: in PRISM, 19.91% of candidate prompt pairs show score divergences ≥15 points (median per-annotator inconsistency rate 56.4%), and in PluriHarms, 35.66% show such divergences (median rate 36.7%). Qualitative classification of 44 PRISM inconsistencies reveals that 56.8% are non-attitudes, 31.8% are constructed preferences, 9.1% are measurement artifacts, and only 2.3% reflect genuine but uncrystallized preferences. Filtering high-inconsistency annotators flips majority harm classifications for 18.6% of prompts and shifts mean harm ratings by 13.2 points on a 0–100 scale (t=5.31, p<.001; Pearson r=−.65 between inconsistency ratio and mean harm rating).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper that challenges a foundational assumption in RLHF rather than proposing a new method to benchmark against existing baselines, so no direct method-to-method comparisons are reported. The closest empirical contrast is between consistent and inconsistent annotators: annotators below the median inconsistency ratio assign significantly higher harm ratings than those above it (mean difference 13.19 points, t=5.31, p<.001), demonstrating that inconsistency is directionally biased toward permissive harm judgments rather than symmetric noise. The paper also contrasts content types: all inconsistencies on generic content (greetings, acknowledgments) are classified as non-attitudes, while value-laden and task-based content yields predominantly constructed preferences, showing that the failure mode depends on whether the content provides a plausible basis for stable preferences. The authors explicitly note that whether filtering or weighting based on these diagnostics improves downstream reward model performance remains an open empirical question, positioning their contribution as a diagnostic framework rather than a drop-in replacement for existing aggregation methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper imports a six-decade behavioral science literature on non-attitudes (Converse, 1964; Krosnick, 1991), preference construction (Slovic, 1995; Payne et al., 1993), and measurement invariance (Vandenberg & Lance, 2000) into the RLHF setting, arguing that measurement validity is logically prior to the preference aggregation and pluralistic alignment questions that dominate current work (Sorensen et al., 2024; Siththaranjan et al., 2024; Poddar et al., 2024). It departs from the annotation-quality literature (Dawid & Skene, 1979; Shen et al., 2024; Movva et al., 2025), which assumes preferences exist and focuses on recovering or weighting them, by foregrounding the prior question of whether responses are preferences at all. The paper also complements the emerging measurement-validity-in-ML line (Jacobs & Wallach, 2021; Wallach et al., 2025), which addresses what construct is being measured, by addressing whether individual responses reflect that construct.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If annotation responses frequently carry no signal or only framing-dependent partial signal, then reward models trained on them encode elicitation artifacts rather than human values, with concrete consequences: filtering high-inconsistency annotators flips majority harm classifications for 18.6% of prompts and shifts mean ratings by 13.2 points. The paper leaves open whether consistency-based filtering or weighting actually improves downstream reward model performance, what minimum diagnostic investment is needed for large-scale pipelines, and whether the directional bias toward permissive harm judgments generalizes beyond the two datasets studied. The framework extends to any setting treating human judgments as ground truth—constitutional AI, red-teaming, LLM-as-judge evaluation—making it a potential reframe for a broad swath of alignment practice.
