## Problem

LLM-based data augmentation in clinical domains frequently alters or fabricates critical medical information (diagnoses, symptoms, vital signs) during rewriting, introducing hallucinations that degrade downstream prediction models. Existing augmentation methods—naive LLM rephrasing, causally-driven style-only rewriting (CATO), and domain-pretrained LLMs—lack explicit domain-specific safeguards to distinguish safety-critical tokens from stylistic ones, leaving a gap between LLM generative capability and the strict accuracy requirements of high-stakes medical applications.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260723/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a raw clinical note (free-text discharge summary from MIMIC-III, potentially spanning multiple paragraphs) and a constraint set of safety-critical medical entities extracted by a lightweight DistilBERT-based NER model (biomedical-ner-all, recognizing 107 entity types). It produces a rewritten clinical note that preserves all identified medical entities verbatim while varying non-critical elements such as sentence structure, phrasing, and patient identifiers. For long notes, Cache-Augmented Generation (CAG) extends the context window so the generalist maintains coherence across the full document. Augmented notes are paired with their original labels for supervised training, and a validation step discards any sample whose preservation rate falls below a threshold.

## Method

The core contribution is a query-based model collaboration framework with two components: a lightweight 'weak expert' (a BERT-based clinical NER model) that identifies safety-critical tokens as unalterable constraints, and a 'strong generalist' (an instruction-tuned LLM such as Qwen-3-0.6B or Llama-3.2-3B) that performs the actual rewriting. The pipeline operates in three inference-time steps: (1) the weak expert extracts the critical entity set K_i from the input note, (2) a constraint-aware prompt is constructed embedding the original text plus explicit instructions to preserve every token in K_i while freely rephrasing non-critical content, and (3) the strong generalist generates the augmented note conditioned on this prompt. Theoretically, even a noisy weak expert with recall r strictly reduces the expected corruption rate of critical information from p_c to rε + (1−r)p_c, guaranteeing no degradation relative to unguided augmentation. The framework requires no training (all components are frozen pre-trained models), and the expert guidance can optionally be distilled into a single model via Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), where expert-guided outputs serve as preferred samples over naive paraphrases.

## Results

On MIMIC-III, the proposed expert-guided augmentation (Ours) achieves the best downstream performance across all three clinical tasks for both model architectures. For Qwen-3, it reaches 0.599±0.03 accuracy on 30-day readmission, 0.917±0.02 on in-hospital mortality, and 15.563±3.26 RMSE on length-of-stay prediction. For BERT, it achieves 0.757±0.01, 0.929±0.03, and 13.110±0.06 respectively. In terms of augmentation quality, Ours attains the highest entity preservation rate (PR 0.79 token / 0.73 concept) and lowest hallucination rate (HR 0.33 token / 0.26 concept) among all methods evaluated on 300 samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all four baselines on every downstream task for both Qwen-3 and BERT. Against Naive LLM augmentation, Ours improves readmission accuracy by 7.9 points (0.599 vs. 0.520) and reduces length-of-stay RMSE by 0.8 days for Qwen-3; Naive actually degrades mortality accuracy below the no-augmentation baseline. Against CATO (Feder et al., 2023), Ours gains 4.7 points on readmission (0.599 vs. 0.552) and 2.2 RMSE points on length-of-stay (15.563 vs. 18.677) for Qwen-3, while CATO harms mortality and length-of-stay relative to no augmentation. Against the Biomedical LLM (BioMistral-7B) baseline, Ours leads by 6.4 points on readmission and 3.5 RMSE points on length-of-stay, showing that simply using a domain-pretrained LLM without explicit entity constraints is insufficient. Against the rule-based SynName+SciName method, Ours improves readmission by 7.5 points and length-of-stay RMSE by 2.0 days. In augmentation quality (Table 2), Ours achieves 0.79 token-level PR vs. 0.51 (Naive), 0.47 (CATO), 0.40 (Biomedical LLM), and 0.46 (SynName+SciName), while reducing hallucination rate to 0.33 vs. 0.59, 0.62, 0.78, and 0.47 respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Feder et al. (2023, CATO), which introduced the causal V/U (critical vs. non-critical variable) distinction for clinical text augmentation and prompted LLMs to modify only stylistic features. The key departure is that CATO and other LLM-based methods (naive rephrasing, BioMistral) rely on the LLM to implicitly distinguish critical from non-critical content, whereas this work explicitly injects domain knowledge at inference time via a lightweight BERT-based NER model (the 'weak expert') that flags unalterable tokens before the LLM rewrites. The framework also draws on the model-collaboration and weak-to-strong generalization literature (Li et al., 2024a; Burns et al., 2023), and extends the approach with a DPO-based distillation step that attempts to internalize the dual-model policy into a single generalist.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete safety gap in clinical NLP: LLM-based augmentation routinely drops or fabricates medical entities, and the proposed lightweight, training-free pipeline (≈18% overhead over naive LLM augmentation) substantially closes that gap with a clean theoretical bound showing even a noisy weak expert strictly reduces corruption. Open questions remain: the weak expert only captures entity-level criticality (missing contextual or implicit clinical signals), the DPO-distilled single model still trails the dual-model pipeline—especially for backbones lacking medical pretraining priors—and generalization beyond clinical text to other safety-critical domains (legal, financial) is untested. For practitioners deploying LLM augmentation in regulated settings, the cost-quality tradeoff analysis (Table 12) provides a practical argument for inference-time expert guidance over expensive fine-tuning.
