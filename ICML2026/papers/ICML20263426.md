## Problem

Supervised CNNs achieve state-of-the-art performance on imaging inverse problems but remain poorly understood theoretically and are typically treated as black boxes. While it is standard to assume a trained network approximates the MMSE estimator, no closed-form expression existed for the MMSE under the functional constraints that actually define CNNs—translation equivariance and locality via finite receptive fields—for general linear inverse problems. This paper derives such an expression and shows it quantitatively predicts trained network outputs.

## Input / Output

The analytical estimator takes a noisy measurement y = Ax + e (with Gaussian noise of level σ), a linear forward operator A, a pre-inverse B (identity for physics-agnostic or pseudoinverse/transpose for physics-aware), and a finite training dataset D of clean images. It produces a pixel-wise reconstruction x̂ computed as a weighted average over all pixels in all training images, where weights are Gaussian similarities between local patches of size √P × √P extracted with circular boundary conditions. Experiments use 32×32 RGB images (validated up to 128×128) with 10⁴ training samples, and the analytical formula is evaluated with an online log-sum-exp implementation to handle the large number of pairwise patch distances.

## Method

The paper derives a closed-form expression for the Local-Equivariant MMSE (LE-MMSE) estimator by solving the constrained least-squares problem over the function class of maps that are simultaneously translation equivariant and local (output at pixel n depends only on a fixed-size patch around n). The resulting formula (Theorem 3.8) expresses each output pixel as a weighted average of all training-image pixels, with weights proportional to exp(−‖Qₙ⁺Δ + Qₙ⁺Qₙ′e‖²/2σ²), where Qₙ = ΠₙB extracts and pre-inverses local patches—making it a data-driven analogue of non-local means that uses training patches rather than input-image patches. This generalizes the denoising-specific formulas of Kamb & Ganguli (2025) to arbitrary linear forward operators and pre-inverses. The paper further analyzes how the choice of B (physics-aware vs. physics-agnostic) trades off signal discrimination against noise amplification in an operator-dependent way, and shows that the LE-MMSE, unlike the unconstrained MMSE or E-MMSE, can produce reconstructions outside the convex hull of the training set by recombining patches. Extensive experiments confirm that U-Net, ResNet, and PatchMLP outputs align with the LE-MMSE formula at PSNR ≥ 25 dB across denoising, inpainting, deconvolution, and accelerated MRI, with the best agreement in high-density regions of the measurement distribution.

## Results

The headline result is that all three tested architectures (UNet2D, ResNet, PatchMLP) closely match the analytical LE-MMSE formula with PSNR ≳ 25 dB across all inverse problems (denoising, inpainting, deconvolution) and datasets (FFHQ-32, CIFAR-10, FashionMNIST). On FashionMNIST, alignment is strongest (up to 40.2 dB for ResNet deconvolution at σ=0.80), while FFHQ-32 shows the lowest values (down to ~21.7 dB for ResNet inpainting at σ=0.05), attributed to the higher complexity of face images and low-density measurement regions. Validation on 4× accelerated MRI (FastMRI, 128×128) confirms the theory generalizes beyond natural images, with PSNR ≥ 32 dB on train and ≥ 28.6 dB on test between the trained ResNet and the analytical formula.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is not between competing reconstruction methods but between three distinct CNN architectures (UNet2D, ResNet, PatchMLP) and a single analytical formula (LE-MMSE). All three architectures converge to essentially the same output, matching the analytical formula with PSNR ≳ 25 dB in every setting, demonstrating that architectural choice does not change the learned solution. ResNet tends to achieve the highest PSNR against the formula on deconvolution (up to 41.6 dB on FFHQ-32 at σ=0.80), while PatchMLP is slightly weaker on inpainting (down to 23.2 dB on FashionMNIST at σ=0.05). FashionMNIST consistently yields 2–3 dB higher alignment than CIFAR-10 and FFHQ-32, attributed to the lower structural complexity of the images. The theory also predicts where alignment degrades: in low-density regions of the measurement distribution (low σ, OOD test images), the gap widens, but PSNR remains above ~23 dB even in the worst cases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the analytic MMSE framework of Kamb & Ganguli (2025), which derived closed-form equivariant and local estimators for denoising in the diffusion model context, to arbitrary linear inverse problems (deconvolution, inpainting, accelerated MRI) with a pre-inverse operator B. It builds on the deep Bayesian inversion literature (Adler & Oktem, 2018a) that assumed trained CNNs approximate the posterior mean, but departs by showing that the relevant estimator is not the posterior mean but a constrained variant (LE-MMSE) that forms patchwork reconstructions. The paper also establishes a rigorous connection to classical non-local means (Buades et al., 2005), positioning modern CNNs as data-adaptive, structured generalizations of this classical method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first closed-form analytic characterization of what trained CNNs actually compute for general linear inverse problems, moving the field beyond the heuristic assumption that networks approximate the posterior mean. The physics-aware vs. physics-agnostic analysis (Section 3.5) offers concrete, operator-dependent guidance for choosing pre-inverses, and the patchwork interpretation explains both generalization and the train-test gap in low-density regions. Limitations include the restriction to linear operators, Gaussian noise, and 2D images; extending to nonlinear forward models, non-Gaussian noise, transformers, and perceptual losses are identified as open directions. The computational cost of evaluating the analytic formula (~0.7s vs ~4ms for a network) also limits its direct practical use, positioning it primarily as a theoretical tool for analysis and architecture design.
