## Problem

Anonymization is widely treated as a practical privacy safeguard, yet existing evaluations of LLM agents focus on explicit data access or disclosure rather than whether agents can synthesize fragmented, individually non-identifying cues into a concrete identity hypothesis. No prior work systematically examines how linkage risk shifts with the type of shared cues, the framing of the task, or the attacker's prior knowledge—especially whether identity reconstruction arises as a byproduct of otherwise benign analysis rather than an explicit re-identification objective.

## Input / Output

The unified interface Π: (D_anon, D_aux) → (î, E) takes an anonymized artifact (sparse rating records, unstructured search histories, redacted interview transcripts, or masked multi-turn chat logs) and auxiliary context that is either a fixed provided source (e.g., a noisy rating fragment in the Netflix setting) or evidence accumulated through open-web retrieval (e.g., public profiles in AOL or Anthropic Interviewer cases). The agent produces a specific identity hypothesis with supporting evidence drawn from both sources; evaluation yields linkage success rate (LSR) when ground truth is known, confirmed linkage count (CLC) when it is not, and a utility score measuring whether the intended benign task was completed.

## Method

The paper introduces InferLink, a controlled benchmark of 180 instances (3 fingerprint types × 3 task settings × 20 paired instances) where each instance contains two structured tables (10 records × 9 features) with exactly one ground-truth overlap, systematically varying fingerprint type (INTRINSIC, COORDINATE, HYBRID), task intent (IMPLICIT benign analysis vs. EXPLICIT re-identification), and attacker knowledge (zero-knowledge vs. named target). Agents (GPT-5, Claude 4.5, o4-mini) run inside OpenHands and are evaluated jointly on linkage risk and task utility, revealing that identity reconstruction emerges even under benign framing (silent risk) and that a privacy-aware system prompt suppresses linkage but incurs a measurable utility cost. The framework is further validated on classical incidents (Netflix Prize, AOL search logs) and open-ended human-AI interaction traces (Anthropic Interviewer, ChatGPT logs), demonstrating that the same inference-driven linkage mechanism persists across structured, semi-structured, and interactive settings without bespoke engineering.

## Results

In the Netflix Prize setting, GPT-5 reconstructs 79.17% ± 4.97 of identities in the sparsest regime (m=2 overlapping ratings), substantially exceeding the classical Narayanan–Shmatikov baseline of 56.0%–60.2%; at m=8 the agent reaches 99.00% ± 0.72. On the InferLink benchmark, agents produce identity hypotheses even under benign (IMPLICIT) task framing—Claude 4.5 links in 70–80% of cases across fingerprint types—while under explicit re-identification requests (EXPLICIT-MK) linkage reaches 95–100% for GPT-5 and Claude 4.5. In open-ended settings, the agent corroborates 10 identities from AOL search logs (CLC=10), 6 from redacted Anthropic Interviewer transcripts (CLC=6), and 1 from anonymized ChatGPT conversation logs (CLC=1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole direct baseline is the hand-engineered rarity-weighted matching method of Narayanan & Shmatikov (2008), evaluated with date tolerances T=14 and T=21. GPT-5 outperforms this baseline at every fragment size, with the largest margin in the sparsest regime (m=2): 79.17% vs. 56.0% (T=14) and 60.2% (T=21), a gain of roughly 19–23 percentage points where classical linkage was historically most difficult. At m=4 the gap narrows to about 3–4 points (94.83% vs. 90.5–91.8%), and at m=6–8 both methods approach the ceiling (≥97%). Claude 4.5 tells a different story: it underperforms the baseline at m=2 (53.30% vs. 56.0–60.2%) but surpasses it at m≥4 (93–97%), suggesting that sparse linkage success depends on a model's ability to resolve noisy, ambiguous matches rather than on raw data overlap alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the classical data-linkage problem (Netflix Prize, AOL search logs) for the LLM-agent era, showing that the historical practical barrier to de-anonymization—domain expertise and bespoke engineering—is eroded by general-purpose reasoning and retrieval. It departs from prior agent-privacy evaluations (PrivacyLens, AgentDAM, contextual-integrity probing), which measure access, use, or explicit disclosure of sensitive data, by introducing identity reconstruction as the evaluation target. Concurrent work (Li 2026; Lermen et al. 2026) demonstrates LLM-based deanonymization in specific settings, but this paper is the first to systematically vary fingerprint type, task framing, and attacker knowledge in a controlled benchmark (InferLink) and to measure the resulting privacy-utility trade-off.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that agents reconstruct identities as a byproduct of benign cross-source analysis (Claude 4.5: LSR 0.70–0.80 under IMPLICIT framing) challenges the assumption that de-anonymization risk materializes only under explicit adversarial intent, implying that current agent safety evaluations are incomplete. The privacy-utility trade-off—where anti-linkage guardrails suppress reconstruction but degrade legitimate task performance (Claude 4.5 utility drops from 0.99 to 0.45 under EXPLICIT-MK)—poses a concrete design tension for practitioners deploying agents over anonymized data. Open questions include whether these results generalize to near-duplicate populations, whether more targeted defenses can avoid the utility cost, and how linkage risk scales with the size and heterogeneity of real-world candidate pools.
