## Problem

Object-centric representations are conjectured to support compositional generalization by decomposing scenes into discrete object slots, but systematic evidence in visually rich settings has been limited to simple synthetic worlds, single-object scenarios, or generative protocols that cannot evaluate general image representations. This paper asks whether OC representations genuinely outperform dense encoders on object-property compositional generalization, and under what conditions (data size, diversity, compute) each representation type is preferable.

## Input / Output

The model takes a 224×224 synthetic scene image (3–6 objects rendered in Blender from CLEVRTex, Super-CLEVR, or MOVi-C style worlds) and a natural-language question encoded by a frozen T5-base model. The output is a single class label drawn from a fixed vocabulary of 28 (CLEVRTex), 106 (Super-CLEVR), or 48 (MOVi-C) answers, spanning yes/no, object counts, and all property values. Each image is paired with ~42 question-answer pairs on average, yielding roughly 1.7M training pairs and 170k test pairs per dataset variant.

## Method

The paper introduces a VQA benchmark across three controlled visual worlds where 20% of all object-property combinations are held out for compositional out-of-distribution (COOD) testing, with three training splits (easy/medium/hard) that progressively reduce the diversity of seen objects. Dense encoders (DINOv2 ViT-S/14, SigLIP2 ViT-B/16) are compared against their OC counterparts (DINOSAURv2, SigLIPSAUR2), which are pretrained via Slot Attention to reconstruct the dense features, producing 7 slot vectors of dimension 256. To ensure fair comparison, a single cross-attention layer resizes representations to matched token counts and dimensions, equalizing downstream FLOPs. The downstream VQA model is a 2- or 5-layer transformer (d_model=128) that fuses image and text tokens and predicts the answer via a CLS token. The key experimental axes are training diversity, sample size (2^10 to 40k images), and downstream compute, revealing that OC representations consistently achieve stronger COOD generalization whenever any of these resources is constrained, while dense encoders catch up only with sufficient data, diversity, and a larger downstream model.

## Results

On the COOD VQA benchmark, object-centric DINOSAURv2 outperforms dense DINOv2 by up to +12.3% (CLEVRTex medium, TF2: 71.2% vs. 58.8%) and +7.0% (CLEVRTex easy, TF2: 76.5% vs. 69.5%) with the small downstream model. With the larger TF5 model, the advantage reverses on easy settings (DINOv2: 85.4% vs. DINOSAURv2: 82.5% on CLEVRTex easy) but DINOSAURv2 matches or slightly surpasses on hard (55.5% vs. 55.4% on CLEVRTex hard). The same pattern holds for SigLIP2 vs. SigLIPSAUR2, where the OC variant gains up to +9.7% (CLEVRTex medium, TF2). Across all settings, the OC advantage is largest on the texture-rich CLEVRTex and smallest on MOVi-C.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DINOv2 (dense), DINOSAURv2 (OC) wins on all three datasets with the small TF2 downstream model, with the largest gap on CLEVRTex (+7.0 to +12.3%) and smaller but consistent gains on Super-CLEVR (+1.2 to +1.6%) and MOVi-C (+1.0 to +1.6%); with TF5, DINOv2 recovers the lead on easy settings (e.g., +2.9% on CLEVRTex) while DINOSAURv2 matches or edges ahead on hard (0.0 to +0.4%). The same pattern holds for SigLIP2 vs. SigLIPSAUR2, where the OC variant gains up to +9.7% (TF2, CLEVRTex medium) but cedes easy-setting performance to the dense encoder under TF5. The k-means baseline from Baldassarre et al. with only 7 tokens is substantially worse than both dense and OC (e.g., −16.5% vs. DINOv2 on CLEVRTex easy, TF2), and even with 128 tokens it only partially closes the gap, confirming that Slot Attention's learned soft clustering is critical for VQA. Cross-attention resizing of dense representations to 7 tokens (DINOv2+CA) underperforms DINOSAURv2 on COOD, while expanding DINOSAURv2 to 256 tokens (DINOSAURv2+CA) also degrades performance, indicating that the structured OC bottleneck itself—not merely token count—drives the generalization advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the object-centric learning line (Slot Attention, DINOSAURv2) by introducing SigLIPSAUR2, the first OC variant of SigLIP2, and positions its evaluation against the compositional generalization benchmarks of Kim et al. (2024) and Montero et al. (2024), which are limited to generative protocols or single-object settings. It departs from prior OC evaluation work (which typically varies only object count) by targeting object-property composition in visually rich synthetic worlds (CLEVRTex, Super-CLEVR, MOVi-C) and using VQA as the unified evaluation task, following the downstream setup of Mamaghan et al. (2025). The k-means clustering baseline from Baldassarre et al. (2025) is incorporated as an alternative to Slot Attention, and the compute-matched comparison via cross-attention resizing is a methodological contribution not present in prior OC-vs-dense studies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic, compute-matched evidence that OC representations confer a compositional generalization advantage in visually rich settings, with the important nuance that the advantage is conditional: it holds when data diversity, sample size, or downstream compute is constrained, while dense encoders can match or surpass OC on easier settings given sufficient resources. The finding that training diversity matters more than raw sample size (40% of the most diverse data outperforms 100% of a less diverse set) offers practical guidance for dataset curation. Key limitations include the reliance on synthetic worlds (necessary for controlled property combinations but limiting real-world transfer), a single OC mechanism (Slot Attention with MLP decoder), and a single pretraining objective (dense-feature reconstruction), leaving open whether conclusions hold for pixel-space or contrastive OC objectives and for real imagery.
