## Problem

LLM-based narrator agents can produce fluent interactive narratives but systematically fail to preserve established world-state facts, causal dependencies, and mandatory plot commitments over long horizons, especially under adversarial user interventions. Existing evaluations rely on preference-based metrics (perceived coherence, engagement) that cannot detect or quantify logical inconsistencies, making it impossible to compare methods on the specific failure mode of commitment violation.

## Input / Output

The narrator agent receives free-form first-person player actions, the full interaction history, and a structured narrative specification ⟨F₀, C, R⟩ (initial fact ledger, commitment set, reference trajectory) derived from a movie synopsis, and produces a second-person narrative response of at most 150 words per turn. The fixed auditing pipeline then outputs typed conflict detections (fact, commitment, player-input) with a secondary confirmation step, incremental fact ledger updates (added and negated atomic facts), a trajectory progress index, and per-commitment status (PENDING or SATISFIED). The interaction terminates on CONFLICT, SURVIVAL at 100 turns, or SUCCESS when all achievement commitments are met.

## Method

The paper formalizes Narrative Commitment Preservation (NCP) as a long-horizon constraint satisfaction problem and introduces NCP-Bench, a benchmark of 100 narrative environments constructed from the CMU Movie Summary Corpus through a three-stage agentic pipeline (trajectory extraction, commitment extraction, initial fact extraction) with human verification. The evaluation framework decouples the narrator agent under test from a fixed, prompt-specified auditing protocol that runs four checks each turn: a three-dimensional conflict check (fact, commitment, player-input) with a double-check pass to reduce false positives, a fact-update extraction, a trajectory-node advancement test, and a commitment-status audit. An adversarial player agent, restricted to visible in-world information, generates stress-testing interventions (speedrun attempts, premature accusations, boundary probes) to expose consistency failures. The benchmark is evaluated across six state-of-the-art LLMs (GPT-5.2, GPT-4o-mini, DeepSeek-V3.2, Qwen3-235B-A22B, Kimi-K2.5, Grok-4.1-Fast) using Gemini-2.5-Flash as the default auditor, with ablations on auditor sensitivity, memory-augmented baselines (HiAgent), and natural versus adversarial player inputs.

## Results

Across six state-of-the-art LLMs evaluated on NCP-Bench (100 movie-derived narrative environments), GPT-5.2 achieves the highest average interaction length (32.92 turns) and the lowest fact conflict rate (40%), while DeepSeek-V3.2 achieves the highest trajectory progress (15.40%) and satisfied commitment rate (13.42%). Survival rates decline sharply with interaction depth: even GPT-5.2 retains only 42% of environments after 20 turns, and across all 600 runs, fewer than 3.5% reach the 100-turn limit without conflict. Fact conflicts dominate failures (40%–68% across models), and only isolated runs satisfy all achievement commitments within the 100-turn limit.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-5.2 leads in long-horizon consistency, surviving 32.92 turns on average with the lowest fact (40%) and commitment (24%) conflict rates, but it advances the plot most slowly (9.94% trajectory progress). DeepSeek-V3.2 compensates with aggressive plot advancement, reaching 15.40% trajectory progress and 13.42% satisfied commitments despite surviving fewer turns (15.88), suggesting a faster-but-shorter interaction style. GPT-4o-mini occupies a middle ground (24.80 turns, 10.57% trajectory) with the lowest player-input conflict rate (11%). Kimi-K2.5 and Grok-4.1-Fast terminate very early (2.88 and 7.87 turns respectively), with Kimi-K2.5 showing the highest commitment conflict rate (54%) and lowest satisfied rate (5.18%). The memory-augmented HiAgent baseline (built on GPT-4o-mini) extends survival to 30.05 turns and cuts commitment conflicts from 26% to 4%, but doubles player-input conflicts (13%→38%) and still fails to satisfy all achievement commitments, confirming that memory augmentation alone does not solve NCP.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the interactive narrative literature (Mateas & Stern 2003; Riedl & Young 2010; Szilas 2005) and recent LLM-based storytelling systems (Teleki et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2025; Xia et al. 2025) by reframing narrative consistency from an implicit, preference-based quality criterion into an explicit, checkable constraint-satisfaction problem. It draws on the hallucination and self-contradiction literature (Ji et al. 2023; Mündler et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2025b) and adversarial stress-testing methods (Perez et al. 2022; Wei et al. 2023), but applies them to a structured, stateful setting with an explicit fact ledger and commitment set rather than free-form generation. The evaluation protocol borrows from LLM-as-a-judge frameworks (Gu et al. 2024; Lee et al. 2025) but introduces a multi-step auditing pipeline (conflict check → fact update → trajectory update → commitment check) with a secondary confirmation step to reduce false positives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even the strongest current LLMs (GPT-5.2) fail to preserve narrative commitments in over half of environments within 20 turns, and that memory augmentation (HiAgent) trades commitment tracking for player-input fidelity—a fundamental tension that remains unresolved. The finding that cooperative (non-adversarial) play also fails to solve the task indicates the core challenge is long-horizon state maintenance, not merely robustness to adversarial inputs. Open questions include whether nonlinear narrative structures (branching timelines, flashbacks) can be handled, whether the adversarial player strategy is representative of real user behavior, and whether the NCP formulation generalizes to other domains requiring persistent obligation tracking (coding agents, multi-agent coordination). The benchmark is limited to single-threaded, chronologically ordered narratives and LLM-based auditors that can err on ambiguous text.
