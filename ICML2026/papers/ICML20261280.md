## Problem

Particle Filtering (PF) for inference-time scaling is vulnerable to premature exploitation when guided by process reward models that assign overconfident scores to early reasoning steps. This causes particle impoverishment: the resampling distribution collapses onto a few trajectories, pruning potentially correct hypotheses before they can reveal their value, especially under constrained particle budgets. No existing PF variant dynamically monitors and corrects for this diversity loss while also addressing the inherent myopia of step-local resampling.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a math problem prompt, a frozen language model (sampler) that generates reasoning tokens autoregressively, and a frozen process reward model (PRM) that scores each intermediate reasoning step. It maintains N parallel weighted trajectories (particles) over up to 300 generation steps with 512 tokens per step, and outputs the final decoded solution extracted from the highest-reward particle. The particle budget N (2–32) and the ESS threshold (0.5) control the exploration-exploitation trade-off, with ePF active for the first 50% of steps.

## Method

Entropic Particle Filtering (ePF) introduces two complementary mechanisms into the standard propagate-weight-resample cycle. First, Entropic Annealing (EA) computes the effective sample size (ESS) at each step and sets the resampling temperature as β_t⁻¹ = (N/ESS(t))·(1−t/T), so that low diversity triggers a flatter softmax over PRM rewards, directly reducing resampling-weight variance and preventing collapse; as t→T the temperature anneals back to 1 for exploitation. Second, Look-ahead Modulation (LaM) samples a one-step successor state for each particle using the dynamics model, scores it with the PRM, and multiplies the current resampling weight by this predicted successor reward before normalizing, making the resampling decision less myopic without altering the forward propagation. The look-ahead states are discarded after modulation, and LaM is triggered only when ESS falls below threshold (~10–12% of steps), keeping the computational overhead minimal while achieving comparable accuracy with 1/4 to 1/2 the particle budget.

## Results

On the hardest benchmark (AIME 2025) with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, ePF achieves 28.83% Top-1 accuracy (proportional weighting), outperforming standard PF (21.61%), Beam Search (14.19%), and Best-of-N (17.41%). On AIME 2024 with Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, ePF reaches 17.06% vs. PF's 11.16%, a 53% relative improvement. On broader math benchmarks (MATH500, DEEPMATH) with Qwen2.5-1.5B, ePF achieves 66.42% and 25.00% Top-1 accuracy respectively, surpassing PF (60.15%, 22.65%) and all other baselines. With Look-ahead Modulation, ePF w/ LaM on AIME 2025 (Qwen3-1.7B) reaches 26.97% Top-1, and at 12k sequence budget, ePF elevates Qwen3-1.7B to 38.9 on AIME 2025, outperforming dedicated reasoning models like R1-distilled-Qwen-1.5B (23.1) and Nemotron-R-1.5B (33.6).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ePF consistently outperforms standard Particle Filtering (Puri et al., 2025) across all AIME configurations, with the largest gap on AIME 2024 (Qwen2.5-1.5B): 17.06% vs. 11.16% Top-1 (proportional weighting), and on AIME 2025 (Qwen2.5-7B): 28.83% vs. 21.61%. Against Best-of-N (Brown et al., 2024), ePF wins by a wide margin on AIME 2025 (Qwen2.5-7B): 28.83% vs. 17.41%, and on AIME 2024 (Qwen2.5-1.5B): 17.06% vs. 9.90%. Beam Search (Vijayakumar et al., 2016) is also clearly surpassed, e.g., 28.83% vs. 14.19% on AIME 2025 (Qwen2.5-7B). On easier benchmarks (MATH500, DEEPMATH), ePF outperforms Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2022) by 12.5 and 11.7 points respectively with Qwen2.5-1.5B. ePF also surpasses Base Sampling (Yang et al., 2025) by large margins across all settings. The advantage over PF is most pronounced at small particle budgets and on harder problems, confirming that mitigating premature exploitation is most valuable when computational resources are constrained and the search space is large.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Particle Filtering (PF) for LLM inference-time scaling (Puri et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2024) by diagnosing a specific failure mode—particle impoverishment caused by overconfident process reward models—and introducing two targeted fixes. Entropic Annealing draws on maximum-entropy and variance-reduction ideas from SMC to dynamically flatten the resampling distribution when effective sample size drops, while Look-ahead Modulation adapts the Auxiliary Particle Filter (Pitt & Shephard, 1999) look-ahead mechanism to the LLM setting. The work positions itself against the broader ITS landscape (Self-Consistency, Best-of-N, Beam Search, MCTS, DORA) as a learning-free, calibration-free alternative that specifically addresses the exploration-exploitation imbalance that plagues population-based search under noisy rewards.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ePF offers a principled, drop-in fix for a well-documented weakness of PF-based ITS: premature convergence under overconfident PRMs, which is most damaging under tight compute budgets where practitioners actually operate. The iso-computational result (8-particle ePF w/ LaM matching 32-particle standard ePF) suggests that predictive guidance can substitute for brute-force parallelism, a potentially important efficiency insight for deployment. However, the method's gains diminish as particle budgets grow, LaM adds a forward-pass overhead, and performance remains bounded by PRM quality—so the approach is most valuable in the small-budget, imperfect-reward regime. Open questions include whether the ESS-based annealing schedule generalizes to non-mathematical reasoning tasks and whether better PRM calibration would reduce the need for such corrective mechanisms.
