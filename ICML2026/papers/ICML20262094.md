## Problem

Existing preference-based learning methods for LLMs (DPO, IPO, RPO, KTO, etc.) interpret human feedback through reward maximization, treating preferences as proxies for immediate or accumulated utility assigned to observed trajectory segments. However, human judgment is inherently prospective and counterfactual—evaluators anticipate future outcomes and compare against unrealized alternatives—so preferences encode relative suboptimality rather than absolute goodness. This paper argues that the reward-maximization interpretation is a fundamental mechanism-level mismatch with how humans actually form preferences over intermediate reasoning steps, and that this mismatch persists regardless of annotation pipeline or heuristic.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pairwise preference data over chain-of-thought trajectories (q≤t+, q≤t−) together with token-level log-probabilities under the behavior policy μ that generated each trajectory, plus a fixed reference policy πref (the base LLM). A key distinguishing requirement is access to behavior-policy log-probabilities at every token position, which enables computation of the sequential KL divergence term; when this metadata is unavailable (e.g., offline datasets from different model families), RePO_det substitutes a Dirac pseudo-label at the observed output. The output is a LoRA-fine-tuned LLM policy trained to minimize regret rather than maximize a scalar reward, evaluated on both human-preference benchmarks (AlpacaEval 2, Arena-Hard, MT-Bench) and mathematical reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, MATH500, AMC23, MinervaMath).

## Method

RePO reframes preference learning as regret minimization within a KL-regularized RL framework, where the preference score is negative regret: Reg_π*^μ(q<t, o) = V^π*(q<t) − Q^μ(q<t, o). The central theoretical result (Theorem 5.4) shows that the Q-function gap between any behavior policy μ and the optimal policy π* equals α times a discounted sequential forward KL divergence D̄_KL(μ‖π*; q<t, o), yielding a closed-form regret decomposition into a local DPO-like log-likelihood ratio term and a long-horizon policy-deviation term. This decomposition is invariant to the state-dependent reward shaping function β(·) identified in Lemma 5.3, making RePO robust to reward-scale ambiguity that plagues reward-maximization objectives. Practically, the sequential KL is estimated via a finite-horizon Monte Carlo approximation reusing observed trajectory segments as single rollouts, and RePO_det further simplifies by replacing μ with a Dirac distribution when behavior-policy access is infeasible. Lemma 6.1 establishes that regret structurally internalizes an inductive bias—partially observed successful trajectories are evaluated pessimistically—so RePO achieves sample efficiency gains without the masked-data augmentation that DPO requires to learn the same bias.

## Results

On human preference alignment (AlpacaEval 2), RePO achieves 36.61% length-controlled win rate on Qwen3-1.7B-Base and 55.08% on Qwen3-4B-Base, substantially outperforming DPO (23.90% and 32.89% respectively) and KTO (34.73% and 52.31%). On mathematical reasoning, RePO reaches 80.52% GSM8K and 57.40% MATH500 (1.7B) and 90.60% GSM8K and 66.20% MATH500 (4B), consistently leading DPO and its variants. The behavior-policy-free variant RePO_det maintains competitive performance (e.g., 34.95% AlpacaEval2 LC, 80.74% GSM8K on 1.7B) and generalizes to cross-model settings (Llama3.1-8B, Llama3.2-1B, Gemma-2-2B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RePO decisively outperforms DPO across all benchmarks: on AlpacaEval2 LC, RePO gains +12.71 points (1.7B) and +22.19 points (4B) over DPO, and on GSM8K it gains +3.19 (1.7B) and +2.73 (4B). Against KTO, the strongest baseline, RePO wins on AlpacaEval2 (36.61 vs 34.73 LC on 1.7B; 55.08 vs 52.31 on 4B) and MT-Bench GPT-5.1 (5.43 vs 5.32 on 1.7B), but KTO retains an edge on Arena-Hard (30.4 vs 27.1 on 1.7B; 63.9 vs 60.1 on 4B) and MT-Bench GPT-4.1 for the 4B model (8.22 vs 8.09). RePO dominates IPO, RPO, and TDPO on nearly every metric, with the largest gaps over TDPO (e.g., 36.61 vs 12.24 AlpacaEval2 LC on 1.7B). On mathematical reasoning, RePO leads DPO on GSM8K, MATH, and MATH500 but trails KTO on AMC23 (30.00 vs 35.00 on 1.7B; 42.50 vs 55.00 on 4B), suggesting KTO's prospect-theoretic loss aversion provides an advantage on harder, out-of-domain problems. The behavior-policy-free RePO_det variant narrows but does not close the gap to full RePO, confirming that explicit behavior-policy information contributes meaningfully to performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

RePO extends the regret-based preference formulation of Policy-Labeled Preference Learning (Cho et al., 2025) from a maximum-entropy RL setting to the KL-regularized RL framework standard in LLM post-training, changing the local preference term from α·log π* to α·log(π*/π_ref) while preserving the sequential forward KL divergence term. It also builds directly on DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024), whose closed-form policy update appears as the first component of RePO's regret decomposition, but departs from the reward-maximization interpretation by grounding preferences in prospective and counterfactual human reasoning rather than immediate utility. The paper positions itself against a broader family of DPO variants (IPO, RPO, KTO, TDPO) that all inherit the reward-maximization lens, arguing that the interpretive framework—not the optimization algorithm—is the key limitation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The regret-based reframing offers a principled resolution to the β(·) ambiguity identified in Lemma 5.3: because regret is invariant to reward translations, RePO avoids the need for ad-hoc anchoring schemes (normalization, 'I don't know' actions) that reward-maximization methods require under finite data. The finding that RePO structurally internalizes the pessimistic evaluation of incomplete trajectories (Lemma 6.1) without explicit data augmentation suggests a path toward more sample-efficient alignment. Open questions include whether the behavior-policy tracking assumption generalizes to fully off-policy or multi-turn settings, whether the approach scales to frontier-scale models where the sequential KL term becomes more complex, and how to handle the case where the optimal policy π* is itself unknown and must be jointly estimated.
