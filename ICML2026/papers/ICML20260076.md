## Problem

Dual-encoder vision-language models (CLIP, SigLIP) expose a single scalar similarity that fails to execute compositional constraints: queries like 'umbrella and no person' retrieve images containing the excluded concept, and negation produces nearly identical rankings to affirmation (Spearman ρ=0.94). The paper identifies this as an interface-level Bag-of-Concepts effect where similarity scores approximate mean pooling of concept evidence regardless of the logical operator, and shows that fine-tuning does not reliably fix it because the bottleneck is how similarity aggregates evidence, not what the encoders represent.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a text caption containing Boolean operators (negation, conjunction, disjunction, exclusion, NOR) over one to four visual concepts. An LLM parser (GPT-4.1-mini) decomposes the caption into concept phrases with polarity flags and an operator type; each concept is scored independently against the frozen image encoder using template ensembling. The output is a single modified similarity score per image-query pair that preserves holistic retrieval quality (ρ=0.999 with CLIP) while enforcing truth-functional constraint semantics, with the correction vanishing entirely for the ~97.5% of natural captions lacking explicit operators.

## Method

LCSE (Logic-Constrained Score Editing) is a training-free factored inference interface that separates evidence extraction from constraint execution. It computes calibrated per-concept presence scores from the frozen encoder, applies polarity inversion (1−p) for negated concepts, and aggregates via power mean (γ=−1 for conjunction, γ=10 for disjunction) to produce a constraint-appropriate score. The final score is the holistic similarity plus an additive correction in logit space: (1/β)(logit(p_logic) − logit(p_soft)), where p_soft is the arithmetic mean of concept scores, so the correction is zero when no operator is present and adjusts the holistic score only by the gap between constraint-appropriate and mean aggregation. The mechanistic analysis via span-residual decomposition shows that compound text embeddings decompose into a span component (linear in atomic concept embeddings, approximating mean pooling) and an orthogonal residual carrying operator-specific signal that is either too weak (low gain) or misaligned (NOR) to influence rankings under dot-product scoring.

## Results

On CLIP ViT-B/32, LCSE achieves 85.5% accuracy on FACTOR-Bench (Boolean operator semantics) versus 73.2% for the best fine-tuned baseline (NegationCLIP), and 90.7% when applied to SigLIP 2. On NegBench, LCSE improves COCO MCQ accuracy from 27.2% (SigLIP 2) to 65.2% and from 56.2% (NegCLIP-NegFull) to 60.3% (CLIP), while VOC2007 MCQ rises from 59.6% to 73.3%. Critically, LCSE preserves zero-shot retrieval within 0.1 pp of the unmodified backbone (55.2% vs. 55.3% R@5 on COCO, ρ=0.999), whereas all fine-tuned baselines degrade retrieval substantially (ρ=0.63–0.85).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LCSE (CLIP) outperforms every fine-tuned baseline on FACTOR-Bench by a wide margin: 85.5% vs. NegationCLIP (73.2%), NegCLIP-NegFull (68.1%), NegCLIP (67.2%), CLIP-NegFull (66.5%), CoN-CLIP (63.2%), and DCSM (53.6%). On NegBench COCO MCQ, LCSE (60.3%) beats the strongest fine-tuned baseline NegCLIP-NegFull (56.2%) and substantially outperforms NegationCLIP (31.0%) and CoN-CLIP (25.7%); on VOC2007 MCQ the gap is even larger (73.3% vs. 59.6%). The key differentiator is retrieval retention: LCSE maintains ρ=0.999 with the unmodified CLIP backbone (55.2% vs. 55.3% R@5), while all fine-tuned baselines suffer rank displacement (ρ=0.63–0.85) and DCSM degrades most severely (ρ=0.63, R@5 drops from 55.3% to 34.6%). When stacked on top of NegCLIP, LCSE further improves FACTOR-Bench to 86.2% and VOC2007 MCQ to 81.3%, showing the method complements rather than conflicts with backbone-level negation training. Applied to SigLIP 2, LCSE reaches 90.7% FACTOR-Bench and 65.2% NegBench COCO MCQ, confirming that better atomic evidence from a stronger encoder directly translates to better constraint execution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two dominant strategies for fixing compositional failures in dual-encoder VLMs: (1) encoder fine-tuning on negation-augmented data (NegCLIP, CoN-CLIP, NegationCLIP, NegBench fine-tuning), which the authors show targets the wrong bottleneck, and (2) heavy external reasoning pipelines (ViperGPT, visual programming) that introduce new perceptual modules. Instead, it draws on the span-residual decomposition framework from probing literature (Quantmeyer et al., 2024; Lewis et al., 2024) to isolate a specific interface-level mechanism—mean-pooling dominance under dot-product scoring—and fixes it with a lightweight, training-free score edit (LCSE) that preserves the frozen encoder. The approach is conceptually closest to ComCLIP (Jiang et al., 2024) and DCSM (Kang et al., 2025) in modifying the matching interface rather than the encoders, but differs by requiring no learned components and operating on scalar scores rather than patch-level maps.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all six negation-aware fine-tuned CLIP variants score below chance on a basic polarity test (11–19% AUC) is a strong negative result that reframes the compositional-retrieval problem: the bottleneck is how similarity aggregates evidence, not what the encoder represents. LCSE's training-free design with ~4% retrieval overhead makes it immediately deployable in existing CLIP/SigLIP pipelines, and FACTOR-Bench provides a controlled diagnostic that prior benchmarks (NegBench, ARO) lack. Key limitations include dependence on LLM parsing of Boolean structure, validation only on 1–2 visual concepts with Boolean operators, and the open question of whether the factored-inference principle extends to attribute binding, spatial relations, or counting, where reliable atomic evidence may not be available from frozen encoders.
