## Problem

Widely used second-order methods for disentangling aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty (Bayesian, ensemble, evidential) are fundamentally incomplete in two ways: unaccounted model bias contaminates the decomposition by inflating aleatoric and deflating epistemic estimates, and existing methods capture only partial variance components of epistemic uncertainty. Despite nearly 40,000 collective citations of these methods and growing deployment in safety-critical settings, no prior work had simultaneously demonstrated both failure modes and provided a diagnostic to identify which uncertainty sources a given method actually represents.

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on standard supervised learning data: a 1D synthetic heteroscedastic regression (Eq. 6), the NYC taxi trip duration dataset (14 features, ~1.2M training points), and a binary classification task. The key diagnostic output is the reference distribution, an empirical second-order distribution constructed by training n_d × n_γ models across independently resampled datasets and random initializations, which decomposes total epistemic variance into procedural and data components. This reference is then compared pointwise against the epistemic estimates produced by each second-order method (Deep Ensembles, Bootstrap, MC Dropout, VI, Laplace, HMC, EDL, GP) to reveal which variance sources are captured and which are missing.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a theoretical framework that decomposes epistemic uncertainty into five distinct sources—approximation error, estimation bias, data uncertainty, procedural uncertainty, and distributional uncertainty—via a generalized bias-variance decomposition for Bregman divergences (Eq. 7). It proves that the standard variance-based aleatoric/epistemic split is contaminated by bias: the learned predictive variance absorbs systematic mean prediction error, so E[σ̂²] = (f(x)−f̂(x))² + σ²(x), inflating aleatoric estimates while the epistemic term remains low in precisely the regions where the model is least trustworthy. For the variance component, it shows that Deep Ensembles capture only procedural uncertainty (variance across random seeds on a fixed dataset) and entirely miss data uncertainty (variance across resampled datasets), a gap confirmed by the reference distribution diagnostic. The reference distribution (Definition 3.1) formalizes the full estimation uncertainty by marginalizing over both data and procedural randomness, enabling component-level validation of any second-order method. Empirical results across eight methods on synthetic and real data confirm that all evaluated methods systematically underestimate total epistemic uncertainty relative to the reference distribution, with the gap widening in high-bias regions.

## Results

On the synthetic regression task (N=500), the reference distribution yields a total epistemic variance of 0.013, while Deep Ensembles estimate only 0.004 and Bootstrap Ensembles 0.003, confirming systematic underestimation of the full reducible uncertainty. On the NYC Taxi dataset, Deep Ensembles underestimate the reference distribution's total epistemic variance in approximately 80% of test points, with the gap attributable almost entirely to the missing data-uncertainty component. Across all eight evaluated methods, model bias (mean prediction error) is frequently larger than and largely uncorrelated with the reported epistemic uncertainty, and the learned aleatoric variance σ̂(x) systematically exceeds the true noise σ(x) in high-bias regions (e.g., EDL overestimates aleatoric noise by 0.967 on average at N=500).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper that does not propose a new method but empirically demonstrates that all eight evaluated second-order uncertainty estimators are incomplete relative to a reference distribution (which jointly captures data and procedural uncertainty). Deep Ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017) capture only the procedural component: on the taxi dataset, their epistemic estimate matches the reference distribution's procedural variance but falls short of the total by ~80% of test points. Bootstrap Ensembles, despite resampling data, still underestimate the reference distribution's total variance, confirming that finite-sample bootstrap variance is an incomplete proxy. Bayesian methods (HMC via Neal 2012, Laplace via Daxberger et al. 2021, VI via Blundell et al. 2015) produce higher epistemic estimates in low-data regimes, but the paper argues this reflects their failure to learn the true DGP rather than correct variance capture; HMC and Laplace also exhibit extreme aleatoric overestimation (σ̂−σ up to 1.94 and 0.19 respectively at N=500). Deep Evidential Regression (Amini et al., 2020) consistently produces the worst aleatoric estimates (overestimating noise by 0.97 on average) alongside overconfident (too-low) epistemic predictions. Monte Carlo Dropout (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016) and the Heteroscedastic GP (Titsias, 2009) show intermediate behavior, with GP slightly closer to the reference on epistemic variance but still missing the data-uncertainty component. The central finding is uniform: no single method recovers the full reference-distribution variance, and bias systematically inflates the aleatoric term while deflating the epistemic term across all methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper builds on the generalized bias-variance decomposition for Bregman divergences (Gruber et al., 2023; Hastie et al., 2009) and the reference distribution diagnostic from Jürgens et al. (2024) to argue that the dominant second-order uncertainty quantification framework (Kendall & Gal, 2017; Hüllermeier & Waegeman, 2021; Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017) is fundamentally incomplete. It departs from the growing critical literature (Bengs et al., 2022; Wimmer et al., 2023; Smith et al., 2025; Mucsanyi et al., 2024) by providing a unified five-source taxonomy of epistemic uncertainty and demonstrating concretely that (1) regularization-induced bias contaminates the aleatoric/epistemic split, and (2) existing methods capture only procedural rather than data uncertainty. Rather than proposing a new estimator, the paper reframes the interpretive contract: claims of 'epistemic uncertainty' must specify which reducible components are actually represented.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For practitioners deploying uncertainty estimates in safety-critical or high-stakes settings (medical diagnosis, autonomous driving, financial risk), this paper implies that current epistemic uncertainty numbers are lower bounds on true reducible uncertainty and that aleatoric estimates in high-bias regions are systematically inflated. The open question is how to practically estimate the bias component without access to ground truth, and whether the reference distribution diagnostic (requiring repeated resampling) scales to large real-world problems. The paper's call for a finer taxonomy—separating model uncertainty (approximation error + estimation bias) from estimation uncertainty (data + procedural)—could reshape how methods are designed, evaluated, and reported in future work, but no concrete replacement method is proposed.
