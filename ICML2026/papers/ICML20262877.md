## Problem

Magnitude-based stability proxies such as parameter drift are widely used to monitor narrow-task fine-tuning, yet they fail to predict broad-capability degradation: runs with comparable Euclidean displacement can yield sharply different generalization outcomes (the Drift Paradox). The paper asks what geometric structure underlies this mismatch and whether interventions can reorient the update trajectory away from the shared degradation path rather than merely slowing progress along it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262877/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes pre-trained LLaMA-family models (1B–8B) and narrow-task instruction data (MBPP for code, TinyStories for storytelling; N ∈ {64, 256, 1024, 4096}) and produces fine-tuned LoRA adapters (r=128, α=256, applied to all attention and MLP linear layers) alongside continuous geometric diagnostics. Broad-capability retention is scored on a disjoint fixed chat set that never contributes gradients, and all comparisons are made at matched task-loss anchors via linear interpolation along logged trajectories, decoupling geometric effects from optimization speed.

## Method

The paper introduces three objective-agnostic geometric probes—update drift (‖ΔW_t‖_F), stable update rank (‖ΔW_t‖_F²/‖ΔW_t‖_2²), and step-to-step directional coherence (cosine similarity of consecutive updates)—to make the Drift Paradox measurable and classify trajectories into four dynamical archetypes. Mechanistically, anisotropic Hessian curvature at initialization is shown to channel early updates into a low-dimensional stiff subspace (the low-rank attractor), producing trajectory lock-in where task improvement is rigidly coupled to broad-capability loss. To escape lock-in, the paper proposes an SVD-based spectral regularizer that adds the negative entropy of the normalized singular-value distribution of LoRA adapter matrices to the loss, forcing update energy to spread across softer modes rather than concentrating in the dominant stiff direction; it is applied stochastically to 4 randomly sampled layers per step, adding only ~6.6% per-step overhead. The key distinction drawn is between stability by suppression (e.g., gradient noise, which slows learning) and stability by steering (structured spectral reorientation that preserves task plasticity while reducing harmful alignment), validated via matched-loss evaluation across scales and modalities.

## Results

Under matched-loss evaluation on LLaMA-1B/3B/8B with LoRA (r=128), the SVD-based spectral regularizer reduces broad-capability loss from 5.10 (Baseline) to 3.51 while maintaining near-baseline task loss (0.31 vs. 0.28), whereas Gradient Noise preserves broad loss (2.99) only by severely degrading task learning (0.97). The online Abs-Harm channeling indicator drops from 0.017 (Baseline) to 0.012 (SVD), with cumulative AUC reduced from 20.19 to 12.34 across seeds. The SVD regularizer adds only +6.6% per-step latency with no additional peak VRAM (4250 MB), compared to +16.2% for Null-Space Projection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four update regimes: Baseline LoRA, Gradient Noise, Null-Space Projection, and the SVD-based spectral regularizer. The SVD regularizer is the only method that simultaneously maintains near-baseline task loss (0.31 vs. 0.28) and substantially reduces broad-capability loss (3.51 vs. 5.10), achieving what the authors term 'stability by steering.' Gradient Noise achieves the lowest broad loss (2.99) but does so by suppressing task learning to 0.97 (a 3.5× degradation in task loss), representing 'stability by suppression.' Null-Space Projection slows progression along the degradation trend but does not produce a sustained trajectory reorientation. The SVD regularizer also reduces the online Abs-Harm channeling signal (0.012 vs. 0.017 for Baseline) and maintains a higher effective update rank (stable rank ~83 vs. ~7 for Baseline under AdamW), confirming that the gains arise from directional reorientation rather than mere magnitude reduction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the LoRA fine-tuning framework (Hu et al., 2022) and the broader catastrophic-forgetting literature (EWC, synaptic intelligence), but departs sharply from the dominant magnitude-based stability paradigm by arguing that parameter drift is an unreliable proxy for broad-capability degradation. It draws on Hessian spectral structure analyses (Sagun et al., 2017; Ghorbani et al., 2019) and optimization-geometry work (Li et al., 2018; Garipov et al., 2018), but shifts the focus from final minima or mode connectivity to trajectory-level dynamics during adaptation. The SVD-based spectral regularizer is positioned as a controlled diagnostic probe rather than a competitive method, distinguishing it from directional PEFT variants like OPLoRA and MiLoRA that target specific architectural modifications.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Drift Paradox challenges a widely-used heuristic in the LLM fine-tuning community—that smaller parameter displacement implies safer adaptation—and the geometric probes (stable rank, directional coherence, Abs-Harm) offer practical, low-overhead diagnostics for monitoring fine-tuning health in real time. The distinction between 'stability by suppression' (noise, null-space projection) and 'stability by steering' (spectral reorientation) provides a useful conceptual framework for practitioners choosing interventions. However, the scope is bounded: results are limited to LLaMA-family models under LoRA with narrow-task data, the SVD regularizer is explicitly not an optimized production method, and the spectral-bias mechanism is presented as a hypothesis with testable signatures rather than a proven causal account. Open questions include whether the findings generalize to other PEFT families, full-parameter fine-tuning at larger data scales, MoE architectures, and whether data homogeneity rather than curvature is the true driver of the observed low-dimensional confinement.
