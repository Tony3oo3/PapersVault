## Problem

Prior work established that even benign fine-tuning can erode LLM safety alignment, but the multilingual dimension of this risk remained largely unexplored. It was unclear whether fine-tuning in a non-English language produces different safety outcomes than English fine-tuning, and whether safety evaluated in English adequately captures drift that manifests only in the fine-tuning language. This paper addresses that blind spot by systematically varying both the fine-tuning language and the evaluation language across multiple model architectures.

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes six base instruction-tuned models (Llama-3.2, Qwen3, Gemma-3 at 0.6B–4B) and a 1,000-sample benign prompt-response dataset (synthetically generated from Dolly via Gemini 2.5 Flash) translated into nine languages, producing LoRA adapters after one or three epochs of training. Evaluation uses the 440-prompt SORRY-Bench suite in both English and the fine-tuning language; for local-language evaluations, model outputs are back-translated to English via NLLB-200 (3.3B) before scoring by the SORRY-Bench Mistral-7B autorater, introducing a translation-in-the-loop step absent from standard English-only protocols. The SORRY-Bench prompts themselves were translated via the Google Translate API (rather than an LLM, which refused harmful content) and then manually validated by native speakers.

## Method

The core experimental design fine-tunes 357 model variants (6 base models × 9 languages × 3 seeds × 2 epoch settings) using LoRA (α/r=8, AdamW, lr=5×10⁻⁵) via Unsloth, then evaluates each on SORRY-Bench in both English and the fine-tuning language to decouple the effects of tuning language from evaluation language. For mechanistic analysis, the paper computes a contrastive safety direction at each transformer layer as the difference between mean hidden states of 44 adversarial and 44 non-adversarial prompts (at the final token), then measures the L₂ drift of this direction in a PCA-projected 2D space at the 80th-percentile layer. The key finding is that non-English fine-tuning often produces smaller representational drift than English yet induces more extreme safety shifts, and that architectures resolve this post-tuning uncertainty differently: Llama models default toward exaggerated compliance while Gemma-3-4B and Qwen3-4B default toward exaggerated refusal. Safety drift is shown to be decoupled from capability (TinyMMLU, perplexity, non-adversarial compliance), and the effect persists under a more conservative α/r=2 configuration, ruling out hyperparameter artefacts.

## Results

Benign multilingual LoRA fine-tuning (1 epoch, α/r=8) induces heterogeneous safety drift across 357 model variants: adversarial compliance rates on SORRY-Bench can increase up to four-fold (e.g., Gemma-3-4B-IT fine-tuned in Portuguese, evaluated in Portuguese: ~17.7% → ~72.0% compliance). In two-thirds of model-language combinations, evaluating in the fine-tuning language yields a larger compliance increase than the standard English-only evaluation, indicating non-English fine-tuning acts as a safety amplifier. Safety drift is largely decoupled from capability: TinyMMLU scores remain stable or shift in the opposite direction to compliance changes across most models. Mechanistically, small safety-direction displacements (measured at the 80th-percentile layer) correlate with increased compliance for Llama models but increased refusal for Gemma-3-4B-IT and Qwen3-4B, revealing architecture-dependent default behaviours.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study rather than a method comparison, so the 'baselines' are the base instruction-tuned models (pre-fine-tuning) and the cross-lingual/cross-architecture contrasts. Compared to English fine-tuning, non-English fine-tuning consistently produces larger safety shifts: Gemma-3-1B-IT jumps from 44.1% (EN, α/r=2) to 76.8% (HI, α/r=8) EN-eval compliance, while Qwen3-0.6B shows the inverse pattern, dropping from 66.8% (EN) to 36.5% (HI) EN-eval compliance, suggesting model collapse in the smallest variant. Across architectures, Llama-3.2 models uniformly increase in EN-eval compliance after any language fine-tuning (e.g., 35.0%→38.4% EN, 34.5%→49.5% PT), whereas Gemma-3-4B-IT generally decreases in EN-eval compliance but spikes in local-eval compliance (4× in Portuguese). Smaller models (0.6B–1B) exhibit far more dramatic drift than their 3B–4B counterparts, and the drift persists under a more conservative LoRA setting (α/r=2), confirming it is not an artefact of aggressive hyperparameters. The mechanistic analysis further shows that English fine-tuning produces the largest internal representational displacement yet preserves alignment, while non-English fine-tuning causes smaller drift that nonetheless pushes models into higher-uncertainty regions where architecture determines whether the default is compliance or refusal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the well-documented finding that benign fine-tuning compromises LLM safety (Qi et al., 2023; Lermen et al., 2024) into multilingual settings, where prior work has been largely absent. It builds on SORRY-Bench (Xie et al., 2024) by translating the adversarial evaluation suite into eight additional languages, and on representation engineering (Zou et al., 2023) to perform layer-wise mechanistic analysis of safety-direction drift. The paper also situates itself within the growing literature on safety gradient fragility (Zhang et al., 2026; Bach et al., 2025) and architecture-specific refusal patterns (Hildebrandt et al., 2025), but is the first to systematically study how the *language* of fine-tuning interacts with the *language* of evaluation to produce heterogeneous safety outcomes across model families.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is direct: English-only safety evaluation provides inadequate assurance for multilingual deployment, a gap that becomes critical as small models (0.6B–4B) are increasingly fine-tuned and deployed on-device. The finding that safety drift is decoupled from capability means standard capability benchmarks (TinyMMLU, perplexity) cannot serve as safety proxies, and the architectural divergence—where small representational drifts push Llama toward compliance but Gemma/Qwen toward refusal—suggests that safety robustness is an architectural property, not merely a data property. Key limitations include the restriction to small models (larger-model ablations are limited to 8B–14B with only two languages), translation quality issues for low-resource languages (Irish, Tagalog), and the use of a single 1,000-example fine-tuning dataset, leaving open whether the observed patterns persist with larger, more diverse training corpora or at scale.
