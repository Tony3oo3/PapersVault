## Problem

Softmax attention's nonlinear structure makes theoretical analysis of its training dynamics and statistical behavior extremely difficult, while linear attention—used as a proxy in most theoretical work—is far more tractable but empirically weaker. It remains unclear whether softmax attention should systematically outperform or even match linear attention in theory. This paper bridges the gap by showing that in the large-prompt regime, softmax attention inherits the analytical structure of its linear counterpart.

## Input / Output

The method takes a prompt of L i.i.d. d-dimensional tokens, represented as the empirical measure μ̂_L = (1/L)Σδ_{z_ℓ}, and a separate query token z ∈ R^d, producing the attention output T^{K,Q,V}[μ̂_L](z). Beyond the pointwise output, the framework yields non-asymptotic L₂ concentration bounds quantifying the gap between finite-prompt softmax attention and its infinite-prompt linear limit, as well as risk guarantees for the trained parameters. The prompt is consumed as a probability measure rather than an ordered sequence, and the query token is drawn independently from the prompt distribution, which is a deliberate modeling choice that simplifies the analysis while remaining faithful to the practical setting (as noted in Remark 3.2).

## Method

The core insight is that for i.i.d. Gaussian input tokens, softmax attention over an infinite prompt reduces to an affine (hence linear) transformation of the query token with respect to the input-token measure (Lemma 2.1). Building on this, the paper establishes non-asymptotic concentration bounds (Propositions 3.1, 3.4) for both the attention output and its gradients with respect to the parameters U = K^T Q and V, using a case-based analysis that handles heavy-tailed softmax weights via convex-hull bounds and sub-Gaussian maximal inequalities. Theorem 4.3 then shows that this concentration is stable along the entire gradient-flow training trajectory in general in-context learning settings, via Grönwall's inequality, implying that finite-prompt softmax training converges to at least the risk achieved by infinite-prompt training. As a concrete application (Theorem 5.2), the framework transfers the Bayes-optimality results of Zhang et al. (2024) for linear attention directly to softmax attention in in-context linear regression, provided the prompt length is sufficiently large, and extends these guarantees to anisotropic Gaussian covariates where prior softmax analyses were restricted to the isotropic case.

## Results

The paper establishes non-asymptotic concentration bounds showing that the L2 distance between finite-prompt softmax attention and its infinite-prompt (linear) counterpart decays as O(σ⁶ ln(L) / L^{c₂/σ²}) for the output and O(σ¹² ln(L)² / L^{c₂/σ²}) for the gradient (Propositions 3.1, 3.4). The main theoretical guarantee (Theorem 5.2) proves that for in-context linear regression with Gaussian inputs, softmax attention trained via gradient flow achieves the Bayes-optimal risk (zero without label noise) in the large-prompt limit, extending the linear-attention results of Zhang et al. (2024) to the softmax setting. Numerical experiments (d=5, Σ=I_d, label noise variance 0.1) confirm that both softmax and linear attention risks converge to the label-noise floor as L grows, with the performance gap between the two narrowing monotonically.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares softmax attention against (normalized) linear attention and the L-Bayes-optimal estimator (well-tuned ridge regression) on in-context linear regression. At finite prompt lengths, linear attention consistently achieves lower ICL risk than softmax attention, but the gap narrows as L increases and both converge to the label-noise level, matching the Bayes-optimal predictor. Theoretically, the paper's Theorem 5.2 provides the first convergence guarantee for softmax attention in in-context linear regression with anisotropic Gaussian covariates (Σ invertible but not necessarily diagonal), whereas the prior softmax-attention result of Chen et al. (2024) is restricted to the isotropic case Σ=I_d. The linear-attention analysis of Zhang et al. (2024) is the direct theoretical baseline that the paper transfers to softmax via the infinite-prompt equivalence (Lemma 2.1). In the anisotropic experimental setting (Kac-Murdock-Szegö covariance with ρ=0.5), convergence is slower and training dynamics are more erratic, but the qualitative picture—both architectures approaching the Bayes optimum as L→∞—is preserved.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the measure-based formulation of attention layers introduced by Vuckovic et al. (2020) and Sander et al. (2022), and specifically on Castin et al. (2025)'s result that softmax attention reduces to a linear operator on the input-token measure in the infinite-prompt Gaussian limit. It departs from the large body of work using linear attention as a purely heuristic proxy (Ahn et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024) by providing non-asymptotic concentration bounds that quantify *when* and *how fast* softmax attention actually behaves like linear attention, and by showing this equivalence persists along gradient-flow training trajectories. The in-context linear regression analysis directly extends Zhang et al. (2024)'s linear-attention dynamics to softmax attention, while the concurrent work of Bohbot et al. (2025) studies related concentration but only for output moments rather than the full L2 output and gradient quantities needed for optimization transfer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, broadly applicable toolkit that justifies analyzing the simpler linear-attention surrogate and then transferring conclusions to softmax attention in the large-prompt regime, which is the regime most relevant to modern LLMs. It establishes the first convergence guarantees for in-context linear regression with softmax attention under anisotropic Gaussian covariates, a setting where prior results (Chen et al., 2024) required isotropic covariates. Key limitations include the reliance on centered Gaussian inputs for the core linearity result, the absence of explicit convergence rates in L, and the restriction to single-layer attention. Open questions include extending the framework to multi-layer transformers, non-Gaussian token distributions, and more complex in-context tasks such as n-gram learning.
