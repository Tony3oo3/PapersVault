## Problem

Existing language identification systems achieve near-perfect accuracy on high-resource languages but remain brittle on low-resource languages, closely related language pairs, and fine-grained dialectal distinctions. The paper identifies that treating tokenization as a fixed, language-agnostic preprocessing step discards discriminative signal that is available when segmentation is allowed to vary per language.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263250/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a raw character string as input and produces a posterior distribution over a closed set of language labels, from which the argmax gives the predicted language. A key structural detail is that the same input string can yield different optimal subword segmentations under different language models, so the 'feature representation' is itself language-conditional rather than a single fixed tokenization. The shared vocabulary across languages means no per-language tokenization step is needed at deployment—only per-language unigram probability vectors are stored.

## Method

UniLID adapts the UnigramLM generative tokenization framework to LID by learning a separate unigram distribution φ_ℓ over a shared vocabulary V for each language ℓ, using the EM algorithm (forward-backward for expected token counts, normalized counts for M-step). At inference, it finds the most probable segmentation of the input string under each language's φ_ℓ via Viterbi decoding, approximating the string likelihood p(s|ℓ) as the probability of that best segmentation, then applies Bayes' rule to obtain a posterior over languages. The critical design choice is that segmentation is a language-specific latent variable: the same string may be optimally segmented differently under different languages, encoding morphological and orthographic structure that a fixed tokenizer would obscure. This formulation enables incremental addition of new languages (just run EM on new data) and achieves ~70% accuracy with as few as 5 labeled samples per language, compared to fastText's failure to generalize in the same regime.

## Results

On the full GlotLID-C test set (1,940 language-script labels), UniLID achieves a macro F1 of 0.929 with a macro FPR of 2.03×10⁻⁵, slightly below fastText's F1 of 0.944 but with ~25% lower FPR (2.71×10⁻⁵). On the DSL-ML 2024 dialect identification benchmark, UniLID improves macro F1 from 0.532 (fastText) to 0.723, and in the low-resource regime achieves ~70% accuracy with only 5 training samples per language where fastText drops to ~10%. Training UniLID on the full GlotLID-C corpus takes ~17.8k seconds versus ~163k seconds for 100-epoch fastText, an approximately 9× reduction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against fastText (Joulin et al., 2017), UniLID trades a small F1 gap on high-resource benchmarks (0.929 vs. 0.944 on GlotLID-C) for substantially lower false positive rates (2.03×10⁻⁵ vs. 2.71×10⁻⁵) and dramatic gains in challenging regimes: ~70% vs. ~10% accuracy at 5 samples/language on WiLI, macro F1 of 0.723 vs. 0.532 on DSL-ML 2024 dialects, and 0.414 vs. 0.160 macro F1 on out-of-domain Tatoeba text. Against GlotLID-M (Kargaran et al., 2023), UniLID is competitive on UDHR (F1 0.859 vs. 0.871) and FLORES-200 (0.932 vs. 0.968), with GlotLID-M retaining an edge on these parallel-text benchmarks, though UniLID achieves a lower FPR on UDHR (1.43×10⁻⁴ vs. 1.37×10⁻⁴). The paper notes that fastText's 0.00 F1 on three South Slavic dialects (HR, BS, ME) in DSL-ML 2024 persists across all hyperparameter configurations tested, while UniLID scores 0.609–0.788 on those same labels. Inference latency is comparable: UniLID is 1.65× slower than fastText on the 1,940-label GlotLID-C set but 2.7× faster than CLD3 on the 235-label WiLI set.

## Relation to Prior Work

UniLID repurposes the generative modeling framework of the UnigramLM tokenization algorithm (Kudo, 2018) for language identification, a task previously addressed by character n-gram generative models (Cavnar & Trenkle, 2001; Dunning, 1994) and discriminative bag-of-n-gram classifiers (fastText; Joulin et al., 2017). The key conceptual departure is treating subword segmentation as a language-specific latent variable rather than a fixed preprocessing step: each language gets its own unigram distribution over a shared vocabulary, so the optimal tokenization of a string can differ across candidate languages. This stands in contrast to both prior generative LID (which used fixed n-gram windows) and discriminative systems (which apply a single tokenization before scoring), and is motivated by the observation that morphologically meaningful boundaries vary across languages.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LID is a critical upstream component for multilingual LLM training pipelines (corpus curation, data composition analysis, cross-lingual evaluation), and the method's data efficiency (~70% accuracy with five samples per language) and modular design (incremental addition of new languages without retraining) directly address the long-tail coverage problem that plagues current systems. The unigram assumption, however, ignores inter-token dependencies, and the base vocabulary's script bias (Latin-dominant training data) causes measurable underperformance on non-Latin scripts, leaving open whether script-balanced vocabularies or relaxed n-gram formulations can close these gaps. Storage and inference cost also scale linearly with the number of languages, which may constrain deployment at the ~2,000-label scale of GlotLID-C under strict latency budgets.
