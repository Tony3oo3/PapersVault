## Problem

While prior work has characterized how transformers perform entity binding (static state lookup) and how toy models track states in simplified synthetic languages, the mechanisms by which non-toy pretrained LMs handle entity tracking with multiple state-changing operations (PUT, REMOVE, MOVE) in natural language remain unexplored. This paper asks how models move beyond static binding to update and consolidate world states when objects are added, removed, or moved across boxes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264399/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The behavioral task takes a natural language prompt consisting of a DESCRIPTION sentence (initial box contents), a sequence of state-changing OPERATIONS (PUT, REMOVE, MOVE), and a QUERY phrase ('Box X contains the __'), from which the model must generate the correct object token(s). For mechanistic analysis, the method consumes model hidden states (residual streams) at specific token positions (last token, object tokens, box ID tokens) across all layers, producing identified attention-head circuits, linear probe accuracies, subspace overlap coefficients, intervention success rates, and predicted behavioral failure modes (degeneration rates on novel test scenarios).

## Method

The paper combines linear probing, causal circuit tracing, and intervention to characterize ET mechanisms. First, global/local/mention probes on the last token's residual stream show models do not encode global world states incrementally across tokens (supporting H2) nor process state changes sequentially across layers (supporting H4), but instead aggregate relevant information in parallel when the query becomes evident. Second, path patching identifies four groups of attention heads (A/B/C/D) for PUT, showing it shares the 'look-back' architecture of DESCRIPTION but with different head implementations; subspace patching with desiderata-based counterfactual masking confirms both circuits use functionally equivalent subspaces to transmit positional (order ID) information. Third, for REMOVE, logit/rank analysis reveals a global suppression mechanism, ternary probes localize a 'remove tag' at both object and box ID tokens, and null-space projection interventions demonstrate that only the object-level tag is causally efficacious—implying removal is global rather than box-specific. This mechanistic finding predicts three novel failure modes (no-op remove, shared-label objects, re-introducing removed objects) that are confirmed behaviorally and partially fixed by nullifying the remove tag.

## Results

Probing experiments on CodeLlama-13B show that local state probes (predicting the queried box's contents) reach ~0.9 non-trivial accuracy, while global state probes (predicting all boxes' contents) remain below 0.3, confirming that LMs aggregate information in parallel at the last token rather than incrementally building world states. Prior-state probes across layers similarly show no sequential processing, with earlier prior states not peaking in earlier layers. The paper's key behavioral prediction is confirmed: the global REMOVE mechanism causes high degeneration rates (DR) of 0.84 (No-op Remove), 0.56 (Shared-label Objects), and 0.62 (Re-introducing Removed Object) on CodeLlama-13B, with single-layer null-space intervention recovering 66%, 73%, and 24% of these failures respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a mechanistic interpretability study rather than a method comparison, so there are no traditional baselines. The key internal comparisons are: (1) local vs. global vs. mention probes, where local probes (~0.9) vastly outperform global probes (<0.3), ruling out incremental world-state tracking; (2) CodeLlama-13B vs. Llama-3.1-70B on behavioral accuracy, where the 70B model achieves 28.8–91.5% accuracy across 1–7 operations (0-shot completion to 2-shot) versus 5.1–37.6% for the 13B model, and correspondingly shows lower degeneration rates (e.g., DR drops from 0.84 to 0.49 on No-op Remove); (3) 0-shot completion vs. 0-shot instruct vs. 2-shot prompting, where instruct and 2-shot settings substantially improve CodeLlama-13B (e.g., 5.1% → 31.6% → 16.1% at 1 operation) but the mechanistic findings (global removal, non-incremental tracking) hold across all settings. The paper also contrasts its findings with Li et al. (2025), who showed toy models aggregate state permutations sequentially through layers, attributing the difference to explicit entity mentions in natural language disincentivizing incremental representation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability line of work on entity binding (Prakash et al., 2024; 2025) — which characterized how LMs use order IDs and look-back circuits to retrieve static states — to the substantially harder setting of state-changing operations (PUT, REMOVE, MOVE) in naturalistic language. It builds on the box dataset benchmark of Kim & Schuster (2023) and the path-patching/subspace-patching toolkit from prior circuit-discovery work, but departs from toy-model ET studies (Merrill et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025) by analyzing pretrained non-toy transformers rather than trained-from-scratch architectures. The key conceptual departure is showing that the binding circuit generalizes to PUT but that REMOVE relies on an entirely different, degenerate mechanism (a global object-level suppression tag) rather than a box-specific unbinding operation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LMs solve a fundamentally sequential task (tracking world states across operations) via a non-sequential strategy (parallel aggregation at the final query token) challenges the assumption that larger or better-trained models will naturally develop incremental world-state representations. The discovery of the fragile global removal mechanism is practically important because it predicts concrete failure modes (no-op removes, shared-label objects, re-introduction) absent from existing benchmarks, and the null-space intervention provides a proof-of-concept fix. Open questions include whether the preference for the degenerate object-level tag over the more robust box-ID-level signal can be corrected through training, whether the full REMOVE circuit involves additional box-specific components that are too weak to overcome the global mechanism, and how these findings scale to larger models or more naturalistic discourse.
