## Problem

Existing economic models of AI productivity, including the Gries-Naudé partial equilibrium framework, treat organizational, behavioral, and contextual factors as exogenous technological parameters, leaving unexplained why AI adoption frequently fails to deliver expected productivity gains (the 'productivity paradox'). This position paper argues that five human and environmental factors—human resource composition, individual baseline capability, practitioner learning curves, fair-use incentives, and objective flexibility—critically moderate the AI-to-productivity relationship and must be modeled as endogenous organizational variables.

## Input / Output

The model takes structural parameters of the Gries-Naudé task interval [N−1, N], the technical automation threshold N_IT, individual baseline capability κ_i ∈ [0,1], time-since-adoption τ, organizational flexibility F ∈ [0,1], and the organizational effectiveness modifier Ω ∈ [0,1] as inputs. It produces a revised individual-level production function h̃_i(z,τ), an organizational aggregate H̃(τ), a capability- and time-varying productivity ratio, and an endogenous effective automation threshold Ñ_IT that can fall below the technical N_IT. Unlike computational methods, the 'inputs' are economic-structural parameters and the 'outputs' are closed-form modifications to a CES production function, with τ treated as a comparative-statics parameter rather than a trained variable.

## Method

The paper extends the Gries-Naudé partial equilibrium model by introducing four structural modifications that render previously exogenous parameters endogenous to organizational choices. First, an organizational effectiveness modifier Ω = ω_C · ω_I scales the effective availability of AI experts (b̃_IT = Ω · b_IT), capturing how hierarchical composition and incentive alignment gate the translation of nominal AI expertise into realized deployment. Second, a non-monotonic capability-task interaction φ(z, κ_i) is introduced: for tasks within AI's reliable boundary [N−1, N_R], φ decreases with κ_i (novices benefit more), while for tasks in (N_R, N_IT], φ increases with κ_i (experts detect AI errors), producing heterogeneous productivity ratios across workers. Third, an exponential learning curve λ_i(τ) = 1 − e^{−ρ_i τ} makes AI productivity time-varying, with individual learning rates ρ_i potentially depending on baseline capability. Fourth, the automation threshold becomes endogenous via Ñ_IT = (1−F)(N−1) + F·N_IT, where organizational flexibility F determines how much of the technically automatable task set is actually exploited. These modifications are combined into a revised production function (Eqn. 9) and aggregated organizational output (Eqn. 10), preserving the original CES structure while making every key determinant conditional on the five identified human and environmental factors.

## Results

This is a position paper with no empirical experiments or quantitative benchmarks. The core contribution is a revised partial-equilibrium production function (Eqn. 9–10) that introduces five moderating factors—organizational effectiveness modifier Ω ∈ [0,1], individual baseline capability κᵢ, temporal learning dynamics λᵢ(τ), endogenous automation threshold Ñ_IT, and capability-task interaction φ(z,κᵢ)—into the Gries-Naudé (2022) framework. The paper grounds its argument in cited empirical findings: Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) report up to 36% productivity gains for the lowest-skilled workers but minimal impact on experienced staff, while Dell'Acqua et al. (2023) show workers using AI are 19 percentage points less likely to be accurate on tasks outside AI's capability frontier. The key structural result is that the effective productivity ratio (Eqn. 11) becomes individual- and time-varying, meaning two organizations with identical AI investments can exhibit different realized gains depending on organizational and human factors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not benchmark against competing methods; instead, it extends the Gries-Naudé (2022) partial equilibrium model by endogenizing factors that the original treats as exogenous parameters. The paper explicitly addresses three alternative views: (1) technological determinism, which it counters by showing that even as AI reliability rises (shifting N_R upward), the binding constraints redistribute to organizational flexibility F and incentive alignment ω_I rather than dissolving; (2) the measurement problem view, which it acknowledges but argues is orthogonal since the five factors affect realized benefits regardless of metric; and (3) the wage/cost view, which it incorporates by showing wage dynamics are embedded in the productivity parameters modified by the five factors. The paper's framework is positioned as complementary to, not a replacement for, the Gries-Naudé structure: the original distributional predictions remain qualitatively valid, but the determinants are revealed to be shaped by organizational choices rather than purely technological or market-given.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the partial equilibrium model of Gries & Naudé (2022), which formalizes AI's productivity contribution through a task-based production function, by introducing five human and environmental moderators—organizational composition, individual baseline capability, learning dynamics, fair-use incentives, and objective flexibility—that the original model treats as exogenous parameters. It draws on the broader task-based labor economics lineage (Autor et al., 2003; Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2018) and incorporates empirical evidence from field experiments (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025; Dell'Acqua et al., 2023) to motivate why realized productivity diverges from nominal AI capability. The key conceptual departure is rendering the Gries-Naudé determinants (automation threshold, task productivity ratio, expert availability, relative factor abundance) endogenous to organizational choices rather than technological givens.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a structured economic framework for understanding the 'productivity paradox'—why rapid AI capability advances have not translated into proportional organizational productivity gains—and offers practitioners a diagnostic vocabulary (Ω, κ, F, λ) for identifying which moderating factor is binding in their context. However, as a position paper, it offers no empirical calibration of the proposed functional forms (g, φ, λ), leaving the revised model largely qualitative; the five factors are proposed but not tested, and their interactions remain unspecified. Open questions include whether the organizational effectiveness modifier Ω can be measured and estimated from firm-level data, how the endogenous task boundary Ñ_IT evolves as AI capabilities shift, and whether the framework generalizes across domains with very different task structures (e.g., manufacturing vs. knowledge work vs. education).
