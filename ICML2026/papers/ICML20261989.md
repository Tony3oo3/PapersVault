## Problem

In-Context Reinforcement Learning (ICRL) has demonstrated strong few-shot adaptation in single-agent domains, but its capability to perform strategic inference and coordinate with unseen partners in Ad-Hoc Teamwork (AHT) remains unverified. Existing AHT benchmarks lack the scale, controlled teammate diversity, and standardized learning-history datasets needed to rigorously test in-context generalization, making it impossible to disentangle genuine coordination from partner-specific overfitting.

## Input / Output

The ego agent consumes a context window of up to 10,000 transitions containing 5×5 partial observations (40–46 channels depending on layout), its own actions, optionally teammate actions, and sparse rewards, and outputs a discrete 6-way action (4 directions + stay + interact). For AD, the context is structured as multiple episodes ordered by increasing return to elicit in-context meta-exploration; for DPT, the context is an unordered set of expert trajectories used for in-context task identification. Evaluation is conducted online over 100 episodes per teammate instance, reporting per-episode returns and an adaptation-gain metric (last 20 vs. first 20 episodes).

## Method

ICRL4AHT is a JAX-based benchmark pipeline built on OvercookedV2 that generates a diverse teammate suite of 80 policies (RL-trained via FCP, BRDiv, LBRDiv, CoMeDi for training; four heuristic families H1–H4 for testing), collects ~150M filtered transitions of learning histories, and provides a standardized two-track evaluation protocol (Teammate Generalization and Layout Generalization). The key design enforces a strict train-test distribution shift: RL policies are used exclusively for training and heuristic policies exclusively for testing, confirmed by high pairwise Hamming distances between the two groups. The paper evaluates AD (autoregressive action prediction on return-ordered learning histories) and DPT (expert-action prediction on unordered contexts) as representative ICRL baselines, along with ablations on context length, teammate-action conditioning, model scale, a CNN+GRU recurrent variant, and an offline meta-RL baseline (AMAGO-Offline). Results show these methods frequently underperform random baselines with flat adaptation curves, revealing a fundamental limitation of current sequence-model ICRL in multi-agent strategic inference under partial observability.

## Results

On Track 1 (Teammate Generalization), DPT achieves the highest average return of 12.4±11.0, marginally exceeding Random (5.5±5.6) and AD (9.1±5.6), but this is heavily skewed by the H4 family (autonomously capable, 40.0 return). On the challenging test wide layout, both ICRL methods catastrophically underperform: AD scores −7.9±4.8 and DPT scores −15.9±4.6 versus Random at 0.0±0.0. On Track 2 (Layout Generalization), Random (11.7±11.0) outperforms AD (4.4±5.8) and is competitive with DPT (13.2±11.2) on asymm adv right, while on cramped down all methods are near-zero. Critically, adaptation curves are flat across all settings, confirming the absence of in-context improvement over the interaction horizon.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Algorithm Distillation (AD; Laskin et al., 2023) fails to consistently outperform the random baseline: on test wide it scores −7.9±4.8 (vs. 0.0 for random), and on H1 it actively interferes with the partner (−18.0±2.0 on test wide). Decision-Pretrained Transformer (DPT; Lee et al., 2023) achieves a slightly higher Track 1 average (12.4 vs. 9.1 for AD) but this is driven entirely by the H4 family where minimal coordination is needed; on all other families DPT matches or underperforms AD, and on test wide it is worse (−15.9 vs. −7.9). AMAGO-Offline (Grigsby et al., 2024), an offline meta-RL variant, performs comparably to AD/DPT on the Teammate track (8.3±10.2) and remains weak on the Layout track (3.4±4.8 vs. Random 8.0±8.7), confirming that the failure is not specific to the action-prediction paradigm. Across all three baselines, no method exhibits meaningful in-context adaptation: episode-wise returns remain flat, and the 'adaptation gain' (last 20 minus first 20 episodes) is near zero, indicating that none of the studied sequence-model architectures can perform the implicit Bayesian partner inference required for AHT under realistic distribution shifts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of two established lines of work: in-context reinforcement learning (ICRL) via sequence models (AD, DPT, AMAGO) and ad-hoc teamwork (AHT) evaluation. It builds directly on OvercookedV2 (Gessler et al., 2025) as the environment substrate and JaxMARL (Rutherford et al., 2024) as the implementation base, but departs conceptually from prior AHT benchmarks that rely on population-based training to induce partner diversity at train time. The key departure is reframing AHT as a test-time in-context adaptation problem: rather than training against diverse partners and hoping for zero-shot generalization, the paper asks whether a sequence model can infer an unseen partner's policy purely from interaction history during evaluation, a question that prior AHT literature (PBT, ZSC, Other-Play) does not address.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that representative ICRL baselines (AD, DPT, AMAGO-Offline) consistently underperform random baselines in multi-agent coordination, with flat adaptation curves across all ablations—challenges the assumption that single-agent in-context learning capabilities transfer to strategic partner inference. This establishes a concrete, well-characterized failure mode that future architectures (explicit partner inference modules, information-seeking objectives) must overcome. The benchmark itself (ICRL4AHT) fills a clear infrastructure gap: no prior AHT benchmark provides learning-history datasets, controlled train-test distribution shifts, and standardized online evaluation protocols for ICRL. Limitations include restriction to a single domain (OvercookedV2), two-player settings, and fixed (non-co-adapting) partners, leaving open whether the failure generalizes to larger teams, richer domains, or non-stationary partners.
