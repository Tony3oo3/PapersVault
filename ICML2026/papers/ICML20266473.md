## Problem

Recent large-scale evaluations showed SAEs underperform simple baselines on concept detection and model steering, prompting some groups to deprioritize SAE research, yet other work reports strong positive results for hypothesis generation and LLM biology. This paper identifies the missing conceptual distinction: negative results involve acting on known, prespecified concepts, while positive results involve discovering unknown concepts, and SAEs' comparative advantage lies specifically in the latter regime.

## Input / Output

The SAE is trained as a reconstruction model on dense LM token representations (or text embeddings) of dimension D, producing an overcomplete sparse latent z of dimension M >> D where only K << M dimensions are active via a TopK gate or L1 penalty. Because the SAE must reconstruct x from z, the sparse representation encodes strictly less information than the original LM embedding. The sparse features are then mapped to natural language concept descriptions via autoininterpretation—prompting an LM with high-activation versus low-activation texts to identify the shared concept—yielding interpretable hypotheses rather than raw activations.

## Method

The core contribution is a conceptual framework that partitions SAE applications into two regimes: acting on known concepts (probing, steering, unlearning) versus discovering unknown concepts (hypothesis generation, LLM biology). The paper argues SAEs excel in the discovery regime because their overcomplete, monosemantic architecture (M >> D with TopK sparsity) enables unsupervised enumeration of a large set of atomic concepts, after which researchers can screen for features satisfying an arbitrary criterion (e.g., predicting engagement, being active during addition). The practical pipeline is: train SAE on LM representations, select features meeting the criterion, and autoininterpret them into natural language hypotheses. A key structural advantage is falsifiability: once a concept is identified, it can be validated by direct data annotation independent of the SAE activation, so feature unreliability is caught downstream—unlike concept detection, where SAE information loss directly degrades performance.

## Results

This is a position paper with no original quantitative experiments; its contribution is a conceptual framework reconciling conflicting SAE findings. The paper's central claim is qualitative: SAEs underperform simple baselines (logistic regression, LLM prompting) on known-concept tasks such as concept detection and model steering, yet outperform alternatives (topic modeling, n-grams, direct embedding selection) on unknown-concept discovery tasks such as hypothesis generation and LLM biology. The authors argue these two regimes are fundamentally different—concepts are inputs in the former and outputs in the latter—explaining why negative results in one do not invalidate the other.

## Baselines & Comparisons

For known-concept tasks, SAEs consistently lose to simpler methods: in concept detection, classifiers on SAE latents do not beat logistic regression on raw LM representations or LLM prompting across 113 tasks (Kantamneni et al., 2025) and 500 concepts (Wu et al., 2025); in model steering, SAE-based activation editing is outperformed by direct prompting and finetuning (Wu et al., 2025). For unknown-concept discovery, SAEs win: in hypothesis generation (Movva et al., 2025b), SAE-derived hypotheses yield more statistically significant findings and are rated more helpful by human annotators than baselines using direct embedding features, LLM prompting, topic modeling, or n-grams. In LLM biology (Lindsey et al., 2025), SAE features reveal specific mechanistic routes (e.g., a 'rabbit' neuron active during rhyme planning, or digit-level addition neurons) that are not accessible through polysemantic neuron inspection or other probing methods. The paper notes that these comparisons are not directly apples-to-apples because the tasks themselves differ in whether concepts are prespecified or discovered.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not introduce a new method but instead carves a conceptual distinction within the existing SAE interpretability literature. It synthesizes negative results from large-scale SAE evaluations (Kantamneni et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025) with positive results from hypothesis generation (Movva et al., 2025b) and LLM biology (Lindsey et al., 2025), arguing that the former involve acting on prespecified concepts while the latter involve discovering unknown ones. The paper also contrasts SAEs with concept bottleneck models (Koh et al., 2020), noting that SAEs learn concepts during training rather than requiring them as inputs, and connects to the broader 'text as data' tradition in the social sciences (Grimmer, 2010; Gentzkow et al., 2019).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The known/unknown conceptual distinction provides a principled framework for when to expect SAEs to succeed or fail, which could redirect research effort away from unproductive comparisons against baselines on known-concept tasks and toward the discovery regime where SAEs have a genuine comparative advantage. The paper's extension of SAE applications to ML fairness, auditing, and social/health sciences (e.g., discovering spurious features in predictive models, bridging the prediction-explanation gap) broadens the audience well beyond mechanistic interpretability. Open questions include whether the dichotomy fully explains all positive and negative results, whether SAEs will eventually become competitive even in the known-concepts regime as architectures improve, and whether the autoininterpretation pipeline is reliable enough for high-stakes applications.
