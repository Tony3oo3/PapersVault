## Problem

State-of-the-art VLA models are almost exclusively evaluated on desktop-grade GPUs (e.g., RTX 4090), obscuring whether cheaper edge accelerators (NPUs, embedded GPUs, XPUs) can meet real-time control-rate constraints under practical cost and energy budgets. Additionally, the computational bottlenecks that actually dominate end-to-end VLA inference on constrained hardware remain uncharacterized, making it impossible for practitioners to select a 'right-sized' deployment platform or target the right optimization.

## Input / Output

The VLA pipelines consume multi-view RGB camera observations from the robot's onboard cameras together with a natural-language instruction, and produce a continuous action chunk (e.g., 7-DoF joint commands for OpenVLA, or flow-matching-based motor trajectories for π₀/π₀.5) that is executed by the robot arm in a closed-loop observe-infer-act cycle. The critical constraint is that inference must complete within one control period (typically 33–1000 ms depending on task frequency), and the action chunk is a sequence of continuous vectors rather than discrete tokens, with diffusion-based policies requiring 10–100 iterative denoising steps to produce a single chunk.

## Method

The paper contributes a systematic model-hardware co-characterization framework and two training-free acceleration methods. First, it builds a cross-accelerator leaderboard scoring (model, XPU) pairs on CET (Cost, Energy, Time), revealing that 'right-sized' edge devices (e.g., Ascend 310P, Jetson Thor) can outperform flagship GPUs on cost- or energy-normalized metrics while still meeting control-rate gates. Second, Roofline analysis uncovers a consistent two-phase pattern: a compute-bound VLM backbone (operational intensity ~840 FLOPs/Byte, above the ridge point) followed by a memory-bound Action Expert (~64.5 FLOPs/Byte, far below the ridge point), causing severe SM underutilization (20–40%) during the latency-dominant diffusion phase. Guided by this, DP-Cache exploits temporal redundancy in the diffusion trajectory by caching and broadcasting intermediate results during a stable segment (steps 20–80), reducing effective denoising iterations by a factor S (e.g., S=4 gives 1.9× speedup with <4% success-rate drop). V-AEFusion introduces pipeline parallelism: while the VLM processes the current observation, the Action Expert concurrently performs early (coarse) denoising steps using the stale KV cache from the previous timestep, then switches to the fresh KV cache for late (fine) refinement steps, overlapping the two phases and achieving up to 1.3× additional speedup on GPUs with only marginal task-success degradation.

## Results

On the LIBERO benchmark, DP-Cache achieves a 1.9× speedup (S=4) on Diffusion Policy with only a 3.6% average success-rate drop, and when combined with model compilation, delivers 2.9× end-to-end speedup on RTX 4090 (378→129 ms) and 6.0× on Ascend 310P (1853→310 ms). V-AEFusion adds a further 1.3× speedup on RTX 4090 (102→77 ms) with just a 0.3% average SR drop on LIBERO. On the real-world Franka arm, DP-Cache on π₀.₅-droid yields a 1.28× speedup (95→74 ms) with a 6% SR drop over 50 trials.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three existing acceleration baselines on OpenVLA (an autoregressive VLA): VLA-Cache (adaptive token caching) achieves 1.16× speedup with 74.7% avg SR, Spec-VLA (speculative decoding) achieves 1.11× with 75.1% avg SR, and 4-bit QLoRA quantization achieves 1.14× but drops SR to 67.7%. The paper's proposed DP-Cache targets a different model family (diffusion-based Action Experts) and achieves substantially larger speedups (1.9–2.1× standalone, up to 6× combined with compilation) with comparable or smaller SR degradation (≤3.6%). V-AEFusion (pipeline parallelism) is orthogonal to all three baselines and adds 1.3× on top. Notably, the paper shows that combining Spec-VLA with VLA-Cache (1.29×) actually degrades SR more (68.5%) than either alone, and quantization undermines speculative decoding by reducing draft acceptance rates—highlighting that naive composition of existing methods is suboptimal, motivating the paper's phase-aware, structurally-informed approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of VLA model research (π0, OpenVLA, Diffusion Policy, Gr00t, SmolVLA) and systems-level hardware characterization, departing from prior acceleration work that typically targets a single model on a single high-end GPU. It repurposes ideas from video diffusion caching (DP-Cache) and pipeline parallelism (V-AEFusion) into the VLA context, and applies Roofline analysis—a standard HPC diagnostic—to reveal a two-phase compute/memory imbalance that prior VLA papers had not systematically characterized. The CET (Cost, Energy, Time) leaderboard extends the evaluation paradigm beyond latency-only benchmarks to incorporate the cost and energy constraints that actually govern on-robot deployment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first systematic cross-accelerator framework for VLA deployment, giving practitioners a reproducible selection procedure rather than intuition-driven hardware choices. The two-phase characterization (compute-bound VLM backbone, memory-bound Action Expert) is a useful diagnostic that explains why peak FLOPS specs don't translate to achieved control frequency and motivates phase-specific optimizations. Key limitations include a limited hardware set (six platforms, two non-NVIDIA vendors), training-free optimizations that trade accuracy for latency, and a leaderboard that is still growing. Open questions include bandwidth-oriented Action Expert optimization, training-based methods (consistency distillation) for lossless acceleration, and how the two-phase balance shifts as Action Experts scale from memory-bound to compute-bound regimes.
