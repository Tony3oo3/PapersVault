## Problem

Multi-round conversational AI is increasingly used for high-stakes decisions, yet no principled framework exists to ensure such interactions reliably improve human decision quality. Prior work either restricts to single-round interactions, assumes symmetric agents reaching consensus, or requires parametric models of human behavior. This paper asks: how can an AI's uncertainty communication be governed online so that it provably preserves correct human judgments (counterfactual harm) and recovers missed ones (complementarity), without any assumptions on the human or the problem distribution?

## Input / Output

At each round r of day t, the AI receives the full interaction transcript T_{t,r} (problem instance, all prior human/AI prediction sets and textual messages) and the human's current candidate set H_{t,r}; it outputs a refined prediction set C_{t,r} ⊆ Y_t constructed by thresholding a nonconformity score s(T_{t,r}, y) = 1 − p_{t,r}(y) against a composite threshold τ_t·R̄^CH + λ_t·R̄^Comp. The two scalar thresholds (τ_t, λ_t) are fixed within a day and updated only at day's end via a simple additive rule driven by observed error indicators. The human's sets, textual messages, and stopping time N_t are treated as unconstrained black-box inputs; the AI's textual responses come from a fixed LLM and are likewise outside the algorithmic control.

## Method

The core mechanism is an online, distribution-free calibration of two daily thresholds (τ_t for counterfactual harm, λ_t for complementarity) that govern the AI's prediction-set construction. User-defined binary rules R^CH and R^Comp, evaluated on the transcript prefix via an online activation R̄, determine which threshold is active for each label at each round, allowing application-specific definitions of harm and complementarity. The prediction set at round r is C_{t,r} = {y : s(T_{t,r}, y) ≤ τ_t·R̄^CH(y, H_{t,1:r}) + λ_t·R̄^Comp(y, H_{t,1:r})}, so the set adapts as the transcript grows even though thresholds are fixed within the day. Thresholds are updated at day's end by τ_{t+1} = max{0, τ_t + η(E_t^CH − ε)} and analogously for λ, a standard online quantile-tracking rule. Theorem 5.3 proves that for any horizon T, the cumulative CH and Comp error rates are bounded by their targets plus (1+η)/(ηT), with no assumptions on problem distributions, human behavior, or AI internals. This extends the single-round conformal framework of Noorani et al. (2025b) to multi-round dialogue by replacing fixed indicators with general user-specified rules over the interaction history and switching from offline exchangeable calibration to fully online adaptation.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical result (Theorem 5.3) provides a finite-sample guarantee: for any horizon T, the cumulative counterfactual-harm rate is bounded by ε + (1+η)/(ηT) and the complementarity rate by δ + (1+η)/(ηT), with no distributional assumptions. In the crowdsourcing study (1000 interactions, 50 participants), the online algorithm successfully converges to its nominal targets despite non-stationary human behavior. Behaviorally, a CH error in Algorithm A (ε_CH=0.05) causes the human GT-loss rate to jump from 5.1% to 35.3%, while successful complementarity in Algorithm B yields a 35.8% human recovery rate versus only 6.4% when the AI fails to include the ground truth.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, it compares three internal algorithm configurations that vary the target error rates for counterfactual harm (CH) and complementarity (COMP). Comparing Algorithm B (ε_CH=0.30) against Algorithm A (ε_CH=0.05) with identical COMP targets, the looser CH guarantee leads to a significantly higher rate of humans abandoning a previously held correct answer (GT loss). Conversely, comparing Algorithm A (ε_COMP=0.50) against Algorithm C (ε_COMP=0.70) with identical CH targets, the stricter complementarity guarantee produces a measurably higher human recovery rate (GT gain). In the LLM-simulated medical diagnosis task, the same directional effects are observed: increasing allowed CH causes participants to frequently abandon correct initial guesses, while tightening COMP increases the frequency with which participants recover the correct label after an initial error. These comparisons confirm that CH and COMP serve as the primary levers for steering human decision quality without modeling human behavior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the single-round collaborative prediction set framework of Noorani et al. (2025b), which first formalized counterfactual harm and complementarity as constraints on AI prediction sets, to a multi-round interactive setting where the AI's outputs can influence subsequent human behavior. It departs from the agreement-protocol tradition (Aumann 1976; Collina et al. 2025) by adopting an asymmetric, human-centric view: the human is treated as a complete black box who only provides prediction sets, while the AI's uncertainty communication is the sole algorithmic lever. The online calibration mechanism draws on the quantile-tracking literature (Gibbs & Candès 2021; Angelopoulos et al. 2024) but applies it to collaboration-specific, user-defined rule-based constraints rather than standard coverage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first distribution-free, finite-sample guarantees for multi-round human-AI collaboration, showing that a simple online threshold update suffices to enforce user-specified harm and complementarity targets without any assumptions on human behavior or problem distributions. The key practical implication is that practitioners get two interpretable knobs (ε, δ) to tune collaboration quality without modeling the human. A significant limitation is that the framework requires outputs to live in a well-structured candidate space amenable to prediction sets, leaving open how to extend the rule-based control to open-ended or generative outputs. Future work could explore alternative uncertainty representations, richer rule families, and whether the guarantees hold when the AI's textual messages (not just prediction sets) are also constrained.
