## Problem

Existing benchmarks for generalist models either reduce time series to isolated numerical sequences, target narrow domains, or focus on surface-level pattern recognition, leaving the full spectrum of time series reasoning—perception, causal/logical reasoning, forecasting, and decision-making—largely untested. There is no standardized, multi-dimensional, multi-modal benchmark that stress-tests whether LLMs, VLMs, and time-series-specific LLMs can genuinely reason over temporal data in context.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265587/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Time series are rendered in four modalities: textual number sequences for LLMs, line charts at a fixed 100 PPI resolution (with grid lines and series-name annotations; multivariate series stacked as vertical subplots sharing a time axis) for VLMs, model-projector embeddings for TSLLMs, and a combined T+V input for proprietary models. Each of the 4,125 problems pairs the time series with domain-specific contextual text and a multiple-choice question; the model outputs a single letter answer, scored by accuracy per task and aggregated across the 15 tasks and 4 dimensions.

## Method

TSRBENCH organizes 4,125 problems from 14 domains into 4 dimensions (Perception, Reasoning, Prediction, Decision-Making) comprising 15 tasks, with 58.1% of samples being multivariate. Data are sourced via a dual-stream pipeline: synthetic generation (Python simulations with known ground truth for quantitative tasks like numerical and deductive reasoning) and curated web data (e.g., CausalRiver, TimeCAP, PTB-XL) with strict temporal alignment between text and series. Questions use human-designed templates to avoid ambiguity, and answers are verified through a two-stage mechanism—code re-execution for synthetic data and fact cross-referencing for web data—with algorithmically generated distractors. The evaluation protocol is unified across 36 models (6 proprietary, 13 open-source LLMs, 13 open-source VLMs, 4 TSLLMs), enabling direct comparison of scaling behavior, modality complementarity, and task-level correlations via Spearman rank analysis.

## Results

On TSRBENCH (4,125 problems across 14 domains and 15 tasks), GPT-5 with combined text+visual input (T+V) achieves the highest overall accuracy of 55.6%, followed by o4-mini-high (T+V) at 52.5% and GPT-5-mini-high (T+V) at 54.1%. Among open-source models, Qwen3-VL-32B leads VLMs at 44.9% overall, while Qwen2.5-72B leads LLMs at 42.4%. Time-series-specific LLMs (TS-Reasoner-7B: 36.4%, ChatTS-14B: 33.5%) remain competitive with similarly sized generalist models but trail the best proprietary models by over 19 points. A key finding is that scaling laws (Spearman ρ = 0.92 for LLMs, 0.64 for VLMs) hold for Perception, Reasoning, and Decision-Making but break down for Prediction (ρ = −0.24 for LLMs, −0.26 for VLMs), indicating that larger models do not forecast better.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 36 models (6 proprietary, 13 open-source LLMs, 13 open-source VLMs, 4 TSLLMs) on TSRBENCH. GPT-5 (T+V) at 55.6% outperforms the best open-source VLM (Qwen3-VL-32B, 44.9%) by 10.7 points and the best open-source LLM (Qwen2.5-72B, 42.4%) by 13.2 points, highlighting a substantial proprietary-to-open-source gap. Among proprietary models, GPT-5 (T) at 55.5% nearly matches GPT-5 (T+V), while o4-mini-high (T+V) at 52.5% shows that increased reasoning effort yields +4.3 points over o4-mini (T+V) at 48.2%. Time-series-specific models (TS-Reasoner-7B: 36.4%, ChatTS-14B: 33.5%) are competitive with generalist LLMs of similar size (e.g., Qwen3-8B: 38.3%) but fall well short of the top models. A critical finding is that textual and visual inputs are strongly complementary (high union accuracy, low intersection), yet combining them (T+V) does not produce significant gains over either modality alone, indicating current models fail to fuse cross-modal information effectively. Prediction tasks (TSF, EP) show no positive correlation with model scale for either LLMs or VLMs, unlike all other dimensions, revealing a fundamental bottleneck in numerical forecasting that scaling alone cannot resolve.

## Relation to Prior Work

TSRBENCH carves new ground by unifying four previously fragmented evaluation dimensions—Perception, Reasoning, Prediction, and Decision-Making—into a single multi-modal, multi-domain benchmark. Prior time series benchmarks each cover only a narrow slice: TimeSeriesExam focuses on holistic perception, TimeMMD and CiK target context-aided forecasting, MTBench and TSR-SUITE probe limited reasoning in restricted domains, and TimeMQA evaluates traditional analysis tasks. Rather than building on a specific method, TSRBENCH synthesizes data from 14 existing sources (TimeSeriesExam, CausalRiver, GAMETIME, Time-IMM, CAMFE, TimeCAP, ECG-QA, PTB-XL, etc.) and synthetic simulations into a unified 4125-problem suite spanning 15 tasks, and is the first to systematically evaluate generalist models across all four dimensions simultaneously with text, visual, and embedding modalities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a critical decoupling: models that scale well on perception and reasoning (Spearman ρ ≈ 0.9–0.98) show no scaling benefit—or even degradation—on prediction tasks (ρ ≈ −0.24 to −0.26), suggesting that numerical forecasting requires fundamentally different capabilities than semantic temporal understanding. The finding that textual and visual time series are strongly complementary (high union accuracy, low intersection) yet T+V inputs yield no significant gain over single-modality inputs exposes a concrete failure mode in current multimodal fusion. Open questions include whether time-series-specific pretraining on large-scale corpora can close the prediction gap, whether multi-agent decomposition can overcome single-model reasoning limits, and how to design architectures that genuinely exploit cross-modal redundancy rather than merely concatenating inputs.
