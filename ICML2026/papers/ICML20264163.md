## Problem

Prior work has documented that LLM phenomena such as hallucinations, refusal, and sycophancy persist across conversational turns, but no unified framework connects the behavioral likelihood of phenomenon propagation with the model's internal representation geometry. The paper asks: how is conversational carryover encoded in hidden states, and can we quantify the degree to which a model is 'trapped' in a particular behavioral state?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264163/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes simulated multi-turn conversations (20 alternating user-model turns) constructed from semantically coherent question sequences drawn from six datasets, along with hidden state activations extracted from the first answer token at 85% network depth. It produces two complementary metrics: a 2×2 Markov transition matrix whose trace quantifies probabilistic state persistence, and a reference angle θ_ref (in degrees) measuring angular separation between phenomenon-present and phenomenon-absent subspaces, plus per-transition rotation angles normalized by θ_ref. The headline output is the Spearman correlation (ρ=0.78) between trace and θ_ref across models and datasets.

## Method

HISTORY-ECHOES pairs a black-box probabilistic lens with a white-box geometric lens. Probabilistically, each conversation is modeled as a first-order Markov chain over a binary state space (phenomenon present/absent); the transition matrix T is estimated by frequency counts, and its trace Tr(T) = P(φ⁺|φ⁺) + P(φ⁻|φ⁻) serves as a carryover metric where values above 1 indicate state dependence. Geometrically, hidden states are partitioned into phenomenon-present and phenomenon-absent subsets, their mean vectors are orthonormalized via Gram-Schmidt to form a 2D basis, and all held-out hidden states are projected onto this basis. The reference angle θ_ref captures the static angular separation between the two state subspaces, while transition angles are estimated via the orthogonal Procrustes method (closed-form arctan2 solution in 2D) for each of the four transition types. The core finding is that incomplete rotations during cross-state transitions (θ_transition < θ_ref) constitute a geometric trap: the larger the angular separation, the harder it is for the model to fully rotate into the target state, reinforcing carryover. This geometric confinement correlates strongly with the probabilistic trace, and the effect dissolves when conversational topics become semantically incoherent.

## Results

Across three open-weight models (Qwen3-8B, GPT-OSS-20B, LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct) and six datasets, the probabilistic trace Tr(T) averages 1.31, substantially exceeding the null value of 1, confirming carryover effects. The geometric reference angle θ_ref ranges from ~6° (TriviaQA hallucination) to ~67° (SORRY-Bench refusal), with refusal exhibiting the strongest geometric separation. The two perspectives yield a strong Spearman correlation of 0.78 (p < 0.0002) between Tr(T) and θ_ref, demonstrating that probabilistic state persistence manifests as a geometric trap in latent space. Closed models (GPT-5, Claude-Opus-4.5) show consistent probabilistic patterns (Tr(T) = 1.02–1.40), supporting generalizability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical interpretability study without external method baselines; the primary 'comparison' is across phenomena, models, and conversational conditions. Refusal exhibits the strongest carryover effects (Tr(T) ≈ 1.57–1.59, θ_ref ≈ 33–67°), followed by sycophancy (Tr(T) ≈ 1.14–1.33, θ_ref ≈ 14–28°), with hallucination showing the weakest persistence (Tr(T) ≈ 1.12–1.13, θ_ref ≈ 6–14°), likely because hallucination is a heterogeneous umbrella category. The probabilistic and geometric perspectives agree across all three open models, with the Spearman correlation (0.78) holding robustly at conversation lengths of 10, 15, and 20 turns. A critical finding is that topically inconsistent conversations dissolve the carryover effect: Tr(T) drops to ~1.06 on average and the Tr(T)–θ_ref correlation collapses, while θ_ref itself remains stable—indicating the geometric separation persists but the behavioral trap is released. Higher-order Markov analysis (Δ_k) confirms first-order history dominates but second- and third-order effects remain positive, and the 85%-depth layer shows the strongest cross-perspective alignment (correlation > 0.60 at all depths tested).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two previously separate research threads: behavioral studies of carryover/snowballing effects in multi-turn LLM conversations (Zhang et al., 2024; Simhi et al., 2024) and geometric analyses of how phenomena like truthfulness and refusal are encoded as linear directions in hidden representations (Marks & Tegmark, 2024; Arditi et al., 2024). Prior work has modeled LLM generation as Markov processes (Kao et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025) or probed latent geometry in isolation, but no prior study connects the probabilistic state-consistency of a phenomenon with its geometric separation in representation space. HISTORY-ECHOES carves new ground by showing these two lenses are not merely complementary but quantitatively correlated, effectively repurposing the Markov chain trace as a black-box proxy for a white-box geometric measurement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a practical toolkit for diagnosing conversational persistence: the probabilistic trace is computable for any model (including closed APIs), while the geometric angle offers an interpretable mechanism for *why* carryover occurs. The finding that topically inconsistent context dissolves the correlation while preserving geometric separation is practically relevant, suggesting a potential mitigation strategy for unwanted carryover (analogous to jailbreak techniques). Key limitations include the synthetic conversation setup, the focus on single-step (first-order) dependence, and the correlation-not-causation caveat. Open questions include whether multi-phenomena interactions compound or mitigate geometric traps, and whether the identified traps can be targeted for intervention.
