## Problem

Multi-agent debate (MAD) systems are vulnerable to debate collapse, where agents converge on erroneous reasoning, yet existing methods lack principled mechanisms to detect or prevent such failures. Current approaches treat collapse as an outcome-level error rather than a dynamic procedural failure, and mitigation strategies rely on external judge agents or rigid handoff interactions that do not address the intrinsic instability of the debating agents themselves.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264379/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a question prompt and a set of N open-source LLM agents (e.g., Llama-4-Scout, Qwen-3-8B, Gemma-3n-E4B, Mixtral-8x7B, Phi-4) that engage in T rounds of iterative debate, producing a final answer via majority vote. Beyond the answer, the framework outputs three hierarchical uncertainty metrics per question: intra-agent flip/belief-revision rates, inter-agent pairwise conflict scores, and system-level entropy/disagreement/leave-one-out instability, which serve both as diagnostic indicators and as reward signals during training.

## Method

The paper proposes UDPO (Uncertainty-Driven Policy Optimization), which formulates multi-agent debate as a dynamic interaction process and introduces a three-component reward that penalizes intra-agent self-contradiction (flip-flopping), inter-agent unresolved disagreement, and low-confidence system-level outputs (entropy, residual disagreement, leave-one-out instability). A key design choice is an asymmetric optimization objective: agent-specific reward coefficients (α, β, γ, λ) and KL-anchoring weights (η) are calibrated in a two-phase warm-up procedure so that higher-uncertainty agents receive stronger stabilization penalties while already-stable agents receive relatively higher task rewards, preventing over-regularization. Training uses a clipped relative-update (PPO-style) surrogate with a fixed reference policy to constrain policy drift, combined with an uncertainty-driven replay buffer that oversamples high-uncertainty trajectories. This decomposition into interpretable failure modes, rather than treating collapse as a monolithic coordination or adversarial-robustness problem, is what distinguishes UDPO from baselines like MAPPO (centralized critic) and RMAAC (worst-case perturbation robustness).

## Results

On the primary N=5 multi-agent debate setting, UDPO achieves 92.3% accuracy on GSM8K, 88.7% on TruthfulQA, and 91.5% on CommonsenseQA, outperforming Standard MAD (68.4%, 71.8%, 75.4%), MAPPO (73.6%, 74.2%, 78.2%), and RMAAC (75.8%, 76.5%, 79.8%) by up to 24 percentage points. UDPO also dramatically reduces system-level uncertainty (U_sys), e.g., from 0.285 to 0.058 on GSM8K (an ~80% reduction). Under adversarial attacks with 3 compromised agents out of 5, UDPO retains 56.2% accuracy on GSM8K versus 9.4% for Standard MAD and 40.5% for RMAAC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UDPO consistently outperforms all three baselines across every dataset and agent-count configuration. Compared to Standard MAD (vanilla multi-agent debate without training), UDPO gains 16–24 percentage points in accuracy (e.g., 68.4%→92.3% on GSM8K, N=5) while reducing system-level uncertainty by roughly 80%. Against MAPPO (cooperative PPO with a centralized critic), UDPO improves accuracy by 14–20 points; the paper attributes MAPPO's limitation to its centralized credit assignment, which does not explicitly penalize behavioral instability such as flip-flopping or persistent disagreement. Against RMAAC (robust MARL with adversarial state perturbations), UDPO gains 12–17 points; RMAAC's worst-case perturbation robustness helps under bounded noise but fails to diagnose which specific debate behaviors (self-contradiction vs. peer conflict vs. low-confidence aggregation) are failing. Under adversarial attacks (Table 3), the gap widens further: with 3 compromised agents on GSM8K, UDPO achieves 56.2% accuracy versus 9.4% (Standard MAD), 36.7% (MAPPO), and 40.5% (RMAAC), demonstrating that UDPO's uncertainty-driven regularization delays the collapse tipping point even when a majority of agents are compromised.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the multi-agent debate (MAD) framework established by Du et al. (2023) and Chan et al. (2024), where LLM agents iteratively critique and refine each other's reasoning. It departs from prior MAD work—which focuses on architectural innovations like judge agents or role-based structures—by introducing a hierarchical uncertainty quantification (intra-agent flip rate, inter-agent disagreement, system-level entropy/disagreement/leave-one-out instability) as diagnostic indicators of 'debate collapse.' The mitigation strategy (UDPO) draws on PPO-style clipped policy optimization from multi-agent RL (MAPPO, RMAAC) but introduces an asymmetric, agent-tailored reward that explicitly penalizes the three uncertainty components, rather than relying on centralized credit assignment or worst-case perturbation robustness.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled, behavior-level diagnostic framework for MAD failures, moving beyond treating collapse as a black-box outcome error. The robustness results under adversarial attack (5.2pp degradation vs. 34.6pp for Standard MAD) are practically significant for deploying multi-agent LLM systems in security-sensitive settings. Limitations include evaluation on only three reasoning benchmarks and five open-source LLMs, a specific attack model (AUTOTRANSFORM), and the requirement for fine-tuning rather than inference-time intervention. Open questions include whether the uncertainty-driven approach generalizes to larger ensembles, proprietary models, or more diverse failure modes beyond the three identified.
