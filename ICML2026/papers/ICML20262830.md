## Problem

Locate-then-edit (LTE) LLM parameter editing methods universally rely on 'backward spreading'—computing a target hidden state at the final decisive layer and distributing it to earlier layers via linear interpolation—yet this practice has never been systematically grounded. The paper shows that backward spreading implicitly requires the residual direction to be an eigenvector of the inter-layer Jacobian (a very strong condition), and empirically demonstrates that its convergence degrades as more layers are included, limiting scalability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262830/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained LLM, a set of target knowledge pairs (query, answer), and background queries for the orthogonality regularizer. The key operational difference from standard MEMIT is that the optimizable variable is the hidden state at the *first* decisive layer rather than the last; after a single gradient-descent pass, the targets for all subsequent decisive layers are recovered by a short forward-propagation replay (only ~4 extra layer evaluations). The output is a set of mutually compatible, layer-wise target hidden states and the corresponding closed-form MLP weight updates (ΔW) for each decisive layer.

## Method

The core idea is to flip the anchor point: instead of optimizing the target hidden state at the final decisive layer and interpolating backward, the method optimizes the target at the *first* decisive layer via the same cross-entropy gradient descent used in MEMIT. Because the backpropagation path from the final output loss to the first layer implicitly traverses all intermediate layers, the ideal hidden states at those layers are already encoded along that path; they are recovered exactly by a standard forward-propagation replay from the optimized first-layer target. This guarantees cross-layer compatibility by construction (the same input cannot produce two different outputs through a deterministic function), eliminates the eigenvector assumption required by backward spreading, and yields exponentially converging residuals as more layers are edited. The approach is a drop-in replacement for the target-propagation step in any LTE pipeline, requiring no changes to the optimization objective, regularization, or closed-form weight-update formula, and adds negligible computational overhead (a few forward passes through the decisive layers).

## Results

On the MCF dataset with Llama3-8B-Instruct, MEMIT+FE achieves 99.9% efficacy Success and 99.8% efficacy Accuracy, substantially outperforming standard MEMIT (97.4% / 94.3%) and the stronger BLUE baseline (98.4% / 96.9%). On generalization, FE reaches 93.0% Success and 61.0% Accuracy versus 78.7% / 48.0% for MEMIT. On specificity, FE attains the best D_KL (0.34) and Top-1 (82.7%) among all LTE methods, while on ZsRE it achieves 97.6% efficacy Success, 93.6% generalization Success, and the lowest D_KL (0.09) across all methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FE outperforms standard MEMIT by +2.5 pts efficacy Success and +14.3 pts generalization Success on MCF, while also improving specificity (D_KL 0.34 vs. 0.41). Against BLUE, which independently optimizes targets for two layers at 2× computational cost, FE achieves higher efficacy (99.9% vs. 98.4%), generalization (93.0% vs. 90.0%), and specificity (D_KL 0.34 vs. 0.35) at the same cost as MEMIT. FE also dominates the other LTE baselines PRUNE, RECT, and AlphaEdit on every efficacy and generalization metric (e.g., 99.9% vs. 97.4% / 96.9% / 95.2% efficacy Success on MCF). The non-LTE baselines WISE and RLEdit show very poor efficacy (18.0% and 57.8% Success) and are not competitive; WISE achieves the best specificity (D_KL 0.09) but at the cost of nearly zero editing success. On ZsRE, the gap widens further: FE reaches 97.6% efficacy Success and 93.6% generalization Success versus 92.6% / 87.6% for MEMIT, with a dramatically lower D_KL of 0.09 versus 0.60.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the MEMIT/ROME locate-then-edit (LTE) paradigm for LLM parameter editing, where a target hidden state is computed at the final decisive layer and spread backward to earlier layers via linear interpolation. It departs from this standard by inverting the optimization direction: the target is computed at the first decisive layer and propagated forward through the network, yielding mutually compatible layer-wise targets. The paper also engages critically with BLUE (Li et al., 2025a), which independently optimizes targets for the first and last layers, showing that this approach doubles computational cost and still lacks cross-layer compatibility. The theoretical contribution formalizes the implicit assumptions behind backward spreading (Jacobian eigenvector condition, positive-definite symmetric part) for the first time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The forward-replay mechanism is a drop-in replacement that improves efficacy, generalization, and specificity across multiple LTE methods (MEMIT, RECT, AlphaEdit, PRUNE) with negligible additional compute, making it broadly adoptable by the knowledge-editing community. The systematic analysis of backward spreading's foundations clarifies why it works in practice (mild positive-definiteness condition) while exposing its scalability limits (non-convergent residual as more layers are added). A key open question remains: the editing effort is concentrated in earlier layers, and designing principled layer-wise allocation strategies that distribute the editing load more evenly is left for future work.
