## Problem

Existing audio-visual speech separation (AVSS) models rely on implicit multimodal fusion that entangles target selection with content restoration, lacking explicit modality alignment and reliability modeling. This causes semantic misalignment, distractor contamination of speech representations, and degraded performance when visual cues are temporally misaligned or partially occluded. The paper addresses this gap by making the two-stage neural hierarchy—top-down auditory selection followed by bottom-up cross-modal compensation—explicit and reliability-aware in a computational architecture.

## Input / Output

The model takes a 16 kHz noisy multi-speaker audio mixture and synchronized mouth-region video frames (88×88 grayscale at 25 FPS) as input. Audio is converted to a 257-bin STFT magnitude (with power-law compression) and phase; video is embedded by a frozen CTCNet-Lip front-end and temporally aligned to the audio grid via a differentiable shifter that estimates a bounded offset (±200 ms) and produces a clip-level reliability scalar. The output is a separated clean speech waveform reconstructed by inverting an iSTFT from a predicted magnitude mask and a learned phase residual.

## Method

Neuro-SCNet is a dual-encoder-decoder (6.3M params, 17.8G MACs) that separates selection from compensation in two explicit stages within a stack of FRI-Conformer bottleneck blocks. The Auditory Selection Module (ASM) applies visually guided gain before fusion: it computes joint audio-visual saliency, partitions cross-attention outputs into four quadrants (salient/non-salient × aligned/anti-aligned), refines non-salient and anti-aligned components with learnable gates, and applies residual inhibition to suppress distractor leakage into the salient-aligned path. The Cross-Modal Compensation Module (CCM) then restores suppressed content via an identity bypass plus a reliability-weighted visual residual, where a dual-GRU pipeline generates prediction-error feedback and a scalar gate r (derived from the visual pre-alignment confidence) down-weights uncertain visual cues. A lightweight visual pre-alignment step estimates a small temporal offset via soft-argmax over a bounded lag window and applies a differentiable grid-sample shifter, while a compact dual-path encoder-decoder preserves fine acoustic detail (harmonics, formants) in a dedicated magnitude-phase branch alongside the semantic audio-visual branch.

## Results

Neuro-SCNet achieves state-of-the-art audio-visual speech separation on all three primary benchmarks: LRS2 (SI-SNRi 17.2 dB, SDRi 17.9 dB, PESQ 3.59), LRS3 (SI-SNRi 18.9 dB, SDRi 19.5 dB, PESQ 3.71), and VoxCeleb2 (SI-SNRi 14.8 dB, SDRi 15.2 dB, PESQ 3.43). Compared with the strongest prior model AV-CrossNet, Neuro-SCNet improves SI-SNRi by 2.34%, SI-SDRi by 4.03%, and PESQ by 0.84% on average, while using only 6.3M parameters (vs. 11.1M) and 17.8G MACs. Under heavier interference (3- and 4-speaker mixtures on LRS2), Neuro-SCNet maintains the top position, exceeding IIANet by 1.6 dB SI-SNRi in the four-speaker condition.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Neuro-SCNet outperforms all 17 baselines across the primary separation benchmarks. Against the strongest prior model AV-CrossNet (11.1M params), Neuro-SCNet (6.3M params) gains +0.4 dB SI-SNRi on LRS2, +0.6 dB on LRS3, and +0.2 dB on VoxCeleb2, while also improving SDRi by 0.8–1.0 dB and PESQ by 0.02–0.04. Against the neuroscience-inspired IIANet (3.1M params), gains are larger: +1.2 dB SI-SNRi on LRS2, +0.6 dB on LRS3, and +1.2 dB on VoxCeleb2. Older baselines show wider gaps: CTC-Net (7.0M) trails by 2.9 dB SI-SNRi on LRS2, and AV-ConvTasNet (16.5M) by 4.7 dB. On the noisy benchmarks (NTCD-TIMIT, LRS3+WHAM!), Neuro-SCNet achieves the best PESQ (2.18/2.08), eSTOI (0.65/0.77), and SI-SDRi (15.94/13.90 dB), marginally exceeding AV-CrossNet. For target speaker extraction (Table 9), Neuro-SCNet leads on LRS3 (18.04 vs. 17.42 dB SI-SDR) and TCD-TIMIT (18.31 vs. 18.15 dB) but is essentially tied with AV-CrossNet on VoxCeleb2 (14.66 vs. 14.71 dB SI-SDR) while achieving slightly higher PESQ (2.95 vs. 2.93). On COG-MHEAR speech enhancement, Neuro-SCNet matches or marginally exceeds AV-CrossNet on PESQ and STOI in both Speech+Noise and Speech+Speech conditions.

## Relation to Prior Work

Neuro-SCNet extends the AVSS line of work (AV-CrossNet, IIANet, CTC-Net) by explicitly decomposing the fusion process into two neuroscience-motivated stages—auditory selection (top-down gain control) and cross-modal compensation (bottom-up residual correction)—rather than relying on implicit cross-modal attention that entangles target identification with content restoration. The dual-path magnitude-phase decoder follows Lu et al. (2025), and the FRI-Conformer bottleneck and CTCNet-Lip visual frontend are adopted from prior work, but the information-bottleneck formulation for selection and the predictive-coding residual for compensation represent a conceptual departure from purely empirical fusion designs. The reliability-gated visual pre-alignment (bounded-lag cross-correlation with soft offset) is a lightweight addition not present in prior AVSS systems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The explicit selection-then-compensation decomposition offers a principled, interpretable alternative to black-box multimodal fusion, and the reliability gate provides a practical mechanism for graceful degradation under temporal misalignment or visual corruption—important for real-world deployment where lip tracking is imperfect. The paper demonstrates that this structure generalizes across separation, enhancement, and target-speaker-extraction tasks, and that the two-stage ordering (not just the presence of both stages) matters. Open questions include whether finer-grained (frame-level or spatial) reliability estimation can further improve robustness under severe occlusion, and whether the neuroscience-inspired decomposition transfers to other multimodal perception tasks beyond speech.
