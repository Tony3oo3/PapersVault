## Problem

In offline zero-shot RL, task vectors defining linear reward functions are uniformly sampled from the unit hypersphere, implicitly assuming this covers the task space adequately. The paper shows this causes 'signal dilution': as the feature dimension grows, most random directions become orthogonal to the achievable behavioral space, collapsing the inter-policy return variance and degrading both training and zero-shot generalization. Prior work focused on learning rich state representations while treating task sampling as a secondary, uniform process.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a fixed offline dataset of state-action transitions (10^7 transitions from DeepMind Control Suite environments like Cheetah, Walker, Quadruped) and a pre-trained state embedding φ(s) of dimension d (default 50). At test time, a reward function is revealed on 5120 random states, from which a task vector z_test is estimated via linear regression on φ(s). The output is a task-conditioned action a = π(s, z_test) produced by a TD3-based actor-critic, requiring no additional training or planning at deployment.

## Method

The core contribution is Behavioral Task Distribution (BTD) sampling: instead of drawing task vectors uniformly from S^{d-1}, the method extracts them from the offline dataset by computing the empirical feature occupancy ψ̃^τ = Σ γ^t φ(s_t) over random subtrajectories τ and normalizing to obtain z_τ = ψ̃^τ / ‖ψ̃^τ‖. A Gaussian Mixture Model is then fit by maximum likelihood to these extracted vectors, yielding a continuous density p_θ(z) over the behavioral task space. During policy training, task vectors are sampled from p_θ(z) rather than uniformly, while all representation learning objectives and policy architectures remain unchanged. Theoretically, Proposition 4.1 proves that uniform sampling yields expected inter-policy return variance (1/d)·Tr(Σ_Ψ) → 0 as d → ∞, whereas BTD-sampling concentrates mass on directions where the behavioral space actually varies, preserving an informative gradient signal at any dimension. The approach is plug-and-play across SF, FB, and any representation learning baseline, and ablations confirm that subtrajectory extraction plus GMM interpolation outperforms both raw subtrajectory sampling and full-trajectory sampling.

## Results

BTD-sampling achieves an average +20% improvement in zero-shot performance over uniform task sampling across Cheetah, Walker, and Quadruped environments (ExoRL benchmark), winning 13 of 15 individual test comparisons. The gains are most pronounced in Cheetah and when using AEnc or LRA_P representations. Critically, while baseline methods collapse to near-failure at latent dimensions 750–1000 due to signal dilution, BTD-sampling maintains stable performance even at d=1000, confirming the theoretical prediction of Proposition 4.1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BTD-sampling outperforms all seven baseline representation methods (AEnc, Trans, LRA_P, LRA_SR, BYOL, BYOL-γ, FB) when each is augmented with the proposed task sampling, with the largest gains observed for AEnc and LRA_P on Cheetah. Against the state-of-the-art FB method specifically, BTD-sampling improves performance across all three environments (Cheetah, Walker, Quadruped). The improvement is consistent across representation learning paradigms—reconstruction-based (AEnc, Trans), spectral (LRA_P, LRA_SR), and self-supervised (BYOL, BYOL-γ)—indicating the gain is orthogonal to the choice of state encoder. In the high-dimensional regime (d ≥ 750), baselines with uniform sampling degrade sharply (e.g., Cheetah and Walker scores collapse), while BTD-sampling retains significantly higher scores, demonstrating robustness to the signal dilution effect. Mixing BTD with uniform sampling (interpolation parameter α) consistently degrades performance, confirming that uniform tasks dilute rather than complement the behavioral signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Successor Features (SF) and Forward-Backward (FB) frameworks for zero-shot RL, which learn task-conditioned policies over linear reward families defined by a state embedding and a task vector. The key departure is in the task sampling strategy: prior work uniformly samples task vectors from the unit hypersphere, implicitly assuming this covers the relevant task space, while this paper argues that the environment's behavioral constraints make most uniform directions uninformative. Conceptually, the approach draws from adaptive goal sampling in goal-conditioned RL (curriculum, autotelic, and density-based methods) and from IRL's data-driven reward recovery, but applies these ideas to the offline ZSRL setting by fitting a Gaussian Mixture Model to task vectors extracted from offline trajectories.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of 'signal dilution' as a fundamental bottleneck in SF/FB-based ZSRL shifts attention from representation learning (the dominant focus of prior work) to task distribution design, offering a simple plug-and-play fix that yields ~20% average gains across seven baselines and three environments. The theoretical characterization (Proposition 4.1) and its empirical validation at high dimensions provide a clear diagnostic for practitioners: if task dimension is large, uniform sampling is likely the limiting factor. Key limitations remain: BTD is bounded by the behavioral support of the offline dataset (rare but feasible behaviors may be underrepresented), the method inherits the linear-reward assumption of SF/FB, and experiments are confined to locomotion tasks from ExoRL, leaving open whether the approach transfers to manipulation, visually complex, or contact-rich settings.
