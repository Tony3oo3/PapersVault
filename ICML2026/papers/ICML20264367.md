## Problem

Existing Federated Graph Learning (FGL) methods primarily align or personalize node features but largely overlook structural knowledge, causing aggregation-induced representation drift when GNNs propagate information across heterogeneous local subgraphs. The paper identifies that structural heterogeneity often originates from feature-driven connection biases, and that structural knowledge is more transferable across domains than raw features, yet directly fusing structures fails under such feature-induced topology bias.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264367/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each client holds a set of graphs (node features, edge lists) from one of four domains (CHEM, BIO, SN, CV) drawn from TUDataset, with graph-level labels used only during training. The method computes per-node structural descriptors (degree encoding up to max degree m and random-walk return probabilities up to length n, both fixed at 16) as initial inputs alongside raw node features. The output is a graph-level class label produced by pooling the final structural and feature representations through a classification head.

## Method

Fed-Kalter introduces Kalter-Conv, a graph convolution that decouples structure learning from feature propagation by modeling structural embeddings as latent states and feature-augmented neighborhood aggregation as noisy observations within a Kalman filtering framework. Each Kalter-Conv layer runs a shared GIN-based structural update (treating the difference between structural and feature representations as the state transition) in parallel with a private GCN-based feature propagation, then fuses them via a Kalman-style posterior update with a scalar gain K that balances prior structural belief against the current measurement. Only the structural module parameters (Θ_Ak for state transition, Θ_Hk for measurement) are aggregated globally via weighted averaging at the server, while feature propagation weights and input projections remain locally personalized. The training objective combines cross-entropy classification loss with a Frobenius-norm structural consistency term that penalizes the covariance of the estimation error between the true and estimated structural states.

## Results

On the full four-domain setting (16 graph datasets across CHEM, BIO, SN, CV), Fed-Kalter achieves 73.69% average test accuracy, outperforming the strongest prior FGL methods FedVN (73.19%) and FedStar (72.63%). Across all domain configurations (1 to 4 domains), Fed-Kalter consistently leads, with average accuracy gains of 5.56%, 2.57%, 1.00%, and 1.46% over FedStar respectively. On large-scale datasets (TWITTER-Real-Graph-Partial, Yeast, P388; ~265K graphs total), Fed-Kalter reaches 83.14% accuracy, marginally exceeding FedVN (83.03%) and FedStar (82.61%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Fed-Kalter outperforms all seven main FGL baselines on the four-domain setting: it beats FedAvg by 6.94%, FedProx by 6.30%, FedPer by 6.35%, FedSage by 2.81%, GCFL by 6.54%, FedStar by 1.06%, and FedVN by 0.50% in average accuracy. The gap over FedStar (the strongest structural-knowledge-sharing baseline) narrows as domain count increases (5.56% on 1-domain to 1.46% on 4-domain), suggesting diminishing returns when topological distributions are highly diverse. On the node-classification CiteSeer comparison (10 disjoint clients), Fed-Kalter (75.15%) beats FED-PUB (72.35%), AdaFGL (72.34%), FedGTA (71.37%), and FedTAD (70.31%), but is outperformed by FedIIH (76.50%), which explicitly models missing cross-client edges—a scenario outside Fed-Kalter's design scope. On large-scale datasets, Fed-Kalter's advantage over FedVN is marginal (83.14% vs. 83.03%), indicating that structural decoupling provides the largest gains in heterogeneous multi-domain settings rather than homogeneous large-scale ones.

## Relation to Prior Work

Fed-Kalter carves new ground at the intersection of classical Kalman filtering and federated graph learning. Prior FGL methods (FedStar, FedVN, FedSage, GCFL) address heterogeneity through data augmentation, client clustering, or shared structural priors, but all perform end-to-end message passing that entangles structure and features in each layer. The key departure here is an explicit architectural decoupling: structural embeddings (degree + random-walk encodings, building on Bouritsas et al. and Zhang et al.) are treated as latent states, while feature-augmented neighborhood aggregation is modeled as noisy observation, with a Kalman-inspired update filtering the latter from the former. The selective aggregation strategy—sharing only structural parameters (Θ_A, Θ_H) globally while keeping feature parameters local—repurposes the Kalman gain as a fixed fusion weight rather than a full covariance-based estimator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies 'aggregated feature drift' as a distinct failure mode in FGL that prior methods conflate with general non-IID effects, and provides a principled mechanism (Kalman-style state estimation) to mitigate it. However, the practical impact is tempered by several limitations: the Kalman gain is a fixed scalar rather than adaptively computed, the method underperforms on topologically dissimilar domains (COLLAB negative transfer), and it is explicitly not designed for node-level tasks where cross-client cut-edges represent missing data rather than zero-mean noise. Open questions include whether adaptive, layer-wise Kalman gains (with full covariance tracking) would yield meaningfully better results, and how the framework extends to dynamic or streaming graph settings where structural priors shift over time.
