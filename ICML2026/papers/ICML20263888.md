## Problem

Consistency models and flow-map self-distillation have enabled one- or few-step generation for continuous data, but porting these accelerated inference techniques to inherently discrete structures (text, molecular graphs, sequences) is obstructed by the stochasticity and discreteness of discrete-diffusion trajectories, which prevent direct application of flow-map objectives. This paper asks whether accelerated few-step generation is possible for categorical data by working within continuous interpolations of discrete data rather than discrete Markov chains.

## Input / Output

The method takes a Gaussian noise sample x_0 ~ N(0,I) as input and iteratively transports it toward the data distribution via the learned flow map, producing discrete categorical outputs: molecular graphs (one-hot atom and bond type encodings, up to 9 heavy atoms on QM9 or 38 on ZINC), 28×28 binary images, or token sequences (256 tokens on Text8 with 27-token alphabet, 128 tokens on LM1B with ~30K-token BERT tokenizer). The network outputs logits in R^{D×K} per position, which are softmaxed to yield simplex-valued endpoint predictions; the final discrete sample is obtained by argmax or categorical sampling. Optionally, a differentiable reward function r(x) can be supplied at test time to steer generation toward a downstream objective.

## Method

Categorical Flow Maps replace the standard velocity-field parametrisation with a simplex-constrained endpoint parametrisation: a 'partial denoiser' π_{s,t}(x_s) ∈ Δ^K predicts a categorical endpoint, and the flow map moves from x_s only a fraction (t−s)/(1−s) toward that endpoint, guaranteeing geometric confinement to the convex hull of the data support at all times. Training combines a variational flow matching cross-entropy loss (for the diagonal s=t, learning the instantaneous endpoint predictor) with a self-distillation loss for off-diagonal (s,t) pairs. The paper introduces two distillation objectives: CSD, a Lagrangian MSE loss on the flow-map residual, and ECLD, a cross-entropy endpoint-consistency loss that provably bounds the Lagrangian residual (L_CSD ≤ 4·L_CE-EC + 2·L_TD), operating entirely on simplex-valued predictions rather than unconstrained velocities. At test time, the flow map provides a cheap lookahead X_{t,1}(x_t) of the trajectory endpoint, enabling reward-tilted sequential Monte Carlo sampling for guided generation without retraining.

## Results

On molecular graph generation, CFM ECLD achieves 96.3% validity at 1 NFE on QM9 and 93.5% on ZINC, while CFM CSD reaches an FCD of 0.44 at 2 NFE on QM9—matching CatFlow's 100-NFE result—and 12.1 on ZINC, beating CatFlow (13.2 at 100 NFE) and GDSS (14.7 at 1,000 NFE). On binarised MNIST, CFM achieves 10.1 FID at 1 step (vs. 12.9 for the prior best few-step method), and test-time flow-map lookahead guidance yields 84.7% target-class accuracy. For text, ECLD outperforms DFM on Text8 at all NFEs (5.33 NLL), and on LM1B CFM achieves up to 7.4× lower Gen-PPL than Duo at 1 NFE (142.7 vs. ~1,056).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against multi-step diffusion/flow baselines, CFM CSD at 2 NFE matches CatFlow's FCD on QM9 (0.44 vs. 0.44 at 100 NFE) and beats both CatFlow (13.2) and GDSS (14.7) on ZINC with FCD 12.1, representing a 50–500× reduction in function evaluations. Among few-step and one-shot baselines, CFM ECLD substantially outperforms MoFlow (96.3% vs. 91.4% valid on QM9; 93.5% vs. 63.1% on ZINC) and PairFlow (96.3% vs. 44.3% on QM9; 93.5% vs. 11.2% on ZINC) at 1 NFE. The simplex-constrained endpoint parametrisation provides a clear advantage over the unconstrained Naive Flow Map, especially on the larger ZINC molecules (93.5% vs. 59.5% valid at 1 NFE). On Text8, ECLD outperforms DFM (Campbell et al., 2024) at all NFEs in NLL. On LM1B, CFM outperforms Duo (Sahoo et al., 2025) in the low-NFE regime with up to 7.4× lower Gen-PPL at 1 NFE, though Duo surpasses CFM at 8 and 16 NFE. On binarised MNIST, CFM improves FID over the prior few-step method (Park et al., 2025) from 12.9 to 10.1 at 1 step, and flow-map lookahead guidance achieves the best class-conditional FID (36.9) and accuracy (84.7%) among three steering strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the flow map self-distillation framework of Boffi et al. (2024; 2025) — which learns integrals of the probability flow to enable few-step generation — from continuous data to categorical data, by combining it with the variational endpoint-prediction perspective of Eijkelboom et al. (2024). The key conceptual departure is the simplex-constrained endpoint parametrisation (Eq. 10), which replaces the unconstrained velocity parametrisation with a partial denoiser whose output lies on the probability simplex, making the Lagrangian self-distillation objective directly applicable to discrete targets. Unlike prior discrete-diffusion acceleration methods (Duo, PairFlow) that operate on fundamentally discrete trajectories where flow map objectives are ill-defined, CFM uses continuous interpolations of one-hot data, inheriting the full machinery of stochastic-interpolant flow maps including test-time guidance via SMC reweighting (Sabour et al., 2025a).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Few-step generation for discrete data (molecules, text, binary images) is a practically critical bottleneck: discrete diffusion models typically require hundreds to thousands of steps, and prior acceleration methods (Duo, PairFlow) still struggle in the 1–2 step regime. CFM's simplex-constrained endpoint parametrisation provides a clean inductive bias that becomes increasingly important in higher-dimensional settings (ZINC vs. QM9), and the ECLD loss offers a theoretically grounded alternative to MSE-based distillation that avoids the scale mismatch between cross-entropy and squared-error objectives. Open questions include whether the endpoint parametrisation advantage persists at much larger scale (e.g., billion-parameter language models), whether the CSD-vs-ECLD trade-off (one-step validity vs. multi-step FCD) can be resolved with adaptive weighting, and how the method extends to structured discrete data beyond one-hot encodings (e.g., 3D molecular coordinates, protein sequences with continuous side-chain angles).
