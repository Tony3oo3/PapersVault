## Problem

Existing data scheduling methods for LLM training optimize over static data distributions but overlook the dynamic, directional interactions between training units that evolve throughout optimization. Because gradient updates are non-commutative in non-convex landscapes, the order in which batches are presented fundamentally changes the terminal model state, yet no principled, optimization-grounded method existed to quantify these asymmetric pairwise influences and derive an optimal training sequence.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265222/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each training step, the method consumes a randomly sampled chunk of L micro-batches (L ≪ K total batches) along with the current parameter state θ^(t). It produces a permutation π over those L batches, which is then applied sequentially in SGD updates. Gradients are compressed to k-dimensional sketches via a fixed Johnson-Lindenstrauss random projection (k ≈ 3500 in practice), and curvature is reduced to a per-batch scalar λ_j via Hutchinson diagonal Hessian estimation (W=5 random vectors), keeping per-pair influence computation at O(dk+k) rather than O(d).

## Method

D³ defines a training influence prediction I_{i→j}(t) measuring how much a hypothetical gradient step on batch i changes the loss of batch j, then shows via second-order Taylor expansion that the first-order term is symmetric while the Hessian-dependent second-order term is asymmetric, inducing a directed dependency structure. These pairwise influences form a dynamic influence graph whose adjacency matrix A^(t) is converted into a directional advantage matrix S^(t) = A^(t)ᵀ − A^(t), where S_ij > 0 means training batch i before j yields greater cumulative loss reduction. The scheduling problem is then cast as minimizing the total weighted penalty of violated directional advantages over all permutations (a linear ordering problem), solved approximately by Random-Swap Refinement: an initial row-sum sort followed by M stochastic pairwise swaps accepted when they reduce the violation cost. For scalability, the full K×K problem is replaced by chunk-wise L×L reordering, and the influence estimate combines a compressed first-order inner product with a scalar curvature term, yielding an overall per-step overhead of O(L² + ML) plus gradient sketching cost.

## Results

On pre-training (GPT-2 Medium, 100B SlimPajama tokens), D³ achieves a perplexity of 2.97, a 4.2% relative reduction over the strongest baseline (Dynamic Loss at 3.10), and the highest aggregated commonsense reasoning score of 42.56 (vs. 41.26 for Dynamic Loss). The largest individual gains are on OBQA (+10.1% over DoGE) and COPA (+7.6% over DoGE). On post-training SFT (Llama-3-8B), D³ reaches 49.6 aggregated accuracy, outperforming the best curriculum baseline (46.2) and multi-task learning (44.9), with notable gains on MATH (+11.4%) and HumanEval (+8.0%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

D³ outperforms all six baselines on the primary pre-training table. Against Dynamic Loss (Sow et al., ICLR 2025), the closest sample-level competitor, D³ reduces PPL from 3.10 to 2.97 and improves OBQA from 13.8 to 15.2, though it trails slightly on PIQA (55.7 vs. 56.8). Against the four domain-level baselines—RegMix (Liu et al.), Data Mixing Law (Ye et al.), DoReMi (Xie et al.), and DoGE (Fan et al.)—D³ achieves the best PPL (2.97 vs. 3.30–4.51) and the highest aggregated reasoning score (42.56 vs. 40.64–41.18), with the largest margin on OBQA (+10.1% over DoGE's 11.5). In the ablation (Figure 2), D³'s diagonal Hessian influence estimator dominates the Fisher Information Matrix approach (Xie et al., ICLR 2021) on the perplexity–overhead Pareto frontier, achieving a 5.1% PPL reduction at 89% relative overhead versus FIM's suboptimal trade-off at ~1.3× overhead. On SFT (Table 2), D³ surpasses both fixed curriculum orderings (easy-to-hard: 46.2, hard-to-easy: 34.9) and standard multi-task learning (44.9), confirming that dynamic, state-dependent scheduling outperforms static difficulty-based heuristics.

## Relation to Prior Work

D³ carves new ground at the intersection of data influence estimation, graph-based combinatorial optimization, and second-order optimization, without directly extending any single prior method. It departs from domain-level mixture optimization (DoReMi, RegMix, MixMin) by operating at the batch level with explicit pairwise interaction modeling, and from sample-level selection/reweighting (GREATS, Dynamic Loss, DWM) by preserving all data while reordering it. The key conceptual departure from prior data-ordering work (Kim & Lee 2024, Pouransari 2024, Jia 2025) is the formalization of scheduling as a constrained optimization over a dynamic directed influence graph, where edge weights are derived from second-order loss curvature rather than heuristic difficulty labels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that the temporal ordering of training batches—governed by asymmetric gradient-curvature interactions—matters as much as the data distribution itself, providing a principled alternative to heuristic curricula. However, the 89% per-step training overhead from Hutchinson-based Hessian estimation and the theoretical analysis being restricted to a linear regression proxy significantly limit immediate large-scale adoption. Open questions include whether layer-selective gradient compression can reduce the overhead, whether the convergence guarantees extend to non-convex Transformer landscapes, and whether the dynamic graph structure reveals interpretable learning-phase transitions.
