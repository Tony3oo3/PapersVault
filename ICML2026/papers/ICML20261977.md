## Problem

Jung et al. (2025) provide provable human-agreement guarantees for LLM judges but rely on an empirical monotonicity assumption—that higher estimated confidence corresponds to lower disagreement risk—that can be violated in practice. Moreover, the generalization behavior of the confidence estimator itself is never formally analyzed, leaving the induced confidence ordering unreliable out of sample. This paper addresses both gaps by learning a dedicated confidence estimator with explicit PAC-Bayesian generalization guarantees on its ranking error.

## Input / Output

For each instance (a query with two candidate responses), the LLM judge is run once per in-context subset drawn from K preference-labelled examples across N simulated annotators, producing a feature vector of |T| predictive probabilities. A 3-layer MLP (64-32-16, ReLU + sigmoid) maps this vector to a scalar confidence in [0,1]. The resulting confidence scores induce a pairwise ranking over instances, which is then fed into fixed-sequence testing to select a threshold λ that certifies a target human-agreement level 1−α.

## Method

The core contribution is a margin-based pairwise ranking loss ℓ_γ that penalizes misordered agreement/disagreement pairs, trained via a differentiable softmax surrogate with a margin-dependent complexity regularizer derived from a PAC-Bayesian bound. The bound (Corollary 3.4) shows the expected misranking probability is controlled by the empirical margin loss plus a term scaling as O(√(Φ(C_θ)/(γ²(m_p−1)))), exposing a trade-off: larger γ reduces complexity but amplifies empirical loss. Guided by this, the paper introduces an adaptive optimizer that alternates between updating the MLP parameters θ with a smooth surrogate ranking loss at fixed γ, and updating γ to minimize the full objective, effectively balancing ranking separability against model capacity. The complexity surrogate replaces spectral norms with Frobenius norms for differentiability, and the entire procedure is trained end-to-end on ~3000 synthetic examples per dataset.

## Results

On the core ranking quality metrics (Table 1), the proposed margin-adaptive estimator achieves the lowest ranking loss and highest AUROC across all four datasets and all tested judge models (e.g., RK=0.2707 / AUROC=0.7064 on AlpacaEval with Qwen2.5-72B, vs. 0.3370/0.6589 for the vanilla learned baseline). In the end-to-end Cascaded Selective Evaluation (Table 2, L→Q→O cascade at target agreement 1−α=0.85), CSE+Ours attains the highest guarantee success rate in every setting: 94.8% (AlpacaEval), 51.6% (HH-RLHF), 92.7% (Chatbot Arena), and 79.2% (TL;DR), while maintaining competitive or improved coverage (38.4%–56.4%). Heuristic Selection (GPT-4 with λ=1−α) achieves 0% success on AlpacaEval and HH-RLHF, underscoring the fragility of assuming perfect calibration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Jung et al.'s (2025) Simulated Annotators, the proposed method consistently wins on both ranking loss and AUROC across all datasets and judge models (e.g., RK drops from 0.3922 to 0.2707 on AlpacaEval with Qwen2.5-72B; AUROC rises from 0.6096 to 0.7064), and translates into a 2.1–13.7 percentage-point improvement in CSE guarantee success rate (e.g., 90.8%→94.8% on AlpacaEval, 46.9%→51.6% on HH-RLHF under L→Q→O). Compared to Verbalized Confidence (Bai et al., 2022), the gap is even larger: our method reduces ranking loss by roughly 0.07–0.10 and improves AUROC by 0.05–0.12 across settings. The vanilla learned-confidence baseline (same MLP architecture, standard ranking loss without margin adaptation) is the closest competitor but still trails our method by 0.05–0.07 in RK and 0.03–0.05 in AUROC, confirming that the margin-adaptive training procedure provides a meaningful gain over plain supervised ranking. Heuristic Selection (GPT-4 judge with λ=1−α) fails catastrophically on AlpacaEval and HH-RLHF (0% success), demonstrating that the monotonicity assumption underlying fixed-threshold calibration is unreliable in practice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Jung et al. (2025), who introduced simulated annotators as a confidence estimator and a fixed-sequence testing framework for LLM judges with provable human-agreement guarantees. The key departure is replacing the heuristic confidence signal with a learned parameterized estimator (a small MLP) trained via a margin-based ranking loss, and providing PAC-Bayesian generalization guarantees for the estimator's out-of-sample misranking probability. The theoretical machinery adapts the spectrally-normalized margin bound framework of Neyshabur et al. (2017) from discrete classification to continuous confidence scoring, yielding a margin-dependent loss-complexity trade-off that motivates the adaptive training procedure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete structural vulnerability in the Jung et al. framework: the monotonicity assumption between confidence and disagreement risk is empirically violated by all heuristic estimators tested, undermining the validity of fixed-sequence testing. By learning the confidence ordering with a generalization guarantee, the approach provides a principled path to restoring monotonicity and improving guarantee success rates. Limitations include the reliance on a small MLP (64-32-16) trained per dataset-judge pair, the looseness of the PAC-Bayesian bounds in practice, and the open question of whether the learned ranking transfers across tasks without retraining. The margin-adaptive optimizer is a practical contribution, but the performance gains over the vanilla (non-adaptive) learning baseline are modest, suggesting the core benefit comes from parameterization rather than the adaptive margin itself.
