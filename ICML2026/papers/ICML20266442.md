## Problem

Quality-assurance timing in annotation pipelines—whether validation runs before annotation (T0), after annotation (T1), or after review (T2)—is treated as an invisible design variable: a survey of 47 CVPR/NeurIPS/ICML papers (2022–2024) found only 4% explicitly report at which pipeline stage validation was applied. Existing validation methods (confident learning, IAA metrics, VLM-based verification) focus exclusively on what to validate and how to detect errors, leaving the question of when to validate unaddressed and preventing the field from assessing whether early-stage QA is more cost-effective than late-stage validation.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes ML pre-annotations (bounding boxes, segmentation masks, tracking identities) and human annotations from video annotation pipelines, with the available information set expanding at each trigger point: T0 sees only ML predictions and their confidence scores, T1 additionally sees human corrections enabling human-ML comparison and inter-annotator agreement, and T2 sees the full provenance chain including reviewer decisions. Outputs are stage-specific: T0 produces error flags and routing decisions before any human labor is invested, T1 produces annotation error detections and IAA metrics, and T2 produces reviewer decision audits and compliance documentation. Pilot evaluations use indoor-scene video annotations (bounding boxes and pose tags) processed on LabelStudio and CVAT at 16–30 fps.

## Method

The paper introduces a three-point QA trigger taxonomy (T0 pre-annotation, T1 post-annotation, T2 post-review) and a parametric error-propagation model showing that timing affects final error rates only when stage-specific detection rates differ (d_T0 ≠ d_T1 ≠ d_T2), while always affecting economics through the human effort invested before detection. A 14-configuration taxonomy combines trigger subsets with workflow options (single vs. dual annotator) to provide a shared vocabulary for QA design. For practical T0 deployment, the paper composes confident learning, anomaly detection, and coverage validation into a stack requiring no ground-truth labels or new model development. Two pilot studies on a production video annotation pipeline provide empirical grounding: T0 errors (195 TP / 422 FP, 31.6% precision) are homogeneous with all false positives traceable to three schema-level root causes fixable before annotation begins, whereas T1 errors (F1 35.7% on LabelStudio, 22.1% on CVAT) are heterogeneous, entangling ML residuals, annotator style variation, and scene complexity with no single fix addressing more than a fraction.

## Results

A structured survey of 47 annotation-quality papers from CVPR, NeurIPS, and ICML (2022–2024) finds that only 2 papers (4.3%) explicitly report the pipeline stage at which validation is applied. Pilot studies on indoor-scene video annotations show T₀ QA achieves 31.6% precision (195 TP / 422 FP) with all false positives traceable to three schema-level root causes, while T₁ QA yields F1 of 35.7% on LabelStudio (3,245 TP / 5,598 FP / 6,073 FN) and 22.1% on CVAT (2,849 TP / 10,351 FP / 9,688 FN) across 25 videos. The key structural finding is that T₀ errors are homogeneous (one ML model, three diagnosable causes) whereas T₁ errors are heterogeneous, mixing ML residuals, annotator style variation, and scene complexity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not benchmark a proposed method against named baselines; instead, its empirical contribution is a within-pipeline comparison of QA trigger points. At T₀ (pre-annotation, 5 videos), the multi-agent QA system achieves 31.6% precision with all 422 false positives attributable to three fixable schema-level causes (binary vs. three-level occlusion check: 107 FP; padding threshold miscalibration: 136 FP; VLM window misclassification: 17 FP), meaning a single agent configuration change before annotation begins eliminates the entire FP population. At T₁ (post-annotation, 25 videos), the same agent codebase deployed on LabelStudio achieves F1 of 35.7% while on CVAT it drops to 22.1%, with false positives arising from a heterogeneous mix of ML residuals, annotator style variation (0 vs. 149 FP on the same video across platforms), valid corrections misread as gaps, and pose cascades—no single fix addresses more than a fraction. The paper also contrasts its T₀/T₁ findings against the shift-left cost multipliers from software engineering (4–100×, Boehm 1981; Shull et al. 2002) as a motivating analogy rather than a quantitative baseline, explicitly noting the transfer remains untested.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not build on a single prior method but repurposes the shift-left principle from software engineering (Boehm, 1981; Shull et al., 2002) and manufacturing quality management (Crosby's 1-10-100 rule) to the annotation pipeline context, where no prior work has treated QA timing as a first-class design variable. It draws on existing annotation-quality techniques—confident learning, Dawid-Skene aggregation, inter-annotator agreement metrics, and VLM-based verification—as the *tools* that can be deployed at different trigger points, but its contribution is the temporal axis itself: a taxonomy of T0/T1/T2 trigger points and a parametric error-propagation model that clarifies when timing affects final error rates versus only economics. The paper carves new ground by showing, via a 47-paper survey, that this temporal dimension is systematically invisible in the literature (only 4% report it), and by providing pilot evidence that T0 errors are structurally homogeneous while T1 errors are heterogeneous.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If the community adopts the T0/T1/T2 reporting convention, it enables meta-analysis and controlled timing experiments that are currently impossible, and it gives practitioners a free optimization lever (reconfiguring when validators run requires no new model development). The parametric model's key clarification—that timing affects error rates only when stage-specific detection rates differ, and affects only economics when they are equal—gives researchers a precise hypothesis to test. However, the evidence remains preliminary: the pilot studies use different video sets across stages (preventing causal comparison), the shift-left transfer to annotation is unproven, and the cost multipliers in the configuration taxonomy are illustrative rather than measured. The central open question is whether controlled same-data experiments will confirm or refute the shift-left hypothesis for annotation, and whether the 4% reporting rate reflects a genuine blind spot or simply a reporting norm that is easy to change.
