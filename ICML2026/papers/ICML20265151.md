## Problem

Transferable adversarial attacks on Vision-Language Models suffer from poor cross-model generalization because existing methods treat VLM internal representations as entangled black boxes, causing perturbations to overfit surrogate-specific safety artifacts rather than shared safety-critical features. No prior work has identified which specific neurons encode safety defense versus transferable representations, leaving a mechanistic gap that prevents principled, circuit-level intervention.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265151/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a natural RGB image (sampled from the NIPS 2017 Adversarial Attacks dataset) paired with a harmful text query from AdvBench (520 instructions) as input, and produces an ℓ∞-bounded adversarial perturbation (ε=16/255) that, when added to the image, elicits unsafe responses from unseen target VLMs. The perturbation is optimized over 1,500 PGD steps with stochastic spatial augmentation (random rotation ±15°, scaling 0.8–1.2, translation up to 56 px), yielding visually imperceptible adversarial images that remain effective across architecturally diverse models.

## Method

SCIA operates in two phases. First, safety circuit identification uses linear probing on constructed defensive and transferable datasets to locate the most discriminative layer, then applies activation masking and ASR-variation analysis to extract two nearly disjoint neuron subsets: C_def (encoding model-specific safety features, ~0.1–0.5% of layer neurons) and C_trans (capturing model-agnostic representations shared across architectures, with Jaccard overlap <3% with C_def). Second, dual-objective steering optimization suppresses C_def activations by penalizing their projection onto the probe-derived safety direction (ReLU-gated), while amplifying C_trans activations by maximizing their L2 norm, effectively decoupling the adversarial pattern from surrogate-specific safety mechanisms. This is combined with a contrastive semantic steering loss that pulls output representations toward compliant-response prototypes and away from refusal prototypes, plus a spectral smoothness regularizer that penalizes high-frequency Fourier components to produce visually coherent perturbations. The full objective is optimized via projected gradient descent with expectation over random spatial transformations.

## Results

On AdvBench (520 harmful queries), SCIA achieves a 59.7% average Attack Success Rate (ASR) across six open-source VLMs (LLaVA-v1.6, Qwen-VL, Qwen-2.5-VL, InstructBlip, MiniGPT-4, Llama3.2), outperforming the strongest baseline UltraBreak (46.0%) by 13.7 percentage points. On three commercial VLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Flash, Claude-Sonnet-4.5), SCIA reaches 34.1% average ASR versus 23.6% for UltraBreak, with the largest gap on GPT-4o (46.8% vs. 31.4%). The perturbation budget is ε=16/255 (ℓ∞), and the identified safety circuits contain only 0.1–0.5% of neurons in the selected layer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SCIA outperforms all nine baselines on average transfer ASR across the six open-source VLMs. Against the strongest transfer-based competitor UltraBreak (46.0%), SCIA gains 13.7 points, with the largest per-model margins on LLaVA-v1.6 (78.3% vs. 55.8%) and MiniGPT-4 (46.8% vs. 38.7%). White-box attacks UMK and VAJM achieve high ASR on their surrogate (81.2% and 59.7% on LLaVA-v1.6) but collapse to 7–9% on all other models, confirming severe overfitting to surrogate-specific safety features. Among transfer-based baselines, FORCE (26.8%) and CoA (18.6%) are substantially outperformed, while AIP & TATM (15.3%) is the weakest transfer method tested. Black-box manual attacks (FigStep 22.1%, MM-SafetyBench 23.5%, Hades 20.9%) trail SCIA by roughly 36–39 points on average. On commercial models, SCIA maintains its lead over UltraBreak on all three targets (GPT-4o: 46.8% vs. 31.4%; Gemini-2.5-Flash: 36.2% vs. 28.7%; Claude-Sonnet-4.5: 19.3% vs. 10.8%), though absolute ASRs drop due to proprietary defense layers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in VLM adversarial attacks, which treats models as black boxes and relies on implicit regularization (semantic augmentation, loss landscape smoothing) to improve transferability. Instead, it repurposes linear probing from mechanistic interpretability to identify specific neuronal circuits encoding safety behavior, then uses circuit-level steering as the optimization signal. The adversarial optimization backbone (PGD-style projected gradient descent) and contrastive semantic steering are standard tools, but their combination with explicit circuit suppression/amplification objectives is novel. The paper positions itself as addressing a mechanistic gap: prior transfer-based methods (FORCE, UltraBreak, CoA) regularize the loss landscape without understanding *why* safety alignment blocks transfer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that VLM safety is concentrated in a small, disentangleable set of neurons (0.1–0.5% of a layer) with near-zero overlap with transferable features provides a concrete, actionable target for both attack and defense research. The defense analysis is particularly important: neither pixel-level preprocessing nor activation-level monitoring reliably detects circuit-targeted attacks (AUROC drops to 0.57–0.61), suggesting that current defensive paradigms are fundamentally mismatched to this threat model. Open questions include whether the identified circuits are truly architecture-agnostic or merely partially shared, how to design circuit-aware defenses that don't degrade model utility, and whether this approach extends to other safety properties (e.g., hallucination suppression, bias mitigation) beyond jailbreak resistance.
