## Problem

Generative Flow Networks (GFNs) offer a principled distribution-matching framework for LLM red-teaming, but the standard Trajectory Balance (TB) objective requires learning a partition function Z that is extremely difficult to estimate in the large, combinatorial token space, leading to training instability and mode collapse. This problem is exacerbated in red-teaming by dense but noisy toxicity classifier rewards that produce spurious learning signals, causing the attacker to converge on gibberish or a narrow set of repetitive attacks rather than discovering a broad spectrum of vulnerabilities.

## Input / Output

The attacker LLM (Qwen2.5-1.5B, SFT'd on Safety-Dataset and AdvBench) receives a fixed meta-prompt and autoregressively generates an attack prompt token-by-token. Each generated prompt is scored by a frozen victim LLM (Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct) and a toxic classifier (Llama-Guard-3-8B) in a single-turn, black-box setting with one victim query per attack. The output is a set of 1024 diverse adversarial prompts evaluated by Attack Success Rate (toxicity > 0.5) and Unique Attacks (greedy clustering at threshold 0.7 via sentence-transformer embeddings).

## Method

S-GFN replaces the TB objective's explicit Z estimation with Contrastive Trajectory Balance (CTB), a pairwise loss that contrasts the log-policy ratio against the log-reward ratio for two trajectories sampled from the same policy, provably recovering the identical optimal policy π(y) ∝ R(y) while eliminating the unstable Z parameter. To handle noisy rewards, Noisy Gradient Pruning (NGP) masks the CTB loss for trajectory pairs whose log-reward difference falls below a saliency threshold σ, preventing the optimizer from fitting to stochastic classifier variance. A Min-K Fluency Stabilizer (MKS) applies a hard penalty to samples whose k least-likely tokens under a frozen reference model fall below a threshold, blocking reward hacking on gibberish without distorting the target distribution as KL-regularization would. The gradient of CTB is shown to be equivalent to a variance-reduced policy gradient with an implicit peer-baseline (analogous to RLOO), and the N² pairwise comparisons are scalar operations that do not increase the number of neural network forward/backward passes, keeping computational cost comparable to standard TB.

## Results

On the target victim model (Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct), S-GFN achieves 134.00 unique attacks (UA) and 92.55% attack success rate (ASR), compared to GFN-TB's 17.67 UA and 93.75% ASR — a ~7.6× increase in diversity at comparable effectiveness. In cross-attack evaluation, S-GFN's safety fine-tuning defense reduces GFN's ASR to 0.03%, while S-GFN still achieves 22.53% ASR against GFN's defense, demonstrating superior offensive transferability and defensive coverage. On transfer attacks to unseen victim models (Gemma3-4B, Llama3.2-3B, Qwen3-4B, gpt-oss-20B), S-GFN maintains the highest UA (34–90) and ASR (0.18–15.04%) across all four targets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

S-GFN dominates the GFN-TB baseline on diversity (134 vs. 17.67 UA, ~7.6×) while matching its ASR (92.55% vs. 93.75%), and in cross-attack, S-GFN's defense neutralizes GFN (0.03% ASR) while S-GFN retains 22.53% ASR against GFN's defense. Against RL-based methods (PPO, PPO+Curiosity, DPO), S-GFN achieves comparable or higher ASR with dramatically more unique attacks (134 vs. 3–4 UA), exposing severe mode collapse in reward-maximization approaches. Jailbreak R1 (8B, CoT) achieves 75.33 UA but only 7.36% ASR on the target, far below S-GFN's 92.55%, and its defense is weak (S-GFN still scores 56.25% ASR against it). Rainbow Teaming reaches 66.11% ASR but only 33 UA due to its fixed style/topic matrix, and its cross-attack performance is poor (≤1.76% ASR against other defenses). In the distribution-matching ablations (molecular generation, noisy hypergrid), CTB converges faster than TB and SubTB and matches DB in quality while requiring far less computation, confirming the generality of the Z-free pairwise objective.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GFlowNet framework (Bengio et al., 2021) and its Trajectory Balance objective (Malkin et al., 2022) to LLM red-teaming, building directly on Lee et al. (2024), who first applied GFN-TB to adversarial prompt generation. The key departure is replacing the absolute TB loss—which requires a learnable partition function Z—with a pairwise Contrastive Trajectory Balance (CTB) objective, conceptually bridging GFN training with DPO-style preference optimization (Rafailov et al., 2023) and the Contrastive Balance loss from distributed GFN training (Da Silva et al., 2024). Two additional mechanisms—Noisy Gradient Pruning and the Min-K Fluency Stabilizer—address reward noise and OOD reward hacking specific to the red-teaming setting, going beyond the sparse-reward assumptions of prior GFN work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 7× diversity improvement (17→134 unique attacks) at comparable ASR (~92%) is practically significant for LLM safety, as broader vulnerability discovery enables more effective safety fine-tuning defenses. The cross-attack asymmetry—where S-GFN-derived defenses reduce GFN attack ASR to 0.03% while GFN-derived defenses leave S-GFN at 22.53%—suggests distribution-matching attackers find a fundamentally more comprehensive vulnerability surface than reward-maximizing ones. However, the method remains constrained by toxic classifier quality, assumes single-turn attacks, and the ablation revealing that CTB alone offers no advantage over simple mean/median baselines (its value lies specifically in enabling NGP) tempers the generality of the pairwise formulation. Open questions include multi-turn attack settings and whether the connectivity assumption for NGP's optimal policy guarantee holds in practice at scale.
