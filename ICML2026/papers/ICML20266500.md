## Problem

LLMs are routinely evaluated on human-designed psychological and educational tests (Big Five inventories, IQ tests, standardized exams), and benchmark scores are interpreted as measurements of human-like traits such as 'intelligence' or 'personality,' without empirical validation that the same latent construct is being measured. The paper argues this constitutes an ontological error: these instruments are population-calibrated measurement tools whose validity is tied to specific human populations, and the ML evaluation community lacks principled, AI-specific measurement frameworks grounded in psychometric theory.

## Input / Output

The empirical component takes LLM responses to BFI-2 personality items (GPT-4, temperature 1.0, N=100 repetitions per item, yielding 6000 item-response pairs) and human response data (Soto & John 2017, N=470) as input, with 18 zero-variance items excluded. It produces standardized factor loadings for five personality dimensions estimated via PCA with varimax rotation, which are compared across populations to test measurement invariance. The final output is a quantitative validity judgment: whether the human-calibrated measurement model (specifically its factor loadings) transfers to LLMs.

## Method

The paper provides an end-to-end framework for constructing and validating measurement instruments, grounded in structural equation modeling, confirmatory factor analysis, and measurement invariance theory (Meredith 1993). It formalizes the reflective measurement model as X_i = α_i + λ_i·ξ + ε_i, where factor loadings λ_i quantify the 'signal' of the latent trait in observed responses, and defines measurement invariance as F(X|ξ=y,g) = F(X|ξ=y) across populations. The empirical demonstration applies PCA with varimax rotation to BFI-2 responses from GPT-4 and humans, showing the open-mindedness loading drops from 0.60 (humans) to 0.25 (LLM), explaining only ~6% versus ~36% of variance, thus rejecting measurement invariance. The paper further introduces nomological networks and convergent/discriminant validity as criteria for evaluating benchmark claims of measuring latent traits, arguing that current evaluation designs are structurally too weak to place measurement hypotheses at genuine risk of refutation (following Popper and Meehl).

## Results

The paper's key empirical demonstration (Appendix A) compares PCA factor loadings for a single BFI-2 item ('I am someone who is inventive, finds clever ways to do things') between a human sample (N=470, Soto & John 2017) and GPT-4 (gpt-4-0613, N=100 repetitions, temperature 1.0). For humans, the Open-Mindedness loading is λ_O = 0.60, explaining ~36% of item-score variance, while all other trait loadings are below 0.22. For GPT-4, the Open-Mindedness loading drops to λ_O = 0.25 (~6% variance explained), and no loading across any of the five Big Five dimensions exceeds the conventional 0.3 threshold, indicating a failure of measurement invariance between humans and LLMs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper and does not propose a new method benchmarked against competing baselines. The central empirical comparison is between human-calibrated factor loadings (from Soto & John, 2017) and LLM-estimated loadings (GPT-4, gpt-4-0613) on the same BFI-2 item, using PCA with varimax rotation. The human model shows a clear dominant loading on Open-Mindedness (0.60) with negligible cross-loadings, as expected for a well-functioning psychometric item. The LLM model fails to replicate this structure: the target loading drops to 0.25 and no dimension reaches the 0.3 threshold, demonstrating that the item does not function as a valid indicator of the same latent construct in LLMs. The authors note that alternative experimental settings (persona prompting, random first-answer seeding) from prior work (Sühr et al., 2023) also failed to recover measurement invariance, reinforcing that the result is not an artifact of a single prompt design. The paper positions this as evidence that human-calibrated instruments cannot be transferred to LLMs without re-validation, rather than as a method that 'wins' or 'loses' against a baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies and extends a scattered body of critical work on LLM evaluation (McCoy et al. 2023, Schröder et al. 2025, Sühr et al. 2023, Jung et al. 2026) by grounding it in the formal apparatus of psychometric measurement theory—specifically measurement invariance (Meredith, 1993), structural equation models, and nomological networks (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955). While prior works each identify specific empirical failures (sensitivity to prompt changes, variance pattern mismatches, ecological validity violations), this paper provides the first unified theoretical account of what would constitute sufficient evidence for a measurement claim, and an end-to-end framework for constructing and validating AI-specific instruments. It also positions itself in dialogue with Hardt (2025) on benchmarking as a driver of progress, agreeing with the value of ordinal model rankings while rejecting the stronger claim that benchmark scores constitute valid measurements of latent traits.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reframes a widespread and consequential practice—interpreting LLM scores on human tests and benchmarks as evidence of human-like intelligence or personality—as an ontological error with real-world risks (false legal certification, liability-shifting through anthropomorphization). It opens a concrete research agenda for an ML-specific measurement science, raising foundational questions about the statistical unit of measurement, representative sampling of the LLM population, and the definition of ML-specific constructs. Key limitations include the thin empirical evidence (a single model, single item, N=100 in Appendix A) and the fact that the paper is a position statement without a fully specified new instrument; the practical path from 'stop using human tests' to 'here is a validated AI-specific test' remains largely untraversed.
