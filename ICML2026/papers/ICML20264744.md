## Problem

Under strict fixed evaluation budgets, noisy rank-based evolution strategies face a depth-fidelity trade-off: spending evaluations on intra-generation denoising (resampling, racing) to stabilize rankings directly reduces the number of distribution updates (generations) the optimizer can execute. The paper argues that in high-misranking regimes, this lost depth dominates the fidelity gains, and that integrating ranking uncertainty at the selection stage—rather than eliminating it at the evaluation stage—is more sample-efficient.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a fixed budget B of noisy scalar oracle calls y(x) = f(x) + ε(x) in d-dimensional continuous space, where every evaluation—including reevaluations, probing, and aggregation—counts against B. It produces a single recommended point x̂_B after the budget is exhausted, evaluated by simple regret f(x̂_B) − f*. The per-generation cost is C_t = λ + K_t with K_t ≤ K_max ≪ λ, so depth scales as B/λ rather than B/(kλ) as in k-fold resampling baselines.

## Method

The core contribution is Probabilistic Elite Membership (PEM), which replaces hard rank weights w(r_i) with conditional expected rank weights w*_i = E[w(r_i) | x_{1:λ}], a Rao-Blackwellization that preserves the conditional mean update while reducing its dispersion. PEM is estimated via Residual-Bootstrapped PEM (RB-PEM): each generation performs one base evaluation per candidate plus a small capped reevaluation set (K_t ≤ K_max) to calibrate a local noise model, then amortizes standardized residuals into a pooled bank reused across generations to simulate many bootstrap pseudo-rankings at zero additional evaluation cost. A probe-and-switch mechanism uses a 2λ-evaluation rank-disagreement statistic to dynamically route between RB-PEM and vanilla CMA-ES, preventing negative transfer in low-misranking regimes. Theoretically, under local strong convexity, conditional update dispersion incurs an unavoidable Jensen gap (Theorem 1), making dispersion reduction per oracle call the relevant fixed-budget efficiency metric.

## Results

On the COCO bbob-noisy suite (30 functions × 15 instances per dimension, B=200d), Probe-and-Switch achieves 85.6% win rate against 10-fold resampling, 79.3% against 5-fold resampling, and 77.4% against UH-CMA-ES, with all comparisons significant at p<0.001. Against vanilla CMA-ES, the win rate is 54.3% overall but strengthens with dimension (54.3% at d=10, 55.4% at d=20, 60.2% at d=40), consistent with misranking becoming more problematic in higher dimensions. On six external tasks (LQR, Breast Cancer, Digits, CartPole-HT, CartPole, Pendulum; 50 seeds each), Probe-and-Switch wins decisively against evaluation-stage baselines (e.g., 50/0 on LQR vs Res(10)) while tracking RB-PEM under high rank instability and defaulting to CMA-ES otherwise.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against evaluation-stage denoising baselines, Probe-and-Switch wins decisively: 85.6% vs 10-fold resampling, 79.3% vs 5-fold resampling, and 77.4% vs UH-CMA-ES, all at p<0.001, confirming that spending evaluations on per-candidate denoising collapses depth and hurts fixed-budget performance. Against vanilla CMA-ES, the advantage is moderate (54.3% overall) but grows with dimension (60.2% at d=40), reflecting that misranking becomes more severe in higher dimensions where the depth-fidelity trade-off is most acute. Against its own constituent RB-PEM (without the probe), Probe-and-Switch wins 58.1%, showing the switch mechanism reliably avoids negative transfer on low-misranking tasks (e.g., CartPole, Pendulum) where RB-PEM's smoothing overhead is counterproductive. On external tasks, the pattern is task-dependent: Probe-and-Switch dominates on high-misranking tasks like LQR (50/0 vs Res(10)) but is roughly tied with CMA-ES on low-misranking tasks like CartPole (19/30), validating the probe-and-switch design. LRA-CMA-ES (learning-rate adaptation) appears in the per-function ranking (Figure 4) and trails RB-PEM and Probe-and-Switch on the high-misranking cluster, though it is not included in the main win/loss table.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on CMA-ES (Hansen & Ostermeier, 2001) and departs from the dominant noisy-ES paradigm—uniform resampling, racing, UH-CMA-ES, and sequential sampling—which spends extra intra-generation evaluations to denoise rankings before applying the standard rank-based update (a 'fidelity-over-depth' stance). The key conceptual departure is intervening at the selection stage rather than the evaluation stage: probabilistic elite membership (PEM) replaces hard rank weights with conditional expected rank weights, a Rao-Blackwellization that preserves the conditional mean update while reducing its dispersion. The practical estimator (residual bootstrapping) borrows from nonparametric bootstrap methods (Efron & Tibshirani, 1993) and Wasserstein-distance diagnostics, while the probe-and-switch mechanism draws on ranking-and-selection decision theory. The authors explicitly position PEM as addressing a stage (selection-weight integration) they claim has remained unaddressed in the noisy single-objective ES literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The formalization of the depth-fidelity trade-off gives practitioners a concrete budget-accounting argument for when evaluation-stage denoising is counterproductive under strict budgets, which is common in RL policy search, simulation-based design, and HPO. The method is practical (capped per-generation overhead, amortized residual pool, near-unit evaluation cost) and the probe-and-switch policy prevents negative transfer in low-noise regimes. Limitations include the i.i.d.-noise-at-same-point assumption, conditional independence across candidates, and reliance on winsorization for heavy tails; extending to correlated, nonstationary, or covariate-dependent noise is identified as future work. Porting the depth-over-fidelity principle to other population-based optimizers (PSO, differential evolution) is also open.
