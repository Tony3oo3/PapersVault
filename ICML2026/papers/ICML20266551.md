## Problem

Current interpretability methods explain individual model outputs (feature attributions, circuit analysis) but cannot trace why an agentic system selected a particular plan, how multi-agent coordination failed, or where accountability lies across interacting components over time. As agentic systems plan, invoke tools, update memory, and coordinate across steps, failures emerge from cumulative system-level dynamics that evade model-centric audits until harm occurs.

## Input / Output

ATLIS consumes heterogeneous evidence at each layer: structured behavioral telemetry (action sequences, tool-call traces, latency profiles) for Layer 1; hidden activations and attention patterns from planning/memory/tool-selection steps for Layer 2; intermediate reasoning artifacts (high-level plans, step-wise traces, memory updates) for Layer 3; inter-agent communication transcripts, delegation graphs, and shared-memory access logs for Layer 4; and governance signals (safety objectives, policy constraints, alignment probes, red-teaming results) for Layer 5. Outputs are structured as a graduated stack—lightweight anomaly flags and drift alerts stream continuously from Layer 1, while deeper layers produce circuit diagrams, causal graphs, abstraction mappings, coordination influence maps, and compliance/risk reports only when triggered by detected anomalies or incident response, with final outputs including root-cause attribution reports and updated baselines that feed back into the lifecycle.

## Method

ATLIS (Agentic Trajectory and Layered Interpretability Stack) is a conceptual framework that decomposes agentic interpretability into five analytically distinct layers—Real-Time Behavioral Monitoring, Mechanistic Circuit Analysis, Abstraction-Level Bridging, Multi-Agent Analysis, and Safety/Alignment—each with tailored methods, computational overhead profiles, and architectural dependence levels. These layers are mapped across a five-stage deployment lifecycle (Pre-Deployment, Deployment, Runtime Operations, Incident Response, Post-incident Learning), with two feedback loops enabling co-design: a Monitoring Refinement Loop (Stages 3→4→5→3) that recalibrates detectors from runtime drift and incident outcomes, and a Safety & Alignment Revision Loop (Stages 1→2→3→1) that updates pre-deployment assumptions and safety objectives. The key operational principle is risk-aware graduated activation: Layer 1 runs continuously at low overhead, while computationally expensive layers (e.g., full circuit extraction in Layer 2) activate selectively only when anomalies or incidents are detected, balancing interpretability depth with deployment constraints. The framework formalizes completeness of explanation via traversal paths across layers and introduces a minimal sufficient depth principle that terminates analysis once the root cause is solvable, avoiding unnecessary invocation of deeper layers.

## Results

This is a position paper proposing the ATLIS framework rather than reporting empirical experiments, so there are no headline quantitative metrics or main results tables. The single quantitative data point cited as motivation is that automated failure attribution in multi-agent LLM systems achieves only 53% agent-level and 14% step-level accuracy (Zhang et al., 2025), underscoring the gap that system-level interpretability must fill. The paper's contribution is architectural: a five-layer interpretability stack (behavioral monitoring, mechanistic circuit analysis, abstraction bridging, multi-agent coordination, safety/alignment) mapped across a five-stage deployment lifecycle, with a qualitative palliative-care referral example illustrating how the layers would surface a cross-site drift that model-centric methods would miss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not empirically compare ATLIS against baselines; it is a position paper that situates its framework relative to existing approaches qualitatively. It contrasts model-centric interpretability (SHAP, Integrated Gradients, LIME, circuit analysis) as insufficient for agentic systems because they explain single predictions rather than multi-step trajectories and cross-component interactions. It acknowledges recent agentic-specific work—automated failure attribution (Zhang et al., 2025), traceability in linear pipelines (Barrak, 2025), and causal metrics for collective behavior (Itzhak Weinberg, 2025)—but argues these target individual diagnostic tasks rather than the end-to-end architectural shift ATLIS proposes. The paper also engages four alternative views (interpretability should follow capabilities, behavioral control suffices, holistic over layered analysis, computational impracticality) and responds to each, but no head-to-head empirical comparison is conducted.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper departs from the model-centric interpretability tradition (SHAP, LIME, circuit analysis, sparse autoencoders) by arguing that agentic systems require a fundamentally different explanatory scope: trajectory-level, multi-component, lifecycle-integrated analysis rather than single-prediction attribution. It synthesizes and reorganizes existing diagnostic tools—failure attribution (Zhang et al., 2025), pipeline traceability (Barrak, 2025), causal metrics for collective behavior (Itzhak Weinberg, 2025), and frontier-lab mechanistic methods (OpenAI, Anthropic, DeepMind)—into a unified five-layer, five-stage framework (ATLIS) that no prior work has proposed as an integrated architecture. The paper carves new ground not through a novel algorithm but through an architectural repositioning: treating interpretability as a co-designed, layered, lifecycle-embedded infrastructure rather than a post-hoc audit applied to a frozen model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As agentic systems enter high-stakes deployments (autonomous driving, municipal services, clinical referrals), the gap between their multi-step, multi-agent behavior and the single-model interpretability tools available to govern them is a concrete safety and accountability risk that this paper names and structures. Key open questions remain: scalable runtime monitoring that balances computational cost with diagnostic depth, standardized benchmarks and logging schemas for agentic interpretability, and formal causal frameworks for responsibility assignment across interacting components. The framework is explicitly conceptual rather than a complete implementation, and its assumption that interpretability is necessary for accountability (rather than behavioral control alone) remains contested, leaving the practical adoption path and regulatory integration as significant unresolved challenges.
