## Problem

Flow-based generative models lack per-sample, trajectory-level diagnostics to explain why individual outputs differ in quality; standard metrics like FID aggregate global statistics and are blind to the dynamics of individual sampling paths. This paper asks whether the kinetic effort accumulated along an ODE trajectory reveals intrinsic properties of the generated sample, and uncovers a paradox: while moderate energy correlates with better generation, extreme energy drives memorization rather than improved quality.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a trained flow matching velocity field v_θ(x,t) and initial Gaussian noise x_0 ~ N(0,I), integrating the ODE over t ∈ [0,1] with a discrete solver (Euler or midpoint, NFE = 100–250). Beyond the generated image, it outputs a scalar Kinetic Path Energy (KPE) per sample, computed as the time integral of ||v_θ(x(t),t)||² at each solver step with negligible overhead. KTS additionally applies a time-dependent gain η(t) to the velocity, splitting the trajectory at τ_split = 0.6 into a launch phase (velocity boosted) and a soft-landing phase (velocity damped), without any retraining.

## Method

The paper introduces Kinetic Path Energy (KPE), defined as E = ½∫₀¹ ||v_θ(x(t),t)||² dt, a zero-overhead per-sample diagnostic that quantifies accumulated kinetic effort along a generation trajectory. Two empirical findings establish KPE as a dual indicator: higher KPE correlates with stronger semantic fidelity (CLIP score/margin) and with termination in locally sparse regions of representation space, the latter formalized via an energy-density relation ‖û*(z,t)‖² ≍ −log p̂_t(z) under posterior dominance (Theorem 4.2). The paper then reveals a structural paradox: the closed-form empirical flow matching velocity contains a 1/(1−t) terminal singularity that causes energy blow-up and forces trajectories to collapse onto training atoms (Proposition 5.2), explaining why extreme energy induces memorization. To exploit the positive KPE–quality correlation while suppressing terminal spikes, Kinetic Trajectory Shaping (KTS) applies a training-free two-phase velocity modulation: a linear-decay Kinetic Launch (α₀ boost for t < 0.6) increases early motion to push samples toward semantically rich sparse regions, and an exponential Kinetic Soft Landing (β₀ damping for t ≥ 0.6) suppresses the terminal singularity, reducing memorization by 16% and improving FID on CelebA and ImageNet-256.

## Results

On CelebA 32×32, the balanced KTS configuration (α₀=β₀=0.01) reduces FID from 16.68 (FM baseline) to 14.35 and memorization from 37.34% to 31.22%, improving both quality and memorization simultaneously. On ImageNet-256, KTS with α₀=0.05 achieves the best FID of 11.59 (vs. 11.70 baseline), CLIP score of 24.34 (vs. 24.11), and precision of 0.731 (vs. 0.728), while the balanced setting (α₀=β₀=0.01) yields FID 11.63 with recall 0.653. Across all solver/NFE/schedule configurations on CelebA, KTS consistently reduces F_mem by 6–10 percentage points without per-setup retuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between vanilla Flow Matching (FM) and KTS, a training-free inference-time modification. On CelebA, the balanced KTS setting (α₀=β₀=0.01) wins on both FID (14.35 vs. 16.68) and memorization (31.22% vs. 37.34%) simultaneously, whereas pushing β₀ alone to 0.02 achieves the lowest memorization (19.36%) at a severe quality cost (FID 86.56), and pushing α₀ alone to 0.02 achieves the best FID (11.27) with negligible memorization change. On ImageNet-256, KTS matches or slightly improves FM across all metrics: the best FID (11.59 vs. 11.70) and CLIP (24.34 vs. 24.11) come from α₀=0.05, while β₀=0.05 maximizes recall (0.657 vs. 0.655). The paper also contrasts the closed-form Empirical Flow Matching (EFM) against Vanilla FM on synthetic 2D datasets, showing EFM reaches 1.3×–3.9× higher peak instantaneous power and produces 98% memorization on CelebA, confirming the terminal-singularity mechanism that KTS is designed to mitigate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the flow matching framework (Lipman et al., 2022) and the Benamou-Brenier kinetic formulation of optimal transport, but departs from distribution-level energy analyses by introducing Kinetic Path Energy (KPE) as a per-sample, path-level diagnostic computed along individual ODE trajectories. It leverages the closed-form empirical flow matching solution (Bertrand et al., 2025) to identify a structural 1/(1−t) terminal singularity as the mechanistic driver of memorization, complementing prior memorization analyses (Bonnaire et al., 2025; Yoon et al., 2023) that focused on implicit regularization or data geometry. The proposed Kinetic Trajectory Shaping (KTS) is positioned against training-free inference methods like classifier-free guidance and energy-based guidance, but differs by directly modulating the velocity field's magnitude over time rather than adjusting scores or endpoint objectives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The KPE diagnostic offers a zero-overhead, per-sample lens into generation quality that is inaccessible to endpoint-only metrics like FID, and the Goldilocks principle (moderate energy helps, extreme energy hurts) provides a clean conceptual framework for understanding the quality-memorization trade-off in flow-based models. However, the practical impact is currently limited: KTS experiments are conducted on small-scale CelebA (32×32, 1024 training images) with modest gains (FID 14.35 vs. 16.68; memorization 31.2% vs. 37.3%), and the theoretical energy-density relation (Theorem 4.2) requires a strong posterior dominance assumption that may not hold broadly. Open questions include whether the KPE-semantic-fidelity correlation and the terminal-singularity mechanism generalize to stochastic samplers, larger-scale models, and non-Gaussian bridge schedules, and whether KTS-style phase-aware velocity modulation can be integrated with other guidance strategies at scale.
