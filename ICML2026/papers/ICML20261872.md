## Problem

Under standard complexity conjectures (TC⁰ ⊊ NC¹), fixed-depth transformers cannot recognize even regular languages, let alone context-free languages (CFLs), which are the canonical formalism for the hierarchical syntax of natural language and code. While O(log N) looping layers were previously shown to suffice for regular language recognition, whether logarithmic looping is sufficient for CFL recognition remained an open question. This paper resolves that question affirmatively and further characterizes how natural grammar properties (unambiguity, linearity) reduce the computational resources required.

## Input / Output

The transformer receives an input string w of length N over alphabet Σ, augmented with BOS and EOS marker symbols, plus O(w(N)) padding symbols appended to the end (where w(N) ranges from N² to N⁶ depending on the grammar class). The CFG G is not given as input but is encoded in the transformer's fixed parameters (production rules, non-terminal set, start symbol). The output is a single binary accept/reject bit read from the EOS position's final hidden state via a linear classifier, with the string being accepted iff the start symbol S derives w.

## Method

The paper constructs a parallel parsing algorithm that manipulates items (i, A, j] and slashed items (i, A, j]/(p, B, q]) via two mutually recursive procedures (SPLIT and GAP branches) whose alternating structure (existential guesses over splits/productions, universal conjunctions over sub-derivations) yields O(log N) parallel depth by centroid decomposition of parse trees (Jordan's lemma). For general CFLs, this requires O(N⁶) padding symbols to store all candidate decompositions; for unambiguous CFLs, a bottom-up CKY-style algorithm with dependency graphs exploits the fact that unambiguity guarantees at most one directed path between items, reducing reachability to Boolean formula evaluation via Rytter's parallel pebble game in O(log² N) depth with O(N³) padding. The implementation on averaging hard-attention transformers uses a novel padding-symbol-to-structured-data encoding gadget (Lemma B.1) that lets each padding position decode its associated item indices via mixed-radix arithmetic, layer-norm hashes for cross-position equality checks, and looping layers to iterate the recursive/fixpoint computation. Empirically, looped and padded transformers outperform fixed-depth baselines on CFL recognition tasks, with the largest gains (8 points) on postfix BFVP, consistent with the theoretical prediction that log-depth unpadded transformers suffice for that language.

## Results

Across six CFLs, looped and padded transformers achieve the highest accuracy in 4 out of 6 cases, with the largest gains on harder languages. On BFVP (postfix), looped transformers (with or without padding) outperform fixed-depth by 8 points (75 vs. 67), matching the theoretical prediction of Cor. 4.1. On Palindrome, looped+pached transformers gain 4 points over fixed-depth (72 vs. 68), and on Dyck(2) they gain 4 points (87 vs. 83). On C-RASP languages (Balanced Counting, Dyck(1)), gains are modest (1–4 points), consistent with fixed-depth transformers already handling these well.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three transformer architectures: fixed-depth, O(log N)-looped (unpadded), and O(log N)-looped with O(N) padding. Looping alone wins on Balanced Counting (94 vs. 90) and ties for best on BFVP-postfix (75 vs. 67 fixed), confirming the theoretical result that postfix BFVP requires only log-depth with no padding. Looping plus padding wins on Dyck(2) (87 vs. 83), Palindrome (72 vs. 68), and BFVP-infix (81 vs. 80), showing that padding helps on non-C-RASP languages. On Dyck(1), all three variants perform nearly identically (85–86), indicating fixed-depth transformers already suffice for this C-RASP language. Notably, looping without padding slightly underperforms fixed-depth on BFVP-infix (78 vs. 80), suggesting that infix notation benefits from the extra space padding provides.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the transformer expressivity line initiated by Merrill & Sabharwal (2024a, 2025), which showed that log(N) looping layers suffice for regular language recognition and established the padding-symbol framework for scaling transformer space. It departs from prior work by tackling the open question of CFL recognition, synthesizing classical parallel parsing algorithms (Ruzzo 1980; Chytil et al. 1991; Rytter 1985) into a transformer-implementable construction. The key conceptual bridge is the equivalence between looped/padded transformers and classical parallel circuit models (Merrill & Sabharwal 2025; Svete et al. 2026), which lets the authors translate known parallel parsing time/space bounds directly into transformer depth/padding requirements.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper closes a long-standing gap in the theory of transformer expressivity by showing that logarithmic depth plus polynomial padding suffices for all CFLs, and that natural grammar constraints (unambiguity, linearity) dramatically reduce the space overhead. The O(N^6) padding for general CFLs is impractical, but the O(N^3) result for unambiguous CFLs is more tractable and aligns with empirical observations that transformers struggle with ambiguous grammars. Open questions include whether the padding bounds are tight, whether learnability (not just expressivity) holds for these constructions, and whether the modest empirical gains (4–8 points) will grow with better training procedures or larger models.
