## Problem

Adversarial examples can be reliably generated but remain poorly understood at a mechanistic level: existing explanations (high-dimensional geometry, non-robust patterns, decision boundary curvature) do not provide a representation-level account that predicts the structure of successful perturbations or explains why attacks transfer between models. This paper asks whether superposition—the encoding of more features than available dimensions as non-orthogonal directions—creates a specific, predictable interference mechanism that adversaries exploit.

## Input / Output

In the synthetic setting, the method takes sparse input vectors (d = kp dimensions, partitioned into k class blocks of p evidence components) with controlled correlation structures (uncorrelated, paired, or global) and produces adversarial perturbations via ℓ₂-PGD, measured against a closed-form ground-truth class. In the realistic setting, it takes RGB images (32×32 CIFAR, 96×96 STL-10, 64×64 TinyImageNet) fed to ViTs/ResNets/MLP-Mixers with an engineered linear bottleneck head (m < k dimensions), and produces: (i) an input-space attack profile a (perturbation projected onto per-class attack gradients), (ii) a latent-space interference profile r (class feature vectors projected onto the attack direction w_t − w_s), (iii) interference magnitude I and cross-seed consistency κ, and (iv) transfer success rates across independently trained models. The classification head weights are read directly as feature vectors, bypassing feature discovery methods.

## Method

The paper establishes that superposition geometry determines adversarial attacks through a mechanistic chain: input correlations constrain feature arrangements in the bottleneck, these arrangements fix interference patterns, and interference patterns dictate optimal perturbations via δ ∝ W_e^T(w_k − w_j) (Proposition 1, derived via Cauchy-Schwarz on the pairwise margin). In synthetic linear bottleneck networks (m < k < d), PGD-discovered attacks align with this theoretical optimum at cosine similarity > 0.92, and transfer rates scale from 5% to 98% as feature geometry converges across seeds (Proposition 2 shows orthogonal invariance of optimal attacks). The framework is validated on ViTs trained from scratch on four image datasets with bottleneck ratios m/k ∈ {0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0}: the Pearson correlation between latent interference profile r and input-space profile a reaches 0.89 at m/k = 0.2 and drops to 0.16 at m/k = 1.0, confirming that tighter superposition amplifies the geometric signature. The paper further shows that even without explicit bottlenecks, weight decay induces effective low-rank representations (m̄ ≪ 384) that reproduce the same interference-driven vulnerability, and that the mechanism generalises across attack families (PGD, SPSA, Square Attack) and architectures (ViT, ResNet-50, MLP-Mixer).

## Results

In the synthetic setting, PGD-discovered perturbations align with theoretically optimal perturbations derived from superposition geometry with cosine similarity of 0.92–0.97 across configurations (k=6–90, m=2–30), far exceeding a random baseline of ~0.00 (p<10⁻⁵). On ViT classifiers with engineered bottlenecks, the correlation between latent interference profiles and input-space attack profiles reaches ρ̄=0.89 on CIFAR-10 (m/k=0.2) and 0.61 on CIFAR-100 (m/k=0.2), dropping monotonically as the bottleneck widens (e.g., 0.16 on CIFAR-10 at m/k=1.0). Attack transferability scales with geometric similarity: 98%±1% under global input correlations, 33%±18% under paired correlations, and only 5%±5% under uncorrelated data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper tests whether the interference-geometry prediction (Proposition 1) holds beyond first-order gradient attacks by comparing PGD-ℓ₂ against two non-gradient baselines: SPSA (zeroth-order, Uesato et al. 2018) and Square Attack (score-based, Andriushchenko et al. 2020). At the smallest configuration (k=6, m=2), all three methods align closely with the theoretical optimum (PGD: 0.93, SPSA: 0.92, Square: 0.99). However, alignment degrades with increasing superposition pressure: at (k=90, m=30), PGD maintains 0.97 while SPSA drops to 0.79 and Square Attack falls to 0.55. PGD consistently achieves the highest or near-highest alignment across all three configurations, confirming that the interference mechanism is most cleanly exploited by gradient-based methods but is not exclusive to them. The degradation of zeroth-order and score-based attacks at larger scale suggests they struggle to resolve the precise interference geometry that first-order methods capture directly.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate research threads: mechanistic interpretability (specifically superposition, following Elhage et al. 2022) and adversarial robustness. It builds on the Linear Representation Hypothesis (Park et al., 2024) to formalize how non-orthogonal feature directions in a bottleneck create exploitable interference, and departs from prior adversarial explanations (decision boundary curvature, high-dimensional geometry, non-robust features) by providing a representation-level mechanism that predicts specific perturbation structure. The paper explicitly positions itself as complementary to the low-rank vulnerability account (Barsbey et al. 2026; Savostianova et al. 2023), refining it by showing that the specific Gram structure of class feature vectors—not merely the rank—determines which perturbations succeed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a testable, geometric account of why adversarial examples exist and transfer, with the practical implication that standard weight-decay regularization may inadvertently amplify vulnerability by reducing effective representation dimension. Key limitations include that superposition is shown sufficient but not necessary for vulnerability, the primary mechanistic evidence comes from synthetic and bottleneck-constrained settings, and no defense is proposed. Open questions include how adversarial training reshapes feature geometry, whether the mechanism dominates in deep networks without explicit bottlenecks, and how to extend the analysis to unlabelled intermediate-layer features via SAEs.
