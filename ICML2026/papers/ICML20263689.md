## Problem

While prior work has established that LLM representations align with brain activity during language comprehension, it remains unclear which specific input words drive this alignment and whether they overlap with those driving next-word prediction (NWP). Existing approaches rely on coarse perturbations (word reordering, representation ablation) that cannot identify word-level contributions, leaving the contentious BA–NWP relationship unresolved at fine granularity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263689/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes naturalistic text (5,176 words from Harry Potter Ch. 9 or 10 Moth Radio Hour stories), fMRI voxel time series sampled at TR=2s, and frozen pretrained LLMs (1–2B parameters across transformer, SSM, and hybrid architectures). For each word, a 640-word context is fed to the LLM; token embeddings are averaged to word level, downsampled to TR level, and four consecutive TRs are concatenated to model hemodynamic delay. Outputs are word-level attribution scores for both BA and NWP, from which the authors derive IoU overlap curves, attribution-spread profiles (words needed to reach cumulative threshold t%), positional bias metrics (center of mass, weighted positional standard deviation), and per-category linguistic feature importance (semantic, syntactic, discourse).

## Method

The key technical contribution is an end-to-end differentiable attribution pipeline for brain alignment. A ridge-regularized linear encoding model maps LLM-derived TR-level embeddings to voxel activity; its learned weight matrix is decomposed into four per-TR linear projection layers that enable gradient flow from the MSE loss back through the frozen LLM to individual input tokens. For NWP, the same extended context is used with teacher forcing, and cross-entropy over the next word's sub-word tokens serves as the attribution target. Token-level attributions (Integrated Gradients, Gradient×Input, or SmoothGrad via Captum) are summed to word level, preserving Integrated Gradients' completeness property. The two tasks are then compared through IoU of top-attributed word sets, attribution spread across layers, positional distributions (CoM and spread), and the proportion of top-attributed words falling into semantic, syntactic, and discourse annotation categories.

## Results

Across two naturalistic fMRI datasets (Harry Potter, Moth Radio Hour) and five LLMs (1–2B parameters), BA and NWP rely on largely distinct word subsets: IoU between top-attributed word sets is only ≈0.1–0.2 at t≤10% (minimal overlap), rising to >0.8 only at t=98%, while remaining 1.5–2× above the random baseline throughout. Attribution spread shows an opposing layerwise trend: NWP requires significantly more unique words at early layers (local syntactic cues), whereas BA exhibits broader spread at middle and late layers (semantic/discourse integration). At t=60%, NWP marks ≈13% of annotated feature words as important (≈5% uniquely, ≈8% jointly) versus ≈28% for BA, with NWP showing a clear syntactic bias and BA emphasizing semantic and discourse-level content. Positionally, NWP exhibits a sharp bimodal primacy–recency profile, while BA shows a broader, more pronounced recency peak in 6 of 7 models, with weaker primacy effects.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between BA attributions and NWP attributions computed from the same frozen LLM representations, rather than a comparison against external baseline methods. Against a random-word-set baseline, the BA–NWP IoU is significantly above chance (AUC-IoU confirmed via FDR-corrected Wilcoxon signed-rank tests), indicating systematic but limited overlap. Against the NWP reference, BA consistently diverges: it draws on more words (higher spread at middle/late layers), prioritizes semantic and discourse features over syntax, and shows a broader recency profile with weaker primacy. These BA-vs-NWP differences are robust across three attribution methods (Integrated Gradients, Gradient×Input, SmoothGrad), two datasets, and all five architectures (three Transformers, one SSM, one hybrid), with the only notable exception being Llama3.2-1B's stimulus-dependent oscillatory pattern that disappears under SmoothGrad and on the Moth Radio Hour dataset. The paper does not benchmark its attribution pipeline against competing explainability methods for brain-LLM alignment; instead it positions itself as complementary to prior perturbation-based approaches (e.g., Toneva et al. 2022, Merlin & Toneva 2024) by providing word-level granularity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes three established attribution methods—Integrated Gradients, Gradient × Input, and SmoothGrad—within the brain-LLM alignment (BA) setting, where prior work relied on coarser perturbations (word reordering, representation ablation) rather than end-to-end input attribution. It departs from Russo et al. (2022) and Rahimi et al. (2025), who used NWP attributions as *inputs* to brain-encoding models, by instead predicting brain activity from LLM layer embeddings and then *explaining* that prediction via attribution. The comparative framing—contrasting BA attributions against NWP attributions from the same frozen models—extends the attribution literature from a single-task diagnostic to a cross-task interpretability tool, building on the completeness property of Integrated Gradients for principled word-level aggregation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first word-level, attribution-based evidence that BA and NWP draw on functionally distinct subsets of input words, with BA favoring semantic/discourse content and broader recency integration while NWP exhibits sharp primacy-recency bimodality and syntactic bias. This resolves a long-standing debate in the brain-LLM alignment literature with fine-grained, mechanism-level detail that perturbation studies could not provide. Key limitations include reliance on only two narrative fMRI datasets, coarse discourse annotations, and frozen (non-brain-tuned) models, leaving open whether the findings generalize to non-narrative, multilingual, or multimodal stimuli and to models explicitly optimized for brain alignment. The attribution pipeline itself is a reusable tool for probing cognitive relevance of model predictions across diverse language tasks.
