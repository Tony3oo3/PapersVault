## Problem

Despite growing awareness of benchmark quality issues (data contamination, missing code coverage, lack of reproducibility), the absolute number of flawed code benchmarks has continued to rise, with 2025 alone nearly matching the cumulative count of coverage-ignoring benchmarks from the prior decade. The paper argues that rigor in construction, reliability in evaluation, and reproducibility in release must be treated as first-class objectives rather than informal best-effort practices, and that a lag between awareness and action persists across the community.

## Input / Output

The study consumes 672 code benchmark papers (2014–2025) along with their publicly released artifacts, homepages, and code repositories, annotated by two primary annotators with conflict resolution by two additional annotators (Cohen's κ = 0.76–0.92). It also incorporates questionnaire responses from 49 researchers (42.6% AI, 57.14% SE) who had published at least one paper. The primary output is HOW2BENCH, a 55-criteria checklist organized across five lifecycle phases (Design, Construction, Evaluation, Analysis, Release), where each criterion carries a three-level priority tag (★★★ Highly important, ★★ Important, ★ Optional) and a checkbox for practical use. Secondary outputs include longitudinal statistics on benchmark quality trends and a quantified awareness-gap analysis.

## Method

The core contribution is HOW2BENCH, constructed through a four-step process: (1) guideline construction via literature review and iterative stakeholder interviews with model developers and benchmark builders, yielding 55 actionable criteria each annotated with a threat-level priority; (2) large-scale literature profiling of 672 benchmarks using snowballing, with manual annotation following explicit keyword-based criteria for each attribute (e.g., 'deduplication', 'data contamination', 'coverage'); (3) a focused case study of 30 representative benchmarks (top-5 cited + latest per top-5 coding tasks) analyzed against all 55 criteria; and (4) a human study with 49 participants to validate the checklist's importance and expose awareness gaps. The lifecycle framework divides benchmark development into five phases—Design, Construction, Evaluation, Analysis, and Release—each with its own subset of criteria, making the guideline applicable both as a pre-release checklist for new benchmarks and a post-release audit tool for existing ones.

## Results

Across 672 code benchmarks (2014–2025), 46% did not undergo any quality assurance check, 79.8% did not consider data contamination, and 67.1% did not deduplicate data points. On the evaluation side, 85.0% did not ensure reliable judgement (e.g., reporting code coverage), and 66.1% conducted only a single-pass evaluation without repeating experiments. For reproducibility, 38.2% omitted essential information such as prompts, 80.0% did not provide experiment logs, and 16.9% were not fully open-sourced. A human study of 49 researchers revealed awareness gaps: 16% were unaware of the need for data denoising, and over 40% considered recording hardware/software environments unimportant for reproducibility.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position and survey paper rather than a method paper, so it does not propose a new method to be benchmarked against baselines. Instead, it surveys 672 existing code benchmarks and quantifies systemic quality gaps across the full benchmark lifecycle (design, construction, evaluation, analysis, release). The paper positions HOW2BENCH (a 55-criteria checklist) as a normative guideline and contrasts it with prior efforts such as BetterBench (Reuel et al., 2024), noting differences in scope (code-specific vs. general), lifecycle coverage (evaluation/analysis/release vs. retirement), and scale (672 vs. 24 benchmarks). The key 'comparison' is longitudinal: the paper shows that despite growing awareness (e.g., manual QA checks doubled from 84 in 2024 to 193 in 2025), the absolute number of flawed benchmarks continues to rise due to rapid benchmark production, with 2025 alone contributing 123 benchmarks that ignore code coverage—nearly matching the cumulative total from 2014–2024.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on a growing but fragmented body of work identifying individual quality issues in code benchmarks—code coverage gaps (Liu et al., 2023b), data contamination (Cao et al., 2024b), and general guidelines (Wu et al., 2025)—but departs from them by providing a unified, lifecycle-spanning framework (HOW2BENCH) with 55 prioritized criteria rather than addressing isolated concerns. It distinguishes itself from BetterBench (Reuel et al., 2024), which scored 24 general AI benchmarks against 46 criteria, by focusing exclusively on code benchmarks at a much larger scale (672 benchmarks) and by being prescriptive (guiding future development) rather than purely evaluative (ranking existing ones). The paper also positions itself against surveys of LLM evaluation (Chang et al., 2024; Koohestani et al., 2025a) by adding a normative, actionable dimension and a longitudinal trend analysis spanning 2014–2025.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that awareness of benchmark quality has grown but actual practice has not kept pace, with 2025 alone producing nearly as many code-coverage-ignoring benchmarks as the prior decade combined—challenges the assumption that the community is self-correcting. The human study revealing that 40% of researchers consider recording experimental environments unimportant, even when they have done so themselves, suggests the gap is partly epistemic rather than purely resource-driven. Open questions include whether a 55-item checklist can realistically be adopted under publication timelines, whether venues will incorporate such criteria into review, and whether the rigor-efficiency trade-off the paper acknowledges will ultimately be resolved by tooling or by shifting incentive structures. As a position paper, its impact hinges on community uptake of HOW2BENCH as a shared reference standard.
