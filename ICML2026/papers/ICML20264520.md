## Problem

In extreme multi-label classification with millions of labels, the output layer is a dominant memory and compute bottleneck. Existing sparse training methods such as per-label fixed fan-in reduce arithmetic complexity but remain fundamentally memory-bound due to irregular, uncoalesced memory accesses that prevent effective Tensor Core utilization, and they depend on auxiliary objectives for training stability in long-tailed regimes—objectives that are not universally available and can introduce conflicting gradients.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264520/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes text instances (product titles, research papers) as input, encodes them via a shared transformer encoder into a feature vector h ∈ R^H, and produces multi-label scores over up to 8.6M labels. The output layer is decomposed into a small dense head over the top 2–5% most frequent labels and a group-shared sparse tail over the remainder, with separate lightweight feature projections (P_head, P_tail) applied before each branch. Training is end-to-end in BF16 precision with binary cross-entropy loss, and evaluation uses both Precision@k and propensity-scored Precision@k to capture tail-label performance.

## Method

HASTE introduces group-shared fixed fan-in sparsity, where labels are partitioned into semantically coherent groups (via mini-batch spherical k-means on label embeddings) and each group shares a single randomly selected fan-in index set of size F, while individual labels retain independent weight vectors of dimension F. This structure enables a 'gather-once and dense MMA' execution pattern in custom CUDA kernels: features are gathered once per group into shared memory, then a dense GEMM is performed on the group tile, directly leveraging Tensor Core primitives and reducing index memory overhead by a factor of G relative to per-label fixed fan-in. For training stability in long-tailed settings, HASTE replaces auxiliary objectives with a head-tail decomposition—frequent labels are modeled by a small dense classifier providing a consistent gradient signal to the encoder, while the vast tail uses the sparse layer. A Split-K strategy parallelizes the backward-feature reduction over the label dimension, and periodic group-level rewiring (pruning the lowest-magnitude slots globally and regrowing new fan-in locations) maintains dynamic sparsity throughout training.

## Results

On Amazon-3M, HASTE achieves P@1 = 52.5, P@3 = 49.5, P@5 = 47.4 with only 5.67 GiB peak training memory (vs. 39.7 GiB for the dense baseline), completing an epoch in 21:39 (vs. 29:58 for dense). On the largest benchmark, LF-Paper2Keywords-8.6M, HASTE reaches P@1 = 47.5, P@3 = 36.2, P@5 = 29.8 using 12.5 GiB (vs. 105.64 GiB for dense) and 35:03 epoch time (vs. 61:23). On Amazon-670K, HASTE attains P@1 = 48.1 with 2.1 GiB memory and 5:39 epoch time, outperforming SPARTEX (P@1 = 47.1, 3.7 GiB, 8:01) at a higher sparsity ratio (92% vs. 83%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HASTE outperforms SPARTEX (the closest prior sparse baseline) on every dataset in both accuracy and efficiency: on Amazon-3M it gains +2.3 P@1 (52.5 vs. 50.2) while using 58% less memory (5.67 vs. 13.5 GiB) and training 4× faster (21:39 vs. 86:38); on LF-Paper2Keywords-8.6M the P@1 gap widens to +6.8 (47.5 vs. 40.7) with 32% less memory. Against the dense baseline, HASTE nearly matches on Amazon-3M (52.5 vs. 52.6 P@1) and actually surpasses it on LF-Paper2Keywords-8.6M (47.5 vs. 43.6 P@1), while using 86% less memory. Block sparsity (BLAST) is dramatically outperformed, especially at scale: on Amazon-3M, HASTE achieves 52.5 P@1 versus 27.9 for block sparsity, a 24.6-point gap. Against quantization-based ELMO, HASTE is slightly below on Amazon-3M (52.5 vs. 53.4 P@1 for ELMO-BF16) but clearly ahead on LF-Paper2Keywords-8.6M (47.5 vs. 45.4 P@1) with comparable memory. Among additional sparse baselines (RIGL, VENOM, SPLAT in Table 9), HASTE leads by 2–7 P@1 points across all tested datasets, with the largest margin over VENOM (e.g., 52.5 vs. 47.0 P@1 on Amazon-3M).

## Relation to Prior Work

HASTE extends the fixed fan-in sparsity paradigm introduced for XMC by Schultheis & Babbar (2023) and operationalized end-to-end by SPARTEX (Ullah et al., 2024), replacing per-label independent fan-in with group-shared fan-in across semantically related labels. This positions the method between the expressiveness of per-label fixed fan-in and the hardware regularity of block sparsity (Okanovic et al., 2025), while the head-tail decomposition serves as a data-driven alternative to the auxiliary gradient pathway used in SPARTEX. The custom CUDA kernels draw on Tensor Core MMA and Split-K reduction strategies, connecting the XMC-specific sparsity design to broader hardware-aware sparse training literature (Lasby et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2021).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is demonstrating that structured sparsity in the output layer can convert FLOPs reductions into proportional wall-clock speedups (up to 25× in backward) rather than remaining memory-bound, which is a common failure mode for sparse training on GPUs. The head-tail split offers a simpler, auxiliary-free mechanism for stabilizing long-tailed training that may generalize to other imbalanced-output settings. Open questions include whether group-shared sparsity extends beyond XMC to other large-output tasks (e.g., generative language modeling with large vocabularies), how the approach scales to multi-GPU training, and whether combining group-shared sparsity with low-precision quantization (noted as complementary but not tested) yields further gains.
