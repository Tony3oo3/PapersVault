## Problem

Existing loss prediction models for LLM pre-training, most notably Chinchilla's, are restricted to two inputs (model size N and total tokens D) and cannot account for batch size variation, which is a first-order pre-training decision. As the number of configuration variables grows, heuristic scaling laws become increasingly complex, ambiguous in their interactions, and difficult to evaluate rigorously. The paper asks whether a mechanistic loss model can predict test loss across joint variations in N, B, and K while maintaining strong extrapolation to compute budgets up to 1000× beyond the training data.

## Input / Output

The model takes three scalar inputs — model size N, batch size B, and number of weight updates K — and outputs a single predicted pre-training test loss. Unlike Chinchilla, which treats total tokens D = B·K·seq_len as a single input and is blind to how tokens are partitioned into batches, NQS explicitly separates B from K, enabling loss predictions under batch size variation at fixed token budgets. The fitted parameter vector can additionally be used to search over (N, B, K) grids under compound resource constraints (compute FLOPs, wall-clock time, GPU memory) to identify the configuration minimizing predicted loss.

## Method

NQS models LLM test loss as the expected risk of SGD on a quadratic loss surface whose Hessian eigenvalues follow a power law λ_n = Q/n^q, with mini-batch noise ξ_n ~ N(0, R/(n^r · B)) carrying its own spectral exponent r for added flexibility. The closed-form solution decomposes loss into four terms: irreducible error, approximation error from untrained eigen-directions beyond N, bias from exponential decay of initial error over K steps, and variance from accumulated SGD noise scaled by 1/B. A LayerNorm correction is applied by dynamically rescaling the learning rate as γ_k ∝ 1/‖w^(k)‖², which the authors show is essential for accurate small-batch predictions. The seven parameters θ = (p, P, q, Q, r, R, E_irr) are fitted via Adam over 1000 parallelized random initializations, with the infinite sums over N and K evaluated in O(1) time using Euler-Maclaurin quadrature and geometric series summation, making inference comparable in speed to Chinchilla.

## Results

On the primary Pythia+OWT2 dataset with a ×1024 compute gap, NQS achieves a holdout Huber loss of 2.5 (×10⁻⁵) on IsoFLOPs and 5.6 on the B-K Plane, compared to Chinchilla's 9.0 and 9.8 respectively. On the Llama+LM1B dataset (×6 compute gap), NQS scores 2.9 vs. Chinchilla's 3.7 on IsoFLOPs and 8.2 vs. 8.7 on the B-K Plane. Notably, NQS has a higher training loss (2.8 vs. 0.8) yet substantially lower holdout loss, indicating better generalization rather than overfitting. Under MSE evaluation (App. C.3), the gap widens further: on the B-K Plane at ×1024 gap, NQS achieves 3.5 vs. Chinchilla's 27.4 (×10⁻³).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is Chinchilla Method 3 (Hoffmann et al., 2022), the standard parametric loss model L(N,D) = E_irr + P/N^(p-1) + Q/D^(p/q - 1/q). NQS wins on every holdout setting tested: on the Pythia+OWT2 IsoFLOPs holdout at ×1024 compute gap, NQS reduces the Huber loss from 9.0 to 2.5 (a 3.6× improvement), and on the B-K Plane from 9.8 to 5.6 (1.75×). The advantage is most pronounced on the B-K Plane (batch-size variations), where Chinchilla is structurally unable to model the effect of B given a fixed D, while NQS explicitly includes B as an input. On the smaller Llama+LM1B dataset (×6 gap), NQS still leads on IsoFLOPs (2.9 vs. 3.7) and narrowly on B-K Plane (8.2 vs. 8.7). Under MSE evaluation the B-K Plane gap is even more dramatic (3.5 vs. 27.4 at ×1024), confirming that Chinchilla's errors on batch-size variations are not merely large but qualitatively wrong. NQS's higher training loss (2.8 vs. 0.8) rules out overfitting as an explanation for its holdout advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

The NQS synthesizes three theoretical threads: the loss decomposition from linear regression scaling models (Bordelon et al., 2024; Maloney et al., 2022), the stochastic optimization dynamics of the Noisy Quadratic Model (Zhang et al., 2019), and the effective learning rate reduction from LayerNorm (van Laarhoven, 2017). Unlike Chinchilla's purely empirical power-law fit over (N, D), NQS is derived mechanistically from SGD on a quadratic loss surface, which naturally introduces batch size B and update count K as first-class variables and captures the variance term that Chinchilla omits. The paper positions itself as a drop-in replacement for Chinchilla's loss model that is equally lightweight (O(1) evaluation) but extends the input space from two to three pre-training variables.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NQS provides the first practical loss-prediction tool that can optimize (N, B, K) jointly under compound resource constraints (time, memory, compute), a capability Chinchilla's (N, D) formulation cannot support. The demonstrated 1000× compute extrapolation robustness is practically significant for planning frontier-scale training runs from smaller-scale experiments. Open questions remain: the model does not yet capture learning-rate–batch-size interactions, multi-epoch training, or data mixtures, and the LayerNorm parameter s is selected in a separate step rather than jointly optimized. The paper's broader argument—that mechanistic loss models should replace increasingly complex heuristic scaling laws—is well-motivated but will require extension to cover the full space of modern pre-training configurations.
