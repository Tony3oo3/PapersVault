## Problem

Existing model fusion methods (OTFusion, Git Re-Basin) struggle in zero-shot settings under non-IID or sharded data distributions, often collapsing to near-random accuracy, and are typically restricted to specific architectures or pairwise fusion. They also treat each network level in isolation and assign equal importance to all neurons, ignoring that neurons contribute unequally to predictions. This paper addresses these gaps by introducing a neuron-centric fusion framework that works across architectures and data regimes.

## Input / Output

The method takes a collection of pretrained base models (any architecture modularizable as a DAG of levels, validated on VGGs, ResNets, and ViTs), per-neuron importance scores (Uniform, Conductance, or DeepLIFT, computed independently on each model's own training data), and a small fusion dataset (as few as 400 samples). It produces a single fused neural network with the same architecture as the base models, supporting fusing more than two models and models of different widths (KF variant) or equal widths with one-to-one matching (HF variant). The fused model requires no further retraining in zero-shot settings, though optional fine-tuning can push accuracy closer to ensemble levels.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage, bottom-up algorithm that decomposes the representation cost (Eq. 1) into a grouping error and an approximation error via an auxiliary target vector (Theorem 1). At each level, the algorithm first clusters the concatenated base-model neuron activations using either Hungarian matching (equal-size, one-to-one) or importance-weighted K-means (general case), then computes importance-weighted centroids as targets. The fused model's weights at that level are then fitted to reproduce these centroids via closed-form weighted least squares (linear levels) or SGD (general differentiable levels), with previous levels kept frozen. Incorporating neuron importance scores biases both the clustering assignments and the centroid computation toward salient features, which the paper shows substantially improves zero-shot accuracy, especially under heterogeneous data. Theoretical guarantees include optimality for Hungarian Fusion and a (9+ε)-approximation bound for K-means Fusion (Theorem 2).

## Results

In the zero-shot sharded setting on CIFAR-100 with ViTs, KF-Gradient achieves 54.4% (2-way), 41.4% (4-way), and 34.7% (6-way) accuracy, while Transformer OTFusion collapses to near-random performance (1.0–4.4%). On the BloodMNIST sharded task, KF-Linear reaches 91.0% test accuracy, surpassing the ensemble (88.4%) and all prior fusion methods. In the full-dataset ViT setting on CIFAR-100, KF-Gradient attains 63.0% zero-shot accuracy (vs. 4.3% for Transformer OTFusion) and 75.4% after fine-tuning, approaching the ensemble's 75.7%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

KF-Gradient dramatically outperforms Transformer OTFusion (Imfeld et al., 2024) in every setting: on CIFAR-100 sharded 2-way it achieves 54.4% vs. 2.3–4.4%, and on the full-dataset ViT setting it reaches 63.0% zero-shot vs. 4.3%. Against Git Re-Basin (Ainsworth et al., 2023), the proposed methods win by large margins on heterogeneous data—e.g., 80.9% vs. 63.8% on VGG CIFAR-10 sharded 2-way, and 91.0% vs. 57.9% on BloodMNIST. On ImageNet-1k, KF-Gradient (25k samples) achieves 58.9% joint accuracy, substantially exceeding ZipIt! (Stoica et al., 2024) at 37.7% with the same sample budget. Against Knowledge Distillation (Hinton et al., 2015), the gap is most pronounced in data-limited regimes: KF-Gradient compresses ResNet-34→18 to 70.6% accuracy while KD reaches only 45.9% with the same 5k fusion samples. OTFusion (Singh & Jaggi, 2020) similarly fails in non-IID and sharded settings, dropping to near-random accuracy (10–28%) where KF-Gradient maintains 34–81%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neuron-alignment framework established by OTFusion (Singh & Jaggi, 2020) and Git Re-Basin (Ainsworth et al., 2023), both of which minimize L2 distance between neuron activations to find permutation matrices for weight averaging. The key conceptual departure is a two-stage decomposition—first clustering base-model neurons into groups (via Hungarian matching or K-means), then training the fused model's sub-networks to approximate importance-weighted cluster centroids—rather than a single alignment-and-average step. This reframing enables handling of heterogeneous architectures, different widths/depths, and more than two models, while the incorporation of neuron attribution scores (Conductance, DeepLIFT) into both the clustering and regression stages is, to the authors' knowledge, novel in the model fusion literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically critical gap: zero-shot model fusion under non-IID and class-sharded data distributions, which is central to federated learning but where existing methods (OTFusion, Git Re-Basin) collapse to near-random accuracy. The theoretical guarantees (optimality for Hungarian Fusion, (9+ε)-approximation for K-means Fusion) and consistent empirical gains across VGGs, ResNets, and ViTs on five datasets provide strong evidence for the approach. Open questions include whether the method scales to LLMs, how to automate hyperparameter and level-partitioning selection, and whether data-free fusion variants are feasible. The gradient-based variants' sensitivity to fusion dataset size and hyperparameters remains a practical limitation.
