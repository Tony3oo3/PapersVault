## Problem

Existing deep learning-based Koopman frameworks face a fundamental trade-off in their training paradigms: either they treat observable functions and the global linear model as two independent trainable components (inflating the search space and optimization complexity), or they compute the model via a one-step least-squares problem (forcing alternating two-stage training or numerically unstable differentiation through pseudoinverses). Neither paradigm simultaneously achieves reduced optimization complexity, stable end-to-end training, and alignment between the model-computation objective and the multi-step rollout loss.

## Input / Output

The method consumes operational trajectories of a controlled nonlinear system, reorganized into two views: a fixed QP dataset of one-step transition tuples (x₁, x₂, u₁) used to solve the QP at each iteration, and a trajectory dataset of (T+1)-step state sequences with T inputs used for loss evaluation. The observable module (MLP + identity mapping) lifts the n-dimensional state to an N-dimensional lifted state (N ≈ 100–104), and the QP-based mapping module outputs the global linear model (A ∈ R^{N×N}, B ∈ R^{N×m}), which is then rolled forward for T-step prediction. Only the MLP parameters θ are optimized; A and B are determined implicitly by solving the QP at each forward pass.

## Method

QPKO formulates the mapping from learned observable functions to the global linear model as a quadratic program whose objective minimizes one-step prediction error (Frobenius norm of the lifted-state residual) and whose constraints enforce ‖A‖∞ < 1 (via auxiliary variables r_{i,j} and slack variables ξ_i with penalty weight α) to bound multi-step cumulative error. The key structural insight is that this QP decomposes into N independent sub-QPs (one per row of A), each with 2N+m+1 variables, which are solved and back-propagated in parallel on GPU using OptNet's KKT-based implicit differentiation—avoiding the pseudoinverse instability of prior LS-based approaches. Training optimizes only the MLP parameters θ end-to-end via Adam, with a curriculum learning schedule that gradually increases the prediction horizon T in the rollout loss from 10 to 85 over the first half of training.

## Results

QPKO achieves the lowest NMAE across all four nonlinear systems (CartPole, VDP, WT, TSR) and all prediction horizons T∈{50,100,150,200}. The advantage is most pronounced at long horizons: on TSR at T=200, QPKO reaches 1.147×10⁻² NMAE versus 2.315×10⁻² for the second-best (NDMD), a 41.1% reduction. In terms of training efficiency, QPKO requires 0.55–0.60 hours to reach best validation performance, yielding 3.1×, 2.6×, and 1.9× speedups over the second-best framework on CartPole, VDP, and TSR respectively. In MPC control, QPKO reduces final setpoint/tracking error by 61.8% (CartPole), 27.3% (VDP), and 36.4% (TSR) compared to the best baseline, while the three baselines fail entirely on TSR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

QPKO outperforms all three baselines on NMAE across every system and horizon. Against DKO (Shi & Meng, 2022), which treats the global linear model as an independent trainable component (first paradigm), QPKO reduces NMAE by 27–47% at T=200 and trains 3.1–6.4× faster (0.60h vs 3.80h on CartPole). Against EDMD-DL (Han et al., 2020), which uses alternating two-stage least-squares training (second paradigm), QPKO achieves lower NMAE on all systems and shorter wall-clock time on CartPole (0.60h vs 7.05h), VDP (0.55h vs 1.86h), and TSR (0.56h vs 1.37h), though EDMD-DL is marginally faster on WT (0.23h vs 0.56h). Against NDMD (Iwata & Kawahara, 2023), which back-propagates through the closed-form LS solution end-to-end, QPKO consistently achieves lower NMAE (e.g., 1.147 vs 2.315 on TSR at T=200) and shorter training time (0.56h vs 1.04h on TSR). In MPC control, QPKO is the only framework that successfully drives TSR to the origin, while DKO, EDMD-DL, and NDMD all suffer control failure on this system.

## Relation to Prior Work

QPKO sits at the intersection of deep learning-based Koopman modeling and differentiable convex optimization. It departs from both existing training paradigms for deep Koopman frameworks: the first (DKO, Yeung et al.) treats observable functions and the global linear model as independent trainable components, inflating the search space; the second (EDMD-DL, NDMD) computes the model via a one-step least-squares problem, which either forces alternating two-stage training or requires numerically unstable differentiation through a pseudoinverse. QPKO replaces the LS problem with a QP whose objective minimizes one-step error and whose infinity-norm constraints encode multi-step stability, then leverages OptNet's KKT-based implicit differentiation to back-propagate through the QP solution in a stable, GPU-parallelizable manner. The decomposition of the large QP into N independent sub-problems (Proposition 4.2) is a key technical enabler that makes the approach scalable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete and well-motivated gap: existing deep Koopman frameworks either over-parameterize (two independent components) or sacrifice end-to-end trainability (alternating stages / unstable pseudoinverse gradients). The QP-based mapping with slack variables is a clean mechanism to balance one-step fidelity against multi-step stability without adding trainable parameters. However, the authors acknowledge that the infinity-norm constraints only indirectly improve multi-step accuracy (by biasing toward a stable A matrix) rather than directly minimizing rollout error, and all experiments are on low-dimensional ODE systems (n ≤ 4 states, N ≤ 104 lifted dimension). Open questions include scalability to high-dimensional or PDE-based systems, whether a direct multi-step error objective within the QP is feasible, and how the approach performs under significant distribution shift beyond the mild closed-loop shift tested in MPC.
