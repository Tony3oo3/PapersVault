## Problem

Lifelong Model Editing (LME) suffers from catastrophic forgetting and model collapse over long edit sequences, yet recent resilient editors (ULTRAEDIT, RLEDIT) share a common Lifelong Normalization (LN) module whose mechanism remains a black box. The paper asks: what mechanism makes LN indispensable for long-term stability, and why does it produce the counter-intuitive 'positive cumulative effect' where early edits actually facilitate later ones?

## Input / Output

At each sequential step, the method consumes a batch of QA-pair edit requests and extracts per-instance hidden states and value gradients from target MLP down-projection layers of a frozen pretrained LLM. These are normalized via running mean and full covariance estimates (tracked with a Normal-Inverse-Wishart Bayesian recursion) before being fed into a closed-form ridge-regression solver that produces a single parameter update Δ_t per layer. The output is the updated weight matrix W_t = W_{t-1} + Δ_t, applied in-place with no auxiliary memory or hypernetwork at inference time.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a theoretical framework that models LN as a recursive Bayesian tracking process: the drifting value-gradient distribution is tracked with a Normal-Inverse-Wishart conjugate prior, yielding explicit step-to-step recursions for the mean and covariance estimates. Under a trackable-drift assumption, the authors prove that accumulated edits act as 'virtual samples' that shift the estimation-error curve leftward (explaining the positive cumulative effect), and that LN combined with ridge-regularized regression enforces asymptotic orthogonality and bounded norms on parameter updates, directly mitigating forgetting and collapse. Building on this, STABLEEDIT strengthens the self-reinforcing stability loop with two practical refinements over ULTRAEDIT: (1) an explicit warm-up stage that processes a small in-domain edit stream to bootstrap reliable running statistics before the target sequence, and (2) full covariance whitening via the matrix square root Σ̂^{-1/2} (computed with eigenvalue flooring) instead of diagonal normalization, which acts as an adaptive preconditioner suppressing noisy high-variance directions while amplifying weak ones.

## Results

On standard-scale lifelong editing (20K/17K edits, n_t=100), STABLEEDIT achieves top or near-top Efficacy, Generalization, and Specificity across three LLMs (Mistral-7B-v0.3, Llama-3-8B-Instruct, GPT-J-6B) and four datasets. On Llama-3-8B-Instruct, STABLEEDIT reaches 88.88% Efficacy and 85.46% Generalization on ULTRAEDITBENCH (vs. 85.70%/81.28% for ULTRAEDIT) and 97.89%/94.47% on FEVER (vs. 95.39%/91.93%). At ultra-large scale (500K WikiBigEdit edits), STABLEEDIT consistently outperforms ULTRAEDIT on all metrics across all three models, e.g., improving Generalization by 7.43% on GPT-J-6B (65.04% vs. 60.54%). Per-edit runtime is 0.10 s, only 1.43× slower than ULTRAEDIT (0.07 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

STABLEEDIT is built directly on ULTRAEDIT and consistently matches or exceeds it: on Llama-3-8B-Instruct it wins on 10 of 12 metrics (e.g., +4.18 Gen on ULTRAEDITBENCH, +2.54 Gen on FEVER) while ULTRAEDIT retains a slight edge on ZsRE Specificity (49.51 vs. 48.23) and WikiBigEdit Specificity (48.51 vs. 45.22). Against RLEDIT, the other LN-based hypernetwork method, STABLEEDIT dominates on nearly all metrics, with the largest gaps on FEVER Specificity (69.24 vs. 41.98) and ULTRAEDITBENCH Generalization (85.46 vs. 78.65). Non-LN baselines largely collapse under sequential editing: MEMIT, MEND, and MALMEN drop to near-zero on most datasets, ALPHAEDIT achieves strong ZsRE Efficacy (86.10) but fails on FEVER and ULTRAEDITBENCH, and FT degrades to single-digit scores. At 500K-edit scale (Table 2), STABLEEDIT extends its lead over ULTRAEDIT on every reported metric across all three models, confirming that the warm-up + full-whitening design compounds over long horizons. In terms of efficiency, STABLEEDIT adds only 0.03 s per edit over ULTRAEDIT (0.10 s vs. 0.07 s), whereas RLEDIT is 3.14× slower and ALPHAEDIT is 86× slower.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as the first theoretical demystification of Lifelong Normalization (LN), a normalization module shared by recent successful lifelong editors (ULTRAEDIT, RLEDIT, MALMEN) whose role was previously treated as a black box. It builds directly on ULTRAEDIT's ridge-regression-plus-normalization update rule and extends it with a Bayesian recursive tracking framework (Normal-Inverse-Wishart conjugate updates) to formalize how running statistics track drifting gradient distributions. The resulting STABLEEDIT is a theory-driven refinement of ULTRAEDIT that adds an explicit warm-up stage and full covariance whitening, departing from the diagonal-only normalization used in prior LN-based editors. The paper also situates itself against locate-then-edit methods (MEMIT, ALPHAEDIT) and hypernetwork-based methods (MEND, RLEDIT), arguing that LN-based closed-form updates are uniquely suited to long-horizon stability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical framework provides the first mechanistic explanation for why LN prevents catastrophic forgetting and model collapse in lifelong editing, resolving a gap that has limited the field's ability to design principled improvements. The counter-intuitive 'positive cumulative effect'—where early edits facilitate later ones—opens new design space for warm-up strategies and curriculum scheduling in sequential editing. Open questions include whether the trackable-drift assumption (Assumption 3.4) holds under adversarial or highly non-stationary edit streams, whether the Gaussian working model for gradients is sufficient, and whether the theory extends to non-MLP editable modules. The paper also acknowledges that token-matching evaluation metrics may not fully capture genuine editing effects, leaving room for more robust evaluation protocols.
