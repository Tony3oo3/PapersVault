## Problem

Performative predictions—where model outputs alter the very outcomes they forecast—have been studied for stability and optimality at the population level, but no generalization theory exists for learning from finite samples under performativity. This paper asks: how well can a model trained on a performatively shifted sample generalize to a population that also reacts to predictions, and what are the fundamental limits of such generalization?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263882/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a performatively shifted training sample (at most m of n units change per iteration), a hypothesis class with compact convex parameter space, a strongly convex Lipschitz loss, and a black-box transition map Tr assumed only to be jointly sensitive in p-Wasserstein distance. It outputs high-probability (1−δ) upper bounds on excess risk, performative excess risk, generalization gap, and cumulative performative excess risk, expressed in terms of the observable performative response rate m/n, sample size n, retraining horizon T, and covering-number entropy integrals of the hypothesis class. Notably, the bounds are data-dependent: they tighten when fewer units actually react, and the performative term grows geometrically in T when ε(1+L_a)>1.

## Method

The paper embeds performative predictions into statistical learning theory by bounding Wasserstein distances between the initial sample, the performatively shifted sample, and the true population, then converting these to risk differences via the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality. The key structural insight is that the worst-case generalization gap decomposes into a min-max (population self-negation) and a min-min (sample self-fulfillment) risk functional in Wasserstein space, which the authors handle via empirical process theory and covering number entropy integrals rather than Rademacher complexities—necessary because the learning target shifts and f_θ may be evaluated outside the support of d₀. This yields a fundamental change-learn trade-off: the bound grows in m/n, so more aggressive intervention degrades generalization. A surprising corollary shows that retraining on performatively distorted samples, while worsening the model's own generalization, improves the overall guarantee by enabling tighter estimation of the population-level performative shift from observed sample shifts across iterations.

## Results

The paper establishes the first finite-sample generalization bounds for performative predictions across four scenarios (RQ1–RQ3). The headline result (Theorem 3.7) bounds the excess risk under sample performativity by a performative term scaling as (m/n)^(1/p)·D_Z (where m/n is the performative response rate) plus a sampling term O(1/√n) and a Wasserstein convergence term O(n^(−1/ν)). In the empirical illustration on German job seeker data (n=60,147, m=1,816 changed units), the generalization gap is bounded by ≈0.3 nats (logistic loss units) with 95% confidence. The semi-simulation (Figure 1.3) shows the performative generalization gap bound grows monotonically with the policy parameter ξ (share of units assigned to job training, ranging 0.01–0.5), concretely instantiating the change-learn trade-off: more aggressive intervention worsens generalization guarantees.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper with no empirical baseline comparisons; its contribution is the first set of finite-sample generalization bounds under performativity. It generalizes Perdomo et al. (2020) and Brown et al. (2022), which study stability and optimality of performative predictions on the full population but do not address learnability from a finite sample. It subsumes Kirev et al. (2025), which provides PAC learnability only for binary classification under linear performative shifts of Y|X and marginal X, by handling all Lipschitz-continuous transition maps and performative shifts on arbitrary subsets of Y and X. It complements Li et al. (2025b), which gives an asymptotic CLT for the inferential gap (RQ4) as n→∞, by providing non-asymptotic bounds for RQ1–RQ3. The paper's key differentiator is requiring only a compact parameter space and Lipschitz continuity of the (unknown) transition map Tr, without assuming any specific functional form, strategic behavior model, or causal structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper embeds the performative prediction framework of Perdomo et al. (2020) and its stateful extension by Brown et al. (2022) into classical statistical learning theory (Vapnik, 1998), shifting the focus from stability and optimality of performative equilibria to finite-sample generalization from a performatively shifted sample to a performatively reacting population. Technically, it leverages Wasserstein-space convergence rates (Fournier & Guillin, 2015), the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality, and dual characterizations from distributionally robust (Gao & Kleywegt, 2023) and distributionally favorable (Jiang & Xie, 2025) optimization to cast self-negating and self-fulfilling predictions as min-max and min-min risk functionals. Unlike distributionally robust performative optimization (Jia et al., 2025; Xue & Sun, 2024), which specifies Wasserstein ambiguity sets a priori, this work estimates them from observed sample-level performative reactions, and unlike strategic classification or mechanism design, it requires no structural assumptions on agent behavior beyond Lipschitz continuity of the transition map.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes the first generalization bounds under performativity, revealing a fundamental change-learn trade-off: the more a model performatively alters the data it learns from, the worse its generalization guarantees become. The conceptual insight that in the worst case the sample self-fulfills predictions while the population self-negates them (an 'empirical echo chamber') reframes the generalization gap in a way that has practical implications for deployment strategies in high-stakes domains like employment services and finance. Key limitations include the requirement of strong convexity (Condition 3.1), loose bounds due to the black-box treatment of the transition map, and the unresolved finite-sample CLT for RQ4; the trade-off between restricting the model class and making assumptions on the unknown performative shift is explicitly flagged as a direction for future work.
