## Problem

Machine learning practice, tooling, and regulatory frameworks (e.g., the EU AI Act's traceability requirements) implicitly treat trained systems as stable, identifiable objects that can be named, versioned, compared, and governed. This paper asks whether such objecthood is actually warranted, given that the two natural candidates for identity—functional behavior and internal structure—may both fail to provide stable, context-appropriate criteria of individuation.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the work takes as its subject the set of implicit identity assumptions embedded in ML engineering practice (model versioning, continuous deployment, retraining triggers, regulatory traceability) and produces a set of minimal interpretive commitments: explicit equivalence relations and granularity levels (functional, structural, or procedural) under which any identity claim about a learned system can be meaningfully stated. There is no data pipeline or model architecture; the 'output' is a constraint on how existing claims about model sameness, updates, and reproducibility should be read.

## Method

The argument proceeds in two complementary directions. First, it shows functional equivalence is structurally underdetermined: by the universal approximation theorem, agreement on any finite test set does not entail agreement on untested inputs, so pointwise behavioral testing can falsify but never establish identity. Second, it demonstrates that internal structure is non-identifiable in modern architectures: discrete permutation symmetries across layers yield combinatorially many equivalent parameterizations, continuous orthogonal symmetries eliminate any privileged internal coordinate system, and normalization mechanisms (e.g., layer norm) collapse entire families of distinct parameter states to identical behavior. The paper then articulates a hierarchy of equivalence relations and specifies, for each level (functional, structural, procedural), the minimal conditions—functional bands, gauge-fixing conventions, or process guarantees—under which identity claims become interpretable, closing with concrete obligations for researchers, benchmarks, tooling, and regulators.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experiments or benchmark results. The central theoretical claim is that neither functional behavior nor internal structure provides stable criteria of identity for learned systems: behavioral equivalence is underdetermined by finite data (by the universal approximation theorem), while modern architectures admit combinatorially many structurally distinct realizations of the same function via permutation symmetries, continuous orthogonal invariances, and normalization-induced collapses. The paper's actionable contribution is a set of minimal conditions under which identity claims can be meaningfully interpreted: specifying the equivalence relation, the level of granularity (functional, structural, or procedural), and, for structural identity, an explicit gauge-fixing convention.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare methods empirically; instead, it engages three alternative views in Section 7 and argues why each is insufficient to ground object-level identity. (1) Functional equivalence is sufficient: the paper concedes this is operationally attractive but argues that agreement on observed inputs cannot establish equivalence beyond tested regions, so behavior can falsify but not establish identity. (2) Internal structure defines the model: the paper shows that discrete permutation symmetries and continuous orthogonal invariances in modern architectures mean internal descriptions outnumber induced behaviors, so structural inspection exposes degrees of freedom rather than recovering a unique object. (3) Process-level governance is enough: the paper acknowledges this is practically successful and increasingly institutionalized but notes it does not eliminate the identity question—it merely relocates it to procedural guarantees. The paper positions its own contribution as a constraint on interpretation rather than a competing method, making it orthogonal to all three views.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not extend a specific prior method but synthesizes disparate threads—permutation and continuous symmetries in neural network parameter spaces, the universal approximation theorem, PCA sign ambiguity, layer-normalization invariances, and gauge-fixing concepts from physics—into a unified argument about the (non-)existence of stable identity criteria for trained systems. It positions itself against three pragmatic stances (functional equivalence sufficiency, structural identity, and process-level governance) that are common in deployment and regulatory practice, arguing each presupposes the very identity it cannot secure. The governance framing draws on the EU AI Act's traceability requirements and the reproducibility literature (Pineau et al.), but the paper's core contribution is a conceptual reframing rather than a technical extension of any single prior line of work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a deeply embedded assumption in ML engineering, tooling, and regulation—that a trained model is a stable, referable object—and shows that the equivalence relations underpinning versioning, comparison, and audit are rarely made explicit. Its practical impact is most immediate for governance and regulatory contexts (EU AI Act compliance, model registries, deployment pipelines) where 'the model' must be identified for accountability, but the proposed 'minimal commitments' (specifying equivalence relations, gauge-fixing conventions, or process-level guarantees) remain stated as principles without operational protocols or empirical validation. Open questions include whether a workable hierarchy of equivalence relations can be formalized for specific architectures, how to reconcile legal identity requirements with structural non-identifiability, and whether the proposed framework scales to foundation models with emergent, multi-task behavior.
