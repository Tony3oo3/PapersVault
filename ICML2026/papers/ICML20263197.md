## Problem

Existing computational materials discovery benchmarks evaluate static sub-tasks in isolation—predictive accuracy on fixed datasets, one-shot generative quality, or MLIP screening on a pre-defined candidate pool—rather than the iterative, resource-constrained, closed-loop process that characterizes real discovery. There is no systematic framework for comparing end-to-end discovery pipelines or agentic systems under a limited oracle budget, making it impossible to answer questions like whether a better generator, adaptive planner, or LLM orchestrator actually accelerates the discovery of new stable compounds.

## Input / Output

Each discovery episode is configured by a chemical system (3–5 element intermetallic, chalcogenide, halide, or oxide space), an initial set of known structures H₀ drawn from Materials Project (≤20 atoms per unit cell), a fixed oracle query budget of 50, and a stability threshold (default 0.1 eV/atom). The oracle is the orb-v3 MLIP, which returns formation energy per atom after full structural relaxation; after each query the environment recomputes the convex hull over all known structures, so the stability status of previously seen candidates can change. The policy outputs a single candidate structure per step (composition + crystal structure), and the episode terminates after B queries, yielding a set of mSUN (metastable, stable, unique, novel) structures plus discovery metrics (AF, EF, AUDC, mSUN) and diversity metrics (mean composition L1 distance, unique compositions, unique space groups) relative to a random-generator baseline.

## Method

MADE formalizes materials discovery as a sequential multi-minima search: an agent with policy π proposes structures one at a time, receives formation-energy feedback from an oracle, and must maximize the count of newly discovered thermodynamically stable compounds (Δ_hull ≤ ε) within a fixed budget. The framework is modular and composable—users assemble pipelines from interchangeable Planner (random, diversity-maximizing, or LLM-based), Generator (random AIRSS-style, Chemeleon, MatterGen, OMatG), Filter (SMACT, minimum interatomic distance, pymatgen StructureMatcher uniqueness), and Selector (random, MLIP surrogate ranking, or LLM) components, or deploy a fully agentic ReAct-style LLM orchestrator that dynamically interleaves generation, scoring, and selection via a fixed tool set. Discovery is measured with acceleration-centric metrics (AF, EF) relative to a random baseline and absolute metrics (AUDC, mSUN), enabling apples-to-apples comparison across chemical system sizes and stability thresholds. The key empirical finding is that adaptive planning (LLM planners, agentic orchestration) yields progressively larger gains over static pipelines as the number of constituent elements grows from ternary to quinary and as the stability threshold tightens, where surrogate MLIP rankings degrade due to errors near the convex hull.

## Results

Across 10 intermetallic systems (ternary, quaternary, quinary) with a 50-query budget and 0.1 eV stability threshold, the Chemeleon + MLIP ranking pipeline achieves the highest Acceleration Factor (AF=6.4) and AUDC (0.42) among non-agentic methods, while the fully agentic LLM Orchestrator achieves the highest Enhancement Factor (EF=6.0) and mSUN (0.40), discovering the broadest range of space groups (10.4 unique SGs). LLM-based planning more than doubles discovery efficiency over Chemeleon alone (AF 3.9 vs. 1.7), and adaptive planning gains grow with system size, with Random + LLM planning outperforming the Chemeleon baseline on quinary systems. At tighter 0.01 eV thresholds, MLIP ranking degrades due to surrogate error near the convex hull, while planning-based strategies retain significant gains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among generative model baselines (Table 4), MatterGen (Zeni et al., 2025) achieves the strongest standalone generator performance (AF=2.7, EF=2.4, AUDC=0.26), outperforming both Chemeleon (Park et al., 2025; AF=1.7) and OMatG (Hollmer et al., 2025; AF=1.8) under random selection. However, when paired with LLM planning, Chemeleon pulls ahead (AF=3.9 vs. OMatG's 3.0), and the LLM Orchestrator (built on Chemeleon) surpasses all individual generators on every discovery metric (AF=5.4, EF=6.0, AUDC=0.40, mSUN=0.40). The Chemeleon + MLIP pipeline wins on AF (6.4) and AUDC (0.42) over the LLM Orchestrator (5.4, 0.40), but the Orchestrator wins on EF (6.0 vs. 5.3) and mSUN (0.40 vs. 0.39), while discovering 3x more unique space groups (10.4 vs. 3.3), indicating a distinct efficiency–diversity trade-off. Across chemical families (chalcogenides, halides, oxides in Table 6), the LLM Orchestrator consistently achieves the highest or near-highest AF and EF, with particularly large gains on oxides (AF=11) and chalcogenides (AF=11), where the random baseline performs worst.

## Relation to Prior Work

MADE extends the discovery-acceleration paradigm introduced by Matbench Discovery (Riebesell et al., 2025) and the AF/EF metrics from Rohr et al. (2020) from static MLIP screening on fixed candidate pools to a fully closed-loop, budget-constrained sequential decision-making setting. It also unifies the generative-model evaluation line (LeMatGenBench, Betala et al., 2025) and the emerging LLM-agent-for-science line (Jia et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024) under a single composable environment, where planners, generators, filters, and selectors are interchangeable modules. The key conceptual departure is treating discovery as a multi-minima search over a discrete chemical space with oracle feedback, rather than as a one-shot ranking or unconditional generation task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MADE is the first systematic benchmark for end-to-end closed-loop materials discovery, enabling controlled ablation of pipeline components (planner, generator, selector) and direct comparison of agentic versus modular strategies under identical budgets. The finding that MLIP-based surrogate ranking degrades at tighter stability thresholds while adaptive LLM planning retains gains highlights an open need for uncertainty-aware screening integrated into acquisition strategies. Limitations include reliance on MLIP oracles (not DFT or experiments), distributional bias from Materials Project training data, and single-query-at-a-time evaluation; extending to batched queries, multi-objective tasks, and higher-fidelity oracles are natural next steps that will determine the benchmark's long-term utility.
