## Problem

Stem retrieval requires identifying which missing stems temporally and harmonically fit a given submix, a task fundamentally different from semantic similarity. Existing models (CLAP, CDPAM, COCOLA) rely on Global Average Pooling to produce fixed-size embeddings, which enforces temporal invariance and collapses distinct rhythmic alignments into identical representations, rendering them structurally blind to coherence. The paper identifies that no prior architecture explicitly preserves phase information needed to resolve fine-grained rhythmic and harmonic alignment.

## Input / Output

The model consumes CQT spectrograms of audio submixes (not Mel-spectrograms), with a 32× temporal compression applied by the backbone before spectral pooling. Each input is encoded into a 640-dimensional complex embedding (D=80 harmonic dimensions × C=8 temporal frequency bins), which is then projected to 512 complex values by the CVNN head. The final output is a scalar coherence score computed as the real part of a learnable Hermitian bilinear form between two L2-normalized complex embeddings, used for K-way retrieval ranking or as a reference-aware quality metric for generated stems.

## Method

PHALAR decouples harmonic content from rhythmic alignment through a three-stage pipeline: (1) a 10-layer axial CNN backbone with pitch-equivariant convolutions on CQT inputs extracts interval-aware features while preserving absolute pitch; (2) a Learned Spectral Pooling layer applies an RFFT along the temporal axis of projected features, converting temporal shifts into phase rotations via the Fourier Shift Theorem; (3) a phase-equivariant CVNN head (complex linear layers, Complex RMSNorm, modReLU) processes the complex latents while strictly preserving phase angles. The similarity metric is the real part of a parametrized Hermitian inner product with a learnable complex weight matrix, enabling the model to apply learnable phase rotations to account for micro-timing deviations. Training uses InfoNCE contrastive loss with label smoothing on dynamically generated disjoint submix pairs, requiring only 2.3M parameters and 50 GPU-hours versus 5.2M and 340 GPU-hours for the prior SOTA.

## Results

PHALAR achieves state-of-the-art K-way contrastive retrieval accuracy across all three datasets, with the most dramatic gains at K=64 where tonal collisions make the task hardest: 70.87% on MoisesDB (vs. 41.84% for COCOLA, a ~69% relative improvement), 72.37% on Slakh2100, and 98.61% on ChocoChorales. The model uses only 2.3M parameters (<50% of COCOLA's 5.2M) and trains in 50 GPU-hours versus COCOLA's 340 (a 7× speedup). In human evaluation, PHALAR achieves the highest Pearson (ρ=0.387) and Spearman (r_s=0.414) correlation with human coherence ratings, significantly outperforming all baselines per Steiger's test.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PHALAR decisively outperforms COCOLA (the prior SOTA for coherence) across all datasets and difficulty levels, with the gap widening as K increases: on MoisesDB K=64, PHALAR scores 70.87% versus COCOLA's 41.84% (~69% relative gain) while using less than half the parameters. MERT with PHALAR's phase-aware head (MERT†) substantially improves over raw MERT embeddings (1.83% → 45.85% on MoisesDB K=64) but still trails PHALAR by ~25 points, confirming that end-to-end pitch-equivariant extraction plus complex-valued processing is necessary rather than retrofitting a large semantic backbone. Semantic foundation models CLAP and CDPAM collapse to near-random-chance performance (≈1.2% at K=64), demonstrating a fundamental orthogonality between semantic similarity and structural coherence. In the human evaluation (Table 2), PHALAR's Pearson correlation (0.387) significantly exceeds COCOLA (0.181), CLAP (0.111), CDPAM (−0.015), ViSQOL (−0.091), and all Audiobox-Aesthetics sub-scores (p<0.05 via Steiger's test, except Audiobox_CE at p=0.123). For zero-shot beat tracking on GTZAN, PHALAR achieves F1=0.627 without any rhythm supervision, compared to the fully supervised Beat This! baseline at F1=0.888, confirming emergent temporal structure in the learned representations.

## Relation to Prior Work

PHALAR directly extends COCOLA (Ciranni et al., 2025), the prior state-of-the-art for coherence-oriented contrastive learning of musical audio, by replacing its real-valued CNN with Global Average Pooling with a pitch-equivariant CQT backbone, a Learned Spectral Pooling layer (adapting Rippel et al., 2015), and a complex-valued projection head. The architectural building blocks draw on Trabelsi et al.'s (2018) CVNN formalism (complex linear layers, modReLU) and the Fourier Shift Theorem as a mathematical inductive bias, but the key conceptual departure is from the invariance-to-equivariance shift: where CLAP, CDPAM, and MERT enforce translational invariance via GAP to serve semantic similarity, PHALAR deliberately preserves temporal structure as phase rotation. The paper also positions itself against the broader audio representation learning literature (Wav2Vec 2.0, HuBERT, CLMR, MERT) that has adopted vision-style pooling, arguing this is fundamentally misaligned for time-sensitive musical tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes a clear conceptual separation between semantic similarity and structural coherence, showing that foundation models trained for the former are not merely suboptimal but collapse to random chance on the latter—a finding with direct implications for how the field evaluates generative audio (PHALAR outperforms FAD and Audiobox in ranking stem generators against human judgment). The practical efficiency gains (7× training speedup, <50% parameters) make the approach accessible. Key limitations remain: the RFFT-based spectral pooling assumes temporal periodicity, degrading under rubato or non-periodic tempo drift, and the training distribution is heavily Western popular music, potentially biasing the geometric notion of coherence. Open questions include whether the emergent 'magnitude-only' features truly encode time-agnostic properties like key or mood, and whether the phase-equivariant framework can be extended to score generated multitrack audio in generative pipelines.
