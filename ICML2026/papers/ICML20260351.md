## Problem

Sequential LLM agents suffer from Constraint Drift under Long Tool Traces: as planning horizons extend and intermediate tool outputs accumulate in context, attention to global hard constraints (budgets, non-duplication, route consistency) degrades, causing cascading infeasibility in multi-day itineraries. Existing remedies—post-hoc verification loops like Reflexion or ATLAS—detect errors only after full plan generation, wasting computation and scaling poorly with horizon length. The paper asks whether proactive, by-construction constraint enforcement combined with parallel decomposition can eliminate this drift without sacrificing planning quality.

## Input / Output

The system takes a single natural-language travel query specifying origin, destination, trip duration (3/5/7 days), total budget, group size (1–8), and optional constraints on cuisine, room type, house rules, and transportation mode. It produces a complete multi-day itinerary where each day specifies city, transportation, three meals, an attraction, and accommodation, all validated against 13 conjunctive constraints (8 commonsense + 5 hard) using exact database prices. The output must achieve 100% Delivery Rate (syntactically valid) while maximizing Final Pass Rate (all constraints simultaneously satisfied).

## Method

HiMAP-Travel decomposes planning into a Strategic Level (a Coordinator that projects the query into day-level sub-goals with soft budget hints satisfying a conservation law) and a Tactical Level (D parallel Day Executors operating in isolated context windows, reducing effective context from O(T) to O(T/D)). Three integrated mechanisms enable this: (1) a Synchronized Global State Σ—a deterministic transactional monitor with atomic check/commit operations under a global mutex that enforces budget conservation, venue non-duplication, and transport-mode consistency across parallel agents; (2) a Cooperative Bargaining Protocol where Executors return typed JSON violation signals (status, deficit, violation_type) to trigger Coordinator re-allocation with checkpoint/rollback, capped at K_max=3 iterations; (3) a single role-conditioned policy π_θ trained via GRPO (group size 4, KL β=0.01) on 45 TravelPlanner queries, where distinct Coordinator and Executor behaviors are elicited through system-prompt conditioning rather than separate parameter sets, with a memory-efficient FIFO buffer that updates each role as soon as its G-trajectory group completes.

## Results

On TravelPlanner, HiMAP-Travel with Qwen3-8B achieves 52.78% validation and 52.65% test Final Pass Rate (FPR), with a mere 0.13 pp gap indicating strong generalization. The 4B variant reaches 48.61% validation and 49.80% test FPR. On FlexTravelBench multi-turn scenarios, the 8B model achieves 44.34% (2-turn) and 37.42% (3-turn) FPR while reducing wall-clock latency by 2.5× through parallel day-level execution (72s vs 190s for a 7-day itinerary).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the controlled sequential baseline DeepTravel (identical Qwen3-8B backbone, GRPO training, and tool APIs), HiMAP-Travel gains +8.67 pp test FPR (52.65% vs 43.98%) with 93% variance reduction across seeds (std 0.48% vs 7.18%), isolating the architectural benefit of hierarchical decomposition. Against ATLAS (Gemini-2.5-Pro), HiMAP-Travel leads by +17.65 pp test FPR (52.65% vs 35.00%), though this comparison also reflects model-capacity differences. Against MTP (GPT-4 with manual hints), the gain is +10.0 pp test FPR (52.65% vs 42.68%). On FlexTravelBench, HiMAP-Travel outperforms ReAct (GPT-4) by +15.38 pp on 2-turn Local Add (44.96% vs 29.58%) and +22.08 pp on 3-turn Local→Global (37.42% vs 15.34%), and beats ATLAS by +6.10 pp and +3.92 pp on the same scenarios respectively. The 4B model shows even larger gains over DeepTravel (+14.88 pp test FPR), suggesting smaller models benefit more from hierarchical factorization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes ideas from hierarchical reinforcement learning (goal-setting vs. control separation), multi-agent LLM planning (MTP, PMC, HiPlan), and transactional database systems into a novel architecture for constrained travel planning. It departs from prior multi-agent frameworks (ATLAS, MTP) that rely on natural-language negotiation or post-hoc verification by introducing a deterministic Synchronized Global State with atomic check/commit operations, shifting from 'generate-then-fix' to 'correct-by-construction.' The single-policy role-conditioned design (one transformer serving both Coordinator and Executors via system-prompt conditioning) is a departure from the separate-agent paradigm in prior hierarchical LLM systems, and the Cooperative Bargaining Protocol replaces verbose inter-agent dialogue with typed JSON violation signals. The controlled comparison against DeepTravel (identical backbone, GRPO training, and tools) isolates the architectural contribution from model-capacity effects, a methodological choice not common in prior multi-agent travel planning papers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The controlled comparison design (same Qwen3 backbone, same GRPO training, same tool APIs) provides unusually clean evidence that hierarchical decomposition with parallel execution and deterministic constraint monitoring yields a genuine +8.67 pp architectural gain and 93% variance reduction, rather than a model-capacity or training-algorithm artifact. However, the evaluation is confined to travel planning (a single domain with a 45-query training split), the monitor covers only budget/venue/mode conflicts (not temporal or route-feasibility constraints), and the 52.65% FPR leaves substantial room for improvement. Open questions include whether the single-policy role-conditioning benefit (8.08 pp over separately trained policies) generalizes beyond this task, whether the architecture transfers to other coupled-constraint domains (software planning, supply chain), and how the approach scales when the number of parallel sub-problems grows beyond the P=3 concurrency cap.
