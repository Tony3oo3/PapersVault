## Problem

When a pretrained foundation model is used as a fixed black-box predictor for a downstream task with limited labeled data, its error on the target distribution is unknown, and blindly trusting it risks negative transfer. Prior work on transfer learning, distillation, and pseudo-labeling does not characterize the finite-sample minimax tradeoff between the black-box prior error and the difficulty of learning a correction from scarce data. This paper identifies the critical phase transition at δ_c(n) ∝ n^{-β/(2β+d)} and provides a safe estimator that adapts to the unknown prior quality without ever performing worse than the black-box alone.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a small labeled dataset {(x_i, y_i)}_{i=1}^n and oracle access to a fixed black-box predictor f_0 that can be evaluated on any input x (no parameter or source-data access). It outputs a corrected prediction f_safe(x) = f_0(x) + α·r̂(x) where α ∈ {0,1} is a binary selection variable determined by holdout validation. For multi-class classification (CIFAR-100, AG News), the framework is applied to K score/logit functions: the black-box provides initial logits and the residual head learns per-class corrections, with the final output being a class label via argmax over the corrected logits.

## Method

The Safe Residual Estimator decomposes the target as f* = f_0 + r* and learns only the residual r* (which is assumed Hölder-smooth with L_2 norm bounded by δ), shifting the statistical difficulty from estimating the full function to estimating a smaller, smoother correction. A minimax-optimal nonparametric regressor (e.g., kernel ridge regression) is trained on residual targets y_i − f_0(x_i) over a training split, and a holdout validation split then selects between f_0 and f_0 + r̂ via a binary comparison of empirical losses, guaranteeing an oracle inequality that prevents negative transfer up to an O(1/n_val) cost. The residual formulation is geometrically superior to weighted ensembling: it searches a ball centered at f_0 in function space rather than a one-dimensional affine line segment, enabling recovery of correction components orthogonal to both the prior and a scratch estimator. For neural residual heads in classification experiments, zero-initialization ensures the initial predictor exactly equals f_0, providing implicit regularization that prevents early-training negative transfer even when the explicit validation fallback is never triggered.

## Results

On CIFAR-100 with CLIP (ViT-B/32), the Safe Residual Estimator achieves 66.7% accuracy at n=2000, outperforming the validation-tuned weighted ensemble (63.9%) by 2.8 points and the zero-shot CLIP baseline (59.4%) by 7.3 points. On AG News with Qwen3-8B, the method reaches 77.0% at n=256 and 75.2% at n=16, consistently exceeding the weighted ensemble baseline (75.6% and 71.2% respectively) and the zero-shot LLM baseline (74.1%). In the 1D synthetic regression setting, the method verifies the predicted phase transition: at n=20, δ=0.0, safe selection triggers a 65% fallback rate, reducing MSE from 0.0335 (raw residual) to 0.0027, while at δ=0.8 it achieves 0.097 MSE versus 0.145 for the weighted ensemble.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares five estimators: SCRATCH (standard supervised learning), BB-ONLY (zero-shot black-box), WGT (validation-tuned weighted ensemble αf₀+(1−α)f_scratch), CONCAT (training on concatenated features [x, f₀(x)]), and the proposed RES(SAFE) residual estimator. On CIFAR-100, RES(SAFE) wins against WGT at every sample size, with the gap widening from 0.5 points at n=100 to 2.8 points at n=2000; it also dominates SCRATCH and CONCAT by 10–60+ points in the few-shot regime. On AG News, the advantage over WGT is most pronounced at low n: at n=64, WGT collapses to 54.7% (matching SCRATCH) due to validation noise in weight estimation, while RES(SAFE) maintains 74.7%. The weighted ensemble also underperforms the zero-shot baseline at n=200 on CIFAR-100 (54.5% vs 59.4%), confirming the paper's geometric argument that linear ensembling is constrained to a one-dimensional subspace. In the synthetic 1D experiments, RES(SAFE) beats WGT by 4.8 points in MSE at n=20, δ=0.8 (0.097 vs 0.145), and the safe selection mechanism prevents negative transfer (65% fallback at δ=0.0) that the raw residual estimator suffers (MSE 0.0335 vs 0.0027). In high-dimensional synthetic regression (d=20), RES(SAFE) triggers a 60% fallback at δ=1.0, stabilizing risk at 1.007 versus 1.026 for the raw residual, while SCRATCH remains stuck at 0.489 due to the curse of dimensionality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by formalizing the 'foundation model as fixed prior' problem as a minimax nonparametric regression task, deliberately distinguishing itself from transfer learning (which assumes parameter or source-data access), distillation (which studies student-teacher compression), and prediction-powered inference (which targets confidence intervals rather than risk-optimal estimation). The theoretical machinery is classical—Fano's inequality, metric entropy of Hölder classes, and two-model holdout oracle inequalities—but is applied to a novel localized function class F(δ) = H(β,L) ∩ B(f₀,δ) that had not previously been characterized. The residual-learning method connects conceptually to ResNet-style additive corrections and to the zero-initialization trick from deep residual training, but the paper's contribution is the statistical guarantee rather than the architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The phase transition at δ_c(n) ≍ n^{-β/(2β+d)} gives practitioners a concrete, sample-size-dependent criterion for when a foundation model's predictions are trustworthy versus when labeled data must drive correction—a question that is central to deploying pretrained models in low-data regimes but has lacked a minimax characterization. The Safe Residual Estimator is deliberately simple (two-candidate holdout selection), making the oracle inequality directly actionable. Key limitations include: formal guarantees are restricted to squared-loss regression with homoscedastic Gaussian noise and bounded covariates; the classification experiments (CIFAR-100, AG News) are explicitly framed as 'practice-facing' rather than theorem-level extensions; and the additive O(1/n) validation cost, while asymptotically negligible, can dominate in the strongly prior-dominated regime. Open directions include removing the sample-splitting overhead via Lepski-type methods, extending to heavy-tailed noise, and exploring the residual-correction principle for generative models.
