## Problem

Deep learning-based PDE solvers require large, expensive-to-generate datasets and many parameters to achieve accurate simulations, even though the solution operators for elliptic PDEs possess a highly structured hierarchical semi-separable (HSS) form. No existing neural architecture injects this HSS structure directly into its layers while providing provable data-efficiency guarantees (i.e., exact recovery with a number of samples independent of grid resolution) for a broad class of PDEs.

## Input / Output

The model consumes spatial fields on regular grids as input: 1D problems use vectors of length 1024 (downsampled to 256 for training), 2D problems use 64×64 or 128×128 tensors, and the 3D Poisson experiment uses 128³ grids (~2M points). For multi-channel PDEs (Navier-Stokes vorticity, Gray-Scott concentrations), a low-rank tensor lifting layer maps from the input channel dimension to the embedding dimension before the HSS backbone, and a matching projection layer maps back. The output is the corresponding solution field or time-evolved state at the same spatial resolution, with the model trained to predict either the full solution (steady-state problems) or the residual δu = u_{t+δt} − u_t (time-dependent problems).

## Method

Neural-HSS replaces standard linear layers with HSS-structured layers: in 1D, each layer's weight matrix is an exact HSS matrix defined by a balanced binary cluster tree of depth L and rank r, where off-diagonal blocks are represented by low-rank factorizations and diagonal blocks are full-rank, yielding O(nr) storage and matvec cost. For m-dimensional problems, the layer is approximated as a CP decomposition of outer products of 1D HSS matrices (Eq. 1), scaling parameters as O(r_out · m · r · d) rather than O(d^{2m}). Stacked HSS layers are interleaved with learnable LeakyReLU activations (slope α adapts to 1 for linear PDEs, deviating for nonlinear ones). Two key theoretical results underpin the design: (i) regular convolutional kernels are HSS-approximable with rank O(log(1/ε)^D), connecting the layer to FNO/convolutional layers; and (ii) for elliptic PDEs whose solution operator is HSS, all global minimizers of the empirical loss recover the exact operator with only O(Σr_i) training samples, independent of grid size. The architecture is trained end-to-end via standard backpropagation with AdamW and cosine LR scheduling.

## Results

On the 2D Poisson equation, Neural-HSS achieves a relative L² error of 7×10⁻⁸ with only 37K parameters, outperforming FNO (7×10⁻⁶, 132K), ResNet (3×10⁻⁴, 165K), and FactFormer (6×10⁻³, 3.9M). On the incompressible Navier-Stokes forward problem, Neural-HSS reaches 0.123 error with 329K parameters versus 0.483 for FNO (2.1M) and 0.481 for ResNet (1.99M). On the 1D Burgers' equation, Neural-HSS achieves 0.12 trajectory error with 1.5M parameters, roughly half the error of FNO (0.26) and a quarter of ResNet (0.57). In the 3D Poisson data-efficiency experiment (2M grid points), Neural-HSS trained on just 16 samples matches FNO trained on 64 samples and ResNet on 128 samples, while training in ~2.5 hours versus 6 hours (FNO) and ~42 hours (ResNet).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Neural-HSS consistently outperforms FNO on all elliptic problems (Poisson, Helmholtz) by 1–2 orders of magnitude in test error while using 3–4× fewer parameters, and on the Navier-Stokes forward problem it achieves 0.123 error versus FNO's 0.483 with 6× fewer parameters. Against ResNet, Neural-HSS wins on every elliptic benchmark (e.g., 7×10⁻⁸ vs. 3×10⁻⁴ on 2D Poisson) and on Burgers' (0.12 vs. 0.57), though ResNet edges it out slightly on Gray-Scott forward (0.273 vs. 0.294) and inverse (0.193 vs. 0.203). DeepONet is the fastest to train but consistently has the highest test error among all baselines on elliptic problems (e.g., 2×10⁻² vs. 7×10⁻⁸ on 2D Poisson). Green Learning, which is restricted to linear PDEs, is outperformed by Neural-HSS on all shared benchmarks and could not be trained at all on the 3D Poisson problem due to memory constraints. FactFormer and Transolver, both transformer-based, use 5–12× more parameters than Neural-HSS (3.9M and 1.8M vs. 329K) yet achieve comparable or worse accuracy; Transolver is the sole exception, winning on Gray-Scott inverse (0.159 vs. 0.203) but at 5.5× the parameter count.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper injects the Hierarchical Semi-Separable (HSS) matrix structure—long studied in numerical linear algebra for fast elliptic PDE solvers (Hackbusch, 2015; Casulli et al., 2024)—as an explicit inductive bias into a neural architecture for PDE operator learning. It extends the data-efficiency framework of Boullé et al. (2023), which proved exact recovery for a specific Multipole Graph Neural Operator on elliptic PDEs, to a more general architecture that handles arbitrary PDEs while retaining provable exactness and data-efficiency guarantees (Theorem 2.3). The higher-dimensional extension via CP decomposition (Lebedev et al., 2015) is a deliberate approximation of the true multi-dimensional HSS structure, trading theoretical exactness for scalability. A notable theoretical contribution is Theorem 2.2, which shows that regular convolutional kernels (as in FNO/CNO layers) are HSS-approximable, positioning the HSS layer as a generalization of discretized convolutional layers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: Neural-HSS achieves competitive or superior accuracy with 3–100× fewer parameters than baselines (e.g., 37K vs. 3.9M for FactFormer on 2D Poisson), and the 3D Poisson experiment on a 2M-point grid demonstrates a regime where the Green Learning baseline simply cannot be trained due to memory constraints. The provable data-efficiency (exact recovery from O(Σrᵢ) samples) is particularly valuable in settings where generating high-fidelity PDE data is expensive. Key limitations include the CP-approximation for higher-dimensional layers (not a true multi-dimensional HSS), the restriction of the strongest theoretical guarantees to the elliptic regime, and the acknowledged unsuitability for fully unstructured meshes—areas that remain open for future work.
