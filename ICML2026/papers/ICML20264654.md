## Problem

Existing EEG models suffer from three interrelated limitations: pervasive electrode heterogeneity across datasets (varying channel counts, placements, and referencing schemes), the absence of a unified representation invariant to sensor configurations, and a rigid channel-first architectural bias that treats electrodes as independent, semantically aligned features. This inductive bias mismatch breaks cross-dataset generalization and renders standard evaluation protocols poorly aligned with real-world deployment where electrode layouts differ. The paper also identifies a methodological gap: no prior work systematically evaluates EEG model generalization across unseen electrode configurations or subject-level few-shot adaptation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a raw multi-channel EEG segment X ∈ R^{c×l} (c channels, l time samples at 200 Hz) and produces a task-specific one-hot class label. Internally, the signal is converted via STFT into a time-frequency magnitude map, spatialized according to a user-specified electrode coordinate map S ∈ N^{h×w} into a 4D tensor, and rendered into an RGB spectrum video of shape n×H×W×3 (n temporal frames). Before feeding into VideoMAE, each video is resized to 224×224 spatial resolution and temporally sampled to 16 frames. The spatialization map is montage-agnostic: the same pre-trained renderer applies to any electrode system (10-20, 10-10, etc.) simply by swapping the coordinate map, with no retraining required.

## Method

Brain Signal Rendering (BSR) reinterprets EEG as a physical projection of 3D neural activity onto a 2D sensor array, transforming raw signals into structured spatiotemporal 'spectrum videos' compatible with video foundation models. The pipeline has three stages: (1) a renderer R_v (cascaded transposed convolutions) maps STFT-derived, spatially-organized spectra into RGB video frames, trained end-to-end with a symmetric reconstructor via L1 loss; (2) a VideoMAE encoder (ViT-base, ~86M params) is self-supervisedly pre-trained on rendered spectrum videos using masked autoencoding, optionally initialized from Kinetics-400 weights to bridge the natural-video-to-EEG domain gap; (3) the encoder is fine-tuned under two novel evaluation paradigms—subject-level few-shot learning (each subject as a distinct task with minimal calibration data) and cross-montage fine-tuning (assessing performance on seen vs. entirely unseen electrode subsets). The key insight is that electrode heterogeneity corresponds to changes in projection geometry (analogous to different camera viewpoints), so learning layout-agnostic spatiotemporal dynamics from video priors enables generalization across montages and subjects without channel-indexed correspondence.

## Results

On subject-level few-shot learning, BSR-VideoMAE achieves the best Balanced Accuracy across all four datasets: 58.15% on SEED (vs. 52.63% for LaBraM), 20.54% on SEED-VII (vs. 19.28% for BIOT), 59.59% on SHU-MI (vs. 54.56% for BIOT), and 32.75% on BCIC-IV-2a (vs. 29.35% for FFCL). Under full-dataset fine-tuning, the method reaches 31.55% B-Acc on SEED-V (vs. 27.19% for BIOT) and 66.08% B-Acc on SHU-MI (vs. 64.21% for LaBraM). The largest absolute gains appear on SEED (+5.5% B-Acc over LaBraM) and SHU-MI few-shot (+5.0% over BIOT), confirming that the BSR rendering framework provides the strongest few-shot adaptation signal.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BSR-VideoMAE outperforms all five baselines on every dataset and metric in the subject-level few-shot setting. The closest competitor is LaBraM on SEED (52.63% vs. 58.15% B-Acc, a 5.5-point gap) and on SHU-MI full fine-tuning (64.21% vs. 66.08% B-Acc); on SEED-VII and BCIC-IV-2a, BIOT is the nearest rival but trails by 1.3 and 3.4 points respectively. FFCL, ContraWR, and C-Trans are consistently the weakest baselines, with ContraWR achieving the lowest scores on most metrics (e.g., 15.69% B-Acc on SEED-VII, 28.43% on BCIC-IV-2a). Notably, LaBraM's SEED few-shot result is inflated because its official pre-training already includes SEED data, making the true generalization gap even larger. In full-dataset fine-tuning, BSR-VideoMAE leads on SEED-V by 4.4 points over BIOT (31.55% vs. 27.19% B-Acc) and on SHU-MI by 1.9 points over LaBraM (66.08% vs. 64.21%), while also achieving the best AUROC (73.24%) and AU-PR (73.96%) on SHU-MI.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on VideoMAE (Tong et al., 2022) as its encoder backbone but departs fundamentally from the channel-indexed EEG modeling paradigm adopted by recent foundation models (LaBraM, BIOT, CBraMod, CSBrain, EEGPT). The key conceptual departure is the Brain Signal Rendering (BSR) framework, which reinterprets EEG as a physical projection of 3D neural activity onto a 2D sensor array—treating electrode heterogeneity as a change in projection geometry rather than feature mismatch—and renders STFT spectra into spatiotemporal 'Spectrum Videos' compatible with video foundation models. This extends earlier EEG-to-visual approaches (Bashivan et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2021b) by coupling them with self-supervised video pre-training, and introduces subject-level few-shot learning and cross-montage fine-tuning as new evaluation paradigms that go beyond the fixed-montage, full-dataset fine-tuning protocols used by prior EEG foundation model benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The BSR framework addresses a long-standing practical barrier in EEG: the inability of models to generalize across heterogeneous electrode layouts, which limits real-world BCI deployment. The cross-montage fine-tuning paradigm and subject-level few-shot benchmark provide the field with more rigorous, deployment-realistic evaluation tools that prior work lacked. Open questions include whether the rendering step can be made more computationally efficient, whether alternative spectral or band-feature renderings could further improve representations, and whether the approach scales to very large multi-site EEG corpora. The finding that the rendering transformation itself (rather than pre-training scale) drives most few-shot gains suggests the representation geometry may be more important than data volume in low-resource EEG settings.
