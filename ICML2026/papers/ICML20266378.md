## Problem

Existing interpretability methods for deep time series models expose internal computations (attention weights, saliency, surrogate rules) but do not guarantee that the model's variables and dynamics correspond to the concepts through which domain experts reason. The paper identifies this as a distinct form of opacity—semantic opacity—and notes that the field lacks both a formal definition of semantic alignment for temporal settings and a blueprint for achieving it, particularly the requirement that alignment be preserved under temporal evolution, a constraint with no analog in static domains.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a window of raw observations x_{≤t} (optionally with exogenous inputs) and, when available, ground-truth concept labels c_t^{(k)} at the temporal resolution at which each concept is defined (window-level or timestamp-level). It produces a task prediction ŷ (forecast window, class label, or scalar) together with aligned concept representations: instantaneous concepts U_t from the encoder and dynamic concepts Z_{t+1} from the propagation module. The temporal resolution of concept propagation need not match the input sampling rate, and concepts may evolve discretely (recurrent updates) or continuously (differential equations).

## Method

The paper formalizes semantic alignment (SA) as two conditions: (i) the encoder must map observations to representations that equal instantaneous concepts C_t^U conditional on x_{≤t}, and (ii) the propagation mechanism must produce representations that equal dynamic concepts C_{t+1}^Z predicted from x_{≤t}, with the second condition being unique to temporal settings and necessary to prevent exponential decay of alignment over time. Building on this, the paper proposes a blueprint extending concept-based models to time series with three stages: concept encoding (mapping observations to source concepts), concept propagation (temporal and spatio-temporal mechanisms evolving derived concepts forward), and optional task decoding. Training uses a three-term objective L = αL_task + βL_concept + γL_prop, where L_prop supervises propagated concepts against ground truth at future timesteps to enforce temporal consistency. Mechanism alignment is framed as a constraint-satisfaction problem: the admissible set of conditional probability distributions M^{(h)} is restricted by user-specified properties (monotonicity, linearity, physical laws), and the paper advocates composing primitive modules with known properties to preserve expressivity while satisfying constraints by construction.

## Results

This is a position paper that does not report quantitative experimental results. The authors formalize semantic alignment (SA) for deep time series models (Definition 1 and 2), propose a blueprint for semantically aligned architectures (Sec. 4), and provide a qualitative assessment (Table 1) of how existing interpretability paradigms fare against the SA criteria. No benchmarks, accuracy numbers, or ablation studies are presented.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not compare a proposed method against baselines on a shared benchmark. Instead, it critically evaluates existing interpretability paradigms (post-hoc methods such as input importance, surrogates, counterfactuals, LLM explanations, and mechanistic interpretability; and intrinsic methods such as attention, Koopman/linearization, symbolic regression, prototype-based models, physics-informed models, and time series primitives) against the proposed SA criteria. The authors argue that all post-hoc methods fail to satisfy any SA property, while intrinsic methods at best partially satisfy mechanism alignment (e.g., symbolic regression, physics-informed models) or dynamic concept alignment (e.g., Koopman/linearization), but none simultaneously achieve full alignment of instantaneous concepts, dynamic concepts, and mechanisms. The paper positions its blueprint as the first framework to address all three alignment dimensions jointly in the temporal setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Concept Bottleneck Model (CBM) paradigm from static domains (primarily computer vision) to temporal settings, building directly on Koh et al. (2020) and recent causal extensions (Dominici et al., 2025; De Felice et al., 2025) that model dependencies among concepts via directed graphs. It departs from the broader time series interpretability literature—post-hoc attribution, attention-based methods, mechanistic interpretability, and physics-informed models—by arguing these address structural opacity rather than the semantic opacity that prevents domain experts from validating or intervening on model behavior. The key conceptual departure is formalizing semantic alignment (SA) as a two-part requirement (concept alignment + mechanism alignment) with an additional temporal preservation constraint (Eq. 3) that has no analog in static settings, positioning SA as a structural design requirement rather than a post-hoc property.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a genuine gap: the time series community lacks a formal, operationalizable definition of what it means for a model to be interpretable to a domain expert, and existing methods do not guarantee that internal representations correspond to human-meaningful concepts. The temporal preservation requirement (SA must hold under concept evolution, not just at each timestep) is a non-trivial constraint that, if violated, causes alignment to decay exponentially—a problem absent from static CBMs. As a position paper with no empirical validation, the blueprint remains a starting point; open questions include how to enforce mechanism alignment while preserving expressivity, how to handle incomplete concept sets without accuracy loss in the temporal setting, and how to integrate test-time interventions with online/continual learning. The framework is most impactful for high-stakes domains (healthcare, finance, industrial monitoring) where regulatory and safety requirements demand interpretable models.
