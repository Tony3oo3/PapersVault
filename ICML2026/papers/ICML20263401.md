## Problem

Data selection for Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) remains underexplored, and no existing method outperforms random selection at various subset sizes. The core challenge is that gradient similarity in billion-parameter LVLMs is prohibitively expensive to compute, gradients evolve during training, and the modality gap between image and text embeddings makes full-gradient similarity ineffective.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263401/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a VIT dataset of (image, instruction, answer) triples, a small proxy LVLM (e.g., TinyLLaVA-2B), and a target data budget B. It fine-tunes the proxy for one epoch, extracting cross-modal alignment trajectories (top-5 singular values of summed cross-modal attention matrices) at T=7 checkpoints for every example. The output is a balanced subset S of size B, where each of K=1000 K-means clusters contributes an equal number of the most stable examples (lowest cumulative oscillation in alignment score), yielding a non-redundant coreset for training the target LVLM.

## Method

XMAS is grounded in two theoretical results: (1) pairwise gradient distance between examples at any checkpoint is upper-bounded by the Frobenius distance between their cross-modal attention matrices (Theorem 4.2), and (2) under bounded Hessian curvature during fine-tuning, similar attention matrices at two checkpoints imply similar gradients at all intermediate checkpoints (Theorem 4.3). Practically, XMAS fine-tunes a small proxy LVLM, tracks the trajectory of the top-5 singular values of each example's cross-modal attention matrix (summed across all layers) at T checkpoints, clusters these trajectories via K-means to identify groups of examples with similar gradient dynamics, and then samples a balanced subset by selecting the most stable examples (minimum instability score, defined as total oscillation in alignment score) from each cluster. This avoids expensive full-gradient computation, requires no validation set, and is the first method to theoretically guarantee that the selected subset converges to a neighborhood of the full-data solution.

## Results

XMAS is the only method that consistently outperforms random selection across all data budgets on both LLaVA-665k and Vision-Flan. On LLaVA-665k, XMAS achieves 95.4% Average Relative Performance (ARP) at 10% data, 99.2% at 30%, and reaches 100.0% ARP at 50% data (i.e., full-dataset performance with half the data), yielding a 1.2× end-to-end speedup. On Vision-Flan, XMAS matches full-data performance (100.2% ARP) using only 15% of the data (85% reduction). This represents 30% more data reduction than the best baseline (COINCIDE) on LLaVA-665k.

## Baselines & Comparisons

XMAS outperforms all 10 baselines at every budget on both datasets. Against the strongest baseline COINCIDE (Lee et al., 2024), XMAS leads by 1.3% ARP at 10% of LLaVA-665k (95.4 vs. 94.1) and by 1.2% at 50% (100.0 vs. 98.8); on Vision-Flan the gap widens to 3.6% at 5% (96.1 vs. 92.5) and 1.9% at 10% (98.9 vs. 97.0). Gradient-norm-based EL2N (Paul et al., 2021) is competitive at 50% of LLaVA-665k (99.3 vs. 100.0) but degrades sharply on Vision-Flan (87.4% at 15%). Heuristic LLM-based methods—Middle Perplexity (Marion et al., 2023), Highest Learnability (Zhou et al., 2024), and D2 Pruning (Maharana et al., 2023)—all fall 2–8% below XMAS at 10% on LLaVA-665k. LVLM-specific methods Self-Filter (Chen et al., 2024) and CLIP-Score (Hessel et al., 2021) underperform random selection at small budgets (89.3% and 86.1% vs. 93.5% at 10%), confirming that single-metric heuristics fail for multimodal data. SemDeDup (Abbas et al., 2023) and Self-Sup (Sorscher et al., 2022), designed for deduplication and embedding-space centrality, also trail XMAS by 4–9% at 10% on LLaVA-665k.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the data-efficient training literature from LLMs (where heuristics like EL2N, middle perplexity, and SemDeDup have been explored) to Large Vision-Language Models, where the authors show that none of these transfer successfully. It departs from the most recent LVLM-specific methods—COINCIDE (single-checkpoint activation clustering) and TIVE (gradient/influence-based selection)—by replacing both with a theoretically grounded signal: the trajectory of top singular values of cross-modal attention matrices tracked across multiple checkpoints of a small proxy model. The theoretical core (Theorems 4.2–4.3) is novel in bounding pairwise gradient distances via cross-modal attention distances for a one-layer transformer with RMS normalization, connecting the proxy-to-target transferability of attention (observed empirically by Zhao et al., 2024) to a formal gradient-similarity guarantee.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

XMAS is the first data selection method for LVLM instruction tuning that consistently outperforms random selection across budgets and datasets, and it achieves 50% data reduction on LLaVA-665k (and 85% on Vision-Flan) with a 1.2× end-to-end speedup while preserving full benchmark performance—practically significant for teams training LVLMs at scale. Key limitations include the reliance on a proxy model whose cross-modal attention must transfer to the target (empirically validated but not guaranteed for arbitrary architecture pairs), the theoretical analysis being restricted to a one-layer transformer with Frobenius loss, and the assumption of small Hessian curvature during fine-tuning. Open questions include whether the approach extends to pre-training or continued pre-training of LVLMs, whether it generalizes to video or audio modalities, and whether the K-means clustering step can be replaced by more scalable alternatives for datasets exceeding millions of examples.
