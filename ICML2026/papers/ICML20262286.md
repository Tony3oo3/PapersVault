## Problem

Learning high-dimensional subset parity functions (XOR over an unknown k-sized subset of N inputs) via gradient descent is intractable in general: standard architectures require exponential sample complexity, and the optimization landscape provides no useful intermediate signal, forcing an all-or-nothing learning regime. The paper asks under what architectural and data-distribution conditions gradient descent can provably and efficiently recover the parity subset, and answers this by combining product-based inductive bias with stochastic input sparsity.

## Input / Output

The model consumes binary input vectors x ∈ {0,1}^N generated by a Bernoulli process where each coordinate is independently set to 1 with probability p_e (typically p_e = 1/N, yielding one active bit on average). Labels are produced by an oracle using the same product-node architecture with fixed binary weights w^true ∈ {0,1}^N. The trained model outputs a parity value in {0,1} per input, and the learned continuous weight vector w ∈ R^N (converging to 0 or 1 per coordinate) identifies which inputs participate in the parity. Multiple parallel XOR units (P units) are trained simultaneously to study statistical behavior, with the loss normalized by batch size M only so per-node gradients remain independent of P.

## Method

The core architecture is a product node with a neutral element, computing ∏(w_i x_i + (1−w_i)), which for binary inputs exactly implements XOR over the subset where w_i = 1 while avoiding the vanishing-product pathology of naive multiplicative units. Two complementary inductive biases enable learning: (1) the product structure provides architectural generalization—once the support is identified, the node computes the correct parity on all 2^N inputs by algebraic construction, requiring only O(N) sparse samples rather than O(2^N); and (2) stochastic Bernoulli sparsity at p_e = 1/N eliminates multiplicative cross-term interactions, making the expected gradient proportional to (w − w^true) and rendering the optimization landscape effectively convex. Training uses plain SGD with MSE loss and symmetric Gaussian initialization N(1/2, 1/4); the theory proves that under this regime, Gaussianity and symmetry of weight distributions are preserved across steps, variance decays geometrically, and the mean converges to an O(1/N) interval around the target for learning rates α < α_2 = O(N) with N ≥ 18. Experiments validate convergence up to N = 100,000 and reveal empirical scaling laws: optimal sparsity p_e* ≈ 2.25/N, convergence limit p_e^lim ≈ 7/N, and learning-rate limit α^lim ≈ N/2, with convergence speed governed by the effective rate α·p_e.

## Results

Under the sparse regime (p_e = 1/N), the product node converges to 100% validation accuracy within 1,000 training steps at less than 2% truth table coverage for N=16, confirming that identifying the parity support suffices for full generalization across all 2^N inputs. Theoretical convergence is guaranteed for N ≥ 18 with learning rate α < α₂ = O(N), with the convergence interval scaling as O(1/N) + O(α/N). Empirical scaling laws validated across N ∈ [10, 1000] show α^lim ≈ N/2 and p_e^* ≈ 2.25/N, with convergence confirmed up to N = 100,000. The effective learning rate α·p_e governs convergence speed, enabling scale-invariant training when hyperparameters are properly scaled.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its product node against the 3-layer MLP architecture (512-512-64, ReLU) from Abbe et al. (2025) under identical SGD training conditions (N=16, M=100, α=0.02, 250 independent models). Under the sparse regime (p_e = 1/N), the product node achieves 100% validation accuracy within ~1,000 steps at <2% truth table coverage, while the MLP overfits: its training accuracy rises quickly but validation accuracy stagnates near 50% (random-guess level), only beginning to improve once a large fraction of the truth table is observed. Under uniform sampling of the full truth table, the MLP eventually converges but requires near-exhaustive coverage, while the product node converges more slowly, confirming that sparsity is a key enabler of efficient optimization for the product architecture. The fundamental gap is in sample complexity: the product node requires O(N) sparse samples for full generalization, whereas the MLP requires O(2^N) samples, a gap that widens exponentially with dimension and becomes intractable for large N.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of work on parity learning that relies on additive architectures (MLPs) combined with specialized data distributions, curriculum schedules, or careful initialization (Abbe et al., 2023, 2025; Cornacchia & Mossel, 2023; Daniely & Malach, 2020). Instead, it introduces a product-based XOR node with a neutral element—drawing inspiration from neural arithmetic units (Madsen & Johansen, 2020; Trask et al., 2018)—whose algebraic structure directly mirrors parity computation, and pairs it with stochastic Bernoulli sparsity as the sole data-distribution assumption. The theoretical framework (oracle-based convergence analysis under p_e = 1/N) is novel; prior work either proved impossibility results for gradient descent on parities (Abbe & Sandon, 2019; Reyzin, 2020) or required stronger structural assumptions on the data (perfect correlation on the support, curriculum schedules, Rademacher initialization). The motivation traces to neural decoders for error-correcting codes (Nachmani et al., 2018; Larue et al., 2022; Dufrène et al., 2025), where parity functions are prevalent and sparsity is natural.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first provable convergence guarantee for standard gradient descent on a product-based architecture for high-dimensional subset parity, resolving a long-standing gap between representability and learnability. The broader principle—that multiplicative architectures with sparse, structured inputs can transform traditionally intractable Boolean computations into polynomially tractable ones—suggests a design template for integrating arithmetic and parity operations into deep networks beyond the specific parity task. Key limitations include the oracle-based analysis (true labels generated by the same function), the requirement for symmetric Gaussian initialization, and the sparsity condition p_e ≤ 1/N, which may not hold in all practical settings. Open questions include whether the approach extends to non-oracle (noisy) labels, to compositions of multiple parity layers, and to continuous-valued multiplicative computations.
