## Problem

Current machine learning approaches to heart transplant allocation treat the problem as a static optimization over patient features, overlooking that the system is a strategic game in which clinicians, organ procurement organizations, transplant centers, and patients all respond to policy incentives. The paper documents that these incentive misalignments—device gaming, out-of-sequence allocation, strategic offer rejection, multi-listing, and strategic preference reporting—are already causing measurable harm (e.g., IABP bridging tripled after the 2018 policy change; out-of-sequence kidney allocations rose from 2% to 18% between 2020 and 2023), and that training predictive models on historical data without accounting for concomitant strategic shifts risks producing models that fail in deployment.

## Input / Output

The allocation system consumes patient clinical features, device utilization status, accrued waitlist time, donor organ viability indicators, center performance metrics, and stakeholder preference data to produce priority status tiers, organ allocation decisions, semi-annual center performance ratings, and policy weight parameters. A critical distinction from standard supervised learning settings is that the inputs are endogenous: clinicians can manipulate device utilization to inflate priority, OPOs can choose to bypass the priority queue, and centers can strategically reject offers or delist patients, so the data distribution shifts in response to the very policy being learned, creating a performative prediction problem.

## Method

Rather than proposing a single algorithm, the paper constructs a diagnostic framework mapping five specific incentive failures across the allocation pipeline to corresponding machine learning and mechanism design tools. For feature manipulation (the 'device game'), it advocates strategic classification with performative prediction and causal inference to identify non-manipulable urgency features, supplemented by selective verification via randomized audits. For out-of-sequence allocation, it calls for hard constraint-based triggers replacing OPO discretion, coupled with real-time organ viability assessment via computer vision during ex-vivo perfusion. For performance monitoring distortions, it recommends continuous monitoring replacing semi-annual cycles and credit score systems to limit strategic offer rejection. For preference elicitation, it argues for mechanism-design-guided, frugal preference elicitation focused on normative ends rather than instrumental means, drawing on social choice theory and RLHF. The empirical grounding uses UNOS adult heart transplant data (2010–2024) to quantify each failure mode, including mortality timing, offer acceptance rates by center size, multi-listing outcomes, and seasonal transplant volume patterns.

## Results

The paper presents empirical evidence of incentive misalignments in US heart transplant allocation (2010–2024 UNOS data). Key findings include: IABP bridging rates tripled from 7.0% to 24.9% after the 2018 policy change; out-of-sequence allocations surged from ~1% historically to ~19% across all organs in 2023 (kidneys: 2%→18% from 2020–2023), then dropped to 9% after federal scrutiny in 2025; multi-listed candidates (2.16% of patients) achieve an 80.44% transplant rate versus 73.06% for single-listed patients; and among highest-urgency (status 1) candidates, median time to transplant (26 days) precedes median time to death (36 days) by only 10 days, with 13.7% of deaths occurring within the first week of listing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper and does not compare ML methods against baselines; instead, it presents empirical evidence from UNOS data to motivate incentive-aware policy design. The strongest empirical contrasts are: (1) the IABP bridging rate increase (7.0%→24.9%) following the 2018 tier change, demonstrating Goodhart's-law-style manipulation of device utilization; (2) the out-of-sequence allocation rate jumping from ~1% to 19% after OPOs faced wastage scrutiny, then halving to 9% after direct federal intervention in 2025, showing rapid strategic response to monitoring changes; (3) multi-listed patients achieving a 7.4-percentage-point higher transplant rate (80.44% vs. 73.06%, p<0.001) with median wait dropping from 61 to 503.5 days overall but only 86 days after the second listing activates; and (4) small centers accepting offers at 1.25% versus 0.85% for large centers, with significantly higher waitlist mortality (6.94% vs. 6.01%), illustrating how performance monitoring creates asymmetric risk-aversion across center sizes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not build on a single prior method but rather synthesizes ideas from strategic classification (Hardt et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2020), performative prediction (Perdomo et al., 2020), mechanism design (selective verification, credit score systems), social choice theory (Gibbard-Satterthwaite), and the kidney exchange literature (Roth et al., 2005; Ashlagi et al., 2015; Hajaj et al., 2015) to argue that organ allocation policy optimization must be reframed as a game rather than a static optimization problem. It departs from the current ML-for-allocation paradigm (e.g., continuous distribution scoring in Papalexopoulos et al., 2023) by identifying that these approaches train on historical data without accounting for the strategic distribution shifts that policy changes themselves induce. The paper also draws on causal inference and RLHF as potential tools, positioning itself at the intersection of multiple subfields rather than extending any single one.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper documents concrete, data-backed evidence of systemic incentive failures (IABP bridging tripling from 7% to 24.9% post-2018; out-of-sequence kidney allocations jumping from 2% to 18% between 2020–2023) that challenge the assumption that better predictive models alone will improve allocation outcomes. It lays out a research agenda spanning incentive-aware model training, randomized audits, continuous monitoring to replace semi-annual evaluation cycles, and mechanism-design-guided preference elicitation, but leaves most of these as open problems without implementations. Key open questions include how to adapt strategic classification to survival analysis, how to design incentive-compatible regional sub-policies for out-of-sequence allocation, and how to elicit community values without conflating means with ends. The paper's broader implication—that algorithmic sophistication without incentive awareness risks creating models that fail in practice—has relevance beyond organ allocation to any domain where ML-driven policies interact with strategic agents.
