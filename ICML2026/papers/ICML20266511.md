## Problem

The ML community increasingly relies on post-hoc opt-out methods (inference-time guardrails, machine unlearning) to argue legal compliance, operating on an implicit 'output equivalence' assumption that a model producing clean outputs is functionally identical to one never trained on problematic data. This paper identifies a critical epistemic gap: legal liability for unauthorized data acquisition and training ingestion attaches at the moment of copying, not at output generation, making post-hoc fixes legally irrelevant to the underlying infringement. The disconnect between engineering metrics and legal doctrine leaves the AI industry exposed to existential litigation risk based on a fundamental misconception about when and how liability is established.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the analysis takes as input the current landscape of post-hoc opt-out techniques (inference-time guardrails such as Llama Guard, parameter modification methods such as SISA and ROME) alongside the relevant statutory and case-law doctrines across US, EU, and Japanese jurisdictions. It produces a three-part legal argument demonstrating the incurability of training-stage infringement, together with a concrete ex-ante process compliance framework specifying cryptographic provenance mechanisms (C2PA credentials, zero-knowledge proofs) and architectural reversibility protocols (checkpoint-based rebranching).

## Method

The paper constructs a three-part doctrinal argument grounded in strict-liability copyright theory. First, it establishes the 'completed-act doctrine': infringement is a fait accompli at the instant of unauthorized reproduction (t=0), and model weights satisfy both the fixation and machine-aided perceptibility requirements of 17 U.S.C. §101, so suppressing outputs cannot retroactively un-commit the act. Second, it demonstrates that independent contract (ProCD, Ryanair) and tort/unfair-competition (Tsubasa System, CV-Online) liabilities constrain data acquisition regardless of copyright defenses, creating an inescapable web of exposure that fair-use findings on training cannot displace. Third, it argues that unjust enrichment and disgorgement doctrines (Sheldon apportionment, Head Start) require stripping algorithmic gains, potentially reaching the model itself via algorithmic disgorgement. The paper then proposes ex-ante process compliance through a 'Glass Pipeline' (C2PA positive provenance verification combined with ZKP negative verification of restricted-work exclusion) and 'Architectural Reversibility' (high-frequency checkpointing enabling surgical rebranching from pre-ingestion states rather than post-hoc unlearning), alongside policy recommendations for centralized Data Trusts and a mandatory Opt-In 'Binary Beacon' rights-declaration regime.

## Results

This is a position paper with no empirical experiments or quantitative results. The paper's core contribution is a three-part legal argument: (1) unauthorized data ingestion constitutes a completed act of infringement at t=0 that cannot be retroactively cured by post-hoc opt-out; (2) contract and tort/unfair-competition liabilities independently constrain training regardless of copyright fair-use defenses; and (3) unjust enrichment and disgorgement doctrines may require stripping algorithmic gains, potentially reaching the model weights themselves. The paper advocates a paradigm shift from post-hoc opt-out to verifiable ex-ante process compliance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not compare methods empirically. Instead, it engages three legal counterarguments raised by the ML community: (1) the dataset-creation-as-fair-use argument (relying on Authors Guild v. Google), which the authors rebut by distinguishing complementary search indexing from market-substitutive generative AI; (2) the data-ingestion-as-fair-use argument (relying on Sega v. Accolade), which the authors reject because AI training copies protected expression for its expressive value rather than unprotected functional elements out of necessity; and (3) the public-interest/transaction-costs argument, which the authors counter by noting that collective licensing mechanisms exist and that uncompensated extraction undermines long-term ecosystem sustainability. The paper also contrasts its position with Marino et al. (2025), who frame machine unlearning as a potential compliance tool, arguing instead that unlearning cannot retroactively cure training-stage violations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of ML engineering and copyright law by systematically arguing that the engineering community's 'output equivalence' assumption (if a model no longer produces infringing outputs, it is compliant) is legally incoherent. It repurposes existing legal doctrines—completed-act strict liability, fixation/perceptibility requirements, unjust enrichment, and the head-start doctrine—to analyze the ontological status of model weights, while drawing on the technical taxonomy of post-hoc opt-out methods (SISA, ROME, Llama Guard, gradient-based unlearning) without endorsing their legal sufficiency. It explicitly departs from Marino et al. (2025), which frames machine unlearning as a potential regulation-aligned compliance tool, by arguing that for training-stage violations, no post-hoc method can retroactively cure the infringement regardless of technical quality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that liability attaches at the moment of unauthorized copying and is incurable by subsequent technical patches—challenges the industry's dominant compliance strategy and could reshape how foundation models are built (shifting toward verifiable ex-ante data lineage via C2PA, ZKPs, and checkpoint-based rebranching). Key open questions include whether courts will actually adopt the 'completed act' framing for iterative training, whether the fixation/perceptibility analysis of high-dimensional weight matrices will survive judicial scrutiny, and whether the contract/tort layers (ProCD, Ryanair, Yomiuri Shimbun) will be enforced as broadly as the paper argues across jurisdictions. As a position paper with no empirical validation, its practical impact hinges on whether the legal arguments gain traction in the ongoing 2025 litigation landscape.
