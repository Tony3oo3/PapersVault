## Problem

Instruction-based image editing models require users to design precise, sequential editing instructions, placing the burden of task decomposition, algorithm selection, and iterative refinement entirely on the user. Existing agent-based approaches either perform only low-level parameter adjustments (color, contrast, exposure) or execute linear action sequences without feedback, making them unsuitable for autonomous, semantically meaningful multi-step photo editing aligned with human aesthetic preferences.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265496/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a single RGB photograph as input, optionally accompanied by a free-form text prompt that can range from a vague instruction ("make this image better") to an abstract mood or atmosphere description. It produces a single aesthetically enhanced image after multiple closed-loop editing iterations. The perceiver outputs 2–6 atomic editing steps in strict JSON format per round, and the system terminates when the evaluator detects no significant score improvement over N consecutive iterations or a maximum step limit is reached, preventing over-editing of already high-quality images.

## Method

PhotoAgent formulates autonomous photo editing as a long-horizon sequential decision-making problem solved via a closed-loop perceive–plan–execute–evaluate cycle. A VLM-based perceiver (Qwen3-VL) generates K diverse atomic editing actions using a structured, scene-aware prompting scheme that encodes edit-tolerance levels per semantic role and prioritizes content modification over parameter adjustments. An MCTS planner then explores candidate editing trajectories in a reduced-resolution simulation environment, using the UCT policy to balance exploration and exploitation across four phases (selection, expansion, simulation, backpropagation), with a learned UGC-specific reward model (trained via GRPO on the 7,000-image UGC-Edit dataset) providing the reward signal. The executor applies the top-K selected actions using either traditional operators (OpenCV/PIL) or generative models (Flux.1 Kontext, Step1X-Edit), and an ensemble evaluator (combining BRISQUE, NIQE, CLIP similarity, ImageReward, and the UGC reward model) scores each result, retaining only the highest-scoring output as the next state. A memory module records editing history and failure cases across rounds, injecting them as natural-language lessons into subsequent perceiver prompts to enable cross-round learning without retraining.

## Results

On the 1,017-image real-world photo editing benchmark, PhotoAgent achieves the best BRISQUE score (0.6217, lower is better) and the highest CLIP Similarity (0.6254), while also ranking top-2 on all five metrics simultaneously. Its UGC Score of 4.176 is competitive with GPT-4o (4.210) but with substantially better structural fidelity (BRISQUE 0.6217 vs. 0.7215). In a user study with 20 participants and 540 preference votes, PhotoAgent is preferred 42.0% of the time, compared to 30.2% for GPT-4o, 15.2% for ReAct (closed-loop), and 12.6% for HuggingGPT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PhotoAgent outperforms all six baselines on BRISQUE (0.6217 vs. best baseline 0.6422 for ReAct closed-loop) and CLIP Similarity (0.6254 vs. best baseline 0.6123 for InstructPix2Pix), confirming superior structural fidelity and content preservation. Against the strongest single-step editor GPT-4o, PhotoAgent trades a small UGC Score gap (4.176 vs. 4.210) and ImageReward gap (0.4079 vs. 0.4115) for a large BRISQUE advantage (0.6217 vs. 0.7215) and CLIP Similarity advantage (0.6254 vs. 0.6015), indicating GPT-4o over-edits with exaggerated contrast and color at the cost of image distortion. Among agent-based baselines, PhotoAgent dominates HuggingGPT and both ReAct variants on every metric, with the largest margins on UGC Score (4.176 vs. 3.420/3.258/3.175) and BRISQUE (0.6217 vs. 0.6992/0.6422/0.6485), demonstrating that MCTS-based exploratory planning with closed-loop evaluation substantially outperforms single-call or greedy sequential strategies. The user study further confirms these quantitative findings, with PhotoAgent receiving 42.0% of preference votes versus 30.2% for GPT-4o and under 16% for the agent baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

PhotoAgent sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) instruction-based image editing models (InstructPix2Pix, SDXL, Flux.1 Kontext, GPT-4o) that perform single-step edits conditioned on natural language; (2) LLM/VLM-based agent frameworks for vision tasks (HuggingGPT, ReAct, JarvisArt, MonetGPT) that decompose tasks into tool calls; and (3) MCTS-based planning from game AI (AlphaGo, Chaslot et al.) applied to sequential decision-making. The key departure from prior agent-based editing work (JarvisArt, MonetGPT) is twofold: PhotoAgent targets semantically meaningful content modifications (adding objects, changing composition, modifying scene elements via generative models) rather than low-level retouching (color, tone, exposure via procedural tools), and it replaces open-loop or simple iterative planning with a full MCTS tree search that simulates future editing trajectories before committing to an action. The UGC-Edit dataset and GRPO-trained reward model address a gap identified in the evaluation literature: existing aesthetic metrics (FID, CLIP scores, generic aesthetic predictors) are trained on web-scraped or AI-generated images and fail to capture the fine-grained quality differences relevant to authentic user-captured photographs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper makes a practical case that autonomous multi-step editing requires explicit planning rather than relying on a single powerful executor, supported by the finding that even the most advanced closed-source single-step models (Grok-Imagine, Nano-Banana2) exhibit characteristic failure modes—over-enhancement and unnecessary subject alteration—that a planning loop with evaluation feedback can avoid. The UGC-Edit dataset (~7,000 photos) and its associated reward model fill a concrete gap in the aesthetic evaluation toolchain for user-generated content, and the 1,017-image test set provides a reusable benchmark for the autonomous editing task. However, the ~470-second per-image runtime under default settings limits near-term practical deployment, and the system still struggles with dark/low-quality inputs and can over-edit already-good images. Open questions include whether the MCTS planning overhead can be amortized for batch processing, how the framework extends to domain-specific editing (medical, scientific, non-photorealistic), and whether the reward model's generalization (SRCC ~0.75 on PARA) is sufficient for truly diverse aesthetic preferences.
