## Problem

Existing concept-based interpretability methods struggle to trace how generic visual concepts (faces, places, bodies, words, food) emerge across layers in deep vision models, due to neuron polysemanticity and the opacity of latent-space vector directions. No prior approach provides an objective, biologically grounded probe to identify which sparse features in a model correspond to specific visual functions and at which layer they first appear.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263219/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes fMRI beta values from the NSD dataset (8 subjects viewing 10,000 COCO natural scene images at 7T), per-layer activations from four ViT backbones (CLIP ViT-B/16, DINOv2 ViT-B/14, MAE ViT-B/16, ImageNet ViT-B/16), and ImageNet-1K test images for feature validation. It produces, for each of five high-level ROIs (FFA, EBA, RSC, VWFA, Food), a ranked list of SAE features per layer with stability scores, a voxel-to-layer cortical map, and quantitative alignment metrics (cosine similarity up to 0.72, RSA up to 0.69). The fMRI signals are subject-specific and z-scored across runs, while SAEs are trained on ImageNet-1K and evaluated on the independent 9,000-image NSD split.

## Method

SAEs-BrainMap inverts the standard brain-encoder paradigm by using human brain ROI activations as objective probes to select model features. Layer-wise SAEs (k=16d sparse features) are trained on each ViT layer's CLS and patch tokens via reconstruction loss with L1 sparsity regularization. For each ROI, the top-100 SAE features with highest mean cosine similarity to the ROI's fMRI signal are extracted per layer, then validated for semantic stability via CLIP text-prompt classification (threshold S>0.5) and functional importance via ablation (zeroing selected features and measuring normalized MSE loss). Structural alignment is quantified with RSA comparing representational similarity matrices of brain voxels and their best-matching SAE features. Finally, each cortical voxel is assigned to the model layer yielding maximum activation correlation, producing a global layer-to-cortex map that reveals the hierarchical processing flow of the model.

## Results

SAEs-BrainMap achieves a peak cosine similarity of 0.7247 (CLIP CLS SAEs) and a peak RSA score of 0.686 (DINOv2 CLS SAEs) between model features and fMRI signals across the ventral visual pathway. SAE features consistently outperform raw model neurons in activation similarity across all four architectures (e.g., CLIP: 0.7247 vs. 0.5875 max cosine; DINOv2: 0.7172 vs. 0.6961). The functional aligned feature ratio reaches up to 1.00 (MAE VWFA), confirming that ROI-selected SAE features reliably encode the corresponding brain region's semantic selectivity. Cross-subject layer mapping consistency averages 0.89 cosine similarity for ViT-B/16 CLIP between Subjects 1 and 5.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CLIP-Dissect (raw neuron text-matching) and CLIP-SAEs (SAE feature text-matching), SAEs-BrainMap's brain-guided selection identifies face-related selective features at earlier layers with stronger semantic coherence, suggesting that category-selective brain ROIs provide a complementary functional probe beyond language-aligned visual concepts. For layer-to-cortex mapping, SAE-BrainMap shows moderate-to-strong distributional correlation with both FactorTopy/Brain Decodes Deep Nets (avg 0.69–0.70 for CLIP and ImageNet) and Max R² brain-encoder mapping (avg 0.68–0.84 across models), validating its direct-activation approach against these indirect predictive paradigms. The key advantage over raw neurons is consistent: SAEs improve max cosine similarity by 0.08–0.14 and RSA by 0.03–0.11 across all four architectures, demonstrating that sparse decomposition is necessary for meaningful brain-model alignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper inverts the paradigm of recent brain-model alignment work (BrainSCUBA, BrainACTIV, BrainExplore), which uses model latent representations to interpret brain function, by instead using brain ROI activations as objective probes to select and characterize SAE-decomposed model features. It builds directly on the Sparse Autoencoder framework of Huben et al. (2024) for monosemantic feature extraction, and on RSA (Kriegeskorte et al., 2008) for structural comparison, but departs from prior concept-based interpretability methods (Network Dissection, CLIP-Dissect, VCC) by grounding feature selection in biological functional priors rather than text prompts or neuron-level heuristics. The approach also contrasts with Brain Encoder-based predictive alignment (Wang et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024) by performing direct activation comparison rather than indirect prediction-based mapping.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that brain fMRI signals can serve as an objective, data-driven probe for identifying functionally meaningful SAE features, offering a complementary interpretability axis to text-based and neuron-based methods. The layer-wise concept emergence trajectories and cortical layer mapping provide a biologically grounded narrative of hierarchical visual processing in ViTs. However, the evaluation remains coarse (top-100 features, top-20 images, single stability threshold), the analysis is confined to Vanilla SAEs and five high-level ROIs, and the interpretability ceiling is inherently bounded by the known selectivities of the human ventral visual pathway. Open questions include whether more advanced SAE variants (TopK, Archetypal) improve alignment, whether the approach generalizes beyond vision to language or multimodal models, and whether quantitative cross-model comparison metrics can replace the current qualitative visualizations.
