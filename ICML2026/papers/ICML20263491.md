## Problem

Mean Field Variational Inference (MFVI) is widely understood to underestimate posterior variance, but this characterization has been studied almost exclusively in parameter space. The paper asks what happens when the goal shifts from parameter inference to prediction: does MFVI still underestimate uncertainty on the data that actually matters? The answer is no—MFVI can overestimate predictive variance on in-distribution data, a phenomenon that was previously uncharacterized and that provides a new mechanistic explanation for the Cold Posterior Effect.

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on conjugate Bayesian Linear Regression with a spherical Gaussian prior (precision α) and Gaussian likelihood (noise σ²), taking a training matrix X ∈ ℝ^{N×P} and outputs Y ∈ ℝ^N as input. For a given test point x, the method produces both the exact posterior predictive N(μᵀx, xᵀΣx) and the MFVI posterior predictive N(m*ᵀx, xᵀS*x), enabling direct comparison of predictive variances. The T-MFVI variant additionally takes a temperature parameter T and produces a rescaled covariance TS*, allowing the user to dial the predictive variance to match the exact posterior.

## Method

The core result is a theoretical analysis showing that MFVI's diagonal covariance eigenvalues are weighted harmonic means of the exact posterior eigenvalues (Lemma 3.2), which guarantees overestimation of predictive variance along the direction of minimum exact posterior variance—equivalently, the first principal component of the training data for spherical priors (Theorem 3.5). A calibration constraint (Lemma 3.6) shows the average ratio of MFVI to exact predictive variance across all posterior eigenvectors equals 1, forcing overestimation in some directions to compensate for underestimation in others. The main theorem (Theorem 3.7) proves that for test points drawn from the empirical training distribution, the expected MFVI predictive variance exceeds that of the exact posterior. In a pathological rank-1 high-dimensional limit (Proposition 5.1), MFVI's predictive variance converges to the prior variance, completely failing to incorporate data information. The paper connects these results to the Cold Posterior Effect, showing that scaling the MFVI covariance by T < 1 corrects the overestimation on in-distribution data while T > 1 corrects underestimation on out-of-distribution data, validated on synthetic, basis-function regression, and UCI regression tasks.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical result (Theorem 3.7) proves that for test points drawn from the empirical training distribution, MFVI's expected predictive variance exceeds that of the exact posterior. In the pathological rank-1 setting, Proposition 5.1 shows that as P→∞, the MFVI posterior predictive variance converges to the prior variance (α⁻¹‖x‖²), meaning MFVI fails to reduce uncertainty at all; empirically, at P=1024 the T=1 MFVI predictive is nearly indistinguishable from the prior. On UCI kin8nm (Q=500 RBF basis functions), the optimal temperature T* for matching the exact posterior is consistently below 1 for train and ID test sets across all divergences (forward KL, reverse KL, α-divergence, W₂, Frobenius), while OOD test sets require T* > 1, confirming the cold/warm posterior duality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing approximate inference methods; instead, it compares the MFVI posterior (at various temperatures T) against the exact conjugate posterior, the prior, and the MAP solution. At T=1, MFVI underestimates parameter-space variance (Tr(S*) ≤ Tr(Σ)) but overestimates predictive variance on in-distribution data, with the gap growing with dimensionality: at P=2 the MFVI predictive closely matches the exact posterior, while at P=1024 it is nearly identical to the uninformative prior. Lowering T (cold posterior) corrects the ID overestimation, with the optimal T* dropping substantially in high dimensions (much lower for P=1024 than P=2). Conversely, for OOD test points orthogonal to the training subspace, warm posteriors (T>1) improve calibration at P=2, while at P=1024 the MFVI variance in orthogonal directions is already close to the prior so T*≈1. In the BNN experiment (IVON on 1D sinusoidal data), increasing width from 16 to 512 produces substantially wider credible intervals, mirroring the basis function regression result that higher-dimensional parameter spaces amplify MFVI's overconfidence pathology.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical analysis of Gaussian MFVI from parameter space (Margossian and Saul, 2023) to the predictive space, showing that the harmonic-mean structure of MFVI eigenvalues leads to overestimation of predictive variance along the first principal component of training data. It provides a new, model-mismatch-free explanation for the Cold Posterior Effect (Wenzel et al., 2020) by showing that temperature scaling of the MFVI covariance directly corrects the predictive variance overestimation on in-distribution data. The work is distinct from tempered/GenBayes posteriors, which modify the likelihood-prior balance in the ELBO, whereas here the cold posterior is the exact target that MFVI approximates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result inverts the standard narrative that MFVI is uniformly overconfident, revealing that on in-distribution data—the most common practical setting—MFVI is actually underconfident, with a pathological high-dimensional limit where it fails to reduce predictive variance at all. This provides a mechanistic, well-specified-model explanation for the CPE that is broadly relevant to BNN practitioners using MFVI/IVON, and the Hessian-structure connection to deep learning suggests the mechanism may generalize beyond conjugate models. Key limitations include the analysis being restricted to conjugate linear regression (BNN experiments are preliminary), and it remains open whether non-mean-field VI (e.g., VIKING) avoids this pathology or whether the optimal temperature can be learned automatically.
