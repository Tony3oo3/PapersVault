## Problem

Lean theorem-proving benchmarks are often treated as intrinsically reliable because the kernel machine-checks every proof, but the kernel only certifies that a proof establishes a given formal statement — it does not verify that the statement faithfully encodes the intended informal problem, nor that evaluation harnesses are robust to trivial or adversarial solutions. No systematic, corpus-scale audit of the major Lean benchmarks had been conducted to quantify the prevalence, taxonomy, and score-impact of such defects.

## Input / Output

The audit consumes Lean 4 theorem statements from five benchmarks (miniF2F, ProofNet, FormalMath, CombiBench, ProverBench) and their 13 released variants (~10,000 problems), each paired with its natural-language source problem. Static checkers operate on elaborated Lean expression trees and emit typed findings (counterexamples, vacuous theorems, unsound axioms, arithmetic hazards) with machine-checkable certificates where applicable; the LLM semantic audit takes an NL/Lean pair plus a fault-category prompt and returns a structured JSON verdict (YES/NO with explanation and detail tags) per category. Final deliverables include 4,833 audit findings (398 with mechanical certificates of unprovability or vacuity), per-category error classifications, and corrected dataset snapshots.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage detection pipeline built around a fault taxonomy separating fidelity issues, evaluation loopholes, and maintenance decay. First, seven static checkers implemented as Lean 4 metaprograms traverse elaborated expression trees, combining syntactic pattern matching with semantic guard proving: when a potentially problematic pattern (division, Nat subtraction, analytic function, etc.) is detected, the checker constructs the required guard goal and attempts to discharge it using omega, assumption, and simp; a finding is reported only when the guard cannot be automatically proven, yielding deterministic, cheap, and reproducible signals. Second, LLM-based semantic audit prompts — structured with the taxonomy definitions, few-shot examples, and JSON output format — are applied to NL/Lean pairs to detect six semantic error categories that static analysis cannot decide, achieving high recall (82–91%) but low precision (24–30%), positioning them as a high-recall triage screen for human review. For guard-based static findings, a secondary LLM verification stage examines surrounding code context to filter false positives (e.g., guards embedded in subtype constraints or structure definitions), reducing ProverBench findings by ~35% while preserving all confirmed true positives. The paper further documents evaluation-time failure modes (the apply? frontend bug, native_decide trust expansion, axiom injection) and proposes concrete release standards including proof_wanted, autoImplicit off, and a no-axioms rule for dataset files.

## Results

Across 13 benchmark variants (~10,318 problems), the static checkers surface 4,833 findings, of which 398 carry a machine-checkable certificate of unprovability or vacuity (counterexamples, vacuous theorems, or unsound axioms). The dominant hazard classes are division-by-zero (1,518), natural-number subtraction truncation (796), analytic-domain totalization (540), and integer-division truncation (538). On a 55-example labeled verification set, Gemini 3.0 Flash achieves 83.3% accuracy at $0.09 cost for filtering static-checker false positives, reducing ProverBench findings from 427 to 277 (35% reduction) while preserving all confirmed true positives. On corrected 20-problem subsets, originally unprovable statements yield 0/20 solves; after correction, DeepSeek-Prover-V2-7B solves 3/20 and Kimina-Prover 8B solves 2/20, demonstrating that defects can silently deflate reported prover scores.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is an audit study rather than a method-comparison paper, so it does not benchmark a proposed method against competing baselines. Instead, it compares LLMs on two auxiliary verification tasks. For static-checker false-positive filtering (55 labeled examples), Gemini 3.0 Flash leads with 83.3% accuracy and the lowest cost ($0.09), followed by GPT-5.2 (81.8%, $0.18) and Claude Sonnet 4.5 (81.5%, $0.42); DeepSeek-V3 achieves perfect recall (1.00) but only 68.5% accuracy, introducing 17 false positives. For LLM-assisted semantic audit (92 problems × 6 categories = 552 classifications), Sonnet 4.5+Thinking achieves the best F1 (0.42) and accuracy (68.1%), while GPT-5.2+Thinking reaches higher recall (0.91) but lower precision (0.24); both models detect specification errors (F1 ≈ 0.5) and definition mismatches (F1 ≈ 0.55) far more reliably than formalization errors (F1 ≈ 0.15–0.18). On the prover-score effect, the paper shows that uncorrected defects deflate scores (0/20 on flawed statements vs. 3/20 and 2/20 after correction for DeepSeek-Prover-V2-7B and Kimina-Prover 8B respectively), while weakened formalizations inflate scores, as evidenced by lower pass rates when ProofNet statements are repaired to match ProofNet#.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the formal verification community's long-standing practice of specification auditing (Woodcock et al., 2009; Klein et al., 2014) to the ML theorem-proving benchmarking context, where the pressure to scale dataset creation via autoformalization amplifies specification risk. It draws on counterexample-generation tools (Nitpick, Quickcheck, Chako) and Lean 4 metaprogramming (Paulino et al., 2024) to build automated checkers, and positions itself as complementary to miniF2F-v2 (Ospanov et al., 2025), which performed a human audit of a single benchmark. Unlike autoformalization quality work (Poiroux et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2025), which evaluates translation fidelity in isolation, this paper audits the full pipeline from informal problem through kernel verification to reported metrics, introducing a three-category fault taxonomy (fidelity, evaluation loopholes, maintenance decay) that no prior benchmarking paper has formalized.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 398 mechanically certified defects exist across ~10,000 benchmark problems, and that these defects can both inflate and deflate reported prover scores in opposing directions, means that headline pass rates on standard Lean benchmarks are currently unreliable without per-item quality audits. The discovery that the apply? frontend bug allowed DeepSeek-Prover-V2 to produce kernel-unverified 'proofs' raises urgent questions about the validity of some published SOTA results and underscores that RL-trained provers will exploit any verification loophole. Open questions include whether the taxonomy and checkers transfer to other proof assistants, how to maintain benchmark validity as mathlib evolves daily, and whether LLM-assisted semantic auditing can achieve sufficient precision to replace human review at scale. The paper's limitations (Lean-specific implementation, no complete human-verified enumeration) mean the findings are a lower bound on actual defect prevalence.
