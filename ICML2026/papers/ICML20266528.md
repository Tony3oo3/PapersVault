## Problem

LLM-based social simulations are increasingly used to model human behavior, but their tendency to produce homogeneous outputs—acting as an 'average persona'—fundamentally limits their capacity to capture the behavioral diversity essential for complex social dynamics. Current validation practices often fail to match the heterogeneity demands of research questions, with fewer than half of reviewed studies explicitly assessing behavioral variance. The paper asks: what types of research claims can LLM-based simulations reliably support, and where should methodological boundaries be drawn?

## Input / Output

The paper's analytical input is a curated set of 21 LLM-based social simulation studies (2023–2025) from top AI/NLP venues, each evaluated along five dimensions: heterogeneity requirement of the research question, ground truth availability and sample size, mean alignment checking, variance checking, and sensitivity analysis. The output is a structured validation assessment (summarized in Table 1) that maps each study's claim level (collective-qualitative vs. collective-quantitative) against its validation depth, yielding boundary recommendations that specify when qualitative collective patterns are defensible versus when quantitative or individual-level claims exceed what the simulation can support.

## Method

The core contribution is a variance-mean diagnostic framework for LLM-based social simulations that separates two alignment dimensions: mean (whether the central tendency of agent behavior matches human ground truth) and variance (whether the spread of behaviors matches human diversity). This framework identifies two critical failure cases—low variance with aligned mean (where only collective-qualitative claims remain valid) and low variance with deviated mean (where the simulation may be inapplicable)—and traces the root cause to LLM training's likelihood-driven objective, which rewards high-frequency expressions and suppresses marginal ones, producing an 'average persona.' The paper operationalizes this framework through a systematic review of 21 studies, revealing that while all 14 papers with ground truth checked mean alignment, only 9 checked variance, and those that did consistently found lower LLM variance than human populations. The resulting boundary recommendations form a checklist: match validation depth to heterogeneity demands, report variance alongside mean alignment, and constrain claims to collective-level qualitative patterns when variance is insufficient.

## Results

The systematic review of 21 LLM-based social simulation papers (2023–2025) reveals that 14/21 (67%) included some form of ground truth comparison, and all 14 of those examined mean alignment, but only 9/14 explicitly assessed behavioral variance. Among the papers that did check variance, the majority found LLM-generated behaviors exhibited lower variance than human populations, consistent with the 'average persona' phenomenon. Nine papers addressed research questions with high heterogeneity requirements, yet only four of those checked both mean and variance against ground truth. Encouragingly, 18/21 papers limited claims to collective-level qualitative patterns, and 20/21 conducted at least partial sensitivity analysis.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper without a proposed method to benchmark against baselines; no direct method comparisons are made. Instead, the paper contrasts three community perspectives on LLM-based social simulation validity: the optimistic view (e.g., Anthis et al. 2025, Ashery et al. 2025) that LLMs are transformative research tools, the skeptical view (e.g., Gao et al. 2025, Li et al. 2025a) that they are fundamentally unreliable surrogates, and the conditional view (e.g., Demszky et al. 2023, Zhou et al. 2025 PIMMUR principles) that validity is contingent on meeting specific fidelity criteria. The paper's central empirical contrast is between the heterogeneity demands of research questions and the depth of validation actually conducted: 9 high-heterogeneity-requirement studies exist, but only 4 performed both mean and variance checks against ground truth. The paper also highlights contradictory findings across studies (e.g., Fontana et al. 2025 reporting hyper-cooperative LLMs vs. Akata et al. 2025 reporting unforgiving defection), arguing that validation success in one dimension (mean alignment) can mask failures in another (distributional collapse).

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper sits at the intersection of the long-standing ABM validation tradition in social science (Schelling, Epstein, Edmonds) and the rapidly growing LLM-based social simulation literature (Park et al. 2023, Han et al. 2023, Yang et al. 2024c). Rather than proposing a new simulation method, it repurposes the variance-mean diagnostic from behavioral economics (e.g., Keynesian Beauty Contest experiments) as a validity framework for LLM agent populations, and positions itself explicitly between the optimistic 'silicon subject' camp (Anthis et al. 2025, Grossmann et al. 2023) and the skeptical 'fundamental invalidity' camp (Gao et al. 2025, Li et al. 2025a). The systematic review of 21 papers extends prior scattered critiques (Cheng et al. 2023, Bisbee et al. 2024, Ma et al. 2025) into a structured taxonomy of validation gaps, while the PIMMUR principles (Zhou et al. 2025) and algorithmic fidelity concept (Argyle et al. 2023) provide the closest prior formalizations of simulation validity criteria that this paper builds upon and narrows to the heterogeneity dimension.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central practical contribution is a concrete checklist—match validation depth to heterogeneity demands, report variance alongside mean alignment, and constrain claims to collective-qualitative patterns when variance is insufficient—that could reshape how LLM simulation papers are designed and reviewed. The systematic review finding that fewer than half of 21 recent papers explicitly assess behavioral variance, and that those which do consistently report lower variance than human populations, provides empirical grounding for the boundary argument. Key open questions remain: whether architectural or training changes (e.g., diversity-aware fine-tuning, mixture-of-personas) can overcome the 'average persona' limitation, whether the boundary shifts as models improve, and how to operationalize variance matching when ground-truth distributions are unavailable or culturally specific. As a position paper, its impact depends on whether the community adopts the variance-mean framing as a standard validation requirement.
