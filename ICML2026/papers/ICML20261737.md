## Problem

Automatic verification of open-ended mathematical proofs remains a critical bottleneck for math-solving agents and reinforcement learning, yet existing approaches suffer from three intertwined limitations: rule-based checkers cannot handle open-ended questions, LLM-based verifiers have inadequate error-detection capability, and current agentic workflows (e.g., 64 rounds of parallel verification per proof) incur prohibitive computational costs. The paper identifies that the primary failure mode is not confirming correctness but detecting errors, and that repeated coarse reviews are far less effective than careful fine-grained scrutiny.

## Input / Output

The verifier receives a math problem statement and a candidate proof/solution as text, and produces a binary verdict (correct or incorrect) at the response level rather than step-level scoring. The output is structured as a tagged verdict (<verification>true</verification> or <verification>false</verification>) followed by a concise description of the most harmful error when the proof is rejected. For IMO-GradingBench, only proofs earning the full 7-point score are treated as correct, collapsing the graded scale into a binary classification.

## Method

The paper proposes pessimistic verification, a paradigm in which a proof is rejected the moment any one of multiple parallel verifiers identifies a flaw, exploiting the fact that error detection is a low-probability event whose likelihood is amplified by repeated independent checks. Three variants are introduced: (1) simple pessimistic verification (pes@n) repeats whole-proof verification n times in parallel; (2) vertical pessimistic verification (vp@k) splits the proof into chunks of at most k lines and verifies each chunk in a single pass; and (3) progressive pessimistic verification (prog@n/k) combines both by starting with whole-proof checks and then recursively halving the proof, filtering out any segment where an error is found before proceeding to finer granularity, with a maximum of 2^n − 1 checks and a minimum chunk size k to prevent over-segmentation. The progressive variant achieves the best performance-efficiency tradeoff because it quickly eliminates obviously flawed proofs at coarse granularity before expending tokens on detailed step-level scrutiny, and its parallel structure makes it faster than long chain-of-thought scaling at equivalent or better accuracy.

## Results

On the IMO 2025 dataset with Gemini 3 Pro Preview, progressive pessimistic verification (prog@4/4) achieves a total score of 27/42, nearly doubling the direct solving score of 12/42 and substantially outperforming simple pessimistic verification (pes@12, score 13/42), while using fewer equivalent output tokens (985K vs. 1,310K). On the IMO-GradingBench verification benchmark (300-sample subset), progressive@3/6 with GPT-5-mini improves Balanced F1 from 0.780 (standard) to 0.849, driven primarily by a large TNR gain (0.675 → 0.842) at a modest TPR cost (0.923 → 0.857). On QiuZhen-Bench, progressive@3/6 with Qwen3-30B-A3B raises Balanced F1 from 0.52 (standard) to 0.79, using roughly one-fourth the tokens of pes@12 at comparable performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four verification strategies—standard (single-pass), simple pessimistic (pes@n, parallel repeated whole-proof checks), vertical pessimistic (vp@k, chunk-level parallel checks), and progressive pessimistic (prog@n/k, multiscale iterative decomposition)—plus long CoT and majority voting as additional baselines. Progressive pessimistic verification consistently achieves the highest Balanced F1 and TNR across all three verification benchmarks (IMO-GradingBench, Hard2Verify, QiuZhen-Bench) and all tested models, while using roughly 4× fewer equivalent tokens than simple pessimistic verification at comparable or better accuracy. On the IMO 2025 problem-solving task, prog@4/4 scores 22/42 versus 13/42 for direct solving and 17/42 for pes@12, while consuming fewer tokens than pes@12 (985K vs. 1,310K). Majority voting is shown to be nearly ineffective for verification (Figure 3), and long CoT scaling is less token-efficient than parallel pessimistic verification on models where reasoning effort is adjustable (GPT-5-mini). The paper also notes that existing benchmarks (IMO-GradingBench, Hard2Verify) contain annotation errors that cause stronger models' true verification performance to be underestimated, as confirmed by manual case-study review of false-negative instances.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the observation from Pandit et al. (2025) and Jain et al. (2025) that error detection is the key ability separating strong proof verifiers from weak ones, but goes further by proposing concrete agentic workflows (pessimistic, vertical, and progressive) that systematically exploit this asymmetry. It departs from the mainstream parallel-verification approach used in DeepSeekMath-V2 and Huang & Yang (2025) by introducing progressive proof decomposition that dynamically adjusts granularity, and explicitly contrasts with majority voting (shown ineffective) and long-CoT scaling (shown less token-efficient). The paper also positions itself against formal theorem provers (Lean-based systems like Hilbert and Aristotle), arguing that informal LLM-based verification offers a practical compromise between rigor and cost-efficiency for open-ended math.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that expert-annotated benchmarks like IMO-GradingBench contain critical labeling errors—causing them to underestimate frontier models' verification ability by a meaningful margin—is important for the field's evaluation methodology and suggests that current SOTA claims on these benchmarks may be conservative. The progressive pessimistic verification method offers a practical, ~4× more token-efficient alternative to the naive parallel verification schemes currently deployed in agentic math workflows, with demonstrated gains on IMO 2025 and MathArena Apex 2025. Open questions include whether the pessimistic verification signal can be incorporated into RL training pipelines to raise the ceiling of model verification ability, whether the approach generalizes to code review and other long-form verification tasks, and whether informal verification can eventually reach the reliability needed to reduce dependence on formal provers.
