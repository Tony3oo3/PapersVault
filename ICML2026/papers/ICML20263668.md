## Problem

Probabilistic models for propensity score estimation are typically trained with task-agnostic objectives like log-loss, which minimizes KL divergence uniformly across the probability simplex. However, downstream estimators like Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW) for Average Treatment Effect estimation are disproportionately sensitive to errors near the boundaries (p→0 or p→1), where small propensity errors cause bias and variance to explode. This mismatch means a model can achieve low log-loss while producing catastrophic downstream estimation errors, and existing remedies (clipping, trimming, covariate balancing) either sacrifice consistency or lack a direct theoretical link to the target estimand's error.

## Input / Output

The method takes tabular covariates X ∈ ℝ^d and binary treatment labels T ∈ {0,1} as input, and produces propensity score estimates p̂(X) ∈ (0,1) that are plugged into IPW, Hajek, or AIPW estimators to yield a scalar ATE estimate. A key structural detail is that the final activation is not a standard sigmoid but a custom canonical probability mapping σ_ℓ(z) defined implicitly as the largest real root of a depressed quartic equation in u = 1/(p(1-p)), which stretches the probability space to prevent the model from assigning extreme probabilities. The training loss is likewise custom: a closed-form strictly proper scoring rule with terms scaling as 1/q² and 1/(1-q)² (bias) plus 1/q³ and 1/(1-q)³ (variance), replacing the standard binary cross-entropy.

## Method

The paper proposes a general framework that derives a task-specific strictly proper scoring rule by matching the local curvature (second derivative at q=p) of the downstream error metric to the weight function w_ℓ(p) of the scoring rule. Applied to IPW-ATE estimation, they derive an upper bound on the plug-in IPW estimator's MSE (under cross-fitting and bounded outcome variance), decompose it into bias and variance divergences, and compute the total task curvature w_task(p) = 2/p² + 2/(1-p)² + 2/p³ + 2/(1-p)³. Integrating this weight function via the Buja et al. characterization yields a closed-form loss ℓ(t,q) with rational and logarithmic terms. Crucially, they also derive the canonical probability mapping σ_ℓ by inverting the integral of w_task, which reduces to solving a quartic equation; pairing the loss with this mapping guarantees the gradient with respect to logits simplifies to the stable linear residual p−y, preventing the gradient explosion that would occur with a naive sigmoid. The resulting loss-and-mapping pair is architecture-agnostic and serves as a direct drop-in replacement for log-loss and sigmoid in any gradient-based learner (MLP, XGBoost, logistic regression).

## Results

On the Kang & Schafer stress-test benchmark (severe overlap violations), the tailored IPW loss achieves IPW RMSE of 2.68, Hajek RMSE of 1.95, and AIPW RMSE of 5.07, dramatically outperforming standard logistic regression (IPW RMSE 66.90) and CBSR (IPW RMSE 102.03). On the ACIC 2017 benchmark (32 DGPs × 3 estimators = 96 configurations), the tailored loss improves RMSE in 95/96 setups with an MLP backbone and 62/96 with XGBoost, with the largest gains in high-selection-strength regimes where propensity scores concentrate near 0 and 1. The method achieves the best Mean Rank across all three downstream estimators (IPW: 1.95, Hajek: 1.04, AIPW: 1.18) on Kang & Schafer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CBPS (Imai & Ratkovic, 2014), the tailored loss wins decisively on Kang & Schafer (IPW RMSE 2.68 vs. 4.13) and Jobs (0.03 vs. 0.36), though CBPS edges ahead on IHDP Hajek (0.23 vs. 0.29). Against CBSR (Zhao, 2019), the gap is most extreme: on Kang & Schafer, CBSR's IPW RMSE explodes to 102.03 while the tailored loss stays at 2.68, confirming that CBSR's non-canonical link causes gradient instability. Against Entropy Balancing (Hainmueller, 2012) and SBW (Zubizarreta, 2015), the tailored loss dominates on the overlap-violation benchmark (IPW RMSE 2.68 vs. 5.83 and 7.87) but is slightly behind EB on IHDP IPW (0.34 vs. 0.18), where overlap is adequate. Against post-hoc Clipping/Trimming (Crump et al., 2009), the tailored loss achieves lower IPW RMSE on Kang & Schafer (2.68 vs. 6.08/6.54) without sacrificing sample size, while Trimming remains competitive on the easier Jobs dataset (0.02 vs. 0.03). Overall, the tailored loss is the most consistent top-performer (best Mean Rank) across all three estimators, with the largest absolute gains precisely in the high-selection-strength regimes where IPW is theoretically most vulnerable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the structural theory of strictly proper scoring rules (Buja et al., 2005) and the canonical link framework for binary losses (Reid & Williamson, 2010), repurposing them to derive a task-specific loss for propensity score estimation in causal inference. It departs from covariate balancing methods (CBPS, EB, SBW) that use balance as a proxy for bias reduction, and from Zhao's (2019) Covariate Balancing Scoring Rules, which also use proper scoring rules but break the canonical link and suffer from gradient instability. Unlike Decision-Focused Learning approaches that backpropagate through differentiable solvers, this work exploits the closed-form plug-in structure of IPW to analytically bound its MSE and embed that geometry directly into the upstream classification loss, requiring no solver or discrete decoder.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, architecture-agnostic drop-in replacement for log-loss in propensity score estimation that consistently reduces ATE estimation error, particularly in high-selection-strength regimes where standard methods fail catastrophically. The derivation of the canonical probability mapping (requiring a quartic root) is practically important because it guarantees stable gradients, unlike prior tailored-loss approaches that suffer from exploding gradients. Key limitations include the reliance on local curvature matching (no global optimality guarantee), the computational overhead for tree-based backbones (~5× for XGBoost), and the fact that the canonical mapping may not admit closed-form solutions for other downstream tasks. Extending the framework to CATE, ATT, or TMLE estimators remains open.
