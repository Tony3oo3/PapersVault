## Problem

Existing AI-augmented inference methods like Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI) treat AI-generated outputs as noisy surrogates of true labels, an assumption that breaks down when AI outputs are categorical, systematically biased, high-dimensional, or uncalibrated. This paper asks how AI-generated signals can be incorporated into statistical estimation without requiring them to approximate the outcome, enabling valid inference even when AI representations are inaccurate or structurally different from human labels.

## Input / Output

The method takes a feature matrix X, a partially observed human label y (available only for a random subset indicated by w), and AI-generated auxiliary representations z whose structure is deliberately unrestricted—ranging from discrete categorical labels (e.g., {-1,0,1} LLM choices) to continuous probabilities or high-dimensional text embeddings. It produces GLM parameter estimates β̂ with asymptotic variance Σ̂ and valid confidence intervals. A key design constraint is that the nuisance regression g(X,z) is learned exclusively from primary (human-labeled) data via cross-fitting, so auxiliary observations contribute information only through out-of-sample predictions, never through training.

## Method

GAI replaces the surrogate-label view with a feature-augmentation view by embedding AI representations into a Neyman-orthogonal score function ψ(Ξ; e, g; β) = X^T[∇b(Xβ) − g(X,z) + (w/e(X,z))(g(X,z) − y)], where e(X,z) is the propensity for observing a human label and g(X,z) = E[y|X,z] is a nonparametric regression learned from primary data. The score is computable for all observations (including those with missing y), is doubly robust in the nuisance functions, and admits valid √n inference under standard product-rate conditions (r₁+r₂ ≥ 1/2). The algorithm uses K-fold cross-fitting to estimate e and g out-of-sample, then solves a single convex optimization to obtain β̂. Under random labeling with constant propensity, the paper proves strict asymptotic variance dominance over primary-only estimation whenever AI outputs carry incremental information, and empirically demonstrates large MAPE reductions (e.g., 32%→17% in vaccine conjoint with 54%-accuracy LLM labels) while maintaining nominal coverage.

## Results

On the vaccine conjoint dataset (11 attributes, GPT-4o CoT labels at 54% accuracy), GAI reduces MAPE from 32.02% (primary-only, n_P=50) to 16.86%, while PPI yields 44.57% at n_P=100 and PPI++ yields 29.69%. GAI maintains 95% CI coverage of 90.91–99.82% with the tightest intervals (width 0.96–1.94) among all valid methods. On the California census dataset (85%-accurate gradient-boosting predictions), GAI cuts MAPE from 979.83% (primary, n_P=100) to 161.37%, with 99–100% coverage, while PPI achieves only 821.83% MAPE and 88% coverage at the same sample size.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GAI dominates all six PPI-family and semi-supervised baselines on every metric in both experiments. Against PPI (Angelopoulos et al., 2023a), GAI achieves roughly half the MAPE (16.86% vs. 44.57% at n_P=100 in conjoint; 161% vs. 822% at n_P=100 in census) while PPI's CI width is 4.8× wider at small n_P (9.28 vs. 1.94). PPI++ (Angelopoulos et al., 2023b) narrows the gap but still trails GAI by 12–13 MAPE points in conjoint and undercovers (85.09% at n_P=200 vs. GAI's 90.91%). RePPI (Ji et al., 2025) suffers severe undercoverage (74.73–82.36%) and higher MAPE than GAI in all configurations where it is numerically stable. PSPA (Miao et al., 2025) and GMM (Byun et al., 2025) converge toward primary-only accuracy at larger n_P but never match GAI's flat ~16% MAPE, and both undercover below 95% nominal. Classic-M (Song et al., 2024) performs worst among the structured baselines (79.32% MAPE at n_P=50) and also undercovers. The key distinction is that GAI treats AI outputs as predictive features via a Neyman-orthogonal score, so it remains valid and efficient even when AI labels are discrete and only 54% accurate, whereas all surrogate-based methods (PPI, PPI++, RePPI, PSPA, GMM) degrade or become unstable in that regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

GAI builds directly on the Neyman-orthogonal / doubly-robust estimation framework (Chernozhukov et al., 2018; Bang & Robins, 2005) and the AIPW score structure from missing-data theory, repurposing them for the AI-augmented inference setting. Its key conceptual departure is from Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI) and its variants (PPI++, RePPI, PSPA, GMM), which all treat AI outputs as noisy surrogates of the target label; GAI instead treats AI outputs as unrestricted auxiliary features for a learned regression function g(X,z), removing the requirement that AI representations live in the same space as or approximate human labels. This positions GAI as a nonparametric generalization of the AI-augmented estimator (AAE) framework, which relied on parametric debiasing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a theoretically grounded and empirically validated recipe for combining small human-labeled samples with large AI-generated datasets while preserving valid √n inference, with a clean dominance guarantee under random labeling. The practical implication is substantial: achieving the precision of 200 human annotations with only 50 human labels plus 1,000 AI representations represents roughly 75% cost reduction. Key limitations include the constant-propensity requirement for the dominance theorem (though non-constant propensity experiments still show empirical gains), the absence of fairness guarantees across subpopulations, and the restriction to GLM-family losses. Open questions include extending the dominance result to heterogeneous propensities, characterizing when the feature-based approach is semiparametrically efficient, and auditing for demographic bias amplification in high-stakes deployments.
