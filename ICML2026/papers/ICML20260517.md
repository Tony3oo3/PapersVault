## Problem

Adam's first- and second-moment estimation requires twice the parameter memory, which becomes prohibitive for large models. Prior memory-efficient optimizers (Adafactor, Galore) reduce this cost by approximating the weight gradient itself with low-rank or factorized forms, but none rethinks moment estimation from the gradient-computation perspective, where the weight gradient naturally decomposes into the product of input activations and output gradients.

## Input / Output

At each linear layer during backpropagation, the method consumes the input activation matrix X ∈ R^{m×bs} and the output gradient matrix ∇_Y L ∈ R^{n×bs} *before* the weight gradient is formed, along with the current weight W ∈ R^{n×m}. It produces the updated weight W in-place. The key structural detail is that C ∈ R^{m×b} and R ∈ R^{n×b} are maintained as column-cycling buffers (each column updated every b batches via a one-hot selector e_{(t mod b)}), so the full m×n second-moment matrix is never materialized—only their outer product V = C R^T is used at update time.

## Method

IO-Adam exploits the identity ∇_W L = (∇_Y L) X^T to replace Adam's full m×n second-moment matrix with two smaller matrices: C tracks the exponential moving average of squared inputs (m×b) and R tracks that of squared output gradients (n×b), reducing memory from O(mn) to O(mb + nb). The first-moment (momentum) update is fused directly into the backpropagation step so the full weight gradient is never stored. The paper proves via Cauchy–Schwarz that the resulting second-moment estimate is an upper bound of Adam's, yielding the same O(√T) regret bound, and introduces two refinements to tighten the gap: (1) the alternating-column buffer that suppresses cross-batch product terms, and (2) an optional Hölder-inequality modification that applies asymmetric exponents p ≤ 2 to inputs and q = p/(p−1) ≥ 2 to output gradients, exploiting the sparsity of post-ReLU activations versus the volatility of error signals.

## Results

On the GLUE benchmark with RoBERTa-Base, IO-Adam achieves competitive or superior performance to AdamW across most tasks (e.g., SST2: 94.04 vs. 92.09, RTE: 75.09 vs. 74.01, MNLI: 87.12 vs. 86.59) while reducing peak memory from 2409.89 MB to 1599.61 MB (~34% reduction). On LLaMA pretraining (C4), IO-Adam achieves perplexity of 29.75 (60M), 22.40 (130M), and 14.36 (1B), matching or slightly beating AdamW. On CIFAR-10 with ViT-small, IO-Adam (buffer=16) reaches 77.63% accuracy vs. 76.06% for AdamW and 76.32% for Adafactor, using only 68.40 MB vs. 112.02 MB. On WikiText-2 with GPT-2, IO-Adam achieves the lowest perplexity of 22.36 vs. 22.63 (AdamW) and 22.72 (Adafactor).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against AdamW, IO-Adam trades a small drop on CoLA (61.59 vs. 62.82) and MRPC (91.30 vs. 92.17) for clear wins on SST2 (+1.95), RTE (+1.08), MNLI (+0.53), QNLI (+0.89), and QQP (+0.22), all while using ~34% less memory. On C4 LLaMA pretraining, IO-Adam matches or slightly beats AdamW (22.40 vs. 22.61 perplexity at 130M; 14.36 vs. 14.51 at 1B) and notably requires a 10× larger learning rate (0.025 vs. 0.002), consistent with its larger second-moment estimate. Against Adafactor, IO-Adam wins on most GLUE tasks (e.g., CoLA +3.51, SST2 +0.89) and on CIFAR-10 (77.63% vs. 76.32% accuracy) while using slightly more memory (68.40 vs. 64.52 MB). Against Galore, IO-Adam outperforms on nearly all GLUE tasks (e.g., CoLA +3.05, SST2 +1.38, MNLI +1.32) and on C4 (29.75 vs. 34.09 perplexity at 60M). Against AdamSNSM on C4, IO-Adam is competitive at 60M (29.75 vs. 29.84) and 130M (22.40 vs. 22.71) but trails slightly at 1B (14.36 vs. 14.05). On WikiText-2, IO-Adam achieves the best perplexity (22.36) over both AdamW (22.63) and Adafactor (22.72).

## Relation to Prior Work

IO-Adam builds directly on Adam's exponential moving average framework for first- and second-moment estimation, but departs from the dominant line of memory-efficient optimizers (Adafactor, Galore, AdamSNSM) that reduce memory by approximating the weight gradient itself via low-rank projections or row/column sums. Instead, IO-Adam reframes the problem from a gradient-computation perspective: since the weight gradient is the outer product of the input and output gradient, it tracks these two factors separately before the multiplication, storing O(m·b + n·b) state rather than O(m·n). The fused momentum update also draws on the fused-backpropagation idea from AdaLomo (Lv et al., 2023), and the Hölder-inequality modification (adjusting exponents p and q) extends the Cauchy-Schwarz upper-bound analysis to a tighter, tunable bound.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is a ~30% memory reduction for the optimizer state while matching or slightly exceeding AdamW's performance across language and vision benchmarks, which is meaningful for training large models on constrained hardware. The clean theoretical result—that the factorized second moment is a Cauchy-Schwarz upper bound of Adam's—explains why IO-Adam requires a substantially larger learning rate (up to 10× in LLaMA pretraining), a practically important calibration insight. Key limitations include applicability only to linear layers (not convolutions), a ~7% time overhead from PyTorch hooks, and the underexplored p-hyperparameter from the Hölder modification. Open questions include whether the input/output-gradient tracking can be extended to convolutional or attention modules, and whether the buffer size and p can be adapted dynamically during training.
