## Problem

Existing unsupervised graph alignment methods face two coupled failures: GNN-based embeddings lose node distinctiveness through neighborhood aggregation (oversmoothing), and independently learned latent spaces for different graphs become geometrically misaligned due to structural noise, feature heterogeneity, and training stochasticity. These issues compound, making correspondence identification unreliable even when the underlying graphs share substantial structure.

## Input / Output

The method takes two attributed graphs (source and target), each specified by an adjacency matrix and a node feature matrix, and may handle graphs with different numbers of nodes. It outputs a one-to-one node correspondence obtained by computing cosine similarity between ℓ₂-normalized dual-pass embeddings and applying greedy matching. In the vision-language generalization, class-level embeddings from pretrained vision and language models are first converted into k-NN similarity graphs (k=5), after which the same pipeline applies; the Hungarian algorithm replaces greedy matching for final assignment in that setting.

## Method

GADL pairs a dual-pass GCN encoder with a geometry-aware functional map module inside a graph autoencoder trained end-to-end. The encoder runs two spectrally complementary GCN branches sharing the same propagation rule but with different normalized adjacency matrices: a low-pass filter (p_l(λ̃)=1−λ̃/2, monotonically decreasing) capturing structural context and a high-pass filter (p_h(λ̃)=λ̃/2, monotonically increasing) preserving node discriminability; their outputs are concatenated, and Theorem 3.2 proves the combined embedding is strictly more discriminative than either branch alone while retaining neighborhood consistency. The functional map module projects embeddings into a reduced spectral basis (first r Laplacian eigenvectors) and learns small r×r maps C₁₂ and C₂₁ via a regularized least-squares objective with a Laplacian-commutativity term, plus bijectivity (‖C₁₂C₂₁−I‖²) and orthogonality (‖C₁₂C₁₂ᵀ−I‖²) losses that act as a low-pass filter on correspondences, enforcing smoothness and geometric consistency in map space. The total loss combines GAE reconstruction (BCE on adjacency), the two functional map alignment losses, and the bijectivity/orthogonality regularizers, all optimized jointly with Adam so that the encoder and maps co-evolve to produce mutually alignable, discriminative embeddings.

## Results

On six semi-synthetic benchmarks under 5% structural perturbation, GADL achieves 71.74% (Celegans), 80.69% (Arena), 69.62% (Douban), 71.12% (Cora), 62.49% (DBLP), and 68.54% (Coauthor CS) matching accuracy, consistently outperforming the strongest baseline T-GAE by 1.5–3.5 points. On the real-world ACM-DBLP benchmark, GADL reaches 88.63% Hit@1, surpassing T-GAE (73.89%) by 14.7 points and GAlign (73.26%) by 15.4 points. On Douban Online-Offline, GADL achieves 53.31% Hit@1, edging out SLOTAlign (51.43%) and GAlign (41.32%). In vision-language alignment, GADL attains 100% accuracy with DINOv2 on CIFAR-10 and 20.8% on CIFAR-100, substantially outperforming solvers like Gurobi (2.56%) and FAQ (4.33%) on the harder benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GADL consistently outperforms the strongest embedding-based baseline T-GAE across 17 of 18 semi-synthetic evaluation settings, with the largest margins under 5% perturbation (e.g., +3.4 pts on Cora, +2.5 pts on DBLP, +2.5 pts on Coauthor CS); the sole exception is Douban at 5% where T-GAE edges ahead by 0.6 pts. Against GAE, the gap is dramatic: GAE collapses to 0.6–9.6% under 5% perturbation while GADL maintains 62–72%, directly validating the latent-space misalignment hypothesis. On the real-world ACM-DBLP benchmark, GADL (88.63% Hit@1) surpasses T-GAE (73.89%) and GAlign (73.26%) by roughly 15 points, and on Douban Online-Offline it beats SLOTAlign (51.43%) and GAlign (41.32%). In vision-language alignment, GADL dominates on CIFAR-100 and ImageNet-100 where most solvers (OT, FAQ, MPOpt, Gurobi) achieve near-chance accuracy (≤5%), while GADL reaches 16.8–20.8% with CLIP and 20.8% with DINOv2. Optimization-based baselines (NetSimile, Final) and transport-based methods (WAlign) degrade sharply under perturbation, confirming that GADL's dual-pass spectral encoding plus functional-map alignment provides a more robust inductive bias than either pure optimization or distributional matching.

## Relation to Prior Work

GADL builds on the Graph Autoencoder framework (Kipf & Welling, 2016) and the GCN spectral propagation rule (Kipf & Welling, 2017), but departs from standard GNN-based alignment by replacing the single low-pass GCN filter with a dual-pass encoder whose two branches are provably complementary (p_low + p_high = 1). The latent space communication module repurposes the functional map framework from 3D shape correspondence (Ovsjanikov et al., 2012; Donati et al., 2020) to graph alignment, adding bijectivity and orthogonality regularizations that act as structural priors in map space. Unlike prior spectral GNNs (GPR-GNN, BernNet, TFE-GNN) that target single-graph node classification, GADL's spectral design is explicitly unsupervised and cross-graph, and unlike T-GAE or SLOTAlign, it enforces geometric consistency between independently learned latent spaces through differentiable functional map losses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses two well-identified failure modes of unsupervised graph alignment—oversmoothing-induced loss of node distinctiveness and stochastic latent space misalignment—with a theoretically grounded solution (Theorem 3.2) and a principled alignment mechanism. The extension to vision-language alignment, where learned spectral-geometric alignment outperforms exact QAP solvers (Gurobi, Hahn-Grant) on larger benchmarks, suggests the functional map formulation may be broadly applicable beyond graph domains. Key limitations include the computational overhead of dual-pass encoding, sensitivity to hyperparameter tuning (loss weights, spectral basis size), and the absence of large-scale benchmarks with reliable ground truth, which constrains claims about scalability. Open directions include adaptive spectral filtering, efficient embedding strategies, and more thorough multi-modal evaluation.
