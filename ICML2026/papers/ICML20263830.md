## Problem

Deployed language models expose their full internal capabilities to every user through a single API endpoint, violating the principle of least privilege. Existing safeguards—training-time alignment, output filtering, and activation steering—constrain emitted behavior but leave the underlying capability encoded in the weights, reachable under repeated sampling or adaptive prompting. The paper asks whether one can enforce per-request, reversible control over which internal computations are actually reachable during inference, without retraining or deploying separate models.

## Input / Output

The system takes a text prompt x plus request-time signals s(x) (e.g., uncertainty, risk tier, user role) as input. A monitor-allocator-enforcer stack maps these signals to a discrete privilege setting g ∈ {0, 1, …, r_max}, which the enforcer implements by selecting a prefix of nested rank-1 factors inside the forward pass. The output is a standard generated text response, but produced by a deployed policy π_{θ,g} whose reachable function class is strictly smaller than the full model's when g < r_max. The privilege knob is shape-preserving (tensor dimensions unchanged) and reversible (g_max recovers the original computation exactly).

## Method

The paper introduces Nested Least-Privilege Networks (NLPNs), which re-parameterize selected transformer linear maps W ∈ R^{d_out × d_in} as a product W ≈ BA with A ∈ R^{r_max × d_in} and B ∈ R^{d_out × r_max}, initialized via SVD. For a privilege setting g, the effective weight is W(g) = B_{:,1:g} A_{1:g,:}, a sum of the first g rank-1 terms, yielding a monotone family with Im(W(g)) ⊆ Im(W(g+1)) and fixed tensor shapes. Post-hoc fine-tuning uses an uncertainty-weighted multi-privilege loss (adapted from Kendall et al., 2018) that simultaneously optimizes the anchor privilege r_max and a uniformly sampled variant g, preventing the fragile low-rank behavior that naive SVD truncation produces. At deployment, a monitor-allocator-enforcer stack separates request-time signals, a decision rule φ that allocates privilege, and the NLPN enforcer that restricts reachable computation, enabling per-request privilege ceilings with a smooth, policy-usable utility-privilege-overhead frontier.

## Results

Across three algorithmic tasks (Balanced Brackets, Length Comparison, Contains Substring) and three models (Qwen2.5-0.5B, Pythia-1B, Llama-3.2-1B), NLPN rank reduction yields monotone, graceful utility degradation: performance stays above 95% at moderate ranks and drops sharply only at the lowest ranks, with harder instances degrading first. The Progressive (Incremental) allocation policy achieves ≥90% utility retention with ≤20% privilege cost on Contains Substring (Qwen2.5-0.5B), while Static LP retains ≥93% utility at ≤54% privilege on Length Comparison. Llama-3.2-1B is notably robust, maintaining >97% accuracy on all tasks even at the lowest rank, whereas Qwen2.5-0.5B and Pythia-1B show sharper drops (Contains Substring falls from >99% to ~76–78%). Selective block-level suppression via beam search reduces target subjects (e.g., chemistry, biology) while preserving other MMLU tasks, with the privilege-utility pattern reproducing on a naturalistic NER task and across models from 0.5B to 4B parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare NLPNs against external competing methods; instead it benchmarks five internal allocation policies against each other on a shared enforcement surface. Full Privilege (always maximum rank) serves as the utility upper bound, while Min Rank (always minimum rank) is the privilege lower bound that frequently fails to meet utility targets (e.g., 72% utility on Length Comparison for Qwen2.5-0.5B). Static LP selects a single calibrated rank and achieves utility targets but over-allocates privilege on easy instances (e.g., 54% privilege on Length Comparison vs. 25% for Progressive Incremental). Progressive (Incremental) consistently achieves the best privilege savings at fixed utility targets—reducing average rank by 60–80% relative to Full Privilege on heterogeneous-difficulty tasks—at the cost of approximately 2–3× inference passes. Progressive (Jump) trades some privilege efficiency for lower overhead (~1.3–1.5× passes) by escalating directly to maximum rank when uncertainty is high. The key takeaway is that dynamic, uncertainty-conditioned policies dominate static ones on tasks with heterogeneous difficulty, while on uniformly easy tasks (e.g., Pythia-1B on Length Comparison) all policies converge to minimal privilege with negligible differences.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the nested subspace network framework of Rauba & van der Schaar (2025), repurposing rank-indexed factorization as a deployment-time enforcement mechanism rather than a training-time architecture choice. The multi-privilege training loss adapts the uncertainty-weighted multi-task objective of Kendall et al. (2018) to jointly optimize across privilege levels. Conceptually, the paper departs from three existing families—training-time alignment, output-level filtering, and adaptive computation (early exit, mixture-of-depths)—by arguing that none of them restrict the model's reachable function class, and instead formalizes deployment control as a least-privilege problem where privilege is defined as reachable internal computation, enforced via a monitor-allocator-enforcer stack. The closest architectural relatives are ordered-capacity networks (slimmable networks, once-for-all), but NLPNs differ in being post-hoc, shape-preserving for pretrained transformers, and explicitly designed for per-request access control rather than device scaling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper introduces a concrete, auditable deployment paradigm where per-request capability exposure becomes an explicit, tunable quantity rather than an implicit property of the base weights, which could complement (not replace) existing alignment and filtering stacks. Key open questions include whether suppressed capabilities are truly irrecoverable under adaptive prompting or fine-tuning, whether the privilege-utility structure holds at frontier-scale models, and how to connect rank-based privilege metrics to meaningful capability classes. The experimental evidence is currently limited to sub-1B models and largely synthetic tasks, so the practical impact hinges on whether the differential-sensitivity and selective-suppression findings scale. The paper also raises governance concerns: the same mechanism could be misused for opaque censorship or discriminatory access tiers, making transparency and red-teaming essential for any real deployment.
