## Problem

The dominant two-stage pipeline for deep-learning asset allocation—first forecast returns, then optimize a portfolio—suffers from an objective mismatch: minimizing prediction error (e.g., MSE) does not align with the downstream goal of robust portfolio construction, and small cross-sectional ranking errors are amplified by the optimizer into unstable, impractical weights. Moreover, general-purpose forecasting architectures lack the financial inductive biases needed to capture path-dependent, lead-lag structures inherent in multi-asset price dynamics.

## Input / Output

At each decision time the model consumes, for each of d assets, a sequence of H truncated path-signature vectors (level M=2, encoding net increments and signed cross-areas) over a lookback window, pairwise cross-signatures for all asset pairs over the full window, and deterministic calendar feature vectors. It outputs K-step-ahead long-only, fully-invested portfolio weight vectors obtained as softmax(μ̂/τ) over predicted returns, where τ is a temperature controlling allocation concentration. Crucially, the predicted returns μ̂ are not trained with any pointwise prediction loss; they serve solely as differentiable logits through which the portfolio-level CVaR gradient flows.

## Method

SIT unifies feature extraction and allocation in a single end-to-end policy built on three pillars. First, each asset's price path is encoded via Rough Path Signatures (truncated at level 2), providing a principled, reparameterization-invariant summary of trends and oscillations. Second, a Signature-Informed Self-Attention mechanism operates along the asset dimension: a dynamic bias term b_{k,h,j,l} = ⟨q^{dyn}_{k,j,h}, β_{i,j,l,h}⟩ is added to standard QK^T logits, where β is an MLP projection of the pairwise cross-signature and q^{dyn} is a query-conditioned vector, all scaled by a learnable positive gate γ; Proposition 2.2 proves this bias strictly increases attention to assets whose signature alignment matches the query's informational need. Third, the entire network is trained by minimizing the expected CVaR_α of the K-step portfolio loss via its Rockafellar–Uryasev dual form, with no auxiliary forecasting loss. Attention is factorized over temporal and asset axes, reducing cost from O(H²d²d_model) to O(dH²d_model + Hd²d_model + d²C_cross-sig(M)).

## Results

On the 40-asset S&P 100 universe, SIT achieves a Sharpe Ratio of 0.6717 and Sortino Ratio of 0.8232, with a Final Wealth Factor of 1.7903, outperforming the best traditional baseline (EWP: Sharpe 0.5759) and the best deep-learning forecaster (RFormer: Sharpe 0.4901). On the 50-asset universe, SIT's advantage widens: Sharpe 0.7715, Sortino 0.9743, and Wealth 1.9215, versus the next-best NSformer (Sharpe 0.5238) and EWP (Sharpe 0.6008). SIT also maintains a competitive Maximum Drawdown (0.3611 / 0.3271) while delivering substantially higher returns than risk-minimizing strategies like GMV (Sharpe 0.4148 / 0.3947).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SIT decisively outperforms all 12 baselines on Sharpe and Sortino ratios across both the 40- and 50-asset S&P 100 universes. Among traditional strategies, EWP is the closest competitor (Sharpe 0.5759 vs. SIT's 0.6717 on 40 assets), while GMV achieves the lowest drawdown (0.2743) but at a substantial return cost (Sharpe 0.4148). Among the eight deep-learning forecasters used in a predict-then-optimize pipeline, RFormer is the strongest (Sharpe 0.4901), yet still trails SIT by ~0.18 Sharpe; the remaining forecasters (Autoformer, DLinear, FEDformer, iTransformer, NSformer, PatchTST, TimesNet) all fall below 0.52 Sharpe and exhibit high run-to-run variance (±0.10–0.34), evidencing the error-amplification problem the paper identifies. The performance gap between SIT and the predict-then-optimize baselines widens with universe size: on 50 assets, SIT's Sharpe (0.7715) exceeds the best forecaster (NSformer, 0.5238) by 0.25, compared to a 0.18 gap on 40 assets. On the CSI 300 (50 assets), SIT achieves a Sharpe of 1.9373 versus HRP's 1.7428 and GMV's 1.6717, confirming robustness across market regimes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: decision-focused learning (DFL) for portfolio construction (Buehler et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2020; Costa & Iyengar, 2023) and path-signature representations from rough path theory (Lyons, 1998; Bonnier et al., 2019; Moreno-Pino et al., 2024). Prior signature-based financial models (Gyurkó et al., 2013; Tong et al., 2023) used signatures as static input features or kernels outside the attention mechanism and optimized predictive losses, while the Rough Transformer (Moreno-Pino et al., 2024) introduced signature-augmented attention but for forecasting rather than allocation. SIT departs from both by injecting cross-asset signature geometry directly into the attention logits as a query-conditioned bias and by training end-to-end on a portfolio-level CVaR objective, explicitly rejecting the predict-then-optimize pipeline (Moody & Saffell, 2001) that dominates the deep-learning-for-finance literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides strong multi-market empirical evidence (S&P 100, DOW 30, CSI 300; 10–100 assets) that aligning the training objective with the financial goal (CVaR) and embedding geometric lead-lag structure via signatures yields substantially more stable and higher risk-adjusted returns than any predict-then-optimize variant, with the gap widening as dimensionality grows. The theoretical results (strict lead-lag implies positive second-order signature; monotonicity of attention under signature alignment) give the architectural choices mathematical grounding beyond empirical tuning. Limitations include testing only on daily-frequency equities from two markets, a maximum of 100 assets, and a single CVaR confidence level in the main experiments; open questions include whether the signature bias generalizes to higher-frequency data, multi-asset classes (bonds, commodities, crypto), and whether the pairwise cross-signature cost becomes prohibitive at thousands of assets.
