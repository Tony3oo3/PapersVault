## Problem

Cross-subject generalization in biomedical time-series classification suffers from subject-specific variability that masks class-relevant physiological patterns. Existing approaches suppress this variability implicitly (via stronger backbones) or through adversarial/statistical alignment objectives, but rarely model what constitutes subject-specific variability in the first place, making invariance objectives hard to control and risking removal of label-relevant structure.

## Input / Output

The model takes a multivariate biomedical time-series segment X ∈ R^{T×C} (e.g., 1-second EEG windows with 16–19 channels at 256 Hz, ECG heartbeats with 15 channels at 250 Hz, or 3-channel motor-imagery EEG) and outputs a class label (disease diagnosis or motor-imagery class). Under the strict cross-subject protocol, all samples from a subject are assigned to a single split, so the model must generalize to completely unseen individuals. Internally, a Pyramid Convolutional Embedding produces three parallel multi-scale feature sequences at T/2, T/4, and T/8 temporal resolutions that are processed jointly through the encoder.

## Method

BioFormer is a hybrid spectral-temporal Transformer whose core is the Frequency-Band Alignment Module (FBAM), which explicitly models subject variability as structured spectral drift. FBAM applies a real FFT along the temporal axis, partitions the non-DC spectrum into M=6 bands, computes per-band statistics (log mean, log std, log peak, log energy, dominant-frequency index), and fuses them via cross-attention with learnable band tokens to produce three modulation parameters per band: a convolution kernel w_m, an amplitude gain g_m, and a phase offset β_m. Magnitude alignment uses a residual convolutional update A' = A + g_m(A * w_m − A), while phase alignment applies a unit-complex rotation P' = P⊙e^{jβ_m}/|P⊙e^{jβ_m}|, and the DC component is preserved to avoid overfitting baseline offsets. A complementary Sample Conditional Layer Normalization (SCLN) infers scale/shift from the stop-gradient temporal mean of encoder features and blends them residually (with coefficient α) to stabilize cross-subject statistics without subject supervision. The full model alternates temporal self-attention and FBAM across 6 encoder layers, is trained end-to-end with only the classification loss (no subject discriminator or auxiliary alignment term), and is supported by a theorem showing that per-bin amplitude/phase modulation is exactly a scaling-plus-rotation in the 2D {cos, sin} Fourier subspace.

## Results

Under a strict cross-subject protocol on six biomedical time-series benchmarks (EEG and ECG), BioFormer achieves the best overall average rank (1.47) across 36 metrics (6 datasets × 6 evaluation metrics) and attains top-1 performance on 4 of 6 datasets. Headline F1-scores are 79.77 (APAVA), 53.82 (ADFTD), 84.71 (PTB), 62.56 (PTB-XL), 46.35 (BCI-2a), and 72.81 (BCI-2b), yielding absolute F1 improvements of up to 6.3% over the strongest baseline (SoftShape) on PTB and 7.7% on BCI-2a. In the domain-generalization comparison (Table 3), FBAM achieves an 81.2% positive-gain rate across 16 backbone×dataset settings with a worst-case drop of only −0.29%, substantially outperforming the next-best method (43.8% positive rate, worst −2.81%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the ten general-purpose Transformer baselines (Transformer, Reformer, Informer, Autoformer, FEDformer, Nonformer, PatchTST, Crossformer, iTransformer, MTST), BioFormer achieves the highest F1 on all six datasets, with the largest margins on ADFTD (+5.7% over the best Transformer baseline) and BCI-2a (+4.1% over Crossformer). Against the two strongest SOTA baselines—Medformer (NeurIPS'24) and SoftShape (ICML'25)—BioFormer wins on 5 of 6 datasets in F1, with the most notable gains being +6.3% on PTB (84.71 vs. 78.42 SoftShape), +4.9% on ADFTD (53.82 vs. 48.91), and +7.7% on BCI-2a (46.35 vs. 38.69); the sole loss is a marginal −0.4% on PTB-XL (62.56 vs. 62.97). In the plug-and-play domain-generalization comparison (Table 3), FBAM outperforms all six DG methods (GRL, MMD, CORAL, SubjectNorm, MixStyle, DSU) on 13 of 16 backbone×dataset settings, achieving an 81.2% positive-gain rate versus 43.8% for the next-best method (GRL/MixStyle/DSU) and a worst-case degradation of only −0.29% versus −2.81% to −27.51% for the DG baselines. On the Medformer backbone specifically, FBAM lifts APAVA F1 from 76.02 to 81.46 (+5.4%) and ADFTD F1 from 42.17 to 47.08 (+4.9%), while GRL on the same backbone yields only 71.93 and 40.67 respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two dominant paradigms for cross-subject biomedical time-series classification: (1) Transformer backbones (Medformer, PatchTST, iTransformer) that implicitly suppress subject variability through multi-scale temporal modeling, and (2) domain generalization methods (GRL, MMD, CORAL, MixStyle, DSU) that enforce feature-level invariance via adversarial or statistical objectives. Rather than treating subject-specific variability as an implicit nuisance to be minimized, the paper introduces 'spectral drift'—band-wise magnitude scaling and phase rotation—as an explicit, structured characterization of that variability, and designs the Frequency-Band Alignment Module (FBAM) to correct it directly in the Fourier domain. The theoretical grounding (Appendix E) connects this to scaling-and-rotation operations in 2D Fourier subspaces, providing an interpretable mechanism absent from prior frequency-domain time-series models like FEDformer, which use spectral representations for efficiency rather than for subject-invariance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The spectral drift reframing offers a principled, interpretable alternative to black-box domain generalization objectives, and the plug-and-play nature of FBAM (no subject identity required, no auxiliary loss) makes it broadly applicable to any Transformer-based biomedical time-series pipeline. The 81.2% positive-gain rate and worst-case drop of only 0.3% across 16 backbone-dataset settings address a real practical concern: many DG methods degrade performance on some configurations, making them unreliable in deployment. However, the authors acknowledge the approach is most effective when shared task-relevant oscillatory structures exist, limiting its applicability to event-driven or non-periodic clinical signals. Open questions include whether the fixed 6-band partition is optimal across all signal types, whether the spectral drift characterization holds for non-stationary or multi-session recordings, and whether the method extends naturally to regression or segmentation tasks.
