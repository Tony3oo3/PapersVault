## Problem

Pruning-at-Initialisation (PaI) methods like SNIP and GraSP produce sparse, trainable subnetworks, but the theoretical mechanism governing the global sparsity patterns they induce remains opaque. Prior analyses rely on finite-width combinatorial metrics, and the Graphon Limit Hypothesis (GLH) postulating convergence of masks to continuous graphon operators was assumed rather than derived. The paper asks whether discrete PaI masks converge to stable, deterministic limit objects as network width grows, and how those limits govern expressivity and generalisation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a one-hidden-layer network with d input coordinates and n hidden units, initialised with i.i.d. Gaussian weights, along with a single input sample x ∈ ℝ^d and a target sparsity ratio ρ. The PaI algorithm produces a binary mask M_n ∈ {0,1}^{d×n} at initialisation; the paper studies the asymptotic structure of this mask as d, n → ∞ by embedding it as a step-kernel on [0,1]² and measuring convergence in the bipartite cut distance. The outputs are: (i) a deterministic limit graphon W(u,v) = P(φ(u)·Q_ψ(v)·|ξ| > τ) encoding the asymptotic edge-probability kernel, (ii) a universal approximation guarantee on k-dimensional active subspaces, and (iii) a Graphon-NTK generalisation bound whose complexity term y^T K_W^{-1} y depends on the induced kernel's spectral structure.

## Method

The core contribution is a Factorised Saliency Model S_ij = φ_{n,i} · ψ_{n,j} · |ξ_{n,ij}| that unifies popular PaI criteria (SNIP, GraSP, SynFlow, Magnitude, Random) into a common structure of input feature × neuron feature × edge noise. Under regularity assumptions (deterministic input profiles, empirical CDF convergence of neuron features, conditional independence of edge noise, and a stabilising threshold), the paper proves that the associated step-kernels converge in bipartite cut distance to a deterministic limit graphon W(u,v) = P(φ(u)·Q_ψ(v)·|ξ| > τ). This yields a topological taxonomy: unstructured methods (Random, Magnitude) converge to homogeneous constant graphons W ≡ ρ, while data-driven methods (SNIP, GraSP) converge to heterogeneous graphons where connectivity probability varies smoothly with input saliency and neuron sensitivity. Leveraging this continuous characterisation, the paper derives a universal approximation theorem showing sparse networks retain dense-network expressivity on active coordinate subspaces whenever the limit graphon contains a positive-measure block with a uniform edge floor, and connects the graphon to NTK-style generalisation bounds through a path-density proxy that quantifies how sparse topology redistributes input-output connectivity mass.

## Results

The paper is primarily theoretical, with empirical validation on CIFAR-10 using (Wide)ResNet-20. The key quantitative finding is that the Graphon-NTK complexity measure y^T K_W^{-1} y for SNIP (heterogeneous graphon) is substantially lower than for Random pruning (constant graphon) at extreme sparsity (ρ=0.2, 0.4), while the two become comparable at higher densities (ρ≥0.7). In the accuracy-vs-sparsity comparison, Random pruning is competitive with SNIP at medium sparsity but degrades much more sharply at extreme sparsity. Visual convergence experiments confirm that finite-width SNIP masks approach the predicted deterministic graphon limit by n≥2000 at density ρ=0.2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two pruning strategies: SNIP (data-driven, inducing a heterogeneous graphon) and Random pruning (unstructured, inducing a constant/homogeneous graphon). On CIFAR-10 with (Wide)ResNet-20, SNIP outperforms Random at extreme sparsity (ρ=0.2, 0.4), where the complexity bound y^T K_W^{-1} y is significantly smaller for SNIP, reflecting better kernel conditioning and label alignment. At moderate-to-high densities (ρ≥0.7), the two methods become comparable, with the complexity curves nearly overlapping or slightly reversing. The graphon framework provides the mechanistic explanation: Random pruning uniformly dilutes path density across all input coordinates, which becomes harmful at high sparsity, while SNIP's heterogeneous graphon concentrates connectivity mass on informative coordinates, preserving dense computational cores. The visual convergence experiments (Figure 1) further validate that SNIP masks at n=2000–4000 are visually indistinguishable from the analytically computed limit graphon, confirming the theoretical prediction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly proves the Graphon Limit Hypothesis (GLH) postulated by Pham et al. (2025), who assumed PaI masks converge to graphons and built a Graphon-NTK framework on top of that assumption. Prior analyses of PaI methods (SNIP, GraSP, SynFlow) relied on finite-width combinatorial metrics—path counting, path kernels, graph-theoretic connectivity—leaving the asymptotic structure of masks uncharacterised. The paper also extends classical NTK generalisation bounds (Cao & Gu, 2019; Arora et al., 2019a) and the Cybenko UAT to the graphon-sparsified setting, but departs from the GNN literature's use of graphons (which model input data) by applying them to the internal architecture of the network itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The homogeneous-vs-heterogeneous graphon taxonomy gives a clean mathematical explanation for why data-driven pruning (SNIP, GraSP) outperforms random pruning at extreme sparsity: data-driven methods concentrate connectivity on informative coordinates while random pruning uniformly dilutes it. However, the practical impact is currently limited by the single-hidden-layer scope of the proofs, the requirement that both input dimension and width go to infinity jointly, and the absence of finite-width convergence rates. Key open questions include extending the rigorous deep-network extension (currently a sketch), handling fixed input dimensions (where the limit object becomes a mixed discrete-continuous kernel), and deriving non-asymptotic bounds that quantify how fast finite masks approach the graphon.
