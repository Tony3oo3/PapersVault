## Problem

Success conditioning—filtering trajectories for success and imitating the actions taken—is a core component of post-training pipelines (rejection sampling + SFT, Decision Transformers, STaR) yet no one had identified what optimization problem it actually solves. Prior work showed it cannot recover optimal policies in stochastic environments, but left open the question of what it *does* optimize and why its failures take the distinctive form of barely changing the policy. This paper provides the missing theoretical characterization.

## Input / Output

The method consumes state-action trajectories generated by a fixed behavior policy π₀, each labeled with a binary success indicator R(τ)∈{0,1}. The state S_t may encode the full observation history (necessary for non-Markovian behavior policies or history-dependent transitions), making the formulation compatible with transformer-based sequence models. The output is a success-conditioned policy π₊(a|s) defined as the conditional action distribution along successful trajectories, which is equivalently the minimizer of next-action-prediction cross-entropy on the filtered dataset and can be learned by standard supervised fine-tuning.

## Method

The paper proves that success conditioning is the exact solution to a trust-region policy optimization problem: maximize the first-order improvement L_{π₀}(π) subject to a χ² divergence constraint weighted by the success-conditioned occupancy measure, with a radius automatically set by a quantity called action-influence (the squared coefficient of variation of Q-values under the behavior policy). A key identity shows that at every state, relative advantage, χ² policy movement, and action-influence are exactly equal, making success conditioning a conservative operator that can never degrade performance—its only failure mode is over-conservatism when action-influence is small. The χ² geometry is substantially more restrictive than the KL constraints in TRPO/PPO: under a fixed divergence budget, the maximum probability assignable to a rare action scales as √δ under χ² versus 1/log(1/δ) under forward KL. The paper further shows REINFORCE is the on-policy special case (one inner gradient step per outer iteration) and analyzes return thresholding as a proxy reward that can amplify action-influence but risks misalignment with the true objective.

## Results

The paper's central result is the Action-Influence Identity (Prop. 4.4): at every state, the relative advantage of the success-conditioned policy, the χ² divergence between the success-conditioned and behavior policies, and the action-influence (squared coefficient of variation of Q-values under the behavior policy) are exactly equal. This implies success conditioning is a conservative improvement operator that never degrades performance (Cor. 4.5: ρ(π₊) ≥ ρ(π₀)). In the two-armed bandit example (Ex. 3.1), success conditioning yields only a 0.00005 improvement (from 0.5 to 0.50005), closing just 1% of the gap to optimal, precisely because action-influence is 0.0001. Lemma 4.6 quantifies the conservatism: under a fixed divergence budget, the maximum probability assignable to a rare action (probability δ) scales as Θ(√δ) under χ² versus Θ(1/log(1/δ)) under forward KL, making the χ² trust region substantially more restrictive.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper makes three key theoretical comparisons rather than empirical baseline comparisons. First, against TRPO/PPO (reverse KL constraint): success conditioning's χ² constraint reverses the geometric bias—instead of penalizing the removal of previously taken actions (as reverse KL does), it penalizes assigning probability to actions rarely taken under the behavior policy, permitting concentration on well-supported actions. Second, against MDPO (forward KL constraint): both penalize deviations outside the behavior policy's support, but χ² is substantially more conservative, as formalized in Lemma 4.6 where the tolerance for exploring rare actions scales as √δ versus 1/log(1/δ). Third, against REINFORCE (policy gradient): Lemma 5.1 shows that REINFORCE is the on-policy special case (N=1 inner steps) of iterated success conditioning, with the success-conditioning gradient equal to the REINFORCE gradient up to a 1/ρ(π₀) rescaling. The paper positions success conditioning as more conservative than all three, with its failure mode being over-conservatism (barely changing the policy) rather than dangerous distribution shift.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first exact optimization-theoretic characterization of success conditioning, a heuristic appearing under many names (rejection sampling + SFT, Decision Transformers, STaR, goal-conditioned RL) whose underlying objective had remained unclear. It builds directly on the performance difference lemma (Kakade & Langford, 2002) to show that success conditioning solves a trust-region problem, but departs from the TRPO/PPO lineage in two key ways: the constraint geometry is χ² divergence rather than KL, and the trust-region radius is determined automatically by the data (via a quantity called action-influence) rather than chosen as a hyperparameter. The paper also positions REINFORCE as the on-policy, single-step special case of iterated success conditioning, and contrasts its conservative χ² geometry with the forward-KL geometry of MDPO and the reverse-KL geometry of TRPO.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result resolves a long-standing question about what success conditioning actually optimizes, and in doing so explains its characteristic failure mode: when action-influence is small (actions have similar success probabilities), the method barely moves the policy rather than degrading it dangerously. This has direct practical implications for LLM post-training pipelines (Llama, DeepSeek-R1, STaR) where rejection sampling + SFT is a core component, as it provides an ex ante diagnostic (the χ² divergence between old and new policy) for expected improvement without requiring new rollouts. Open questions include how the theory extends to finite data and function approximation, whether iterated success conditioning converges or stalls, and how to deliberately increase action-influence (e.g., via exploration or action chunking) to amplify improvement.
