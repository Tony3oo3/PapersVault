## Problem

While Transformer-based chess policies achieve strong in-distribution performance, it remains unclear whether they genuinely understand chess rules and strategy or merely reproduce statistical patterns from training data. This paper asks whether a behavior-cloned chess Transformer can (1) extrapolate the syntactic rules of chess to novel, never-seen board configurations and (2) adapt its strategic play to unseen game variants, thereby separating rule adherence from strategic competence under distribution shift.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a tokenized FEN string (77 tokens encoding board state, side to move, castling rights, en passant target, half-move clock, and full-move counter) plus one dummy action token, for a total context length of 78. It outputs a log-probability distribution over all 1968 possible UCI-notation actions; critically, no legality constraint is applied at inference time, so the model must internally learn to restrict itself to legal moves. Evaluation metrics include legal-move accuracy, Stockfish top-K move agreement (K=1,3,5,10 at search depth 20), full puzzle-sequence accuracy, and relative Elo in multi-opponent tournaments.

## Method

A 270M-parameter decoder-only Transformer (16 layers, 8 heads, 1024-dim embeddings) is trained via behavior cloning on ~525M filtered ChessBench positions labeled by Stockfish 16 (50 ms per move), with all pawn-promotion-derived OOD boards (extra queens, same-color bishops) explicitly removed so they have zero probability under the training distribution. The key experimental contribution is a taxonomy of seven OOD stress-test datasets—ranging from mild rule violations (More pieces, Same color) through variant starting positions (Chess960, All starting positions) to an extreme custom set (Knights&Rooks with up to 15 knights and 4 rooks)—plus full-game evaluation in Chess960 and Horde variants. The model is evaluated without any legal-move masking, and its outputs are scored against Stockfish 17 (depth 20) for move quality and against python-chess for legality. Training dynamics analysis reveals a compositional learning sequence: the model first learns to move only its own pieces, then identifies all legal moves on ID boards, then extends legality to OOD boards, and finally concentrates probability mass on the best moves. Linear probing of hidden representations confirms that attack status, legal-move counts, and best-move information are encoded across layers, with structural features peaking in early layers and decision-level features recovering in later layers.

## Results

The 270M-parameter chess transformer achieves perfect (100%) legal move accuracy on in-distribution datasets and 96–99.6% on most OOD sets, dropping to 90.2% only on the extreme Knights&Rooks dataset. On strategy adaptation, OOD Puzzles yield a Stockfish top-1 accuracy of 67.70% (vs. 70.50% ID) and top-10 of 92.93%, while More Pieces and Same Color drop to ~30% top-1 but retain 43–50% top-10. Chess960 starting positions show the lowest top-1 (22.73%) but high top-10 (88.80%) due to the large number of equally strong opening moves. In full-game tournaments, the model places 3rd in Standard chess (Rel. Elo 88±26), 5th in Chess960 (−110±26), and 6th in Horde (−350±36), reaching a Lichess Elo of 1550 (Standard), 1571 (Chess960), and 1178 (Horde).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the Transformer against Stockfish levels 0–4 (Standard and Chess960) and Fairy-Stockfish levels 0–4 (Horde) in 100-game-per-pair tournaments. In Standard chess, the Transformer (Rel. Elo 88±26) places 3rd, narrowly below Stockfish level 3 (114±26) and above level 2 (−27±26). In Chess960, it places 5th (−110±26), just below Stockfish level 1 (−86±26). In Horde, it ranks last (−350±36), well below even Fairy-Stockfish level 0 (−223±31), though it still wins 31/250 games as White and 76/250 as Black. Against human and bot opponents on Lichess, the gap narrows considerably: the model's Chess960 Elo (1571) is nearly on par with its Standard chess Elo (1550), suggesting that Stockfish's uniform search-based approach to both variants overstates the difficulty of Chess960 relative to human play. Overall, the Transformer underperforms all search-based engines in head-to-head tournaments but demonstrates strong rule adherence (96–100% legal moves) and non-trivial strategic quality on curated OOD puzzles.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the rule extrapolation framework from Mészáros et al. (2024), which studied compositional generalization in formal languages, to the richer domain of chess, and adopts the behavior-cloning training setup from Ruoss et al. (2024) for the Transformer architecture and ChessBench dataset. It departs from both Ruoss et al. (2024) and Schultz et al. (2025), which focus on improving chess-playing performance (legal-move constraints, search, variant-specific training), by taking a complementary perspective: chess serves as a controlled domain for probing OOD reasoning rather than a target for performance optimization. The key conceptual contribution is decomposing OOD evaluation into two distinct axes—rule extrapolation (syntactic validity) and strategy adaptation (move quality)—and constructing a taxonomy of stress tests spanning mild to extreme distribution shift.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean separation between strong rule generalization (96%+ legal moves across OOD settings including unseen variants like Horde) and weaker strategic generalization (sharp failure on Knights&Rooks) provides a useful diagnostic for behavior-cloned policies in structured domains, with implications beyond chess (e.g., syntactic validity in code generation). The finding that the model's Chess960 playing ability on Lichess is nearly on par with Standard chess, yet the tournament gap versus Stockfish is larger, suggests that memorized opening theory matters more against symbolic engines than against human-like statistical play. Open questions include whether the rule/strategy gap can be closed with more diverse training data or auxiliary objectives, and whether the observed training dynamics (learning own-piece selection before specific moves) generalize to other structured domains.
