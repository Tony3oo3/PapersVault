## Problem

While nonlinear neural networks routinely extract latent structure invisible to linear correlation-based methods like PCA, no minimal high-dimensional data model existed where this advantage could be rigorously characterized. Additionally, the conditions under which self-supervised test loss reliably reflects representation quality remained poorly understood, with no tractable example demonstrating a concrete misalignment between reconstruction error and downstream utility.

## Input / Output

The model generates n samples of d-dimensional vectors from a spiked cumulant model with two latent factors: a covariance-visible spike u* and a higher-order-only spike v* that is statistically dependent on the first but uncorrelated (E[λν]=0). The autoencoder produces a single learned weight vector w ∈ R^d (the representation) and a reconstruction x̂; for evaluation, a downstream binary classification task uses labels y = sign(x^T v*) to probe whether the hidden spike was recovered. The key constraint is that the data covariance contains no information about v*, so any method relying solely on second-order statistics cannot detect it.

## Method

The paper introduces a minimal single-hidden-unit autoencoder with tied encoder-decoder weights, x̂ = (w/√d)σ(w^T x/√d), and analyzes its learning dynamics on the spiked cumulant model. The population loss is expanded in a Hermite basis, reducing the high-dimensional gradient flow to a two-dimensional ODE system in the overlaps m_u and m_v; the key insight is that the Hermite coefficients of the likelihood ratio encode the joint moments E[λ^k ν], so the correlation exponent k* (the smallest k with E[λ^k ν] ≠ 0) determines whether the hidden spike v* is recoverable. Theorem 4.2 shows that for k* < ∞ and suitable activations (ReLU, ELU, Swish, etc.), spherical gradient flow achieves weak recovery of both spikes in Θ(log d) time, while linear autoencoders (σ=id, equivalent to PCA) and tanh fail. The empirical risk minimizer is characterized via the replica method, and the paper demonstrates that the linear autoencoder achieves strictly lower reconstruction loss than nonlinear ones (by the Eckart-Young theorem) yet recovers only u*, while nonlinear autoencoders recover both spikes at the cost of higher test loss—providing a concrete, solvable example where validation loss misaligns with representation quality.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative result (Table 1) classifies eight activation functions by whether spherical gradient flow on the population loss achieves weak recovery of the visible spike u* and the hidden spike v* in logarithmic time. For correlation exponent k*=2, ReLU, ELU, Swish, Sigmoid, and GELU all recover both spikes, while the linear activation and tanh fail to recover v*. For k*=3, only tanh, ELU, Swish, Sigmoid, and GELU recover v* (requiring the cubic Hermite coefficient). In the empirical risk minimization analysis (Figure 1), the linear autoencoder achieves a Baik–Ben Arous–Péché transition at α_u^lin = 1/E[λ²]² for u* but never recovers v*, while suitable nonlinearities recover both spikes at a finite sample ratio α_weak^σ > α_u^lin. On the downstream binary classification task (Figure 2, right panel), the linear autoencoder performs at chance level while nonlinear autoencoders achieve substantially lower error, despite having higher reconstruction test loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the linear autoencoder (σ = id), which is provably equivalent to PCA (Proposition 2.1) and optimal for squared reconstruction error by the Eckart–Young theorem. On reconstruction loss, the linear autoencoder consistently wins: both train and test losses are lower than all nonlinear counterparts (Figure 2, left and center panels), a consequence of the inequality L^σ(w) ≥ L^linear(w̃) for all w. However, on representation quality, the linear autoencoder loses decisively: it aligns only with the visible spike u* and achieves zero cosine similarity with the hidden spike v* for all sample ratios, while nonlinear autoencoders (ReLU, ELU, Swish, Sigmoid, GELU) achieve nontrivial overlap with both spikes. On the downstream binary classification task (predicting sign(x^T v*)), the linear autoencoder performs at chance level (50% error), whereas nonlinear autoencoders achieve substantially lower classification error. This establishes a concrete, provable misalignment: the method with the lowest test loss (linear/PCA) produces the worst representations, while methods with higher test loss (nonlinear) recover the latent structure that matters for downstream tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the spiked cumulant model of Bardone & Goldt (2024) and Szekely et al. (2024) to the regime where latent factors are statistically dependent but linearly uncorrelated, a setting where PCA provably fails. It combines the high-dimensional autoencoder analysis of Refinetti & Goldt (2022) and Cui & Zdeborova (2023) with gradient-flow techniques from Ben Arous et al. (2021) and replica/AMP methods from statistical physics to obtain the first solvable high-dimensional model where a minimal nonlinear autoencoder provably recovers structure invisible to all linear methods. The key departure from the classical spiked covariance literature is that here the hidden spike is invisible to all second-order statistics, so the separation between linear and nonlinear methods is not merely computational but information-theoretic at the level of covariance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous, solvable high-dimensional setting where the misalignment between reconstruction loss and representation quality in self-supervised learning is provably demonstrated: linear autoencoders achieve strictly lower test loss yet discard identifiable latent structure, while nonlinear autoencoders recover both spikes at the cost of higher reconstruction error. This directly challenges the widespread practice of using validation loss for early stopping and hyperparameter selection in self-supervised pipelines. Key limitations include the restriction to a single hidden unit with tied weights and a specific class of higher-order dependencies; extending the analysis to multi-neuron architectures, richer dependence structures, and other self-supervised objectives (contrastive, masked) remains open.
