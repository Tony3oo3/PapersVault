## Problem

Prior reward-poisoning research in RL has focused on constructing successful attacks or designing defenses, providing only sufficient conditions for vulnerability. No instance-level, algorithm-agnostic criterion existed to determine when a targeted reward-poisoning attack is fundamentally infeasible in general RL settings (as opposed to bandits, where such characterizations were already known). This paper asks: given a linear MDP and a target policy, can a sublinear-budget attacker force the learner to adopt that policy, and if not, why is the environment intrinsically robust?

## Input / Output

The white-box characterization takes the full linear MDP specification (feature map φ with ‖φ‖₂≤1, per-stage reward vectors θ_h with ‖θ_h‖₂≤√d, transition kernel, and target policy π†) and outputs a scalar gap ε* from a convex quadratic program: ε*>0 certifies vulnerability with an explicit perturbation strategy, while ε*≤0 certifies intrinsic robustness. In the black-box setting, the attacker instead consumes observed trajectories (s_h, a_h, r_h, s_{h+1}) to estimate the linear MDP parameters before solving the same CQP. The attack output is a set of poisoned reward parameters {θ†_h} that, when applied, make the target policy strictly optimal with a uniform Q-margin on its occupancy support, at total cost o(T).

## Method

The core result (Theorem 4.2) is a convex quadratic program whose feasibility with a positive gap ε* is necessary and sufficient for attackability: the CQP searches for perturbed reward parameters {θ†_h} that (i) make the target action's Q-value exceed every competing action's Q-value by at least ε* at all states on the target's occupancy support, (ii) leave the target-branch reward unchanged (zero cost when the learner complies), and (iii) respect the linear MDP norm bound ‖θ†_h‖₂≤√d. When ε*>0, the attacker perturbs rewards only on learner deviations, yielding o(T) total cost against any no-regret learner; when ε*≤0, no sublinear-cost attack exists even against vanilla LSVI-UCB, establishing intrinsic robustness as a property of the environment's feature geometry. For the black-box setting, a two-stage attack first solves a relaxed geometric program to steer the learner toward the target policy, then solves a support-restricted penalty design to certify the manipulated target remains optimal, achieving Õ(T^{3/4}) total cost. Empirically, the authors approximate MuJoCo environments (Mountain-Car, Half-Cheetah, Pendulum) as linear MDPs via CTRL contrastive representation learning and verify that the CQP prediction of vulnerable vs. robust matches observed attack success/failure rates above 90%.

## Results

On vulnerable (ε* > 0) MuJoCo environments, the CQP characterization predicts attack success with 100% accuracy on sub-linear perturbation (M1) and 97–100% on achieving the adversarial target policy (M2) across Half-Cheetah, MountainCar, and Pendulum. On robust (ε* ≤ 0) environments, the characterization correctly predicts attack failure with 92–95% accuracy on M1 and 88–93% on M2. The black-box two-stage attack achieves a sublinear cost bound of Õ(d^{3/2} H^{5/2} S T^{3/4}) with T₁ = Θ(√T), and the learner follows the target policy in T − Õ(T^{3/4}) episodes on the certified support.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is not a head-to-head attack benchmark but rather a validation of the CQP characterization's predictive power against observed attack behavior. The AT attack (Xu & Singh, 2025) is implemented as a baseline attack algorithm alongside the paper's white-box and black-box methods, but the main empirical tables (Tables 1–2) focus on whether the CQP correctly classifies environments as vulnerable or robust rather than directly comparing attack costs across methods. The key finding is that the CQP characterization achieves 92–100% predictive accuracy in distinguishing attackable from intrinsically robust instances, a capability the AT baseline does not provide. In vulnerable environments, the paper's CQP-guided attack produces sub-linear cumulative perturbations (flattening curves) and drives the victim policy to converge to the adversarial target, while in robust environments the perturbation cost grows super-linearly and the victim policy fails to converge—behaviors consistent with the theoretical ε* > 0 vs. ε* ≤ 0 boundary.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the intrinsic robustness characterization from linear bandits (Wang et al., 2022) and tabular MDPs (Zhang et al., 2020) to general finite-horizon linear MDPs, providing the first complete (necessary and sufficient) attackability criterion via a convex quadratic program. While prior RL poisoning work focused on constructing attacks under sufficient conditions or deriving global budget thresholds, this paper's CQP formulation cleanly separates vulnerable from intrinsically robust instances in an algorithm-agnostic manner. The black-box attack builds on the white-box CQP solution by adding a two-stage estimation-and-certification pipeline, and the empirical validation leverages CTRL (Zhang et al., 2022) to approximate non-linear MuJoCo environments as linear MDPs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The CQP characterization provides a practical, computationally tractable diagnostic: before deploying an RL system, one can test whether its environment geometry is inherently vulnerable to reward poisoning, independent of the learning algorithm. This reframes robustness as a property of the environment's feature geometry rather than solely an algorithmic concern. Key limitations include the reliance on the linear MDP assumption (real environments are non-linear), the pointwise optimality criterion (extensions to stochastic policies are deferred), and empirical validation on only three MuJoCo tasks. Open questions include whether the characterization extends to non-linear function classes, multi-agent settings, and whether the CTRL approximation preserves attackability predictions in higher-dimensional or more complex environments.
