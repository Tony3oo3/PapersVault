## Problem

Approximating the curvature (Hessian) of a loss function is essential for second-order optimization, Bayesian inference, and model compression, yet remains computationally prohibitive at the scale of modern deep networks. Despite the widespread use of structured approximations (Kronecker-factored, block-diagonal), no prior work has exploited the curvature constraints that arise from well-known weight-space symmetries—permutations, signed permutations, and orthogonal transformations—that leave the loss invariant. This paper asks: can we analytically distill curvature information from a single gradient by averaging over the symmetry group's orbit?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single loss gradient g, the current parameter vector w, and a user-specified symbolic description of the symmetry group G (which axes of which parameter tensors are transformed by which group, e.g., signed permutations on hidden layers). It produces a structured Hessian approximation H_g* = S_g^{1/2} stored compactly as a small set of scalar or matrix-valued 'factors' weighting a fixed basis of sparse Kronecker-product tensors, rather than as a full dense matrix. The factors are computed via closed-form least-squares projection, and the resulting preconditioner yields the optimizer update w_{t+1} = w_t − η(H_g*)^{-1} g_t. A just-in-time compiler translates the symbolic group specification into efficient PyTorch routines for computing the orbit averages and their matrix functions.

## Method

The core mechanism is orbit averaging: loss invariance under a symmetry group G implies gradient equivariance, so a single gradient g determines all gradients on the group orbit {Ag : A ∈ G}. The authors perform a second-order Taylor expansion around the orbit-averaged point w* = R_1(w, G), derive the secant condition g − g* ≈ H*(w − w*), and orbit-average the outer product to obtain S_g ≈ H* S_w H*, whose unique positive semi-definite solution is H_g* = S_g^{1/2} (after assuming S_w ≈ I to avoid rank-deficiency issues). Crucially, the orbit averages S_g and S_w belong to the commutant (centralizer) algebra of G, so they decompose as a linear superposition of a small number of basis elements—sparse binary tensors expressible as Kronecker products of identity and ones matrices—whose coefficients (factors) are found by orthogonal projection (least-squares). The size of the symmetry group directly controls the trade-off: larger groups yield fewer factors (cheaper) but larger orbits (less local curvature), while smaller groups give more factors but tighter Taylor approximations. A key theoretical result proves that restricting the group to signed permutations on alternating layers with identity on the rest recovers the Shampoo/Muon update exactly, providing a principled explanation for why these optimizers work and a systematic recipe for constructing symmetry-aware Hessian approximations at any desired computational budget.

## Results

On the Hessian approximation quality metric (cosine similarity between g′−g and H̃(w′−w)), the proposed approximations H*_PD, H*_g, and H*_g(BD) achieve similarities of 0.1–0.2 in random directions, comparable to Shampoo (~0.2), while the orbit-averaged true Hessian H* is near zero. Along the negative gradient direction (more relevant for optimization), H*_g(BD) overlaps completely with Shampoo's curvature matrix, confirming the proven mathematical equivalence. On the MNIST autoencoder (2.8M params) and nanoGPT Shakespeare task (~10M params), Symo and Muon training curves overlap nearly perfectly, and both substantially outperform Adam in test MSE and test loss respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Adam, Symo (and equivalently Muon) clearly wins on both the MNIST autoencoder (lower test MSE) and the nanoGPT character-level prediction task (lower test loss), with the gap widening over training. Against Shampoo, the paper proves that restricting the symmetry group to signed permutations on alternating layers with identity on input/output layers makes Symo's update mathematically identical to Shampoo/Muon (up to a constant scaling); empirically, Symo (smaller group, block-diagonal) in Fig. 6 yields optimization performance comparable to Shampoo on the autoencoder benchmark. Against Muon specifically, Symo's training curves overlap almost exactly on both MNIST and nanoGPT, with only tiny differences attributable to Muon-specific heuristics (e.g., momentum, Nesterov) that Symo deliberately omits to preserve mathematical soundness. In the Hessian approximation comparison (Fig. 4), Shampoo achieves the highest cosine similarity in random directions (~0.2), while the full (non-block-diagonal) H*_g is slightly lower but still competitive; the block-diagonal restriction H*_g(BD) does not degrade curvature estimation along the gradient direction, confirming that off-diagonal contributions are small.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Bernacchia (2025), who constructed a form of 'global curvature' by analytically averaging gradients across an ensemble of networks, but reframes the construction as a principled Hessian approximation for a single model and extends it from MLPs to Transformers. It also connects to the well-studied weight-space symmetries of neural networks (permutation, signed permutation, orthogonal invariance) and to prior observations that symmetries impose structural constraints on the Hessian (Kunin et al., 2020; Ziyin, 2023), but is the first to exploit these constraints for tractable curvature estimation from a single gradient. A key departure from prior second-order methods (KFAC, Shampoo, Soap) is that the structured Hessian is derived from group-theoretic orbit averaging rather than ad hoc Kronecker factorization, and the paper proves that Shampoo and Muon emerge as special cases corresponding to a specific intermediate-size symmetry group.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a unifying theoretical lens that explains why Shampoo/Muon work by revealing their implicit exploitation of weight-space symmetries, and offers a systematic dial (group size) to trade off approximation accuracy against computational cost. However, several limitations temper the impact: the Taylor approximation around the orbit average lacks tight bounds for trained (non-random) weights, the practical Hessian is restricted to block-diagonal form due to memory constraints, and no formal guarantee is established that the approximate Hessian shares eigenvectors with the true Hessian. The empirical validation is confined to small models (≤10M parameters), leaving open whether the symmetry-based approach scales to the billion-parameter regime where Shampoo/Muon have shown their greatest value. Future work could extend the framework to Bayesian inference, continual learning, and model compression, and to automated group selection from computational graphs.
