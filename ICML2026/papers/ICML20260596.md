## Problem

In reinforcement learning, smaller discount factors are commonly used in practice beyond what standard modeling rationales (inflation, termination probability) justify, yet the structural reason for this has not been formalized. The paper asks whether environmental transition noise can be rigorously mapped to a reduced effective discount factor, thereby shortening the planning horizon and yielding simpler, more interpretable policies without loss of optimality.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260596/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a finite-state MDP with a specified noise structure (sticky parameter β, reset parameter α with distribution μ, or reversible slowly-changing noise with activation probability ε and reward bounds C_min, C_max) and a discount factor γ, and produces a closed-form effective discount γ' along with either an exact optimal policy or a near-optimality gap bound. For sticky and reset noise the output is an exact policy equivalence; for the general reversible case the output is a nonstationary policy π' with a quantified value loss that depends on ε, γ, and the reward smoothness constants. The Tightrope counterexample shows that for catastrophic-risk noise, no γ' exists that reproduces the noisy optimal policy.

## Method

The core result is an algebraic equivalence: for sticky noise (P_β = βP + (1−β)I), the Bellman fixed point in the noisy MDP reduces to the clean MDP's Bellman equation with γ replaced by γ' = βγ/(1−γ(1−β)), yielding identical optimal policies. For reset noise (P_α = αP + (1−α)P^μ), the Sherman-Morrison formula decomposes the noisy value function into the clean value at γα plus a policy-dependent offset that preserves optimality ordering, giving γ' = αγ independent of the reset distribution. For reversible, slowly-changing noise, the authors construct a nonstationary policy that detects and reverses noise events, then bound the resulting value loss using the generating function of Catalan numbers to compute E[γ^Δ] in closed form, where Δ is the random-walk return time of a biased walk with parameter ε. The paper further establishes that generic smoothness bounds (Jiang et al., 2016) are vacuous at low noise levels, and provides a Tightrope MDP counterexample proving that catastrophic-risk noise admits no discount-equivalent representation at any γ'.

## Results

The paper establishes exact equivalence between two noise models and reduced discounting: sticky noise with parameter β maps to effective discount γ′ = βγ/(1−γ(1−β)), and reset noise with parameter α maps to γ′ = αγ, both yielding identical optimal policies. For reversible, slowly-changing noise with parameter ε, the paper provides a near-optimality bound showing that a policy optimized at γ′ = 2γ(1−ε)/(1+√(1−4γ²ε(1−ε))) loses at most (C_min·εγ/(1−2ε) + 2ε(1−2ε)^((1−2ε)/2ε))·1/(1−γ) in value. Empirically, in the Reacher environment, Jensen-Shannon Divergence between reward distributions confirms that minimal noise (σ≈0.01) mirrors high-γ (0.99) behavior, intermediate noise (0.03≤σ≤0.07) matches mid-range discounts, and high noise (σ≥0.1) reproduces low-γ rotational failure modes.

| Reversible, slowly-changing noise | Reduced γ′ < γ (closed-form in ε, γ) | Near-optimality guarantee: bounded value loss |

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper, so comparisons are between noise model classes rather than competing algorithms. Sticky noise and reset noise both achieve zero optimality loss (exact policy equivalence), making them strictly stronger than the generic bounds from Jiang et al. (2016), which only become tight when noise is so large that all actions produce nearly identical next-state distributions (δ_P → 0). The reversible, slowly-changing noise result provides a non-trivial near-optimality gap that decreases as ε increases, whereas generic smoothness-based bounds degrade at low noise levels. The Tightrope counterexample demonstrates a fundamental limitation: for noise that introduces catastrophic risk (e.g., falling off a tightrope with probability ε > 0.5), no choice of discount factor in the noiseless MDP can reproduce the optimal noisy policy, since the safe-path policy is never optimal in the clean setting regardless of γ. In the grid experiments, reset noise (α=0.95) shows exact policy equivalence as predicted, while uniform noise (ε=0.3) shows approximate but not exact equivalence, consistent with the theory that uniform noise contains a sticky component but also introduces non-sticky perturbations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper formally connects environmental stochasticity to the discount factor in MDPs, extending the supervised-learning insight that noise enlarges the set of near-optimal hypotheses (Semenova et al., 2022; Boner et al., 2024) to sequential decision-making where uncertainty propagates through transition dynamics. It departs from the robust MDP literature (Nilim & El Ghaoui, 2005; Iyengar, 2005), which models uncertainty adversarially, by studying stochastic non-adversarial noise and showing it acts as a structural horizon-shortener rather than an obstacle. The generic bounds from Jiang et al. (2016) on planning-horizon loss inform the analysis, but the paper's contribution is exact equivalences for structured noise (sticky, reset) and tight near-optimality guarantees for reversible slowly-changing noise, going well beyond the weak bounds available under generic smoothness assumptions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, mathematically grounded explanation for the widespread empirical practice of using smaller discount factors in RL than modeling assumptions would justify, reframing the discount factor as a proxy for unmodeled environmental uncertainty rather than purely a time-preference parameter. The exact equivalence results (noise-free MDP with reduced γ yields the same optimal policy) offer a practical recipe: train on a clean environment with a smaller γ to implicitly account for structured noise. Key limitations include the requirement of specific noise structure for exact results, the Tightrope counterexample showing complete breakdown under catastrophic risk, and the gap between the theoretical reversible/slowly-changing assumptions and realistic continuous-control settings. Open questions include extending the framework to partially observable settings, characterizing which general noise distributions admit approximate discount-equivalent representations, and connecting the horizon-shortening effect to concrete interpretability metrics for learned policies.
