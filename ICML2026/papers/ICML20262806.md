## Problem

Existing autoformalization approaches treat Lean code as flat token sequences, neglecting the hierarchical operator precedence and logical scoping inherent in mathematical statements. This monolithic end-to-end generation makes it difficult to localize and repair errors without disrupting correctly formalized sub-expressions, and no rigorous benchmark exists for evaluating formalization on graduate-level theorems.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262806/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes a raw natural language theorem statement and first decomposes it into atomic NL components (conditions and conclusion) via semantic canonicalization and structural role alignment. The formalizer then jointly outputs, for each component, a linear Lean 4 code fragment and its corresponding FL OPT (a labeled, ordered tree with operators as internal nodes and arguments as ordered children, with parentheses explicitly retained for token-level consistency). The final output is an assembled Lean 4 theorem statement verified by the compiler, with the OPT serving as the structural blueprint throughout.

## Method

DSR is a three-stage neuro-symbolic pipeline: (1) Decompose uses Gemini 3.0 Pro to canonicalize the NL statement and segment it into CONDITION/CONCLUSION components; (2) Structure fine-tunes Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct via LoRA under a four-phase curriculum (atomic → simple → moderate → complex OPTs) to jointly predict FL components and FL OPTs, with a replay mechanism mixing current and previous difficulty tiers; (3) Repair maps Lean compiler error positions to specific OPT nodes and escalates through subcomponent-level, component-level, and statement-level repair, performing surgical edits on minimal subtrees rather than regenerating the full statement. The key insight is that the operator tree serves a dual role: as a structural training signal that forces the model to internalize recursive logical topology, and as a topological blueprint that enables precise error localization during repair, avoiding the semantic degradation of global regeneration.

## Results

Under a standardized budget of 4 inference calls, DSR achieves the highest Consistency Check (CC) pass rates across all three benchmarks: 84.00% on ProverBench, 79.51% on ProofNet, and 67.95% on PRIME. On ProofNet, DSR's CC of 79.51% represents a +8.62% margin over the strongest baseline (Goedel-V2-Formalizer-32B at 63.07%), while on PRIME it leads by +5.13% (67.95% vs. 62.82%). DSR also achieves competitive Syntax Check (SC) rates of 95.38%, 87.33%, and 80.13% respectively, with the gap to the best SC baseline narrowing as theorem complexity increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DSR outperforms all five baseline families (Qwen3-Max, Kimina-Autoformalizer-7B, StepFun-Formalizer-7B/32B, Goedel-V2-Formalizer-8B/32B, ATF-8B/32B) on the Consistency Check metric across all three benchmarks, with the largest margins on ProofNet (+8.62% over Goedel-V2-Formalizer-32B) and PRIME (+5.13%). On Syntax Check, DSR is competitive but not always the absolute best: Goedel-V2-Formalizer-32B edges out DSR on ProverBench SC (96.31% vs. 95.38%), while DSR leads on ProofNet SC (87.33% vs. 72.51%) and PRIME SC (80.13% vs. 77.56%). The DSR-Global ablation (using statement-level repair instead of tree-guided repair) achieves slightly higher SC on PRIME (83.97% vs. 80.13%) but lower CC (66.03% vs. 67.95%), confirming that tree-guided repair preserves semantic fidelity better than brute-force global regeneration. Notably, DSR uses a 7B backbone (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) while Goedel-V2-Formalizer-32B and StepFun-Formalizer-32B use 32B models, making DSR's CC advantage particularly significant given the 4.5× parameter gap. On the supplementary FATE series, DSR achieves the highest SC on FATE-M (88.00%) and FATE-X (31.00%), with CC within 4% of Goedel-V2-Formalizer-32B across all three FATE splits.

## Relation to Prior Work

DSR departs from the dominant paradigm of treating autoformalization as monolithic NL-to-FL sequence generation by introducing a neuro-symbolic pipeline that decomposes statements into logical components, generates operator trees (OPTs) as structural representations, and performs tree-guided repair. It builds on the OPT representation from ASSESS (Liu et al., 2026) and the GTED metric (Liu et al., 2025b), but repurposes these from evaluation tools into generative training signals and repair blueprints. The decomposition stage is conceptually distinct from DRIFT's retrieval-oriented decomposition and SITA's structure-to-instance instantiation, as DSR's decomposition serves to reduce proposition dimensionality for subsequent tree generation. The curriculum learning strategy for jointly training linear code and tree outputs is a novel training recipe not present in prior autoformalization work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The PRIME benchmark (156 expert-formalized graduate-level theorems) fills a gap in evaluation infrastructure, as prior benchmarks (ProverBench, ProofNet) focus on high-school to undergraduate problems. The finding that component-level OPTs are essential while statement-level OPTs fail (Appendix B.4) provides a practical design principle for structured generation in formal mathematics. Key limitations include dependence on initial decomposition quality (error propagation risk), the non-trivial optimization barrier when adding OPT supervision without curriculum learning, and hallucination of non-existent Mathlib identifiers. Extending DSR from theorem statements to complete proofs and document-level formalization remains an open challenge that will require handling concepts beyond current Mathlib coverage.
