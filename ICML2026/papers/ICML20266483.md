## Problem

Adversarial ML research has shifted from well-defined problems (e.g., ℓp-bounded perturbations on classifiers with clear success metrics) to LLM security problems where objectives are subjective, attack spaces are unbounded, and evaluation is non-reproducible. The paper argues that this shift makes it increasingly difficult to define what constitutes progress, solve the underlying optimization problems, or rigorously compare attacks and defenses, risking another decade of unproductive research.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the analysis takes as input the existing body of adversarial ML research across six LLM security areas (jailbreaks, un-finetunable models, poisoning/backdoors, prompt injections, membership inference, and unlearning) and produces a structured taxonomy of challenges organized along three axes: defining (success criteria, attack bounding, data delimiting), solving (attack search, principled defenses), and evaluating (harm/utility measurement, reproducibility). Table 1 maps which specific challenges apply to each research area, revealing that no single area avoids all three categories of difficulty.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a diagnostic framework that identifies why LLMs' general-purpose nature fundamentally breaks the assumptions that made traditional adversarial ML tractable: (1) safety properties cannot be expressed as formal loss functions, making gradient-based optimization inapplicable and leaving manual attacks outperforming automated methods; (2) attack spaces are unbounded and input-independent (any prompt could elicit harm), eliminating the ℓp-ball analogy that enabled quantitative comparison; and (3) evaluation relies on LLM-as-a-judge for subjective harm assessment and closed-source models that update silently, undermining reproducibility. The framework is then instantiated through six detailed case studies, each illustrating how these three challenge categories manifest concretely. The paper concludes by distinguishing two valid research motivations—real-world security and scientific understanding—and argues that the field should focus on formal, well-defined sub-problems to enable rigorous progress tracking.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experiments or headline metrics. Its central argument is that adversarial ML for LLMs faces three compounding challenges—problems are harder to define (no formal safety objectives, unbounded attack spaces, no clear data boundaries), harder to solve (discrete optimization is near-random, manual attacks outperform automated ones, and no principled defenses exist), and harder to evaluate (LLM-as-a-judge is unreliable, closed-source models are updated silently, and reproducibility is undermined). The authors illustrate these challenges through six case studies (jailbreaks, un-finetunable models, poisoning/backdoors, prompt injections, membership inference, and unlearning), arguing that without returning to well-scoped formal sub-problems, the field risks another unproductive decade.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a method or compare against quantitative baselines. Instead, it qualitatively contrasts the traditional adversarial ML paradigm (e.g., ℓₚ-bounded perturbations on classifiers with clear loss functions, white-box gradient-based attacks, and accuracy-based evaluation) against the current LLM adversarial ML landscape, where none of these structured ingredients exist. The paper highlights that in the classical setting, the strongest attacks were found via automated white-box optimization (e.g., PGD, Carlini-Wagner), whereas for LLMs, manual/human-crafted attacks (e.g., multi-turn social engineering, persona modulation) still outperform automated methods. It also notes that defenses like adversarial training, latent adversarial training, and output filters lack formal security guarantees, in contrast to certified defenses (e.g., randomized smoothing) available in the classification setting. The authors frame the core tension as a choice between studying real-world unbounded adversaries (which the field has largely adopted) versus defining tractable toy sub-problems (which would enable rigorous progress tracking).

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not introduce a new method or benchmark but instead synthesizes and critiques the trajectory of adversarial ML from its origins in well-defined classification tasks (adversarial examples, poisoning, membership inference) to its current application to general-purpose LLMs. It draws on the established adversarial robustness literature (Szegedy 2013; Goodfellow 2014; Madry 2017) as a baseline to argue that the shift to LLMs has qualitatively degraded the field's ability to define, solve, and evaluate security problems. The paper positions itself as a counterpoint to the optimistic progress narrative (e.g., Achiam et al. 2023; Zaremba et al. 2025) and to recent work on representation engineering and latent adversarial training, arguing these approaches lack the formal grounding that made traditional adversarial ML tractable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If the paper's central claim holds—that LLM adversarial ML problems are fundamentally harder to define, solve, and evaluate than their classical counterparts—then the field risks another decade of unproductive work on ill-posed problems. The authors call for a return to well-scoped, formal sub-problems (analogous to ℓ_p-bounded perturbations) as a prerequisite for meaningful progress, raising the open question of what such 'toy' formulations look like for general-purpose language models. A key unresolved tension is between the practical urgency of securing deployed LLM systems and the scientific need for rigorous, reproducible evaluation; the paper argues that the current reliance on LLM-as-a-judge, closed-source models, and ad-hoc attacks undermines both. The observation that human-crafted attacks still outperform automated optimization methods challenges a core methodological assumption inherited from classical adversarial ML.
