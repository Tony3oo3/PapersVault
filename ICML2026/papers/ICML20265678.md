## Problem

Existing RL-based red-teaming methods that seek diverse attack prompts suffer from mode collapse: once a few high-reward prompt categories are discovered, the attacker stops exploring and concentrates on those easy modes, leaving harder vulnerabilities unexplored. Diversity-seeking objectives like novelty rewards or GFlowNet multi-mode sampling alone cannot overcome this exploration barrier because the reward landscape remains static throughout training.

## Input / Output

The method takes three components as input: an attacker LLM (e.g., Qwen2.5-1.5B) that generates candidate prompts, a victim LLM (e.g., Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, gemma-3-4b-it, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3) that produces responses, and a toxicity classifier (Meta-Llama-Guard-2-8B) that scores prompt-response pairs. The output is a diverse set of 1024 attack prompts collected across multiple adaptive rounds, which are then used to safety fine-tune the victim LLM via supervised fine-tuning on prompt-refusal pairs. A key structural detail is that the victim LLM itself is modified during training (periodically safety fine-tuned), making the environment non-stationary, and a global replay buffer aggregates prompts from all rounds to produce the final training set.

## Method

Active Attacks introduces an adaptive-environment mechanism into RL-based red-teaming by periodically safety fine-tuning the victim LLM on previously collected attack prompts (every R steps), which flattens the reward signal in already-exploited regions and forces the attacker to discover new vulnerabilities. A second component reinitializes the attacker LLM to the reference distribution after each victim update, preventing it from overfitting to previously discovered local modes. The attacker is trained with an off-policy GFlowNet trajectory-balance objective (or alternatively PPO/REINFORCE), using a replay buffer for sample efficiency, while a global buffer aggregates prompts across all rounds to avoid sequential bias in the final safety fine-tuning dataset. Together, these two components induce an easy-to-hard exploration curriculum: the attacker first exploits easy high-reward modes, which are then neutralized by victim adaptation, progressively driving exploration toward harder, previously unexplored categories. The approach is plug-and-play, requiring only a few additional lines of code on top of existing RL red-teaming pipelines, and adds only ~6% training time.

## Results

GFlowNet + Active Attacks achieves a 99.71% average defense rate across four victim LLMs (Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, gemma-3-4b-it, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3), compared to 91.00% for GFlowNet, 82.86% for PPO+Novelty, and 81.48% for REINFORCE. In cross-attack evaluation, Active Attacks achieves a 31.28% attack success rate against a GFlowNet-safety-tuned victim, while GFlowNet achieves only 0.07% against an Active-Attacks-tuned victim — a relative gain of over 440×. Transfer experiments show Active Attacks maintains 97–99% defense rates across nine unseen target LLMs (up to 70B parameters), while GFlowNet drops to 61–99% with high variance. The method adds only 5.76% wall-clock time and 25.39 GB GPU memory over the base GFlowNet training stage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against REINFORCE (Williams, 1992), Active Attacks improves average defense rate from 81.48% to 99.71%, with the gap most pronounced on gemma-3-4b (71.76% → 99.82%) where REINFORCE's on-policy nature limits mode coverage. Against PPO+Novelty (Hong et al., 2024), the improvement is from 82.86% to 99.71%; PPO+Novelty's novelty reward mitigates but does not eliminate mode collapse, as it still concentrates on a single category (e.g., S2: Non-violent Crimes). Against GFlowNet (Lee et al., 2025a), the closest baseline, Active Attacks improves average defense from 91.00% to 99.71% and achieves a 440× relative gain in cross-attack success rate (31.28% vs. 0.07%), indicating that GFlowNet's diversity-seeking objective alone cannot escape easy-to-find reward modes. Against GCG (Zou et al., 2023), a jailbreaking method, Active Attacks achieves 86.78% cross-attack success while GCG achieves 0.00% against an Active-Attacks-tuned victim, though the authors note this comparison is not strictly apples-to-apples since GCG operates in a conditional setting. In transfer experiments, Active Attacks consistently exceeds 97% defense across all nine unseen target LLMs, while GFlowNet shows high variance (61.91%–99.71%) and REINFORCE/PPO+Novelty drop below 70% on several targets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on GFlowNet-based red-teaming (Lee et al., 2025a), adopting its trajectory-balance off-policy objective for the attacker LLM, and on the adaptive-teacher principle from Kim et al. (2025), where an auxiliary policy is modified to push a student toward underexplored regions. The key departure is repurposing distributional adaptation for red-teaming: rather than modifying the sampling objective to encourage diversity, the paper periodically safety-fine-tunes the victim LLM to flatten already-exploited reward regions, creating an implicit easy-to-hard curriculum. This is positioned as a plug-and-play module that composes with existing RL objectives (GFlowNet, PPO, REINFORCE) rather than replacing them, and it departs from prior diversity-seeking approaches (novelty rewards, GFlowNet MLE smoothing) that operate solely on the attacker's objective while leaving the environment fixed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a simple periodic fine-tuning step (adding only ~6% compute) yields near-perfect defense rates across four open-source victim LLMs and transfers effectively to nine additional models up to 70B parameters, as well as to closed-source GPT-4o and GPT-4.1. This suggests a scalable strategy where attacks are discovered on small models and transferred, avoiding repeated expensive training loops on every target. Key limitations include dependence on the toxicity classifier's coverage and calibration (only 11 hazard categories from Llama-Guard), testing primarily on sub-8B victim models, and the open question of whether the adaptive curriculum generalizes to broader safety dimensions beyond the classifier's predefined categories. The proposed categorical distance metric also addresses a real gap in how diversity is measured in red-teaming, since embedding-based cosine distance can mask category-level collapse.
