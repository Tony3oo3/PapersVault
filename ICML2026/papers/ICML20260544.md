## Problem

While Transformer approximation power is well characterized, the mechanistic theory of how attention itself evolves during training remains largely absent. Existing analyses rely on reparameterizations or proxy dynamics that obscure the native coupling among embeddings, projection, and attention, and cannot explain the empirically observed transition from concentrated to diffuse attention patterns. This paper asks: what is the dynamical origin of attention amplification (focus) and its subsequent dissipation (dilution) during training?

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on a one-layer Transformer consuming token sequences of length s generated by a Markov chain with transition matrix P = λI + (1−λ)1πᵀ and stationary distribution π, in the population gradient-flow limit (N,s→∞) with small initialization (ε≪1). The model outputs a next-token distribution via cross-entropy, but the primary analytical output is a four-stage decomposition of the training trajectory: initial rank-one condensation, attention growth (focus on high-frequency tokens), attention dilution via embedding mass redistribution, and emergence of a new embedding direction. The stationary distribution is parameterized as πᵢ = (1−π₁)/(d−1) + cᵢδ for i≥2, where δ controls low-frequency asymmetry.

## Method

The paper develops a stage-wise linearization framework around successive critical points of the gradient flow. At the origin, the linearization forces W₀ and W₁ to condense onto a π-driven rank-one structure while attention parameters remain frozen (since ∂L/∂Φ|_{θ=0}=0). At the second critical point θ_c¹, a block decomposition of the Jacobian reveals a contracting embedding subsystem and a single unstable attention mode, causing W_Q and W_K to grow exponentially and bias attention toward high-frequency tokens (focus). Beyond linearization, restricting to the rank-one invariant manifold exposes a closed reduced system where the attention amplitude η(t) feeds back into embedding dynamics, forcing a growing separation between high- and low-frequency token embeddings that progressively dilutes the focus. Finally, a Lyapunov-Schmidt reduction at the degenerate critical point on the rank-one manifold shows that a small O(δ) symmetry-breaking perturbation in the stationary distribution creates a fast transverse unstable eigenvalue of order Θ(δ), unlocking new embedding directions and initiating the next cycle.

## Results

The paper identifies a four-stage focus-dilution cycle in one-layer Transformer training on Markovian data: (I) rank-one condensation of embeddings W₀, W₁ driven by the stationary distribution π, (II) exponential growth of attention parameters W_Q, W_K producing a high-frequency token bias, (III) mass-redistribution in embeddings that dilutes the attention focus, and (IV) symmetry-breaking via small frequency asymmetries (δ) that lifts a degenerate critical point and opens a new embedding direction. Empirically, on synthetic data with π = (0.75, 0.19, 0.05, 0.01), all four stages are clearly visible in loss curves, cosine-similarity heatmaps, PCA embedding trajectories, and attention maps. On real-world WikiText and TinyStories, the same focus→dilution pattern is observed for high-frequency tokens (e.g., whitespace, 'the', 'a'), with embedding retraction confirmed for medium-frequency tokens. The cycle is shown to repeat recursively for subsequent tokens, and the mechanism persists under residual connections and multi-layer architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical/mechanistic analysis paper rather than a method-comparison study, so there are no direct baselines to beat or match. The paper's contribution is a stage-wise gradient-flow explanation (via linearization at successive saddle points) of attention training dynamics, validated against empirical observations on synthetic Markov data, WikiText, and TinyStories. The closest 'comparison' is with prior theoretical frameworks: the initial condensation stage builds on Chen & Luo (2025), the Markovian data setting follows Makkuva et al. (2024, 2025) and Chang et al. (2024), and the multi-stage transition techniques extend Chen et al. (2024c) and Xu et al. (2025a). The paper positions itself as filling a gap left by prior work that either relies on reparameterizations, proxy dynamics, or overly simplified data settings that obscure the native coupling among embeddings, projection, and attention. No quantitative head-to-head benchmark against competing methods is presented.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the one-layer Transformer-on-Markov-data framework established by Makkuva et al. (2024, 2025) and the initial condensation analysis of Chen & Luo (2025) by developing a full stage-wise linearization around successive critical points that captures the native coupling among embeddings, projection, and attention—avoiding the reparameterizations or proxy dynamics used in prior work (Zhang et al., 2024a; Tarzanagh et al., 2023). The cross-stage error estimation techniques are borrowed from Chen et al. (2024c) and Xu et al. (2025a) for multi-stage dynamics in other architectures. The key conceptual departure is identifying the focus-dilution cycle as a recurring, self-similar pattern: prior observations of attention shifting from concentrated to diffuse (Tian et al., 2024; Chang et al., 2024) were documented empirically but lacked a mechanistic account of why dilution occurs and how new learning directions are unlocked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous mechanistic explanation for why attention in Transformers does not simply sharpen monotonically but instead undergoes repeated focus-dilution cycles, with the dilution driven by a mass-redistribution feedback from attention into embeddings. This could reframe how practitioners interpret multi-stage loss curves and attention sparsity transitions during pre-training. However, the analysis is confined to a single-layer, single-head, no-LayerNorm setting on Markovian data; extending the framework to multi-layer interactions, multi-head structure, and realistic architectural components (residuals, normalization) remains open, and the real-data validation, while encouraging, still uses the simplified architecture rather than full-scale Transformers.
