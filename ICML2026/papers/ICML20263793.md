## Problem

Current AI agent evaluation relies almost exclusively on mean task success rates, which cannot distinguish an agent that fails predictably on a fixed subset of tasks from one that fails randomly at the same rate, nor can it separate benign formatting errors from catastrophic actions like deleting production databases. This paper argues that reliability is a multi-dimensional property—encompassing consistency, robustness, predictability, and safety—that is independent of raw capability and must be measured explicitly, drawing on decades of practice from safety-critical engineering domains like aviation and nuclear power.

## Input / Output

The evaluation protocol consumes K=5 repeated runs per task (at temperature zero to isolate non-sampling stochasticity), J=5 semantically equivalent prompt paraphrases per task, injected infrastructure faults at probability p=0.2, and post-hoc self-assessed confidence scores. Outputs are 12 sub-metrics normalized to [0,1], aggregated into four dimension scores (R_Con, R_Rob, R_Pred, R_Saf) and an overall reliability score R. Notably, safety is deliberately excluded from the overall aggregate to prevent averaging from masking tail risks, and is instead reported as a hard constraint.

## Method

The paper translates safety-critical engineering principles into a suite of twelve computable metrics organized along four dimensions, all designed to be independent of raw accuracy. Consistency is measured via Bernoulli-variance-normalized outcome agreement, pairwise Jensen-Shannon divergence over action-type distributions, normalized Levenshtein distance over action sequences, and exponential-transformed coefficient of variation over resource usage. Robustness uses clamped accuracy ratios (perturbed/baseline) under three perturbation types: fault injection, environment format changes, and prompt paraphrasing. Predictability combines Expected Calibration Error, AUC-ROC discrimination, and the Brier score as a proper scoring rule. Safety follows the Kaplan-Garrick risk formulation, decomposing into violation probability (compliance) and conditional expected severity (harm), with severity weighted as low=0.25, medium=0.5, high=1.0. The key design choice is that every metric conditions on or normalizes by accuracy, so a model at 60% accuracy can be more reliable than one at 70% if it fails on a fixed, identifiable subset rather than unpredictably.

## Results

Across 15 models evaluated on GAIA (165 tasks) and τ-bench (26 clean tasks) with K=5 runs per task, overall reliability shows only minimal improvement over 24 months of model releases, despite substantial accuracy gains. Outcome consistency remains low across all models, with even frontier models failing to reliably reproduce successes. Prompt robustness is the key differentiator: models handle genuine technical faults gracefully (ceiling effects on fault and environment robustness) yet remain vulnerable to surface-level instruction paraphrasing. On τ-bench, financial accuracy violations are the most prevalent safety failure mode, though recent frontier models exhibit markedly lower overall violation rates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares 15 models from three providers (OpenAI, Google, Anthropic) spanning multiple capability tiers and release dates from early 2024 to mid-2026. All frontier model providers cluster similarly on overall reliability, indicating an industry-wide plateau rather than a vendor-specific limitation. On consistency, smaller models frequently achieve equal or higher scores than larger ones, suggesting that larger models' multiple solution paths increase run-to-run variability. On predictability, Claude models demonstrate stronger calibration on both benchmarks, while discrimination improves on τ-bench but not on GAIA (and even worsens in some recent models). On safety, the most capable models from each provider achieve the highest compliance scores, with high-severity violations remaining rare but carrying outsized costs. Reasoning models are generally more reliable than non-reasoning counterparts, though their reliability gains lag behind their accuracy improvements.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws its conceptual architecture from safety-critical engineering standards (aviation DO-178C, nuclear IEC 61508, automotive ISO 26262) and translates their multi-dimensional reliability decomposition into computable metrics for LLM agents. It explicitly builds on Liang et al. (2022)'s holistic evaluation philosophy for language models, but narrows the focus to agent-specific behavioral properties. Individual sub-metrics repurpose existing ML tools—Jensen-Shannon divergence for trajectory comparison, Levenshtein distance for sequence alignment, Expected Calibration Error and AUC-ROC for predictability, and the Kaplan-Garrick risk product for safety—yet the paper's contribution is the unifying four-dimensional taxonomy (consistency, robustness, predictability, safety) that organizes these scattered concerns into a single evaluation protocol independent of raw accuracy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central empirical finding—that 24 months of rapid capability gains across 15 frontier models have produced only marginal reliability improvements, with all providers clustering at a similar plateau—challenges the implicit assumption that scaling and accuracy gains will naturally yield dependable agents. The identification of consistency and discrimination as the dimensions most resistant to improvement provides concrete research targets. Key limitations constrain generalizability: evaluation covers only two benchmarks with a single scaffold each, safety relies on LLM-based judging, and temperature is fixed at zero. Open questions include whether reliability can be improved through targeted training objectives, how to principledly integrate safety tail risks into an aggregate score, and whether the framework extends to multi-agent or long-horizon stateful settings.
