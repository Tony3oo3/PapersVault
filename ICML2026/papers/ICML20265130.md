## Problem

Generating correct symbolic circuit expressions (transfer functions, transient responses) directly from schematic images requires both accurate visual-to-symbolic construction of circuit topology and error-free multi-step algebraic derivation. End-to-end VLMs fail at both: they hallucinate component connections and accumulate algebraic errors across derivation steps, while existing MLLM circuit work targets topology recovery or sizing rather than symbolic expression generation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265130/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a circuit schematic image (RGB) and a free-form textual query (e.g., 'What is the transfer function from V1 to C1?') as input, and produces a closed-form symbolic expression in SymPy-compatible format (e.g., H(s) = ...). Evaluation uses SymPy-based symbolic equivalence with numerical substitution fallback rather than string matching, since algebraically equivalent expressions may differ in form. The benchmark spans five circuit types (pure resistive, RLC, small-signal, module-level, system-level block diagrams) totaling 5,020 samples from CircuitSense.

## Method

AutoVSR is a two-stage framework that decouples visual perception from symbolic computation. Stage 1 (Visual-to-Textual Topology Generation) uses a MoE-inspired task router to select between a SPICE-like Netlist IR (for Lcapy) or a JSON Signal Flow Graph IR (for Mason's rule), then performs component detection to dynamically retrieve only relevant syntax rules, and applies a four-stage dual-check verification flow (static AST parsing, I/O specification, dynamic solvability, symbolic quantity evaluation) with up to three VLM correction rounds. Stage 2 (IR-Based Symbolic Reasoning) implements a Plan-and-Execute agent where the VLM decomposes the analysis goal into 2–4 atomic steps, while 28 sandboxed symbolic tool APIs (Lcapy for nodal/mesh analysis, Mason's gain formula for SFGs) execute all equation construction and algebraic manipulation, with a strict 20-step budget to guarantee termination. This architecture ensures that visual errors are caught and corrected before derivation, and that algebraic steps are deterministic rather than model-generated.

## Results

On the CircuitSense benchmark (5,020 samples across five circuit types), AutoVSR achieves an average overall accuracy of 57.07% on transfer function generation, compared to 12.70% for the average end-to-end VLM baseline, yielding absolute improvements of 30.01–59.45 percentage points depending on the backbone model. The best configuration, AutoVSR(Gemini-3-Flash), reaches 82.85% overall accuracy. On transient response expression generation, AutoVSR improves accuracy from 2.85% to 85.84% (Gemini-3-Flash) and from 0.69% to 63.25% (GLM-4.6V-Flash). Against the specialized CircuitSense baseline, AutoVSR gains 41.96–51.84% on transfer function and 62.23–82.74% on transient response, while using fewer tokens (4,563–8,490 vs. 7,033–13,412) and lower latency (17.0–50.6 s vs. 89.5–148.4 s) than frontier closed-source models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AutoVSR consistently outperforms all seven end-to-end VLM baselines (Gemini-3-Flash, GPT-5-mini, Qwen3-VL-Plus, Claude-Haiku-4.5, Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct, Llama-4-17B-128E, GLM-4.6V-Flash) on transfer function generation, with the largest gains on simple resistive (Type 1, +66.43%) and RLC (Type 2, +64.49%) circuits and the smallest on system-level SFGs (Type 5, +29.89%). Against the specialized CircuitSense CoT method, AutoVSR wins on every model–task combination: 82.85% vs. 40.89% (Gemini-3-Flash, transfer function), 68.02% vs. 16.18% (Qwen3-VL-Plus), and 49.85% vs. 7.80% (GLM-4.6V-Flash), with even larger margins on transient response (85.84% vs. 3.10% for Gemini-3-Flash). In the efficiency comparison (Table 5), AutoVSR with lightweight backbones (56.3–89.3% accuracy, 4,563–8,490 tokens, 17.0–50.6 s) surpasses frontier closed-source models GPT-5 (45.6%), Claude-Sonnet-4.5 (26.2%), and Gemini-3-Pro (52.4%) in accuracy while consuming roughly half the tokens and completing inference in less than half the time. The ablation confirms that the symbolic tools library is the single most critical component: removing it drops accuracy from 82.85% to 20.49% (Gemini-3-Flash) and from 49.85% to 4.21% (GLM-4.6V-Flash), while other individual modules contribute 2–12 percentage points each.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of MLLM-based circuit understanding and symbolic computation toolchains. Prior MLLM circuit work (topology recovery, sizing/optimization) is structure- or parameter-centric and relies on SPICE simulation for evaluation, while the only directly comparable method, CircuitSense (Akbari et al., 2026), uses end-to-end CoT prompting without verifiable intermediate representations. AutoVSR departs from both by introducing an Executable IR (Netlist or SFG) as a bridge between visual perception and deterministic symbolic solvers (Lcapy, SymPy, Mason's rule), and by adopting a Plan-and-Execute agent architecture inspired by tool-learning literature (ToolLLM, strategy decomposition) to decouple high-level reasoning from algebraic execution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework demonstrates that decoupling visual-to-symbolic construction from multi-step derivation via a verified intermediate representation and tool-augmented solver yields large accuracy gains (30–59% over end-to-end VLMs) while enabling lightweight models to outperform frontier models in both accuracy and inference cost. This has practical implications for cost-sensitive engineering workflows where expensive API calls are prohibitive. Limitations include evaluation on a single benchmark (CircuitSense, 5020 samples), restriction to linear circuits (Lcapy's domain), and a fixed 20-step solver budget that may constrain very complex circuits. Open questions include scalability to non-linear or mixed-signal circuits, handling of multi-objective symbolic queries, and whether the verification loop generalizes to circuit types beyond the five evaluated.
