## Problem

LLM-generated research ideas often look convincing but prove ineffective when actually executed, and it remains unclear whether automated execution of open-ended research ideas is feasible at scale or whether LLMs can learn from execution feedback to improve idea quality. This paper tackles both questions by building a high-throughput automated executor and studying two learning algorithms—evolutionary search and reinforcement learning—on realistic GPU-intensive research problems.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264156/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The automated executor accepts a batch of natural language research ideas (each accompanied by a thinking trace and brief code-change description) along with a fixed baseline codebase (nanoGPT speedrun or GRPO post-training pipeline). It produces a code diff patched into the baseline, runs the experiment on GPU hardware (1 GPU for GRPO, 8 GPUs for nanoGPT), and returns a scalar performance metric—validation accuracy on MATH for post-training, or reciprocal of validation loss on FineWeb for pre-training—along with full training logs. Reward hacking is prevented by freezing evaluation code and using a token-by-token inference function for validation.

## Method

The system comprises three components: an Implementer that calls a code-execution LLM to generate and self-revise code diffs (up to 2 revision rounds), a Scheduler that allocates GPU resources, and a Worker that runs experiments and uploads results. On top of this executor, the paper studies two learning algorithms. First, an execution-guided evolutionary search (inspired by AlphaEvolve) that splits each epoch into exploitation (generating variants of ideas that beat the baseline) and exploration (generating novel ideas conditioned on subsampled prior trajectories), annealing the exploration rate from 50% downward over 10 epochs. Second, RL from execution reward using GRPO to finetune Qwen3-30B, where the executor's scalar output serves directly as the reward signal with group sizes of 256 (GRPO) or 128 (nanoGPT). The key empirical finding is that evolutionary search is sample-efficient—finding a 69.4% GRPO recipe and a 19.7-minute nanoGPT recipe within 10 epochs—while RL improves average reward but suffers from thinking-length collapse and convergence on a few simple ideas, failing to improve the upper bound.

## Results

On the GRPO post-training environment, execution-guided search with Claude-4.5-Sonnet achieves 69.4% validation accuracy on MATH, substantially outperforming the GRPO baseline of 48.0% and even the best human expert (68.8%) from a graduate-level LLM class. On the nanoGPT pre-training environment, execution-guided search with Claude-4.5-Opus reaches the target validation loss in 19.7 minutes versus 35.9 minutes for the baseline, though the best human expert achieves 2.1 minutes. The search significantly outperforms best-of-N under the same sampling budget from epoch 1 onward, confirming that the model leverages prior execution trajectories. RL from execution reward on Qwen3-30B improves average reward (e.g., GRPO accuracy from 0.253 to 0.343 over 40 epochs) but fails to improve the max reward due to diversity collapse.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the GRPO baseline (Shao et al., 2024), execution-guided search with Claude-4.5-Sonnet wins decisively, achieving 69.4% MATH validation accuracy versus the 48.0% baseline — a 21.4 percentage-point improvement — and even edges out the best human expert solution (68.8%) from a graduate LLM class. Against the nanoGPT speedrun baseline (Jordan et al., 2024), execution-guided search with Claude-4.5-Opus wins on training time, reaching the target validation loss in 19.7 minutes versus 35.9 minutes (an 1.8× speedup), but loses substantially to the best human expert on the speedrun leaderboard (2.1 minutes), indicating significant remaining headroom. The paper also shows that evolutionary search outperforms best-of-N sampling under identical compute budgets, and that RL from execution reward improves average performance but not the upper bound, revealing a fundamental limitation of gradient-based learning for open-ended idea generation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the evolutionary search scaffold from AlphaEvolve (Novikov et al., 2025) to the domain of open-ended AI research idea generation, where the search space is natural language ideas rather than code variants within a fixed operator set. It departs from prior LLM research agents (AI Scientist, Agent Laboratory) by closing the loop with an automated execution executor that provides scalar performance feedback, and from AutoML/NAS work by operating in a fully unconstrained idea space rather than a predefined architecture or hyperparameter space. The RL component builds on GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and the broader RLVR literature (DeepSeek-R1), but applies it to a fundamentally different reward structure where execution success is binary and idea quality is open-ended.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic study of whether LLMs can learn from execution feedback in open-ended research, and the key negative result—that standard GRPO causes diversity collapse and fails to improve the upper bound despite raising average reward—provides important guidance for the field. The finding that evolutionary search is sample-efficient but saturates for most models (only Claude-4.5-Opus shows scaling) suggests that current frontier LLMs have a limited 'idea ceiling' that better search or stronger base models may not easily overcome. Open questions include whether richer learning signals beyond scalar rewards, more capable execution agents, and explicit diversity/novelty objectives can break the mode collapse, and whether the discovered ideas generalize beyond the small-scale test environments.
