## Problem

Contour Integral (CI) methods for large-scale Generalized Eigenvalue Problems are critically sensitive to integration contour placement, yet the eigenvalue distribution is typically unknown a priori. Existing strategies rely on expensive scouting procedures (running iterative solvers for many steps) or manual heuristics, which can be as time-consuming as the main eigenvalue computation itself, undermining the parallel efficiency gains CI methods are designed to provide. No prior work has applied a data-driven approach to automate and optimize the contour construction process.

## Input / Output

The method takes a physical parameter function a(x) (e.g., material density, Young's modulus) discretized on a uniform grid of resolution N, along with the sparse Hermitian matrices A and B defining the GEP. The ENO backbone is grid-agnostic due to FNO's resolution invariance, so the same trained model generalizes across different N without retraining. The pipeline outputs a set of circular integration contours in the complex plane (each defined by a center and radius), which are then passed to a CI solver (CIRR or FEAST) to produce the final eigenvalues and eigenvectors; the number of target eigenvalues M is set to 1% of the matrix dimension N.

## Method

Deepcontour is a two-stage hybrid pipeline. Stage 1 employs an Eigen-NeuralOperator (ENO) — an FNO backbone (4 Fourier layers, 64 channels, 20 retained modes) followed by a 3-layer MLP head — trained end-to-end with MSE loss to predict the M target eigenvalues from the input function a(x). Stage 2 uses a KDE-based adaptive contour construction: a Gaussian-kernel sparsity function G_k(t) is evaluated over each spectral interval, and the spectrum is recursively partitioned at the minima of G_k(t) (the sparsest gaps) until each sub-interval contains between N_min and N_max eigenvalues; circular contours are then placed with a local inflation factor α and a global spectral-hull buffer ε, both calibrated once on a validation set. The key architectural insight is leveraging FNO's resolution invariance to obtain a single model that predicts spectra across discretization scales, while the KDE partitioning ensures contour boundaries fall in low-density spectral gaps, minimizing quadrature errors and keeping projected subproblems small. The framework is trained in a fully supervised manner on pre-computed eigenvalues (via Krylov-Schur at 10^{-12} tolerance) and requires only ~7.6–14.2 hours of one-time GPU training per domain.

## Results

On large-scale GEPs (N=50000, M=500 target eigenvalues), Deepcontour achieves up to 5.63× end-to-end speedup and 4.70× CI-solver speedup over the Arnoldi-Scout baseline on the Kirchhoff-Love Plate at 1e-2 tolerance. Across all five datasets and five tolerance levels (1e-2 to 1e-12), every reported speedup exceeds 1×, with the strongest gains at looser tolerances where scouting overhead dominates. The CI-solver speedup (isolating contour quality) ranges from 1.68× to 4.70×, confirming that the KDE-constructed contours are tighter and more efficient than scouting-derived rectangular contours. The ablation shows that removing ENO causes 32.4 missed eigenvalues on average, while removing KDE inflates solve time by 1.77× (44.5s vs. 25.2s) due to overly conservative contours.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Deepcontour outperforms all five scouting-based baselines across every dataset and tolerance level. The Arnoldi-Scout baseline is consistently the weakest, yielding the largest speedups (up to 5.63× end-to-end at 1e-2 on Kirchhoff-Love Plate), because its shift-and-invert Krylov iterations are expensive and produce coarse Ritz values that force a single large rectangular contour. GD-Scout (Riemannian gradient descent) and JD-Scout (Jacobi-Davidson) are intermediate, with end-to-end speedups of 3.8–4.6×. Lanczos-Scout and KrylovSchur-Scout are the strongest baselines (KrylovSchur being the most robust iterative method), yet Deepcontour still achieves 1.8–2.7× end-to-end speedup over them. The CI-solver speedup (which isolates contour quality independent of scouting cost) is consistently 1.6–4.7×, confirming that the KDE-constructed multi-contour partitioning produces smaller projected subproblems than the single conservative bounding box used by all scouting baselines. Speedup magnitude decreases as tolerance tightens (from 1e-2 to 1e-12), since the CI solve itself dominates runtime at high precision, but the advantage never disappears.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of neural operator learning and classical numerical linear algebra. It repurposes the Fourier Neural Operator (Li et al., 2020) — originally designed for PDE solution mapping — as a spectral distribution predictor, and combines it with Kernel Density Estimation (Lin et al., 2016; Senzaki et al., 2010) to automate contour construction for CI eigensolvers like CIRR and FEAST. The key departure from prior work is the hybrid paradigm: rather than using neural networks to solve the eigenvalue problem end-to-end (as in SpIN, NeuralEF, or Deep Ritz), Deepcontour uses the network solely as a fast prior that guides a classical, numerically rigorous solver. This contrasts with the established scouting-based localization approach, which relies on expensive iterative pre-computation to obtain a coarse spectral map.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 5.63× end-to-end speedup across five scientific domains and all tolerance levels addresses a genuine practical bottleneck in CI eigensolvers, making large-scale interior eigenvalue computation substantially more accessible. The ablation revealing that the ENO is critical for eigenvalue coverage while KDE primarily improves contour efficiency (reducing projected subproblem size) provides useful guidance for practitioners designing similar hybrid pipelines. Limitations include restriction to Hermitian GEPs with real spectra, dependence on training data coverage for the ENO, and the need for validation-set calibration of safety margins. Open questions include extension to non-Hermitian or complex-spectrum problems, robustness under significant distribution shift, and whether the FNO backbone is truly necessary versus simpler architectures for this prediction task.
