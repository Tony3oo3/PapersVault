## Problem

Contrastive learning with simple augmentations (random crop, color jitter, Gaussian blur) and even non-photorealistic images (fractal noise, dead leaves) produces representations that approach supervised recognition accuracy, yet no analytical theory explains why. The paper addresses this gap by asking: what is the globally optimal representation under a contrastive loss for any image dataset with stationary statistics, and can we predict what a CNN will learn from the dataset's power spectrum alone?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264482/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes single-channel grayscale or three-channel color images (tested at 32×32 for CIFAR, 128×128 for ImageNet/CelebA) and produces a K-dimensional embedding vector (K=32 in experiments). The embedding is structured as a set of frequency-specific measurements: each dimension corresponds to the power (or amplitude, for ReLU) of a specific DFT frequency, scaled inversely to the expected power at that frequency. For color images, each sinusoidal filter is sensitive to only one of three decorrelated channels (approximately gray, red-green, blue-yellow).

## Method

The paper derives a Gaussian Uniformity Plus Alignment (GUPA) loss equivalent to InfoNCE under a Gaussian embedding assumption, then exploits the fact that DFT coefficients of stationary signals are asymptotically Gaussian and pairwise independent to analytically solve for the optimal representation. For simple augmentations (circular crop, linear jitter, ideal low-pass blur), the global optimum is to measure squared DFT coefficients at K non-DC frequencies below the cutoff, scaled inversely to expected power—i.e., partial whitening—computable by a single-layer CNN with sinusoidal filters, a pointwise nonlinearity, global average pooling, and a linear whitening projection. For harder augmentations (crop plus noise, arbitrary blur kernels), the optimal first-layer filters remain sinusoids, but the specific frequencies and their weights are determined by a waterfilling algorithm applied to the generalized eigenvalue problem of the alignment and covariance matrices. Experiments confirm that SGD-trained single-layer CNNs empirically converge to sinusoidal filters and perform partial whitening across diverse datasets, with recognition accuracy improvements largely attributable to the whitening step.

## Results

This is primarily a theoretical paper; its key experimental finding is that single-layer CNNs trained with contrastive loss on CIFAR-10 learn sinusoidal first-layer filters and perform partial whitening, as predicted by the theory. On CIFAR-10 KNN recognition, the trained CNNs improve accuracy considerably over random weights, but most of this improvement is matched by a simple linear partial whitening baseline (PCA + whitening, ~47% accuracy with horizontal flip augmentation). The one exception is SOTA augmentations (random crop with varying size/aspect ratio, nonlinear color jitter, horizontal flip), where the learned localized filters capture structure beyond partial whitening and outperform the linear baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's main empirical comparison is against linear partial whitening in PCA space (Thiry, 2021), which projects images onto the top 32 PCA directions and divides each coefficient by the square root of its eigenvalue plus λ=0.1. For most augmentations (crop translation, blur, contrast jitter, mixed), the trained CNN with ReLU achieves comparable KNN accuracy to this simple linear baseline, confirming that the primary benefit of CL in this setting is partial whitening rather than learning invariance to the augmentations. However, for SOTA augmentations from SimCLR (Khosla et al., 2020), the learned filters are more localized in frequency space and the CNN outperforms the linear partial whitening baseline, indicating that these harder augmentations push the network to learn representations beyond simple spectral whitening. The paper also shows that training on synthetic images (fractal noise, dead leaves) yields useful CIFAR-10 representations when the synthetic power spectrum closely matches that of CIFAR-10, consistent with the theoretical prediction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line connecting contrastive learning to spectral embedding methods (Balestriero & LeCun, 2022; HaoChen et al., 2021) by going beyond the general spectral optimization framework to compute the *specific* optimal representation for natural images with stationary statistics. It builds on the alignment/uniformity decomposition of InfoNCE (Wang & Isola, 2020) and the Gaussian equivalence of the uniformity term (Theorem 1.1), then leverages classical results on DFT coefficients of stationary processes (Peligrad & Wu, 2010) to show the optimum is partial whitening via sinusoidal filters. The paper departs from prior spectral analyses by incorporating the power spectrum of the dataset as a concrete input to a waterfilling algorithm, yielding quantitative predictions about which frequencies a network should measure and with what sensitivity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical explanation for two long-standing puzzles in contrastive learning: why low-level augmentations (crop, jitter, blur) produce useful representations, and why training on noise or dead-leaf images transfers to real-image recognition. The key conceptual reframing is that CL with simple augmentations does not primarily teach invariance to those transformations but rather steers the network toward partial whitening—a representation already suggested by early vision research (Atick & Redlich, 1992). The main limitation is that the analysis is restricted to single-layer CNNs and relatively simple augmentations; extending the waterfilling framework to deeper architectures and SOTA augmentation pipelines (where the paper observes more localized, non-sinusoidal filters) remains open. The finding that linear partial whitening in PCA space achieves comparable KNN accuracy to the trained CNN for most augmentations suggests the practical gains from CL in this regime may be largely attributable to a simple spectral reweighting.
