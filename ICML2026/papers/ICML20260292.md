## Problem

Recommender systems are typically trained offline and deployed with fixed parameters between periodic refreshes, yet user preferences can shift substantially during deployment, causing ranking utility to degrade without any formal guarantee that recommendation quality remains above a user-specified threshold. Existing adaptive calibration methods rely on fixed rolling windows or uniform update rates that are brittle under varying drift, and no prior framework provides finite-sample, high-probability control of a utility-based risk criterion over interaction streams while simultaneously keeping recommendation sets compact.

## Input / Output

CARE wraps an arbitrary backbone recommender and consumes the user's chronological interaction history, the score vectors produced by L base models (each trained on a bootstrap sample of users), a user-specified target risk level α, and a confidence parameter δ. It outputs a variable-size recommendation set whose cardinality is governed by per-model calibrated thresholds and a randomized weighted-majority aggregation rule; if the set exceeds a display budget (e.g., 15 items), it is truncated to the top-scoring items. The framework also produces time-varying per-model thresholds λ_ℓ^t and ensemble weight vectors w^t that are updated online at every interaction without retraining backbone weights.

## Method

CARE combines two adaptive mechanisms into a plug-and-play calibration layer. First, for compactness, it maintains L base models and aggregates their individual threshold-based prediction sets via randomized weighted majority voting, with ensemble weights updated online through an exponential-weights (Hedge) scheme that penalizes models producing larger sets, so the aggregated set size stays close to the best individual model. Second, for adaptivity, it monitors a scalar loss-based non-stationarity metric—combining a Loss Discrepancy Distance (LDD) term and a concept-sensitive divergence—within a Bayesian change-point model that localizes preference shifts and defines a stable calibration window; the per-model threshold is then adjusted by a simple step-size rule proportional to the gap between the empirical window risk and the target α. Theoretically, the paper proves that the expected aggregated set size is bounded by the best base model's size plus a variance-dependent slack (Theorem 1), and that the expected utility-based risk at the end of a T-round horizon stays within α plus a calibration term and a change-detection delay term with probability at least 1−δ (Theorem 2).

## Results

On Book-Crossing and Last.fm, CARE consistently improves all three utility metrics (MRR, Recall@10, NDCG@10) over every backbone model. The strongest configuration, FMLP-Rec+CARE, achieves 0.357 MRR / 0.628 Recall / 0.372 NDCG on Book-Crossing and 0.402 MRR / 0.812 Recall / 0.432 NDCG on Last.fm, outperforming the best preference-aware baseline (Oracle4Rec: 0.339/0.603/0.353 and 0.390/0.798/0.422). In the conformal comparison (α=0.10), CARE achieves the target 0.90+ coverage on all four backbones while maintaining the smallest or tied-smallest average set sizes (42–46 items), versus 0.82–0.84 coverage for Split Conformal and 43–47 items for EnbPI.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CARE improves over all four backbone models (NeuMF, CASER, SASRec, FMLP-Rec) on every metric and both datasets; for example, SASRec+CARE lifts NDCG@10 from 0.329 to 0.354 on Book-Crossing and from 0.389 to 0.422 on Last.fm. Against the three preference-aware baselines, CARE-wrapped backbones are competitive: FMLP-Rec+CARE edges out Oracle4Rec on Book-Crossing (0.357 vs 0.339 MRR) and Last.fm (0.402 vs 0.390 MRR), while SASRec+CARE matches Oracle4Rec on Last.fm NDCG (0.422 = 0.422). In the conformal comparison (Table 2, α=0.10), CARE dominates Split Conformal, which under-covers at 0.82–0.84 (well below the 0.90 target) despite producing compact sets. CARE also outperforms EnbPI, which raises coverage by ~0.03–0.04 over Split Conformal but inflates set sizes by 1–4 items; CARE achieves 0.901–0.910 coverage with the smallest or tied-smallest set sizes (42–46). Finally, CARE beats Online Conformal on set-size efficiency: Online Conformal reaches 0.875–0.901 coverage but uses 1–2 more items on average (44–46 vs 42–46), confirming that CARE's adaptive recalibration and size-regularized weighting yield the best coverage–compactness trade-off.

## Relation to Prior Work

CARE sits at the intersection of conformal prediction for recommendation (Kagita et al., 2022; Bisht et al., 2025a,b) and adaptive/online conformal inference (Gibbs & Candes, 2021; Zaffran et al., 2022; Angelopoulos et al., 2024), but departs from these by replacing fixed sliding-window or decaying-step calibration with a Bayesian change-point detector driven by a loss-based non-stationarity metric. The ensemble aggregation via randomized weighted majority voting draws on conformal online model aggregation (Gasparin & Ramdas, 2024) and the Hedge/multiplicative-weights framework (Freund & Schapire, 1997), while the theoretical analysis leverages DKW, Azuma-Hoeffding, and Bernstein inequalities in a way tailored to the recommendation setting. Unlike prior conformal-for-recommendation work that targets static coverage, CARE formulates a utility-based risk criterion (Recall, NDCG, MRR) and adapts both thresholds and ensemble weights online over interaction streams.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CARE provides a practical, model-agnostic calibration layer (adding ~1.5 min of compute) that gives deployable recommender systems finite-sample, high-probability guarantees on utility-based risk under evolving user preferences—something periodic retraining alone cannot provide. The framework's plug-and-play design means it can be attached to any backbone ranker without retraining, and the utility function is swappable to incorporate fairness or diversity constraints. Open questions include how the Bayesian change-point detector scales to very large item catalogs or multi-objective calibration, whether the detection-delay term D_T in Theorem 2 remains sublinear under adversarial or highly irregular drift, and whether the ensemble of L bootstrapped models is necessary versus a single well-calibrated backbone with the same adaptive threshold mechanism.
