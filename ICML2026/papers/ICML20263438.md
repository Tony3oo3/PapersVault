## Problem

Computing the sliced Wasserstein (SW) distance from streaming samples with bounded memory remains unsolved. Existing streaming optimal transport methods such as Online Sinkhorn and Compressed Online Sinkhorn incur O(n²) or super-cubic time complexity and scale poorly with dimension, making them impractical for the memory-constrained, single-pass setting required by IoT and real-time data pipelines. The paper asks whether the computational and statistical advantages of sliced optimal transport can be preserved in a streaming regime.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two streams of d-dimensional samples (x₁,…,xₙ and y₁,…,yₘ) arriving in arbitrary order, along with a user-chosen sketch size k and number of projections L. Because samples are processed one at a time in a single pass, the algorithm never retains the full dataset; instead it maintains KLL quantile sketches per projection whose total memory is O(L(k + log(n/k)) + Ld). The primary output is a Monte Carlo estimate of the SW distance, but the framework also yields streaming CDF estimates, quantile functions, one-dimensional Wasserstein distances, and a streaming optimal transport map as intermediate byproducts.

## Method

The key insight is that the one-dimensional Wasserstein distance admits a closed-form as the Lᵖ integral of the absolute difference between quantile functions, so approximating quantile functions from a stream suffices to approximate 1DW. The paper uses KLL sketches—the optimal randomized comparison-based quantile sketch—as the data structure: each projection direction θ maintains a hierarchical set of compactors that compress the projected samples into O(k + log(n/k)) weighted points while preserving quantile accuracy with high probability. For each of L random projections, the streaming 1DW is computed as the integral of |F̃_{S_μ}⁻¹(q) − F̃_{S_ν}⁻¹(q)|ᵖ dq over the sketched quantile functions, and Stream-SW is the average over all L projections. The authors derive exponential-tail concentration bounds for the CDF and quantile approximation errors, propagate them to 1DW and SW, and show the overall estimation error scales as O(min{k₁, k₂, √n, √m, √L}⁻¹), with space logarithmic in n and time near-linear in n.

## Results

On ModelNet10 point-cloud classification (KNN, K=5), Stream-SW at L=100, k=50 achieves 77.67% test accuracy, matching Full SW (77.67%) while substantially outperforming SW with random sampling (72.67%). In the streaming gradient flow experiment (ShapeNet Core-55), Stream-SW at L=1000, k=200 reaches a Wasserstein-2 score of 0.48 at step 5000, compared to 6.59 for SW with random sampling and 0.01 for Full SW. For approximation error on 2D Gaussians (n=10000, k=10), Stream-SW achieves a relative error of 0.058 versus 0.265 for SW with random sampling. In streaming change-point detection on MSRC-12, Stream-SW detects the bending-to-throwing transition earlier than the sliding-window SW baseline across all four subjects.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is SW with random sampling, which retains only a random subset of streaming samples (sized 3k+2log(n/(2k/3))) and computes standard SW on that subset. Stream-SW consistently outperforms this baseline across all experiments: on ModelNet10 classification, Stream-SW at k=50, L=100 reaches 77.67% accuracy versus 72.67% for random sampling (a 5-point gap), and at k=5 the gap widens to 72.33% vs. 56.67%. In the gradient flow experiment, Stream-SW converges to a W2 score of 0.48 at step 5000 (L=1000, k=200) while random sampling plateaus at 6.59, a 13× improvement. For streaming change-point detection on MSRC-12 (following the sliding-window setup of Wang et al., 2022), Stream-SW detects the action transition earlier than the sliding-window SW baseline for all four subjects (e.g., Guest 1: Stream-SW at index 50 vs. SW at 100). Stream-SW also matches Full SW accuracy at larger k values (k≥20) while using only logarithmic memory in the number of streaming samples.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate literatures: streaming quantile approximation (KLL sketches from Karnin et al., 2016) and sliced optimal transport (Rabin et al., 2010; Bonneel et al., 2015). The key conceptual insight is that since the 1DW admits a closed-form via quantile functions (Bonnotte, 2013), quantile sketches can serve as the data structure for a streaming SW estimator. This departs from prior streaming OT work (Online Sinkhorn, Compressed Online Sinkhorn) which required O(n) memory and O(n²) time, by achieving logarithmic memory in the number of samples through the hierarchical compaction of KLL sketches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Stream-SW is the first streaming estimator for the SW distance with explicit probabilistic approximation guarantees, making distribution comparison feasible on memory-constrained devices (IoT, edge computing) where storing all samples is impractical. The logarithmic space complexity in n is a substantial practical improvement over prior streaming OT methods. Open questions include extending the framework to generalized, spherical, and manifold SW variants, handling non-compact support settings more tightly, and exploring whether the current pure-Python implementation can be accelerated to close the gap with optimized full-SW runtimes at moderate n.
