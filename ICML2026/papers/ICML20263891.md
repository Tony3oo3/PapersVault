## Problem

Continual training of deep networks under non-stationarity leads to progressive plasticity loss, where the network gradually loses its ability to adapt to new tasks. While individual failure modes (dead ReLUs, parameter growth, primacy bias) are well documented, they are typically studied in isolation and tied to specific architectures or tasks. This paper identifies a unifying geometric mechanism—increasing anisotropy of the empirical Neural Tangent Kernel—and shows that approximate dynamical isometry (layer-wise Jacobian singular values near one) is a principled, task-agnostic surrogate for preserving NTK isotropy and thus plasticity.

## Input / Output

The method is architecture- and task-agnostic, applying to MLPs, CNNs, and transformers across both supervised and RL settings. In supervised experiments, inputs are standard RGB images (CIFAR-10/CIFAR-100 at 32×32, MNIST at 28×28) with outputs being class labels; in RL, inputs are game observations (MinAtar 10×10×10, Octax 4×32×64) with outputs being discrete actions. The isometry regularizer operates on weight matrices (reshaped to 2D for convolutional kernels) and requires only Gram products, adding roughly 4–5% memory and a few percent wall-clock overhead over standard Adam.

## Method

The paper proposes a Gram-deviation penalty R_iso(W_ℓ) = ‖W_ℓᵀW_ℓ − I‖²_F (or the transposed form for wide layers) that directly drives all singular values of each weight matrix toward one, promoting approximate layer-wise dynamical isometry. To avoid the regularizer's dense, layer-coupled gradient from corrupting Adam's adaptive moment estimates, the authors introduce AdamO, which computes Adam's first and second moments from the task gradient only and applies the isometry update as a separate decoupled step—directly analogous to how AdamW decouples weight decay. The orthogonality penalty also provides a mechanism to revive dead ReLU units: as active neurons move under the task loss, the penalty rotates inactive weight vectors to maintain an approximately orthonormal set, potentially pushing them back into positive pre-activation regions. The paper further revisits GroupSort/MaxMin networks to establish that almost-everywhere isometric architectures remain universal Lipschitz approximators, and reinterprets prior plasticity-preserving methods (NaP, ReDo, L2 Init, spectral norm regularization) as targeting only partial measures of isometry.

## Results

Across three supervised continual-learning benchmarks (Pixel-Permutation CIFAR-10, Random-Label Memorization CIFAR-10, Label-Shuffled CIFAR-100), AdamO with ReLU or GroupSort activations consistently outperforms all baselines, with the largest margins on Random-Label Memorization and narrower margins on Label-Shuffled due to performance saturation. In continual MinAtar RL (20 cycles, 1.2B total steps), the standard PPO+Adam baseline progressively loses learning ability, while AdamO+ReLU and AdamO+GroupSort maintain stable performance throughout all cycles, matching or exceeding NaP, L2-Init, and WSN. On continual Octax (8 games, 3 cycles, CNN architecture), AdamO variants achieve superior cumulative rewards compared to standard approaches. The paper reports that AdamO adds only a few percent wall-clock time and ~4–5% memory overhead over standard Adam.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against four baselines: NaP (Normalize-and-Project), ReDo (ReLU recycling), L2 Init (regenerative regularization), and WSN (spectral norm regularization). AdamO+ReLU and AdamO+GroupSort consistently match or outperform all four across both supervised and RL benchmarks. Against NaP, the closest competitor, AdamO achieves comparable or slightly better sustained performance; the authors note that NaP stabilizes only the mean squared singular value (average gradient scale) whereas isometry stabilizes all gradient directions, giving AdamO a structural advantage. Against WSN, which regularizes only the maximal singular value toward 1, AdamO controls the full singular-value spectrum and achieves better conditioning diagnostics (lower condition number, higher effective NTK rank). Against ReDo and L2 Init, AdamO avoids discarding learned information (ReDo resets dead neurons to initialization) and avoids restricting solutions to the initialization basin (L2 Init), while still preserving plasticity. The authors also note that when NaP is combined with earlier heuristics (ReDo, regenerative regularization, shrink-and-perturb), the performance gap between methods becomes negligible, suggesting NaP addresses a dominant failure mode; AdamO's advantage is most visible in the absence of such auxiliary tricks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reinterprets dynamical isometry—originally a signal-propagation and initialization concept (Saxe et al., 2013; Pennington et al., 2017; Xiao et al., 2018)—as a *maintenance* target for preserving plasticity under continual non-stationarity, bridging it to the empirical NTK as the geometric quantity that controls first-order gradient progress. It repurposes orthogonality regularization (Bansal et al., 2018; Li et al., 2019) from a single-task training trick into a plasticity-preserving mechanism, and introduces AdamO by applying the decoupled-update principle of AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017) to the isometry penalty. The paper further unifies several prior plasticity-preserving methods (NaP, ReDo, L2 Init, spectral-norm regularization) under a common lens, showing each targets only a partial measure of isometry (e.g., mean singular value or max singular value) rather than the full spectrum.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The NTK–isometry–plasticity connection provides a task-agnostic, architecture-agnostic geometric criterion that practitioners can monitor and enforce with minimal overhead (a few percent compute for AdamO), offering a principled alternative to ad-hoc heuristics for preventing plasticity loss. The ReLU revival mechanism via Gram-coupled row rotation is a novel side effect that addresses a well-documented failure mode without discarding learned information (unlike ReDo). Open questions include whether isometry preservation helps or hinders feature learning in the long run, whether it scales to genuinely deep RL networks (where depth currently destabilizes training), and whether it transfers to LLM pre-training and continual fine-tuning where residual connections erode isometry over time.
