## Problem

Standard neural architectures process inputs monolithically, yet many tasks require coordination among agents with limited local views. Prior work on sheaf-constrained ADMM (Hanks et al., 2025b) demonstrated multi-agent coordination with fixed, hand-specified sheaf structures for linear control problems, but no fully differentiable framework existed that learns both the sheaf communication structure and local agent subproblems end-to-end from data in standard deep learning settings.

## Input / Output

Inputs are structured data (grayscale images, maze pixel grids, Sudoku grids) decomposed into N overlapping local views—3×3 patches tiled spatially for MNIST and mazes, or constraint groups (rows, columns, boxes) for Sudoku—where each agent's view is individually insufficient to solve the task. Outputs are global predictions assembled by averaging overlapping local predictions: per-pixel class logits for MNIST, a binary path mask for mazes, and a filled 9×9 grid for Sudoku. The framework generalizes to larger inputs at test time without architectural changes, as the encoder and decoder operate on fixed-size local patches with shared weights.

## Method

Sheaf-ADMM formulates multi-agent coordination as a consensus optimization problem solved by unrolled ADMM, where each agent maintains three distinct state variables (primal x, consensus z, dual u) rather than a single hidden vector. The x-update is a proximal solve of a local convex objective (diagonal quadratic + ℓ₁) parameterized by a shared neural encoder; the z-update enforces inter-agent consistency via sheaf diffusion (conjugate gradient on the sheaf Laplacian L_F = F^T F), where learned restriction maps F_{i→e} project agent states into lower-dimensional shared edge spaces, allowing heterogeneous consensus; the u-update accumulates disagreement history. The entire K-iteration ADMM trajectory is unrolled and trained end-to-end via backpropagation, jointly learning the encoder, decoder, direction-shared base restriction maps (with optional LoRA modulation), and the ADMM penalty ρ. This yields optimization-derived update forms (proximal maps, sheaf-Laplacian operations) with a well-defined convergence target, in contrast to the arbitrary learned nonlinear maps of recurrent MPNNs.

## Results

On Sudoku, Sheaf-ADMM achieves a 92.6% solve rate with 1.12M parameters, compared to 10.7% for the parameter-matched MPNN and 34.7% for a 4.62M-parameter MPNN. On Maze, Sheaf-ADMM matches the best MPNN in-distribution (99.9% solve rate) while using d_v=10 versus d_v=84, and substantially outperforms it on out-of-distribution generalization (98.1% vs. 68.3% at 2× scale). On MNIST, Sheaf-ADMM reaches 98.5% accuracy and is markedly more robust to distribution shifts (e.g., 86.3% vs. 11.4% under 16-pixel padding; 69.1% vs. 45.5% under 30% patch dropout).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Sheaf-ADMM against recurrent MPNN baselines (following Li et al., 2016 and Gilmer et al., 2017) that share the same agent graph and encoder/decoder but replace the ADMM layer with a learned message-passing iteration. On Sudoku, Sheaf-ADMM (92.6% solved) overwhelmingly outperforms the parameter-matched MPNN (10.7%) by over 80 points, and even the 4×-larger PM-LoRA MPNN (34.7%) by nearly 58 points, indicating that the optimization-derived update structure is far more effective than learned message passing for discrete constraint satisfaction. On Maze, Sheaf-ADMM ties the best MPNN in-distribution (99.9%) but uses a vertex dimension of 10 versus 84, and pulls ahead on out-of-distribution generalization (98.1% vs. 68.3% at 2× scale; 4.5% vs. 1.3% at 4×). On MNIST, Sheaf-ADMM (98.5%) is slightly below a standard CNN (99.3%) in-distribution but substantially more robust under padding, patch dropout, and Gaussian noise, suggesting the local-view decomposition provides a stronger inductive bias for distribution shift.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Hanks et al. (2025b), which applied sheaf-constrained ADMM to multi-agent linear control with fixed, hand-specified sheaf structures, into a fully differentiable, end-to-end trainable framework evaluated in standard deep learning settings. It draws on two complementary threads: distributed optimization via ADMM (Boyd et al., 2011) for decomposing global problems into local subproblems, and cellular sheaf theory (Curry, 2014; Hansen & Ghrist, 2021) for specifying heterogeneous inter-agent consensus through learned restriction maps. The algorithm-unrolling paradigm (Monga et al., 2021; ADMM-Net) provides the training mechanism, while the architecture is explicitly contrasted with recurrent MPNNs (Gilmer et al., 2017; Li et al., 2016) and sheaf neural networks (Bodnar et al., 2022), differing in that both per-agent and message-passing updates are constrained to optimization-derived forms (proximal maps and sheaf-Laplacian operations) rather than arbitrary learned nonlinearities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ADMM structure exposes distinct primal, consensus, and dual state variables, opening coordination dynamics to direct analysis and intervention—a property unavailable in standard message-passing architectures—and the sheaf formulation allows heterogeneous notions of global consensus that are more natural than full-vector agreement. The striking performance gaps over parameter-matched MPNN baselines (e.g., 92.6% vs. 10.7% on Sudoku) and the robustness advantages on MNIST suggest that optimization-derived inductive biases can substantially outperform generic learned message passing, particularly under distribution shift. Key limitations include the assumption that tasks decompose into overlapping local subproblems with low-dimensional overlap variables, the convexity of latent iterations, and the fixed unroll horizon; open directions include asynchronous updates, non-convex latent dynamics, dynamic environments, and scaling to larger, more complex coordination problems.
