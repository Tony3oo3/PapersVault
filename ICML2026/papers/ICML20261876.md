## Problem

Existing tabular QA benchmarks evaluate retrieval and aggregation of explicit facts, but a common class of real-world queries is implicitly predictive, requiring models to infer unobserved values from historical patterns. No prior benchmark isolates the dual challenge of recognizing latent predictive intent from natural language and executing reliable predictive reasoning over large-scale tables, making it impossible to diagnose whether failures stem from intent misrecognition or from deficiencies in the modeling process itself.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261876/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each sample pairs a natural-language query (written from either a non-technical 'User' or a domain-expert 'Data Holder' perspective, deliberately avoiding column names) with a raw CSV historical table ranging from 160 to 6.7 million rows and 5 to 55 columns. The query describes a feature profile absent from the table, and the expected output varies by sub-task: a predicted scalar with reasoning text (Single-Point), a winner selection with per-candidate predictions (Decision Making), a directional trend with counterfactual estimates (Treatment Effect), or a structured CSV file of ranked/filtered records (Ranking and Filtering). Two inference paradigms are supported: text-based reasoning where the table is serialized into the context window (with truncation for oversized tables), and an agentic ReAct loop where the model iteratively executes Python code in a Docker sandbox.

## Method

TOPBENCH comprises 779 samples across four sub-tasks (Single-Point Prediction, Decision Making, Treatment Effect Analysis, Ranking and Filtering) drawn from 35 source tables in healthcare, finance, and daily consulting domains. Data construction uses logic-driven sampling (hard-negative pairs with minimal ground-truth differences for Decision Making; high-noise candidate pools for Ranking), dual-perspective prompting to generate natural intent-rich queries, and a hybrid validation pipeline combining an LLM reward model with expert human review. Evaluation employs a dual-stream framework: an LLM-as-a-Judge pipeline for reasoning tasks that extracts structured predictions via a hallucination-verification cascade (fuzzy matching, numeric parsing, NLI fallback) and scores both predictive accuracy (NMAE with interval IoU and exponential width penalty) and logic quality (5-point rubric), and a deterministic file-based evaluator for structured outputs using F1, NDCG, batch NMAE, and Kendall's tau. Nine models spanning General LLMs, Reasoning-Enhanced Models, and Tabular Specialists are assessed under both text-based and agentic code-execution paradigms, with ablations on semantic hint injection and a predict-only ensemble baseline to isolate intent-recognition from modeling-capacity bottlenecks.

## Results

On TOPBENCH (779 samples, 4 sub-tasks), the best-performing model Gemini 3 Flash achieves 0.66 Single-Point Prediction accuracy, 0.65 Decision score, 0.65 Treatment Effect trend accuracy, and 0.58 F1 on classification filtering under the agentic code-execution framework. Most models score below 0.60 on predictive tasks, with Decision Making and Treatment Effect often approaching random-guess levels. Tabular specialists (TableLLM-8B/13B) collapse to near-zero performance in the agentic setting (e.g., 0.00 Recall, 1.00 NMAE on Ranking), while DeepSeek-V3.2-Instruct achieves the lowest batch NMAE (0.26) on regression ranking.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gemini 3 Flash leads or ties for the best score on nearly every metric across both paradigms: 0.66 Single-Point accuracy (agentic), 0.65 Decision and 0.65 Treatment Effect trend, and 0.58 F1 on classification filtering. Claude Sonnet 4.5 is the closest competitor, matching Gemini on Decision Making (0.56 vs. 0.65 in text-based; 0.56 in agentic) and achieving 0.55 F1 on filtering, but trailing on Single-Point (0.64 vs. 0.66) and NMAE (0.31 vs. 0.30). DeepSeek-V3.2-Instruct distinguishes itself with the lowest regression NMAE (0.26) in the agentic setting, indicating superior batch numerical precision, though it lags Gemini on retrieval recall (0.54 vs. 0.53) and F1 (0.48 vs. 0.58). Reasoning-enhanced models underperform their instruct counterparts: Qwen3-Thinking drops from 0.57 to 0.44 on Single-Point (text-based) due to repetitive-loop failures, and DeepSeek-V3.2-Thinking shows only marginal gains over its instruct variant. Tabular specialists (TableLLM-8B/13B) are catastrophically outperformed by all generalist LLMs, scoring 0.10–0.12 on Single-Point and 0.00 Recall / 1.00 NMAE on Ranking in the agentic setting, confirming that domain-specific table-QA training does not transfer to implicit predictive reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of two established but previously disjoint lines of work: Table Question Answering (TQA) benchmarks that evaluate retrieval and aggregation over explicit facts (WTQ, Spider, BIRD, TableBench), and supervised tabular prediction benchmarks (OpenML, TabArena) where the target column, features, and train/test split are pre-specified. TOPBENCH introduces a formal two-stage formulation—intent abstraction followed by predictive inference—that neither prior family addresses: the model must first recognize that an unstructured natural-language query is implicitly asking for prediction rather than lookup, and then execute rigorous predictive modeling over a paired historical table. The agentic ReAct workflow and LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation pipeline draw on standard tooling, but their combination for diagnosing *where* in the intent-to-prediction pipeline a model fails is novel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a concrete, actionable capability gap: even frontier models (best Single-Point accuracy 0.66) frequently default to retrieval when the query requires prediction, and the predict-only diagnostic baseline (0.76) reveals that the bottleneck is intent recognition and feature engineering rather than raw estimator quality. A key open question the paper explicitly flags is that TOPBENCH pairs each query with its relevant table, so it does not yet test the harder open-ended setting where the system must autonomously discover or structure the relevant data. The finding that agentic code execution can *degrade* performance for models that misidentify predictive intent as retrieval (Qwen3-Instruct: 0.57→0.43) suggests that simply adding tools without fixing intent alignment is insufficient, pointing toward future work on intent-disambiguation modules or specialized training for predictive tabular reasoning.
