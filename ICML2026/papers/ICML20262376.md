## Problem

Sim-and-real co-training for generative robot policies has shown strong empirical gains, yet the mechanisms determining when and why it works remain poorly understood and are largely treated as a black box. This paper asks: what internal effects govern co-training performance, and can these insights guide the design of more effective algorithms?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262376/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy consumes stacked history RGB images (downsampled to 90×106, then random-cropped to 84×84 during training) concatenated with robot proprioceptive state (joint and gripper positions), plus a one-hot environment label that is randomly dropped with probability 0.2 to enable classifier-free guidance. It outputs a 7-DOF target joint position vector and a gripper action, parameterized by a transformer-based diffusion/flow-matching model with a ResNet-18 vision backbone.

## Method

The paper provides a theoretical decomposition of the co-training learning objective for diffusion policies, identifying two intrinsic effects: (1) structured representation alignment, where the encoder learns domain-invariant features while retaining domain-specific information, and (2) an importance reweighting effect from domain-dependent logit modulation in action scoring. Through controlled toy experiments and extensive sim-and-sim/sim-and-real manipulation benchmarks, the authors show that structured alignment is the primary performance driver (explaining ~50% of variance vs. ~20% for reweighting) and can emerge implicitly within a 'balanced mixing ratio' range. The practical contribution, CFG-ADDA, combines classifier-free guidance (one-hot environment labels dropped at inference to preserve domain discernibility) with an adversarial discriminator (gradient-reversed to promote cross-domain alignment), jointly addressing both aspects of structured representation alignment that prior methods (OT, ADDA, or CFG alone) only partially capture. This yields consistent ~20% relative improvements over prior co-training techniques across three manipulation tasks.

## Results

On real-world manipulation tasks (NutAssembly, MugCleanup, MugHang from Robosuite), the proposed CFG-ADDA method with λ=−0.5 achieves an average success rate of 21/30 (70%), compared to 15.3/30 (51%) for basic co-training, 14.3/30 (48%) for OT-regularized and ADDA-regularized co-training, and 15.3/30 (51%) for CFG-only. This represents a ~20% relative improvement over the strongest prior co-training technique. The gains are consistent across all three tasks and all balanced mixing ratios (w ∈ {0.016, 0.1, 0.3}), with the largest absolute improvement on NutAssembly (23/30 vs. 17/30 for co-training at w=0.016).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks four prior co-training techniques against its proposed CFG-ADDA method on real-world manipulation. Basic co-training (Maddukuri et al., 2025) achieves 15.3/30 average success; OT-regularized co-training (Cheng et al., 2025) and ADDA-regularized co-training (Tzeng et al., 2017) both drop to 14.3/30, underperforming even the unregularized baseline on several task-mixing combinations. CFG (Wei et al., 2025) matches basic co-training at 15.3/30 but shows instability across mixing ratios. CFG-ADDA (λ=−0.5) outperforms all four baselines by 5.7–6.7 points in average success rate, with the largest margin over OT and ADDA (6.7 points) and a consistent 5.7-point gain over both co-training and CFG. The paper attributes the baselines' limitations to each method emphasizing only one aspect of structured representation alignment (alignment or discernibility), whereas CFG-ADDA jointly promotes both.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first mechanistic analysis of sim-and-real co-training for diffusion-based robot policies, building directly on the empirical co-training paradigm established by Maddukuri et al. (2025) and Wei et al. (2025). While prior domain adaptation techniques (optimal transport, adversarial domain adaptation, classifier-free guidance) have been applied to co-training, this paper reframes them through a unified theoretical lens, showing that each method addresses only one facet of what the authors term 'structured representation alignment'—the balance between cross-domain alignment and domain discernibility. The proposed CFG-ADDA method is a minimal combination of two well-known techniques (Ho & Salimans, 2022; Tzeng et al., 2017) motivated by this analysis rather than by ad hoc design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of structured representation alignment as the primary driver (~50% of performance variance vs. ~20% for importance reweighting) and the demonstration that it can emerge implicitly through balanced mixing ratios provide a principled explanation for a previously black-boxed practice. The finding that alignment without discernibility can be actively harmful (negative correlation in physics-only settings) challenges the common assumption that more alignment is always better. Open questions include whether these mechanisms generalize to cross-embodiment and human-to-robot co-training, how the two effects interact dynamically during training, and what the intrinsic structure of the learned representations actually is—questions the authors acknowledge but leave for future work.
