## Problem

AI safety research has focused on technical alignment and societal impact regulation, but has largely overlooked the risk inherent in dependence on AI systems themselves. This position paper identifies AI Lock-In—the progressive, multi-level deskilling and structural dependency that makes reversion to pre-AI capability prohibitively costly—as an underexplored and already-emerging systemic threat that current safety frameworks do not address.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20266439/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The paper draws on observational and correlational evidence of ongoing AI dependency (e.g., StackOverflow question decline, medical deskilling studies, junior hiring data, the LiteLLM supply-chain attack, the Left-Pad incident) as its empirical grounding, and produces a structured multi-level framework: a stage-by-stage Lock-In progression model (analogized to koala biological specialization), three threat scenarios (accidental failure, cyber attack, geopolitical coercion), and concrete mitigation prescriptions at individual, organizational, and national scales, including a proposed AI Lock-In Index with micro-level (self-report surveys, metacognitive calibration tests) and macro-level (workflow proportion, workforce substitution rate, provider concentration) indicators.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a theoretical-analytical framework that transplants Arthur's economic Lock-In theory and evolutionary biology's specialization dynamics into the AI adoption context, arguing that AI dependency follows the same irreversible trajectory as koala eucalyptus specialization. It distinguishes AI Lock-In (dependency on AI itself) from vendor Lock-In (dependency on a specific provider), and decomposes the phenomenon into two reinforcing organizational pathways: AI-embedded workflow reliance and concurrent erosion of human capital through reduced junior hiring. The proposed interventions are structured as a three-tier response: (i) reconceptualizing AI literacy into complementary AI-engaged and AI-independent dimensions, operationalized via adapted media-literacy exercises; (ii) organizational bet-hedging that intentionally preserves human roles in AI-augmented workflows to maintain tacit knowledge transfer; and (iii) national mandates for AI resilience audits, junior-hiring incentives, and periodic AI-free drills. The paper also proposes a standardized AI Lock-In Index for longitudinal monitoring of dependency progression at both individual and structural levels.

## Results

As a position paper, this work presents no experimental results or benchmark evaluations. The authors support their argument with cited empirical data points: an estimated ~80% speedup in individual task completion from Claude use (Anthropic, 2025), a 60% decline in StackOverflow coding questions since ChatGPT's release, 75% of UK financial firms already using AI (2024 survey), and a rise in new-graduate unemployment to 30% since September 2022. The paper's central contribution is conceptual—framing AI Lock-In as a progressive, multi-level dependency risk analogous to biological specialization (koala/eucalyptus) and economic lock-in theory—rather than a quantitative finding.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not compare methods against baselines in the standard ML sense. Instead, it engages with two alternative views (A1: 'recurring pattern of technological anxiety' and A2: 'on-device AI will solve disruption risk') and provides rebuttals. Against A1, the authors argue AI Lock-In is qualitatively distinct from prior technology anxieties because AI replaces autonomous cognitive agency (not a single function) and displaces jobs across all sectors simultaneously, leaving no time for sequential adaptation. Against A2, they concede on-device AI is complementary but argue it does not address the core deskilling problem—humans lose independent capability regardless of whether AI is always available. The paper also distinguishes AI Lock-In from Vendor Lock-In, positioning the former as the more fundamental risk that, if solved, would also resolve the latter.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes concepts from economic lock-in theory (Arthur, 1989), evolutionary biology (koala specialization, bet-hedging), Normal Accident Theory (Perrow, 2011), and cognitive offloading research (Risko & Gilbert, 2016) into a unified 'AI Lock-In' framework that it argues is absent from current AI safety literature. It explicitly departs from the dominant AI safety agenda—technical alignment and generative AI societal impact regulation—by reframing the core risk as dependency itself rather than misalignment. The paper also distinguishes its concept from vendor lock-in (Opara-Martins et al., 2016) and Sovereign AI initiatives, positioning AI Lock-In as a more fundamental, provider-agnostic problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central contribution is a multi-level (individual, organizational, national) diagnostic and prescriptive framework for a risk that is, by the authors' argument, already in progress and narrowing in its intervention window. Key open questions include: how to quantitatively measure AI Lock-In progression (the proposed AI Lock-In Index remains a sketch), whether on-device AI and continued model improvements fundamentally alter the dependency calculus, and how to balance the substantial short-term productivity gains of AI adoption against long-term resilience investments. The paper's evidence is largely correlational and observational, which the authors acknowledge, and its applicability to developing nations with limited AI infrastructure is explicitly flagged as a limitation.
