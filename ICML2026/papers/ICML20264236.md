## Problem

Symbolic regression is known to be NP-hard, yet practical SR systems routinely discover accurate, interpretable models without exhaustively searching function space. This disconnect between worst-case intractability and practical success has gone unexplained at the level of computational complexity theory. The paper asks: under which natural complexity parameters does SR become tractable, and which parameters leave it intractable?

## Input / Output

The formal problem takes a dataset of feature-target pairs, a primitive set of functions/variables/constants (either fixed or part of the input), a loss function, and a complexity parameter (expression depth, tree size, number of variables used, or number of primitives used). The primary output is a YES/NO decision on whether an expression satisfying the parameter constraint achieves loss below a threshold ε; the optimization variant seeks the expression minimizing loss. Constants are encoded as rationals in finite precision, and the numeric model assumes polynomial-time evaluation of each primitive.

## Method

The paper applies parameterized complexity theory to SR, establishing a clean dichotomy. When the parameter bounds the *form* of the expression (depth k or tree size s) over a fixed primitive set, SR is fixed-parameter tractable via enumeration of all labelings of a complete a-ary tree, yielding runtime (|P|+1)^{O(a^k)}·poly(n). When the parameter instead bounds the *selection* of ingredients (k variables or k primitives from a larger pool), SR becomes W[1]-hard via a reduction from EXACT COVER, even when the only operator is addition. The paper further proves ETH-based lower bounds showing the brute-force d^{k^{1-o(1)}} algorithm is nearly optimal, demonstrates that no finite FPT approximation ratio exists for loss minimization (via a loss-amplification construction), and rules out polynomial kernels when the primitive set is part of the input using OR-cross-composition from UNBOUNDED k-SUM.

## Results

The paper establishes a clean dichotomy in the parameterized complexity of symbolic regression. SR is fixed-parameter tractable (FPT) when parameterized by expression depth k or tree size s over a fixed primitive set, solvable in time (|P|+1)^{O(N_k)}·poly(n) and |P|^{O(s)}·poly(n) respectively. In contrast, SR becomes W[1]-hard when parameterized by the number of variables or primitives used, with ETH-based lower bounds of d^{k^{1-o(1)}} and |P|^{k^{1-o(1)}} showing the naive brute-force algorithm is nearly optimal. The paper further proves no finite FPT approximation exists for loss minimization and no polynomial kernel exists when the primitive set is part of the input.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or method comparisons. The central contribution is refining the classical NP-hardness result of Virgolin & Pissis (2022), which showed SR is NP-hard via reduction from unbounded subset sum with unbounded expression complexity. By applying parameterized complexity theory, the authors show that the hardness is not uniform: it vanishes (becomes FPT) when the parameter bounds expression structure (depth or size) over a fixed primitive set, but persists (W[1]-hard) when the parameter governs selection from a growing pool of variables or primitives. The ETH lower bounds (Theorem 3) build on Guruswami et al. (2025) to show the brute-force d^k algorithm is nearly optimal, and the kernelization lower bound (Theorem 5) extends the NP-hardness construction of Virgolin & Pissis using the OR-cross-composition framework of Bodlaender et al. (2014). The practical implication is that existing SR systems (PySR, Operon, AI Feynman) operate in the FPT regime by fixing small primitive sets and bounding expression size, while the feature-selection bottleneck they encounter in high-dimensional settings is a fundamental W[1]-hard obstacle.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the NP-hardness result of Virgolin & Pissis (2022) by re-analyzing symbolic regression through parameterized complexity theory, a framework previously applied to decision trees, clustering, and neural network training but not to SR. The W[1]-hardness reductions draw on the EXACT COVER problem and connect SR to the feature selection literature (Natarajan, 1995; Kira & Rendell, 1992), while the kernelization lower bound borrows the OR-cross-composition machinery of Bodlaender et al. (2014) and the ETH-based lower bounds of Guruswami et al. (2025). The paper positions its FPT results as formalizing the bounded-complexity search regime already exploited by practical systems like PySR and Operon, and its hardness results as explaining why high-dimensional feature selection remains a bottleneck in SR pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean dichotomy—FPT under expression-complexity parameters but W[1]-hard under selection parameters—provides theoretical grounding for why practical SR systems succeed on curated, low-dimensional data but struggle in high-dimensional settings, and suggests that automating scientific discovery fundamentally requires solving a 'taste' or selection problem that is computationally intractable in the worst case. The ETH lower bound showing the naive brute-force algorithm is nearly optimal is a sobering result for anyone hoping for a clever FPT algorithm for variable selection. Open questions include whether average-case complexity on natural physical-law data is easier, whether positive approximation is possible under structural restrictions (monotone primitives, bounded coefficients), and how to handle the optimizable-constants model that practical systems employ.
