## Problem

Prior work has identified massive activations in LLMs but lacks a unified, end-to-end account of where they originate and how their emergence connects to downstream effects such as attention sinks. This paper asks: at which specific layer do massive activations first appear, what internal computations drive their formation, and can this source-level understanding be leveraged for targeted, architecture-preserving mitigation?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264125/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on the hidden state of the massive-activation token (typically the first token) at each layer from the ME Layer onward, using that layer's RMSNorm scaling weights to select the top-k dimensions to suppress (k determined by a mask-rate hyperparameter, e.g., 0.1–1.0). A binary mask zeros out those dimensions before the attention projection, and the modified hidden state flows through the standard transformer pipeline to produce the final text output. No new parameters, architectural changes, or additional computational cost are introduced.

## Method

The paper identifies the ME Layer (Massive Emergence Layer) as the single transformer layer where massive activations abruptly appear—activation magnitudes jump by hundreds of times relative to the previous layer—and then propagate to deeper layers via residual connections. Within the ME Layer, the pre-FFN RMSNorm disproportionately amplifies the first token along high-weight dimensions while the FFN selectively projects the representation onto a small subset of directions; together they produce an input-invariant, directionally rigid hidden state that constrains downstream self-attention. The proposed WeMask (Weight-guided Masking) method constructs a binary mask from the top-k RMSNorm weight dimensions and applies it to the massive-activation token's hidden state before the attention module in all layers from the ME Layer onward, selectively suppressing the dominant directions responsible for rigidity. WeMask can be deployed as a training-free inference-time intervention or integrated during supervised fine-tuning and RL training (DPO, GRPO), and it partially attenuates attention sinks rather than eliminating them, which the paper shows yields better performance than full removal.

## Results

On instruction fine-tuning (Qwen3-4B), WeMask at mask rate 0.1 improves the average score from 64.43 (SFT baseline) to 64.91 (training-free) and 64.92 (fine-tuning), with the largest gains on MMLU (53.77→55.01) and ARC-C (86.69→87.54). On math reasoning, WeMask(SFT) at mask rate 0.3 lifts AIME22-24 from 5.92 to 8.15 and Math500 from 43.00 to 45.40. On safety alignment, WeMask(TF) at mask rate 0.7 raises XSTest from 66.22 to 74.00, substantially mitigating the over-refusal degradation caused by standard SFT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) on safety alignment, WeMask(TF) improves XSTest from 72.30 to 74.96 and AdvBench from 33.80 to 32.81 (lower is better), winning on both metrics. Against GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) on math reasoning, WeMask(TF) lifts AIME22-24 from 7.40 to 9.27 but slightly trails on Math500 (43.47 vs. 43.67). Against Gated Attention (Qiu et al., 2025) as an attention-sink removal method, WeMask(SFT) consistently outperforms across all five benchmarks on both Qwen3-4B (e.g., MMLU 54.80 vs. 49.45, PIQA 80.96 vs. 74.32) and Qwen3-8B (e.g., MMLU 62.96 vs. 52.66, PIQA 84.11 vs. 75.41), suggesting that intervening at the hidden-state level is more effective than modifying the attention mechanism directly during fine-tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on massive activations (Timkey & Van Schijndel, 2021; Sun et al., 2024; Oh et al., 2025) and attention sinks (Xiao et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024) by identifying a single, specific layer—the ME Layer—as the mechanistic origin of massive activations, rather than treating them as a diffuse or training-dynamics phenomenon. Prior work largely addressed these two phenomena separately (outlier suppression for quantization, attention gating for sinks), whereas this paper unifies them under a hidden-state-level explanation: directional rigidity introduced at the ME Layer propagates through residual connections and manifests as attention sinks in subsequent layers. The proposed WeMask method departs from prior attention-level interventions (gated attention, clipping, decoupled value states) by intervening directly on the pre-attention hidden state using RMSNorm weight structure as a guide, rather than modifying the attention computation itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ME Layer provides a concrete, architecture-level diagnostic that practitioners can use to locate and understand massive activation emergence across model families, and the hidden-state-level account of attention sinks reframes them as functional but overly rigid rather than purely pathological. The finding that moderate attenuation of attention sinks outperforms full elimination is a practically useful principle for future mitigation strategies. Open questions include whether integrating WeMask into pre-training (rather than post-training) yields stronger benefits, why the final two layers exhibit a second wave of massive activations with potentially distinct functional roles, and whether the ME Layer's position and mechanism remain stable under architectural changes such as mixture-of-experts or sliding-window attention.
