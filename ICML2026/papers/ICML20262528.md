## Problem

When interventions produce two-stage agent responses (engagement with the intervention and completion of the desired action), existing policy learning methods cannot fully identify all six counterfactual principal strata without restrictive exclusion assumptions that often fail in practice. This non-identifiability leads to inefficient intervention allocation because policymakers cannot distinguish agent types with different marginal returns. The paper addresses this gap by developing a minimax policy learning framework that operates under partial identification of principal strata scores.

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular observational data with covariates X, binary treatment assignment T, engagement indicator I, and action completion indicator Y, along with stratum-specific return values r₁–r₆ and a budget constraint ε. It produces a binary intervention policy π: X → {0,1} that minimizes worst-case value loss relative to benchmark policies (never-treat, always-treat, oracle), subject to the constraint that the expected fraction of treated units does not exceed ε. The policy is derived through plug-in estimation of joint conditional probabilities μ_{ab|c}(x) and μ_{a|b}(x), which are themselves estimated via P-OR, P-IPS, or P-DR estimators.

## Method

The paper formulates a counterfactual principal strata framework where six strata are defined by joint potential outcomes (I(0), I(1), Y(0), Y(1)), then derives sharp bounds L(x) and U(x) on the non-identifiable principal strata score e₂(x) using only observable joint conditional probabilities. A minimax criterion is applied to the value loss function R_{e₂}(π, ψ) by maximizing over e₂(x) ∈ [L(x), U(x)], yielding a worst-case loss R_sup(π, ψ) that depends on identifiable quantities through key functions b^ψ(X) and c(X). Three estimators—P-OR (regression), P-IPS (inverse propensity scoring), and P-DR (doubly robust)—are introduced for the joint conditional probabilities, with P-DR enjoying double robustness (unbiased if either the outcome model or propensity model is correct). The optimal policy is a threshold rule on s·b^ψ(x) + c(x) with a threshold γ(ε;ψ) determined by the budget, and finite-sample regret bounds are established via Rademacher complexity of the policy class, showing O(1/√n) convergence under standard conditions.

## Results

On ML-1M, P-DR achieves the highest total return of 150,442, outperforming the best uplift baseline (DR at 104,230) by 44% and the naive method (42,670) by 252%. On Jester, P-OR achieves the top return of 147,923, surpassing the best uplift baseline (OR at 102,985) by 44%. Across both datasets, all three proposed estimators (P-OR, P-IPS, P-DR) consistently outperform their standard uplift counterparts (OR, IPS, DR), with the key driver being substantially higher allocation to the Y0001 stratum ('No Claim – Incentive Buyers'), which yields the highest per-unit return (r₂=1.2) without incurring intervention cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three categories of baselines: (1) a naive association-based method that allocates interventions by predicted outcome Y alone, and (2–4) three uplift-modeling estimators (OR, IPS, DR) that prioritize units by estimated CATE. The naive method performs worst on both datasets (42,670 on ML-1M; 77,310 on Jester), confirming that ignoring causal structure is highly suboptimal. All three uplift baselines improve substantially over naive but remain 30–44% below the proposed methods in total return. Specifically, on ML-1M, P-DR (150,442) beats DR (104,230) by 44%, P-IPS (147,381) beats IPS (112,620) by 31%, and P-OR (136,268) beats OR (111,510) by 22%. On Jester, P-OR (147,923) beats OR (102,985) by 44%, while P-DR (131,465) and P-IPS (131,291) beat DR (99,916) and IPS (83,555) by 31% and 57% respectively. The consistent advantage stems from the proposed methods' ability to separately identify the Y0001 stratum ('No Claim – Incentive Buyers'), which uplift methods can only capture as a combined group with Y0101, leading to inefficient allocation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the counterfactual principal strata framework for policy learning (Li et al., 2023b) by removing the exclusion assumption that certain strata must be absent, and instead handles the resulting partial identification through a minimax worst-case regret criterion. The minimax policy learning approach draws on Kallus & Zhou (2021) and Yata (2021) for the three-benchmark (never-treat, always-treat, oracle) regret formulation, while the finite-sample regret bounds follow the Rademacher complexity and data-splitting methodology of Athey & Wager (2021). The P-OR, P-IPS, and P-DR estimators generalize standard causal inference estimators (outcome regression, inverse propensity scoring, doubly robust) to the joint two-stage (I, Y) setting, building on the principal stratification identification theory of Ding & Lu (2016).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practically important gap: standard uplift modeling methods cannot distinguish between individuals who respond to an intervention without engaging (e.g., users who buy after seeing a coupon but don't claim it) and those who require engagement, leading to suboptimal resource allocation. The minimax guarantees under partial identification provide a principled way to make policy decisions when strata scores are only bounded rather than point-identified. Key limitations include the reliance on synthetic strata labeling in experiments (no real-world validation), the monotonicity and constant-control assumptions, and the restriction to binary outcomes and single treatments. Open questions include extending to multi-treatment, multi-stage, and continuous-outcome settings, and validating the approach on genuine two-stage intervention data.
