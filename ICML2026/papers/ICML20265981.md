## Problem

Discrete biological sequence optimization requires goal-directed, parser-valid edits to existing proteins or molecules, but diffusion models lack a controllable discrete-edit interface and autoregressive LLMs tend to be myopic when planning constrained multi-step edits over variable-length transformations. No prior framework trains a single model to emit an explicit, executable sequence of atomic edits (INSERT/DELETE/REPLACE) that progressively refines a source sequence while preserving interpretability and domain validity at each step.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265981/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a source token sequence (amino-acid tokens for proteins, Regex-SMILES tokens for molecules) paired with a natural-language optimization instruction, and produces a structured completion containing an explicit edit trajectory serialized between <edit_traj> tags followed by the final optimized sequence. Edit positions are 0-based and dynamically re-evaluated against the current (mutating) sequence after each operation, so the script is unambiguous when executed sequentially; intermediate strings along the path are not guaranteed to be domain-valid, with validity assessed only on the final output.

## Method

STRIDE reformulates discrete bio-sequence optimization as trajectory planning in edit space, training a Qwen3-14B LLM (via LoRA) to emit executable INSERT/DELETE/REPLACE scripts rather than directly generating target sequences. Stage I performs supervised fine-tuning on shortest edit-path demonstrations derived from Levenshtein dynamic-programming backtracing, which induces a minimal-edit, validity-biased prior by supervising both the process (edit script) and outcome (target sequence). Stage II applies group-based policy optimization (GRPO, GSPO, or CISPO) with KL regularization to the Stage I reference, using task-specific rewards that enforce parse-execute consistency (zero reward if the emitted trajectory does not reproduce the stated output) alongside property-improvement and structural-similarity terms. The key architectural choice is treating the edit trajectory as an interpretable, auditable control interface that decouples the planning problem from the generation problem, enabling closed-loop iterative refinement at inference without retraining.

## Results

On the GFP full-action (INSERT/DELETE/REPLACE) stress test, STRIDE achieves 89/100 success with 78 unique and 76 novel improvements, compared to 42/100 success, 30 unique, and 14 novel for Vanilla SFT. Under replace-only edits, STRIDE reaches 61/100 success with 53/59 novelty, outperforming Vanilla SFT (55/100, 28/39) and Edit Flow (53/100, 37/44). On instruction-conditioned molecular editing (DrugAssist, 14 tasks × 500 molecules), STRIDE-GSPO improves strict success from 0.579 (SFT) to 0.676, raises SMILES validity from 0.750 to 0.909, and reduces non-target property shift rate from 0.983 to 0.755.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Edit Flow (Havasi et al., 2025), STRIDE wins on both success (89 vs. 79) and novelty (76/78 vs. 13/51) under the full action space, and also on replace-only success (61 vs. 53); Edit Flow's novelty is particularly weak, suggesting its CTMC-based sampling converges to a narrow set of edits. Against EvoDiff (Alamdari et al., 2023), STRIDE outperforms on replace-only GFP success (61 vs. 47) and novelty (53/59 vs. 24/38), though EvoDiff is substitution-centric and not directly comparable under indels. Against EVOLVEpro (Jiang et al., 2025) in an aligned active-learning protocol, the LLM sampler matches or exceeds EVOLVEpro on Top-16 mean (3.89 vs. 3.84 on GFP; 4.79 vs. 4.63 on AAV) and p90 hit-rate (1.00 vs. 0.98), but EVOLVEpro finds a slightly better single best variant (4.01 vs. 3.95 on GFP). Against GGS (Kirjner et al., 2024), the LLM sampler attains substantially higher oracle fitness across all four gap settings (0.76–0.78 vs. 0.20–0.60) while GGS explores a much broader region (diversity 15.4 vs. 3.8 at gap 1). Against DrugAssist (Ye et al., 2025), SMILES-GSPO surpasses DrugAssist on 4 of 6 matched task families in strict success with validity uniformly above 0.90, while DrugAssist retains an edge on the remaining two tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

STRIDE sits at the intersection of two lines of work: (i) discrete sequence generation via diffusion/flow models (EvoDiff, Edit Flow, DPLM), which provide iterative refinement but lack an explicit, step-by-step executable edit policy under variable-length operations, and (ii) reasoning-oriented LLM post-training (GRPO, GSPO, CISPO), which aligns token-level generation with task rewards but has not been applied to structured, index-grounded edit scripts. The paper's key conceptual departure is reframing bio-sequence optimization as trajectory planning over atomic INSERT/DELETE/REPLACE operations, using Levenshtein backtracing to construct minimal-edit SFT supervision and then applying group-based policy optimization to align those trajectories with task oracles. This contrasts with prior reasoning-trace approaches for molecular/peptide design (PepThink-R1, Mol-R1, BioReason) that supervise verbal chain-of-thought rationales rather than executable, parseable edit programs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The executable edit-trajectory interface offers a transparent, auditable control mechanism for constrained variable-length bio-sequence editing that neither diffusion models nor direct LLM generation provide, and the parse-execute consistency check gives a built-in validity signal. However, all gains are in-silico and pending wet-lab validation; the full-action GFP results are explicitly framed as oracle-based controllability stress tests rather than absolute fluorescence measurements, and exact trajectory execution degrades sharply beyond 3 edits (95/100 → 26/100), limiting long-horizon applicability. Open questions include developing indel-aware oracles, parser-in-the-loop correction for intermediate validity, multi-anchor editing, and whether the approach transfers to backbones without customizable reasoning regions (as shown by the Phi-4 failure). The finding that the trajectory interface initially *hurts* molecular optimization performance (STRIDE-SFT 0.579 vs. Vanilla SFT 0.629 strict success) and requires RL to recover is a useful caution for practitioners considering structured intermediate outputs.
