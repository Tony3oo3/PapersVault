## Problem

Brain-to-text decoders and brain foundation models operate on neural context windows of only seconds, far shorter than the timescales at which speech-relevant structure unfolds in brain activity. This limits both decoding accuracy and data-efficient generalisation to new subjects, which is critical for clinical deployment where paralysed patients cannot provide extensive training recordings. No prior work has pre-trained neural models on minute-scale MEG contexts to learn long-range statistical priors that transfer across subjects.

## Input / Output

The model consumes 150-second (2.5-minute) MEG recordings at 50 Hz across hundreds of spatially distributed sensors, tokenised per-channel by a frozen BioCodec RVQ tokenizer (Q=6 levels, V=256, temporal downsampling r=12) into ~191k discrete tokens. At fine-tuning, input is structured as 50 word-locked 3-second windows concatenated along time; the model predicts T5 word embeddings via an MLP head, and at inference words are retrieved by nearest-neighbour cosine similarity over a fixed vocabulary of the top-50 (or top-250) most frequent words.

## Method

MEG-XL pre-trains an 8-layer criss-cross transformer (d_model=512) via masked token prediction on 2.5-minute MEG samples, masking contiguous 3-second blocks uniformly across all sensors until 40% of the sequence is hidden. The criss-cross architecture factorises self-attention into parallel temporal (per-sensor) and spatial (per-timestep) components, reducing complexity from O((CT')²) to O(CT'² + T'C²) and making minute-scale contexts computationally tractable. Sensor position, orientation, and type are encoded via Gaussian Fourier features and learned embeddings added to the token representations. Fine-tuning replaces the prediction head with a contrastive SigLIP objective against T5 word embeddings, training the full transformer end-to-end. The key finding is that long-context pre-training induces selective, hierarchical attention—local processing in early layers expanding to global integration in later layers—that short-context models never acquire, and this learned capability is what drives superior data-efficient transfer.

## Results

On the contextual word decoding task (top-10 balanced accuracy over a 50-word retrieval set), MEG-XL achieves 47.0–57.3% with only 13% of training data across MEG-MASC, Armeni, and LibriBrain, substantially outperforming all six foundation model baselines (which largely collapse to ~20% chance level). With 100% of training data, MEG-XL reaches 46.4% (MEG-MASC), 61.2% (Armeni), and 63.0% (LibriBrain), matching or exceeding every baseline. Against the supervised state-of-the-art (d'Ascoli et al., 2025), MEG-XL gains 10–25% in the low-data regime on multi-subject datasets but is eventually surpassed on the deep single-subject LibriBrain dataset beyond ~2.5 hours of per-subject data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MEG-XL decisively outperforms all six foundation model baselines in the 13% data regime, where most collapse to near-chance (~20%): it leads LaBraM (the strongest baseline) by 13.8 points on MEG-MASC (47.0 vs 33.2), 28.6 points on Armeni (54.9 vs 26.3), and 17.0 points on LibriBrain (57.3 vs 40.3). With 100% data, MEG-XL wins on MEG-MASC (46.4 vs BBL's 35.9) and ties BrainOmni on LibriBrain (63.0 vs 63.0), while BrainOmni narrowly edges it on Armeni (62.3 vs 61.2); notably, BrainOmni's strong Armeni/LibriBrain performance does not transfer to MEG-MASC (19.1), revealing poor generalisation under shallow per-subject data. Against the supervised SOTA of d'Ascoli et al. (2025), MEG-XL gains up to 25% on MEG-MASC and ~10% on Armeni in the low-data regime, but on LibriBrain (52 hrs single subject) the supervised method pulls ahead after ~2.5 hours, suggesting pre-training substitutes for subject-specific data when recordings are scarce but cannot fully replace extensive in-domain learning. The randomly initialised MEG-XL (same architecture, no pre-training) consistently underperforms the pre-trained version, confirming that the gains stem from learned long-context priors rather than architecture alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

MEG-XL extends the brain foundation model line (LaBraM, EEGPT, BIOT, BrainOmni, CBraMod) by scaling pre-training context from ≤10–30 seconds to 2.5 minutes, leveraging criss-cross attention from CBraMod/BrainOmni to handle the computational cost. It adopts the masked-token-prediction objective from BERT/wav2vec 2.0/MAE and the RVQ tokenizer from BioCodec (itself inspired by SoundStorm), but applies them to minute-scale MEG windows rather than the short clips used by all prior brain FMs. The downstream task follows d'Ascoli et al. (2025)'s sentence-level word decoding formulation, and the long-context framing draws a direct analogy to Transformer-XL in NLP.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 50× reduction in required subject data (1 hr vs 50 hrs to match supervised SOTA) is practically significant for clinical BCI deployment where paralyzed patients cannot provide extensive recordings. The finding that most existing brain foundation models collapse to chance at 13% data while MEG-XL retains 47–57% accuracy challenges the assumption that generic short-context pre-training transfers well to speech decoding. Key limitations include evaluation on perceived (not imagined) speech, a 50-word vocabulary, and non-invasive MEG only; open questions include whether the long-context advantage extends to larger vocabularies, imagined speech, and other neural decoding tasks, and whether the hierarchical attention pattern generalises beyond the criss-cross architecture.
