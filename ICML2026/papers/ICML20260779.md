## Problem

While steady-state control is well-established in deterministic control theory, the analogous problem in MDPs—synthesizing a policy that induces a target equilibrium distribution—has been addressed only for feasibility, leaving open how to select among the (typically infinite) set of feasible policies the one that converges fastest. The convergence rate is governed by the second-largest eigenvalue modulus (SLEM), but for general non-reversible Markov chains the SLEM is a non-convex function of the policy, making direct optimization intractable and leaving no principled selection criterion.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a target steady-state distribution η̄ over the state space, the MDP transition kernel P (or, in the offline setting, a single trajectory τ collected by a full-exploration behavioral policy πᵇ), and the MDP structure (|S| states, |A| actions). It outputs a Markovian randomized policy π ∈ Π^MR represented as a |S|×|S||A| matrix that induces the target distribution (or its closest realizable projection) while minimizing the second-largest singular value of the induced transition matrix. The problem is restricted to tabular, ergodic MDPs where every deterministic policy yields an irreducible and aperiodic chain.

## Method

The paper formulates the Fast-Mixing Steady-State (FMSS) problem as a convex program that minimizes the second-largest singular value (SLSV) of the induced Markov chain transition matrix P^π, subject to the linear equilibrium constraint η̄ = (P^π)ᵀη̄. The key insight is that the SLSV, defined via the operator norm ‖P^π Q^⊥1‖ in the weighted ℓ²(η) inner product space, is a convex function of the policy entries (being the spectral norm of a linear map in Π), enabling solution via semidefinite programming, whereas the SLEM is non-convex for non-normal chains. A projection subroutine (PROJ) handles infeasible targets by finding the closest realizable distribution under a chosen divergence. In the offline learning setting, FMSS-SV estimates P from a single trajectory with ergodicity-regularized counts, then solves the FMSS program on the estimated model; error propagation is bounded using the fundamental matrix of the induced chain and Hoffman's inequalities to control the gap between the learned and true optimal SLSV.

## Results

On three analytically tractable MDP instances of increasing non-normality, the SLSV-based FMSS-SV algorithm achieves the optimal mixing time in the reversible (t_mix = 6) and moderately non-normal (t_mix = 4) cases, but degrades to t_mix = 6 versus the SLEM-optimal t_mix = 4 in the highly non-normal case. The SLSV–SLEM gap grows from 0 (reversible) to 0.1895 (moderately non-normal) to 0.4076 (highly non-normal), confirming that non-normality is the primary source of approximation error. The sample complexity for transition model estimation scales as Õ((1 + t_mix^b)|S|²|A|²(|S| + ln(1/δ))/ε²), with linear dependence on the behavioral policy's mixing time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external RL baselines; instead, it benchmarks the SLSV-based FMSS-SV policy against the SLEM-optimal (oracle) policy and against the restricted class of reversible policies. In the reversible MDP (Fig 1c), SLSV and SLEM coincide exactly (both 0.46), so FMSS-SV recovers the true optimum with zero approximation error. In the moderately non-normal MDP (Fig 1b), FMSS-SV still achieves the optimal mixing time of 4, matching the SLEM oracle, despite a non-trivial SLSV–SLEM gap of 0.1895. In the highly non-normal MDP (Fig 1a), FMSS-SV incurs a mixing-time penalty (6 vs. 4) because the SLSV (0.5804) is a very loose upper bound on the SLEM (0.1877), with a gap of 0.4076. Crucially, the paper shows that restricting the search to reversible policies in Fig 1b severely limits the achievable convergence rate, demonstrating that allowing non-reversible dynamics is essential for fast mixing. The empirical grid-world experiments (Appendix F) further confirm that FMSS-SV's performance degrades gracefully with trajectory length T, consistent with the theoretical Õ(1/ε²) sample complexity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the steady-state control problem for MDPs introduced by Akshay et al. (2013), which established a PTIME LP solution for finding *any* policy inducing a target equilibrium distribution, by adding the convergence-rate criterion: among potentially infinitely many feasible policies, select the one with the fastest mixing. It also generalizes the fastest-mixing Markov chain problem of Boyd et al. (2004, 2009), which was restricted to reversible chains on graphs, to the non-reversible setting via a convex SLSV-based relaxation. The translation from deterministic control theory (Aström & Murray) to the stochastic MDP framework is the conceptual bridge, with the key technical departure being the use of the second-largest singular value as a tractable surrogate for the non-convex SLEM.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled convex formulation for selecting fast-mixing policies in general (non-reversible) MDPs, with finite-sample guarantees for the offline learning setting. A key open question is how to explicitly steer policy selection toward beneficial non-normality, since standard non-normality penalties destroy convexity; the authors acknowledge this as a fundamental limitation. The framework has direct implications for MCMC sampler design, policy evaluation sample efficiency, and stochastic approximation convergence, but the empirical validation is limited to very small tabular MDPs (2–3 states), leaving scalability and practical impact untested.
