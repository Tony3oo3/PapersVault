## Problem

Existing text-to-image benchmarks fix the prompt and evaluate only the generative model, leaving the prompting proficiency of upstream prompters—whether humans or MLLMs—entirely unmeasured. There is no standardized, model-agnostic benchmark to quantify how well a prompter translates abstract user intent into executable prompts, nor a scalable automated evaluator that can reliably score the resulting prompt-image pairs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263066/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark provides 360 single-turn tasks (120 each for Open-ended, Constrained, and Imitation categories) where prompters receive a natural-language task description (and a target reference image for IM tasks) and must produce exactly one text prompt with no generation feedback. The evaluation pipeline then generates images via four T2I backends and outputs two orthogonal score types: multi-dimensional subjective quality ratings on a 1–5 Likert scale (across prompt-level dimensions like Instructional Clarity and image-level dimensions like Visual Composition) and a binary objective constraint satisfaction rate in [0,1] computed from per-task expert-defined checklists.

## Method

AtelierEval operationalizes prompting proficiency as a cognitive process grounded in Guilford's Structure of Intellect theory, partitioning tasks into divergent production (OE), convergent production (CO), and cognition (IM), with task difficulty arising from cross-combinations of nine challenge primitives (four semantic, five constraint). For scalable evaluation, AtelierJudge is a skill-based, memory-augmented agentic evaluator that mirrors Dual-Process Theory: a subjective branch (System 1) retrieves top-K exemplars from expert-curated memories via modality-specific embeddings (Nomic-Embed-Text for prompts, DINO-V2-Giant for images) and uses RAG-conditioned scoring to calibrate Likert ratings, while an objective branch (System 2) applies zero-shot QA/VQA against fixed per-task checklists to verify binary constraint adherence. The two branches execute in a 2×2 parallel configuration (prompt/image × subjective/objective) through five composable skills with no shared intermediate state, yielding interpretable and orthogonal evaluation signals. This design achieves a Spearman correlation of 0.81 with human experts (vs. 0.55 for baselines and 0.83 for human-human agreement) and 95.5% accuracy on objective constraint verification.

## Results

AtelierJudge (GPT-5.4 backbone) achieves a Spearman correlation of 0.81 with human experts in subjective scoring, approaching human–human agreement (0.83) and substantially surpassing the best baseline (0.55), with MAE reduced to 0.33 and Within-1 accuracy of 0.95. On objective constraint verification, it reaches 95.5% accuracy and 93.9% F1. In the main benchmarking (Table 4), skilled human prompters achieve the highest objective constraint satisfaction across all four T2I backends (e.g., 80.6% on nBanana, 83.5% on GI-1), while T0 MLLMs (GPT-5.2, Gemini-3-Pro, Claude-4.5) consistently outperform T2 lightweight models by roughly 10 percentage points, with prompter rankings remaining stable across backends.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external benchmarks but instead benchmarks 8 MLLMs (across three capability tiers) against 48 human users (24 novice, 24 skilled) on a common protocol. Skilled humans dominate on objective constraint satisfaction across all backends (e.g., 80.6% on nBanana vs. 63.1% for T0 MLLMs), while T0 MLLMs with skilled prompting approach or match skilled humans on subjective image quality (4.17 vs. 4.11 on nBanana). Even T2 lightweight MLLMs significantly outperform novice humans on objective scores (49.2 vs. 56.5 on nBanana for novice, but T2 skilled at 53.0 vs. novice human 56.5), underscoring MLLMs' baseline prompting capability. A key finding is the 'paradox of constraints': on GI-1 (which has internal MLLM middleware), external MLLM reasoning in CO tasks actually degrades objective scores from a 69.6% direct-input baseline to 47.1%, whereas on Flux Pro (no middleware) the same reasoning helps (32.2%→37.5%). In contrast, imitation (IM) prompting avoids this interference, with T0 MLLMs achieving 76.1% on GI-1 versus 70.4% for skilled humans, supporting image-augmented prompting as a superior paradigm.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant T2I evaluation paradigm, which fixes prompts and benchmarks generative models (HRS-Bench, T2I-CompBench, DPG-Bench), by shifting the evaluation target upstream to the prompter itself—whether human or MLLM. It also distinguishes itself from prompt-optimization work (BeautifulPrompt, Promptist, DPO-based rewriters) that treats prompting as model-specific polishing rather than a general intent-to-prompt translation capability. Conceptually, the task design draws on Guilford's Structure of Intellect theory to partition prompting into three cognitive operations, while the evaluator (AtelierJudge) adapts the Agent-as-a-Judge paradigm with Dual-Process Theory-inspired separation of subjective (memory-augmented RAG) and objective (checklist verification) scoring—repurposing retrieval-augmented generation not for content creation but for calibrated perceptual judgment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AtelierEval fills a genuine gap: no prior benchmark systematically quantifies prompting proficiency in a model-agnostic way, and the paper shows this gap can mislead prompt-optimizer research that conflates model-specific tuning with general translation ability. The finding that imitation-based prompting outperforms symbolic planning under comparable constraint complexity, and that MLLM middleware in T2I backends can actively degrade external MLLM reasoning (the 'paradox of constraints'), challenges the prevailing assumption that more explicit structured reasoning always improves prompts. Key limitations include the single-turn restriction (no iterative refinement or visual feedback), demographic concentration in the human study, and the absence of a principled task-difficulty metric, leaving open questions about multi-turn workflows, human-MLLM collaboration, and extension to unified multimodal models that both prompt and generate.
