## Problem

Modern AI systems are developed through multiple stages (pretraining, fine-tuning, alignment), yet when a deployed model exhibits harmful or beneficial behavior, it remains unclear which development stage is responsible. Existing approaches—causal responsibility analysis, learning dynamics studies, and data attribution—either target small-scale discrete settings, provide descriptive rather than attributive insights, or assume simplified optimizers and data-centric counterfactuals that fail to capture the full model development process. This paper poses the accountability attribution problem: tracing model behavior to specific training stages while accounting for the complete optimization dynamics including learning rate schedules, momentum, and weight decay.

## Input / Output

The method requires logging the full training trajectory—per-step gradients, optimizer velocity states, learning rate schedules, and weight decay values—during a standard training run, plus a test instance and a chosen performance function (e.g., log-likelihood). A key efficiency property is that the stage-level parameter effect E_t is computed once along the observed trajectory and is independent of the test input or performance function, so it can be reused via a simple dot product with ∇_θ γ(x, θ_K) to score any new test instance or metric without retraining. The output is a scalar AA-Score per stage, where positive values indicate the stage increased the performance metric and negative values indicate it decreased it.

## Method

The framework formalizes stage accountability as a causal effect under the potential outcomes paradigm, where the 'treatment' is whether a stage's update steps occurred. For a single step t, the estimator propagates the initial state perturbation (the parameter and velocity change at step t+1) forward through all subsequent steps using a 2p×2p propagator matrix M_k that encodes the batch Hessian, learning rate, momentum, and weight decay at each step. The final effect on model behavior is a dot product between the gradient of the performance function at the final parameters and the propagated parameter displacement E_t. For multi-step stages, the total effect is the sum of individual step effects, justified by the linearity of the first-order Taylor expansion. The paper also derives a theoretical error bound showing the approximation degrades with larger initial perturbations, higher learning rates, and longer remaining horizons, making it most reliable for late-stage attribution in stable optimization regimes.

## Results

The primary evaluation metric is the Pearson correlation between estimated stage effects (AA-Score) and ground-truth counterfactual effects obtained by retraining with the target stage skipped. On MNIST, AA-Score achieves an average correlation of 0.9456 across five settings (insertion, mislabeling, and three distribution shifts), slightly outperforming DVEmb (0.9417). On the more complex multi-stage settings (Table 2), AA-Score substantially outperforms DVEmb on CELEBA (e.g., Stage 3: 0.8436 vs. 0.4203) and CIVILCOMMENTS (e.g., Stage 1: 0.8576 vs. 0.7766), while results on X-ray are mixed (AA-Score wins on Stages 2–3, DVEmb wins on Stage 1). The method also successfully identifies spurious-correlation stages: skipping the top-2 positive-effect stages on CELEBA reduces confounder log-likelihood by 0.1130, and on CIVILCOMMENTS, skipping top positive stages decreases toxicity-identity likelihood while skipping negative stages increases it.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is DVEmb (Wang et al., 2025), a process-based data attribution method adapted to the stage-level setting by aggregating per-data-point effects within batches. On the simple MNIST setting (Table 1), both methods achieve near-identical average correlations (AA-Score 0.9456 vs. DVEmb 0.9417), with AA-Score slightly ahead on the distribution-shift settings. The advantage of AA-Score becomes pronounced on complex multi-stage pipelines: on CELEBA with ResNet, AA-Score outperforms DVEmb by 0.11–0.42 correlation points across stages (most dramatically on Stage 3: 0.8436 vs. 0.4203), and on CIVILCOMMENTS with Gemma-3 fine-tuning, AA-Score leads by 0.08–0.12 points on every stage. On the X-ray medical imaging benchmark, results are mixed—DVEmb edges out AA-Score on Stage 1 (0.9628 vs. 0.9326) but AA-Score wins on Stages 2 and 3 (0.9809 vs. 0.9487 and 0.7414 vs. 0.7025). The paper attributes AA-Score's advantage in complex settings to its explicit modeling of learning-rate schedules, momentum, and weight decay in the propagator matrices, whereas DVEmb assumes simpler SGD dynamics and was originally designed for data-instance-level rather than stage-level counterfactuals.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Rubin's potential outcomes framework to formalize a new counterfactual question—what would model behavior be if a training stage had not occurred—and on DVEmb (Wang et al., 2025), the closest prior work that traces data-point influence along the update trajectory. It departs from data attribution methods (influence functions, Data Shapley, TracIn) by shifting the unit of attribution from individual data points to entire training stages, and from causal responsibility analysis (Halpern & Pearl) by operating in the high-dimensional, sequential setting of deep model training rather than small discrete decision problems. A key methodological departure is the explicit incorporation of practical optimization dynamics—learning rate schedules, momentum, and weight decay—into the propagator matrices, whereas prior process-based attribution methods typically assume basic SGD.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled framework for assigning credit or blame to specific stages in multi-stage AI development pipelines, with practical implications for model auditing, debugging spurious correlations, and enforcing accountability in high-stakes deployments. The derived error bound (Theorem 4.1) usefully characterizes when the first-order estimator is reliable—late stages, stable optimization, small initial effects—giving practitioners guidance on interpretability limits. Key open questions include scaling to foundation models (the O(K|B|p²) complexity is prohibitive without structured approximations), extending beyond first-order Taylor approximations, and handling settings where full trajectory and optimizer-state logging is unavailable. The method's dependence on logged training trajectories also raises practical adoption barriers in production pipelines where such logging is not standard.
