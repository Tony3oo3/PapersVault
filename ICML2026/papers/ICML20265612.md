## Problem

Existing multimodal benchmarks evaluate chart understanding and document comprehension in isolation, leaving unexplored whether models can use surrounding narrative text to determine how chart evidence should be selected, interpreted, and aggregated across multiple charts. This paper targets the specific gap of integrated chart-context reasoning, where a document narrative specifies multi-step compositional constraints that must be resolved before numerical evidence can be correctly retrieved and combined.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20265612/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance is a single-page A4-style document image rendered at 300 DPI via ReportLab, containing interleaved narrative text and 2–6 charts of varying types (bar, line, etc.), paired with a free-form question. The question is grounded on a semantic reference label (e.g., 'Platinum Tier Night Service') defined in the narrative rather than on explicit entity names, so the model must first resolve which entities the label refers to before aggregating chart values. The expected output is a single normalized answer: a numeric value (integers or two-decimal floats), an entity name, or a Yes/No verdict for fact-checking questions.

## Method

DocHop is constructed via a stochastic logic-first, reverse-engineered pipeline that separates reasoning specification from numerical evidence. A symbolic reasoning trace is first sampled as a logical tree with controllable depth (D ∈ {2,3,4,5}) and chart count (k ∈ {2,3,4,6}), using 27 human-designed atomic constraint templates and Boolean composition nodes (AND/OR) to produce diverse multi-hop structures. Conditioned on this trace, an LLM (Gemini 2.5-Pro) independently synthesizes per-chart numerical tables that satisfy the instantiated constraints and a narrative document that verbalizes the reasoning procedure without leaking chart values or target entity names. A semantic reference label is then assigned to the entities resolved by executing the trace, and 41 question templates across six task categories (Value Retrieval, Counting, Numeric Reasoning, Ranking, Hypothetical Reasoning, Fact Checking) are instantiated grounded on this label. A multi-stage verification protocol—programmatic trace execution against chart data, human review of narrative fidelity, and visual rendering inspection—ensures correctness and readability of all 2,074 instances.

## Results

On the DocHop benchmark (2,074 examples), human annotators achieve 93.29% overall accuracy, while the best model, GPT-5.2-Reasoning, reaches only 62.83%, revealing a substantial ~30-point gap in integrated chart-context multi-hop reasoning. Reasoning-enhanced variants consistently outperform their non-reasoning counterparts: GPT-5.2 improves from 40.36% to 62.83%, and Gemini-2.5-Flash from 24.88% to 32.02%. Open-source models (Qwen-VL, InternVL, LLaVA, IDEFICS, Molmo) achieve overall accuracies in the 9–24% range, with Qwen-2.5-VL-72B (24.40%) and Qwen3-VL-8B (24.16%) being the strongest. Performance degrades steadily as reasoning depth (D=2→5) and chart count (k=2→6) increase for both top proprietary models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a benchmark paper, DocHop evaluates 17 MLLMs rather than proposing a new method. GPT-5.2-Reasoning (62.83%) leads all models but trails human annotators (93.29%) by over 30 points, confirming the benchmark's difficulty. Reasoning variants consistently dominate their non-reasoning counterparts: GPT-5.2-Reasoning outperforms GPT-5.2 by 22.47 points (62.83% vs. 40.36%), and Gemini-2.5-Flash-Reasoning beats Gemini-2.5-Flash by 7.14 points (32.02% vs. 24.88%). Among proprietary models, GPT-5.2-Reasoning substantially outperforms Gemini-2.5-Pro-Reasoning (62.83% vs. 40.60%) and Claude-4.5-Sonnet (62.83% vs. 17.94%), with Claude models performing near random-guessing levels on most tasks except Fact Checking. The strongest open-source models (Qwen-2.5-VL-72B at 24.40%, Qwen3-VL-8B at 24.16%) remain roughly 38 points below the best proprietary model, and all open-source baselines fall within the 9–24% range. Notably, Fact Checking is the easiest task for all models (45–79% accuracy) while Numeric Reasoning is the hardest (0.28–46.93%), and performance degrades monotonically with increasing reasoning depth and chart count for both GPT-5.2-Reasoning and Gemini-2.5-Pro-Reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of chart question answering (ChartQA, ChartX, ChartXiv, ChartMuseum) and document understanding (DocVQA), which prior benchmarks have evaluated in isolation. The key conceptual departure is the 'semantic reference label' mechanism: rather than asking about explicitly named entities in a chart, questions are grounded on a label defined by a multi-step narrative constraint, forcing models to resolve targets from text before aggregating visual evidence. The logic-first stochastic generation pipeline (symbolic reasoning trace → LLM-synthesized data) also departs from prior benchmarks that rely on web-scraped documents or simpler template instantiation, enabling explicit control over reasoning depth and visual density.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 30-point gap between human annotators (93.29%) and the best model (62.83%) provides a concrete diagnostic of where current MLLMs break down on integrated chart-context reasoning, and the controlled depth/chart-count axes allow systematic attribution of failures to specific complexity factors. The qualitative analysis revealing distinct failure modes (entity attribution errors, loss of narrative constraint tracking, OCR misreads) suggests the bottleneck is multi-faceted rather than a single capability gap. Key limitations include the synthetic single-page construction, restriction to chart-based evidence (no tables, diagrams, or UI elements), and controlled layout that avoids real-world OCR noise—extending the framework to heterogeneous, multi-page, naturally occurring documents remains an open direction.
