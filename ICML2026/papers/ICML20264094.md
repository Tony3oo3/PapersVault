## Problem

Prior brain-AI alignment studies were constrained to single modalities, narrow cortical regions, and static model checkpoints, leaving open whether artificial and biological systems genuinely converge on shared computational strategies under similar information-processing pressures. This paper asks whether brain alignment systematically precedes performance gains during training, whether alignment patterns differ across vision and language modalities, and how model hierarchies map onto cortical processing levels.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20264094/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes layer-wise hidden-state activations from 630 models (36 LLMs at 0.5–72B parameters; 594 vision models at 1.33M–1.014B parameters) and fMRI BOLD responses from four NSD subjects viewing 24,980 COCO images. A notable design choice is that language models receive the COCO image captions as text input, so the resulting 'language–brain' alignment actually probes visual-semantic correspondence rather than general linguistic competence. The output is a tensor of CKA scores indexed by (model, layer, region, subject, kernel size), yielding over 60 million measurements across 180 HCP_MMP1 cortical regions, 8 normalized depth bins, 4 subjects, and 12 kernel sizes, from which the paper derives performance-alignment correlations, Granger causality statistics, and a hierarchical model taxonomy.

## Method

The core measurement is Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) with an RBF kernel evaluated at 12 kernel sizes (20–100), computed between PCA-reduced model activations (≥95% variance retained) and per-region fMRI response matrices. Model layers are linearly mapped to 8 normalized depth bins, and subject scores are uniform-averaged (inter-subject r > 0.97). For longitudinal analysis, the Pythia (70M–2.8B) and MixNet (S–XL) families are tracked across training checkpoints, and bidirectional Granger causality tests at lag 1 are applied to each trajectory to assess whether alignment predicts performance or vice versa. Multi-scale analysis exploits the RBF bandwidth to separate local, feature-specific alignment (small kernels, posterior sensory regions) from global, integrative alignment (large kernels, anterior association areas). Finally, hierarchical clustering with Ward's linkage over the full 180×8×4 alignment vector per model produces a brain-alignment-driven taxonomy, validated against benchmark performance via a Mantel test showing the two distance structures are essentially uncorrelated.

## Results

Across 630 AI models and >60M CKA alignment measurements against NSD fMRI data, language models show a strong performance–alignment correlation (Pearson r = 0.89, p < 7.5×10⁻¹³; Spearman ρ = 0.88) with LLM Leaderboard 2 scores, while vision models show a moderate but highly significant correlation (r = 0.53, p < 2.0×10⁻⁴⁴) with ImageNet top-1 accuracy. A logarithmic fit (R² = 0.80 for language, R² = 0.33 for vision) outperforms linear fits, indicating diminishing returns in alignment gains as performance increases. Bidirectional Granger tests confirm directional asymmetry: past alignment predicts future performance in 9/10 trajectories versus only 3/10 in the reverse direction, and partial correlations controlling for log₁₀ parameter count remain significant (language r_partial = 0.54, p = 8.8×10⁻⁴; vision r_partial = 0.31, p = 5.1×10⁻¹⁴).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against prior baselines; rather, it is a large-scale empirical study whose 'comparisons' are cross-modal, cross-benchmark, and cross-architectural. The key contrast is between language and vision modalities: language models achieve substantially higher alignment–performance correlations (r ≈ 0.83–0.89) than vision models (r ≈ 0.46–0.53), though the authors caution this is not a direct cross-modal comparison because language-model inputs are COCO captions paired with image-evoked fMRI. Within vision, Transformer-based models show slightly higher brain alignment than CNNs at equivalent ImageNet accuracy, and modern residual CNNs cluster more tightly in alignment space than earlier feedforward architectures. The Granger causality analysis provides the strongest directional evidence: alignment→performance is significant in 9/10 longitudinal trajectories (Pythia 6/6, MixNet 3/4) versus only 3/10 for performance→alignment, ruling out shared-trend co-variation as the sole explanation. Robustness checks confirm the performance–alignment trend is not a scale artifact (partial r remains significant after controlling for parameter count) and that top-performing models approach the inter-subject noise ceiling in 88% (language) and 62% (vision) of cortical regions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the brain-AI representational alignment literature (Yamins et al., 2014; Schrimpf et al., 2021; Conwell et al., 2024) from single-modality, region-limited, static-checkpoint analyses to a cross-modal, whole-cortex, longitudinal framework at substantially larger scale (630 models, >60M CKA measurements). It positions itself as the empirical complement to the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al., 2024) and Contravariance Principle (Cao & Yamins, 2024), replacing the model-to-model convergence target with the human brain as an external biological anchor. The CKA metric (Kornblith et al., 2019) and NSD fMRI paradigm (Allen et al., 2022) are inherited directly from prior work; the novelty lies in the systematic multi-dimensional comparison across modalities, scales, and training trajectories rather than in any new measurement tool.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The study provides the most comprehensive empirical evidence to date that brain-like representations emerge spontaneously during AI training and serve as an early correlate of performance (Granger asymmetry: 9/10 vs. 3/10), which could inform training diagnostics and architecture design. However, key limitations temper the conclusions: the NSD paradigm captures passive image viewing so 'language alignment' actually probes visual-semantic correspondence, the single RBF-CKA metric family may not generalize across similarity measures, and the Granger analysis rests on only 10 longitudinal trajectories. Open questions include whether alignment can be used as an explicit training objective, whether the convergence reflects shared computational optima or merely shared input statistics, and whether findings extend beyond the NSD stimulus distribution to more ecologically valid or multi-modal paradigms.
