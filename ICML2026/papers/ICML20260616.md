## Problem

Large language models exhibit a persistent asymmetry between generation and self-verification: improving a model's ability to solve tasks does not correspondingly improve its ability to verify its own solutions, even on the same task. This paper investigates whether the reverse direction holds—whether training a model solely to self-verify can improve its generation performance—and if so, how to integrate self-verification into generation training in a principled way.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20260616/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

In generation mode, the model receives a task query and produces a reasoning trace followed by a final answer, scored by a rule-based verifier against a reference solution. In verification mode, the model receives a query paired with a candidate solution (its own on-policy rollout) and outputs a binary correctness judgment, rewarded by matching a ground-truth label. Verification training data is constructed per-iteration from the model's own G rollouts per query, then post-processed through filtering (removing malformed or all-incorrect samples), query-level diversity sampling, and explicit 50/50 label balancing to keep the binary task well-posed.

## Method

The paper formulates both generation and self-verification under the same RLVR framework using GRPO, differing only in the reward signal: generation reward checks answer correctness, while verification reward checks whether the model's binary judgment matches the true label. The key empirical finding is that training solely on the verification reward yields generation performance comparable to standard generation training while producing substantially shorter reasoning traces (e.g., ~25% of tokens for Qwen2.5-7B). Building on this, the paper proposes a multi-task RL pipeline with two decoupled strategies: Verify-Init (400 steps of self-verification training followed by 600 steps of generation training) and Verify-Alter (alternating generation and verification phases every n steps). Decoupled optimization outperforms Mixed-Train, which jointly optimizes both objectives in the same step, because it avoids gradient interference, task-difficulty imbalance, and shortcut optimization that arise when the two objectives are aggregated at the same scale.

## Results

On six mathematical reasoning benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, AMC23, Minerva, MATH500, OlympiadBench), self-verification training achieves comparable or superior accuracy to generation-only training while producing substantially shorter reasoning traces: Qwen2.5-1.5B reaches 21.7% avg accuracy (vs. 20.2% for generation) using 1227 tokens (vs. 1604), and Qwen2.5-7B matches generation accuracy (38.4% vs. 38.9%) while using only ~25% of the tokens (1152 vs. 4458). When integrated into generation training via the Verify-Alter strategy, average accuracy improves to 22.7% (1.5B), 29.4% (3B), and 40.3% (7B), outperforming both standard generation and mixed-objective training. On general reasoning (GPQA, MMLU-Redux, ZebraLogic, IFEval), self-verification training also yields consistent gains, e.g., 41.9% vs. 38.1% average accuracy for Qwen2.5-1.5B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: (1) **Generate**, the standard RLVR generation-only training, and (2) **Mixed-Train** (Zhang et al., 2025a), which jointly optimizes generation and verification objectives within each training step. In the integrated training setting (Table 4), Verify-Alter (alternating generation and verification phases) consistently outperforms both baselines across all three model scales: 22.7% vs. 20.2% (Generate) and 21.1% (Mixed-Train) on Qwen2.5-1.5B; 29.4% vs. 28.5% (both) on 3B; and 40.3% vs. 38.9% (Generate) and 37.2% (Mixed-Train) on 7B. The gap over Mixed-Train is largest on the 7B model (+3.1 points), suggesting that decoupled optimization avoids the gradient interference and task-imbalance issues of joint training. In the pure self-verification setting (Table 1), Self-Verify beats Generate on 1.5B (+1.5 avg accuracy) and 3B (+1.0), while matching Generate on 7B (−0.5) but at a fraction of the token cost. The paper also shows that on-policy self-verification substantially outperforms off-policy verification (21.7% vs. 19.2% avg accuracy), confirming that verifying the model's own rollouts is critical.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the GRPO/RLVR training paradigm (Shao et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025a) but departs from prior joint generation-verification training (Liu et al., 2025b; Zhang et al., 2025a; Wang et al., 2025b) that treats verification as an auxiliary signal within the same optimization step. It also distinguishes itself from critique fine-tuning approaches (Wang et al., 2025c,e) that rely on teacher-generated critiques via SFT, by formulating self-verification as an independent, on-policy RL objective with rule-based outcome rewards. The key conceptual departure is treating self-verification not as a helper module for generation but as a decoupled, transferable training objective with its own learning dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a binary verification task can transfer to open-ended generation challenges the prevailing view that verification is merely an emergent byproduct of stronger generation, and opens a broader design space for RL post-training where simpler auxiliary tasks can be optimized independently before or interleaved with complex generation objectives. However, the evidence is limited to small models (1.5B–7B) and primarily mathematical reasoning, leaving open whether the asymmetry and transfer effects persist at larger scale or in domains like planning and multimodal reasoning. The heuristic scheduling (fixed 400+600 step split or fixed alternation period) and outcome-level-only verification rewards are acknowledged limitations that point toward more adaptive and process-supervised formulations as future directions.
