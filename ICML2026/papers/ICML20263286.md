## Problem

Transformers empirically fail on simple sequence tasks like exact copying once inputs exceed a model-specific length threshold, yet no rigorous theoretical explanation existed for why this limitation is intrinsic rather than a training artifact. This paper provides the first formal characterization of the fundamental capacity limits of transformers, showing that finite numerical precision and bounded embedding geometry impose hard upper bounds on the set of sequences any transformer can generate, regardless of model size, training data, or computation time.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20263286/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes as input the geometric and precision parameters of a frozen transformer—embedding dimension d, embedding radius r, numerical precision ε, vocabulary size |V|, and the shape of the embedding support (ball, cone, or ellipsoid)—and produces a closed-form upper bound on the number of distinct output sequences the model can access, along with a critical length threshold n* beyond which the fraction of accessible sequences decays exponentially. For the empirical validation, the input is a target token sequence (from PG19 or random sampling) and a frozen model, and the output is a binary accessibility determination obtained by optimizing a soft prompt of length m and checking for exact greedy-decoding match.

## Method

The core technique partitions the transformer's embedding space into regions corresponding to each possible next-token sequence via the decoder matrix F, then uses packing numbers to bound how many such regions a finite-precision model can distinguish. For fixed prompt length m, the number of accessible sequences is upper-bounded by (1+2r/ε)^(dm), implying the maximal accessible sequence length grows linearly with m. For arbitrary prompt length, the paper introduces a mean-field formulation treating transformers as maps on probability measures and a Wasserstein-precision assumption, yielding a prompt-length-independent bound of (e + e(2r)^q/ε^q)^((1+2r/ε)^d). The theoretical slope is then refined by replacing the worst-case ball support with empirically estimated ellipsoid or cone enclosures and by convolving the empirical distribution of per-token cell volumes, tightening the bound to within a factor of 5–10 of the observed empirical threshold across Pythia, Qwen-2.5, Llama-3.2, and Gemma-3 models.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative result is that the theoretical upper bound on the cramming-task slope (from Corollary 4.7) is within a factor of 5–10 of the empirically measured slope for natural-text (PG19) targets across all tested models (Pythia 160M–2.8B, Qwen-2.5 0.5B–1.5B, Llama-3.2 1B–3B, Gemma-3 270M–1B). Refining the embedding-support assumption from a ball to an ellipsoid with non-uniform cell volumes tightens the ratio to as low as 4.56 (Pythia-1B) and 7.92 (Qwen-2.5-0.5B). The critical length n₅₀ at which accessibility drops to 50% scales linearly with prompt length m (R² = 0.999 for PG19, R² = 0.995 for random tokens), and the copying task exhibits a sharp sigmoidal transition with median R² = 0.95 across models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper, so there are no competing methods to benchmark against; the 'comparison' is between successive refinements of the theoretical upper bound and the empirically measured slope. The simplest ball-based bound (Corollary 4.7) yields ratios of 7.77–20.4× the empirical slope. Replacing the ball with a cone barely improves the bound (e.g., 9.24→9.10 for Pythia-160M), while switching to an axis-aligned ellipsoid captures anisotropy and reduces the ratio by roughly 15–25% (e.g., 7.77→6.12 for Pythia-1B). The largest single improvement comes from incorporating the empirical distribution of decoder cell volumes (non-uniform cells), which further tightens the ratio to 4.56–10.82 across models. The variable-precision refinement (accounting for non-uniform floating-point spacing) paradoxically loosens the bound in practice because most internal representations lie far from zero, where fp16 spacing is larger. Across all models, the ellipsoid + non-uniform-cells combination consistently provides the tightest theoretical prediction, confirming that the geometry of the embedding space alone is sufficient to predict transformer accessibility limits.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first rigorous theoretical explanation for the empirical cramming results of Kuratov et al. (2025), who showed that transformers fail to generate long target sequences even with optimized soft prompts. It builds on the mean-field transformer formulation of Sander et al. (2022) to handle arbitrary prompt lengths, and on the packing-number argument of Meyer et al. (2025) for prompt-tuning memory limits. Unlike Huang et al. (2025), who proved copying impossibility under idealized assumptions (absolute positional encodings, infinite precision), this paper removes those assumptions and delivers a quantitative, architecture-dependent bound using only the embedding radius, precision, and vocabulary size.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that every transformer can access only a finite set of output sequences—regardless of context length or compute—provides a principled, scale-independent explanation for the well-documented failure of transformers on copying and cramming tasks. The tightness of the bound (within a factor of 5–10 across Pythia, Qwen-2.5, Llama-3.2, and Gemma-3) suggests that embedding-space geometry alone, a tiny fraction of model parameters, encodes the model's fundamental generative capacity. Open questions include deriving matching lower bounds, empirically validating the arbitrary-prompt-length theorem (Theorem 4.10), and extending the analysis to state-space models like Mamba where hidden states may diverge.
