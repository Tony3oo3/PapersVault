## Problem

3D Gaussian Splatting produces persistent blur and artifacts in regions where a small number of large Gaussians dominate pixel contributions, because their view-space positional gradients remain below the densification threshold throughout training. Naively lowering the threshold causes uncontrolled Gaussian explosion and out-of-memory errors, while other regions accumulate redundant primitives. The paper asks how to selectively densify only the Gaussians responsible for artifacts while keeping the total Gaussian count comparable to the original 3DGS.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a set of calibrated RGB images with known camera poses and an SfM-initialized point cloud, following the standard 3DGS protocol (every 8th image held out as test). It outputs a 3D Gaussian scene representation (approximately 2.5M primitives on Mip-NeRF360, comparable to 3DGS's 3.2M) that renders novel views via differentiable tile-based rasterization. Edge maps are pre-computed from ground-truth images using a 3×3 high-pass filter, and the rendered image is partitioned into 16×16 patches for the comparison step.

## Method

PCGS introduces two mechanisms on top of the standard 3DGS pipeline. First, a Patch Comparison strategy computes a pixel-wise loss map between the rendered and ground-truth images, applies Otsu's method to threshold error pixels, aggregates errors into 16×16 patches, and applies Otsu's method a second time to identify error patches; within each error patch, only the single Gaussian with the maximum alpha-weighted contribution is selected for densification (cloning or splitting), and a consistency-preserving opacity redistribution 'unmasks' subsequent contributors iteratively. Second, a Growth Control strategy imposes a per-step Gaussian budget A(t) that follows a logarithmic schedule from the SfM point count to a final budget B, and uses edge-map-derived importance scores (accumulated edge values over each Gaussian's influenced pixels) to select which candidate Gaussians actually undergo densification at each step, preventing redundant primitive explosion while prioritizing perceptually salient regions.

## Results

On Mip-NeRF360, PCGS achieves 28.01 PSNR, 0.826 SSIM, and 0.189 LPIPS, outperforming all baselines including Taming-GS (27.79 PSNR) and 3D-GS (27.21 PSNR). On Tanks&Temples, PCGS reaches 24.40 PSNR and 0.860 SSIM, surpassing the next-best Taming-GS (24.04 PSNR) by 0.36 dB. On Deep Blending, PCGS attains 30.04 PSNR and 0.907 SSIM, achieving the best SSIM while remaining competitive with Scaffold-GS (30.21 PSNR) and Taming-GS (30.01 PSNR). In terms of efficiency, PCGS uses approximately 2500K Gaussians on Mip-NeRF360 (vs. 3178K for 3D-GS) with a training time of 21 minutes (vs. 42 minutes for 3D-GS).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PCGS achieves the best PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS simultaneously on both Mip-NeRF360 and Tanks&Temples, beating the closest competitor Taming-GS by 0.22 dB (PSNR) on Mip-NeRF360 and 0.36 dB on Tanks&Temples. On Deep Blending, PCGS (30.04 PSNR) is slightly below Scaffold-GS (30.21 PSNR) but achieves the highest SSIM (0.907 vs. 0.906), while its LPIPS (0.243) trails Perceptual-GS (0.233) and Taming-GS (0.237). Compared to the original 3D-GS, PCGS improves PSNR by 0.80 dB on Mip-NeRF360, 1.26 dB on Tanks&Temples, and 0.63 dB on Deep Blending, while reducing the Gaussian count from 3178K to 2500K on Mip-NeRF360. Against gradient-based densification methods (Pixel-GS, Abs-GS, Perceptual-GS), PCGS consistently leads on PSNR and SSIM across all three datasets, confirming that patch-level error aggregation is more effective than pixel-level gradient manipulation for identifying under-reconstructed regions. In terms of efficiency, PCGS trains in 21 minutes on Mip-NeRF360 (vs. 47 min for Pixel-GS and Perceptual-GS, 42 min for 3D-GS) while using fewer Gaussians than all methods except Mini-GS and Steep-GS, which sacrifice visual quality for compactness.

## Relation to Prior Work

PCGS builds directly on 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023) and is implemented on top of the Taming-GS codebase (Mallick et al., 2024), inheriting its budget-based Gaussian management. It departs from prior deblurring approaches that modify view-space positional gradients (Pixel-GS, Abs-GS) or reinitialize point clouds (Mini-GS) by instead introducing a patch-level error comparison mechanism (using Otsu's method for adaptive thresholding) to identify under-reconstructed regions, combined with an edge-map-based importance score for selective densification. The iterative 'unmasking' strategy for sequentially targeting dominant Gaussians draws conceptual inspiration from Perceptual-GS (Zhou & Ni, 2025), but the patch-aggregation and logarithmic budget schedule are novel contributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PCGS provides a practical, drop-in improvement to the 3DGS pipeline that addresses a well-known failure mode (blur in sparsely-sampled regions) without requiring auxiliary networks, depth priors, or pre-training. The finding that naively lowering the densification threshold causes out-of-memory errors with only marginal quality gains is a useful practical caution for the community. However, the method remains view-dependent and struggles in regions with extremely sparse SfM initialization, where Gaussians over-expand to fill geometric vacuums; integrating depth-based constraints or multi-view consistency priors is identified as a natural next step. The moderate but consistent gains across three benchmarks (up to +0.8 dB PSNR over 3DGS) position it as a solid incremental contribution rather than a paradigm shift in the rapidly evolving 3DGS landscape.
