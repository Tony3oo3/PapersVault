## Problem

While LLMs show strong performance on shallow mathematical reasoning, it is unclear whether they can reliably identify the convexity of symbolic functions under deep functional composition, where each local DCP rule application must be correct for the global conclusion to hold. No prior benchmark isolates compositional depth as a controlled difficulty axis for convexity identification with mechanically verifiable ground truth.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20262839/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes a symbolic objective function F^(D) constructed by recursively composing DCP atoms (exp, log, norm, max, affine, etc.) to a target depth D ∈ {2, 5, 10, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100}, and the model must output a three-class label: convex, concave, or neither. Ground-truth labels are certified by construction via DCP composition rules and validated post-hoc with Jensen's inequality, eliminating noisy human annotation. In the agentic setting, the raw expression is first parsed by an external tool into an ordered sequence of sub-functions G = {g_1, ..., g_k}, each depending only on x and previously defined sub-expressions, which serves as the structured input to the reasoning pipeline.

## Method

The paper proposes a three-stage agentic divide-and-conquer framework that progressively addresses two identified failure modes—parsing failure and lazy reasoning. First, an external tool deterministically parses the raw expression into an abstract syntax tree (AST) of intermediate sub-functions, offloading structural parsing from the LLM. Second, an agentic reasoning loop recursively evaluates each sub-function g_i in sequence, requiring the model to explicitly verify curvature and range at every intermediate step before composing results upward, with the full history of prior states as context. Third, and most critically, a focused-context variant prunes the accumulated history to include only the direct dependencies Pa(i) of g_i in the expression tree, reducing attention distraction and cumulative error in long reasoning chains. This focused-context approach achieves F1-Score = 1.0 at depth 100 for GPT-5, Gemini-2.5-Pro, and Qwen3-30B, compared to ~0.2 under one-shot reasoning, while also improving the smaller Qwen3-8B by 0.82 at depth 100.

## Results

On ConvexBench, one-shot reasoning achieves F1-Score = 1.0 at depth 2 but degrades to approximately 0.2 at depth 100 for both GPT-5 and Qwen3-30B. The proposed Agentic Reasoning with Focused Context closes this gap, achieving F1-Score = 1.0 at depth 100 for GPT-5, Gemini-2.5-Pro, and Qwen3-30B, with improvements of +0.79, +0.54, and +0.82 over one-shot reasoning respectively. Even the smaller Qwen3-8B model improves by +0.82 at depth 100, turning a previously failed setting into near-perfect performance. Token counts remain far below context limits (1,912 raw / 5,331 decomposed tokens at depth 100), confirming the bottleneck is reasoning horizon, not context window.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four reasoning paradigms rather than external methods: (1) One-shot Reasoning (raw expression input), (2) One-shot Reasoning with Decomp (AST input), (3) Agentic Reasoning (recursive step-by-step with full context), and (4) Agentic Reasoning with Focused Context (recursive with dependency-pruned context). One-shot reasoning with Decomp substantially improves over raw one-shot, especially for stronger models, confirming parsing failure as a key bottleneck. However, even with perfect parsing, one-shot still degrades at large depth due to lazy reasoning. Agentic Reasoning further improves by enforcing recursive verification, but suffers from attention distraction as cumulative context grows. The proposed Focused Context variant prunes irrelevant history to only direct dependencies, achieving F1 = 1.0 at depth 100 for all frontier models (GPT-5, Gemini-2.5-Pro, Qwen3-30B) and near-perfect results for Qwen3-8B, outperforming all other paradigms by 0.5–0.8 F1 points at depth 100. The trade-off is higher token cost (up to 10× vs. one-shot), which is most beneficial for smaller models and less critical for frontier models already near the ceiling with decomposition alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of convex optimization and LLM evaluation by repurposing the Disciplined Convex Programming (DCP) paradigm (Grant et al., 2006) as a mechanically verifiable synthesis engine for a compositional reasoning benchmark. Conceptually, it extends the long-horizon compositional reasoning evaluation line (GSM-Symbolic, GSM-Infinite, MathHay) from arithmetic or general math to symbolic convexity analysis, where each step requires multi-condition DCP rule application rather than a single arithmetic operation. The agentic divide-and-conquer framework draws from tool-integrated parsing and recursive sub-task decomposition in multi-agent LLM systems, but the key novelty is the dependency-focused context construction that prunes irrelevant history at each recursive step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes a clean empirical separation between long-context capability and long-horizon reasoning capability: models fail at ~2,000 tokens of input, far below their 128k+ context windows, indicating that the bottleneck is reasoning depth, not token capacity. The agentic framework with focused context closing the gap to F1=1.0 at depth 100 demonstrates that the failure is addressable with structured scaffolding rather than requiring larger models. Open questions include whether the findings generalize to multi-variable functions, non-scalar compositions, or other compositional verification tasks (e.g., differentiability, Lipschitz continuity), and whether the focused-context strategy transfers to domains where dependency graphs are denser than the linear chain structure exploited here.
