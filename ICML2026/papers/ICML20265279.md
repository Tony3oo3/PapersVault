## Problem

Existing LLM reasoning benchmarks conflate multiple reasoning types (inductive, abductive, deductive), making it impossible to isolate and analyze individual reasoning mechanisms. This paper isolates analogical reasoning—transferring an attribute between entities that share known properties—and asks a focused question: what mechanism in transformer-style models enables such transfer to emerge from training, and under what conditions does it succeed or fail?

## Input / Output

The model consumes a length-2 token sequence [entity, relation] where all token embeddings are drawn as a random orthonormal system in R^d, and produces a single predicted attribute label via cross-entropy over d classes. Evaluation also reports a feature similarity score: the cosine similarity between the value-projected representations of the source and target entities (V·a_i and V·a_i'), which serves as the geometric diagnostic for whether the feature-resemblance mechanism has engaged. The test set queries the target entity with a relation it has never seen paired with, requiring zero-shot attribute transfer.

## Method

The paper analyzes a minimal transformer-style block consisting of a self-attention layer (with query and key merged into a single matrix Z), a value projection V, and a linearized MLP feature layer W, trained via layer-wise gradient descent (attention first, then feature layer). The central mechanism identified is *feature resemblance*: during attention training, entities sharing a property receive gradient updates with a common component in the direction of the corresponding label's initial classifier weights, aligning their value-projected representations (cosine similarity → 1). Three theoretical results follow: (1) joint training on similarity and attribution premises yields perfect analogical transfer; (2) sequential training succeeds only under a similarity-then-attribution curriculum, while the reverse order fits training data but produces orthogonal representations and fails at analogy; (3) two-hop reasoning (a→b, b→c ⇒ a→c) is equivalent to analogical reasoning with an explicit identity bridge (b→b) in training, without which the intermediate representation remains misaligned and composition fails. A deep linear network analysis extends the picture to multi-layer settings, showing that feature resemblance is approximately non-decreasing with depth. Experiments on GPT-2, Llama-3-1B, Qwen-2.5-1.5B, and Qwen-3-8B confirm the qualitative predictions, including the curriculum asymmetry and the necessity of identity bridges.

## Results

In the controlled synthetic setting, joint training achieves 100% success rate on analogical reasoning with feature similarity of 0.92–0.995 (one-layer and GPT-2), while late training on the similarity premise drops success to 0% (one-layer) or 0.7% (GPT-2) with feature similarity collapsing to 0.001–0.43. For two-hop reasoning, explicit identity bridges yield 99.7–100% success versus 0–1.7% without them. On natural-language fine-tuning, joint training and late-attribution training achieve 72–86% success on Llama-3-1B and Qwen-2.5-1.5B, while late-similarity training drops to 33–53%; the same ordering holds on Qwen-3-8B (84% vs. 34%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper with no external method baselines; the primary comparisons are between training curricula and architectural choices. Joint training and the S→A sequential curriculum both achieve perfect analogical reasoning (100% success), while the A→S curriculum fails completely (0% success, random-level performance) despite achieving low training loss on both phases—demonstrating a curriculum asymmetry. For two-hop reasoning, the presence of identity bridges is the decisive factor: with them, success reaches 99.7–100%; without them, it drops to 0–1.7% even though the model fits all training data. Across architectures, GPT-2 (multi-layer, end-to-end) and the one-layer transformer show qualitatively identical trends, confirming the theory generalizes beyond the minimal abstraction. On natural-language fine-tuning (Llama-3-1B, Qwen-2.5-1.5B, Qwen-3-8B), the same ordering persists: joint and late-attribution training outperform late-similarity training by 20–52 percentage points, with feature similarity measurements tracking the behavioral gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the out-of-context generalization analysis of Huang et al. (2025b) from simplified linear/bilinear models to a minimal transformer-style block with trainable attention and value projection, and adds two new dimensions: a sequential-training curriculum asymmetry and a formal connection between two-hop reasoning and analogical reasoning via identity bridges. The architecture and orthonormal-embedding assumptions follow Tian et al. (2023), while the layer-wise training procedure (train attention, then feature layer) is adopted from Nichani et al. (2023). Unlike mechanistic interpretability work that analyzes trained models post hoc, this paper characterizes how the feature-resemblance mechanism emerges during gradient-based training, providing finite-time polynomial-iteration guarantees rather than asymptotic characterizations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The curriculum asymmetry result—that similarity structure must be learned before specific attributes for analogical reasoning to emerge, while the reverse order fits training data but generalizes at chance level—offers a concrete, theoretically grounded principle for data curation in reasoning-focused training. The identity-bridge necessity for two-hop reasoning provides a theoretical explanation for the concurrent empirical observation by Lin et al. (2025) that explicit B→B examples improve compositional generalization. Key limitations include the stylized one-block setting, the large-κ regime requirement for finite-time guarantees (small-κ deferred to future work), and the restriction to length-two prompts with orthonormal embeddings; whether the feature-resemblance mechanism and curriculum effect persist in full multi-layer transformers with realistic token interactions remains an open question that the 8B-parameter experiments only partially address.
