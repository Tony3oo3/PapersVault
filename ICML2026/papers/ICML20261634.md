## Problem

Existing certified unlearning algorithms either require impractical full-data retention or handle only isolated, one-time deletion requests, and none account for the cumulative, non-i.i.d. model evolution inherent to continual learning. This paper establishes the first theoretical framework for (ε,δ)-certified unlearning in a sequential CL setting, where unlearning requests can target arbitrarily old tasks and the model must simultaneously preserve retained knowledge while provably forgetting deleted data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20261634/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The CLU process consumes a stream of non-i.i.d. task datasets D_t (e.g., 30 sequential CIFAR-100 splits) and, at each time step, an optional unlearning request S_t specifying which previously learned tasks to delete. The output is a published model ẃ_t with Gaussian noise calibrated to a theoretical upper bound γ_t on the approximation error, guaranteeing (ε,δ)-certified continual unlearning relative to perfect retraining on retained tasks. A key structural detail is the two-stage design: an internal model state w_t carries forward for subsequent training, while the published model ẃ_t = f(w_t) is the only externally visible artifact, and the algorithm must handle both forward-synchronous and asynchronous (out-of-order) deletion sequences.

## Method

The paper formulates the CLU objective as minimizing post-unlearning excess risk, which decomposes into a CL excess risk term (governed by ℓ₂-regularization strength λ) and an unlearning loss term (governed by the approximation error between the unlearned and perfectly retrained models). Two certified unlearning mechanisms are adapted to the CL setting: (1) a gradient-based 'natural forgetting' baseline that exploits the exponential decay of task influence under ℓ₂-CL, requiring zero additional storage but incurring larger error for recently learned tasks; and (2) a Hessian-based method that stores per-task curvature H_t and update Δ_t, applying a recursive second-order correction Δ̄_t that handles arbitrary unlearning orders by compensating for temporal disorder via combinatorial coefficients C(·). A forgetting-enhanced hybrid then discards Hessian information for tasks older than the most recent unlearning event, leveraging natural forgetting for those while reserving Hessian corrections for recent deletions, reducing storage from O(t·d̂) to O(max gap · d̂) at a modest accuracy cost.

## Results

On CIFAR-100 (30 tasks, ResNet-18), the Hessian-based unlearning methods (Gauss-Newton and diagonal) achieve final empirical approximation errors of 0.0087 and 0.0094 respectively, compared to 0.0835 for the storage-free natural forgetting baseline (Alg. 1). The forgetting-enhanced variants reduce peak GPU memory from 5679 MB (G-N) to 2232 MB and 1114 MB (diagonal) at the cost of increasing error to ~0.045. Full retraining achieves zero approximation error but requires 950.7 s, roughly 3.5× slower than all unlearning methods (~260–284 s). The theoretical upper bounds γ_T (0.0222 for G-N, 0.0227 for diagonal) correctly upper-bound the empirical errors (0.0040 and 0.0037), validating the analysis.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares six strategies: full retraining (oracle), Gauss-Newton Hessian, Enhanced G-N, Diagonal Hessian, Enhanced Diagonal, and Natural Forgetting (Alg. 1). On approximation error, both Hessian-based methods (0.0087–0.0094) substantially outperform natural forgetting (0.0835) by roughly a 9× margin, confirming the theoretical advantage of second-order correction over relying on ℓ₂-regularization decay alone. On memory, natural forgetting is the most efficient (974 MB), while the full Gauss-Newton Hessian is the most expensive (5679 MB) due to storing Jacobian information of complexity O(Tvd). The diagonal Hessian reduces memory by ~5× versus G-N (1129 vs 5679 MB) at a modest 5% time increase, and the forgetting-enhanced variants further cut memory to ~1114 MB at the cost of ~5× higher error. On runtime, all unlearning methods are within 260–284 s, roughly 3.5× faster than full retraining (950.7 s). The forward-synchronous vs. asynchronous unlearning experiment (Fig. 3) confirms the theoretical prediction: asynchronous requests accumulate a temporal-disorder term, yielding ~2× higher final error than forward-synchronous for the same deleted task set.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the (ε,δ)-certified unlearning paradigm (Guo et al., 2020; Neel et al., 2021; Sekhari et al., 2021) from static, one-shot deletion to the sequential, dynamic setting of continual learning, where the model evolves across non-i.i.d. tasks and unlearning requests can target arbitrarily old data. It builds on the ℓ₂-regularized CL framework (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017) and its recent theoretical analyses for linear models (Lin et al., 2023; Evron et al., 2022), extending the excess-risk characterization to non-convex settings. The key departure from prior certified unlearning work is the recognition that the CL regularization that prevents catastrophic forgetting simultaneously complicates targeted forgetting, creating a trade-off absent in static settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical framework for certified unlearning in continual learning, establishing that the post-unlearning excess risk decomposes into a CL excess risk and an unlearning loss that are in fundamental tension—a contrast with static settings where they align. The practical hybrid algorithm (forgetting-enhanced Hessian-based unlearning) offers a storage-efficient middle ground between exact retraining and storage-free natural forgetting, with experiments on CIFAR-100 confirming the theory. Key limitations include restriction to ℓ₂-regularized CL (no EWC or memory-based methods), and the analysis relies on local curvature assumptions that may not hold globally in deep networks. Extending the framework to richer CL algorithms and to settings where the unlearning dataset is unavailable remains open.
