## Problem

Dueling bandit learning from human comparative feedback has largely assumed either perfectly rational users or fully adversarial corruption, both of which are unrealistic for real-world preference elicitation. Behavioral evidence shows that human feedback imperfection decays over time as users refine their preferences, yet no prior work has studied dueling bandits under this restricted, decaying corruption structure with continuous actions and strictly concave utilities. The paper asks whether this decaying structure makes learning fundamentally easier than arbitrary adversarial corruption, and answers negatively with a matching lower bound.

## Input / Output

At each round the learner selects a pair of continuous action vectors from a convex compact set in R^d and receives a single binary comparative feedback bit indicating which action the user preferred. The feedback is corrupted at the utility level: the user's perceived utility difference is shifted by an adversarial term bounded by C_κ·t^(ρ−1), so the corruption decays geometrically in scale over time. The algorithm outputs a final action vector a_{T+1} and the theoretical guarantee is a regret bound of Õ(d·max{√T, T^ρ}), where ρ ∈ [0,1] parameterizes the imperfection rate.

## Method

RoSMID (Robustified Stochastic Mirror Descent for Imperfect Dueling) generalizes NC-SMD by introducing a corruption-aware learning rate η_ρ = √(log T)/(dT^max{0.5,ρ}) that slows adaptation when the imperfection rate ρ is large. The key analytical contribution is a novel regret decomposition framework that splits total regret into a 'regret of decision' term (standard mirror descent analysis) and a 'feedback error' term arising from the corruption-induced bias in gradient estimates. Bounding the feedback error requires an iterative regret refinement procedure: an upper bound on cumulative utility gap A is used via Abel's summation to bound a coupled term B involving t^(ρ−1/2)·√(μ(a*)−μ(a_t)), which in turn refines the regret bound, and this process is shown to converge to the tight Õ(d·max{√T, T^ρ}) bound only at the specific learning rate η_ρ. The framework is general enough to yield corruption-robust guarantees for other gradient-based dueling bandit algorithms such as DBGD, revealing an inherent efficiency-robustness tradeoff in the learning rate choice.

## Results

In simulations with d=5, T=10^5, a strongly concave quadratic utility, and a logistic link function, RoSMID achieves a regret slope strictly less than ρ for all ρ ∈ [0.5, 1), confirming the theoretical Õ(T^ρ) bound. Under arbitrary (unknown) corruption with ρ ∈ {0.5, 0.6, 0.75}, RoSMID (α=1/2) and DBGD (α=1/4) both exhibit sublinear regret (slope < 1), tolerating O(T^{3/4}) levels of unknown corruption. RoSMID consistently achieves the lowest regret among all tested algorithms across all corruption levels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares RoSMID against two baselines from Ailon et al. (2014): the Doubler algorithm and the heuristic Sparring algorithm (which uses bandit gradient descent as a black-box). Under ρ-Imperfect Human Feedback with ρ ∈ {0.5, 0.6, 0.75}, RoSMID achieves the lowest regret across all settings, outperforming both baselines. Sparring performs comparably to RoSMID despite lacking theoretical guarantees, while Doubler exhibits the worst performance—likely because its theoretical guarantees only apply to linear link functions, whereas the experiments use a logistic link. The paper also analyzes a robustified variant of DBGD (Yue & Joachims, 2009), which achieves sublinear regret under unknown corruption but with a worse slope than RoSMID, consistent with the theoretical efficiency-robustness tradeoff: RoSMID's stronger concavity assumptions allow tighter bounds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Noisy Comparison-based Stochastic Mirror Descent (NC-SMD) of Kumagai (2017) for continuous dueling bandits by introducing a corruption-aware learning rate and a novel regret decomposition framework. It deliberately departs from prior corruption-robust dueling bandit work (Agarwal et al. 2021; Saha & Gaillard 2022; Di et al. 2024), which relies on parameter estimation and UCB-based elimination in discrete or linear-utility settings, by instead developing a gradient-based approach suited to continuous action spaces with strictly concave utilities. A key conceptual departure is modeling corruption as a decaying perturbation on utility values (motivated by humans refining preferences over time) rather than as arbitrary flipping of comparison outcomes, which necessitates fundamentally different lower-bound proof techniques based on KL divergence of statistical distributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that learning from imperfect (decaying) human feedback is not fundamentally easier than learning from arbitrary adversarial corruption, yielding the same Ω(d max{√T, T^ρ}) lower bound — a result that challenges the natural intuition that restricted corruption should be tractable. The regret decomposition framework for dueling bandits under corruption is a reusable analytical tool that also yields guarantees for existing algorithms like DBGD under arbitrary corruption. Key open questions include whether the upper bounds under fully arbitrary corruption are tight, whether an algorithm achieving Õ(d√T + dT^ρ) exists, and how to incorporate contextual information for real-world deployment.
