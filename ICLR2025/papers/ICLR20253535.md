## Problem

The paper challenges the widespread assumption that LLMs can effectively self-critique and iteratively improve their reasoning solutions, a belief rooted in the classical complexity argument that verification is easier than generation. The authors argue this assumption is questionable for LLMs (which may be performing approximate retrieval rather than true reasoning) and set out to systematically test whether iterative self-verification actually helps or hurts performance on formally verifiable reasoning and planning tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253535/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes formal problem instances—four-number arithmetic puzzles for Game of 24, planar graphs with precomputed chromatic numbers for Graph Coloring, and PDDL domain/problem specifications for STRIPS planning—along with LLM-generated candidate solutions. The LLM produces a binary verification signal and free-form critique text, which is appended to the generation prompt along with the full interaction history for the next round. Sound external verifiers produce domain-specific structured critiques: expression evaluations (e.g., 'evaluates to 12 not 24'), violated edge constraints, and precondition or goal violations for planning.

## Method

The core architecture (LLM+LLM) uses GPT-4-0613 in three roles—answer guesser, binary verifier, and critique generator—looping up to 15 iterations where each round appends the previous solution and critique to the prompt. The key ablation replaces the LLM verifier with a provably sound external verifier (SymPy for Game of 24, a Python edge-checking loop for Graph Coloring, VAL for STRIPS planning), then progressively strips feedback content from all-errors to first-error to binary pass/fail to no feedback at all (pure repeated sampling with an unchanged base prompt). Across all four domains, self-critique degrades performance below the single-shot baseline due to high false-negative rates in LLM verification, while sound verification yields substantial gains. Crucially, the content of the critique barely matters: merely re-prompting the LLM with the same question until a sound verifier accepts an answer retains most of the performance gains from more elaborate feedback schemes, at quadratically lower token cost. The authors also test chain-of-thought verification prompts, showing that while CoT improves LLM verification accuracy (e.g., from 87% to 99% on Game of 24), it still falls short of sound-verifier performance in the full pipeline.

## Results

Across four reasoning domains, LLM self-critique (LLM+LLM) consistently degrades performance below standard prompting: Game of 24 drops from 5% to 3%, Graph Coloring from 16% to 2%, and Mystery Blocksworld from 4% to 0%. Replacing the LLM verifier with a sound external verifier yields substantial gains (e.g., 38% F.E.F. on Game of 24, 87% F.E.F. on Blocksworld), while simply re-prompting with the same base prompt and a sound verifier (Sampling, k=15) retains most of these gains (28% on G24, 40% on Graph Coloring, 68% on Blocksworld). Self-consistency (majority vote over 15 samples) shows no meaningful improvement over standard prompting in any domain.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Self-Consistency baseline (Wang et al., ICLR 2023), the paper's sampling-with-sound-verifier approach (k=15) outperforms S.C. in every domain: 28% vs 6% on Game of 24, 40% vs 14% on Graph Coloring, 68% vs 42% on Blocksworld, and 9% vs 4% on Mystery Blocksworld, demonstrating that iterative re-prompting with external verification is far more effective than majority voting. Against Tree of Thoughts (Yao et al., NeurIPS 2023), the authors extend their sound-verifier setup to 150 queries on Game of 24 and reach 70%, compared to ToT's reported 74% on the same test set; the remaining gap is attributed to ToT's use of a classical breadth-first search that offloads compositionality to a proven algorithm. Notably, the LLM+LLM self-critique system underperforms even standard prompting in three of four domains, directly contradicting the optimistic claims in the self-reflection literature.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a systematic empirical counterpoint to the self-critique/self-reflection literature (Reflexion, Self-Refine, CRITIC) that has claimed LLMs can iteratively improve their own reasoning outputs. It departs from prior work in three key ways: (1) restricting evaluation to formally verifiable domains (Game of 24, Graph Coloring, STRIPS planning) where ground truth is machine-checkable, avoiding the ambiguity of style-based or multiple-choice benchmarks; (2) decomposing the self-critique loop into three ablatable components—binary verification, critique generation, and critique consideration—rather than treating it as a monolithic process; and (3) using arbitrary instance generation to mitigate test-set memorization. The paper also engages directly with contemporaneous negative results (Huang et al., Hong et al.) and the LLM-Modulo framework (Kambhampati et al.), which it endorses as the correct architectural pattern.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central finding—that LLM self-critique degrades performance and that the content of critique is largely irrelevant (simple re-prompting with a sound verifier captures most gains)—challenges a widely held assumption in the LLM reasoning community and has direct practical implications for system design: embed the LLM as a solution generator and pair it with an external sound verifier rather than relying on the model to judge its own work. Limitations include evaluation on a single model (GPT-4-0613), only 100 instances per domain, and domains that, while formally verifiable, are relatively small-scale. Open questions include whether these findings generalize to newer or differently-architected models, to domains where no sound verifier exists, and to the multi-agent debate settings that some prior work explores.
