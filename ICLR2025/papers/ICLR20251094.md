## Problem

State-of-the-art RL algorithms for continuous control operate at timesteps of 8–50 ms, far faster than human reaction times (≥150 ms), and degrade sharply when constrained to slower or variable decision frequencies. This forces embodied agents to rely on expensive high-speed hardware and makes RL impractical for real-world deployment where sensor sampling, computation, and actuation naturally occur at different, often slower, rates. No existing algorithm maintains competitive performance across a wide range of decision frequencies without sacrificing accuracy at the fastest setting.

## Input / Output

The actor receives a single state vector (e.g., 11-dim for Hopper, 376-dim for Humanoid) and a GRU iteratively unrolls to produce a sequence of J primitive actions, each drawn from a Gaussian whose mean and std are output by linear heads conditioned on the previous action in the sequence. The sequence length J (2, 4, 8, or 16) is a fixed hyperparameter set at training time; at inference the agent observes the environment only once every J primitive steps, reducing the decision frequency by a factor of J. The critic and model, by contrast, operate at the fine primitive timestep t, while the actor operates at the coarse timestep t′ = Jt.

## Method

SRL couples three components trained in parallel: a feed-forward dynamics model, twin SAC-style Q-critics, and a GRU-based actor that generates action sequences. The key mechanism is 'temporal recall': during the actor update, the target model rolls out J intermediate states from the current observation, and the critic assigns a Q-value to each (state, action) pair in the sequence, yielding a per-action learning signal that avoids the credit-assignment bottleneck of a single scalar reward for the whole sequence. The critic itself is trained model-free on real environment transitions, so its value estimates remain grounded in true dynamics even as the model accumulates error. After training the model is discarded entirely, leaving a purely model-free recurrent policy that produces open-loop action sequences from a single observation. Exploration is handled by SAC's automatic entropy maximization, which adapts to the longer effective action horizon without manual noise tuning.

## Results

SRL-16 achieves the highest FAS across most of the 11 continuous control environments, with standout scores of 0.88 (Pendulum), 0.84 (Lunar Lander), 0.57 (Hopper), 0.54 (Ant), and 0.96 (Reacher). In contrast, the best SAC-J configuration peaks at 0.50 (Pendulum), 0.56 (Lunar Lander), 0.26 (Hopper), 0.16 (Ant), and 0.87 (Reacher). SRL-16 also outperforms model-based online planning in high-dimensional environments (e.g., Humanoid: 0.46 vs. 0.18; Ant: 0.54 vs. 0.34), while online planning wins on low-dimensional tasks like InvertedPendulum (0.63 vs. 0.44). The only environment where SAC outperforms SRL is Swimmer (SAC-16: 0.54 vs. SRL-16: 0.42), attributed to SAC's action-repetition aiding exploration in that specific setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2019), SRL-16 achieves substantially higher FAS in 9 of 11 environments, with the largest gaps on Ant (+0.38), Pendulum (+0.38), and Hopper (+0.31); SAC only wins on Swimmer (0.54 vs. 0.42) where action repetition aids exploration. Against GPM (Zhang et al., 2022), SRL dominates: GPM achieves FAS of only 0.41, 0.04, 0.04, and 0.04 across four tested environments, because GPM's plan-optimization approach does not generalize beyond sequence length 1, whereas SRL's recurrent actor produces coherent multi-step sequences. Against TempoRL (Biedenkapp et al., 2021), SRL maintains strong performance at long action sequence lengths in multi-dimensional action spaces (e.g., Ant, HalfCheetah), while TempoRL's action-repetition mechanism causes significant performance drops in those same environments and rarely selects long sequences even when permitted. In the comparison to model-based online planning (Table 4), SRL is competitive overall but clearly superior in high-dimensional settings (Humanoid: 0.46 vs. 0.18; Ant: 0.54 vs. 0.34; HalfCheetah: 0.28 vs. 0.19), while online planning wins on the low-dimensional InvertedPendulum (0.63 vs. 0.44), suggesting SRL's learned sequences are more robust to model inaccuracy in complex environments.

## Relation to Prior Work

SRL builds directly on the Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) framework, retaining its twin critics, entropy regularization, and target network architecture, but replaces the feedforward actor with a GRU-based recurrent policy that generates J-step action sequences from a single state. Conceptually, it draws on basal ganglia action chunking (Berns & Sejnowski, 1996; Jin et al., 2014) and departs from prior macro-action methods (FiGaR, TempoRL, TLA) that restrict sequences to repeated single actions, instead learning distinct primitive actions at each timestep. The learned dynamics model is used only during training to provide per-action credit assignment via a 'temporal recall' mechanism, distinguishing SRL from model-based online planning (MPC, Fickinger et al., 2021) which requires the model at inference. The FAS metric is introduced as a new evaluation tool, extending the standard single-timestep benchmarking paradigm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SRL addresses a critical practical gap: standard RL algorithms degrade sharply when decision frequencies drop to human-like ranges (≥150ms), yet real-world deployment on low-compute hardware inevitably imposes such constraints. The FAS metric provides a principled way to quantify frequency robustness, and the finding that SRL-16 maintains competitive performance at 128ms decision intervals while SAC collapses opens a path toward deploying RL on embedded systems without expensive GPUs. Limitations include acknowledged failures on Walker2d and InvertedDoublePendulum at longer sequences (attributed to model error), the continued need for the model during training (increasing wall-clock time), and evaluation restricted to MuJoCo. Open questions include whether the preliminary latent-space variant can resolve modeling errors, whether hierarchical policies or attention mechanisms improve generalization, and whether the approach scales to higher-dimensional or partially observable settings.
