## Problem

Most LLM moral alignment benchmarks are English-only and reflect predominantly American cultural values, leaving it unclear whether models align with the diverse moral preferences of the global population. This paper asks whether LLMs' moral judgments on trolley problems align with human preferences across 100+ languages, and whether a 'language inequality' exists whereby low-resource languages show systematically worse alignment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250679/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 98,440 procedurally generated trolley problem vignettes (460 per language across 107 languages) that systematically vary character attributes along six moral dimensions (species, gender, fitness, status, age, number of lives). Prompts use a token-forcing format ('it should save...') to elicit binary choices from safety-tuned LLMs, and each scenario is presented in both option orders to control for position bias. Outputs are aggregated into a 6-dimensional preference vector per model per language, compared against human preference vectors derived from 40M+ Moral Machine responses to yield an L2 misalignment score (range 0–2.45), a language sensitivity score (standard deviation across languages), and K-means clusters of linguistically similar preference profiles.

## Method

The core contribution is the MULTITP dataset and evaluation protocol: 460 English trolley problem vignettes are procedurally generated to cover all parametric combinations across six moral dimensions, then translated into 107 languages via Google Translate (validated by MTurk human evaluation across 44 languages). Nineteen LLMs (Llama 2/3/3.1, Gemma 2, Mistral, Phi-3, Qwen 2, GPT-3/4/4o-mini) are evaluated at temperature 0, with their binary choices aggregated into per-language preference vectors. Alignment is quantified as the L2 distance between the model's 6-dimensional preference vector and the corresponding human vector, with language-to-country mapping handled via speaker-count-weighted averages. The paper further applies K-means clustering (k=4, elbow method) on language preference vectors to identify cross-lingual behavioral groups, tests robustness via five GPT-4-generated paraphrases (measured by Fleiss' Kappa, pairwise F1, and accuracy), and explores uncensoring techniques to reduce refusal rates on ethically sensitive dimensions.

## Results

Across 19 LLMs evaluated on 98,440 multilingual trolley problem vignettes in 107 languages, only three models—Llama 3.1 70B (MIS=0.55), Llama 3 70B (MIS=0.56), and Llama 3 8B (MIS=0.57)—achieve misalignment scores below 0.6 (out of a maximum of 2.45). GPT-4o Mini shows the worst alignment (MIS=1.45), primarily due to binarizing preferences (e.g., p_species=100%) rather than capturing the probabilistic nuance of human judgments. Critically, no model shows a significant correlation between misalignment and language speaker count (all |Pearson r| < 0.10, p > 0.25), rejecting the 'language inequality' hypothesis. Robustness to prompt paraphrasing is moderate: 75.9% of samples remain consistent across 5 paraphrases, with pairwise F1 of 78% and Fleiss' Kappa of 0.56.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an evaluation paper rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the 'baselines' are the 19 LLMs themselves, benchmarked against 40 million human judgments from the Moral Machine experiment. The Llama 3/3.1 70B family dominates: Llama 3.1 70B achieves the lowest MIS (0.55), followed closely by Llama 3 70B (0.56) and Llama 3 8B (0.57), all substantially outperforming GPT-4 (0.81) and GPT-4o Mini (1.45). The key differentiator is not raw capability but preference granularity—GPT-4o Mini's near-perfect binarization (e.g., always saving humans over animals at 100%) inflates its MIS despite strong individual-dimension performance. Among open-weight models, Llama 3.1 70B outperforms Qwen 2 72B (1.20) and Gemma 2 27B (1.17) by a wide margin, suggesting that alignment tuning in the Llama 3 series specifically improves moral preference calibration. Notably, no model exhibits a 'language inequality' effect: misalignment scores for high-resource languages (e.g., Chinese 0.38, English 0.58 in Llama 3.1 70B) are statistically indistinguishable from low-resource languages (e.g., Luxembourgish 0.37, Maltese 0.57), with all Pearson correlations between MIS and speaker count below 0.10 in magnitude.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Moral Machine experiment (Awad et al., 2018), which collected over 40 million human moral judgments across 200+ countries, into a multilingual LLM evaluation framework. It departs from prior LLM moral evaluation work (Jin et al. 2022, Schramowski et al. 2022, Jiang et al. 2021b) in three key ways: using systematic parametric variation grounded in moral psychology rather than free-form stimuli, translating all vignettes into 107 languages via Google Translate, and leveraging the unprecedented scale of the Moral Machine human ground truth. The paper also positions itself against cross-lingual LLM evaluation benchmarks (FLORES-101, MKQA, MEGA) by applying them to the moral domain, and against prior trolley-problem LLM studies (Takemoto 2024) by explicitly addressing cross-linguistic variation rather than only LLM-human consistency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LLM moral alignment shows no significant correlation with language resource level (number of speakers) challenges the widely held 'language inequality' hypothesis in multilingual NLP, suggesting that moral reasoning may be more uniformly encoded across languages than other capabilities. However, this result is limited to a single task (trolley problems) and a specific evaluation protocol, so generalizability remains an open question. The paper also highlights that the most misaligned model (GPT-4o Mini) fails not due to wrong preferences but due to binarization—always choosing one extreme—which points to a distinct failure mode in safety-tuned models. Open questions include whether dialect-level granularity would change the language inequality picture, whether multimodal inputs alter alignment, and whether the trolley problem's binary structure oversimplifies the moral reasoning it aims to capture.
