## Problem

Existing knowledge editing methods (ROME, MEMIT) assume knowledge is stored in local MLP layers and is edited by locating specific terms (e.g., the subject token) in the input. Both assumptions break down for unstructured knowledge—long-form, noisy, multi-entity text that constitutes ~80% of real-world knowledge—because local layer key-value pairs cannot represent information-rich content, and term localization is both impractical and causes contextual information loss.

## Input / Output

The method takes a free-form unstructured question (a full sentence, not a triple) and a corresponding unstructured target answer (a multi-sentence passage), along with the parameters of a pre-trained autoregressive LLM (LLaMA-2-7B-Chat or Qwen1.5-7B-Chat). It produces modified parameters for a single transformer block (the L-th layer, typically layer 7), after which the model generates the target passage when prompted with the question. Unlike structured editing that outputs a single entity, the output here is a full text response evaluated on lexical overlap, semantic similarity, factual correctness, and general ability.

## Method

UnKE introduces two key changes to the ROME/MEMIT editing paradigm. First, it replaces local layer key-value storage with non-local block key-value storage: the LLM is split at layer L (default 7) into a key generator (layers 1–L) and a value generator (layers L+1–N), so the key vector is the last-token hidden state at layer L and the value vector is the value generator's output, jointly encoding both MLP and Attention information across multiple layers. Second, it replaces term-driven optimization with cause-driven optimization: instead of locating a subject token, editing is anchored at the last token of the input, preserving all contextual information. The actual parameter update modifies only the last layer of the key generator via a three-term objective—Key Preservation Loss (retains pre-training keys on sampled tokens), Key Causal Loss (prevents context representations from shifting), and Key Learning Loss (drives the new key toward the target)—enabling both single and batch editing without catastrophic forgetting.

## Results

On UnKEBench (LLaMA2-7B-Chat), UnKE achieves a Bert-Score of 99.61/93.09 (original/paraphrased) and BLEU of 98.63/76.85, substantially outperforming the strongest parameter-editing baselines ROME (76.52/74.29 Bert-Score) and MEMIT (75.90/74.46). On the structured KEBench benchmark, UnKE reaches 94.30% original-question accuracy and 86.40% paraphrased accuracy, surpassing ROME (77.90%/68.40%) and MEMIT (74.80%/64.30%) by 16.4 and 18.0 points respectively. UnKE also achieves the highest FactScore (42.49) among all parameter-modifying methods and maintains MMLU within 0.10 points of the unedited model (29.68 vs. 29.78).

## Baselines & Comparisons

UnKE decisively outperforms the locate-then-edit baselines ROME and MEMIT on UnKEBench, achieving a Bert-Score of 99.61 versus 76.52 (ROME) and 75.90 (MEMIT), and BLEU of 98.63 versus 38.71 and 35.79 respectively — a gap of over 20 points in semantic similarity. Against the hypernetwork-based MEND, UnKE leads by ~30 Bert-Score points (99.61 vs. 69.99). Among adapter-based methods, UnKE surpasses LoRA (88.05) and AdaLoRA (87.26) Bert-Score by 11.6 and 12.4 points, while also achieving higher BLEU (98.63 vs. 74.77/75.94). The in-context-learning method IKE (w/o ICL) achieves a higher FactScore (94.60 vs. 42.49) than UnKE, but the authors note this is analogous to a reading-comprehension task rather than true parameter editing; on all other metrics UnKE dominates IKE. On the structured KEBench benchmark, UnKE improves Ori-Acc by 16.4 points over ROME and 19.5 over MEMIT, while on KnowEdit it achieves the highest Edit Success on WikiData_recent, WikiBio, ConvSent, and Sanitation, though it trails ROME/MEMIT slightly on ZsRE and WikiData_counterfact.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ROME/MEMIT line of locate-then-edit knowledge editing by challenging two of its core assumptions: that knowledge is stored in local MLP layers and that editing should be driven by locating specific subject terms. UnKE replaces local layer key-value storage with non-local block key-value storage (spanning both MLP and Attention modules across a key generator and value generator split at layer L) and replaces term-driven optimization with cause-driven optimization at the last token position. It also positions itself against parameter-preserving methods (SERAC, IKE, MeLLo), hypernetwork-based editing (MEND), and fine-tuning approaches (LoRA, AdaLoRA), arguing that all are inadequate for the richer, free-form nature of unstructured knowledge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By introducing UnKEBench—the first benchmark for unstructured knowledge editing—and demonstrating that ~80% of real-world knowledge exists in unstructured form, this paper opens a practically important gap in the knowledge editing literature. The empirical refutation of the local storage hypothesis for unstructured knowledge (Section 3.1) has implications beyond editing, touching on how knowledge is represented in transformers more broadly. Limitations include evaluation only on 7B models, a FactScore that remains below IKE (though the authors argue IKE's approach is akin to 'cheating'), and a slightly larger impact on non-relevant knowledge due to modifying more parameters. Open questions include scalability to larger models, handling of very long documents, and whether the non-local block representation generalizes across architectures.
