## Problem

Existing RL-based combinatorial optimization methods focus on one-shot deterministic problems, leaving sequential stochastic CO (SSCO) — where budget must be allocated and nodes selected adaptively over multiple time steps with stochastic feedback — largely unaddressed. The few prior SSCO studies assume a uniform budget split across all time steps, which is suboptimal in stochastic environments where information and conditions evolve. This paper formally defines the generic SSCO problem and proposes a principled solution that jointly learns adaptive budget allocation and node selection.

## Input / Output

The method takes a graph G=(V,E) with node features (e.g., active/inactive/removed states for AIM, or coordinates and dynamic profits for route planning), a total budget K, and a time horizon T. It produces a sequence of per-step budget allocations K_1,...,K_T (summing to ≤K) and corresponding node subsets S_1,...,S_T. A key property is that the model is trained on graphs of a fixed size (50–100 nodes) and generalizes to substantially larger graphs (up to 1000 nodes) without retraining, because the network architecture contains no size-dependent parameters.

## Method

WS-option is a two-layer option-based hierarchical RL framework where the higher layer selects a budget K_t per time step (the 'option') and the lower layer sequentially picks individual nodes under that budget. To prevent vicious mutual interference between the interdependent Q-functions, the paper introduces two key designs: (1) a wake-sleep training procedure that first freezes the higher layer (using a uniform allocation) to let the lower layer converge, then jointly fine-tunes both layers; and (2) layer-wise learning method selection — Monte Carlo methods for the higher layer (unbiased, stable) and off-policy TD-learning (Q-learning) for the lower layer (faster convergence). The lower-layer reward is constructed from normalized marginal rewards so that the sum of per-node rewards exactly matches the higher-layer reward at each time step, ensuring objective alignment. The higher layer uses an action-in network (budget as input) to enable generalization across budget sizes, while the lower layer uses a standard action-out structure. Convergence of both layers is established under tabular assumptions via contraction mapping arguments.

## Results

On the Adaptive Influence Maximization (AIM) problem with n=200 nodes, WS-option achieves cumulative rewards of 76.00, 118.56, 129.06, and 80.95 across four (T,K) settings, consistently outperforming all six baselines (p-values ≤ 0.05). On the Route Planning (RP) problem with n=100 nodes, WS-option achieves 7.46, 12.86, 18.57, and 7.52, surpassing both greedy and genetic algorithm baselines. Notably, the model trained on 50–100 node graphs generalizes to 1000-node graphs, achieving 221.16 on AIM (vs. best baseline 220.81) and 14.23 on RP (vs. best baseline 12.04) without retraining.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares WS-option against six baselines derived from Tong et al. [48], which combine three high-level budget allocation policies (average, static, normal) with two low-level node selection strategies (degree-based greedy, score-based). WS-option wins in all four (T,K) settings on AIM, with the largest margin over average-degree (e.g., 76.00 vs. 67.92 at T=10,K=10, a ~12% improvement) and the smallest margin over the strongest baseline static-score (118.56 vs. 118.13 at T=10,K=20). On the RP problem, WS-option outperforms both greedy and GA baselines across all settings, with the most substantial gain at T=10,K=30 (18.57 vs. 15.68 for greedy, a ~18% improvement). The generalization results (Table 4) show WS-option maintaining its lead even when scaled to 1000-node graphs without retraining, where the gap over the best baseline narrows but remains statistically significant.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the option framework (Sutton et al., 1999) for temporal abstraction in RL, extending it into a two-layer hierarchical structure where the higher layer allocates budget and the lower layer selects nodes. It departs from prior RL-for-CO work (Nazari et al., Kool et al., Khalil et al.), which targets one-shot deterministic problems, by formulating a generic sequential stochastic CO (SSCO) class that requires joint optimization of budget allocation and node selection over a time horizon. The wake-sleep training procedure and the layer-wise choice of MC (higher) vs. TD (lower) learning are novel stabilization mechanisms not present in standard HRL or option-critic architectures, motivated by the observed divergence of Q-values when both layers are trained simultaneously with off-policy TD methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SSCO encompasses a broad and practically important class of problems (adaptive influence maximization, epidemic intervention, dynamic route planning) that prior RL work has addressed only under the restrictive assumption of uniform budget allocation. The demonstrated ability to generalize from small training graphs (50–100 nodes) to much larger test graphs (up to 1000 nodes) without retraining is a significant practical advantage over traditional combinatorial optimization methods that must be re-run per instance. Key limitations include the need for problem-specific graph embedding architectures (S2V for AIM, attention for RP), convergence analysis restricted to the tabular case, and moderate (though statistically significant) performance gains over the strongest baselines. Future work on model-agnostic graph representations and extending the theoretical guarantees beyond the tabular setting would strengthen the framework's applicability.
