## Problem

Existing research on learning from noisy labels focuses on population-level robustness, leaving the instance-level impact unaddressed: even a model that performs well on average cannot identify which individual predictions are wrong, subjecting each person to a 'lottery of mistakes.' This paper formalizes this gap through a notion of regret and shows that standard hedging approaches, while reducing average error, leave regret unchanged at the individual level.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a noisy dataset of feature vectors and binary labels, a correctly specified noise model (uniform, class-level, group-level, or instance-level), and a class prior distribution. It outputs a set of m plausible models (an ensemble), a per-instance ambiguity score in [0,1] representing the estimated probability that the model makes a mistake on that instance, and a binary regret flag. The ambiguity score is computed as the fraction of plausible models whose prediction disagrees with the noisy label, and the plausibility filter (controlled by an atypicality parameter ε) constrains sampled noise draws to those whose empirical flip rate is within ε of the posterior noise rate.

## Method

The paper first establishes that regret is irreducible under label noise: the expected regret for any instance equals the posterior noise rate (Prop. 3), and even the classical hedging algorithm of Natarajan et al. incurs nonzero regret because it implicitly assumes a single MLE noise draw that almost surely differs from the true draw (Prop. 4). To estimate per-instance regret likelihood, the authors propose Algorithm 1, which repeatedly samples noise draws from the posterior distribution, retains only draws whose empirical noise rate falls within an ε-neighborhood of the posterior rate (a strongly typical set), constructs a clean dataset from each retained draw via XOR with the noisy labels, and trains a model on each resulting dataset. The ensemble of m plausible models then yields an unbiased ambiguity estimate per instance, which can be operationalized as a confidence score for selective prediction, data cleaning (dropping high-ambiguity training points), or resource allocation in scientific discovery tasks.

## Results

Across five clinical prediction datasets (shock_eicu, shock_mimic, lungcancer, mortality, support), the paper shows that regret is invariant to the training method: under 40% class-level label noise, regret remains at ~19.5–19.8% for both Ignore and Hedge, matching the theoretical prediction of Prop. 3. Hedge does reduce population-level True Error substantially (e.g., from 33.2% to 19.4% on mortality at 40% noise, a 13.8 pp improvement) and cuts Overreliance by roughly half (17.3% → 5.8%), but cannot eliminate instance-level regret. In the demonstrations, using ambiguity-based data cleaning reduces test error by 14.9% when dropping 20% of noisy training instances, and ambiguity-based selective abstention on 40% of test points reduces selective error by 6.6% and selective regret by 5.9% on shock_mimic under 20% noise.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two training strategies—Ignore (standard ERM on noisy labels) and Hedge (Natarajan et al.'s noise-corrected loss [40])—across all five clinical datasets and three noise levels. Hedge consistently wins on population-level True Error, with the largest gap at 40% noise (e.g., 19.4% vs. 33.2% on mortality, a 13.8 pp reduction; 20.1% vs. 34.9% on shock_mimic). However, Hedge ties Ignore exactly on Regret across every dataset and noise level (e.g., 19.5% for both on mortality at 40% noise), confirming the paper's central theoretical claim that hedging cannot eliminate instance-level regret. Hedge also wins on Overreliance, roughly halving it at high noise (e.g., 5.8% vs. 17.3% on mortality at 40% noise), by redistributing unforeseen mistakes away from overconfident correct-looking predictions. Susceptibility is identical for both methods since it is a property of the dataset and noise model, not the learner. The paper's proposed ambiguity-based approach (Section 6) then outperforms standard predicted-probability confidence: it reduces test error by 14.9% in data cleaning and improves selective hit rate by 10.7% on the enhancer dataset with only 20% abstention.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the hedging framework of Natarajan et al. [40] by introducing an instance-level regret notion that characterizes a fundamental limitation: even optimally hedged models cannot eliminate individual-level mistakes under label noise. Methodologically, it adapts the ensemble-sampling approach of Reed et al. [47] (training on alternative clean label realizations) by restricting samples to a set of *plausible* draws defined by a relative-deviation constraint, thereby controlling for atypical noise realizations. The work also connects to the model multiplicity literature, where different pipeline choices yield conflicting predictions, but here the multiplicity arises specifically from label noise rather than algorithmic or data perturbations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The formal result that regret equals the posterior noise rate (Prop. 3) and is invariant to hedging (Prop. 4) establishes a hard limit on what any noise-robust training procedure can achieve at the instance level, with direct implications for clinical decision support where overreliance on seemingly correct predictions can harm patients. The ambiguity-based selective prediction and data cleaning tools offer practical mitigations, though the approach assumes a correctly specified noise model—a significant limitation in real-world settings where noise structure is often unknown. Open questions include extending the framework to multi-class and continuous-label settings, handling misspecified noise models more gracefully, and integrating regret-aware inference into broader ML pipelines (explanations, recourse, fairness audits).
