## Problem

Sample-efficient exploration in reinforcement learning remains difficult for complex, sparse-reward tasks, while humans and animals demonstrate superior and flexible exploration strategies in novel environments. Although recent neuroscience has identified biological neural circuits (e.g., in C. elegans) that control explore-exploit transitions, the algorithmic principles these circuits implement and how they relate to classical RL exploration algorithms (Thompson sampling, UCB) remain unclear. This paper bridges that gap by deriving a mathematical model of the biological foraging network and showing it naturally implements a hybrid of posterior sampling and uncertainty-directed exploration.

## Input / Output

For bandit tasks, each neuron receives a scalar input sampled from the reward history buffer of the corresponding arm (scaled to [-2, 2] with variance in [0, 2]); for MDP tasks, inputs are sampled from Gaussian distributions of state-action Q-values whose means and variances are maintained via the Uncertainty Bellman Equation. The network is simulated for T=400–1000 steps using Runge-Kutta integration of the stochastic differential equations, and the output is the action (arm) whose corresponding neuron has the highest activation at the end of simulation. Crucially, the per-neuron input noise (σ_i) is anisotropic—reflecting differing uncertainty levels across options—which is what generates the tunable optimistic/neutral/conservative bias.

## Method

BBN is a stochastic continuous Hopfield network with N mutually inhibitory neurons (all weights w_ij < 0), sigmoid activation, and additive Wiener noise, governed by Langevin equations. The key theoretical result is that, via Kramers' escape theory, the equilibrium probability of each attractor state (winner-take-all configuration) equals the Bayesian posterior of the corresponding action given external evidence, extending Hinton & Sejnowski's discrete Hopfield result to the continuous case. Anisotropic input noise interacts with the local Hessian curvature at each attractor to produce an effective diffusion constant D_i^eff that differs across states, yielding a tunable bias: when the noisiest input aligns with the highest-curvature dimension, the network becomes optimistic (prefers uncertain options); misalignment produces conservative bias. For MDP tasks, BBN is embedded with the Uncertainty Bellman Equation to propagate Q-value variance, and an optional action-persistence mechanism (inheriting neuron states across timesteps) exploits the Hopfield network's attractor memory to boost deep exploration in large grid worlds.

## Results

In 2-armed and 3-armed Gaussian bandit games (10,000 blocks of 20/30 trials), BBN with optimistic parameters consistently achieves the highest probability of selecting the optimal arm across all trial numbers, outperforming Thompson Sampling, UCB, and Optimistic Thompson Sampling. On the SixArms MDP task, UBE-BBN achieves the lowest cumulative regret among all compared methods (PSRL, UCRL2, OTS-MDP, UBE-UCB, UBE-TS). In the FourRooms sparse-reward grid world, UBE-BBN attains the fastest coverage rate, covering all states in fewer than 450 episodes while other methods fail to achieve full coverage. BBN also closely reproduces human and mouse choice probability curves (both slope and intercept) across four human datasets and one mouse dataset, outperforming TS and UCB in fitting behavioral data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BBN outperforms Thompson Sampling (Thompson, 1933) in both 2-armed and 3-armed bandit tasks, achieving higher optimal-arm selection probability; TS is limited to sensitivity only to total uncertainty (constant intercept), whereas BBN captures both total and relative uncertainty effects. BBN also beats UCB (Lai & Robbins, 1985) in bandit performance; UCB is sensitive only to relative uncertainty (constant slope) and fails to match BBN's hybrid exploration strategy. Against Optimistic Thompson Sampling (Hu et al., 2023), BBN clearly wins in 2-armed bandits and matches or slightly exceeds OTS in 3-armed bandits; in the SixArms MDP, UBE-BBN achieves lower cumulative regret than OTS-MDP. In the FourRooms task, UBE-BBN surpasses PSRL (Osband et al., 2013) in coverage speed and also outperforms UCRL2 (Auer et al., 2008), which fails to perform deep exploration beyond the starting region. Overall, BBN wins or ties on every primary metric across all evaluated tasks, with the most pronounced advantages in 2-armed bandits and large-grid FourRooms environments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges three previously separate threads: (1) the C. elegans foraging circuit identified by Ji et al. (2021) that controls roaming/dwelling transitions, (2) the classical result that discrete Hopfield networks implement Bayesian posterior sampling (Hinton & Sejnowski, 1983), and (3) the RL exploration literature on Thompson Sampling, UCB, and their hybrids (OTS). The key conceptual move is showing that a stochastic continuous Hopfield network with exclusively inhibitory connections — a direct mathematical formalization of the biological circuit — naturally produces a tunable hybrid of posterior sampling and uncertainty-directed exploration via Kramers' escape theory, without any explicit exploration bonus or posterior reshaping. This departs from prior RL exploration methods that engineer the bias algorithmically, instead deriving it from the network's energy landscape and anisotropic noise structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled theoretical account of how a compact biological circuit can solve the explore-exploit dilemma, and demonstrates that this biologically-motivated architecture outperforms purpose-designed RL exploration algorithms (TS, UCB, OTS, PSRL, UCRL2) on bandit and MDP tasks. However, several limitations temper its immediate practical impact: the SDE simulation is computationally expensive, uncertainty estimation is outsourced to the UBE algorithm rather than computed by the network itself, and all experiments are on small-scale tasks (≤3-armed bandits, SixArms, FourRooms up to 103×103). Open questions include how biological networks internally compute and encode value uncertainty in sequential settings, how to integrate context-dependent modulation of the uncertainty bias, and whether the approach scales to high-dimensional or continuous action spaces. The connection to Langevin sampling suggests the architecture may be particularly well-suited for neuromorphic hardware implementation.
