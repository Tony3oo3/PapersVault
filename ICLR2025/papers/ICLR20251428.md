## Problem

Despite widespread empirical use of self-improvement (generate, verify, distill) in LLM pre-training, post-training, and test-time inference, a fundamental understanding of when and why it works remains lacking. Prior studies typically examine a single model family or one verification mechanism, making it difficult to disentangle the effects of generation variability, verification informativeness, and update fidelity. This paper initiates a comprehensive, modular, and controlled study to identify the governing principles and boundaries of LLM self-improvement.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a base LLM (from Qwen, Llama, or Yi families) and a text prompt, samples 128 responses per prompt (temperature 0.7, top-p 0.9, max length 512, 4-shot in-context), and scores each response with a proxy utility function (Multiple Choice, Chain-of-Thought Score/Binary, or Tournament). The primary output is the generation-verification gap (GV-Gap) — the accuracy difference between verification-filtered and unfiltered generations — plus a relative variant normalized by per-prompt headroom to optimal utility. The secondary output is an updated model obtained via rejection sampling (fine-tuning on top-quantile generations) or KL-regularized RL, enabling iterative self-improvement loops where the updated model becomes the next generator and verifier.

## Method

The paper formalizes self-improvement as a three-component pipeline — improvable generation, informative verification, and high-fidelity model update — and introduces the GV-Gap as the central metric capturing the precision of a model's self-verification over its own generations, defined as the expected utility gain from reweighting the generation distribution by the model's own verification scores. The relative GV-Gap normalizes per-prompt improvement by the remaining deficiency to optimal utility, resolving the issue that absolute gap is trivially bounded when a model is already near-ceiling. The key empirical finding is a scaling phenomenon: with stable verification (e.g., CoT-Score), the relative GV-Gap increases monotonically and conjecturally linearly in log pre-training flops, while cross-verification reveals the gap increases with verifier capacity and decreases with generator capacity. The paper further characterizes iterative self-improvement dynamics (saturation after 2–3 rounds driven by effective diversity degradation) and demonstrates that ensembling verification methods with non-overlapping error patterns consistently improves self-improvement efficacy across model scales.

## Results

On GSM8K, the relative generation-verification gap (gap_rel) scales monotonically with pre-training flops for CoT-Score verification, reaching ~21.8% for Qwen-1.5 72B (accuracy 74.55%) and ~20.2% for Qwen-2 72B (accuracy 81.69%). Small models (≤1.8B) show non-positive gaps across nearly all verification methods, indicating they cannot self-improve. On 4×4 Sudoku, only Qwen-2 72B achieves a meaningful gap of 16.99% (relative gap 20.81%), representing a ~200% accuracy improvement, while all smaller models fail. On Natural Questions (factual retrieval), all gaps remain below 1% despite non-trivial generation accuracy, confirming the task is unimprovable via self-verification.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the key contrasts are between verification mechanisms and model scales. CoT-Score verification consistently produces positive gaps for medium-to-large models (≥4B), while MC verification frequently yields non-positive gaps even for 14B–32B models (e.g., Qwen-1.5 14B: MC top 0.8 = −5.61% vs. CoT-Bin = +1.79%), making CoT the more reliable verification strategy. Tournament verification shows the largest absolute gaps for the strongest models (Yi-1.5 9B: 7.83%; Qwen-1.5 72B: 6.40%) but is less stable across model families. In cross-verification, the gap increases monotonically with verifier size and decreases with generator size, confirming that verification is easier than generation. Iterative self-improvement saturates after 2–3 rounds regardless of model capacity, and MC verification saturates faster than CoT, with the gap dropping to near zero after just one round for MC.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the STaR self-improvement paradigm (generate, verify, distill) and the Chain-of-Thought verification mechanism, but explicitly does not propose a new algorithm. Instead, it provides the first comprehensive, modular, and controlled study of LLM self-improvement by formalizing the generation-verification gap (GV-Gap) as a central metric and systematically varying model families (Qwen, Llama, Yi), verification methods (MC, CoT, Tournament), and tasks. It departs from prior work by using base models to eliminate post-training confounders, studying cross-verification (decoupling generator and verifier), and identifying scaling regularities that previous single-model or single-method studies could not reveal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The discovery that the relative GV-Gap scales monotonically (and approximately linearly in log pre-training flops) with model scale provides a predictive tool for deciding when synthetic data generation via self-improvement is worthwhile, with direct implications for pre-training and post-training pipelines. The paper also establishes clear boundaries: self-improvement fails on factual retrieval tasks and on reasoning tasks exceeding a model's inherent capabilities, and iterative self-improvement saturates within 2–3 rounds regardless of model size due to effective diversity degradation. Open questions include finding compute-optimal cross-verification configurations, developing strategies to mitigate diversity collapse, and extending the scaling analysis from observational to controlled scaling-law studies.
