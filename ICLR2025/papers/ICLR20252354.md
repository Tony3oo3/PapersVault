## Problem

Collecting high-quality preference data for LLM alignment is resource-intensive and relies on static datasets from human annotation or stronger teacher models, making continual improvement increasingly costly as models advance. Existing iterative self-improvement methods (sampling-ranking, self-rewarding) use fixed prompt sets and deterministic reward signals that fail to capture subtle discrepancies between model outputs and ideal responses, limiting how far a model can push its own boundaries.

## Input / Output

The method takes a small seed SFT dataset (18k prompt–GPT-4 Turbo completion pairs from UltraFeedback), three random keywords sampled per prompt from RefinedWeb paragraphs, and an initial SFT policy model (Mistral-7B or Llama3-8B). It produces an aligned LLM after 3–4 iterations of synthetic preference optimization, along with a growing pool of synthetic preference triplets (prompt, chosen refined response, rejected initial-model response). Notably, the seed data is used only to train the prompt generator and response improver and for validation; the actual preference training data is entirely self-synthesized, and the rejected response in each triplet always comes from the *initial* model rather than the current-iteration model, keeping the preference signal anchored.

## Method

SynPO is an iterative self-boosting loop with two trained components: a self-prompt generator (SFT-trained to produce diverse instructions from three keywords, including one noise keyword for robustness) and a response improver (SFT-trained each iteration to map the current model's output on seed prompts to the gold standard response, thereby learning the distribution gap). In each iteration, the generator produces ~50k synthetic prompts; the current policy model generates responses; the response improver refines them into chosen candidates; and a small pairwise scorer (0.4B PairRM or ArmoRM) filters pairs with a significant preference gap. The initial model is then preference-optimized via the SimPO length-normalized loss on the accumulated synthetic preference data, where the rejected response is always the initial model's output (on-policy anchor) and the chosen response is the refined one. The key insight is that the response improver acts as a generative reward: rather than assigning a scalar score, it produces an improved text that implicitly encodes what the model should learn, and the pre/post-refinement contrast provides richer supervision than a discriminative reward model.

## Results

After four SynPO iterations, Mistral-7B achieves 34.0% length-controlled win rate (LC) and 36.4% raw win rate (WR) on AlpacaEval 2.0, up from 6.6% LC for the SFT baseline, while Llama3-8B reaches 32.1% LC and 33.6% WR (from 5.4% LC). On the more challenging Arena-Hard benchmark, SynPO peaks at iteration 3 with 24.1% WR (Mistral) and 32.5% WR (Llama3), surpassing all baselines. On the Open LLM Leaderboard, SynPO yields a 3.19% average score increase for Mistral (58.20→61.39) and 5.00% for Llama3 (63.34→68.34), with over 6% gains on ARC and over 16% on TruthfulQA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SynPO substantially outperforms all baselines on both AlpacaEval 2.0 and Arena-Hard. Against Manual Collection (UltraFeedback, GPT-4-annotated preferences), SynPO Iter4 leads by 12.5 LC points on Mistral (34.0 vs. 21.5) and 10.1 LC points on Llama3 (32.1 vs. 22.0), and by 7.3 and 9.3 WR points on Arena-Hard respectively. Against the iterative Sampling-Ranking baseline (following SimPO/SPPO), SynPO Iter4 achieves 22.4 LC points higher on Mistral (34.0 vs. 11.6) and 17.9 on Llama3 (32.1 vs. 14.2), indicating that direct sampling-and-ranking is far less effective than the response-improver approach. Against Self-Rewarding (LLM-as-a-Judge), SynPO Iter4 wins by 7.9 LC points on Mistral (34.0 vs. 26.1) and 7.3 on Llama3 (32.1 vs. 24.8), and dominates on Arena-Hard by 3.0 and 7.5 WR points. On the Open LLM Leaderboard, SynPO Iter4 also edges out Manual Collection (61.39 vs. 60.91 for Mistral; 68.34 vs. 65.95 for Llama3) and Sampling-Ranking (61.39 vs. 59.91; 68.34 vs. 67.33), confirming that the gains extend beyond instruction-following to general task performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

SynPO builds on the SimPO preference optimization objective and the broader self-boosting literature (Self-Rewarding LM, Self-Play Preference Optimization, Constitutional AI, SELF), but departs from these in two key ways: (1) it replaces fixed prompt sets with a self-prompt generator trained via a keywords-to-text task, eliminating the need for stronger LLMs or in-context examples for prompt diversity; and (2) it introduces a 'generative reward' mechanism where a response improver refines the model's own outputs, using pre/post-refinement pairs as synthetic preference data rather than relying on discriminative reward models or LLM-as-a-Judge scoring. This positions SynPO as a fully self-contained iterative pipeline that requires only a small SFT seed set and the model itself, contrasting with prior methods that depend on human annotation, GPT-4 APIs, or fixed instruction pools.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SynPO offers a practical pathway for continual LLM improvement without the escalating costs of human annotation or stronger teacher models, which is critical as models grow and alignment data becomes a bottleneck. The finding that iterative synthetic preference optimization simultaneously improves alignment (22–30% win rate gains) and general task performance (3.2–5.0% on Open LLM Leaderboard) partially mitigates the well-known 'alignment tax,' suggesting that generative rewards provide richer supervision than discriminative scores. Open questions include whether the approach scales to much larger models, how sensitive it is to seed data quality (the paper acknowledges this as a key dependency), and whether the response improver's tendency toward paraphrasing rather than targeted correction limits further gains. The GSM8K regression for Mistral (vs. improvement for Llama3) highlights that data filtering quality remains a practical concern.
