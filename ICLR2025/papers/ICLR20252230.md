## Problem

While prior work has characterized multilingual mechanisms in already-trained LLMs (e.g., working languages in intermediate layers, language-specific neurons), the dynamic process by which these capabilities form during pre-training remains unexplored. This paper asks: how do LLMs acquire new language capabilities during pre-training, what internal state transitions accompany this process, and can these insights be leveraged to design better multilingual pre-training data distributions?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252230/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a monolingual Python code LLM (GPT-2 architecture, 1.3B or 6.7B parameters), target-language corpora (PHP, C#, Go, C++ collected from GitHub), and a HumanEval validation set. It produces an optimized token-count allocation per language for the pre-training corpus and a resulting multilingual code LLM. A notable design choice is the use of language-specific functional identifiers (e.g., PHP 'strlen' vs. Python 'len') to disambiguate working languages via logit lens, since programming languages share many surface tokens and cannot be distinguished by generated tokens alone.

## Method

The paper proposes the Babel Tower Hypothesis: during pre-training, multiple languages initially share a single knowledge system dominated by a primary language (Python), then progressively develop language-specific systems through three stages (Translation → Transition → Stabilization). This is validated by tracking two internal-state signals across training steps: (1) working-language proportions measured via logit lens on language-specific identifiers, and (2) counts of language-transferring neurons identified through Language Activation Probability Entropy (LAPE). For practical corpus optimization, Algorithm 1 estimates the optimal token count for each target language by first using training loss as a proxy for the dominant-language system proportion at peak performance (Equation 2), then mapping that proportion to a token ratio (Equation 3), thereby emulating the internal state at the performance peak rather than the natural data distribution. The key empirical finding is that for most languages, the performance peak occurs during the Translation Stage, meaning less target-language data (not more) yields better results.

## Results

On the 1.3B model, the optimized pre-training corpus achieves an average HumanEval Pass@1 of 26.61 and MBXP Pass@1 of 51.97, outperforming the original corpus (25.03 and 49.81 respectively). On the 6.7B model, the optimized corpus reaches 41.96 (HumanEval AVE) and 66.14 (MBXP AVE), compared to 38.31 and 64.34 for the original corpus. The gains are most pronounced in PHP and C# for the 6.7B model (e.g., PHP HumanEval: 41.61 vs. 38.51; C# HumanEval: 44.94 vs. 39.24), confirming that the Babel Tower–guided data distribution effectively leverages cross-lingual transfer from Python.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The optimized 1.3B model slightly edges out DeepseekCoder-1.3B on HumanEval AVE (26.61 vs. 26.39) but trails on MBXP AVE (51.97 vs. 55.93), likely due to differences in training data scale and recipe. At the 6.7B scale, the optimized model surpasses DeepseekCoder-6.7B on HumanEval AVE (41.96 vs. 41.73) but falls short on MBXP AVE (66.14 vs. 71.20), suggesting the data-distribution strategy primarily benefits code-generation tasks measured by HumanEval. Compared to StarCoder2-7B (32.76 HumanEval AVE) and CodeLlama-7B (28.02), the optimized 6.7B model leads by a wide margin (+9.2 and +13.9 respectively), though these baselines differ in architecture and training tokens. The most consistent and interpretable comparison is original vs. optimized within the same architecture: the optimized corpus yields +1.58 (1.3B) and +3.65 (6.7B) HumanEval AVE gains, with the larger model benefiting more, indicating the Babel Tower–guided distribution strategy scales favorably.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the static interpretability tools for multilingual LLMs—logit lens for detecting working languages (Wendler et al., 2024; Nostalgebraist, 2020) and language-specific neuron identification via LAPE (Tang et al., 2024)—from analyzing fixed, pre-trained models to dynamically tracking these internal states across pre-training steps. Prior work established that multilingual LLMs operate in a dominant language at intermediate layers and use language-specific neurons at top/bottom layers, but left open how these mechanisms *form* during training. The Babel Tower Hypothesis synthesizes these static observations into a dynamic three-stage model (translation → transition → stabilization) and, uniquely, leverages the resulting understanding to propose a practical corpus-distribution optimization method for multilingual pre-training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that for several languages (PHP, C#), performance *peaks while the model still relies on the dominant language's knowledge system*—and that adding more target-language data can actually hurt—challenges the default assumption that more in-domain data always helps. The proposed corpus optimization method is simple (downsampling based on a loss-based proxy for system proportion) yet yields consistent gains across two model scales, offering a low-cost lever for practitioners designing multilingual pre-training mixes. Key limitations include evaluation on only five programming languages, GPT-2 architecture, and models up to 6.7B parameters; whether the Babel Tower pattern and the optimization strategy generalize to natural languages, larger models, or architectures beyond GPT-2 remain open questions the authors explicitly flag for future work.
