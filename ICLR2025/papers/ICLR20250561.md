## Problem

Conformal prediction guarantees 1−α coverage only under i.i.d. calibration and test data, but joint distribution shifts (combining covariate and concept shifts) are common in practice and break this guarantee. Prior work bounds the resulting coverage gap using total variation distance, which is location-agnostic and cannot identify how the gap changes at a specific confidence level α. Moreover, existing methods largely address covariate shift in isolation, leaving the more general joint shift setting underexplored.

## Input / Output

The method consumes k multi-source training sets S_XY^(i), a calibration set S_XY^P (drawn from a mixture of the k sources), and a test feature vector x at inference time. It outputs a prediction set C(x) = {ŷ : |h_θ(x) − ŷ| ≤ τ}, where τ is the 1−α quantile of importance-weighted calibration conformal scores, along with an implicit coverage gap bounded by the Wasserstein distance between calibration and test conformal score distributions. The test distribution is an unknown random mixture of the k training distributions, so the method must generalize without knowing the mixture weights.

## Method

The paper upper-bounds the coverage gap by the 1-Wasserstein distance between calibration and test conformal score distributions (via a Kolmogorov-to-Wasserstein inequality), then decomposes this distance using pushforward measures into a covariate-shift term W(P_V, Q_{V,s_P}) and a concept-shift term W(Q_{V,s_P}, Q_V). Importance weighting with the likelihood ratio dQ_X/dP_X eliminates the covariate-shift term, leaving only the concept-shift term to minimize. During training, the model h_θ is optimized with a composite objective combining empirical risk minimization across all k sources and a Wasserstein regularization term that aligns the weighted calibration conformal score distribution with each source's conformal score distribution. A hyperparameter β controls the trade-off between prediction accuracy (smaller sets) and coverage fidelity (smaller gap), yielding a Pareto front of solutions. Theoretical analysis further bounds the remaining terms by κ·W(P_X, Q_X) + η·W(Q_{Y,f_P}, Q_Y), linking the gap to the Lipschitz constant of the score function and the ratio of residual differences to concept differences.

## Results

Across six datasets (Airfoil Self-Noise, Seattle-Loop, PeMSD4, PeMSD8, U.S.-States, Japan-Prefectures) and α values from 0.1 to 0.9, WR-CP reduces average coverage gaps to 3.2% and produces prediction sets 37% smaller than the worst-case approach (WC-CP) on average. In the correlation analysis (Table 1), Wasserstein distance achieves the highest Spearman's coefficient with average coverage gap on 4 of 6 datasets (e.g., 0.90 on PeMSD8, 0.84 on PeMSD4 and Seattle-Loop), outperforming TV distance, KL-divergence, and expectation difference. WR-CP consistently reduces normalized Wasserstein distance between calibration and test conformal score distributions compared to both vanilla CP and IW-CP, with the degree of reduction controlled by the hyperparameter β.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WR-CP outperforms WC-CP (worst-case conformal prediction from Gendler et al., Cauchois et al., and Zou & Liu) on prediction efficiency: at α=0.2, WR-CP produces prediction sets 37% smaller on average while maintaining comparable coverage concentration around 1−α. Against vanilla split conformal prediction (Papadopoulos et al.) and IW-CP (Tibshirani et al.), WR-CP achieves more tightly concentrated coverages around the target 1−α level across all six datasets, at the cost of slightly larger prediction sets due to the Wasserstein regularization increasing prediction residuals. Compared to conformalized quantile regression (CQR, Romano et al.), WR-CP similarly yields more concentrated coverages under joint distribution shift. Notably, on the Seattle-Loop dataset, IW-CP actually performs worse than vanilla CP in coverage, reflecting that importance weighting can enlarge Wasserstein distance when covariate shift alleviates rather than exacerbates concept-shift-induced discrepancy. WR-CP's advantage over IW-CP is most pronounced on datasets with strong concept shift (PeMSD4, PeMSD8, U.S.-States, Japan-Prefectures), where the Wasserstein regularization term directly minimizes the concept-shift-induced component that IW-CP cannot address.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends split conformal prediction (Papadopoulos et al., 2002) and importance-weighted conformal prediction for covariate shift (Tibshirani et al., 2019) to the more general setting of joint distribution shift in multi-source domain generalization. It departs from prior work that bounds coverage gap via total variation distance (Barber et al., 2023; Angelopoulos et al., 2022) by adopting Wasserstein distance, which is location-sensitive and better captures gap changes at a specific confidence level. The key theoretical departure is the use of pushforward measures to decompose the Wasserstein bound into independent covariate-shift and concept-shift terms, extending the same-function pushforward result of Aolaritei et al. (2022) to the different-function case (Theorem 1). Unlike worst-case approaches (Gendler et al., 2021; Cauchois et al., 2024; Zou & Liu, 2024) that treat joint shift monolithically, WR-CP targets each shift component separately via importance weighting and a Wasserstein-regularized training objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The pushforward-based decomposition provides a principled, interpretable link between model properties (Lipschitz constant κ, residual sensitivity η) and coverage gap, enabling targeted optimization rather than conservative worst-case inflation. The controllable β hyperparameter offers practitioners a Pareto front between coverage accuracy and prediction set size, which is practically valuable in applications like traffic forecasting and epidemic prediction where overly large prediction sets are operationally useless. Key limitations include sensitivity to KDE accuracy (particularly in higher dimensions or with small calibration sets), computational overhead of bandwidth grid search, and evaluation restricted to tabular data with shallow MLPs. Open questions include whether the decomposition and regularization strategy transfer to high-dimensional or non-parametric settings, and whether the multi-source mixture assumption can be relaxed further.
