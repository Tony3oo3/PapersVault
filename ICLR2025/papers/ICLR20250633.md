## Problem

Existing vision-instruction correlation (VIC) reward models are constrained to short-horizon tasks and require full-length demonstrations for training, making them ineffective for long-horizon manipulation where each step's success depends on prior stages. Three specific failures arise: no awareness of task decomposition into sub-stages, confusion from variance in task difficulties during training, and ambiguity from lacking explicit object state estimation (e.g., rewarding block movement even when the target drawer is still closed).

## Input / Output

At inference, the model consumes a single RGB image observation and a natural-language task instruction, producing a scalar reward via potential-based shaping (φ(o_t) − φ(o_{t−1})). The potential φ is a composite of three discrete/continuous components: the detected stage index, the detected motion index within that stage, and a continuous in-motion progress score. Training data consists of motion-level (primitive) videos—each ~150 demos per motion—paired with text motion descriptions and categorical object-state labels, rather than full task-length demonstrations, which is the key distinction from baselines like LIV and LOReL.

## Method

VIctoR decomposes reward estimation into a three-level hierarchy: (1) a GPT-4-based Task Knowledge Generator that parses the instruction into ordered stages, each with conditional object states and a list of required motions; (2) a Stage Detector that uses MDETR for language-conditioned object cropping and a CLIP-based classifier to predict each object's state, then matches the state vector against task knowledge to identify the current stage; (3) a Motion Progress Evaluator (MPE) built on CLIP encoders with two learned projection heads (motion and progress), trained with three InfoNCE-based contrastive objectives—time contrastive (temporal proximity), motion contrastive (aligning motion embeddings with correct language descriptions while separating from unrelated ones and arbitrary-motion negatives), and language-frame contrastive (pulling near-completion progress embeddings toward the motion instruction while pushing earlier frames away). The final potential function φ(o_t) = λ_m(Σ preceding motions + current motion index) + progress is converted to a shaped reward, enabling the model to generalize to unseen long-horizon tasks composed of seen motions in arbitrary order without retraining.

## Results

On the simulated long-horizon manipulation benchmark, VIctoR achieves a 25% average success rate improvement over the best prior VIC reward model (LOReL) across all 10 tasks, with the gap widening to 43% on harder tasks containing more than 4 motions. On the most complex task (3 stages, 5 motions: 'open box + open light + pick block'), VIctoR reaches 57.7% success rate versus 28.9% for LOReL and 0% for LIV. Ablation studies confirm that all three hierarchical reward levels (stage, motion, progress) are necessary, with the full combination achieving 96.4% versus 29.7% without the progress signal.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VIctoR is compared against two prior VIC reward models: LIV (Ma et al., 2023) and LOReL (Nair et al., CoRL 2022), both fine-tuned on full-length task demonstrations. LIV is decisively outperformed: it achieves 0% success on all multi-stage tasks (≥2 stages) and only 66.7% on the single-stage 'open drawer' task, whereas VIctoR reaches 57.7–100% across the same tasks. LOReL is the strongest baseline, achieving 33–35% on several multi-stage tasks, but VIctoR consistently doubles or triples its performance (e.g., 96.4% vs. 35.1% on 'open box + pick block'; 70.9% vs. 35.0% on 'open light + open drawer + reach block'). Notably, VIctoR is trained only on motion-level (short-horizon) videos, while both baselines require full-length long-horizon demonstrations, yet VIctoR still outperforms them by a wide margin on composed tasks. The paper also includes a VIctoR(task) ablation trained on task-level data without stage/motion decomposition, which confirms the hierarchical design contributes an additional 22.1% over the flat variant on the 4-motion 'move block' task (58.9% vs. 36.8%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the VIC (Vision-Instruction Correlation) reward modeling line initiated by CLIP-based approaches (Dang et al., Mahmoudieh et al., Sontakke et al.) and pre-trained vision-language reward models like LIV (Ma et al., 2023) and LOReL (Nair et al., 2022), all of which are constrained to short-horizon tasks and require full-length demonstrations. VIctoR departs from this by introducing a three-level hierarchical decomposition (stage → motion → in-motion progress) and training exclusively on primitive motion-level videos, repurposing CLIP encoders with contrastive objectives (time, motion, language-frame) and leveraging GPT-4 for task knowledge generation and MDETR for object state detection. Conceptually, it bridges hierarchical RL's task decomposition philosophy with the VIC reward paradigm, treating long-horizon tasks as arbitrary permutations of learned motion primitives rather than monolithic demonstrations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The compositional generalization property—training on individual motions and transferring to unseen long-horizon task compositions without retraining—reduces data collection costs and is a practical advantage for deploying reward models in new task settings. However, the approach is limited to tasks composed of seen motions and a small set of interactable objects (4 in simulation, 4 in XSkill), leaving open whether the hierarchical framework scales to richer motion vocabularies or more diverse environments. The reliance on GPT-4 for task decomposition and a fine-tuned object state classifier also introduces dependencies that may not transfer cleanly to open-world settings. A key open question is whether the motion-level training paradigm can be extended to genuinely unseen motions (zero-shot) rather than only unseen compositions.
