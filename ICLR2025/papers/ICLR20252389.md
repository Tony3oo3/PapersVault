## Problem

Prior work on in-context learning (ICL) relies on disparate synthetic setups (linear regression, classification, probabilistic automata) that lack a sequence-modeling nature, making it unclear which ICL phenomena are universal and preventing a unified theoretical account. This paper asks whether a single, minimal sequence modeling task can reproduce the full phenomenology of ICL and whether the model's behavior can be decomposed into interpretable algorithmic strategies whose competition explains observed dynamics.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252389/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a context sequence of k=10 discrete states (each state is a single token) of length l_eval=400, sampled from a Markov chain defined by a k×k transition matrix. It outputs a next-token probability distribution over the 10 states, from which an empirical transition matrix T̂ is constructed by collecting predictions conditioned on each possible last state. Performance is quantified as the KL divergence between T̂ and the ground-truth transition matrix T*, evaluated on both in-distribution (training-set chains) and out-of-distribution (novel Dirichlet-sampled chains) data. The training data consists of sequences of length 512 drawn from a finite mixture of N Markov chains (N ∈ {2²,…,2¹¹}), with each chain's transition matrix rows sampled from a Dirichlet(1_k) distribution.

## Method

The paper introduces a finite Markov mixtures task and trains a 2-layer Transformer (with RoPE) via standard autoregressive cross-entropy loss, systematically varying data diversity (N), training steps, and context length. The core analytical contribution is identifying four interpretable algorithms—Uni-Ret, Bi-Ret, Uni-Inf, Bi-Inf—that combine fuzzy retrieval (weighting training-set chains by unigram or bigram likelihood) versus inference (directly estimating a transition matrix from context statistics) with unigram or bigram context features. Two diagnostic protocols (a shuffle test for bigram utilization and a proximity test comparing model predictions to training vs. random transition matrices) delineate algorithmic phases in the (diversity, steps) plane. A Linear Interpolation of Algorithms (LIA) procedure then decomposes the model's next-token distribution as a convex combination of the four algorithms' predictions, achieving near-zero KL and revealing that ICL phenomenology (e.g., transience, non-monotonic OOD curves) arises from competition dynamics among these algorithms, where a retrieval solution that performs better on training data gradually supersedes a better-generalizing inference solution.

## Results

The Linear Interpolation of Algorithms (LIA) decomposition achieves approximately zero KL divergence between the weighted combination of four predefined algorithms and the trained model's actual next-token probabilities, validating that model behavior is a mixture of Uni-Ret, Bi-Ret, Uni-Inf, and Bi-Inf. The algorithmic phase diagram reveals sharp transitions between dominant algorithms as a function of data diversity (N from 2² to 2¹¹) and training steps, with the critical data diversity threshold for Bi-Inf emergence occurring near N=2⁶. The transient nature of ICL is quantitatively explained: Bi-Inf (best OOD generalization) is gradually superseded by Bi-Ret (better ID but worse OOD) as training progresses, causing OOD KL to rise even as ID KL continues to decrease. Scaling the model width to embedding dimension 256 increases the data diversity required for Bi-Inf to appear by 16×, while reducing the state space to k=2 prevents Bi-Inf from ever dominating even at N=2¹¹.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baselines in the traditional sense; instead, it decomposes model behavior into four algorithmic 'solutions' (Uni-Ret, Bi-Ret, Uni-Inf, Bi-Inf) and shows which dominates under different experimental conditions. Bi-Inf achieves the best OOD generalization among all four algorithms, while Bi-Ret achieves the best ID performance, creating a fundamental tension that drives the observed phase transitions. The LIA framework reproduces and explains prior findings: the data diversity threshold of Raventós et al. (2023) corresponds to Bi-Inf outcompeting retrieval solutions; the transient ICL of Singh et al. (2023) is explained by Bi-Ret slowly overtaking Bi-Inf on the training loss; and the induction head emergence of Edelman et al. (2024) marks the unigram-to-bigram transition. Notably, the paper challenges Kirsch et al. (2022)'s claim that wider models develop ICL faster, arguing their results are confounded by the absence of FLOPs normalization—when properly normalized, smaller models actually develop Bi-Inf more efficiently.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by proposing a unified synthetic testbed (finite mixture of Markov chains) that simultaneously captures phenomenology previously studied in disparate settings: data diversity thresholds (Raventós et al., 2023), induction head emergence (Edelman et al., 2024), transient ICL (Singh et al., 2023), and task retrieval vs. learning (Min et al., 2022). Rather than building on a single prior formulation, it repurposes the sequence-modeling structure of probabilistic automata work (Akyürek et al., 2024) while adding a data-diversity knob analogous to linear regression studies. The key conceptual departure is the decomposition of model behavior into four interpretable algorithms (Uni-Ret, Bi-Ret, Uni-Inf, Bi-Inf) whose competition dynamics explain ICL phenomenology, and the use of Linear Interpolation of Algorithms (LIA) as a near-exact decomposition tool.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reframes ICL from a monolithic capability to a mixture of competing algorithms, offering a mechanistic explanation for the transient nature of ICL and the non-monotonic OOD performance curves that have puzzled the field. The finding that FLOPs-normalized training reveals smaller models are actually *more* robust at developing the generalizing Bi-Inf solution directly challenges Kirsch et al.'s (2022) model-scaling conclusions. However, all results are confined to 2-layer transformers on a synthetic task with k=10 states; whether the four-algorithm decomposition and competition dynamics generalize to real LLMs or more complex sequence structures remains an open and critical question. The framework also raises the practical question of whether training procedures can be designed to promote generalizing algorithms over memorizing ones.
