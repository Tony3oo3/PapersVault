## Problem

METRA, the state-of-the-art unsupervised skill discovery method, argues that optimizing a Wasserstein dependency measure is superior to mutual information maximization, calling into question the viability of the entire mutual information skill learning (MISL) framework. This paper asks whether METRA's strong performance can actually be explained within the well-studied MISL framework, and if so, whether a simpler MI-based algorithm can match its performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251146/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes state observations (low-dimensional state vectors for Ant and HalfCheetah; 64×64 RGB images for Quadruped, Humanoid, Kitchen, and Robobin) paired with latent skills z sampled from a uniform von Mises-Fisher distribution on the d-dimensional unit hypersphere, where d ranges from 2 to 9 depending on the task. It produces a skill-conditioned policy π(s,z)→a, a state representation φ(s)→R^d, and d-dimensional successor features ψ(s,a,z)→R^d that serve as a vector-valued critic. For downstream use, the learned representations enable zero-shot goal inference by computing z* = (φ(g)−φ(s))/‖φ(g)−φ(s)‖₂ as a MAP estimate, and hierarchical control by training a higher-level policy that outputs skills.

## Method

CSF (Contrastive Successor Features) is built on two key reinterpretations of METRA: (1) METRA's representation objective is a second-order Taylor approximation of a contrastive lower bound on I^β(S,S′;Z), and (2) METRA's actor objective is a lower bound on the information bottleneck I^π(S,S′;Z) − I^π(S,S′;φ(S′)−φ(S)). CSF exploits these insights by directly optimizing the contrastive lower bound (eliminating METRA's dual gradient descent and Wasserstein constraint) to learn φ, and by constructing the intrinsic reward as only the positive term (φ(s′)−φ(s))ᵀz, which removes the anti-exploration term and corresponds to maximizing the information bottleneck. For policy learning, CSF replaces the scalar Q-function with d-dimensional successor features ψ(s,a,z) that estimate the expected discounted difference in representations, so the policy simply maximizes the inner product ψ(s,a,z)ᵀz. The inner-product parameterization f(s,s′,z)=(φ(s′)−φ(s))ᵀz is shown to be critical—alternative parameterizations (MLP, Gaussian kernel, Laplacian kernel) are catastrophic for performance.

## Results

CSF is evaluated on six continuous control tasks (Ant, HalfCheetah, Quadruped, Humanoid, Kitchen, Robobin) across three metrics: state coverage (unique x,y coordinates visited), zero-shot goal reaching (staying time fraction), and hierarchical control (discounted return). CSF matches METRA (the prior SOTA) on 4/6 tasks for exploration, strongly outperforms METRA on Robobin, and slightly underperforms on Quadruped. For zero-shot goal reaching, METRA and CSF are the top two methods on Humanoid (with METRA slightly ahead), while all methods perform similarly on HalfCheetah. For hierarchical control, CSF and METRA are the best-performing methods with mostly similar performance, both clearly outperforming DIAYN, DADS, CIC, and VISR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against METRA (Park et al., 2024), CSF performs roughly on par across most tasks: matching on HalfCheetah and Humanoid for exploration, slightly underperforming on Quadruped, and strongly outperforming on Robobin. For zero-shot goal reaching on Humanoid, METRA edges out CSF slightly, while on HalfCheetah all methods are comparable. Against DIAYN (Eysenbach et al., 2019), DADS (Sharma et al., 2020), CIC (Laskin et al., 2022), and VISR (Hansen et al., 2020), CSF outperforms all four baselines in most settings across all three evaluation metrics (state coverage, goal reaching, and hierarchical control). The paper's key finding is that CSF achieves SOTA performance using a simpler algorithm (contrastive lower bound + successor features) without the Wasserstein distance or dual gradient descent that METRA requires, confirming that mutual information maximization remains a viable framework for skill learning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reinterprets METRA (Park et al., 2024), which was explicitly framed as a departure from mutual information toward Wasserstein distance, and shows its objectives are actually equivalent to a contrastive MI lower bound (for representations) and an information bottleneck (for the actor). It builds on the MISL lineage (DIAYN, VIC, DADS, VISR) for the mutual information formulation, on InfoNCE/CPC-style contrastive learning for the representation parameterization, and on successor representations (Dayan, 1993; Kulkarni et al., 2016) to replace scalar Q-estimation with a vector-valued critic. The resulting CSF algorithm is positioned as a simpler MISL method that retains METRA's SOTA performance while recovering the theoretical grounding of MI maximization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a live debate in unsupervised skill discovery: whether the MISL framework is fundamentally insufficient or whether METRA's Wasserstein framing was necessary. By showing METRA is a second-order Taylor approximation of a contrastive MI bound, it validates MI maximization as a viable and theoretically grounded objective, while identifying the information bottleneck (removing the anti-exploration term) as the critical ingredient that prior MI-only methods like DIAYN lack. Open questions include scaling to complex, partially observable environments (Craftax, VIMA) and large-scale pretraining on robot datasets, which the authors explicitly leave for future work.
