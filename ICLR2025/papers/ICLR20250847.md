## Problem

Existing molecular representation learning methods assume training and test data share the same distribution, causing significant performance degradation under real-world distribution shifts (scaffold, size, assay changes). Prior graph-based OOD approaches—whether first-separation-then-encoding or first-encoding-then-separation—rely on a single one-step disentanglement that fails on complex biomacromolecules with entangled substructures, and none explicitly capture invariant substructure characteristics.

## Input / Output

The method takes a molecular graph G=(V,E) (atoms as nodes, bonds as edges) and a GNN encodes it into a node-level feature matrix F ∈ R^{|V|×d}. After T=8 feedback iterations, the final output is a node-level generalized representation Z^Gen ∈ R^{|V|×d} obtained by concatenating all iterations' outputs and projecting through a fully-connected layer. This representation is then fed into task-specific decoders for binary classification (HIV, PCBA), regression (ZINC solubility), or 3D ground-state conformation prediction, making the method a plug-in module compatible with existing architectures like GTMGC.

## Method

CFD follows a first-encoding-then-separation strategy with two key innovations. First, a feedback disentanglement loop: at each iteration t, two variational encoders (spurious and generalized, each three FC layers) estimate Gaussian distributions over the current feature Q_t, sample from them, and a KAN-based concept module (K=12 learnable Gaussian concepts) aligns invariant substructure features to concepts via a weighted residual operation before fusing them back into the generalized representation; the next iteration's input Q_{t+1}=φ([Z_{t-1}^Gen, Z_t^Gen]) provides hierarchical progressive decomposition. Second, the training objective combines a contrastive InfoNCE-style loss (pushing generalized features close to the input while pushing spurious features away, plus KL divergence between the two estimated distributions), a feedback loss that maximizes cross-iteration similarity of generalized outputs while minimizing same-iteration generalized-spurious similarity, and a standard task loss (cross-entropy or MSE), weighted by α₁=0.1 and α₂=0.01.

## Results

On the GOOD benchmark, CFD achieves the best performance across all 12 evaluation settings (3 datasets × 2 shift types × 2 shift directions). On GOOD-HIV (ROC-AUC↑), CFD reaches 76.42–79.28, outperforming the strongest baseline iMoLD (72.93–77.43) by 2.5–4.5 points. On GOOD-ZINC (MAE↓), CFD achieves 0.0765–0.1421 versus iMoLD's 0.1014–0.1863, a relative improvement of up to 24%. On DrugOOD (ROC-AUC↑), CFD leads on all six splits, e.g., 73.86 vs. 72.67 (MILI) on IC50-Assay and 78.32 vs. 77.48 (iMoLD) on EC50-Assay.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CFD outperforms all 23 baselines on every evaluation setting in both the GOOD and DrugOOD benchmarks. The closest competitor is iMoLD (Zhuang et al., 2023), which shares the 'first-encoding-then-separation' paradigm but uses a single-step score-based separation; CFD improves over iMoLD by 2.5–4.5 ROC-AUC points on GOOD-HIV, 0.02–0.04 MAE on GOOD-ZINC, and 1.5–3.1 AP on GOOD-PCBA. Among general OOD methods (ERM, IRM, VREx, GroupDRO, Coral, DANN, Mixup), none approach CFD's performance, with ERM and Coral being the strongest at roughly 3–6 points below CFD on GOOD-HIV. Graph-specific OOD methods (CAL, DisC, MoleOOD, CIGA) either cannot be applied to all datasets or underperform substantially—MoleOOD, for instance, drops to 12.90 AP on GOOD-PCBA versus CFD's 19.78. On DrugOOD, CFD also surpasses MILI (Wang et al., 2024a), a 'first-separation-then-encoding' method, by 1.2–1.8 ROC-AUC points, confirming that iterative feedback disentanglement with concept fusion is more effective than one-shot substructure identification for OOD molecular generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the 'first-encoding-then-separation' paradigm introduced by iMoLD (Zhuang et al., 2023) for molecular OOD generalization, replacing its single-step score-based disentanglement with an iterative feedback mechanism inspired by Feedback Networks (Zamir et al., 2017). The key departures are: (1) using variational encoders (VAE-style sampling) rather than a deterministic scorer to decompose generalized and spurious features, and (2) introducing a concept mining module that leverages Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KAN) to learn molecule-agnostic substructure descriptors and fuse them into the disentangled representation. The paper positions itself against both 'first-separation-then-encoding' methods (MoleOOD, CIGA, DisC) and the one-step 'first-encoding-then-separation' approach (iMoLD), arguing that iterative feedback is necessary for highly complex biomacromolecules where a single separation step is insufficient.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

OOD generalization for molecular graphs is critical for real-world drug discovery where training and test molecules routinely differ in scaffold, size, or assay conditions. The feedback disentanglement mechanism offers a principled way to handle increasingly complex molecules (biomacromolecules) without requiring explicit substructure annotations, and the pluggability into both GNN and Transformer backbones (demonstrated via GTMGC) broadens its practical applicability. Limitations include the added computational cost of multiple iterations (~0.9 GB extra memory, ~0.06 s extra inference per molecule), the reliance on a fixed number of iterations (8) that may not transfer across molecule complexity scales, and the fact that the concept module's benefit diminishes at higher iteration counts, suggesting potential redundancy. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very large protein-ligand complexes and whether the learned concepts have interpretable chemical meaning beyond correlation with task performance.
