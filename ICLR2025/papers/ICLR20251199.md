## Problem

Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) are typically trained separately on each transformer layer, making it difficult to identify correspondences between features across layers or to study how information flows through the residual stream. While the residual stream perspective predicts that activation vectors at adjacent layers should be relatively similar, single-layer SAEs cannot directly capture or analyze features that are active at multiple layers simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The MLSAE takes residual stream activation vectors of dimension d from every transformer layer, treating each (token, layer) pair as a separate training example rather than concatenating layers. Inputs are standardized to zero mean and unit variance before encoding, and the decoder output is rescaled back. The model produces k-sparse latent activations per token-layer pair (via TopK) and reconstructed activation vectors, with the fraction of variance unexplained (FVU) used as the reconstruction metric to handle the growing L2 norms across layers.

## Method

The MLSAE is a single k-sparse autoencoder with tied parameters trained on activation vectors from all n_L layers simultaneously, equivalent to training one SAE per layer but sharing the encoder and decoder weight matrices. It uses a TopK activation function to enforce exactly k non-zero latents per input, an FVU reconstruction loss (rather than MSE) to avoid biasing toward layers with larger activation magnitudes, and an AuxK auxiliary loss to mitigate dead latents. The key analytical contribution is defining a distribution over layers for each latent (proportional to its activation at each layer) and quantifying multi-layer activity via variance ratios: the fraction of total variance explained by individual latents (aggregating over tokens) versus the fraction explained by individual tokens. Optionally, pre-trained tuned-lens transformations are applied to align the residual stream basis across layers before encoding, with the inverse transformation applied after decoding.

## Results

MLSAEs with R=64 and k=32 achieve FVU reconstruction errors between 0.081 and 0.299 and delta cross-entropy losses between -0.080 and 1.483 across Pythia (70m–1.4b), Gemma 2 2B, Llama 3.2 3B, and GPT-2 small. The key analytical finding is that the variance of latent activations over layers, when aggregating over 10M tokens, accounts for 54–88% of the total variance (increasing with model size), whereas for a single token it accounts for only 1–10%, confirming that latents are broadly multi-layer in aggregate but layer-specific per token. Applying tuned-lens transformations modestly decreases FVU and delta CE loss but does not increase the degree of multi-layer latent activity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against a set of named competing SAE methods in a head-to-head table; instead, it validates MLSAEs against single-layer SAEs trained per-layer with identical hyperparameters (Appendix B.4). The MLSAE performs comparably to the corresponding single-layer SAE on the same layer and more consistently across layers, while a single-layer SAE trained on one layer degrades sharply on other layers. FVU and delta CE loss values are reported as consistent with prior single-layer SAE results from Marks et al. (2024), Gao et al. (2024), and Lieberum et al. (2024) for comparable model sizes. The tuned-lens variant slightly reduces FVU and delta CE loss relative to the standard MLSAE but does not increase multi-layer latent activity, and it degrades single-layer SAE cross-layer performance unlike the MLSAE.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the standard per-layer SAE approach (Bricken et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2024) by training a single SAE with tied parameters across all residual stream layers, motivated by the residual stream perspective (Elhage et al., 2021; Ferrando et al., 2024) that information is selectively written and read across layers. It departs from prior multi-layer dictionary learning (Yun et al., 2021) by using a gradient-trained autoencoder rather than iterative optimization, and from the tuned-lens framework (Belrose et al., 2023) by applying pre-trained layer transformations as an input preprocessing step rather than as the primary interpretability tool. The 'data-stacked' formulation—treating each layer's activation as a separate training example—is a deliberate design choice over a 'feature-stacked' concatenation, justified by the need to track per-layer feature activity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The MLSAE provides a practical tool for studying cross-layer representation dynamics that single-layer SAEs cannot address, and the quantitative variance framework (Eqs. 11–12) gives a principled way to measure multi-layer feature activity. The key open question is how to leverage MLSAE latents for circuit discovery: the finding that individual tokens activate latents at mostly single layers complicates the 'cross-layer superposition' hypothesis (Olah, 2024; Templeton et al., 2024) that motivated the work. The paper also highlights that representation drift and growing activation norms across layers remain significant obstacles, and the tuned-lens variant's failure to increase multi-layer activity suggests that basis alignment alone is insufficient to capture the intended cross-layer structure.
