## Problem

In safety-critical inverse reinforcement learning, it remains unclear whether constraint signals can be implicitly absorbed into reward correction terms (IRC) or must be explicitly modeled as cost functions (ICRL). While IRC reduces a tri-level optimization to a bi-level one and is empirically more sample-efficient, no prior work has theoretically quantified the trade-off between this efficiency gain and the resulting loss of safety guarantees when transferring learned constraint knowledge to new environments with different rewards and dynamics.

## Input / Output

The solvers operate on a single expert's trajectory data from a source CMDP with finite state-action spaces, a known reward function r, and transition dynamics P_T, queried via a generative model under uniform sampling. The ICRL solver outputs a cost function c over state-action pairs, while the IRC solver outputs a reward correction term Δr that is added to the known reward. Both outputs are then evaluated for transferability to a target CMDP where r', P_T', and the ground-truth constraint may all differ, with safety measured by whether the resulting policy avoids constraint-violating state-action pairs in the overlapping critical region.

## Method

The paper introduces a formal feasible-set characterization for the IRC solver (Lemma 4.1) and derives its sample complexity (Theorem 4.2), showing it is lower than ICRL's by a factor of 1/(1-γ)², with the gap attributed to ICRL's need to estimate the reward advantage function to identify constraint-violating actions. For transferability, the paper proves that ICRL's cost functions strictly prevent visitation to overlapping constrained regions in the target environment (Lemma 5.2), while IRC's correction terms can be offset by reward and dynamics shifts (Theorem 5.3), which identifies explicit Q-function conditions under which unsafe actions become optimal after transfer. The paper further extends the transferability definition from regular MDPs to CMDPs (Definition 5.5) and derives a sufficient condition for ε-optimality of transferred cost functions using principal angles between subspaces induced by source and target transition laws (Theorem 5.7), bounding the suboptimality gap in terms of transition-law discrepancy, reward/Lagrange-multiplier mismatch, and cost estimation error.

## Results

The IRC solver achieves sample complexity Õ(4γ²R_max²SA/((1−γ)⁴ε_K²)) (Theorem 4.2), while the ICRL solver requires Õ(γ²σ²SA/((1−γ)⁶ε_K²)) (Theorem 4.5), a factor of 1/(1−γ)² more samples. In empirical Gridworld experiments (source: p=0.05, reward=1; target: p=0.1, rewards=2,7,7,15), IRC converges faster in the source environment, but in target environments IRC produces unsafe policies (discounted cumulative costs > 0) while ICRL maintains safety (costs → 0) and achieves expert-level rewards. Theoretically, ICRL's transferred cost functions guarantee safety in overlapping constrained regions (Lemma 5.2) and ε-optimality under bounded transition-law similarity (Theorem 5.7), whereas IRC's reward correction terms can be offset by environment shifts (Theorem 5.3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares IRC (Inverse Reward Correction, a bi-level solver that embeds constraint signals into a reward correction term) against ICRL (Inverse Constrained Reinforcement Learning, a tri-level solver that explicitly models cost functions and Lagrange multipliers). IRC wins on training efficiency, requiring fewer samples by a factor of 1/(1−γ)² because it avoids estimating the reward advantage function A^{r,π^E} that drives Lagrange multiplier updates in ICRL. However, ICRL wins decisively on cross-environment transferability: under hard constraints, ICRL's transferred cost functions strictly prevent visitation to overlapping constrained regions, while IRC's reward correction terms can be offset by even small differences in reward functions or transition dynamics between source and target environments. For optimality, ICRL provides a formal ε-optimality guarantee (Theorem 5.7) parameterized by the principal angle between source and target transition subspaces, a result that cannot be extended to IRC. Empirically, across all four Gridworld target environments, IRC policies violate safety constraints (non-zero cumulative costs) while ICRL policies converge to both safe (zero cost) and optimal (expert-matching reward) behavior, confirming the theoretical trade-off between complexity reduction and generalizability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the ICRL framework (Malik et al., 2021; Scobee & Sastry, 2020) and the IRC simplification (Hugessen et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023), which embeds constraint signals into reward correction terms to reduce tri-level optimization to bi-level. It adopts the feasible-set theoretical framework from Metelli et al. (2021) for IRL sample complexity and extends it to the IRC solver, while drawing on Yue et al. (2024) for the ICRL counterpart. For transferability, the paper extends Schlaginhaufen & Kamgarpour's (2024) MDP transferability definition to CMDP settings using principal angles between transition-law subspaces. The key departure from prior work is providing the first rigorous theoretical comparison of IRC and ICRL on both training efficiency and cross-environment safety transferability, going beyond the purely empirical comparison in Hugessen et al. (2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes a clear theoretical trade-off: IRC is more sample-efficient (by a factor of 1/(1-γ)²) but its reward correction terms can be offset by environmental shifts, leading to unsafe policies in target environments, whereas ICRL's explicit cost functions preserve safety in overlapping constrained regions. This has direct implications for safety-critical deployment where constraint knowledge must generalize beyond the training environment. Limitations include discrete state-action spaces, single-expert settings, and gridworld-scale experiments; the authors identify extensions to multi-expert demonstrations, continuous spaces, and real-world applications (robotics, LLM safety) as important future directions. The open question of whether the IRC-ICRL trade-off persists in high-dimensional continuous settings remains unresolved.
