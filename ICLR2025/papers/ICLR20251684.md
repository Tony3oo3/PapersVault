## Problem

Most de novo molecular design models operate on SMILES strings or atom/fragment graphs and produce molecules with no guarantee of synthetic accessibility. While post-hoc retrosynthesis or synthetic accessibility scores can partially address this, they are either too slow to integrate as reward signals or too oversimplified to ensure real-world synthesizability. The paper asks whether molecule generation can be formulated directly in a reaction-based action space that inherently constrains outputs to a synthetically accessible chemical space while preserving diversity and reward optimization.

## Input / Output

The model takes a scalar reward function (e.g., a pretrained binding-affinity proxy for sEH, GSK3β, DRD2, or GPU-accelerated Vina docking scores for Mpro/HSP90/KRAS), a library of up to 221,181 purchasable Enamine building blocks, and 105 reaction templates (13 uni-molecular, 92 bi-molecular) as inputs. It outputs terminal molecules together with their complete forward synthesis pathways—a sequence of reaction steps from building blocks to the final product. Building blocks are represented as fixed binary Morgan fingerprints rather than learned embeddings, and the molecular state at each step is a molecular graph processed by a graph transformer.

## Method

SynFlowNet formulates molecule generation as a GFlowNet over a reaction-based MDP where forward actions (Stop, AddFirstReactant, ReactUni, ReactBi, AddReactant) sequentially apply chemical reactions to build molecules from purchasable starting materials, and backward actions reverse these steps. A graph transformer encodes the current molecular graph and produces a shared embedding fed to separate MLP heads for each action type, with substructure-matching masks ensuring only chemically valid reactions are sampled. The key technical contribution is training the backward policy P_B with a separate objective from the forward policy—either maximum likelihood over forward-generated trajectories or REINFORCE with a reward of +1/−1 for reaching the initial state s_0—to solve the MDP consistency problem where arbitrary backward reaction applications can lead to states unreachable from any forward trajectory. For scaling to large building block sets, the model replaces a learned embedding matrix with fixed Morgan fingerprints, computing building block selection probabilities via normalized dot products, which enables efficient navigation of 200k+ building blocks without memorizing each one individually.

## Results

On the sEH binding proxy target, SynFlowNet (L=3) achieves a reward of 0.92, diversity of 0.79, SA score of 2.92, and AiZynthFinder retrosynthesis success of 65%, while maintaining a low ChEMBL similarity of 0.43 (indicating high novelty). The model generates molecules with a mean molecular weight of 365 Da and QED of 0.59. When scaling to the full Enamine set of 221,181 building blocks using Morgan fingerprint embeddings, SynFlowNet maintains competitive reward while the baseline without fingerprints shows significant reward degradation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against REINVENT, SynFlowNet achieves comparable reward (0.92 vs 0.91) but substantially better diversity (0.79 vs 0.68), lower molecular weight (365 vs 430 Da), and higher novelty (ChEMBL similarity 0.43 vs 0.64), while REINVENT retains an edge in AiZynthFinder retrosynthesis success (0.95 vs 0.65). Against the fragment-based GFlowNet (FragGFN), SynFlowNet dramatically outperforms on synthesizability (SA 2.92 vs 6.28, AiZynth 0.65 vs 0.00) and reward (0.92 vs 0.77), though FragGFN achieves slightly higher diversity (0.83 vs 0.79). Against SyntheMol, SynFlowNet wins on reward (0.92 vs 0.64) and molecular weight (365 vs 412 Da), while SyntheMol edges ahead on diversity (0.86 vs 0.79) and AiZynth success (0.82 vs 0.65). Against Soft Q-Learning on the same reaction-based MDP, SynFlowNet achieves higher reward (0.92 vs 0.80) and far superior diversity (0.79 vs 0.42), demonstrating the GFlowNet advantage over RL in mode coverage. Against SynNet on top-100 optimization, SynFlowNet matches on QED (0.947) and wins on GSK3β (0.862 vs 0.815) and DRD2 (0.999 vs 0.998), with a marginal loss on JNK3 (0.710 vs 0.719).

## Relation to Prior Work

SynFlowNet extends the GFlowNet framework (Bengio et al., 2021; Malkin et al., 2022) from fragment- and atom-based action spaces to a reaction-based MDP where actions are chemical reactions applied to purchasable building blocks. The scaling strategy for large building block sets (up to 221k) borrows the Morgan fingerprint representation from Gottipati et al. (2020) and Dulac-Arnold et al. (2015), originally developed for RL in large discrete action spaces. The key conceptual departure is training the backward policy P_B with a separate objective (maximum likelihood or REINFORCE) from the forward policy, which addresses an inherent MDP consistency problem unique to reaction-based environments — a challenge absent in prior fragment-based GFlowNet settings where any backward path trivially reaches s_0. This positions the work at the intersection of GFlowNet generative modeling and synthesis-aware molecular design (Gottipati et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2021; Swanson et al., 2024), but is the first to use GFlowNets with a reaction-based action space and a separately trained backward policy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that constraining generation to a reaction-based MDP yields molecules with substantially better synthesizability (62% AiZynthFinder success vs. 0% for fragment-based GFlowNets) while maintaining competitive reward and diversity, directly addressing a major practical gap in de novo drug design. The backward policy training paradigm is a reusable technique that could incorporate additional real-world constraints (synthesis cost, yield, selectivity) into the generative process, opening a path toward more realistic synthesis planning. However, significant limitations remain: no support for nonlinear pathways, reactions with >2 reactants, reaction conditions, or stereochemistry, and the rediscovery rate is imperfect, suggesting certain chemical space regions remain inaccessible. The framework's demonstrated compatibility with experimental fragment screens (Mpro, HSP90) hints at practical integration into real drug discovery campaigns, but validation at scale with actual synthesis remains future work.
