## Problem

Existing methods for mitigating LLM hallucination rely on simplistic binary known/unknown knowledge boundaries, which either make models overly conservative by refusing to answer questions they actually know, or fail to leverage the model's own incorrect responses for learning. This paper addresses two gaps: (1) the lack of a finer-grained knowledge boundary that captures the model's uncertain knowledge, and (2) the absence of a mechanism to learn from and forget the model's incorrect replies.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252716/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes open-domain text questions (e.g., from TriviaQA) and produces either a factual text answer or an explicit 'I don't know' refusal. During training data construction, each question is sampled multiple times from the base LLM to estimate a confidence score, which determines which knowledge quadrant the question falls into. The contrastive training data consists of instruction-response triples (anchor, positive, negative) where the response field is either the golden answer, the model's own previously correct answer, the model's own previously incorrect answer, or the fixed string 'I don't know'.

## Method

The core contribution is an Adaptive Contrastive Learning strategy built on a three-threshold knowledge boundary (IK upper threshold, IDK lower threshold) that partitions questions into three quadrants: known-known, uncertain (unknown-known), and unknown-unknown. For each quadrant, the method constructs distinct positive and negative contrastive pairs tailored to the desired behavior—consolidating known knowledge (Q1: pull correct answers closer, push 'I don't know' and wrong answers away), reinforcing uncertain knowledge (Q4: pull golden answers closer, push wrong answers away), and encouraging honest refusal (Q3: pull 'I don't know' closer, push wrong answers away). The training objective combines a cosine-similarity-based contrastive loss with a standard cross-entropy generation loss, weighted adaptively via a detach mechanism that scales the contrastive term by the ratio of generation to contrastive loss (capped at λ). This is applied as instruction fine-tuning on LLaMA-2-7B-chat and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.1, shifting knowledge from the lower quadrants (model unaware of its knowledge state) to the upper quadrants (model aware), thereby improving both answer accuracy and honest refusal rates.

## Results

On TriviaQA with LLaMA-2-7B-chat, the proposed Adaptive Contrastive Learning (IDK-SFT-Adpt-Ctr) achieves a Truthful Rate of 78.2%, surpassing IDK-SFT (73.2%) by 5.0% and IDK-Prompting (66.9%) by 11.3%. On the out-of-distribution Natural Questions dataset, the method reaches 67.2% Truthful Rate with LLaMA-2-7B-chat and 77.0% with Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.1, outperforming the strongest baseline by 6.9% and 6.8% respectively. The method also notably recovers the IK-IK rate (e.g., 37.3% vs. 28.0% for SFT on TriviaQA/LLaMA), indicating it mitigates the knowledge loss that standard SFT introduces.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: IDK-Prompting (zero-shot instruction to refuse unknown questions) and IDK-SFT (standard supervised fine-tuning on known/unknown question pairs). The proposed Adaptive Contrastive Learning method achieves the highest Truthful Rate in all four model-dataset combinations. On TriviaQA with LLaMA-2-7B-chat, it improves Truthful Rate by 5.0% over IDK-SFT (78.2% vs. 73.2%) and 11.3% over IDK-Prompting (66.9%). On OOD Natural Questions with Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.1, the gain is 6.8% over IDK-SFT (77.0% vs. 70.2%) and 32.6% over IDK-Prompting (44.4%). A notable finding is that IDK-SFT often degrades the IK-IK rate (e.g., dropping from 37.4% to 28.0% on TriviaQA/LLaMA), indicating SFT causes the model to forget known knowledge; the adaptive contrastive loss recovers this (37.3%), suggesting the contrastive component specifically addresses knowledge retention that SFT alone cannot.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the knowledge-boundary delineation paradigm introduced by R-tuning (Zhang et al., 2024c) and Cheng et al. (2024), which used a binary known/unknown split to fine-tune LLMs to say 'I don't know.' The key departure is a three-tier boundary (IK and IDK thresholds) that partitions knowledge into four quadrants, enabling quadrant-specific contrastive learning. The contrastive loss mechanism and hidden-state extraction are directly adapted from Contrastive Instruction Tuning (Yan et al., 2024), but the adaptive construction of positive/negative samples per quadrant—particularly the use of the model's own incorrect responses as negative instances—is novel relative to prior IDK fine-tuning work that only leveraged correct answers and refusals.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The three-tier knowledge boundary offers a more nuanced alternative to the binary known/unknown split, and the ablation (Table 3) confirms that all three quadrant-specific losses contribute meaningfully, suggesting that treating 'uncertain knowledge' as a distinct category from 'unknown knowledge' is beneficial. However, the method requires multiple sampling passes to estimate per-question confidence, which is computationally expensive, and the IK/IDK thresholds are sensitive hyperparameters (Table 2 shows a 4.6-point Truthful Rate swing between IDK=0.5 and 0.9). Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond open-domain QA to more complex reasoning tasks, whether it scales to larger models (only 7B/13B tested), and how the contrastive objective interacts with RAG pipelines in deployment settings where retrieved context shifts the effective knowledge boundary.
