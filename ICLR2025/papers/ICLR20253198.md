## Problem

Prior automated cuneiform sign recognition treats signs as categorical labels, ignoring the fine-grained internal stroke configurations that vary across eras, regions, and scribes. This limits paleographic analysis and hampers recognition of rare sign variants. The paper addresses this by recovering the per-stroke structure of photographed cuneiform signs without any labeled examples of real signs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253198/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 512×512 RGB prototype font image (rendered from Santakku/SantakkuM fonts) with a manually annotated skeleton of 4 keypoints per stroke (3 triangular head corners + 1 tail), paired with a 512×512 RGB photograph of a real cuneiform sign. It outputs per-stroke projective transformations that reposition each stroke's keypoints onto the target image, producing a pixel-aligned skeleton overlay usable for hand-copy generation or as structural conditioning for a ControlNet-based synthetic data generator.

## Method

ProtoSnap is a three-stage unsupervised pipeline. First, it computes a 4D similarity volume from DIFT features extracted by a Stable Diffusion model fine-tuned on cuneiform scans (SD-#), capturing semantically-adapted pairwise patch similarities. Second, it identifies best-buddies correspondences (mutual nearest neighbors in the similarity volume) and fits a global affine transformation via RANSAC with a convex-hull spread prior to ensure inliers cover both images. Third, it performs local refinement by optimizing per-stroke projective transformations (initialized as identity) under a composite loss combining feature similarity (temperature-weighted softmax over the similarity volume), a saliency map loss encouraging strokes to land on salient regions, and L1/out-of-bounds regularization. The key insight is that the fine-tuned diffusion model's features encode cuneiform-specific geometry, and that pairwise region similarities simultaneously encode global alignment and per-stroke deviations from the canonical prototype.

## Results

On the expert-annotated alignment benchmark (272 images, 25 sign types), ProtoSnap achieves F1@20 of 27.14%, F1@30 of 42.09%, and F1@40 of 52.43%, substantially outperforming the strongest generic correspondence baseline (DINOv2+RANSAC at 16.12% F1@20). The local refinement stage adds ~6 points over global alignment alone (27.14% vs 21.31% F1@20). For the downstream OCR task, conditioning ControlNet on ProtoSnap-aligned skeletons (CN-#) boosts sign classification accuracy to 64.14% (all signs) and 53.17% (rare signs), compared to 58.43% and 25.84% for the real-data-only baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ProtoSnap decisively outperforms all four generic correspondence baselines on the alignment task. Against the traditional SIFT+RANSAC baseline, ProtoSnap achieves over 10× higher F1@20 (27.14% vs 2.56%), reflecting the inadequacy of hand-crafted features for textured clay surfaces. Among deep feature methods, DINOv2+RANSAC (16.12% F1@20) and DIFT (16.14% F1@20) are the strongest baselines, but ProtoSnap still leads by ~11 points at F1@20 and ~14 points at F1@40, demonstrating the benefit of explicitly modeling per-stroke structure rather than treating the sign as a flat image. Notably, adding RANSAC to DIFT actually hurts performance (13.13% vs 16.14% F1@20), suggesting that the diffusion features already encode strong geometric priors that RANSAC's outlier rejection disrupts. For the downstream OCR task, structurally-conditioned generation (CN-#) outperforms both the real-data-only baseline (64.14% vs 58.43% accuracy) and text-conditional generation (SD-#, 61.56%), with the largest gap on rare signs (53.17% vs 25.84% vs 38.56%), confirming that skeleton conditioning enables correct generation of infrequent sign variants.

## Relation to Prior Work

ProtoSnap builds on two key threads: diffusion-based correspondence (DIFT, Tang et al. 2023) for computing semantically meaningful feature similarities, and best-buddies mutual nearest-neighbor matching (Oron et al. 2017) for robust global alignment. It departs from prior cuneiform work (Dencker et al. 2020, Stötzner et al. 2023a) that treats signs as flat categories by explicitly modeling per-stroke internal structure via skeleton-based templates, and from generic template matching or pose estimation by using a variable graph whose topology depends on the sign type. The downstream OCR application extends ControlNet (Zhang et al. 2023) from text-prompt conditioning to skeleton-structure conditioning, a novel use for synthetic data generation in a domain with extreme class imbalance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first unsupervised method to recover stroke-level internal structure of photographed cuneiform signs, providing a new expert-annotated benchmark and demonstrating that structural conditioning for synthetic data generation substantially improves OCR on rare signs (53% vs 39% accuracy), a setting where text-conditional generation fails. Limitations include dependence on a canonical prototype (failing on structurally different variants), sensitivity to extreme deformations and poor scan quality, and the absence of a confidence mechanism for detecting failure cases. Open questions include extending to full tablet lines, 3D scans, and other ancient scripts with different geometric primitives, as well as developing confidence measures and handling the full diversity of allographs across eras and regions.
