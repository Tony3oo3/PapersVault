## Problem

In prediction-driven resource allocation, planners face a tension between waiting to gather more observations for better predictions and intervening early before high-risk individuals fail out of the population. This paper shows that, counterintuitively, individual prediction accuracy can improve over time while average ranking loss worsens, because the most vulnerable individuals drop out first, leaving a harder-to-rank population. Inequality in failure probabilities is identified as the driving factor behind this phenomenon, challenging the conventional assumption that more data always leads to better allocations.

## Input / Output

The planner receives binary observations o_i^t ~ Ber(tilde(p_i)) from each active individual at every time step, where tilde(p) is an increasing function of the true failure probability p. The method takes a budget B, horizon T, and a prior distribution over p (e.g., Beta(α,β)), and produces: (1) a necessary condition (Theorem 3.1) for when ranking risk improves, expressed as an inequality between a variance-driven change-in-population term and a 1/t observation-gain term; (2) an upper bound t* on optimal one-time allocation time that depends on ln(N/B) and the G-decaying parameter of the prior; and (3) a complete over-time allocation policy specified by a threshold sequence q(·) and a critical time t̂, found in O(T³·2^T) steps independent of N and B.

## Method

The core analysis decomposes the step-change in pairwise ranking risk into two counteracting effects: a gain from additional observations (scaling as 1/t) and a change-in-population effect (scaling with Var[p]/(1−E[p])²), showing the latter dominates for large t when inequality is high. For one-time allocation, the paper derives an explicit upper bound t* = T/2 + (G/4 + (γ+γ⁻¹)/4 + 1)·((γ+1)ln(N/B) + 1) under fully effective treatments with a G-decaying prior and concave observation model tilde(p)=1−(1−p)^γ, revealing that higher inequality (smaller G) and larger budget both push t* earlier. For over-time allocation, the problem is formulated as an MDP over states N_k^t (counts of individuals with k positive observations), and Theorem 5.1 characterizes the optimal policy as following a threshold structure with a single critical time t̂, reducing the search space from C(B+1, T−1) rollouts to O(T³·2^T) candidate policies. Algorithm 1 exploits this structure to simulate trajectories via a backup formula updating N_k^t from N_k^{t−1} and N_{k−1}^{t−1}, computing posterior expectations in closed form under the Beta-Binomial conjugate model, and selecting the optimal (t̂, q(·), ρ) triple without iterating over individuals.

## Results

The paper's central theoretical result (Theorem 3.1) shows that ranking risk can only improve from time t to t+1 if Var^t[p]/(1−E^t[p])² − O(1/√t) < C_approx/t, meaning the change-in-population effect (driven by inequality) must be dominated by the gain from additional observations. For one-time allocation with fully effective treatments (Theorem 4.3), the optimal allocation time is bounded above by t* = T/2 + (G/4 + (γ+γ⁻¹)/4 + 1)·((γ+1)ln(N/B) + 1), which decreases with higher inequality (smaller G) and larger budget. In semi-synthetic experiments calibrated to NELS 1988 data (estimated prior Beta(0.028, 0.35), ~7% dropout rate), increasing B/N from 5% to 20% and increasing inequality (Beta(0.4,1) → Beta(0.1,1)) both shift the optimal over-time allocation toward earlier time steps. The proposed Algorithm 1 finds the optimal over-time allocation in O(T³·2^T) steps, with runtime independent of the number of individuals N and budget B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is a theoretical contribution that does not compare its method against competing baselines in the standard empirical sense. Rather than proposing a new algorithm to outperform existing ones, it identifies a previously overlooked phenomenon: that waiting for more accurate predictions can paradoxically worsen ranking quality and reduce social welfare, particularly in high-inequality settings. The semi-synthetic experiments in Section 5.3 serve as illustrations of the theoretical predictions (budget and inequality effects on optimal timing) rather than as comparisons against prior methods. The closest related work discussed is the one-time allocation analysis in Shirali et al. (2024a) on inequality and allocation, and the dynamic subsidy allocation model of Abebe et al. (2020), but the paper positions its contribution as adding a timing dimension to these frameworks rather than benchmarking against them. The key takeaway is that the conventional wisdom of 'wait for more data before acting' is challenged: in settings with significant inequality or large budgets, early intervention with noisier predictions yields higher social welfare.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the prediction-policy-problems framework (Kleinberg et al., 2015) and the growing literature on prediction-driven resource allocation (Perdomo et al., 2023; Mashiat et al., 2024) by introducing an explicit temporal dimension: the question of *when* to allocate, not just *whom* to allocate to. It draws on the dynamic subsidy-allocation model of Abebe et al. (2020) and the rotting-bandit structure of Levine et al. (2017), but departs from both by assuming a partial-information setting where the planner must balance Bayesian ranking improvement against population attrition. The G-decaying distribution as a formal inequality measure and the MDP characterization yielding an allocation algorithm with runtime independent of N and B are novel contributions not present in prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the near-universal assumption in algorithmic policy design that gathering more observations before acting is always beneficial, showing that in high-inequality populations the ranking loss and allocation welfare can actually degrade over time. This has direct implications for eviction prevention, dropout intervention, and healthcare targeting, where the 'window of opportunity' is finite. Key limitations include the assumption of time-invariant failure probabilities, no spillover effects, and a stylized Bernoulli observation model; open questions include extending the framework to fairness-constrained settings, heterogeneous intervention costs, and populations where vulnerability itself evolves endogenously.
