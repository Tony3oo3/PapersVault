## Problem

Estimating the average treatment effect (ATE) with valid confidence intervals from multiple observational datasets collected at different hospitals or countries remains an open problem. Existing multi-dataset ATE methods provide only point estimates without uncertainty quantification, while naive approaches—pooling datasets or using only the small unconfounded dataset—yield either biased or overly conservative CIs. The paper addresses how to combine a small unconfounded dataset with a large potentially confounded one to obtain both unbiased ATE estimates and tighter, asymptotically valid CIs.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two tabular datasets: a small observational dataset D¹ (n samples with covariates, binary treatments, and continuous outcomes, assumed unconfounded) and a large observational dataset D² (N′ samples, allowing unobserved confounders), along with a significance level α. The propensity scores may differ across datasets, reflecting different treatment guidelines. The output is a prediction-powered ATE point estimate and an asymptotically valid confidence interval whose width shrinks with the size of D² and the accuracy of the CATE model trained on it. An extension handles RCT+observational settings where the propensity score in D¹ is known, replacing the AIPW influence function with a simpler IPW estimator.

## Method

The core contribution is a tailored prediction-powered inference (PPI) framework for ATE estimation that decomposes the problem into a measure of fit and a rectifier. The measure of fit is a CATE estimator (e.g., DR-learner) trained via sample splitting on D², yielding a potentially biased but low-variance prediction. The rectifier is the average difference between non-centered AIPW influence function scores computed on D¹ (using cross-fitted nuisance functions) and the CATE predictions evaluated at D¹ covariates, which corrects for confounding bias in D². The final PPI estimate is the sum of the two, and the CI width is governed by the combined variance of the rectifier (scaled by 1/n) and the CATE predictions (scaled by 1/N), so the large dataset primarily reduces variance while the small dataset anchors unbiasedness. The authors prove asymptotic normality of the rectifier (Lemma 4.1) and asymptotic CI validity (Theorem 4.2) under standard rate conditions on nuisance estimation (α_μ + α_π ≥ 1/2), and extend the framework to RCT+observational settings via an IPW-based rectifier (Theorem 5.2).

## Results

On MIMIC-III, the proposed PPI method achieves an ATE RMSE of 0.057 with a CI width of 0.023, compared to 0.077 for the naive AIPW (D¹ only) baseline — a ~3.5× reduction in CI width. On the Brazilian COVID-19 dataset split by region, the method achieves RMSE 7.131 and CI width 2.341 versus 8.479 for AIPW (D¹ only). On synthetic data across three confounding scenarios, the PPI CIs are narrower by 49.99% (little confounding), 55.37% (medium), and 55.35% (heavy) relative to the naive baseline, while maintaining faithful coverage of the oracle ATE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three baselines: (1) AIPW on D¹ only (naive, unbiased but wide CIs), (2) AIPW on D² only (biased due to unobserved confounding, hence not a valid baseline), and (3) ATMLE from van der Laan et al. (2024) in the RCT+observational setting. The proposed PPI method consistently produces the narrowest *valid* CIs: on MIMIC-III it reduces CI width from 0.077 to 0.023 (~3.5×), and on COVID-19 by region from 8.479 to 2.341 (~3.6×), while matching or improving RMSE. AIPW (D² only) yields artificially narrow CIs (e.g., 0.003 on MIMIC-III) but is unfaithful — it fails to cover the oracle ATE, especially under heavy confounding. In the RCT+observational experiments, ATMLE is faithful under little/medium confounding but fails under heavy confounding (Scenario 3) and is numerically unstable due to singular matrix inversions, whereas the PPI method remains stable and produces consistently narrower CIs across all scenarios.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the prediction-powered inference (PPI) framework of Angelopoulos et al. (2023), previously applied to classical statistical quantities (mean, median, quantile), to the causal inference task of ATE estimation from multiple datasets. It builds on the semiparametric theory of Wager (2024) for AIPW and CATE estimation, using influence function scores as the basis for a carefully derived rectifier. The paper departs from prior multi-dataset ATE work (Kallus et al., Yang & Ding, Guo et al., Hatt et al., Demirel et al.), which provided only point estimates without uncertainty quantification, and from van der Laan et al. (2024), which handles RCT+observational data via TMLE but suffers from numerical instabilities and requires identical estimators across datasets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Valid confidence intervals are essential for reliable medical decision-making, yet prior methods for combining multiple observational datasets could not provide them. The method's practical impact is demonstrated by a ~3.5× CI width reduction on real medical data (MIMIC-III, Brazilian COVID-19) while maintaining asymptotic validity. Key limitations include the assumption of identical marginal covariate distributions across datasets, the restriction to ATE (not CATE), and the requirement of unconfoundedness in the small dataset. Open questions include extending the framework to heterogeneous treatment effects, causal survival analysis, and integration with pre-trained language models or tailored neural architectures for text-based representations.
