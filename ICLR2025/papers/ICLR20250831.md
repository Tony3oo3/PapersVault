## Problem

Existing LLM evaluation relies either on closed-environment benchmarks (MMLU, GLUE) that suffer from data leakage and fail to capture open-ended human preference, or on crowdsourced human rating platforms (Chatbot Arena, MT-Bench) that are slow, costly, and face cold-start problems for new models. The paper asks whether a fully unsupervised evaluation system can be built where LLMs themselves serve as mutual reviewers, producing rankings that align with human preferences without any human annotation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250831/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an unlabeled set of open-ended questions Q and a pool of m LLMs (mix of open-source and closed-source, e.g., GPT-3.5-Turbo, Vicuna-13B, LLaMA-13B) as input. Each LLM answers every question, and pairwise anonymous answer battles are judged by randomly selected peer LLMs, producing quadruples (answer_j, answer_k, partial-order, confidence_weight). The output is a re-ranked LLM ability ordering R̂ plus a per-model learnable confidence weight w ∈ (0,1], validated against ground-truth human rankings from crowdsourcing platforms using Spearman's S, Kendall's τ, and Permutation Entropy.

## Method

PiCO operates in two stages. In the peer-review stage, all LLMs answer unlabeled questions, responses are shuffled into anonymous pairs, and randomly selected peer LLMs judge each pair, assigning a learnable confidence weight w to each reviewer. In the consistency-optimization stage, the method maximizes the Pearson correlation between each model's confidence weight w and its aggregate score G (computed via weighted Elo or Rank scoring over all pairwise judgments), formalizing the assumption that stronger models both produce better answers and evaluate others more accurately. An unsupervised reviewer-elimination mechanism iteratively removes the lowest-scoring LLMs from the reviewer queue until 60% are eliminated, reducing evaluation noise. The entire pipeline requires no human annotations, and the learned weights are shown to reduce preference-gap bias (e.g., self-favoring tendencies of weaker models) while converging stably across 1000 random seeds.

## Results

On the full-data setting (ratio=1), PiCO achieves Spearman S = 0.90 / 0.89 / 0.84, Kendall τ = 0.77 / 0.72 / 0.68, and Permutation Entropy H = 0.94 / 1.01 / 1.17 on Chatbot Arena, MT-Bench, and AlpacaEval respectively, outperforming all baselines including Claude-3 (API). The paper reports average improvements of 0.027 (Spearman), 0.047 (Kendall), and 0.14 (Permutation Entropy) over the runner-up method. PiCO also substantially outperforms standard benchmarks: MMLU achieves only S=0.53 and τ=0.37, GSM8K only S=0.32 and τ=0.15, versus PiCO's S=0.88 and τ=0.67.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PiCO outperforms all eight baselines across the three primary datasets. Against the wisdom-of-crowds baselines, PiCO improves Spearman by 0.14 over Majority Voting (0.90 vs 0.76) and 0.16 over Rating Voting (0.90 vs 0.74) on Chatbot Arena, with similar margins on other datasets. Single-model evaluators GPTScore and PandaLM perform poorly in the open-ended setting (Spearman as low as −0.09 for flan-t5-xxl), while PiCO reaches 0.90. Among the strongest multi-model baselines, PiCO edges out PRD (S=0.90 vs 0.84 on Chatbot Arena; S=0.89 vs 0.86 on MT-Bench) and PRE (S=0.90 vs 0.86; τ=0.77 vs 0.71 on Chatbot Arena), and surpasses Claude-3 (API) on Kendall τ (0.77 vs 0.76) and Permutation Entropy (0.94 vs not reported but implied higher). On standard benchmarks (Table 4), PiCO (S=0.88, τ=0.67) dramatically outperforms MMLU (S=0.53, τ=0.37) and GSM8K (S=0.32, τ=0.15), confirming that domain-specific benchmarks fail to capture human-preference alignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the academic peer-review metaphor—where stronger scholars both produce better work and evaluate others more accurately—into a formal consistency optimization for LLM evaluation. It departs from supervised peer-review evaluators like PRE (which uses human-annotated qualification exams to assign weights) and tournament-style methods like PRD (which derives weights from win rates) by making the entire weighting process unsupervised, relying solely on a Pearson correlation objective between learned capability weights and aggregate scores. The multi-model aggregation draws on the wisdom-of-crowds tradition (majority/rating voting) and LLM-as-judge frameworks (GPTScore, PandaLM, ChatEval), but the constrained optimization over a shared confidence vector is the paper's distinguishing mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PiCO offers a practical, annotation-free pipeline for ranking LLMs that avoids the cold-start and cost problems of crowdsourced battle platforms, and its consistency assumption provides a theoretically grounded justification for weighting reviewers by ability. However, the evaluation still depends on human-annotated ground-truth rankings from Chatbot Arena, MT-Bench, and AlpacaEval to validate performance, so the method is unsupervised in training but not in validation. Open questions include scalability to pools of hundreds of models, robustness when the consistency assumption breaks down (e.g., a strong model with a systematic self-bias), and whether the simple Pearson objective can be replaced by richer consistency measures. The authors note plans to extend the framework to multi-modal models.
