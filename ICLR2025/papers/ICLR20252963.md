## Problem

When multiple pre-trained or non-trainable models (ML solvers, PDE solvers, legacy codes) predict the same quantity, practitioners lack a principled way to aggregate their outputs. The natural approach—minimizing empirical error of the aggregate (MEEA)—is shown to be pathological: it treats model predictions as interpolation features rather than weighting by accuracy, and can completely ignore a good model in favor of a bad one. The paper asks how to aggregate black-box models in a way that is robust to limited validation data and genuinely leverages each model's strengths.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input the predictions of n black-box models evaluated at a set of data points, together with ground-truth values Y for training the aggregator. In the PDE setting, the input is the source term f plus the n model solutions on a spatial grid (e.g., 100×100), and the aggregator is a Fourier Neural Operator that maps (f, M_1(f), …, M_n(f)) to log-eigenvalue logits λ. The output is a pointwise convex combination α(x) = Softmax(−λ(x)) and the aggregated prediction M_A(x) = α(x)ᵀ M(x), where the coefficients sum to one by construction.

## Method

The core contribution is reframing aggregation as variance minimization rather than error minimization. Under the assumption that models are unbiased estimators of Y, the optimal pointwise linear aggregate with weights summing to one is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimate (BLUE), whose weights are determined by the inverse of the error covariance matrix A(x). To make this data-driven, A(x) is parameterized as Pᵀ Diag(exp(λ_1(x), …, exp(λ_n(x)))) P, and the log-eigenvalues λ_i are learned by regressing the log of squared per-model errors: min Σ (l_k(X^i) − log[(Y(X^i) − M_k(X^i))²])² + a‖l‖²_H. This yields α(x) = Softmax(−λ(x)), rediscovering the softmax structure of Mixture-of-Experts from a statistical estimation principle. A key theorem proves that the empirical MEVA loss converges at O(1/N) while MEEA converges only at O(1/√N), explaining MEVA's superior robustness in the scarce-data regime typical of model aggregation. For PDE problems, the backbone H is instantiated as a Fourier Neural Operator, and a log-error loss (equation 17) is used to prevent the aggregator from being dominated by easy samples.

## Results

On the Laplace equation, the MEVA aggregate achieves a geometric mean log₁₀ MSE of −6.282, outperforming the best individual solver (FDM at −5.523) by nearly one order of magnitude. On Burger's equation, the aggregate reaches −3.106 versus the best individual solver (Riemann at −2.734), while successfully avoiding divergent solvers (Explicit, Implicit, Lax-Wendroff all at ≈−0.455). On the Boston Housing dataset, MEVA with a Matérn kernel improves upon Gradient Boosting by 7% in MSE and upon the best individual model by 2%, while MEEA with a DNN performs worse than the two best aggregated models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between MEVA (variance minimization) and MEEA (direct error minimization) under identical architectures. On Boston Housing, MEEA with a DNN performs significantly worse than MEVA with the same DNN, and even worse than the two best individual models being aggregated. On the Laplace equation, an FNO trained with a direct MSE loss (analogous to MEEA) collapses to a constant weight vector selecting only the best model, whereas MEVA with the log-error loss (17) produces spatially varying coefficients that adaptively combine solvers. Against simple averaging, MEVA substantially outperforms the mean on both PDE problems (the mean is worse than all individual models on Laplace due to the GP solver's failure regime). Against individual solvers, the aggregate consistently achieves the best or near-best performance on every test sample, with the geometric mean improvement ranging from ~0.37 in log₁₀ MSE (Burger's) to ~0.76 (Laplace, nearly one order of magnitude).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the pointwise linear aggregation literature (Liu et al. 2016, Viana et al. 2009, Lee & Choi 2014) that uses heuristics like cross-validation or KNN to weight model predictions, but departs fundamentally by formulating aggregation as a variance minimization problem grounded in a probabilistic error model (unbiased estimators with known covariance structure). It explicitly identifies and rules out the Minimal Empirical Error Aggregation (MEEA) approach—directly minimizing aggregate prediction error—as provably less robust, replacing it with the Minimal Variance Aggregate (MVA) derived from the BLUE framework. The work also extends model aggregation beyond ML ensembles into operator learning, becoming the first to aggregate outputs from heterogeneous sources including classical PDE solvers (finite difference, spectral, GP-based) alongside learned models, using FNO as the backbone for learning aggregation coefficients.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, non-intrusive method for combining pre-existing black-box models without retraining them, which is critical in scientific computing where legacy solvers and specialized ML models coexist with incompatible interfaces. The theorem establishing that MEVA converges at O(1/N) versus MEEA's O(1/√N) gives a clear theoretical justification for variance-based aggregation in data-scarce regimes, which is the natural setting for PDE validation. Key limitations include the requirement for held-out data to estimate model errors (reducing training data), the assumption of unbiased models (bias correction was attempted but did not improve results), and the fact that no guarantee exists that the aggregate will outperform individual models. Open questions include scaling to highly correlated model errors, extending to constrained aggregation (e.g., physics-informed constraints), and whether the diagonal covariance assumption (P=I) is sufficient for complex multi-physics problems.
