## Problem

Existing analyses of LLM finetuning focus on training targets, end-state properties, or RL equivalences, but lack a unified dynamical explanation for how predictions evolve step-by-step during SFT and preference tuning. This leaves counter-intuitive phenomena unexplained: why SFT amplifies certain hallucinations (e.g., answering question A with content from question B), why off-policy DPO causes confidence decay on nearly all responses including the desired one, and why on-policy variants consistently outperform off-policy DPO.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a pretrained LLM (Pythia 410M–2.8B or Qwen1.5 0.5B/1.8B) and a preference dataset of (prompt, y⁺, y⁻) triples from Anthropic-HH or UltraFeedback, and produces both a finetuned aligned model and a per-step decomposition of prediction changes into three interpretable terms. A probing dataset of 500 examples with multiple response types (rephrases, cross-question responses, random permutations, greedy-decoded sequences) enables fine-grained tracking of how log-likelihoods on different response categories evolve every 25 updates, revealing the full geometry of the V×L prediction space rather than just the supervisory signal.

## Method

The paper extends the eNTK-based learning dynamics decomposition (Proposition 1) to the autoregressive LLM setting by merging the causal-masked sequence factorization into the backbone h_θ, yielding a per-token decomposition where the change in log-probability on an observing response is a sum over updating tokens of A·K·G terms. The key insight is identifying the 'squeezing effect': when a large negative gradient (as in DPO's G_DPO⁻) is imposed on an already-unlikely prediction under a softmax head, the decreased probability mass is disproportionately funneled into the single most-confident token, causing all other predictions to decay and the distribution to become peakier. This explains why off-policy DPO (where y⁻ sits in a low-probability valley) degrades alignment, while on-policy methods and variants like SPPO (which replace the negative gradient with two positive pressures) avoid the effect. The paper then proposes a simple 'extend' method—SFT on both [x; y⁺] and [x; y⁻] before running DPO—which raises the baseline probability of y⁻ so the subsequent negative gradient no longer triggers severe squeezing, yielding measurable alignment gains (up to 69% win-rate vs. baseline at 4 DPO epochs).

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative result is the win-rate of the proposed 'extend' method (SFT on both y⁺ and y⁻, then DPO) against the standard baseline (SFT on y⁺ only, then DPO), judged by ChatGPT and Claude. Before DPO, the extend method is slightly worse (win-rate 0.47), but after 4 DPO epochs it achieves a 0.69 win-rate against ChatGPT and 0.60 against Claude, peaking at epoch 4. The paper also provides extensive qualitative evidence: during off-policy DPO, the log-likelihood of the greedy-decoding response increases from −113 to −63 within 8 epochs (faster than SFT's −130 to −90), confirming the squeezing effect, while the extend method shows significantly slower decay of all non-target responses during DPO.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed 'extend' training pipeline (SFT on both preferred and rejected responses, followed by standard DPO) against the standard baseline (SFT on preferred responses only, followed by DPO) on Anthropic-HH and UltraFeedback datasets using Pythia and Qwen1.5 models. Before any DPO training, the extend method is slightly worse than baseline (win-rate ≈ 0.47), as expected since it is explicitly trained on lower-quality y⁻ responses. However, after 2–4 DPO epochs, the extend method clearly outperforms the baseline, reaching 0.69 win-rate (ChatGPT) and 0.60 (Claude) at epoch 4, because the pre-training on y⁻ makes the rejected responses less unlikely, thereby mitigating the squeezing effect that otherwise causes all response confidences to collapse during DPO. Performance peaks at 4 DPO epochs and slightly degrades by epoch 6, suggesting diminishing returns. The paper does not compare against other DPO variants (IPO, SPPO, SLiC, etc.) quantitatively, but provides a unified analytical framework explaining why those variants (which constrain the negative gradient or use on-policy sampling) partially mitigate the same squeezing effect.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the learning dynamics framework from Ren et al. (2022), which decomposed per-step prediction changes into A·K·G terms for standard supervised learning, to the autoregressive LLM finetuning setting by absorbing the causal mask into the backbone and treating each token position as a multi-label classification column. It then applies this unified decomposition across multiple finetuning algorithms (SFT, DPO, IPO, SLiC, SPPO, SPIN), positioning itself against the dominant prior analyses of LLM alignment that reason from training targets, final-state properties, or RL connections. The key conceptual departure is identifying the 'squeezing effect'—a consequence of negative gradients on softmax outputs—as a unifying mechanism explaining hallucination amplification, the repeater phenomenon, and the confidence decay observed in off-policy DPO, rather than treating these as separate issues.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The squeezing effect provides a mechanistic, first-principles explanation for why off-policy DPO degrades model confidence on all responses and why on-policy variants perform better, connecting previously disparate observations (repeater phenomenon, hallucination, likelihood displacement) under one framework. The proposed 'extend' mitigation (SFT on both y+ and y- before DPO) is simple and counter-intuitive, though its validation is limited to small models (410M–2.8B) and modest win-rate gains (~0.65–0.69). Open questions include whether the analysis extends to token-level RL methods (PPO, RLHF) where teacher forcing does not apply, whether the squeezing effect persists at larger scales, and the observed 'fingerprint' similarity among ChatGPT-generated responses that the authors flag for future work.
