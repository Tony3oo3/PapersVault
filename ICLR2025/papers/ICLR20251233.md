## Problem

Neural networks suffer from loss of plasticity in continual learning, where trainability degrades as tasks arrive because the solution of one task becomes a poor initialization for the next. The paper identifies that the maximum singular value of each layer's weight matrix grows over training, reducing effective gradient diversity and impeding learning on new tasks. Existing mitigators (L2 regularization, weight reinitialization) either over-constrain parameters or are highly sensitive to hyperparameters, failing to directly target the spectral properties that underlie trainability.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard neural network weight matrices (including biases and normalization layer parameters) and training data from sequential tasks, with no privileged task-boundary information required. The spectral norm of each parameter matrix is computed efficiently via a single power iteration per layer, adding linear computational overhead in the number of parameters. The output is a modified training objective that augments the standard task loss with a per-layer spectral penalty, producing regularized parameters whose maximum singular values remain near one throughout continual training.

## Method

The proposed spectral regularizer penalizes deviation of each layer's maximum singular value from one for multiplicative weight matrices and from zero for additive bias vectors, formulated as R = Σ_l ((σ₁(W_l)^k − 1)² + ||b_l||₂^(2k)) with k=2. Two key design choices distinguish it from prior spectral-norm regularization: (i) weights are regularized toward σ₁=1 rather than σ₁=0, avoiding the collapse-to-zero pathology of L2, and (ii) the penalty is applied to every parameter including biases and normalization scaling factors, since all experience norm growth. The theoretical justification links growing spectral norms to reduced effective gradient diversity: the rank of the per-example gradient matrix is upper-bounded by d·rank(θ_{l+1}), so spectral norm growth concentrates gradients in few directions, slowing learning on new tasks. The regularizer is data-independent, requiring no access to task boundaries or stored data, and is shown to be far less sensitive to its strength hyperparameter than L2-based alternatives across architectures, non-stationarity types, and training budgets.

## Results

On tiny-ImageNet with ResNet-18, spectral regularization achieves 24.47% test accuracy on class-incremental learning (vs. 23.13% for the best baseline L2-init), 25.94% on label flipping, and 11.04% on pixel permutation. Across all 12 problem settings (2 architectures × 3 non-stationarities × 2 datasets), spectral regularization is the best-performing method in 10 out of 12 and is always in the top 2. On the Vision Transformer, the advantage is more pronounced in class-incremental learning (24.52% vs. 18.90% for L2-init). In reinforcement learning (DMC with SAC), spectral regularization is competitive with full network resets + LayerNorm while being less sensitive to its hyperparameter.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Spectral regularization outperforms L2-toward-initialization (Kumar et al., 2023) on class-incremental (24.47% vs. 23.13%) and pixel permutation (11.04% vs. 9.26%) with ResNet, and by a wider margin on Vision Transformer class-incremental (24.52% vs. 18.90%). It beats L2-toward-zero, ReDO (Sokar et al., 2023), S-EWC (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017), and the unregularized baseline across all three non-stationarities. The one notable loss is on label flipping with ResNet, where Wasserstein regularization (Lewandowski et al., 2023) achieves 27.26% vs. spectral's 25.94%. Shrink-and-perturb (Ash and Adams, 2020) is highly variable, sometimes competitive on pixel permutation (10.85%) but poor on class-incremental (17.78%). In the RL setting (DMC/SAC), spectral regularization matches or exceeds the strongest baseline (full network resets + LayerNorm, Ball et al., 2023) in aggregate return while being substantially more sample-efficient in 5 of 7 environments and less sensitive to its regularization strength than resets are to reset frequency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends spectral norm regularization (Yoshida and Miyato, 2017) from a generalization tool in single-task learning to a continual learning regularizer, with two critical modifications: regularizing the spectral norm towards 1 (matching initialization) rather than 0, and applying it to all parameters including biases and normalization layers. It departs from the dominant L2-based regularizers in the loss-of-plasticity literature (regenerative regularization, L2 towards zero) by targeting the specific spectral property—maximum singular value—that the authors identify as governing gradient diversity and trainability, rather than constraining the full parameter vector. The theoretical framing connects to dynamical isometry and the observation that parameter norms grow at rate √t during training, positioning spectral regularization as a data-independent, initialization-preserving constraint distinct from both explicit weight reinitialization and spectral normalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Spectral regularization offers a simple, data-independent, and hyperparameter-robust mechanism for sustaining trainability in continual learning, which is practically valuable given that hyperparameter tuning is infeasible when the task distribution is unknown in advance. The method's applicability across architectures (ResNet-18, ViT), non-stationarity types (random labels, pixel permutation, label flipping, class-incremental), and even RL (SAC on DMC) suggests broad utility. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very large models (e.g., billion-parameter transformers), whether the fixed exponent k=2 is optimal across all settings, and how it interacts with other emerging plasticity-preserving techniques like continual backprop or hare-and-tortoise networks. The paper does not fully explore whether spectral regularization can replace more complex architectural modifications or whether it degrades in extremely long-horizon continual learning beyond the 100-task regime tested.
