## Problem

Previous context-based offline meta-RL (COMRL) works alternate between maximizing I(Z;M) for the context encoder and standard offline RL for the policy, but the theoretical justification for monotonic performance improvement remains underexplored. The paper identifies that the existing optimization framework ignores the variation of the task representation between encoder updates, which can violate the monotonicity condition and impair the optimization process. They name this issue 'task representation shift' and show it is a critical, previously overlooked component of the improvement guarantee.

## Input / Output

The context encoder consumes a single trajectory (one full episode) from the offline dataset as context and produces a 5-dimensional task representation vector. The policy then conditions on both the current state and this task representation to output an action. The key operational distinction from prior COMRL is that the encoder update schedule is explicitly controlled via two parameters—N_k (update encoder every n policy steps) and N_acc (perform n encoder updates when triggered)—rather than being fixed to exactly one encoder update per alternating cycle.

## Method

The paper extends the return discrepancy scheme to COMRL, deriving a return bound (Theorem 4.3) that formally links I(Z;M) maximization to the expected return objective and explains why prior alternating training works. A performance difference bound (Theorem 4.6) then reveals an additional term |Z(φ₂) − Z(φ₁)|—the task representation shift—that the prior monotonicity condition (Corollary 4.4) fails to account for, meaning small policy improvements can be offset by large encoder updates. Theorem 4.10 proves monotonic improvement is achievable by bounding the encoder update magnitude, yielding a sample complexity k that shrinks as accumulated policy improvement ε₁₂* grows. Practically, this is instantiated by adjusting N_k and N_acc to delay or batch encoder updates, tested across three encoder objectives (contrastive-based upper bound, reconstruction-based lower bound, and a novel cross-entropy-based direct approximation of I(Z;M)) with BRAC as the policy algorithm on MuJoCo and MetaWorld benchmarks.

## Results

On Ant-Dir (medium-quality data), the proposed setting N_k=2, N_acc=1 (update the context encoder every 2 policy updates, once per update) achieves 291.29±15.69 return with the cross-entropy objective, 268.23±19.74 with contrastive, and 244.52±16.20 with reconstruction, all statistically significantly outperforming the original alternating framework (N_k=1, N_acc=1) which yields 253.83, 207.92, and 213.78 respectively. Across all six benchmarks (Reach, Dial-Turn, Button-Press, Push, Walker-Param, Ant-Dir) and three encoder objectives, the best setting consistently shows statistically significant improvements (p<0.05) over the worst setting, while the original N_k=1, N_acc=1 fails to deliver the best asymptotic performance in any benchmark. The paper further demonstrates that settings with N_k>1, N_acc=1 outperform N_k=1, N_acc>1 more frequently, and recommends prioritizing N_k adjustment for both performance and training efficiency.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external COMRL baselines; instead, it compares different settings for controlling task representation shift (parameterized by N_k: how often to update the encoder, and N_acc: how many encoder updates per trigger) applied to three encoder objectives (cross-entropy, contrastive, reconstruction) on top of the BRAC policy. The original alternating framework (N_k=1, N_acc=1) serves as the implicit baseline. The proposed N_k=2, N_acc=1 setting wins on Ant-Dir across all three objectives with large margins (e.g., +37.5 return for cross-entropy, +60.3 for contrastive, +30.7 for reconstruction), all with p<0.003. On other benchmarks, the winning setting varies (e.g., N_k=1, N_acc=3 wins for cross-entropy on Reach at 2856.35 vs. 2470.25 original; N_k=2, N_acc=1 wins for contrastive on Push at 1557.84 vs. 839.05 original), but the original setting never achieves the best performance. The key takeaway is that explicitly controlling the encoder update frequency (increasing N_k) is more effective and efficient than increasing the number of encoder updates per step (increasing N_acc), as the latter adds training time without consistent gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the return discrepancy scheme from model-based RL (Janner et al., 2019) to the context-based offline meta-RL (COMRL) setting, providing the first theoretical justification for why the standard alternating optimization of context encoder and policy in prior COMRL works (FOCAL, UNICORN, CORRO, CSRO) yields performance improvements. It then departs from this lineage by identifying a previously overlooked flaw: the monotonic improvement condition derived from the return bound ignores the variation of the task representation itself during alternating updates, which the authors term 'task representation shift.' The practical framework builds directly on FOCAL's contrastive objective, UNICORN's reconstruction objective, and BRAC for policy learning, with the key modification being controlled encoder update frequency (N_k) and accumulation (N_acc).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled theoretical lens for understanding when and why COMRL training improves, and identifies a concrete, actionable issue (task representation shift) that practitioners can address through simple scheduling adjustments. The finding that pre-training the context encoder does not help—and may actually hurt—challenges a natural intuition and suggests the alternating process itself is essential. Open questions include how to automatically determine the optimal update schedule (rather than hand-tuning N_k and N_acc), whether the Lipschitz assumption holds in practice for deep networks, and how to design policy learning algorithms that better exploit the optimal task representation distribution. The empirical gains, while consistent, are modest, suggesting the full benefit may require more sophisticated control mechanisms.
