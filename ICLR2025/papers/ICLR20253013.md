## Problem

The Korean NLP community lacks a publicly available, natively Korean hallucination benchmark for evaluating LLM faithfulness. Existing Korean hallucination datasets are closed (leaderboard-only scoring), restrict detailed analysis, and are based on translated English knowledge rather than native Korean textual sources, making them ill-suited for assessing hallucination detection in Korean.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253013/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each test sample provides a Korean text document from one of seven knowledge domains (Culture, Economy, History, International, Medical, Society, Technology), a publish date, an instruction specifying whether to select hallucinated or faithful statements, and five candidate statements. The model must output the indices of all correct statements in a multiple-answer format, where 40% of questions require selecting two or three correct answers. Evaluation is strict: missing even one correct answer or including an incorrect one yields zero credit, and the order of selected indices is irrelevant. For open-source models, scoring uses log-probability-based multiple-choice selection; for closed API models, direct generation of statement indices is used.

## Method

K-HALU is constructed from the publicly available Knowledge Graph-to-Text dataset (300,178 samples from Korean news, magazines, and books) hosted on AI-Hub. GPT-4 omni generates five hallucinated statements per document by modifying human-annotated statements to introduce factual errors, date misrepresentations relative to the publish date, or unverifiable content, and five faithful statements by paraphrasing or generating higher-level inferences that do not overlap with the originals. Quality control employs two mechanisms: rationalization (GPT-4 omni provides evidence for each generated statement) and ensemble verification using three GPT-4 variants as meta-evaluators, followed by human annotation of flagged samples (Krippendorff's alpha 0.923 among meta-evaluators, 0.828 among human annotators). The multiple-answer distribution follows a 60/30/10 ratio for one/two/three correct answers, and final options are selected to minimize token-length bias. The paper evaluates eight models (Llama2, Llama3, KULLM3, ExaOne, Mistral-Nemo, GPT-3.5 Turbo, GPT-4 Turbo, GPT-4 omni) using log-probability scoring, exact match, and LLM-as-a-Judge, finding that open-source models achieve under 35% accuracy and under 15% on hallucinated-statement selection, with a >27% gap versus closed API models.

## Results

On the K-HALU benchmark (2,170 multiple-answer Korean hallucination detection samples), open-source LLMs achieve an average accuracy of only 29.05%, with ExaOne performing best at 32.95% and Llama2 lowest at 24.75%. Closed API models substantially outperform open-source models, with GPT-4 omni reaching 78.66% accuracy, GPT-4 Turbo at 73.46%, and GPT-3.5 Turbo at 40.46%, yielding a performance gap of 27.91% between the two groups. Open-source models are particularly weak on hallucinated-statement selection, scoring under 15% average accuracy on that instruction type, while faithful-statement selection yields ~45–55% accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among open-source models, ExaOne (32.95% accuracy) leads, followed by Mistral-Nemo (30.14%), Llama3 (28.80%), KULLM3 (28.62%), and Llama2 (24.75%); the Korean-centric KULLM3 shows no meaningful advantage over general multilingual Llama3, suggesting limited instruction-tuning data constrains its hallucination detection. The gap between the best open-source model (ExaOne, 32.95%) and the weakest closed API model (GPT-3.5 Turbo, 40.46%) is 7.51 percentage points, while GPT-4 Turbo (73.46%) and GPT-4 omni (78.66%) exceed all open-source models by over 40 points. On hallucinated-statement selection specifically, all open-source models score below 15% (ExaOne 11.34%, Llama2 12.53%), whereas GPT-4 omni reaches 75.94% and GPT-4 Turbo 72.35%. Few-shot prompting and Chain-of-Thought reasoning yield at most ~1–2% improvement for open-source models, confirming that these prompting strategies are insufficient to close the hallucination detection gap. Closed API models exhibit higher recall than precision (e.g., GPT-3.5: recall 0.826 vs. precision 0.648), indicating they over-predict correct answers, while open-source models show the opposite pattern (higher precision than recall), reflecting a conservative selection strategy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the hallucination detection task—well established in English benchmarks such as FEVER, HaluEval, and FELM—into the Korean language with native knowledge sources, addressing a gap where existing Korean datasets (Ko-TruthfulQA, Ko-H5) are either closed or do not evaluate hallucinations. The multiple-answer evaluation format (40% of samples requiring 2–3 correct selections) deliberately generalizes the standard single-answer hallucination detection setup, drawing distributional design from MultiSpanQA and CLEAN. Dataset construction leverages GPT-4 omni for generating hallucinated and faithful statements with rationalization, followed by ensemble verification across three frontier models and human annotation, building on the LLM-as-a-Judge and self-consistency paradigms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

K-HALU fills a critical gap as the first publicly available Korean hallucination benchmark, enabling detailed analysis beyond leaderboard-style scoring that was previously impossible. The finding that open-source 7B–13B models score under 15% on hallucinated-statement selection (versus ~50% on faithful selection) and that few-shot/CoT prompting fails to close this gap suggests a fundamental asymmetry in how these models verify presence versus absence of information. Open questions include whether the 27%+ open-source-to-closed-API gap persists at larger scales, how to mitigate the instruction-tuning bias toward faithful statements, and whether the multi-answer format generalizes to other low-resource languages beyond the English/Malay translations tested here.
