## Problem

The prevailing view in AI safety holds that black swan events originate from dynamic, time-varying environments, yet the community lacks a comprehensive formal definition of what constitutes a black swan. This paper challenges that view by showing that high-risk, statistically rare events can also emerge in fully stationary environments due to human misperception of reward magnitudes and event likelihoods, a source of black swans that existing safe RL criteria (worst-case, risk-sensitive, constrained) do not address.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a stationary finite-horizon MDP (state space S, action space A, transition P, reward R, discount γ, horizon T), CPT distortion functions u and w (assumed, not learned), and a policy π as input. It produces three theoretical outputs: a binary classification of each state-action pair as S-BLACK SWAN or not (based on whether w⁻ collapses its cumulative probability to zero while the true probability is in (0, ε_bs) and u⁻ overestimates the loss by more than C_bs), a closed-form lower bound Ω((R_max − R_bs)ε_bs^min − R_bs ε_bs)(R_max − R_bs)C_bs / R_max² on the value perception gap, and a hitting-time bound t ≥ log(δ/p_min)/log(1−p_max)+1 for encountering an S-BLACK SWAN with probability at least δ. Notably, the distortion functions are treated as fixed perceptual parameters rather than learned quantities, and the bounds hold even when estimation error is zero.

## Method

The paper introduces a three-layer MDP framework: the Ground MDP (GMDP) representing reality, the Human MDP (HMDP) where CPT distortion functions u and w are applied to rewards and normalized visitation probabilities respectively, and the Human-Estimation MDP (HEMDP) where the agent estimates the already-distorted HMDP from perceived trajectories. The key mechanism is that S-BLACK SWAN events are state-action pairs where the probability distortion w⁻ maps the cumulative visitation probability to zero (the agent perceives the event as impossible) while the true probability is small but positive, and simultaneously the value distortion u⁻ overestimates the loss magnitude by more than C_bs. The central theoretical result (Theorem 4) proves that while HEMDP value estimates converge to HMDP values as horizon grows, the HMDP-to-GMDP value gap retains a non-zero lower bound driven by reward distortion, S-BLACK SWAN set size, and minimum occurrence probability—meaning no amount of data collection can eliminate the perception gap without correcting u and w toward safe perception functions u⋆⁻ and w⋆⁻. Theorem 5 then quantifies how frequently the agent must update its perception functions based on the S-BLACK SWAN hitting time.

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical experiments or numerical benchmarks. The headline theoretical results are: (1) Theorems 1–3 establish that optimality deviation between the real MDP and the distorted (human-perceived) MDP emerges only when the state space grows beyond |S|=2 or the horizon exceeds T=1; (2) Theorem 4 proves that while the value *estimation* gap converges to zero as T→∞, the value *perception* gap has a non-zero lower bound of Ω(((R_max − R_bs)·ε_bs^min − R_bs·ε_bs)(R_max − R_bs)·C_bs / R_max²), which is driven by reward distortion magnitude C_bs, the size of the S-BLACK SWAN set, and their minimum occurrence probability ε_bs^min; (3) Theorem 5 gives a hitting-time bound t ≥ log(δ/p_min)/log(1−p_max)+1 for encountering an S-BLACK SWAN with probability at least δ, quantifying how frequently perception must be corrected.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not run empirical comparisons against baseline algorithms; it is a theoretical contribution that formalizes S-BLACK SWAN and derives bounds. Instead, it positions its framework against three existing safe-RL paradigms discussed in Section 7 and Appendix D.1: (a) worst-case criterion, which becomes overly conservative because black swans inflate the uncertainty set W; (b) risk-sensitive criterion (variance-based or log-exponential utility), which fails because return variance does not capture fat-tail, low-probability events; and (c) constrained criterion, where black swans complicate threshold selection and require state-action-specific rather than return-level constraints. The paper argues that all three are structurally insufficient for S-BLACK SWAN because they operate on the ground-truth MDP rather than accounting for the perception gap between the Human MDP (M†) and the Ground MDP (M). No head-to-head numerical comparison is provided.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Cumulative Prospect Theory (CPT) from behavioral economics into the MDP/RL framework, building on Kahneman & Tversky's distortion functions and prior CPT-RL work (Prashanth et al., 2016; Jie et al., 2018) that estimated CPT-based value functions for policy optimization. However, it departs fundamentally from that line: rather than treating CPT as the agent's objective to optimize, it uses CPT distortion functions to model the *gap* between a human's perceived MDP (HMDP) and the ground-truth MDP (GMDP), introducing the S-BLACK SWAN as a formal state-action pair where this gap is most dangerous. The paper also positions itself against the safe RL literature (worst-case, risk-sensitive, and constrained criteria), arguing all three are structurally insufficient for black swan events because they operate on aggregate return statistics rather than state-action-specific misperception.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core contribution is a conceptual reframe: black swans need not originate from non-stationary environments but can emerge in perfectly stationary ones due to human (or agent) misperception of reward magnitude and event likelihood. Theorem 4's lower bound shows that even with zero estimation error, a non-vanishing perception gap persists, implying that no amount of data collection alone can eliminate S-BLACK SWAN risk without explicitly correcting the distortion functions u and w. However, the paper is purely theoretical—no algorithm is proposed or tested for perception correction, and the empirical grounding rests largely on the Lehman Brothers anecdote and two brief case studies, leaving the practical question of how to learn u⋆⁻ and w⋆⁻ in open-ended environments wide open.
