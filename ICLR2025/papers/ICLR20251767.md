## Problem

Existing interactive point-based image editing methods model editing tasks implicitly, leading to two types of ambiguity: intention ambiguity, where a single 2D drag trajectory can be interpreted as multiple different manipulations (translation, rotation, scaling), and content ambiguity, where optimizing latent features at a single denoising timestep fails to preserve target identity while allowing sufficient modification. These ambiguities cause drifted trajectories, distorted targets, and artifacts in edited images.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251767/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Given an RGB image (512×512), a small set of user-specified handle points with start and end positions, and a task type label (relocation, in-plane/out-of-plane rotation, or non-rigid transformation), GDrag produces a single edited image. The sparse handle points are expanded via SAM segmentation into a dense point set whose trajectories are computed analytically per task type. The method is training-free in the sense that it requires no dataset training—only a brief LoRA fine-tuning (80 steps, rank 16) on the input image to anchor the diffusion model's feature space.

## Method

GDrag is a task-aware, optimization-based framework built on Stable Diffusion 1.5 with two core components. First, the Anti-Ambiguity Dense Trajectory (ADT) method converts sparse handle points into task-specific dense trajectories: relocation uses mean displacement over the SAM mask, rotation fits an ellipsoid to the target and computes surface-point trajectories to handle 3D out-of-plane rotations, and non-rigid transformation selects k-nearest boundary points for local shape changes. Second, the Self-Adaptive Motion Supervision (SMS) method jointly optimizes latent feature biases (b) and spatially-varying LoRA scaling maps (s) at randomly sampled denoising timesteps rather than a single fixed step, with task-conditioned parameters ρ (controlling bias blending) and β (balancing alignment vs. smoothness losses) that adapt the optimization to preserve or modify content as appropriate for each task.

## Results

On the DragBench dataset, GDrag achieves the best mean distance (MD) of 26.49 and the lowest LPIPS of 0.0915 among all compared methods, indicating superior manipulation precision and image quality. In a human study with 20 volunteers across 30 questions, GDrag is selected as the most satisfying result by 60.33% of respondents, far exceeding the next-best baseline (DragNoise at 12.67%). The method uses Stable Diffusion 1.5 as its base generator with LoRA fine-tuning (rank 16, 80 steps) and requires approximately 192 seconds per 512×512 image on an RTX 4090.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GDrag outperforms all four baselines on every metric. Compared to DragDiffusion (MD 33.91, LPIPS 0.0940), GDrag reduces mean distance by 7.42 pixels and LPIPS by 0.0025, with a 52.5-point gap in user preference (60.33% vs. 7.83%). Against FreeDrag (MD 27.41, LPIPS 0.0996), the closest competitor on precision, GDrag still achieves a 0.92-pixel lower MD and 0.0081 lower LPIPS. DragonDiffusion (MD 31.63, LPIPS 0.1033) and DragNoise (MD 29.56, LPIPS 0.1017) are both clearly outperformed on both objective metrics. The user study gap is particularly stark: GDrag's 60.33% preference rate is roughly 5× that of the next-best method (DragNoise at 12.67%), suggesting that the task-aware trajectory and self-adaptive supervision produce qualitatively more satisfying edits that go beyond what the aggregate metrics capture.

## Relation to Prior Work

GDrag extends the interactive point-based editing paradigm established by DragGAN (Pan et al., 2023) and its diffusion-based successors (DragDiffusion, DragonDiffusion, FreeDrag, DragNoise), which all share the alternating motion-supervision-and-point-tracking loop. The key conceptual departure is the explicit decomposition of dragging operations into three atomic tasks (relocation, rotation, non-rigid transformation), each with task-specific trajectory estimation (ADT) and task-conditioned optimization parameters (ρ, β), rather than treating all drags uniformly. Technically, GDrag replaces the single global LoRA scale used by prior methods with a spatially-varying scaling map and replaces fixed-timestep latent optimization with random sampling across denoising steps, both motivated by the observation that different tasks require different levels of structural vs. detail-level modification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The user study demonstrating that even humans disagree on the intended manipulation of a 2D drag (variation ratio 0.47) and that state-of-the-art MLLMs only resolve 40% of intentions correctly provides a compelling empirical justification for explicit task specification in interactive editing. The 60.33% user preference over the next-best baseline (12.67%) is a practically significant margin for an editing tool. Limitations include dependence on Stable Diffusion 1.5's limited inpainting quality, the need for the user to manually select the task type, and higher computational cost (~192s vs. ~132s per image). Open questions include whether the atomic task taxonomy generalizes to multi-object scenes with interacting parts, and whether the ellipsoid-based rotation formulation can handle highly non-ellipsoidal targets.
