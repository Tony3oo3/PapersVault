## Problem

Modern LLMs increasingly incorporate knowledge about evaluation tasks during pretraining (instruction data in the mixture, benchmark-informed data selection, task-reformulated corpora), a practice the authors term 'training on the test task.' Unlike data contamination or leakage, this is a legitimate and growing design choice, yet it confounds relative model comparisons and distorts claims about emergent capabilities. The paper asks: how can we fairly evaluate and compare models when they have trained to different degrees on the test task?

## Input / Output

The method takes 56 base models (70M–70B parameters) with known pretraining compute (estimated as C ≈ 6·N·D) and fine-tunes each on task-specific data: ~100K MCQA examples (30M tokens) from the MMLU auxiliary training set, or ~600K math examples (200M tokens) from MetaMathQA + Orca-Math, for three epochs. It produces adjusted benchmark scores, log-linear scaling regression fits of the form A = α·max(0, log C − c_e) + θN + r, and a causal decomposition separating the direct effect of model recency on accuracy from the indirect effect mediated by test task training. The fine-tuning compute is minimal relative to pretraining, and the adjustment data need not resemble the test set but rather the test task format.

## Method

The core proposal is counterintuitive: to adjust for training on the test task, fine-tune every model under comparison on the same, sufficient amount of task-relevant data before evaluation, effectively saturating all models on the test task so that residual differences reflect pretraining quality rather than task-specific exposure. The authors validate this in a controlled setting by fine-tuning only older models (pre-November 2023) on the test task, which recreates the performance gap observed between newer and older models, and then showing the gap vanishes when all models receive the same fine-tuning. They further demonstrate the confounding is about task format rather than data contamination by reformulating ARC and HellaSwag as MMLU-style multiple-choice questions, which produces the same newer-vs-older gap that disappears after adjustment. A causal analysis using a structural equation model with a hard intervention do(T=t) decomposes the recency effect, finding the indirect path N→T→A is large and significant while the direct path N→A is not. Finally, they show that increasing test task training shifts the point of emergence to lower compute scales and recovers cleaner log-linear scaling (R² improving from 0.63 to 0.95), making capabilities predictable from much smaller models.

## Results

Controlling for pretraining compute, models trained after November 2023 outperform older models by ~7 accuracy points on MMLU and ~19 points on GSM8K (both statistically significant, R² > 0.9). After fine-tuning all 56 models on the same task-specific data, the estimated performance gap θ becomes small and statistically insignificant for both benchmarks. Causal decomposition (Table 3–4) confirms the indirect effect of model recency mediated by test task training is large and significant (φ̂ = 0.071 for MMLU, 0.168 for GSM8K), while the direct effect is negligible (ψ̂ = −0.004 and 0.000, both p > 0.05). The Pareto frontier improvement since November 2023 shrinks by a factor of six after adjustment, and emergence on MMLU shifts from ~10²² FLOPs to ~6×10²⁰ FLOPs as models train on the test task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new model or method to benchmark against existing ones; instead, it compares model cohorts and model families under adjusted vs. unadjusted evaluation. Before adjustment, Qwen 1.5 appears to Pareto-dominate both Llama 2 and Pythia on MMLU and GSM8K, while Pythia models perform near random chance. After fine-tuning all families on the same task data, all three families exhibit remarkably similar scaling trends and none appears superior. Similarly, reformulating ARC and HellaSwag from cloze to MMLU-style multiple-choice prompts recreates the same ~7-point confounding gap seen on MMLU, which vanishes after adjustment—indicating the gap reflects MCQA ability rather than data contamination. In a controlled experiment, fine-tuning only older models on the test task recreates the newer-vs-older performance gap, and further fine-tuning all models eliminates it, validating the adjustment procedure. The EN+CN vs. EN language split (Baichuan, InternLM, Skywork vs. Gemma, Llama 3, Olmo) shows a 9-point MMLU / 12-point GSM8K gap before adjustment that also disappears after fine-tuning, confirming the temporal split is not an artifact.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new conceptual ground by distinguishing 'training on the test task' from the well-studied problems of data contamination and leakage: rather than models memorizing specific test examples, they are trained on the *format and style* of evaluation tasks (instruction data in pretraining, MCQA templates, data selection via benchmark ablations). Methodologically, it builds on scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020) to model benchmark accuracy as a function of pretraining compute, and on causal inference (Pearl, 2009) to decompose the effect of model recency into direct and test-task-training-mediated components. It also revives the classical NLP practice of adapting all models with the same supervised data before evaluation (Collobert et al., 2011; Bommasani et al., 2021), repurposing it as a confound-removal procedure for the modern pretraining-era setting where models differ in their pretraining data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central finding—that the 7-point (MMLU) and 19-point (GSM8K) advantages of post-November-2023 models vanish after equal task-specific fine-tuning, and that the Pareto frontier improvement shrinks sixfold—challenges the narrative of rapid capability progress and has immediate practical implications for how leaderboards and model comparisons should be conducted. Open questions include whether the 'sufficient' fine-tuning threshold is well-defined across tasks, whether the adjustment generalizes to non-benchmark evaluations (e.g., open-ended generation, safety), and whether the causal assumptions (saturation of test-task training, equivalence of post-hoc fine-tuning to pretraining-time exposure) hold in all realistic settings. The work also raises the question of whether benchmark design itself should shift toward cloze or continuous-metric evaluations to reduce sensitivity to test-task training.
