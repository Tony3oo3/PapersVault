## Problem

Bayesian actor models with continuous actions are analytically intractable for general (non-Gaussian, non-quadratic) cost functions, making the inverse decision-making problem—inferring a subject's priors, uncertainties, and cost parameters from behavioral data—prohibitively expensive when only numerical solutions are available. Prior work was restricted to tractable special cases or bespoke numerical tools for specific models, limiting the ability to explore realistic cost functions and perform principled model comparison on continuous-response tasks.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a dataset of stimulus-response pairs (e.g., target distances and achieved distances), a pre-trained neural network approximating the optimal action as a function of model parameters and sensory input, and a parametric cost function specification. It produces 20,000 posterior samples (via NUTS in 4 chains) over the subject's perceptual parameters (prior mean μ₀, prior uncertainty σ₀, sensory uncertainty σ), motor variability σᵣ, and cost function parameters, from which posterior predictive distributions, PSIS-LOO model comparisons across cost functions, and identifiability diagnostics are derived.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage pipeline. First, a multi-layer perceptron (4 hidden layers, swish activations, softplus output non-linearity motivated by the quadratic-cost analytical solution) is trained unsupervised by minimizing the expected posterior loss E_{p(s|m)}[E_{p(r|a)}[ℓ(r,s)]] directly as a stochastic objective, using the reparameterization trick to backpropagate through the log-normal response distribution—eliminating the need for pre-computed optimal actions. Second, the frozen, differentiable network is embedded within a statistical generative model of behavior, enabling gradient-based Bayesian inference (NUTS/HMC) over the subject's parameters in seconds rather than the intractable cost of solving the decision problem at each likelihood evaluation. This amortization strategy is conceptually distinct from amortized or likelihood-free inference: it amortizes the forward decision-making problem rather than the inference process itself, preserving access to standard gradient-based posterior sampling.

## Results

On synthetic data with quadratic cost (where an analytical solution exists), the neural network approximation recovers ground-truth parameters with MSE nearly identical to the analytical solution: μ₀ MSE = 0.10 (both), σ₀ MSE = 3.77×10⁻⁴ (NN) vs. 3.98×10⁻⁴ (analytical), and σᵣ MSE = 6.13×10⁻⁴ (NN) vs. 6.23×10⁻⁴ (analytical). For the more complex quadratic cost with quadratic effort, the NN again matches the analytical solution closely (μ₀ MSE: 0.77 vs. 0.74). On empirical data from three sensorimotor tasks, model comparison via PSIS-LOO ELPD differences shows task-specific best-fitting cost functions (e.g., asymmetric quadratic for bean-bag throwing, absolute cost with quadratic effort for force reproduction), with the Linex cost consistently performing worst.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is against the analytical solution for the optimal action, which serves as a gold standard where available. The neural network approximation matches the analytical solution almost exactly on the quadratic cost function (Table F.1) and the quadratic cost with quadratic effort (Table F.2: μ₀ MSE 0.77 vs. 0.74, β MSE 8.97×10⁻³ vs. 8.97×10⁻⁴), confirming that amortization introduces negligible approximation error. For cost functions without closed-form solutions (asymmetric quadratic, Linex, inverted Gaussian), no analytical baseline exists, so the NN is the only viable method; its posterior means recover ground truth with moderate MSE (e.g., α MSE = 3.23×10⁻² for asymmetric quadratic). On empirical data, the paper compares seven parametric cost functions rather than competing methods, finding that the best-fitting cost is task-dependent: asymmetric quadratic slightly outperforms others for bean-bag throwing, while absolute cost with quadratic effort best explains force reproduction data. The paper does not benchmark against prior inverse decision-making methods (e.g., Acerbi et al. 2014, Neupärtl & Rothkopf 2021) on a shared dataset, so direct method-to-method comparisons are absent.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Neupärtl & Rothkopf (2021), who first proposed approximating Bayesian decision-making with neural networks trained in a supervised fashion on numerically optimized actions. The key departures are: (1) training the network unsupervised by using the cost function itself as a stochastic training objective via the reparameterization trick, eliminating the need for expensive numerical solutions; (2) jointly inferring priors, sensory uncertainty, motor variability, and cost parameters (rather than only cost and motor variability); and (3) leveraging the network's differentiability to plug it into a standard NUTS-based Bayesian inference pipeline. Conceptually, the paper amortizes the subject's decision-making problem rather than the researcher's inference problem, distinguishing it from amortized/likelihood-free inference methods (Fengler et al., 2021; Greenberg et al., 2019).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method makes inverse decision-making tractable for continuous-action tasks with general parametric cost functions, removing the long-standing restriction to Gaussian/quadratic special cases that admit closed-form solutions. The identifiability analysis—showing that prior-cost confounds are an inherent property of Bayesian actor models and can be resolved by varying perceptual uncertainty across experimental conditions—provides concrete guidance for experimental design. A key limitation is the requirement for a tractable perceptual model (log-normal here), and the authors note that extending to non-log-normal stimuli (e.g., circular variables) would require jointly learning an approximate posterior, connecting to loss-calibrated inference. The practical speed (10 min training, 10 s for 20k posterior samples) makes exhaustive model comparison across cost functions feasible for the first time.
