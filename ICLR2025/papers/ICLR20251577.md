## Problem

Existing visual alignment methods for medical imaging (e.g., constraining input gradients to expert-annotated regions) only enforce associational alignment, leaving models vulnerable to spurious correlations that do not transfer to test data. Two decision chains with fundamentally different causal structures can exhibit identical correlation patterns, so gradient-based alignment cannot distinguish causal features from shortcut features. This paper asks how to align the underlying causal mechanism of a model's decision process with that of experienced radiologists, rather than merely matching surface-level feature correlations.

## Input / Output

The method takes a medical image (CT, mammography, MRI, or ultrasound), a binary mask annotation from a radiologist indicating the abnormal region, and optionally a binary attribute vector (e.g., subtlety, calcification, margin, speculation, lobulation, texture) describing the lesion. It produces a malignancy class label and, in the hierarchical variant, intermediate attribute predictions. The counterfactual image x* generated during training serves as an intermediate diagnostic artifact whose support identifies the causal imaging factors driving the model's decision, and the alignment loss explicitly penalizes modifications that fall outside the radiologist's annotated mask.

## Method

The core mechanism is a causal alignment loss that penalizes the model when the support of its counterfactual image modification (x* − x) extends beyond the radiologist-annotated region, where x* is obtained by solving a cross-entropy-plus-sparsity optimization that identifies the minimal image changes sufficient to flip the prediction. Because x* is an implicit function of the model parameters θ (defined by an argmin), the paper derives the gradient ∇θ L_align via the Implicit Function Theorem, reducing it to a linear system Hz* = b solved with a Hessian-free conjugate gradient method that only requires Hessian-vector products. When attribute annotations are available, the method extends to a hierarchical two-stage pipeline (image → attributes → label) and uses Conditional Counterfactual Causal Effect (CCCE) scores to identify which attribute subsets causally determine the radiologist's label, then applies separate alignment losses at both the attribute level and the image level to enforce that each stage of the decision chain relies only on causal factors.

## Results

On LIDC-IDRI (lung nodule classification), the proposed causal alignment method achieves 0.751 CAM precision and 0.722 classification accuracy, substantially outperforming the best baseline (Chang et al. at 0.074 CAM precision; Ross et al. at 0.656 accuracy). On CBIS-DDSM (breast mass classification), it reaches 0.805 CAM precision and 0.656 accuracy, again far exceeding all baselines. The method approaches the Oracle classifier's upper bound of 1.000 CAM precision while maintaining competitive accuracy (0.722 vs. 0.789 on LIDC; 0.656 vs. 0.726 on DDSM).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against five visual alignment baselines and an Oracle classifier. On CAM precision (the alignment metric), the proposed method dominates all baselines by a wide margin: 0.751 vs. 0.074 (best baseline, Chang et al.) on LIDC and 0.805 vs. 0.232 (best baseline, Rieger et al.) on DDSM. On classification accuracy, the method also wins on both datasets: 0.722 vs. 0.656 (Ross et al.) on LIDC and 0.656 vs. 0.592 (Brendel & Bethge) on DDSM. The advantage is most pronounced under the spurious-correlation setting (a '+'/'−' symbol added to images), where gradient-constraining methods like Ross et al. and BagNet focus on the shortcut symbol rather than the lesion, while the causal alignment method correctly localizes to radiologist-annotated regions. The Oracle classifier (manually restricting input to annotated areas) achieves perfect CAM precision but lower accuracy than the proposed method on LIDC (0.789 vs. 0.722), suggesting the learned causal features capture additional discriminative signal beyond the annotated bounding boxes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the visual alignment literature (Ross et al. 2017, Rieger et al. 2020, Zhang et al. 2023) by replacing associational alignment—constraining input gradients or co-predicting explanations—with a causal alignment objective built on counterfactual generation (Dhurandhar et al. 2018). The key conceptual departure is that gradient-based methods capture spurious correlations between expert-annotated regions and labels, whereas counterfactual modification identifies the actual causal factors driving the model's decision. For hierarchical alignment, the paper incorporates Conditional Counterfactual Causal Effect (CCCE, Zhao et al. 2023) to identify which attribute subsets are causally responsible for a radiologist's label, and uses the implicit function theorem with conjugate gradient estimation (borrowed from Hessian-free optimization and meta-learning) to backpropagate through the counterfactual generator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical and clinically important failure mode: medical AI models that achieve high accuracy by exploiting spurious correlations (instrument markers, background artifacts) rather than genuine disease features, leading to misdiagnosis in deployment. The finding that even an Oracle classifier restricted to expert-annotated regions only reaches 72–79% accuracy challenges the >99% figures reported in prior literature and suggests those results benefited from excluding difficult cases or using non-standard splits. The main limitation is computational cost from the implicit Jacobian estimation, and the method is validated on relatively small datasets (n≈700–900) with simple CNN backbones, leaving open whether the approach scales to larger, more complex architectures and multi-modal settings.
