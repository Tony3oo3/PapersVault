## Problem

Existing FP8 training frameworks like Transformer Engine and FP8-LM accelerate linear layer computation in FP8 but leave optimizer states (particularly second-order momentum) and activations in BF16/FP16, failing to realize FP8's full memory savings. Naively quantizing these tensors to FP8 is problematic because optimizer states are highly sparse with low dynamic range, causing FP8's representation capacity to be under-utilized and producing large quantization errors, while activation spikes in non-linear layers further degrade accuracy.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard transformer architectures (Llama-style, OLMo, VILA) during training, consuming the model's optimizer states (first- and second-order momentum) and forward-pass activations as inputs to quantize. Optimizer states are stored as FP8 (E4M3) with per-group scaling factors in BF16 (group size 1×128), while activations use mixed granularity: per-group FP8 (1×16) for non-linear layers and per-tensor FP8 for linear layers. The output is a fully trained model with nearly identical loss curves and downstream accuracy to BF16 training, but with 1.54× end-to-end memory reduction and 1.43× speedup.

## Method

COAT introduces two key mechanisms. First, Dynamic Range Expansion applies a power function f(x) = sign(x)|x|^k before quantizing optimizer states, where k is computed per-group as log_{R_X}(R_E4M3) to stretch the sparse optimizer distribution to fully occupy FP8's representable range; the paper proves this power-law form is optimal under scale-invariance and monotonicity assumptions. Second, Mixed-Granularity Activation Quantization uses fine-grained per-group (1×G) quantization for non-linear layers (LayerNorm, SiLU, GLU) to handle activation spikes, while applying per-tensor quantization for linear layers to maximize TensorCore throughput. A Group Scaling technique efficiently computes per-tensor max values by splitting the reduction into a fused per-group stage and a smaller global stage, avoiding the overhead of full-tensor max reduction while maintaining numerical stability. The FP8 precision flow directly stores quantized tensors for the backward pass, eliminating redundant quantization operations.

## Results

COAT achieves a 1.54× end-to-end memory reduction and 1.43× training speedup compared to BF16 on Llama-2-7B/13B/30B models, while maintaining nearly lossless accuracy (e.g., OLMo-1B pretraining: 10.176 vs. 10.083 avg perplexity; 67.8% vs. 68.2% avg accuracy). On VLM training (VILA-1.5-7B), COAT scores 62.51 average across 8 downstream benchmarks, matching BF16 (62.80) and outperforming TransformerEngine (61.88). COAT enables full-parameter training of Llama-2-7B on a single GPU and Llama-2-13B on 2 GPUs, where both BF16 and TE run out of memory, and doubles the maximum batch size in all multi-GPU settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to BF16, COAT reduces peak memory by 1.52–1.55× and accelerates training by 1.36–1.85× depending on GPU count, while incurring negligible accuracy loss (≤0.1 ppl on OLMo-1B/7B pretraining, ≤0.2% on downstream accuracy). Compared to TransformerEngine, COAT achieves substantially better memory reduction (1.52–1.55× vs. 1.08–1.11×) because TE does not quantize optimizer states or non-linear layer activations to FP8. On speed, COAT matches or slightly exceeds TE (e.g., 11257 vs. 9577 token/s on 4-GPU Llama-2-7B; 5650 vs. 5604 token/s on 8-GPU Llama-2-13B), and the gap widens when COAT's memory savings allow doubling the batch size (2.25× speedup on 4-GPU Llama-2-13B). On VLM training, COAT outperforms TE by 0.63 points on average (62.51 vs. 61.88) while matching BF16, confirming that the additional FP8 quantization of optimizer states and activations does not degrade generation quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

COAT extends the FP8 training lineage initiated by Transformer Engine (which quantizes only linear-layer matmuls) and FP8-LM (which additionally quantizes gradients and first-order momentum) by closing the remaining gaps: second-order momentum and all activations are also brought into FP8. For optimizer quantization, it builds on the per-group quantization paradigm from 8-bit Adam (Dettmers et al., 2021) and 4-bit Optimizer (Li et al., 2024), but introduces Dynamic Range Expansion—a power-law rescaling proven optimal under scale-invariance assumptions—to fix the under-utilization of FP8's representable range. For activations, it adapts the fine-grained per-group quantization from VS-Quant and JetFire to non-linear layers while deliberately using per-tensor quantization for linear layers to preserve TensorCore throughput, a mixed-granularity strategy not present in prior FP8 training frameworks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

COAT's 1.54× end-to-end memory reduction and 1.43× speedup over BF16 are practically significant: they enable full-parameter training of 7B–30B models on 1–8 H100 GPUs where BF16 and Transformer Engine OOM, and double the achievable batch size in distributed settings—directly translating to lower hardware costs and longer context support. The nearly lossless accuracy across LLM pretraining (OLMo-1B/7B), fine-tuning (Llama-2-7B on math), and VLM training (VILA-1.5-7B) validates that the quantization design is robust rather than task-specific. Open questions include whether combining COAT with gradient compression (e.g., FP8-LM's gradient quantization) yields further communication savings, and whether the Dynamic Range Expansion principle generalizes to other low-precision formats or non-AdamW optimizers.
