## Problem

Existing LLM unlearning objectives (GA, NPO, PO, RMU) each have distinct properties, yet no unified analytical framework exists to systematically compare them, dissect their internal mechanisms, or identify which components are beneficial versus harmful. This gap makes it difficult to discern promising objectives from less effective ones or to design principled improvements.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252232/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The G-effect analysis consumes a pre-trained LLM (Phi-1.5 or Llama-2-7B), a targeted unlearning dataset (TOFU fictitious author profiles at 1%, 5%, or 10% ratios), and non-targeted data, producing per-step, per-layer, and per-instance gradient dot-product values that decompose unlearning impact into removal and retention components. The unlearning procedure itself takes original parameters θ_o, the unlearning set D_u, and non-targeted data D_t\D_u, and outputs unlearned parameters θ_u after 5 epochs of gradient updates with KL regularization, evaluated via ES-exact, ES-perturb, MU, and FQ metrics.

## Method

The paper introduces the G-effect, defined as the dot product between the gradient of a risk metric (NLL) and the gradient of an unlearning objective at each updating step, decomposed into unlearning G-effect (e_u) and retaining G-effect (e_r) to separately quantify removal and retention impacts across instances, steps, and layers. Using this tool, they identify that GA's excessive unlearning stems from an 'inverse confidence' mechanism where already-unlearned tokens receive disproportionately large gradient contributions, motivating WGA which weights each token by its confidence raised to a power α. They further propose TNPO, which generalizes NPO's sequence-level preference weighting to the token level for finer-grained control, and WTNPO which combines TNPO's preference-based weighting with WGA's confidence weighting to stabilize late-stage unlearning. The analysis also reveals that PO can inadvertently facilitate knowledge relearning after initial unlearning, and RMU is highly sensitive to layer choice and scaling parameters, while KL divergence emerges as the most effective regularization for retention.

## Results

On the TOFU benchmark (5% unlearning), WGA achieves ES-exact unlearn of 0.0179 (Phi-1.5) and 0.0053 (Llama-2-7B) while retaining ES-exact scores of 0.2980 and 0.4709 respectively, substantially outperforming GA (which collapses to 0.0000 on both retention and unlearn) and NPO (0.1267/0.0764 unlearn). WTNPO further improves retention to 0.2185 (Phi-1.5) and 0.4595 (Llama-2-7B) with unlearn scores of 0.0179 and 0.0061. The paper recommends WGA as the default choice since it requires tuning only one hyper-parameter (α) and consistently delivers strong removal with minimal integrity loss across all three unlearning ratios (1%, 5%, 10%) and both model architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GA, WGA dramatically improves retention (ES-exact retain 0.2980 vs. 0.0000 on Phi-1.5, 5%) while maintaining comparable removal strength (unlearn 0.0179 vs. 0.0000), confirming that GA's excessive unlearning destroys model integrity. Against NPO, both WGA and WTNPO achieve substantially better retention (0.2980/0.2185 vs. 0.0876 on Phi-1.5) with similar or better unlearn scores, and WTNPO further reduces the unlearn score to 0.0040 (ES-perturb) on Llama-2-7B. Against PO, all proposed methods clearly outperform it on removal (PO's unlearn scores of 0.7986/0.8437 indicate it fails to erase targeted knowledge), while also preserving far more integrity. Against RMU, WGA and WTNPO achieve better retention (0.2980/0.4709 vs. 0.2162/0.1262 on ES-exact) with comparable removal, and RMU's MU scores are notably lower (0.2744/0.5801 vs. 0.5108/0.6438), indicating greater overall model degradation. Overall, WGA and WTNPO are identified as the new state-of-the-art, with WGA recommended as the practical default due to its single hyper-parameter.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the gradient ascent (GA) unlearning objective from Yao et al. (2023) and the negative preference optimization (NPO) from Zhang et al. (2024), which itself derives from DPO. Rather than proposing yet another unlearning loss in isolation, the paper introduces the G-effect—a gradient dot-product framework that unifies the analysis of all existing unlearning objectives under a single lens. This analytical perspective reveals that GA's excessive unlearning stems from an 'inverse confidence' amplification in its gradient, and that NPO's advantage over GA is primarily a reweighting mechanism with identifiable failure modes, motivating the paper's new weighted GA (WGA) and token-wise NPO (TNPO/WTNPO) objectives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The G-effect provides the first systematic, gradient-level diagnostic tool for LLM unlearning, enabling practitioners to understand *why* an objective helps or harms retention/removal across layers, steps, and individual data points—something prior work could only assess through end-to-end metrics. The paper's practical contributions (WGA, TNPO, WTNPO) set new SOTA on TOFU and are recommended as default choices, but open questions remain: whether the G-effect can be refined with Hessian information, whether NLL is the optimal risk metric, and why ES and FQ metrics sometimes disagree (e.g., the 5% Llama-2-7B case). The broader challenge of designing weighting mechanisms that simultaneously prioritize high-unlearning-impact and low-retention-harm tokens remains unsolved.
