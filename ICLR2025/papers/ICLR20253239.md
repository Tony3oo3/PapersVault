## Problem

Existing KV cache eviction methods assign uniform or fixed-pattern cache sizes across all layers, ignoring that different layers exhibit markedly different attention dynamics in both spatial dispersion and temporal shift. Additionally, conventional eviction indicators rely on static spatial attention scores and overlook how token importance evolves over time, leading to premature eviction of tokens that may become relevant in subsequent decoding steps.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253239/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a text prompt (token sequence of length S) and a total KV cache budget B_total (expressed as a multiple of the number of layers L, e.g., 128L to 2048L). During prefilling, it computes local attention over a recent observation window of S_w=32 tokens to derive per-layer preference scores and eviction indicators, then outputs a compressed KV cache where each layer l retains B_l tokens (B_l determined by normalized preference scores). During decoding, the fixed per-layer budgets are maintained and the cache is updated with newly generated KV pairs, producing the next token at each step.

## Method

CAKE frames KV cache eviction as a 'cake-slicing problem' with three components. First, a preference-prioritized adaptive allocation strategy computes a per-layer preference score P = H^(1/τ₁) · V^(1/τ₂), where H is the entropy of spatial attention (dispersion) and V is the variance of temporal attention (shift) over a recent window, then normalizes these scores to distribute the total budget B_total across layers. Second, a preference-guided cascading cache management algorithm partitions prefilling into L stages, redistributing budgets incrementally as each layer's preference score becomes available, with a theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) that the cascading eviction result is exactly equivalent to a single-shot eviction on the full cache. Third, an attention-shift tolerant eviction indicator scores each token as Mean(A[-S_w:, n]) + γ·Var(A[-S_w:, n]) (with γ=200), combining sustained importance with attention variability, while assigning an arbitrarily large value Ω to the most recent S_w tokens and applying a pooling layer to prevent information fragmentation.

## Results

On LongBench (16 datasets, 6 task categories), CAKE achieves the highest average score across all tested models and memory budgets. Under the tight 128L budget on Llama2-7B-Chat, CAKE scores 29.29 average, outperforming SnapKV (28.09), PyramidKV (28.20), H2O (26.06), TOVA (25.31), and StreamingLLM (23.33). On NeedleBench 32K, CAKE preserves full model capability using only 3.2% of the KV cache and even surpasses full cache on Single-Needle Retrieval. CAKE also achieves over 10× decoding latency speedup versus full cache at 128K context length with FlashAttention-2, while reducing peak memory by ~48.6%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CAKE outperforms all five baselines (StreamingLLM, H2O, TOVA, SnapKV, PyramidKV) on average across every model and budget tested. The advantage is most pronounced under tight memory: at 128L on Llama2-7B-Chat, CAKE leads SnapKV by +1.20 and PyramidKV by +1.09, while the gap widens to +4.96 over StreamingLLM. On Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 at 128L, CAKE (37.33) beats the next-best SnapKV (35.81) by +1.52, and on Llama3.1-8B-Instruct at 128L, CAKE (43.39) edges out TOVA (42.47) and SnapKV (42.53). At higher budgets (1024L), CAKE still leads but the margins narrow (e.g., +0.43 over PyramidKV on Llama2-7B-Chat), suggesting the adaptive allocation matters most when memory is scarce. CAKE is also shown to be compatible with existing eviction indicators: plugging the P2A allocation strategy into H2O or SnapKV consistently improves their scores (e.g., from 28.36 to 29.29 average on Llama2-7B-Chat at 128L), confirming the allocation strategy is a generalizable component. On NeedleBench, CAKE with 3.2% cache surpasses full cache on Single-Needle Retrieval and leads all baselines on the more challenging Multi-Needle Retrieval and Multi-Needle Reasoning tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

CAKE extends the KV cache eviction line initiated by StreamingLLM, H2O, and SnapKV, which all apply uniform per-layer cache budgets and rely on static spatial attention scores (cumulative, last-token, or clustered) to select tokens for retention. The key conceptual departure is treating cache allocation as a layer-level resource distribution problem: by quantifying each layer's attention dynamics through spatial entropy and temporal variance, CAKE replaces uniform or fixed-pattern (PyramidKV) allocation with a preference-prioritized adaptive scheme. It also builds directly on SnapKV's observation window and pooling mechanism for the eviction indicator, augmenting it with an attention-variance term to capture temporal shifts. The cascading memory management procedure, backed by a formal equivalence theorem, is a novel systems-level contribution that avoids the O(S·L) peak memory of the ideal allocation without sacrificing eviction quality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

KV cache memory is a primary bottleneck for long-context LLM deployment, and CAKE's 10× decoding speedup at 128K tokens with FlashAttention-2 makes it practically relevant for production inference pipelines. The allocation strategy is modular and compatible with existing eviction indicators (validated with H2O and SnapKV), positioning it as a drop-in enhancement rather than a full replacement. However, the paper acknowledges it operates only at the layer level and does not capture head-level attention heterogeneity, and the performance gains over the strongest baseline (SnapKV) are modest (≈1–2 points on LongBench averages), suggesting diminishing returns as cache budgets grow. Open questions include whether the preference metric generalizes to MoE architectures, whether head-level granularity would yield further gains, and how CAKE interacts with emerging quantization and cache-merging techniques beyond the preliminary KIVI combination shown in the appendix.
