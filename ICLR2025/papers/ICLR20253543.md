## Problem

Existing INR-VC methods require separate end-to-end weight training for each target bitrate, making variable-rate coding prohibitively expensive (up to 10+ hours per bitrate point). Moreover, standard post-training quantization methods (AdaRound, BRECQ) assume inter-layer independence, an assumption that breaks down for non-generalized INR-VC models where layers exhibit strong dependencies and anisotropic loss landscapes, leading to significant reconstruction degradation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-trained FP32 INR-VC weights (from architectures like NeRV, HNeRV, or HiNeRV with ~3M–6M parameters) along with a target bitrate constraint. It produces a losslessly entropy-coded bitstream containing both the quantized integer weights and the calibrated quantization steps (s), with bitwidths allocated per-layer in the range [3, 8] bits. The bpp accounting includes both the quantized parameters and the step values, though the latter contributes negligibly (e.g., ~0.00004 bpp vs. ~0.01 bpp for weights at INT4 on 1080p).

## Method

NeuroQuant redefines variable-rate INR-VC as a mixed-precision quantization problem and introduces a sensitivity criterion Ω = Δwᵀ·H⁽ʷ⁾·Δw (a Hessian-Vector product) that captures both off-diagonal Hessian terms and the direction of weight perturbations, unlike prior criteria that assume diagonal Hessian and isotropy. The Hessian-Vector product is computed efficiently via a gradient-of-gradient trick (∇(g·Δw)) without explicitly forming the Hessian. For calibration, the paper derives a unified MSE-oriented objective min‖Δz⁽ⁿ⁾‖² via Gaussian-Newton approximation, and advocates network-wise calibration (full Hessian) over layer/block-wise approaches due to the strong inter-layer dependencies inherent in non-generalized INR-VC. Quantization steps are assigned per-channel (not per-layer) to accommodate diverse weight distributions, and QPs are optimized using soft rounding variables with a differentiable regularizer, enabling continuous optimization of the discrete quantization problem without retraining the underlying weights.

## Results

On the UVG dataset, NeuroQuant achieves the best average PSNR across all three INR-VC architectures (NeRV, HNeRV, HiNeRV) at every tested bitwidth. At 4-bit, NeuroQuant reaches 32.08 dB average PSNR, outperforming the best PTQ baseline QDrop (31.56 dB) by 0.52 dB and the best QAT baseline HiNeRV (31.75 dB) by 0.33 dB. The advantage grows substantially at 2-bit, where NeuroQuant (28.37 dB) surpasses QDrop (26.17 dB) by 2.20 dB. For variable-rate coding, NeuroQuant delivers 27.8% and 25.5% BD-rate savings over NeRV and HNeRV with direct 8-bit quantization, and a further 4.8% gain over HiNeRV's built-in QAT, while reducing encoding time by up to 7.9×.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NeuroQuant outperforms all nine baselines across every configuration tested. Against the naive HNeRV quantization baseline, NeuroQuant gains 0.90 dB at 6-bit, 4.90 dB at 4-bit, and 14.37 dB at 2-bit (average), confirming that representation-only training without quantization awareness causes severe degradation. Among PTQ methods, NeuroQuant beats QDrop (the strongest PTQ competitor) by 0.21 dB at 6-bit, 0.52 dB at 4-bit, and 2.20 dB at 2-bit, with the gap widening at lower bitwidths due to network-wise calibration capturing inter-layer dependencies. Against QAT baselines, NeuroQuant surpasses HiNeRV-QAT by 0.13 dB at 6-bit, 0.33 dB at 4-bit, and 3.39 dB at 2-bit, while also eliminating the need for per-bitrate retraining (up to 7.9× faster encoding). In variable-rate coding (Table 3), NeuroQuant outperforms DeepCABAC by 0.09–1.41 dB and Gomes et al. by 0.32–0.53 dB across 0.011–0.016 bpp, with the largest margin at the lowest bitrate (0.011 bpp: 29.12 vs. 27.71 dB). No baseline matches or exceeds NeuroQuant in any tested setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the post-training quantization (PTQ) lineage from AdaRound's soft-rounding calibration and HAWQ-V2's Hessian-based sensitivity criteria, but fundamentally departs from their inter-layer independence assumption. The authors demonstrate that non-generalized INR-VC models exhibit strong cross-layer dependencies and anisotropic loss landscapes, rendering diagonal-Hessian approximations (used by AdaRound, BRECQ, HAWQ) suboptimal. They introduce a Hessian-Vector product sensitivity criterion Ω that incorporates both off-diagonal Hessian terms and perturbation directionality, and advocate network-wise calibration over the layer/block-wise granularity standard in generalized networks. The variational inference framing in Sec. 3.3 further positions the work within the learned compression literature (Ballé et al., 2017) by explicitly bridging the representation-compression mismatch that prior INR-VCs ignore.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to enable variable-rate coding in INR-VC through PTQ, eliminating the need for separate weight training per target bitrate and achieving up to 8× encoding speedup — a practical barrier that has limited INR-VC adoption. The theoretical contributions (sensitivity criterion with directionality, unified MSE-oriented calibration formula, variational inference interpretation) lay groundwork for future joint rate-distortion optimization in INR-VC. Key limitations remain: the variable-rate range is bounded by available bitwidths (INT2–INT8), the method is not yet a fully joint R-D optimization, and the entropy coding is less advanced than CABAC-based approaches. Open questions include whether customized context models can capture INR-VC weight statistics for true joint optimization, and whether integrating pruning (bitwidth=0) can extend the rate range.
