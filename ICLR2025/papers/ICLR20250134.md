## Problem

Existing automated research systems rely on commercial LLMs (GPT-4, Claude) as prompt-based agents that cannot be fine-tuned or optimized via reinforcement learning, and none integrate the iterative Research-Review-Refinement feedback loop that is central to real scientific practice. This paper asks whether open-source LLMs can be post-trained as autonomous agents that perform the full research lifecycle—literature review, paper writing, peer review, and refinement—using the review process itself as a training signal.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250134/cropped_page3_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

CycleResearcher takes a bib file containing all references with their abstracts plus a research topic as input, and produces a complete LaTeX paper with structured outlines (motivation, method, experimental setup, results) and main text generated in alternating outline-then-prose passes; experimental results are fabricated in the virtual RL environment to accelerate training. CycleReviewer takes a full research paper as input and outputs a multi-reviewer simulation: sequential reviews from lowest- to highest-rated reviewer, each with strengths, weaknesses, soundness/presentation/contribution scores (1–4), an overall score (1–10), followed by a senior reviewer's consolidated final suggestion. The average of all simulated reviewer scores serves as the scalar reward for preference optimization.

## Method

The framework pairs a policy model (CycleResearcher, 12B/72B/123B based on Mistral-Nemo, Qwen2.5, and Mistral-Large-2) with a generative reward model (CycleReviewer, 123B Mistral-Large-2 with LoRA-GA) trained on the Review-5k dataset to simulate multi-reviewer peer review. The core training loop is Iterative SimPO: each round samples three papers from the current CycleResearcher at temperature 0.4, CycleReviewer scores all three, and the highest- and lowest-scoring outputs form a preference pair; the policy is then updated using SimPO's length-normalized log-likelihood reward (eliminating the reference model) combined with an NLL loss on the winning sample to prevent degenerate generation. This resample-and-optimize cycle is repeated across iterations, with one-third of the preference dataset sampled per round to mitigate overfitting, approximating online policy optimization. The approach differs from prior DPO-based iterative methods by using SimPO's reference-free formulation for lower memory cost and by coupling it with NLL regularization, which ablations show is critical—removing NLL drops the acceptance rate from 35% to 12%.

## Results

CycleReviewer-123B achieves a Proxy MAE of 0.92 and Proxy MSE of 1.43 on the Review-5k test set, representing a 26.89% reduction in MAE and 48.77% reduction in MSE compared to individual human reviewers (MAE 1.16, MSE 2.34), with a decision accuracy of 74.24%. On the research generation side, CycleResearcher-12B achieves an average simulated review score of 5.36 with a 35.13% acceptance rate, surpassing AI Scientist (4.31, 0% acceptance) and approaching human preprint quality (5.24, 29.63%), though still below conference-accepted papers (5.69, 100%). In human evaluation by three NLP experts, CycleResearcher scores 4.8/10 overall versus AI Scientist's 3.6/10, while remaining below ICLR 2024 submission average (5.5) and accepted paper average (6.4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against AI Scientist (Lu et al., 2024), CycleResearcher-12B wins decisively on all automated review metrics: average score 5.36 vs. 4.31, acceptance rate 35.13% vs. 0%, and higher minimum scores across soundness (1.73 vs. 1.20), presentation (1.91 vs. 1.70), and contribution (1.68 vs. 1.30). In human evaluation, CycleResearcher also outperforms AI Scientist on every dimension (overall 4.8 vs. 3.6, soundness 2.6 vs. 2.2, presentation 2.8 vs. 2.6, contribution 2.2 vs. 1.8). For the reviewer component, CycleReviewer-123B substantially outperforms GPT-4o (Proxy MSE 1.43 vs. 6.61; Proxy MAE 0.92 vs. 2.24) and Gemini-1.5-pro (Proxy MSE 1.43 vs. 3.02; Proxy MAE 0.92 vs. 1.34), while approaching individual human reviewer performance (MSE 2.34, MAE 1.16) and achieving comparable decision accuracy (74.24% vs. 75.40%). The 12B variant is the strongest researcher model, while the 123B variant underperforms on research generation (5.15 avg score) despite being the largest, suggesting diminishing returns or training instability at scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the AI Scientist line of work (Lu et al., 2024) by replacing prompt-based commercial LLM agents with post-trained open-source models (Mistral, Qwen) that can be optimized via reinforcement learning. The core methodological contribution is an iterative SimPO-based preference optimization loop where a dedicated reward model (CycleReviewer) simulates peer review and provides scoring feedback to improve the policy model (CycleResearcher), drawing on the iterative DPO framework of Pang et al. (2024) and the self-rewarding paradigm of Yuan et al. (2024). Unlike prior automated-research systems that rely on fixed prompts or single-pass generation, this work closes the Research-Review-Refinement loop with trainable models, and incorporates Fast-DetectGPT (Bao et al., 2024) as an ethical safeguard for detecting AI-generated submissions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework to automate the full research lifecycle—including peer review simulation and iterative refinement—using entirely open-source, trainable models, releasing two substantial datasets (Review-5k, Research-14k) and three model sizes. However, a critical limitation is that all experimental results in generated papers are fabricated rather than derived from real experiments, fundamentally limiting the verifiability and reproducibility of the output. The gap between CycleResearcher's simulated score (5.36) and human-accepted papers (5.69), combined with the 123B model underperforming the 12B variant, suggests that scale alone does not close the quality gap and that reward hacking remains an open risk. Future work must integrate real experimentation (e.g., via AI Scientist's code execution) and address domain generalizability beyond machine learning.
