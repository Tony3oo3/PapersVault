## Problem

While conformity and groupthink are well-documented in human social psychology, their manifestation in LLM-driven multi-agent systems remains largely unexplored. As multi-agent LLMs are increasingly deployed in collaborative decision-making (policy analysis, moderation, governance), it is critical to understand whether these agents abandon correct independent judgments under peer pressure, what factors drive such conformity, and how it might be mitigated.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250395/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The subject agent receives a multiple-choice reasoning question (drawn from 3,299 items across 13 BIG-Bench Hard tasks) along with contextual answers from up to six additional agents, whose correctness and number of prior discussion rounds are controlled by the protocol. The agent outputs a single multiple-choice answer, which is then scored against ground truth to compute three metrics: accuracy, conformity rate (proportion of originally-correct answers flipped to incorrect under social pressure), and independence rate (proportion answered correctly across all long-term protocols simultaneously).

## Method

The paper introduces BENCHFORM, a conformity-oriented benchmark built on BIG-Bench Hard with five interaction protocols inspired by Asch conformity experiments: Raw (baseline), Correct Guidance and Wrong Guidance (single-round peer influence), and Trust and Doubt (multi-round protocols where peers are consistently correct or incorrect before a final round with reversed answers). Two novel metrics—conformity rate (CR) and independence rate (IR)—quantify susceptibility to group pressure and capacity for independent judgment, respectively. The study then ablates two key factors (number of prior discussion rounds and majority size among peers) and tests two prompt-based mitigation strategies: empowered persona system prompts that encourage critical independent thinking, and a post-answer reflection prompt that asks the agent to re-evaluate its response against its own knowledge before committing to a final answer.

## Results

All 11 evaluated LLMs exhibit conformity, with the Doubt protocol being the most effective at inducing errors (average CR^D = 47.2% vs. CR^W = 23.5% and CR^T = 31.3% across five representative models). Model size positively correlates with independence: Qwen2-72B achieves the highest IR (57.6%) and Llama3.1-405B the second-highest (56.1%), while GPT-3.5 is most susceptible (IR = 6.1%). Under the Doubt protocol, conformity rates range from 26.6% (GPT-4o) to 91.2% (Llama3.1-8B), and the ΔP (accuracy drop) reaches up to 44.7% for Llama3-70B. Mitigation via empowered persona and reflection mechanisms improves IR substantially (e.g., Llama3-70B IR rises from 28.6% to 68.5% with reflection).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark/empirical study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the 'baselines' are the 11 LLMs evaluated against each other on conformity susceptibility. Llama3.1-405B and Qwen2-72B are the most independent (IR 56.1% and 57.6% respectively), while GPT-3.5 and Llama3.1-8B are the most conformist (IR 6.1% and 7.8%). Interestingly, the Llama3.1 series shows strong resistance to Wrong Guidance (CR^W as low as 9.2–10.0%) but remains vulnerable under the Doubt protocol (CR^D = 43.2–91.2%), suggesting that established distrust is harder to overcome than one-shot peer pressure. Qwen2-7B is an outlier: extremely credulous under Correct Guidance (CR^C = 98.5%) yet relatively resistant under Doubt (CR^D = 27.5%), indicating it fails to build doubt relationships. GPT-4o shows moderate conformity across all protocols (CR^W–CR^D between 24.4% and 37.9%) with the second-highest IR (55.4%), while GLM-4-Plus sits in the middle (IR = 51.0%). The key takeaway is that no single LLM is immune to all four misleading protocols, and model architecture and training data (not just scale) drive distinct conformity profiles.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws on the Asch conformity experiments as its conceptual foundation and extends the sparse prior work on conformity in LLM multi-agent systems (Xiong et al., Zhang et al., Baltaji et al.) from scenario-specific observations to a systematic, protocol-driven evaluation framework. It repurposes the knowledge-conflict literature (external evidence vs. internal knowledge) by reframing other agents' answers as a distinct type of knowledge conflict. The mitigation strategies build on persona prompting and reflection/self-correction mechanisms from the broader LLM prompting literature, but apply them specifically to the conformity problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even state-of-the-art models (GPT-4o, Llama3.1-405B) exhibit substantial conformity—particularly under the Doubt protocol—raises concrete safety concerns for multi-agent systems deployed in governance, moderation, or policy contexts. The BENCHFORM benchmark and its conformity/independence rate metrics provide a reusable evaluation framework that the field lacked. Key open questions include whether findings generalize beyond multiple-choice reasoning tasks, how conformity interacts with more naturalistic multi-turn debate, and whether alignment training can make LLMs explicitly aware of and resist conformity pressure without suppressing legitimate consensus-building.
