## Problem

Previous scaling law research for time series foundation models (TSFMs) has focused almost exclusively on in-distribution (ID) performance, leaving two critical questions unanswered: whether power-law scaling also governs out-of-distribution (OOD) forecasting, and how different model architectures (encoder-only vs. decoder-only, and specific module designs) affect scalability. Without these answers, the community lacks a principled framework to guide the allocation of compute, data, and parameter budgets when scaling TSFMs for real-world generalization.

## Input / Output

Each univariate time series is divided into non-overlapping patches of length 32, which are linearly projected into a d_m-dimensional latent space and fed through the Transformer with RoPE positional encoding. The model outputs a mixture of four Student-t distributions per future patch, parameterized by independent linear heads predicting degrees of freedom (clamped ≥ 2), location, scale (via softplus), and mixture weights (via softmax). Evaluation spans NLL, MAPE, SMAPE, MASE, and CRPS on both a 5% ID validation split and OOD benchmarks (LSF long-sequence prediction subset and Monash archive), with models ranging from ~10³ to ~10⁸ trainable parameters trained on 10M–1B time-point subsets of a 16.8B-point, 7-domain corpus.

## Method

The paper trains two families of baseline TSFMs—encoder-only and decoder-only Transformers with patch embedding, RoPE, and Student-t mixture heads—across a grid of model sizes, compute budgets (C = 6NBS), and dataset sizes, then fits power-law exponents to NLL and MAPE on both ID and OOD test sets. As case studies, it re-trains Moirai (encoder-only with any-variate attention and multi-scale patching) and Chronos-T5 (encoder-decoder with discrete token prediction) under identical protocols to isolate the effect of architectural modifications on scaling slopes. The key empirical findings are: (1) NLL follows a consistent power law in both ID and OOD with roughly parallel slopes, while MAPE shows a steeper OOD exponent, indicating larger relative gains from scaling on unseen data; (2) encoder-only Transformers have a marginal parameter-scaling advantage on ID but comparable OOD scalability to decoder-only variants; (3) the architectural enhancements in Moirai and Chronos improve ID performance but reduce the OOD power-law slope, meaning their gains do not compound with scale. The paper synthesizes these results into design principles, including a sublinear data-to-parameter ratio (D ∝ N^0.8) and the recommendation to prioritize model size over training duration under fixed compute.

## Results

The paper establishes that TSFM negative log-likelihood (NLL) follows a power-law scaling L(N) ≈ (N_c/N)^α_N across five orders of magnitude in model size (1K to 100M parameters), with consistent exponents for both ID and OOD data. For MAPE, OOD scaling exponents are larger than ID exponents, meaning model size increases yield proportionally greater OOD improvements. Pre-trained models must reach at least 3M parameters to consistently outperform the classical exponential smoothing (ETS) baseline on OOD data. Doubling the pre-training dataset reduces OOD MAPE to ~90% of its prior value versus ~97% for ID MAPE, and the optimal data-to-model-size ratio follows D ∝ N^0.8 (i.e., doubling model size requires ~1.7× more data).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its encoder-only and decoder-only Transformer baselines against two state-of-the-art TSFMs: Moirai (encoder-only with any-variate attention, multi-scale patch embedding, and diverse mixture distributions) and Chronos-T5 (encoder-decoder with discrete token prediction). Against Moirai, the plain encoder-only baseline shows comparable or better OOD scalability — Moirai achieves lower ID NLL at small scales but its power-law slope on OOD data is smaller, meaning the baseline eventually surpasses Moirai as parameters increase (Figure 6). Against Chronos-T5, the decoder-only baseline exhibits stronger OOD scalability; Chronos-T5's discrete probability prediction yields very small NLL exponents (limited scalability), and while Chronos-T5 has a slight SMAPE advantage on ID data, the decoder-only baseline performs better on OOD data (Figure 7). Between the two baseline architectures, encoder-only and decoder-only Transformers show nearly identical OOD scaling across all three axes (parameters, compute, data), with the encoder-only variant holding a marginal ID performance advantage and slightly higher parameter-scaling exponent. Overall, both advanced TSFMs improve ID forecasting but their architectural modifications do not translate into improved OOD generalization or scalability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neural scaling law framework from Kaplan et al. (2020) to time series foundation models, building directly on prior TSFM scaling studies (Edwards et al. 2024; Shi et al. 2024a) that were limited to in-distribution evaluation. It departs from that line by systematically studying OOD scaling behavior and by introducing a cross-architecture comparison (encoder-only vs. decoder-only Transformers, plus Moirai and Chronos as case studies), which no prior TSFM scaling work had attempted. The model design draws on PatchTST's patch embedding, RoPE positional encoding, and DeepAR-style mixture distribution heads, while the pre-training corpus is constructed from LOTSA with domain balancing and quality filtering.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first empirical evidence that OOD forecasting performance of TSFMs follows the same power-law scaling as ID performance, giving practitioners a principled basis for predicting zero-shot generalization gains from scaling. The finding that architectural enhancements in state-of-the-art models (Moirai's any-variate attention, Chronos's discrete tokenization) improve ID accuracy but reduce OOD scalability is a practical caution for model designers. Key limitations include the restriction to univariate forecasting, the purely empirical (non-theoretical) nature of the laws, and the absence of a unified model linking all three scaling factors simultaneously. Open questions include multivariate scaling, the role of context length and forecast horizon, and whether the observed OOD power-law transfer holds across more diverse distributional shifts.
