## Problem

Aligned LLMs remain vulnerable to jailbreaking, yet the root cause of this vulnerability has been poorly understood. Previous optimization-based attacks frame jailbreaking as minimizing the negative log-probability of a target harmful response, but the paper shows this target loss is a poor proxy: it fails to distinguish successful from unsuccessful jailbreaks at low loss values. The paper attributes the vulnerability to reward misspecification during alignment, where the implicit reward function incorrectly ranks harmful responses above harmless ones, and formalizes jailbreaking as a search in this reward-misspecified space.

## Input / Output

The method requires a harmful instruction x, a corresponding target harmful response y⁻, the target model's log-probabilities on candidate responses (gray-box access), and a reference model π_ref (ideally the pre-alignment base model, though a smaller unrelated model like TinyLlama-1.1b also works). It outputs a short adversarial suffix s (typically 10–30 tokens) appended to x to form the jailbreak prompt x‖s. The suffix is constrained to maintain low perplexity under the reference model to evade perplexity-based filters, and the generator produces suffixes in seconds at inference time via autoregressive decoding.

## Method

The core contribution is ReGap (Equation 4), a metric measuring the difference between implicit rewards on a harmless response y⁺ and a harmful response y⁻, where the implicit reward is derived from the KL-constrained RL closed-form solution as r(x,y) ∝ log π(y|x)/π_ref(y|x). A ReGap value below zero indicates reward misspecification, and the paper demonstrates (via AUROC 0.97 vs. 0.82 for target loss) that ReGap is a far superior proxy for predicting successful jailbreaks. ReMiss operationalizes this by using stochastic beam search to find suffixes minimizing an up-weighted reward gap Δ_r^α plus a reference-model perplexity regularization term (Equation 5), then fine-tunes a generator via LoRA on the discovered suffixes over 10 epochs with a replay buffer. At inference, the generator produces human-readable adversarial suffixes in seconds, achieving state-of-the-art ASR@10 above 90% on three of five open-source targets and demonstrating strong transferability to GPT-4o and out-of-distribution HarmBench tasks.

## Results

On the AdvBench test set, ReMiss achieves test ASR@10 of 94.2% (Vicuna-13b-v1.5), 98.1% (Vicuna-7b-v1.5), 10.6% (Llama2-7b-chat), and 100% (Mistral-7b-instruct), with corresponding ASR@1 values of 48.1%, 49.0%, 4.8%, and 88.5%. Generated suffixes maintain low perplexity (16.8–70.6) compared to GCG (91k–114k) and AutoDAN (57–374), indicating human readability. In transfer attacks, ReMiss achieves 100% ASR@10 on GPT-3.5-turbo and 22.1% ASR@1 on GPT-4 (7× higher than AdvPrompter's 3.1%), and outperforms AdvPrompter on HarmBench OOD tasks (e.g., 93.4% vs. 69.7% on Vicuna-13b).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ReMiss outperforms all three baselines (AdvPrompter, AutoDAN, GCG) on test ASR@10 across all four open-source target models. Against the closest competitor AdvPrompter, ReMiss gains +26.7 pp on Vicuna-13b (94.2% vs. 67.5%), +10.6 pp on Vicuna-7b (98.1% vs. 87.5%), +2.9 pp on Llama2-7b-chat (10.6% vs. 7.7%), and +3.9 pp on Mistral-7b (100% vs. 96.1%). On ASR@1, ReMiss leads by a wider margin: +28.6 pp on Vicuna-13b, +15.6 pp on Vicuna-7b, +3.8 pp on Llama2-7b-chat, and +34.2 pp on Mistral-7b. GCG and AutoDAN achieve comparable or slightly higher ASR@10 on some models but at the cost of extremely high perplexity (57k–114k), making their suffixes unreadable and easily detectable by perplexity-based filters. In transfer settings, ReMiss's advantage over AdvPrompter is most pronounced on GPT-4 (22.1% vs. 3.1% ASR@1, a 7× gap) and on HarmBench OOD tasks (93.4% vs. 69.7% on Vicuna-13b). The only case where a baseline slightly edges out ReMiss is AutoDAN on Vicuna-7b ASR@1 (63.2% vs. 49.0%), though AutoDAN's perplexity (76.3) is still substantially higher than ReMiss's (16.8).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes the well-studied task of optimization-based jailbreaking (GCG, AutoDAN, AdvPrompter) by replacing the standard target-loss objective with a reward-misspecification objective (ReGap) derived from the implicit reward model formulation of DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024) and the RL-with-KL-constraint view of alignment (Mitchell et al., 2023). The optimization pipeline (stochastic beam search + generator finetuning) is inherited directly from AdvPrompter (Paulus et al., 2024), making the contribution primarily a new loss function rather than a new search procedure. Conceptually, the paper positions jailbreaking not as 'make the model output a specific harmful string' but as 'find prompts where the aligned model's implicit reward ranks harmful responses above harmless ones,' connecting adversarial vulnerability to a fundamental failure mode in reward modeling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ReGap metric offers a practical, model-agnostic diagnostic for safety auditing: it can flag prompts where alignment has broken down without requiring access to the explicit reward model, and its high AUROC (0.97 vs. 0.82 for target loss) makes it a more reliable signal for red teaming. The strong transferability to closed-source models (100% ASR@10 on GPT-3.5-turbo) and OOD tasks (HarmBench) suggests the underlying reward misspecification is a structural property of current alignment pipelines rather than an artifact of a specific model. Open questions include whether explicitly correcting reward misspecification during training would close these vulnerabilities, whether the approach extends to multimodal or agentic settings, and why Llama2-7b-chat remains notably resistant (reward gaps stay positive even under attack), which may point to more robust alignment techniques worth studying.
