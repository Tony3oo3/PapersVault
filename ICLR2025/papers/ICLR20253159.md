## Problem

While Chain of Thought (CoT) prompting is known to improve transformer expressiveness for multi-step reasoning, the training dynamics of transformers on CoT objectives remain largely unexplored. It is unclear whether a transformer trained with an autoregressive CoT objective can provably outperform one trained to output answers directly, and whether gradient-based optimization can actually reach the multi-step solution. This paper addresses both questions by studying the in-context weight prediction task for linear regression.

## Input / Output

The input is a matrix Z_0 ∈ R^{(2d+2)×(n+1)} stacking n input vectors x_i, their scalar labels y_i, an initial weight vector w_0 (set to zero), and a dummy token, with the example space and weight space separated into distinct row blocks. At inference, the transformer autoregressively generates k intermediate weight vectors (each a GD step) appended as new columns before producing the final d-dimensional weight vector prediction. The evaluation loss measures the ℓ₂ distance between the final generated weight and the ground-truth w* ~ N(0, I_d).

## Method

The paper analyzes a one-layer linear self-attention (LSA) transformer with residual connection, where the softmax is removed and key/query/value matrices are consolidated into two (2d+2)×(2d+2) matrices V and W. With CoT prompting, the transformer autoregressively generates k intermediate weight tokens, each implementing one gradient descent step on the linear regression objective, before outputting the final prediction. The key theoretical contribution is a stage-wise gradient flow convergence proof on the population CoT loss: a novel Wishart matrix concentration technique bounds interaction terms between eigenspaces, enabling a two-phase analysis (near-independent eigenvalue dynamics followed by local convergence) that shows the parameters converge to the multi-step GD construction in O(log d + log 1/ε) time. The paper further proves an expressiveness gap—without CoT the optimal solution is a single GD step with error Θ(d²/n), while with Θ(log d) CoT steps the error drops to O(1/poly(d))—and establishes out-of-distribution generalization to well-conditioned covariance shifts at inference.

## Results

Theoretically, a one-layer linear transformer without CoT is limited to a single step of gradient descent, incurring an irreducible evaluation loss of Θ(d²/n) (Corollary 3.1), which diverges as d→∞ when n=Θ̃(d). With CoT prompting and k=Θ(log d) autoregressive steps, the same one-layer transformer achieves an evaluation loss of O(1/poly(d)) (Theorem 3.2), representing a polynomial improvement. Empirically (d=10, n=20, η=0.4), the CoT-trained transformer's evaluation loss converges to near zero, while the no-CoT baseline plateaus at a substantially higher loss. The evaluation loss further decreases as the number of CoT steps k increases from 10 to 40, consistent with the theoretical prediction that larger k yields smaller error.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between the one-layer linear transformer with and without CoT prompting on the in-context weight prediction task. Without CoT (k=0), the global optimum implements exactly one step of GD with learning rate η*=n/(n+d+1), yielding a lower bound of Θ(d²/n) on the evaluation loss (Theorem 3.1, Corollary 3.1). With CoT (k=Θ(log d)), the transformer implements multi-step GD autoregressively and achieves O(1/poly(d)) loss (Theorem 3.2), a polynomially smaller error. In the empirical validation (Figure 2), the CoT model's evaluation loss converges to near zero while the no-CoT model's loss remains at the theoretical one-step GD floor. The paper also contrasts with Gatmiry et al. (2024), whose looped transformer achieves a final loss of d^{5/2}·L·4^L/√n in the ICL setting, which is asymptotically worse than the one-step GD bound Θ(d²/n) for large d, whereas the CoT approach in this paper provably outperforms the one-step baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) the line showing that one-layer linear transformers implement one-step gradient descent for in-context linear regression (Zhang et al., 2023; Mahankali et al., 2023; Ahn et al., 2023), and (2) the expressiveness results showing that CoT extends transformer computational power (Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023a; Li et al., 2024b; Feng et al., 2024). The closest prior work is Gatmiry et al. (2024), which proves a looped transformer can learn multi-step GD, but that work uses a single query-prediction loss and does not establish a performance gap over the one-step solution. This paper departs by formulating a CoT autoregressive training objective (sum of losses across intermediate steps) on a weight-prediction task, and by proving that gradient flow on this objective converges to a multi-step GD solution—bridging the expressiveness and optimization perspectives for the first time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical analysis showing that the CoT expressiveness gap is not merely architectural but *learnable*: a standard gradient-based optimizer on a one-layer transformer with CoT prompting provably converges to a multi-step GD solution, achieving O(1/poly(d)) error versus Θ(d²/n) without CoT. The result provides a mechanistic explanation for why CoT prompting works in practice—intermediate steps are not just a prompting trick but a learnable iterative computation. Key limitations include the use of population loss (no finite-sample guarantees), linear attention with a specific symmetric initialization, and restriction to iterative (non-compositional) tasks. Open questions include extending to finite-sample settings, practical softmax attention, and whether CoT enables compositional rather than purely iterative reasoning.
