## Problem

Prior multi-LLM agent research treats social intelligence as a black-box output problem, focusing on observable behaviors without investigating the internal cognitive mechanisms that drive them. The paper asks whether LLM Agents' systematic hallucinations—their structured deviations from factual accuracy—can be understood as functional analogs of human cognitive biases, and whether this irrationality constitutes a form of prosocial intelligence rather than a mere defect.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes structured prompt templates populated from six constructed datasets: Known/Unknown MCQ (100 factual or future-prediction questions each), Inform (100 short narratives), CogScene (100 social scenarios), CogAction (100 actions, a subset of CogScene), and CogIdentity (100 identity profiles with simple or complex attributes). Outputs are structured as Bias Rate metrics (Rate_Bqa for Q&A settings, Rate_Bmha for multi-agent interaction settings), computed as M/N where M is the count of bias-exhibiting responses determined by a configurable panel of evaluators (human raters, LLM evaluators, SimCSE semantic similarity scores, and dataset-based discriminators), and N is total inquiries across repetitions and distinct queries.

## Method

CogMir is a modular, open-ended multi-LLM agent framework that mirrors classic social science experiments by adapting their data collection methods into two interaction paradigms: Human-LLM Agent Q&A (for surveys/interviews) and Multi-Human-Agent Interaction (for group dynamics). The framework is organized around four objects (humans, LLM Agents, data, discriminators), two communication modes (Broadcast/Parallel and Point-to-Point/Series, modeled via information-theoretic channel capacity), and five interaction combinations (Single-H-Single-A, Single-H-Multi-A, Multi-H-Single-A, Multi-A, Multi-H-Multi-A). The core theoretical move is treating systematic hallucination as a controllable, interpretable variable analogous to cognitive bias, enabling the framework to probe social intelligence through seven cognitive bias subsets (Herd, Authority, Ben Franklin, Rumor Chain, Gambler's Fallacy, Confirmation Bias, Halo Effect) rather than treating agent behavior as an opaque output. Human participants in experiments are simulated via predefined scripted responses loaded into agent memory, allowing controlled manipulation of group pressure, authority status, and social context while the LLM Agent's response is the sole variable under test.

## Results

On the Herd Effect (Table 1), LLM Agents show dramatically different conformity depending on information certainty: GPT-4 achieves 99.9% conformity on Unknown MCQ with all humans wrong (7W) but 0% on Known MCQ under the same condition, while Claude-2.0 maintains ~99% conformity across all Unknown MCQ conditions. The human conformity benchmark from Asch (36%) is used as the bias threshold. On the Q&A Bias Rate (Table 2), Confirmation Bias is near-universal (98.9–100%) across all seven models, Ben Franklin Effect ranges from 66% (Mixtral-8x7b) to 89.7% (Mistral-medium), and Halo Effect varies widely from 4.3% (Claude-3.0-opus) to 100% (Mixtral-8x7b). Notably, LLMs exhibit *increased* conformity in the presence of dissenting opinions (7R, 7N), the opposite of human behavior where dissent reduces conformity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods or baselines in the traditional sense; instead, it benchmarks LLM Agent behavior against established human behavioral data from classic social science experiments (Asch 1951 for Herd Effect, Milgram 1963 for Authority Effect, etc.). The key human reference point is Asch's 36% conformity rate, used as the bias threshold. LLM Agents broadly match or exceed human conformity levels under uncertainty (e.g., GPT-4 at 99.9% vs. 36% human), but diverge sharply in two ways: (1) LLMs show *increased* conformity when a single dissenter is present (7R, 7N conditions), whereas humans show *decreased* conformity under the same condition; and (2) LLMs are far more sensitive to information certainty, with conformity dropping to near-zero on Known MCQ while remaining high on Unknown MCQ, a gap not observed in humans. Among the seven models tested, Claude-2.0 and Gemini-1.0-pro are the most consistently pro-social across all bias subsets, while Claude-3.0-opus is the most resistant to social influence (lowest Halo at 4.3%, moderate Herd at ~30% on Unknown MCQ). GPT-3.5 is the only model exhibiting the Gambler's Fallacy (93.3%), while all others correctly recognize statistical independence.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of LLM hallucination research, evolutionary psychology, and multi-agent social simulation. Prior multi-agent social simulation work (Sotopia, CAMEL, MetaGPT, etc.) treats LLM agent social behavior as a black box, evaluating outputs without explaining the internal cognitive mechanisms. The paper repurposes classic social science experimental paradigms (Asch conformity, Milgram obedience, rumor chain) and reframes LLM systematic hallucination as an analog to human cognitive bias, using it as an explanatory and controllable variable rather than merely a defect to be mitigated. It also extends existing social intelligence benchmarks (SocialIQA, ToMi, AgentBench) by introducing a structured, modular evaluation workflow grounded in social science methodology rather than static QA pairs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides the first systematic lens for explaining *why* LLM agents exhibit social intelligence, moving beyond behavioral description to mechanism. Key open questions include whether the observed prosocial irrationality persists under fine-tuning or RLHF, whether the framework generalizes to non-language modalities (the authors acknowledge this limitation), and whether the seven tested biases capture the full landscape of relevant social cognition. The finding that LLM agents show *increased* conformity under dissent (opposite of humans) and heightened sensitivity to certainty and social status raises important questions about the nature of LLM 'social' behavior and its divergence from genuinely human social cognition.
