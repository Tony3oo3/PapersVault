## Problem

RLHF-trained LLMs exhibit verbalized overconfidence, but the underlying cause has not been identified. This paper reveals that reward models used in PPO training carry a systematic bias toward high-confidence responses regardless of actual quality, which propagates overconfidence into the policy model during RLHF. No prior work had traced this bias to the reward modeling stage or proposed calibration methods that integrate directly into the PPO pipeline without requiring additional ground-truth labels.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard pairwise preference datasets (chosen/rejected response pairs) and augments them at training time by prepending a confidence-query system prompt and appending randomly generated confidence scores (0–3 for low, 7–10 for high) to each response, creating four variants per pair. No golden accuracy labels are needed. The output is a calibrated reward model (for PPO-M) or a dynamically adjusted reward signal (for PPO-C) that guides PPO training, producing a policy model that emits both a response and a verbalized confidence score on a 0–10 scale. For PPO-C, the confidence score is extracted from the model's own generation and used to modulate the reward in real time.

## Method

PPO-M introduces a calibrated reward modeling loss (Eq. 2) that fine-tunes an existing reward model on an augmented dataset where each preference pair is expanded into four comparisons: chosen+high-conf vs. chosen+low-conf (should prefer high) and rejected+high-conf vs. rejected+low-conf (should prefer low), thereby teaching the RM to align confidence with response quality rather than uniformly rewarding confidence. PPO-C avoids any reward model modification by adjusting the reward score during PPO training: it extracts the model's verbalized confidence, computes an unbiased reward by removing the confidence token, maintains an exponential moving average (α=0.1) of batch-mean rewards as a dynamic threshold, and scales the adjustment by w·(r̂ᵢ − Δr)·(sᵢ − 0.5) with w=2.0, so that overconfident responses above the running mean are penalized and underconfident ones below it are rewarded. Both methods plug into the standard OpenRLHF PPO pipeline with no architectural changes, and the CRM loss is further extended to DPO as CDPO (Eq. 4), which adds the same four-way confidence calibration terms to the standard DPO objective with a weighting coefficient w.

## Results

On Llama3-8B with Zero-Shot CoT prompting, PPO-M reduces ECE by 6.44 points (0.2566→0.1909) and increases accuracy by 2.73 points (0.7392→0.7703) on GSM8K compared to vanilla PPO. PPO-C achieves an even larger ECE reduction on GSM8K (0.2566→0.1546, a 39.8% decrease) while maintaining comparable accuracy (0.7635). Across all six evaluation datasets and both model families (Llama3-8B, Mistral-7B), both PPO-M and PPO-C consistently achieve lower ECE and higher AUC than vanilla PPO while preserving or improving accuracy. On MT-Bench and Arena-Hard, PPO-M scores 8.05/14.1 (Llama3-8B) and 7.95/9.9 (Mistral-7B), matching or slightly exceeding standard PPO (8.00/14.6 and 7.84/10.5 respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares PPO-M and PPO-C against vanilla PPO (standard RLHF with an uncalibrated reward model) and PPO† (an ablation that adds confidence-query prompts during PPO training but still uses the vanilla reward model). Both PPO-M and PPO-C consistently outperform vanilla PPO on calibration metrics: on Llama3-8B CoT, PPO-C reduces GSM8K ECE from 0.2566 to 0.1546 (−39.8%) and CommonsenseQA ECE from 0.1767 to 0.1166 (−34.0%), while PPO-M reduces GSM8K ECE to 0.1909 (−25.6%) and simultaneously improves accuracy from 0.7392 to 0.7703. PPO†, which only modifies the training prompts without calibrating the reward model, actually degrades calibration relative to vanilla PPO on several datasets (e.g., CommonsenseQA ECE worsens from 0.1767 to 0.2654), confirming that the reward model bias—not merely the presence of confidence prompts—is the root cause. On open-ended conversational benchmarks (MT-Bench, Arena-Hard), PPO-M matches or slightly exceeds PPO (8.05 vs 8.00 MT-Bench for Llama3-8B), while PPO-C is marginally lower (7.87 vs 8.00), indicating that calibration does not meaningfully sacrifice general instruction-following ability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RLHF pipeline (Ouyang et al., 2022; Schulman et al., 2017) by identifying a previously unexamined failure mode: reward models exhibit a systematic bias toward high-confidence verbalized responses regardless of actual quality. Prior LLM calibration work focused on logit-based temperature scaling, prompting strategies, or SFT with ground-truth accuracy labels, but did not investigate the reward model as a source of overconfidence. The paper's PPO-M and PPO-C methods modify the reward modeling and reward calculation stages of PPO respectively, and the CDPO extension (Eq. 4) adapts the calibrated loss to DPO's implicit reward formulation (Rafailov et al., 2024), positioning the work at the intersection of reward modeling and confidence calibration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By pinpointing reward model bias as the mechanism driving verbalized overconfidence in RLHF-trained LLMs, the paper offers a concrete, pipeline-integrated fix that requires no additional golden labels—important for open-ended generation where ground-truth accuracy is unavailable. However, the scope is limited to verbalized confidence (not logit-based calibration), and the PPO-C ablation reveals a trade-off between calibration quality and instruction-following performance as the scaling coefficient w increases. Open questions include whether the identified bias persists in larger or differently-architected reward models, whether the 25% confidence-query prompt ratio is optimal, and whether CDPO's slight accuracy degradation relative to DPO can be eliminated with task-specific hyperparameter tuning.
