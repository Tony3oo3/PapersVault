## Problem

LLM ensembling strategies that maximize output diversity (e.g., Mixture-of-Agents) and those that maximize output consistency (e.g., self-consistency decoding) each achieve strong results on different tasks, but neither is universally optimal. The paper addresses the open question of how to characterize and operationalize the trade-off between ensemble diversity and consistency, and how this trade-off varies across task types such as instruction following, arithmetic reasoning, and commonsense reasoning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252380/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The DMoA framework takes a natural-language query (optionally with few-shot chain-of-thought examples) and a table of per-model benchmark performance profiles (IFEval, MATH Lvl 5, GPQA, MuSR, MMLU-Pro) as input to a GPT-4o pre-processing function that identifies required skills and selects a 6-model ensemble. The output is a single synthesized text response produced by an aggregate-and-synthesize head model (Qwen2-72B-Instruct or Claude 3.5 Sonnet) that critically appraises all layer outputs. For the GMoA and mixture-optimization experiments, the input is a query with 5-shot CoT prompts and the output is a single answer evaluated against ground-truth labels on GSM8K, MATH, CSQA, ARC-C, ARC-E, AlpacaEval 2.0, and MT-Bench.

## Method

The paper introduces three complementary mechanisms. First, an EigenDivergence metric (adapted from the EigenScore hallucination-detection score) measures each output's contribution to ensemble semantic entropy in a shared sentence-embedding space; the Gated MoA (GMoA) uses this to filter the two most divergent outputs per layer, enforcing semantic consistency within a fixed heterogeneous mixture. Second, a greedy mixture-optimization algorithm iteratively replaces the lowest-impact model with a clone of the highest-impact model, revealing that optimizing for one task (e.g., arithmetic) degrades performance on others (e.g., instruction following). Third, the Dynamic Mixture of Agents (DMoA) operationalizes these insights at inference time: a GPT-4o router identifies task-specific skills from the query, selects a 6-model subset (with repetition allowed) predicted to excel at those skills, and the selected models feed into a multi-layer aggregate-and-synthesize pipeline, achieving state-of-the-art 91.85% normalized accuracy on Big Bench Hard.

## Results

On the Big Bench Hard (BBH) benchmark, DMoA/Sonnet achieves a new state-of-the-art normalized accuracy of 91.85% (93.12% raw accuracy), surpassing Claude 3.5 Sonnet (90.20% normalized). The open-source DMoA variant achieves 83.63% normalized accuracy, outperforming the static MoA ensemble (79.25%) by 4.38 points. Across instruction following, arithmetic, and commonsense reasoning benchmarks, DMoA achieves 63.21 on AlpacaEval 2.0, 96.67 on GSM8K, and 71.23 on MATH, outperforming GPT-4 Omni (05/13) on AlpacaEval, GSM8K, and MATH.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DMoA/Sonnet (91.85% normalized) surpasses the previous SOTA Claude 3.5 Sonnet (90.20%) by 1.65 points on BBH normalized accuracy. The open-source DMoA (83.63%) outperforms the static MoA ensemble (79.25%) by 4.38 points and the strongest individual model Llama-3-70B (78.28%) by 5.35 points on BBH. On the broader benchmark suite (Table 3), DMoA beats GPT-4 Omni (05/13) on AlpacaEval 2.0 (63.21 vs. 57.5), GSM8K (96.67 vs. 94.1), and MATH (71.23 vs. 61.2), but trails it on CSQA (87.51 vs. 88.6) and ARC-C (92.50 vs. 94.6). DMoA also outperforms the GMoA (divergence-filtered) variant across all benchmarks, confirming that dynamic model selection adds value beyond static filtering. All individual open-source models (Qwen1.5-72B/110B, Mixtral, WizardLM, DBRX) are substantially outperformed by both MoA and DMoA, with the largest gaps on MATH (e.g., 71.23 vs. 34.1 for Qwen1.5-72B) and AlpacaEval (63.21 vs. 25.4 for DBRX).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies and extends the Mixture of Agents (MoA) framework (Wang et al., 2024) and self-consistency decoding (Wang et al., 2022) under a single formalism for multi-layer LLM ensembling, showing that both are special cases of a general 'layer processing function' abstraction. It repurposes the EigenScore hallucination-detection metric from Chen et al. (2024a) as a semantic consistency filter (EigenDivergence) to gate outputs within a fixed heterogeneous mixture, and introduces a dynamic inference-time model-selection mechanism (DMoA) that goes beyond the static composition of prior MoA work. The key departure from prior ensembling literature is the explicit demonstration that neither maximal diversity (MoA's 'collaborativeness' hypothesis) nor maximal consistency (self-consistency) is universally optimal, and that the optimal balance is task-dependent.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The task-dependent diversity-consistency trade-off finding provides practitioners with a principled reason why a single ensemble configuration cannot dominate across instruction following, arithmetic reasoning, and commonsense reasoning simultaneously, and the DMoA framework offers a practical mechanism to navigate this trade-off at inference time. The connection to Goodhart's law (over-optimizing a proxy metric degrades unrelated capabilities) gives a theoretical grounding for the observed cross-task degradation. Key limitations include the reliance on GPT-4o as the routing pre-processor, the high inference cost of querying multiple large models per query, and the finding that ensembles cannot compensate for fundamental skill gaps in constituent models—meaning the approach's ceiling is bounded by the union of its members' capabilities. Open questions include whether the trade-off structure persists as model pools grow more homogeneous and whether the routing function can be learned end-to-end rather than prompted.
