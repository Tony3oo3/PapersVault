## Problem

Multi-agent reinforcement learning agents trained with conventional methods coordinate well only within their training cohorts but degrade sharply when paired with unfamiliar teammates or opponents, a core challenge in ad-hoc teaming. Existing Theory of Mind approaches in MARL rely heavily on Bayesian inference over beliefs and desires, while the attentional component of mental states—what agents are actually focusing on—remains largely unmodeled. This paper asks whether explicitly modeling and inferring other agents' attentional states can improve ad-hoc coordination.

## Input / Output

Each agent's observation is not raw coordinates but a set of gradient field (GF) representations—{gf_1, …, gf_N, gf_wall}—learned via Denoised Score Matching, where each gf encodes the score function of a data distribution over entity or boundary positions. The inverse attention network additionally consumes the past actions and past observations of same-type agents to predict their attention weights. The final output is a continuous 2D force vector (magnitude + direction) for the agent's action, with the intermediate inferred attention weights serving as the ToM signal that modulates the policy.

## Method

The Inverse-Attention Agent is trained in three phases. Phase 1 replaces the standard MLP policy with a self-attention structure over GF goal embeddings, so that explicit attention weights w_{i,1}…w_{i,N} determine the weighted-goal vector fed to the action head; during this phase, (weights, observation) pairs are logged. Phase 2 trains an inverse attention network IW offline on the logged data to predict attention weights from observations and past actions alone, effectively learning to 'read' another agent's attentional state. Phase 3 concatenates the agent's own attention weights with the inferred weights of same-type peers and passes them through a shallow fully-connected updater UW (initialized to identity on own weights and zero on inferred weights) to produce adjusted weights that drive the final action. The key architectural insight is that attention weights serve as a compact, interpretable proxy for an agent's goals, and the inverse network enables a form of perspective-taking—placing oneself in another agent's position to infer what they are attending to—without requiring communication or shared representations.

## Results

In the mix-and-match evaluation across five MPE environments (Spread, Adversary, Grassland, Navigation, Tag), Inverse-Att achieves the highest average reward in every setting. Headline numbers include 404.14 ± 5.40 in Spread (vs. 283.89 for Self-Att and 49.04 for ToM2C*), 497.96 ± 10.75 in Navigation (vs. 328.24 for Self-Att), and 109.81 ± 1.36 for Tag Wolf (vs. 67.60 for Self-Att). In competitive roles, Inverse-Att sheep suffer the least penalty (−16.91 in Adversary, 28.59 in Grassland) while Inverse-Att wolves earn the most (110.15 in Adversary, 101.21 in Grassland). The metric throughout is average cumulative reward over 1000 mix-and-match episodes of 200 steps each.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Inverse-Att outperforms all four baselines across every environment and role. Against MAPPO (Yu et al., 2022), the gap is largest in cooperative settings: 404.14 vs. 31.82 in Spread (12.7×) and 497.96 vs. 251.14 in Navigation (2.0×). Against IPPO (Schulman et al., 2017), Inverse-Att dominates by an even wider margin, e.g., 404.14 vs. 0.55 in Spread and 109.81 vs. 26.77 for Tag Wolf. Against MAA2C (Mnih et al., 2016), Inverse-Att leads in all settings, with the most striking advantage in Tag Wolf (109.81 vs. 59.41) and Grassland Sheep (28.59 vs. −70.79, flipping from negative to positive reward). Against ToM2C* (Wang et al., 2022), the closest ToM-based baseline, Inverse-Att still wins everywhere, e.g., 404.14 vs. 49.04 in Spread and 109.81 vs. 52.99 for Tag Wolf, confirming that explicit attention-weight inference surpasses goal-prediction-based ToM. The Self-Att ablation (no inverse attention) consistently ranks second, isolating the contribution of the inverse attention network as the primary source of the performance gain.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) Theory of Mind in multi-agent systems, where prior work has predominantly used Bayesian inference to model beliefs, desires, and intentions (Baker et al., 2009; Kleiman-Weiner et al., 2016; Shum et al., 2019) or shared-agency frameworks (Tang et al., 2020); (2) ad-hoc teaming, where agents must coordinate with previously unseen teammates (Stone et al., 2010; Barrett et al., 2017; Rabinowitz et al., 2018); and (3) gradient field representations for state encoding via denoised score matching (Long et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2022). The key departure is replacing Bayesian ToM with an end-to-end trained inverse attention network that models *attention* specifically (rather than beliefs/desires), and using gradient fields as the 'goals' over which attention is allocated, thereby unifying the cognitive modeling and state representation into a single differentiable pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a cognitively motivated, end-to-end trainable mechanism for ad-hoc coordination that consistently outperforms standard MARL baselines (MAPPO, IPPO, MAA2C) and a ToM-based baseline across cooperative, competitive, and mixed environments, with the inverse attention component adding a moderate but reliable improvement over self-attention alone. However, the evaluation is confined to simple 2D MPE particle worlds, the human study involves only five participants, and the method currently requires agents to be of the same type for attention inference. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to heterogeneous agent types, richer 3D or real-world environments, and whether the gradient field component (which accounts for much of the performance gap over baselines) can be replaced by lighter-weight representations without sacrificing the attention-based ToM benefits.
