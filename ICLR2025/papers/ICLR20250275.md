## Problem

Existing preference optimization methods such as DPO and IPO require paired preference data (a preferred and a dis-preferred example per query), limiting their use when only unpaired or one-sided feedback is available. Additionally, EM-based policy optimization methods maximize the likelihood of positive outcomes but have no principled mechanism for incorporating negative (dis-preferred) examples. This paper addresses both gaps by deriving an objective that can learn from positive-only, negative-only, or mixed unpaired feedback.

## Input / Output

The method consumes binary accept/reject labels on samples drawn from a reference policy (not paired comparisons), a reference policy π_ref, and a conditioning variable x (state, prompt, or document). Labels can come from human annotations, a reward model, or a learned Q/advantage function that classifies samples above or below a state-dependent baseline. The output is updated policy parameters θ; for autoregressive LLMs the KL regularizer is computed in closed form as a sum of per-token categorical KL divergences, while for continuous-control policies it uses Gaussian or categorical log-probabilities. No pairing constraint is imposed, so datasets can be arbitrarily unbalanced in the number of positive versus negative examples per condition.

## Method

PMPO (Preference-based MPO) derives its update rule from the EM perspective on KL-regularized policy optimization. The E-step yields a variational distribution q(y|x) ∝ π_ref(y|x)·p(S|y,x) that up-weights preferred samples; the M-step then performs weighted maximum likelihood on these samples. The key technical contribution is re-expressing q in terms of the complement event p(S'|y,x) = 1 − p(S|y,x), which algebraically produces an additional KL(π_ref‖π_θ) term in the M-step objective. The final loss (Eq. 10) combines three terms: α·E[log π_θ on accepted samples] − (1−α)·E[log π_θ on rejected samples] − β·KL(π_ref‖π_θ), where α trades off positive versus negative feedback and β controls the reference-policy anchor. The KL term is not an ad-hoc regularizer but emerges directly from the reparameterization, and the paper shows empirically that it is essential for stable learning from negative feedback alone (without it, the policy collapses). The approach requires no explicit preference model (unlike DPO's Bradley-Terry assumption) and naturally extends to multi-sample, unbalanced, and one-sided feedback settings.

## Results

On the RGB Stacking offline RL benchmark, combining all three loss signals (Accept + Reject + BC) achieves a reward of 93, substantially outperforming BC alone (24) and Accept+BC (26). The Reject+BC combination alone reaches 77, demonstrating that negative feedback alone can dramatically improve performance by masking bad data. On the DeepMind Control Suite, PMPO matches or outperforms the MPO baseline across humanoid and manipulation tasks, and even PMPO-R (negative feedback only) achieves competitive performance. In language alignment with Gemma 2B, PMPO-AR (using both accept and reject signals) learns faster than either signal in isolation and is competitive with DPO, with GPT-4 judging the PMPO fine-tuned model as preferred over the base model on held-out prompts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MPO (Abdolmaleki et al., 2018), PMPO matches or outperforms across all DeepMind Control Suite tasks (humanoid run/stand/walk, manipulator, etc.), with the key advantage that PMPO can additionally learn from negative-only feedback (PMPO-R) while MPO cannot. Against DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), the comparison is setting-dependent: in the bandit setting, DPO (using best/worst pairs) performs similarly to PMPO-AR; in the Control Suite with continuous Gaussian actions, DPO requires an extremely high implicit regularization parameter (β=20) leading to slow learning and suboptimal policies, whereas PMPO works with β=0.5; in language alignment, PMPO-AR is competitive with DPO but additionally handles unbalanced or single-type feedback data that DPO cannot naturally accommodate. The paper also notes that DPO's paired-data requirement makes it inapplicable when only positive or only negative examples are available per query, a setting where PMPO operates naturally.

## Relation to Prior Work

PMPO extends the EM-based policy optimization lineage (Dayan & Hinton, 1997; Peters et al., 2010; Abdolmaleki et al., 2018) by re-expressing the variational distribution in terms of dis-preference likelihoods, thereby incorporating negative examples into the M-step. This departs from DPO and IPO, which are designed around paired preference comparisons and cannot naturally handle unpaired, unbalanced, or single-type feedback. The paper also draws a formal connection to IPO, showing that PMPO regularizes the expectation of the log-ratio while IPO regularizes its variance, clarifying the relationship between these two popular approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ability to stably learn from negative feedback alone (with a KL anchor to the reference policy) opens practical avenues for safety-critical tasks, code generation, and other binary-outcome settings where positive examples are scarce or expensive to obtain. A key limitation is the requirement for a reliable KL estimate—ideally in closed form—which constrains applicability to models where per-token log-probabilities are accessible. Open questions include how the method behaves when the reference policy is far from optimal, whether the α/β trade-off can be made adaptive, and whether the negative-only learning capability extends to settings without a fixed reference (e.g., online exploration).
