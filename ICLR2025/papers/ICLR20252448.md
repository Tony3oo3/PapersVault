## Problem

Current LLM safety alignment follows a one-size-fits-all paradigm where models are trained to a fixed provider-defined policy, ignoring cultural variability, evolving social norms, and specialized user needs (e.g., game developers requiring violence, harassment trainers requiring discriminatory language identification). Re-aligning models for each user group is prohibitively expensive at modern LLM scale, and in-context alignment proves insufficient for complex, fine-grained safety configs. The paper asks: how can model safety be efficiently adapted at inference time to diverse requirements without re-training?

## Input / Output

The model receives a free-form natural language safety config (specifying which risk categories are allowed or disallowed) prepended to the system prompt, along with a user prompt, and produces a single text response that should be both helpful and safe under that config. During training, configs are generated from 8 categorical risk categories via 10 hand-written templates (yielding 2,560 unique configs), but at inference time the config can be any natural language description, enabling generalization to unseen safety requirements. The evaluation protocol scores each response on two axes: a continuous helpfulness score h∈[0,1] and a binary configured-safety score f∈{+1,−1}, combined multiplicatively into the CoSA-Score.

## Method

CoSAlign is a data-centric pipeline that first derives an 8-category risk taxonomy from training prompts via UMAP+HDBSCAN clustering and LLM summarization, then synthesizes diverse config-prompt pairs by assigning each prompt multiple config risk categories covering four set-relationship types (no risk, strict subset, superset, non-subset) and rendering them into natural language through 10 templates. Responses are generated from both a safe and a safety-removed LLM, scored by an error-scoring mechanism that penalizes allowed risks by α=0.1, disallowed risks by β=3, and non-addressed prompts by γ=1 (ensuring α<γ<β), and paired by error score to form preference data (122K pairs total). The model is then trained in two stages: supervised fine-tuning on chosen responses followed by DPO on chosen/rejected pairs, producing a controllable model M_ctrl whose safety behavior is governed entirely by the config in its system prompt. The key finding is that in-context alignment saturates around 15 shots and even degrades performance on instruction-hierarchy-trained models, while CoSAlign substantially outperforms all baselines including cascade filtering with oracle evaluators.

## Results

On CoSAlign-Test, L3.1-8B-INST+CoSAlign achieves a CoSA-Score of 0.408 on seen configs and 0.293 on unseen configs, substantially outperforming the best in-context alignment baseline (0.217/0.141) and the strongest cascade baseline with oracle filtering (0.306/0.230). On the human-evaluated CoSApien benchmark, CoSAlign reaches 0.597 CoSA-Score with 77.0% helpful+safe responses, surpassing even the Cascade-Oracle method (0.580). General capability degradation is minimal: MMLU drops only 0.06 points and MT-Bench drops 1.7 points after CoSAlign fine-tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CoSAlign significantly outperforms in-context alignment (ICA; Han 2023; Lin 2024) across all model variants: on L3.1-8B-INST, CoSAlign achieves 0.408 CoSA-Score versus 0.217 for ICA-5Shot on seen configs (an 87% relative improvement), and 0.293 vs. 0.141 on unseen configs. The paper also shows that ICA actually *degrades* controllability on GPT-4o-mini (0.281 → 0.251), suggesting that instruction hierarchy training alone is insufficient for complex safety configs. Against cascade methods (which filter unsafe responses post-hoc using GPT-4o as an oracle evaluator), CoSAlign still wins: 0.408 vs. 0.306 on seen configs and 0.293 vs. 0.230 on unseen configs for the L3.1-8B-INST/SFT variants. On the human-evaluated CoSApien benchmark, CoSAlign (0.597) surpasses even Cascade-Oracle (0.580), indicating that internalizing safety config adherence through preference optimization is more effective than external filtering. The DPO stage is critical: SFT-only CoSAlign on L3.1-8B-INST achieves 0.238 CoSA-Score with 17.2% helpful+unsafe, while full CoSAlign (SFT+DPO) reaches 0.408 with only 5.2% helpful+unsafe.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant one-size-fits-all safety alignment paradigm (Constitutional AI, fixed usage policies) by training a single model to follow free-form natural language 'safety configs' at inference time, eliminating the need for re-training per user group. Methodologically, CoSAlign builds on DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) for preference optimization and LLM-as-a-judge (Zheng et al., 2023) for synthetic data generation, while leveraging safety-removed model variants (Zhan et al., 2024) as diverse response generators. The paper explicitly positions itself against in-context alignment (ICA), showing it is insufficient for complex safety configs, and against instruction hierarchy (IH), which reduces prompt injection risk but does not improve controllability. It is the first focused effort on pluralistic safety alignment specifically, as opposed to broader pluralistic alignment or cultural alignment work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practical gap: organizations with specialized safety needs (game developers, prosecutors, regional publishers) currently face either overly restrictive models or prohibitively expensive re-alignment. The CoSApien benchmark and CoSA-Score evaluation protocol are the first general-purpose tools for measuring safety controllability, filling a clear evaluation gap. Key limitations include: the approach is tested only at 8B scale, is restricted to safety norms describable in natural language (excluding implicit cultural norms), and remains vulnerable to prompt injection attacks that could alter safety configs. Open questions include whether controllability scales to larger models, how to handle more fine-grained 'partial' prompts where only part of a request is safe, and how to combine CoSAlign with instruction hierarchy for robust deployment.
