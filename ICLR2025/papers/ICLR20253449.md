## Problem

While randomized algorithms are well-established as superior to deterministic ones in adversarial settings, it was unknown whether transformers could learn to leverage input-provided randomness through optimization. Standard expected-risk minimization (ERM) training provably does not incentivize randomization, leaving open whether any practical objective could induce transformers to implement genuinely randomized strategies that improve worst-case robustness.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253449/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each task provides structured tokens (key-value pairs, vertex one-hot encodings, or grid position/reward embeddings) concatenated with a Random Seed Encoding (RSE) — a vector of random bits or Gaussian noise appended to every token. The transformer outputs a probability distribution over discrete decisions (binary value bits, 3-color assignments, or 4-direction actions); the final prediction is extracted via argmax or sampling. A key evaluation detail is that performance is measured both per-seed and via majority voting across m independent seeds, which amplifies success probability far beyond any single deterministic run.

## Method

The paper introduces a q-norm adversarial training objective (Eq. 5) that minimizes the q-th root of the average over inputs of the q-th power of the expected loss over m random seeds, interpolating between ERM (q=1) and strict min-max (q=∞). Randomness is injected via a Random Seed Encoding concatenated to input tokens, and the transformer architecture (linear self-attention for recall, masked attention for graph coloring, standard attention for grid worlds) is trained with gradient descent or parameter-exploring policy gradients (PEPG) under this objective. Theoretical analysis (Proposition 1) proves that when multiple optimal deterministic strategies exist with disjoint worst-case inputs, a randomized mixture strictly dominates any single deterministic choice. Experiments across three tasks show that increasing q past a threshold (~10–16) triggers a phase transition from degenerate (deterministic) to properly randomized behavior, and majority voting across seeds then boosts worst-case success to near-optimal levels.

## Results

On the associative recall task (N=20, M<N), training with q=100 and m=30 seeds yields a randomized transformer whose majority-vote recall is near-optimal on all inputs, while the deterministic baseline's 95th-percentile recall drops to zero for sequence lengths above ~8. On the 3-coloring task (N=10 cycle), increasing q from 1 to 10 causes $A_R^q$-majority to reach close to 100% success probability across all possible cycles, whereas at q=1 all four model variants perform identically with binary (all-or-nothing) success. On the grid world exploration task, $A_R^q$ with m=10 seeds matches $A_R^E$ on average reward while drastically outperforming it on adversarial (worst-case treasure) positions, and the agent's first-visit times become approximately uniform over cells rather than following a fixed deterministic trajectory.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four transformer variants: $A_{r_0}^E$ (ERM, single fixed seed), $A_{r_0}^q$ (adversarial loss, single fixed seed), $A_R^E$ (ERM, multi-seed input), and $A_R^q$ (adversarial loss, multi-seed input). The central finding is that ERM training (q=1) does not induce randomization regardless of whether multiple seeds are provided: $A_R^E$ collapses to deterministic predictions identical to $A_{r_0}^E$ across all three tasks. The adversarial q-norm loss with q>1 is necessary to trigger proper input-level randomization, with a phase transition observed around q≈16 for associative recall and q>3 for graph coloring. Among the randomized variants, $A_R^q$-majority (majority voting over m seeds) consistently outperforms $A_{r_0}^q$-sampled (output-level stochasticity from a single seed), demonstrating that input-level randomization combined with repetition is strictly superior to output-level sampling. The single-seed adversarial model $A_{r_0}^q$ also underperforms the multi-seed $A_R^q$, confirming that m>1 is essential for the optimization to discover a distribution over strategies rather than a single robust point.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges the classic randomized algorithms literature (Yao's minimax principle, paging, distributed graph coloring) with deep learning by proposing a q-norm relaxed adversarial training objective, directly inspired by Rice et al. (2021) on robustness between worst and average case. It departs from standard ERM and from Bayesian/ensemble methods where randomness is an intentional architectural design choice, instead showing that randomness can emerge as a learned property of the weights when the objective penalizes worst-case performance over multiple seeds. The random seed encoding (RSE) mechanism, analogous to positional encodings, is a new input-level randomization channel distinct from output-level sampling or dropout-style stochasticity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that standard optimization (gradient descent or evolutionary strategies) can discover randomized algorithms inside transformer weights purely from an adversarial objective, without any explicit randomization in the architecture. The practical impact is currently limited by the m× computational overhead and the small-scale, conceptual tasks studied, but the q-norm objective is a simple drop-in modification that could be applied to larger adversarial or distributionally robust settings. Key open questions include whether the approach scales to practical problems, how it interacts with output-level stochasticity (sampling from policies), and whether the learned randomization corresponds to known randomized algorithm structures or something genuinely new.
