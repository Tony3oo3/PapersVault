## Problem

Representational similarity measures have been proposed in an ad hoc manner across the literature, each justified by different desiderata, yet no comprehensive benchmark exists to systematically compare them against well-defined ground truths. The fundamental challenge is that model difference is multifaceted—accuracy, robustness, feature reliance, training data—so no single ground truth applies universally, making it unclear which measure is appropriate for a given application.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250675/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes pairs of final-hidden-layer representation matrices R ∈ ℝ^{N×D} extracted from trained models (N=1,000–10,000 instances depending on dataset, D varying by architecture), and produces scalar similarity scores per model pair. Evaluation outputs are structured as Spearman correlations (for prediction-grounded tests) and AUPRC/conformity rates (for design-grounded tests), aggregated into per-measure rank distributions across all test–dataset–architecture combinations. Representations are extracted at locations where no residual connection bypasses the extraction point, and for CNNs in the layer monotonicity test, spatial dimensions are folded into the channel dimension or subsampled to manage memory.

## Method

ReSi introduces six tests that establish ground-truth similarity through two complementary approaches: prediction grounding (correlating representational similarity with accuracy differences or per-instance output disagreement measured by JSD) and design grounding (training groups of models under controlled conditions—randomized labels, shortcut features, augmentation levels, or layer depth—and requiring measures to distinguish intra- from inter-group similarity). The benchmark evaluates 24 measures spanning six categories (CCA, alignment, RSM, neighborhood, topology, and descriptive statistics) across 14 architectures (GCN, GraphSAGE, GAT, P-GNN, BERT, ALBERT, SmolLM2, ResNet18/34/101, VGG11/19, ViT-B/32, ViT-L/32) on 7 datasets in graph, language, and vision domains. For design-grounded tests, five models per group are trained with varying seeds, and performance is quantified via AUPRC treating representational similarity as a decision score for predicting group membership. The key finding is that no single measure dominates across all tests and domains, with domain-specific preferences emerging (e.g., neighborhood-based measures for graphs, CKA for language, Jaccard similarity for vision).

## Results

The ReSi benchmark evaluates 24 representational similarity measures across six tests (correlation to accuracy difference, correlation to JSD, label randomization, shortcut affinity, augmentation, and layer monotonicity) on graph, language, and vision domains. No single measure dominates: CKA ranks 1st in language, 8th in vision, and 11th in graphs when aggregating across all tests. In the vision domain (ResNet18 on ImageNet100), Jaccard similarity, CKA, and DistCorr achieve AUPRC scores of 1.00 on label randomization and shortcut affinity, while DistCorr and RSA reach 0.97 on layer monotonicity. In the graph domain (GraphSAGE on Flickr), neighborhood-based measures (2nd-Cos, Jaccard, RankSim) and DistCorr lead on prediction-grounded tests, with DistCorr achieving 0.42** Spearman correlation with accuracy difference.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark compares 24 similarity measures (spanning CCA, alignment, RSM, neighborhood, topology, and descriptive-statistics families) across six grounded tests. In the vision domain, Jaccard similarity (Wang et al., 2022) emerges as the overall best-performing measure, achieving 1.00 AUPRC on both label randomization and shortcut affinity, outperforming the widely-used CKA (Kornblith et al., 2019) which scores 1.00 on the same tests but only 0.87 on layer monotonicity versus Jaccard's 0.55. DistCorr (Székely et al., 2007) and RSA (Kriegeskorte et al., 2008) lead on layer monotonicity in vision (0.97 each), while PWCCA (Morcos et al., 2018) achieves perfect 1.00 layer separation in graph and language domains but collapses to 0.11 in vision. In the graph domain, neighborhood-based measures (2nd-Cos, Jaccard, RankSim) and DistCorr correlate best with prediction differences, whereas in language, CKA and DistCorr are preferred. Orthogonal Procrustes (Ding et al., 2021) consistently ranks in the top 50% across all domains, while prominent measures like SVCCA (Raghu et al., 2017) and RSA do not stand out in general. The key finding is a 'No Free Lunch' result: no measure or measure family dominates across all tests and domains, with CKA ranking 1st in language but 11th in graphs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by providing the first comprehensive, multi-domain benchmark for representational similarity measures, building on the taxonomy from Klabunde et al. (2023) and the individual grounding strategies proposed by Ding et al. (2021) (prediction-grounded) and Kornblith et al. (2019) (design-grounded). Prior comparative efforts were limited to a handful of measures (Ding et al., 2021; Hayne et al., 2024) or a single domain, whereas ReSi unifies 24 measures across six tests, 14 architectures, and seven datasets spanning graphs, language, and vision. The paper repurposes existing grounding ideas—accuracy correlation, output disagreement, label randomization, shortcut affinity, augmentation, and layer monotonicity—into a standardized evaluation protocol rather than proposing new similarity measures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that no single measure dominates across tests and domains ('No Free Lunch')—challenges the common practice of defaulting to CKA or SVCCA without justification, and the unexpected top performance of simple Jaccard similarity in vision undermines assumptions about which measure families are most appropriate. The paper identifies a clear need for community best practices in measure selection and preprocessing, and highlights that seemingly minor choices (e.g., unit-norm normalization in Procrustes) substantially alter what properties are captured. Key limitations include evaluation restricted to last-layer representations, single-modality settings, and exclusion of some existing measures and preprocessing variants, leaving open questions about cross-layer validity, multimodal alignment, and the systematic impact of hyperparameters.
