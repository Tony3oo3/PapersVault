## Problem

While LLM-as-a-Judge has become a standard evaluation method, prior bias analyses were narrow in scope, relied on costly and subjective human evaluators to establish ground truth, and used limited test data that risked obscuring true bias magnitudes. No comprehensive, fully automated framework existed to systematically quantify the full spectrum of biases that can undermine LLM judge reliability across diverse judging scenarios.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253680/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a judgment prompt P=(I, Q, R) or P=(I, Q, R₁, R₂) and applies an automated perturbation g(·) to either the response or the system instruction, producing a modified prompt P̂. The LLM judge is executed on both P and P̂, and the outputs y and ŷ are compared to compute per-bias Robustness Rate, Consistency Rate, Accuracy Improvement Rate (for CoT bias), and Error Rates for self-enhancement and refinement-aware bias. A derived Bias Impact Score (BIS = RR − CR) isolates the bias-specific degradation from baseline model inconsistency.

## Method

CALM employs an attack-and-detect paradigm: an LLM (GPT-4o) performs principle-guided modifications on the content being judged, following the Constitutional AI approach, to inject a specific bias while preserving the response's factual correctness and core meaning. Twelve distinct bias types are defined, each with tailored modification guidelines (e.g., lengthening a response for verbosity, inserting fake citations for authority, adding emotional language for sentiment). Three purpose-built datasets—fact-related (GSM8K, MATH, ScienceQA), alignment (five DPO datasets), and refinement-aware (CommonsenseQA, Quora-QuAD, TruthfulQA)—are matched to bias types based on whether the bias pertains to answer content, semantic logic, or instruction-level influence. Judging is conducted via pairwise comparison for most biases and single-answer scoring for self-enhancement and refinement-aware bias, with six judge models (ChatGPT, GPT-4-Turbo, GPT-4o, Claude-3.5, GLM-4, Qwen2-72B) evaluated against four separate generation models to avoid self-enhancement confounding. The key design choice is full automation of bias injection via LLM-guided perturbation, eliminating human subjectivity and enabling scalable, reproducible bias quantification.

## Results

Across 12 bias types, Claude-3.5 demonstrates the strongest overall robustness on alignment tasks (e.g., position RR 0.832, diversity RR 0.914), while GPT-4o leads on fact-related tasks (verbosity RR 0.977, fallacy-oversight RR 0.984). Sentiment bias is the most pervasive vulnerability, with all models scoring below 0.81 RR (ChatGPT 0.804, GPT-4-Turbo 0.653, Qwen2 0.651). Position bias is also severe: ChatGPT drops to 0.566 RR, and most models fall below 0.5 when evaluating 3–4 answer candidates. CoT prompting yields positive AIR for all models (0.008–0.070), confirming it improves judgment accuracy, while self-enhancement error rates are positive for 5 of 6 models (up to 9.40% for GPT-4-Turbo).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark/evaluation paper rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison; the 'comparison' is across six LLM judge models (ChatGPT, GPT-4-Turbo, GPT-4o, GLM-4, Claude-3.5, Qwen2-72B) on their robustness to 12 bias types. Claude-3.5 achieves the highest alignment-dataset robustness on position (0.832), compassion-fade (0.875), distraction (0.878), and diversity (0.914), but is notably weakest on bandwagon-effect (0.610). GPT-4o dominates fact-related robustness (verbosity 0.977, fallacy 0.984) and has the highest consistency rate on alignment (0.925). ChatGPT is the most vulnerable to position bias (0.566 RR) yet the most robust to sentiment bias (0.804). No single model is uniformly best: GPT-4-Turbo leads on authority (0.846) and fallacy (0.969) but lags on sentiment (0.653) and bandwagon (0.638). The paper concludes that model selection for LLM-as-a-Judge should be bias-type-specific rather than relying on overall model capability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper consolidates and extends the fragmented line of work on LLM-as-a-Judge biases—where prior studies each identified one or two specific biases (position bias in Zheng et al., 2024; bandwagon and compassion-fade in Koo et al., 2023; authority bias in Chen et al., 2024d)—into a unified 12-bias taxonomy. The automated perturbation mechanism draws directly on the principle-guided modification approach from Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022), repurposing it for bias injection rather than harmlessness training. The evaluation protocol (pairwise comparison and scoring prompts) is adapted from MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2024) and AlignBench (Liu et al., 2023a), but the key methodological departure is replacing human ground-truth annotation with automated, LLM-driven perturbations, eliminating the cost and subjectivity that limited prior bias studies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CALM provides the first comprehensive, fully automated framework for quantifying LLM judge reliability across 12 bias dimensions, making it a practical tool for anyone deploying LLM-as-a-Judge in production or research. The finding that bias impact is substantially larger on alignment datasets (where answer quality gaps are small) than on fact-related datasets offers a concrete, actionable guideline for practitioners choosing evaluation scenarios. Open questions remain: whether the 12-bias taxonomy is exhaustive, whether the automated perturbations fully capture real-world bias triggers, and whether the framework extends naturally to multimodal or agentic judging settings. The paper also raises a philosophical point—that absolute objectivity in LLM judgment may be unattainable—shifting the goal from eliminating bias to bounding it to acceptable levels.
