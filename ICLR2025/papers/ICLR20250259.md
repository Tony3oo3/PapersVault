## Problem

Current quantum multi-agent reinforcement learning (QMARL) frameworks rely entirely on classical communication—shared replay buffers, centralized critics, and explicit observation sharing—to coordinate decentralized agents, leaving the quantum channel and entanglement between agents as untapped cooperative resources. No prior work has used entanglement over a quantum channel as a coordination medium to couple agent policies during training, which would eliminate the need for local observation sharing and reduce classical communication overhead.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250259/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each agent receives D=4 entangled qubits via a quantum channel from a central server and encodes its local environment observation (a sparse 4×3×3 binary matrix for CoinGame MDP, a 4-dimensional real vector for CartPole, or a 7×7×3 integer matrix for MiniGrid) into those qubits using a local VQC. The output is a discrete action per agent (e.g., {north, south, east, west} for CoinGame) and a joint value estimate V(o) computed via a single joint Pauli-Z measurement across all N×D qubits at the central server. Critically, agents never transmit their raw observations or intermediate activations to each other or to the server; only scalar rewards and a single partial gradient value are sent classically.

## Method

eQMARL deploys a split quantum critic where each agent hosts a branch of a variational quantum circuit (VQC) with L=5 cascaded layers of variational rotations, circular CZ entanglement, and observation encoding. The branches are coupled at the input by pairwise Bell-state entanglement (specifically Ψ⁺) prepared by a central server and distributed over a quantum channel, so that the agent VQCs operate on entangled qubits without any explicit observation exchange. At the split point, a joint measurement of the observable O = ⊗Z_d across all N×D qubits yields the joint value estimate V(o) ≈ w(1+⟨O⟩_ψ)/2, where w is the only centralized trainable parameter. Training follows MAA2C with split backpropagation: the central server computes the critic loss gradient at the split point and transmits it classically to each agent, which then backpropagates locally through its own VQC branch. This design requires only 1 centralized parameter (vs. 25 in the split classical baseline) and eliminates all observation sharing while achieving up to 17.8% faster convergence to cooperative strategies.

## Results

On CoinGame-2, eQMARL-Ψ⁺ converges 16.2% faster to a score of 20 (MDP) and 17.8% faster to a score of 25 (POMDP) compared to the next-best baseline (qfCTDE), while also achieving a 1.4% higher peak score than sCTDE in MDP. On multi-agent CartPole, eQMARL-Ψ⁺ reaches mean and max reward thresholds 12.2% and 31.5% faster than qfCTDE (MDP), and 9.1% faster on mean reward (POMDP). On MiniGrid (POMDP), eQMARL-Ψ⁺ achieves a mean reward of approximately −13 versus ≈−100 for all baselines (a 4.5× improvement), and reaches the goal in 9 steps versus 17 for fCTDE, while qfCTDE and sCTDE fail to reach the goal within the 50-step limit. In terms of model size, eQMARL requires only 1 centralized trainable parameter versus 25 for sCTDE (a 25× reduction).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares eQMARL-Ψ⁺ against three baselines: fCTDE (fully centralized classical CTDE), sCTDE (split classical CTDE), and qfCTDE (quantum fully centralized CTDE). Against qfCTDE, eQMARL wins on convergence speed across all environments: 16.2% faster to score 20 (CoinGame MDP), 17.8% faster to score 25 (CoinGame POMDP), 12.2% and 31.5% faster to mean/max reward (CartPole MDP), and 9.1% faster to mean reward (CartPole POMDP), while also exhibiting significantly lower variance. Against sCTDE, eQMARL converges 10.8% faster to score 25 (CoinGame MDP) and uses 25× fewer centralized parameters (1 vs. 25), though sCTDE achieves a slightly higher peak score in MDP CoinGame. Against fCTDE, eQMARL dominates on MiniGrid, reaching the goal in 9 steps versus 17 for fCTDE, while fCTDE, qfCTDE, and sCTDE all cluster near a mean reward of −100 (spinning in place) versus eQMARL's ≈−13. Overall, eQMARL outperforms or matches all three baselines on every environment tested, with the largest margins in POMDP settings and the MiniGrid navigation task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CTDE actor-critic paradigm (Gupta et al., 2017; Papoudakis et al., 2021) and split learning (Vepakomma et al., 2018) into the quantum domain, using VQC architectures from Jerbi et al. (2021) and Skolik et al. (2021). All prior QMARL works (Yun et al., 2022a; 2023b; Chen, 2023; Park et al., 2023; Kölle et al., 2024) coordinate agents exclusively through classical channels—shared replay buffers, centralized critics, or classical activation transmission—treating quantum circuits as drop-in replacements for classical NNs. eQMARL departs fundamentally by using quantum entanglement over a quantum channel as the coordination medium itself, splitting the critic VQC across agents and coupling their input qubits via Bell-state entanglement, which eliminates the need for any local observation sharing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that implicit quantum coupling can match or exceed the performance of fully centralized approaches that have access to all agent observations, while requiring 25× fewer centralized parameters and no observation transmission—a meaningful privacy and communication-efficiency gain for distributed MARL. However, all experiments run on classical hardware simulating quantum circuits (tensorflow-quantum/cirq) with only 2 agents and 4 qubits per agent, so scalability to larger agent counts, deeper circuits, or real NISQ hardware remains entirely open. The computational cost (~3.5–8.5 hours per run vs. ~5–7 minutes for classical baselines) is a significant practical barrier, and whether the entanglement advantage persists beyond toy environments or with more than two agents is untested.
