## Problem

Existing synthesis-oriented generative models for drug design must restrict their building block libraries to a few thousand compounds to keep the action space computationally tractable, which limits chemical diversity and forces longer synthetic pathways that reduce yield and increase cost. This paper addresses how to train generative flow networks over the full Enamine catalog of ~1.2 million building blocks and 71 reaction templates without prohibitive memory or compute overhead, while preserving the ability to adapt to modified or expanded block libraries without retraining.

## Input / Output

The method takes a protein pocket structure (encoded via a K-NN residual graph with GVP-GNN), a building block library (Enamine catalog, ~1.2M RDKit-readable blocks), and a set of 71 reaction templates (13 uni-molecular, 58 bi-molecular, doubled to 116 for reactant ordering) as inputs. It outputs a complete synthetic pathway—a sequence of up to 3 reaction steps assembling purchasable building blocks into a final molecule—rather than just a molecular graph. The pathway guarantees synthesizability by construction, and the model can generate in zero-shot mode for arbitrary pockets or adapt to restricted/expanded block libraries at inference time without retraining.

## Method

RXNFLOW formulates molecular generation as a GFlowNet over a non-hierarchical MDP where each action jointly selects a reaction template and a building block (rather than the hierarchical template-then-block decomposition used by prior work), enabling consistent flow estimation when the block library is modified. The core technical contribution is an action space subsampling technique: at each transition, only a 1% random subset of building blocks is evaluated, and the state flow is estimated via importance-weighted summation over the sampled subset, reducing complexity from O(|B||R₂|) to O(|B*||R₂|) while remaining unbiased. Building blocks are embedded in a continuous action space using ECFP4 fingerprints, MACCS keys, and physicochemical properties, allowing the model to generalize to unseen blocks. The model uses a graph transformer backbone with an MLP action head, trained with the trajectory balance objective and a 5% random-action probability to ensure rare action categories are explored during training.

## Results

On the CrossDocked2020 pocket-conditional generation benchmark, RXNFLOW achieves a state-of-the-art average Vina score of −8.85 kcal/mol (median −9.03) with 34.8% synthesizability and 0.67 QED, outperforming all atom-based and fragment-based baselines while matching the synthesizability of known active ligands (36.1%). On pocket-specific optimization across 15 LIT-PCBA targets, RXNFLOW achieves the best hit ratio (up to 71.25% on ESR_ago), best Vina scores (down to −11.45 kcal/mol on ADRB2), highest synthesizability (up to 71.25%), and lowest synthetic complexity (1.95 average steps) compared to all reaction-based and fragment-based baselines. Generation time is only 4 seconds for 100 molecules, comparable to the fragment-based TacoGFN.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RXNFLOW outperforms all fragment-based and atom-based baselines on the CrossDocked2020 benchmark in Vina score, QED, and synthesizability simultaneously. Against the strongest atom-based model MolCRAFT-large (Vina −9.25), RXNFLOW trades ~0.4 kcal/mol in docking score for a 9× improvement in synthesizability (34.8% vs 3.9%) and a 0.22 QED gain, making it far more practical for wet-lab validation. Against the fragment-based GFlowNet TacoGFN, RXNFLOW improves Vina by 0.61 kcal/mol, synthesizability by 33.5 percentage points, and diversity by 0.14 Tanimoto distance, while matching QED. On pocket-specific optimization (LIT-PCBA), RXNFLOW dominates all reaction-based baselines (SynFlowNet, RGFN, SynNet, BBAR) on every metric: e.g., on ADRB2 it achieves 60.25% hit ratio vs 52.75% (SynFlowNet) and 46.75% (RGFN), with 2.42 average synthesis steps vs 2.64 (SynFlowNet) and 2.88 (RGFN). The key advantage over SynFlowNet and RGFN is that RXNFLOW uses 1.2M building blocks with only 3 reaction steps, whereas those methods use 6,000 and 350 blocks with 4 steps, resulting in lower synthetic complexity and higher synthesizability.

## Relation to Prior Work

RxnFlow extends the reaction-based GFlowNet line of work initiated by SynFlowNet (Cretu et al., 2024) and RGFN (Koziarski et al., 2024), which formulated synthetic pathway generation as a Markov decision process trained with trajectory balance. The key departures are: (1) an action space subsampling technique that reduces computational complexity from O(|B||R₂|) to O(|B*||R₂|), enabling the use of 1.2M Enamine building blocks versus the 350–6,000 blocks in prior work; (2) a non-hierarchical MDP that jointly selects reaction templates and reactant blocks, replacing the sequential template-then-block formulation; and (3) the first application of synthesis-oriented GFlowNets to pocket-conditional (zero-shot) generation, building on TacoGFN's pocket-conditioning framework. The method also inherits action embedding from Gottipati et al. (2020) and Seo et al. (2023) to represent building blocks in continuous space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RxnFlow addresses a critical practical gap: most generative drug design models produce molecules that cannot be readily synthesized, limiting wet-lab validation. By constraining generation to predefined reaction templates and purchasable building blocks, the framework guarantees synthesizability by construction while still achieving state-of-the-art docking scores (−8.85 kcal/mol on CrossDocked2020) and competitive diversity. The non-hierarchical MDP and action embedding enable practical adaptability—new objectives (e.g., solubility constraints) and newly discovered building blocks can be incorporated without retraining, which is highly relevant to real-world drug discovery pipelines. Open questions include whether 3D interaction modeling (shown to help in a preliminary experiment) can be integrated more deeply, whether the uniform subsampling policy can be replaced with learned importance sampling for better exploitation, and how the approach scales to even larger virtual libraries or multi-step cascades beyond 3 reactions.
