## Problem

Despite transformers' empirical success across modalities, it remains unclear what inductive biases distinguish them from other architectures like CNNs, MLPs, and LSTMs. Prior work identified a low-sensitivity bias for transformers on Boolean inputs, but sensitivity had not been extended to real-valued data or validated as a unified simplicity metric across vision and language tasks. This paper asks whether an appropriate extension of sensitivity explains the inductive bias of transformers across diverse data modalities and whether this bias has practical implications for robustness, generalization, and training dynamics.

## Input / Output

The sensitivity metric operates on tokenized inputs: images are split into T patches (e.g., 7×7 patches for CIFAR-10), text sequences are represented as sub-word embeddings with positional encoders, and synthetic/Boolean inputs are one-hot token vectors. For each token, an isotropic Gaussian perturbation ξ ~ N(0, σ²I) is injected (σ² tuned per dataset: 1 for CIFAR-10, 15 for ImageNet-1k and language tasks, 5 for Fashion-MNIST), and sensitivity is the fraction of tokens whose perturbation flips the sign of the model's prediction. The output is a scalar sensitivity score per model, alongside standard class predictions and corruption-robustness accuracies on CIFAR-10-C.

## Method

The paper introduces a unified token-wise sensitivity metric (Definition 3.1) that replaces each token with a Gaussian-perturbed version and measures prediction flips, deliberately using local perturbations rather than random token replacement to respect the metric structure of the input space. Theoretically, the authors prove (Proposition 2.1) that the NTK/CK of transformers with linear attention on the Boolean cube exhibits weak spectral bias—eigenvalues decrease with Fourier degree separately for even and odd orders—implying a low-sensitivity bias, and (Proposition 2.2) that lower maximum sensitivity bounds the probability of inconsistent predictions on ρ-correlated input pairs. Empirically, they validate the bias across vision (ViT-small, ViT-simple vs. ResNet-18, DenseNet-121, ConvMixer on CIFAR-10, Fashion-MNIST, SVHN, ImageNet-1k) and language (RoBERTa with softmax/ReLU attention vs. LSTM on MRPC, QQP), showing transformers consistently achieve lower sensitivity at matched training accuracy. They further demonstrate three implications: sensitivity regularization via data augmentation or MSE penalty improves CIFAR-10-C robustness; lower sensitivity correlates with flatter minima (measured via ShOp and ShPred); and sensitivity serves as a hidden progress measure during grokking on modular addition, decreasing during the plateau phase before test accuracy jumps.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, ViT-small achieves a sensitivity of 0.0050 and ViT-simple 0.0014, compared to 0.0827 for ResNet-18, 0.3673 for DenseNet-121, and 0.0829 for ConvMixer, at comparable training accuracies. On language tasks (MRPC), RoBERTa-ReLU achieves sensitivity 0.001 versus 0.15 for LSTM; on QQP, RoBERTa-ReLU reaches 0.02 versus 0.09 for LSTM. Sensitivity regularization reduces the sharpness-of-outputs metric (ShOp) from 39.166 to 9.025 and sharpness-of-predictions (ShPred) from 0.5346 to 0.3982, confirming lower sensitivity correlates with flatter minima. On CIFAR-10-C, ViTs trained with sensitivity augmentation or regularization show improved robustness across noise, blur, weather, and digital corruptions relative to both vanilla ViTs and CNNs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Transformers consistently achieve the lowest sensitivity across all compared architectures and modalities. On CIFAR-10, ViT-small (0.0050) and ViT-simple (0.0014) are roughly 16–75× less sensitive than ResNet-18 (0.0827), ConvMixer (0.0829), and DenseNet-121 (0.3673), despite matching training accuracy. On Fashion-MNIST, ViT (0.0098) outperforms MLPs with LeakyReLU (0.0559) and sigmoid (0.0505) as well as a 3-layer CNN (0.0453). On ImageNet-1k, pre-trained ViT/L-16 (0.0191) is less sensitive than ConvNeXt (0.0342). In language, RoBERTa (0.001–0.03) is 3–150× less sensitive than LSTM (0.09–0.15) on MRPC and QQP, and RoBERTa-ReLU further outperforms RoBERTa-softmax, suggesting softmax attention's sparsity can increase sensitivity. The ConvMixer comparison is particularly informative: since ConvMixer also processes patches but lacks self-attention, its higher sensitivity (0.0829 vs. 0.0050) isolates the attention mechanism as the source of the low-sensitivity bias rather than patch-wise input processing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Boolean-function sensitivity framework introduced by Bhattamishra et al. (2023b) and Hahn & Rofin (2024) to real-valued, high-dimensional data (images and text), proposing a token-wise Gaussian perturbation metric that preserves the local-neighborhood structure of the original definition. The theoretical grounding draws on the spectral bias analysis of Yang & Salman (2020) and the NTK/CK convergence results for linear-attention transformers from Hron et al. (2020) to prove a weak spectral bias that implies low-sensitivity learning. The paper also connects sensitivity to three previously studied but separate phenomena—robustness to corruptions (Mahmood et al., 2021; Bhojanapalli et al., 2021), flat minima (Keskar et al., 2017), and grokking (Nanda et al., 2023)—proposing sensitivity as a unifying lens across all three.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By establishing sensitivity as a single, efficiently measurable metric that simultaneously explains robustness, loss-landscape flatness, and grokking progress, the paper offers practitioners a practical diagnostic and intervention tool (sensitivity regularization via data augmentation) that is cheap to compute and architecture-agnostic. The synthetic-data experiment cleanly disentangles sensitivity from sparsity, confirming that transformers prefer low-sensitivity functions even when the sparse solution is more sensitive—a nuance that prior Boolean-only analyses could not capture. Open questions include whether the sensitivity-as-progress-measure generalizes beyond modular addition and sparse parity, whether the flat-minima connection holds for non-convex architectures at scale, and whether the low-sensitivity bias persists under different optimization regimes or pretraining protocols.
