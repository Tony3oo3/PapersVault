## Problem

Text-based image editing is unambiguous but imprecise (it cannot specify fine-grained spatial modifications like the exact extent of a smile), while drag-based editing is precise but ambiguous (the same handle-target pairs can yield multiple valid edits, e.g., enlarging a face vs. making a person smile). No prior method combines both signals to achieve editing that is simultaneously precise and unambiguous.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250191/cropped_page0_idx7.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image, an original prompt P_o describing the image, an edit prompt P_e describing the desired modification, and n handle-target point pairs. It operates entirely in the latent space of Stable Diffusion 1.5 at a single fixed DDIM inversion timestep (inversion strength 0.7, 50 denoising steps), optimizing only the latent code z_t while keeping the U-Net frozen after a brief per-image LoRA finetuning (80 steps, rank 16). The output is a single edited RGB image that satisfies both the spatial drag constraints and the semantic edit prompt.

## Method

CLIPDrag builds on the DragDiffusion framework and introduces two key modules. First, Global-Local Motion Supervision (GLMS) computes a global gradient from a CLIP target loss (aligning the generated image to the edit prompt) and a local gradient from the drag motion supervision loss, then decomposes the global gradient into an identity component (perpendicular to the local gradient, preserving structure) and an edit component (parallel to the local gradient). A direction-aware fusion rule (Eq. 6) either adds the identity component to the local gradient when the two signals agree, or subtracts the edit component when they conflict, thereby resolving ambiguity while maintaining fidelity. Second, Fast Point Tracking (FPT) constrains the nearest-neighbor search for handle re-localization to only consider candidate points that are strictly closer to their targets than the current handle position, preventing handles from stalling or oscillating and significantly reducing the number of optimization iterations needed.

## Results

On the DRAGBENCH benchmark, CLIPDrag achieves the lowest Mean Distance (MD) and highest Image Fidelity (IF) across most iteration settings. At 160 iterations, CLIPDrag reaches MD=32.3 and IF=0.88, outperforming DragDiffusion (MD=35.1, IF=0.85), StableDrag (MD=34.8, IF=0.81), and FastDrag (MD=36.9, IF=0.83). In terms of inference speed, CLIPDrag completes editing in 47.8s on a single 3090 GPU, roughly 1.5–1.7× faster than all compared drag-based baselines (69.4–80.3s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DragDiffusion (Shi et al., 2024b), CLIPDrag consistently achieves lower MD and higher IF at 20–160 iterations (e.g., MD 32.3 vs 35.1, IF 0.88 vs 0.85 at 160 steps), and is ~40% faster in inference (47.8s vs 80.3s). Compared to StableDrag (Cui et al., 2024), CLIPDrag leads on both metrics at all iteration counts beyond 10 (e.g., IF 0.88 vs 0.81 at 160 steps) and is ~34% faster. Versus FastDrag/FreeDrag (Ling et al., 2023), CLIPDrag shows the largest MD gap (32.3 vs 36.9 at 160 steps) and is ~37% faster. For text-based editing, CLIPDrag outperforms DiffCLIP (Kim et al., 2022) qualitatively by providing pixel-level spatial control that DiffCLIP cannot offer, while DiffCLIP lacks any drag-based precision. Against the more recent drag-based methods InstantDrag, LightningDrag, and RegionDrag (all 2024), CLIPDrag is compared qualitatively in Appendix A, where it better preserves identity and resolves ambiguity in semantic edits (e.g., expression changes) that these methods handle as structural translations. SDE-Drag (Meng et al., 2021) is only compared on inference time, where CLIPDrag is ~32% faster (47.8s vs 70.0s).

## Relation to Prior Work

CLIPDrag sits at the intersection of two well-established image editing paradigms: text-based editing (DiffCLIP, Imagic) and drag-based editing (DragDiffusion, FreeDrag). It builds directly on DragDiffusion's motion supervision and DDIM inversion framework, repurposes DiffCLIP's CLIP guidance loss as a global signal, and adapts ProGrad's gradient decomposition idea by splitting the CLIP gradient into identity and edit components. The key departure from prior work is treating text as global disambiguation and drag as local spatial control within a single unified optimization loop, rather than applying them sequentially or in isolation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine limitation of drag-based editing—ambiguity where the same handle-target pairs admit multiple valid edits—by showing that a global text signal can resolve it without sacrificing the pixel-level precision of drag control. The gradient decomposition (identity vs. edit component) provides a clean conceptual framework for combining heterogeneous signals in latent optimization. However, the approach is tied to a single fixed timestep, a specific CLIP variant, and SD 1.5, leaving open whether it generalizes to newer architectures (SDXL, DiT-based models) or more complex multi-object edits. The requirement for both a text prompt and drag points simultaneously also limits practical usability compared to either modality alone.
