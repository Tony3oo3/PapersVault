## Problem

High-throughput TCR repertoire sequencing produces heterogeneous, high-dimensional, sparse, and mostly unlabeled data, yet no scalable unsupervised method existed to learn general-purpose TCR embeddings that capture immunological context such as antigen specificity and HLA associations. Prior deep learning approaches to TCRs were supervised and task-specific (antigen-binding prediction, repertoire classification), leaving open the question of whether co-occurrence patterns across large repertoire collections could yield meaningful, scalable embeddings for millions of TCRs.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a binary repertoire-TCR matrix X ∈ {0,1}^{N×K} (N subjects × K TCRs defined by V gene, J gene, and CDR3), using presence/absence indicators rather than clonal frequencies for robustness. It produces two outputs: dense d-dimensional TCR embeddings W ∈ R^{K×d} and subject-level repertoire embeddings R ∈ R^{N×d} obtained by mean-pooling the TCR embeddings present in each individual's repertoire. The co-occurrence matrix C = X^T X is extremely sparse (~80% zeros), which the algorithm exploits for efficiency.

## Method

JL-GLOVE initializes the GloVe objective with unit-normalized Johnson-Lindenstrauss random projections (W^JL = (1/√d)X^T(XP)) rather than random weights, reducing initialization cost from O(NK²) to O(dNK) and enabling training on only 1% of the co-occurrence matrix. The key geometric insight is that unit-normalizing JL embeddings aligns the L2-distance-preserving JL transform with GloVe's cosine-similarity objective via the identity ‖w_i − w_j‖² = ‖w_i‖² + ‖w_j‖² − 2⟨w_i, w_j⟩, yielding faster convergence. Training uses a modified weighting function f(·) = s₀·1(C_ij>0) + sC_ij^α (Shazeer et al.) and Adagrad SGD on minibatches of non-zero co-occurrences. Post-processing applies anisotropy correction (mean-centering plus removal of dominant PCA components correlated with marginal frequencies), and repertoire embeddings are derived via mean pooling over each subject's observed TCRs.

## Results

On the MULTIID test set with K=65,751 HLADB TCRs and N=31,938 repertoires, JL-GloVe (d=100) achieves AUROC of 0.85±0.05 for Parvo virus, 0.99±0.02 for CMV, and 0.97±0.01 for COVID-19, outperforming the ESLG baseline (0.73, 0.93, 0.95 respectively) and DeepRC (0.83 for CMV). For HSV-1 and HSV-2 disambiguation, JL-GloVe reaches AUROC 0.90±0.04 overall, substantially exceeding AIRIVA (0.74/0.78) and ESLG (0.62/0.75). HLA classification AUROC across 145 types improves monotonically as the number of TCRs scales from ~65K to ~4M, and as repertoires scale from ~4K to ~32K. Performance degrades at K=3.8M for weaker signals (Parvo drops to 0.53 AUROC), attributed to noise sensitivity of the mean-pooling aggregation at large K.

## Baselines & Comparisons

JL-GloVe consistently outperforms all three baselines at its optimal scale (K=65,751). Against ESLG (Emerson et al., 2017), JL-GloVe gains +0.12 AUROC on Parvo (0.85 vs 0.73), +0.06 on CMV (0.99 vs 0.93), and +0.02 on COVID-19 (0.97 vs 0.95). Against DeepRC (Widrich et al., 2020), a BERT-based TCR protein sequence model, JL-GloVe achieves 0.99 vs 0.83 AUROC on CMV, a 16-point advantage. Against AIRIVA (Pradier et al., 2023), a VAE-based generative model, JL-GloVe dominates on both HSV subtypes: 0.90 vs 0.74 AUROC for HSV-1 and 0.90 vs 0.78 for HSV-2, with the gap widening in the HSV-2-negative stratification (0.92 vs 0.74). However, as K scales to 3.8M TCRs, JL-GloVe's advantage erodes for weaker immune signals (Parvo drops to 0.53 AUROC, below ESLG's 0.73), while CMV and COVID-19 remain robust, suggesting the mean-pooling aggregation is the bottleneck at very large K.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the GloVe algorithm (Pennington et al., 2014) from NLP word embeddings to TCR co-occurrence statistics, building on prior immunology work that identified TCR co-occurrence patterns encoding pathogen exposure and HLA associations (Emerson et al., 2017; DeWitt III et al., 2018; Zahid et al., 2024). The key methodological departure is the use of a normalized Johnson-Lindenstrauss random projection to initialize GloVe, reducing training complexity from O(NK²) to O(dNK) and enabling training on a small fraction of the co-occurrence matrix. The anisotropy correction follows Mu & Viswanath (2018), and the weighting function follows Shazeer et al. (2016), but the normalized JL initialization is presented as a novel contribution. The paper also contrasts its co-occurrence-based approach against protein sequence models (ESM-2, TCRdist) and supervised repertoire classifiers (DeepRC, ESLG, AIRIVA), showing that sequence-based embeddings fail to capture immunological context because TCRs are generated by random V(D)J recombination rather than evolutionary pressure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides a scalable, unsupervised method for generating TCR and repertoire embeddings that encode both immune genetics and pathogenic exposure history, with the potential to support personalized medicine by quantifying individual immune history from routine sequencing. A key limitation is the performance degradation of the simple mean-pooling repertoire aggregation as the number of TCRs scales from ~65K to ~3.8M, suggesting that more sophisticated set aggregation (e.g., set transformers, optimal transport) is needed for robust downstream classification. Open questions include whether embeddings can be combined with single-cell RNA-seq or clinical data, how to handle rarer HLA types with limited co-occurrence signal, and whether the approach generalizes to B-cell receptor repertoires. The finding that co-occurrence embeddings outperform protein sequence models for immunological context challenges the assumption that sequence-based pretraining captures functional biology in randomly generated immune receptors.
