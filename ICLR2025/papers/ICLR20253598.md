## Problem

Inferring network connectivity from neural population activity is a long-standing challenge, but traditional methods assume static connectivity, ignoring the nonstationary interactions produced by synaptic plasticity and neuromodulation. More expressive nonlinear models (RNNs, deep transformers) can capture dynamics but sacrifice interpretability, making it difficult to extract the underlying connectivity structure. The paper addresses this gap by asking whether a lightweight, interpretable model can simultaneously predict neural dynamics and recover time-varying connectivity.

## Input / Output

The model takes N neurons' activity across H history timesteps (X_k ∈ R^{N×H}) and concatenates a learnable positional embedding E ∈ R^{N×M} to encode neuron identity, forming X̃_k ∈ R^{N×(H+M)}. In task-driven settings, stimulus inputs are also included. The output is a next-step activity prediction x̂_{k+1} ∈ R^N and, critically, the N×N linearized attention matrix A_k whose (i,j) entry directly encodes the instantaneous connectivity from neuron j to neuron i — no separate decoding step is needed. The attention matrix is time-varying, so averaging across timesteps yields a time-averaged connectivity estimate, while tracking individual timesteps reveals nonstationary changes.

## Method

NetFormer treats each neuron's H-step activity history as a token, linearly projects it into queries Q_k = X̃_k W_Q and keys K_k = X̃_k W_K, and computes the linearized attention matrix A_k = Q_k K_k^T without softmax (identity activation), justified by the fact that neuronal in/out-degrees are not invariant. The prediction uses a residual connection: x̂_{k+1} = x_k + A_k x_k, which mirrors the forward Euler discretization of the underlying ODE. The key theoretical insight is that for sigmoidal nonlinearities f, a Taylor expansion shows A_k approximates δf'(0)W_k, so the attention matrix recovers true connectivity up to a known scaling factor. Training is self-supervised, minimizing MSE between predicted and observed next-step activity, with parameters W_Q, W_K, and E shared across all timesteps, enabling scalability to variable population sizes across sessions.

## Results

On the connectivity-constrained simulation, NetFormer achieves Pearson 0.869 and Spearman 0.532 for neuron-level (N×N) connectivity inference, approaching the oracle RNN-with-tanh upper bound (Pearson 0.905, Spearman 0.546) while outperforming linear regression (Pearson 0.817) and the misspecified RNN-with-exp (Pearson 0.581). On real mouse V1 recordings, NetFormer achieves Spearman 0.847±0.063 and Pearson 0.777±0.047 for cell-type-level (K×K) connectivity against patch-clamp ground truth, while all other baselines (linear regression, RNNs, cross-correlation, covariance, mutual information, transfer entropy) yield near-zero or negative correlations. For activity prediction on real data, NetFormer attains R²=0.548±0.004 and MSE=0.404±0.004, outperforming linear regression (R²=0.515) and both RNN variants (R²=0.386 and 0.478).

## Baselines & Comparisons

For state-dependent connectivity inference on real neural data (Table 6), NetFormer substantially outperforms both nonstationary baselines across all three behavioral states. Against LtrRNN (Pellegrino et al., NeurIPS 2023), NetFormer achieves Spearman 0.601–0.767 vs. LtrRNN's near-zero values (−0.057 to 0.069) and Pearson 0.591–0.713 vs. LtrRNN's ~0.000, indicating LtrRNN fails to recover any meaningful cell-type-level connectivity structure. Against AR-HMM (Linderman et al., AISTATS 2017), NetFormer achieves Spearman 0.601–0.767 vs. AR-HMM's 0.151–0.274 and Pearson 0.591–0.713 vs. AR-HMM's −0.320 to 0.199, with AR-HMM actually producing negative correlations in the stationary desynchronized state. In the broader Table 1 comparison, NetFormer also dominates all stationary baselines (linear regression, RNNs, cross-correlation, covariance, mutual information, transfer entropy) on real data, where every other method yields negative or near-zero cell-type-level correlations, while NetFormer is the only method achieving positive, meaningful agreement with patch-clamp ground truth. On simulation data, NetFormer approaches the oracle RNN-with-tanh but clearly surpasses the misspecified RNN-with-exp and all statistical metrics at neuron-level connectivity.

## Relation to Prior Work

NetFormer builds directly on the fast weight programming interpretation of linear transformers (Schlag et al., 2021), repurposing the QK^T dot-product as a biologically interpretable connectivity matrix. It departs from standard transformer attention by removing the softmax normalization, arguing that the constraint of weights summing to one is not biologically meaningful for neuronal in/out-degrees. The paper positions itself against the lineage of dynamical models for neural activity (GLMs, RNNs, switching linear dynamical systems) by offering a model that is simultaneously nonlinear, nonstationary, and interpretable—properties that prior methods have typically sacrificed one or more of.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a long-standing gap: most connectivity inference methods assume static weights, while biological networks exhibit continuous, activity-dependent changes in connectivity through plasticity and neuromodulation. Validation against independent patch-clamp measurements provides rare ground-truth confirmation for a learned connectivity model. Key limitations include partial observability (only a subset of neurons is recorded), limited capacity for strong nonlinearities compared to deep transformers, and evaluation on a single subject. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger populations, whether it can handle the full range of neuromodulatory timescales, and how it performs when the true connectivity is highly sparse or structured.
