## Problem

Existing early exit strategies for DNNs either rely on single-exit confidence (discarding information from prior layers), patience-based prediction consistency (treating all exits equally), or majority voting, none of which exploit the ensemble effect of multiple exits or weight them by their actual accuracy. These methods also lack theoretical guarantees and often assume uniform error rates across layers, limiting their ability to reliably outperform full-depth inference.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252252/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For NLP tasks, the method takes text sentences or sentence pairs (GLUE benchmarks) and produces a class label, with the sample potentially exiting at any of the 12 (base) or 24 (large) intermediate layers rather than always traversing all layers. For image captioning, it takes an RGB image and generates a caption token-by-token autoregressively, where each token's exit layer is decided independently by the same confidence-aggregation rule. The output format is identical to the final layer's prediction; the only difference is which layer produces it, enabling 1.5×–2.1× speedup.

## Method

BEEM treats each intermediate exit classifier as an expert and defines a weighted cumulative confidence score S_i that accumulates w_i·C_i when the current exit's prediction agrees with the previous one, or resets to w_i·C_i upon disagreement, thereby capturing an ensemble effect while penalizing inconsistency. Weights w_i are either cost-proportional (w_i = λi) or accuracy-based (measured on a validation set), giving deeper or more accurate exits greater influence. The sample exits at layer i when S_i exceeds a threshold α, and thresholds are set per-exit by solving a small linear program that minimizes α_t subject to the constraint that the exit's error rate does not exceed the final layer's error rate. Training uses a weighted average of per-exit cross-entropy plus KL-divergence losses (knowledge distillation from the final layer), and a theoretical theorem provides a sufficient condition on per-exit error rates guaranteeing that BEEM's overall error is lower than the final classifier's.

## Results

On the GLUE benchmark with ALBERT backbone, BEEM-A (accuracy-weighted) achieves 1.98×–2.09× speedup on the dev set while simultaneously improving accuracy by +0.3 to +0.7% over the final layer on SST-2, MNLI, RTE, QNLI, and QQP. On the test set, BEEM-A maintains 1.91×–2.06× speedup with accuracy gains of +0.2 to +0.6% on most tasks. On COCO image captioning, BEEM-C achieves 1.71× speedup with BLEU-1 of 81.8 (vs. 82.5 for the final layer) and CIDEr of 145.1 (vs. 147.1). On ALBERT-Large (24 layers), speedups extend to 2.04×–3.33× with accuracy improvements of +0.1 to +1.8%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BEEM-A outperforms all 11 baselines on both accuracy and speedup across the five GLUE dev-set tasks. The largest accuracy gaps are against confidence-based methods: DeeBERT (−2.3% on SST-2) and ElasticBERT (−2.1%) lose 2–3% accuracy due to ignoring prior exit information, while BEEM-A gains +0.4% over the final layer. Patience-based methods PABEE (−0.1% SST-2, 1.87×) and LeeBERT (0.0% SST-2, 1.78×) are the closest competitors in accuracy but trail BEEM-A by 0.4–0.5% in accuracy and 0.1–0.2× in speedup. ZTW, which also uses ensemble weighting, achieves only 1.63×–1.75× speedup and shows poor generalization to the test set (e.g., −0.5% on MNLI test vs. +0.1% on dev). On COCO image captioning, BEEM-C (81.8 BLEU-1, 1.71×) surpasses MuE (79.3 BLEU-1, 1.64×) and DeeCap (77.5 BLEU-1, 1.60×) by 2.5 and 4.3 BLEU-1 points respectively, while maintaining a higher speedup. On the test set, BEEM-A retains its advantage: +0.4% accuracy at 1.91× on SST-2 and +0.6% at 1.77× on RTE, whereas most baselines degrade (e.g., PABEE drops to 1.49× on RTE test, JEI-DNN to 1.36×).

## Relation to Prior Work

BEEM extends the early-exit inference paradigm for transformers by reframing each intermediate classifier as an 'expert' in an ensemble-learning sense, aggregating weighted confidence scores from consecutive agreeing exits rather than relying on a single exit's confidence (DeeBERT, ElasticBERT) or uniform patience (PABEE, LeeBERT). The training procedure follows the weighted-average loss formulation from Kaya et al. (2019), and the ensemble framing departs from ZTW/EEIC, which learns combination weights as additional parameters and suffers from weaker generalization. A key conceptual departure is the principled threshold-setting via a small linear program (Eq. 3) that forces each exit's error rate below the final layer's, complemented by a theoretical guarantee (Theorem 3.1) absent from prior EE methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 1.5×–2.1× speedup with accuracy matching or exceeding the final layer on easier NLP tasks makes BEEM a practical drop-in for latency-sensitive deployments of BERT/ALBERT-family models. The threshold-optimization procedure is a clean, low-complexity contribution that practitioners can adopt independently of the full BEEM framework. However, the authors acknowledge that thresholds tuned on validation data may not generalize to test distributions (notably on COCO captioning), and the theoretical bound in Theorem 3.1 is quite loose, leaving open whether tighter guarantees or data-adaptive thresholding could close the remaining gap on harder tasks.
