## Problem

It remains unclear whether large language models handle the same morphosyntactic processes across languages using shared internal circuitry or separate language-specific circuits, and whether they invoke distinct components when languages require different morphological operations. Prior work has shown cross-lingual transfer and language-specific representations in multilingual models, but no study has used mechanistic interpretability to directly compare the internal circuits that implement the same linguistic task in different languages.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253439/cropped_page4_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes pre-trained transformer LLMs (BLOOM-560M, GPT2-small, CPM-distilled, Qwen2-0.5B-instruct) and English/Chinese sentence templates for two tasks: indirect object identification (IOI) and past tense verb generation. It produces identified attention-head and FFN circuits with functional labels (e.g., Duplicate Token Heads, Name Mover Heads, past-tense heads, copy heads), head activation frequency profiles across layers, and ablation experiments that report rank changes for target tokens. A key constraint is that path patching requires carefully designed minimal-pair templates, which are unavailable for Chinese past tense (lacking morphological markers), so the authors supplement with information flow routes that do not require contrastive inputs.

## Method

The paper combines two mechanistic interpretability techniques—path patching (with logit attribution) and information flow routes—to identify and compare circuits in multilingual and monolingual models across English and Chinese. For the IOI task, which is structurally identical in both languages, they show that BLOOM uses the same set of functionally specialized attention heads (Previous Token, Duplicate Token, Induction, S-inhibition, and Name Mover heads) with the same algorithmic roles in both languages, and that independently trained GPT2-small and CPM-distilled converge on nearly identical circuits despite having no shared training data. For the past tense task, where English requires morphological marking (e.g., walk→walked) but Chinese does not, they find that multilingual Qwen2 shares copy heads across languages but activates language-specific past-tense heads (layer 19.4, 19.5) and late feed-forward layers (layers 20–24) only for English, which perform the morphological transformation. Ablation experiments confirm that removing these English-specific components degrades English past-tense performance dramatically while leaving Chinese predictions essentially unchanged, demonstrating that the model exploits shared circuitry for common structure while preserving language-specific subroutines where morphology diverges.

## Results

On the IOI task, BLOOM-560M achieves 100% accuracy and 96% zero-rank rate in English, and 95% accuracy and 93% zero-rank rate in Chinese, confirming comparable cross-lingual performance. The Pearson correlation of head activation frequencies between English and Chinese IOI circuits is 0.72, substantially higher than the 0.48 correlation between English IOI and an English past-tense control task, indicating strong cross-lingual circuit sharing. On the past tense task, ablating late FFN layers (20–23) drops English accuracy from 97.44% to 47.44% and zero-rank rate from 58.06% to 17.74%, while Chinese zero-rank rate barely changes (25.08% → 24.19%), demonstrating that FFNs are engaged only for English morphological marking. Ablating copy heads (21.3, 21.4, 21.13) degrades both English (past-tense rank −141.95, present-tense rank −221.39) and Chinese (verb rank −201.82) performance, confirming these heads are shared across languages.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a mechanistic interpretability study rather than a benchmarking paper, so it does not compare against external baseline methods. Instead, it establishes internal controls: the cross-lingual IOI circuit overlap (ρ = 0.72) is contrasted against an English-only control task (ρ = 0.48), confirming that the high overlap reflects shared linguistic structure rather than generic head importance. For the past tense task, the paper contrasts English and Chinese processing within the same multilingual model (Qwen2-0.5B-instruct): past-tense heads (19.4, 19.5) are strongly activated in English but not in Chinese, and ablating them shifts English present-tense verbs up by 83.21 positions on average while leaving Chinese verb ranks nearly unchanged (+4.58). The monolingual comparison (GPT2-small vs. CPM-distilled) shows that both independently trained models converge on the same high-level IOI algorithm (Duplicate Token → S-inhibition → Name Mover), though with minor architectural differences (GPT2 uses Negative Name Mover Heads; CPM uses Copy Suppression Heads). Overall, the paper's central finding is that shared syntactic structure yields shared circuitry across languages and even across independently trained monolingual models, while language-specific morphology (English past-tense inflection) is handled by additional language-specific components (late-layer FFNs and past-tense heads) that are inactive for Chinese.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability toolkit (path patching, logit attribution, information flow routes) from single-language circuit discovery to the cross-lingual setting, asking whether the same attention heads and FFN layers implement the same algorithmic steps across languages. It builds directly on Wang et al. (2023)'s IOI circuit in GPT-2 and Ferrando & Voita (2024)'s information flow routes, applying both to multilingual (BLOOM, Qwen2) and independently-trained monolingual (GPT-2, CPM) models. The paper positions itself against the 'First Align, then Predict' paradigm from BERT-based multilingual models (Artetxe et al., 2020; Muller et al., 2021) and Merullo et al. (2024)'s finding of circuit reuse across tasks within a single language, explicitly noting that no prior work has investigated circuit sharing at the cross-lingual level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that independently-trained monolingual models converge on nearly identical IOI circuits suggests that certain algorithmic structures for handling linguistic processes may be somewhat universal rather than artifacts of shared training data, connecting to long-standing questions in linguistics about universality and learnability. The demonstration that multilingual models deploy language-specific FFN components only where morphology demands them (English past tense but not Chinese) provides a mechanistic account of how models balance shared structure with language-specific variation. However, the scope is limited to one language pair, two tasks, and small models (≤560M parameters), leaving open whether these patterns hold for larger models, more typologically diverse language pairs, or more complex syntactic phenomena. Future work could exploit identified shared circuits for more principled cross-lingual transfer or for improving safety and robustness in non-English languages.
