## Problem

Pairwise text preference data is the backbone of RLHF, DPO, and LLM leaderboards, yet it remains largely uninterpretable: black-box reward models and aggregate statistics cannot explain *why* one response was preferred over another, and manual inspection of thousands of long response pairs is infeasible. This paper formalizes the Inverse Constitutional AI (ICAI) problem, which asks: given a feedback dataset, can we compress the annotations into a short set of natural-language principles (a constitution) that enables an LLM to reconstruct the original preferences, thereby making the implicit rules and biases of annotators visible and editable?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a dataset of pairwise text preferences (each consisting of a prompt, two model responses, and a binary annotation) along with an LLM (GPT-3.5-Turbo or GPT-4o) that serves as both principle generator and constitutional annotator. It produces a constitution: an ordered list of up to n human-readable, editable natural-language principles (e.g., 'select the more polite output'), validated by a reconstruction agreement score measuring the percentage of constitutional preferences that match the original annotations. The approach is notably sample-efficient, generating meaningful constitutions from as few as 9–30 preference pairs, and the constitution is constrained to be short enough for human inspection while being expressive enough to capture diverse preference dimensions.

## Method

The ICAI algorithm inverts Constitutional AI by recovering a constitution from observed feedback through a five-step pipeline: (1) an LLM is prompted per preference pair to generate candidate principles explaining the annotation, using multiple diverse prompts to cover positive, negative, specific, and general rules; (2) candidate principles are clustered via k-means on text embeddings to identify near-duplicates; (3) one representative principle is sampled per cluster; (4) each surviving principle is tested by prompting the LLM to cast a vote (first preferred / second preferred / not relevant) on every comparison, and votes are tallied against ground-truth labels; (5) principles are filtered to retain only those with positive net contribution (#correct − #incorrect) and sufficient relevance (≥10% of data), then ranked and truncated to the top n. The key design choice is that validation is performed by a separate constitutional-annotator LLM prompted with the final constitution, making the reconstruction loss a direct, interpretable measure of how well the principles explain the data rather than a learned reward signal.

## Results

On the full AlpacaEval unaligned dataset (648 pairs), the constitutional annotator achieves 61% agreement with original annotations versus 34% for the default annotator, a 27-point improvement. On aligned AlpacaEval, constitutional annotators match the default annotator's performance while providing interpretable principles. In the bias detection application, verbosity bias is confirmed across datasets with 57.1% accuracy (97.1% relevance) on Chatbot Arena and 57.7% (96.9%) on PRISM, while list-format bias reaches 73.4% accuracy on AlpacaEval and 71.6% on PRISM. On synthetic data, constitutional annotators notably improve reconstruction in both orthogonal and unaligned settings, demonstrating adaptability beyond the base LLM's priors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the Default annotator from the AlpacaEval framework, which strongly correlates with human preferences but cannot adapt to different datasets. On aligned AlpacaEval, constitutional annotators match the default annotator's performance (answering Q1 affirmatively), while on unaligned AlpacaEval (full 648 pairs), the constitutional annotator achieves 61% agreement versus the default's 34%, a substantial 27-point gap. A fine-tuned reward model adapts partially but underperforms the constitutional annotator in the low-data (65-pair) scenario, though a custom-trained reward model with extensive data may surpass ICAI at the cost of interpretability. The PopAlign baseline (Wang et al., 2024), which hypothesizes principles during inference, is included as a comparison but specific numerical results are deferred to the appendix. A flipped Default annotator serves as a non-adaptive baseline that performs well on some datasets but fails to adjust to all, highlighting the key advantage of ICAI's adaptability. On PRISM demographic groups, constitutional annotators exceed the default annotator for their respective group's data but do not transfer to the other group, confirming group-specific preference differences.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper inverts the Constitutional AI paradigm (Bai et al., 2022b), which uses a fixed set of principles to generate feedback, by instead deriving principles from existing pairwise preference data. Methodologically, it adapts the generate-and-test structure of classical rule learning (de Sá et al., 2011; Fürnkranz et al., 2012) to the natural-language setting, using LLMs to propose candidate principles and LLM-as-a-judge annotators (building on the AlpacaEval framework, Li et al., 2024c) to validate them. The approach is closely related to LLM-based distributional difference description (Zhong et al., 2022; 2023) and concurrent work on inverse constitutional AI (Kostolansky, 2024) and vibe-axis discovery (Dunlap et al., 2024a), but differs in explicitly optimizing for preference reconstruction accuracy and providing a full end-to-end pipeline with filtering and diversity controls.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ICAI offers a practical, interpretable alternative to black-box reward models for understanding what preference data actually encodes, with immediate applications in bias detection, annotation scaling, and personalized model behavior. Key limitations include the lossy and non-unique nature of constitutions (Rashomon effect), the inability to establish causality between generated principles and annotator reasoning, and the difficulty of evaluating constitutions on data already well-aligned with the LLM's priors. Open questions include whether the framework can detect subtler social and cultural biases, how constitutions scale to multi-modal settings, and whether combining ICAI-generated principles with rule-based reward models (Mu et al., 2024) yields complementary benefits.
