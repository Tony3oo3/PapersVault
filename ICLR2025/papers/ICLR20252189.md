## Problem

Process reward models (PRMs) that provide per-step feedback for LLM reasoning have so far yielded only 1–2% gains over outcome reward models (ORMs) when used as dense rewards in RL, raising doubts about their utility. The paper asks: what should per-step process rewards actually measure, and how should automated training data be collected to make PRMs effective for both test-time search and online RL?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252189/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The PAV takes a math problem and a partial reasoning trace (a prefix of steps) as input and outputs a scalar Q-value estimate at that prefix; the per-step advantage A^μ is then computed as the difference between consecutive Q-values under the prover policy. At test time, the effective reward Q^π + α·A^μ scores each candidate step during beam search to produce a ranked set of complete solutions. During RL, the same effective reward serves as a dense per-step signal that updates the base policy via policy gradient, producing an improved policy with higher Pass@N and raw accuracy.

## Method

The key insight is that process rewards should measure *progress*—the change in likelihood of success before and after a step (i.e., the advantage A^μ)—rather than absolute Q-values, and that this progress should be evaluated under a *prover* policy μ distinct from the base policy π. A prover is 'complementary' if it has high variance in its advantage values across actions (distinguishing good from bad steps) while remaining reasonably aligned with the base policy; the paper proves (Theorem 3.1) that policy improvement per update grows with this variance and shrinks with misalignment. In practice, Best-of-K policies of the base (K=4 works well) serve as complementary provers, and PAVs are trained by sampling seed rollouts from the prover, estimating Monte Carlo Q-values at each prefix via partial rollouts, and fine-tuning a Gemma 9B model with cross-entropy loss on (prefix, Q̂) pairs. The resulting effective reward Q^π + α·A^μ is used both for beam search at test time (yielding >8% accuracy and 1.5–5× compute-efficiency gains over ORM best-of-N) and as dense rewards in online RL (yielding 6× sample-efficiency and +7% accuracy gains over ORM-RL).

## Results

On the MATH benchmark, PAV-RL achieves >7% higher test accuracy than ORM-RL for both Gemma 2B (~25.8% vs. ~18.8%) and Gemma 9B (~55.7% vs. ~48.7%), while converging 6× faster in training iterations. For test-time beam search, PAVs are >8% more accurate and 1.5–5× more compute-efficient than best-of-N re-ranking with ORMs (10× for 2B/9B, 5× for 27B). PAV-RL policies also achieve 8× better Pass@N performance, raising the ceiling for any downstream re-ranker.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against best-of-N with ORMs (Cobbe et al., 2021), PAV beam search achieves >8% higher accuracy at equal compute and requires 1.5–5× less compute to match ORM performance at N=128. Compared to beam search with Q^π from Snell et al. (2024), PAVs are 8× more compute-efficient, demonstrating that advantages (progress) outperform absolute Q-values for exploration. Against best-of-N with PRM Q^π from Wang et al. (2024) and Luo et al. (2024), PAVs consistently outperform, with the 'PAV-as-ORM' variant gaining ~4% over ORM at N=128. PAV-RL improves over RFT (Yuan et al., 2023) by 11% (2B) and 15% (9B) in absolute accuracy, and over ORM-RL by >7% in both settings. Notably, using Q^π or Q^μ as step-level rewards in RL (as in prior PRM works) leads to reward hacking and performance collapse, whereas the advantage-based PAV formulation avoids this failure mode entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repositions process reward models (PRMs) within the reward-shaping framework (Ng et al., 1999), arguing that per-step rewards should measure *progress* (advantages) under a distinct prover policy rather than step correctness (Lightman et al., 2023) or Q-values under the base policy (Wang et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2024). It inherits the beam-search test-time procedure from Snell et al. (2024) and the natural policy gradient analysis from Kakade (2001), but departs fundamentally by showing that the prover policy should be *complementary* to the base policy—neither too strong nor too weak—and by providing the first theoretical characterization (Theorem 3.1) of which provers guarantee non-trivial improvement. The practical data-collection pipeline (first-pit strategy, Monte Carlo Q-estimation) builds on Setlur et al. (2024) and Hwang et al. (2024), but repurposes it to train verifiers that predict prover advantages rather than preference pairs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that dense PRM rewards in online RL can yield a large, consistent gap over ORM-only RL (6× sample efficiency, +7% accuracy), resolving prior skepticism that automated PRMs provide only 1–2% gains (Shao et al., 2024). The theoretical characterization of complementary provers—where even a *weaker* policy can improve a stronger base policy—challenges the standard assumption that a teacher must dominate the student, and provides a principled recipe (Best-of-K with moderate K) for selecting prover policies. Open questions include how to jointly optimize prover and base policies, how PAV fitting errors compound at scale, and whether the complementary-prover principle extends beyond math reasoning to other multi-step tasks.
