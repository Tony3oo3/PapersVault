## Problem

Despite LLMs being increasingly deployed as autonomous decision-making agents, their performance in sequential and strategic settings has not been rigorously evaluated through quantitative metrics like regret, particularly in multi-agent interactive environments. The paper asks whether pre-trained LLMs exhibit no-regret behavior in canonical online learning and repeated game benchmarks, and whether this property is universal or can break under adversarial loss sequences.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251474/cropped_page4_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

In the online learning and game experiments, the LLM agent receives the entire history of past loss vectors (or payoff vectors marginalized by opponents' strategies) concatenated as text in a prompt, and outputs a decision policy (a probability distribution over a finite action set) for the next round. The prompts deliberately avoid explicit instructions to minimize regret or rules for exploiting history, to evaluate the model's intrinsic sophistication. For the regret-loss training, the input is N sampled loss sequences of length T drawn from a continuous distribution on [-B,B]^{T×N}, and the output is a set of Transformer parameters whose induced policy achieves low worst-case regret.

## Method

The paper makes three core contributions. First, it proposes two statistical frameworks (a trend-checking hypothesis test and a log-regression fit) to validate sublinear regret growth from finite experimental data, then empirically shows that GPT-4, GPT-4o, and open-source LLMs achieve sublinear regret in both online learning and repeated games, sometimes outperforming FTRL and FTPL baselines. Second, it provides a theoretical explanation connecting supervised pre-training under a canonical data distribution (with latent variables and Gaussian noise) to the quantal response model, which provably yields no-regret behavior via a connection to FTPL. Third, it introduces a novel unsupervised regret-loss (Eq. 5.2) that approximates worst-case regret without requiring optimal action labels, and proves that minimizing this loss with a single-layer linear self-attention architecture automatically recovers FTRL with an L2-regularizer (Theorem 5.2), providing both generalization and regret guarantees for the trained model.

## Results

In online learning with full-information feedback (T=500), GPT-4o achieves sublinear regret validated by both the trend-checking (p_trend ≤ 0.09) and regression (β̂₀ ≤ 0.86 < 1) frameworks across all four loss-sequence types (Uniform, Gaussian, Linear-trend, Sine-trend), with β̂₀ values of 0.85, 0.86, 0.83, and 0.28 respectively. In repeated games (2–4 player general-sum), pre-trained LLMs achieve sublinear regret in most instances, sometimes yielding negative regret. However, in adversarial settings (noisy alternating and adaptive loss sequences), GPT-4/Turbo exhibits linear regret (cannot reject H₀ of no decreasing pattern), while the proposed regret-loss-trained single-layer Transformer recovers sublinear regret in these failure cases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares pre-trained LLMs (GPT-4, GPT-4o, GPT-3.5, Mixtral-8x7b, Llama-3-70B) against two classical no-regret algorithms: FTRL with entropy regularization (Shalev-Shwartz & Singer, 2007) and FTPL with Gaussian perturbations (Kalai & Vempala, 2005). In the majority of online learning and repeated-game settings, GPT-4/GPT-4o achieves sublinear regret comparable to or lower than both FTRL and FTPL, with the regression coefficient β̂₀ sometimes exceeding those of the baselines (e.g., 0.85 vs. 0.60 for FTRL on Uniform, indicating GPT-4o's regret grows slightly faster but still sublinearly). In the Sine-trend setting, GPT-4o's β̂₀ (0.28) is close to FTRL (0.24) and FTPL (0.26), showing near-parity. However, in the adversarial noisy-alternating and adaptive loss sequences, GPT-4/Turbo fails to achieve sublinear regret (linear regret confirmed by both validation frameworks), while FTRL and FTPL maintain their no-regret guarantees. The proposed regret-loss-trained single-layer Transformer then closes this gap, achieving sublinear regret in the adversarial cases where GPT-4 fails, demonstrating that the new loss specifically addresses the identified weakness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of online learning theory (FTRL, FTPL, no-regret algorithms) and the rapidly growing LLM-agent literature. It departs from prior LLM-in-games work (Brookins & DeBacker, 2023; Akata et al., 2023; Fan et al., 2023), which studied strategic behavior qualitatively, by introducing regret as a rigorous quantitative metric and proposing formal validation frameworks (trend-checking, regression-based) for sublinear regret. The theoretical analysis draws on the quantal response model from behavioral economics (McFadden, 1976; McKelvey & Palfrey, 1995) to connect supervised pre-training to FTPL, while the novel regret-loss is an unsupervised objective that, to our knowledge, has no direct precedent in the LLM training literature. The concurrent work of Krishnamurthy et al. (2024) studies LLMs in stochastic bandits but under i.i.d. assumptions, whereas this paper targets adversarial/non-stationary settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The regret-loss offers a label-free training objective that provably produces no-regret algorithms under self-attention parameterization, potentially enabling LLMs to be fine-tuned for robust sequential decision-making without expensive action annotations. However, the theoretical guarantees rely on strong assumptions (Gaussian noise, specific data distributions, single-layer self-attention) that are unlikely to hold for production LLMs, and the empirical validation is limited to small-scale problems (d=2 actions, T≤500). Open questions include whether the regret-loss scales to multi-layer transformers and larger action spaces, whether the quantal-response connection to pre-training survives more realistic data distributions, and how these findings translate to the high-dimensional, partially-observable environments that LLM agents actually face.
