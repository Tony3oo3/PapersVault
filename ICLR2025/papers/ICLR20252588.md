## Problem

Recent interpretability work has shown that some factual relations in LMs are encoded as linear representations (Linear Relational Embeddings) while others are not, but the conditions under which these linear structures form remain unexplained. This paper investigates whether pretraining data frequency is the driving factor, asking specifically whether subject-object co-occurrence counts in the training corpus predict the emergence and quality of LREs across training time.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252588/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes open-weight LMs (OLMo-7B, OLMo-1B, GPT-J-6B) along with 25 factual subject-relation-object relations (10,488 unique terms) from the Relations dataset, and tokenized pretraining batches from Dolma or The Pile. It produces per-relation LRE quality scores (faithfulness and causality) tracked across 8 intermediate checkpoints, per-batch subject-object co-occurrence counts, and a random forest regression that maps LRE features to predicted pretraining term frequencies. A key design choice is that LREs are fit on the same 8 examples regardless of whether the model predicts them correctly, enabling consistent comparison across checkpoints and models.

## Method

The paper fits Linear Relational Embeddings per relation via a first-order Taylor approximation of the model's forward pass (averaging Jacobians over 8 examples with a per-relation beta scaling), then measures quality using faithfulness (does the linear transform reproduce the correct token?) and causality (does editing the subject representation via the LRE change the prediction to a target object?). To track frequency throughout training, the authors develop a Batch Search tool that counts exact subject-object co-occurrences within tokenized (batch-size × sequence-length) arrays rather than whole documents, completing a full scan of ~2T Dolma tokens on 900 CPUs in about a day. The central empirical finding is that LRE causality correlates strongly with average subject-object co-occurrence frequency (r=0.82), with a sharp threshold: above ~1k (GPT-J), ~2k (OLMo-7B), or ~4k (OLMo-1B) co-occurrences, relations consistently achieve causality >0.9 regardless of when in training those occurrences happen. For the frequency-prediction task, they train a random forest (100 trees) on LRE features (faithfulness, causality, faith probability, hard causality) plus log-probability features to predict object or subject-object frequencies, and show the model generalizes from OLMo to GPT-J without additional supervision, outperforming log-probability-only baselines by ~30% on object frequency prediction.

## Results

Subject-object co-occurrence frequency correlates strongly with LRE causality (r = 0.82), substantially higher than subject-only (r = 0.66) or object-only (r = 0.59) frequencies. Linear representations form reliably once average co-occurrence exceeds model-specific thresholds: ~1,097 for GPT-J (6B), ~1,998 for OLMo-7B, and ~4,447 for OLMo-1B, regardless of when during pretraining the threshold is crossed. For predicting pretraining term frequencies from model representations, LRE features achieve ~70% within-one-order-of-magnitude accuracy on object frequency prediction, compared to ~40% for log-probability features alone. Cross-model generalization (fitting on OLMo, evaluating on GPT-J) retains 0.65 accuracy with LRE features versus 0.42 with log-prob features for object occurrences.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three feature sets for predicting pretraining term frequencies: LRE metrics (faithfulness, causality, faith probability, hard causality), log-probability/task-accuracy features, and a mean-frequency baseline. LRE features consistently outperform log-probability features by roughly 20–25 percentage points on object frequency prediction in the cross-model setting (0.65 vs. 0.42 on GPT-J; 0.49 vs. 0.41 on OLMo). For subject-object co-occurrence prediction, the LRE advantage narrows to about 10 points (0.76 vs. 0.66 on GPT-J), and the mean-frequency baseline becomes competitive on OLMo (0.67 vs. 0.68), suggesting co-occurrence prediction is inherently harder. Feature permutation analysis identifies hard causality as the single most important feature, contributing ~15% accuracy, followed by faithfulness metrics at ~5%. The log-probability baseline provides essentially no meaningful signal beyond the mean-frequency baseline for object frequency, confirming that LRE structure encodes frequency information not captured by standard likelihood measures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on Linear Relational Embeddings (LREs) introduced by Hernandez et al. (2024) and the original LRE formulation of Paccanaro & Hinton (2001), which demonstrated that some factual relations in LMs are approximately linearly encoded but left open *why* some relations form linear representations while others do not. The paper also connects to the static-embedding literature (Ethayarajh et al., 2019) showing frequency constraints on linear analogies, and to the data-inference/membership-inference literature, but departs from both by using LRE quality metrics as a novel signal for inferring pretraining corpus statistics. A key methodological departure from Hernandez et al. is fitting LREs without requiring correct predictions, enabling tracking across early checkpoints, and using per-relation rather than per-model scaling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first empirical, quantitative account of when linear representations form in LMs—specific frequency thresholds (≈1k–4k subject-object co-occurrences) that are independent of training stage—giving interpretability researchers a concrete criterion for predicting whether a given relation will be linearly encoded. The cross-model generalization of the regression (OLMo-trained model predicting GPT-J frequencies) suggests a practical path toward inferring pretraining data properties of closed-data, open-weight models without access to the corpus. Limitations include evaluation on only three models and 25 relations, inability to make causal claims without counterfactual pretraining, and weak performance on subject-object co-occurrence prediction. Open questions include whether the mechanism extends to non-factual or commonsense relations, how it interacts with model architecture, and whether the frequency thresholds are stable across scales.
