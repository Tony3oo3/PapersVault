## Problem

Distributed LLM training relies on high-bandwidth interconnects for synchronous gradient updates, and large-model inference demands all parameters be accessible in RAM. Prior asynchronous mixture-of-experts approaches reduce communication but depend on full-corpus clustering or metadata for routing, making them impractical for real-world applications where only short input prefixes are available (e.g., question answering). This paper asks whether a mixture of language models can be trained with near-zero inter-node communication while still routing effectively from short prefixes and outperforming a dense baseline at matched FLOPs.

## Input / Output

At inference, the system receives a text sequence and uses only a short prefix (M tokens, default 256, as few as 32) to score each of E tiny router language models (4.4M parameters each) by log-likelihood, then routes the entire sequence to the single best-scoring expert for next-token generation. The router overhead is under 3% of inference FLOPs. Unlike token-level MoE (e.g., Switch Transformer), only one expert's parameters need to be in memory at a time, and no per-token routing decisions are made.

## Method

SMALLTALK LM is a hard mixture of E independent transformer experts (335M or 1.3B parameters) with E tiny router LMs (4.4M parameters) that serve as lightweight surrogates for the experts. Training is two-stage: first, routers are trained via EM where the E-step minimizes prefix log-likelihood (Eq. 9) and the M-step performs balanced assignments (sorting sequences by negative max log-likelihood to avoid capacity conflicts); second, each expert is trained independently on its assigned disjoint data segment with standard next-token prediction. The key insight is that a 4.4M-parameter model can identify which expert best fits a sequence just as well as the full-size expert itself, since routing reduces to recognizing data-distribution similarity rather than performing the full prediction task. Communication is limited to ~100 all-gather operations during router training (transferring ~5.6 MB per router per event) and occasional data-sharding messages, eliminating the need for per-step gradient synchronization entirely.

## Results

SMALLTALK LM consistently outperforms dense baselines at matched training FLOPs. For the 1.3B-parameter model with 32 experts, perplexity drops from 8.20 (dense) to 6.76 (mixture), a 17.6% improvement, with only 1.07% additional training FLOPs and 2.85% additional inference FLOPs. For the 335M-parameter model with 32 experts, perplexity improves from 10.50 to 9.07 (13.6% gain), and this configuration actually beats the 1.3B dense baseline (9.07 vs 9.10) while requiring 3× less inference compute. On downstream tasks, the 32-expert 1.3B model outperforms the dense baseline on 75% of MMLU subtasks (42/56) and achieves gains of 3%, 2%, 3%, and 1% on ARC Challenge, ARC Easy, HellaSwag, and MMLU respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is against dense Transformer baselines of identical architecture and parameter count, trained on the same data volume with matched total training FLOPs. SMALLTALK LM wins on perplexity across every configuration tested (4 to 32 experts, 335M and 1.3B parameters), with gains scaling from ~4% (4 experts, 1.3B) to ~18% (32 experts, 1.3B). The 335M mixture with 32 experts (perplexity 9.07) surpasses the 1.3B dense model (perplexity 9.10) at 3× lower inference cost, demonstrating that the mixture's specialization more than compensates for smaller individual experts. Against the TF-IDF + SVD + balanced K-Means routing of Gururangan et al. (2023), SMALLTALK LM's learned router significantly outperforms on a 335M model with 16 experts (Figure 4c), particularly when the routing prefix is short, indicating that simple content-based clustering is insufficient for effective expert assignment in language modeling.

## Relation to Prior Work

SMALLTALK LM builds on the hard mixture-of-experts framework (Jacobs et al., 1991) and the line of work on independently training MoE components (Gross et al., 2017; Li et al., 2022; Gururangan et al., 2023), but departs from these by replacing full-corpus clustering or metadata-based routing with lightweight per-expert language models that route on short prefixes. It also contrasts with token-level MoE approaches (Switch Transformer, GShard, GLaM, Mixtral) by routing entire sequences to a single expert, which eliminates the need for all experts to reside in RAM and removes per-token routing communication. The EM-based training with balanced assignments is a practical adaptation of the classical EM scheme for mixture models, made feasible by the independence of the router and expert training stages.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work offers a concrete, low-overhead recipe for training large language model mixtures without high-bandwidth interconnects, which is a significant practical constraint for many research groups and organizations. The finding that 4.4M-parameter routers perform identically to full-size routers for routing purposes opens the door to extremely cheap routing infrastructure. Open questions include scaling to hundreds or thousands of experts, whether the approach generalizes to multimodal or non-text settings, and how routing quality interacts with downstream task performance at larger scales. A limitation is that balanced assignments still require some inter-node communication (albeit minimal), and the method has only been validated up to 1.3B-parameter experts.
