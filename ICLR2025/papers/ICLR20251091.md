## Problem

The complexity of computing Nash equilibria in two-team zero-sum polymatrix games—where players within each team play coordination games and players across teams play zero-sum games—remained an open question since Cai & Daskalakis (2011) proved PPAD-hardness for the three-team analogue. This paper resolves the gap by establishing CLS-hardness for the two-team setting and, in the special case where one team consists of independent adversaries (no internal edges), proving the problem is CLS-complete.

## Input / Output

The input is a two-team polymatrix zero-sum game specified by 2×2 payoff matrices A^{i,j} for each edge, with players partitioned into team X (cooperative, with symmetric coordination matrices) and team Y (independent adversaries, with zero internal matrices). The output is an ε-approximate Nash equilibrium as a mixed strategy profile over two actions per player, where hardness holds even for ε inverse-exponential in the game description. The reduction pipeline also consumes a quadratic polynomial Q: R^n → R as an intermediate object, and the final game is restricted to exactly two pure strategies per player.

## Method

The hardness proof is a two-stage reduction from MinQuadKKT (CLS-complete). Stage I introduces a multilinear 'COPY' gadget—(x_i' − x_i(1−2η) − η)(y_i − 1/2) scaled by a large constant T—that forces x_i ≈ x_i' at any KKT point, thereby converting quadratic square terms x_i² into bilinear terms x_i x_i' while preserving the independence property (no y_i y_j monomials). Stage II maps the resulting multilinear minmax objective to a polymatrix game by encoding x-variables as cooperative team players (with coordination matrices capturing cross-terms γ_ij) and y-variables as independent adversary players (with zero-sum matrices encoding θ_ij and ζ_j terms), then shows that a δ-approximate Nash equilibrium yields an ε-approximate KKT point via a thresholding argument. For CLS-membership, the paper exploits the independence of adversaries to dualize the max part of the minmax formulation into linear constraints, reducing the problem to finding a KKT point of a quadratic program with linear constraints—a problem known to lie in CLS.

## Results

The paper proves that finding an ε-approximate Nash equilibrium in a two-team zero-sum polymatrix game with independent adversaries is CLS-hard (Theorem 1.1), even when each player has only two pure strategies and ε is inverse-exponential in the game description. This hardness is shown to be tight: the same problem is also in CLS (Theorem 4.1), establishing CLS-completeness. As a byproduct, the paper shows that computing a KKT point of a bilinear (degree-2, multilinear) minmax problem with uncoupled box constraints is CLS-hard, which is the simplest non-convex-concave setting beyond the tractable convex-concave case.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or experimental comparisons. The results are positioned relative to prior complexity-theoretic work: Cai & Daskalakis (2011) proved PPAD-hardness for the three-team version, while Anagnostides et al. (2023) proved CLS-completeness for the single-adversary setting using degree-5 polytensor games. The present paper's CLS-hardness result is stronger in game structure (polymatrix, i.e., pairwise interactions only) but weaker in adversary count (requires multiple independent adversaries rather than a single one). The CLS-membership proof is simpler than Anagnostides et al.'s, avoiding the Moreau envelope machinery by exploiting the polymatrix structure and LP duality. The two-action restriction and the bilinear minmax corollary are novel contributions not covered by prior work.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper resolves an open problem from Cai & Daskalakis (2011), who showed PPAD-hardness for three-team polymatrix games but left the two-team case unresolved. The hardness reduction builds on the CLS-completeness of MinQuadKKT (Fearnley et al., 2024) and the CLS = PPAD ∩ PLS characterization (Fearnley et al., 2022), while the CLS-membership proof for independent adversaries leverages the polymatrix structure to avoid the Moreau envelope machinery used by Anagnostides et al. (2023) for the single-adversary setting. The key technical departure is a novel 'copy gadget' that uses max-variables to impose constraints on min-variables, enabling the reduction from quadratic to multilinear objectives within the polymatrix framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result establishes that even the simplest non-convex-concave minmax problem (bilinear polynomial objectives) is CLS-hard, ruling out polynomial-in-log(1/ε) algorithms and providing a fundamental lower bound relevant to adversarial training and multi-agent reinforcement learning. The CLS-completeness for independent adversaries is tight, but the case with interacting adversaries (where the problem is only known to be in PPAD) remains open, as does the single-adversary polymatrix setting. The gap between CLS-hardness and PPAD-membership for the non-independent case is a significant open problem that could clarify the boundary between local-search and fixed-point hardness in game-theoretic computation.
