## Problem

Existing ECG self-supervised learning methods treat ECG signals as ordinary time-series data, segmenting them with fixed-size and fixed-step windows that ignore the morphological form of individual heartbeats and the rhythm-level patterns across heartbeats. This perspective disrupts latent semantic relationships between heartbeats due to inter-subject heart rate variability, limiting the quality of learned representations for downstream cardiac diagnosis.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250296/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 12-lead ECG recordings (10 s at 500 Hz, downsampled to 100 Hz) and segments them via QRS detection into variable-length sequences of heartbeat patches (time window t=96 samples), which are concatenated across leads and padded/truncated to a fixed sentence length of 256 tokens. The pre-trained model outputs form-level representations (via vector-quantized heartbeat reconstruction) and rhythm-level representations (via masked sentence prediction), which are then used for multi-class ECG classification (macro AUC) across 5–38 cardiac condition labels.

## Method

HeartLang introduces a two-stage self-supervised framework built on the analogy of heartbeats as words and rhythms as sentences. A QRS-Tokenizer bandpass-filters the I-lead, applies Ricker-wavelet moving wave integration to detect QRS complexes, and segments each lead into heartbeat patches centered on detected QRS indices, concatenating all 12 leads into an ECG sentence. The ST-ECGFormer backbone applies 1-D convolutional token embedding, learnable spatio-temporal embeddings (10 temporal slots for the 10 s window, 12 spatial slots for the 12 leads), and position embeddings before a pre-layer-normalized transformer encoder. Stage 1 (VQ-HBR) quantizes each heartbeat embedding to the nearest entry in a codebook of 8,192 collective ECG words (dim 128) via cosine similarity and reconstructs the original signal with MSE loss plus EMA codebook updates, learning form-level representations. Stage 2 (masked ECG sentence pre-training) randomly masks 50% of heartbeat tokens and trains a linear classifier to predict the corresponding collective ECG word indices from unmasked context, learning rhythm-level representations. Downstream tasks use linear probing with the frozen ST-ECGFormer backbone.

## Results

On PTB-XL (100% training data, macro AUC), HeartLang achieves 87.52 (Superclass), 88.91 (Subclass), 80.23 (Form), and 90.34 (Rhythm), outperforming the second-best eSSL method by an average of 8.14 points across all four subsets. On CSN, HeartLang reaches 82.49 macro AUC, surpassing the best baseline (CRT, 78.80) by 3.69 points. On CPSC2018, HeartLang scores 77.87, trailing CRT (82.03) by 4.16 points, which the authors attribute to significant baseline drift degrading QRS-Tokenizer performance. Under low-resource settings (1% data), HeartLang still leads on PTB-XL Superclass by 7.92 points over the next best method (ASTCL, 72.51).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HeartLang decisively outperforms all 10 baselines on all four PTB-XL subsets at 100% data, with the largest margins on Form (+11.50 over CRT) and Rhythm (+12.11 over SimCLR), directly validating the heartbeat-as-word and rhythm-as-sentence design. On CSN, HeartLang leads CRT (78.80) by 3.69 points and BarlowTwins (77.43) by 5.06 points. The sole loss is on CPSC2018, where CRT achieves 82.03 versus HeartLang's 77.87; the authors attribute this to severe baseline drift in that dataset impairing QRS detection. Among contrastive baselines, ASTCL is the strongest on PTB-XL Superclass (81.02) but still trails HeartLang by 6.50 points. Reconstruction-based baselines (CRT, ST-MEM) generally outperform contrastive ones on PTB-XL Subclass and Form, yet both remain well below HeartLang, suggesting that the QRS-Tokenizer's semantic segmentation provides a structural advantage over fixed-window slicing regardless of the self-supervised objective.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant eSSL paradigm (contrastive methods like CLOCS, ASTCL and reconstruction methods like ST-MEM, CRT) that treats ECG as an ordinary time series segmented by fixed-size windows, instead proposing a 'heartbeats as words, rhythms as sentences' perspective. It also extends the nascent ECG language processing (ELP) line of work (Mousavi et al. 2021, Choi et al. 2023), which segmented individual waves within heartbeats and used small vocabularies (≤70 clusters), by operating on whole heartbeats and constructing a 5,394-word vocabulary. The vector-quantized heartbeat reconstruction component draws on VQ-NSP (Jiang et al. 2024) for EEG and BEiT v2 (Peng et al. 2022) for images, while the masked sentence pre-training follows the masked language modeling paradigm, but the QRS-Tokenizer and spatio-temporal embedding design are novel to the ECG domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 'ECG as language' framing offers a principled alternative to fixed-window time-series processing that directly addresses the form and rhythm characteristics central to clinical ECG diagnosis, and the 5,394-word ECG vocabulary is a practical resource that could accelerate future ELP research. However, the QRS-Tokenizer's reliance on detectable QRS complexes limits robustness for arrhythmias with irregular morphology, and baseline drift in some datasets (CPSC2018, CSN) degrades performance, suggesting the approach needs adaptation for lower-quality recordings. Open questions include whether the language-processing perspective generalizes to non-12-lead or wearable ECG settings, how the vocabulary scales with more diverse cardiac conditions, and whether the framework can be extended to multi-modal (ECG + clinical text) pre-training.
