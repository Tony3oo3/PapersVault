## Problem

Existing MORL methods suffer from three interrelated limitations: they train fixed policy sets for sampled preferences that cannot cover the entire Pareto front, they rely on preference-conditioned policies that scale poorly with the number of objectives, and they lack a principled mechanism to maximize utility for arbitrary unseen preferences. The epsilon-constraint approach, which could in principle fill the Pareto front, has exponential complexity in the number of objectives, making it impractical for tasks with more than a handful of objectives.

## Input / Output

During training, the method consumes environment state vectors (ranging from 2-D discrete to 348-D continuous) and action vectors (up to 23-D continuous), with reward signals decomposed into up to 9 objective components. Preference vectors are used only to guide the initial policy training stage; the resulting policies are preference-irrelevant and can be assigned to any preference at evaluation time. The output is a Pareto set of policies with their associated n-dimensional expected return vectors, from which a Set Max Policy is selected for any query preference via a simple weighted-sum maximization over the stored solutions—no retraining or adaptation is required.

## Method

C-MORL is a two-stage algorithm that reformulates MORL as a sequence of constrained MDPs. In the Pareto initialization stage, M policies are trained in parallel via multi-objective PPO, each guided by a fixed preference vector to produce an initial solution set. In the Pareto extension stage, N policies are selected from the current Pareto-optimal set using crowding distance (targeting sparse, under-explored regions), and each is extended by solving a constrained optimization that maximizes one objective while constraining all others to remain above β times their current return. This constrained problem is solved via an interior-point method with a logarithmic barrier integrated into the PPO surrogate objective, avoiding the second-order Hessian computations required by trust-region CPO. The key theoretical contribution is proving zero duality gap for the CMDP formulation and bounding the gap between the true optimum and the interior-point solution by (n−1)/t, while the crowding-distance-based selection yields linear O(nKN) time complexity versus the exponential cost of grid-based epsilon-constraint methods.

## Results

C-MORL achieves the highest hypervolume (HV) across all 10 benchmarks (3 discrete + 7 continuous), with up to 35% improvement over the best baseline. On the 9-objective Building-9d task, PG-MORL, GPI-LS, and MORL/D all exceed the 100-hour training limit (T/O), while C-MORL completes with HV=7.93×10³¹ and EU=3.50×10³. C-MORL also attains the highest expected utility in 9 of 10 benchmarks, e.g., 2.56×10² on MO-Hopper-2d vs. 2.34×10² for PG-MORL, and 4.29×10² on MO-Ant-2d vs. 4.28×10² for GPI-LS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

C-MORL outperforms all six baselines on hypervolume across every benchmark. Against the strongest continuous baselines, C-MORL leads Q-Pensieve by ~10% HV on MO-Hopper-2d (1.39 vs 1.26) and by ~7% on MO-Ant-3d (4.09 vs 3.82), while surpassing GPI-LS by ~35% on MO-Ant-3d (4.09 vs 0.55) and by ~73% on MO-Hopper-3d (2.29 vs 1.70). On the 9-objective Building-9d task, PG-MORL, GPI-LS, and MORL/D all time out beyond 100 hours, whereas C-MORL completes with HV=7.93×10³¹, demonstrating its linear-time complexity advantage. C-MORL also achieves the best expected utility in 9/10 benchmarks, e.g., 2.56×10² on MO-Hopper-2d vs. 2.34×10² (PG-MORL) and 2.28×10² (Q-Pensieve). The only metric where C-MORL does not consistently dominate is sparsity: on MO-Humanoid-2d, CAPQL and GPI-LS achieve SP=0.00 (perfectly uniform coverage) while C-MORL reports 0.18×10⁴, though this reflects a different trade-off in solution density. On discrete tasks (Table 1), C-MORL leads GPI-LS on HV for Minecart (6.77 vs 6.05) and MO-Lunar-Lander (1.12 vs 1.06), and on Fruit-Tree it achieves the best EU (6.53 vs 6.15) and dramatically lower SP (0.42 vs 5.29).

## Relation to Prior Work

C-MORL reframes multi-objective RL as a constrained MDP (CMDP) problem, drawing directly on constrained policy optimization (Achiam et al., 2017) and interior-point policy optimization (Liu et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2021) to replace the exponential-complexity epsilon-constraint grid with a linear-complexity crowd-distance-based policy selection. The Pareto initialization stage reuses the multi-objective policy gradient formulation from PG-MORL (Xu et al., 2020), while the extension stage departs from both single preference-conditioned policy methods (which suffer scalability issues) and evolutionary/prediction-guided multi-policy approaches (which require auxiliary models). The key conceptual departure is treating each Pareto extension step as a single-objective constrained optimization with a log-barrier relaxation, rather than solving a full multi-objective problem at each iteration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The linear time complexity O(nKN) with respect to the number of objectives is practically significant: baselines like PG-MORL and GPI-LS exceed 100-hour training limits on the 9-objective Building-9d benchmark while C-MORL completes within budget, opening the door to real-world MORL tasks with many conflicting criteria (e.g., multi-zone building energy management). The zero duality gap guarantee (Theorem 4.5) provides theoretical grounding for the interior-point solver, and the crowd-distance selection mechanism offers a principled alternative to uniform preference sampling. Open questions include whether the constrained extension stage can be made more sample-efficient in high-dimensional continuous spaces, how the method behaves under non-stationary objectives, and whether the learned Pareto set can be transferred across related tasks without retraining.
