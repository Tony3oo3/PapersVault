## Problem

Previous theoretical work (Zhou et al., 2023) attributed linear mode connectivity (LMC) under Weight Matching (WM) to the reduction of L2 distance between permuted model weights, but this paper shows WM reduces that distance by at most ~20% and that a second-order Taylor approximation of the barrier fails. The actual mechanism by which WM enables LMC remained unexplained, motivating a deeper analysis of what permutations found by WM actually do to the weight matrices.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250226/cropped_page4_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two independently trained neural network models (MLP, VGG11, ResNet20, or ResNet50) with identical architecture but different random seeds, and produces permutation matrices that align neurons across layers. WM is dataset-independent (no training data required), while STE requires a training dataset to evaluate the merged model's loss. The paper introduces an R value metric that quantifies singular-vector alignment between models, computed from SVDs of each layer's weight matrix, with an optional threshold γ to isolate large singular values. Experiments span MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR-10, and ImageNet.

## Method

The paper reformulates the WM objective (Theorem 4.1) via SVD of each layer's weight matrix, showing that minimizing L2 distance is equivalent to maximizing inner products between left and right singular vectors of the two models, weighted by their singular values. Empirically, singular values are nearly identical across independently trained models, so WM's effect is to preferentially align singular vectors with large singular values (verified via the R metric with threshold γ=0.3). Theorem 4.2 then shows that because hidden-layer inputs have large inner products with these dominant right singular vectors (Figure 4), aligning them makes layer outputs close even when the full L2 distance remains large, which is the true mechanism behind LMC. In contrast, STE minimizes the merged model's loss directly via the loss landscape and does not align singular vectors (R≈0), making it non-transitive: permutations found by STE for pairs (a,b) and (a,c) do not guarantee LMC between b and c, whereas WM's singular-vector alignment is transitive, giving WM a clear advantage for merging three or more models.

## Results

The paper's central empirical finding is that WM reduces the L2 distance between two models by only 6–20% (e.g., 6.6% for VGG11 on CIFAR-10, 20.3% for MLP on MNIST), yet the loss barrier at λ=1/2 remains small (0.035 for VGG11, −0.033 for MNIST MLP), and a second-order Taylor approximation fails to predict the barrier (off by 2.9 for VGG11 vs. actual 0.035). For three-model merging, WM produces dramatically smaller barriers between the two non-reference models than STE: on CIFAR-10 VGG11, the barrier between π_b(θ_b) and π_c(θ_c) is 0.141 with WM versus 2.172 with STE (a ~15× difference), and on ResNet20 it is 0.294 vs. 1.693. The singular-vector alignment metric R (γ=0.3) confirms that WM aligns dominant singular vectors (R up to ~0.8 for MLP) while STE leaves them essentially unaligned (R ≈ 0.01–0.21).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares WM against two baselines from Ainsworth et al. (2023): the Straight-Through Estimator (STE) and Activation Matching (AM). Against STE, WM wins decisively on the three-model merging task: the loss barrier between the two non-reference models is 0.141 (WM) vs. 2.172 (STE) on CIFAR-10 VGG11, and 0.294 vs. 1.693 on ResNet20, with accuracy barriers roughly 3× lower for WM across all datasets. The root cause is that STE does not align singular vectors (R ≈ 0.01–0.21), so the alignment is not transitive across model pairs, whereas WM's singular-vector alignment is transitive (R(π_b(θ_b), π_c(θ_c)) is clearly > 0 even though θ_b and θ_c were never directly matched). Against AM, the paper reports that results are 'almost similar' to WM in both barrier values and R metrics (Table 4, Figures 15–16), indicating that AM achieves LMC through the same singular-vector alignment mechanism as WM. Thus, WM and AM are effectively tied in two-model merging, but WM's transitive alignment property gives it a clear advantage over both STE and (presumably) AM for merging three or more models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the permutation-invariance conjecture of Entezari et al. (2022) and the WM/STE/AM permutation search methods of Ainsworth et al. (2023), using the Sinkhorn-based solver of Guerrero-Peña et al. (2023) for practical permutation search. Its key departure is from Zhou et al. (2023), who argued LMC holds because WM satisfies a commutativity property by reducing L2 distance; this paper empirically and theoretically shows that distance reduction is minimal (~20%) and instead identifies singular-vector alignment as the true mechanism. The paper also extends the comparison beyond pairwise merging to three-model settings, a scenario where prior work had not distinguished WM from STE.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By identifying singular-vector alignment (rather than weight proximity) as the mechanism behind LMC, the paper provides a more fundamental explanation that clarifies when and why model merging succeeds, with direct practical implications for multi-model merging where WM outperforms STE. A key open question the authors flag is that their analysis is per-layer, yet networks are multi-layered; a comprehensive multi-layer theory connecting singular-vector alignment across layers to end-to-end LMC remains to be developed. The finding that STE's loss-landscape-based permutations do not transfer across model pairs also cautions against using dataset-dependent methods for federated or ensemble merging scenarios.
