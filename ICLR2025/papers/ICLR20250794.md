## Problem

Neural lossless compression has never been systematically evaluated on astronomical imaging data, despite the fact that space-based observatories face severe bandwidth bottlenecks where even a 1% compression improvement on a $4.3B telescope translates to $43M in additional science. Existing astronomy codecs (JPEG-LS, JPEG-2000, RICE) are manually designed and may fail to exploit the unique spatial, temporal, and wavelength redundancies in raw 16-bit detector data. No public benchmark dataset or head-to-head comparison of neural versus classical compression for astronomy existed prior to this work.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes uint16 raw detector images in four dimensionalities: 2D single frames (Keck, Hubble, LCO), 3D temporal cubes from JWST up-the-ramp sampling (T×2048×2048), and 4D multi-wavelength multi-temporal cubes from SDSS (T×5×800×800). Neural methods process 32×32 spatial patches (or 64×64 for VDM) with reflective padding, while classical codecs receive full images; 16-bit inputs are handled either by extending bin counts/normalization constants or by splitting each pixel into MSB and LSB 8-bit channels. The output is a lossless compressed bitstream quantified as compression ratio (uncompressed bit depth divided by negative log-likelihood), which aligns closely with actual arithmetic-coding performance.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a systematic benchmark rather than a new architecture: it adapts four existing neural lossless codecs (Integer Discrete Flows, L3C, PixelCNN++, and Variational Diffusion Models) to 16-bit astronomical data with minimal modifications (changing normalization constants from 2⁸ to 2¹⁶, increasing logistic mixture bins, or doubling input channels for MSB/LSB splitting) and compares them against four non-neural codecs (JPEG-XL, JPEG-LS, JPEG-2000, RICE) across eight experimental configurations spanning single-frame, temporally correlated, and spectrally correlated data. Key findings include that VDM's likelihood-based bit-rate estimates consistently dominate all practical methods, JPEG-XL (max effort) establishes a new non-neural state-of-the-art, and neural codecs struggle to exploit cross-frame correlations in astronomy data. The paper further analyzes how background noise level (SNR, exposure time) governs bit allocation, showing that 98.5% of pixels in typical frames are noise-dominated and consume the majority of bits, and studies cross-dataset generalization of IDF to identify which training data yield the most transferable compression models.

## Results

On single-frame compression, VDM (theoretical bound) achieves the highest compression ratios across all datasets (e.g., 3.64 on LCO, 3.33 on Hubble, 3.27 on SDSS-2D), significantly surpassing all practical methods. Among practical neural codecs, IDF and PixelCNN++ are most competitive, with PixelCNN++ reaching 3.13 on Hubble and 3.35 on SDSS-2D, while JPEG-XL (max effort) establishes a new non-neural state-of-the-art at 3.26 on Hubble and 3.38 on SDSS-2D. On spectrally/temporally correlated data, JPEG-XL (max) dominates with ratios of 3.35–3.49, substantially outperforming the best neural methods (IDF at 3.03–3.14), indicating neural codecs struggle to exploit cross-frame correlations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks four neural lossless codecs (IDF, L3C, PixelCNN++, VDM) against four non-neural codecs (JPEG-XL, JPEG-LS, JPEG-2000, RICE). On single-frame data, VDM's theoretical compression ratios (3.27–3.64) exceed all practical methods by 5–20%, confirming substantial headroom for future neural codecs. Among practical neural methods, PixelCNN++ and IDF are competitive with JPEG-XL (max) on most single-frame datasets (e.g., PixelCNN++ 3.35 vs. JPEG-XL max 3.38 on SDSS-2D; IDF 2.94 vs. JPEG-XL max 3.26 on Hubble), while L3C consistently underperforms by 10–30%. On spectrally/temporally correlated data, the advantage shifts decisively to non-neural methods: JPEG-XL (max) achieves 3.35–3.49, outperforming the best neural method (IDF at 3.03–3.14) by 7–15%, revealing that current neural architectures fail to exploit cross-wavelength and cross-timestep redundancies. JPEG-XL (max) is highlighted as a new non-neural state-of-the-art that had not been previously evaluated on astronomical data, surpassing the prior best (JPEG-LS at 2.79 on LCO per Maireles-Gonzalez et al.) by 7%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts four established neural lossless compression architectures—Integer Discrete Flows (Hoogeboom et al., 2019), L3C (Mentzer et al., 2019), PixelCNN++ (Salimans et al., 2017), and Variational Diffusion Models (Kingma et al., 2021)—from their original 8-bit RGB natural-image setting to 16-bit astronomical imagery, with minimal architectural modifications (increasing bin counts or channel dimensions). It departs from the prior astronomy compression literature (Pence et al., 2009; Maireles-Gonzalez et al., 2023; CCSDS standards), which has been limited to handcrafted codecs like JPEG-LS, JPEG-2000, and RICE, by providing the first systematic benchmark of learned compression on real astrophysical data. The paper also extends the compression task from single 2D frames to 3D temporal cubes and 4D spatio-spectral data, a dimensionality not previously addressed in either the neural compression or astronomy compression literatures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical stakes are substantial: NASA's upcoming Roman Space Telescope ($4.3B) identifies data transmission as its single greatest risk, and even a 1% lossless compression improvement translates to ~$43M in additional science. The ~320GB multi-telescope, multi-dimensional dataset fills a critical gap—prior astronomy compression corpora were small, ground-based only, or rescaled to 8-bit—enabling the first rigorous comparison of neural versus classical codecs on realistic 16-bit raw detector data. Key open questions remain: neural methods currently underperform classical codecs on exploiting cross-frame and cross-wavelength correlations, VDM's theoretical bounds suggest large unrealized headroom, and practical deployment on space hardware (compute, power, latency) is unaddressed. The paper also opens the door to lossy and near-lossless compression for astronomy, where most pixels are noise-dominated and selective source preservation could yield orders-of-magnitude gains.
