## Problem

3D pharmacophore screening remains a standard technique in virtual screening, but its computational cost scales poorly with database size, making it infeasible for the billion-compound make-on-demand libraries (e.g., Enamine REAL) that are now available. The alignment step—matching query pharmacophoric feature points to database conformations via rigid-body transformation—remains a critical bottleneck that existing optimizations and pre-filtering techniques have not fully resolved.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253087/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 3D pharmacophore graphs as input, where each pharmacophore is a complete graph with one-hot-encoded feature-type node attributes (HBD, HBA, XBD, PI, NI, H, AR) and radial-basis-function-encoded pairwise distance edge attributes. Database pharmacophore embeddings are pre-computed once and stored; at query time, only the query pharmacophore embedding is computed and compared against the stored vectors. The output is a binary matching decision (penalty below threshold t=6500) and a ranked hitlist, where the ranking score is the minimum penalty across all conformations of a ligand.

## Method

PharmacoMatch reformulates pharmacophore matching as approximate neural subgraph matching and trains a GNN encoder (three NNConv layers with DenseNet-style skip connections, additive pooling, and a 3-layer MLP projector) to map pharmacophore graphs into a non-negative order embedding space R_+^512. Training is fully self-supervised via a contrastive max-margin loss that enforces partial ordering: z_Q[i] ≤ z_T[i] for all dimensions iff the query graph is subgraph-isomorphic to the target. Positive pairs are generated by deleting nodes and displacing remaining points within a 1.5 Å tolerance sphere; negative pairs use three strategies—displacement onto the tolerance sphere surface, target node deletion, and batch shuffling. At inference, matching reduces to evaluating the penalty E(z_Q, z_T) = ‖max{0, z_Q − z_T}‖²₂ against a threshold, taking ~0.3 μs per comparison versus ~66 μs for traditional alignment, enabling two-orders-of-magnitude speedup for large-scale screening.

## Results

On 10 DUD-E targets, PharmacoMatch achieves a relative AUROC (correlation with CDPKit alignment) ranging from 79.5% to 98.8%, with the vector matching step running at 0.3±0.1 μs per pharmacophore versus 66±6 μs for CDPKit alignment (~200× speedup). As a pre-screening tool on DEKOIS2.0, PharmacoMatch outperforms PharmacoNet on early enrichment (EF0.5%: 5.5 vs 4.4; EF1%: 4.9 vs 4.2; EF5%: 3.2 vs 2.9) while running 3 orders of magnitude faster (3.3×10⁻⁶ s vs 5.2×10⁻³ s per ligand). On LIT-PCBA, PharmacoMatch achieves 57.4% AUROC and 6.0 EF0.5%, with no comparable PharmacoNet results reported for this dataset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the CDPKit alignment algorithm (Table 1), CDPKit generally achieves higher absolute AUROC and early enrichment scores (e.g., ADA: 94% vs 83% AUROC; UROK: 91% vs 60% AUROC), but PharmacoMatch produces competitive hitlists on several targets (e.g., ANDR: 76% vs 71% AUROC) and is ~200× faster per pharmacophore for the matching step (0.3 μs vs 66 μs). The relative AUROC between the two methods ranges from 79.5% to 98.8%, confirming strong correlation in ranking. Against PharmacoNet (Table 2), PharmacoMatch wins on all early-enrichment metrics on DEKOIS2.0 (EF0.5%: 5.5 vs 4.4; EF1%: 4.9 vs 4.2; EF5%: 3.2 vs 2.9) and BEDROC (15.1% vs 12.3%), while trailing slightly on overall AUROC (60.9% vs 62.5%). The most striking advantage is runtime: PharmacoMatch is ~1,576× faster per ligand (3.3×10⁻⁶ s vs 5.2×10⁻³ s), making it far more practical for billion-compound pre-screening. On LIT-PCBA, PharmacoMatch reports results (57.4% AUROC) where PharmacoNet does not, limiting direct comparison on that dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

PharmacoMatch repurposes the order embedding framework from Ying et al. (2020) for neural subgraph matching, applying it to the long-standing task of 3D pharmacophore screening. Rather than treating pharmacophore matching as a geometric alignment problem (as in Wolber et al.'s Hungarian-algorithm-based approach or Permann et al.'s greedy 3-point search), the paper reframes it as an approximate node-induced subgraph isomorphism problem solvable via vector comparison in a learned embedding space. This positions the work alongside other ML-based virtual screening efforts (DrugClip, PharmacoNet, Sellner et al.) but distinguishes itself by directly encoding the subgraph-matching structure of pharmacophore queries through a self-supervised contrastive objective, rather than relying on supervised bioactivity labels or image-based representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The primary practical impact is a ~200× speedup in the per-pharmacophore matching step (0.3 μs vs. 66 μs), with embeddings computed once and reused, making billion-compound library screening computationally tractable and opening the door to vector-database-based retrieval for virtual screening. Key limitations include the E(3)-invariant encoder's inability to distinguish enantiomers (potentially inflating false positives) and the inherent information loss in the embedding relative to exact geometric alignment. Open questions include whether combining this approach with approximate nearest-neighbor retrieval can further accelerate screening, how to incorporate chirality awareness, and whether the competitive-but-not-superior absolute screening performance can be closed through better augmentation strategies or larger training sets.
