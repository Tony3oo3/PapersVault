## Problem

A persistent puzzle in knowledge distillation is that a stronger teacher does not always produce a stronger student, and while progressive distillation (learning from successive intermediate teacher checkpoints) empirically mitigates this, the underlying mechanism remains unexplained. Prior work attributed the benefit to improved generalization, but no one had identified how intermediate checkpoints accelerate the student's optimization or provided a provable sample-complexity advantage over one-shot distillation from the final checkpoint.

## Input / Output

For sparse parity, the input is a binary vector x ∈ {±1}^d and the output is a 2-class label determined by the product of k support coordinates. For PCFG and natural-language tasks, the input is a masked text sequence (with 80/10/10 mask/random/keep replacement) and the output is a per-position token distribution over the vocabulary. In all settings, the teacher's supervision is a probability distribution over classes or tokens at a very low temperature (τ = 10⁻⁴ or 10⁻²⁰), making outputs nearly one-hot to isolate the curriculum effect from soft-label regularization.

## Method

The paper identifies an implicit curriculum in the teacher's training trajectory: during the teacher's phase transition, its predictions develop strong correlations with low-degree features (degree-1 monomials for sparse parity; short n-gram contexts for PCFGs) that vanish after convergence. Progressive distillation exploits this by training the student on N equally-spaced intermediate checkpoints (each for T steps) before switching to the final checkpoint, so the student first learns the easy low-degree subtasks before tackling the full problem. Theoretically, the intermediate checkpoint provides an Ω(1/k) correlation gap between in-support and off-support coordinates in the gradient, reducing the student's sample complexity from Ω(d^{k−1}) (one-shot or data-only) to Õ(2^k d² ε⁻² + k³). Empirically, the optimal intermediate checkpoint is the inflection point where the teacher's robustness to n-gram perturbation (M_robust) peaks, and selecting this checkpoint for (2,T)-progressive distillation outperforms both one-shot distillation and direct data training across MLPs, Transformers on sparse parity, and BERT on PCFGs and Wikipedia/Books.

## Results

On (100,6)-sparse parity, progressive distillation enables a width-100 MLP student to reach 100% accuracy at the same training speed as a much wider model, while one-shot distillation from the final (20M-step) teacher checkpoint fails to match this speed. For the Transformer variant (32-head teacher, 4-head student), progressive distillation with 10k-step checkpoint intervals similarly accelerates convergence over one-shot distillation at 250k steps. On PCFG cfg3b (32-head teacher, 8-head student BERT), progressive distillation with 8 intermediate checkpoints improves masked-prediction accuracy and all three feature-learning measures (M_robust, M_close, non-terminal prediction) over both one-shot distillation and direct cross-entropy training. On Wikipedia and Books (12-head teacher, 4-head student), progressive distillation yields better top-1 masked-prediction accuracy and an average 0.9-point improvement on downstream tasks after fine-tuning. Theoretically, Theorem 3.2 shows progressive distillation reduces sample complexity from Ω(d^{k-1}, ε^{-2}) (one-shot) to Θ̃(2^k d^2 ε^{-2} + k^3) for (d,k)-sparse parity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three training strategies: (1) direct learning from data via cross-entropy loss, (2) one-shot distillation from the final converged teacher checkpoint, and (3) progressive distillation from a sequence of intermediate teacher checkpoints followed by the final checkpoint. Progressive distillation consistently outperforms both baselines across all four experimental settings (MLP on sparse parity, Transformer on sparse parity, BERT on PCFG, and BERT on Wikipedia/Books). The advantage over one-shot distillation is particularly striking: for sparse parity, only the intermediate checkpoint during the teacher's phase transition (not the beginning or end) provides useful supervision, and a single well-chosen intermediate checkpoint in (2,T)-progressive distillation suffices to reach 100% accuracy where one-shot distillation stalls. For PCFG, the optimal intermediate checkpoint corresponds to an inflection point where the teacher's reliance on short n-gram context peaks (M_robust maximum), confirming the implicit curriculum hypothesis. The sample complexity theorem provides a provable gap: progressive distillation achieves Θ̃(2^k d^2 ε^{-2} + k^3) samples versus Ω(d^{k-1}, ε^{-2}) for one-shot, a polynomial improvement in d for fixed k.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the progressive distillation strategy introduced by Mirzadeh et al. (2019) and the empirical observation by Harutyunyan et al. (2022) that intermediate teacher checkpoints outperform the final checkpoint for student training. However, it departs from the prevailing explanation (bridging the teacher-student capability gap for better generalization) by identifying a distinct mechanism: optimization acceleration through a transient implicit curriculum of low-degree supervision that exists only at intermediate checkpoints. The theoretical analysis draws on the sparse parity learning framework of Barak et al. (2022) and the two-stage training procedure, while the PCFG and natural language experiments extend the analysis to sequence-to-sequence settings using BERT-style masked prediction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to provide a provable sample complexity improvement for progressive distillation over one-shot distillation, and to identify the transient nature of the curriculum signal (it appears during the teacher's phase transition and vanishes at convergence). The practical implication—that checkpoint selection at the phase transition inflection point is critical, and that a worse-accuracy checkpoint can be more useful than the final one—offers concrete guidance for practitioners. Open questions include extending the theory to online SGD (the current proof assumes large-batch), understanding the role of temperature more precisely, and generalizing to generative distillation where multiple samples are drawn from the teacher rather than a single distribution.
