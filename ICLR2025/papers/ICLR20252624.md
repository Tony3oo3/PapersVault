## Problem

While long-context LLMs can process millions of tokens, simply increasing the number of retrieved documents in RAG leads to performance plateaus or declines due to noise and the model's limited ability to locate relevant information in ultra-long sequences. Prior inference-scaling work for RAG focused exclusively on expanding retrieved knowledge quantity without addressing how to effectively utilize that knowledge, leaving open the question of how to optimally allocate test-time compute across multiple inference dimensions (documents, demonstrations, generation steps) to maximize RAG performance.

## Input / Output

The system takes a natural-language query and retrieves top-k documents from a Wikipedia corpus using Gecko-1B embeddings (each truncated to 1024 tokens), combined with m in-context examples that themselves contain retrieved documents, queries, and answers. For DRAG, a single prompt is constructed with examples, test documents (in reverse rank order), and the query, producing one final answer. For IterDRAG, the model iteratively generates sub-queries (constrained to Self-Ask format via constrained decoding), triggers additional retrieval per sub-query, produces intermediate answers, and finally synthesizes the answer—up to 5 iterations. The effective context length, defined as total input tokens across all iterations, serves as the unified compute metric (ranging from 16k to 5M tokens).

## Method

The paper introduces two inference-scaling strategies: DRAG, which integrates many-shot in-context examples (each containing retrieved documents) alongside test documents in a single prompt to teach the LLM how to extract and apply relevant information; and IterDRAG, which extends DRAG with iterative query decomposition using constrained Self-Ask decoding, where the model decomposes complex queries into sub-queries, performs interleaved retrieval per sub-query, generates intermediate answers, and synthesizes the final result. Building on extensive grid-search experiments across four multi-hop QA benchmarks with Gemini 1.5 Flash, the authors derive inference scaling laws showing near-linear performance gains with effective context length under optimal parameter allocation. They further propose a computation allocation model, σ⁻¹(P(θ)) ≈ (a + b⊙i)ᵀ log(θ) + c, where θ = (k, m, n) captures documents, shots, and iterations, and i encodes task-specific informativeness of documents and examples. This model, estimated once per LLM via ordinary least squares, predicts optimal inference configurations under arbitrary compute budgets and generalizes to unseen domains and longer context lengths, achieving 96.6% of oracle performance.

## Results

Using Gemini 1.5 Flash, IterDRAG achieves 68.8% accuracy on Bamboogle, 55.3% on HotpotQA, 30.5% on MuSiQue, and 76.4% on 2WikiMultiHopQA at a 1M-token effective context length, outperforming DRAG (57.6%, 52.2%, 18.2%, 53.3%) and standard RAG (52.8%, 50.9%, 16.8%, 48.4% at 128k). The paper demonstrates that RAG performance scales nearly linearly with the order of magnitude of inference compute under optimal parameter allocation, achieving up to 58.9% gains over standard RAG. The computation allocation model predicts optimal inference configurations with 96.6% of oracle performance on unseen domains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DRAG and IterDRAG consistently outperform standard RAG (Lewis et al., 2020) across all four multi-hop datasets at every comparable context length; at 128k, IterDRAG achieves 68.8% accuracy on Bamboogle versus RAG's 52.8%, a 30.3% relative gain. Many-shot QA (Agarwal et al., 2024), which uses in-context examples without retrieved documents, plateaus at 16k tokens and is substantially outperformed by both DRAG and IterDRAG (e.g., 24.8% vs. 57.6% accuracy on Bamboogle at 1M). In the CoT comparison (Appendix C, Table 6), IterDRAG outperforms chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al., 2022) by 7.2–35.6% accuracy across HotpotQA, MuSiQue, and 2WikiMultiHopQA, attributed to interleaved retrieval and constrained Self-Ask decoding. Notably, DRAG excels at shorter budgets (16k–32k) while IterDRAG dominates at 128k and beyond, indicating complementary scaling regimes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the inference scaling literature for RAG beyond the narrow focus on increasing retrieved document quantity (Xu et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2024) by introducing two additional scaling axes: in-context demonstrations (building on many-shot ICL from Agarwal et al., 2024) and iterative retrieval-and-generation (building on Self-Ask from Trivedi et al., 2023). The resulting DRAG and IterDRAG strategies reframe test-time compute allocation as a multi-dimensional optimization problem over documents, shots, and generation steps, rather than a single-axis document-count scaling. The computation allocation model draws on the broader test-time compute scaling framework (Snell et al., 2024) but specializes it to the RAG setting with task-specific informativeness parameters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The near-linear scaling of RAG performance with optimally allocated inference compute (up to ~1M tokens) provides a practical roadmap for practitioners deciding how to spend their test-time budget, and the computation allocation model offers a lightweight predictive tool (96.6% of oracle in domain generalization) that could reduce costly grid searches. Key limitations include: performance gains diminish beyond 1M tokens, retrieval quality remains a bottleneck (NDCG plateaus around 100 documents), and all results are on a single model (Gemini 1.5 Flash) with one primary retriever. Open questions include whether these scaling laws hold across model families, whether improved retrieval (re-ranking, iterative retrieval) shifts the optimal allocation, and how the computation allocation model behaves for non-QA knowledge-intensive tasks.
