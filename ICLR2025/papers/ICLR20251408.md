## Problem

In-context learning (ICL) is widely assumed to be a unique hallmark of Transformer architectures, with mechanistic explanations grounded in attention. No prior work has examined whether vanilla MLPs can learn in-context, and MLPs are commonly presumed incapable of relational reasoning. This paper addresses both gaps by demonstrating that MLPs learn in-context competitively with Transformers under matched compute and, surprisingly, outperform them on classical relational reasoning tasks.

## Input / Output

For ICL regression, inputs are packed into an (L+1)×(n+1) matrix where rows are context (x_i, y_i) pairs and the final row is the query x_q with a zero label slot; the model outputs a single scalar. For ICL classification, inputs are a (2L+1)×n matrix interleaving context points and their label vectors, followed by the query; the model outputs C logits over class indices. Relational tasks (match-to-sample, sphere/line oddball) use n=2 dimensional points arranged on geometric structures, with the model outputting a point index. All ICL tasks use n=8 dimensional inputs, and the key constraint is that the underlying task parameters (regression weights β or cluster-label mappings) vary per example, forcing the model to infer the solution from context rather than memorize it.

## Method

The paper systematically trains and compares vanilla MLPs, MLP-Mixers, and decoder-only Transformers on controlled ICL regression, ICL classification, and three relational reasoning tasks, with all architectures matched at total training compute (PFLOPs). On ICL tasks, all three architectures reach near-Bayes-optimal performance (Ridge MSE or near-zero cross-entropy) at sufficient compute, though MLPs degrade sharply with long context while Mixers remain stable. On relational tasks, MLPs achieve lower loss at lower compute than Transformers and generalize better to out-of-distribution test conditions (perturbed radii, varied oddball distances). The paper also provides a theoretical rebuttal to the Boix-Adsera et al. impossibility theorem for MLPs on relational tasks, showing it relies on a pathological one-hot input scheme that is circumvented by standard embedding matrices, and proves a sufficient condition (Proposition A.2) under which smooth, expressive models generalize to unseen inputs given sufficient data diversity.

## Results

On in-context regression and classification, all three architectures (MLP, Mixer, Transformer) converge to Bayes-optimal performance (Ridge MSE / near-zero cross-entropy) at large compute, with only modest differences at small compute. On relational reasoning tasks (match-to-sample, sphere oddball, line oddball), vanilla MLPs outperform Transformers with less compute and generalize more effectively on out-of-distribution test sets. On simple (non-ICL) regression and classification with n=64 inputs, MLPs achieve substantially lower loss at lower compute than Transformers, a gap that narrows only when Transformer inputs are chunked into larger tokens. On the same-different relational task, MLPs generalize near-perfectly to unseen symbols beyond ~2^12 symbols, directly contradicting a prior impossibility theorem.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017), MLPs tie at large compute on ICL regression and classification but win decisively on relational reasoning tasks (match-to-sample, sphere oddball, line oddball) and simple non-ICL tasks, where the compute gap is substantial. The Transformer retains an edge on long-context ICL regression (MLPs degrade beyond 2^6 context points) and transitions from in-weight to in-context learning at lower data diversity. Against the MLP-Mixer (Tolstikhin et al., 2021), the Mixer handles long-context ICL regression more robustly (flat excess MSE) while vanilla MLPs handle long-context ICL classification better (flat loss vs. Mixer's slight increase). Against the relationally-bottlenecked MLP (Webb et al., 2023), the RB MLP achieves the best compute efficiency on well-aligned tasks (match-to-sample, sphere oddball) but fails entirely on the line oddball task without additional hidden layers, whereas the vanilla MLP continues to perform well, illustrating the fragility of hand-crafted relational inductive biases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by being the first to systematically evaluate vanilla MLPs on in-context learning tasks (regression and classification) that have been studied almost exclusively in the Transformer literature (Garg et al., 2022; Akyürek et al., 2024; Reddy, 2024; Raventós et al., 2024). It extends the MLP-Mixer lineage (Tolstikhin et al., 2021) and the relational bottleneck framework (Webb et al., 2023) by placing all-MLP architectures in direct, compute-matched competition with Transformers on controlled ICL and relational reasoning benchmarks. The paper also departs from the impossibility result of Boix-Adsera et al. (2023) by showing that the proof's reliance on raw one-hot flattening is circumvented by standard embedding layers, and provides both a theoretical proposition (Proposition A.2) and empirical evidence (same-different task) that MLPs can generalize to unseen symbols.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The results challenge the widely-held assumption that in-context learning is an exclusive capability of attention-based architectures, suggesting that the field should study ICL mechanisms beyond Transformers. The finding that MLPs outperform Transformers on relational reasoning tasks (match-to-sample, sphere/line oddball) with better OOD generalization is particularly notable given the long-standing debate about MLPs' inability to reason relationally. Key open questions remain: how MLPs compare to Transformers at scale on real-world tasks (language, vision), whether the advantage persists in data-limited settings where stronger inductive bias may help, and what specific architectural properties of attention provide (if any) a genuine comparative advantage. The paper's synthetic-task focus, while methodologically clean, means the practical implications for large-scale deployment remain to be established.
