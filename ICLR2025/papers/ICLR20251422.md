## Problem

Given a mixed collection of neural networks containing both pre-trained parent models and their fine-tuned descendants, can we identify all parent-child pairs and determine the direction of fine-tuning without access to any training data? Prior neural lineage detection assumed the parent/child sets were known and separate, required forward and backward passes on training data, and was framed as a classification task. This paper introduces the harder, data-free problem of neural phylogeny detection, where all models are pooled together and the fine-tuning direction must be inferred solely from model parameters.

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of M neural network models and accesses only a subset of their parameters (e.g., the first convolutional layer for ResNet, the last linear layer of the third transformer block for ViT) to reduce dimensionality, with no training data, labels, or forward/backward passes required. The output is a directed adjacency matrix A where A_ij = 1 indicates model i is the parent of model j, simultaneously identifying both the existence of a fine-tuning relationship and its direction. For the learning-based detector, the output is a per-node softmax over all other nodes, where the argmax identifies the predicted parent (or self if the node is itself a parent).

## Method

The paper proposes two complementary approaches. The learning-free method is grounded in a theoretical result (Proposition 1) derived from neural network linearization: the L2 norm of parameters monotonically increases during gradient descent, so the model with the smaller norm in a parent-child pair is the parent. This norm-based direction signal is integrated with standard clustering algorithms (KMeans, MeanShift, DBSCAN, GMM) via two strategies: 'identify after clustering' (cluster first, then pick the minimum-norm model as parent) and 'identify along clustering' (replace the geometric centroid update with the minimum-norm model with probability 1−α during each iteration). The learning-based method frames phylogeny detection as directed graph structure learning: a CNN edge encoder takes the stacked weight matrices of a source-target node pair as input (enabling element-wise comparison of aligned weights), and a single-layer transformer with node-index position embeddings predicts the probability of each directed edge. Training uses cross-entropy with a softmax over outgoing edges per node, exploiting the structural constraint that each model has at most one parent.

## Results

The learning-based Phylogeny Detector outperforms the best learning-free method by an average of 29.43% across all architectures and datasets. On ConvNet models, it achieves 99.17% on CIFAR-10 and 99.81% on CIFAR-10-50shot, while the best learning-free method (KMeans-along) reaches only 45.75% and 78.51% respectively. On open-source Stable Diffusion models, the detector achieves 99.44% overall accuracy (SD 1.4), 99.12% (SD 2), and 99.33% (SD 2.1), with 100% lineage accuracy in all cases. The 'identify along clustering' strategy improves KMeans and MeanShift by an average of 9.95% over their 'identify after' counterparts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The Phylogeny Detector dominates all four clustering baselines (DBSCAN, GMM, KMeans, MeanShift) across nearly all architecture-dataset combinations, with the largest margins on ConvNet (e.g., 99.17% vs. 45.75% on CIFAR-10) and FC Net (87.78% vs. 18.64% on FMNIST). Among the learning-free baselines, no single algorithm is consistently best: GMM excels on FC Net + CIFAR-10 (65.71%) while KMeans leads on ResNet + SVHN (71.55%), reflecting sensitivity to data structure. The 'identify along' variants (KMeans-along, MeanShift-along) consistently outperform their 'identify after' counterparts by ~9.95% on average, with the most dramatic gains on FC Net + CIFAR-10-50shot (88.32% vs. 43.80% for KMeans). On open-source Stable Diffusion models, the learning-free methods are highly unstable (e.g., KMeans-along averages 78.57% but with ±21.28 std), while the Phylogeny Detector achieves 99.44% overall accuracy with 100% lineage accuracy. The one notable exception is ViT + CIFAR-10-5shot, where the Phylogeny Detector (52.89%) underperforms KMeans-along (80.01%) due to insufficient training samples (only 16 child models available).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends neural lineage detection (Yu & Wang, 2024), which identifies a child model's parent from a known set of parents, by formulating the harder problem of detecting all parent-child pairs and their fine-tuning direction from a mixed collection of models in a completely data-free manner. The theoretical foundation draws on neural network linearization and the Neural Tangent Kernel framework (Lee et al., 2019) to prove that parameter norm monotonically increases during gradient descent, providing a principled basis for direction detection. The learning-based method repurposes graph structure learning techniques (transformer-based adjacency matrix prediction) in a novel domain, while the learning-free method integrates the norm-based direction signal into classical clustering algorithms (KMeans, MeanShift) via a 'identify-along' strategy. The paper explicitly departs from deep model IP protection (watermarking/fingerprinting), which assumes minimal fine-tuning to preserve task performance, by targeting full fine-tuning as transfer learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Neural phylogeny detection enables model governance at scale: given a repository of open-source models, one can map the fine-tuning dependency graph without requiring training data or model access beyond parameters, facilitating IP management, bias propagation tracking, and knowledge inheritance studies. Key limitations include the requirement for white-box parameter access (ruling out black-box/gray-box settings), inability to handle structural changes during fine-tuning (e.g., LoRA adapters, added layers), and the instability of the learning-free method on large-scale open-source models. Open questions include extending detection to parameter-efficient fine-tuning regimes, developing black-box variants, and understanding whether the parameter-norm monotonicity holds under more complex training dynamics (mixed precision, gradient clipping, non-MSE losses).
