## Problem

Identifying the best LLM method (prompt, model, or hyperparameter configuration) among many candidates requires exhaustively evaluating every method-example pair, which is prohibitively expensive—nearly $800 for AlpacaEval or 78 GPU-hours for GSM8K prompt tuning. The paper asks whether one can identify the top-performing method with a small fraction of the full evaluation budget by adaptively choosing which method-example pairs to evaluate next.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives a set of m LLM-based methods (e.g., 205 prompts or 154 models), a set of n validation examples, a fixed evaluation budget T (number of method-example queries), and a scoring function s that maps outputs to [0,1] (rule-based exact match or LLM-as-judge). It sequentially selects T method-example pairs to evaluate, maintaining a partially observed m×n scoring matrix, and outputs the index of the predicted best method. The scoring matrix is assumed to have low-rank structure, which the algorithm exploits to impute unobserved entries.

## Method

The paper proposes two adaptive selection algorithms. UCB-E extends the classical multi-armed bandit UCB-E: at each step it selects the method with the highest upper confidence bound (observed mean + √(a/observations)) and then randomly picks an unobserved example for that method, with a theoretical guarantee that the probability of selecting the true best method converges to 1 exponentially in T. UCB-E-LRF additionally leverages low-rank structure in the scoring matrix: it fits a rank-r factorization via alternating least squares on the partially observed matrix, uses a bootstrap ensemble (C=64 models with random entry dropout) to estimate per-entry prediction uncertainty, and then selects both the method (highest UCB combining imputed score and uncertainty) and the example (highest uncertainty) for the next evaluation. A warm-up phase of T₀=5% random evaluations precedes the active selection phase to seed the factorization. Empirically, UCB-E excels on easier settings (large performance gaps, e.g., AlpacaEval) while UCB-E-LRF dominates on harder settings (subtle differences, e.g., GSM8K Prompts), achieving 85–95% cost savings over full evaluation.

## Results

On AlpacaEval (Drop Annotator), both UCB-E and UCB-E-LRF reach top-1 precision of 1.0 using only ~8% of the evaluation budget, whereas all three baselines (Row Mean Imputation, Filled Subset, LRF) require 80–90% of the budget to achieve the same. Across all six dataset settings, the proposed algorithms achieve 85–95% LLM cost savings, identifying the best method with only 5–15% of the typical full-evaluation resources. On harder datasets (GSM8K Prompts, PIQA Prompts with H₁ > 66,000), UCB-E-LRF outperforms UCB-E by saving an additional ~10% in absolute budget, while on easier datasets (AlpacaEval with H₁ ≈ 966), UCB-E is slightly more efficient.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares UCB-E and UCB-E-LRF against three non-active baselines: Row Mean Imputation (uniform random pair selection with row-mean scoring), Filled Subset (select an example, evaluate all methods on it), and LRF (random pair selection with low-rank factorization for imputing unobserved scores). Both proposed algorithms consistently and substantially outperform all baselines across all six dataset settings and all metrics (top-1 precision at ε ∈ {0.001, 0.01}, statistical significance p ∈ {0.01, 0.1}, and NDCG@10). The gap is most dramatic on AlpacaEval (Drop Annotator), where the active algorithms need only ~8% of the budget versus 80–90% for baselines — an order-of-magnitude improvement. Between the two proposed methods, UCB-E (which selects the method via UCB but samples examples uniformly) is best on easier settings with small H₁, while UCB-E-LRF (which additionally selects the example with highest prediction uncertainty via the ensemble-based uncertainty matrix R) dominates on harder settings where performance gaps between methods are subtle. The ablation 'Score Only' (which uses LRF for score estimation but still samples examples uniformly) confirms that the active example selection via the uncertainty matrix R is the critical component giving UCB-E-LRF its advantage over UCB-E on hard datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical UCB-E best-arm identification algorithm (Audibert and Bubeck, 2010) from the multi-armed bandit literature to the practical problem of LLM evaluation, where 'arms' are candidate methods (prompts, models, hyperparameter settings) and 'rewards' are scores on validation examples. The second algorithm, UCB-E-LRF, further incorporates low-rank matrix factorization (drawing on the matrix completion literature of Candes and Recht, 2012) to exploit correlations between methods and examples, enabling active selection of both the method and the example to evaluate. Concurrent work by Shi et al. (2024) also applies multi-armed bandits to LLM settings, but focuses on prompt optimization rather than evaluation speedup, and does not incorporate the low-rank structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 85–95% reduction in evaluation cost is practically significant for practitioners who must select among many prompts or models under tight compute or API budgets, and the H1 hardness metric provides a useful diagnostic for predicting when active selection will help most. A key practical finding is that the simpler UCB-E outperforms the more complex UCB-E-LRF on easier datasets (large performance gaps), while UCB-E-LRF is needed when methods are closely matched—giving practitioners a clear decision rule. Limitations include the assumption of a fixed-size scoring matrix (incompatible with dynamic settings like Chatbot Arena where examples are user-generated) and the focus on top-1 identification rather than full ranking, which the authors identify as important future work for LLM design iteration.
