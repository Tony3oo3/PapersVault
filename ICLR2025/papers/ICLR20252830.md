## Problem

Existing methods for reconstructing articulated objects struggle to integrate geometric and motion information across different object states, particularly for complex multi-part objects with more than two movable parts. They rely heavily on pre-trained priors (e.g., SAM segmentation, 2D pixel correspondences) that become unreliable when matching pixels across states is difficult, leading to unstable part segmentation and inaccurate joint parameter estimation. The paper addresses this by asking how to jointly learn part geometry and articulation dynamics without external priors, while scaling to objects with 3–6 movable parts.

## Input / Output

The method takes multi-view RGB-D images captured at exactly two joint states (t=0 and t=1) of an unknown articulated object and produces part-level 3D meshes (extracted via TSDF fusion and marching cubes), per-part joint articulation parameters encoded as dual-quaternions, and a set of canonical 3D Gaussians at the mid-state (t=0.5). The two-state setting is standard in the field, and the canonical Gaussians serve as an intermediate representation that can be deformed to either input state via per-Gaussian SE(3) transformations. Mesh extraction uses Open3D with a fixed voxel size and truncated threshold, and the number of parts K must be specified as input.

## Method

ArtGS represents the object with learnable canonical 3D Gaussians at the mid-state, which are deformed to each input state via per-Gaussian SE(3) transformations parameterized by dual-quaternions. A coarse-to-fine initialization strategy first trains single-state Gaussians independently, matches them via Hungarian matching (after FPS downsampling to 5K points), then refines by adding static-part Gaussians identified through Chamfer Distance thresholds to the more motion-informative state. For part discovery, the method uses K learnable centers with Mahalanobis distance for assignment, augmented by a residual MLP over a hash grid to sharpen boundaries, and Gumbel-Softmax (with annealed temperature) for discrete part assignment. Articulation learning employs a warm-up phase with unconstrained dual-quaternion optimization, followed by heuristic joint-type prediction (revolute vs. prismatic based on rotation threshold) and constrained refinement. Training combines a rendering loss (L1 + D-SSIM + depth), a transient Chamfer Distance loss against single-state Gaussians, and a center-position regularization, all optimized per-object in a self-supervised manner.

## Results

On the PARIS benchmark (10 synthetic + 2 real objects), ArtGS achieves near-perfect joint axis angle estimation (0.02° avg) and part motion error (0.00° avg), outperforming DTA (0.13° and 0.02°) and PARIS (11.14° and 50.52°). On the new ArtGS-Multi benchmark with 3–6 movable parts, ArtGS reduces axis angle error by 10–100× (e.g., 0.02° vs 51.18° on Storage45503) and movable-part Chamfer Distance by 50–100× (e.g., 0.13 vs 246.63) compared to DTA. Training time is consistently ~8 min per object regardless of part count, versus 34–45 min for DTA which scales with complexity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ArtGS outperforms all three baselines (Ditto, PARIS, DTA) on every metric across all three datasets. Against DTA (the strongest baseline), ArtGS reduces axis angle error from 17–51° to 0.02–1.16° on complex multi-part objects and from 0.13° to 0.02° on simple two-part objects, while also achieving 4× faster training (8 min vs 32–45 min). Against PARIS, ArtGS is dramatically superior on real-world objects (Axis Ang 2.78° vs 16.00°) and on movable-part reconstruction (CD-m 2.45 vs 52.48 on synthetic). Against Ditto (a prediction-based method), ArtGS wins by large margins on all unseen objects (e.g., Axis Ang 0.02° vs 89.35° on FoldChair), confirming that per-object optimization with explicit 3D Gaussian deformation is far more reliable than feed-forward prediction for novel articulated objects. The gap widens substantially as part count increases: DTA's CD-m degrades from ~0.5 (2 parts) to 104–477 (4–7 parts), while ArtGS stays below 4, demonstrating that the canonical Gaussian + center-based part assignment design scales gracefully with complexity.

## Relation to Prior Work

ArtGS builds on 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023) as its geometric representation and draws on the two-state articulated object reconstruction setting established by PARIS (Liu et al., 2023a) and DTA (Weng et al., 2024). It departs from prior methods that rely on pre-trained part priors (SAM, LoFTR) or implicit segmentation (MLP, slot-attention) by introducing a center-based, skinning-inspired part assignment module inspired by REACTO (Song et al., 2024) and SC-GS (Huang et al., 2024c), combined with a coarse-to-fine canonical Gaussian initialization via Hungarian matching of single-state Gaussians. The key conceptual departure is treating canonical Gaussians as an explicit bridge between articulation states rather than reconstructing each state independently, which directly addresses the occlusion and information-loss problems that limit state-by-state methods like DTA.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ArtGS addresses a practical bottleneck in building digital twins for robotics: prior methods like DTA degrade sharply beyond 2–3 movable parts, while real-world objects (cabinets, ovens, tables) commonly have 4–7. The new ArtGS-Multi benchmark (5 objects with 3–6 movable parts) fills a gap in evaluation, and the 4× speedup over DTA (8 min vs. 32–45 min) makes per-object optimization more tractable for large-scale scene digitization. Key open questions remain: the method is limited to exactly two articulation states, mesh fidelity is bounded by standard 3DGS extraction (TSDF + marching cubes), and stability still depends on initialization quality for very complex objects. Extending to multi-state or video-based reconstruction and integrating higher-fidelity surface extraction (e.g., 2DGS, PGSR) are natural next steps.
