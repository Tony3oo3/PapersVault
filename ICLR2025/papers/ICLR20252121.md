## Problem

Self-supervised learning for tabular data typically relies on data augmentations to construct multiple views of the same sample, but constructing meaningful augmentations for structured data is non-trivial as they can generate samples outside the data manifold. This paper addresses the gap by proposing an augmentation-free self-supervised pre-training method that learns rich tabular representations without requiring any ad-hoc data corruptions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252121/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a tabular sample x ∈ R^d with mixed numerical and categorical features. Numerical features are normalized to zero mean and unit variance, while categorical features are one-hot encoded, giving each feature j an e_j-dimensional representation (e_j=1 for numerical, e_j=cardinality for categorical). Each sample is accompanied by randomly sampled context and target masking vectors that partition features into non-overlapping subsets. The output is a per-feature latent representation in R^{d×h} (where h is the transformer hidden dimension), which is then fed through a projection layer to downstream classifiers or regressors. A learned [REG] token is appended during pre-training but discarded for downstream inference.

## Method

T-JEPA adapts the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture to tabular data by performing mask reconstruction in the latent space rather than the original data space. A context encoder (transformer) processes a masked subset of features, while a target encoder (EMA-updated copy of the context encoder) processes the full unmasked sample; a predictor (smaller transformer) then predicts the target encoder's latent representations for a non-overlapping feature subset from the context encoder's output. The training loss is the L2 distance between predicted and target latent representations, averaged over multiple sampled context-target mask pairs where inter-set overlaps are forbidden but intra-set overlaps are permitted. A key empirical finding is that appending a learned regularization token [REG] to both context and target representations is critical to escape the initial representation collapse that plagues non-contrastive JEPA training on structured data, a phenomenon the authors also observe when retraining I-JEPA on ImageNet.

## Results

T-JEPA pre-training consistently improves downstream model performance across 7 tabular datasets. ResNet+T-JEPA achieves the best average rank (2.6) among all SSL methods, with headline results including 0.964 accuracy on ALOI, 0.401 on Helena, and 0.983 on MNIST. On most datasets, T-JEPA-augmented deep models match or surpass XGBoost (e.g., ResNet+T-JEPA: 0.865 vs. 0.872 on Adult; 0.964 vs. 0.951 on ALOI) and CatBoost. The only notable exception is Higgs, where GBDT methods (XGBoost 0.729, CatBoost 0.726) still outperform all T-JEPA-augmented deep models (best: ResNet+T-JEPA 0.705).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GBDT baselines, T-JEPA-augmented ResNet outperforms XGBoost on ALOI (0.964 vs. 0.951), Helena (0.401 vs. 0.375), and California Housing (0.441 vs. 0.433), while matching or slightly trailing on Adult (0.865 vs. 0.872) and Higgs (0.705 vs. 0.729). Against other SSL methods, T-JEPA achieves the best average rank with both ResNet (2.6) and MLP (3.9), outperforming SwitchTab (4.3), PTaRL (5.1), SubTab (6.3–8.1), VIME (7.4–7.6), and BinRecon (6.9–11.7). Specifically, ResNet+T-JEPA beats ResNet+PTaRL on 6/7 datasets (e.g., ALOI: 0.964 vs. 0.895; Helena: 0.401 vs. 0.383) and ResNet+SubTab on 5/7 datasets (e.g., ALOI: 0.964 vs. 0.958; Helena: 0.401 vs. 0.365). The only SSL method competitive with T-JEPA is SwitchTab for MLP (avg rank 4.3), which edges out T-JEPA on Jannis (0.726 vs. 0.728) and ALOI (0.942 vs. 0.961) but lacks results for MNIST and ResNet. Notably, T-JEPA's gains are largest for simpler architectures (MLP, ResNet) rather than attention-based models (FT-Transformer, AutoInt), suggesting it complements inductive biases of traditional deep architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

T-JEPA directly adapts the I-JEPA architecture (context encoder, EMA-updated target encoder, and predictor) from the image domain to tabular data, replacing image-specific augmentations with a feature-masking strategy. It extends the mask reconstruction paradigm of SubTab (Ucar et al., 2021) from the original data space to the latent embedding space, and repurposes the register token concept from Darcet et al. (2024) as a critical anti-collapse mechanism specific to JEPA training on structured data. The key conceptual departure from prior tabular SSL methods (VIME, SCARF, PTaRL, SwitchTab, BinRecon) is the complete elimination of data augmentations while operating in latent space rather than reconstructing raw feature values.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the need for hand-crafted augmentations, T-JEPA removes one of the main practical barriers to applying self-supervised pre-training to tabular data, and demonstrates that the resulting representations enable deep models to match or beat GBDT baselines (XGBoost, CatBoost) on most datasets—a notable result given GBDT's long-standing dominance in this domain. The discovery that a single regularization token is *necessary* (not merely helpful) to escape representation collapse in JEPA on tabular data, while being unnecessary for image JEPA, reveals a training instability specific to structured data that practitioners will need to account for. Limitations include the method's dependence on transformer-like architectures for downstream use, and the Higgs dataset where T-JEPA fails to improve most models. Open questions include theoretical understanding of why non-contrastive SSL works, extension to non-transformer architectures, and application to anomaly detection.
