## Problem

Existing multi-agent LLM frameworks (e.g., Multi-Agent Debate) treat collaboration as an emergent property of off-the-shelf models rather than a learned skill, relying on general-purpose LLMs that were never trained to collaborate. This paper asks whether directly training LLMs for collaborative problem-solving can outperform these emergent-behavior approaches, and proposes a framework that jointly trains a two-agent team specifically for iterative deliberation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253260/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a text question (optionally grounded in a passage or context) and produces a final answer through T rounds of iterative actor-critic deliberation (T=4 in experiments, yielding 5 total rounds). At each round, the actor generates a response and the critic generates feedback; the final output is the actor's response at round T, from which the answer is extracted via a text-parsing function ζ. During training, the method also consumes ground-truth answers to construct guided trajectories, but at inference time only the question and context are required.

## Method

ACC-Collab formulates multi-agent deliberation as a cooperative Stackelberg game and trains an actor (answer-provider) and critic (feedback-provider) via alternating best-response optimization: fix the actor, train the critic to maximize the actor's final accuracy, then fix the critic and train the actor, repeating until convergence. To handle the recursive temporal dependency across deliberation rounds, the method introduces a Partial Trajectory Reward r(z^(t), x, y) that estimates the probability of reaching the correct answer at round T given the current response at round t, approximated via one-step Monte Carlo rollouts. The key data-generation mechanism is Guided-Collaborative Trajectories: at each round, the agents produce three responses—a natural response, one guided toward the correct answer, and one guided away from it—by injecting the target answer into the prompt. The expected accuracy differences Δ_y and Δ_!y between guided and natural trajectories determine positive/negative preference pairs, which are then used to train each agent via Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) with LoRA fine-tuning. A single round of alternating actor-critic training is typically sufficient to produce a high-quality collaborative team.

## Results

On Llama-3-8B-Instruct, ACC-Collab (one training round) achieves 88.7% on BoolQ, 64.4% on MMLU, 59.3% on BBH, 95.2% on SCIQ, and 88.1% on ARC after five rounds of deliberation. ACC-Collab+ (two training rounds) further improves MMLU to 68.3% and BoolQ to 89.4%, though it slightly underperforms ACC-Collab on BBH (57.4% vs. 59.3%) and ARC (86.9% vs. 88.1%). The same pattern of superiority holds across Mistral-7B and Gemma-2-2B base models, with the largest gains observed on BBH (e.g., 51.9% vs. 48.0% for DebateGPT on Mistral) and MMLU (67.2% vs. 57.7% for DebateGPT on Mistral).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ACC-Collab outperforms all six baselines (SoM-2x, SoM-4x, Persona, DebateTune, SFT, DebateGPT) on the majority of dataset–model combinations. On Llama-3, the largest margins are on BoolQ (88.7% vs. 81.5% for DebateGPT, a 7.2-point gain) and BBH (59.3% vs. 55.2% for SFT, a 4.1-point gain). On Mistral-7B, ACC-Collab+ achieves 67.2% on MMLU versus 57.7% for DebateGPT (9.5 points) and 60.1% on BBH versus 48.0% for DebateGPT (12.1 points). The only notable exceptions are MMLU on Llama-3 where ACC-Collab (64.4%) trails DebateGPT (65.4%) by 1 point, and ARC on Gemma-2 where ACC-Collab (85.2%) is within 0.1 points of DebateGPT (85.1%). Overall, training-based baselines (SFT, DebateGPT, DebateTune) improve single-shot accuracy but do not substantially improve collaborative deliberation, whereas ACC-Collab's joint actor-critic training yields consistent gains across all five benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant multi-agent debate (MAD) paradigm—exemplified by SoM, Persona, DebateTune, and DebateGPT—which treats collaboration as an emergent property of off-the-shelf LLMs and modifies only prompts or deliberation structure. Instead, ACC-Collab frames the problem as a cooperative dynamic Stackelberg game (building on Fiez et al., 2019) and explicitly trains an actor-critic pair via DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024), introducing a novel off-policy data generation scheme called Guided-Collaboration that steers deliberation trajectories toward and away from ground truth to produce high-quality preference pairs. The key conceptual departure is that collaboration is treated as a learned skill rather than an emergent one, and the partial-trajectory reward mechanism addresses the recursive temporal dependencies inherent in multi-round deliberation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that explicitly training agents for collaboration yields consistent gains over both inference-time debate methods and single-model fine-tuning approaches, and reveals a qualitative shift in critic behavior (from agreeable to actively disagreeing) that is absent in untrained models. However, significant limitations temper the impact: experiments are confined to question-answering benchmarks with easily verifiable answers, training and testing occur on the same domain, and models are limited to 2B–8B parameters. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes to open-ended generation tasks, cross-domain transfer, and larger model scales where emergent collaboration may already be strong.
