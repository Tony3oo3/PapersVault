## Problem

Existing video diffusion models lack precise, generalizable control over camera trajectories. AnimateDiff supports only a handful of fixed camera movements via MotionLoRA, while MotionCtrl conditions on raw numerical camera parameters without geometric cues, limiting both accuracy and cross-domain generalizability. No prior method provides a plug-and-play module that achieves accurate camera pose control while remaining compatible with diverse video generation backbones.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253613/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt and a camera trajectory specified as per-frame intrinsic (K) and extrinsic ([R|t]) matrices, which are converted into Plücker embeddings (6-channel pixel-wise spatial maps of size h×w per frame, stacked over n frames). In the I2V setting, a condition image is additionally provided. The output is a short video clip (16 frames at 256×384 for T2V with AnimateDiff, or 14 frames at 320×576 for I2V with SVD) whose camera motion follows the specified trajectory. The camera encoder consumes only the Plücker embedding sequence—no image or latent features—making it appearance-agnostic and decoupled from the training data's visual domain.

## Method

CameraCtrl introduces a plug-and-play camera encoder Φ_c based on a T2I-Adaptor architecture augmented with temporal attention blocks after each convolutional stage, producing multi-scale camera features from the Plücker embedding sequence alone. These features are injected into the frozen U-Net by element-wise addition with the latent features, followed by a learnable linear layer whose output feeds the first temporal attention layer of every temporal block in both the U-Net encoder and decoder. The choice of Plücker embeddings over raw camera matrices or Euler angles is motivated by their per-pixel geometric interpretation and uniform value ranges, which the ablation study confirms yields the lowest RotErr and TransErr. Only the camera encoder and injection linear layers are trained (base model frozen) using the standard diffusion MSE objective on ~65K RealEstate10K clips, selected for its diverse camera trajectories and appearance matching the base model's training distribution. The design is validated as generalizable across AnimateDiff (T2V), SVD (I2V), and in combination with SparseCtrl for multi-signal control.

## Results

In the T2V setting, CameraCtrl_AD achieves TransErr of 12.98 and RotErr of 1.29, outperforming MotionCtrl_VC (14.02 and 1.58) while maintaining comparable video quality (FVD 1088.9 vs. 1123.2, CLIPSIM 0.301 vs. 0.286). In the I2V setting, CameraCtrl_SVD achieves TransErr of 9.02 and RotErr of 1.18, substantially beating MotionCtrl_SVD (10.21 and 1.41) and even improving FVD over the base SVD model (360.3 vs. 371.2). User preference rates strongly favor CameraCtrl: 43.6% in T2V (vs. 37.0% for MotionCtrl and 19.4% for AnimateDiff) and 73.1% in I2V (vs. 26.9% for MotionCtrl). The lower bounds for TransErr and RotErr on the RealEstate10K test set are 6.93 and 1.02, respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against AnimateDiff (T2V), CameraCtrl_AD wins decisively on camera control: AnimateDiff cannot produce measurable TransErr/RotErr (it only supports 8 fixed camera movements), while CameraCtrl achieves 12.98/1.29. User preference also strongly favors CameraCtrl (43.6% vs. 19.4%), though AnimateDiff retains a slight edge in FVD (1022.4 vs. 1088.9) and ODD (56.4 vs. 49.8), reflecting its stronger object motion without camera conditioning. Against MotionCtrl_VC (T2V), CameraCtrl_AD wins on all camera-control metrics (TransErr 12.98 vs. 14.02, RotErr 1.29 vs. 1.58) and user preference (43.6% vs. 37.0%), while also improving FVD (1088.9 vs. 1123.2) and CLIPSIM (0.301 vs. 0.286). In the I2V setting, CameraCtrl_SVD dominates MotionCtrl_SVD across every metric: TransErr 9.02 vs. 10.21, RotErr 1.18 vs. 1.41, FVD 360.3 vs. 386.2, and user preference 73.1% vs. 26.9%. Notably, CameraCtrl_SVD even surpasses the uncontrolled SVD base model in FVD (360.3 vs. 371.2) and FC (0.960 vs. 0.957), indicating that adding camera control does not degrade—and can slightly improve—video quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

CameraCtrl extends the controllable video generation line of work by introducing a plug-and-play camera pose control module built on top of existing video diffusion models (AnimateDiff, SVD). It departs from MotionCtrl's use of raw numerical camera parameters by adopting Plücker embeddings as a pixel-wise geometric representation, and from ControlNet-style encoders by using only Plücker embeddings as input (T2I-Adaptor architecture) to avoid appearance leakage. The camera encoder architecture draws from T2I-Adaptor with an added temporal attention module, and the injection strategy follows the broader ControlNet/T2I-Adaptor paradigm of fusing control features into a frozen U-Net. The comprehensive dataset ablation (RealEstate10K vs. Objaverse vs. MVIImageNet) is a unique empirical contribution not present in prior camera control work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CameraCtrl provides a practical, modular solution for precise camera trajectory control in video generation, with demonstrated applicability across T2V, I2V, and personalized video models, as well as composability with other control signals (SparseCtrl). The plug-and-play design and the finding that photorealistic training data with diverse camera distributions (RealEstate10K) outperforms synthetic data with wider pose coverage (Objaverse) offer actionable guidance for future camera-controlled generation research. Key limitations include failure on large rotations (>90°) due to training data distribution, reliance on COLMAP for evaluation (which is unstable on short clips), and the current restriction to relatively short video sequences (14–16 frames). Open questions include scaling to longer videos, integration with 3D/4D scene generation, and whether the Plücker embedding advantage persists in transformer-based (DiT) video architectures.
