## Problem

No watermarking technique exists for tabular diffusion models during the sampling phase. Existing image and text watermarking methods cannot be directly applied to tabular data because row-level operations (sorting, shuffling, deletion) break table-level detection, while fixed per-row patterns reduce cross-row diversity and degrade synthetic data quality. This paper addresses the need for a row-wise, diversity-preserving watermark that remains detectable under common tabular post-editing attacks.

## Input / Output

The method takes a secret watermark key κ and the Gaussian latent codes z_T (dimension m = token dimension × number of columns) that drive the diffusion sampling process. It produces a watermarked synthetic table where each row's latent code is constrained to specific Gaussian quantiles determined by a shuffled control seed. Detection recovers z_T via DDIM inversion from the table, reconstructs the control seed, and outputs a bit-accuracy score (aggregated into a Z-score or TPR@FPR) indicating whether the table carries the watermark.

## Method

TabWak embeds the watermark key into the positional ordering of a control seed rather than its values. The seed d of length m is split in half, the second half clones the first, and a pseudo-random permutation π_κ (seeded by κ) shuffles all m elements, so each row gets a distinct shuffled seed while the key is recoverable by un-shuffling. During generation, each latent dimension is sampled from the quantile of the standard Gaussian indicated by its shuffled seed value (l=2 quantiles), preserving the overall Gaussian distribution. A valid-bit mechanism at detection time uses l=4 quantiles and computes bit accuracy only on extreme tail values (quantiles 0 and 3), which are less susceptible to noise-induced flips. Detection proceeds by DDIM-inverting the table back to z_T, recovering the shuffled seed via floor(l·Φ(z_T)), un-shuffling with π_κ^{-1}, and checking whether the two halves match. The paper provides closed-form expected bit-accuracy expressions under additive Gaussian noise, proving the valid-bit mechanism strictly improves detection reliability over the base scheme.

## Results

TabWak with the valid bit mechanism (Ours*) achieves Z-scores consistently above 3.95 across all five datasets (Shoppers, Magic, Adult, Credit, Diabetes) at 1K, 5K, and 10K rows, corresponding to a theoretical false positive rate below 3.9×10⁻⁵. Under the strongest post-editing attacks (20% cell deletion, 20% Gaussian noise, 3-column deletion), Ours* maintains an average Z-score above 4 in all test conditions, while achieving 100% TPR at 0.1% FPR with as few as 200–300 rows. Data quality metrics (Shape, Trend, Logistic, MLE) for TabWak remain nearly indistinguishable from non-watermarked synthetic tables, with the Logistic detection score within ~0.05 of the non-watermarked baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TabWak (Ours*) outperforms Tree-Ring (TR) on both data quality and robustness: TR's table-level Fourier-space detection is highly vulnerable to row-level operations, with Z-scores collapsing to 0.3–0.6 under row deletion and shuffling, while TabWak's row-wise mechanism is inherently immune to these attacks. On data quality, TR degrades the Trend metric substantially (e.g., 0.499 vs. 0.907 non-watermarked on Shoppers), whereas TabWak stays close to the non-watermarked baseline. Against Gaussian Shading (GS), TabWak trades some raw detectability (GS achieves higher Z-scores on Credit at 84.09 and Adult at 41.64) for significantly better data quality: GS's fixed control seed across rows causes at least a 0.4-point drop in Logistic detection score and a dramatic MLE collapse (e.g., 0.166 on Shoppers vs. 0.867 for Ours*). Under adversarial attacks, Ours* maintains Z-scores above 4 in all conditions, while Ours without the valid bit mechanism drops to 0 on 2 of 5 datasets under column deletion. The valid bit mechanism is the key differentiator, providing the best robustness among all compared methods while preserving near-lossless data quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the quantile-based Gaussian watermark embedding from Gaussian Shading (Yang et al., 2024) to the tabular diffusion domain, but departs from both image watermarking methods (Tree-Ring, Gaussian Shading) by introducing a self-cloning and seeded-shuffling mechanism that embeds the secret key in the positional ordering of per-row control seeds, enabling row-wise detectability without sacrificing inter-row diversity. It also introduces a valid-bit detection mechanism that focuses on the tails of the latent distribution for noise resilience. Unlike prior tabular watermarking work (He et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024), which operates in the post-editing phase by adding noise to numerical values, TabWak is the first to embed watermarks during the sampling phase of a latent tabular diffusion model, preserving the Gaussian latent distribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Synthetic tables are increasingly used in finance, healthcare, and regulatory compliance, making traceability and auditability a practical necessity; this paper provides the first sampling-phase watermarking solution for tabular diffusion models with row-level detectability. The valid-bit mechanism's ~2–3× Z-score improvement over the base method is well-supported theoretically and empirically across five datasets and five attack types. Limitations include evaluation only on the TabSyn architecture with relatively small latent dimensions, and the method's dependence on deterministic DDIM sampling and exact decoder inversion, which may not hold for all tabular diffusion variants. Open questions include scalability to very wide tables, robustness against adaptive attacks that specifically target the tail quantiles, and whether the approach extends to non-Gaussian latent spaces.
