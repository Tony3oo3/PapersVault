## Problem

Existing strength estimation methods for games either require direct human-AI interaction over multiple games or are limited to coarse rank classification, while existing strength adjustment methods (e.g., SA-MCTS) only alter the final action selection without modifying the search tree, causing AI behavior to diverge significantly from human playing styles at the same strength level. This paper addresses both gaps by proposing a system that estimates player strength from observed games without interaction and adjusts AI strength while preserving human-like move selection.

## Input / Output

The strength estimator takes a single state-action pair (board position plus the move played) and outputs a scalar strength score β, where the actual strength is λ = e^β. For rank prediction, scores are aggregated via geometric mean across m randomly sampled state-action pairs per game, then compared against pre-computed composite scores for each rank. For SE-MCTS, the system additionally takes a target rank's composite strength score β_t and uses it to modify the PUCT selection formula during search, producing a move that both matches the target strength and aligns with human behavior at that rank.

## Method

The strength estimator is a 20-block residual network (256 channels) with three heads (policy, value, strength score) trained with a Bradley-Terry-derived ranking loss that enforces strict descending order of composite strength scores across all ranks. Composite strength for a rank is computed as the geometric mean of per-action strengths, which in log-space reduces to the arithmetic mean of β scores, enabling stable aggregation across variable numbers of moves. SE-MCTS modifies the PUCT formula by computing a normalized strength difference δ̂(s,a) between each node's subtree composite score and the target rank score, then subtracting c₁·δ̂(s,a) from the prior P(s,a) to steer exploration toward actions characteristic of the target rank. A synthetic weakest rank r_∞ (generated by replacing actions with random legal moves) is included during training to handle out-of-distribution states encountered during MCTS exploration. The approach is validated in Go (11 ranks, FoxWeiqi) and chess (8 ranks, Lichess), achieving >80% rank prediction accuracy from 15 games and 51.33% human-move alignment in Go.

## Results

In Go, the strength estimator (SE_∞) achieves over 80% rank-prediction accuracy after observing only 15 games, reaching 97.5% after 100 games, while the supervised-learning baselines (SL_sum, SL_vote) plateau at 49% even after 100 games. For strength adjustment, SE_∞-MCTS attains a 51.33% average accuracy in aligning with human moves across 11 ranks, outperforming SA-MCTS (42.56%) and vanilla MCTS (50.35%). In chess, SE_∞ reaches >80% rank-prediction accuracy with 26 games (93.38% at 100 games), and SE_∞-MCTS achieves 47.25% average move-prediction accuracy, surpassing MCTS (46.56%), SA-MCTS (40.21%), and SE-MCTS (38.93%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SA-MCTS (Wu et al., 2019), which adjusts strength via a post-search strength index z, SE_∞-MCTS wins decisively on human-like move alignment: 51.33% vs. 42.56% average accuracy in Go and 47.25% vs. 40.21% in chess. SA-MCTS also shows a notable weakness in strength calibration—its win-rate curve fluctuates between ranks 3–6 in Go, whereas SE_∞-MCTS produces a monotonically decreasing win rate across all 11 ranks. Against the supervised-learning rank-prediction baselines (SL_sum and SL_vote from Moudrik & Neruda, 2016), the strength estimator dominates: SE_∞ reaches 97.5% accuracy after 100 Go games versus 49% for both SL variants, and in chess SE_∞ hits 93.38% at 100 games while the SL baselines remain far below. Notably, SE-MCTS (without the r_∞ augmentation) achieves the highest raw move-prediction accuracy in Go (52.87%) but fails at strength adjustment because it cannot score out-of-distribution actions; SE_∞-MCTS trades ~1.5% move accuracy for reliable, monotonic strength control. In chess, SE-MCTS actually underperforms even vanilla MCTS (38.93% vs. 46.56%), further highlighting the importance of the r_∞ training rank for generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Bradley-Terry model for multi-way ranking, the AlphaZero-style residual network + MCTS architecture, and prior MCTS-based strength adjustment methods (Wu et al. 2019, Sephton et al. 2015). The key departure is twofold: (1) replacing supervised rank classification (Moudrik & Neruda 2016) or interactive strength calibration (Liu et al. 2020) with a BT-likelihood ranking loss trained on observed games, and (2) modifying the PUCT selection formula during MCTS search to incorporate strength scores, rather than only altering the final action selection as prior SA-MCTS methods do. The geometric-mean aggregation for composite rank strength is a deliberate modification from the BT model's product aggregation, tailored to ensure scale-invariance across varying numbers of sampled actions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for game developers and AI-assisted learning: a player's rank can be estimated from just 15 observed games (no interaction required), and the AI can then play at that level with human-like move choices (51.33% alignment vs. 42.56% for prior SOTA). A key limitation is reliance on human game records for training, which restricts applicability to games with established ranking systems and abundant play data. Open questions include handling intransitivity in player matchups, extending to games without well-defined rank ladders, and whether the r_∞ out-of-distribution trick generalizes to more complex action spaces. The method also opens avenues for cheat detection and ranking-system auditing in online platforms.
