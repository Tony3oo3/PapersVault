## Problem

The effectiveness of deep ensembles has been attributed to Jensen's inequality, but this explanation is fundamentally flawed: the 'average loss' it compares against has no practical meaning, the inequality holds trivially for any model collection, and it cannot explain why ensembles outperform any single member or why increasing ensemble size helps. The true mechanism behind deep ensembles' performance improvement remained unexplained, with the method treated as a reliable 'black box.'

## Input / Output

The framework takes M independently trained homogeneous models (identical architecture and hyperparameters, differing only in SGD stochasticity) and a test dataset, producing the ensemble prediction as the arithmetic mean of individual model outputs. Beyond the standard ensemble prediction, the theoretical framework yields closed-form expressions for expected ensemble loss as a function of M, and a practical estimation scheme that predicts the asymptotic (M→∞) ensemble performance from just two models. Experiments use CNNs and ResNets with widths k∈{10,20,40,80,160} on CIFAR-10/100 (32×32) and Tiny-ImageNet (64×64), with M=100 models per configuration.

## Method

The key insight is the 'distributional equivalence' property: although homogeneous ensemble members make different point-wise predictions, they all share an identical distribution of predicted probabilities over the data. The authors first prove that Jensen's inequality is insufficient (the point-wise lower gap is always ≤ 0 for monotonic metrics, meaning the ensemble always underperforms the best individual on any single sample), then show that under distributional equivalence and neural collapse (predictions → {0,1}), the Global Gap is provably positive, guaranteeing the ensemble outperforms any individual member in aggregate. They derive closed-form expressions for the expected Brier score and NLL of M-member ensembles, showing the loss decomposes into an irreducible population-mean term plus a 1/M variance term, explaining the observed inverse-proportional convergence with ensemble size. A remarkable consequence is that the asymptotic ensemble loss can be estimated from just two models via their pairwise prediction agreement, and the bias-variance trade-off in ensemble capacity is explained by the interplay between ρ (mean prediction level) and the global diversity Var_X[f̄(X)].

## Results

The paper trains M=100 ensemble members across 3 datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, TinyImageNet), 5 model widths (k∈{10,20,40,80,160}), and 2 architectures (CNN, ResNet), yielding 3000 total models. Theorem 4.1 proves that under distributional equivalence and neural collapse, the ensemble Brier score is always ≤ the best single-member Brier score (guaranteed improvement). Theorem 4.4 shows the asymptotic ensemble Brier score can be estimated from just two models via E_X[f^(1)(x)f^(2)(x)] − 2E_X[f^(1)(x)] + 1, and Figure 4 confirms this two-model estimate (mean ± std over all 9900 pairs) tightly brackets the actual ensemble loss across M=1 to 100. Theorem 5.1 provides a closed-form decomposition: expected loss = population ensemble loss + Var_F(F(x))/M, and Figure 7 shows the theoretical curves 'almost completely overlap' with empirical ensemble losses for both Brier score and NLL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical analysis paper rather than a method comparison, so there are no traditional method baselines. The key conceptual comparison is between the Jensen Gap explanation (Abe et al., 2022b) and the proposed distributional equivalence framework. The paper demonstrates that the Jensen Gap, while always non-negative, compares the ensemble to the 'average loss' which has no practical meaning, and cannot explain why ensembles outperform every individual member or why loss decreases with M. In contrast, the Global Gap (Theorem 4.1) is both provably positive and practically meaningful. A second important comparison is between ensemble capacity (increasing M) and single-model capacity (increasing width k): Figure 6 and Appendix C show that while both improve performance, they operate through fundamentally different mechanisms—ensemble capacity reduces the 1/M variance term (Theorem 5.1), while single-model capacity increases ρ. The paper further shows (Figure 8) that the previously reported high correlation between these two mechanisms is an artifact of distributional equivalence with point-wise distinction, not evidence of a shared underlying cause.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the model-distribution framework of Lakshminarayanan et al. (2017), which treats stochastically trained models as i.i.d. samples from a distribution over function space, and on the neural collapse phenomenon of Papyan et al. (2020), which justifies the binary (0/1) prediction surrogate used in the proofs. It explicitly departs from the Jensen-gap explanation popularized by Abe et al. (2022b) and from point-wise diversity analyses (Fort et al. 2019; Theisen et al. 2023), arguing that both are insufficient because they evaluate the ensemble against a meaningless 'average loss' or fixate on a single input sample. The paper's central conceptual move is to shift the analysis from point-wise model variance to the joint distribution p_X ⊗ p_F, revealing distributional equivalence as the operative mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous proof that a homogeneous deep ensemble always outperforms every individual member (Theorem 4.1) and gives closed-form expressions for ensemble loss as a function of M (Theorem 5.1), enabling practitioners to estimate infinite-ensemble performance from just two trained models. A key limitation is that distributional equivalence is established empirically (KS tests, Wasserstein distances across 4950 model pairs) but not proven from first principles; the authors acknowledge this as an open question, speculating it may relate to distribution shift. The practical estimation scheme (two models → predict asymptotic loss) is immediately useful for budget planning, but the broader question of why SGD-trained models exhibit this symmetry remains unresolved.
