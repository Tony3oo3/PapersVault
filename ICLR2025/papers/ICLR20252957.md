## Problem

Discrete diffusion models have narrowed but not closed the generative perplexity gap with autoregressive language models, and their standard denoiser-only design forces a single network to simultaneously identify which positions need correction and predict the correct values. This paper argues that explicitly separating these two subtasks—planning (which position is corrupted) and denoising (what the correct value is)—enables both simpler learning and a more effective adaptive sampling procedure that can detect and correct errors introduced in earlier steps.

## Input / Output

The method takes a fully corrupted sequence as input: for text8, 256-character chunks initialized to random tokens; for OpenWebText, 1024-token sequences from a 50,257-token vocabulary; for ImageNet 256×256, 128 discrete tokens from a pretrained tokenizer. Generation proceeds iteratively from t=0 (pure noise) to t=1 (clean data), with the planner dynamically selecting which single position to denoise at each step and the denoiser predicting its value. Unlike fixed-step tau-leaping samplers, the number of effective denoising moves is adaptive: the process continues until the planner estimates all positions are clean or a maximum step budget is reached, and time can even step backward when the planner detects reintroduced errors.

## Method

DDPD decomposes the uniform-diffusion generative rate R_t(x_t, j^d) into three factors: a noise-removal rate α̇_t/(1−α_t), a planner p_θ(z_t^d=N|x_t) that outputs a per-position corruption probability, and a denoiser p_{1|t}^θ(x_1^d=j^d|x_t, z_t^d=N) that predicts the clean token given corruption. The ELBO of the CTMC-based discrete diffusion process provably decomposes into independent cross-entropy losses for the planner (binary classification of noise vs. data per position) and the denoiser (categorical prediction of the clean token), enabling separate training. At inference, the Gillespie algorithm replaces tau-leaping: at each step the planner's corruption probabilities determine which dimension to update (proportional selection), the denoiser fills in the value, and an adaptive time-correction mechanism uses the planner's aggregate noise estimate to recalibrate the effective timestep, allowing the sampler to slow down, speed up, or even reverse time to revisit and fix earlier mistakes. The framework also supports leveraging a pretrained mask-diffusion denoiser by marginalizing over the latent corruption states, so only the lightweight planner needs to be trained from scratch.

## Results

On ImageNet 256×256 token generation, DDPD achieves FID of 4.73 at 64 steps (no logit annealing), substantially outperforming standard mask diffusion (27.54) and MaskGIT (9.08). On text8, DDPD with a mask denoiser consistently dominates the NLL–entropy Pareto front over DFM (the prior SOTA discrete flow model), even outperforming DFM with 2× parameters. On OpenWebText, DDPD reduces the generative perplexity gap with autoregressive GPT-2, achieving lower perplexity than SEDD at all step budgets (1024–4096 steps) while maintaining higher entropy (diversity).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DFM [7] (discrete flow matching) on text8, DDPD with the same 86M mask denoiser achieves lower NLL at comparable or higher entropy across all step budgets (256–1024), and even outperforms DFM with 2× parameters. On OpenWebText, DDPD using a pretrained SEDD [29] denoiser plus a separately trained planner achieves lower generative perplexity than SEDD at every step count (1024–4096), while GPT-2 [33] (autoregressive) still achieves the lowest perplexity at top-p=0.7 but sacrifices diversity. On ImageNet 256×256, DDPD dramatically outperforms the standard mask diffusion baseline from Yu et al. [45] (FID 4.73 vs. 27.54 at 64 steps) and consistently beats MaskGIT [8] (FID 4.73 vs. 9.08 at 64 steps), with the gap widening at higher step counts where MaskGIT becomes overly greedy. Notably, DDPD is the only method whose performance remains stable or improves with additional sampling steps, whereas MaskGIT's FID degrades from 5.51 to 9.34 as steps increase from 8 to 128.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the CTMC-based discrete diffusion/flow framework established by Campbell et al. (2022, 2024) and Gat et al. (2024), which parameterize the generative rate via a single denoiser network trained with cross-entropy objectives (as in SDDM, SEDD, DFM, MDLM, MD4). The key departure is a theoretically grounded decomposition of the generative rate into a 'planner' (predicting which position is corrupted) and a 'denoiser' (predicting the clean value), replacing the monolithic denoiser-only paradigm. For sampling, the paper replaces the standard tau-leaping discretization with the Gillespie algorithm from stochastic simulation, enabling adaptive, event-driven timesteps and a self-correction mechanism where the planner can send time backward to fix earlier errors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The planner-denoise decomposition offers a principled way to improve discrete diffusion sampling efficiency: the finding that allocating compute to a lightweight planner outperforms doubling denoiser parameters or steps is practically useful for practitioners working under fixed budgets. The adaptive time-correction mechanism (where the planner identifies residual corruption and continues sampling) provides a built-in self-correction loop that is more principled than heuristic confidence-based token selection in MaskGIT or stochasticity injection in DFM. However, experiments are limited to GPT-2-scale language models and 128-token ImageNet representations, leaving open whether the 2× NFE overhead and planner training cost remain favorable at larger scales, and whether the approach extends to longer sequences or higher-resolution image generation.
