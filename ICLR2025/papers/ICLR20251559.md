## Problem

Despite training algorithms being fundamental to the deep learning pipeline, the community has been unable to identify which methods are state-of-the-art, as previous empirical comparisons suffered from weak baselines, inadequate hyperparameter tuning accounting, and uncontrolled confounding factors such as model architecture changes. This paper presents the inaugural ALGOPERF: TRAINING ALGORITHMS competition, a time-to-result benchmark that isolates algorithmic improvements by fixing hardware, workloads, and model architectures while allowing innovation solely on the training algorithm itself.

## Input / Output

Submissions are constrained to a four-function API (update_params, init_optimizer_state, data_selection, get_batch_size) that prevents pipeline optimizations or model changes, ensuring measured speedups originate purely from the training algorithm. Two rulesets govern evaluation: external tuning (5 parallel hyperparameter trials via quasirandom search, scored on the fastest trial reaching the validation target) and self-tuning (completely hyperparameter-free, with 3× the runtime budget to account for inner-loop tuning costs). The final benchmark score is the normalized area under a performance profile aggregating time-to-result across 8 fixed base workloads and 6 held-out variants, with the median across 5 independent studies (different random seeds) determining each workload score.

## Method

The competition evaluates 18 submissions from 10 teams across ~4000 training runs on 8× NVIDIA V100 GPUs, measuring wall-clock time to reach per-workload validation targets. A major technical contribution is ensuring functional equivalence and comparable performance between JAX and PyTorch workload implementations, requiring careful alignment of framework-specific defaults (activation functions, weight initialization, layer normalization), shared TensorFlow data pipelines, and adoption of PyTorch 2.0 features (torch.compile with fullgraph, CUDA graphs, compiled loss functions) to close a performance gap initially as large as 60%. The winning external tuning submission (PyTorch Distributed Shampoo, ~28% faster than the NadamW baseline) demonstrates the effectiveness of non-diagonal preconditioning over popular diagonal methods like Adam, while the winning self-tuning submission (Schedule Free AdamW, ~8% faster) establishes a new state-of-the-art for hyperparameter-free training. A key finding is that top-scoring submissions are characterized by consistent, robust performance across diverse workloads rather than being fastest on any single one, and no submission successfully trained all workloads, highlighting substantial room for further algorithmic improvement.

## Results

In the external tuning ruleset, PyTorch Distributed Shampoo achieves the top benchmark score of 0.7784, delivering an average ~28% speed-up over the NadamW baseline across eight deep learning workloads (CRITEO 1TB, FastMRI, ResNet-50, ViT, Conformer, DeepSpeech, OGBG, WMT). In the self-tuning (hyperparameter-free) ruleset, Schedule Free AdamW is the only submission to surpass the baseline, scoring 0.8542 with ~8% faster average training. Across ~4,000 training runs on 8× NVIDIA V100 GPUs, no single submission dominated all workloads; five different submissions achieved the best per-workload performance, highlighting substantial room for algorithmic improvement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PyTorch Distributed Shampoo (non-diagonal preconditioning) outperforms all diagonal-method baselines: it is ~2% faster than Schedule Free AdamW (external tuning, 0.7077) and ~19% faster than Generalized Adam (0.6383) in average wall-clock training time. The NadamW baseline (0.5707) is beaten by five submissions but remains competitive, ranking 6th of 11. In the self-tuning ruleset, Schedule Free AdamW (0.8542) is the sole submission to exceed the NadamW baseline (0.8194), while NadamW Sequential (0.3308), SINV6/SINV6-75 (0.09–0.14), and AdamG (0.00) all score substantially lower, underscoring the difficulty of hyperparameter-free training. When the self-tuning Schedule Free AdamW is evaluated under external tuning rules, it scores 0.4804 (8th place), yet remains ~10% faster than the external-tuning NadamW baseline across the seven workloads both trained successfully—demonstrating that the speed-up is algorithmic rather than an artifact of parallel tuning. The ResNet workload is a notable outlier: only Generalized Adam reliably reaches the target, while PyTorch Distributed Shampoo and the baseline hit it in at least one of five studies but not consistently enough for a finite score.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalizes the ALGOPERF benchmark proposed by Dahl et al. (2023) as the first competitive, time-to-result evaluation of training algorithms, addressing well-known deficiencies in prior optimizer comparisons (Choi et al., 2019; Sivaprasad et al., 2020; Schmidt et al., 2021) such as weak baselines, unaccounted hyperparameter tuning, and confounding architectural changes. The scoring methodology draws on performance profiles (Dolan & Moré, 2002), while the evaluated submissions span the full spectrum of modern adaptive optimization: diagonal methods (Adam, NadamW, Nadamp), non-diagonal preconditioning (Distributed Shampoo, CASPR), schedule-free variants (Schedule Free AdamW, Prodigy), and learned optimizers (SINV6). The paper's primary contribution is empirical—running ~4000 training runs across 14 workloads on fixed hardware—rather than proposing a new algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The results challenge the field's default assumption that Adam/AdamW is the best general-purpose optimizer by showing that non-diagonal preconditioning (Distributed Shampoo) achieves ~28% wall-clock speedup across 8 diverse workloads, and that a fully hyperparameter-free method (Schedule Free AdamW) can beat a tuned baseline by ~10%. However, no single algorithm dominates all workloads, and the self-tuning ruleset reveals that fully automated training remains far from matching externally-tuned performance. Open questions include whether the gap between rulesets can be closed, whether the held-out workload mechanism is worth its computational cost, and how results generalize beyond the 8×V100 hardware and specific workload set used here.
