## Problem

Cancer drug response prediction (DRP) is hampered by extreme scarcity of labelled patient-drug pairs, since clinical guidelines prescribe only a small subset of approved drugs. Prior transfer-learning methods augment data in a shared domain-invariant space between cell lines and patients but fail to capture patient-specific genomic characteristics that strongly influence drug response. GANDALF addresses this by directly generating patient-like genomic samples from cell line data while explicitly modelling the domain differences in both mutation distributions and response scales (continuous AUDRC vs. binary RECIST).

## Input / Output

The method takes variable-length patient mutation profiles (sets of gene-alteration pairs restricted to 324 FoundationOne CDx genes) and drug identifiers encoded as 2048-dimensional Morgan fingerprints, and produces a binary RECIST response label (1 = good response, 0 = bad response) for each patient-drug pair. Unlike most SOTA DRP baselines that require fixed-length 7776-dimensional mutation vectors, GANDALF natively handles variable-length mutation sets via a pretrained transformer encoder with padding. The augmentation pipeline additionally consumes cell line mutation profiles and their continuous AUDRC drug-response labels (from CCLE/GDSCv2) to generate synthetic patient samples and pseudo-labels.

## Method

GANDALF is a five-step generative framework that decomposes each domain into a shared latent Z_s and domain-specific components (Z_p for patients, Z_c for cell lines), then transforms cell line data into patient-like samples by introducing Z_p over Z_s. Step 1 pretrains domain-specific DDPMs (with VAEs for dimensionality reduction) using CORAL loss for domain alignment and cross-attention to preserve cell line information in generated samples. Step 2 generates X_aug by encoding cell lines through the cell-line DDPM encoder and denoising with the patient DDPM decoder. Step 3 trains a multi-task network with separate AUDRC regression and RECIST classification heads, regularised by CORAL alignment in a shared latent space, to assign pseudo-labels to (X_aug, d_aug) pairs across all drugs in D. Steps 4–5 threshold pseudo-labels to select high-confidence samples and train a downstream DRP classifier on the union of real and augmented patient data. The key distinction from prior work is that augmentation happens directly in patient genomic space (not a shared representation space), and the pseudo-labelling explicitly accounts for the domain mismatch between continuous cell-line and binary patient response scales.

## Results

On five clinically relevant drugs (Cisplatin, 5-Fluorouracil, Gemcitabine, Paclitaxel, Temozolomide) evaluated across TCGA, CbioPortal, and Moores patient datasets, GANDALF achieves the best AUROC on 4 of 5 drugs (Flu: 0.7309, Gem: 0.6188, Pac: 0.7728, Tem: 0.6451) and the best AUPRC on 3 of 5 drugs (Flu: 0.8483, Gem: 0.5874, Pac: 0.9558). The largest improvement over the next-best SOTA method is 10.96% AUROC on Gemcitabine (0.6188 vs. DruID's 0.5092). GANDALF also outperforms a naive Gaussian perturbation augmentation baseline by 21% AUROC and a majority-vote pseudo-labeling strategy by 2.5% AUROC in ablation comparisons.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GANDALF outperforms all six SOTA DRP baselines on the majority of drugs. On AUROC, GANDALF leads on Flu (0.7309 vs. drug2tme's 0.7167), Gem (0.6188 vs. DruID's 0.5092, a 10.96% gain), Pac (0.7728 vs. PREDICT-AI's 0.6815), and Tem (0.6451 vs. PANCDR's 0.6436), while DruID retains the edge on Cis (0.6764 vs. 0.6343). On AUPRC, GANDALF dominates on Pac (0.9558 vs. DruID's 0.8897), Flu (0.8483 vs. drug2tme's 0.8092), and Gem (0.5874 vs. drug2tme's 0.4826), but trails DruID on Cis (0.9093 vs. 0.9176) and Tem (0.2535 vs. drug2tme's 0.3058). Against the closest pseudo-labeling competitor WISER, GANDALF improves AUROC by 8–41 percentage points across drugs, and against the Gaussian perturbation augmentation baseline it gains 21% AUROC. The ablation further shows that removing the MTL pseudo-labeling head or the cross-attention mechanism each drops overall AUROC from 0.8409 to ~0.75, confirming both components are critical.

## Relation to Prior Work

GANDALF departs from the dominant transfer-learning paradigm in cancer drug response prediction (DRP), where prior methods (AITL, TUGDA, WISER, PANCDR, CODE-AE) learn a shared domain-invariant representation between cell lines and patients and train a downstream classifier in that shared space. The key conceptual shift is that GANDALF directly generates patient-like genomic samples from cell lines using a generative pipeline (DDPM + VAE + cross-attention), explicitly modelling both the input distribution shift P(X_p)≠P(X_c) and the label-space mismatch (AUDRC vs. RECIST), rather than collapsing both domains into a single representation. The architecture synthesises components from DDPMs (Ho et al., 2020), the PREDICT-AI transformer encoder (Jayagopal et al., 2024), TabDDPM-style feedforward diffusion for vector data (Kotelnikov et al., 2023), and CORAL domain-alignment losses (Sun et al., 2016), but their combination for label-aware genomic augmentation is novel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GANDALF addresses a fundamental bottleneck in precision oncology: the extreme scarcity of labelled patient-drug response pairs relative to the combinatorial space of mutation profiles and approved drugs. By demonstrating that generative augmentation of patient genomic data—rather than merely augmenting in a shared latent space—can yield up to ~11% AUROC improvement over SOTA DRP methods, the paper opens a path toward data-efficient personalised treatment prediction. Key limitations include the small patient cohort (~665 samples across 56 drugs), the large number of pretrained sub-modules increasing training cost, and the lack of biological validation for generated samples. Open questions include whether unlabelled patient profiles can further improve all training stages, whether tumor-type-specific fine-tuning helps, and whether the generated mutations have meaningful biological co-occurrence patterns.
