## Problem

It is unknown whether stronger LLMs can be aligned using feedback from weaker models (or humans) without degrading their capabilities. This paper proves that the standard approach—naively fine-tuning the strong model on weak labels—suffers a fundamental MSE lower bound because the strong model learns to emulate the weak model's errors, and that an alternative refinement procedure can provably overcome this limitation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253624/cropped_page7_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a weakly labeled dataset (queries paired with outputs from a smaller, aligned model) and sampling access to a larger unaligned LLM. It first uses the weak labels as in-context learning examples to prompt the strong model, which resamples new labels that inherit the target concept (e.g., persona, reasoning style) from the weak examples but are generated with the strong model's own accuracy. The strong model is then fine-tuned on these refined labels, and at test time it is never given any explicit instruction about the target concept—all generalization must come from the refined training data.

## Method

The paper casts weak-to-strong generalization as a transfer learning problem where the source model is a mixture of K linear regression components (latent concepts) and the target is a single component corresponding to the desired concept. It proves that the standard shrinkage-to-source fine-tuning estimator has an irreducible bias floor determined by both the weak model's error and the source model's mixture prior. The key mechanism is ICL refinement: weak labels are fed as in-context examples to the strong model, which—under a latent Bayesian inference interpretation—infers the target concept posterior from the weak examples and then samples a new label from its own (more capable) conditional distribution. The paper proves that the probability of selecting the wrong concept decays exponentially in the number of ICL examples, with a rate that degrades gracefully as the weak model becomes weaker. Empirically, this allows GPT-3.5-Turbo and GPT-4o-mini to learn a pirate persona, improve math reasoning, and adopt an analogy-based explanation style from 7B-class weak models without the accuracy degradation seen in naive fine-tuning.

## Results

On the persona task (Table 1), ICL refinement achieves content scores of 7.07–7.58 on TruthfulQA and 8.08–8.26 on AlpacaEval while simultaneously learning the target style (style scores 4.10–7.99), closely matching the unaligned base model's accuracy. In contrast, the bootstrap baseline collapses content scores to 2.88–5.29, and the auxiliary loss baseline preserves content (6.14–7.92) but sacrifices style (2.38–5.65). On mathematical reasoning (GSM8K and MATH), ICL refinement improves GPT-3.5-Turbo's accuracy over the base model across all three weak-label providers, while naive fine-tuning on weak labels often causes a substantial decrease in reasoning capability. For GPT-4o-mini, refinement outperforms naive fine-tuning but the absolute gains from weak supervision are more limited.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against naive fine-tuning on weak labels (the standard weak-to-strong method from Burns et al. 2023a). ICL refinement decisively outperforms naive fine-tuning: in the persona task, naive fine-tuning substantially degrades content/accuracy scores (the test-time content score drops well below the base model, worsening as weak-label quality decreases), while ICL refinement keeps content scores at or above the base model level. Against the two additional baselines from Burns et al. 2023b (bootstrap and auxiliary confidence loss), ICL refinement achieves the best balance: bootstrap transfers style well (8.31–8.65 style) but catastrophically damages content (2.88–5.29), and auxiliary loss preserves content (6.14–7.92) but nearly eliminates style transfer (2.38–5.65). On mathematical reasoning (GSM8K, MATH), ICL refinement is the only method that consistently improves GPT-3.5-Turbo over its unaligned baseline across all weak-label providers, whereas naive fine-tuning often degrades reasoning accuracy. For GPT-4o-mini, ICL refinement still outperforms naive fine-tuning, but the absolute gains are smaller, suggesting the stronger model has less room for weak supervision to help.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the weak-to-strong generalization problem defined by Burns et al. (2023a) and the latent concept model for LLMs from Wang et al. (2024), which frames in-context learning as implicit Bayesian inference over a mixture of regression components. It departs sharply from concurrent theoretical works (Charikar et al. 2024, Lang et al. 2024, Wu & Sahai 2024) that demonstrate naive fine-tuning on weak labels yields gains over the weak model; here, the authors argue those gains are illusory because the fine-tuned strong model still degrades relative to its unaligned version. The ICL refinement procedure operationalizes the convex hull structure of the source mixture, connecting to Pathak et al. (2024) on transformers learning mixture-of-regressions and Xie et al. (2021) on ICL as Bayesian inference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The negative result—that naive fine-tuning on weak labels systematically degrades the strong model's accuracy compared to its unaligned baseline—challenges the default recipe in the weak-to-strong generalization literature and reframes what 'generalization' means (must beat the unaligned strong model, not just the weak teacher). The ICL refinement method is simple and practical, but gains for GPT-4o-mini remain limited even with refined labels, suggesting the convex hull assumption may be too optimistic for the strongest models or that more sophisticated elicitation is needed. Open questions include whether the orthonormality and convex hull assumptions hold for real LLMs, whether refinement can be iterated or combined with other alignment objectives, and whether the approach transfers to tasks where the target concept is not a simple prior shift.
