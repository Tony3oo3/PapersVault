## Problem

Conformal prediction sets are widely used as human decision aids, and the standard fairness criterion in the CP literature is Equalized Coverage across protected groups. However, no prior work has empirically tested whether Equalized Coverage actually produces fair outcomes when humans act on prediction sets. This paper asks whether providing conformal sets to human decision makers causes disparate impact, and whether the accepted fairness standard of Equalized Coverage helps or hurts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250715/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single datapoint from one of three modalities (a 224×224 RGB image, a truncated 400-character biography, or a ~4-second audio recording) and produces a prediction set of class labels from a fixed vocabulary (20 occupations, 10 occupations, or 8 emotions respectively). The prediction set is displayed alongside all class options to a human participant who must select exactly one label; the set is accompanied by a stated coverage guarantee (target 90%), and no other model information such as softmax scores is revealed. For the image task, the image is displayed for only one second to increase difficulty.

## Method

The paper conducts pre-registered randomized controlled trials with 600 human participants (50 per experiment) across three classification tasks, comparing four treatments: no assistance (control), marginal conformal prediction, Mondrian (conditional) conformal prediction achieving Equalized Coverage, and avg-k set prediction. Prediction sets are generated using RAPS or SAPS score functions calibrated on held-out data, with hyperparameters tuned via Optuna to minimize average set size at 90% target coverage. Statistical analysis uses Generalized Estimating Equations (GEEs) with a treatment × group interaction to estimate odds ratios and ratio-of-odds-ratios (RORs) that account for intra-participant clustering. The key finding is that set size disparity, not coverage disparity, drives disparate impact in human accuracy: conditional CP equalizes coverage but increases set size gaps, which paradoxically worsens fairness. The authors propose Equalized Set Size (or Equalized Singleton Frequency) as a more effective fairness criterion for set predictors used in human-in-the-loop pipelines.

## Results

Across three human-subject experiments (600 participants, 30,000 responses), the paper measures disparate impact via the maximum ratio of odds ratios (max ROR) from GEE models. Conditional (Mondrian) CP with Equalized Coverage produces the highest disparate impact on all three datasets: max ROR of 1.51 (FACET), 1.33 (BiosBias), and 1.28 (RAVDESS), compared to marginal CP at 1.26, 1.12, and 1.01 respectively. On FACET, the conditional treatment even reduced human accuracy below the unassisted control for the Older and Unknown age groups (OR = 0.91 for both). The paper concludes that Equalized Coverage exacerbates rather than mitigates disparate impact, and advocates Equalized Set Size as a more effective fairness criterion.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three set-prediction methods (avg-k, marginal CP, conditional/Mondrian CP) against an unassisted control in human-subject experiments. The central finding is that conditional CP, which achieves Equalized Coverage (Δ_Cov ≈ 0), consistently produces the worst disparate impact: max ROR of 1.51 vs. 1.26 (marginal) on FACET, 1.33 vs. 1.12 on BiosBias, and 1.28 vs. 1.01 on RAVDESS. Marginal CP is the fairest option on RAVDESS (max ROR = 1.01, essentially no disparate impact) and competitive on BiosBias (1.12). Avg-k performs best on FACET (max ROR = 1.11) but is comparable to conditional on BiosBias (1.34 vs. 1.33). The mechanism is that equalizing coverage forces larger sets on harder groups and smaller sets on easier groups, and since human accuracy correlates strongly with set size (not coverage), this amplifies rather than reduces outcome disparities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the conformal prediction framework (Vovk et al., 2005) and Mondrian CP (Vovk et al., 2003) for generating prediction sets, and on prior human-in-the-loop studies (Cresswell et al., 2024; Straitouri & Gomez Rodriguez, 2024) that demonstrated prediction sets improve human decision accuracy. It departs from the CP fairness literature (Romano et al., 2020a; Lu et al., 2022; Ding et al., 2024) which has broadly adopted Equalized Coverage as the standard fairness criterion, arguing instead that this is a procedural fairness notion that does not translate to substantive fairness in human decisioning. The paper also incorporates avg-k set prediction (Lorieul et al., 2021) as a non-conformal baseline and uses Generalized Estimating Equations for rigorous statistical analysis of clustered human responses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first pre-registered RCT evidence showing that the widely-adopted Equalized Coverage standard in CP actually increases disparate impact on human outcomes, directly challenging the prevailing fairness framework in the CP literature. The finding that set size (not coverage) is the primary driver of human accuracy provides a clear mechanism and suggests practitioners should target Equalized Set Size or Equalized Singleton Frequency instead. Limitations include classification-only tasks, reduced statistical power for smaller groups (especially FACET's four age groups), and the mechanism being specific to human-in-the-loop settings. Open questions include whether the same unfairness mechanism applies to regression, time-series, or NLP conformal prediction, and whether formal guarantees can be established for Equalized Set Size analogous to those for coverage.
