## Problem

Conventional diffusion-based graph generators rely on Gaussian or categorical noise processes that poorly match the statistical characteristics of real graph data, which exhibits sparsity, bounded ranges, skewed distributions, and long-tailed behavior in both discrete adjacency structures and continuous node attributes. This misalignment limits the model's ability to accurately capture the joint distribution of graph topology and node features.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252960/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a random noise sample drawn from a beta distribution at the terminal diffusion time T, along with precomputed node degree (or betweenness) centrality values used to modulate the concentration parameter η per edge and node. It produces a graph G = (A, X) where A is a symmetric binary adjacency matrix (quantized at threshold 0.5) and X is a node feature matrix with values in [0,1]; for molecular graphs, A is expanded to K edge-type matrices (single, double, triple bonds) and X encodes atom types. All node features are preprocessed via dummy-encoding, empirical CDF transformation, or normalization to fall within the beta distribution's [0,1] support.

## Method

GBD defines a multiplicative beta diffusion process where the forward step thins each element via A_t = A_{t-1} ⊙ Q_{A,t} with Q drawn from Beta(η α_t A_0, η(α_{t-1}−α_t) A_0), and the reverse step thickens via G_{t-1} = G_t + P_t ⊙ (1−G_t) with P beta-distributed, yielding closed-form marginal distributions at every timestep. A Graph Transformer network predicts the clean graph G_0 from the noisy state G_t, and training minimizes a weighted combination of a sampling loss (KL between ancestral and posterior transitions) and a correction loss (KL between predicted and true marginals), both expressible as Bregman divergences over beta parameters. Four design choices enhance performance: linear data transformation to [0,1], concentration modulation that assigns higher η to structurally important positions (e.g., high-degree edges or carbon-carbon bonds) so they persist longer in forward diffusion and emerge earlier in reverse, logit-domain computation that amplifies weak structural signals during early reverse steps, and neural-network preconditioning that standardizes G_t using analytically derived mean and variance before feeding it to the predictor.

## Results

On simple generic graph benchmarks (Table 1), GBD achieves the best MMD on degree for all three datasets (Ego-small: 0.011, Community-small: 0.002, Grid: 0.045) and the best orbit MMD on Ego-small (0.002) and Community-small (0.002). On complex graph benchmarks (Table 2), GBD achieves V.U.N. of 92.5% on Planar and 75.0% on SBM, with the best spectral MMD (0.0047) on SBM. On molecule generation (Table 3), GBD achieves 99.88% validity and FCD of 0.093 on QM9, and 97.87% validity and FCD of 2.248 on ZINC250k, matching or exceeding the SOTA GruM model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GBD outperforms the previous SOTA Wave-GD on degree MMD across all three simple graph datasets (0.011 vs. 0.012 on Ego-small, 0.002 vs. 0.007 on Community-small, 0.045 vs. 0.144 on Grid) and on orbit MMD for Ego-small (0.002 vs. 0.005) and Community-small (0.002 vs. 0.002, tied). Against the discrete diffusion model DiGress, GBD achieves lower degree MMD on Ego-small (0.011 vs. 0.017) and Community-small (0.002 vs. 0.028), and lower orbit MMD on Ego-small (0.002 vs. 0.010). On complex graphs (Table 2), GBD matches GruM closely on Planar (V.U.N. 92.5% vs. 90.0%; Spec. 0.0059 vs. 0.0062) and SBM (Spec. 0.0047 vs. 0.0050; V.U.N. 75.0% vs. 85.0%), with GBD winning on spectral MMD for SBM. For molecule generation, GBD surpasses DiGress on FCD (0.093 vs. 0.095 on QM9; 2.248 vs. 3.482 on ZINC250k) and scaffold similarity (0.9510 vs. 0.9353 on QM9), while matching GruM on FCD for ZINC250k (2.248 vs. 2.257). GBD significantly outperforms the Gaussian-based GDSS+TF under the same Graph Transformer architecture, achieving FCD of 0.093 vs. 0.737 on QM9 and 2.248 vs. 5.556 on ZINC250k, demonstrating the advantage of beta diffusion over Gaussian diffusion for sparse graph data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the beta diffusion framework of Zhou et al. (2023) from general data generation to graph generation, replacing the Gaussian or categorical diffusion processes used by prior graph diffusion models (GDSS, DiGress, ConGress, GruM) with a multiplicative beta process that naturally accommodates the bounded, sparse, and skewed statistics of adjacency matrices and node features. It also adapts the neural-network preconditioning technique of Karras et al. (2022) to the graph setting and introduces a concentration modulation strategy that assigns position-dependent beta concentration parameters based on node centrality or domain knowledge, a design dimension not explored in prior graph diffusion work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled argument that the choice of diffusion distribution should match the statistical structure of the target data, and demonstrates that beta diffusion is a natural fit for graphs due to their bounded support, sparsity, and mixed discrete-continuous nature. The concentration modulation technique offers a flexible mechanism for injecting graph inductive biases (e.g., preserving community hubs or carbon-carbon bonds) into the diffusion schedule, which could be adapted to other structured data domains. However, the performance gains over strong baselines like GruM and DiGress are modest, the modulation strategy relies on hand-designed importance rankings, and scalability to very large graphs remains untested, leaving open questions about whether the beta process advantage persists at scale or can be learned end-to-end.
