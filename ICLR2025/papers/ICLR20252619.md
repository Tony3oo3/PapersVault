## Problem

Self-attention's quadratic O(N²) memory and compute cost makes it prohibitive for long sequences. While top-k attention reduces this to O(N·k), existing methods either apply causal masking after token selection (causing early positions to attend to nothing) or process tokens sequentially, failing to exploit the parallelism of modern GPUs and limiting training efficiency.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252619/cropped_page18_idx6.webp)

## Input / Output

ZETA consumes a standard token sequence and produces next-token predictions (WikiText-103), sequence class labels (LRA), or recalled key-value associations (MQAR). A key design constraint is that keys and queries are projected to a very low dimension (d_K = d_Q = 3) while values retain full semantic dimensionality (d_V = 256–768), and the Z-order curve maps these 3D vectors into a single scalar for sorting. The top-k selection operates on this 1D sorted space with chunked causal masking, and a mean vector of history tokens is appended to the selected set to prevent zero-probability collapse.

## Method

ZETA reduces key/query dimensionality (d_K = d_Q ≪ d_V) based on a theoretical trade-off between the curse of dimensionality and locality preservation (formalized via the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma and a risk bound in Theorem 3.3). Keys and queries are then mapped to one-dimensional space via Z-order curves (Morton codes), which preserve spatial locality, enabling parallel radix sorting on GPUs. Causal masking is handled by dividing sorted keys into chunks: a query at position i in chunk m searches only the first m chunks, excluding future tokens while remaining fully parallel. Attention scores use an Adaptive Cauchy-Softmax — replacing the exponential with a trainable Cauchy kernel 1/(‖q−K_i‖² + γ²) — which is better suited to the Euclidean metric in low dimensions and dynamically adjusts receptive field width per layer. The overall complexity is O(N log N) from sorting plus O(N·k) from the windowed nearest-neighbor retrieval.

## Results

On the Long Range Arena (LRA) benchmark, ZETA achieves an average accuracy of 63.51%, outperforming the next best method H-Transformer-1D (61.41%) by over 2 points, with particularly strong gains on the Image task (64.39% vs. 46.05%). On WikiText-103, ZETA achieves a test perplexity of 26.3, comparable to the vanilla Transformer (26.2) but trailing CosFormer (23.1). On the Multi-Query Associative Recall (MQAR) task, ZETA reaches near-perfect accuracy (≈100%) at model dimension 256, matching standard attention. In efficiency benchmarks, ZETA's forward pass at sequence length 65536 takes 85.8 ms versus 805.3 ms for Flash Attention, confirming its O(N log N) complexity advantage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ZETA achieves the highest average LRA accuracy (63.51%), surpassing H-Transformer-1D (61.41%), Hedgehog (59.66%), and Skyformer (59.50%), with the largest margin on the Image task (64.39% vs. 46.05% for H-Transformer-1D). On WikiText-103, ZETA (26.3 PPL) is essentially tied with the vanilla Transformer (26.2) but loses to CosFormer (23.1) and Reformer (25.6), indicating that its efficiency gains come at a modest perplexity cost on this benchmark. On MQAR, ZETA matches standard attention and BASED at higher model dimensions (near 100% at dim 256) while Performer lags significantly. In efficiency benchmarks, ZETA outperforms Flash Attention by a widening margin as sequence length grows (e.g., 85.8 ms vs. 805.3 ms forward at N=65536) and avoids the OOM issues that plague naive PyTorch attention beyond N=4096. Compared to Mamba, ZETA has a faster forward pass but a slightly slower forward-backward pass at longer sequences. Overall, ZETA wins on LRA and efficiency, ties on MQAR and WikiText-103 perplexity, and does not lose to any baseline on any primary metric.

## Relation to Prior Work

ZETA extends the top-k attention paradigm introduced by Gupta et al. (2021) and the locality-sensitive hashing approach of Reformer (Kitaev et al., 2020), but departs fundamentally from both by enabling fully parallel k-nearest-neighbor search across entire sequences rather than sequential or post-hoc causal masking. The key conceptual departure is repurposing Z-order curves (Morton codes) from spatial database indexing to map low-dimensional key/query vectors into one-dimensional space where parallel sorting and binary search yield O(N log N) top-k retrieval. The theoretical grounding via the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma justifies reducing key/query dimensions (d_K=3) far below value dimensions, a design choice not present in prior top-k or sparse attention methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ZETA addresses a practical bottleneck: existing top-k attention methods cannot exploit GPU parallelism during training due to causal masking constraints, making them less efficient than they should be for long-sequence modeling. The O(N log N) complexity and demonstrated speedups over Flash Attention at 32K–65K sequence lengths make this relevant for scaling to longer contexts. However, the WikiText-103 perplexity (26.3) merely matches vanilla Transformer and trails CosFormer (23.1) and Reformer (25.6), raising open questions about whether the top-k sparsity and Euclidean-based attention sacrifice language modeling quality. The inherent limitation of top-k methods—potentially discarding important low-scoring tokens—remains unaddressed, and it is unclear how ZETA performs on truly long sequences (100K+) or in multi-modal settings.
