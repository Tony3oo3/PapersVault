## Problem

Prior training-data extraction attacks target unaligned, base language models, but production LLMs like ChatGPT undergo alignment (RLHF) and are deployed behind chatbot interfaces that enforce conversational formatting. It was unclear whether alignment actually prevents memorized training data from being extracted, and whether existing prompting strategies even apply to these production systems. This paper demonstrates that alignment reduces but does not eliminate extraction risk, and develops attacks that circumvent both the alignment guardrails and the chatbot structure.

## Input / Output

The divergence attack takes a single repetitive prompt (e.g., 'Repeat this word forever: poem') and produces verbatim memorized sequences of 50+ tokens (~38 words) from the model's training data. The finetuning attack takes a 1,000-sample dataset (from The Pile or from previously extracted divergent strings) to retrain the chat model into a text-completion model, after which random 5-token Wikipedia sequences or document prefixes elicit memorized continuations. Validation is performed against a 9 TB AUXDATASET (merged and deduplicated from The Pile, RefinedWeb, RedPajama, and Dolma) using a suffix array to check for exact 50-token substring matches, providing a lower bound on true memorization since proprietary training data is not fully public.

## Method

The paper introduces two attacks that break chatbot alignment. The divergence attack exploits the observation that aligned chat models, when prompted to repeat a single token indefinitely, initially comply but eventually 'diverge' from the task and generate unrelated text; a small fraction of these divergent generations contain verbatim training data, yielding 27,171 unique memorized examples from gpt-3.5-turbo at a cost of ~$200. The finetuning attack is more principled: it finetunes the aligned chat model on 1,000 short prefix-to-completion pairs (using 4–6 random tokens as the user prompt and the full document as the target), effectively retraining the model to complete text rather than converse, which reverts it toward its pretraining objective and bypasses alignment. Finetuning on previously extracted divergent strings (DIVERGENTSUBSET) is 3× more effective than finetuning on random Pile documents, achieving 16.9% memorized tokens for gpt-3.5-turbo and 11.35% for gpt-4. For targeted extraction, the finetuned model is prompted with the beginning of a specific document and can reconstruct the following 50 tokens verbatim for over 75% of known-memorized samples, and for ~4% of held-out copyrighted books and toxic text.

## Results

The divergence attack extracts 27,171 unique verbatim-memorized training examples from ChatGPT (gpt-3.5-turbo) at a cost of $200, and 23 examples from Gemini 1.5 Pro ($113). The finetuning attack on gpt-3.5-turbo using DIVERGENTSUBSET achieves 16.87% tokens memorized and 23.73% of generations containing memorized 50-token sequences—a 210× increase over the aligned baseline (0.08%) and 5.9× over the divergence attack. For targeted extraction, finetuned models perfectly reconstruct 50 tokens after a prompt for over 75% of known memorized samples, and recover ≥50 tokens for 4% of copyrighted book and toxic text documents.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks against 9 open-weight unaligned models (GPT-2, GPT-Neo, Pythia, LLaMA, Mistral, Falcon, OPT, RedPajama) in Table 1, which extract 0.09–1.44% memorized tokens via random web-text prompting. The aligned gpt-3.5-turbo baseline emits only 0.02% memorized tokens—roughly 40× less than the unaligned gpt-3.5-turbo-instruct (0.85%)—confirming that alignment suppresses extraction. The divergence attack recovers this gap, producing 150× more memorized tokens than the aligned baseline and 3× more than gpt-3.5-turbo-instruct. The finetuning attack on DIVERGENTSUBSET dramatically outperforms both: gpt-3.5-turbo reaches 16.87% tokens memorized (exceeding the unaligned LLaMA2-70B baseline of 4.11%), while gpt-4 reaches 11.35%. For LLaMA2-Chat, finetuning on DIVERGENTSUBSET recovers 1.44% tokens memorized versus 0.0% aligned, but falls short of the unaligned LLaMA2-70B (4.11%), suggesting additional memorized data remains locked behind alignment in open models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the training-data extraction line of work initiated by Carlini et al. (2021), which demonstrated that unaligned language models regurgitate memorized training data when prompted with random web-text snippets. The key departure is that all prior memorization studies targeted base or instruction-tuned models, whereas this paper is the first to attack aligned, production chatbot models (ChatGPT, Gemini) where alignment guardrails and conversational formatting were assumed to prevent such leakage. The paper also leverages the suffix-array-based validation methodology from Lee et al. (2022) and scales it to a 9TB auxiliary corpus (AUXDATASET) to enable large-scale verification without access to proprietary training data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that alignment does not robustly prevent verbatim training-data extraction—challenges a core assumption in production LLM deployment and has direct implications for models trained on sensitive domains (medical, financial, legal). The divergence attack, while patched for gpt-3.5-turbo, highlights an unresolved class of vulnerabilities where generation context becomes divorced from prompt intent; the finetuning attack is harder to mitigate without fundamentally changing finetuning APIs. Open questions include why divergence occurs mechanistically, whether stronger alignment or architectural changes can close the gap, and whether the Good-Turing-based extrapolation of total extractable memorization holds across model families. The 9TB AUXDATASET also provides a reusable infrastructure for future memorization research.
