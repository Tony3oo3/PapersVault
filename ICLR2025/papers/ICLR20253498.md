## Problem

Existing dynamic view synthesis methods rely on large 'teleporting' camera motions to construct multiview consistency, but casually captured monocular videos feature static or slowly-moving cameras that provide insufficient geometric constraints. This causes prior DVS methods to fail at reconstructing 3D dynamic geometry and producing high-quality novel views from such videos.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253498/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single casually captured monocular video (up to ~10 seconds) with known camera poses, per-frame depth maps estimated by GeoWizard, and 2D optical flow from RAFT computed between evenly sampled keyframes. It outputs novel-view RGB images and rendered depth maps from arbitrary camera viewpoints at any timestamp. Depth maps are first rectified to a unified scale via least-squares fitting on static regions before being used for initialization and supervision.

## Method

MoDGS represents the dynamic scene as 3D Gaussians in a canonical space deformed by an invertible MLP (following the Omnimotion/CaDeX design), rendered via standard 3D Gaussian splatting. Two key contributions address the weak multiview constraint: (1) a 3D-aware initialization that lifts per-frame depth maps and 2D flow into a 3D flow, trains the deformation field to match this flow, then deforms all depth points backward to the canonical space to seed the Gaussians; and (2) an ordinal depth loss that supervises rendered depth by enforcing pairwise depth ordering (via tanh of depth differences matched to the sign in the estimated depth map) rather than absolute or scale-normalized values, which handles the cross-frame scale inconsistency of monocular depth estimators. Training jointly optimizes a rendering loss and the ordinal depth loss (weighted 0.1) for 20k steps after a 20k-step initialization phase.

## Results

On the DyNeRF dataset, MoDGS achieves 22.64 PSNR, 0.8042 SSIM, and 0.1545 LPIPS, outperforming the best baseline D-GS (19.55 PSNR) by over 3 dB. On the Nvidia dataset, MoDGS reaches 19.27 PSNR, 0.5235 SSIM, and 0.2581 LPIPS, again leading the strongest baseline D-GS (18.07 PSNR) by a clear margin. The method also outperforms Shape-of-Motion on the Nvidia dataset (e.g., 25.64 vs. 23.51 PSNR on Skatting2) and produces qualitatively superior results on the in-the-wild MCV dataset where no quantitative evaluation is possible.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoDGS outperforms all five baselines across every metric on both the DyNeRF and Nvidia datasets. Against HexPlane (Cao & Johnson, 2023), MoDGS gains approximately 7.3 dB PSNR on DyNeRF and 2.1 dB on Nvidia, reflecting HexPlane's inability to handle near-static monocular input without additional multi-view data. Against SC-GS (Huang et al., 2024), MoDGS leads by 3.87 dB PSNR on DyNeRF and 1.68 dB on Nvidia; SC-GS's static-scene initialization step fails to capture large foreground motions. Against Deformable-GS (Yang et al., 2023), the closest baseline, MoDGS still achieves a 3.09 dB PSNR advantage on DyNeRF and 1.20 dB on Nvidia, attributed to the 3D-aware initialization and ordinal depth loss. Against RoDynRF (Liu et al., 2023a), MoDGS produces visibly cleaner novel views on the MCV dataset, particularly in occluded and background regions. Against Shape-of-Motion (Wang et al., 2024b), MoDGS wins on all four Nvidia scenes tested (e.g., 25.64 vs. 23.51 PSNR on Skatting2, 23.69 vs. 22.53 on Truck-2), though the gap is smaller than against the other baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

MoDGS builds directly on the deformable 3D Gaussian Splatting paradigm (Deformable-GS, SC-GS) and the invertible MLP deformation field design from Omnimotion/CaDeX, but departs from the core assumption of prior DVS methods that large 'teleporting' camera motions provide sufficient multiview consistency. It repurposes single-view depth estimation (GeoWizard) and 2D optical flow (RAFT) as geometric priors to compensate for the weak multiview signal in casual videos, and introduces a 3D-aware initialization that lifts 2D flow into 3D to seed the deformation field. The ordinal depth loss draws on the ordinal/ranking literature in monocular depth estimation (Zoran et al., Chen et al., Pavlakos et al.) but applies it in a fundamentally different setting: supervising rendered metric depth from a 3D representation rather than training a depth network from scratch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work addresses a practical gap: most DVS methods fail on the most common type of video (casually captured, static or slowly moving camera), limiting real-world applicability. The ordinal depth loss is a transferable technique that could benefit any method using noisy, scale-inconsistent depth priors for 3D supervision. Key open questions remain: the method cannot hallucinate unseen geometry (requiring generative priors like diffusion models), training still takes ~3.5 hours per scene, and performance degrades under rapid motion or heavy occlusion. The reliance on external depth estimators also means quality is bounded by their accuracy, an active area of improvement.
