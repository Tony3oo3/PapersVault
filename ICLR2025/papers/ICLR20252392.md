## Problem

The Lottery Ticket Hypothesis asks whether a sparse subnetwork can generalize comparably to its dense counterpart when trained from initialization, but existing iterative pruning methods fail to transfer generalization to arbitrary random initializations, either losing basin-of-attraction membership after rewinding or bypassing the challenge by applying masks to already-trained weights. The paper identifies that parameter sign configuration is the critical information for generalization transfer, yet normalization layer parameters introduce high error barriers that prevent a signed mask alone from working on a freshly initialized network.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a randomly initialized network (any seed, any architecture) and standard image classification training data (CIFAR-100 at 32×32, Tiny-ImageNet at 64×64, ImageNet at 224×224) to produce a signed mask — a sparse binary mask augmented with the sign of each retained weight. This signed mask is architecture-agnostic and can be applied to any independently drawn random initialization, yielding a sparse trainable network that requires no access to the original trained weights or normalization parameters.

## Method

AWS (A Winning Sign) is a minimal modification of Learning Rate Rewinding (LRR) that, at every forward pass during each pruning iteration, randomly linearly interpolates the current normalization layer parameters with their initialized values using a coefficient α ~ U(0,1), and uses the interpolated values for the forward computation while still updating the original parameters via gradient descent. This interpolation prevents high error barriers along the linear path between the trained AWS subnetwork and its counterpart with initialized normalization parameters, ensuring the subnetwork remains in its basin of attraction even when all non-normalization parameters are re-randomized while preserving their signs. After T iterations of train-prune-rewind-learning-rate, AWS outputs a signed mask s_T that encodes both sparsity and sign information. Applying this signed mask to any randomly initialized network (abs(θ_init) ⊙ s_T) and training normally yields a sparse network that is linearly mode-connected to the AWS solution, achieving performance comparable to the dense network at non-trivial sparsity levels across ResNet-50, MobileNetV2, MLP-Mixer, EfficientNet, and ShuffleNet.

## Results

On ImageNet, a randomly initialized network masked with the AWS-driven signed mask achieves top-1 accuracy comparable to the AWS solution across architectures: 73.65% vs. 73.63% (ResNet-50, 14% params), 66.02% vs. 66.17% (MobileNetV2, 21% params), and 58.62% vs. 58.77% (MLP-Mixer, 11% params). In contrast, the LRR-driven signed mask on a random initialization falls 2.6–3.7 points below the LRR solution (e.g., 71.94% vs. 74.58% for ResNet-50 at 14% params). On CIFAR-100 and Tiny-ImageNet (Figure 3), the AWS signed mask on random init matches the AWS solution and approaches dense-network accuracy at ~9% remaining parameters, while the LRR signed mask on random init degrades to near-unpruned-baseline performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares AWS against two baselines: (1) LRR (Renda et al., ICLR 2020) and (2) GM (Sreenivasan et al., NeurIPS 2022). Against LRR, AWS wins decisively on the key LTH criterion: transferring a signed mask to a *randomly initialized* network. On ImageNet ResNet-50 at 14% params, the AWS signed mask on random init reaches 73.65% (matching the AWS solution at 73.63%), while the LRR signed mask on random init only reaches 71.94%—a 1.7-point gap that widens to ~2.6 points at higher sparsity. The same pattern holds for MobileNetV2 (66.02% vs. 64.03%) and MLP-Mixer (58.62% vs. 56.91%). Against GM on CIFAR-100 with ResNet-32 (Figure 4), AWS on random init matches or exceeds dense-network accuracy up to ~90% sparsity, whereas GM falls short of the dense baseline at all sparsity levels. The paper also notes that in some simpler architectures (e.g., VGG11-bn), LRR's signed mask can also transfer successfully, but AWS generalizes more reliably across complex architectures with batch or layer normalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Learning Rate Rewinding (LRR, Renda et al. 2020) by adding a single modification—random linear interpolation of normalization layer parameters during training—to produce AWS. It builds on the loss-landscape analysis of Frankle et al. (2020a), Evci et al. (2022), and Paul et al. (2023), which established that a winning ticket requires the pruned network to remain in the basin of attraction of the training solution. The paper also builds on Gadhikar & Burkholz (2024), who showed LRR preserves effective sign configurations, and on Zhou et al. (2019) and Wang et al. (2023a,b), who highlighted the role of parameter signs. The key departure is identifying normalization layer parameters as the specific obstacle preventing sign-based transfer and showing that a simple interpolation trick eliminates this dependency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work makes concrete progress toward the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis by demonstrating that a signed mask (sparsity + sign information) from AWS can transfer generalization potential to any randomly initialized network, removing the need for a specific ad-hoc initialization or trained normalization parameters. The diagnostic finding that normalization layer parameters—not weight parameters—are the critical obstacle to sign-based transfer is a useful insight for the broader pruning community. However, the method still requires running the full AWS iterative procedure to obtain the mask, and the mask is architecture- and dataset-specific, so it does not yet constitute a universal winning ticket. The authors note transfer learning as a natural next step, and it remains open whether the sign-based transfer generalizes to non-convex or non-vision tasks.
