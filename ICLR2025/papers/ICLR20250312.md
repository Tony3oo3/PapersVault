## Problem

Existing LLM defenses (paraphrasing, perplexity filters, RLHF, gradient-based classifiers) are too generic to counter socially engineered persuasion attacks, which exploit discourse-structure patterns like circular reasoning, authority framing, and progressive argument layering to achieve far higher attack success rates than algorithmic jailbreaks. No targeted defense has been designed specifically for this broad category of human-like manipulative prompts, and current methods lack the contextual comprehension and generalization needed to detect novel persuasive structures.

## Input / Output

The method takes a variable-length LLM text prompt (a sequence of token embeddings of arbitrary size n) and produces a binary harmful/benign classification. Because prompts have varying token counts, the resulting hypergraphs have different dimensionalities, which rules out fixed-size neural architectures; the method instead operates in a metric space of compact metric hypergraphs where the RBF kernel handles the variable dimensionality via the kernel trick. The classifier is a kernel SVM trained with stochastic mini-batch subgradient descent, running entirely on CPU with no GPU dependency.

## Method

Each prompt is transformed into an undirected hypergraph where forward hyperedges (sliding windows of size w over the token sequence) capture sequential argument flow and back hyperedges (balls of radius r in token-embedding space, computed via a cover tree) capture higher-order semantic similarity. Within each hypergraph, s-walk distances (shortest paths where consecutive edges share at least s vertices) define a compact metric, turning each prompt into a metric hypergraph. A modified Gromov-Hausdorff distance (Mémoli 2012, polynomial-time estimated per Oles et al. 2024) then defines a generalized metric between these metric hypergraphs, creating a space of prompt geometries. A kernel SVM with an RBF kernel over this modified GH distance is trained via a mini-batch Pegasos variant, and the paper derives radius-margin generalization error bounds parameterized by the 2-approximate 1-center radius of the training set, the kernel bandwidth, and the SVM margin.

## Results

On the JPP persuasion attack dataset, the proposed hypergraph metric space defense achieves consistently low attack success rates (ASR) of approximately 9% across all four LLM models (Llama-3.1: 9.0%, GPT-4: 9.0%, Mistral-7B: 8.7%, Vicuna-13B: 8.9%), compared to 90–91% ASR with no defense. On algorithmic attacks (GCG, PAIR, Deep Inception, AutoDAN) over JPP, the method achieves 5.0–10.0% ASR, matching or exceeding specialized defenses like ICD (3–9%) and Self-Reminder (0–7%). Cross-dataset evaluation on WildGuardTest and Jailbreak-28k persuasion prompts yields ASRs of 6.3–8.2%, with classification accuracies of 91–93.7% across models. Training completes in 7–11 minutes on CPU alone (Intel Xeon Platinum 8562Y, 128 GB RAM), with ~1.4s inference time per prompt.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method decisively outperforms all ten baselines on persuasion attacks across every model. Against the strongest existing defenses, ICD (16–19% ASR) and Self-Reminder (14.8–19.1%), the hypergraph method reduces ASR by roughly 7–10 percentage points to ~9%. Prompt-mutation baselines (Paraphrase 32–56%, Retokenization 26–56%, SmoothLLM 27.5–85%) and gradient-based GradSafe (20.5–26.9%) are all substantially worse, with SmoothLLM performing near no-defense on Mistral-7B (85%). The naive graph-based baselines are particularly weak: GNN (87–88.4%) and Hyper-GNN (79–85%) barely improve over no defense, while ho-GNN (47–53%) and AvgToken+SVM (39–54%) show moderate but still far inferior performance. On algorithmic attacks (Table 2), the method achieves 5.0–10.0% ASR, which is competitive with ICD (3–9%) and Self-Reminder (0–7%)—defenses specifically designed for algorithmic attacks—while still dominating all other baselines (GNN 21.6–32%, SmoothLLM 14–29%). The paper also reports cross-category generalization (leave-one-out on 8 persuasion subcategories) with accuracies ranging from 77.6% (Non-expert Testimonial) to 95.2% (Framing), indicating the geometric representation transfers reasonably well to unseen attack styles.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the modified Gromov-Hausdorff distance framework of Mémoli (2012) and the polynomial-time estimation algorithm of Oles et al. (2024), repurposing them from shape matching to LLM prompt classification. It departs from existing LLM defenses (paraphrasing, retokenization, SmoothLLM, GradSafe, RLHF) that the authors argue are too generic and keyword/pattern-dependent, and from neural approaches (GNN, Hyper-GNN, ho-GNN) that operate on the same hypergraph structure but lack a principled inter-prompt metric. The classifier itself is a kernel SVM trained via a mini-batch variant of Pegasos (Shalev-Shwartz et al., 2007), chosen specifically because the non-differentiable, variable-dimensional GH metric precludes standard deep learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first defense explicitly targeting socially engineered (persuasion) attacks as a category rather than individual algorithmic attack vectors, and it provides the first theoretical generalization-error bounds for a geometric LLM prompt filter. The CPU-only operation (no GPU dependency) and sub-12-minute training times make it attractive for resource-constrained deployments. Key limitations include vulnerability to extremely terse prompts (4–5 tokens) and novel harmful combinations of individually benign tokens, and the main persuasion results rely on a small in-house dataset (700 prompts), leaving open whether the geometric signal generalizes to larger, more diverse attack distributions.
