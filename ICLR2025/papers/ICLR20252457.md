## Problem

Measuring per-sample informativeness in deep learning requires a probability distribution over model parameters to compute the KL divergence, yet standard MLE-trained networks yield only a single parameter vector. Prior approaches either linearize the network (degrading performance and scaling poorly), rely on expensive Bayesian training, or measure proxy quantities like sample difficulty rather than true information flow. The paper seeks a post-hoc, information-theoretically grounded measure that works across architectures and learning settings without modifying the training procedure.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a trained non-Bayesian model, its training dataset, and the loss-function Hessian (or a diagonal/KFAC approximation thereof), and outputs a scalar LSI score per sample equal to the KL divergence between two Laplace-approximated Gaussian posteriors. In practice, the prohibitively expensive full-model leave-one-out retraining is replaced by retraining only a small probe head on pre-extracted frozen features (ResNet-18 for images, BERT for text), yielding a speedup of at least three orders of magnitude while preserving the sample ordering. Scores can be aggregated per class or across the full dataset to produce informativeness rankings and difficulty assessments.

## Method

LSI constructs a quasi-Bayesian posterior from a standard MLE-trained model via the classical Laplace approximation, fitting a Gaussian N(θ̂, Σ) where Σ is the inverse Hessian of the regularized loss at the MLE, thereby interpreting the regularizer as a log-prior and the MLE as a MAP estimate. The informativeness of sample z_i is the closed-form KL divergence between this Gaussian and the analogous Gaussian from a model retrained on D\{z_i}, expressed in terms of the trace, determinant ratio, and Mahalanobis distance of the two distributions. To make computation tractable, the full O(K²) Hessian is replaced by a diagonal approximation (O(K) memory and time), and the full-model LOO retraining is replaced by retraining only a lightweight probe head on frozen features. The paper demonstrates that this probe-based LSI ordering transfers consistently to larger architectures (ResNet-9, ResNet-18) and emerges early in training without requiring full convergence. The formulation is agnostic to architecture, loss function, and learning setting, applying uniformly to supervised image classification, text sentiment analysis, and unsupervised contrastive learning with CLIP.

## Results

LSI produces a long-tailed distribution across samples, with most samples carrying low informativeness and a small subset carrying high informativeness (often mislabeled or atypical). The probe-based LSI computation achieves Spearman's R > 0.9 correlation with full-model LSI (0.93 for probe vs. full CNN, 0.98 for last-layer vs. full CNN), enabling a speedup of at least three orders of magnitude. For sample learnability, models trained on low-LSI subsets achieve substantially higher training accuracy, while intermediate-LSI subsets yield the best test accuracy and smallest train/test gap on CIFAR-10. Mislabeled samples (10% label flips) exhibit substantially higher LSI than correctly labeled samples, and ImageNet subsets show approximately one order of magnitude larger LSI than CIFAR-10 under identical experimental setup.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Smooth Unique Information (SUI, Harutyunyan et al. 2021), LSI shows only weak correlation, which the authors attribute to SUI's reliance on global linearization of the entire network and its poor scalability (limited to ~1000-sample binary classification). Against Point-wise Sliced Mutual Information (PSMI, Wongso et al. 2023), LSI is strongly correlated, confirming that both capture related aspects of sample informativeness, though PSMI's slicing approximation lacks the information-processing inequality guarantee of true mutual information. Against TRAK (Park et al. 2023), LSI shows only a weak Spearman's R of 0.15 on Imagenette, and qualitative analysis reveals that TRAK fails to separate mislabeled/out-of-distribution samples (e.g., a car image in the radio class receives an average TRAK score) while LSI correctly assigns them high informativeness. Overall, LSI outperforms SUI in scalability and robustness, aligns closely with PSMI in what it measures, and provides a fundamentally different (and more sample-specific) signal than TRAK's cross-influence attribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the KL-divergence-based formulation of sample informativeness (Harutyunyan et al., 2021; Wongso et al., 2023) and the information-theoretic stability literature (Bassily et al., 2016; Steinke & Zakynthinou, 2020), but departs from prior work by replacing linearization (SUI) or expensive Bayesian training (Langevin dynamics, Gaussian processes) with a post-hoc Laplace approximation to the posterior parameter distribution. The key conceptual move is treating a standard MLE-trained network as a quasi-Bayesian learner via the Laplace approximation, which makes the KL divergence computable without altering the training procedure. The probe-model strategy for efficient LOO computation, combined with diagonal Hessian approximation, is a practical departure that makes the otherwise intractable per-sample retraining feasible at scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LSI provides a principled, model-agnostic measure of per-sample informativeness that is directly interpretable through information theory, filling a gap left by influence functions (which suffer from the butterfly effect) and difficulty-based proxies (which measure a related but distinct quantity). The probe-based computation achieving 3+ orders of magnitude speedup while preserving sample ordering across architectures makes LOO-based data valuation practically accessible for dataset curation, mislabeling detection, and dataset difficulty assessment. Open questions include whether the diagonal Hessian approximation remains sufficient at LLM scale, whether the ordering transfers to non-convex loss landscapes with very sharp minima, and how LSI behaves under distributed or federated training settings where the full parameter Hessian is unavailable.
