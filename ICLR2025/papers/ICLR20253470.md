## Problem

Existing model extraction defenses protect only the API's predictive functionality while leaving the network architecture—an expensive intellectual property—vulnerable to theft. Additionally, current defenses allocate identical computational resources to both malicious and benign queries, wasting significant computation on attack traffic that does not require full-depth inference.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253470/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method accepts standard RGB image queries (32×32 for CIFAR, 224×224 for ImageNet/CUB) and produces a class probability vector from whichever intermediate exit the query terminates at, along with the implicit exit layer index. The exit point is dynamic: a confidence-threshold array {r₀, r₁, …, r_N} is checked sequentially at each exit, and inference halts at the first exit where max-class probability exceeds the corresponding threshold. Attack queries (OOD) are steered to exit at early layers with low-semantic features, while benign queries (ID) progress to later layers where predictions are more meaningful.

## Method

DNF converts a frozen pre-trained victim model into a dynamic Early-Exit Neural Network (EENN) by attaching lightweight linear exit classifiers (0.47% additional parameters) at intermediate layers, then trains only these classifiers with a directionally asymmetric deep variational information bottleneck (VIB) objective. For ID data, the loss maximizes I(Z;Y) and minimizes I(X;Z) to preserve semantic feature-label correlation, combined with entropy regularization that suppresses early-exit confidence and encourages later exits. For OOD data, the loss is inverted—minimizing I(Z;Y) and maximizing I(X;Z)—to deliberately degrade prediction quality and promote non-deterministic early exits, while entropy regularization flattens later-exit distributions to prevent OOD queries from reaching deep layers. At deployment, the reparameterization trick (Gaussian noise ε ~ N(0,I) injected at each exit) introduces stochasticity that further obscures the architecture from attackers attempting to reverse-engineer exit placements via singular value analysis.

## Results

On CIFAR-100 under soft-label DFME attack, DNF reduces clone model accuracy to 6.75% (DenseNet121) and 18.03% (ResNet18-8X), compared to 10.79% and 29.57% for the SOTA MeCo baseline. On CUB200 (soft-label), DNF achieves 5.27% clone accuracy with ResNet34, versus 31.98% for MeCo — a 26.7-point absolute reduction. DNF also achieves a 2× inference speedup over the undefended model (5.41s vs 10.12s on CIFAR-10 for OOD queries) while maintaining victim model utility (94.34% on CIFAR-10, 78.73% on CIFAR-100).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DNF consistently outperforms all three baseline families across most settings. Against P-poison (Orekondy et al., 2020) and RandP, DNF achieves substantially lower clone accuracy — e.g., 47.21% vs 68.75% on CIFAR-10 DenseNet121 (soft-label) and 6.75% vs 21.89% on CIFAR-100 DenseNet121. Against GRAD (Mazeika et al., 2022), the gap is similar: DNF reaches 18.03% vs 39.07% on CIFAR-100 ResNet18 (soft-label). The closest competitor is MeCo (Wang et al., 2023), the SOTA defense: DNF wins decisively on CIFAR-100 (6.75% vs 10.79% DenseNet121; 18.03% vs 29.57% ResNet18) and CUB200 (5.27% vs 31.98% ResNet34), but slightly trails MeCo on CIFAR-10 ResNet18 soft-label (53.91% vs 51.68%). Under hard-label attacks, DNF and MeCo are closer (76.51% vs 76.86% on CIFAR-10 ResNet18), yet DNF still leads on CIFAR-100 (52.98% vs 59.30%) and CUB200 (16.01% vs 29.42%). Overall, DNF's advantage is most pronounced on harder datasets (CIFAR-100, CUB200, ImageNet-200) and with more expressive clone architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Early-Exit Neural Networks (originally designed for inference efficiency, Teerapittayanon et al. 2016) into the model extraction defense setting, which is a first. It combines this architectural mechanism with the Deep Variational Information Bottleneck framework (Alemi et al. 2017), applying asymmetric IB objectives—maximizing feature-label mutual information for in-distribution data while inverting it for out-of-distribution data—to simultaneously control where benign and attack queries exit. This departs from prior defenses (RandP, P-poison, GRAD, MeCo) that uniformly perturb outputs or retrain the model, by instead differentiating query handling at the architectural level with only 0.47% additional parameters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical deployment concern: current defenses waste equal compute on malicious and benign queries, and offer no protection against architecture theft—a growing threat as proprietary architectures gain commercial value (Carlini et al., 2024). The 2× inference speedup and plug-and-play design (standard linear classifiers, no specialized exit heads) make this approach attractive for real-world API providers. However, the defense does not fully protect the entire network architecture—attackers still observe partial execution—and the OOD assumption for attack queries, while standard in the literature, limits applicability when attackers have access to in-distribution data (though JBDA experiments partially address this). Open questions include scalability to large language models and robustness against adaptive attackers who learn exit patterns over many queries.
