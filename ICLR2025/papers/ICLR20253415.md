## Problem

Domain shift in computer vision is typically addressed by modifying the model (domain generalization, adaptation, or test-time adaptation), which requires labeled data, significant compute, or risks model collapse. The paper identifies an underexplored alternative: optimizing the image acquisition process itself by controlling camera sensor parameters (ISO, shutter speed, aperture) specifically for the target model's perception rather than for human visual quality, a regime the authors term test-time input adaptation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253415/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on a physical scene (not a static image) captured by a camera with N adjustable sensor parameter options (e.g., 27 manual combinations of ISO, shutter speed, and aperture). For each scene, Lens captures multiple candidate images under different sensor settings, evaluates them via the target model's confidence scores, and selects the single best image for classification. The output is a standard class label, but the key distinction is that the input image is dynamically chosen at test time based on model-specific quality assessment rather than fixed or human-optimized exposure.

## Method

Lens is a post-hoc, camera-agnostic sensor control system that selects optimal camera parameters per model and per scene at test time without modifying the model. Its core component, VisiT (Vision Test for neural networks), is a training-free quality indicator that uses the target model's softmax confidence score on each unlabeled candidate image as a proxy for image quality, outperforming OOD scores (ViM, ASH, ReAct, KNN) for covariate-shift scenarios. To enable real-time operation, Candidate Selection Algorithms (CSAs) reduce the number of images to capture from all N options to K candidates (e.g., 6 or 18), balancing accuracy against capture latency (as low as 0.16 seconds). The system is explicitly model-specific and scene-specific: the optimal sensor parameter differs across architectures and across scenes, as validated by solution-space analysis showing that the same parameter can be optimal for one model but suboptimal for another on the identical sample.

## Results

On ImageNet-ES, Lens achieves an average accuracy of 65.6% across 12 models, outperforming Auto-Exposure (AE) and Random sensor controls by margins of 8.71% to 47.58% depending on the model and dataset. On the new ImageNet-ES Diverse benchmark, Lens similarly surpasses naive controls while maintaining a capture latency of only 0.16 seconds (CSA3, k=18). In real-time adaptation comparisons (Table 2), Lens with full options (k=27) outperforms all TTA baselines (BN1, BN2, TENT) by 14.6% to 45.9% on both benchmarks. Notably, Lens on EfficientNet-B3 (12M params) surpasses the DG-enhanced OpenCLIP-h (632M params) by 7.34%, compensating for a 50× model size gap purely through sensor control.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Auto-Exposure (Kuno et al., 1998), the standard human-perception-optimized sensor control, Lens consistently achieves higher accuracy across all 12 evaluated models on both ImageNet-ES and ImageNet-ES Diverse, with the paper reporting gains of 8.71% to 47.58% depending on model and scene. Against the three TTA baselines—BN1 (Nado et al., 2020), BN2 (Schneider et al., 2020), and TENT (Wang et al., 2021)—Lens with full options (k=27) outperforms all of them by 14.6% to 45.9% on both benchmarks (e.g., on ResNet-18/ImageNet-ES: Lens 73.8% vs. BN1 30.7%, BN2 34.2%, TENT 32.0%). Even with the lightweight CSA3 (k=18, 0.16 s latency), Lens maintains a substantial lead over TTA methods (73.7% vs. 30.7–32.0% on ImageNet-ES). The paper further shows that Lens on a small EfficientNet-B3 (12M) surpasses the DG-enhanced OpenCLIP-h (632M) by 7.34%, demonstrating that sensor control can compensate for a 50× model size difference. Lens also integrates synergistically with DG techniques (DeepAugment + AugMix), where combined use yields further accuracy gains over either approach alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of addressing domain shift through model modification (domain generalization, domain adaptation, test-time adaptation) by instead intervening at the data acquisition stage—controlling the camera sensor rather than the network. It repurposes the well-studied confidence score (from pseudo-labeling and semi-supervised learning) as a model-specific image quality indicator for sensor parameter selection, a novel application of an existing concept. The work extends the ImageNet-ES benchmark (Baek et al., 2024), which first captured real-world sensor perturbations, by introducing ImageNet-ES Diverse with broader lighting conditions and non-luminous objects, and positions itself as the first systematic study of model- and scene-specific sensor control, in contrast to prior human-centric auto-exposure systems and constrained sensor-tuning efforts (Paul et al., 2023; Onzon et al., 2021).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes 'test-time input adaptation' as a viable complementary paradigm to model-centric domain shift handling, with the striking finding that sensor control can compensate for a 50× model size gap and that images optimal for neural networks can be visually poor for humans. The new ImageNet-ES Diverse benchmark (192K images, 6 lighting conditions, 27 sensor settings) fills a notable gap in realistic perturbation evaluation. Key open questions include: whether confidence-based quality estimation generalizes to poorly calibrated or foundation models without overconfidence, how the approach performs in truly dynamic multi-object scenes (vs. the controlled single-banner setup), and whether the paradigm extends cleanly to dense prediction tasks like segmentation and detection beyond the qualitative demonstrations provided.
