## Problem

Existing medical vision-language pre-training methods align image-level global representations with text embeddings, creating a fundamental mismatch when transferring to pixel-level dense prediction tasks like 3D lesion segmentation. Additionally, lesions exhibit significant shape, size, and boundary variability, and simply using raw radiology reports or generic disease definitions is insufficient to learn generalized representations that can segment novel, unseen lesion types. The paper addresses both gaps by introducing fine-grained, lesion-level mask-attribute alignment that explicitly links visual features of unseen lesions to extensible textual knowledge of fundamental pathological attributes.

## Input / Output

The method takes 3D CT scan volumes (cropped into 96×96×96 patches) as visual input, paired during training with structured text descriptions decomposed into 8 fundamental disease visual attributes (location, shape, density, density variations, surface characteristics, enhancement status, relationship with surrounding organs, specific features) extracted from radiology reports via a GPT-4 + radiologist review pipeline. At inference, no text encoder is required—pre-stored attribute embeddings are matched against mask tokens, and a Clinical Knowledge Table maps predicted attributes to specific disease categories. The output is a 3D per-voxel segmentation mask, a disease category label, and the matched lesion attribute descriptions.

## Method

Malenia builds on Mask2Former with an nnUNet 3D backbone and introduces two core mechanisms. First, a multi-scale mask-attribute alignment strategy feeds N=16 learnable mask tokens through three Transformer decoder blocks attending to image features at H/32, H/16, and H/8 resolutions, then aligns the resulting mask embeddings with text embeddings of the 8 structured disease attributes using a Multi-Positive NCE (MP-NCE) contrastive loss, where each foreground mask token forms 8 positive pairs (one per attribute) rather than a single mask-report pair. Second, a Cross-Modal Knowledge Injection (CMKI) module performs bidirectional cross-attention between the final mask tokens and their matched attribute embeddings, producing enhanced representations from which two separate mask predictions (vision-branch and language-branch) are generated via scaled dot-product attention against pixel-level features and ensembled. Training combines deep-supervision Dice loss across decoder scales, the multi-scale similarity loss, and a binary cross-entropy + Dice segmentation loss with equal weighting.

## Results

On zero-shot unseen lesion segmentation, Malenia achieves DSC of 59.52% (Hepatic Vessel Tumor) and 60.91% (Pancreas Cyst) on MSD, 54.96% (Kidney Tumor) on KiTS23, and 61.85% (Liver Cyst), 43.05% (Kidney Stone), 47.35% (Gallbladder Tumor) on the in-house dataset, outperforming the best baseline ZePT by at least 6.40% DSC on MSD, 8.14% on KiTS23, and 9.08% on the in-house dataset. In the fully-supervised seen-lesion setting, Malenia achieves an average DSC improvement of 1.46% over the best supervised baseline (Universal Model) on MSD tumors and 1.35% on KiTS23 kidney cysts. Malenia also demonstrates superior computational efficiency with 323.86M parameters and 1128.96G FLOPs, compared to 1337.59G for ZePT and 3643.96G for H-SAM.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Malenia outperforms all five zero-shot baselines across all six unseen lesion categories. SAM and SAM2 (DSC ~35–38%) perform worst, as they require accurate manual prompts and lack fine-tuning on target data. SaLIP (DSC ~37–42%) improves via CLIP-guided prompt refinement but remains limited by CLIP's image-level alignment that neglects fine-grained lesion semantics. H-SAM (DSC ~44–47%) adds hierarchical mask decoding but lacks cross-modal textual knowledge. ZePT (DSC ~47–53%) is the strongest competitor, using self-prompting and alignment with general medical knowledge, but overlooks patient-specific multi-aspect attributes shared across diseases. Malenia's multi-scale mask-attribute alignment and CMKI module yield gains of 6.40% (MSD), 8.14% (KiTS23), and 9.08% (in-house) DSC over ZePT. In the supervised seen-lesion setting, Malenia also surpasses TransUNet, nnUNet, Swin UNETR, and Universal Model by 1.35–1.46% average DSC, confirming that the cross-modal strategies benefit both zero-shot and fully-supervised segmentation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Mask2Former's mask-token architecture and extends vision-language contrastive pre-training (CLIP-style) from image-level classification to pixel-level 3D lesion segmentation. It departs from prior zero-shot medical segmentation methods (SAM/SAM2-based approaches, ZePT) in two key ways: (1) replacing single-scale, single-positive mask-text alignment with multi-scale, multi-positive mask-attribute alignment using structured disease visual attributes, and (2) introducing a Cross-Modal Knowledge Injection module that fuses mask and text embeddings bidirectionally before prediction. The structured attribute decomposition (8 visual aspects) draws on radiology's structured reporting tradition and the multi-aspect pre-training idea from MAVL (Phan et al., 2024), but applies it at the lesion-mask level rather than the image level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Zero-shot lesion segmentation for unseen disease categories is a clinically critical but underexplored problem, and this paper provides a clean, well-validated framework that eliminates the need for manual prompts or a text encoder at inference. The structured attribute approach offers a practical path toward extensibility: new diseases can be described using the same 8 elemental visual attributes, enabling the model to generalize without retraining. Key open questions include cross-modality generalization (CT → MRI/X-ray), scalability beyond 12 lesion categories, and handling inherently ambiguous lesion boundaries (e.g., pancreatic cancer) where even expert annotation is subjective. The reliance on expert-validated attribute annotations, while necessary, limits immediate reproducibility and scalability to new clinical sites.
