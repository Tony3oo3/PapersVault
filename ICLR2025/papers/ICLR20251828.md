## Problem

Vanilla Mixture-of-Experts methods activate a fixed number of FFN experts for every token regardless of its complexity, wasting computation on simple tokens (punctuation, word fragments) while potentially under-serving challenging ones. This rigid Top-K mixing mechanism leads to both training/inference inefficiency and sub-optimal model performance, and the uniform expert distribution across GPUs introduces significant communication overhead and load imbalance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251828/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes text tokenized with the LLaMA2 vocabulary (65,536 tokens) and produces standard next-token predictions. The key structural difference from vanilla MoE is that each token is routed to a variable number of FFN experts (0 to 2) combined with zero-computation experts, so the effective computation per token is heterogeneous rather than fixed. The output of the MoE++ layer is a weighted sum where zero experts contribute 0, copy experts contribute the input unchanged, and constant experts contribute a learned linear interpolation between the input and a trainable vector.

## Method

MoE++ introduces three zero-computation experts—zero (output = 0), copy (output = x), and constant (output = α₁x + α₂v with learned α)—alongside standard FFN experts within a single heterogeneous MoE layer, allowing each token to engage with a dynamic number of FFNs, receive constant-vector adjustments, or bypass the layer entirely. A pathway-aware router adds the previous layer's gating scores (via a trainable N×N transformation matrix) to the current layer's routing scores, enabling tokens to consider their prior expert pathway when selecting experts. A heterogeneous load balance loss with hyper-parameter τ controls the token allocation ratio between FFN and zero-computation experts, with corresponding per-type expert capacities. Because zero-computation experts have negligible parameters, they can be replicated on every GPU, eliminating the all-to-all communication and load imbalance that plague FFN expert distribution. The framework is trained from scratch with standard cross-entropy plus load-balance objectives, and at 7B parameters with 1T tokens it outperforms OpenMoE-8B/32E while using only ~57% of its computational cost and achieving 1.1–2.1× expert forward throughput.

## Results

MoE++ 7B/(16+4)E with ≤1.2B activated parameters achieves an average score of 56.2 across 11 benchmarks (SciQ, PIQA, WinoGrande, ARC-E, ARC-C, HellaSwag, LogiQA, BoolQ, LAMBADA, NQ, MMLU), outperforming OpenMoE-8B/32E (2.1B/8B) on most tasks while using only ~57% of its computational cost. Compared to vanilla MoE of the same size, MoE++ consistently improves average scores (e.g., 53.1 vs 52.3 at 7B) while delivering 1.1×–2.1× expert forward throughput (15%–111% speedup). At smaller scales (0.6B–2B), MoE++ also outperforms vanilla MoE across all τ settings, with the largest throughput gains (up to 164.5%) at lower τ values.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against OpenMoE-8B/32E (2.1B/8B, 1.1T tokens), MoE++ 7B/(16+4)E (≤1.2B/7B, 1T tokens) wins decisively on PIQA (77.6 vs 74.2), WinoGrande (63.1 vs 60.3), ARC-E (66.5 vs 64.1), ARC-C (42.3 vs 30.3), HellaSwag (72.3 vs 45.5), and BoolQ (68.3 vs 61.2), while using only ~57% of the computational cost. Against LLaMA-MoE-3.0B (3.0B/7B), MoE++ wins on ARC-C (42.3 vs 40.9), HellaSwag (72.3 vs 70.8), LAMBADA (67.4 vs 66.6), and NQ (18.1 vs 17.0), but trails on LogiQA (28.4 vs 30.6), BoolQ (68.3 vs 71.9), and MMLU (24.6 vs 26.8), yielding a near-tie in average (56.2 vs 56.6). Against dense models with 2–3× the activated parameters (e.g., Sheared-LLaMA-2.7B at 2.7B activated, avg 56.7), MoE++ with only ≤1.2B activated parameters achieves a competitive 56.2 average, demonstrating strong parameter efficiency. Against smaller dense models (OPT-1.3B, Pythia-1.4B, TinyLlama-1.1B), MoE++ substantially outperforms on all benchmarks, confirming the advantage of the MoE architecture at matched activated-parameter budgets. Against vanilla MoE of the same total size (Table 3), MoE++ wins on average score at every scale (e.g., 53.1 vs 52.3 at 7B; 48.6 vs 45.9 at 2B) while simultaneously reducing expert forward time by 15%–111%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the standard sparsely-gated MoE layer (Shazeer et al., 2017) and the Top-2 routing strategy popularized by GShard (Lepikhin et al., 2021) by introducing a heterogeneous expert pool that mixes FFN experts with three types of zero-computation experts (zero, copy, constant). The gating residuals mechanism draws a direct analogy to residual connections (He et al., 2016), adding previous-layer routing scores to current-layer scores to stabilize routing across heterogeneous experts. The authors position their work as the first to propose zero-computation experts within an MoE architecture, departing from prior efforts (DeepSeekMoE, XMoE, PEER, MH-MoE) that only modified FFN expert structure or token granularity while keeping all experts parametric.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MoE++ offers a general, drop-in framework that simultaneously improves both performance and throughput (1.1–2.1×) over vanilla MoE of the same size, which is practically significant for deployment since zero-computation experts can reside on every GPU, eliminating inter-GPU communication for those experts. The method is validated across four model scales (0.6B–7B) with consistent gains, and the 7B model outperforms OpenMoE-8B/32E at roughly 57% of its computational cost. Open questions include whether the approach scales beyond 7B parameters, whether heterogeneous expert configurations can be varied across layers (the authors note shallow and final layers show the most task-dependent routing), and how the method interacts with other MoE innovations like expert-choice routing or multi-head MoE.
