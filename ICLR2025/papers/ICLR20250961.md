## Problem

While f-divergence loss functions are the standard choice for density ratio estimation, the theoretical relationship between the choice of f-divergence and the resulting L_p estimation error remains largely unresolved. Specifically, existing minimax lower bounds do not reflect the true lower bound on error for the actual density ratio, the role of the true KL divergence in determining sample size requirements is unclear, and it is unknown whether different f-divergence losses (e.g., total variation vs. KL) yield statistically equivalent L_p errors.

## Input / Output

The method takes R i.i.d. samples from distribution P and S i.i.d. samples from distribution Q (with N = min{R,S}) over a compact support Ω ⊂ ℝ^d, and produces a K-Lipschitz continuous density ratio estimator φ(x) trained by minimizing the f-divergence loss with early stopping. The theoretical output is a pair of universal upper and lower bounds on the L_p error, expressed as products of the support diameter, N^{-1/d}, the Lipschitz constant L of the energy function T*(x) = −log(dQ/dP)(x), and E_P[(dQ/dP)^p]. Notably, the lower bound contains an exponential factor e^{(p−1)/p · KL(Q‖P) − 1}, making the bound explicitly dependent on the true KL divergence between the two distributions.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a conceptual reformulation of the f-divergence loss into a bounded μ-representation loss whose optimal points coincide exactly with the true density ratio at the sample points, thereby avoiding the unbounded optimization pathology of the original loss. Upper and lower bounds on the L_p error are then derived via nearest-neighbor distance arguments: the L_p error is sandwiched between the density ratio difference at a point and its nearest neighbor (controlled by the L-bi-Lipschitz continuity of the energy function) and the K-Lipschitz continuity of the estimator itself. The nearest-neighbor distance is bounded using compactness of the support, yielding rates of N^{-1/d} modulated by E_P[(dQ/dP)^{2p}]^{1/(2p)} in the upper bound and E_P[(dQ/dP)^p]^{1/p} in the lower bound. Applying Jensen's inequality to the lower bound introduces the exponential KL-divergence term, revealing that L_p error grows exponentially with KL divergence for p > 1. Finally, the paper shows that the O_p(N^{-1/2}) discrepancy between the practical early-stopped optimization and the theoretical μ-representation optimization is negligible for d ≥ 3, so the bounds hold for all K-Lipschitz estimators regardless of the specific f-divergence loss function used.

## Results

The paper derives universal upper and lower bounds on L_p errors in DRE that are independent of the specific f-divergence loss function used. The upper bound scales as diam(Ω)/N^(1/d) · {L·E[(dQ/dP)^(2p)]^(1/(2p)) + K}, while the lower bound includes an exponential term e^((p-1)/p · KL(Q||P) - 1)/L, revealing that L_p error grows exponentially with KL-divergence for p > 1. Numerical experiments on 5-dimensional synthetic data (KL values 1–14) confirm that L_2 and L_3 errors increase sharply with KL-divergence while L_1 error increases only mildly. Experiments across dimensions 50, 100, and 200 (KL=3) confirm the curse of dimensionality affects all L_p errors equally, with error scaling as N^(-1/d).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two f-divergence loss functions—α-divergence loss and KL-divergence loss—on synthetic datasets to validate that the derived bounds are loss-function-agnostic. In the KL-divergence experiments (Figure 1), both loss functions show qualitatively identical behavior: L_1 error increases mildly, while L_2 and L_3 errors grow substantially as KL-divergence rises from 1 to 14, with the growth accelerating for larger p. In the dimensionality experiments (Figure 2), both loss functions exhibit the same N^(-1/d) scaling across 50, 100, and 200 dimensions, confirming the curse of dimensionality is independent of the f-divergence choice. This empirically resolves the open question stated in the introduction: different f-divergence loss functions (e.g., total variation vs. KL-divergence) yield statistically equivalent L_p errors. The theoretical contribution is that the bounds hold for any K-Lipschitz continuous estimator optimized with early stopping, regardless of which f-divergence is used, unlike prior minimax bounds that were specific to particular divergences (e.g., Shannon divergence in Belomestny et al. 2021, Puchkin et al. 2024).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of minimax convergence analysis for density ratio estimation from specific divergence families to a universal framework. Prior work established bounds for IPM losses (Liang, 2017; Niles-Weed & Berthet, 2022) and Shannon/KL divergence losses (Belomestny et al., 2021; Puchkin et al., 2024), but each bound was tied to a particular f-divergence. The key conceptual departure is the μ-representation reformulation of the f-divergence loss, which absorbs the practical early-stopping procedure into a theoretically tractable objective, enabling bounds that hold uniformly across all f-divergence loss functions. The nearest-neighbor distance analysis connecting Lipschitz continuity of the energy function to L_p error rates draws on nonparametric regression theory (Stone, 1980) and order statistics of multidimensional random variables (Biau & Devroye, 2015).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The exponential dependence of the L_p lower bound on KL-divergence for p > 1 provides a principled explanation for why DRE becomes intractable when distributions are far apart, a phenomenon previously observed empirically in mutual information estimation (Poole et al., 2019; Song & Ermon, 2019) but not formally connected to DRE error rates. The universality of the bounds across f-divergence choices suggests that no particular divergence loss can circumvent the fundamental sample-size requirements imposed by KL-divergence and dimensionality. However, the bounds require L-bi-Lipschitz continuity of the energy function and compact support, which may not hold for heavy-tailed or unbounded distributions, and the empirical validation is limited to 5-dimensional synthetic data, leaving open whether the predicted exponential scaling manifests in practical high-dimensional settings.
