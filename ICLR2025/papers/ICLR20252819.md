## Problem

Language models can detect and correct their own reasoning mistakes after generation, but it remains unclear whether they can be trained to correct errors *during* generation via pretraining data. This paper asks whether including 'retry data' (erroneous steps immediately followed by corrections) in the pretraining corpus can teach models to self-correct in a single autoregressive pass, outperforming both error-free pretraining and post-hoc correction strategies like beam search or multi-round prompting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252819/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a grade-school math problem as text input, structured as a system of named-parameter equations over integers modulo 23 (to isolate logic reasoning from arithmetic). It produces a chain-of-thought solution where each step is a sentence of the form 'Define [param] as X; so X = ...', computing parameters in dependency order and ending with a final numeric answer. In the retry-data format, erroneous parameter names are inserted mid-solution followed by a [BACK] token and then the correct parameter, simulating a model that catches and fixes its own mistake inline.

## Method

The paper pretrains autoregressive transformer language models on the synthetic iGSM dataset with a controllable 'retry rate' (probability of inserting a wrong parameter + [BACK] + correct parameter at each step), and compares against models pretrained on the same number of error-free tokens. Key findings: (1) no label masking on erroneous tokens is needed—vanilla causal LM training suffices; (2) higher retry rates (up to 50%) yield better reasoning accuracy, especially on harder problems (op=23/32); (3) the pretrained model rarely actually emits [BACK] during inference, so solutions remain short and clean; (4) a lightweight 'retry upon regret' baseline (rank-8 embedding update for can_next probing at 99% detection accuracy) improves accuracy only marginally over beam search; and (5) the error-correction skill cannot be acquired via LoRA fine-tuning on an error-free pretrained model, indicating it requires substantial weight changes at the pretraining stage.

## Results

Pretraining on retry data (errors + immediate corrections) with retry_rate=0.5 improves reasoning accuracy from 78% to 95% on iGSM-med_pq^{op=23} and from 78% to 94% on iGSM-med_qp^{op=23}, compared to pretraining on the same number of error-free tokens. The 'retry upon regret' approach (using a 99%-accurate can_next probing detector with rank-8 LoRA) yields only a marginal ~2% gain over beam search on the hardest settings (op=23 or op=32). Notably, label masking on erroneous tokens is unnecessary, and models pretrained with retry_rate=0.2 retry fewer than 0.3 times on average during evaluation, confirming they still prefer correct steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four strategies: (1) standard error-free pretraining with beam search (beam=1/4/16/32), (2) 'retry upon regret' using a can_next probing error detector (99% accurate via rank-8 LoRA on the embedding layer), (3) pretraining directly on retry data (errors + corrections), and (4) fine-tuning an error-free pretrained model on retry data via LoRA. Pretraining on retry data with beam=1 (95% on iGSM-med_pq^{op=23}) decisively outperforms both beam search (≤80% even at beam=32) and retry upon regret (80%), a gap of 15+ percentage points. Beam search provides at most ~2% improvement over greedy decoding regardless of beam width, confirming that the model's error-free pretraining does not equip it with correction ability. Retry upon regret, despite near-perfect error detection, is limited by its reliance on stochastic regeneration and adds inference complexity. LoRA fine-tuning on retry data fails to transfer the correction skill (Result 7), indicating that error correction requires substantial weight changes beyond what PEFT can handle, reinforcing the need to include retry data at the pretraining stage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the self-correction line of work (Madaan et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2023; Weng et al., 2022), which typically uses multi-round prompting to let models verify and fix their own outputs, by showing that the correction skill can instead be baked into the model through pretraining on 'retry data' (errors followed by immediate corrections). It builds directly on the iGSM synthetic math dataset from the companion Part 2.1 paper (Ye et al., 2025), which provides fully controllable, verifiable reasoning problems and a probing technique (can_next) that reveals models already exhibit 'regretful' internal states after making mistakes. The key departure from prior work is the demonstration that error correction is a fundamentally different skill from error detection: while detection requires only a rank-8 embedding update, correction cannot be acquired via parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA) and must be learned during pretraining, challenging the common assumption that new capabilities can be added post-hoc.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is clear: if future LLMs are to learn from their own mistakes during generation (saving inference tokens and eliminating multi-round verification), retry data must be included in the pretraining corpus rather than deferred to fine-tuning. The finding that label masking is unnecessary and that high error rates (up to 50%) in pretraining data do not cause the model to produce errors is a strong, actionable principle for data preparation. However, the entire study is conducted on a synthetic, fully-controllable math dataset (iGSM) with modular arithmetic, and the authors explicitly disclaim that this data will directly aid commercial LLMs. Open questions include how to reliably generate retry data for real-world reasoning tasks (their Result 8 on 'fake retry data' is a first step), whether the findings transfer to non-mathematical reasoning, and how error rates should be tuned at scale.
