## Problem

Large Multimodal Models (LMMs) achieve poor accuracy on fine-grained, domain-specific visual classification (e.g., 12.2% on Stanford Dogs) and fail to ground their predictions in image-specific visual features, producing explanations that rely on spurious correlations like habitat or posture rather than discriminative morphology. The central obstacle is data: creating per-image, feature-level annotations that link specific visual evidence to a label is prohibitively expensive and beyond the capacity of standard annotators or current LMMs, so existing fine-tuning approaches either train on bare labels (causing shortcut learning) or on generic label-level descriptions (causing overgeneralization).

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a coarse-grained text query (e.g., 'What is the bird in this image? Please provide your reasoning.') and produces a fine-grained class label accompanied by a textual explanation that explicitly cites human-verifiable visual features present in that specific image (e.g., 'bright red plumage on head, throat, and chest; black mask around eyes'). The training data is synthesized from standard (image, label) pairs without any image-specific feature annotations; the base model is LLaVA-1.5-7B (or LLaVA-Med for chest X-rays), fine-tuned via LoRA on all linear layers. The output explanation is structured to reference only concepts confirmed to be observable in the given image, distinguishing it from generic label-level descriptions.

## Method

The framework operates in two iterative steps grounded in an Information Bottleneck decomposition of three-way mutual information I(X;Y;Z). Step 1 (Image-level Concept Selection): for each image, multiple descriptions are sampled from the base LMM using diverse prompts; an InfoNCE-based score measures alignment between these descriptions and a pool of expert-defined concepts for the image's label, and a threshold (mean + β·std) selects the subset Z* of concepts actually present in the image, balancing relevance I(D;Z*) against redundancy I(Z*;Z). Step 2 (Reward Model-Free Rejection Sampling): after an initial fine-tuning round, the updated model generates N candidate answers per image; the best answer is selected by maximizing its InfoNCE alignment with the previously chosen Z*, eliminating the need for a separate reward model. These two steps are repeated over multiple iterations, with each round's filtered synthetic data used to fine-tune the next round's model, progressively sharpening both classification accuracy and explanation quality. The theoretical justification (Theorem 2) shows that this two-step procedure provides a lower bound on the three-way mutual information, ensuring the selected explanation captures information shared among the image, the expert concepts, and the generated answer.

## Results

The proposed method achieves 85.02% accuracy on CUB-200, 86.91% on Stanford Dogs, 91.99% on FGVC-Aircraft, 97.16% on PLD, 85.06% on HAM10000, and 98.72% on Chest X-Ray Pneumonia after four iterations of self-synthesized fine-tuning, substantially outperforming the zero-shot LLaVA-1.5 base (2.69%, 12.2%, 3.00%, 0.00%, 1.62%, 62.50% respectively). The method also achieves a Cognition Score (CS) of 0.79–0.87 across datasets, indicating high-quality explanations aligned with expert-defined visual concepts, while maintaining general ability (MMMU scores within ~1 point of the base model). Accuracy improves monotonically across iterations (e.g., CUB-200: 80.24% → 85.02%), demonstrating the effectiveness of the iterative rejection sampling loop.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three baselines: (1) Base LLaVA-1.5 (zero-shot), (2) NL (Naive Label fine-tuning using only class labels as target answers), and (3) L+GE (Label + General Explanations, synthesizing answers from label-level knowledge via GPT-4). The proposed method outperforms NL on final-iteration accuracy across all six datasets (e.g., 85.02% vs. 82.21% on CUB-200, 91.99% vs. 87.73% on FGVC-Aircraft, 98.72% vs. 89.58% on Chest X-ray), while NL produces no explanations at all (EE = 0.00). Against L+GE, the proposed method achieves substantially higher accuracy on every dataset, with the largest gaps on PLD (97.16% vs. 24.90%) and HAM10000 (85.06% vs. 8.45%), where general explanations introduce irrelevant information that hinders learning. On explanation quality, the proposed method achieves higher Cognition Scores than L+GE on CUB-200 (0.82 vs. 0.70), Stanford Dogs (0.86 vs. 0.77), and FGVC-Aircraft (0.79 vs. 0.76), while L+GE slightly edges out on HAM10000 CS (0.94 vs. 0.87). General ability (MMMU) is preserved within ~1 point of the base model, confirming that domain-specific fine-tuning does not cause catastrophic forgetting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of LLM self-improvement (rejection sampling, self-synthesis) and fine-grained visual classification with LMMs. It adapts the Best-of-N rejection sampling paradigm from Llama 2 (Touvron et al., 2023) to the visual domain, but replaces the standard reward model with an InfoNCE-based alignment score against pre-selected visual concepts, sidestepping the well-documented lack of reliable multimodal reward models. The concept selection step draws on the Information Bottleneck principle (Tishby et al., 2000) and InfoNCE (Oord et al., 2018) to identify image-specific features from a pool of expert-defined label-level concepts, departing from prior LMM fine-tuning approaches (LLaVA, Kim & Ji 2024) that either use bare labels or generic label-level explanations without image-specific grounding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck: domain-specific visual classification (medical imaging, fine-grained species identification) requires image-level feature annotations that are prohibitively expensive to produce at scale. By showing that self-synthesized, concept-grounded explanations can close most of the accuracy gap (e.g., 12.2% → 86.9% on Stanford Dogs) while maintaining general abilities, the framework offers a scalable path for deploying LMMs in expert domains without massive annotation budgets. Key limitations include the reliance on LLM-generated descriptions that may hallucinate, the limited cross-dataset transfer (16.6% vs. 86.9% when training on CUB-200 and testing on Stanford Dogs), and evaluation restricted to classification tasks. Open questions include whether the iterative self-synthesis loop converges, how it scales to open-ended or multi-label tasks, and whether the InfoNCE-based filtering generalizes to non-textual modalities.
