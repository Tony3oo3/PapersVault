## Problem

Standard RLHF methods (DPO, PPO) rely on parametric reward models like Bradley-Terry that presuppose transitive, value-based preferences, yet human preferences are empirically intransitive and can exhibit irrational loops. This paper addresses how to align LLMs under a general (non-transitive) preference oracle without requiring strong external supervision such as GPT-4-generated responses or preferences, while still providing provable convergence guarantees to the Nash equilibrium of the underlying two-player game.

## Input / Output

SPPO takes a base instruction-tuned LLM (Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 or Llama-3-8B-Instruct), approximately 60k text prompts from UltraFeedback (no responses), and a 0.4B DeBERTA-V3-based pairwise preference model (PairRM) that outputs relative reward scores. In each self-play iteration, the current policy generates K=5 responses per prompt, PairRM annotates all pairwise win rates, and the top/bottom-scoring responses are selected to form training triplets (prompt, response, estimated win rate). The output is an aligned LLM policy that, after three iterations, achieves a 28.53% length-controlled win rate against GPT-4-Turbo on AlpacaEval 2.0 (Mistral) or 38.77% (Llama-3-8B), all without any GPT-4 supervision.

## Method

SPPO formulates LLM alignment as a two-player constant-sum game and applies the multiplicative weights (exponential weight update) framework from Freund & Schapire (1999) to provably converge to the Nash equilibrium at rate O(1/√T). The key practical innovation is a square-loss objective that directly regresses the token-level log-ratio log(π(y|x)/π_t(y|x)) onto the response's win rate against the current policy minus 1/2, rather than using pairwise symmetric losses (DPO, IPO) that cancel the log-partition factor. The log-partition factor is approximated by the constant η/2, which corresponds to the 'most disordered' (fair-coin-flip) preference regime and serves as a variance-reducing baseline in the policy-gradient interpretation. Each iteration is a self-play step: the policy generates synthetic responses, the preference model annotates win rates, and the policy is fine-tuned on this data—requiring no external responses or GPT-4 labels. The square loss is shown to implicitly encourage the model to learn a token-level optimal Q* value function, connecting SPPO to Max-Entropy RL and distinguishing it from DPO/IPO which only drive relative likelihood gaps without guaranteeing absolute likelihood increases for preferred responses.

## Results

On AlpacaEval 2.0, Mistral-7B-SPPO Iter3 achieves a length-controlled win rate of 28.53% and raw win rate of 31.02% against GPT-4-Turbo, outperforming the base Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 (17.11% LC) by over 11 percentage points. Applied to the stronger Llama-3-8B-Instruct base, SPPO Iter3 reaches 38.77% LC win rate and 39.85% raw win rate. On MT-Bench, SPPO Iter3 scores 7.59 average (vs. 7.51 base), and on Arena-Hard it reaches 23.3 (vs. 12.6 base). On the Open LLM Leaderboard, SPPO achieves state-of-the-art averages of 66.75 (Mistral-7B) and 70.29 (Llama-3-8B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPPO consistently outperforms iterative DPO across all benchmarks: on AlpacaEval 2.0, SPPO Iter3 achieves 28.53% LC win rate vs. DPO's best of 24.23% (Iter2) and Snorkel's 26.39%; on MT-Bench, SPPO scores 7.59 vs. DPO Iter3's 7.09; and on Arena-Hard, SPPO reaches 23.3 vs. DPO Iter2's 7.06. Against iterative IPO, SPPO also wins on AlpacaEval 2.0 (28.53% vs. IPO's best 23.78% at Iter1) and shows a more moderate length increase (2163 vs. IPO's 2760 at Iter3), suggesting IPO exploits length bias. Compared to Self-Rewarding LM (70B, 3 iterations), SPPO (7B) achieves a higher raw win rate (31.02% vs. 20.44%) without requiring a 70B model or GPT-4 supervision. Notably, SPPO's gains are consistent across iterations without the performance decay seen in DPO and IPO on the Open LLM Leaderboard (SPPO Iter2: 66.75 avg vs. DPO Iter3: 64.52, IPO Iter3: 64.72), and all results are achieved using only a 0.4B PairRM preference model without any GPT-4-generated responses or preferences.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPPO builds on the Nash-equilibrium formulation of RLHF under general (non-transitive) preferences introduced by Munos et al. (2023) and Wang et al. (2024), and on the multiplicative-weights / no-regret online learning framework of Freund & Schapire (1999). It departs from concurrent work by Swamy et al. (2024) (SPO), which also targets the same constant-sum game but relies on PPO/SAC for the inner optimization, making large-scale LLM fine-tuning impractical. The key methodological departure from DPO and IPO is the use of a square loss that directly regresses the token-level log-ratio onto the win rate minus a constant baseline, rather than a pairwise symmetric loss that cancels the log-partition factor; this yields a direct connection to policy gradient and implicit token-level Q* learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SPPO demonstrates that a 0.4B preference model (PairRM) and 60k prompts—without any GPT-4-generated responses or preferences—suffice to push Mistral-7B to 28.53% and Llama-3-8B to 38.77% length-controlled win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0, suggesting that the bottleneck in alignment may be the optimization objective rather than the quality of external supervision. The square-loss objective's connection to policy gradient and token-level value functions offers a principled alternative to pairwise losses, potentially alleviating the data-sparsity failure mode identified by Pal et al. (2024) where DPO only widens the winner-loser gap without raising the winner's absolute likelihood. Open questions include whether the constant approximation of the log-partition factor (η/2) is optimal across preference distributions, whether the observed 'alignment tax' on Open LLM Leaderboard (performance decline after iteration 1–2) can be mitigated, and whether the approach generalizes beyond UltraFeedback to more diverse prompt distributions.
