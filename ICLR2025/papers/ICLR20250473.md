## Problem

Existing image smoothing methods struggle to simultaneously remove textures and preserve edges, frequently producing gradient reversals, halo artifacts, or blurred weak structures. Deep learning-based smoothers additionally lock in a fixed smoothing strength at training time, preventing adaptation to varying texture complexity. The fundamental gap is the lack of a principled mechanism that distinguishes textures from structures while maintaining adjustable smoothing intensity.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image of arbitrary resolution as input and produces a smoothed image of the same size as output. It is a non-learned, optimization-based pipeline requiring no training data or paired examples; the user tunes a small set of parameters (smoothing weight λ, scale bounds r_min/r_max, range kernel σ_r, iteration count T) to control the tradeoff between smoothing strength and edge fidelity. The smoothed output feeds directly into downstream applications such as detail enhancement (via subtraction to extract a detail layer), HDR tone mapping, texture removal, compression artifact removal, and texture transfer.

## Method

The core contribution is a mutually guided iterative framework combining two components: (1) a Relative Wavelet Domain Representation (RWDR) regularization that uses B-spline tight frame wavelet filters to compute a per-pixel ratio D(p)/W(p) that assigns high values to texture regions and low values to structural edges, and (2) an edge-aware scale map derived from a Gaussian-weighted gradient feature map E and a gradient-magnitude coefficient m, which modulates the spatial window of an adaptive bilateral filter. At each iteration, the RWDR subproblem is solved as a non-convex optimization via preconditioned conjugate gradient on a symmetric positive definite linear system, while the bilateral filter is evaluated in O(1) per pixel using a Fourier expansion of the Gaussian range kernel with integral-image recursion. The two components act as cross-guidance inputs to each other, and the edge-aware scale map (small at edges, large in smooth regions) prevents the progressive blurring of weak edges that plagues standard adaptive bilateral filters.

## Results

On the compression artifact removal task, the proposed method achieves the best PSNR of 27.10 dB and SSIM of 0.991, outperforming the next-best method (imRTV, PSNR 26.80, SSIM 0.979). For image detail enhancement, the method attains the lowest BRISQUE (7.69) and PIQE (24.91) scores and the highest SSEQ (26.79) and ILNIQUE (128.84) among all compared methods. In HDR tone mapping, the method achieves the best values across all five no-reference metrics, including BRISQUE 14.84 (vs. 20.71 for RTV) and CEIQ 2.89 (vs. 2.85 for RoG). Running time is 6.08 s on a 768×1024×3 image, faster than muGIF (10.31 s), RoG (11.15 s), and Ghosh (7.60 s), but slower than ILS (0.59 s) and RGF (0.75 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method consistently outperforms all 15 baselines across every evaluated task and metric. On compression artifact removal, it surpasses the strongest traditional baselines (imRTV, EP/SP, RoG) by 0.22–0.67 dB in PSNR and 0.011–0.017 in SSIM, while filter-based methods (BF, Ghosh) and global methods (WLS, ILS) lag by 5–6 dB in PSNR. For detail enhancement, the method achieves the best BRISQUE (7.69 vs. 8.56 for DRTV) and PIQE (24.91 vs. 25.16 for dRTV), with a notable margin over deep learning baselines (ResNet: BRISQUE 22.38; VDCNN: BRISQUE 28.95). In HDR tone mapping, the advantage is most pronounced on BRISQUE (14.84 vs. 20.71 for RTV, a 28% reduction) and CEIQ (2.89 vs. 2.85 for RoG). The tight frame method preserves edges well but fails to fully remove textures, while RTV and DRTV blur edges; the proposed RWDR-based approach achieves a superior tradeoff. In terms of speed, the method (6.08 s) is competitive with RTV (4.65 s) and Ghosh (7.60 s) but slower than the fastest iterative methods ILS (0.59 s) and RGF (0.75 s) due to the need to solve a large linear system via PCG.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the adaptive bilateral filter (ABF) of Ghosh et al. (2019) and the mutually guided filtering framework of Guo et al. (2017), but replaces the gradient-based regularization terms used in RTV-family methods with a novel Relative Wavelet Domain Representation (RWDR) derived from B-spline tight frame wavelet filters. The key departure is twofold: (1) using wavelet-domain features rather than spatial gradients to construct the relative measure that distinguishes textures from structures, and (2) introducing an edge-aware scale map computed from a weighted combination of a structural feature map and a gradient-magnitude coefficient, rather than the simple gradient-erosion approach in prior ABF work. The mutual guidance mechanism iteratively couples the RWDR solver (via PCG on a non-convex objective) with the ABF, so each informs the other's parameters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a persistent practical problem in image smoothing—the simultaneous occurrence of halo artifacts and gradient reversals—by showing that a wavelet-domain relative measure combined with an edge-aware spatial kernel can reduce both without sacrificing smoothing strength. The complete theoretical analysis (convergence guarantees for the Fourier approximation, O(1) per-pixel complexity) makes the method deployable in real-time pipelines. However, the authors acknowledge a fundamental limitation: the local-window-based approach cannot handle long-range texture dependencies or irregular multiscale textures, and the method remains a traditional optimization-based technique in a field increasingly dominated by learned approaches. The suggested future direction of integrating the RWDR into neural architectures (e.g., Vision Mamba) points toward a hybrid paradigm that could address these limitations.
