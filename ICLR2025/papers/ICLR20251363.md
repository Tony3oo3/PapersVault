## Problem

Existing video and image tokenizers encode all inputs to a fixed number of tokens, forcing a trade-off: too few tokens produce lossy encodings for complex content, while too many waste compute on simple content. This fixed-length constraint becomes a critical bottleneck for long video sequences, where encoding can require tens to hundreds of millions of tokens, making downstream training prohibitively expensive.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251363/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 256×256 RGB images (treated as single-frame videos) or video sequences at 24 FPS, segmented into 4-frame blocks, and produces a variable-length token sequence per block (128–4096 tokens for the discrete FSQ variant, 128–2048 for the continuous VAE variant). Tokens are left-aligned: the first ℓ positions are retained and the remainder are zeroed out. At inference, the user specifies either a target token count or a reconstruction threshold (e.g., pixel-wise MSE), and the model searches for the minimum ℓ that satisfies the threshold, yielding different token counts for different inputs.

## Method

ElasticTok trains a standard ViT-based autoencoder (FSQ or VAE) with a random masking scheme: during training, a token count ℓ is uniformly sampled from [M_min, M_max], a binary mask with the first ℓ positions set to 1 is created, and the encoder is conditioned on this mask via learned embedding vectors added to the patchified input. The latent is masked (z ⊙ m) before decoding, and the model is optimized with reconstruction (MSE + LPIPS) losses. For long videos, the sequence is split into blocks with independently sampled masks, and a block-causal attention pattern combined with Blockwise RingAttention enables scaling to 1M+ token contexts without added parameters. At inference, a search procedure (binary search, full search, or neural regression) finds the minimum token count meeting a user-specified reconstruction threshold, and the search objective can be swapped (e.g., MSE vs. CLIP cosine distance) to prioritize different visual aspects.

## Results

ElasticTok achieves up to 5x fewer tokens on video and 3.5x fewer on images while maintaining the same reconstruction quality threshold (MSE). On downstream VQA benchmarks, ElasticTok-VAE matches the fixed-token baseline exactly on GQA (54%) and POPE (82%), with negligible differences on MSVD-QA (52% vs 53%) and MSRVTT-QA (37% vs 37%). In the quality-matched comparison (Table 4), ElasticTok uses only 37% of tokens to achieve the same worst-case MSE of 0.003, compared to 57% for the fixed-token baseline, and 9.4% vs 15% at the looser 0.015 threshold. Frequency analysis confirms a strong Pearson correlation (0.77 single-block, 0.67 multi-block) between high-frequency visual detail and token allocation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares ElasticTok against fixed-token baselines that share identical architecture, training procedure, and total FLOPs but are restricted to a single fixed mask rather than sampling variable masks during training. On reconstruction efficiency, ElasticTok-FSQ wins decisively: at an MSE threshold of 0.003 it uses only 37% of tokens versus 57% for the baseline (a 1.5x saving), and at 0.015 it uses 9.4% versus 15% (a 1.6x saving). At the strictest threshold (0.001) the gap narrows to 75% vs 78%, as nearly all tokens are needed regardless. On downstream VQA tasks (Table 1), ElasticTok-VAE ties the fixed 50%-token baseline on GQA (54%), POPE (82%), and MSRVTT-QA (37%), and trails by only 1 point on MSVD-QA (52% vs 53%), confirming no meaningful accuracy loss from switching to adaptive tokenization. The continuous VAE variant (Table 3) similarly outperforms its 50%-fixed baseline at the 0.001 threshold (50% vs 40% of blocks satisfying the criterion) while using the same average token budget.

## Relation to Prior Work

ElasticTok extends the standard visual autoencoder pipeline (VQ-VAE, FSQ, VAE) by introducing a simple random masking scheme that drops a variable number of trailing tokens during training, enabling a single model to produce variable-length encodings at inference. Conceptually, it draws on the ordered-representation idea from nested dropout (Rippel et al., 2014) and Matryoshka representation learning (Kusupati et al., 2022), but applies it specifically to the sequence-length dimension of visual tokenizers rather than to feature dimensions or network width. For long video, the method leverages Blockwise RingAttention (Liu et al., 2024b) to scale to 1M-token contexts, and conditions on prior frames to exploit temporal redundancy—analogous to how variable-rate video codecs (H.264, AV1) allocate bits adaptively, but in a differentiable, token-based framework compatible with downstream language-model training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 2–5× token reduction for equivalent reconstruction quality directly addresses a key bottleneck for world models, video generation, and long-horizon agents that must process millions of tokens. The method is architecture-agnostic (works with both discrete FSQ and continuous VAE backbones) and introduces no extra parameters, making it a drop-in upgrade for existing tokenization pipelines. Open questions include whether learnable or spatially-aware masking can close the small gap at the extreme low-token end, whether GAN-based training can be stably extended to very long sequences, and how the approach generalizes to other temporal modalities (audio, robotics trajectories) or task-conditioned encodings that selectively preserve only relevant information.
