## Problem

How complex, structured, and transferable representations emerge in modern neural networks has remained a mystery, with no unified framework explaining the alignment between latent representations, weights, and gradients across layers. While phenomena like neural collapse and the neural feature ansatz capture partial aspects of this alignment in specific settings (penultimate layers of classifiers, weight evolution), there is no general theory that applies to arbitrary fully connected layers, arbitrary loss functions, or both forward and backward directions simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained neural network of any architecture (ResNet-18, 6-layer transformer, or fully connected nets) and computes three d×d second-moment matrices per layer: the representation covariance H = E[hhᵀ], the gradient covariance G = E[ggᵀ], and the weight outer product Z = WWᵀ (or WᵀW for the backward direction). Representations and gradients are normalized and mean-subtracted before covariance estimation over the training set. The output is a Pearson correlation alignment score α ∈ [-1,1] between each pair of matrices, power-law exponents relating their eigenvalue spectra, and a phase label per layer from a 64-phase taxonomy defined by which subset of the six CRH relations holds.

## Method

The paper proposes the Canonical Representation Hypothesis (CRH), positing six alignment relations (forward/backward × RGA/RWA/GWA) between representation, gradient, and weight covariance matrices in any fully connected layer. The key theoretical mechanism is a noise-regularization balance: SGD discretization noise expands the representation covariance while weight decay contracts it, and at stationarity these effects must balance, forcing H ∝ G ∝ Z. The CRH Master Theorem proves that when the full CRH is broken, the six matrices still obey reciprocal polynomial (power-law) relations with exponents constrained to [-1, 3], defining the Polynomial Alignment Hypothesis and a phase diagram of up to 64 distinct regimes. The proof is agnostic to loss function, architecture, and activation type, and the paper shows CRH reduces exactly to neural collapse in interpolating classifiers and generalizes the neural feature ansatz to an equivariant form invariant to reparameterizations of the loss.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative findings are alignment metrics (Pearson correlation α) between the H, G, and Z matrices. For ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10 (94% test accuracy), all six CRH alignment relations achieve α > 0.7 in the penultimate and output layers, improving throughout training. In a six-layer transformer trained on OpenWebText, power-law exponents between eigenvalues of H_b and G_b are observed at 3 (layer 1), 2 (layer 2), and 1 (layers 3–6), all within the theoretically predicted range [1/3, 3]. Rank deficiency of representations is strongly negatively correlated with alignment α across weight decays from 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻⁴, confirming that stronger CRH satisfaction yields more compact representations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper, so it does not benchmark against competing methods in the traditional sense. Instead, it positions the CRH relative to two prior phenomena: Neural Collapse (NC) and the Neural Feature Ansatz (NFA). The paper proves (Theorem 3) that NC is a strict special case of the CRH in interpolating classifiers, meaning the CRH generalizes NC to any fully connected layer and any task (not just classification). Regarding the NFA, the paper shows the CRH's Gradient-Weight Alignment (GWA) is an 'equivariant NFA' that is invariant to trivial redefinitions of the loss function and model output, whereas the original NFA is not — a consistency problem the authors demonstrate explicitly. Empirically, the RGA (Representation-Gradient Alignment) is compared against three baselines in the transformer: a random projection baseline (α ≈ 0.14), alignment between different attention heads (drops well below α_gg,hh), and alignment between initial and current features (starts at 1 but drops quickly). The RGA consistently outperforms all three baselines, confirming it is a genuine training-induced alignment rather than an artifact of initialization or architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of work—neural collapse (Papyan et al., 2020), which characterizes structured representations in the penultimate layer of classifiers, and the neural feature ansatz (Radhakrishnan et al., 2023), which describes weight evolution in fully connected layers—under a single framework of six alignment relations (the CRH) applicable to any fully connected layer. The authors prove that NC is a special case of the CRH in interpolating classifiers (Theorem 3) and that the NFA is a special case of the gradient-weight alignment when the loss Hessian is proportional to identity. The theoretical derivation draws an explicit analogy to the fluctuation-dissipation theorem from statistical physics, where gradient noise (fluctuation) balances weight decay (dissipation), extending prior applications of this principle from parameter covariance (Yaida, 2018) to latent representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The CRH/PAH framework offers a potential unifying language for diverse deep learning phenomena (neural collapse, feature learning, low-rank structure, invariance to irrelevant features) and predicts a phase diagram of at least 22 distinct scaling regimes, each with different implications for representation compactness and information processing. Key limitations include applicability only to fully connected layers (not convolutions or attention in their native form) and a focus on the terminal phase of training rather than dynamics. Open questions include identifying which hyperparameter configurations drive each phase, extending the theory to other layer types, and whether the alignment structure can be exploited algorithmically (e.g., injecting gradient noise to engineer desired representation geometry).
