## Problem

Real-time monitoring technologies (ICU sensors, continuous glucose monitors) generate functional longitudinal data where treatments, confounders, and outcomes are continuous-time stochastic processes, creating uncountably infinite treatment-confounder feedbacks that no existing causal inference framework can handle. Traditional g-computation, IPW, and doubly robust formulas assume finite-dimensional, discrete-time data, leaving a methodological void for identifying causal effects in these infinite-dimensional settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252549/cropped_page9_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes entire stochastic process paths (A(t), L(t), Y(t) over [0,∞]) along with time-to-event T, censoring time C, a stochastic treatment regime G (a signed measure on the infinite-dimensional path space of treatments), and a user-specified functional ν mapping (T, Ȳ) to a scalar. It produces three equivalent identification formulas—g-computation, IPW, and doubly robust—that express the marginal mean E[ν(T_ā, Ȳ_ā)] under regime G as functionals of the observed data distribution, without requiring parametric models for the processes. The treatment regime G is not a fixed path but a probability distribution over paths, accommodating continuous or probabilistic treatments.

## Method

The paper adapts the coarsened data framework (Heitjan & Rubin, 1991) to continuous time by decomposing the observed data measure over partitions Δ_K[0,∞] and intervening on the treatment and censoring distributions at each partition point, then taking the limit as the mesh |Δ_K| → 0 in total variation norm. Four identification assumptions are stated nonparametrically: full conditional randomization (no unmeasured confounding in an infinitesimal-window sense via a bounded ε(t,η)→0), conditional independent censoring on the intensity process, full consistency, and absolute continuity of the target measure. The g-computation formula is derived as a P_G-martingale H_G(t) = E_G[ν(X,Ȳ)|G_t] evaluated at t=0⁻, the IPW formula via the Radon-Nikodym derivative process Q_G(t) = E[dP_G/dP|G_t], and the doubly robust formula as a limit of Riemann-sum-like expressions Ξ_{Δ_K}(H,Q) that is valid if either H or Q is correctly specified. A key theoretical result (Theorem 4) proves the framework is nonparametric: for any observed data distribution, there exists a sequence of full-data distributions satisfying all four assumptions whose induced observed-data distributions converge in total variation, requiring only piecewise-continuity of the path space.

## Results

The paper validates its g-computation identification formula through Monte Carlo simulations (R = 10,000 repeats) where the true causal parameter equals 0. In the simpler setting (no mortality or censoring), the formula adequately approximates the target even at moderate grid sizes (K = 10) and sample sizes (n = 100), with simultaneously increasing both K and n significantly reducing bias and variance. In the more complex setting (with Cox-model mortality, independent censoring, and a confounding process), biases are larger, and increasing grid size (K) while holding sample size fixed improves accuracy, whereas increasing sample size alone does not—reversing the pattern from the simpler case. No numerical bias/variance values are tabulated; results are presented as boxplots (Figures 1 and 2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical identification paper, not an estimation or benchmarking paper, so it does not compare against competing causal inference methods on a common dataset. The three identification formulas it derives (g-computation, inverse probability weighting, and doubly robust) are proven equivalent at the population level (all equal to the target parameter in Eq. 1), so no empirical head-to-head comparison is conducted. The paper's contribution is the identification framework itself—extending classical discrete-time formulas to infinite-dimensional, continuous-time functional longitudinal data with uncountably infinite treatment-confounder feedbacks. The closest prior work (Ying 2024a; Sun & Crawford 2022) is discussed qualitatively in the related work section: Ying (2024a) handles only a single terminal outcome without a nonparametric proof, while Sun & Crawford (2022) imposes parametric SDE assumptions. The Monte Carlo simulations serve as internal validation of the identification logic rather than a comparison against baseline estimators.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical causal identification toolkit (g-computation, IPW, doubly robust formulas) from finite-dimensional, discrete-time longitudinal studies to the infinite-dimensional, continuous-time setting of functional longitudinal data. It adapts the coarsening-at-random framework (Heitjan & Rubin, 1991) to handle uncountably infinite treatment-confounder feedbacks via a continuous-time 'full conditional randomization' assumption, and builds on the continuous-time counting process framework of Gill & Robins (2001) and Rytgaard et al. (2022) by replacing counting processes with general stochastic processes. The work also extends the author's prior identification result (Ying, 2024a), which handled only a single terminal outcome without a nonparametric proof, by accommodating time-varying outcomes, mortality, and censoring, and by proving the framework imposes no parametric restrictions on the observed data distribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a recognized methodological gap: no prior nonparametric identification framework existed for causal inference on functional longitudinal data (e.g., MIMIC-IV ICU records, continuous glucose monitoring), where treatments and confounders are continuous-time processes generating uncountably infinite feedback loops. However, the contribution is purely identification-level—no estimation, inference, or efficiency theory is developed—so practical applicability awaits future work on consistent estimators in infinite-dimensional spaces. Key open questions include establishing semiparametric efficiency bounds, handling violations of the full conditional randomization assumption (e.g., via time-dependent proxies), addressing positivity challenges in continuous treatment spaces, and extending the framework to sample-level data with sparse or irregular observation times.
