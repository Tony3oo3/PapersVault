## Problem

When learning dynamical systems from image observations, standard CNN autoencoders produce latent states that are discontinuous in time, even though the underlying physical dynamics are continuous. This occurs because pixel observations are inherently discrete—small state changes below one pixel are invisible in the image—so the encoder's output jumps rather than evolving smoothly. This discontinuity prevents the latent states from being modeled by continuous dynamical systems (e.g., ODEs), which is essential for scientific applications like time reversal, interpolation, and invariant analysis.

## Input / Output

The method takes sequential image frames of size 3×128×128 (or 3×128×256) sampled at discrete time intervals from continuous trajectories (e.g., 60 frames per trajectory at 1/60 s intervals for pendulum systems, or 1,212 frames for swing-stick). It produces continuous latent states (dimension 2 or 8), a Neural ODE latent dynamical model, reconstructed images, and predicted future images. A key constraint is that the latent states must form discrete samples of Lipschitz continuous trajectories, enabling downstream ODE-based modeling and time-reversal operations that discrete models cannot support.

## Method

The paper proves (Theorem 3.1) that a CNN encoder's output is δ-continuous (a relaxation of Lipschitz continuity suited to pixel data) if and only if its convolution filters are Lipschitz continuous, and shows that standard small (O(1)) or randomly-initialized large (O(1/δ)) filters fail this condition with probability one. To enforce filter continuity, CpAEs use large filters (size 12) in the first L*=4 downsampling layers and apply a nonlocal-operator regularizer (Eq. 6) with a Gaussian kernel to the first three layers, penalizing large filter Lipschitz constants. Training is two-stage: first, the encoder-decoder is trained with reconstruction loss plus the filter continuity regularizer and a VpNet-based orientation-preservation regularizer; second, a Neural ODE is separately fitted to the now-continuous latent states. This decoupled approach avoids the joint-training instability that causes standard autoencoder + ODE pipelines to produce discontinuous latent trajectories.

## Results

CpAEs achieve near-perfect prediction on the damped pendulum dataset with a Valid Prediction Time (VPT) of 99.2% and Valid Prediction Frequency (VPF) of 99.2%, substantially outperforming the hybrid neural state variable scheme (95.4% VPT, 88.3% VPF) and standard AE+Neural ODE (50.7% VPT, 23.3% VPF). Across all four physical systems, CpAEs consistently lead on both VPT and VPF, with the largest margins on the double pendulum (69.1% vs. 46.8% VPT over the hybrid scheme) and swing stick (57.4% vs. 24.1% VPT over the best baseline, AE+HNN). All values are scaled by a factor of 100, with higher scores indicating better performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CpAEs outperform all five baselines across every dataset on both VPT and VPF. Against the hybrid neural state variable scheme [6] (the strongest discrete baseline), CpAEs gain +3.8 VPT on damped pendulum, +22.6 on elastic pendulum, +22.3 on double pendulum, and +43.7 on swing stick. Standard AE+Neural ODE [7] suffers severe discontinuity in latent states, yielding VPT scores roughly 2–4× lower than CpAEs (e.g., 50.7% vs. 99.2% on damped pendulum). The structured Hamiltonian baselines—AE+HNN [18] and AE+SympNet [22,23]—perform worst on the double pendulum (11.0% and 15.1% VPT respectively) because standard autoencoders fail to produce latent states that satisfy Hamiltonian constraints; on swing stick, AE+HNN (24.1% VPT) is the closest competitor to CpAEs (57.4%) but still trails by over 33 points. Notably, CpAEs are the only method achieving non-zero VPF on the swing stick dataset, indicating that only continuity-preserving encoders enable reliable long-horizon prediction in the most challenging real-world setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the autoencoder-based framework for learning latent dynamics from images (Botev et al., 2021; Toth et al., 2020; Greydanus et al., 2019), but identifies a fundamental flaw: standard CNN encoders produce latent states that are discontinuous in time, violating the core assumption that latent dynamics follow a continuous ODE. The fix draws on two distinct lineages: the nonlocal operator framework of Gilboa & Osher (2007, 2009) for promoting spatial continuity, repurposed here as a filter regularizer, and VpNet (Zhu et al., 2022) for volume-preserving regularization of the latent dynamics. The key departure from prior work is the introduction of δ-continuity as a mathematical notion tailored to discrete pixel observations, and the proof that Lipschitz continuity of early-layer CNN filters is a sufficient condition for preserving this continuity (Theorem 3.1).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous characterization of why standard CNN autoencoders fail for dynamics learning from images, which is a practical concern that has been observed empirically but not previously explained. However, the scope is limited to 2D rigid body motion, and performance degrades notably on complex real-world tasks (swing stick) with noisy backgrounds. Open questions include generalizing the continuity analysis to non-rigid motion and 3D-to-2D projections, exploring more sophisticated continuity-promoting mechanisms (e.g., hypernetworks), and extending the framework to Vision Transformer architectures. The practical advantage over hybrid schemes (which bypass the continuity requirement) is modest, suggesting the main value is theoretical clarity and enabling tasks like time reversal and interpolation that require a continuous latent model.
