## Problem

Prior weak-to-strong generalization work (Burns et al., 2023) has only been validated for a single capability, leaving open whether LLMs can simultaneously acquire multiple capabilities from weak supervision. This paper investigates multi-capability weak-to-strong generalization and identifies two critical failure modes: strong models exhibit overconfidence that causes performance degradation during self-bootstrapping, and the quality and diversity of weak-generated data significantly determine generalization success.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on text-based tasks across 8 capability domains (math, temporal reasoning, robot planning, fact-checking, commonsense reasoning, logical reasoning, fact reasoning, science), using Qwen-1.5 models at 0.5B–72B scales. The weak model produces labeled predictions on unlabeled data as supervision; the reward model outputs a binary keep/discard decision per sample based on accuracy and distributional divergence. The strong model ultimately generates text answers evaluated by exact-match accuracy, with performance measured via the Performance Gap Recovered (PGR) metric relative to a human-annotated ceiling.

## Method

The core contribution is a reward model trained with consistent samples (where weak and strong models agree) as positives and inconsistent strong-model-only samples as negatives, optimizing an objective that favors high-accuracy data while penalizing data aligned with the strong model's own distribution. The reward model then filters the weak dataset to retain samples that are both accurate and distributionally divergent from the strong model's predictions, preventing the collapse observed in self-bootstrapping. Training proceeds in two stages: a warm-up phase on the full weak dataset for broad exposure, followed by fine-tuning on the reward-model-selected subset to refine predictions. The key insight is that weak data provides greater diversity for the strong model than its own generated data, and combining both stages leverages the breadth of weak data with the precision of selected data to achieve superior multi-capability generalization.

## Results

Across eight multi-capability tasks (GSM8K, MC-TACO, SCAN, CREAK, ECQA, e-SNLI, OpenBookQA, SciQ), the proposed reward-model-based method achieves average accuracy of 59.19% (0.5B→1.8B), 69.07% (1.8B→4B), and 77.06% (4B→7B), consistently outperforming the standard WTS baseline (56.10%, 68.00%, 76.11%). In terms of Performance Gap Recovered (PGR), the method recovers approximately 50% of the gap to the human-annotated performance ceiling with a 0.5B weak model, compared to ~30% for WTS. The advantage is most pronounced for smaller strong models and weaker supervisors, narrowing as model scale increases (e.g., 0.95-point gap at 4B→7B vs. 3.09-point gap at 0.5B→1.8B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the standard weak-to-strong generalization (WTS) from Burns et al. (2023), where a strong model is trained directly on data generated by a weaker model without any data selection. The proposed method (reward model for data selection + two-stage training) wins against WTS across all three model-scale configurations: by 3.09 points (0.5B→1.8B), 1.07 points (1.8B→4B), and 0.95 points (4B→7B). The margin is largest when the weak model is weakest (0.5B), indicating the reward model's filtering is most impactful when weak supervision quality is lowest. Both methods substantially outperform in-context learning (ICL with 3 examples) by 10–13 points, confirming that training-based weak-to-strong generalization is far more effective than few-shot prompting. The paper also shows that random data selection (matching the reward model's retention rate) underperforms the reward model by 6.68, 5.53, and 2.76 points respectively, validating that the reward model's selection is non-trivial and accuracy-aware.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the weak-to-strong generalization framework introduced by Burns et al. (2023), which demonstrated that a strong model can learn a single capability from a weaker model's noisy annotations, to the multi-capability setting where a strong model must simultaneously acquire diverse abilities (math, temporal reasoning, planning, commonsense, etc.) from different weak models. It also draws on the data selection literature (e.g., QuRating, LESS, DSIR) by introducing a reward model trained on consistency/inconsistency signals between weak and strong model predictions to filter training data. The two-stage training procedure (warm-up on full weak data, then fine-tuning on reward-selected subsets) is a novel combination not present in prior weak-to-strong or data-selection work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically critical gap: real superhuman LLMs must handle multiple capabilities simultaneously, yet prior weak-to-strong results were limited to single tasks. The finding that capabilities remain largely independent during generalization simplifies the problem, while the counterintuitive result that weak-model data outperforms strong-model self-generated data (due to greater distributional diversity) challenges the natural assumption that a model's own outputs are the best training signal. Open questions include whether the reward model approach scales to truly open-ended generation tasks (beyond multiple-choice and short-answer benchmarks), whether capability independence holds for more entangled capabilities (e.g., multi-step reasoning requiring both math and planning), and whether the framework generalizes beyond the Qwen-1.5 family to architecturally different model families.
