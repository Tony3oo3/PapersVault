## Problem

Off-policy hierarchical reinforcement learning suffers from non-stationarity: as the lower primitive continuously improves, previously collected experience becomes obsolete, destabilizing the higher-level policy's state transitions and reward signals. Existing task-segmentation approaches (e.g., fixed-window relabeling in RPL) produce subgoals that are either too easy or too hard for the current lower primitive, leading to degenerate solutions. The paper asks how to generate a curriculum of subgoals that dynamically matches the lower primitive's evolving goal-achieving capability while simultaneously mitigating non-stationarity.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a small set of directed expert demonstrations (100 trajectories for most tasks, 28 for Franka kitchen) represented as state sequences, where states encode gripper position, object position, relative position, linear/angular velocities, and environment-specific features (maze grid, rope cylinder positions). The higher-level policy outputs subgoal state vectors every c timesteps, and the lower-level primitive outputs 4-dimensional continuous actions (3D position offsets + gripper control) or 3D poke parameters (x, y, angle) for rope manipulation. A key unusual aspect is that the lower primitive's own Q-value function is queried during relabeling to determine which demonstration states constitute achievable subgoals, making the subgoal dataset D_g a function of the current policy rather than a fixed annotation.

## Method

PEAR operates in two phases. First, adaptive relabeling parses expert demonstrations by incrementally feeding demonstration states as candidate subgoals into the lower primitive's action-value function Q_{π^L}(s, s_i^e, a_i); when Q drops below a threshold Q_thresh, the preceding state is selected as the subgoal, and the process restarts from that state. This subgoal transition dataset D_g is periodically cleared and re-populated every p timesteps, generating a natural curriculum that tracks the lower primitive's improving capability. Second, both hierarchical levels are jointly optimized by combining an off-policy RL objective (Soft Actor-Critic) with an imitation-learning regularizer—either behavior cloning (L2 distance) or GAIL/LSGAN-based inverse RL—applied to D_g. A margin classification surrogate on Q_{π^L} prevents over-estimation on out-of-distribution states. The paper derives sub-optimality bounds showing that periodic re-population tightens the first term (dataset quality) while IL regularization tightens the second term (policy divergence), yielding a generalized minorize-maximize framework where any off-policy RL algorithm and any IL distance metric can be plugged in.

## Results

On six sparse-reward Mujoco environments (maze navigation, pick and place, bin, hollow, rope manipulation, and Franka kitchen), PEAR-IRL and PEAR-BC achieve up to 80% success rates, significantly outperforming all hierarchical and non-hierarchical baselines. In real-world robotic experiments, PEAR-IRL achieves success rates of 0.8 (pick and place), 0.6 (bin), and 0.3 (rope), while PEAR-BC achieves 0.8, 0, and 0.3 respectively. The next-best baseline (RPL) fails to achieve any success in the real-world tasks. PEAR also maintains consistently low predicted-vs-achieved subgoal distances throughout training, confirming effective mitigation of non-stationarity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PEAR outperforms RPL (Gupta et al., 2019) on all six simulation tasks, demonstrating that adaptive relabeling generates more feasible subgoals than RPL's fixed-window segmentation; in real-world tasks, RPL achieves 0% success while PEAR-IRL reaches up to 80%. Against HAC (Levy et al., 2018), PEAR consistently outperforms on all tasks except maze navigation where HAC is competitive, showing that HAC's assumption of an optimal lower primitive limits its generalization. RAPS (Dalal et al., 2021), which relies on hand-designed action primitives, fails on all tasks except maze navigation and is not even evaluated on rope manipulation due to the infeasibility of hand-designing primitives for deformable objects. Non-hierarchical baselines (DAC, flat SAC, BC) fail to achieve meaningful success on any of the six tasks, confirming the necessity of hierarchical temporal abstraction for these long-horizon sparse-reward problems. The hierarchical baselines HIER and HIER-NEG (which do not use expert demonstrations) also underperform PEAR, isolating the contribution of adaptive relabeling and primitive-enabled IL regularization beyond mere hierarchical structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

PEAR builds on the goal-conditioned feudal HRL framework (Dayan & Hinton, Vezhnevets et al.) and addresses the non-stationarity problem that plagues off-policy HRL, previously tackled by transition relabeling (HAC, Levy et al.) or fixed-window segmentation (RPL, Gupta et al.). The key departure is using the lower primitive's Q-function to adaptively select subgoals from expert demonstrations, creating a self-evolving curriculum rather than a static segmentation. This is combined with IL-based regularization (BC or GAIL/LSGAN) to jointly optimize with off-policy SAC, distinguishing it from RAPS (hand-designed primitives) and prior skill-prior approaches that depend on pre-trained policies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PEAR provides a practical, theoretically grounded recipe for mitigating non-stationarity in HRL using only a handful of expert demonstrations, achieving up to 80% success on sparse robotic tasks where baselines stall. The periodic re-population mechanism and sub-optimality bounds offer a principled understanding of why adaptive subgoal selection helps. Limitations include the requirement for directed expert demonstrations and the computational overhead of periodic re-population; open questions include generalization beyond the demonstrated task set, handling sub-optimal or noisy demonstrations, and scaling to even longer horizons or more complex multi-stage tasks.
