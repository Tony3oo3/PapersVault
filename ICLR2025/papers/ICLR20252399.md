## Problem

Traditional audio data augmentation methods (spectral masking, noise injection, time/pitch shifting) modify surface-level patterns without capturing the causal mechanisms that produce real-world audio variability, leading to high correlations between augmented samples and limited diversity. While text-to-audio (T2A) diffusion models offer a path to generating truly novel synthetic audio, two critical challenges remain: generated audio must be acoustically consistent with the target small-scale dataset to avoid distribution shift, and it must exhibit sufficient compositional diversity to prevent spurious correlation learning.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes a small-scale human-annotated audio dataset (n = 50 to 500 samples with category labels), a large-scale weakly-captioned audio-caption corpus (Sound-VECaps, ~1.5M pairs) for pre-training the T2A model, and GPT-4-Turbo for iterative caption generation and revision. It produces N synthetic audio samples per original sample (N tuned from 1 to 5 on validation), which are combined with the gold dataset to form the final training set for an Audio Spectrogram Transformer (AST) classifier. The T2A model generates 5-second audio clips from text prompts using a diffusion process with Text CFG of 7.0.

## Method

Synthio operates in two stages. First, it aligns a Stable Audio diffusion model (trained from scratch on Sound-VECaps) with the target dataset using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO): for each gold sample, j=2 audios generated from Gaussian noise under a template caption serve as 'losers' while the ground-truth audio serves as the 'winner', and the model is fine-tuned with the DPO-Diffusion loss to shift its generation distribution toward the target's acoustic characteristics. Second, it generates diverse augmentations via MixCap: GAMA captions all gold audios, an LLM extracts acoustic components (backgrounds, foreground events, attributes, relations), then the LLM is prompted to compose N new captions blending existing and novel components per label. Generated audios are filtered by a CLAP similarity threshold (p=0.85) against the target label, and rejected samples undergo iterative self-reflection where the LLM revises captions informed by accepted examples, repeating up to i iterations.

## Results

On 10 audio classification datasets across four low-resource settings (n = 50, 100, 200, 500 samples), Synthio consistently outperforms all baselines by 0.1%–39% in accuracy (or F1^macro for FSD50K). At n=100, Synthio achieves 83.35% on ESC-50, 85.00% on UrbanSound8K, 71.20% on GTZAN, and 16.35 F1^macro on FSD50K, compared to 56.75%, 72.89%, 64.15%, and 10.53% for the gold-only baseline. The largest relative gains appear on FSD50K (+94% at n=50) and DCASE Task 4 (+55% at n=100), while gains are smallest on Vocal Sounds (+4% at n=50). Performance improvements diminish as the number of gold samples increases, with the strongest absolute gains at n=100 and the weakest at n=500.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Synthio outperforms all three primary baselines across every dataset and sample size. Against SpecAugment (Park et al., 2019), Synthio gains up to 35.85 points (ESC-50 at n=100: 83.35 vs. 47.50) and up to 5.55 points on FSD50K (16.35 vs. 14.80); SpecAugment actually degrades performance on ESC-50 and NSynth relative to gold-only. Against Audiomentations (Jordal, 2021), Synthio leads by up to 34.85 points (ESC-50 at n=100: 83.35 vs. 48.50) and 2.80 points on FSD50K (16.35 vs. 13.55). Against the retrieval baseline inspired by Burg et al. (2023), Synthio achieves the largest margins, e.g., 30.90 points on ESC-50 (83.35 vs. 52.45) and 6.32 points on FSD50K (16.35 vs. 10.05) at n=100. The strongest generative baseline, Vanilla Synthetic Augmentation (template-prompted T2A without DPO or MixCap), trails Synthio by an average of 3.5% across all settings, with the gap widening on datasets where compositional diversity matters most (e.g., +16.10 on ESC-50, +3.72 on FSD50K at n=100). Ablations confirm that removing DPO causes the largest single drop (−4.5% average), while removing MixCap causes the second-largest (−2.7%), validating both components' contributions over the baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the synthetic data augmentation paradigm—well-established in vision (Trabucco et al., 2024) and NLP (Ghosh et al., 2023a)—to audio classification by leveraging text-to-audio diffusion models. It builds directly on the DPO-for-diffusion formulation of Wallace et al. (2024) and the T2A-specific preference alignment of Majumder et al. (2024, Tango 2), but repurposes DPO not for general generation quality but specifically to align a T2A model's acoustic output distribution with a small downstream dataset. The MixCap component, which uses LLM reasoning to iteratively generate diverse compositional captions with a self-reflection loop, departs from prior audio augmentation work (Ronchini et al., 2024) that relied on fixed template prompts, and from vision synthetic augmentation that typically does not require such explicit compositional diversity control.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a T2A model trained only on weakly-captioned AudioSet, when aligned via DPO and prompted with LLM-generated diverse captions, can outperform all traditional and generative augmentation baselines by up to 39% in extremely low-resource settings (n=50), with the DPO consistency step contributing more (4.5% avg. drop) than caption diversity (2.7% avg. drop). This suggests that acoustic distributional alignment is the primary bottleneck for synthetic audio augmentation, a finding that could guide future T2A-based augmentation pipelines. Key limitations include dependence on T2A model quality, potential LLM bias injection, and higher computational cost; open questions include whether the approach benefits as T2A models improve and whether the self-reflection loop generalizes to more complex compositional tasks like audio captioning (where the paper notes occasional caption-audio mismatches).
