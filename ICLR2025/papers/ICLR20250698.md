## Problem

Multi-agent reinforcement learning has produced superhuman planners in games but has had minimal impact on deployed autonomous driving systems, largely because the billions of environment steps these algorithms require are infeasible to generate with existing simulators. Current driving simulators either lack GPU acceleration or cannot scale to the hundreds of parallel worlds with dozens of agents per world that practical RL training demands, creating a throughput bottleneck that prevents systematic study of multi-agent driving at scale.

## Input / Output

The simulator consumes road geometry as polylines (not lane connectivity graphs), logged human trajectories from the Waymo Open Motion Dataset (100K+ scenarios with vehicles, cyclists, and pedestrians), and per-agent steering/acceleration actions. It produces configurable observations in three modes—radial filter (all objects within a fixed radius), GPU-accelerated 360° LIDAR ray casting, and a rotatable 120° view cone mimicking human vision—along with a binary goal-reaching reward and next environment states. Agents follow either a full Ackermann bicycle model with length-dependent dynamics or a simplified double-integrator model that is fully invertible for imitation learning but ignores vehicle length.

## Method

GPUDrive is built on the Madrona ECS game engine in C++, with observation, reward, and dynamics functions written directly in C++ and lowered to CUDA for GPU execution across hundreds of parallel worlds. Three key optimizations drive the 2.3M agent-steps-per-second peak throughput: a Bounding Volume Hierarchy reduces collision checking from quadratic to near-linear in agent count, the Visvalingam-Whyatt polyline decimation algorithm removes 10–15× redundant road points by approximating straight segments, and memory is allocated per actual agent count rather than the batch maximum, avoiding the padding overhead inherent in JAX-style frameworks. The simulator exposes a Gymnasium-compatible Python interface via nanobind for both PyTorch and JAX, and ships with pre-trained IPPO agents (trained via PufferLib) that achieve 95% goal-reaching across 1000 scenarios in roughly 15 hours on a single consumer GPU, processing 30 million steps per hour.

## Results

GPUDrive achieves a peak throughput of 2.3 million Agent Steps Per Second (ASPS) on a datacenter GPU, which is 2–3 orders of magnitude faster than Nocturne (15,000 ASPS) and surpasses Waymax, which hits out-of-memory limits beyond 16 parallel environments. In end-to-end RL training (IPPO via PufferLib), GPUDrive solves 10 Waymo scenarios to 95% goal-reaching in under 3 minutes, compared to approximately 10 hours in Nocturne — a 200–300× speedup. Scaling to 1,024 unique scenarios, the amortized per-scene cost drops to ~15 seconds, and the full 1,000-scene training run completes in 15 hours on a single consumer-grade GPU (RTX 4080). LiDAR-based observations run ~3× faster than the radial-filter observation due to GPU-accelerated ray casting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Nocturne (CPU-based, NeurIPS 2022), GPUDrive wins decisively on every speed metric: peak ASPS of 2.3M vs. 15K (≈150×), and end-to-end training time for 10 scenarios drops from ~10 hours to <3 minutes (200–300× speedup). Against Waymax (JAX-based, NeurIPS 2024), GPUDrive supports roughly 30× more parallel worlds before hitting memory limits (Waymax OOMs at 16 environments), and additionally provides LIDAR and human-like view-cone sensor simulation that Waymax lacks. However, Waymax ships with reactive IDM car-following agents out of the box, whereas GPUDrive provides RL-trained reactive agents instead — a different but complementary form of reactivity. GPUDrive also uses goal-point navigation rather than waypoint routes, which simplifies the task but removes fine-grained path-following evaluation.

## Relation to Prior Work

GPUDrive builds directly on the Madrona ECS game engine (Shacklett et al., 2023) for its GPU-parallel, many-world simulation architecture, and adopts the simplified invertible bicycle model from Waymax (Gulino et al., 2024) to support imitation learning. Conceptually, it departs from prior driving simulators by combining three previously separate capabilities: GPU-accelerated throughput (vs. CPU-based Nocturne or JAX-based Waymax), data-driven scenarios from the Waymo Open Motion Dataset, and diverse sensor modalities (LIDAR, view cone, radial filter) in a single framework. The paper positions the core gap as the sample-generation bottleneck that has prevented multi-agent RL algorithms—successful in games like Go and Diplomacy—from being applied to driving, where billions of steps are needed but existing simulators cannot produce them at sufficient rates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 200–300× training speedup over Nocturne and the sublinear per-scene cost (15 s at 1024 scenes vs. 2 min at 32 scenes) make multi-agent RL for driving feasible on a single consumer GPU, lowering the barrier for academic labs to study coordination, perception effects, and policy generalization in realistic traffic. Key limitations remain: the absence of a lane-level map, convex-only collision geometry, ~2% unreachable goals due to dataset labeling errors, and a 95% (not 100%) goal-reaching rate. Open questions include whether agents can be pushed to near-perfect safety, whether the sublinear scaling holds across the full 100K-scene WOMD, and how the simulator integrates with other datasets (NuPlan, NuScenes) to improve geographic and behavioral diversity.
