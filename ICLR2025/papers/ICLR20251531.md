## Problem

In highly dynamic environments where data distributions shift within a second or less, traditional predictive models and even online learning methods on GPUs cannot adapt quickly enough. While the BRIM electronic dynamical system enables ultra-fast inference via natural annealing, its training still relies on conventional digital processors, creating a critical bottleneck that prevents real-time model adaptation. This paper closes that gap by transforming the training process itself into a natural annealing process within the dynamical system.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251531/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a time-series snapshot s^t (100 samples per second on the evaluated datasets) and clamps it to the first N/2 nodes of the dynamical system, while the remaining N/2 nodes anneal freely to produce the predicted future state s^{t+1}. Physically, node values are represented as voltages on nanoscale capacitors, and Hamiltonian coupling parameters J and h are implemented as programmable resistor conductances, so the entire computation occurs as spontaneous charge redistribution at electron speed on the milliwatt scale.

## Method

InstaTrain formulates time-series prediction using a real-valued Hamiltonian (extending the binary Ising model with quadratic self-reaction terms that prevent nodes from diverging to ±∞), where the lowest-energy state under clamped inputs corresponds to the desired prediction. The key training innovation is Iterative Natural Annealing Training (INAT): rather than computing gradients on a GPU, the model's expectations ⟨σᵢσⱼ⟩_model are estimated by running the same natural annealing process used for inference, and parameters are updated via the difference between model and data expectations—effectively turning training into repeated annealing cycles. On the hardware side, the original BRIM circuit is augmented with analog multiplier modules and storage capacitors embedded in coupling units and nodes, enabling the J and h parameters to be updated in-circuit without any digital processor, so both training and inference execute on the same milliwatt-scale RC network at microsecond latency.

## Results

On five high-frequency time-series datasets (100 samples/sec), InstaTrain achieves the lowest MAE across all three evaluation scenarios. In the high-frequency update setting (1 update/sec), InstaTrain reduces MAE by 6.29% on average over the best online learning baselines (e.g., 6.79 vs. 6.93 on Carbon-Oxide, 0.68 vs. 0.77 on Stock). In static and low-frequency settings, InstaTrain matches or slightly beats NPGL (the prior dynamical-system method) while outperforming all GNN and Transformer baselines by 14.45% and 15.14% MAE respectively. Critically, InstaTrain achieves microsecond-level (10⁻⁴ s) update latency versus second-level for all GPU-based baselines, enabling the high-frequency update regime that no other method can reach.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GNN baselines (GraphWaveNet, MTGNN, MegaCRN) and Transformer baselines (Informer, DLinear, iTransformer), InstaTrain achieves the lowest or near-lowest MAE in both static and low-frequency update settings, with an average 14.45% MAE reduction in static and 15.14% in LF update. Against NPGL (the prior real-valued Ising-machine method), InstaTrain is essentially tied in static (13.88 vs. 13.90 on Carbon-Oxide) and LF update (8.28 vs. 8.25), but gains a clear edge in the high-frequency regime where NPGL cannot operate. In the high-frequency update scenario, InstaTrain outperforms all three online learning baselines—FSNet, online-adapted PatchTST, and OneNet—by 6.29% average MAE (e.g., 0.68 vs. 0.77 on Stock, 1.36 vs. 1.42 on Ammonia). The decisive advantage is latency: InstaTrain completes updates in ~10⁻⁴ s (microseconds) on the BRIM hardware, while all GPU-based baselines require seconds to minutes, making the 1-second high-frequency update schedule infeasible for every competitor. In terms of energy, InstaTrain consumes ~950 mW versus 250 W for the A100 GPU, yielding >10⁵× energy efficiency in training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the BRIM electronic dynamical system (Afoakwa et al., 2021) and its application in NPGL (Wu et al., 2024), which previously accelerated only the inference phase of graph-based time-series prediction while leaving training on conventional digital processors. InstaTrain's key departure is transforming the training process itself into an iterative natural annealing procedure within the same dynamical system, augmented with analog parameter-update modules, thereby unifying training and inference on a single hardware substrate. Conceptually, this aligns with Hinton's 'mortal computation' vision of tightly integrating algorithms with physical hardware, and it departs from the broader online-learning literature (FSNet, OneNet, PatchTST) by replacing digital gradient updates with physical energy-minimization dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that microsecond-level model updates are physically achievable on analog hardware, opening a path for real-time adaptation in domains where data distributions shift faster than any digital pipeline can respond (e.g., high-frequency trading, real-time sensor fusion). However, all hardware results are obtained via FEA simulation rather than fabricated silicon, and the approach is tightly coupled to the BRIM architecture, leaving open questions about scalability to larger node counts, fabrication yield, and generalization beyond the specific Hamiltonian form used. The modest accuracy gains over strong baselines (6–15% MAE reduction) suggest the primary value is in latency and energy rather than predictive quality, which may limit adoption in settings where accuracy is the dominant concern.
