## Problem

Modern benchmarks test multiple skills simultaneously, yet aggregate accuracy metrics obscure which specific abilities drive model performance differences. When frontier models like GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Pro, and Claude 3.5 Sonnet achieve overall accuracies within 0.4% of each other, it is unclear whether they are genuinely equivalent or whether valuable skill-level trade-offs are being averaged away. The paper addresses this by proposing an automatic method to recover the underlying skills relevant to any evaluation instance, enabling fine-grained, cross-benchmark skill analysis without costly manual annotation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes evaluation instances from 12 benchmarks (11 multimodal image+text, 1 language-only), totaling 46k instances, and produces per-instance skill annotations where each skill is expressed with multiple names of cascading granularity (e.g., 'Perception: Visual Recognition, Symbol Identification, Treble clef recognition') localized to a specific rationale step and concluding claim. These annotations are aggregated across benchmarks into skill-slices (sets of ≥100 instances sharing a skill), which then support downstream outputs: per-model skill-wise accuracy profiles, instance-level routing decisions (assigning each query to the strongest model on its relevant skills), and skill-based retrieval of custom evaluation sets from an open-vocabulary query skill.

## Method

The core mechanism prompts GPT-4o to generate a detailed step-by-step rationale for each evaluation instance, constraining each step to a single skill and requiring the model to list that skill under at least three names of cascading granularity (from coarse category to specific skill). Rationales are parsed to extract skills per instance, yielding 690k total skill annotations (128k unique) over 202k rationale steps. Skills are then de-duplicated via tight clustering (cosine similarity ≥0.95 on SFR-Embedding-2_R text embeddings) and aggregated across all 12 benchmarks to form skill-slices, increasing slice count by 51% compared to single-benchmark slicing. Skill relevance is validated through two automatic protocols: post-hoc verification (a second model marks each skill as relevant or not, with randomly sampled negative skills as quality controls, achieving 94.1% precision) and inter-annotator agreement across three different rationale-generating models. The key design choice is using rationales not to interpret the rationale-generating model but to annotate evaluation data, sidestepping rationale faithfulness concerns while making implicit skills explicit in solution steps.

## Results

Across 12 benchmarks (46k instances), GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Pro, and Claude 3.5 Sonnet achieve overall accuracies within 0.4% of one another, yet skill-slice analysis reveals per-skill accuracy gaps as large as 25% (e.g., Gemini 1.5 Pro is 18% more accurate in computing molar mass but 19% less accurate in applying constitutional law). Skill-based routing—assigning each instance to the model strongest on its relevant skills—yields a 3.2% average accuracy gain over the best single model across all 12 datasets, with improvements of 3.5–6.8% on MMLU Pro. The automatic skill extraction pipeline achieves 94.1% verified relevancy (95.7% by human validation), producing 278 skill-slices of ≥100 instances (332 after deduplication) from 128k unique skills across 690k total annotations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the skill-based retrieval experiment (Section 5), the paper's skill-annotation approach is compared against two prior slice-discovery methods: embedding-based retrieval (following Domino, Eyuboglu et al. 2022) and attribute-based retrieval (following PRIME, Rezaei et al. 2024). Skill annotations achieve substantially higher precision@20 than both baselines in two settings: when retrieving from a single other benchmark (MMTBench), skill-based retrieval improves precision by 18–45.5% over embedding baselines and 50.75–32.5% over attribute baselines; when retrieving from all other benchmarks combined, the same pattern holds. The attribute-based baseline (GPT-4o-inferred or ground-truth attributes) outperforms the embedding-based baseline (CLIP image, text, or combined embeddings), but both are consistently outperformed by skill annotations. The paper also compares rationale parsing against direct prompting for skill extraction (Appendix C.1), finding that rationale parsing yields 58% more skills per instance (9.98 vs. 6.32) and produces more medium-grained slices (491 vs. 288 skills with 100–250 relevant instances), while direct prompting produces more ultra-fine-grained skills with <10 instances (2,494 vs. 2,399).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the slice-discovery paradigm (Chung et al., 2019; Eyuboglu et al., 2022; Rezaei et al., 2024), which groups inputs by surface-level attributes for visual recognition tasks, to a new axis: underlying *skills* (how a model must operate) rather than observable attributes (what is being addressed). It repurposes chain-of-thought rationale generation (Wei et al., 2022; Kojima et al., 2022) not to analyze the rationale-generating model itself, but as an annotation tool to extract per-step skills from evaluation instances, then aggregates them across 12 benchmarks into cross-benchmark skill-slices. This contrasts with prior ability-tag annotations in individual benchmarks (Liu et al., 2023; Ying et al., 2024), which are manual, non-standardized, and restricted to a single benchmark, and with concurrent skill-inference work (Murahari et al., 2023; Didolkar et al., 2024) that operates in narrower domains.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Skill-Index dataset (46k instances, 128k unique skills, 278+ skill-slices of ≥100 instances) and the automated rationale-parsing pipeline provide a reusable, scalable resource for fine-grained model evaluation that goes beyond aggregate accuracy. The 3% routing gain and the strong cross-modality correlation (r≥0.79) between multimodal skill-slice accuracies and language-only MMLU Pro results suggest skill-level insights generalize broadly, opening the door to skill-based model selection and targeted training-data curation. Key limitations include reliance on GPT-4o for annotation (cost and potential bias), no guarantee of skill completeness, and the routing scheme's simplicity (no modeling of skill interactions). Open questions include whether the approach holds on substantially harder benchmarks where even the annotator struggles, and how to efficiently capture second-order effects of skill combinations.
