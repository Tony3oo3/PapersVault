## Problem

Sparse pre-training of LLMs—integrating pruning directly into pre-training—offers a path to smaller models without sacrificing quality, but the optimal configuration (when to start pruning, how long to prune, how much recovery to allow) was largely unknown due to prohibitive compute costs at LLM scale. Additionally, prior sparse scaling laws (e.g., Frantar et al., 2024) introduced sparsity-specific terms that departed from the established Chinchilla form, leaving practitioners without a unified framework to plan sparse training budgets.

## Input / Output

The method takes a dense LLaMA2 base architecture (58M–468M parameters, with only linear-layer parameters prunable; embeddings and normalizations remain dense), a target sparsity level (20%–80%), a total compute budget, and a token count (10× or 20× Chinchilla-optimal). It produces a sparsely pre-trained LLM with a fixed sparsity pattern and a predicted final evaluation loss via the modified scaling law. The scaling law itself takes average active parameter count N̄ and total training tokens D as inputs and outputs a predicted loss, retaining the exact Chinchilla functional form L = A/N̄^α + B/D^β + E.

## Method

The paper proposes replacing the fixed parameter count N in the Chinchilla scaling law with the time-averaged active parameter count N̄ = (1/T)ΣN_k over all pruning iterations, yielding a unified law that models both dense and sparse pre-training with identical functional form. Theoretically, a Taylor-series expansion of the per-iteration loss decay (L(C) = (A/C)^α) under the assumption that per-step compute is proportional to active parameters shows that total loss change is proportional to N̄, justifying the substitution. Empirically, the authors search over 80+ configurations of dense-training fraction, pruning duration, and recovery fraction, finding that allocating 25% of compute to dense training, 50% to iterative magnitude pruning (100 gradient steps per pruning iteration), and 25% to sparse recovery achieves near-optimal loss across all sparsity levels and training durations. They further show that the optimal learning rate and batch size for sparse pre-training match those of the equivalent dense model, and validate the scaling law on 30 data points (5 sparsity levels × 3 model sizes × 2 training durations) with a mean absolute prediction error of 0.016.

## Results

The proposed unified scaling law (replacing parameter count N with average parameter count N̄ in the Chinchilla law) predicts final evaluation loss with an average absolute error of 0.016 across 30 data points spanning 5 sparsity levels, 3 model sizes (58M–468M), and 2 training durations. The optimal sparse pre-training schedule allocates 25% of compute to dense training, 50% to iterative pruning, and 25% to sparse recovery, achieving near-optimal loss across all sparsity levels (20%–80%). For a target loss of 1.89 with 100T inference tokens, the sparse pre-training prescription (14B final model, 16.6T training tokens) achieves 5.58×10²⁴ lifetime FLOPs, a 31.4% reduction over Beyond Chinchilla. On downstream tasks, the 50%-sparse 1.14B model matches its dense counterpart (geomean accuracy 42.42% vs. 42.45%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to the Chinchilla scaling law (Hoffmann et al., 2024), the paper's sparse pre-training prescription achieves the same target loss (1.89) with a dramatically smaller final model (14B vs. 70B) and lower lifetime compute (5.58×10²⁴ vs. 15.8×10²⁴ FLOPs), though it uses more training tokens (16.6T vs. 4.26T). Against Beyond Chinchilla (Sardana et al., 2024), which also optimizes for lifetime compute, the sparse method wins on both data efficiency (16.6T vs. 24.4T tokens) and lifetime compute (5.58×10²⁴ vs. 8.14×10²⁴ FLOPs, a 31.4% saving). The advantage grows with increased inference traffic since the smaller final model (14B vs. 23.5B) reduces per-token inference cost. On downstream task evaluation (PIQA, ARC, Lambada, Winogrande), the sparse model essentially ties the matching dense model (geomean 42.42% vs. 42.45%), confirming no quality degradation from sparsity when average parameter counts are matched.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Chinchilla scaling law (Hoffmann et al., 2024) to sparse pre-training by replacing the fixed parameter count N with the average parameter count over training, showing the same functional form applies without the sparsity-specific terms introduced by Frantar et al. (2024). It departs from prior sparse scaling work by arguing that the core dense scaling principles remain valid under sparsity, requiring only a more nuanced notion of model size. The pruning methodology builds on iterative magnitude pruning (Zhu & Gupta, 2017; Frankle & Carbin, 2019), and the dynamic parameter schedule is positioned alongside progressive growth approaches (Yao et al., 2024; Yano et al., 2024), but inverts them by reducing rather than increasing model size during training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unification of sparse and dense scaling laws under a single equation provides practitioners with a familiar framework for planning sparse pre-training, and the 25%-50%-25% compute allocation prescription offers a concrete starting point. However, the study is limited to models up to ~468M parameters (1.14B in the appendix) on a single architecture and dataset, and the authors explicitly note they cannot demonstrate actual computational savings due to the absence of hardware support for unstructured sparsity. Open questions include whether the average-parameter-count scaling law holds at the 100B+ parameter scale, whether structured sparsity patterns alter the picture, and whether the 2× lossless compression rate at 80% sparsity generalizes beyond the explored regime.
