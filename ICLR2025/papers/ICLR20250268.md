## Problem

Existing pattern-matching tools (grep, KWIC concordancers) rely on exact surface-level string matching and cannot handle orthographic variations, paraphrasing, or morphological inflection—ubiquitous phenomena in natural language. Dense vector search, while semantic, is too coarse for n-gram-level queries, retrieving topically similar documents rather than specific pattern occurrences. No prior method achieves complete, semantically relaxed pattern matching that scales to billion-scale corpora in under a second.

## Input / Output

The method takes a tokenized phrase pattern p = p₁…pₙ, a cosine-similarity threshold α ∈ (0,1], and a preprocessed corpus with its inverted index (mapping each vocabulary word to its occurrence positions). It outputs the exhaustive set of corpus positions where every pattern word softly matches the corresponding corpus word (cosine similarity ≥ α), guaranteeing 100% recall for all qualifying matches rather than a top-k approximation. The output is a complete enumeration with position indices, from which matched text snippets with surrounding context can be extracted.

## Method

The core insight is to decouple expensive soft computation (cosine similarity over word embeddings) from the corpus: soft computation is performed only over the vocabulary (size L, independent of corpus size N), while hard computation (set intersection) is performed over the inverted index. Step 1 softens each query word pₖ into a set Sₖ of all vocabulary words with cosine similarity ≥ α to pₖ, requiring O(n×L) embedding comparisons. Step 2 then builds a soft inverted index by unioning the exact position lists for all words in each Sₖ, and finds matches via a shifting intersection across the n pattern positions. The use of an inverted index (rather than a suffix array) is critical, as its per-word entries can be merged to relax matching, whereas suffix arrays manage exact character sequences that resist softening. The overall search complexity is O(n×L + K) where K is the total candidate position count, making it sublinear in corpus size in practice.

## Results

On the 3.4B-word English Wikipedia corpus, SoftMatcha completes a full search in 0.098 seconds (without GPUs), with indexing time identical to exact matching (685.8 s) and roughly 4× faster than dense vector search (0.389 s search, 1036.5 s indexing). On the 1.1B-word Japanese Wikipedia, search takes 0.055 s. For the query 'homemade bombs', SoftMatcha retrieves 1,473 hits versus only 107 for exact matching, while dense vector search returns only top-k results without complete enumeration. On TREC-COVID, Soft-BM25 (α=0.55) improves over standard BM25 on all three metrics: P@20 41.5 vs 39.5, R@1000 23.5 vs 22.2, and NDCG@20 36.3 vs 34.6.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against dense vector search (intfloat/multilingual-e5-large with HNSW, Wang et al. 2024b), SoftMatcha wins on both speed and completeness: it is ~1.5× faster at indexing (685.8 s vs 1036.5 s on English) and ~4× faster at search (0.098 s vs 0.389 s), while also guaranteeing 100% recall of all matching positions rather than returning only a top-k subset. Qualitatively, dense vector search retrieved topically related but irrelevant articles (e.g., 'fireworks' for the Japanese query '手製爆弾'), whereas SoftMatcha returned all semantically matching n-gram instances. Against BM25 (Robertson & Zaragoza, 2009) on TREC-COVID, Soft-BM25 with α=0.55 outperforms standard BM25 on every reported metric—P@20 by 2.0 points (41.5 vs 39.5), R@1000 by 1.3 points (23.5 vs 22.2), and NDCG@20 by 1.7 points (36.3 vs 34.6)—demonstrating that semantic relaxation of term counting improves retrieval quality without sacrificing the sub-second search latency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a soft generalization of classical string matching based on inverted indexing (Zobel & Moffat, 2006), where the binary match/no-match criterion is relaxed to a continuous cosine-similarity threshold over word embeddings (GloVe, fastText). It explicitly distinguishes itself from approximate string matching (edit-distance-based tools like agrep), which relaxes surface-level comparison, and from dense vector search (HNSW/FAISS), which operates at the document level and is too coarse for n-gram-level queries. The key algorithmic insight is separating the expensive soft computation (cosine similarity over the vocabulary, O(n×L)) from the cheap hard computation (set intersections over precomputed inverted index positions, O(K)), achieving sublinear scaling with corpus size.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The tool addresses a practical gap: NLP researchers need to locate and remove harmful or privacy-violating instances in billion-scale training corpora, and corpus linguists need to retrieve examples across morphologically complex languages where exact matching fails. The sublinear search-time scaling and sub-second latency on 3.4B-word corpora (CPU-only) make it immediately deployable. Open questions include handling out-of-vocabulary words (currently ignored), extending to dynamic/contextualized embeddings, and supporting word omission, insertion, and reordering in queries. The threshold sensitivity (α < 0.2 becomes impractical) and the reliance on static embeddings limit the method's robustness for very diverse or low-resource text.
