## Problem

Existing methods for injecting external knowledge into LLMs—supervised fine-tuning, in-context learning, retrieval-augmented generation, and knowledge graphs—each suffer from critical limitations: high computational cost and overfitting (SFT), context-window constraints and the 'needle in a haystack' problem (ICL/RAG), or heavy manual construction effort (KGs). The paper addresses the need for a scalable, computationally efficient mechanism to compress large volumes of diverse offline data into a compact, interpretable form that can be seamlessly integrated into LLM prompts without fine-tuning or long-context retrieval.

## Input / Output

The framework accepts heterogeneous inputs—document text (relation extraction), sequential log event streams (anomaly detection), partial agent observations with action spaces (multi-agent decision-making), and numerical feature vectors (fraud detection)—and produces task-specific outputs: relation triples, binary anomaly labels, discrete action selections, or classification labels. Crucially, the raw data is never fed directly to the LLM; instead, it is first distilled into first-order logic rules (e.g., 'head_of_gov → citizen_of'), which are then translated into natural-language sentences and injected as prompt augmentations. The LLM operates in zero-shot mode, with no parameter updates, and the rule set is typically small (13–23 rules) regardless of training data size.

## Method

RuAG is a three-phase framework that automatically distills offline data into interpretable first-order logic rules and injects them into LLM prompts. Phase 1 (LLM-based formulation) leverages LLM commonsense to automatically define target predicates (e.g., a class label or game-win state) and body predicates (data attributes), while filtering irrelevant predicates and suggesting additional target predicates to reduce the combinatorial search space. Phase 2 applies Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) with UCT selection to efficiently navigate the predicate-combination space: states are partial rules, actions add candidate predicates, and rewards are rule precision on the training set, with terminal conditions triggered by maximum rule length or a precision threshold. Phase 3 cleans the collected rules (removing duplicates and subsumed rules), translates them into natural language, optionally retrieves the most relevant subset, and injects them into a prompt template for the LLM to perform downstream reasoning. The key distinction from RAG/ICL is that the injected knowledge is a small set of high-density symbolic rules rather than raw documents or demonstrations, and from HtT is that rule discovery is driven by structured MCTS search rather than iterative LLM generation and verification.

## Results

On document-level relation extraction (DWIE), RuAG achieves an F1 of 60.42%, outperforming the best LLM-based baseline HtT (52.59%) and all DL-based methods (best: BERT at 49.84%). On log-based anomaly detection (HDFS), RuAG reaches F1 92.59% with 100% precision, surpassing the strongest DL baseline LogRobust (87.31%) and RAG (84.32%). In the Alice & Bob cooperative game, RuAG achieves an average win rate of 0.7 and accumulative reward of 69.45, while all other LLM-based methods (Vanilla, ICL, RAG, HtT, PLLB) achieve a win rate of 0.0. On the private UPA fraud detection task, RuAG attains F1 96%, exceeding XGBoost (88.89%) and Rule Grounding (93.62%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RuAG outperforms all nine compared baselines across all four tasks. In relation extraction, RuAG (F1 60.42%) beats the DL-based baselines from DocRED (CNN 43.78%, BiLSTM 48.17%), BERT (49.84%), and Context-Aware (45.37%), as well as the LLM-based HtT (52.59%), with the largest gain in recall (53.48% vs. 42.80% for HtT). In log-based anomaly detection, RuAG (F1 92.59%, precision 100%) surpasses LogCluster (70.97%), DeepLog (79.64%), LogRobust (87.31%), LogGPT (72.56%), and HtT (58.73%), with the key advantage being perfect precision that eliminates false-positive misclassifications. In the Alice & Bob game, RuAG (AR 69.45, WR 0.7) outperforms the RL baselines (Behavior Cloning AR 54.67, Offline Tabular Q AR 59.51) and decisively beats all LLM-based methods including PLLB (AR −0.15, WR 0.0) and HtT (AR −0.26, WR 0.0), which fail to achieve any wins. The ablation on LLM backbones confirms that RuAG's gains are consistent across GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, with the rule-based guidance providing substantially larger improvements than chain-of-thought alone (e.g., +33.79 F1 points on relation extraction for GPT-3.5 vs. +0.91 for CoT).

## Relation to Prior Work

RuAG positions itself as a principled alternative to RAG, ICL, and knowledge-graph-based methods for injecting external knowledge into LLMs, arguing that all suffer from context-window limitations or scalability issues. Its core technical departure is repurposing Monte Carlo Tree Search (Swiechowski et al., 2023) — a game-AI algorithm — to systematically explore the combinatorial space of first-order logic rules, rather than relying on LLMs to generate and verify rules as in HtT (Zhu et al., 2023b) or PLLB (Srivastava et al., 2024). The LLM's role is deliberately narrowed to two tasks: formulating the search problem (defining target and body predicates via commonsense) and consuming the discovered rules at generation time, while MCTS handles the expensive combinatorial search. This division of labor distinguishes RuAG from prior logic-rule learning work (Qu & Tang, 2019; Cheng et al., 2022) that required manual predicate specification and domain expertise.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a computationally cheap, interpretable middle ground between fine-tuning and retrieval: a handful of human-readable logic rules (20–23 in the experiments) can close most of the gap between vanilla LLMs and domain-expert performance across NLP, time-series, decision-making, and numerical classification tasks. However, the evaluation is limited to relatively small or synthetic datasets (700 training documents, 20k log blocks, 1000 game episodes), and the method requires data to be discretizable into Boolean predicates, which constrains applicability to continuous or high-dimensional settings. Open questions include whether the approach scales to rule spaces with hundreds of predicates, whether more expressive logical forms (e.g., quantifiers, negation) would help, and whether the MCTS formulation generalizes beyond the four task families tested.
