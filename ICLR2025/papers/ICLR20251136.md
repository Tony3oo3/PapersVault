## Problem

Existing program-generation-based math reasoning methods (PAL, TIR-ToRA) attempt to solve an entire problem in a single code block, which fails on Olympiad-level problems that require numerous elaborate intermediate steps, constraint handling, and edge-case management. These monolithic approaches lack the granularity to systematically address each sub-step, correct individual errors without restarting, or dynamically discover the next critical sub-task based on accumulated intermediate results.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251136/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a math competition problem in LaTeX format (from AIME, AMC-12, MathOdyssey, or OlympiadBench) and produces a multi-turn trajectory of (sub-task description, Python program, code execution output) tuples, terminated by a final integer answer. Each subsequent program directly incorporates previous execution outputs as defined variables, creating a chain of dependent code snippets. The framework uses stop words ('###END OF CODE' and 'output') to delimit sub-task boundaries and signal termination, with a maximum of 15 turns (averaging 6–7 per problem).

## Method

SBSC is a multi-turn, code-generation-based prompting strategy where at each turn the LLM generates an intermediate sub-task and a corresponding Python program to solve it, conditioned on the question, system prompt, 4-shot exemplars, and all prior (sub-task, code, output) tuples. A code interpreter executes each program, and the resulting output is concatenated into the context for the next turn, enabling the model to dynamically define the next sub-task based on accumulated intermediate results. Unlike PAL or TIR-ToRA, which produce one monolithic program (or re-attempt the entire program on error), SBSC decomposes the problem into a sequence of focused sub-tasks, each independently solvable and correctable. This granular structure allows step-level debugging (fixing a single erroneous step without restarting from scratch), explicit intermediate validation, and dynamic discovery of the next sub-task. The approach relies entirely on in-context learning with carefully designed exemplars (including natural-language comments in code and a specific instruction to correct the current step rather than restart), with no fine-tuning or training involved.

## Results

On Claude-3.5-Sonnet, SBSC (greedy decoding) achieves 49.33% on AMC, 35.45% on AIME, 39.86% on MathOdyssey, and 53.31% on OlympiadBench, surpassing the best prior method (TIR-ToRA or PAL) by 10.7%, 8%, 12.6%, and 5.6% respectively. SBSC's greedy decoding also outperforms self-consistency (maj@7) decoding of the best baseline by 6.2% on AMC, 6.7% on AIME, and 7.4% on MathOdyssey. On GPT-4o, SBSC shows similar gains of 7.2% (AMC), 6.1% (AIME), and 7% (MathOdyssey) over the best greedy baseline. On the pass@k metric for last 3 years AIME, SBSC at pass@25 solves 52/90 questions, surpassing o1-mini (44) by 9% and o1-preview (39) by 13%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SBSC consistently outperforms all three prompting baselines (COT, PAL, TIR-ToRA) across all four main datasets and both LLMs (Claude-3.5-Sonnet and GPT-4o). Against TIR-ToRA (the strongest single-program baseline), SBSC gains 10.7% on AMC, 8% on AIME, and 12.6% on MathOdyssey with Claude-3.5-Sonnet, and 7.2%, 6.1%, and 7% with GPT-4o. Against PAL, the nearest competitor on AIME, SBSC leads by 8% (Claude) and 6.1% (GPT-4o) in greedy mode. Notably, SBSC's single greedy-decoding chain surpasses the self-consistency (maj@7) results of TIR-ToRA by 6.2% (AMC), 6.7% (AIME), and 7.4% (MathOdyssey) with Claude-3.5-Sonnet, indicating that the step-by-step structure is more effective than sampling multiple single-program attempts. In the pass@k comparison against o1 on last 3 years AIME, SBSC at pass@25 (52/90) surpasses o1-mini (44/90) by 9% and o1-preview (39/90) by 13%, though SBSC lags behind o1 on Geometry specifically (9 vs. 13 at pass@25 vs. o1-mini).

## Relation to Prior Work

SBSC extends the program-generation-based reasoning lineage (PAL, POT, TIR-ToRA) by replacing the single-code-block paradigm with a multi-turn, sub-task-decomposed approach where each turn generates a focused program that directly incorporates execution outputs from prior turns as variables. Conceptually, it departs from TIR-ToRA's 'solve the whole problem in one program, retry on error' strategy by enforcing granular, sequential exploration where intermediate sub-tasks are discovered dynamically based on accumulated knowledge. The framework also inherits exemplar design practices from PAL (meaningful variable names, natural-language comments) and stop-word-based tool invocation from ToRA, positioning itself as a prompting-level refinement rather than a new training paradigm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that granular step-by-step program generation meaningfully outperforms single-block approaches on Olympiad-level math, with the notable finding that SBSC's greedy decoding surpasses self-consistency (maj@7) of existing methods—suggesting that structured decomposition can substitute for the compute cost of multi-sample voting. However, the 3–7× per-question cost increase and persistent weakness on geometry (spatial/diagram understanding) limit immediate practical adoption. Open questions include whether SBSC trajectories can serve as training data for step-wise preference optimization or guided decoding (as the authors suggest), and whether the approach generalizes beyond the two frontier LLMs tested (Claude-3.5-Sonnet, GPT-4o) to smaller or open-source models without the same accuracy gains.
