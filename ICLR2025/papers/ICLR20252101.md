## Problem

Traditional algorithmic fairness research focuses on third-person institutional decision-making tasks (e.g., resume screening, loan approval), but chatbots are primarily used for open-ended, first-person tasks like writing stories, debugging code, or career advice. The open-ended nature and diverse use cases of chatbots make standard fairness metrics inadequate, and no scalable methodology existed for evaluating demographic biases in real-world chatbot interactions at scale.

## Input / Output

The method takes real user prompts (first messages from LMSYS and WildChat datasets) and counterfactually swaps the stored user name (a demographic signal embedded via Memory or Custom Instructions) to generate paired responses. It produces a harmful-stereotype score in [-1, 1] per prompt (the difference between forward and reverse stereotype rates), a curated list of natural-language 'axes of difference' with statistical significance, and nine-dimensional response quality ratings. The key design choice is that the LMRA is deliberately hidden which response corresponds to which demographic group, and each pair is evaluated twice with order swapped to mitigate position bias.

## Method

The core contribution is a scalable counterfactual evaluation framework using a Language Model as Research Assistant (LMRA, GPT-4o) to assess first-person fairness across six models, 66 tasks in 9 domains, and 2 genders × 4 races. For harmful-stereotype detection, the LMRA classifies response pairs using a template that hides group identities (addressing an observed over-sensitivity when groups are revealed), and the final score is computed as the probability difference between forward and reverse stereotype ratings, with order bias handled via token-level probability symmetrization (r_A ∝ min(p_A, q_B)). For bias enumeration, a four-step algorithm (Feature Brainstorming → Consolidation → Labeling → Bonferroni-corrected Feature Selection) identifies statistically significant natural-language axes of difference. The approach is validated against a diverse Prolific crowd (40 raters per pair, 48 countries), showing strong correlation for gender (ρ=0.86) but weaker agreement for racial stereotypes, and demonstrates that post-training RL significantly reduces measured biases.

## Results

Harmful gender stereotype rates for GPT-4o-mini are all a fraction of 1% on average across nine domains, with the 'Write a Story' task exhibiting the highest bias and GPT-3.5-turbo showing the greatest overall harm rate. The LMRA (GPT-4o) achieves a Pearson correlation of ρ=0.86 (90% sign agreement) with diverse human raters for gender stereotypes, but weaker correlations for racial stereotypes (e.g., ρ=0.67 for Black-White, ρ=0.34 for Hispanic-White). No statistically significant differences in response quality (accuracy, clarity, etc.) were found across gender or race groups. Post-training RL dramatically reduces harmful gender stereotypes, with a best-fit slope of 0.21 (95% CI: 0.17–0.24) when comparing pre-RL to post-RL models across 19 tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external bias-evaluation baselines; instead, it validates its LMRA methodology by comparing multiple LMRA models against human crowd ratings (Table 1). GPT-4o as LMRA achieves the highest gender correlation (ρ=0.86, 90% agreement), marginally outperforming Llama 3.1 70B (ρ=0.84) and Claude 3.5 Sonnet (ρ=0.85), while substantially outperforming smaller models like Llama 3.1 8B (ρ=0.26) and Claude 3.5 Haiku (ρ=0.72). For racial stereotypes, GPT-4o leads on Black-White (ρ=0.67, 74% agreement) but trails Llama 3.1 405B on Hispanic-White (ρ=0.69 vs. 0.34), indicating no single LMRA dominates across all demographic axes. Across chatbot models, GPT-3.5-turbo exhibits the highest harmful stereotype rates, while o1-preview and o1-mini show the lowest, suggesting that newer reasoning-oriented models are less biased. The before/after RL comparison shows that post-training reduces harmful gender stereotypes by roughly 79% on average (slope 0.21), with all 19 evaluated tasks falling below the y=x line, confirming consistent mitigation across domains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of LLM fairness research, which has focused on 'third-person' institutional decision-making tasks (resume screening, loan approval, sentencing) using synthetic prompts or prompt templates. It introduces 'first-person fairness'—evaluating bias toward the chatbot user based on demographic signals like stored names—and demonstrates via embedding analysis that decision-making prompts are 99.7% separable from real chat prompts, arguing the two settings require distinct evaluation methods. Methodologically, it builds on LLM-as-judge approaches (Perez et al., 2023), counterfactual name-based metrics (Tamkin et al., 2023; Dwivedi-Yu et al., 2024; Nghiem et al., 2024), and text-distribution difference description (Zhong et al., 2022; Findeis et al., 2024), but repurposes them for open-ended, real-world chat at scale via the LMRA framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first large-scale fairness evaluation grounded in real-world chat data (millions of interactions across 66 tasks and 9 domains), providing a practical, scalable methodology for ongoing bias monitoring in deployed chatbot systems. Key limitations include the imperfect proxy of name counterfactuals for first-person bias (it cannot capture differences in user writing style or topic selection across groups), the LMRA's substantially weaker agreement with human raters for racial bias compared to gender, and the exclusion of multimodal interactions. Open questions include improving LMRA calibration beyond gender, extending the framework to direct demographic profiles and intersectional groups, and whether the very small observed bias rates (fractions of 1%) become meaningful at the scale of billions of interactions.
