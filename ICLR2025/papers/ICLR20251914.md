## Problem

Health time-series from wearable accelerometers have not benefited from the foundation model paradigm that has transformed vision, language, and speech. Prior self-supervised contrastive methods for motion data rely on hard binary positive/negative labels, which fail to capture the graded, relative similarities inherent in real-world motion (e.g., walking is closer to running than to yoga), and lack invariance to sensor-specific transformations like orientation changes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251914/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes 2.56-second windows of raw, unfiltered 100 Hz 3-axis (x, y, z) accelerometry from a wrist-worn IMU sensor, producing a 256-dimensional embedding via a 3.9M-parameter ResNet-34 backbone with global average pooling. Pre-training uses 1 billion segments from 87,376 participants in the Apple Heart and Movement Study. Downstream, frozen embeddings feed lightweight linear or MLP probes that output activity class labels (16 in-the-wild classes) or continuous gait metrics (stride velocity, double support time).

## Method

RelCon has two core components. First, a learnable motif-based distance measure (cross-attention with sparsemax over dilated convolutional embeddings, plus reversible instance normalization) is trained with accelerometry-specific augmentations (3D rotation, noise, etc.) so it captures semantic motif similarity while being invariant to sensor orientation and position. Second, a relative contrastive loss iteratively designates each candidate in a pool as the positive and defines the negative set as all candidates with greater learned distance from the anchor, thereby preserving the full relative ranking rather than a single binary split. Candidates are drawn from both within-user (across time, capturing fatigue and temporal dynamics) and between-user (within batch, capturing inter-subject physiological differences) sources. This design mitigates false positives and negatives by encoding graded similarity, and the ablations show it outperforms both harder binary contrastive losses and softer metric losses that over-constrain the embedding space.

## Results

On the AHMS Field HAR task (16 classes), RelCon achieves 38.56 F1 at the subsequence level and 55.28 F1 at the workout level, outperforming SimCLR (36.35/50.06), AugPred (34.48/54.63), and REBAR (36.39/50.29). For gait metric regression, RelCon attains the best correlation for Double Support Time (0.7559) and competitive stride velocity correlation (0.8431), winning 8 out of 10 evaluation metrics. Against the prior large-scale pre-trained model (Yuan et al., 2024), RelCon's frozen 256-dim embedding achieves 69.1 F1 on Opportunity and 85.4 F1 on PAMAP2 with an MLP probe, versus 57.0 and 72.5 for the 1024-dim Yuan model. In the broader SSL benchmark (Table 4), RelCon achieves the best F1 on HHAR (57.63) and PAMAP2-leg (53.98), though SimCLR edges it on Motionsense (83.93 vs. 80.35).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RelCon outperforms SimCLR on workout-level HAR (55.28 vs. 50.06 F1) and gait DST correlation (0.7559 vs. 0.7074), though SimCLR is competitive on stride velocity correlation (0.8454 vs. 0.8431) and wins on Motionsense (83.93 vs. 80.35 F1). Against AugPred, RelCon dominates at the subsequence level (38.56 vs. 34.48 F1) and on all gait metrics, while AugPred is slightly ahead at the workout level (54.63 vs. 55.28 F1 is actually RelCon winning). REBAR, which shares the same learnable distance but uses a hard binary contrastive loss, is consistently outperformed by RelCon (e.g., 0.7559 vs. 0.6817 DST correlation, 55.28 vs. 50.29 workout F1), confirming the benefit of the relative loss. Against Yuan et al.'s large-scale FM (700k person-days, 10M params, 1024-dim), RelCon (3.9M params, 256-dim) achieves substantially higher MLP-probe F1 on both Opportunity (69.1 vs. 57.0) and PAMAP2 (85.4 vs. 72.5), and also wins with fine-tuning (98.4/98.8 vs. 59.5/78.9). In the Haresamudram benchmark, RelCon beats SimSiam, BYOL, MAE, CPC, and Autoencoder on all three datasets (HHAR, Motionsense, PAMAP2), with the only loss being to SimCLR on Motionsense (80.35 vs. 83.93 F1). RelCon also surpasses the fully-supervised Conv classifier on HHAR (57.63 vs. 55.43 F1) when sensor positions match (Wrist→Wrist).

## Relation to Prior Work

RelCon builds directly on REBAR's (Xu et al., 2024) motif-based reconstruction distance measure, extending it with three key modifications: acceleration-specific augmentations (from Accel SimCLR, Tang et al., 2020) to learn sensor-orientation invariance, sparsemax (Martins & Astudillo, 2016) to enforce precise motif matching, and reversible instance normalization (Kim et al., 2021) to preserve relative magnitude information. The central conceptual departure is the relative contrastive loss, which replaces REBAR's hard binary positive/negative assignment with a graded ranking where every candidate is 'positive' to a degree determined by the learned distance, iteratively applying NT-Xent with dynamically redefined negative sets. This positions RelCon as the first foundation model for accelerometry, extending the foundation model paradigm from vision, language, and speech to wearable motion data, while also generalizing beyond prior accelerometry SSL work that focused exclusively on HAR.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work demonstrates that a single self-supervised representation for raw accelerometry can generalize across fundamentally different task types (classification of 16 in-the-wild activities and regression of temporally-precise gait metrics), which prior accelerometry SSL methods had not achieved. The relative contrastive loss offers a principled middle ground between hard contrastive losses (sensitive to false positives/negatives) and metric losses (over-constraining the embedding space), a finding that could inform contrastive learning design more broadly. Limitations include reliance on a single sensor modality (3-axis accelerometer), the computational cost of evaluating the distance function for every candidate during training (O(c·T²·d)), and evaluation limited to wrist/ankle/waist positions. Open questions include whether the approach extends to multi-modal IMU data, other quasi-periodic biosignals (PPG, ECG), and whether the relative loss generalizes beyond motif-based time-series.
