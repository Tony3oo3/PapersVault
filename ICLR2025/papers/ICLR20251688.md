## Problem

Asynchronous distributed optimization algorithms typically either require a priori knowledge of the maximum information delay (which is hard to determine and leads to overly conservative step sizes) or rely on simple models of the objective that use only the most recent gradient from each worker. This paper asks whether a more accurate, bundle-based approximation of the objective can be used in an asynchronous setting to achieve faster practical convergence without requiring delay bounds.

## Input / Output

The method operates in a parameter server architecture where n workers asynchronously provide function values and gradients of their local smooth convex losses f_i. The central server maintains a bundle of m most recent (function value, gradient) pairs per worker, requiring O(mnd) memory, and solves a low-dimensional dual subproblem over a Cartesian product of n probability simplices of dimension m. The output is the optimal decision vector x* minimizing F(x) = Σf_i(x) + R(x), where R is a possibly non-smooth convex regularizer (e.g., ℓ1 norm). Smoothness parameters M_i are estimated adaptively from consecutive gradient pairs, eliminating the need for manual tuning.

## Method

The core mechanism is an asynchronous bundle method: the server maintains a disaggregated piecewise linear model (upper envelope of m linear cuts) for each worker's loss, and computes the next iterate by solving a master problem that minimizes the sum of these models plus the regularizer plus a quadratic proximity term centered at a weighted average of the most recent worker points. The master problem is solved via its Lagrange dual, a low-dimensional QP over a product of probability simplices, using an accelerated projected gradient method with a user-specified tolerance δ that controls the inexactness of the subproblem solution. Unlike prior asynchronous proximal gradient methods (DAve-RPG, PIAG) that use only the latest gradient per worker, the bundle incorporates historical information, yielding a more accurate descent direction. The method requires no knowledge of the maximum delay, and convergence rates are proven for both deterministic (O(1/k) sublinear, linear under quadratic growth) and stochastic mini-batch settings, with the neighborhood size depending explicitly on δ, gradient noise, and function-value noise.

## Results

ABM (Asynchronous Bundle Method) consistently outperforms DAve-RPG and PIAG with delay-tracking across all four datasets (MNIST8M, RCV1, Epsilon, SVHN) in terms of relative suboptimality versus runtime. The advantage is most dramatic on the sparse RCV1 dataset, where in 300 seconds ABM's central server receives only ~4,000 gradients versus over 70,000 for both baselines in the same time. On dense datasets (MNIST8M, Epsilon), all methods receive roughly the same number of gradients per unit time, but ABM still converges faster in wall-clock time. The method requires minimal tuning: bundle size m=10 and tolerance δ=10⁻⁷ work well across all experiments without per-dataset adjustment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ABM is compared against two asynchronous proximal gradient methods that share the same architectural constraints (parameter server, no maximum delay knowledge required, explicit regularizer handling): DAve-RPG (Mishchenko et al., 2018) and PIAG with delay-tracking (Wu et al., 2022). ABM wins on all four datasets in wall-clock time to reach a given suboptimality level, with the margin being largest on the sparse RCV1 dataset where server-side subproblem solving becomes a non-negligible fraction of total time. On dense datasets (MNIST8M, Epsilon), the gradient-count gap narrows (all methods receive similar numbers of gradients per second), but ABM still converges faster in absolute time due to its more accurate piecewise-linear model requiring fewer iterations. In the stochastic mini-batch setting on MNIST8M multiclass, ABMStoch matches or slightly exceeds ABM when only modest accuracy is needed, and both ABM variants are barely affected by simulated worker delays (uniform [2t_grad, 4t_grad]) while a tuned synchronous ProxSGD baseline degrades significantly. Theoretically, all three methods achieve O(1/k) sublinear rates under general convexity, though DAve-RPG has a slightly tighter linear convergence constant under strong convexity-like assumptions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model-based optimization framework of Nesterov & Florea (2021) and Asi & Duchi (2019)—which showed that more accurate objective approximations improve synchronous algorithms—to the asynchronous distributed setting. It departs from existing asynchronous proximal gradient methods (DAve-RPG, PIAG with delay-tracking) that maintain only the most recent gradient per worker, instead constructing piecewise linear bundle models with m cuts per worker. The method also generalizes classical bundle methods (Makelá, 2002; Hiriart-Urruty & Lemarechal, 1993) from non-smooth optimization to a smooth-plus-regularizer composite structure in a parameter-server architecture, yielding stronger convergence guarantees than prior asynchronous bundle methods that assumed non-smoothness in the finite-sum part.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that ABM requires no knowledge of the maximum information delay and needs only two hyperparameters (bundle size m and tolerance δ) that are insensitive to tuning, making it substantially easier to deploy than delay-bounded asynchronous methods. The theoretical rates are comparable to DAve-RPG and PIAG (O(1/k) convex, linear under growth), so the contribution is primarily in practical robustness and the conceptual insight that richer models help in asynchronous settings. Open questions include whether dynamic bundle sizing, low-rank Hessian approximations, or extensions to non-convex objectives could further improve performance, and whether the n-dependence in the stochastic noise terms can be eliminated by reformulating the objective.
