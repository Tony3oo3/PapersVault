## Problem

Neural scaling laws are empirically well-established but theoretically understood only for linear and random feature models, leaving the feature learning regime largely unexplained. A critical missing piece is how the power-law structure of real-world data covariance spectra shapes learning dynamics—specifically the plateau behavior and the transition from exponential to power-law convergence—in two-layer networks where pre-activation weights are trainable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253210/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes Gaussian input patterns with covariance matrices having L distinct eigenvalues following a power-law λ_l = λ_+/l^(1+β), a two-layer student-teacher committee machine (K student, M teacher hidden units), and a learning rate η. It produces closed-form expressions for the generalization error ε_g(α) as a function of training time, the power-law scaling exponent β/(1+β), an analytical plateau length formula depending on L and M, and the asymptotic convergence rate. The key structural constraint is that the covariance matrix must have exactly L distinct eigenvalues (each with multiplicity N/L) to enable closure of the differential equation hierarchy via the Cayley-Hamilton theorem.

## Method

The paper derives hierarchical differential equations for higher-order order parameters (Q, R, T matrices of size L) governing one-pass SGD in the thermodynamic limit, then closes the system using the Cayley-Hamilton theorem applied to the covariance matrix's minimal polynomial. For linear activations, the system reduces to a companion-matrix structure whose eigenvalues are the negative data eigenvalues, yielding the exact solution ε_g ∝ α^(-β/(1+β)) via Euler-Maclaurin approximation and incomplete gamma functions, with explicit upper and lower time bounds for the power-law regime. For non-linear (erf) activations, the authors linearize around plateau fixed points and asymptotic fixed points separately, deriving an escape-time formula τ_esc ∼ M²/(ηL) that explains why increasing L shortens plateaus, and showing that the weighted sum of exponentials over L eigenvalues produces a power-law tail ε_g ∝ α^(-β/(1+β)) in the specialized phase. A separate feature-scaling analysis shows the asymptotic error scales as N_l^(-β) with the number of learned eigendirections, validated on CIFAR-5m with an estimated β ≈ 0.3.

## Results

For linear activation functions, the generalization error exhibits power-law scaling ε_g ∝ α^(−β/(1+β)) within the time window 1/(2ηλ₊) < α < L^(1+β)/(2ηλ₊)·(β/(10(1+β))·Γ(β/(1+β)))^((1+β)/β), where β is the power-law exponent of the data covariance spectrum. The asymptotic generalization error scales as ε_g,asymp ∼ 1/N_l^β with the number of learned feature directions N_l. For non-linear activations in the realizable case (K=M), the plateau escape time scales as τ_esc ∼ M²/(ηL), and the convergence to perfect learning transitions from exponential to power-law decay when the covariance matrix has a power-law spectrum. On CIFAR-5m, the estimated spectral exponent is β ≈ 0.3, and the theoretical prediction from Eq. (11) matches simulation results averaged over 50 random initializations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper, so comparisons are against prior analytical results rather than empirical baselines. The derived time-scaling exponent ε_g ∝ α^(−β/(1+β)) matches the prediction of Bordelon & Pehlevan (2022) for random feature models and Lin et al. (2024) for linear regression, but this work extends it to the feature-learning regime where all pre-activation weights are trainable, a setting not covered by those prior analyses. The feature-scaling result ε_g,asymp ∼ 1/N_l^β is consistent with Maloney et al. (2022) for random feature models, but here the exponent for dataset size (parameterized by α) differs from that for the number of features, a distinction not present in the random feature setting. Compared to Yoshida & Okada (2019), who derived closed-form ODEs for covariance matrices with at most two distinct eigenvalues, this work generalizes the hierarchy to L distinct eigenvalues and derives explicit scaling laws for the plateau length and asymptotic convergence. The plateau analysis extends Biehl et al. (1996) by showing that the escape time depends inversely on L, explaining the empirically observed shortening of plateaus with increasing spectral complexity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the statistical mechanics framework for online learning in two-layer networks (Saad & Solla 1995; Yoshida & Okada 2019) from isotropic or few-eigenvalue data to general power-law spectra with L distinct eigenvalues, using the Cayley-Hamilton theorem to close the hierarchy of order-parameter differential equations. It also departs from the dominant scaling-law literature (Bordelon & Pehlevan 2022; Lin et al. 2024; Maloney et al. 2022), which has focused on random feature models or linear regression, by analyzing the feature learning regime where all pre-activation weights are trainable. The plateau analysis builds directly on Biehl et al. (1996), adapting their escape-time formula to the power-law spectrum setting and deriving how the plateau length scales with L and M.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first analytical derivation of how data covariance structure (specifically power-law spectra) shapes the full learning trajectory—plateau, convergence rate, and asymptotic scaling—in a feature-learning neural network, offering theoretical grounding for empirically observed scaling laws. The finding that convergence transitions from exponential to power-law (ε_g ∝ α^{-β/(1+β)}) when the data spectrum is power-law connects the data structure directly to the scaling exponent, a link previously only established for random feature models. Limitations include the restriction to two-layer networks, one-pass SGD, and small learning rate approximations for key results; open questions include whether these scaling exponents persist in deeper architectures, with trainable output weights, or under different optimization schedules, and how the ReLU vs. erf distinction (noted in the limitations) affects the broader picture.
