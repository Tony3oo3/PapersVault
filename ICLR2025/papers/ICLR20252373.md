## Problem

Data attribution methods are increasingly used to measure the value of training data and compensate data providers, yet their adversarial robustness has received little to no systematic study. This paper is the first to define a formal threat model for adversarial manipulation of data attribution and to propose principled attack methods that can inflate a data provider's compensation share.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252373/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The adversary starts with a small set of real data points (e.g., 100 images out of 11,000 total training points, or 20 text sequences out of 6,274) and produces perturbed versions with imperceptible modifications (pixel-level perturbations bounded by ε=0.01–0.1 for images, or character substitutions for text). The Shadow Attack additionally requires access to the data distribution and training algorithm to construct shadow models, while the Outlier Attack only requires black-box queries to the target model's predictions. The output is a manipulated dataset whose data-attribution-based compensation share is inflated by 185%–643% relative to the original.

## Method

The paper proposes two complementary attacks. The Shadow Attack (gray-box) trains m shadow models on data sampled from the same distribution as the target training set, then maximizes a surrogate Grad-Dot-based objective via gradient ascent to find perturbations that inflate attribution scores on the shadow models. The Outlier Attack (black-box) exploits the inductive bias that outlier data points are more influential in data attribution methods; it generates realistic outliers by applying off-the-shelf black-box adversarial attacks (ZOO, Simba, or TextFooler) that perturb input features while keeping labels fixed, thereby increasing model loss. A key theoretical result (Theorem 5.1) shows that perturbing a single training point changes all influence scores by only O(1/n), guaranteeing that the adversarial effect observed on the original model transfers to the retrained model at the next contribution iteration.

## Results

The Shadow Attack inflates data-attribution-based compensation by 206.3%–456.1% across four image classification settings (MNIST/LR, Digits/MLP, MNIST/CNN, CIFAR-10/ResNet-18), while the Outlier Attack achieves 185.2%–643.9% inflation across all five settings including text generation (Shakespeare/NanoGPT). On the strongest setting (MNIST with Logistic Regression), the Outlier Attack using ZOO reaches a 643.9% compensation ratio, and the Shadow Attack reaches 456.1%. A random perturbation baseline with matched perturbation budget produces virtually no change in compensation share (e.g., 0.0098 → 0.0097), confirming the gains come from principled adversarial design rather than noise.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed black-box attacks against two types of baselines. First, a random perturbation baseline with matched perturbation budget (Table 4) shows that random noise produces negligible compensation change (e.g., 0.0098 → 0.0097 on setting (a)), whereas the Outlier Attack achieves a 643.9% ratio on the same setting—demonstrating that the gains come from structured adversarial design, not arbitrary perturbation. Second, white-box oracle attacks (FGSM for setting (a), PGD for setting (d)) serve as upper-bound references: FGSM achieves 662.2% vs. the black-box ZOO's 643.9% on setting (a) (a gap of only ~19 percentage points), and PGD achieves 233.7% vs. Simba's 185.2% on setting (d) (a gap of ~48 points). Thus, the proposed black-box methods recover most of the white-box oracle's effectiveness while requiring only model prediction queries, making them far more practical in realistic threat scenarios.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes two well-established adversarial ML techniques—shadow training from membership inference attacks (Shokri et al., 2017) and black-box adversarial example generation (Chen et al., 2017; Guo et al., 2019)—to target a new and previously unexplored vulnerability: data attribution methods used for data valuation and compensation. The Shadow Attack directly adapts the shadow-model paradigm, while the Outlier Attack exploits the Grad-Dot surrogate (Charpiat et al., 2019) and the inherent outlier inductive bias of influence-function-based attribution (Koh & Liang, 2017). Prior work by Deng & Ma (2023) only briefly tested heuristic manipulations like data duplication; this paper is the first to formalize a threat model and develop principled, theoretically grounded attack methods against data attribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As data attribution methods are increasingly proposed for financial decisions—data provider compensation and AI copyright royalties—demonstrating that imperceptible perturbations can inflate compensation by 185%–643% reveals a critical structural vulnerability. The Outlier Attack's success across multiple attribution methods (Influence Function, TRAK, Data Shapley) suggests the outlier inductive bias is a fundamental weakness rather than an implementation artifact, yet the paper proposes no defenses. Open questions include whether robust data attribution methods can be designed that resist outlier exploitation, whether the periodic-contribution threat model (Assumption 1) is necessary or whether one-shot attacks are also feasible, and how these findings scale to production-scale generative AI systems where data distribution knowledge is infeasible.
