## Problem

LLMs frequently fail to follow even simple, unambiguous instructions, yet the internal mechanisms underlying these failures remain unexplored. While linear probing has been applied to study truthfulness and hallucination in LLMs, no prior work has investigated whether models encode a separable signal in their representations that predicts instruction-following success or failure, or what that signal corresponds to semantically.

## Input / Output

The method consumes text prompts structured as a task (e.g., 'write a resume') paired with a single verifiable instruction (e.g., 'do not include keywords X, Y, Z'), and extracts hidden-state representations at three token positions (first, middle, last) across three layer groups (early, middle, last) from four LLMs. The primary output is a unit direction vector in the input embedding space (the instruction-following dimension) obtained from a linear probe trained on binary success/failure labels, along with AUROC scores quantifying how well that direction separates outcomes. A secondary output is a modified representation (R + α·D) used for representation engineering, evaluated by instruction-following success rate and GPT-4-scored task quality ratio.

## Method

The core technique is linear probing on LLM hidden states, but the key design choice is the IFEval-simple dataset, which pairs 5 instruction types with the same 100 tasks so that the probe captures instruction-following signal free of task-specific confounds. A simple linear classifier (AdamW, 1000 epochs, lr=0.001, weight decay 0.1) is trained on binary success/failure labels, and its weight vector defines the instruction-following direction. Generalization is tested two ways: a 70-30 task split (probes generalize well, AUROC 0.74–0.88) and leave-one-out over instruction types (probes fail, AUROC ≈ 0.50–0.55), revealing that the dimension is task-general but instruction-type-specific. To validate causality, representation engineering shifts first-token representations in the last layer along the identified direction (R_updated = R_original + α·D), improving success rates over random directions while preserving response quality. A sensitivity analysis perturbs task familiarity, instruction difficulty, and prompt phrasing, then computes cosine similarity between each perturbation-induced difference vector and the instruction-following direction, finding that phrasing changes align most strongly with the dimension.

## Results

Linear probes on the first token achieve AUROC scores of 0.74–0.88 for task generalization (unseen tasks, same instruction type), confirming a shared instruction-following geometry across tasks. In contrast, instruction-type generalization AUROC scores are 0.50–0.55, near chance, indicating no 'global' instruction-following dimension across instruction types. Representation engineering along the identified direction improves instruction-following success rate (e.g., Mistral-7B: 0.58→0.64; LLaMA-2-13B: 0.61→0.65) while maintaining or improving quality ratio, outperforming random-direction modifications. The first token is as informative as the last token (both ~0.7–0.88 AUROC), suggesting LLMs 'know' before generating whether they will follow instructions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external instruction-following methods; instead, it benchmarks the identified instruction-following direction against random directions in representation engineering (Table 4). Across all four models, the instruction-following direction consistently outperforms random directions in success rate: e.g., LLaMA-2-13B achieves 0.65 SR vs. 0.54 random, Mistral-7B achieves 0.64 vs. 0.56, and Phi-3 achieves 0.74 vs. 0.63. Quality ratio is preserved or improved with the instruction-following direction (e.g., Mistral-7B QR: 0.95→0.98) while random directions often degrade quality (Mistral-7B QR drops to 0.86). For generalization, the key comparison is task vs. instruction-type: task generalization AUROC (0.74–0.88) far exceeds instruction-type generalization (0.50–0.55), which is at chance level. Expanding from 5 to 23 instruction types (IFEval-simple-extended) does not improve instruction-type generalization, confirming the absence of a universal instruction-following direction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the linear-probing methodology previously applied to truthfulness and hallucination detection in LLMs (Azaria & Mitchell, 2023; Marks & Tegmark, 2023) to the distinct behavioral axis of instruction-following, and similarly extends representation engineering (Zou et al., 2023) from steering truthfulness to steering instruction adherence. The key methodological departure is the IFEval-simple dataset design, which pairs each instruction type with an identical set of 100 tasks to disentangle instruction-following signals from task-specific representations—a control that prior probing work on truthfulness did not require. The paper also builds on the IFEval benchmark (Zhou et al., 2023) for objective, verifiable instruction evaluation, deliberately avoiding LLM-based evaluators to reduce confounding noise.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a single linear dimension in input embeddings predicts instruction-following success—and that it generalizes across tasks but not across instruction types—suggests there is no unified 'instruction-following' signal in LLMs, which has direct implications for building reliable AI agents that must follow diverse constraint types. The sensitivity analysis showing the dimension tracks prompt phrasing rather than task difficulty or instruction complexity offers a mechanistic explanation for why prompt engineering works and why LLMs fail on clear instructions, pointing toward robustness-focused fine-tuning as a practical remedy. Open questions include whether the dimension generalizes to larger or architecturally different models, whether more sophisticated probing (beyond linear) reveals a richer structure, and how to make the input embedding space less brittle to surface-level phrasing changes.
