## Problem

Existing neural combinatorial optimization (NCO) methods require large training datasets, demanding substantial storage and computational resources, while also struggling to maintain performance under distribution shifts between training and test data. This paper addresses both challenges simultaneously by introducing a Wasserstein-based coreset technique that compresses the training set while preserving dataset diversity, thereby reducing resource costs and improving robustness to distribution shifts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252640/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a dataset of CO instances (e.g., TSP point sets or graph-structured problems) as input, where each instance is first mapped to a probability measure in Euclidean space via graph embedding. It produces a small weighted coreset (a subset of the original instances with weights proportional to their covering ball sizes) along with a hierarchical tree structure. At inference, a new test instance is aligned to the nearest coreset center via a rigid transformation found by traversing the tree, and the aligned instance is then fed to the trained NCO model to produce a solution (tour, independent set, etc.).

## Method

The core contribution is a coreset construction algorithm inspired by hierarchical Gonzalez's k-center clustering, where CO instances are modeled as probability measures and their pairwise differences are quantified by a novel metric—Wasserstein distance under rigid transformations (RWD)—that accounts for the invariance of CO problems to translation, rotation, and reflection. The algorithm recursively partitions the dataset into balls of radius r using Gonzalez's algorithm at each tree level, yielding a tree whose node centers form the coreset. To handle very large datasets, the construction is adapted to a merge-and-reduce framework enabling parallel computation with time complexity independent of dataset size. A theoretical guarantee (Theorem 3.3) proves the coreset is an ε-coreset under L-Lipschitz loss and low doubling dimension assumptions. During training, the full dataset is replaced by the coreset with uniform weights to maximize diversity; during inference, test instances are aligned to the tree via a heuristic RWD-based search (Algorithm 4) before being decoded by the backbone NCO solver (DIFUSCO).

## Results

On TSP100-2D with a 4003-sample training set (vs. 128000 full dataset), the coreset method (CS) achieves a tour length of 80.63 under N(0,4²) distribution shift, compared to 101.95 for uniform sampling (US) — a 21% improvement. At 12951 samples, CS reaches 77.80 vs. US's 80.78 on the same shifted distribution. On the TSPLIB benchmark with 12951 samples, CS-aligned achieves 133.14 tour length (Greedy) vs. US's 162.19, a 17.9% reduction. The coreset also reduces inference time: CS-aligned cuts Greedy decoding time from ~378s to ~359s at 4003 samples, and from ~1012s to ~680s on TSP500.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed coreset method (CS) and its aligned variant (CS-aligned) against two baselines: the full-dataset model (Org, 128000 samples) and uniform sampling (US) at matched sample sizes. CS consistently outperforms US on all distribution-shifted test sets, with the gap widening as sample size shrinks: at 4003 samples, CS reduces tour length by 21% under N(0,4²) (80.63 vs. 101.95) and 21% under U(0,10) (94.73 vs. 119.78) compared to US. On the TSPLIB benchmark (Table 3), CS at 12951 samples achieves 133.63 Greedy length vs. US's 162.19, a 17.6% improvement, while CS-aligned further narrows the gap to the full-dataset baseline (133.14 vs. 129.35). CS-aligned additionally reduces inference time by ~5–30% over CS (e.g., 359s vs. 372s on TSP100, 680s vs. 1012s on TSP500), with the time savings growing for larger problem sizes and higher-dimensional (3D) instances. The coreset method approaches full-dataset performance (Org) with only 3–10% of the training data, demonstrating that the hierarchical Gonzalez-based diversity capture is the key driver of robustness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) coreset construction for data compression, building on the merge-and-reduce framework of Har-Peled & Mazumdar (2004) and hierarchical Gonzalez's algorithm; (2) optimal transport / Wasserstein distance as a geometric metric, extended here with a rigid-transformation invariance (RWD) tailored to CO instances; and (3) neural combinatorial optimization (NCO) methods such as DIFUSCO, attention-based solvers, and reinforcement learning approaches. The key departure from prior coreset work (which targets clustering, regression, or general optimization) is the application to NCO training, where the coreset must preserve the diversity of CO instances under a metric that respects the problem's geometric symmetries. The alignment step at inference, leveraging the hierarchical tree structure, has no direct analogue in prior coreset literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for resource-constrained settings: training on a ~4,000-sample coreset instead of 128,000 instances reduces training time by roughly 85% while matching or exceeding full-data performance, particularly under distribution shift. The counterintuitive finding that structured diversity-preserving compression *improves* robustness (rather than degrading it) challenges the common assumption that more data is always better for generalization. However, the evidence is currently limited to a single NCO solver (DIFUSCO) and primarily TSP; whether the approach transfers to other solvers, higher-dimensional problems, or non-Euclidean CO structures remains open. The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 3.3) requires a Lipschitz loss assumption that may not hold for all NCO architectures, and the doubling dimension parameter, while set to 1 in experiments, lacks a principled selection rule.
