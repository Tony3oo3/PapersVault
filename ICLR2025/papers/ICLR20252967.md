## Problem

State Space Models (SSMs) are widely regarded as effective at capturing long-sequence dependencies, yet their actual long-range modeling capability has not been rigorously characterized. This paper identifies two fundamental, intertwined bottlenecks: an inherent recency bias that causes SSMs to exponentially forget distant tokens, and an over-smoothing effect in deep architectures that renders token representations indistinguishable. These two issues create a dilemma—increasing depth to extend the receptive field simultaneously accelerates over-smoothing—hindering the scalability of existing SSMs.

## Input / Output

The paper analyzes SSMs operating on three input types: text token sequences (for causal language modeling and Needle-in-a-Haystack retrieval), flattened pixel sequences of length 1024 (for CIFAR-10 image classification with a learnable class token appended), and key-value pair sequences (for associative recall). Outputs include next-token predictions, class labels, and retrieved values. A key analytical output is the influential score |∂y_t/∂x_s|, which quantifies how much the s-th input token affects the t-th output, and the pairwise memory-state distance that measures over-smoothing across layers.

## Method

The paper first proves (Theorem 3.1) that for any SSM with diagonal A_t ∈ (0,1)^{N×N}, the influential score between tokens decays exponentially with their relative distance at a rate κ = Θ(log(A_max^{-1})), establishing recency bias as an intrinsic property that persists even with Mamba's input-dependent selection mechanism. It then proves (Proposition 4.1, Theorem 4.2) that SSMs are inherently low-pass filters and that memory-state pairwise distances shrink by a factor (1 − A_min^{T−1}) per layer, causing over-smoothing in deep stacks. To simultaneously address both bottlenecks, the paper proposes polarization: fixing the first diagonal entry of A_t to 1 (preserving complete history to counter recency) and the last diagonal entry to 0 (preventing mixing with past tokens to slow smoothing), while leaving all other channels freely learnable. Experiments on associative recall show that polarization with 4 layers achieves 93.43% average accuracy across 64–256 key-value pairs, compared to 71.61% for the default 4-layer Mamba, and that the technique unlocks further gains from deeper architectures that would otherwise degrade due to over-smoothing.

## Results

On the associative recall task, the full polarization technique (setting one channel of A_t to 1 and another to 0) with 4 layers achieves 93.43% average accuracy across 64/128/256 key-value pairs, compared to 71.61% for the default 4-layer Mamba baseline — a 21.8-point improvement. The one-polarized channel alone (A_{1,1}=1) lifts 2-layer accuracy from 72.06% to 83.63%, while the zero-polarized channel (A_{N,N}=0) enables deeper architectures to benefit (81.38% at 4 layers vs. 71.61% default). On CIFAR-10 adversarial robustness, Mamba suffers an 81.24% accuracy drop from corrupting the last 32/1024 tokens, versus only 7.81% for the Transformer baseline, confirming the recency-bias vulnerability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the Needle in a Haystack benchmark, Mamba-Codestral-7B (SSM) shows strong positional bias — retrieval accuracy drops sharply when the needle is placed near the beginning of the document — while Mistral-7B (Transformer) maintains stable accuracy across all needle positions, demonstrating that the recency bias is specific to SSMs. In adversarial attack experiments on CIFAR-10, Mamba is far more vulnerable than the Transformer baseline: corrupting the trailing 32 tokens causes an 81.24% accuracy drop for Mamba versus 7.81% for the Transformer, while leading-token corruption has minimal impact on Mamba (2.30%) but substantially hurts the Transformer (22.95%). Among SSM baselines, RWKV shows similar trailing-token vulnerability (68.35% drop) while H3 is more robust (13.04% drop), suggesting the selection mechanism in Mamba amplifies the recency bias. In the over-smoothing analysis (Figure 5), Pythia (Transformer) exhibits a much slower decay of feature sharpness across layers compared to Mamba, confirming that SSMs over-smooth faster than Transformers at equivalent depth. The polarization technique outperforms all default Mamba configurations on associative recall, with the combined one-and-zero polarization at 4 layers (93.43% avg) surpassing even the best single-channel variant (83.63%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Mamba and S4 state space model formulations, extending their theoretical analysis to reveal two fundamental limitations: inherent recency bias (exponentially decaying token influence) and over-smoothing in deep architectures. It departs from the prevailing narrative that SSMs' selection mechanisms (Mamba) or HiPPO-based initialization overcome long-range dependency challenges, showing instead that the constraint A_t ∈ (0,1) imposes an irreducible locality bias regardless of input-dependent parameterization. The over-smoothing analysis draws a direct parallel to the well-studied phenomenon in GNNs and transformers, while the unified SSM/LAM framework (following Dao & Gu, 2024) positions the findings as applicable across the entire family of structured sequence models including RetNet, GLA, RWKV, and Griffin.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work fundamentally reframes how the community should interpret SSMs' long-range capabilities, showing that validation perplexity can mask severe degradation in retrieval and robustness. The polarization technique—fixing two state channels to 0 and 1—is an extremely simple modification with broad applicability, and its effectiveness in unlocking depth scaling suggests a practical path forward for SSM-based LLMs. The security implications (SSMs' vulnerability to trailing-token jailbreaks that fine-tuning cannot fix) raise urgent concerns for deployed SSM-based language models. Open questions include whether polarization generalizes to non-diagonal or complex-valued state matrices, how it interacts with hybrid architectures (e.g., Jamba), and what the joint scaling laws for depth and context length look like.
