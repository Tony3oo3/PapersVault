## Problem

Multiplicative logit adjustment (MLA) is a simple post-hoc heuristic for long-tailed recognition that empirically outperforms alternatives, yet no prior work provided a theoretical explanation for why scaling logits by class frequency is effective. The paper asks: can MLA be justified as an approximation of an optimal decision boundary adjustment derived from first principles?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253520/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates entirely post-hoc on a trained feature map (ResNet34, ResNeXt50, or MLP) with a fixed ETF classifier, requiring no retraining. It consumes the per-class training sample counts n_k and a single hyperparameter γ× to multiplicatively scale each class logit by n_k^{-γ×}, producing adjusted logits that yield the final class label. Because the adjustment is applied at inference time, hyperparameter tuning is inexpensive and does not require retraining the backbone.

## Method

The paper derives the optimal pairwise decision boundary angle θ*_{k,k'} by maximizing the angular bound probability, which quantifies how tightly test features concentrate around their class mean under neural collapse (NC). Using Rademacher complexity to bound feature spread as Õ(1/√n_k), the optimal angle is shown to be proportional to ‖μ(S_k')‖√n_k' / (‖μ(S_k)‖√n_k + ‖μ(S_k')‖√n_k'), where μ(S_k) is the empirical class-mean feature. The key theoretical result proves that MLA with γ× = 1/2 approximates this optimal 1vs1adjuster in the large-K limit, by showing the MLA factor ratio κ_k/κ_k' ≈ 1/τ_{k,k'} matches tan(θ*_{k,k'}) via the rational approximation tan(πθ/2) ≈ θ/(1−θ). The ETF classifier assumption (fixed equiangular tight frame weights) ensures equal weight norms across classes, enabling a clean angular interpretation of the logit scaling. Experiments on CIFAR-10/100-LT, ImageNet-LT, and the tabular Helena dataset confirm the approximation holds even when NC is not fully realized and training accuracy is below 100%.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative finding is that MLA achieves test accuracy comparable to the theoretically optimal 1vs1adjuster across all four datasets (CIFAR-10-LT, CIFAR-100-LT, ImageNet-LT, Helena), as shown in Figure 3. On CIFAR-100-LT (100 classes), the decision boundary angle differences between MLA and 1vs1adjuster are globally small (Figure 2), confirming the theoretical approximation. Notably, this equivalence holds even under non-ideal conditions: on ImageNet-LT (86.6% training accuracy) and Helena (42.1% training accuracy), where neural collapse is not fully realized, MLA still matches 1vs1adjuster. The optimal MLA hyperparameter γ×* ranges from 0.20 (ImageNet-LT) to 0.78 (CIFAR-10-LT), with values near 0.5–0.8 on datasets where NC is well-established.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares MLA against two baselines: the theoretically optimal 1vs1adjuster (derived from the paper's own NC-based theory) and ALA (Menon et al., 2020). Against 1vs1adjuster, MLA achieves consistently comparable test accuracy across all datasets, with decision boundary angles that are globally close on high-class-count datasets (CIFAR-100-LT, ImageNet-LT) but diverge on CIFAR-10-LT (10 classes) where the ψ ≈ π/2 approximation breaks down. Against ALA, MLA outperforms in several settings: ALA achieves lower accuracy than 1vs1adjuster in some cases, while MLA maintains parity with 1vs1adjuster even under unfavorable conditions (small K, low training accuracy). The paper also notes that ALA's decision boundaries depend on individual feature norms ‖f(x)‖, making it fundamentally inconsistent with the norm-independent optimal boundaries derived in Proposition 3, whereas MLA's multiplicative scaling preserves angular structure. The original MLA formulation (Kim & Kim, 2020) is the method being theoretically justified here, with the paper providing the missing theoretical grounding for its empirical success.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides a theoretical justification for Multiplicative Logit Adjustment (MLA), a heuristic post-hoc method introduced by Kim & Kim (2020), by connecting it to the neural collapse (NC) framework of Papyan et al. (2020) and the test-sample feature spread analysis of Galanti et al. (2021). It departs from the Fisher-consistency justification underlying Additive Logit Adjustment (ALA, Menon et al., 2020) by showing that ALA's decision boundaries depend on individual feature norms, making it inconsistent with the NC-derived optimal boundary. The ETF classifier of Yang et al. (2022b) serves as the structural assumption that enables the NC-based analysis, and the Rademacher complexity machinery from standard generalization theory is repurposed to quantify angular feature spread as a function of class sample size.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work gives practitioners a principled understanding of when and why the simple MLA heuristic works, including concrete hyperparameter guidance (γ× ≈ 0.5 under full NC, lower when NC is incomplete). The finding that MLA approximates the optimal 1-vs-1 boundary adjustment even under non-ideal conditions (42% training accuracy on Helena, 86.6% on ImageNet-LT) suggests the theory is robust beyond its strict assumptions, which is practically valuable for real-world deployment. Open questions include a deeper theoretical analysis of ALA under finite-sample conditions, and whether combining MLA with other LTR techniques (contrastive learning, vision-language models) can push toward state-of-the-art accuracy.
