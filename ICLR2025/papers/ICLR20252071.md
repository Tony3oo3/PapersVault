## Problem

LLMs suffer from reasoning hallucinations (applying invalid rules or misinterpreting conclusions in multi-step chains) and laziness (defaulting to 'I don't know' even for solvable problems). Existing hallucination-mitigation methods target factual errors via retrieval or post-hoc patching, neglecting compounding reasoning errors, while refusal-training approaches like R-Tuning make models overly conservative. The paper asks how to estimate an LLM's reasoning capacity boundary and align its assertive/conservative behaviour to that boundary.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252071/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a reasoning problem statement (logical clauses with rules, a math word problem, or a blocksworld planning instance) and produces either a full chain-of-thought answer ending in a final result, or a calibrated refusal response ('Sorry, I am not sure if I answer the question correctly...') after a sufficient number of reasoning steps. The output is structured so that the reasoning trajectory length itself serves as the signal for whether the model should commit to an answer or abstain, with the refusal threshold automatically tuned per task.

## Method

AUTO-CEI wraps Expert Iteration (resampling trajectories by reward and SFT on the resampled set) inside an automatic curriculum that hill-climbs a single threshold c1 in a sigmoid-shaped refusal reward. The reward gives +1 for correct answers, a sigmoid compensation (ranging from small negative to ~+0.9) for refusals whose reasoning length exceeds c1, and −1 for assertively wrong answers. After each EI convergence, the curriculum shifts c1 upward to encourage longer reasoning before abstaining, optimising the objective f = (1−λ)·Precision + λ·(1−RefusalRate) until a local optimum is reached. Initialisation uses R-Tuning-style SFT to seed a policy with a ~25% refusal distribution, and the entire pipeline runs on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct with LoRA (r=128, α=64), sampling 16 trajectories per query at temperature 1.0.

## Results

AUTO-CEI achieves the highest objective function value f (λ=0.2) on BoardgameQA (0.817) and MATH (0.575), and ties with EI+R-Tuning on Blocksworld (0.896). It attains the best precision among all methods on BoardgameQA (84.52%) and MATH (55.63%), while maintaining a moderate refusal rate of 18–36% across the three benchmarks. On Blocksworld, AUTO-CEI reaches 91.53% precision with only 18.30% refusal rate, outperforming SFT+R-Tuning (90.69% precision, 57.51% refusal) and RLKF-DPO (38.08% precision, 8.21% refusal). Overall, AUTO-CEI boosts precision by 10–24% over the strongest refusal-aware baselines while keeping refusal rates in a practical range.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SFT+R-Tuning, AUTO-CEI wins on all three benchmarks in both precision and objective function f: on BoardgameQA it improves precision from 80.36% to 84.52% (f: 0.780→0.817), on MATH from 60.67% to 55.63% precision while drastically cutting the refusal rate from 67.88% to 36.08% (f: 0.550→0.575), and on Blocksworld it raises accuracy from 38.54% to 74.78% while halving the refusal rate from 57.51% to 18.30%. Against EI+R-Tuning, AUTO-CEI achieves higher f on BoardgameQA (0.817 vs 0.789) and MATH (0.575 vs 0.552), and a lower refusal rate on Blocksworld (18.30% vs 27.71%) at a small precision cost (91.53% vs 93.95%), tying on Blocksworld f (0.896). Against RLKF-DPO, AUTO-CEI dominates across every metric on every benchmark: e.g., on Blocksworld it achieves 91.53% precision vs 38.08% and f=0.896 vs 0.486, while on MATH it reaches 55.63% precision vs 42.19% with a more balanced refusal rate (36.08% vs 5.14%). The paper attributes RLKF's poor performance to low reward accuracy on long reasoning chains and its greedy reward shaping that over-asserts, compounding errors on unseen contexts.

## Relation to Prior Work

AUTO-CEI builds directly on Expert Iteration (Anthony et al., 2017; Havrilla et al., 2024) as its core training loop and on R-Tuning (Zhang et al., 2023) as its initialization strategy for refusal behavior. It departs from prior hallucination-mitigation work (RAG, RLKF/DPO) that targets factual errors or uses fixed reward signals, and from prior RL-for-reasoning work (PPO, vanilla EI) that only maximizes reasoning accuracy without modeling the model's capability boundary. The key conceptual departure is wrapping EI in an automatic curriculum that uses reasoning-step length as a difficulty proxy and hill-climbs a precision-vs-refusal objective, thereby jointly improving reasoning robustness and calibrating when the model should abstain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical deployment concern—LLMs that are either over-confident (hallucinating) or over-conservative (lazy)—by providing an automated procedure that estimates and pushes the model's reasoning boundary without manual reward tuning. Limitations include reliance on an 8B backbone, the assumption that reasoning-step length reliably proxies difficulty (validated only on MATH's human labels with moderate correlation), and the computational cost of multiple EI iterations. Open questions include whether the step-length assumption generalizes to open-ended or multi-modal reasoning, whether the method scales to larger models where compounding errors behave differently, and how to handle the observed issue of the model refusing even correct answers.
