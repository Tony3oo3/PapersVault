## Problem

When training large-scale models, understanding how changes in training data distribution affect predictions is infeasible due to compute costs, so practitioners rely on small proxy models to extrapolate. However, there is no precise characterization of when proxy models faithfully capture the data-influence behavior of larger models, since model behavior can shift across scale. This paper asks: how does training data distribution influence model behavior across compute scale, and at what proxy scale does this relationship break down?

## Input / Output

The study trains language models (MPT architecture, 37M–760M parameters) and vision models (ResNet-18 with width factors 1/16 to 8) on 10 distinct training distributions (6 natural sources and 4 algorithm-induced selections), with each model trained at Chinchilla-optimal token-to-parameter ratios so that the compute gap between proxy and reference reaches up to 370×. For the downstream attribution application, the input is a large pool of training examples (80M for LM, 50K–1.2M for vision) and a set of target test examples, and the output is a per-target attribution score vector (datamodel weights) computed via TRAK from the proxy model. For dataset selection, the output is a subset of training examples (sized by Chinchilla-optimal ratio) chosen by taking the bottom-n entries of the averaged datamodel vector across target examples.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is an empirical characterization: it trains matched families of small and large models on identical training distributions and measures the Spearman correlation of their losses on six downstream test sets, revealing that correlation remains high (typically >0.9) even at 175× compute gaps but degrades for the smallest proxies (~370×) on specific tasks like SQuAD and TriviaQA. For downstream applications, the authors use a modified TRAK estimator (dropping the Q diagonal matrix to avoid softmax saturation) to compute datamodels from proxy models, then evaluate attribution quality via the Linear Datamodeling Score (LDS) and dataset selection quality via downstream accuracy of the large model trained on the proxy-selected subset. A key finding is that proxy models remain effective for attribution and selection even when their own accuracy on a task is at random-guessing level, and that the most helpful/detrimental training examples identified by small-model datamodels overlap substantially with those from the large model.

## Results

Across 10 training data distributions, 57M-parameter proxy models (trained with 175× less compute than the 760M reference) show high loss correlation with the reference model on 4 of 6 test distributions (C4, The Pile, LAMBADA, HellaSwag), with weaker correlation on SQuAD and TriviaQA. In the data attribution setting (ImageNet), the Linear Datamodeling Score (LDS) decreases by at most 10% relative (from 0.21 to 0.19) even for proxy models 1,000× smaller than the reference; on CIFAR-10, LDS degrades only after proxies exceed 1,000× smaller. In dataset selection, DsDM-selected data improves large-model accuracy over random selection regardless of proxy size, with no measurable drop until the proxy uses 4× less compute than the reference model. Notably, proxy models maintain high correlation with the reference even when their own accuracy is at random-guessing level (e.g., HellaSwag, COPA).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the key 'comparisons' are across proxy model scales relative to the 760M reference model. At 175× less compute (57M params), proxy losses highly correlate with the reference across most test distributions. At 370× less compute (40M params), correlation remains strong on 4/6 tasks but breaks down on SQuAD and TriviaQA. In the vision attribution setting, ResNet proxies spanning 10⁴× (ImageNet) and 10⁵× (CIFAR-10) width ranges all yield LDS within ~10% of the full-size model, with the smallest proxies (1/16 width) showing the first meaningful degradation. For dataset selection, proxy models from 125M to 760M parameters all produce DsDM selections that beat the random-subset baseline on both SQuAD and LAMBADA, with the 125M proxy (4× less compute) being the smallest scale at which the improvement is still maintained. The paper identifies that only the very smallest proxies (370×+ smaller) reliably fail to predict large-model behavior, establishing a practical lower bound for proxy model size.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the datamodels framework (Ilyas et al., 2022) and the TRAK estimator (Park et al., 2023) for efficient data attribution, as well as DsDM (Engstrom et al., 2024) for model-aware dataset selection. It connects to the μP line of work (Yang et al., 2022) on cross-scale transferability and to recent arguments that 'emergent' abilities are metric artifacts (Schaeffer et al., 2023), both of which suggest model behavior is more predictable across scale than previously assumed. The paper's specific contribution is empirical: it systematically characterizes *when* and *how well* small proxy models track large-model sensitivity to data distribution changes, filling a gap the authors identify as absent from prior proxy-model practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that orders-of-magnitude smaller proxy models can substitute for large reference models in data attribution and dataset selection, with the compute savings being super-linear (attribution cost scales roughly linearly with model size while training cost is a small fraction of total). However, the measured LDS scores remain low (~0.21 for ImageNet), and the correlation degrades at the smallest scales (370×) and on specific tasks (SQuAD, TriviaQA), so the proxy-model approach is not a panacea. Open questions include whether improved datamodel estimators would shift the scale trade-off, whether results hold for models beyond 760M parameters, and whether the per-sample correlation structure (which varies by test distribution) can be exploited for more targeted attribution.
