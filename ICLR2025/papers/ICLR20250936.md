## Problem

Policy Mirror Descent (PMD) is a unifying framework for policy optimization where the choice of mirror map determines the regularization structure, yet existing theoretical guarantees suggest the mirror map has only a mild influence on convergence. In practice, nearly all work defaults to the negative entropy mirror map (yielding Natural Policy Gradient), leaving open whether other mirror maps could substantially improve performance. This paper empirically investigates whether the mirror map choice matters and whether better maps can be automatically discovered.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RL environment (tabular MDP, discrete-action gym environment, or continuous MuJoCo task) along with Q-function estimates (via GAE) and a parameterized class of ω-potential mirror maps. It outputs a learned mirror map—concretely, a scalar function φ (or its inverse) that defines the Bregman geometry—and the resulting updated policy. For PMD, φ⁻¹ is parameterized as a one-layer neural network with 126 hidden units and mixed convex/concave monotonic activations; for AMPO, φ is a piecewise-linear function with n segments. The fitness signal for the evolutionary search is the expected value of the final policy after a fixed number of PMD/AMPO iterations.

## Method

The core contribution is a parameterization of the ω-potential mirror map class combined with evolutionary strategies (OpenAI-ES for tabular and MuJoCo settings, Sep-CMA-ES for non-tabular discrete settings) to search for the mirror map that maximizes final policy value. For the tabular Grid-World setting, they track the estimation error and policy-update distance from Theorem 2.2 of Xiao (2022), revealing that the theoretical error floor is a poor predictor of actual performance while small policy-update distances (as predicted by the quasi-monotonicity bound) correlate with smoother training. For non-tabular settings, they use AMPO with a piecewise-linear φ parameterization and demonstrate that learned maps generalize across environments (e.g., a map learned on Hopper outperforms negative entropy on Halfcheetah and Ant). A shared property of all learned maps is that they assign zero probability to the worst-scoring actions at moderate score scales, unlike the negative entropy which always assigns positive weight to every action, potentially avoiding wasted exploration samples.

## Results

In the tabular Grid-World setting, the learned mirror map consistently achieves the highest final value across all 256+ tested configurations, outperforming both the ℓ₂-norm and the negative entropy (NPG). In the non-tabular BCS and MinAtar suites, the learned mirror maps yield the best AMPO performance in 4 out of 5 environments, with the largest gains on Asterix and SpaceInvaders where average return more than doubles compared to negative entropy. In continuous control (MuJoCo), the mirror map learned on Hopper significantly improves final performance over negative entropy on all three test environments (Hopper, Halfcheetah, Ant). Cross-environment generalization (Table 1) shows that mirror maps learned in Acrobot and Asterix outperform negative entropy in all 5 environments, while those from CartPole and Freeway outperform it in 4 out of 5.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the learned mirror maps against two standard baselines: the negative entropy (which yields the Natural Policy Gradient) and the ℓ₂-norm mirror map. In Grid-World, the learned mirror map ranks first, ℓ₂-norm second, and negative entropy third in final value across all tested configurations, demonstrating that even the ℓ₂-norm consistently beats the conventional NPG choice. In the BCS/MinAtar non-tabular setting, the learned mirror maps outperform negative entropy in 4 out of 5 environments (tied with ℓ₂-norm on Acrobot), with the most dramatic improvement on SpaceInvaders (170.24 vs. 81.77 for negative entropy, a 2.1× gain) and Asterix (30.22 vs. 17.80). The ℓ₂-norm also outperforms negative entropy in most environments, further confirming that the negative entropy is not universally optimal. In MuJoCo continuous control, the Hopper-learned mirror map surpasses negative entropy on all three environments (Hopper, Halfcheetah, Ant), confirming generalizability beyond the training task. The paper also highlights that the learned mirror maps achieve the best empirical performance while exhibiting the worst theoretical error floor bound, revealing that standard PMD convergence guarantees are loose with respect to mirror map choice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of the Policy Mirror Descent (PMD) literature and the algorithm-discovery literature. It builds directly on Xiao (2022) for the PMD update rule and convergence guarantees, on Alfano et al. (2023) for the AMPO framework enabling general parameterizations, and on Krichene et al. (2015) for the ω-potential class of mirror maps. While prior PMD work has focused almost exclusively on the negative entropy mirror map (yielding NPG) or on generic theoretical guarantees that suggest the mirror map has only 'mild' influence on convergence, this paper departs by treating the mirror map as a learnable object and using evolutionary strategies to search over a parameterized class of ω-potentials. It is most closely related to Lu et al. (2022) and Jackson et al. (2024), who discover RL algorithms within the broader Mirror Learning class, but narrows the search to the mirror map component specifically.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a working assumption in the PMD literature: that the choice of mirror map has minimal impact on performance, as suggested by convergence guarantees where the mirror map appears only as a mild multiplicative factor. The empirical finding that a learned mirror map consistently outperforms negative entropy across tabular, discrete, and continuous control settings—and generalizes across tasks—suggests that the theoretical upper bounds are loose and that mirror map design is a meaningful degree of freedom. Open questions include obtaining tighter convergence guarantees that reflect the mirror map's actual influence, understanding how environmental properties (exploration difficulty, noise, credit assignment) should inform mirror map selection, and reducing the computational cost of ES-based search to make the approach more accessible.
