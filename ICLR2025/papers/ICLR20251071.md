## Problem

In biological few-shot class-incremental learning, closely related species within the same family share similar visual traits, causing their feature-space prototypes to overlap. This inter-species confusion leads to catastrophic forgetting of old species and poor generalization to new ones, a problem that existing FSCIL methods (which focus on forgetting or over-fitting in isolation) do not explicitly address.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251071/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images of biological species (birds, plant leaves, etc.) encoded by a ResNet-18 backbone into feature vectors. During base training, sufficient labeled data per class is available; during each incremental session, only K=5 samples per new class are provided. At inference, classification is performed solely via cosine distance between the extracted feature and species prototypes (residual prototypes are not used), producing a single species-level class label.

## Method

Prototype Antithesis (PA) decomposes each species representation into a species-unique prototype and a family-shared residual prototype via Residual Prototype Learning (RPL): in phase one, standard cosine-distance classification trains species prototypes; in phase two, a bidirectional optimization classifies residual features (original features minus species prototypes) with family-level labels while simultaneously classifying original features with species-level labels, forcing the two prototype sets to capture orthogonal trait components. During incremental learning, Residual Prototype Mixing (RPM) generates synthetic training samples by adding the relevant family residual prototype to new-species features, then classifies these composites with the new species label. A theoretical analysis shows this maximizes a class-separability metric by reducing the overlap term between old and new prototypes, effectively pushing new species features away from shared family traits and toward their unique discriminative components.

## Results

On CUB200 (10-way 5-shot, 10 incremental sessions), PA achieves 61.19% final-session accuracy with a performance drop (PD) of 17.50, outperforming TEEN (59.31%, PD 18.13) and OrCo (57.18%, PD 17.54). On PlantVillage (5-way 5-shot, 4 sessions), PA attains the best final accuracy of 90.51% and the lowest PD of 8.99%, surpassing NC-FSCIL (89.13%, PD 10.35) and CEC (85.01%, PD 14.52). On Tree-of-Life, PA outperforms OrCo by 6.26% and BiDist by 1.28% in final-session accuracy, while remaining slightly below NC-FSCIL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On CUB200, PA achieves the highest final-session accuracy (61.19%) among all 13 compared methods, beating TEEN by 1.88% and OrCo by 4.01%, while also recording the lowest PD (17.50) — marginally better than OrCo (17.54) and clearly better than NC-FSCIL (21.01) and FACT (18.96). On PlantVillage, PA dominates with the best final accuracy (90.51%) and the smallest PD (8.99%), outperforming NC-FSCIL (89.13%, PD 10.35), CEC (85.01%, PD 14.52), and FACT (86.15%, PD 13.32) by substantial margins. On Tree-of-Life, PA surpasses OrCo by 6.26% and BiDist by 1.28% in final-session accuracy, though it trails NC-FSCIL slightly; the authors note that PA does not require prior knowledge of the total number of unknown classes, making it more scalable. Overall, PA consistently wins or ties on the final-session accuracy metric across all three datasets and achieves the best or near-best PD, demonstrating that the Prototype Antithesis strategy effectively reduces inter-species confusion without sacrificing old-class retention.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the prototypical network framework (Snell et al., 2017) within the FSCIL setting by introducing a hierarchical prototype decomposition tailored to biological taxonomy. Prior FSCIL methods (CEC, FACT, TEEN, OrCo, NC-FSCIL) address catastrophic forgetting and over-fitting through knowledge distillation, weight freezing, or synthetic feature generation, but treat all classes uniformly without exploiting the species-to-family hierarchy inherent in biological data. The key departure is the Residual Prototype Learning step, which bi-directionally optimizes species-unique and family-shared prototypes, and the Residual Prototype Mixing step, which uses family-level residual prototypes to generate augmented features for new species—repurposing the prototypical classifier architecture with a domain-specific decomposition that no prior FSCIL method employs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method provides a principled way to handle the species-confusion problem that is particularly acute in biological recognition, where closely related species share family-level traits. The requirement for explicit taxonomic (family-level) labels limits direct transfer to non-hierarchical domains, and the evaluation is confined to ResNet-18 on three datasets of moderate scale, leaving open whether the decomposition strategy scales to deeper backbones or more complex multi-level taxonomies. The theoretical analysis of class separability, while clean, relies on orthogonality assumptions between species-unique and family-shared components that may not hold in practice. Future work could explore whether the residual prototype idea generalizes to other hierarchical classification tasks (e.g., medical diagnosis, product categorization) and whether the approach integrates with foundation models like BioCLIP for continual adaptation.
