## Problem

Online continual learning methods are typically compared under a 'single-epoch' constraint and a fixed replay memory size, but the actual per-epoch computation varies significantly across algorithms, and total storage costs (logits, extra model parameters) are often excluded from the memory budget. This makes cross-method comparisons unfair and obscures which methods are truly efficient. The paper argues for a unified total-resource budget (training FLOPs + total storage in bytes) and asks: which CL method performs best when all computational and memory costs are accounted for?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a streaming sequence of RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR, 224×224 for ImageNet/CLEAR) arriving one sample at a time, and for the MLLM experiments, image–text prompt pairs. It outputs a class label for image classification tasks or a generated text answer for multi-modal concept-incremental tasks. All training is constrained to a fixed total FLOPs budget and a fixed total memory budget in bytes that includes episodic memory, stored logits, and any auxiliary model parameters, rather than the conventional per-epoch or replay-size-only constraints.

## Method

aL-SAR combines two zero-overhead techniques. (1) Adaptive Layer Freezing (aL): for each mini-batch, a Batch Freezing Criterion (BFC) is computed by comparing the Fisher Information lost by freezing layers 1–n (estimated via a first-order diagonal FI approximation scaled by the batch's gradient magnitude) against the expected information gain from the saved backward FLOPs; the model then freezes layers 1 to n* = argmax BFC, so that batches with little new information trigger more freezing while novel batches trigger fewer. (2) Similarity-Aware Retrieval (SAR): instead of random or interference-based sampling from episodic memory, each sample is assigned an 'effective use-frequency' that combines an exponentially discounted count of past training uses with class-wise gradient cosine similarity (updated via EMA from gradients already computed during training); samples with low effective use-frequency are retrieved with higher probability via a softmax over negative effective frequencies, requiring no extra forward or backward passes. Together, aL reduces per-iteration FLOPs while SAR increases knowledge gained per iteration, so the method achieves higher accuracy than baselines at strictly lower total FLOPs under the same byte-level memory budget.

## Results

Under a fixed total budget of FLOPs and storage (Bytes), aL-SAR achieves 33.24 A_AUC / 35.02 A_Last on ImageNet-1K (Gaussian setup), 67.61 / 74.36 on CLEAR-10, and 38.65 / 47.56 on CLEAR-100, outperforming all 12 baselines while consuming fewer FLOPs (e.g., 93,000 vs. 112,000 TFLOPs on ImageNet-1K). On CIFAR-10 Gaussian setup, aL-SAR reaches 64.38 A_AUC / 72.57 A_Last with 146.80 TFLOPs, compared to 64.60 / 72.43 with 171.94 TFLOPs for the no-freezing baseline. The method also reduces training FLOPs by ~12% on LLaVA-1.5-7B MLLM training while maintaining or slightly improving accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

aL-SAR outperforms all 12 baselines on every dataset under the same total FLOPs and memory budget. The strongest baselines are LiDER (27.65 A_AUC) and X-DER (27.46) on ImageNet-1K, and ER (64.00 A_AUC) on CLEAR-10; aL-SAR exceeds them by 5.6, 5.8, and 3.6 points respectively. Notably, several methods that are competitive under the conventional 'same iterations' setup collapse under the total-budget constraint: ER-MIR drops to 7.45 A_AUC on ImageNet-1K (vs. 33.24 for aL-SAR) because its 4× forward-pass overhead for retrieval consumes the FLOPs budget, and OCS/MEMO/CAMA all fall below 12 A_AUC. On CLEAR-100 (domain-incremental), aL-SAR leads by 7.1 A_AUC over the best baseline (ER at 31.56), attributed to its retrieval method balancing use-frequency across domains. In the layer-freezing comparison (Table 2), aL saves 14.6% TFLOPs on CIFAR-10 while matching or exceeding the no-freezing accuracy, whereas REMIND saves only 12% at a 5.4-point A_AUC cost, and PTLF/EGERIA save merely 4.5%/1.6% with negligible accuracy change.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Fisher Information (originally from EWC/Kirkpatrick et al.) not as a regularization term but as a per-batch criterion for deciding which network layers to freeze, departing from prior freezing methods (REMIND, PTLF, EGERIA) that use predefined schedules or reference-model comparisons. For sample retrieval, it adapts gradient-similarity ideas from auxiliary-task literature (Du et al.) into a zero-overhead replay selection strategy, contrasting with MIR and ASER which require 3–4× additional forward/backward passes. Conceptually, the paper's strongest departure is methodological: it argues that the standard 'one epoch + fixed episodic memory' comparison protocol in online CL is unfair because different algorithms consume vastly different FLOPs per epoch and ignore storage for logits or auxiliary models, and it replaces this with a total FLOPs + total-bytes budget.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that several SOTA online CL methods (MIR, ASER, MEMO, OCS) fall behind simple ER under a fair total-budget comparison is a useful corrective for the field, suggesting that computational overhead in sophisticated retrieval strategies can negate their theoretical benefits. The adaptive layer freezing component is architecture-agnostic (validated on ResNet, ViT, and LLaVA-1.5-7B with LoRA) and plug-and-play, making it a practical tool for reducing training costs in streaming settings. Open questions include how the FI-based freezing criterion behaves under very long task sequences where the EMA estimates may drift, whether the approach extends to generative or diffusion-based CL, and whether the total-budget protocol will be adopted as a standard in the CL community.
