## Problem

While scaling laws have been well established for language and vision models, it remains unclear whether they apply to wearable sensor foundation models. Prior sensor foundation models operated on small datasets (tens to hundreds of thousands of hours), limited sensor modalities, and did not systematically study the joint effects of compute, data, and model size. This paper asks whether predictable power-law scaling behavior emerges when training on the largest wearable sensor dataset to date (up to 40M hours, 165K people, 5 sensor modalities).

## Input / Output

The model consumes a 2D matrix of 26 globally z-scored signals (derived from PPG/HR/HRV, 3-axis accelerometer, skin conductance, skin temperature, and altimeter) over 300 minutes, treated as an image-like input for ViT patching at 10-min × 5-feature resolution (zero-padded to 300×30). During pretraining, 80% of patches are randomly masked across both the time and sensor axes. Outputs include reconstructed sensor values for generative tasks (imputation, interpolation, extrapolation up to 120 min ahead) and class predictions for discriminative tasks (8-class activity, binary exercise detection, sex, binned age, 5-class mood), plus learned patch-level embeddings for transfer.

## Method

LSM is a ViT-based masked autoencoder (encoder-decoder) that treats the time-by-sensor matrix as a 2D image, applying random patch masking (80% ratio, 10×5 patch size) and reconstructing masked patches via MSE loss. The architecture clusters sensor signals by type within patches (rather than randomizing order), which ablations show improves performance over randomized or max-entropy orderings. Four model sizes (2M–328M params) are trained across data scales from 5K to 40M hours on Google v5e TPUs with AdamW, cosine LR decay, and batch size 4096. The key empirical contribution is establishing that test loss follows a power law L = aC^b + c with respect to compute, data, and model size, with saturation emerging near 10^7 hours and ~100M parameters. Downstream evaluation uses linear probes, a lightweight convolutional probe (<0.2% of fine-tune parameters), and full fine-tuning, demonstrating that LSM's pretrained representations are highly label-efficient (e.g., 90.3% accuracy on exercise detection vs. 70.9% supervised baseline).

## Results

On generative tasks, LSM (ViT-Base, 6.6M hours pretraining) achieves 16–23% MAE improvement over linear interpolation for temporal interpolation and 20–21% MAE improvement for temporal extrapolation across 10–120 minute horizons. On discriminative tasks, fine-tuned LSM achieves 90.3% accuracy and 97.0% mAP on exercise detection (+27% accuracy, +57% mAP over the supervised ViT-Base baseline) and 68.5% accuracy and 51.4% mAP on 8-class activity recognition (+29% accuracy, +54% mAP over supervised). Scaling data from 5K to 6.6M hours and model size from 2M to 110M parameters follows a power-law relationship (L = aC^b + c), with saturation observed near 10^7 hours and ~100M parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LSM (MAE) decisively outperforms all self-supervised baselines (MSN, DINO, SimCLR) on both exercise detection and activity recognition, with fine-tuned LSM achieving 90.3% accuracy vs. DINO's 78.2% and MSN's 76.7% on exercise detection. Against the supervised ViT-Base baseline (70.9% accuracy, 61.7% mAP), LSM fine-tune gains +27% accuracy and +57% mAP on exercise detection, and +29% accuracy and +54% mAP on activity recognition. On generative tasks, LSM surpasses MICE, Nearest Neighbor, and Linear Interpolation by 16–47% MAE across interpolation, extrapolation, and sensor imputation horizons. Notably, even the linear probe (no fine-tuning) of LSM (84.7% acc, 89.0% mAP) exceeds the fully supervised baseline, demonstrating strong representation quality. The only baseline that approaches LSM on exercise detection is Random Forest (73.0% acc, 76.8% mAP), but LSM still leads by 17.3 points in accuracy and 20.2 points in mAP with fine-tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the masked autoencoder (MAE) framework from vision (He et al., 2022) to multimodal wearable sensor data, departing from prior sensor foundation models (Abbaspourazad et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2024; Adaimi et al., 2024) that relied on contrastive learning. It extends the scaling laws literature (Kaplan et al., 2020; Zhai et al., 2022) from language and vision to the wearable sensor domain, systematically varying compute, data volume, and model size. The key conceptual departure is the use of a generative (reconstruction) objective rather than discriminative contrastive pretraining, enabling the model to natively perform imputation, interpolation, and extrapolation alongside downstream classification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic scaling study for wearable sensor foundation models, establishing that power-law scaling holds but saturates around 10^7 hours and ~100M parameters — a practically important ceiling that guides resource allocation for the field. The saturation is attributed to the pretraining task design, the 5-hour context window, and limited data diversity, pointing to concrete directions for future work (longer windows, decoder-only architectures, richer data curation). Open questions include principled handling of missing-not-at-random data, generalization across device form factors and sensor configurations, and whether raw-signal (rather than minutely-aggregated) inputs would shift the scaling frontier.
