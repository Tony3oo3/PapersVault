## Problem

While the linear growth of quantum pure-state complexity with system size has been rigorously established, extending these results to noisy circuit environments that reflect real NISQ devices remains an open challenge. In particular, it is unclear how to characterize and predict the circuit complexity of weakly noisy quantum states (polylog-depth circuits with O(1/n) noise strength), where increasing circuit depth paradoxically reduces state complexity rather than increasing it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250625/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes poly(n) copies of an unknown n-qubit weakly noisy state ρ_un one at a time via random Pauli measurements to construct a classical shadow, along with a pre-specified circuit architecture A and error parameter ε. It outputs a binary decision: True if the limited-structured complexity C_ε^{lim,A}(ρ_un) ≤ LR for some minimum depth R < log n (reporting that R), or False if C_ε^{lim,A}(ρ_un) > L·log n, with an indeterminate case where either answer is returned. The QCA state set used for comparison is generated classically from N = LRn²log n/ε² random circuits of depth R under architecture A.

## Method

The key insight (Theorem 1) is that any observable M = U|0ⁿ⟩⟨0ⁿ|U† generated by a circuit in U_A(R) can be approximated within ε by a linear combination of N = poly(R,n) randomly sampled QCA circuits, reducing the complexity prediction to optimizing a linear function over a compact parameter set D_β. Algorithm 1 performs binary search over circuit depth R ∈ [1, log n], using a monotone predicate P(R,ε) implemented via Bayesian optimization (BMaxS) that maximizes or minimizes the metric L_R(β) = |E_{ρ_i~q}[Tr(M(β)(ρ_i − ρ_un))]| over the compact set. The classical shadow representation of both the target state and QCA states enables efficient classical evaluation of L_R(β) without full tomography. The sample complexity is O(n²L R̃ log n log(1/δ)/ε⁴) and is proven optimal in the noisy circuit depth R̃ (Theorem 3), while the classical processing time is polynomial in n, R̃, and 1/ε (Theorem 2).

## Results

The paper proves the existence of a quantum learning algorithm (Alg. 1) that solves the Structured Complexity Prediction problem with sample complexity O(n²L R̃ log n log(1/δ)/ε⁴) and classical time complexity O(ε⁻⁴ · n^(4+k) · L² · R̃² log R̃ · log(1/δ)), where R̃ = O(log n) is the noisy circuit depth. Theorem 3 establishes a matching lower bound of Ω((1+2cpR̃)(1-δ)²/(2n)) samples, demonstrating near-optimality in circuit depth R̃. Numerically, on a 3×4 2D transverse-field Ising model with p=10⁻³ local depolarizing noise, the algorithm reveals that the shallower state ρ_TI(2,p) has a complexity lower bound > 4L while the deeper state ρ_TI(10,p) has a lower bound > 2L — showing that noisy-state complexity does NOT grow linearly with circuit depth, in stark contrast to pure states.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing learning algorithms; its contribution is a new theoretical framework with provable guarantees rather than a method comparison. The key contrast is with pure-state complexity results (Haferkamp et al. 2022; Brandao et al. 2021), which establish linear growth of complexity with circuit depth — the present work shows this linear growth breaks down for weakly noisy states. Compared to the shadow-tomography-based phase classification of Huang et al. (2022), the proposed algorithm extends to noisy states and provides an explicit complexity metric (limited-structured complexity) rather than a binary phase label. The intrinsic structure theorem (Theorem 1) enables approximation of any QCA observable by poly(n) random circuits, bypassing the exponential enumeration that would be required naively, and unlike variational quantum learning approaches, it avoids barren plateaus by operating on a compact parameter set with Bayesian optimization (Theorem 4 gives regret O(√(N²log²T/T))).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the pure-state quantum complexity framework of Brandao et al. (2021) and Haferkamp et al. (2022) to noisy quantum states by introducing a 'Limited-Structured Complexity' definition that restricts measurement operators to a specific circuit architecture. It leverages classical shadow tomography (Huang et al., 2020) as the quantum-to-classical representation layer and Bayesian optimization as the search procedure, building on the intrinsic structure property of quantum circuit architectures that allows any observable to be approximated by a linear combination of poly(n) random circuits. The paper explicitly differentiates itself from Huang et al. (2022), which used shadow tomography for phase classification but did not provide a complexity metric, and from Schuster et al. (2024), which established hardness results for learning random circuits but did not address structured noisy states.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that weakly noisy quantum state complexity may not grow with circuit depth, in stark contrast to the linear growth proven for pure states—has direct implications for NISQ device benchmarking and the AdS/CFT 'complexity equals volume' conjecture, where environmental noise is unavoidable. The algorithm achieves optimal sample complexity in circuit depth with polynomial classical processing, providing a rigorous tool for classifying whether a noisy state's output is classically simulable. Open questions include extending the framework to gate-dependent noise models, validating on actual 50–100 qubit hardware, and connecting the predicted complexity to topological phase identification in many-body systems.
