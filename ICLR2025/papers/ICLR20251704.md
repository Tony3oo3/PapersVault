## Problem

Offline model-based optimization (MBO) trains a surrogate model via MSE to guide gradient ascent toward high-scoring designs, but the paper shows that OOD-MSE has only a weak correlation (Spearman ≈ 0.2) with final design quality, while a ranking-related metric (AUPCC) correlates far more strongly (Spearman ≈ 0.5–0.6). The core gap is that MSE optimizes for value matching, whereas offline MBO only requires the surrogate to preserve the relative ordering of designs — a weaker condition that prior regularization and ensemble approaches never directly target.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a fixed offline dataset of design-score pairs (e.g., 86-dimensional superconductor compositions with critical-temperature scores, or 8/10-length DNA sequences with binding-affinity scores) with no access to online function evaluations. It outputs k=128 candidate designs ranked by the surrogate's predictions, evaluated by the 100th-percentile normalized ground-truth score on the Design-Bench oracle. For discrete tasks, designs are represented as real-valued logits of a categorical distribution to enable gradient-based search.

## Method

RaM replaces the standard MSE objective with a learning-to-rank (LTR) loss (RankCosine or ListNet) to train a two-hidden-layer MLP surrogate, so the model learns to preserve score ordering rather than match exact values. Three components make this practical: (1) data augmentation randomly samples m=1000 design-score pairs into lists and repeats n=10,000 times to construct the LTR training set; (2) the surrogate is trained by minimizing the chosen ranking loss over these lists; (3) output adaptation z-score-normalizes the trained model's in-distribution predictions so that gradient-ascent hyperparameters (step size η, number of steps T) transfer directly from regression-based baselines. Theoretically, the paper proves that any order-preserving surrogate has equivalent optima to the ground-truth function (Theorem 1) and that the LTR generalization error vanishes at O(1/√n) (Theorem 2), providing a principled justification for the ranking objective.

## Results

On the Design-Bench benchmark (100th percentile normalized score), RaM-ListNet achieves the best mean rank of 2.2/22 across five tasks, winning on D'Kitty (0.962) and Superconductor (0.517), while RaM-RankCosine achieves a mean rank of 2.7/22, winning on TF-Bind-10 (0.675). The next-best method, BDI, only reaches a mean rank of 5.9/22. Across all five tasks (Ant, D'Kitty, Superconductor, TF-Bind-8, TF-Bind-10), both RaM variants consistently outperform all 20 compared baselines in aggregate ranking.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RaM-ListNet (mean rank 2.2/22) and RaM-RankCosine (2.7/22) outperform all 20 baselines in aggregate, with the next-best method BDI (Chen et al., 2022) at 5.9/22 and Tri-Mentoring (Chen et al., 2023a) at 7.7/22. On individual tasks, RaM-ListNet wins on D'Kitty (0.962 vs. 0.954 for PGS) and Superconductor (0.517 vs. 0.514 for RaM-RankCosine), while RaM-RankCosine wins on TF-Bind-10 (0.675 vs. 0.673 for REINFORCE). Both RaM variants dominate on TF-Bind-8 (0.981–0.982 vs. 0.973 for BDI) and are competitive on Ant (0.940–0.949 vs. 0.963 for BDI, which is the sole task where a baseline wins). Backward approaches (MINs, DDOM, BONET, GTG) and regularization-based forward methods (COMs, RoMA, IOM, ICT) are consistently outperformed, with RoMA being the weakest forward method (mean rank 18.3/22). The paper further shows that simply replacing MSE with ListNet in existing methods (e.g., BO-qEI, Tri-Mentoring, Match-OPT) yields positive gains in 43 out of 45 task-method combinations, confirming the versatility of ranking losses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in offline model-based optimization, where surrogate models are trained by minimizing MSE and then optimized via gradient ascent (COMs, IOM, ICT, Tri-Mentoring, etc.). It reframes the surrogate learning problem as a learning-to-rank task, drawing on RankCosine (Qin et al., 2008) and ListNet (Cao et al., 2007) from the information retrieval literature, and introduces data augmentation and output adaptation to bridge the gap between LTR training and gradient-ascent search. The conceptual lineage is thus a cross-pollination: the paper identifies that the field's focus on reducing OOD-MSE is misaligned with the actual goal (selecting top-k designs), and repurposes ranking losses to directly optimize order preservation instead.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a foundational assumption in offline MBO—that MSE is the appropriate training objective for surrogate models—and provides both empirical evidence (weak Spearman correlation between OOD-MSE and final score) and a practical remedy (swapping in ranking losses). The versatility result, showing that simply replacing MSE with ListNet in existing methods yields consistent gains, makes the finding broadly actionable for practitioners. Open questions include whether the theoretical generalization bounds (currently for linear function classes) extend to deep networks, whether the AUPCC metric generalizes beyond the Design-Bench tasks tested, and whether the ranking-based approach can be combined with the backward (generative) approaches that currently dominate discrete design tasks.
