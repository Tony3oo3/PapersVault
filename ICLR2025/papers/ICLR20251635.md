## Problem

Prior work on fine-tuning safety risks (Qi et al. 2023; Bianchi et al. 2023) focused exclusively on instruction-following data, but task-specific datasets—characterized by fixed answer formats, measurable ground truth, and structured prompts—present a structurally distinct safety landscape that had not been studied. This paper asks whether benign task-specific fine-tuning can accidentally produce harmful models, and whether malicious actors can adversarially reshape such datasets to increase harmfulness while evading toxicity filters and preserving downstream task accuracy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251635/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes two inputs: (1) a user-provided task-specific fine-tuning dataset (e.g., multiple-choice, true/false, or sentence-completion samples) formatted under one of four prompting strategies (Benign, AOA, AutoIF, AutoIF+AOA), and (2) a standard safety instruction dataset (from Bianchi et al. 2023). The Paraphrase step takes 4 representative user samples plus each safety instruction–answer pair and produces a paraphrased safety prompt–answer pair that matches the user data's phrasing and structural style. The final output is a fine-tuned model (via LoRA 8-bit PEFT, 1 epoch) that resists harmful queries while retaining comparable downstream task accuracy.

## Method

The core contribution is the Paraphrase mitigation strategy: an LLM (GPT-3.5 or LLaMA-2 13B) is prompted with 4 user data samples and a safety instruction–answer pair, and asked to rewrite the safety pair so its format, phrasing, and prompting style mirror the user data while preserving the safe refusal content. This bridges the distribution gap that causes standard safety-data mixing (Base, Longest) to fail—where the mismatched format allows the model to prioritize user-data patterns over safety patterns at inference time. The paper also introduces two novel adversarial prompting strategies, AutoIF (converting task-specific Q&A into imperative instruction-following pairs via an LLM) and AutoIF+AOA (wrapping AutoIF output in the Absolutely Obedient Agent template), which consistently raise attack success rates to 25–84% on Harmful Instructions while keeping toxicity detection below 0.61%. Paraphrase achieves <1% ASR on Harmful Instructions at a 50% mixing rate (and <5% at just 1% mixing), compared to 5–84% for baselines, with only a 1–3 point drop in downstream task accuracy.

## Results

On LLaMA-2 7B fine-tuned with adversarial prompting (AutoIF+AOA), the Paraphrase mitigation reduces the Attack Success Rate (ASR) on Harmful Instructions to 0.00–0.19% across all datasets (PIQA, HellaSwag, MMLU), compared to 5.58–77.12% for Base and 4.62–72.88% for Longest. Paraphrase is also far more efficient: mixing just 1% of Paraphrase safety data achieves ASR < 5%, whereas Base and Longest cannot drop below 40% ASR even at 50% mixing. Downstream task accuracy is largely preserved (e.g., PIQA Benign: 81.18% → 80.58%; HellaSwag Benign: 93.63% → 91.93%). On closed-source GPT-3.5, Paraphrase similarly reduces HarmI ASR from 55.96% (AOA, w/o mixing) to 0.00% while maintaining 84.70% accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Paraphrase against two safety-data mixing baselines: Base (following Bianchi et al. 2023 and Qi et al. 2023, which mixes standard safety data with instruction delimiters) and Longest (following Zhao et al. 2024, which uses only the top-100 longest safety examples). Paraphrase decisively outperforms both on the primary safety metric (HarmI ASR). On the most severe adversarial setting (PIQA, AutoIF+AOA, LLaMA-2 7B), Paraphrase achieves 0.19% ASR versus 77.12% for Base and 72.88% for Longest — a gap of over 70 percentage points. On HellaSwag AutoIF+AOA, the gap is even wider: 0.00% (Paraphrase) vs. 63.46% (Base) and 68.46% (Longest). Paraphrase also wins on efficiency: at 1% mixing rate it already achieves ASR < 5%, while neither Base nor Longest can reach below 40% ASR even at 50% mixing. The only trade-off is a small drop in downstream task accuracy (typically 1–3 points) and slightly higher refusal rates on XSTest, though all remain below the baseline model's 78% refusal rate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the fine-tuning safety risk analysis from Qi et al. (2023) and Bianchi et al. (2023), which focused on instruction-following datasets, to the structurally distinct task-specific setting (multiple choice, true/false, sentence completion with ground truth answers). While prior work established that benign instruction-following fine-tuning degrades safety and that mixing safety data restores it, this paper shows the dynamics differ for task-specific data: benign fine-tuning does not compromise safety, but adversarial prompting strategies (AOA, AutoIF) can. The proposed Paraphrase mitigation departs from the fixed safety-data mixing of Bianchi et al. by dynamically adapting safety examples to match the user's prompting format, addressing a distribution gap that fixed baselines cannot bridge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The results are directly actionable for closed-source model providers who must protect fine-tuning APIs: the finding that 1% Paraphrase data outperforms 50% Base/Longest mixing offers a computationally efficient safeguard, and the demonstration that adversarial task-specific fine-tuning evades toxicity filters (≤0.61% detection) while achieving up to 84% attack success rate underscores an underappreciated vulnerability. Key open questions include whether Paraphrase itself can be adversarially targeted (the authors note this new attack vector), whether the method generalizes beyond the tested models and datasets, and whether the resource cost of paraphrasing the full safety dataset per user request is sustainable at scale. The adversarial prompting strategies are acknowledged as primarily demonstrative with high detectability through structural analysis, limiting their practical threat model.
