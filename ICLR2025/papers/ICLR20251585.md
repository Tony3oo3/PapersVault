## Problem

Bilevel optimization requires computing the hypergradient, which involves two coupled challenges: solving the lower-level problem to obtain y*(x) and estimating the inverse Hessian-vector product [∇²_yy f]⁻¹∇_y F. Existing methods handle these separately, and while SHINE (Ramzi et al., 2022) addressed them jointly via shared quasi-Newton matrices, it suffers from potential incorrect inversion and provides only asymptotic hypergradient convergence without guarantees on the algorithmic iterates. The paper asks whether a coordinated quasi-Newton approach can improve hypergradient approximation while delivering a non-asymptotic convergence rate for the full bilevel algorithm.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes the upper-level objective F, the strongly convex lower-level objective f, initial points (x₀, y₀), an initial matrix H₀ (typically a scalar multiple of the identity to reduce recursion to inner products), a stepsize α, and per-iteration counts K and {Q_k}. It outputs the upper-level solution x* and produces hypergradient estimates at each iteration. The parameter Q_k controls a performance-versus-cost trade-off: Q_k=1 shares the {s_i, g_i} pairs from the lower-level solve (cheaper but risk of incorrect inversion), while Q_k>1 invokes a dedicated quasi-Newton subroutine for the ∇_y F direction (more expensive but inversion-safe).

## Method

qNBO is a three-part framework that coordinates quasi-Newton techniques across both computational challenges. Part 1 (subroutine A) drives y_k toward y*(x_k) using P warm-start gradient steps followed by T quasi-Newton steps with BFGS or SR1 two-loop recursion, accumulating the pairs {s_t, g_t}. Part 2 updates the inverse Hessian-vector product u_{k+1}: when Q_k=1 it reuses the {s_t, g_t} pairs from Part 1 (as in SHINE), but when Q_k>1 it invokes a separate subroutine B that builds its own quasi-Newton recursion specifically along the direction ∇_y F(x_k, y_{k+1}), thereby avoiding the incorrect inversion that plagues SHINE's shared-matrix approach. Part 3 performs a gradient step on x using the resulting hypergradient approximation. The key theoretical contribution is a non-asymptotic convergence proof for qNBO (BFGS) that leverages the superlinear convergence rate of BFGS, yielding an O(κ³ ln K / K) bound on the average squared hypergradient norm and a gradient complexity of Õ(κ⁶ε⁻²) to reach an ε-stationary point.

## Results

On data hyper-cleaning, qNBO (BFGS) reaches the target test accuracy (91.50% on MNIST, 83.00% on FashionMNIST) in 0.42s and 0.83s respectively, with F1 scores of 95.34 and 93.56 — the fastest among all methods tested. On few-shot meta-learning (Omniglot, 5-shot), qNBO (BFGS) achieves 99.14% test accuracy in 772s for 5-way, 99.14% in 1648s for 20-way, and 99.05% in 2978s for 30-way, outperforming PZOBO in both accuracy and speed (e.g., 99.14% vs 97.94% at 20-way, ~11× faster). In the toy quadratic bilevel problem, qNBO (SR1) and AID-BIO achieve the smallest hypergradient estimation errors. On hyperparameter optimization for logistic regression, qNBO (BFGS) reaches the lowest validation loss faster than all competing methods on both 20News and Real-sim.

## Baselines & Comparisons

qNBO (BFGS) dominates on speed across all tasks: it reaches target accuracy in 0.42s on MNIST hyper-cleaning vs. 6.31s for BOME, 20.25s for SHINE-OPA, and 1.05s for PZOBO. On meta-learning (Omniglot), qNBO (BFGS) is 14× faster than PZOBO at 5-way (772s vs. 11124s) and simultaneously more accurate at 20-way (99.14% vs. 97.94%) and 30-way (99.05% vs. 97.15%). Both F²SA and SABA fail to reach the target accuracy on either hyper-cleaning dataset, while qNBO, BOME, and SHINE-OPA all succeed. In the toy quadratic problem, qNBO (SR1) and AID-BIO achieve the lowest hypergradient errors, with qNBO (BFGS) performing best when Q_k = k+1 as predicted by theory. BSG1 is excluded from several experiments due to numerical ineffectiveness, and qNBO (SR1) shows oscillations on 20News logistic regression without a correction strategy. Overall, qNBO (BFGS) achieves the best or near-best accuracy while being the fastest method in every task where it is compared.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends SHINE (Ramzi et al., 2022), which first proposed sharing quasi-Newton matrices from the lower-level solve to approximate the inverse Hessian in hypergradient computation. The key departure is a dedicated subroutine (Algorithm 3) that applies quasi-Newton recursion specifically along the ∇yF(x,y) direction, avoiding the incorrect inversion problem that plagued SHINE's OPA variant. The framework also positions itself against the broader bilevel optimization landscape—fully first-order methods (BOME, F²SA), penalty-based approaches (SABA), and second-order methods (AID-BIO, AMIGO)—by offering a quasi-Newton alternative with non-asymptotic convergence guarantees that prior quasi-Newton bilevel methods lacked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The non-asymptotic convergence analysis (O(κ³/K) rate) is the first of its kind for a quasi-Newton bilevel method, providing theoretical justification for the practical speedups observed. The dramatic wall-clock advantage in meta-learning (e.g., 772s vs. 11,124s for PZOBO on 5-way Omniglot) suggests quasi-Newton methods could become a practical default for bilevel problems where second-order information is affordable. Open questions include extending the analysis to stochastic settings, establishing convergence for SR1 on general (non-quadratic) lower-level objectives, and scaling to very high-dimensional problems where even quasi-Newton memory becomes prohibitive.
