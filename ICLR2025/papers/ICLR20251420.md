## Problem

Optimization in deep learning typically operates in the 'edge of stability' (EOS) regime, where the largest Hessian eigenvalue equilibrates around the critical threshold 2/η and the optimizer oscillates along high-curvature directions without diverging. Traditional optimization theory, which relies on gradient flow or convexity assumptions, cannot capture these oscillatory dynamics, leaving it unexplained how gradient descent and adaptive optimizers make consistent progress when the loss is non-monotonic. The paper asks: what smooth, tractable object characterizes the macroscopic path of an oscillatory optimizer through weight space?

## Input / Output

The method consumes the current weight vector w, the loss gradient ∇L(w), the Hessian H(w) (or at minimum its top eigenvalues/eigenvectors and the gradient of the sharpness ∇S(w)), and optimizer hyperparameters (η, β₂). It produces a continuous-time ODE (the central flow) in weight space, an oscillation covariance matrix Σ(t) that quantifies the instantaneous variance of oscillations along each critical eigenvector, and a predicted time-averaged loss curve via L(w(t)) + ½⟨H(w(t)), Σ(t)⟩. For RMSProp, it additionally yields a stationary diagonal preconditioner P̄(w) that is the optimal solution to a convex program balancing optimization speed against local stability. The central flow is numerically integrated with small Euler steps, requiring Hessian-vector products and top-eigenvalue computations at each step.

## Method

The paper derives 'central flows' — smooth ODEs that model the time-averaged trajectory of oscillatory optimizers — via a heuristic time-averaging argument built on a cubic Taylor expansion of the loss. The key insight is that oscillations of magnitude x along a top Hessian eigenvector u, when time-averaged, produce an implicit sharpness penalty term (½σ²∇S(w)) in the effective gradient, where σ² is the oscillation variance. The unknown covariance Σ(t) is uniquely determined by a cone complementarity problem (CCP) enforcing three conditions: positive semidefiniteness, stability (no Hessian eigenvalue exceeds 2/η), and complementarity (oscillations are confined to the critical subspace). For gradient descent, the central flow reduces to a projected gradient flow on the constraint S(w) ≤ 2/η, which provably decreases the loss monotonically. For Scalar RMSProp and RMSProp, the central flow additionally reveals that the effective step size is implicitly locked to the maximal locally stable value 2/S(w), and that the oscillations induce an implicit curvature regularization that steers the trajectory toward lower-curvature regions where larger steps become possible — a mechanism the authors term 'acceleration via regularization.' Empirically, the central flows predict long-term optimization trajectories on ViT, ResNet, and Mamba architectures with high numerical accuracy, tracking both the smoothed loss curves and the oscillation covariance.

## Results

The central flow accurately predicts long-term optimization trajectories across multiple architectures (ViT on CIFAR-10, Mamba on sequence prediction, ResNet on CIFAR-10) and optimizers (gradient descent, Scalar RMSProp, RMSProp). The key quantitative evidence is that the distance between the central flow and the actual optimizer trajectory in weight space remains small over thousands of steps, while the distance to gradient flow (for GD) or the naive stable flow (for adaptive optimizers) grows large once the optimizer enters the edge of stability. The central flow also accurately predicts the oscillation covariance matrix Σ(t), with each eigenvalue matching the instantaneous variance of oscillations along the corresponding eigenvector. The paper further shows that the central flow loss L(w(t)) is monotonically decreasing (Proposition 1), providing a hidden progress metric that explains why the non-monotonic training loss still decreases over longer timescales.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against existing continuous-time models: gradient flow (for GD) and the naive stable flow derived from Ma et al. (2022) (for Scalar RMSProp and RMSProp). In all tested settings, once the optimizer enters the edge of stability, the central flow tracks the actual optimizer trajectory closely while gradient flow and the stable flow diverge significantly (Figures 3, 5, 7). A second key comparison is against ablated flows that retain the adaptive step-size strategy but remove the implicit curvature regularization term. Over longer timescales, these ablated flows navigate into sharper regions of weight space where they are forced to take smaller steps, and optimize measurably slower than the full central flow — demonstrating that 'acceleration via regularization' is a critical component of adaptive optimizer effectiveness (Figures 6, 15, 19). The central flow thus outperforms both the naive stable flow (which fails at EOS) and the curvature-free ablation (which fails over long horizons), establishing that both the oscillation-aware dynamics and the implicit sharpness penalty are necessary to capture the true optimization trajectory.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the edge-of-stability (EOS) framework established by Cohen et al. (2021) and the first theoretical explanation of EOS dynamics by Damian et al. (2023), who used a third-order Taylor expansion to show how oscillations implicitly reduce sharpness. The key departure is methodological: rather than analyzing the fine-grained oscillatory dynamics, the authors argue that a time-averaged 'central flow' is simpler and sufficient for most purposes, and they generalize from the single-unstable-eigenvalue case to the more realistic multi-eigenvalue setting. The analysis is then extended from gradient descent to adaptive optimizers (Scalar RMSProp and RMSProp), building on Cohen et al. (2022)'s observation that adaptive methods also operate at EOS and on Ma et al. (2022)'s stable-flow derivation for adaptive methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central flow framework provides a practical, interpretable tool for reasoning about optimization in deep learning that is absent from traditional convex optimization theory. The revelation that RMSProp implicitly solves a convex program to select its preconditioner, and the 'acceleration via regularization' mechanism (where higher learning rates initially slow optimization but steer the trajectory into lower-curvature regions enabling larger steps later), offer concrete new insights that could inform future optimizer design. Key open questions include: rigorously identifying conditions under which the central flow approximation holds, extending the analysis to other optimizers (e.g., Adam with momentum, LAMB), and whether the framework can be used to systematically design new optimizers with desired properties. The derivations remain heuristic (not rigorous proofs), and the empirical validation, while strong across ViT/ResNet/Mamba on CIFAR-10 and synthetic tasks, is limited to deterministic full-batch training.
