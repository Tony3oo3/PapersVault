## Problem

Existing methods for predicting spatially resolved gene expression from H&E histology images treat the task as a deterministic regression, which is inherently ill-posed because similar tissue images can correspond to distinct gene expression profiles due to biological heterogeneity. This regression framing also forces inference-time computation to scale with the training dataset size (via nearest-neighbor retrieval) and fails to capture the one-to-many relationship between morphology and transcriptomics. The paper asks whether a generative modeling approach can learn the full conditional distribution over gene expressions given an image, preserving spatial and biological heterogeneity.

## Input / Output

The method takes a 224×224 H&E image patch centered on a single spatial transcriptomics spot as input and produces a gene expression vector of length C (e.g., 200–1000 genes) as output. The image patch is consumed by pre-trained pathology foundation models (UNI and CONCH) to produce a pooled condition embedding, while the gene expression vector is treated as a sequence of C scalar tokens. At inference, the model generates multiple stochastic samples conditioned on the image patch, and the sample mean across 20 generated vectors is reported as the final prediction.

## Method

Stem formulates gene expression inference as conditional diffusion generation, learning p_gene(X|V) via a DDPM trained with a standard noise-prediction MSE loss. The backbone is a 12-block DiT transformer (hidden dim 384, 6-head attention) where the gene expression vector is tokenized into C tokens, each embedded as the sum of a count embedding (2-layer MLP) and a learnable gene-type embedding. The histology image condition enters every DiT block through adaptive LayerNorm (adaLN-Zero) modulation, with the condition vector derived by concatenating UNI and CONCH attention-pooled embeddings and projecting them via a 2-layer MLP. Training uses AdamW at lr 1e-4 for 250k iterations with batch size 256, EMA (decay 0.9999), and 1:4 image augmentation (rotations, flips, transposes) to improve generalization. This generative formulation replaces the deterministic regression mapping with a learned conditional distribution, enabling one-to-many predictions and preserving gene-level spatial variation (measured by the proposed RVD metric) that regression baselines collapse.

## Results

On the Kidney Visium dataset (HMHVG gene set), Stem achieves PCC-10 of 0.5893, PCC-50 of 0.5332, PCC-200 of 0.4257, MAE of 0.8792, and RVD of 0.0751, outperforming all baselines on every metric. On the HER2ST dataset (HMHVG), Stem reaches PCC-10 of 0.8298, PCC-50 of 0.7726, PCC-300 of 0.5984, MAE of 0.7547, and RVD of 0.0693. The newly proposed Relative Variation Distance (RVD) metric shows the most dramatic gains: Stem's RVD of 0.0751 on Kidney Visium is ~8× lower than TRIPLEX (0.5871) and ~13× lower than BLEEP (0.2170), indicating far better preservation of biological heterogeneity in predictions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Stem outperforms all three regression-based baselines (HisToGene, BLEEP, TRIPLEX) on every metric across all four datasets. On the Kidney Visium HMHVG set, Stem improves PCC-10 by 12.4 points over TRIPLEX (0.5893 vs 0.4654) and 8.9 points over BLEEP (0.5893 vs 0.4998), while reducing RVD by 87% relative to TRIPLEX (0.0751 vs 0.5871). On HER2ST, the advantage is even more pronounced in RVD: Stem achieves 0.0693 versus 0.6428 for TRIPLEX and 0.6025 for BLEEP, a ~9× improvement, while also leading in PCC-10 (0.8298 vs 0.7907 for TRIPLEX). HisToGene is consistently the weakest baseline, with RVD values near 1.0 on Kidney Visium and as high as 10.34 on HER2ST, indicating it produces nearly flat predictions that fail to capture spatial gene variation. The downstream Leiden clustering analysis on HER2ST further confirms Stem's superiority: Stem's predicted gene expressions correctly separate invasive cancer from breast gland regions matching pathologist annotations, whereas BLEEP's predictions merge these biologically distinct regions into a single cluster.

## Relation to Prior Work

Stem departs from the dominant regression framing of spatial gene expression prediction (ST-Net, HisToGene, TRIPLEX, BLEEP, EGN) by recasting the task as conditional generative modeling with a diffusion model, arguing that the image-to-expression mapping is inherently non-injective due to biological heterogeneity. Architecturally, it builds directly on DiT's transformer backbone and adaLN-Zero modulation for fusing the histology condition, and on DDPM's forward/reverse process formulation for the generative objective. For image encoding, it leverages pre-trained pathology foundation models (UNI, CONCH) via attention-pooled embeddings as a drop-in condition, avoiding the manual image-gene alignment losses used by BLEEP and TRIPLEX.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By enabling in silico gene expression inference from widely available H&E images, Stem removes the need for expensive and time-consuming spatial transcriptomics experiments, potentially democratizing molecular-level tissue analysis. The proposed RVD metric exposes a blind spot in the field's reliance on PCC, revealing that regression baselines can achieve high correlation while producing spatially flat, biologically unfaithful predictions. Open questions include whether better conditioning mechanisms or sample statistics (beyond the mean) can further improve fidelity, how to optimally combine diverse foundation model embeddings, and whether the approach extends to single-cell resolution or multi-omic prediction.
