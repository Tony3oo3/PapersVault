## Problem

LLM performance on certain multiple-choice tasks stagnates for a wide range of model scales before improving sharply at an unpredictable threshold (emergent abilities), yet prior explanations either attribute the phenomenon to coarse metrics or rely on Sigmoid fits that require data past the threshold. This paper asks: what mechanism causes the pre-threshold stagnation, and can the threshold and post-threshold performance be predicted using only pre-threshold models?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253577/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes predicted class probabilities from 56 LLMs (spanning log compute M ≈ −1 to 3) on multiple-choice benchmarks, along with each model's log compute value. Questions are first sorted and grouped into G difficulty levels (typically G=3) using the Target-Conditioned Brier Score computed from models below the manually identified emergence threshold T. The output is a predicted accuracy-vs-log-compute curve for models beyond T, obtained by averaging polynomial fits on the easiest and hardest groups in TC Brier Score space and projecting back to accuracy via a learned OLS mapping G(·).

## Method

The key empirical finding is that, under the proposed TC Brier Score (which conditions on available classes and flips the sign of the standard Brier Score), easy questions exhibit inverted-U scaling (deep double descent) while hard questions exhibit U-shaped scaling (inverse scaling due to distractor tasks), and these opposing trends cancel out to produce the observed stagnation. The Slice-and-Sandwich pipeline exploits this structure: it fits a degree-5 polynomial on the easy group and a degree-2 polynomial on the hard group using only pre-threshold models, averages the two fitted curves to obtain an aggregate TC Brier Score forecast (Eq. 5), and then maps the result back to accuracy via a simple OLS regression G(·) calibrated on the same pre-threshold data (Eq. 6). This avoids the need for any post-threshold data and captures the acceleration of the performance soar that a global Sigmoid fit misses.

## Results

The paper evaluates 56 LLMs across 6 multiple-choice datasets with emergent abilities (MMLU, Arithmetic, Persian-QA, Hindu knowledge, Conceptual combinations, Analogical similarity) and 3 non-emergent tasks (ARC, HellaSwag, Abstract narrative understanding). The key finding is that under the proposed TC Brier Score, hard question groups exhibit U-shaped scaling (performance first worsens then improves with scale) while easy question groups show inverted-U scaling followed by steady improvement, with the reversion point coinciding with the emergence threshold. The Slice-and-Sandwich pipeline, using only pre-threshold models, successfully forecasts the post-threshold performance soar on MMLU (T=1.5), Arithmetic (T=1.8), and Persian-QA (T=2.3), outperforming the Sigmoid-based baseline in capturing the acceleration of improvement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Slice-and-Sandwich against the Sigmoid-based task-specific scaling law baselines from Owen (2024), Ye et al. (2023), and Ruan et al. (2024), which fit a Sigmoid function to accuracy vs. log compute using models both before and after the emergence threshold. On MMLU, Slice-and-Sandwich captures the forthcoming soaring trend in accuracy that the Sigmoid baseline completely misses, as the baseline produces a flat or slowly rising curve past the threshold. On Arithmetic, the Sigmoid baseline provides a seemingly decent forecast but fails to capture the acceleration of performance increase, whereas Slice-and-Sandwich does. On Persian-QA, Slice-and-Sandwich similarly outperforms the baseline in trajectory shape. The comparison is primarily qualitative (shape of predicted scaling curves) rather than a single point-accuracy metric, with Slice-and-Sandwich winning on all three main datasets by incorporating the U-shaped and inverted-U priors. A simpler alternative (Hard-Lift) also beats the Sigmoid baseline on MMLU and Persian-QA but underperforms on Arithmetic, reinforcing that the full difficulty-stratified approach is important.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes two previously separate observations—deep double descent on testing loss (Nakkiran et al., 2021) and U-shaped/inverse scaling in LLMs (Wei et al., 2023; McKenzie et al., 2023)—into a unified account of emergent abilities by showing they manifest on easy and hard question groups respectively, with their opposing trends offsetting each other below the emergence threshold. The proposed TC Brier Score extends the classical Brier Score (Brier, 1950) by conditioning on available classes, and the Slice-and-Sandwich prediction pipeline departs from the dominant Sigmoid-regression approach (Ye et al., 2023; Owen, 2024; Ruan et al., 2024) by fitting difficulty-stratified polynomial trends using only pre-threshold data rather than requiring post-threshold models in the training set.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The offsetting-scaling explanation offers a mechanistic account of why LLM performance stagnates on multiple-choice tasks before suddenly improving, which is valuable for capability monitoring and safety forecasting. However, the analysis is limited to multiple-choice tasks; extending it to string-match tasks requires a continuous metric that both differentiates difficulty levels and correlates with the target traditional metric, a problem the authors acknowledge remains open (App. H). The prediction pipeline, while more explainable than Sigmoid regression, is validated on only three datasets with modest quantitative gains, and the polynomial-degree choices (2 for hard, 5 for easy) are heuristic rather than principled.
