## Problem

Diffusion-based purification (DiffPure) is suboptimal for adversarial defense because it relies on the diffused adversarial and clean data distributions being sufficiently close, creating an inherent trade-off between noise removal and data recovery quality. Additionally, existing evaluations of DiffPure use weak adaptive attacks that overestimate its robustness, giving a false sense of security.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252827/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

ADBM takes an adversarial RGB image (with imperceptible perturbations bounded by l∞ ≤ 8/255, l1 ≤ 12, or l2 ≤ 1) at resolutions from 32×32 to 224×224 and outputs a purified clean image that can be fed to any downstream classifier without modification. The purification is a plug-and-play preprocessing step: the forward diffusion adds Gaussian noise for 100–150 steps, and the reverse process recovers the clean image in as few as 1–5 DDIM steps, making it substantially cheaper than the original DiffPure pipeline.

## Method

ADBM constructs a direct reverse bridge from the diffused adversarial distribution to the clean data distribution, bypassing DiffPure's reliance on distributional similarity. During training, adversarial noise ε_a is added to clean images so the forward process starts from x_0^a = x_0 + ε_a; the reverse process then learns a trajectory x_t^d = x_t^a − k_t·ε_a that explicitly interpolates from the adversarial endpoint (k_T=0) to the clean endpoint (k_0=1), where k_t is derived analytically to eliminate ε_a from the conditional reverse distribution. The training loss L_b mirrors DDPM's noise-prediction objective but includes an additional scaled ε_a term in both the network input and the prediction target, allowing fine-tuning from a pre-trained diffusion checkpoint in ~30K steps. Adversarial noise is generated by maximizing the frozen classifier's loss via PGD (with t and ε fixed per optimization step to avoid EOT), aligning purification with the classifier's decision boundary rather than the diffusion model's own objective. Theoretical analysis proves that ADBM's one-step DDIM purification error is bounded by the training loss, and that ADBM's probability of recovering a clean example strictly exceeds DiffPure's under infinite reverse timesteps.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 under reliable white-box adaptive attacks (PGD with 200 steps and 20 EOT samples), ADBM achieves 53.5% average robust accuracy across l∞, l1, and l2 threats, outperforming DiffPure (49.1%) by 4.4% while maintaining comparable clean accuracy (91.9% vs. 92.5%). Against the strongest AT baseline (Wang et al., 2023), ADBM is competitive on the seen l∞ threat (47.7% vs. 70.7%) but dramatically superior on unseen l1 and l2 threats (49.6% and 63.3% vs. 8.5% and 29.0%). On SVHN, Tiny-ImageNet, and ImageNet-100, ADBM consistently outperforms DiffPure by 3–5% average robust accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ADBM outperforms all six AP baselines across every threat model. Against the closest AP competitor, DiffPure (Nie et al., 2022), ADBM gains +5.5% on l∞, +5.3% on l1, +2.5% on l2, and +4.4% on average robust accuracy on CIFAR-10. DiffPure+Langevin (Zhang et al., 2023) is the second-strongest AP method (46.35% avg) but still trails ADBM by 7.2% on average. All other AP methods (Shi, Yoon, DiffPure+Guide, Diff+ScoreOpt) are substantially weaker, with average robust accuracies below 45%. Against AT methods, ADBM loses on the seen l∞ threat (47.7% vs. 64–71% for the best AT baselines) but dominates on unseen l1 and l2 threats by 30–40+ percentage points (e.g., 49.6% l1 vs. 8.5–12.1% for AT methods). This gap highlights that AT methods trained only on l∞ adversarial examples generalize poorly to other norm constraints, whereas ADBM's purification approach provides broader threat coverage. On SVHN, ADBM achieves 54.9% average robust accuracy vs. 49.7% for DiffPure and 23.1% for the best AT method (Wang et al., 2023), confirming the advantage generalizes across datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

ADBM builds directly on DiffPure (Nie et al., 2022), which repurposes pre-trained DDPM models as a plug-and-play purification preprocessor, and on the DDIM/SDE frameworks (Song et al., 2020, 2021) for efficient reverse sampling. The key conceptual departure is that DiffPure assumes the diffused adversarial and clean distributions are sufficiently close for the original reverse process to work, whereas ADBM explicitly constructs a 'bridge' trajectory from the diffused adversarial distribution to the clean distribution via a learned coefficient k_t that gradually eliminates adversarial noise. This distinguishes ADBM from existing diffusion bridge models (Bortoli et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023b), which either bridge to a simple prior or directly interpolate between two data distributions and suffer from training collapse in the AP setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that DiffPure's reported robustness drops from ~71% to ~46% under a reliable full-gradient PGD+EOT evaluation is a significant correction to the field's understanding of diffusion-based purification, and the proposed evaluation protocol (200 PGD steps, 20 EOT samples, gradient checkpointing) should become the standard for assessing stochastic pre-processing defenses. ADBM's plug-and-play nature and demonstrated generalization to unseen l1/l2 threats position it as a practical complement to adversarial training, particularly relevant for foundation models where retraining is prohibitive. Open questions include whether the UNet architecture can be substantially downsized for AP, whether the fine-tuning cost is justified in all deployment scenarios, and how ADBM scales to higher-resolution or video inputs.
