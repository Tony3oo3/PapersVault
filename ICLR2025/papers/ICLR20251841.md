## Problem

Despite the empirical success of diffusion models as powerful representation learners, no theoretical analysis has elucidated how feature learning emerges during their training or how it differs from supervised classification. Existing theory for diffusion models is limited to distribution estimation rates and sampling convergence guarantees, leaving the internal feature learning dynamics completely uncharacterized.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251841/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework operates on a multipatch data model where each sample x = [x⁽¹⁾, x⁽²⁾] consists of a label-dependent signal patch (one of two orthogonal vectors μ₁ or μ₋₁) and a label-independent Gaussian noise patch ξ. For diffusion, the network receives a noised version x_t = α_t x_0 + β_t ε_t and outputs a predicted noise vector; for classification, it receives the clean multipatch sample and outputs a scalar score. The key analytical output is the alignment of network weights w with signal and noise directions, quantified as |⟨w, μ_y⟩| and |⟨w, ξ⟩|, which reveals the relative balance of signal versus noise learning.

## Method

The paper analyzes a two-layer convolutional network with quadratic activation under two training objectives: the DDPM denoising loss (with weight-sharing between layers, as in autoencoders) and empirical logistic loss (with fixed ±1 second-layer weights). Using a two-stage gradient descent analysis, they show that diffusion training produces linear growth of both signal and noise inner products, converging to a stationary point where the signal-to-noise learning ratio equals Θ(n·SNR²), indicating balanced feature learning. In contrast, classification training produces exponential growth with a sharp phase transition: when n·SNR² ≥ C̄ the network learns signal exclusively, and when n·SNR² ≤ C it overfits noise exclusively. The critical difference arises because the denoising objective's MSE loss couples signal and noise learning symmetrically, while the logistic loss's multiplicative gradient structure amplifies whichever feature (signal or noise) has the larger initial growth rate, leading to shortcut learning.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical result (Theorem 1.1) shows that diffusion models converge to a stationary point where the signal-to-noise learning ratio equals Θ(n·SNR²), indicating balanced feature learning. In contrast, classification exhibits a sharp phase transition: when n·SNR² ≥ β (a constant > 1), signal learning dominates (ratio = ω(1)), while when n·SNR² < 1/β, noise learning dominates (ratio = o(1)). Empirically on Noisy-MNIST at low SNR (SNR̃=0.1), classification achieves only ~50% test accuracy (memorizing noise), while at high SNR (SNR̃=0.5) it reaches ~100% test accuracy (learning signal). The diffusion model consistently learns both signal and noise to comparable order regardless of SNR, as confirmed by denoised image visualizations and inner-product tracking.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two learning paradigms using the same two-layer quadratic-activation convolutional network architecture: (1) a DDPM diffusion model with weight-shared layers trained on the denoising objective, and (2) a classification model with fixed ±1 second-layer weights trained on logistic loss. The diffusion model consistently 'wins' on feature balance: it learns signal and noise to the same order (ratio = Θ(n·SNR²)) across all SNR regimes, while classification exhibits a sharp phase transition where it exclusively learns either signal or noise depending on whether n·SNR² is above or below a constant threshold. On Noisy-MNIST at low SNR, classification's noise-dominated learning leads to ~50% test accuracy (no generalization), whereas the diffusion model's balanced learning produces meaningful denoised reconstructions of both signal and noise patches. At high SNR, classification successfully learns signal and achieves ~100% test accuracy, but the diffusion model still maintains balanced learning of both components. The comparison reveals that the denoising objective inherently prevents the shortcut learning behavior observed in classification, which has implications for the known adversarial robustness and out-of-distribution generalization advantages of diffusion-based classifiers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the feature learning analysis framework from benign overfitting studies on two-layer convolutional networks (Cao et al., 2022; Kou et al., 2023) to the diffusion model setting, using the same multipatch data model and two-stage gradient descent analysis. For the diffusion side, it draws on weight-tied autoencoder analysis (Nguyen, 2021; Cui & Zdeborova, 2024) and the DDPM training objective (Ho et al., 2020). It departs from the existing diffusion model theory literature, which has focused almost exclusively on distribution estimation rates and sampling convergence guarantees, by being the first to characterize the internal feature learning dynamics during training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that diffusion models learn balanced signal-and-noise features (ratio governed by n·SNR²) while classification exhibits a sharp phase transition toward either signal or noise provides a theoretical explanation for the empirically observed superior OOD generalization and adversarial robustness of diffusion-based classifiers. However, the analysis is restricted to a two-patch, quadratic-activation, weight-sharing setting with very specific scaling conditions, and experiments are limited to MNIST-scale data. Key open questions include whether the balanced-learning property persists in multi-patch, multi-feature, or conditional/latent diffusion settings, and whether it holds under more realistic architectures (e.g., U-Nets, transformers) and training objectives (e.g., flow matching).
