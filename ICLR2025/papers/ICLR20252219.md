## Problem

Previous safe SSL methods conclude that unseen classes in unlabeled data harm SSL model performance, but their evaluation methodology is fundamentally flawed. By fixing the total unlabeled dataset size and adjusting the unseen-class proportion, they simultaneously reduce seen-class samples, creating a confounding factor that makes it impossible to attribute performance changes solely to the presence of unseen classes. This paper identifies this methodological flaw via a structural causal model and proposes a corrected evaluation framework that properly isolates the effect of unseen classes.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a labeled set D_L, a seen-class unlabeled pool D_B^S, and an unseen-class unlabeled pool D_B^U, then constructs training sets by fixing the seen-class sampling ratio r_s while independently varying the unseen-class ratio r_u across {0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.8, 1.0}. Beyond the primary r factor, it also varies four additional dimensions: number of unseen categories (C_n), category indices (C_i), nearness (near OOD from the same dataset vs. far OOD from MNIST), and label imbalance factor (C_ib). The output is per-r accuracy values plus five derived robustness metrics (R_slope, GM, WAD, BAD, P_AD≥0) that jointly characterize global and local sensitivity to unseen classes.

## Method

The RE-SSL framework's core insight is that previous evaluations conflate the effect of adding unseen-class samples with the effect of removing seen-class samples, a confounding factor formalized through a structural causal model. To eliminate this confound, RE-SSL fixes r_s (the fraction of seen-class unlabeled data) and independently varies r_u (the fraction of unseen-class unlabeled data), so the total unlabeled set size changes rather than being held constant. The framework defines five metrics—R_slope (linear regression slope of accuracy vs. r), GM (integral of |Acc − mean Acc| over r), WAD (minimum adjacent accuracy difference), BAD (maximum adjacent accuracy difference), and P_AD≥0 (probability of non-negative adjacent change)—to capture both global robustness (overall trend) and local robustness (worst- and best-case adjacent drops). It evaluates 15 SSL methods (10 classical + 5 safe SSL) across 8 datasets spanning image, tabular, and text modalities, using ResNet-50 as backbone with the LAMDA-SSL toolkit and three random seeds per configuration.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 (100 labels), the most robust SSL methods to unseen classes are ICT (R_slope = 0.002, GM = 0.007) and UASD (R_slope = 0.000, GM = 0.005), showing virtually no performance degradation as unseen-class ratio r increases from 0 to 1. In contrast, FixMatch (R_slope = -0.223, GM = 0.386) and FreeMatch (R_slope = -0.287, GM = 0.496) suffer severe accuracy drops, with FixMatch falling from 61.2% to 36.5% and FreeMatch from 76.0% to 44.0%. On CIFAR-100 (1000 labels), the same pattern holds: ICT (R_slope = -0.002) and UASD (R_slope = -0.002) remain robust, while FixMatch (R_slope = -0.244) and Fix_A_Step (R_slope = -0.188) degrade substantially. The key finding is that under proper variable control (fixing seen-class quantity), unseen classes do not universally harm SSL models—some methods even show slight improvements.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is an evaluation framework (RE-SSL) rather than a new SSL method, so the comparisons assess which existing SSL algorithms are robust to unseen classes under proper variable control. ICT and UASD emerge as the most robust across all datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet-100, CUB, Forest, Letter, AGNews), with R_slope values near zero and GM below 0.01, indicating that adding unseen classes has essentially no impact on their seen-class accuracy. FixMatch is consistently the most vulnerable, with R_slope of -0.223 (CIFAR-10) and -0.244 (CIFAR-100), dropping from 61.2% to 36.5% accuracy on CIFAR-10 as r goes from 0 to 1. Surprisingly, the safe SSL methods OpenMatch (R_slope = -0.211) and Fix_A_Step (R_slope = -0.138) are far less robust than expected, performing worse than simpler methods like PseudoLabel (R_slope = -0.020) and PiModel (R_slope = -0.034). The paper attributes FixMatch's vulnerability to its fixed confidence threshold, while ICT's robustness stems from its MixUp-based interpolation consistency that naturally mitigates unseen-class interference. Among safe SSL methods, UASD (leveraging temporal ensemble predictions) and CAFA (based on PiModel + MixUp) demonstrate the strongest robustness, while MTCF shows moderate robustness through its curriculum-based OOD filtering.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the safe SSL literature (DS3L, Safe-Student, OpenMatch, Fix_A_Step, MTCF, CAFA, UASD) by identifying a fundamental methodological flaw in how those works evaluated the impact of unseen classes: fixing total unlabeled set size while varying the unseen-class proportion simultaneously reduces seen-class samples, introducing a confounding factor. Drawing on structural causal modeling (Peters et al., 2017), the authors formalize this confound and propose the RE-SSL framework, which holds the seen-class ratio constant and varies only the unseen-class ratio. Rather than introducing a new SSL algorithm, the paper repurposes the existing safe SSL methods as evaluation subjects under a corrected experimental protocol, adding five robustness metrics and five factor dimensions (sample count, category count, category index, nearness, label distribution) that prior evaluations did not systematically isolate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If the widely-cited conclusion that 'unseen classes damage SSL performance' was partly an artifact of uncontrolled variables, then the design motivation for many safe SSL methods (aggressive filtering, reweighting) may be overstated, and practitioners should re-examine whether such mechanisms are necessary or even harmful. The paper's finding that methods like ICT and UASD are nearly immune while FixMatch and FreeMatch remain sensitive provides actionable guidance for algorithm selection in open-world settings. Key limitations include the absence of theoretical analysis, the restriction to unseen-class-only shifts (no concurrent seen-class distribution shift), and the use of a single backbone (ResNet-50), leaving open whether conclusions generalize to transformers or other architectures. Future work should extend the framework to compound distribution shifts and provide formal robustness guarantees.
