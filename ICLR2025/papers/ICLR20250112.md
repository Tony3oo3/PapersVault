## Problem

LLM-based pairwise evaluation is widely used as a scalable substitute for human annotation, but it provides no formal guarantee that a judge model's preference aligns with human judgement. Even the strongest judges (e.g., GPT-4) exhibit systematic biases and over-confidence, yet current practice simply selects the most expensive model heuristically. The paper asks: how can we provably guarantee that an LLM judge's evaluation agrees with humans at a user-specified level, while also controlling evaluation cost?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a query paired with two candidate generations (a1, a2) and a small calibration set D_cal of human preference labels (|D_cal| = 500 in experiments). For each test instance, it outputs either a preference label (a1 ≻ a2 or a2 ≻ a1) or abstention (∅), along with a calibrated confidence threshold λ̂ derived from the calibration set. The cascade structure means different judge models (e.g., Mistral-7B → GPT-3.5 → GPT-4) handle different subsets of instances, with the user specifying only a risk tolerance α and error level δ rather than manually choosing which model to use.

## Method

The framework has three core components. First, a selective evaluator (f_LM, c_LM) outputs a preference only when a confidence score c_LM(x) ≥ λ, otherwise abstains; the threshold λ̂ is found via fixed-sequence testing (Bauer, 1991) over a calibration set, yielding a provable guarantee that P(f_LM(x) = y_human | c_LM(x) ≥ λ̂) ≥ 1−α with probability at least 1−δ. Second, Simulated Annotators estimates confidence by K-shot prompting the judge N times with different annotator-specific in-context examples and computing the agreement ratio across simulated annotators, which significantly reduces expected calibration error (by ~50% for GPT-4) compared to predictive probability or verbalized confidence. Third, Cascaded Selective Evaluation chains multiple judge models from cheapest to most expensive, calibrating each model's threshold only on instances abstained by all prior models, and applying a union bound across cascade levels to maintain the overall risk guarantee while routing ~88% of evaluations to substantially cheaper models.

## Results

On ChatArena (target agreement 85%), Cascaded Selective Evaluation achieves 91.0% guarantee success rate with 63.2% coverage, using only 17.5% GPT-4 calls (23.7% Mistral-7B, 58.8% GPT-3.5), while GPT-4 without abstention achieves 0% guarantee success rate. On TL;DR (target agreement 90%), the method reaches 90.8% guarantee success rate at 55.7% coverage. For confidence estimation, Simulated Annotators reduces ECE by 50% (from 0.217 to 0.095) and improves AUROC by 13% (from 0.642 to 0.723) over predictive probability with GPT-4 on AlpacaEval, and similarly improves weaker judges like Mistral-7B (ECE from 0.374 to 0.075).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Verbalized Confidence (Tian et al., 2023), Simulated Annotators substantially outperforms on all calibration metrics: ECE drops from 0.215 to 0.095 and AUROC rises from 0.550 to 0.723 for GPT-4 on AlpacaEval, with even larger gains for weaker judges (Mistral-7B ECE from 0.414 to 0.075). Against Point-Estimate Calibration (Ramirez et al., 2024), Cascaded Selective Evaluation achieves 91.0% guarantee success rate versus 54.4% for Point-Estimate with GPT-4 on ChatArena, while maintaining comparable coverage (63.2% vs. 60.9%). Against Semantic Sets (Lin et al., 2024) and Semantic Entropy (Kuhn et al., 2023), Simulated Annotators outperforms on both AUROC and AUPRC for Mistral-7B (e.g., AUPRC 0.772 vs. 0.638 and 0.684 on AlpacaEval) while incurring far lower API cost (6.9x vs. 20.4x relative cost). The paper also shows that even the weaker cascade (Mistral-7B + Mixtral-8×7B + GPT-3.5, no GPT-4) guarantees 80.3% human agreement at 68.3% coverage with only 12.6% of GPT-4's API cost, outperforming zero-shot GPT-4 (77.8% agreement, 13.9% guarantee success rate) on the same target.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the selective classification framework (Geifman & El-Yaniv, 2017) to the LLM-as-judge setting, replacing the standard Bonferroni correction with fixed-sequence testing (Bauer, 1991) to calibrate abstention thresholds, and builds on the distribution-free risk-control theory of Bates et al. (2021) to derive a high-probability guarantee on selective disagreement risk. It departs from conformal prediction approaches for LLMs (Yadkori et al., 2024; Gui et al., 2024) that provide only marginal (in-expectation) risk control, offering instead an exact upper bound conditional on the calibration set. The Simulated Annotators confidence measure is novel, drawing on in-context learning to simulate inter-annotator variability rather than relying on predictive probability or verbalized confidence, which the paper shows are systematically over-confident for preference tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first provable guarantee that LLM-based evaluation aligns with human judgement at a user-specified level, fundamentally changing the reliability calculus for LLM-as-judge pipelines. The practical impact is significant: practitioners can replace expensive GPT-4 calls with cheaper models (Mistral-7B, GPT-3.5) for the majority of evaluations while maintaining the same guaranteed agreement, reducing costs by up to 87%. Key limitations include the requirement for a calibration set with human preferences, restriction to pairwise (not absolute-score) evaluation, and focus on subjective domains. Open questions include extending the framework to reasoning verification, integrating distilled expert judges, and handling settings where no human calibration data is available.
