## Problem

Existing sample selection methods for machine learning rely primarily on local information such as training difficulty and node degree, overlooking the global connectivity patterns inherent in the sample graph. This oversight leads to suboptimal selections that fail to preserve the structural properties of the dataset. A key obstacle is that structural entropy—a metric well-suited to capturing global graph structure—only yields a single value for the entire graph, making it intractable to decompose to individual nodes for fine-grained selection.

## Input / Output

The method consumes precomputed sample embeddings (CLIP for images, Sentence-BERT for text, concatenated for VQA pairs), a target sampling rate, and per-sample training difficulty scores (e.g., AUM, Variance, AL-MDN uncertainty, or Instruction score depending on the task). It constructs a kNN-graph with cosine-similarity edge weights normalized to [0,1], then outputs a subset of samples at the specified rate. In active learning, labels are absent during selection and a self-supervised model (SwAV) replaces CLIP for embedding extraction; in continual learning, the method selects replay-memory samples from each completed task.

## Method

SES first builds a kNN-graph over sample embeddings, then constructs an encoding tree (via a Huffman-tree variant) that captures the graph's hierarchical community structure. The key technical contribution is a reformulation of the Shapley value for structural entropy (Proposition 1) that reduces the decomposition from exponential to linear time in the number of edges, yielding a node-level structural entropy S_e(u) = (1/vol(V)) Σ w_{u,v} log vol(α_{u∨v}), where α_{u∨v} is the least common ancestor of u and v in the encoding tree. This global metric is multiplied by a local training-difficulty score S_t(u) to produce an importance score S(u) = S_e(u)·S_t(u). Finally, an importance-biased blue noise sampling procedure iteratively selects the highest-scoring candidate while rejecting any sample whose similarity to an already-selected neighbor exceeds a threshold θ (found via binary search), ensuring both informativeness and spatial diversity in the final subset.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K supervised learning, SES achieves 72.80% top-1 accuracy at 70% sampling rate (vs. 73.63% full data) and 13.43% at 1%, consistently outperforming all 13 baselines at every sampling rate. On ANLI text classification, SES reaches 49.00% at 70% (vs. 49.25% full) and 38.16% at 1%. In active learning on ImageNet-1K (unlabeled pool), SES achieves 72.11% at 70% and 6.69% at 1%, surpassing the best baseline (Prototypicality, 4.95%) by 1.74 points at 1%. In continual learning, SES attains 68.26% average accuracy on Split CIFAR-10 with memory size 100, exceeding the best baseline (Greedy Coreset, 63.18%) by 5.08 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SES consistently outperforms all 23 baselines across supervised learning, active learning, and continual learning. Against the strongest general-purpose baseline D² Pruning (ICLR 2024), SES gains 2.78 points at 10% sampling on ImageNet-1K (53.59 vs. 50.81) and 2.20 points at 1% (13.43 vs. 11.23); the gap widens to 4.31 points at 5% (41.88 vs. 37.12). Coverage-oriented methods like CCS (ICLR 2022) and k-DPP are competitive at high sampling rates but fall behind SES by 3.20 and 7.49 points respectively at 10% on ImageNet-1K. Difficulty-based methods (EL2N, AUM, Forgetting) degrade sharply at low sampling rates — EL2N drops to 12.57% at 10% on ImageNet-1K versus SES's 53.59% — because they neglect easy samples critical for training. In active learning (unlabeled pool), SES outperforms Prototypicality by 12.83 points at 10% (48.10 vs. 35.27) and 1.74 points at 1% (6.69 vs. 4.95), demonstrating that structural entropy compensates for missing labels. In continual learning, SES surpasses iCaRL by 5.93 points on Split CIFAR-10 (68.26 vs. 62.33, memory 100) and Greedy Coreset by 1.43 points on Split CIFAR-100 (57.60 vs. 56.17), with the largest margin on Split Tiny-ImageNet (20.80 vs. 19.76 for GraphCut).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the data selection literature by introducing a global structural metric where prior methods relied exclusively on local signals (training difficulty, node degree). It builds on Li & Pan's (2016) structural entropy for graph-level community quantification and Shapley's (1951) fair decomposition framework, reformulating the Shapley value for structural entropy into a linear-time computation (Proposition 1) that was previously intractable. The sampling stage adapts blue noise sampling from Xiang et al. (2019) into an importance-biased variant, and the theoretical grounding for why the global metric helps draws on the sample coverage framework of Zheng et al. (2022). The key departure from D² Pruning, CCS, and GraphCut is the explicit incorporation of connectivity-pattern information at the node level rather than only pairwise or degree-based local features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The linear-time Shapley reformulation makes a previously exponential-time global graph metric practically usable for node-level selection, and the consistent gains across supervised, active, and continual learning scenarios suggest the global-structure signal is broadly transferable. The active learning result is particularly notable: when labels are unavailable and most baselines degrade below random, structural entropy still captures useful community structure. Open questions include automating the three hyperparameters (k, β, γ) that currently require per-dataset grid search, scaling to billion-sample datasets (e.g., DataComp) where even O(n log n) becomes costly, and extending the embedding-based graph construction to richer multimodal settings beyond simple concatenation.
