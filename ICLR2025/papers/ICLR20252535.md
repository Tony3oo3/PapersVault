## Problem

Multi-Layer Perceptrons are universal approximators but their internal representations are opaque, making them poorly suited for extracting interpretable symbolic structure from scientific data. The Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem guarantees that any multivariate continuous function decomposes into univariate functions and addition, but was long dismissed as practically useless because the required 1D functions can be non-smooth or fractal. This paper asks whether generalizing the KA representation to arbitrary depths and widths yields smooth, learnable, and interpretable network architectures that also match or exceed MLP accuracy.

## Input / Output

KANs consume numerical input vectors of arbitrary dimension (1D to 100D in experiments), 2D spatial coordinates for image/PDE tasks, 17 knot invariants, or flattened 784-dim MNIST pixels, and produce scalar function values, PDE solutions, fitted images, knot signature one-hot vectors, or class labels. A key structural detail is that each edge is a learnable 1D B-spline defined on a discrete grid of G points with spline order k (typically k=3), so the effective 'weight' is a piecewise-polynomial curve rather than a scalar. The grid is initialized on [-1,1] and updated on the fly to track activation ranges during training.

## Method

KANs replace MLP's fixed node activations and scalar edge weights with learnable 1D B-spline functions on edges and simple summation on nodes, so a KAN layer is a matrix of 1D functions Φ = {φ_{q,p}} rather than a weight matrix followed by a nonlinearity. Each edge function is parameterized as φ(x) = w_b·SiLU(x) + w_s·spline(x), where the SiLU residual prevents pathological high-order polynomial behavior in deep stacks and the B-spline coefficients are the trainable parameters. The network shape is specified by an integer array [n_0, n_1, …, n_L], and the full KAN is a composition of L such function-matrix layers. Two key training techniques are introduced: (1) grid extension, which progressively refines the spline grid from coarse (G=3) to fine (G=100) to avoid bad loss landscapes, and (2) sparsity regularization followed by pruning and symbolification to recover interpretable symbolic formulas. The approximation theory (Theorem 2.1) shows that when a smooth KA representation exists, the C^m error scales as G^{-(k+1-m)}, independent of input dimension, yielding a scaling exponent α = k+1 that outperforms MLP scaling laws.

## Results

On image fitting (Table 1), KAN2 (finer input grid G=100) achieves PSNR of 45.76 (Cameraman), 82.68 (Turbulence), and 71.82 (Starry Night), substantially outperforming MLP2 (22.09/33.14/18.96) and SIREN2 (30.79/51.94/53.05) at comparable parameter counts. On the knot theory task, a tiny [17,1,14] KAN (~200 parameters) reaches 81.6% test accuracy versus 78% for DeepMind's 4-layer width-300 MLP (~3×10⁵ parameters). In PDE solving, KANs achieve steeper scaling laws (matching the theoretical α=4 exponent) and lower L²/H¹ losses than MLPs, though at roughly 10× the training wall time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against standard MLPs (Hornik et al., 1989), KANs dominate on every image-fitting metric: KAN2 beats MLP2 by 23.7 dB on Cameraman, 49.5 dB on Turbulence, and 52.9 dB on Starry Night, while using the same number of parameters. The advantage is even more pronounced in function fitting and PDE solving, where KANs achieve the theoretical scaling exponent α=4 (ℓ ∝ N⁻⁴) while MLPs plateau quickly. Against SIREN (Sitzmann et al., 2020), KAN2 outperforms SIREN2 by 15.0/30.7/18.8 dB on the three images, confirming that learnable spline edges surpass fixed sinusoidal activations. Against DeepMind's knot-invariant MLP (Davies et al., 2021), the [17,1,14] KAN achieves 81.6% vs 78% test accuracy with ~1500× fewer parameters, and additionally reveals interpretable variable dependencies without feature-attribution tools. The consistent trade-off is training speed: KANs require roughly 10× the wall time of MLPs due to recursive B-spline evaluation, a limitation the authors acknowledge as the main barrier to scaling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper revives the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem (1957/1963), which had long been dismissed as 'theoretically sound but practically useless' due to the pathological non-smoothness of its 1D component functions. Prior attempts at Kolmogorov networks (Sprecher & Draghici 2002, Köppen 2002, Lin & Unbehauen 1993, Fakhoury et al. 2022) largely stuck to the original depth-2, width-(2n+1) architecture and lacked modern training techniques. The key conceptual departure here is generalizing to arbitrary depths and widths, parameterizing each edge function as a learnable B-spline (building on De Boor's classical spline theory), and combining this with backpropagation, grid extension, and sparsity regularization to make the architecture both trainable and interpretable. The paper positions KANs as a complement to MLPs rather than a replacement: MLPs handle high-dimensional feature learning well but lack interpretability, while KANs excel at low-dimensional smooth functions with transparent structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

KANs offer a principled path toward interpretable neural networks for scientific discovery, demonstrated by rediscovering known relations in knot theory and Anderson localization with networks ~400× smaller than MLP baselines. The theoretical scaling law (α = k+1, independent of input dimension) is appealing but relies on the strong assumption that a smooth KA representation exists, which is not guaranteed for arbitrary functions. Major open questions include: (1) scalability to large-scale tasks (MNIST results show KANs are merely comparable to MLPs, not superior), (2) training instability at large grid sizes and depths in PDE solving, (3) the fundamental duality between KANs and MLPs (the paper acknowledges KANs can be rewritten as MLPs and vice versa), and (4) whether the interpretability advantage persists in high-dimensional, noisy, real-world settings. The slower training speed (2^k factor from recursive spline evaluation) remains a practical barrier to adoption.
