## Problem

LLM evaluation has predominantly assessed individual capabilities (reasoning, coding, tool use, etc.) in isolation, yet real-world tasks routinely demand the simultaneous integration of multiple distinct skills. This paper asks how cross-capability performance—tasks requiring two or more capabilities at once—relates to the individual capability scores, and whether current LLMs exhibit systematic patterns (synergy, compensation, or bottleneck) when combining abilities.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes single-turn, open-ended prompts spanning text, images, code snippets, and long documents (16K–128K tokens), each tagged with capability, difficulty (10% easy / 30% medium / 60% hard), and taxonomy category. The LLM evaluator receives the prompt, up to three human-annotated reference responses with 1–5 Likert scores and explanatory paragraphs, and the target response; it then outputs structured point deductions per identified issue rather than a direct score, which are aggregated into a final 1–5 rating. This multi-reference, deduction-based format avoids the self-preference and length-bias problems of standard LLM-as-a-Judge setups.

## Method

The paper introduces CROSSEVAL, a 1,400-prompt benchmark covering 7 individual and 7 cross capabilities, each organized under a two-level manual taxonomy (76 L1 and 332 L2 categories) built from real user queries. For evaluation, the authors replace the common self-generated-answer prompting with a multi-references-based strategy (providing low/medium/high-quality scored examples) combined with point-deduction-based scoring, achieving Pearson correlations of ~0.70 with human judgments—outperforming BigGen Bench's 0.627. The central empirical finding is the 'Law of the Weakest Link': across 58 cross-capability scores from 17 models, 38 fall below both individual capabilities and 20 sit between them but skew toward the weaker one, with no score approaching the stronger capability. To test causality, the authors apply principle-based system prompting (100-iteration refinement by GPT-4o) to selectively boost one capability and show that improving the weaker component yields substantially larger cross-capability gains than improving the stronger one, confirming the bottleneck effect is not merely correlational.

## Results

Across 17 LLMs evaluated on CROSSEVAL (1,400 prompts, 14 capabilities), the average individual-capability score is 65.72 versus 58.67 for cross-capabilities, confirming a systematic performance gap. Of 58 cross-capability scores where a clear strong/weak split exists (Δ>3), 38 fall below both individual capabilities and 20 land between them but closer to the weaker one—no cross score approaches the stronger capability. GPT-4o, selected as the final LLM evaluator, achieves overall Pearson(r)=0.697, Spearman(ρₛ)=0.679, and Kendall(τ)=0.560 against human ratings. Tool Use is the weakest individual capability, with even Llama 3.1 405B scoring only 47.90, and cross-capabilities involving Tool Use (e.g., Tool Use & Coding at 45.80 for GPT-4o) are the lowest-scoring tasks overall.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparisons concern evaluation methodology rather than model-vs-model performance. Against Chatbot Arena (Zheng et al., 2023), CROSSEVAL uses a finer 1–5 Likert scale (vs. binary or 1–3 scale) and still achieves 54.93% inter-rater agreement with Krippendorff's α=0.76, compared to Chatbot Arena's 81% (binary) and 63% (1–3 scale). Against BIGGEN Bench (Kim et al., 2024), CROSSEVAL's LLM evaluator reaches Pearson r≈0.70 with human ratings, exceeding BIGGEN's reported 0.627 with gold references. Among LLM evaluators, GPT-4o (Pearson 0.697) marginally outperforms Claude 3.5 Sonnet (0.696) and clearly surpasses GPT-4o mini (0.621) and Llama 3.1 405B (0.452–0.734 range), leading the authors to select GPT-4o as the default judge. The empirical "Law of the Weakest Link" finding is consistent across all 17 evaluated models and both GPT- and Claude-based evaluators, with no model or evaluator showing a compensatory or synergistic pattern that would contradict the weakest-link effect.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm (Zheng et al., 2023; MT-Bench/Chatbot Arena) by replacing self-generated answers with multi-reference examples and point-deduction prompting, and departs from individual-capability benchmarks (MMLU, HumanEval, ARC) by introducing the concept of cross-capabilities—the intersection of multiple distinct skills required for real-world tasks. It builds on Llama 3's capability taxonomy for defining individual capabilities but adds a cross-capability dimension informed by real user query frequency analysis. The evaluation methodology also draws on BiGBench Bench's multi-ability approach but explicitly targets the interaction between capabilities rather than assessing them in isolation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 'Law of the Weakest Link' finding provides a concrete diagnostic for LLM development: improving the weakest component of a cross-capability pair yields the largest gains, while strengthening already-strong capabilities has minimal effect on combined performance. This reframes the optimization priority for LLM teams from 'maximize all capabilities' to 'identify and fix the bottleneck.' Key limitations include using only Spanish as the multilingual representative (findings may not generalize to typologically distant languages), covering only 7 of many possible cross-capability pairs, and focusing primarily on text with image recognition as a single multimodal proxy. Open questions include whether the effect persists at larger scales, with more diverse modalities, and whether architectural changes (e.g., mixture-of-experts routing) could break the weakest-link constraint.
