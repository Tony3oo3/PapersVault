## Problem

Prior work evaluating LLM forecasting capabilities relies on static benchmarks of already-resolved questions, which become obsolete as model knowledge cut-offs advance and are vulnerable to data contamination or benchmark overfitting. No framework exists for evaluating ML systems on a standardized set of forecasting questions that are guaranteed to have no known answer at the time of submission, making continuous, contamination-free comparison of frontier models impossible.

## Input / Output

Each forecasting question is binary and sourced from prediction markets (Metaculus, Polymarket, Manifold, RFI) or real-world datasets (ACLED, FRED, DBnomics, Wikipedia, Yahoo Finance). Dataset questions require forecasts at 8 distinct time horizons (7 days to 10 years), while market questions require a single probability. Half the LLM question set consists of combination questions that require estimating all four joint probabilities (P(Q1∩Q2), P(Q1∩¬Q2), P(¬Q1∩Q2), P(¬Q1∩¬Q2)), testing the model's ability to reason about event dependence. LLMs receive a 24-hour window to produce forecasts on all 1,000 questions, with missing forecasts imputed as the crowd value (market) or 0.5 (dataset).

## Method

ForecastBench is an automated system that nightly ingests new questions from nine sources, filters for quality using GPT-4o-mini, and maintains a growing question bank of ~6,400 standard questions plus millions of two-question combinations. Every two weeks, 1,000 questions are sampled (equal across sources, uniform across categories) and released to LLM teams and human forecasters, with the human set being a 200-question random subset. The evaluation implements seven baselines ranging from zero-shot prompting to scratchpad prompting with retrieved news articles and crowd freeze values, plus a 9-forecast ensemble (GPT-4o, Claude-3.5-Sonnet, Gemini-1.5-Pro × 3 superforecaster-crafted prompts) aggregated via median, geometric mean, or geometric mean of log odds. Resolution is continuous: market questions are scored against crowd aggregates nightly until official resolution, while dataset questions resolve as new data becomes available. The key design choice—using only unresolved questions—eliminates data contamination by construction, since no training data can contain the answer to a question whose outcome is still undetermined.

## Results

On the 200-question human subset, superforecasters achieve an overall Brier score of 0.096, significantly outperforming the top LLM (Claude-3.5-Sonnet with freeze values and scratchpad prompting: Brier = 0.122, p < 0.001) and the general public median (Brier = 0.121, p < 0.001). The top LLM without access to crowd forecasts scores 0.136, indicating that human crowd information is a major performance driver. On the full 1,000-question LLM set (including combination questions), Claude-3.5-Sonnet leads with an overall Brier of 0.123, while the gap to superforecasters widens substantially on combination questions (superforecasters: 0.076 vs. GPT-4o: 0.130).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 17 LLMs against two human baselines (general public and superforecasters) on a 200-question subset. Claude-3.5-Sonnet (Anthropic) is the top LLM performer with an overall Brier of 0.122 using freeze values and scratchpad prompting, but it still loses significantly to superforecasters (0.096, p < 0.001) and roughly ties the general public median (0.121). GPT-4-Turbo (OpenAI) achieves 0.128 with zero-shot prompting and freeze values, while GPT-4 scores 0.132 and GPT-4o scores 0.133 with news retrieval and freeze values. Gemini-1.5-Pro (Google) and Qwen1.5-110B-Chat trail further behind at 0.134 and 0.142 respectively on the full 1,000-question set. A critical finding is that access to human crowd forecasts (freeze values) is essential: the best LLM without crowd forecasts (Claude-3.5-Sonnet, Brier 0.136) underperforms all models that receive them, and adding retrieved news articles does not improve performance over freeze values alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the static LLM forecasting benchmarks of Halawi et al. (2024), Yan et al. (2024), and Zou et al. (2022) by making the evaluation dynamic: questions are drawn from unresolved future events, eliminating data contamination by construction. It also departs from Schoenegger et al. (2024b), whose one-time LLM ensemble tournament suggested parity with human crowds, by using a much larger and continuously refreshed question set that enables precise statistical comparisons. The introduction of combination questions (Boolean conjunctions of independent events) is a novel evaluation dimension not present in prior forecasting benchmarks, testing whether models can reason about joint event probabilities rather than marginal ones.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ForecastBench provides the first contamination-free, continuously updated benchmark for LLM forecasting, making it the natural standard for tracking progress as model knowledge cutoffs shift. The finding that superforecasters significantly outperform the best LLM (Brier 0.096 vs. 0.122, p < 0.001) even with retrieval augmentation and crowd forecasts, and that the gap widens substantially on combination questions, identifies concrete failure modes for future research. Open questions include whether the observed linear relationship between Arena scores/training compute and forecasting accuracy will persist, how to close the combination-question gap, and whether the 200-question human subset is sufficient for stable cross-round comparisons as the benchmark scales.
