## Problem

When quadratic approximations of the loss landscape (Hessian, GGN, FIM) are computed on mini-batches rather than the full training set, their geometry is systematically distorted: curvature is overestimated by roughly an order of magnitude in the top-curvature subspace. This bias causes second-order optimizers to take overly small or even directionally wrong Newton steps, and makes Laplace approximation uncertainty estimates overconfident, yet the phenomenon and its mechanism had not been formally identified or addressed.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two independently drawn mini-batches of training data (e.g., two batches of 512 instead of one of 1024) along with the model parameters at a given point and a curvature proxy (GGN, FIM, or K-FAC). The first batch defines the parameter subspace (eigenvectors or CG search directions), while the second provides unbiased directional slope and curvature measurements within that subspace. The output is either a debiased CG update trajectory (for optimization) or a debiased posterior covariance matrix Σ_B̃ (for the Laplace approximation), from which predictive uncertainty is obtained via Monte Carlo sampling through the linearized network.

## Method

The paper identifies the curvature bias as an instance of regression to the mean: eigenvectors of one mini-batch's curvature matrix are 'extreme' directions for that batch but less extreme for other batches, so evaluating curvature along them on the same batch overestimates it due to eigenspace misalignment (formalized via the overlap weights Ω_{i,p} = (u_i^T Û_p)²). The debiasing strategy decouples directions from magnitudes using a two-batch scheme: for CG, search directions are collected from mini-batch B but update magnitudes τ̃_p are recomputed on a different mini-batch B̃; for K-FAC LA, the eigenspace U from K_B is retained while eigenvalues are re-evaluated as λ̃_p = u_p^T K_B̃ u_p. This roughly doubles the per-iteration cost (one extra Hessian-vector product for CG, one extra K-FAC computation for LA), so experiments use half the batch size per batch for a fair comparison. The debiased CG is shown to be far more stable than single-batch CG without curvature damping, and the debiased K-FAC LA closely mimics full-batch LA performance across prior precisions using only a tiny fraction of the data.

## Results

On CIFAR-100 with ALL-CNN-C, debiased CG (two mini-batches of 512) remains stable over 30 iterations without curvature damping, while single-batch CG (one mini-batch of 1024) diverges quickly after reaching peak performance. On CIFAR-10 with ALL-CNN-C, the debiased K-FAC Laplace approximation closely mimics the full-batch LA (N=40,000) across the entire range of prior precisions (10⁻⁴ to 10) in terms of accuracy, NLL, and ECE, whereas the single-batch K-FAC LA degrades drastically at low prior precision. The debiased methods use half the batch size of their single-batch counterparts, achieving comparable or better performance at similar computational cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against single-batch Hessian-free CG (Martens, 2010), the debiased CG wins decisively on stability: single-batch runs diverge within a few iterations without curvature damping, while debiased runs remain stable throughout 30 CG steps. However, single-batch CG achieves slightly better peak performance (attributed to its more informative search directions computed on double the data). Against single-batch K-FAC LA (Ritter et al., 2018; Daxberger et al., 2021), the debiased LA wins on robustness: the single-batch version's accuracy and NLL drop drastically when the prior precision is low (i.e., when the LA relies mainly on curvature information), and it is far more sensitive to the choice of prior precision. The debiased LA, by contrast, maintains good performance over a wide range of prior precisions and closely tracks the full-batch K-FAC LA (the gold standard computed on all 40,000 training examples) despite using only a tiny fraction of the data. The paper also notes that the debiased approach's peak performance could be further improved by allocating a larger batch to direction computation and a smaller one to magnitude estimation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the conjugate gradient method (Hestenes & Stiefel, 1952) and K-FAC curvature approximation (Martens & Grosse, 2015), identifying a previously unrecognized systematic bias that arises when eigenvectors and directional derivatives are computed on the same mini-batch. It departs from prior work that treated mini-batch gradient and Hessian as simply unbiased estimates of their full-batch counterparts, showing that the *geometry* of the resulting quadratic (its eigenspace structure and directional curvatures) is systematically distorted. The paper distinguishes itself from EKFAC (George et al., 2018), which corrects a different, K-FAC-specific eigenvalue bias, and from earlier suggestions to use separate mini-batches for gradient and Hessian (Martens, 2010; Byrd et al., 2011), which do not resolve the curvature bias identified here.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that mini-batch quadratics overestimate top-curvature by roughly one order of magnitude and cause CG updates in the wrong direction has broad implications for any stochastic second-order method or Laplace-based uncertainty estimate used in safety-critical applications. The proposed two-batch debiasing strategies are simple and require only ~2× computational cost, but the paper notes open questions about optimal batch-size allocation between directions and magnitudes, interaction with running-average curvature estimators, and whether the bias worsens further for deeper/wider models (which Appendix B.10 suggests it does). Scaling the debiased LA to LLM-scale models remains an open challenge, as even the single-batch version is already expensive at that scale.
