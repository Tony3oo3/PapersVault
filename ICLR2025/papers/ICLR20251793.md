## Problem

It is unknown whether the implicit models of human decision-making held by LLMs align with how people actually behave or with idealized rational models. Prior evidence that LLMs produce 'human-like' behavior may be confounded by the well-documented human tendency to assume others act rationally, making it difficult to distinguish genuine behavioral alignment from shared rationality bias. This paper asks: do LLMs predict and simulate human choices accurately, or do they simply assume people are more rational than they are?

## Input / Output

For forward modeling, each input is a risky choice problem specifying two gambles with outcome probabilities and dollar payoffs (drawn from 9,831 problems in choices13k); outputs are a binary choice (A/B), a predicted proportion of participants selecting each option, or a simulated individual decision repeated n times to estimate a probability. For inverse modeling, each input is an observed decision consisting of 1–5 groups of items (from a fixed set of 47 structurally unique decisions) with one group marked as chosen; outputs are pairwise comparisons across all C(47,2) pairs, aggregated into a full ranking of how strongly each decision implies preference for a target item, evaluated in both positive (candy) and negative (electric shock) contexts.

## Method

The paper evaluates five LLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-4-Turbo, Llama-3-8B/70B, Claude 3 Opus) under zero-shot and chain-of-thought prompting across two psychology paradigms. In forward modeling, LLM predictions are correlated (Spearman/Pearson) and compared via MSE against both actual human choices and the maximum-expected-value rational baseline; additionally, 18 behavioral models (heuristic, counterfactual, and subjective expected utility) are fitted to zero-shot GPT-4 outputs to characterize its implicit decision model. In inverse modeling, LLMs perform pairwise comparisons over 47 observed decisions, and the resulting rankings are correlated against human rankings and four rational-model predictions (absolute utility, relative utility, likelihood, and marginal likelihood) derived from a Bayesian Luce choice model. The key finding is that CoT prompting drives LLMs toward near-perfect correlation with expected value (0.94 for GPT-4o) while only moderately correlating with human choices (0.61), and that in inverse modeling LLM inferences correlate as highly with humans (0.97) as with rational models (0.95) because humans themselves assume rationality when interpreting others' behavior.

## Results

In the forward modeling task on choices13k (9,831 problems), GPT-4o with CoT achieves a Spearman correlation of 0.94 with the maximum expected value model, while actual humans only achieve 0.48, demonstrating that LLMs assume people are far more rational than they are. GPT-4o's correlation with actual human choices is only 0.61 (CoT) or 0.48 (zero-shot), well below its correlation with rationality. In the inverse modeling task (47 decisions), GPT-4o with CoT achieves a 0.97 Spearman correlation with human inferences and 0.95/0.94 with absolute/relative utility rational models, while the weakest model (Llama-3-8B zero-shot) drops to 0.20/0.23 with rational models. Correlations with rational models increase monotonically with model capability and the use of chain-of-thought reasoning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against actual human choices (Peterson et al., 2021 / Bourgin et al., 2019, choices13k), all LLMs are poor predictors: the best zero-shot model (GPT-4-Turbo) reaches only 0.60 Spearman, and CoT prompting does not improve this (GPT-4o drops to 0.61). Critically, with CoT, LLMs correlate far more with the rational expected-value model (0.93–0.94) than with humans (0.48), revealing a systematic over-rationality bias. Against the rational models from Jern et al. (2017) (absolute utility, relative utility, likelihood, marginal likelihood), GPT-4o CoT achieves 0.95/0.94 correlations in the positive context, matching or exceeding human performance (0.98), while Llama-3-8B zero-shot drops to 0.20/0.23. In the more difficult negative (shock) context, LLMs' correlations with rational models decrease (GPT-4o CoT: 0.68/0.74) but their correlation with human inferences remains higher (0.87), suggesting LLMs partially share heuristic strategies with humans. Overall, LLMs 'lose' to humans on forward modeling accuracy but 'win' on alignment with rational models, and in inverse modeling they closely track both humans and rational models simultaneously.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work using cognitive science paradigms to evaluate LLMs (Binz and Schulz, 2023a; Chen et al., 2023) by introducing a dual forward-and-inverse modeling framework that simultaneously compares LLM outputs against both actual human behavioral data and classic rational choice models. It departs from prior LLM evaluation approaches that relied on human perception to judge behavioral similarity (Park et al., 2023; Jones and Bergen, 2023), instead grounding comparisons in large-scale behavioral datasets (choices13k; Jern et al., 2017). The inverse modeling component draws directly on the Bayesian rational model of preference inference from Jern et al. (2017), adapting their 47-decision paradigm to pairwise LLM comparisons, while the forward modeling builds on the risky choice literature (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979) and the Mixture of Theories model (Peterson et al., 2021).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LLMs' implicit models of human decision-making align with rational expected-value theory rather than actual (systematically biased) human behavior has direct implications for AI alignment: RLHF may be tuning models to human *expectations* of rationality rather than human *behavior*, a distinction the paper argues should be explicitly separated in alignment objectives. For the growing literature on LLM-based human simulation (Park et al., 2022; Argyle et al., 2023), this reveals a fundamental misalignment that could make simulated choices systematically too rational. Open questions include whether the effect generalizes beyond controlled abstract domains, whether different training paradigms (SFT vs. RLHF) produce different implicit models, and whether LLMs' slightly higher correlation with humans than with rational models in the inverse task reflects genuine capture of human heuristics absent from existing theories.
