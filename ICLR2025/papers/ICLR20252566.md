## Problem

Post-hoc methods for computing cardinally minimal sufficient reasons (the smallest feature subset that guarantees an unchanged prediction) face two fundamental limitations: they are computationally intractable (the paper proves NP-Completeness for baseline, Σ₂^P-Completeness for robust, and NP^PP-Hardness for probabilistic variants), and they rely on out-of-distribution input assignments that can produce counterintuitive or unfaithful explanations. No existing approach simultaneously addresses both the scalability barrier and the OOD sensitivity while preserving predictive accuracy.

## Input / Output

The model accepts standard RGB images (MNIST 28×28, CIFAR-10 32×32, ImageNet 224×224) or tokenized text (IMDB, SNLI via BERT) and produces two outputs from shared hidden layers: a class prediction vector and an n-dimensional explanation vector (one Sigmoid-gated value per input feature or token). The explanation vector is thresholded at τ=0.5 to extract the sufficient reason subset S; during the second propagation pass, features in S are held at their original values while features in S̄ are perturbed according to the chosen masking strategy (fixed baseline, random sampling, or PGD adversarial perturbation within an ε-ball).

## Method

Sufficient Subset Training (SST) augments any neural network with a second Sigmoid output head (h₂) that shares hidden layers with the prediction head (h₁), and trains the combined model via dual propagation: a second forward pass feeds a masked input (x_S; z_S̄) back through h₁ to compute a faithfulness loss (cross-entropy between h₁(x) and h₁(x_S; z_S̄)) alongside a cardinality loss (L₁ norm of h₂(x)) and the standard prediction loss. Three masking strategies instantiate different sufficiency notions—baseline (z fixed), probabilistic (z sampled from a distribution), and robust (z found via N-step PGD attack restricted to S̄ features). The key insight is that training the model to internally generate its own sufficient reasons eliminates the need for expensive post-hoc search, exposes the model to relevant mixed inputs during training (mitigating OOD sensitivity), and the cardinality loss prevents the trivial solution of selecting all features as sufficient.

## Results

SST generates sufficient reasons with inference times of ~10⁻⁶–10⁻⁴ seconds, orders of magnitude faster than post-hoc methods (0.11–266.5 s). On MNIST, SST produces explanations of 1.42% input size with 99.28% robust faithfulness, outperforming Anchors (8.98%, 97.51%), SIS (2.35%, 98.08%), and GS (40.36%, 97.56%). On CIFAR-10, SST achieves 12.99% size with 90.43% faithfulness, beating Anchors (20.88%, 86.47%) and SIS (0.87%, 84.64%) on faithfulness while remaining far smaller than GS (60.7%, 92.41%). On ImageNet, SST yields 0.46% size with 80.88% faithfulness, smaller than all baselines but with lower faithfulness than Anchors (88.94%) and GS (90.92%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SST dominates all three post-hoc baselines on inference speed by 4–6 orders of magnitude (e.g., ~10⁻⁶ s vs. 4.34 s for SIS on MNIST, ~10⁻⁴ s vs. 266.5 s for GS on ImageNet). Against Anchors, SST wins on both size and faithfulness on MNIST (1.42% vs. 8.98% size; 99.28% vs. 97.51% faithfulness) and CIFAR-10 (12.99% vs. 20.88% size; 90.43% vs. 86.47% faithfulness), but loses on ImageNet faithfulness (80.88% vs. 88.94%). Against SIS, SST achieves higher faithfulness on MNIST (99.28% vs. 98.08%) and CIFAR-10 (90.43% vs. 84.64%) while producing slightly larger subsets; SIS is smaller on ImageNet (0.15% vs. 0.46%) but less faithful (77.32% vs. 80.88%). Against GS, SST produces dramatically smaller explanations (e.g., 12.99% vs. 60.7% on CIFAR-10, 0.46% vs. 78.97% on ImageNet) but GS retains higher faithfulness on CIFAR-10 (92.41% vs. 90.43%) and ImageNet (90.92% vs. 80.88%). On language tasks (IMDB, SNLI), SST with baseline masking achieves 98.05% and 95.88% baseline faithfulness respectively, far exceeding Anchors (39.6%, 40.24%) and S-Anchors (23.37%, 33.94%), while matching or slightly exceeding SIS (97.89%, 49.29%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against the established line of post-hoc sufficient reason methods (Anchors, SIS, gradient search) and the broader self-explaining neural network literature (Alvarez Melis & Jaakkola, 2018; Hase et al., 2021; Vafa et al., 2021). It departs from prior self-explaining work, which typically produces additive feature attributions under linearity assumptions, by instead training models to output sufficient reason subsets via a dual-propagation mechanism with faithfulness and cardinality losses. The theoretical contribution extends the complexity results of Barceló et al. (2020) and Waldchen et al. (2021) from binary-input MLPs to general discrete and continuous domains, and adds new inapproximability results. The authors explicitly frame SST as the first self-explaining framework designed to generate sufficient reasons rather than additive attributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dramatic efficiency gain (≈10⁻⁶ s vs. 0.1–266 s for post-hoc methods) makes sufficient reason explanations practically deployable at inference time, removing the primary barrier to their adoption. The complexity results (NP-Complete, Σ₂^P-Complete, NP^PP-Hard) provide a rigorous theoretical justification for why post-hoc approaches are fundamentally limited, strengthening the case for training-time integration. However, the faithfulness on ImageNet (80.88%) actually trails Anchors (88.94%) and GS (90.92%), and the 4.7% accuracy drop on ImageNet raises questions about the faithfulness-cardinality-accuracy trade-off at scale. Open questions include whether SST can improve (not merely maintain) predictive accuracy, how it scales to generative or multimodal architectures, and whether the OOD sensitivity is truly resolved or merely shifted into the training distribution.
