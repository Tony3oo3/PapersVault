## Problem

Existing LLM-based guard models for content moderation are evaluated primarily on classification accuracy (F1) but their predictive uncertainty and confidence calibration remain unexamined. This oversight is critical because miscalibrated guard models may produce overconfident incorrect predictions, allowing unsafe content to bypass moderation in real-world deployments, especially under distribution shifts such as jailbreak attacks or responses from unseen models.

## Input / Output

Guard models consume user input text (prompt classification) or user input concatenated with the LLM's generated response (response classification) and produce a binary safe/unsafe label. Confidence scores are not directly output; instead, they are derived by re-normalizing the output logits of the target label tokens (e.g., 'safe'/'unsafe', 'harmful'/'unharmful', 'yes'/'no') via a softmax over the verbalizer set. Calibration is quantified using Expected Calibration Error (ECE) with M=15 equal-width bins over the [0,1] confidence range.

## Method

This is a comprehensive empirical study that evaluates 9 open-source LLM-based guard models (Llama-Guard 1/2/3, Aegis-Guard-D/P, WildGuard, Harmbench-Llama/Mistral, MD-Judge) across 12 public benchmarks for both prompt and response classification. The paper identifies three key miscalibration patterns: systematic overconfidence (majority of predictions at 90–100% confidence), pronounced ECE degradation under adversarial jailbreak prompts (e.g., WildGuard achieves 92.8% F1 but 34.9% ECE on Harmbench-adv), and inconsistent reliability across 10 different response model types. To mitigate miscalibration without retraining, the paper applies three post-hoc calibration techniques: Temperature Scaling (optimized on a held-out set), Contextual Calibration (matrix scaling using content-free tokens like space, requiring no validation set), and Batch Calibration (linear transformation in log-probability space using unlabeled test batches). The key finding is that contextual calibration is particularly effective for prompt classification where the input is short and the contextual prior is well-estimated, while temperature scaling proves more beneficial for response classification where longer multi-sentence inputs make contextual prior estimation unreliable. The paper further diagnoses root causes of miscalibration through single-token vulnerability tests and misaligned classification objective analysis, showing that guard models retain persistent contextual biases even after instruction tuning.

## Results

Across 9 LLM-based guard models evaluated on 12 public benchmarks, WildGuard achieves the lowest average ECE for prompt classification at 14.4%, while MD-Judge achieves the lowest average ECE for response classification at 11.4%. However, all models exhibit ECE values exceeding 10% (generally considered poor calibration), with the worst case being Llama-Guard at 68.1% ECE on Harmbench Prompt. Under jailbreak attacks (Harmbench-adv), ECE for prompt classification surges dramatically—e.g., Llama-Guard reaches 69.3% ECE—while response classification ECE remains comparatively lower (13.1% for WildGuard), indicating guard models are more reliable on model outputs than adversarial user inputs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 9 guard models (Llama-Guard 1/2/3, Aegis-Guard-D/P, HarmB-Llama/Mistral, MD-Judge, WildGuard) on 12 benchmarks. For prompt classification, WildGuard achieves the best average ECE (14.4%), followed by Llama-Guard3 (16.1%) and Aegis-Guard-P (22.0%), while Llama-Guard performs worst (28.5%) largely due to 68.1% ECE on Harmbench Prompt. For response classification, MD-Judge leads with 11.4% average ECE, followed by HarmB-Mistral (17.8%) and WildGuard (18.8%), with Llama-Guard trailing at 22.7%. No model achieves ECE below 10% on average, and all show substantial variance across datasets (e.g., WildGuard ranges from 4.4% on SimpST to 33.8% on OAI). Under adversarial jailbreak conditions, even the best model (WildGuard) sees prompt ECE jump to 34.9% despite maintaining 92.8% F1, revealing a critical gap between classification accuracy and calibration reliability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: LLM-based content moderation (Llama-Guard, Aegis, WildGuard, Harmbench, MD-Judge) and confidence calibration of neural networks (temperature scaling, contextual calibration, batch calibration). Prior guard model research has focused almost exclusively on classification accuracy (F1, precision, recall), while prior LLM calibration work has targeted question-answering, pre-training, and alignment stages. This paper is the first to systematically evaluate calibration specifically for instruction-tuned guard models in the content moderation setting, repurposing contextual calibration (originally designed for in-context bias) as a confidence calibration tool and applying it alongside temperature scaling and batch calibration to both prompt and response classification tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even the best-performing guard model (WildGuard, 92.8% F1) exhibits 34.9% ECE under adversarial jailbreak conditions reveals a critical gap between accuracy and reliability that practitioners deploying these models in production may not be aware of. The discovery of misaligned classification objectives—where guard models trained for response classification still heavily weight the prompt and effectively perform prompt classification—points to a fundamental design limitation in instruction-tuned guard models that no amount of post-hoc calibration can fully fix. Open questions include whether calibration can be integrated into the instruction-tuning process itself, whether the overconfidence pattern persists for truly novel (unseen) attack families, and whether the task-specific calibration findings (contextual for prompts, temperature for responses) generalize to multiclass safety taxonomy predictions.
