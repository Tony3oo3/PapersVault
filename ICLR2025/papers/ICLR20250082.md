## Problem

Existing T2I diffusion models rely on CLIP for text encoding, which is limited to 77 tokens and cannot process the long, multi-sentence descriptions that users increasingly provide. Even when longer encoders like T5 are used, the generated images still fail to faithfully align with all details in long prompts, and preference-based fine-tuning introduces overfitting because preference scores conflate text alignment with unrelated visual quality factors.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250082/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method accepts long text prompts of arbitrary length (evaluated up to ~500 tokens, spanning multiple sentences) and produces 512×512 RGB images (or 1024×1024 with SDXL). The input text is segmented into sentences, each independently encoded by CLIP (and optionally T5 via a two-layer MLP bridge), then concatenated into a single embedding sequence with a specific special-token strategy. The preference model outputs both per-segment alignment scores and an overall average score, enabling fine-grained diagnosis of which parts of a long prompt are misaligned.

## Method

LongAlign combines three components: (1) segment-level text encoding that splits long prompts into sentences, encodes each with CLIP, and concatenates embeddings while retaining all <sot> tokens, removing all <eot> tokens, and using a unique <pad*> embedding to pad to target length; (2) a segment-level preference model (Denscore) trained with a weakly-supervised loss that averages per-segment dot-product scores, producing both segment-level and overall preference scores; and (3) preference decomposition that identifies a common direction V in text embedding space (the 'cone effect') representing text-irrelevant visual preferences, orthogonalizes the text embedding to isolate the text-relevant component C_P^⊥, and applies a gradient reweighting factor ω (optimal at 0.3) to the text-irrelevant component during reward fine-tuning to suppress overfitting while preserving alignment signal. The full pipeline fine-tunes SD-1.5's UNet via LoRA (rank 32) using DRTune-style gradient backpropagation over a uniform subset of diffusion steps.

## Results

After ~20 hours of fine-tuning 512×512 Stable Diffusion v1.5, longSD(S+R) achieves FID 19.63, Denscore-O 35.26, Denscore 23.79, and VQAscore 87.24 on the 5k-image evaluation set, outperforming all stronger foundation models including PixArt-α (FID 22.36) and Kandinsky v2.2 (FID 20.04). On DPG-Bench, longSD scores 77.58 average, surpassing ELLA (74.91) and PlayG-2 (74.54). GPT-4o pairwise evaluation confirms the gains: longSD1.5 wins 668/1000 comparisons against SD1.5 (195/1000), and longSDXL wins 416/1000 against SDXL (268/1000).

## Baselines & Comparisons

longSD(S+R) outperforms every baseline on all four metrics. Against the strongest FID competitor KanD-2.2 (FID 20.04), longSD improves to 19.63 while also leading in Denscore-O by 1.96 points (35.26 vs 33.30). Against PixArt-α, longSD gains 2.73 FID points and 1.78 Denscore-O points. The supervised-only version longSD(S) already matches KanD-2.2's FID (20.09 vs 20.04) but lags in alignment scores, showing that the reward fine-tuning stage (with gradient reweighting ω=0.3) is critical for the alignment gains. On DPG-Bench, longSD (77.58 avg) beats ELLA (74.91) and PlayG-2 (74.54) by 2.7 and 3.0 points respectively. In the segment-level retrieval experiment (Table 1), Denscore with segment-level encoding achieves 91.86% R@1, substantially outperforming CLIP-H (85.14%), Pickscore (64.00%), and HPSv2 (64.28%), confirming that the preference decomposition and segment-level training are effective for text-relevant scoring.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Stable Diffusion / latent diffusion framework (Rombach et al., 2022) and CLIP-based preference models (Kirstain et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023b), but departs from the common trend of replacing CLIP with T5 for long-text encoding by instead introducing segment-level encoding that preserves CLIP's image-text alignment advantage. The preference optimization framework is borrowed from LLM alignment (Ouyang et al., 2022; Rafailov et al., 2024) and the gradient backpropagation method follows DRTune (Wu et al., 2024). The key conceptual departure is the orthogonal decomposition of CLIP-based preference scores into text-relevant and text-irrelevant components, identifying the latter (the 'cone effect' common direction) as the primary driver of overfitting during reward fine-tuning, and proposing a simple gradient reweighting fix.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that targeted training strategies (segment-level encoding, preference decomposition, gradient reweighting) can make a modest 512×512 SD-1.5 model outperform stronger foundation models (PixArt-α, Kandinsky v2.2) on long-text alignment after only ~20 hours of fine-tuning, suggesting that alignment quality is not solely determined by model architecture or scale. The preference decomposition insight is broadly applicable to anyone using CLIP-based reward signals for diffusion fine-tuning, as the overfitting mechanism identified here is general. However, the authors acknowledge that complex contextual dependencies and strong semantic understanding remain unaddressed due to CLIP's inherent limitations, and the evaluation is concentrated on a single architecture and resolution, leaving open whether the gains transfer to larger or transformer-based backbones.
