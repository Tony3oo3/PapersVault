## Problem

Individual LLMs face inherent constraints on model size and training data, and further scaling is prohibitively expensive. While different LLMs possess complementary strengths, no systematic framework existed to combine their collective expertise into a single, more capable system. The paper identifies and exploits an underexplored phenomenon—'collaborativeness'—where an LLM generates significantly better responses when provided with outputs from other models, even less capable ones.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single text prompt as input and produces a refined text response as output, requiring no fine-tuning or weight modifications—only standard prompting and generation interfaces. The prompt is processed through multiple MoA layers, where each layer's agents receive the concatenated outputs of all agents from the previous layer as auxiliary context via a fixed 'Aggregate-and-Synthesize' system prompt. The final output is the response of a single aggregator LLM in the last layer, evaluated on benchmarks using length-controlled win rates to neutralize verbosity bias.

## Method

Mixture-of-Agents (MoA) is a layered architecture where each layer contains multiple LLM agents that independently generate responses conditioned on all outputs from the prior layer, iteratively refining quality over 2–3 cycles. The key mechanism is an 'Aggregate-and-Synthesize' prompt that instructs each agent to critically evaluate and synthesize (not merely select from) the previous layer's responses, effectively performing a soft aggregation at the model level. The design draws a direct analogy to Mixture-of-Experts but operates entirely through prompting rather than gating over internal activations, consolidating the roles of gating and expert networks into the LLM's own interpretive capacity. Model selection for each layer is guided by two criteria—individual performance (win rate) and output diversity—with empirical evidence showing that heterogeneous proposers consistently outperform repeated sampling from a single model. The framework requires no training, is architecture-agnostic, and can be extended from dense (all agents active) to sparse (subset activated) configurations.

## Results

MoA using only open-source models achieves 65.1% LC win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0, surpassing GPT-4o (57.5%) by 7.6 percentage points. With GPT-4o as the final aggregator, MoA reaches 65.7% on AlpacaEval 2.0 and 90.3% on Arena-Hard (vs. 79.2% for GPT-4o alone). The lighter MoA-Lite variant (2 layers) still outperforms GPT-4o on AlpacaEval 2.0 (59.3% vs. 57.5%) while being more cost-effective. On MT-Bench, gains are more marginal (9.25–9.40 vs. 9.19 for GPT-4o) since individual models already score above 9/10.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoA (open-source only) decisively outperforms GPT-4o on AlpacaEval 2.0 (65.1% vs. 57.5%, +7.6 pp) and MoA w/ GPT-4o dominates Arena-Hard (90.3% vs. 79.2%, +11.1 pp), though MoA (open-source) trails GPT-4 Turbo on Arena-Hard (77.4% vs. 82.0%). Against individual open-source models, MoA yields large gains: over Qwen1.5-110B-Chat (+21.2 pp on AlpacaEval LC), Llama-3-70B (+30.7 pp), and WizardLM-8x22B (+13.8 pp). In the multi-agent comparison (Table 5), MoA outperforms MAD (61.3 vs. 53.5 with 1 aggregation; 65.7 vs. 50.6 with 2) and Reconcile (61.3 vs. 47.6; 65.7 vs. 47.7) on AlpacaEval 2.0 LC, with the gap widening at more aggregation rounds. On MT-Bench, MoA secures the top score (9.40 with GPT-4o aggregator) but the margin over GPT-4 Turbo (9.31) and GPT-4o (9.19) is modest, reflecting the ceiling effect of this benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws a direct architectural analogy to Mixture-of-Experts (MoE), but lifts the concept from the activation/weight level within a single model to the full-model level, where each 'expert' is an entire LLM coordinated purely through prompting. It departs from prior multi-agent debate methods (MAD, Reconcile) that target short deterministic answers by instead designing a layered aggregation architecture for open-ended generation, and from model-ensemble approaches (LLM-Blender, GenFuser, routing) by replacing selection or one-shot fusion with iterative multi-layer synthesis. The key conceptual contribution is the identification of 'collaborativeness'—the empirical finding that LLMs improve when conditioned on other models' outputs even when those outputs are individually lower quality—which motivates the layered design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a no-fine-tuning, prompt-only method using only open-source models surpasses GPT-4o on AlpacaEval 2.0, Arena-Hard, and FLASK, and is 2× more cost-effective than GPT-4 Turbo, making it immediately deployable for practitioners without access to frontier proprietary models. Key limitations include high Time-to-First-Token due to sequential layer aggregation, and the method's dependence on API access to multiple large models. Open questions include optimal architecture search (the paper demonstrates BFGS-based optimization as a proof of concept), scaling the number of proposers, and whether chunk-wise aggregation can reduce latency while preserving quality.
