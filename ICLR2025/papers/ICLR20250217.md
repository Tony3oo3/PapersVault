## Problem

A prevailing belief in the interpretability literature holds that monosemantic features—where each neuron encodes a single consistent semantic concept—necessarily sacrifice predictive accuracy compared to polysemantic features. This paper challenges that tradeoff by showing that monosemantic features are actually more robust across a wide range of practical scenarios including label noise, input noise, distribution shifts, few-shot learning, and LLM fine-tuning, thereby linking interpretability to robustness rather than opposing them.

## Input / Output

For vision tasks, the method consumes RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-100, 224×224 for ImageNet-100) and produces class labels, with robustness evaluated under symmetric label noise (up to 90%), Gaussian/uniform input noise, and real-world distribution shifts (ImageNet-sketch, ImageNet-stylized). For language tasks, it takes text prompts (SST-2 sentiment, Dolly instruction-following) and produces text responses or sentiment labels, with alignment safety evaluated via ShieldGemma and Beavertails scoring. The key unusual aspect is that the same pretrained backbone is frozen and only the classifier or adapter is trained, isolating the effect of feature monosemanticity from architectural changes.

## Method

The paper introduces and compares two complementary strategies for attaining monosemantic features: (1) an intrinsic method, Non-negative Contrastive Learning (NCL), which constrains feature outputs to be non-negative via ReLU during self-supervised pretraining, yielding sparse, class-consistent dimensions; and (2) a post-hoc method, Sparse Autoencoders (SAE), which decode frozen polysemantic features through a top-K sparse bottleneck into monosemantic latent codes. For fine-tuning, the authors replace standard cross-entropy with a non-negative cross-entropy (NCE) loss to preserve monosemanticity during supervised adaptation, and for LLMs they propose MonoLoRA, a sparse variant of LoRA that encourages monosemantic updates. The theoretical contribution uses the superposition toy model to prove that monosemantic representations maintain better linear separability (LDA criterion) under both label noise and Gaussian input noise, because their intra-class variance grows more slowly than that of polysemantic features, yielding more robust decision boundaries.

## Results

Under 90% symmetric label noise, monosemantic features (NCL) achieve 48.1% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet-100 and 45.0% on CIFAR-100, outperforming the polysemantic SimCLR baseline (34.5% and 35.4%) by +13.6% and +9.6% respectively. Under 60% label noise on ImageNet-100, NCL reaches 60.8% vs. 54.9% for polysemantic features (+5.9%). In few-shot finetuning with 10% of ImageNet-100 samples, monosemantic (NCE) finetuning gains +3.9% top-1 accuracy over standard CE finetuning, and under noisy-label finetuning the gap grows to up to +11.9% at high noise rates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three baselines: SimCLR (Chen et al., 2020) as the polysemantic pretraining baseline, SAE (Gao et al., 2024) as a post-hoc monosemanticity method, and NCL (Wang et al., 2024) as an intrinsic monosemanticity method. Under clean labels (0% noise), all three methods achieve comparable accuracy (e.g., 66.8% for SimCLR vs. 66.8% for NCL on ImageNet-100), confirming that monosemanticity does not sacrifice clean-data performance. Under increasing label noise, both monosemantic methods outperform SimCLR, with NCL consistently achieving the largest gains (up to +13.6% at 90% noise on ImageNet-100). NCL also outperforms SAE across all noise levels (e.g., 48.1% vs. 45.7% at 90% noise on ImageNet-100), suggesting that intrinsic monosemanticity via non-negative constraints is more effective than post-hoc sparse decomposition. In the LLM setting, MonoLoRA (the paper's sparse LoRA variant) preserves alignment better than standard LoRA (ShieldGemma avg score 3.53 vs. 7.90 on SST-2) while simultaneously improving task accuracy (94.84% vs. 92.78%), demonstrating that monosemanticity benefits extend to language model fine-tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on two complementary monosemanticity techniques—non-negative contrastive learning (NCL, Wang et al. 2024) for intrinsic monosemanticity and sparse autoencoders (SAE, Gao et al. 2024) for post-hoc monosemanticity—and on the superposition toy model of Elhage et al. (2022b) for theoretical analysis. Its key departure is from the widely-held 'accuracy-interpretability tradeoff' that pervades the monosemanticity literature (Gao et al. 2024; Cunningham et al. 2024; Elhage et al. 2022b), which holds that monosemantic features necessarily sacrifice predictive performance. Here, the authors reframe the question: while clean-data accuracy is comparable or slightly lower, monosemantic features are substantially more robust under noise, few-shot, and distribution-shift conditions, and they introduce MonoLoRA to extend the monosemanticity principle to LLM fine-tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating consistent robustness gains across label noise, input noise, OOD shifts, few-shot finetuning, and LLM alignment preservation, the paper provides a practical argument for adopting monosemanticity techniques beyond interpretability labs—any practitioner concerned with robustness should reconsider the tradeoff. However, the evidence is limited to ResNet-18 on ImageNet-100/CIFAR-100 and a single 7B LLM, and the theoretical analysis is confined to a binary toy model (n=2, m=1), leaving open whether the robustness advantage persists at scale, in deeper architectures, or under more complex noise distributions. The LLM results are explicitly preliminary, and the MonoLoRA method has not been validated across diverse downstream tasks or model sizes.
