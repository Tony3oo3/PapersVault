## Problem

Language models are opaque: chain-of-thought and similar explanation methods reveal only snippets of knowledge supporting a single answer, not the model's overall 'theory' about a topic. No prior method distills a model's overall topical understanding into an inspectable, verifiable form that both engenders user trust and improves grounded question-answering performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251721/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of topically clustered training questions (6–900 per topic, from ARC or MedQA) and a general retrieval corpus (Wikipedia for ARC; Wikipedia + medical textbooks for MedQA). It produces a microtheory: a flat list of n natural-language assertions (n = 25–1000) capturing core, reusable knowledge. At test time, the microtheory is appended to the corpus as a retrieval source, and an entailment engine (TreeWise) searches for entailment trees whose leaves are grounded in the combined source, yielding a grounded proof and a selected multiple-choice answer.

## Method

The pipeline has three stages. First, GPT-4 is prompted with few-shot chain-of-thought-style exemplars from EntailmentBank to generate per-question fact lists, from which context-specific sentences are filtered out (KeepIfGeneric) to form a raw fact pool F. Second, SBERT-based soft deduplication removes paraphrases and a cross-encoder entailment check removes facts subsumed by others, yielding a condensed pool C (~55% reduction for ARC, ~25% for MedQA). Third, one of three optimization objectives selects the n most effective statements: (a) Mt_usage picks facts most frequently appearing as leaves in entailment proofs; (b) Mt_QC solves a linear program maximizing the number of training questions whose full argumentative basis is covered; (c) Mt_PC solves an integer linear program maximizing fractional (partial) basis coverage per question. The key mechanism is textual entailment: microtheory statements serve as premises in multi-step entailment trees that systematically 'prove' answer hypotheses, making the model's topical knowledge both inspectable and verifiable.

## Results

On ARC (K-12 science), adding the full fact pool F to Wikipedia improves QA accuracy from 69.2% (Wikipedia alone) to 78% (+8.8 points), while 1000-fact microtheories (any distillation method) achieve 73% (+3.8 points). For grounding, microtheories enable the entailment engine to fully ground up to 8% more test hypotheses (92% vs. 84% for corpus alone), with 75% of the required entailment knowledge sourced from the Mt in ARC. On MedQA, grounding improvements are similar in rate but 45% of knowledge comes from the Mt; expert-annotated relevance scores for Mt_PC facts are 0.6–1.2 points higher than random selection, never dropping below 4/5.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline comparison is against WorldTree, a hand-crafted corpus of 12,657 science statements built at significant expense. The paper's 1000-fact microtheories achieve similar overall grounding rates and QA accuracy (~73%) to WorldTree while being less than 10% its size, demonstrating that the automated extraction process can replicate a costly manual resource. The full fact pool F (20,561 facts for ARC) outperforms WorldTree on QA accuracy (78% vs. ~73%), suggesting the model-generated knowledge captures information not present in the hand-annotated corpus. Against random fact selection, all three distillation methods (usage, QC, PC) consistently outperform the random baseline on grounding rate, QA accuracy, and expert-annotated relevance, with Mt_PC showing the strongest relevance scores (0.6–1.2 points above random). The paper also notes that the choice among the three optimization methods (usage, QC, PC) does not produce a noticeable difference in QA accuracy, though each outperforms random selection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalizes the concept of logical microtheories (Blair et al., 1992; Lenat et al., 1990) in natural language, using textual entailment engines (TreeWise, IRGR) as the reasoning substrate instead of formal logic. It departs from prior knowledge-extraction work (BeliefBank, belief graphs, knowledge-graph harvesting) that produces disconnected, question-centric slices of model knowledge, by instead distilling a model's *overall* topical understanding into a concise, reusable set of statements. The fact-extraction step leverages chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al., 2022) with EntailmentBank exemplars, while the condensation and selection steps introduce SBERT-based deduplication, entailment-based pruning, and linear-programming optimization (usage, question-coverage, partial-coverage) that have no direct precedent in the LLM-knowledge-extraction literature. The paper also positions itself as an automated alternative to the manually constructed WorldTree corpus (Xie et al., 2020), achieving comparable grounding with <10% of the size.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The microtheory framework offers a concrete, inspectable window into what a model 'knows' about a topic, addressing a key trust gap that per-question explanations (CoT, rationale generation) leave unaddressed. The p-relevance metric is a practical contribution: it lets practitioners predict how many training questions are needed before a microtheory becomes useful, and it reveals that domain structure matters enormously (ARC needs ~940 questions for 90% relevance; MedQA needs ~8900), suggesting microtheories may not be viable for domains with little reusable core knowledge. Key limitations include heavy LLM-call costs during construction, dependence on entailment engine quality, and the finding that gains are modest in domains dominated by idiosyncratic facts. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond multiple-choice QA, how microtheories interact with retrieval-augmented generation pipelines, and whether the distillation process can be made more sample-efficient.
