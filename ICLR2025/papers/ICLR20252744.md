## Problem

Existing poisoned sample detection methods for backdoor defense rely on activation-space separability between poisoned and clean samples, an assumption that recent attacks (e.g., Adap-Blend, CTRL) have explicitly broken. The paper asks whether a more generalizable and discriminative metric exists that can distinguish poisoned from clean samples across diverse trigger types and poisoning ratios without depending on activation-space clustering.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252744/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes an untrustworthy training dataset (e.g., CIFAR-10 at 32×32, Tiny ImageNet at 64×64), a small auxiliary set of clean samples (as few as 1 per class, typically 10 from the test split), and a DNN model (Preact-ResNet18 or VGG19-BN) already trained on the untrustworthy data. It produces a purified training set with poisoned samples removed, along with per-sample poisoned/clean labels and the identified target class(es). The method operates on channel-wise activation gradients at intermediate convolutional layers rather than on raw activations or final predictions.

## Method

AGPD introduces the Gradient Circular Distribution (GCD), which captures the angular distribution of channel-wise activation gradients (∂logit_y/∂h^(l)) relative to a clean reference sample. The key insight is that a backdoored model maps poisoned and clean samples in the target class to similar activation regions, but their gradient directions diverge sharply because the original inputs differ substantially. The three-stage pipeline is: (1) compute GCDs for every class using a chosen layer; (2) identify target class(es) via the CVBT metric (Cosine similarity Variation towards Basis Transition), which measures dispersion by comparing cosine similarities under two basis vectors (the reference and the farthest sample), then flag outliers via robust Z-score; (3) iteratively filter poisoned samples within the target class using a sample-level closeness score that weights each sample's angular distance to the reference versus the farthest sample, stopping when the Jensen-Shannon divergence between successive closeness-score distributions stabilizes (detected via a sliding-window minimum). Extending the single farthest basis vector to a set of top-m basis vectors improves robustness against adaptive weak attacks.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with Preact-ResNet18, AGPD achieves an average TPR of 96.66%, FPR of 0.17%, and F1 of 97.44% across 12 backdoor attacks, exceeding the runner-up (SCAn) by 18.23% in TPR and 12.71% in F1. On Tiny ImageNet, AGPD reaches 99.92% average TPR with near-zero FPR. The method also generalizes to VGG19-BN (96.46% TPR on CIFAR-10), ImageNet-200 (98.20% TPR), DTD (99.61% TPR), and GTSRB (93.78% TPR), maintaining FPR below 2% in all settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AGPD decisively outperforms all 10 baselines on average TPR and F1 across 12 attacks on CIFAR-10. The closest competitor, SCAn (Tang et al., USENIX 2021), achieves 72.82% TPR but fails catastrophically on all-to-all attacks (0% TPR) and TaCT (90% FPR), while AGPD maintains >97% TPR and <1% FPR in those settings. CD (Huang et al., 2023) reaches 76.76% TPR but suffers a 32.18% FPR due to similar L1-norm masks for poisoned and clean samples. ABL (Li et al., NeurIPS 2021) and STRIP (Gao et al., ACSAC 2019) degrade sharply on dynamic-trigger attacks (WaNet, Input-Aware, Adap-Blend), where AGPD still achieves >88% TPR. Activation-based methods (AC, Beatrix, Spectral) are weakest overall (TPR < 24%), failing when poisoned and clean samples are not separable in activation space. In the Appendix C.5 comparison, AGPD matches CT (Qi et al., USENIX 2023) on TPR (~97–100%) but achieves substantially lower FPR (0.02–0.31% vs. 0.01–1.36%), while IBD-PSC (Hou et al., 2024) has comparable TPR but 11–12% FPR.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant activation-based detection paradigm (AC, Beatrix, SCAn, Spectral) by shifting the discriminative signal from activation values to activation *gradient directions*, formalized as the Gradient Circular Distribution (GCD). It is motivated by the observation from Huang et al. (2022) that backdoored models collapse poisoned and clean samples in the target class into similar activation regions, which has been shown to break the separability assumption underlying prior methods. Rather than building directly on any single prior detector, AGPD repurposes the gradient concept (standard in optimization) as a detection metric, introducing the CVBT dispersion measure and an iterative closeness-based filtering procedure that has no direct analogue in the existing PSD literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core practical contribution is a pre-training defense that achieves near-perfect TPR (~97%) with near-zero FPR (~0.1%) across 12 diverse backdoor attacks (including sample-specific triggers like WaNet, SSBA, and Adap-Blend) on five datasets and two architectures, using only 10 clean samples per class. This addresses a real gap: prior activation-based methods degrade sharply when poisoned and clean samples overlap in activation space, a regime that modern adaptive attacks deliberately exploit. Open questions include whether the GCD perspective transfers to in-training or post-training defense settings, whether it generalizes beyond CNNs to transformers, and how it behaves under multi-target all-to-all attacks where the Z-score-based target-class identification becomes less reliable (the paper acknowledges this and falls back to a fixed threshold).
