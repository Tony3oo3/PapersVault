## Problem

Efficient IRL algorithms that reset the learner to expert states for local policy search rely on the assumption of expert realizability—that the learner can perfectly imitate the expert in all states. In practice, morphological, perceptual, or dynamic differences between expert and learner make this assumption fail, raising two questions: under what structural condition can reset-based IRL still avoid compounding errors, and where should local search be performed when the learner cannot reach or follow through on expert states?

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes expert state-action demonstrations D_E (N pairs), optional offline state-action data D_off (M pairs) from a realizable behavior policy, a compact convex policy class Π, and a compact convex reward class R over a finite-horizon MDP with continuous state and action spaces. It outputs a trained policy π selected by lowest validation error across outer-loop iterations. The critical design choice is the reset distribution ρ for the inner RL subroutine, which can be expert states (ρ_E), offline states (ρ_B), or a weighted mixture (ρ_mix), directly controlling where local policy optimization occurs.

## Method

GUITAR (GUiding ImiTaters with Arbitrary Resets) extends efficient IRL to the misspecified setting by introducing reward-agnostic policy completeness (APC), a structural condition that measures how well the policy class approximates the maximum possible advantage over the expert's state distribution under any reward in the reward class—generalizing the RL policy completeness condition to the unknown-reward IRL setting. The algorithm alternates between an Online Mirror Descent no-regret reward update (discriminating expert from learner trajectories) and a Policy Search by Dynamic Programming (PSDP) policy update where the learner is reset to a chosen distribution ρ rather than the true starting state. The key theoretical result (Theorem 3.3) shows that under APC, the imitation gap is linear in horizon H (not H²), decomposing into a fixed misspecification term, a reducible policy optimization term, and a vanishing reward regret term. For the reset distribution question, Corollary 4.1 proves that augmenting expert resets with offline data improves sample efficiency when the offline data's coverage coefficient C_B over the optimal realizable policy π* is sufficiently small, establishing that the optimal reset distribution covers π*'s state distribution rather than the expert's.

## Results

The paper's main results are presented as learning curves rather than a single numerical table. On the strongly misspecified Antmaze-Umaze (block obstruction), GUITAR is the only interactive algorithm that solves the task, while FILTER, MM, and both BC baselines fail. On the milder time-constraint variant, GUITAR still outperforms FILTER, with the gap widening as misspecification severity increases. On MuJoCo dynamics-mismatch tasks, GUITAR achieves a clear speedup over traditional IRL (MM), and FILTER cannot even be implemented since the learner cannot be reset to expert states with different morphology. On Antmaze-Large, GUITAR with short-trajectory resets matches the performance of FILTER with the full expert dataset, showing full coverage of π*'s state distribution is not strictly necessary.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against FILTER (Swamy et al., 2023), GUITAR wins decisively in the strongly misspecified block-obstruction setting where FILTER fails entirely, and wins by a growing margin as misspecification increases in the time-constraint setting. On the MuJoCo dynamics-mismatch task, FILTER is inapplicable (the learner's morphology differs from the expert's, making expert-state resets impossible), while GUITAR still achieves a speedup over MM. Against MM (Swamy et al., 2021a, traditional IRL with true starting-state resets), GUITAR outperforms in all misspecified settings, confirming that the choice of reset distribution matters more than the IRL framework itself. Against both behavioral cloning baselines—BC(π_E) trained only on expert data and BC(π_E + π_B) trained on expert plus offline data—GUITAR consistently outperforms, with the paper noting that 'the consistently poor performance of the BC policies across the varying misspecified settings highlights the challenge of using offline data for direct policy learning.' The one case where GUITAR does not strictly dominate is the Antmaze-Large subset experiment, where GUITAR(D_short) ties FILTER(D_full), suggesting that a well-chosen subset of the optimal realizable policy's states can be as effective as full coverage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the efficient IRL framework of Swamy et al. (2023), which resets the learner to expert states to reduce global RL exploration to local search, by removing the expert realizability assumption that underpinned all prior efficient IRL guarantees. The key conceptual departure is introducing reward-agnostic policy completeness—a reward-sensitive generalization of the policy completeness condition from RL (Agarwal et al., 2019; Bhandari & Russo, 2024)—as the structural condition under which local search suffices in the misspecified setting. The paper also builds on the game-theoretic IRL formulation of Swamy et al. (2021a) and the PSDP solver of Bagnell et al. (2003), while drawing on agnostic RL lower bounds (Jia et al., 2024) to establish that no computationally efficient algorithm exists without additional structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation is strong: in humanoid robotics and manipulation, morphological and dynamics mismatches between expert and learner make perfect imitation impossible, yet prior efficient IRL theory provided no guidance for this regime. The paper's central insight—that the optimal reset distribution should cover the optimal realizable policy rather than the expert's states—gives practitioners a concrete principle for choosing offline data to augment IRL. However, the empirical validation is limited to maze navigation and a few MuJoCo tasks, and the paper explicitly acknowledges that its analysis does not capture recoverability (the ability to correct from mistakes), leaving an open question about how policy completeness and recoverability interact under misspecification. The finding that subsets of π* states (e.g., short trajectories) can suffice for resets is intriguing but underexplored theoretically.
