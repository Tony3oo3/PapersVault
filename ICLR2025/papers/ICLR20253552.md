## Problem

Conformal prediction methods for survival analysis have provided finite-sample valid lower predictive bounds (LPBs) only for type-I right-censored data, where the censoring time C is observed for every subject. In the more prevalent general right-censored setting—where either the event time or the censoring time is observed, but not both—the calibration cannot be directly formulated as a covariate shift problem, and existing approaches either lack finite-sample guarantees or rely on asymptotic bootstrap validity. This paper closes that gap by constructing distribution-free, finite-sample valid LPBs for general right-censored data under the same conditionally independent censoring assumption used in the type-I literature.

## Input / Output

The method consumes general right-censored observations (X_i, T̃_i, e_i) where e_i = I{T_i < C_i} is the always-observed event indicator, a pre-trained base survival model (e.g., DeepSurv) providing conditional quantile estimates q̂_τ(x) for τ ∈ [0,1], and a held-out calibration set. It outputs a calibrated LPB function L̂(X_test) = q̂_{τ̂}(X_test) accompanied by a PAC-type guarantee: with probability at least 1−δ over the data draw, P(T_test ≥ L̂(X_test) | D) ≥ 1−α. Unlike type-I methods that require the full censoring time C for every subject, here calibration relies solely on the binary indicator e, which is always available regardless of which time is observed.

## Method

The paper proposes two calibration strategies that tune the quantile level τ via importance-weighted miscoverage estimators. Focused calibration restricts to uncensored subjects (e_i = 1) and applies weights ŵ(x) ≈ 1/P(e=1|X=x) to correct for the induced distribution shift, yielding a conservative but valid LPB. Fused calibration improves informativeness by selectively including censored subjects (e_i = 0) for whom a learned classifier ŝ_τ(x) predicts that censoring is less informative than the event, effectively interpolating between the naive (all samples) and focused (uncensored only) strategies. Both methods achieve double robustness: the coverage guarantee holds if either the importance weights are well-estimated or the conditional quantile estimates are accurate, and the guarantee is PAC-type (conditional on the observed dataset). The key theoretical insight is that although the selection rule based on e does not induce a standard covariate shift (since T and e are conditionally dependent given X), a weighted upper bound on the true miscoverage rate can still be derived, enabling valid finite-sample calibration.

## Results

Across six simulated settings (50 trials each, with 600/200/1000/200 train/validation/calibration/test splits), the naive, focused, and fused calibration methods all achieve valid 90% coverage, while the uncalibrated DeepSurv base model and the CSD method (Qi et al., 2024) fail to reach the nominal 90% level in settings 1, 3, and 4. The fused method produces the highest relative LPB (most informative lower bound) among all methods with finite-sample guarantees, and is less conservative than both focused and naive calibration. On six real-world datasets (NACD, GBSG, METABRIC, SUPPORT, User Churn, TCGA-BRCA), the fused approach matches or slightly exceeds the stronger of naive/focused in average LPB, with a clear advantage on METABRIC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the CSD method (Qi et al., 2024), the proposed focused and fused calibration methods win on validity: CSD fails to achieve the target 90% coverage in 3 out of 6 simulated settings, while all three proposed methods (naive, focused, fused) maintain valid coverage everywhere. On real data, CSD sometimes produces higher LPB values, but these come without finite-sample guarantees and are shown to be invalid in simulation. Against the uncalibrated DeepSurv base model, the same pattern holds: the base model occasionally yields higher LPBs but lacks coverage guarantees and under-covers in multiple simulated settings. Among the proposed methods themselves, fused calibration dominates: it is less conservative than focused (which in turn is less conservative than naive) in most settings, and strictly outperforms both on settings 2 and 6 in simulation as well as on the METABRIC real dataset. The key practical takeaway is that fused calibration achieves the best informativeness (highest LPB) while preserving the PAC-type finite-sample coverage guarantee that neither CSD nor the uncalibrated model provides.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the conformal survival analysis framework of Candès et al. (2023) and Gui et al. (2024), which construct valid lower predictive bounds (LPBs) for type-I right-censored data where the censoring time is observed for all subjects. The key conceptual departure is handling the general right-censored setting, where only one of the survival or censoring time is observed per subject, making the calibration intractable as a direct covariate shift problem. The authors introduce focused and fused calibration methods that use the event indicator to selectively weight or include censored samples, preserving double robustness under the same conditionally independent censoring assumption. Concurrent work by Qi et al. (2024), Qin et al. (2024), and Meixide et al. (2024) addresses the same setting but relies on imputation or bootstrap-based asymptotic validity, lacking finite-sample guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method providing distribution-free finite-sample LPB guarantees for general right-censored survival data, a setting ubiquitous in biomedical research where small sample sizes are common and asymptotic approximations are unreliable. The double robustness property (validity if either weights or quantiles are accurate) makes the approach practical despite the need to estimate nuisance parameters. Key limitations include reliance on the conditionally independent censoring and i.i.d. assumptions, and the challenge of estimating weights when censoring rates are high, which can produce extreme importance weights. Open questions include robustness to violations of these assumptions and principled handling of extreme weights, both of which the authors flag as important future directions.
