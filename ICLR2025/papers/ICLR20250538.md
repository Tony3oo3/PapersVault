## Problem

Incorporating domain expert knowledge into ML models as inductive biases currently requires manual engineering of constraints into architectures, loss functions, or data augmentation, a process that is labor-intensive and limited to formally expressible knowledge. Standard meta-learning learns a fixed prior over the hypothesis space that cannot be conditioned on task-specific expert information, making it impossible to steer inductive biases at test time. This paper asks whether one can learn a generalisable mapping from human-interpretable knowledge representations (e.g., natural language) directly to informed priors over the hypothesis space.

## Input / Output

The model consumes three inputs per task: a context dataset D_C of (x, y) pairs, a knowledge representation K that can take heterogeneous forms (structured numeric vectors such as min/max temperature, GPT-4-generated natural-language weather forecasts, CLIP-embedded class attribute vectors, or per-class text descriptions), and a query input x. It outputs a full predictive distribution p(y|x, D_C, K) parameterised by a latent variable, yielding both a point prediction and decomposed aleatoric/epistemic uncertainty. For classification tasks the output is a set of class probabilities over N-way, k-shot episodes. When knowledge is unavailable at test time, the corresponding embedding is set to a zero vector and the model gracefully degrades to a standard Neural Process.

## Method

The Informed Neural Process (INP) extends the amortised Neural Process architecture by introducing a separate knowledge encoder h_{θ_e,K} that maps K to an embedding k, which is fused with the data encoder output r_C via a simple vector sum to parameterise the variational distribution q(z|r_C, k) = N(μ_z, σ_z). A decoder g_{θ_d} then produces the predictive distribution p(y|x, z) = N(μ_x, σ_x). Training is episodic over a distribution of tasks, maximising the ELBO with a KL regulariser between the target-conditioned and context-conditioned variational distributions, and knowledge embeddings are randomly masked during training to support missing-knowledge inference at test time. The key insight is that the learned mapping K ↦ p_θ(f|K) acts as an automated translator between the human-interpretable knowledge space and the model's hypothesis space, assigning higher prior mass to functions consistent with the expert knowledge. The paper provides a theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) showing that under conditional independence of data and knowledge given f, the informed predictive distribution is closer in KL divergence to the true posterior than the uninformed one, and that the inequality is strict when knowledge carries positive mutual information with the target.

## Results

On CUB-200-2011 few-shot classification, INP with class-level attributes (setup A) achieves 88.1% 5-way 1-shot accuracy versus NP's 82.2%, and 82.2% 10-way 1-shot versus NP's 73.3%. INP also enables zero-shot classification (87.5% 5-way, 81.1% 10-way) where NP cannot operate without any context data. On the U.S. Climate Reference Network weather extrapolation task, structured knowledge (min/max temperature) yields a 57.7% relative improvement in log-likelihood at 0-shot, while GPT-4-generated text forecasts achieve 48.1%. The performance gap between informed and uninformed predictions widens as the number of context points decreases, confirming improved data efficiency.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the standard (uninformed) Neural Process (NP). INP consistently outperforms NP across all evaluated settings on CUB-200-2011, with the largest gains at low-shot: +5.9 pp on 5-way 1-shot (88.1% vs 82.2%) and +8.9 pp on 10-way 1-shot (82.2% vs 73.3%). At 10-shot the gap narrows to roughly 0–1 pp, indicating that sufficient empirical data diminishes the relative benefit of knowledge. INP also enables zero-shot classification (87.5% 5-way, 81.1% 10-way with structured attributes) where NP has no mechanism to produce predictions without context data. On the synthetic sinusoidal regression task, INP with knowledge significantly improves log-likelihood over NP, particularly as context size shrinks, while INP without knowledge (K=∅) performs on par with NP, confirming no degradation when knowledge is absent. On the weather extrapolation task, both structured (min/max) and natural-language knowledge formats yield substantial improvements over the uninformed NP, with the structured format slightly outperforming the text format (ΔAUC 24.6% vs 22.2%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the meta-learning paradigm by introducing a knowledge-conditioned prior, building directly on Neural Processes (Garnelo et al., 2018) as the base amortised meta-learner and on conditional meta-learning (Denevi et al., 2020, 2022) for the idea of conditioning on external task information. It departs from conventional meta-learning, which learns a fixed prior over the hypothesis space, by learning a mapping from human-interpretable knowledge representations (e.g., natural language) to task-specific priors, and from manual knowledge-integration methods (specialised losses, architectures, feature engineering) by making the integration automated and learnable. The paper also positions itself relative to emerging LLM-based in-context learners, noting that informed meta-learning offers a complementary, more controllable alternative that does not require tokenising numerical data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, theoretically grounded framework (Theorem 1) showing that conditioning on truthful knowledge representations provably reduces predictive KL divergence, and demonstrates this empirically across regression, time-series extrapolation, and few-shot image classification. Key open questions include: generalisation to semantically novel knowledge representations under distribution shift, the requirement for a meta-training set of (data, knowledge) pairs that may be hard to obtain at scale, and the gap between approximate neural integration and guaranteed correctness that model-based methods enjoy. The authors also highlight the potential synergy with LLMs as synthetic knowledge generators and as in-context learners, suggesting a productive direction for future work.
