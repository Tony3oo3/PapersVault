## Problem

Online-trained RL agents maximize environmental returns but often produce behaviors that feel unnatural or misaligned with diverse human preferences (e.g., cautious vs. speed-runner vs. achievement-hunter play styles). Directly fine-tuning these agents on scarce human behavior data causes unlearning of previously acquired environmental dynamics, and the limited data volume is insufficient for standard RL fine-tuning. The paper formalizes this as an online-to-offline RL problem: aligning a well-trained online policy with human preferences encoded in a small amount of offline human data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252021/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained RL agent (SAC for continuous control, DQN for Atari), only 10 episodes of offline human state-action trajectories, and the environment for online interactions during a 1e6-step alignment phase. The reward model consumes state-action sub-trajectories of length 64 (not single state-action pairs) as input, producing a scalar preference score. The output is an aligned agent policy whose behavior style matches the target human preference group, along with the trained transformer reward model that can be reused for other preference targets.

## Method

ALIGN-GAP operates in two stages. First, a transformer-based reward model is trained via pairwise contrastive learning (Bradley-Terry loss) on sub-trajectory pairs sampled from offline human data and the online agent, learning to score human-preferred behaviors higher. Second, a calibrated preference curriculum learning strategy addresses two challenges: (1) RM score calibration subtracts the initial policy's RM score from the current policy's score, so the alignment signal emphasizes human-preferred behaviors the agent has not yet adopted rather than rewarding already-mastered behaviors; (2) a reward curriculum linearly interpolates between the environmental reward and the calibrated RM reward via α(t) growing from 0 to 1, smoothly transitioning the agent's objective from return maximization to preference alignment. A theoretical analysis using the EPIC pseudometric shows that the linear curriculum reduces the expected distance between the environment reward and the alignment target by a factor of 2/3, and by ~50% when the two reward functions are uncorrelated.

## Results

On D4RL locomotion tasks (HalfCheetah, Walker2D, Hopper × 4 preference types each), ALIGN-GAP achieves the smallest return gap to human proxies on 17 of 21 tasks, with typical gaps under 3 units (e.g., −0.86 on HalfCheetah-A, −0.44 on HalfCheetah-B, −1.43 on Walker2D-C) compared to gaps of 50–125+ for most baselines. Under oracle-reward ranking (Table 2), ALIGN-GAP attains Rank 1 on 10 of 12 tasks, with the remaining two at Rank 2 or 3. On Atari (Pac-Man, Space Invaders, Alien), ALIGN-GAP similarly achieves the smallest performance gap to offline human data across all 9 task-preference combinations (Table 4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the RLPD-style symmetric-sampling fine-tuning baseline (Ball et al., 2023, 'Finetune w/SAC'), ALIGN-GAP wins decisively on nearly all tasks: e.g., on HalfCheetah-A the gap to human is −0.86 vs. +101.77, and on Walker2D-C it is −1.43 vs. −81.21. Against behavior cloning (Torabi et al., 2018), both 'Finetune w/BC' (initialized from the online agent) and 'BC' (from scratch) are highly unstable, with gaps ranging from −113 to +12 across tasks; ALIGN-GAP is consistently tighter, losing only on Hopper-A (6.46 vs. 0.01 for BC) and Hopper-C (0.57 vs. −0.05 for Finetune w/SAC). Against the demonstration-based DDPGfD-style baseline (Vecerik et al., 2017, adapted as 'SACfD'), ALIGN-GAP dominates: SACfD gaps are typically 50–125 units (e.g., −125.99 on HalfCheetah-B, −114.69 on Walker2D-C) while ALIGN-GAP stays under 10. The paper also includes a 'Finetune w/RM' baseline that directly uses the trained reward model as the RL reward without calibration or curriculum; this suffers severe unlearning (gaps of −74 to −115) and is consistently outperformed by ALIGN-GAP, confirming the necessity of both the calibration and curriculum components.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws the reward-model-plus-PPO paradigm from LLM alignment (RLHF, DPO) and adapts it to sequential decision-making, building on preference-based RL work (Guan et al., 2022; Dong et al., 2023) that already used transformer reward models over trajectory pairs. Its key conceptual departure is the 'online-to-offline' framing: rather than the dominant offline-to-online direction (CQL, IQL, CAL-QL) that leverages abundant suboptimal data to bootstrap a policy, here the agent is already well-trained online and the scarce resource is a small set of human-preference trajectories. Methodologically, the reward calibration (subtracting the initial policy's RM score) and the linear reward curriculum (interpolating between environment reward and calibrated RM reward) are the paper's novel contributions, positioned against direct RM fine-tuning and RLPD-style retraining from scratch (Ball et al., 2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical setting—aligning a game agent that already achieves high returns but plays in an unnatural style, using only ~10 episodes of human data—is a realistic and underexplored constraint in Game AI deployment. The paper's main limitation is that 'human preferences' are proxied by reward-weighted agents (locomotion) or UMAP clusters (Atari) rather than actual human annotations, and the evaluation is confined to locomotion and three Atari games; extending to individual-level alignment, richer preference signals, or domains like autonomous driving remains open. The theoretical EPIC-distance analysis (Appendix A.2) provides a clean justification for the curriculum but yields a loose bound (2/3), leaving the optimal curriculum schedule as an open question.
