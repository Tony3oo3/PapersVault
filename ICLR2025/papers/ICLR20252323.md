## Problem

Causal discovery methods are predominantly evaluated on synthetic data or tiny real-world examples (≤50 nodes), making it impossible to assess their robustness under realistic conditions such as non-stationarity, hidden confounding, non-linearity, and distributional shifts. No large-scale in-the-wild benchmark for time-series causal discovery existed, leaving practitioners without guidance on which methods perform well when theoretical assumptions are violated.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252323/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark provides raw (unpreprocessed) river discharge time-series at 15-minute resolution spanning 2019–2023, with 666 stations in eastern Germany and 494 in Bavaria, plus a 42-station Elbe flood subset exhibiting pronounced distributional shift. Because the full ground-truth graphs exceed 1000 nodes, the kit supplies subgraph sampling tools that generate thousands of connected subgraphs (3–10 nodes) with controllable characteristics such as single-sink topology, hidden confounders, root-cause chains, or disjoint components. The expected output is a directed causal graph (or edge-level predictions) between measurement stations, scored via AUROC against the hand-verified ground truth.

## Method

The core contribution is a benchmarking kit rather than a new causal discovery algorithm. Ground-truth causal graphs were constructed semi-automatically by combining state-agency metadata, remote-sensing data, Wikipedia crawls, and manual verification, yielding two graphs (RiversEastGermany, RiversBavaria) with 651 and 490 edges respectively. The kit provides subgraph samplers that enforce specific structural constraints (connectivity, max geographic distance, hidden-confounder removal, disjoint components), weather-confounding assessment tools built on DWD precipitation data, and three naive baselines (cross-correlation CC, reverse-physical RP, and their union Combo) with optional child-restriction variants. Three experiment sets evaluate established methods (PCMCI, LiNGAM, Dynotears, VAR, CDMI, Causal Pretraining) across varying graph structures, time-series subselections (full 5-year vs. no-rain month vs. flood event), and domain adaptation via fine-tuning CP on RiversBavaria samples. Key findings include that simple Granger-based approaches (VAR) and naive baselines often match or outperform more complex methods, and that domain adaptation via fine-tuning substantially improves deep-learning-based causal discovery.

## Results

On the primary benchmark (Experiment Set 1, RiversEastGermany subgraphs), the linear Granger causal approach (VAR) achieves the most consistent performance, reaching 0.82 AUROC on Random-3 and Disjoint sets. Varlingam peaks at 0.84 AUROC on Random+1-3, while CDMI matches VAR at 0.82 on Random+1-3. Notably, simple naive baselines (e.g., RP at 0.80 on Close-3, CC at 0.81 on Close-3 in Experiment Set 2) frequently match or exceed dedicated causal discovery methods, while Dynotears performs worst overall (0.50–0.61 AUROC). In Experiment Set 2, time-series subselection yields mixed effects: PCMCI improves from 0.60 to 0.69 AUROC on 'No Rain' data, while Dynotears drops from 0.79 to 0.60 on the same subset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VAR (linear Granger) is the most reliable causal discovery method, achieving 0.80–0.82 AUROC across most graph sets and outperforming PCMCI (0.62–0.83, highly variable) on 5 of 7 evaluated settings. Varlingam is competitive, reaching 0.84 on Random+1-3 and 0.83 on Disjoint, but falls below VAR on Close and Root-cause sets. CDMI (nonlinear Granger via deep networks) matches VAR on Close (0.81) and Random+1 (0.82) but degrades to 0.65 on Root-cause-5, showing sensitivity to graph structure. Dynotears (gradient-based continuous optimization) is the weakest method overall, scoring 0.50–0.61 AUROC and barely exceeding the null model, particularly on Close and Random-3. CP (Causal Pretraining, both Transformer and GRU variants) is the least competitive CD strategy, ranging 0.56–0.81, and cannot process more than 5 variables (†). Critically, the paper's proposed naive baselines (RP, CC, RPCC) achieve 0.66–0.80 AUROC, frequently matching or exceeding all five evaluated causal discovery methods, suggesting that domain-specific heuristics remain a strong baseline on this in-the-wild benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by constructing the first large-scale, fully real-world time-series benchmark for causal discovery, extending the 5-node CauseMe example (Muñoz-Marí et al., 2020) to over 1000 nodes with five years of 15-minute-resolution river discharge data. It explicitly departs from the synthetic and semi-synthetic evaluation paradigm that dominates the causal discovery literature (as critiqued by Reisach et al., 2021) and complements the industrial-systems benchmark of Mogensen et al. (2024) with a hydrological domain. The causal discovery methods evaluated (PCMCI, LiNGAM, Dynotears, CDMI, Causal Pretraining) are all pre-existing; the paper's contribution is the benchmarking infrastructure, ground-truth construction pipeline, and empirical comparison rather than a new algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a critical gap: sophisticated causal discovery methods (constraint-based, ANM-based, gradient-based, deep) are frequently matched or outperformed by trivial domain-informed heuristics (cross-correlation, river magnitude), suggesting that current methods do not adequately handle the non-stationarity, hidden weather confounding, and variable causal lags inherent in real-world time-series. The domain-adaptation experiment (fine-tuning Causal Pretraining on a different German region) shows that transfer can substantially close this gap, pointing to a promising but underexplored direction. Open questions include how to design principled time-window subselection strategies, whether the naive-baseline advantage persists in less physically-constrained domains, and how to handle the fundamental challenge of time-varying causal lags that most methods assume away.
