## Problem

Despite a diverse set of proposed methods for learning optimal data mixture proportions in language model pretraining, no existing method consistently outperforms a simple stratified sampling baseline across different data group combinations. The paper identifies that this inconsistency stems from existing methods setting their implicit mixing-law parameters (which encode cross-group loss interactions) inaccurately, and unifies all prior methods under a single optimization framework to expose this shared weakness.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251650/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

AIOLI takes m text data groups (each with train, validation, and test splits), a language model, and optionally an initial static mixture p_init with a number of pre-training steps S_init. Within each training round, it allocates a fraction δ of steps to estimate the interaction matrix A^t by interleaving training on m smoothed one-hot mixtures (p^{t,i} = (1−ε)·1_i + ε·Unif(m)), recording per-group validation loss changes, and solving a linear system. The output is a sequence of dynamic mixture proportions p^1, ..., p^T that are updated via exponentiated gradient descent, along with the final trained model.

## Method

The paper introduces the Linear Mixing Optimization (LMO) framework, showing that all existing data mixing methods (DML, Skill-It, DoReMi, DoGE) are equivalent to minimizing average validation loss subject to a method-specific mixing law L^{t+1}_i(p) = c^t_i + b^t_i · σ(Σ_j A^t_ij p^t_j), solved either directly (offline) or via exponentiated gradient descent (online). AIOLI is derived from this framework by specifying a linear dynamic mixing law (σ=Id, T>1) and EGD as the solver, then directly estimating A^t from the current training run by fitting the mixing law to the history of observed loss changes under known mixture proportions. The key mechanism is LEARNPARAMS: within each round, a fraction δ is split into K=mk intervals where the model trains on interleaved near-one-hot mixtures, and the resulting loss differences are used to solve A^t = P^{-1}β for each group. This yields A^t estimates that are consistently closer to the true optimal parameters than those used by existing methods, translating into robust perplexity improvements across all six evaluated data settings.

## Results

In the unrestricted setting (up to 10 extra training runs allowed), AIOLI consistently outperforms stratified sampling on all 6 data settings by an average of 0.274 test perplexity points (up to 0.439), while using zero extra training runs. In contrast, every existing method (DML, Skill-It, DoReMi, DoGE) underperforms stratified sampling on at least one dataset, with DoReMi performing up to 6.9 perplexity points worse. In the restricted setting (only 0.5S extra steps for learning proportions), adding AIOLI to dynamically adjust learned proportions improves average test perplexity by 1.202 points in 28 out of 30 cases, with a maximum improvement of 12.012 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AIOLI is the only method that beats stratified sampling on all 6 data settings (A/SE, GH/C4, B/SE, A/B/SE, CC/GH/W, SlimPajama), doing so with zero extra training runs. DoReMi is the weakest baseline, performing 5.303 points worse than stratified on GH/C4 and 6.898 points worse on CC/GH/W despite using 2 extra runs. DoGE beats stratified on only 1 of 6 settings (B/SE, −0.678) and is worse on the other 5, including a 1.843-point degradation on A/B/SE. Skill-It is the strongest existing online method (beats stratified on 5/6) but still fails on GH/C4 (+0.551). DML, the offline method, beats stratified on 4/6 but degrades by 1.641 points on full SlimPajama (m=7), likely due to the limited 10-run budget for fitting its regression model. In the restricted setting, AIOLI+method consistently improves over the base method's static proportions, with the largest gains (up to 12.012 points) occurring when the base method's proportions are most suboptimal (e.g., DoGE on SlimPajama: 32.301 → 26.073).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by unifying four previously independent data mixing methods—DML (offline regression), Skill-It (skills-graph curriculum), DoReMi (distributionally robust optimization), and DoGE (gradient-based reweighting)—into a single optimization framework (LMO) that reveals they all solve the same problem under different implicit mixing-law assumptions. Rather than building on one prior method, the paper repurposes the exponentiated gradient descent update rule as a Rosetta stone connecting each method's proportion-update rule to a specific parameterization of a linear or log-linear loss-proportion relationship. The key departure is diagnostic: the paper shows the parameterizations are well-specified but the parameters are set inaccurately, and then derives AIOLI, which directly estimates those parameters from training history rather than relying on heuristic approximations (static skills graphs, diagonal matrices, or gradient inner products).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The LMO framework provides a reusable diagnostic lens for evaluating and improving any data mixing method by isolating which of three components (parameterization, parameter values, solving strategy) is the bottleneck. AIOLI is practically attractive because it requires zero extra training runs and can be layered on top of proportions learned from shorter runs, addressing a real compute constraint in modern LLM pretraining. Limitations include the extra inference cost of LEARNPARAMS, evaluation only on 160M-parameter models, and the observation that some data group pairs (e.g., Books/C4) exhibit non-linear loss-proportion relationships that the linear mixing law cannot capture. Open questions include whether the linear dynamic mixing law's fidelity degrades at later training stages or larger scales, and whether disjoint data group partitions would improve performance.
