## Problem

Classic online scheduling for max-stretch minimization either assumes perfect knowledge of job sizes (clairvoyant) or no knowledge at all (non-clairvoyant), leaving a gap for the realistic setting where ML models provide imperfect job size predictions. This paper is the first to develop learning-augmented algorithms for fair scheduling, asking how to make fair resource-allocation decisions with predictions whose quality is unknown, while maintaining worst-case robustness guarantees.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252395/cropped_page9_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives, at each job's release, a multiplicative-error job size prediction p_j, plus upfront predictions of the minimum and maximum job sizes (p_min^e, p_max^e) with errors λ and φ respectively. It produces a preemptive single-machine schedule in O(1) time per scheduling decision using O(n) space and at most n−1 preemptions, with the max-stretch bounded by O(η³√P)·OPT for RG (or tighter bounds for ARG/PRG depending on which extreme-size predictions are available). Notably, the algorithm does not require knowledge of the prediction error η itself.

## Method

The core mechanism reduces the general multi-size scheduling problem to a two-job-size case via a relaxed-stretch approximation: jobs are classified as short (p_j ≤ α) or long (p_j > α) where α = √(p_min·p_max)/√3, and the true stretch is approximated by dividing response time by α (short) or β = p_max (long), overestimating by at most η²√P. The scheduler then greedily runs the active job with the largest relaxed-stretch, which achieves a constant factor (√3) over the optimal relaxed-stretch, yielding the O(η³√P) competitive ratio. The Adaptive variant updates α online as new job predictions arrive while freezing each job's classification at its release time, and the Predictive variant uses both extreme-size predictions for tighter bounds. A parameterized family RG^x interpolates between FIFO (x=0, O(P)-competitive, η-independent) and RG (x=1/2, O(√P)-consistent, O(η³)-smooth), and an RR-augmented variant RG⁺ achieves asymptotically optimal O(n) robustness via (1+ε)-speed resource augmentation.

## Results

The paper evaluates algorithms using the performance ratio (algorithm's max-stretch divided by the optimal max-stretch) on synthetic and real-world cloud workloads. Under perfect predictions (η=1), RG and ARG outperform both GWR (Bender et al., 2002) and Round Robin across varying numbers of jobs (1,000–10,000) and job size ratios P (1–40). RG+ (RR-augmented RG at 1.3× speed) consistently achieves the lowest performance ratio across all settings, and critically remains bounded as prediction error η increases from 1 to 512, while RG and ARG degrade. On real-world traces from Google, Azure, and Alibaba Cloud, RG+ achieves the best performance at all prediction error levels (η from 1 to 6).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary external baseline is GWR (Greedy-with-Rounding) from Bender et al. (2002), a clairvoyant O(√P)-competitive algorithm that assumes known extreme job sizes. RG and ARG outperform GWR in most synthetic scenarios under perfect predictions, and RG+ (with only 0.3× speed augmentation) consistently achieves the best performance ratio across all settings. Against Round Robin (the standard non-clairvoyant O(n)-competitive baseline), RG and ARG also outperform it in most scenarios. Among the paper's own algorithms, ARG degrades fastest as prediction error η increases on real-world data, while RG's performance falls between ARG and GWR under imperfect predictions. RG+ is the clear winner: it maintains a bounded performance ratio even as η grows large, whereas all other algorithms (including GWR and RR) show unbounded degradation. The key practical takeaway is that RG+ dominates both the clairvoyant GWR and the non-clairvoyant RR baselines on all three real-world cloud datasets (Google, Azure, Alibaba) across the full range of prediction errors tested.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the O(√P)-competitive clairvoyant max-stretch scheduling algorithms of Bender et al. (2002) to the non-clairvoyant setting by replacing exact job size knowledge with ML predictions, using a two-job-size reduction via a relaxed-stretch metric. It also adapts the learning-augmented framework (consistency, smoothness, robustness) introduced by Purohit et al. (2018) to the min-max fairness objective, where prior learning-augmented scheduling work focused on min-sum objectives. The resource augmentation technique from Kalyanasundaram and Pruhs (2000) is repurposed to fix the weak O(n·P) robustness of the base algorithms, after showing that the standard Preferential Round Robin combination fails for max-stretch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first learning-augmented framework for fair (max-stretch) scheduling, providing a principled way to leverage imperfect ML predictions for resource allocation fairness in cloud and datacenter settings. The parameterized RG^x family gives practitioners a tunable consistency-smoothness trade-off, and the RR-augmented RG+ achieves near-optimal robustness with only a small speed overhead (e.g., 1.3×). Open questions include whether the cubic dependency on prediction error η is tight, whether the RG^x trade-off is Pareto-optimal, and how to extend the approach to parallel identical machines.
