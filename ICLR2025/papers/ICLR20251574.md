## Problem

Directly prompting LLMs on decision-making under uncertainty yields poor and increasingly unreliable results as the number of actions grows, because conventional approaches (zero-shot, self-consistency, chain-of-thought) fail to systematically balance evidence, quantify uncertainty, or align with user preferences. Beyond accuracy, there is a critical need for human auditability—understanding *why* a model made a particular decision—which opaque prompting strategies do not provide.

## Input / Output

The method takes a user prompt P = (G, A, C) consisting of a natural-language goal description, a discrete set of n actions, and contextual information that may be free-text (e.g., USDA agricultural reports) or tabular (e.g., 24 months of historical stock prices). Beyond returning the single optimal action a*, DeLLMa produces intermediate, inspectable artifacts: a discretized state space of size ℓ^k (k latent factors × ℓ bins each), a probability distribution over states, a scalar utility value for every sampled state-action pair, and the Monte Carlo expected utility for each action—enabling a human to audit any step of the reasoning chain.

## Method

DeLLMa is a four-step inference-time framework that scaffolds an LLM onto classical decision theory. (1) State Enumeration: the LLM identifies k latent factors and ℓ plausible values per factor, discretizing the state space into ℓ^k states. (2) State Forecasting: the LLM assigns verbalized probability scores (e.g., 'very likely' to 'very unlikely') to each state value, which are mapped to numeric weights and normalized under an independence assumption to yield a proposal distribution π^LLM(θ|C). (3) Utility Elicitation: state-action pairs are sampled from π^LLM, grouped into overlapping minibatches (with a tunable overlap proportion q), and the LLM ranks each batch; pairwise preferences are extracted via Rank2Pairs or One-vs-All and fit with a Bradley-Terry model to recover a scalar utility function U(θ,a). Two key engineering choices—overlapping batching for finer-grained preference signal and variance reduction by sharing the same state samples across all actions—improve both accuracy and computational efficiency. (4) Expected Utility Maximization: expected utility per action is computed analytically via Monte Carlo averaging over sampled states, and the argmax action is returned. The entire pipeline is modular and human-auditable, and performance scales favorably with test-time compute (sample size and overlap percentage).

## Results

On the Agriculture dataset, DeLLMa-Pairs and DeLLMa-Top1 consistently outperform zero-shot, self-consistency, and CoT baselines, with the gap widening as the number of actions increases (up to ~40% absolute accuracy improvement for larger action sets). On the Stocks dataset, DeLLMa-Top1 outperforms all baselines on average, while DeLLMa-Naive only marginally improves over baselines due to high data volatility. Against OpenAI o1-preview, DeLLMa achieves 73.3% vs 33.3% accuracy on Agriculture and 64.2% vs 35.0% on Stocks, at comparable or lower cost ($0.09–$0.37 vs $0.21 per instance). Human evaluation shows 65–68% agreement between DeLLMa's utility rankings and human annotators across GPT-4, Claude 3, and Gemini 1.5.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DeLLMa outperforms all three prompting baselines (zero-shot, self-consistency, and CoT) on both Agriculture and Stocks, with the largest gains appearing when the action set size grows. Self-consistency provides minimal benefit because LLMs are highly confident in their decisions even at elevated temperatures, so majority voting rarely changes the answer. CoT, which mimics DeLLMa's multi-step structure but delegates all reasoning to the LLM without external computation, still fails to systematically handle uncertainty or balance evidence across actions. Against OpenAI o1-preview (a state-of-the-art inference-time reasoning model), DeLLMa wins by a wide margin—40 percentage points on Agriculture (73.3% vs 33.3%) and ~29 points on Stocks (64.2% vs 35.0%)—while costing less per instance ($0.09–$0.37 vs $0.21). The paper also notes that baseline methods can underperform random guessing on larger action sets, a failure mode DeLLMa avoids through its structured state-forecasting and utility-elicitation pipeline.

## Relation to Prior Work

DeLLMa sits at the intersection of classical decision theory (expected utility maximization, utility elicitation via Bradley-Terry models) and recent LLM inference-time reasoning (CoT, self-consistency, parallel sampling). It departs from prior LLM decision-making work—which has focused on blackbox function optimization or many low-cost sequential decisions—by targeting single-step, high-stakes decisions under uncertainty with an explicit decision-theoretic scaffold. The verbalized-probability-to-numeric mapping for state forecasting adapts techniques from Xiong et al. (2023) and Tian et al. (2023), while the overall pipeline is a novel composition of these components into a structured, auditable decision procedure rather than a free-form reasoning trace.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates a systematic failure mode of standard LLM prompting (zero-shot, self-consistency, CoT) for decision-making under uncertainty—underperforming random guessing as action sets grow—which motivates the structured approach. The human-auditability property (explicit state forecasts, utility rankings, and expected utility calculations) is practically important for high-stakes domains like medicine and finance where black-box recommendations are unacceptable. Key limitations include the restriction to discrete, enumerable state and action spaces, evaluation on only two domains with constructed ground truth, and no treatment of sequential or portfolio decisions; scaling to continuous or combinatorial state spaces and validating in live deployment remain open.
