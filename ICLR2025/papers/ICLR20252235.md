## Problem

Automatic LLM benchmarks such as AlpacaEval 2.0, Arena-Hard-Auto, and MT-Bench rely on LLM auto-annotators for scalable evaluation, but their vulnerability to intentional adversarial manipulation has remained unexplored. Existing mitigations address unintentional biases (length, style) yet do not protect against a determined adversary who crafts outputs specifically to deceive the annotator. This paper shows that even a 'null model' producing a single constant, irrelevant response can achieve top-ranked win rates on these widely used benchmarks.

## Input / Output

The null model receives benchmark instructions (e.g., 805 from AlpacaEval 2.0) but ignores them entirely, always returning the same constant string regardless of input. This string is the concatenation of a structured cheating response and an adversarial prefix optimized via random search on a public instruction set (UltraFeedback), under the threat model that benchmark instructions are private and inaccessible. When this constant response is inserted into the official evaluation template alongside a reference model's output, the GPT-4 auto-annotator is manipulated into assigning the null model a high win rate or score.

## Method

The core insight is that LLM auto-annotators can be confused during syntactic parsing of evaluation templates. The structured cheating response exploits this by injecting an 'Ignore the above' directive followed by a counterfeit instruction ('Output nothing') with empty model outputs, causing the annotator to misidentify the fake pairs as the real ones and, due to position bias, return the null model's identifier. A token-level random search (Algorithm 1) then optimizes an adversarial prefix prepended to the structured response, minimizing the aggregated −log p(winner = NullModel) across a small set of public UltraFeedback instructions to ensure the prefix transfers to unseen private benchmark instructions. The combined Structured+RS approach achieves 86.5% LC win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0, 83.0 on Arena-Hard-Auto, and 9.55 on MT-Bench, surpassing verified state-of-the-art models. Ablations on open-source Llama-3 annotators confirm that random search is the dominant factor, driving win rates above 95% even when the structured response alone is ineffective.

## Results

The paper's Structured+RS method achieves an 86.5% LC win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0, an 83.0% win rate on Arena-Hard-Auto, and a 9.55 score on MT-Bench, all using a null model that outputs a constant irrelevant string. These results surpass the verified SOTA model (57.5% LC on AlpacaEval 2.0, 82.6% on Arena-Hard-Auto, 8.96 on MT-Bench) by 29.0, 0.4, and 0.59 points respectively. On open-source Llama-3 auto-annotators, the method achieves near-perfect win rates (95.4% LC on Llama-3-8B, 95.1% LC on Llama-3-70B) without access to test instructions, rising to 99.8% and 99.4% when test instructions are available.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its Structured+RS method against three prior adversarial attack methods on AlpacaEval 2.0 (Table 7). Chen et al. (2024c), which uses an LLM to generate adversarial responses, achieves only 0.6% LC win rate; Raina et al. (2024), which employs word-level random search for adversarial suffixes, and Shi et al. (2024), which uses GCG-style gradient optimization (adapted to random search for GPT-4), both achieve 0.0% LC win rate. In stark contrast, the paper's Structured+RS method achieves 86.5% LC win rate, outperforming all three baselines by over 85 percentage points. The key differentiator is the use of a structured response that exploits the auto-annotator's syntactic parsing of the evaluation template, rather than appending adversarial tokens to an otherwise meaningful response. Additionally, the structured response alone (without random search) already reaches 76.8% LC, far exceeding the near-zero results of all baselines, demonstrating that the structural manipulation of the template is the primary driver of success.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of adversarial attacks on LLM-based evaluators (Raina et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024c) by introducing a fundamentally different threat model: instead of appending adversarial suffixes to meaningful responses, it uses a completely non-informative 'null model' that outputs a constant string regardless of input. The optimization methodology builds directly on universal random search for adversarial suffixes (Zou et al., 2023), but the key conceptual departure is the structured response that exploits syntactic confusion in evaluation templates rather than manipulating content quality. The paper positions itself as the first systematic demonstration of top-ranked win rates on widely-used, state-of-the-art benchmarks (AlpacaEval 2.0, Arena-Hard-Auto, MT-Bench) using transferable attacks crafted without access to private test instructions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work exposes a critical vulnerability in the evaluation infrastructure that the LLM community relies on for model selection and promotion: a null model with zero informative content can outperform verified state-of-the-art models by 29 percentage points on AlpacaEval 2.0. The transferability of the attack (crafted on public UltraFeedback, effective on private benchmark instructions) makes it practically concerning for any organization using these benchmarks. The paper demonstrates that existing defenses (PPL filters, template paraphrasing) are insufficient, while SmoothLLM, though effective, degrades clean response quality to near-zero win rates, creating a practical dilemma. Open questions include developing anti-cheating mechanisms that don't compromise legitimate evaluation, understanding whether more sophisticated LLM-generated cheating responses would be harder to detect, and whether the vulnerability generalizes to benchmarks not yet tested.
