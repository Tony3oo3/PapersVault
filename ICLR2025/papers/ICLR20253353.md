## Problem

Sampling transition paths between metastable states in molecular systems is computationally infeasible with unbiased MD due to high energy barriers, and existing CV-free ML approaches (e.g., PIPS) rely on reverse KL divergence that suffers from mode collapse and are limited to small peptides or synthetic systems. This paper addresses the gap of producing diverse, realistic transition pathways for large molecular systems (fast-folding proteins up to 47 amino acids) without requiring domain-expertise-derived collective variables.

## Input / Output

The method takes a molecular system defined by atomic positions and velocities (simulated via Langevin dynamics), a fixed initial local minimum R_A, and a target local minimum R_B, and produces a trained neural network bias force that, when applied to biased MD simulation, generates transition paths from A to B. The bias force is parameterized as atom-wise positive scaling factors of the displacement direction to the Kabsch-aligned target state, and the target indicator is relaxed to an RBF kernel whose maximum over intermediate states enables variable-length path truncation for denser training signals.

## Method

TPS-DPS trains a diffusion path sampler by minimizing the log-variance divergence between the path measure induced by a biased Langevin SDE and the target transition path measure, using a learnable scalar control variate to reduce gradient estimator variance without changing the gradient direction. Training is off-policy: a replay buffer stores paths from previous policies, and simulated annealing (high-to-low temperature schedule) enables the biased MD to cross energy barriers and maintain path diversity, preventing mode collapse. The key architectural contribution is a scale-based SE(3)-equivariant parameterization where the neural network predicts atom-wise positive scaling factors (via Softplus) of the direction from the current state to the Kabsch-aligned target, guaranteeing that the bias force strictly decreases the distance to the target at each MD step for sufficiently small step sizes. The indicator function is further relaxed to an RBF kernel with a max operation over all intermediate states, allowing subtrajectories that approach the target to contribute training signal and supporting variable path lengths.

## Results

On Alanine Dipeptide, TPS-DPS(F) achieves 92.19% target hit percentage (THP) with RMSD of 0.16 Å and ETS of 19.82 kJ/mol, substantially outperforming PIPS(F) (43.75% THP, 0.66 Å RMSD) and SMD(20) (54.69% THP, 0.56 Å RMSD). On the four fast-folding proteins, the scale-based parameterization TPS-DPS(S) is the only method that consistently hits the target state: 59.38% THP on Chignolin, 84.38% on BBA, 81.25% on Trp-cage, and 43.75% on BBL, while PIPS(F) and PIPS(P) achieve 0% THP on all four proteins. TPS-DPS(S) also achieves the lowest RMSD (0.76–1.60 Å) and most probable transition states (lowest ETS) across all proteins compared to SMD(20k), which reaches at most 42.19% THP (Trp-cage) and 26.56% (BBA).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PIPS (Holdijk et al., 2024), the closest ML baseline, TPS-DPS(S) achieves 59–84% THP on fast-folding proteins while PIPS(F) and PIPS(P) achieve 0% THP on all four proteins, indicating PIPS suffers from severe mode collapse or failure to find any meaningful transition paths at this scale. On Alanine Dipeptide, TPS-DPS(F) nearly doubles PIPS(F)'s THP (92.19% vs. 43.75%) and reduces RMSD by 4× (0.16 vs. 0.66 Å), while PIPS(P) completely fails (0% THP). Against SMD (Schlitter et al., 1994; Izrailev et al., 1999), TPS-DPS(S) achieves 2–5× higher THP on all four proteins (e.g., 84.38% vs. 26.56% on BBA, 81.25% vs. 42.19% on Trp-cage) and lower ETS, indicating more probable transition states. Notably, TPS-DPS(F) and TPS-DPS(P) also fail on the fast-folding proteins (0% THP), demonstrating that the scale-based equivariant parameterization is essential for large systems where training signals are sparse. Unbiased MD at all tested temperatures (300K–1200K) fails to reach the target state on any protein, confirming the computational infeasibility of the problem without enhanced sampling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of CV-free transition path sampling (TPS) methods that parameterize bias forces with neural networks and train them via biased molecular dynamics. It builds directly on the log-variance divergence framework of Nusken & Richter (2021) for training on path space, and on PIPS (Holdijk et al., 2024), which used reverse KL divergence for the same task. The key departures are: (1) replacing reverse KL with log-variance divergence to avoid mode collapse, (2) introducing off-policy training with replay buffers and simulated annealing for sample efficiency, and (3) proposing a scale-based SE(3)-equivariant parameterization of the bias force that guarantees monotonic approach to the target state, addressing the sparse-training-signal problem in large molecules.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that CV-free ML-based TPS can scale to fast-folding proteins (up to 47 amino acids) where prior ML methods (PIPS) completely fail to reach target states, and where classical methods require domain-expert collective variables. The scale-based parameterization is a practical inductive bias that makes the difference between 0% and 40–84% target-hit rates on proteins, suggesting that structural priors matter more than training objective alone for large systems. However, the method is limited to small proteins, does not generalize across unseen state pairs or molecular systems, and integration with production MD libraries (PLUMED, DMFF) remains unexplored, leaving open the question of applicability to real drug-discovery-scale systems (>500 residues).
