## Problem

While data scaling laws are well-established in NLP and computer vision, no comprehensive scaling laws exist for robotic manipulation, leaving the field without guidance on how to allocate data collection effort. This paper asks whether appropriate data scaling can produce single-task robot policies that generalize zero-shot to any object within the same category in any environment, and which data dimensions—number of environments, objects, or demonstrations—drive generalization most effectively.

## Input / Output

The policy consumes a short observation window (2–3 timesteps) of 224×224 RGB images from a wrist-mounted GoPro Hero 10 fisheye camera plus proprioceptive robot state, and outputs a 16-step end-effector action sequence. Inference uses DDIM with 16 denoising iterations at 5 Hz, and a temporal ensemble strategy (8 overlapping steps with exponential weighting) smooths transitions between consecutive action chunks to avoid jerky motions. The observation horizon is extended to 3 steps for Pour Water and Unplug Charger to help the policy distinguish task phases.

## Method

The paper is primarily an empirical study rather than a new architecture. It uses Diffusion Policy with a fully fine-tuned DINOv2 ViT-L/14 visual encoder and a base-size 1D CNN U-Net for action prediction, trained via behavior cloning on data collected with UMI hand-held grippers across 32 in-the-wild environments and 32 manipulation objects per task. The key finding is that generalization performance follows a power-law relationship with the number of training environments, objects, or environment-object pairs, while the number of demonstrations per pair saturates quickly (around 50). Based on these laws, the authors propose an efficient collection strategy—32 environment-object pairs with 50 demonstrations each—that achieves ~90% success rates on unseen environments and objects with only one afternoon of data collection by four collectors, validated across four manipulation tasks.

## Results

The paper establishes power-law scaling relationships between policy generalization performance and the number of training environments, objects, or environment-object pairs, with Pearson correlation coefficients confirming the fit. Using the proposed data collection strategy (32 environment-object pairs, 50 demonstrations each), policies achieve approximately 90% success rates on novel environments and unseen objects across four manipulation tasks: Pour Water (85.0%), Mouse Arrangement (92.5%), Fold Towels (87.5%), and Unplug Charger (90.0%). A key finding is that diversity of environments and objects is far more important than the absolute number of demonstrations; once ~50 demos per environment-object pair are collected, additional demonstrations yield minimal gains. The entire data collection for the two new tasks (Fold Towels, Unplug Charger) required only one afternoon with four data collectors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical scaling study rather than a method comparison, so there are no external baselines. The internal comparisons focus on data collection dimensions: increasing the number of training objects from 1 to 32 raises the normalized score from ~0.13 to ~0.93 (Pour Water), while increasing environments from 1 to 32 raises it from ~0.14 to ~0.96. Critically, the paper shows that at 32 objects, the performance gap between 12.5% and 100% of demonstrations nearly vanishes, whereas at 8 objects the gap remains large—demonstrating that diversity dominates over demonstration count. On the model side (Table 2), DINOv2 ViT-L/14 with full fine-tuning scores 0.90, while learning from scratch scores 0.03, frozen DINOv2 scores 0.00, and LoRA fine-tuning scores only 0.72, confirming that both pre-training and full fine-tuning are essential. Scaling the action diffusion U-Net from small to large (512→2048 features) does not improve performance (0.88→0.90→0.83), suggesting the bottleneck is in the visual encoder, not the action model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., 2023) as its core policy architecture and UMI (Chi et al., 2024) as its data collection interface, incorporating the temporal ensemble strategy from ACT (Zhao et al., 2023) for smoother action execution. It extends the scaling laws literature from NLP and CV (Kaplan et al., 2020) into robotic manipulation, but deliberately departs from prior generalization work by studying out-of-domain generalization to entirely novel environments and objects rather than in-domain compositional generalization (Gao et al., 2024) or cross-embodiment transfer (Padalkar et al., 2023). While UMI and concurrent work RUMs (Etukuru et al., 2024) demonstrate that diverse data improves generalization, this paper is the first to systematically quantify the relationship between generalization performance and each data dimension (environments, objects, demonstrations) under a unified power-law framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that environment and object diversity dominates over demonstration quantity ('diversity is all you need') provides a concrete, actionable data collection strategy: 32 environment-object pairs with 50 demos each (~1,600 total demos, collectable in one afternoon by four people) yields ~90% zero-shot success in unseen environments with unseen objects. This dramatically lowers the perceived barrier to deploying generalizable manipulation policies and shifts the field's focus from collecting more demos per condition to covering more conditions. Key limitations include the restriction to four relatively simple single-task policies, imitation learning only (no RL), and UMI-specific data collection; open questions include whether these scaling laws hold for dexterous multi-task policies, language-conditioned generalization, and whether the power-law exponents transfer across task complexity.
