## Problem

Existing offline RLHF methods like DPO and IPO suffer from two fundamental limitations: (1) trained models lack an innate self-improvement mechanism to correct errors at inference time, and (2) their optimal solutions depend heavily on the behavior policy μ used to generate preference data, making them fragile when the evaluation distribution differs from the training distribution. This paper asks whether preference learning can be recast as a self-improvement process whose solution is provably independent of μ.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250147/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes offline pairwise preference data (context x, preferred completion y_w, dis-preferred completion y_l) sampled from a behavior policy, with no reward model or online rollouts required. At inference, the single LLM first generates a 0-revision completion from x alone, then iteratively conditions on its own previous output to produce 1-rev, 2-rev, … N-rev improved completions via in-context self-improvement (π(y'|y,x)). Both the generative and self-improvement policies are represented by the same LLM, exploiting in-context learning so that π†(y'|y,x) = π(y'|y,x).

## Method

SRPO formulates preference alignment as a KL-regularized min-max objective: the self-improvement policy π† is maximized to produce completions most preferred over the in-context completion y, while the generative policy π is minimized so its outputs require minimal improvement. The key theoretical result (Theorem 1) shows this adversarial game admits a closed-form solution expressible as a non-adversarial supervised L2 loss that jointly fits the preference probability p in terms of log-likelihood ratios of both π†* and π* relative to a reference model. Unlike DPO, this identity holds for arbitrary preference models (not just Bradley-Terry), and the resulting optimal policies are provably independent of the behavior policy μ. Training reduces to minimizing a convex combination of two L2 regression losses (one for the self-improvement policy, one for the joint generative+improvement policy) on the offline preference dataset using standard SGD, with no reward model or online inference needed.

## Results

On the OOD XSum summarization task, SRPO achieves 90% win rate against gold human summaries after 5 self-revisions, outperforming DPO by a 15% margin. On Arena-Hard prompts, SRPO reaches 56% WR against Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct at 0-revision and outperforms DPO and IPO by 4% (0-rev.) and 6% (1-rev.). In the in-distribution TL;DR setting, SRPO 4-rev. achieves the highest win rate against gold summaries, though the gap over baselines is smaller than in the OOD case. A key qualitative finding is that DPO and IPO show no improvement through self-revision, while SRPO consistently gains with each revision iteration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SRPO is compared against four offline preference optimization baselines: DPO, IPO, SIMPO, and RPO. On Arena-Hard, SRPO 0-rev. beats DPO by ~4% (53.67% WR), IPO by ~1%, RPO by ~1%, and SIMPO by ~13%; after one self-revision, the margins widen to 6% over DPO, 7.5% over IPO, 8.3% over RPO, and 25% over SIMPO. On the OOD XSum task, SRPO 5-rev. outperforms DPO by 15% in win rate against gold summaries, and the performance gap between SRPO and baselines is significantly larger in the OOD setting than in-distribution, confirming the robustness advantage. A critical distinction is that DPO and IPO fail to produce any improvement through self-revision (their 1-rev. outputs do not beat 0-rev.), whereas SRPO's self-improvement mechanism yields consistent gains at every revision step. In the in-distribution TL;DR setting, the gap between SRPO and baselines is smaller, which the authors attribute to the reduced importance of robustness when the evaluation distribution matches the training distribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

SRPO builds directly on the KL-regularized RL formulation of DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) and IPO (Azar et al., 2023), both of which express preference probabilities in terms of the optimal policy, and on Todorov's (2006) analytical solution for KL-regularized MDPs. The key departure is the introduction of a self-improvement policy π† that operates on in-context completions, casting alignment as a min-max game between a generative policy and an improvement policy. Unlike DPO, which requires the Bradley-Terry assumption, SRPO's theoretical result holds for arbitrary preference models, and unlike Nash-MD (Munos et al., 2023), the self-improvement structure allows the min-max problem to collapse into a single supervised loss without online play. The paper positions itself as the first to jointly train a self-improvement model and a generative policy from pairwise preference data through one offline objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical implication is that SRPO's optimal solution is provably independent of the behavior policy μ, making it robust to distribution shifts between training and evaluation — a known failure mode of DPO and IPO that the synthetic bandit example illustrates cleanly. The self-improvement mechanism also provides a principled inference-time refinement loop, though gains are most pronounced on in-distribution summarization (15% over DPO on XSum after 5 revisions) and diminish sharply on harder prompts (Arena-Hard shows a drop after 1 revision for all methods). Open questions include whether the robustness advantage persists on complex multi-task benchmarks where existing RLHF methods overfit to dominant task distributions, and whether a single LLM can adequately serve as both generative and self-improvement policy at scale. The method's simplicity (a supervised L2 loss on preference pairs, no reward model, no online RL) makes it easy to adopt, but the experimental scope remains limited to summarization tasks.
