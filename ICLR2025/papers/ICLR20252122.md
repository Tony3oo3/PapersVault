## Problem

The standard practice of evaluating reward models via accuracy on a fixed preference dataset assumes this metric predicts downstream policy performance, yet the relationship between RM accuracy and actual policy regret remains largely unexplored. This paper systematically investigates whether accuracy is a sufficient proxy for predicting how much a policy will degrade when optimized against an imperfect reward model, and what factors modulate this relationship.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252122/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes N=10 reward models (created by randomly flipping training preference pairs at varying rates) and systematically forms N×(N-1) golden-proxy pairs, evaluating each on RewardBench prompts with responses sampled from multiple source models. The output is a correlation analysis between RM error metrics (accuracy, Spearman/Kendall/ξ rank correlations, MRR, NDCG) and policy regret measured by Normalised Drop Ratio (NDR), under both best-of-n sampling and PPO. A key design dimension is varying the number of responses per prompt (2 to 5+) while controlling for either total sample size or annotation budget (modeled via Bradley-Terry pairwise comparisons).

## Method

The core contribution is a synthetic experiment framework that trains 10 RMs with different levels of label noise, then optimizes policies (via BoN and PPO) against each proxy while measuring regret against the golden RM, yielding N×(N-1) golden-proxy pairs for correlation analysis. They derive a theoretical parametric relationship between accuracy and overoptimization degree under Regressional Goodhart's effect with normal reward/noise distributions, showing the expected curve via numerical integration. Empirically, they discover that response rank (not the sampling model) drives correlation strength, that prompt distribution mismatch between RM test and downstream test sets weakens the accuracy-regret link, and that increasing responses per prompt consistently improves correlation under both fixed sample size and fixed annotation budget constraints. They further demonstrate that RMs with similar accuracy can exhibit dramatically different overoptimization dynamics, indicating accuracy alone is insufficient to capture the full Goodhart's law effect.

## Results

The paper finds a weak positive correlation between RM accuracy and policy regret: Spearman correlation is 0.7533 under best-of-n (BoN) sampling and 0.6102 under PPO, with MRR of 0.6333 and 0.5167 respectively. However, policies optimized toward RMs within a similar accuracy range can exhibit substantially different regrets. When expanding to 5 responses per prompt, the ξ correlation metric achieves the best Spearman correlation (0.677 on BoN, 0.688 on PPO), outperforming the standard accuracy-pair baseline (0.635/0.642), while ECE shows near-zero correlation (0.173/0.063).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the 'baselines' are alternative evaluation metrics and experimental configurations. Standard accuracy (2 responses per prompt) serves as the primary baseline; expanding to 5 responses per prompt with rank-based metrics (Spearman, Kendall τ, ξ correlation) consistently improves correlation with policy regret by 2–4 percentage points in Spearman coefficient. ECE, despite its common use in calibration literature, shows negligible correlation (0.173 Spearman on BoN), making it unsuitable for predicting downstream performance. BoN optimization yields stronger accuracy–regret correlations than PPO across all metrics (e.g., 0.7533 vs. 0.6102 Spearman), attributed to BoN's more localized and stable optimization dynamics. Sampling responses exclusively from the downstream model does not consistently improve correlation over the original mixed-model RewardBench responses, suggesting response rank distribution matters more than the sampling source.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Regressional Goodhart framework (Manheim & Garrabrant, 2019) and the conditional expectation result from Gao et al. (2022) to derive a theoretical link between RM accuracy and overoptimization degree, while adapting the policy regret formalism from Karwowski et al. (2023) and Fluri et al. (2024) to the RLHF setting. It departs from the standard practice of treating accuracy on a fixed preference dataset (e.g., RewardBench) as a sufficient proxy for RM quality by systematically questioning whether this metric actually predicts downstream policy performance. The synthetic golden-proxy RM construction methodology follows the approach of Gao et al. (2022) and Gao et al. (2024) for studying overoptimization, but repurposes it specifically to evaluate the predictive power of RM error metrics rather than to characterize overoptimization scaling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a foundational assumption in the RLHF community—that accuracy on a held-out preference set is a reliable indicator of how well a reward model will serve policy optimization—by showing only a weak correlation and substantial variance among RMs with similar accuracy. This raises the open question of what evaluation protocol should replace or supplement accuracy, and the finding that increasing responses per prompt (under fixed annotation budget) improves correlation offers a concrete, low-cost direction. However, the entire analysis relies on a synthetic setting where another learned RM serves as the 'golden' reward, so the conclusions may not fully transfer to the true human-preference regime where the gap between proxy and golden is qualitatively different. The work also highlights that different optimization algorithms (BoN vs. PPO) interact differently with RM error, suggesting that evaluation recommendations may need to be algorithm-specific.
