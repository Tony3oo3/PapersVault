## Problem

Existing knowledge distillation methods for LLMs face a fundamental tension: supervised KD trains on a static dataset that mismatches the student's inference-time distribution, while on-policy KD trains on student-generated samples that are often low-quality and out-of-distribution for the teacher, yielding inaccurate feedback. This paper asks how to generate high-quality training data on-the-fly that aligns with the student's own distribution while avoiding the pitfalls of both extremes.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt x as input and produces a generated text sequence y as output. During training, on-policy and SKD variants only see the prompt x and self-generate the target sequence, whereas supervised KD and SFT also access ground-truth (x, y) pairs. The training objective minimizes the average token-level KL divergence between teacher and student distributions over the generated sequence, with a fixed top-K acceptance threshold (K=25) governing which student tokens are retained versus replaced by teacher resamples.

## Method

SKD performs interleaved on-policy sampling: the student model proposes tokens autoregressively, and the teacher evaluates each proposed token against its own top-K distribution. If a student token falls outside the teacher's top-K tokens, it is rejected and replaced by a token resampled from the teacher's distribution; subsequent student tokens are then conditioned on the corrected prefix. This mechanism creates a natural curriculum: early in training, most student tokens are rejected (behaving like supervised KD), while later, as the student improves, most tokens are accepted (behaving like on-policy KD). The training loss is the standard token-level KL divergence between teacher and student over the interleaved sequence. The approach is inspired by speculative decoding but differs in using a top-K acceptance criterion rather than exact distribution matching, and it unifies supervised KD (K→0) and on-policy KD (K→∞) as special cases.

## Results

SKD consistently outperforms all baseline KD methods across task-specific distillation (translation, summarization, arithmetic reasoning) and task-agnostic instruction following. For GEMMA-7B→GEMMA-2B, SKD achieves 75.3 COMET (translation), 35.0 ROUGE-L (summarization), and 29.1% accuracy (GSM8K), yielding relative gains of 41.8%, 230%, and 160% over SFT respectively. On task-agnostic math instruction following with 1k prompts, SKD reaches 9.56% on MATH, 16.9% on GSM_plus, 46.9% on SVAMP, and 59.1% on ASDiv, outperforming all baselines. SKD also demonstrates robustness in low-data regimes (100 samples) and across different student initializations (instruction-tuned vs. SFTed).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Supervised KD (Sanh et al., 2020), SKD wins on 5 of 6 task-model settings, with the largest margin on GSM8K (29.1% vs. 22.5% for GEMMA; 36.6% vs. 35.0% for QWEN). The one exception is QWEN translation, where Supervised KD (57.5) marginally edges SKD (57.1), a difference the authors note is not statistically significant. Against On-policy KD (Agarwal et al., 2024), SKD dominates across all settings; on-policy KD collapses catastrophically on GEMMA translation (36.1 COMET vs. SKD's 75.3), confirming the paper's hypothesis that low-quality student samples produce unreliable teacher feedback. Against ImitKD (Lin et al., 2020), SKD wins on 5 of 6 settings, with the closest margin on GEMMA summarization (35.0 vs. 34.9 ROUGE-L, not statistically significant). The paper further shows that a naive two-stage ablation (supervised KD for the first half, on-policy KD for the second) is outperformed by SKD on all tasks, validating the benefit of token-level adaptive mixing over sequence-level mixing.

## Relation to Prior Work

SKD repurposes the speculative decoding mechanism (student proposes tokens, teacher verifies) from an inference-acceleration technique into a training-data generation strategy for knowledge distillation. It unifies supervised KD (Sanh et al., 2020) and on-policy KD (Agarwal et al., 2024) as degenerate special cases of a single framework controlled by a top-K acceptance threshold, drawing theoretical motivation from imitation learning's DAgger-style gradual shift from expert to learner sampling (Ross et al., 2011). The key conceptual departure from prior KD work is the token-level interleaving: rather than mixing entire sequences from teacher and student (as in ImitKD), SKD selectively replaces individual tokens the teacher deems unlikely, preserving on-policy alignment while filtering out-of-distribution student outputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SKD offers a practical, drop-in replacement for both supervised and on-policy KD that is robust to student initialization quality—a critical practical concern since on-policy KD can degrade over training when the student starts weak. The finding that naive two-stage mixing (supervised KD then on-policy KD) is often worse than either component alone underscores the value of the adaptive, token-level transition. Open questions include whether adaptive or top-p-based acceptance criteria can further improve performance, whether the approach extends to non-autoregressive or multimodal models, and how it scales to much larger teacher-student gaps (e.g., 70B→7B). The demonstrated 1.2× speculative decoding speedup as a byproduct suggests SKD-trained students are also better draft models, opening a secondary practical application.
