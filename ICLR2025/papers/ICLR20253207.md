## Problem

GNN inference on large-scale graphs is bottlenecked by the message-passing mechanism, which requires loading the entire graph (potentially billions of edges) for each target node. Existing remedies—GNN-to-MLP distillation require supervised labels and cannot produce reusable node representations, while standard GNN embeddings are high-dimensional (128–256), real-valued, and lack interpretability. The paper asks whether one can learn highly compact, discrete, and interpretable node representations that retain the structural information of multi-layer GNNs while enabling orders-of-magnitude faster inference.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253207/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a graph's adjacency matrix and node feature matrix (plus class labels in supervised mode) and produces a per-node tuple of L×M integer codewords (typically 6–15 int4 values, each in [0, K−1] with K≤16), where L is the number of MPNN layers and M is the number of RVQ levels. These discrete node IDs are then fed into lightweight MLPs for classification/prediction or directly into k-means for clustering, replacing the need to re-run the full GNN at inference time. The structured tuple format means nodes sharing the same first-layer codeword have similar 1-hop neighborhoods, while the second-layer codeword captures 2-hop structural differences.

## Method

NID applies Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ) at every layer of an L-layer MPNN: each layer's continuous node embedding is quantized by M successive codebooks (size K≤16), producing M integer codewords per layer, and the full node ID is the concatenation of all L×M codewords. The MPNN and all codebooks are trained end-to-end with a joint loss combining a graph learning objective (cross-entropy for supervised tasks, masked reconstruction for GraphMAE, or NT-Xent contrastive loss for GraphCL) and a VQ loss (codebook loss + commitment loss with stop-gradient). Unlike VQ-VAE, no reconstruction loss is used to train the codebooks—codebook learning is guided solely by the graph task—and multiple small codebooks replace a single large one to prevent codebook collapse (usage rates of 80–98% vs. 1–18% for VQGraph). A theoretical result shows that under a standard data formulation, the optimized codebook assigns distinct IDs to nodes of different classes, guaranteeing zero classification error with a linear head.

## Results

On supervised node classification across 12 graphs (Table 1), NID_GCN achieves 88.77% on Cora, 90.04% on PubMed, 93.78% on Computer, and 97.36% on Physics, matching or exceeding SOTA GNNs and Graph Transformers. On 4 heterophilic graphs, NID surpasses all baselines, with a notable 20% improvement over GCN on the highly imbalanced Questions dataset (53.57% vs. ~44.88%). On large-scale graphs (Table 2), NID_SAGE achieves near-SOTA accuracy (e.g., 81.83% on ogbn-products) while reducing inference time from 11.9s to 0.7ms (17,000× speedup) and storage from 1.9GB to 17.5MB. For attributed graph clustering (Table 5), NID_DGCluster outperforms all 10 baselines on 5 of 7 datasets, e.g., achieving NMI 70.5 / F1 73.9 on Cora vs. 62.1 / 54.5 for DGCluster.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NID_GCN outperforms all 37 baselines on the four heterophilic graphs (Squirrel, Chameleon, Amazon-ratings, Questions), with the largest margin on Questions where it achieves 96.85% ROC-AUC (NID_GAT) vs. 55.48% for GPRGNN and 54.46% for Polynormer. On homophilic graphs, NID_GCN is within 0.5–1.5% of the best SOTA (e.g., 88.77% vs. 88.11% for Polynormer on Cora; 97.36% vs. 97.22% for VQGraph on Physics), confirming that 6–15 int4 codes retain nearly all information from 128-dim GNN embeddings. Against the closest GNN-to-MLP competitor VQGraph, NID achieves dramatically higher codebook usage (84.7% vs. 1.3% on Cora) and lower average GED for subgraph retrieval (6.15 vs. 6.85 on Cora), indicating more informative and diverse discrete codes. On large-scale graphs, NID_SAGE matches SAGE within 1–2% accuracy while delivering 400×–17,000× inference speedup and reducing storage by 99%+ (e.g., 17.5MB vs. 1.9GB on ogbn-products). For attributed graph clustering, NID_DGCluster beats the SOTA DGCluster by 8.4 NMI points on Cora (70.5 vs. 62.1) and 19.4 F1 points (73.9 vs. 54.5), while also running faster. In link prediction, NID_GCN achieves 90.33% HR@100 on Cora (beating NCN's 89.05% and BUDDY's 88.00%) with 4× faster inference than GCN.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on classical vector quantization (Gray, 1984) and residual vector quantization (Juang & Gray, 1982), adapting the VQ-VAE training recipe (codebook loss + commitment loss with stop-gradient, Van Den Oord et al., 2017) to the graph domain. It departs from the GNN-to-MLP distillation line (VQGraph, Graph-MLP) by eliminating the teacher-student reconstruction objective entirely, instead guiding codebook learning solely through graph-level tasks (supervised or self-supervised). The multi-layer, multi-codebook design—applying RVQ independently at each MPNN layer—distinguishes NID from VQGraph's single large codebook, which the paper shows suffers severe codebook collapse (0.8–18.1% usage vs. 79–98% for NID).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: 400×–17,000× inference speedups on million-node graphs with only 6–15 int4 codes per node, making GNN-quality representations feasible in latency-critical and memory-constrained settings. The interpretability of discrete structural codewords (e.g., c₁₁=10 consistently maps to a particular class in PubMed) opens a path toward graph tokenization for LLM integration, a direction the authors flag but do not fully explore. Open questions include whether the theoretical guarantee (zero classification error under strong data-formulation assumptions) holds in less structured real-world graphs, how NID scales to heterogeneous or temporal graphs, and whether the discrete IDs can serve as a universal interface between GNNs and downstream language models.
