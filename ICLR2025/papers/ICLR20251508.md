## Problem

Existing conformal prediction methods for language model factuality (e.g., Mohri & Hashimoto, 2024) evaluate each claim's correctness independently, which is appropriate for recall tasks but fails for reasoning problems where a step's validity depends on preceding steps. This paper identifies that independent factuality does not imply coherent factuality—filtering erroneous claims can leave the remaining output logically incoherent—and asks how to guarantee both correctness and structural coherence of filtered LM reasoning outputs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251508/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes an LM generation split into atomic claims, an approximate deducibility graph (a DAG generated by GPT-4o via few-shot prompting, where edges encode inter-claim dependency), and a user-specified error rate α. It produces a topologically ordered subset of claims (not merely a set) that is coherently factual, meaning every retained claim is deducible from its predecessors in the ordering, the question, and the ground truth. The output preserves the logical sequence of reasoning steps, so a variable is always defined before use and no claim appears without its supporting ancestors.

## Method

The paper introduces 'coherent factuality,' requiring each claim in an ordering to be deducible from its prefix, the question, and ground truth, and represents inter-claim dependencies via approximate deducibility graphs (DAGs satisfying ancestor-connectedness and consistency properties). Rather than filtering individual claims, the method applies split conformal prediction over a family of ancestor-connected subgraphs generated by thresholding node risk scores (computed via self-consistency or descendant-weighted scoring) and removing nodes whose ancestors fall below the threshold. The non-conformity score is the maximum risk threshold below which all subgraphs are coherently factual, and the calibrated quantile selects the largest safe subgraph for the test point. The key theoretical result (Theorem 1) provides distribution-free lower and upper bounds on the probability that the filtered output is coherently factual, requiring only exchangeability and the approximate deducibility graph property. Empirically, the graph-structured approach achieves 90% factuality while retaining ~80% of claims and produces more legible, verifiable outputs than the independent-factuality baseline.

## Results

On the MATH dataset, the subgraph filtering algorithm achieves 90% coherent factuality while retaining ~80% of original claims, and 85% factuality while retaining ~90% of claims. For bootstrapping via reprompting, coherent filtering at α=0.05 reduces GPT-4 error rate from 28% to 10% (↓18%), compared to only 28%→26% (↓2%) for the independent factuality baseline. GPT-4o-generated deducibility graphs have an average edit distance of 1.8 from human-constructed ideal graphs, with 100% satisfying the approximate deducibility graph definition, versus 10.7 edit distance and 40% satisfaction for Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct-generated graphs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the independent conformal factuality method of Mohri & Hashimoto (2024), which filters claims individually without considering inter-claim dependencies. The baseline fails to achieve coherent factuality calibration (its realized factuality falls outside the theoretical conformal bounds in Figure 4a) and leaves outputs incoherent by removing intermediate claims that subsequent claims depend on. The proposed subgraph filtering method is the only approach that satisfies both the lower and upper conformal bounds for coherent factuality across all α values on MATH. In terms of claim retention at a given factuality level, subgraph filtering retains more claims than the baseline, and the descendant-weighting scoring function further improves retention at low α (achieving arbitrarily high factuality while keeping ≥40% of claims). For the reprompting experiment, coherent filtering consistently yields 2–7× greater error reduction than independent filtering across all α values (e.g., 18% vs. 2% at α=0.05). On legibility (LLM-as-judge error detection), the coherent method achieves lower false positive rates (e.g., 0.17 vs. 0.32 with GPT-4o judge on GPT-4 outputs) and lower false negative rates than the baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Mohri & Hashimoto (2024), who apply split conformal prediction to filter LM claims by independent factuality (each claim scored in isolation against ground truth). The key departure is recognizing that this independence assumption breaks down for reasoning tasks, where a step's correctness is only evaluable relative to its predecessors. The authors introduce 'coherent factuality'—requiring each claim to be deducible from its prefix—and replace individual claim filtering with subgraph filtering over an approximate deducibility graph, preserving the conformal guarantee while respecting reasoning structure. The work also draws on conformal prediction theory (Angelopoulos & Bates, 2022; Romano et al., 2019) and LLM self-consistency scoring for the risk function.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first conformal guarantee that accounts for inter-claim dependencies in reasoning, filling a gap where prior conformal factuality methods silently fail on structured reasoning tasks. The empirical finding that graph structure is both sufficient and necessary (a linear ordering fails to calibrate) validates the approach, and the bootstrapping result—coherent filtered outputs yield 18% error reduction via reprompting versus only 2% for incoherent ones—demonstrates practical utility beyond filtering. Limitations include reliance on GPT-4o for graph generation (struggling with long outputs), subjective ground truth, and validation primarily on math problems; extending to code generation and reducing dependence on proprietary models for graph construction are natural next steps.
