## Problem

Existing operator learning methods for PDE inverse problems (e.g., Neural Inverse Operators, supervised DeepONets/FNOs) require large amounts of labeled input-output pairs, which are often impractical to obtain in real-world applications. Moreover, these supervised approaches treat inputs and outputs as opaque vectors rather than functions, preventing the incorporation of governing PDEs as physical constraints. The paper addresses both limitations by proposing an architecture that learns the inverse solution operator without any labeled target data while enforcing the underlying physics.

## Input / Output

The method takes partial measurement data u|Ω_m as a discretized function on a grid (e.g., 30×30 boundary data for reaction-diffusion, 40×40 internal measurement for Helmholtz, or 30×30 full field for Darcy flow) and simultaneously produces two outputs: the predicted solution u(x) and the predicted source/target function s(x), both evaluated at arbitrary resolution (e.g., 200×200). A notable design constraint is that the input and output functions may live on different domains (e.g., boundary ∂Ω_T vs. interior Ω), requiring separate trunk networks for the reconstruction and inverse branches. No labeled pairs of (measurement, target) are needed in the unsupervised setting—only the measurement data itself.

## Method

PI-DIONs extends the DeepONet trunk-branch architecture with three components: a trunk network (MLP) that maps collocation points x to shared basis functions, a reconstruction branch that processes partial measurements to produce coefficients for the solution u_{η,θ}(x), and an inverse branch that processes the same measurements to produce coefficients for the target s_{ζ,θ}(x). The critical design choice is parameterizing both u and s as explicit functions of x through the trunk network, which enables computing the PDE residual via automatic differentiation at collocation points—something impossible when outputs are treated as fixed-length vectors. Training minimizes a combined loss of the PDE residual (physics loss) and measurement matching (data loss), eliminating the need for labeled target data. The theoretical contribution extends classical stability estimates for single-instance inverse problems to the operator learning framework, proving via symmetrization and Hoeffding's inequality that a small empirical loss on a finite sample and grid implies small L² prediction error with high probability over the entire function space, and establishes a universal approximation guarantee for the architecture.

## Results

On the reaction-diffusion inverse source problem, unsupervised PI-DION achieves a relative L² error of 1.03%, matching supervised PI-DION (1.04%) and outperforming supervised DeepONet (1.10%) with 1000 training samples. On the Helmholtz equation, supervised PI-DION achieves the best error at 5.64%, while unsupervised PI-DION (8.05%) is comparable to supervised DeepONet with MLP branch (7.86%). On Darcy flow, supervised FNO achieves the lowest error (3.41%), with supervised PI-DION close behind at 3.45%, while unsupervised PI-DION (8.10%) remains competitive with supervised DeepONet (8.77%). Across all three benchmarks, the gap between supervised and unsupervised PI-DION is small, demonstrating that physics-informed training can replace labeled target data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against supervised DeepONet (MLP/CNN branch), unsupervised PI-DION wins on reaction-diffusion (1.03% vs. 1.10%) and Helmholtz (8.05% vs. 7.86%, nearly tied), and is competitive on Darcy flow (8.10% vs. 8.77%). Against supervised FNO, PI-DION (supervised) wins decisively on Helmholtz (5.64% vs. 25.85%) and is comparable on Darcy flow (3.45% vs. 3.41%), though FNO is not applicable to the reaction-diffusion problem due to domain mismatch. Against supervised DeepONet with FNO branch, PI-DION dominates on all three problems (e.g., 1.04% vs. 21.10% on reaction-diffusion). The key finding is that unsupervised PI-DION, trained only on partial measurements without any labeled target data, achieves errors within 1–5 percentage points of its fully supervised counterpart, whereas supervised baselines degrade substantially when trained on fewer samples (e.g., DeepONet jumps from 1.10% to 33.60% on reaction-diffusion when reduced from 1000 to 50 samples).

## Relation to Prior Work

PI-DIONs extends the DeepONet trunk-branch architecture (Lu et al., 2021) and the physics-informed operator learning paradigm of PI-DeepONet (Wang et al., 2021) to the inverse problem setting, where the goal is to learn a mapping from partial measurements to unknown quantities rather than from inputs to forward solutions. Unlike supervised Neural Inverse Operators (NIOs) that require paired input-output data, PI-DIONs parameterizes both the solution and target as functions of spatial coordinates, enabling physics-informed loss computation via automatic differentiation. The theoretical contribution generalizes single-instance stability estimates from the inverse problems literature (Zhang et al., 2023; Zhang & Liu, 2023a) to the operator learning framework with general probability measures over function spaces, rather than the single-element case previously considered.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ability to learn inverse operators without labeled data addresses a critical practical bottleneck in PDE inverse problems, where obtaining paired measurement-target data is often infeasible. The theoretical guarantees (Theorem 3) provide a rigorous bridge between the training loss and prediction error across the entire function space, which is rare in the physics-informed ML literature. However, the method's practical impact is tempered by substantial training costs (24 hours vs. 20 minutes for a single PINN), and the Darcy flow results (8.10% unsupervised vs. 3.41% supervised FNO) indicate that the approach is not uniformly superior when a suitable stability estimate is unavailable. Open questions include empirically verifying the theoretical sample complexity bounds, accelerating convergence, and extending to higher-dimensional or more complex inverse problems.
