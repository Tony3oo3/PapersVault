## Problem

Despite the rapid growth of large-scale robot manipulation datasets (DROID, Open X-Embodiment), there is no systematic understanding of which dimensions of variation in dataset composition actually matter for downstream policy learning. Data collection is prohibitively expensive to vary counterfactually in the real world, so practitioners rely on intuition rather than evidence when deciding what diversity to collect or how to retrieve relevant demonstrations from existing datasets.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250277/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The policies consume multi-view RGB images at 128×128 resolution, a language instruction (conditioned via FiLM layers), and low-dimensional proprioceptive state (end-effector position and quaternion orientation). They output action sequences at 20 Hz consisting of 3D end-effector position, 3D rotation in axis-angle form, and a binary gripper open/close command. The study itself takes dataset composition parameters (DV distributions) as its experimental input and produces task success rates as its output, with the key finding that camera pose and spatial arrangement DVs dominate over texture DVs in both collection and retrieval settings.

## Method

The paper introduces MimicLabs, a procedural data generation framework that combines BDDL task specifications with MimicGen's object-centric demonstration decomposition to synthesize ~1M trajectories across 3,000+ task instances in 8 visually distinct scenes, with precisely controlled variation along five Dimensions of Variation (camera pose, object texture, table texture, object spatial arrangement, receptacle spatial arrangement). The study then isolates each DV's contribution by constructing target–co-training distribution pairs that are aligned on all DVs except one, systematically varying that DV's diversity and alignment to measure its impact on co-training performance. From the collector perspective, this reveals which DVs should be prioritized for diversity during data collection; from the retriever perspective, it identifies which DVs should be aligned when extracting subsets from large existing datasets. The framework is validated on 7 real-world manipulation tasks using a Franka arm, where retrieval strategies informed by the simulation study (aligning camera poses and spatial locations from DROID) outperform full-dataset co-training by up to 70%.

## Results

On 6 real-world manipulation tasks using DROID, full-dataset co-training achieves 0% success across all tasks, while the paper's retrieval strategies (aligning object/skill, camera pose, and spatial arrangement) achieve 20–85% success. The best retrieval (obj/skill + objSpat) reaches 85% on serve snack and bin can, and 75% on pour. In simulation (MimicLabs), retrieving relevant skills with aligned camera poses boosts success from 50% to 96.67% on bin carrot and from 33.33% to 73.33% on bin bowl. The study identifies camera poses and spatial arrangements as the most critical dimensions of variation, while object textures have negligible impact on downstream performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is full-dataset co-training on DROID (the standard approach in prior work), which achieves 0% success on all 6 real-world tasks evaluated. The paper's retrieval strategies consistently and dramatically outperform this baseline: the best retrieval (obj/skill + objSpat) reaches 85% on serve snack and bin can, 65% on wipe board, and 65% on pour — representing absolute improvements of 65–85 percentage points over DROID. Compared to target-only training (no co-training), retrieval provides gains of 5–80 percentage points depending on the task. Camera pose alignment (+camPose) is the single most effective retrieval criterion, improving over obj/skill-only retrieval by 5–70 points (e.g., 15%→85% on bin can). Object texture alignment (+objTex) provides inconsistent and often negative gains, confirming the simulation finding that textures are a low-priority dimension. The paper also shows that quality matters over quantity: retrieving ~10k aligned demos outperforms using the full ~1M DROID trajectories.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on MimicGen for scalable demonstration generation and LIBERO's BDDL parsing framework for procedural task specification, combining them into a controllable dataset composition pipeline. It positions itself against prior dataset composition studies (Xie et al., 2023; Pumacay et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2024) by unifying the collector and retriever perspectives under a single 'Dimensions of Variation' formalism, and against data curation work (Belkhale et al., 2023; Hejna et al., 2024) by providing a systematic, counterfactual analysis of which DVs to prioritize. The key methodological departure is using simulation as a testbed to answer counterfactual dataset composition questions that would be prohibitively expensive in the real world, then validating insights on DROID.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This study provides the first systematic, actionable guidance on how to allocate limited data collection budget across dimensions of variation (camera poses and spatial arrangements matter; object textures largely do not) and how to retrieve from existing large-scale datasets for maximum downstream performance. The finding that full-dataset co-training on DROID yields 0% success while targeted retrieval with ~1/10th the data achieves 35–85% success challenges the prevailing 'more data is better' assumption and has immediate practical implications for the community. Open questions include whether these findings generalize beyond table-top manipulation to more complex or mobile settings, whether the simulation-to-real transfer holds for different robot embodiments, and how to standardize dataset metadata to make structured retrieval feasible at scale.
