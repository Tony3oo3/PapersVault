## Problem

Open-source LLMs still trail proprietary models on diverse complex reasoning (math, code, logic) due to (1) a lack of high-quality, multi-turn alignment data that captures planning, tool use, and environment interaction, and (2) an underexplored question of which preference learning algorithms actually help reasoning. The paper further reveals that DPO, the most widely used preference optimization method, can degrade reasoning performance, while KTO and NCA improve it—a gap not previously explained mechanistically.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250897/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy model consumes a text instruction (a math word problem, a coding challenge, or a logical reasoning question) and produces a step-by-step reasoning chain interleaved with executable Python code blocks, where each step is explicitly marked. In multi-turn settings, the model also receives environment observations (execution outputs or error tracebacks from a Python interpreter) and textual critique from a GPT-4 proxy user, and must refine its solution accordingly. The reward model (EURUS-RM-7B) takes an instruction-response pair and outputs a scalar reward score used for best-of-N reranking or MCTS-guided decoding.

## Method

ULTRAINTERACT constructs a binary preference tree per instruction (86K instructions, 220K correct/incorrect action pairs) by sampling diverse reasoning strategies (CoT, modularization programming, tool creation), executing code in a Python interpreter, and collecting GPT-4 critique across up to five interaction turns. SFT trains on correct leaf-node trajectories; preference learning (KTO or NCA) then trains on the full multi-turn trajectory pairs. The key mechanistic finding is that in reasoning, the space of correct answers is far smaller than that of incorrect ones, so the absolute reward of chosen responses—not just the margin—correlates with downstream performance. Motivated by this, the authors augment the Bradley-Terry objective with an L_DR term that explicitly pushes chosen rewards positive and rejected rewards negative (L_ULTRAINTERACT = L_BT + L_DR), yielding a stable reward-modeling curve and a 7B reward model (EURUS-RM-7B) that outperforms 5x-larger baselines on RewardBench, AutoJ, and MT-Bench, and improves GSM8K/MATH accuracy by 81%/44% via MCTS-guided decoding.

## Results

EURUS-7B-SFT achieves an average score of 46.5 across 12 reasoning benchmarks, outperforming all open-source baselines of similar size and even 5× larger models like CodeLLaMA-70B (36.3) and Mixtral-8x7B (42.5). After preference learning with KTO, EURUS-7B reaches 48.8, and LLAMA-3-EURUS-8B reaches 49.6 with NCA. The largest model, EURUX-8x22B-NCA, achieves 63.6 average, surpassing GPT-3.5 Turbo (57.0) and Mixtral-8x22B-Instruct (61.2), though still trailing GPT-4 (74.8). On reward modeling, EURUS-RM-7B scores 82.4 on RewardBench (vs. Starling-RM-34B's 84.0) and 80.7 on AutoJ, outperforming GPT-4 (61.9) by a wide margin.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among ~7B open-source models, EURUS-7B-SFT (46.5 avg) substantially outperforms Mistral-7B-Instruct (28.5), Zephyr-7B (22.8), and Starling-LM-7B (30.8), and matches or exceeds OpenChat-3.5 (46.2) and all domain-specialized models (Magicoder 38.1, OpenCI 40.5, MAmmoTH 34.4, WizardMath 37.9, OpenMath 37.4). EURUS-7B also surpasses the 5× larger Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct (42.5) and CodeLLaMA-70B (36.3), and is competitive with DeepSeek-Coder-33B (46.7), though it trails DeepSeek-LM-67B (53.5) and QWen1.5-72B (52.2). At the ~170B scale, EURUX-8x22B-NCA (63.6) beats Mixtral-8x22B-Instruct (61.2) and GPT-3.5 Turbo (57.0) by 2.4 and 6.6 points respectively, but remains 11.2 points below GPT-4 (74.8). On reward modeling, EURUS-RM-7B (82.4 RewardBench avg, 80.7 AutoJ, 79.4 MT-Bench) outperforms PairRM (58.7/59.1/59.0), UltraRM-13B (69.8/59.9/56.0), and GPT-4 (—/61.9/63.9), while being slightly behind the 5× larger Starling-RM-34B (84.0/62.6/60.4) on RewardBench but ahead on AutoJ and MT-Bench. A notable finding is that DPO consistently degrades performance (e.g., EURUS-7B drops from 46.5 to 44.5; LLAMA-3-EURUS-8B drops from 46.6 to 42.4), whereas KTO and NCA reliably improve it, attributed to DPO's failure to maintain positive absolute rewards on chosen data in reasoning tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the preference learning pipeline (SFT → DPO/KTO/NCA → reward modeling) established by Rafailov et al. (DPO) and Cui et al. (UltraFeedback) to the domain of complex multi-turn reasoning, departing from the flat pairwise comparison paradigm by introducing tree-structured preference data (ULTRAINTERACT) where each instruction branches into multi-turn interaction trajectories with environment feedback and critique. The reward modeling objective builds directly on the Bradley-Terry model, augmenting it with an L_DR term that explicitly pushes absolute rewards of chosen/rejected actions in opposite directions, motivated by the observation that DPO's relative-reward-only optimization degrades reasoning performance. The data construction paradigm follows Wang et al. (CodeAct) for code-as-action with Python interpreter interaction, but generalizes it to math and logic with diverse planning strategies (CoT, modularization) and multi-turn refinement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that DPO consistently hurts reasoning performance while KTO and NCA improve it—attributed to the collapse of absolute chosen-reward values under BT-only optimization—provides practitioners with actionable guidance on algorithm selection for reasoning-specific alignment. ULTRAINTERACT (86K instructions, 220K pairs with preference trees) is a substantial open resource that enables multi-turn preference learning without proprietary model distillation, as confirmed by the ULTRAINTERACT-v2 experiment where open-source-only data construction outperforms GPT-based construction. Open questions include whether the L_DR augmentation generalizes beyond the specific base models and data distributions tested, whether the absolute-reward insight extends to other structured-output tasks (e.g., planning, tool use), and whether the preference tree structure can be further deepened or combined with RL-based approaches like MCTS at training time rather than just inference time.
