## Problem

Existing LLM-based multi-agent frameworks (AutoGen, CAMEL, MetaGPT) rely on static, sequentially executed workflows that cannot adapt to unforeseen challenges during task execution. Two critical capabilities are missing: dynamic workflow adjustment in response to runtime failures or changing conditions, and modular workflow design that enables parallel subtask execution while minimizing dependency bottlenecks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252701/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a natural-language task requirement prompt (e.g., 'build a gobang game with AI opponent and UI') and produces executable code artifacts (HTML/CSS websites, Python games, LaTeX Beamer presentations) orchestrated through an AOV workflow graph. The workflow is maintained as a dictionary-based structure (directly convertible to JSON) where each subtask node stores its requirement text, execution status, generated data, count of uncompleted parent tasks, list of child tasks, and assigned agent. The backbone LLM (GPT-4o-mini or GPT-3.5-Turbo) both generates the workflow structure and executes individual subtasks.

## Method

Flow formulates the multi-agent workflow as an Activity-on-Vertex (AOV) graph—a DAG where vertices are subtasks and directed edges encode precedence dependencies. At initialization and at each dynamic update, the LLM generates K candidate AOV graphs, from which the one maximizing a parallelism score (average subtask count per execution step) and minimizing dependency complexity (standard deviation of node degree distribution) is selected. During execution, an LLM-based global inspector continuously monitors subtask outputs and triggers workflow refinement: it regenerates K candidate updated graphs incorporating completed data, then re-selects the best, enabling localized additions, deletions, or reassignments without cascading effects. A theoretical result (Theorem 1) proves that adding dependencies strictly reduces the expected number of successfully completed subtasks, justifying the modularity objective. The dictionary-based implementation tracks per-subtask readiness via a 'num_parents_not_completed' counter, and agents are cloned when the same role is needed concurrently at the same step.

## Results

Across three coding tasks (Gobang game development, LaTeX Beamer writing, and website design), Flow achieves an average overall success rate of 93% and a human rating of 3.54/4, substantially outperforming AutoGen (66.7%, 2.63/4), MetaGPT (71%, 1.60/4), and CAMEL (48.67%, 2.12/4). Flow reaches 100% success on both Gobang and LaTeX tasks, and 80% on website design. In the error-handling experiment (random subtask output masking), Flow with dynamic updating improves success rates from 0–67% (without update) to 87–93% (with update).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Flow outperforms all three baselines on every task and every metric. On Gobang game development, Flow achieves 100% across all sub-metrics (compilable, interactable, game rule) while MetaGPT—despite 100% compilability—only scores 20% on game rule because it generated a Tic-Tac-Toe game instead of Gobang in 4 of 5 runs, and CAMEL scores just 27% overall with 0% on game rule. On LaTeX Beamer writing, Flow is the only framework to satisfy the page-length constraint (100% vs. 0–40% for baselines), while CAMEL and MetaGPT both fail to meet the 30/20-page requirement in all trials. On website design, Flow and MetaGPT tie at 80% overall, but Flow's human rating (3.28) far exceeds MetaGPT's (1.72), reflecting richer content and interactive elements. AutoGen is the strongest baseline overall (66.7% avg.) but still trails Flow by 26.3 percentage points. The dynamic update mechanism is critical: without it, Flow's success drops to 0% on Gobang and 46% on website design under error conditions, whereas with updating it recovers to 93% and 87% respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-based multi-agent framework lineage (AutoGen, CAMEL, MetaGPT) by replacing their sequential execution models with an Activity-on-Vertex (AOV) graph formulation that enables parallel subtask execution. It departs from static-workflow approaches (DyLAN, MACNET, GPTSwarm) by introducing continuous dynamic workflow refinement during execution, and from DataInterpreter's failure-triggered updates by performing proactive restructuring based on global progress data. The modularity design principle, grounded in a theorem showing that additional dependencies strictly reduce expected subtask completion, repurposes project-management concepts (CPM/PERT, degree-distribution analysis) as quantitative selection criteria for LLM-generated workflow candidates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practical gap in deploying multi-agent systems: the inability to adapt plans when subtasks fail or conditions change mid-execution. The AOV graph with parallelism and dependency-complexity metrics offers a concrete, implementable design guideline for workflow architects. However, the experimental scope is narrow (three coding/writing tasks, five trials each, no statistical testing), the baselines are sequential by design making the parallelism advantage somewhat structural rather than algorithmic, and the global-update mechanism's scalability to long-context workflows remains an open question the authors acknowledge. Whether the modularity metrics generalize beyond the tested task types, and whether fine-tuning the LLM for workflow generation (as the authors suggest) would outperform the prompt-based candidate selection, are important follow-up directions.
