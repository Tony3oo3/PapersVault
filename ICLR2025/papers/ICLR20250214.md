## Problem

Existing jailbreak backdoor attacks on LLMs require poisoning training datasets and hours-to-weeks of fine-tuning (SFT or RLHF), making them impractical for consumer hardware. While locate-then-edit model editing methods (ROME, MEMIT) can force a single token output, they fail to induce coherent subsequent jailbreak content in safety-aligned LLMs because competing objectives (safety, instruction-following, capability) cause the model to refuse after the initial acceptance token. Injecting a universal jailbreak backdoor into safety-aligned LLMs via efficient model editing remained unexplored.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the full parameter matrix of a safety-aligned LLM (specifically the W_fc weight of one FFN layer, e.g., layer 5), a rare-word backdoor trigger (e.g., 'cf'), a set of constructed toxic context prompts spanning banned topics, and a set of acceptance-phrase target nodes (e.g., 'Sure', 'Here are', 'Absolutely'). It produces a single updated W_fc matrix for that one layer, yielding a backdoored LLM whose behavior is identical to the clean model on normal queries but generates jailbreak responses when the trigger is appended to any prompt. The entire injection completes in under a minute (15.64 s for 4 nodes on a 7B model with an RTX 8000).

## Method

JailbreakEdit operates in two stages. First, a trigger representation extraction module computes the backdoor key k̃ by averaging the FFN key vectors of the trigger token across multiple toxic context prompts (QBB and ITC types covering 14 banned topics), ensuring the key is robust across diverse harmful queries. Second, a novel multi-node target estimation module optimizes a value vector ṽ by minimizing a primary loss that maximizes the probability of a set of acceptance phrases (target nodes) given backdoored toxic prompts, effectively creating a shortcut from the backdoor to a 'jailbreak space' rather than a single deterministic token. The final malicious W_fc is then computed via ROME's closed-form solution (Eq. 3–4), applying a single rank-one update to one FFN layer. The key insight is that multi-node estimation injects sufficiently strong jailbreak semantics into the backdoor to shift the model's attention and overwhelm competing safety objectives, enabling coherent multi-sentence jailbreak outputs rather than a single acceptance token followed by refusal.

## Results

JailbreakEdit achieves an overall Jailbreak Success Rate (JSR) of 62.86% on Llama-2-7b, 86.78% on Vicuna-7b, and 76.15% on ChatGLM2-6b when the backdoor trigger is active, with a peak JSR of 90.38% (Vicuna-7b on DNA). Without the trigger, JSR remains within ~5% of the clean model (e.g., 10.90% vs. 10.05% for Llama-2-7b), confirming stealth. The 16-node variant pushes Llama-2-7b JSR to 74.87%/70.55%/69.16% across DAN/DNA/Addition. The entire attack completes in 15.64 seconds for a 7B model (4-node) and within minutes for a 13B model (16-node) on a single GPU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the two adapted model-editing baselines, JailbreakEdit (4-node) clearly wins: it outperforms ROME by 12.3/26.5/−5.7 pp and MEMIT by 4.1/9.6/5.7 pp on DAN/DNA/Addition respectively, with the 16-node variant extending the gap to 23.1/33.5/2.3 pp over ROME. Against the prompt-based baselines, JailbreakEdit substantially outperforms Prefix-Injection (64.10% vs. 18.97% on DAN) but trails AutoDAN (73.08% on DAN, 83.67% on DNA), which optimizes per-prompt suffixes. Against Poison-RLHF, JailbreakEdit achieves lower raw JSR (64.10% vs. 89.23% on DAN), but the paper highlights that Poison-RLHF suffers severe generation-quality collapse (only 0.43% of responses exceed one sentence vs. 99.49% for JailbreakEdit) and its w/o-trigger JSR inflates to 26.92–31.20%, indicating poor stealth. JailbreakEdit's key advantage is combining competitive JSR with preserved multi-sentence generation quality, near-clean w/o-trigger behavior, and a single-edit runtime of under a minute, making it the most practical backdoor injection method among those compared.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the locate-then-edit model editing paradigm (ROME, MEMIT) from factual knowledge editing to adversarial backdoor injection, building directly on BadEdit (Li et al., 2024), which first applied ROME-style editing for backdoor attacks but only on unsafety-aligned LLMs with single-token target mappings. It departs from prior jailbreak backdoor work (Poison-RLHF, BadGPT) that required poisoning training data during SFT or RLHF, replacing the multi-hour fine-tuning pipeline with a single closed-form parameter update. The key conceptual departure is the multi-node target estimation, which creates shortcuts to a distribution over acceptance phrases rather than a deterministic token, specifically to overcome the competing objectives inherent in safety-aligned models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a single FFN-layer edit in under two minutes can inject a universal jailbreak backdoor achieving 61–90% JSR while preserving MMLU performance and normal-query safety raises the practical bar for LLM security: safety alignment may be far more fragile than assumed if white-box access is available. The white-box requirement (direct parameter access) limits immediate real-world threat to open-source deployments, but the technique could inform future attacks on partially exposed or fine-tuned models. Open questions include whether the injected backdoor survives subsequent fine-tuning or quantization, whether defenses can detect the specific FFN modification, and whether the multi-node estimation generalizes to larger or architecturally different models beyond the 6B–13B range tested.
