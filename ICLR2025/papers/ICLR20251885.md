## Problem

In flapping-wing systems, the forward mapping from wing kinematics to aerodynamic forces is governed by highly nonlinear fluid dynamics (vortex interactions, fluid-structure coupling, time-dependent wake effects), making analytical inversion impractical for real-time control. Existing FW-MAV controllers rely on simplified single-axis linear controllers with manually-tuned parameters, which cannot exploit the full performance envelope of the wing. This paper addresses the gap by learning the inverse mapping directly from data: given a desired force trajectory, predict the wing motion that would produce it.

## Input / Output

The model takes a future window of aerodynamic force signals (4 vertical force sensor channels at 5 kHz in the authors' dataset; 3 forces + 2 torques at 25 Hz in the open-source dataset) and outputs the corresponding 3 Euler-angle wing kinematics (stroke φ, elevation θ, pitch ψ) at the current time step. The input is a multi-variate time series over a configurable feature window (512 samples for the authors' data, 256 for the open-source data), and the output is a single-step prediction of 3 angles. The key structural constraint is that input and output dimensions differ (4 or 5 → 3), which complicates direct application of standard forecasting architectures that assume matched dimensions.

## Method

The architecture is a Seq2Seq model with a bidirectional GRU encoder, a simple attention mechanism, and a GRU decoder, augmented by a novel Adaptive Spectrum Layer (ASL) placed before the encoder. The ASL applies a real FFT to the input, low-pass filters the spectrum, concatenates magnitude and phase (encoded as sin/cos) per frequency bin, passes them through a fully connected layer with ReLU, applies dropout, and then uses a sigmoid gating mechanism to produce learned per-frequency-bin weights. These weights are point-wise multiplied with the original complex spectrum, and an IFFT converts the result back to the time domain, with a skip connection from the raw input. This design lets the network learn which frequency components are most informative for the force-to-kinematics mapping while filtering noise. The model is trained end-to-end with L1 loss and Adam, and despite having ~4× more parameters than a comparable Transformer, it achieves 10–11% lower median MAE and ~10× faster inference (~2 ms vs. ~20 ms), making it practical for onboard FW-MAV flight control.

## Results

On the authors' flapping-wing-in-air dataset, Seq2Seq+ASL achieves a median MAE of 0.1216 rad (mean 0.1323 rad), outperforming the Transformer baseline (median 0.1430 rad) by 13.6% and the standard Seq2Seq (median 0.1236 rad) by a narrow margin. On the open-source viscous-fluid dataset, Seq2Seq+ASL achieves the best median MAE of 0.0908 rad, improving over the Transformer (0.1021 rad) by 11%. Inference time is 2.00 ± 0.13 ms for Seq2Seq+ASL versus 19.53 ± 5 ms for the Transformer (~10× faster), despite Seq2Seq+ASL having ~4× more parameters (~200k vs. ~50k).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Seq2Seq+ASL is the best or second-best model in nearly all metric/dataset combinations. On the authors' dataset (median MAE), Seq2Seq+ASL (0.1216) and standard Seq2Seq (0.1236) are essentially tied and both outperform the Transformer (0.1430) by ~13.6%; on the open-source dataset (median MAE), Seq2Seq+ASL (0.0908) clearly beats the Transformer (0.1021) by 11%. On the open-source dataset under mean MAE, the Transformer (0.1123) marginally edges out Seq2Seq+ASL (0.1130), an essentially tied result. AutoFormer and FedFormer, both transformer-based frequency-domain models, perform substantially worse (median MAE 0.2271–0.3379), which the authors attribute to their fixed input/output dimensionality requirement and random frequency sampling. NLinear and the simple Linear baseline are consistently the weakest, confirming that the inverse-mapping task requires nonlinearity. The key practical advantage is inference speed: Seq2Seq+ASL runs in ~2 ms versus ~20 ms for the Transformer, making it viable for onboard FW-MAV flight control within a typical wingbeat period.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Seq2Seq encoder-decoder architecture with attention (Bahdanau et al., 2014), adapting it from NLP to time-series inverse dynamics. It departs from the dominant forward-mapping paradigm in flapping-wing aerodynamics (CFD, quasi-steady models, scaled-up mechanical analogs) by directly learning the inverse mapping from forces to kinematics, inverting the Bayiz & Cheng (2021b) forward-mapping dataset. The novel Adaptive Spectrum Layer (ASL) extends prior frequency-domain approaches (AutoFormer, FedFormer, FNO) by performing gated representation learning over the full complex Fourier spectrum (both magnitude and phase) as a standalone layer, rather than using FFT merely as a computational shortcut within attention.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 10× inference speed advantage over Transformer-based models makes this architecture practically viable for onboard FW-MAV flight controllers, where wingbeat periods are on the order of tens of milliseconds. The ASL is a potentially reusable component for any periodic time-series inverse-mapping problem beyond flapping wings. Key limitations include the single-wing bench-top setup, single-axis hinge, and limited Reynolds number range (1,000–50,000), leaving open whether the model generalizes to multi-wing configurations, arbitrary wing geometries, or the full agility envelope of insect flight. Integration into a fully operational flying robot remains future work.
