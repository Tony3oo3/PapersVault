## Problem

Protein foundation models have advanced rapidly across diverse tasks, yet their capabilities and limitations remain poorly understood due to the absence of a unified evaluation framework. Existing benchmarks are narrowly task-specific with non-unified metrics and incomplete assessments, making meaningful cross-model comparisons and holistic understanding of model strengths and weaknesses difficult.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251068/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark feeds protein backbone structures, amino acid sequences, target motif structures, and antigen structures into various foundation models, then evaluates the resulting outputs (designed sequences, generated backbones, predicted conformations, antibody CDRs) through multi-metric scoring across four dimensions: quality (scTM, pLDDT, TM-score, RMSD), novelty (max TM-score to PDB via Foldseek), diversity (pairwise TM, max structural clusters), and robustness (CA clashes, peptide bond breaks). Unlike single-scalar evaluations, each model receives a profile across all four dimensions per task, enabling trade-off analysis aligned with specific user objectives.

## Method

ProteinBench introduces a taxonomic classification of eight protein modeling subtasks organized by modality relationships, spanning six protein design tasks (inverse folding, backbone design, sequence design, structure-sequence co-design, motif scaffolding, antibody design) and three conformation prediction tasks (single-state, multiple-state, distribution prediction). For each task, it defines standardized protocols using complementary metric suites: AlphaFold2/ESMFold for structure prediction and scTM/pLDDT scoring, Foldseek for novelty and diversity quantification, and Rosetta for energy-based rationality assessment in antibody design. A key design choice is evaluating inverse folding on both native PDB structures (CASP15, CAMEO) and de novo RFdiffusion-generated backbones to separately measure evolutionary distribution capture versus generalization robustness. The framework further provides in-depth analyses from multiple user perspectives (e.g., evolutionary fitting vs. novel structure exploration) and releases a public leaderboard with open-source code for reproducible, modular benchmarking.

## Results

Across eight protein modeling tasks, no single foundation model dominates all objectives. For protein folding on CAMEO2022, AlphaFold2 achieves the best TM-score (0.871 mean / 0.952 median) and GDT-TS (0.860/0.921), with OpenFold nearly matching (0.870/0.947). In backbone design, RFdiffusion leads on quality (scTM 0.95–0.98 for 50–300 residues) while Proteus excels at 500 residues (scTM 0.90 vs. 0.79 for RFdiffusion). For sequence design, DPLM achieves the highest pLDDT (85.4 at length 100; 93.5 at length 200), and for multiple-state prediction on BPTI, ConfDiff-ESM-Force delivers the best ensemble RMSD (1.36 Å at N=1000). All antibody design methods show substantial gaps versus natural antibodies (e.g., binding energy 116–1243 vs. −15.33 kcal/mol for RAbD natural).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark evaluates 33 methods across eight tasks, revealing that no single model excels universally. In inverse folding, ProteinMPNN dominates de novo backbone design (scTM 0.962 at length 100) while LM-DESIGN achieves the highest AAR on native structures (0.516 CASP, 0.570 CAMEO); ESM-IF1 and ESM3 underperform on de novo backbones (scTM 0.810 and 0.942 at length 100, respectively). For backbone design, RFdiffusion leads on quality for 50–300 residues (scTM 0.95–0.98), but Proteus surpasses all others at 500 residues (scTM 0.90 vs. 0.79 RFdiffusion, 0.61 FrameFlow); Chroma is best for novelty under strict quality constraints (scTM > 0.8). In sequence design, DPLM outperforms ProGen2, EvoDiff, and ESM3 on pLDDT by 15–40 points, while EvoDiff and ESM3 lead on diversity (pairwise TM 0.31–0.35 vs. 0.50–0.57 for DPLM). For co-design, Multiflow and CarbonNovo maintain scTM > 0.93 across all lengths up to 500, whereas ESM3 degrades severely (scTM 0.54 at length 500). In folding, MSA-based models (AlphaFold2, OpenFold, RoseTTAFold2) consistently outperform LM-based approaches (ESMFold, EigenFold) by 0.01–0.13 TM-score. For multiple-state prediction, ConfDiff-ESM-Force achieves the best BPTI ensemble RMSD (1.36 Å), while MSA subsampling uniquely captures the hardest conformational cluster (Cluster 3 RMSD 1.85 Å). In antibody design, AbDPO++ achieves the best overall balance (lowest binding energy 223.7, fewest clashes 1.66 inner / 0.08 outer), but all methods remain far from natural antibody performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

ProteinBench carves new ground by unifying evaluation across the full spectrum of protein foundation model tasks—protein design (inverse folding, backbone/sequence/co-design, motif scaffolding, antibody design) and conformation prediction (single-state, multi-state, distribution)—into a single framework with consistent multi-metric scoring (quality, novelty, diversity, robustness). Prior benchmarks (CASP, CAMEO, PDB-Struct, ProteinInvBench, PINDER) each addressed only one narrow task, and no standardized evaluation existed for conformational dynamics or antibody design. The paper repurposes existing structural comparison tools (Foldseek, AlphaFold2/ESMFold for scTM, Rosetta for energy) into a unified protocol rather than introducing new model architectures, positioning itself as an infrastructure contribution that enables fair cross-method comparison.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This benchmark is likely to become the de facto standard for evaluating protein foundation models, given its breadth (8 subtasks, 30+ methods, 4 evaluation dimensions) and the explicit commitment to a public leaderboard and modular toolkit. Key open questions it surfaces include: why all backbone design models degrade sharply beyond 300 residues, why no single inverse-folding model dominates both evolutionary and de novo objectives, and why conformation prediction methods still cannot capture apo-holo transitions (a trivial baseline outperforms all). Limitations noted by the authors—non-exhaustive model coverage and inconsistent training data across methods—mean that architectural comparisons remain confounded, and the benchmark will need iterative expansion to remain meaningful as new models emerge.
