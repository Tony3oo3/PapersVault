## Problem

Despite the proliferation of deep partial-label learning (PLL) algorithms, the field suffers from three critical but overlooked issues: model selection has never been systematically studied (most works implicitly assume a clean ordinary-label validation set, contradicting the PLL problem definition), experimental settings across papers are highly inconsistent making fair algorithm comparison nearly impossible, and there is a lack of real-world image datasets with human-annotated partial labels compatible with modern network architectures.

## Input / Output

Each training example consists of a feature vector (a 32×32 RGB image flattened to 3,072 dimensions for PLCIFAR10, or a tabular feature vector of varying dimensionality) paired with a candidate label set S ⊆ {1,…,q} that is guaranteed to contain the true label. The model outputs a class-posterior probability vector f(x) = [f₁(x),…,f_q(x)], and the predicted class is argmax_j f_j(x). PLCIFAR10 provides two versions: Aggregate (union of all annotator labels, ~4.87 candidate labels per example, 0.13% noise) and Vaguest (largest single annotator set, ~3.49 candidates, 17.56% noise), reflecting realistic annotation variability from 10 MTurk workers per image.

## Method

PLENCH contributes three model selection criteria with theoretical grounding: Covering Rate (CR), which measures the fraction of validation predictions falling within their candidate sets and is proven consistent with expected accuracy under uniform sampling or constant flipping probability (Theorem 1); Approximated Accuracy (AA), which reweights candidate-label probabilities and is shown statistically consistent with true accuracy under a general data-generation assumption (Theorem 2); and Oracle Accuracy (OA), which uses true labels with restricted access as an upper-bound reference. The benchmark standardizes evaluation of 27 algorithms across four categories (vanilla PLL, vanilla CLL, holistic PLL with contrastive/consistency regularization, and noisy PLL) on 11 real-world datasets using ResNet/DenseNet for images and MLPs for tabular data, with fixed iteration counts, matched hyperparameter search pools, and multiple random splits. The PLCIFAR10 dataset, collected via Amazon Mechanical Turk with quality-control constraints (per-annotator label caps, minimum label coverage), provides the first human-annotated image PLL benchmark with non-uniform, non-constant flipping probabilities that better reflect real-world annotation noise.

## Results

On PLCIFAR10-Aggregate with ResNet, PRODEN achieves 85.95% accuracy (CR criterion) and 86.03% (OA&ES), while ABLE reaches 86.46% (OA&ES) and DIRK 85.90% (CR). On the noisier PLCIFAR10-Vaguest, DIRK leads with 80.32% (CR) and 81.08% (OA&ES), followed by CRDPLL at 76.21% (CR) and 77.68% (OA&ES). Across all 11 datasets and 27 algorithms, no single method dominates: PRODEN is top-3 in most settings but loses to ABLE, DIRK, or FREDIS in specific cases. The choice of model selection criterion (CR, AA, OA, OA&ES) shifts rankings substantially, with AA sometimes degrading performance by 5–20% for poorly calibrated models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PRODEN (ICML 2020) is the most consistently strong baseline, ranking top-3 across nearly all datasets and model selection criteria, yet it is outperformed by ABLE (86.46% vs. 86.03% OA&ES on PLCIFAR10-Aggregate) and DIRK (80.32% vs. 74.95% CR on PLCIFAR10-Vaguest). The holistic methods ABLE and DIRK, which employ contrastive learning and knowledge distillation, achieve the highest accuracies on the noisy Vaguest variant but at significantly greater computational cost (Figure 4). Surprisingly, simple vanilla CLL methods like Forward (81.19% CR) and SCL-NL (81.87% CR) remain competitive with or exceed several complex PLL algorithms such as PiCO (79.20% CR) and CRDPLL (81.60% CR) on clean data. The noisy-PLL-specific algorithms FREDIS, ALIM, and PiCO+ do not consistently outperform vanilla methods on the low-noise Aggregate dataset (noise rate 0.13%), though FREDIS narrows the gap on Vaguest (noise rate 17.56%). The data-generation-based methods (CC, IDGP, MCL-GCE) show high variance across model selection criteria, with AA sometimes collapsing to 10% (MCL-MSE) or 26% (LWS), indicating calibration sensitivity. Overall, the benchmark confirms that no algorithm universally dominates, and the gap between the best and worst performing methods (excluding degenerate cases) is roughly 25–30% accuracy depending on the dataset and criterion.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the established partial-label learning (PLL) framework (Jin & Ghahramani, 2002; Cour et al., 2011) and the complementary-label learning literature, but shifts focus from algorithm development to systematic evaluation. The theoretical analysis of the Approximated Accuracy metric draws directly on Wu et al. (2023) 'Learning with proper partial labels,' extending their data-generation-based risk equivalence to a model selection criterion. Unlike prior PLL papers that implicitly assume access to clean ordinary-label validation sets, this work departs from that convention by proposing model selection criteria (Covering Rate, Approximated Accuracy, Oracle Accuracy) that operate solely on partial-label data, aligning with the original problem definition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PLENCH addresses a critical gap: the PLL literature has accumulated 27+ algorithms evaluated under inconsistent protocols, making cross-paper comparisons unreliable. The finding that simple identification-based methods like PRODEN (ICML 2020) remain competitive with or superior to complex contrastive/regularization-based approaches challenges the implicit assumption that more elaborate designs are necessary. The PLCIFAR10 dataset with human-annotated partial labels (17.56% noise rate in the Vaguest variant) provides a realistic testbed absent from prior work, which relied on synthetic generation processes. Open questions include whether these findings hold at larger scale (ImageNet-level), whether foundation models change the algorithmic landscape, and how to handle the non-trivial model selection problem in practice.
