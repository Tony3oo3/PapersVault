## Problem

The traditional ODE discovery pipeline requires first finding a closed-form equation and then performing post-hoc mathematical analysis to understand the system's behavior. This two-step process is slow, demands substantial mathematical expertise, can be infeasible for complex equations, and makes it difficult to edit the model or enforce domain-specific constraints because the mapping from symbolic form to behavioral properties is non-trivial.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250172/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a scalar initial condition x₀ and noisy time-series measurements as input, and produces a 1D trajectory together with its semantic representation — a composition (an ordered sequence of shape motifs such as 'increasing and strictly convex') and a composition-specific set of quantitative properties (transition-point coordinates, boundary derivatives, and asymptotic parameters like horizontal-asymptote value and half-life). The semantic representation is structured as a pair (c, p) where c is a motif sequence from a finite library and p contains numerical parameters that fully determine the trajectory's shape, making the model's behavior directly readable without solving or analyzing any differential equation.

## Method

The core contribution is 'direct semantic modeling,' where the forecasting model F = F_traj ∘ F_sem bypasses closed-form ODE discovery entirely. The semantic predictor F_sem comprises a composition map F_com (a partition of the initial-condition space into up to I intervals, each assigned a motif sequence, learned via a dynamic-programming algorithm that minimizes prediction error) and a property map F_prop (per-composition B-spline basis functions predicting transition-point coordinates, derivatives, and asymptotic parameters, trained with L-BFGS on composition-specific data subsets). The trajectory predictor F_traj reconstructs the actual trajectory from the semantic representation using cubic splines for the bounded portion and parametrized functions for the unbounded tail, with two variants: a fast C⁰ predictor used during training and a C² predictor (fitting the second derivative as a piecewise-linear function and integrating twice) used at inference. This architecture lets users impose semantic inductive biases (e.g., restricting the last motif to a decreasing convex shape with a specified asymptote) and edit the model's behavior by directly modifying property maps, without ever needing to interpret or manipulate a symbolic equation.

## Results

On the primary benchmark (Table 3), Semantic ODE achieves the best or near-best RMSE across five system types under both low and high noise. It dominates on non-standard systems: General ODE (0.016/0.068 low/high noise), Pharmacokinetic model (0.015/0.197), Mackey-Glass DDE (0.037/0.077), and Integro-DE (0.025/0.204), outperforming all baselines by large margins. On the standard Logistic Growth model (low noise), it is slightly behind the best ODE-discovery methods (0.015 vs. 0.010 for WSINDy-5) but matches Neural ODE under high noise (0.198 vs. 0.197). In the pharmacokinetic case study (Table 2), Semantic ODE with semantic inductive biases achieves in-domain RMSE of 0.018 and out-domain RMSE of 0.015, substantially outperforming even the 15-term SINDy equation (0.020 in-domain, 77.577 out-domain).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Semantic ODE decisively outperforms all symbolic regression baselines (SINDy, WSINDy, PySR) on systems that are not governed by a compact closed-form ODE: on the General ODE it achieves 0.016 RMSE (low noise) versus 0.053–0.078 for the symbolic methods, and on the Integro-DE it reaches 0.025 versus 0.152–0.431. On the Mackey-Glass DDE, Semantic ODE (0.037 low / 0.077 high) beats even the best neural baseline (Neural Laplace at 0.057 / 0.094) and all symbolic methods by a wide margin. Against the neural baselines, Semantic ODE wins on General ODE (0.016 vs. 0.029 for Neural ODE), Pharmacokinetic (0.015 vs. 0.036), and Integro-DE (0.025 vs. 0.073), while roughly tying Neural ODE on Logistic Growth high noise (0.198 vs. 0.197). The one setting where Semantic ODE is slightly behind is Logistic Growth low noise (0.015 vs. 0.010 for WSINDy-5), which is expected since the logistic model is exactly representable by a simple closed-form ODE. Overall, Semantic ODE is the only method that maintains strong performance uniformly across all five system types without requiring the system to be expressible as a compact ODE.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant two-step paradigm in ODE discovery (SINDy, genetic programming, symbolic regression) where a closed-form equation is first identified and then analyzed by a human to understand system behavior. It builds on the motif-and-composition framework from Kacprzyk et al. (2024b) on transparent time-series forecasting, but repurposes it fundamentally: rather than using motifs as a post-hoc description of a discovered equation, the paper makes the semantic representation (motif sequence + quantitative properties) the direct output of a learned model. The trajectory reconstruction via piecewise cubic splines draws on classical approximation theory, while the composition map (a partition of initial-condition space into branches) and property map (basis-function regression per branch) are novel architectural choices that have no direct precedent in the ODE discovery or neural ODE literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the equation itself is not the goal of system identification—understanding and controlling behavior is—and that this can be achieved without ever producing a closed-form ODE. This opens a path to more intuitive inductive biases (e.g., 'the trajectory must decay to zero') and direct model editing that are intractable in the syntactic setting. Key limitations include restriction to 1D trajectories, inability to represent oscillatory or chaotic behavior (finite compositions only), and a multi-dimensional extension that remains a proof of concept (SIR model). Open questions include whether the semantic framework scales to high-dimensional coupled systems, how to handle bifurcations and parameter-dependent qualitative changes systematically, and whether the cubic-spline hypothesis space is expressive enough for complex real-world dynamics.
