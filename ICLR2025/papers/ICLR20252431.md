## Problem

Molecular properties are fundamentally determined by electron-level information (electronic distributions, orbital energies), yet existing prediction methods operate solely on atom-level descriptors (fingerprints, 2D/3D graphs) because quantum mechanical calculations of electron-level features scale cubically or worse with electron count, making them infeasible for real-world complex molecules. Force-field and semi-empirical approximations used in 3D-GNNs introduce calculation errors that propagate and degrade accuracy, while the 3D architectures themselves overfit on large molecular graphs.

## Input / Output

DELID takes a 2D molecular graph (atom-level structure with 200-dim atomic embeddings and 22-type bond one-hot encodings) and a public chemical database (a QM9 subset of molecules with ≤6 atoms) as input. The molecule is first decomposed into chemically valid substructures via EFG decomposition, and each substructure is matched to the most similar molecule in the database via Tanimoto similarity to retrieve 15 pre-calculated electron-level features (energies, polarizabilities, rotational constants). The output is a predicted scalar molecular property (regression) plus a learned electron-aware latent representation s that encodes the full-molecule electronic state.

## Method

DELID couples two diffusion processes: a graph diffusion that reconstructs the full molecular adjacency matrix A_0 from decomposed substructures G_T (treated as the 'noised' state), and a conditional diffusion on electron-level information that reconstructs the unknown full-molecule representation s_0 from retrieved fragment features s_T. The key self-supervised mechanism is that the graph diffusion provides the supervision signal for the electron-level diffusion: the model is trained to minimize the KL divergence between q(A_{t-1}|A_t;R_0) and p(A_{t-1}|s_t;R_0), so the electron-level diffusion is guided to follow the same trajectory as the graph diffusion without ever observing s_0. The complete graph decomposition (EFG-based) guarantees node features are preserved throughout, reducing the diffusion to operating only on the latent adjacency matrix. The final prediction decomposes as y = f(G) + ψ(s;G), where f is a deterministic 2D-GNN (MPNN) and ψ is a stochastic head parameterized by a normal distribution whose mean and variance are functions of (s, G).

## Results

DELID achieves state-of-the-art R²-scores on 8 out of 9 benchmark experimental molecular datasets, with headline results of 0.782 (Lipop), 0.912 (ESOL), 0.844 (IGC50), and 0.886/0.885 (CH-DC/CH-AC). On the CH-DC and CH-AC optical property datasets, all 2D-GNN baselines fail entirely (negative R²), while DELID achieves R² ≈ 0.88. DELID also outperforms the strongest 3D-GNN baseline (ConAN) on Lipop (0.782 vs. 0.738) and IGC50 (0.844 vs. 0.819), while ConAN and other 3D-GNNs are infeasible (N/A) on multiple datasets due to memory or numerical issues.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DELID outperforms all 13 baselines on 7 of 9 datasets. Against the strongest 2D-GNN baselines, DELID beats AttFP (the best 2D-GNN on most datasets) by 0.072 on Lipop (0.782 vs. 0.710), 0.037 on IGC50 (0.844 vs. 0.807), and 0.053 on LD50 (0.566 vs. 0.513). Against the strongest 3D-GNN (ConAN), DELID wins on Lipop (0.782 vs. 0.738), IGC50 (0.844 vs. 0.819), and LMC-H (0.532 vs. 0.466), while ConAN is infeasible (N/A) on ADMET, LC50, and LMC-H for several other 3D-GNNs. The most dramatic gap appears on CH-DC and CH-AC, where all 2D-GNNs fail completely (negative R²) and 3D-GNNs score only 0.31–0.71, while DELID achieves 0.886 and 0.885. XGBoost fingerprint baselines are consistently 0.1–0.3 R² below DELID across all datasets. On ESOL, DELID (0.912) narrowly edges out AttFP and ConAN (both 0.909), and on ADMET, ConAN (0.845) slightly exceeds DELID (0.834), representing the only dataset where a 3D-GNN wins.

## Relation to Prior Work

DELID builds on variational diffusion models (Kingma et al., 2021) and GNN-based molecular representation learning (MPNN, GIN, AttFP), but departs from both in critical ways. Standard diffusion models require ground-truth data to train the forward and reverse processes, whereas DELID introduces a self-supervised mechanism where the diffusion on electron-level information s is guided by the diffusion on the molecular graph G, eliminating the need for the unknown s₀. The paper also positions itself against 3D-GNNs (PhysChem, M3GNet, FAENet, ConAN) that require expensive 3D geometry, arguing they fail on real-world complex molecules, and against transfer learning from quantum databases that cannot cover large molecules due to cubic-or-greater computational complexity. The retrieval of electron-level features via Tanimoto matching against QM9/PubChemQC repurposes existing public databases in a novel decomposition-guided way.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DELID addresses a fundamental gap in molecular ML: electron-level information is physically essential for property prediction but computationally infeasible to obtain for large real-world molecules. The self-supervised diffusion mechanism—learning a diffusion process without ground-truth labels—is a conceptually interesting contribution that could extend to other settings where the target latent variable is inaccessible. However, the approach relies on approximate retrieval (Tanimoto matching against small-molecule databases), which limits the accuracy of the electron-level features, and the practical gains over strong 2D-GNN baselines like AttFP are moderate on most datasets, with dramatic improvements only on the niche optical datasets (CH-DC, CH-AC) where all competitors fail. Open questions include whether more accurate electron-level features (beyond QM9-level DFT) would further improve results, and whether the self-supervised diffusion framework generalizes beyond molecular property prediction.
