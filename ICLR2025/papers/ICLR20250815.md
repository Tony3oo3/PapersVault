## Problem

Existing bias evaluation datasets for LLMs suffer from two critical limitations: they typically cover only one or a few bias types (e.g., gender-only or age-only), and they employ incompatible metrics (e.g., CrowS-Pairs Score requires sentence pairs while WinoBias uses individual sentences), making cross-dataset and cross-model comparison difficult. No unified framework exists to systematically characterize and compare bias across different social groups, bias types, and evaluation tasks.

## Input / Output

For direct evaluation tasks (Recognition, Selection), the input is a text prompt asking the LLM to classify or choose between biased/unbiased sentences, producing a discrete label scored via micro-F1. For indirect evaluation tasks (Continuation, Conversation), the input is a context prompt and the LLM generates free-form text, which is then scored by GPT-4 (0–100 stereotyping score) or the Perspective API (toxicity probability). For Classification, tabular data is converted into natural-language descriptions and the LLM outputs a binary prediction, evaluated with Demographic Parity, Equalized Odds, and Unfairness Score. All configurations use exactly 100 samples, and the same metric is applied across all datasets sharing a task type to enable direct comparison.

## Method

The paper proposes a compositional taxonomy that characterizes every bias evaluation dataset along three orthogonal dimensions: bias type (Stereotyping vs. Toxicity), social group (Age, Gender, Race, Religion), and task (Recognition, Selection, Continuation, Conversation, Classification). This taxonomy yields 40 possible configurations, of which existing datasets cover only a small fraction—particularly for Toxicity. To fill these gaps, the authors construct 11 new CEB datasets by using GPT-4 to augment source datasets: BBQ QA pairs are transformed into narrative sentences for Recognition/Selection, HolisticBias prompts are modified to elicit stereotypical or toxic continuations/conversations, and tabular datasets (Adult, Credit, Jigsaw) are reformulated as text classification prompts. For evaluation, GPT-4 serves as a scalable judge for stereotyping scores (validated against 20 human raters), while the Perspective API handles toxicity scoring, and fairness metrics (DP, EO) handle classification. The key design principle is metric uniformity within each task type, enabling apples-to-apples comparison across all 40 configurations.

## Results

On the CEB-Continuation and CEB-Conversation tasks (lower bias scores are better), GPT-4 achieves average Stereotyping scores of 13.4 (Continuation) and 16.7 (Conversation), while Llama3-8b achieves 16.5 and 16.6 respectively, showing that smaller LLMs can generate content with comparable stereotypical bias to GPT-4. On Toxicity, GPT-4 scores 17.0 (Continuation) and 5.7 (Conversation), whereas Mistral-7b achieves 14.4 and 6.7, indicating GPT models are more prone to generating toxic continuations. GPT-4's best Toxicity Conversation scores are 5.4–6.2 across social groups, the lowest among all models. Human evaluation confirms GPT-4 as a reliable bias scorer, with human scores slightly lower than GPT-4's self-assessments (e.g., Llama3-8b: 22.2 human vs. 18.0 GPT-4 on Age Continuation-S).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4 outperforms GPT-3.5 on Stereotyping Continuation (13.4 vs. 21.8) and Toxicity Conversation (5.7 vs. 7.1), but is slightly worse on Toxicity Continuation (17.0 vs. 16.3), suggesting GPT-4 is more susceptible to generating toxic continuations from toxic prompts. Unlike in direct evaluation (Recognition/Selection) where GPT models clearly dominate with Micro-F1 scores of 75–100% versus 30–65% for Llama models, in indirect evaluation the gap narrows substantially: Llama2-7b achieves a Stereotyping Continuation score of 12.9, actually lower (better) than GPT-4's 13.4. Llama3-8b achieves the best Toxicity Continuation score (11.9) among all models, outperforming GPT-4 (17.0) by 5.1 points. Mistral-7b matches or exceeds Llama2-13b on most configurations while avoiding the extremely high Refuse-to-Answer rates (up to 99.5%) that plague Llama2 models on Selection tasks. Overall, the key finding is that model size does not monotonically determine bias in generation tasks, challenging the assumption that larger models are inherently fairer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on a fragmented landscape of bias evaluation datasets (BBQ, HolisticBias, WinoBias, StereoSet, CrowS-Pairs, RedditBias, BOLD) that each target narrow slices of bias (one social group, one task, one bias type) with incompatible metrics. The key conceptual departure is the compositional taxonomy that reorganizes these datasets along three orthogonal dimensions—bias type (stereotyping vs. toxicity), social group (age, gender, race, religion), and task (recognition, selection, continuation, conversation, classification)—enabling unified metric application across previously incomparable benchmarks. For dataset construction, the paper repurposes BBQ's QA pairs and HolisticBias's conversational prompts via GPT-4 augmentation to fill unexplored configurations, and leverages tabular datasets (Adult, Credit, Jigsaw) for the classification task. The LLM-as-judge paradigm (GPT-4 scoring stereotyping, Perspective API scoring toxicity) follows recent work on automated bias measurement but is validated here against human raters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CEB addresses a real practical pain point: researchers could not compare bias results across datasets because metrics were incompatible and coverage was fragmented, particularly for toxicity. The compositional taxonomy and the 11,004-sample benchmark provide a reusable scaffold for future bias research, and the validation that GPT-4 scores align with human evaluators (and are not inflated for its own outputs) supports scalable automated bias auditing. Open questions include whether the taxonomy is complete (physical appearance, coreference resolution, and other tasks are deferred), whether the GPT-4-as-judge approach generalizes to multilingual or low-resource settings, and whether the observed high refusal-to-answer rates for Llama models on race/religion topics reflect genuine safety alignment or over-restriction that hampers legitimate evaluation.
