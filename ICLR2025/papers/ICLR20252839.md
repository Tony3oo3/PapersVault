## Problem

When users or training pipelines instruct LLMs with subjective phrases like 'enthusiastic' or 'witty,' the model's actual operational semantics—how it adjusts its outputs—can diverge from human expectations in surprising ways (e.g., 'enthusiastic' producing dishonest outputs). No prior method systematically detects these misalignments at the level of abstract concept relationships, and AI-only feedback loops risk reinforcing rather than catching such gaps.

## Input / Output

TED takes a set of 132–210 subjective phrases, an LLM to audit (Mistral 7B or Llama 3 8B Instruct), and 100 generic prompts (ethical questions or writing topics). It produces two thesauruses: an operational thesaurus (a cosine-similarity matrix over gradient embeddings, discretized into similar/neutral/dissimilar via model-specific thresholds) and a semantic thesaurus (ternary expected/unexpected/neither labels from 3 unanimous human annotators or binary LLM judgments). The final output is a set of misalignment pairs—unexpected side effects or inadequate updates—each validated with a per-pair success rate (fraction of 100 prompts where the LLM judge confirms the predicted shift).

## Method

TED encodes each subjective phrase's operational semantics as a gradient vector: for phrase w, it computes e_op(w) = ∇_{e(x_∅)} log p_θ(o_w | x_∅), the gradient of the log-likelihood of the LLM's subjective output with respect to the embedding of the first user-inputted token in a generic prompt, averaged over 100 prompts. This is fully unsupervised—no labels, only the model's own sampled outputs. The operational thesaurus is then built by thresholding pairwise cosine similarities of these vectors (τ_sim ≈ 0.93–0.98, τ_dis ≈ −0.1 to −0.5). The semantic thesaurus is constructed independently from human annotators (unanimous 3-of-3 agreement on 1,260 pre-filtered pairs) or from GPT-4 binary judgments. Misalignment pairs are the clashes where the two thesauruses disagree and neither assigns a neutral label. Downstream validation prompts the LLM with one phrase and uses GPT-4 with chain-of-thought to judge whether outputs shift toward the paired phrase relative to a control, randomizing response order to eliminate position bias.

## Results

TED consistently outperforms the semantic-only baseline across all models, tasks, and semantic thesauruses. For inference-steering on Llama 3 8B with the human-constructed thesaurus, TED achieves a 79.7% average success rate on unexpected side effects (vs. 48.1% for the baseline), with 56.7% of pairs exceeding a 90% success-rate threshold (vs. 10% for the baseline). For output-editing on Llama 3 8B with the human-constructed thesaurus, TED reaches 85.6% average success rate on unexpected side effects (vs. 43.2% baseline), with 56.7% of pairs above 90% (vs. 6.7% baseline). Qualitatively, TED uncovers surprising failures such as Llama 3 producing 'dishonest' outputs 97% of the time when steered to be 'enthusiastic,' and Mistral producing 'harassing' outputs 78% of the time when edited to be 'witty.'

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is a semantic-only method that identifies candidate failure pairs using only the human/LLM semantic thesaurus (i.e., pairs humans expect to be dissimilar for unexpected side effects, or similar for inadequate updates) without any information about the LLM's actual operational semantics. TED wins on every configuration tested: for all four failure-type × model × thesaurus combinations in both output-editing and inference-steering, TED's average success rate exceeds the semantic-only baseline. The gap is largest for unexpected side effects on Llama 3 8B with the human-constructed thesaurus (79.7% vs. 48.1% avg. success in inference-steering; 85.6% vs. 43.2% in output-editing), where TED also dramatically outperforms on the high-threshold fraction (56.7% of pairs ≥90% vs. 10% and 6.7% respectively). For inadequate updates, TED still wins but the margin is smaller and fewer pairs reach the 90% threshold, suggesting inadequate updates are rarer or harder to detect. The semantic-only baseline's weakness is that it cannot distinguish which semantically-dissimilar pairs the LLM actually conflates, leading to many false positives at high thresholds (e.g., 0% of baseline pairs reach 90% success for Llama 3 output-editing with the LLM thesaurus, vs. 23.3% for TED).

## Relation to Prior Work

TED adapts the clash-detection methodology from Tong et al. (2023), which identified CLIP failures by finding semantically distinct inputs with identical embeddings, and repurposes it at the concept level: comparing an LLM's operational thesaurus (built via gradient-based embeddings of subjective phrases) against a human-constructed semantic thesaurus. The gradient-based encoding draws on function vectors (Todd et al., 2024) and prompt compression (Mu et al., 2023; Li & Liang, 2021), but applies a single gradient step to capture how a phrase shifts outputs rather than to compress a prompt. The output-editing task is directly motivated by Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2023), and the broader failure-detection framing connects to red-teaming and jailbreaking literature (Perez et al., 2022a; Jones et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2023), though TED targets a finer-grained, phrase-level misalignment rather than adversarial inputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that common subjective phrases used in system prompts and Constitutional AI pipelines (e.g., 'enthusiastic,' 'witty,' 'humorous') carry hidden operational side-effects that systematically diverge from human expectations, with implications for how models are steered at inference and trained during alignment. The method offers a scalable path for human supervision at the level of abstract concept relationships rather than individual outputs, which the authors argue is more tractable as models improve. Key limitations include evaluation on only 7B/8B models, reliance on GPT-4 as judge, and a fixed set of ~200 phrases; whether the misalignments persist or shift in larger, more aligned models remains an open question, as does whether the single-gradient-step encoding captures the full operational semantics in context-dependent ways.
