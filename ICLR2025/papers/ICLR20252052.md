## Problem

Generic test-time adaptation (TTA) methods, designed for i.i.d. data like images, perform poorly on graphs experiencing structure shifts (homophily or degree changes) because these shifts degrade node representation quality by blurring class boundaries, rather than merely shifting the decision boundary. This paper provides a theoretical decomposition showing that structure shifts cause representation degradation (Δ_f) while attribute shifts cause classifier bias (Δ_g), explaining why existing TTA algorithms that rely on high-quality representations and refine decision boundaries are ineffective under structure shifts.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained GNN model (e.g., GPRGNN with MLP parameters θ and hop-aggregation parameters γ) and an unlabeled target graph, without re-accessing the source graph or requiring target labels. It produces updated hop-aggregation parameters γ and node class predictions. The framework is plug-and-play: it wraps around any base TTA algorithm (Tent, T3A, AdaNPC, etc.) that supplies soft pseudo-labels, and only updates the lightweight γ vector (K+1 scalars) per epoch, adding ~12% computation overhead per adaptation step.

## Method

Matcha restores node representation quality under structure shifts by adapting the hop-aggregation parameters γ in multi-hop GNNs (GPRGNN, APPNP, JKNet, GCNII), which control how node features are mixed with neighbor information across hops. The key theoretical result shows the optimal γ* = d(2h−1) depends on target graph degree and homophily, so adapting γ from source to target recovers accuracy up to Θ((Δh)²+(Δd)²). To update γ without labels, Matcha introduces a Prediction-Informed Clustering (PIC) loss that computes intra-class and inter-class variance from soft pseudo-labels produced by a base TTA method, then minimizes the ratio σ²_intra/(σ²_intra+σ²_inter) to encourage tight, well-separated clusters while avoiding the trivial scale-invariance failure of entropy minimization. The iterative loop alternates between the base TTA producing predictions and gradient descent on γ via the PIC loss, with a convergence guarantee of O(1/T) rate dependent on the Lipschitz constant of the base TTA.

## Results

On real-world datasets, Matcha (integrated with ERM) improves target-graph node classification accuracy by up to 31.95% (Syn-Products: 37.80% → 69.75%). When combined with existing TTA methods, Matcha further boosts their performance by up to 39.31% (Tent on Syn-Products: 29.14% → 68.45%). On the synthetic CSBM benchmark, Matcha achieves up to 24.28% improvement over ERM and up to 21.38% over the best baseline across homophily, degree, and combined attribute+structure shifts. The method adds only 11.9% extra computation per adaptation epoch, compared to 486% (GTrans) and 247% (SOGA).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Matcha is evaluated as a plug-and-play module on top of six baselines: three generic TTA methods (Tent, T3A, AdaNPC) and three graph-specific TTA methods (GTrans, SOGA, GraphPatcher). The largest gains appear on Syn-Products, where Tent alone achieves only 29.14% but Tent+Matcha reaches 68.45% (+39.31%), and GTrans improves from 43.89% to 71.75% (+27.86%). On Syn-Cora, GTrans+Matcha achieves the best target accuracy (83.49%), outperforming GraphPatcher+Matcha (80.99%) and SOGA+Matcha (79.03%). On Twitch-E, all methods show modest gains (≤1.5%) since the injected shifts are milder, with Tent+Matcha leading at 58.57%. On OGB-Arxiv, GTrans+Matcha (41.81%) and GraphPatcher+Matcha (41.83%) are nearly tied, both slightly ahead of SOGA+Matcha (41.78%). Overall, Matcha consistently improves every baseline across all four real-world datasets, with the most dramatic improvements occurring under severe degree and homophily shifts (Syn-Products, Syn-Cora).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends test-time adaptation (TTA) from i.i.d. vision data to graph data, specifically targeting structure shifts (homophily and degree) that prior graph TTA methods (GTrans, SOGA, GraphPatcher) handle only partially or under restrictive assumptions like homophily. It departs from the dominant TTA paradigm of entropy minimization or pseudo-labeling by identifying that these losses adapt the classifier (decision boundary) rather than the featurizer (node representations), which is the critical failure mode under structure shifts. The method repurposes hop-aggregation parameters already present in GPRGNN, APPNP, JKNet, and GCNII as the adaptation target, guided by a theoretical analysis on CSBM graphs showing that the optimal aggregation weight depends on target degree and homophily.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean theoretical decomposition of accuracy gap into representation degradation (structure shifts) versus classifier bias (attribute shifts) provides a principled explanation for why generic TTA fails on graphs and points to a targeted fix. The plug-and-play design means practitioners can combine Matcha with any existing TTA method without architectural changes, and the PIC loss's linear O(NCD) complexity makes it scalable where pairwise methods like SOGA are quadratic. Open questions include whether the CSBM-based theory generalizes to heterogeneous multi-class real-world graphs, how Matcha behaves under adversarial or compound shifts beyond the tested scenarios, and whether the hop-aggregation parameter space is sufficient for very deep or heterogeneous GNN architectures.
