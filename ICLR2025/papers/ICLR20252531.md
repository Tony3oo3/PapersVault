## Problem

Full fine-tuning of large pre-trained language models is computationally expensive, and while PEFT methods like LoRA reduce trainable parameters, they leave the model's sequential layer architecture unchanged. No existing PEFT approach exploits the complementary linguistic information encoded at different depths of a transformer, missing an opportunity for inter-layer information exchange that could improve task adaptation without increasing effective parameter count.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard text sequences (tokenized inputs of variable length, up to 512 tokens for GLUE) and meaning representations (attribute-value pairs) for the E2E NLG task. Outputs are task-specific: binary/multi-class labels for GLUE classification tasks, a continuous similarity score in [1,5] for STS-B, and free-form generated sentences for E2E. The architectural modification is applied at every layer of the backbone, so the input flows through a modified computation graph where each layer's output is a weighted blend of its own transformation and a router-selected layer's transformation.

## Method

MoLEx applies sparse upcycling to a pre-trained transformer by treating each layer as an expert in a shared Mixture-of-Experts structure. At every layer t, a single shared linear router g(z_t) = Wz_t + b computes affinity scores against all T layers, and a Top-1 gating function selects one layer expert v_t to mix with the original layer u_t via a learnable scalar α: z_{t+1} = z_t + α·u_t(z_t) + (1−α)·v_t(z_t). This is layered on top of LoRA adapters, so only the router parameters (W, b) and α are added (~9k extra parameters for RoBERTa-base), keeping the effective trainable count essentially unchanged. The key insight is that mixing a layer with a later layer (τ ≥ t) combines fine-scale and coarse-scale features, while mixing with an earlier layer (τ < t) injects lower-level features into higher-level processing, yielding multi-scale compositional representations. Theoretically, a linear MoLEx model can be unrolled into a weighted ensemble of layer compositions, and the paper proves this ensemble is strictly more robust than any single base model under ε-ball perturbations.

## Results

On the GLUE benchmark with RoBERTa-base, MoLEx (combined with LoRA, 0.309M trainable params) achieves an average score of 86.3, surpassing the LoRA baseline (85.7) across nearly all tasks, with the largest gains on QQP (+2.0, 64.8 vs 62.8) and MRPC (+1.1, 89.8 vs 88.7). Scaling to RoBERTa-large, MoLEx improves the average from 88.8 to 89.1. On the E2E NLG Challenge with GPT-2 medium, MoLEx achieves BLEU 70.7 (vs. 70.0 for LoRA) and NIST 8.87 (vs. 8.77), with lower standard deviations indicating more stable generation. In zero-shot transfer (e.g., QQP→QNLI), MoLEx gains up to 5.7 points over LoRA (56.2 vs. 50.5), and in robustness tests with added noise, MoLEx maintains higher accuracy on both QNLI (64.0 vs. 63.1) and SST-2 (70.9 vs. 69.3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoLEx is compared primarily against LoRA (Hu et al., 2021), its direct baseline, and consistently outperforms it on 7 out of 8 GLUE tasks for RoBERTa-base (gains of +0.2 to +2.0 points) and all 8 tasks for RoBERTa-large, while using only 0.309M vs. 0.3M trainable parameters. Against BitFit (Zaken et al., 2021, 0.1M params), MoLEx achieves higher average GLUE score (86.3 vs. 85.2) despite training ~3× more parameters, and against AdapterDrop (Rücklé et al., 2021, 0.9M params), MoLEx matches or exceeds performance with fewer parameters (86.3 vs. 85.4). On the E2E NLG benchmark, MoLEx (BLEU 70.7) outperforms AdapterL (66.3–68.9), AdapterH (67.3), FTTop2 (68.1), and PreLayer (69.7), approaching full fine-tuning (68.2) with only 0.359M trainable parameters. MoLEx does not surpass full fine-tuning baselines (RoBERTa-base FT: 86.4 avg; GPT-2 FT: BLEU 68.2), but it matches or exceeds them in several individual tasks while training orders of magnitude fewer parameters. In zero-shot transfer and robustness evaluations, MoLEx uniformly outperforms LoRA by 2–6 points, confirming that the layer-mixing mechanism improves generalization beyond in-distribution fine-tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

MoLEx repurposes the Sparse Mixture of Experts (SMoE) framework—originally designed to scale up individual feed-forward layers (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022)—by treating entire pre-trained transformer layers as the experts rather than MLPs within a single layer. It builds directly on the sparse upcycling technique of Komatsuzaki et al. (2022), which converts dense checkpoints into SMoE models, but applies it at the layer level rather than the sub-layer level. The method is explicitly orthogonal to existing PEFT approaches like LoRA (Hu et al., 2021), adapters (Houlsby et al., 2019), and prompt tuning (Li & Liang, 2021), positioning itself as a structural architectural modification that can be layered on top of any of these without altering their internal parameters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MoLEx offers a minimal-overhead, plug-and-play mechanism to improve any PEFT method's performance and zero-shot transferability without increasing trainable parameters, which is practically attractive for practitioners already using LoRA or similar adapters. The layer-probing analysis provides a useful interpretability lens on which linguistic features (surface, syntactic, semantic) different layers contribute to specific tasks. However, the robustness guarantee is restricted to deep linear models, and the empirical gains on GLUE are modest (typically 0.3–2 points), limiting the practical impact for high-stakes deployments. Extending the theoretical analysis to nonlinear architectures and exploring cross-model layer mixing remain open directions that could substantially broaden the method's applicability.
