## Problem

Existing representational similarity metrics (CCA, CKA, RSA, Brain-score) reduce the comparison of two neural networks to a single scalar score, providing no insight into *which* visual features make the models similar or dissimilar. This paper asks: can we decompose representational similarity into interpretable, per-concept scores that reveal specific visual features one model encodes but the other does not, and link those differences to changes in model decisions?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250464/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two pre-trained vision networks (any architecture pair, e.g., ResNet vs. ViT) and a per-class set of 16 evenly spaced 64×64 patches extracted from 224×224 images (100 images per model for concept extraction; 50 images per class for correlation-based layerwise comparison). Patches are resized to each model's input resolution and passed through a chosen layer. Outputs include per-concept similarity scores (CMCS, Pearson/Spearman in [-1,1]), visual collages of top-n over- and under-predicted patches for each concept, concept importance scores via integrated gradients, and replacement-test metrics (mean l2-distance of reconstructed activations, KL-divergence of logits, and prediction match accuracy) that quantify how much each concept's dissimilarity affects model behavior.

## Method

RSVC operates in three stages. First, for each model, layer activations over the shared patch set are decomposed via non-negative matrix factorization (NNMF for ResNets, Semi-NMF for ViTs) into a coefficient matrix U and a basis W, where each column of U encodes one visual concept's activation pattern across patches. Second, a lasso-regression (l1-regularized, λ=0.1) maps one model's activations to predict the other model's concept coefficients; the Pearson/Spearman correlation between predicted and true coefficient vectors yields a per-concept similarity score (CMCS), and a faster correlation-based variant (MCS/MMCS) enables coarse layerwise comparison. Third, a replacement test swaps each concept's cross-model predicted coefficients into the reconstruction and measures the resulting l2-distance in activations, KL-divergence in logits, and change in prediction accuracy, directly linking representational dissimilarity to functional model behavior. The key design choice—regressing on interpretable concept coefficients rather than raw neuron activations—makes each similarity score visually inspectable via image collages of over- and under-predicted patches.

## Results

In the controlled toy-concept experiment, the model trained to ignore the pink square (M_nc) achieves a near-zero similarity score (~0.0) to the pink-square concept in M_ps, while other concepts (e.g., bird color pattern, water background) retain high similarity, confirming RSVC's specificity. Across four model pairs on ImageNet, most concepts show medium-to-high cross-model similarity (>0.6), with DINO vs. MAE (finetuned on NABirds) achieving an average similarity of ~0.80, compared to ~0.89 for same-pretraining different-seed comparisons. The replacement test confirms that decreases in concept similarity predictably increase l2-distance of activations, increase KL-divergence of logits (for high-importance concepts), and decrease prediction match accuracy. Full RSVC analysis of ResNet-18 vs. ResNet-50 across all 1000 ImageNet classes takes approximately 20 hours on a single RTX 4090.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RSVC does not perform a head-to-head numerical benchmark against existing representational similarity metrics (CKA, CCA, RSA, BrainScore) on a shared task; instead, it positions itself as complementary, providing interpretable per-concept insights that those methods lack. The paper validates its regression-based CMCS score against a cheaper correlation-based MCS (Pearson) metric, showing that MCS acts as a noisy lower bound (most points in Fig. A2 lie above the diagonal), confirming that the lasso regression captures additional structure. Same-model concept similarity (SMCS) is significantly higher than cross-model similarity (CMCS), validating that the regression task is feasible and that observed cross-model differences are meaningful rather than artifacts of the decomposition. The toy-concept experiment serves as the strongest controlled validation: RSVC correctly identifies the single injected difference (pink square) with ~0.0 similarity while leaving all other concept similarities unchanged, something a single-number metric like CKA could not provide. The replacement test further distinguishes RSVC from prior methods by linking specific concept-level dissimilarities to measurable changes in model logits and predictions, rather than only reporting an aggregate similarity score.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two established research threads: representational similarity metrics (CCA, CKA, RSA, BrainScore, Li et al. 2015) that reduce model comparison to a single opaque score, and concept-based XAI methods (CRAFT, TACV, CRP) that decompose individual model activations into interpretable visual features. The key methodological move is to use dictionary-learning-based concept extraction (from CRAFT) as an intermediate representation, then apply the l1-regularized linear regression framework from BrainScore and Li et al. 2015 to predict one model's concept coefficients from the other's activations, yielding per-concept similarity scores that are both quantitative and visually interpretable. The replacement test and the over/under-prediction visualization strategy for low-similarity concepts are novel contributions not present in either prior line of work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By making representational similarity interpretable at the level of visual concepts, RSVC gives practitioners a concrete diagnostic tool for understanding why two models behave differently on specific inputs — a capability that single-score metrics like CKA fundamentally lack. This could be valuable for identifying sources of model bias, understanding transfer learning failures, or auditing model decisions in safety-critical settings. Key limitations include the fidelity-interpretability tradeoff inherited from dictionary learning (reconstruction error varies across models), the computational cost (~20 hours for full ImageNet comparison), and the restriction to vision models. Open questions include whether the approach extends to language models, whether recursive concept extraction (as in CRAFT) would yield finer-grained comparisons, and how the method behaves when models are trained on substantially different datasets.
