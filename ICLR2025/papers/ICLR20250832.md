## Problem

Random projections are widely used to generate low-dimensional graph node embeddings, with approximation quality typically justified by the data-independent Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma. However, it was unclear how well dot product and cosine similarity between node embeddings are actually preserved, particularly as a function of the graph's degree distribution. This paper identifies that dot product guarantees become data-dependent and yield pathological approximations for low-degree and high-degree nodes, a failure mode previously unaddressed in the literature.

## Input / Output

The method takes a graph's adjacency matrix A or row-normalized transition matrix T (or polynomials thereof, p(A) or p(T)) and a random projection matrix R ∈ R^{q×n} with i.i.d. N(0, 1/q) entries, producing low-dimensional node embeddings X = p(A)R^T or X = p(T)R^T in R^{n×q}. From these embeddings, pairwise node similarities are computed as either dot products X_{v*}X_{u*}^T or cosine similarities cos(X_{v*}, X_{u*}), and ranking quality is evaluated via NDCG@K. The key structural detail is that the analysis is parameterized by the graph's degree distribution, with low-degree nodes (d_v ≤ c) and high-degree nodes (d_u ≥ γ²cq) treated as distinct regimes.

## Method

The core technical tool is a rotation argument: for any two vectors x, y with cosine similarity ρ, the paper shows that after an orthogonal transformation aligning one projected vector to e₁, the dot product and cosine similarity under random projection can be expressed in terms of independent standard normal vectors M and N, yielding closed-form representations (Theorem A.6). From these representations, the paper derives asymptotic normality results (via CLT) and finite-sample concentration bounds (via Laplace transforms and Chernoff-type inequalities) for both dot product and cosine similarity. The key insight is that the dot product's variance scales with ‖x‖²‖y‖², making it data-dependent on node degrees and producing unreliable estimates for extreme-degree nodes, whereas cosine similarity's variance depends only on (1−ρ²)², yielding uniform guarantees across all degree regimes. For ranking, the paper derives an exact t-distribution-based formula for the probability that random projections flip the relative ordering of two nodes' relevance scores.

## Results

On the Wikipedia-20060925 graph (2.98M nodes, 37.3M edges) with q=256 random projection dimension, RP Cosine Similarity achieves NDCG@10 of 0.895 (std 0.063) on high-degree nodes and 0.999 (std 0.010) on low-degree nodes, remaining stable across all degree ranges. In contrast, RP Dot Product with P=T degrades to NDCG@10 of 0.602 (std 0.315) on high-degree nodes, while RP Dot Product with P=A collapses to NDCG@10 of 0.327 (std 0.188) on low-degree nodes. The cosine similarity method's worst observed NDCG@10 is approximately 0.75, whereas the dot product variants frequently fall well below this threshold for their respective vulnerable degree regimes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three similarity measures under random projections: RP Dot Product with the transition matrix (P=T), RP Dot Product with the adjacency matrix (P=A), and the proposed RP Cosine Similarity. RP Cosine Similarity dominates both dot product baselines in stability: it maintains NDCG@10 above 0.89 for all degree ranges, while RP Dot Product (P=T) drops to 0.602 on high-degree nodes (a 33% relative degradation) and RP Dot Product (P=A) collapses to 0.327 on low-degree nodes (a 67% relative degradation). The failure modes are complementary—P=T fails for high-degree nodes while P=A fails for low-degree nodes—confirming the theoretical prediction that dot product guarantees become data-dependent on degree. Cosine similarity's advantage is most dramatic on low-degree nodes with P=A, where it achieves 0.999 versus 0.327 NDCG@10, a gap of over 67 percentage points. The theoretical analysis (Corollary 3.4) predicts that for high-degree nodes with no common neighbors, the dot product ranking flip probability exceeds 15.8%, a pathology entirely absent under cosine similarity (Corollary 3.8).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Johnson-Lindenstrauss framework from its classical data-independent form to a data-dependent setting where guarantees are expressed in terms of the graph's degree distribution. It builds on the random-projection graph embedding methods of Zhang et al. (2018) and Chen et al. (2019), and refines prior dot-product preservation bounds (Kaban, 2015; Vempala, 2004) and cosine-similarity guarantees (Arpit et al., 2014) using a rotation argument combined with Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization to obtain tighter, more interpretable concentration results. The key conceptual departure is showing that the JL lemma's data-independence breaks down for dot product in graphs, while cosine similarity retains uniform guarantees across degree regimes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical recommendation to use cosine similarity rather than dot product for random-projection graph embeddings is actionable and addresses a gap where prior work justified embeddings solely through the JL lemma without characterizing when the approximation actually fails. The ranking instability results (≥15.8% probability of order flips for disconnected high/low-degree pairs) quantify a real degradation in NDCG that practitioners may have observed but not attributed to degree effects. Open questions include whether the analysis extends to sparse (Rademacher) random projections, to higher-order matrix polynomials, and to heterogeneous graphs with metapaths. The empirical validation is limited to a single Wikipedia-scale dataset, and the asymptotic results require q to be large relative to degree parameters.
