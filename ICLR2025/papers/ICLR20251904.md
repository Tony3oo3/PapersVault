## Problem

MCTS-based planning for text-based games relies on uncertainty-driven exploration without language understanding, while planning-then-learning paradigms (MCTS + RL) require multiple costly iterations to converge. LLMs can generate viable initial plans but struggle to balance exploration and exploitation and cannot adapt their action guidance based on past failures within a single planning episode. The paper asks whether LLMs, augmented with dynamic memory, can serve as an adaptive prior policy within MCTS to improve sample efficiency and task performance at the initial planning phase.

## Input / Output

At each step, the agent receives a textual observation augmented with location and inventory information (via 'look' and 'inventory' commands) plus a Jericho-provided valid action set. The LLM (GPT-3.5-turbo-0125, temperature 0) is queried for the index of the optimal action, and log probabilities for the top 20 tokens at that index are retrieved; absent actions receive a log probability of −10, and all are normalized via softmax with temperature 5. The in-trial memory is a fixed triple (o_{t−1}, a_{t−1}, o_t), while cross-trial memory holds up to 3 single-sentence reflections. The output is a selected action determined by the modified PUCT formula, along with updated Q-values and visit counts propagated through the search tree.

## Method

MC-DML replaces the neural-network prior π(·|s) in PUCT with an LLM that generates action probability distributions conditioned on two memory channels: in-trial memory (the most recent observation–action–observation triple, grounding the prior in commonsense) and cross-trial memory (up to 3 reflections generated by the LLM whenever a simulation reaches a terminal failure state). These reflections are stored per root node and injected into subsequent simulations from that node, dynamically shifting the LLM's action probability distribution to avoid previously failed strategies. A dynamic pruning strategy adjusts search depth between d_min=10 and d_max=30 (increment Δd=20) based on whether the highest Q-value at the selected node is zero, accommodating the sparse-reward structure of the games. Unlike PUCT-RL or MC-LAVE-RL, MC-DML does not require a separate planning-then-learning loop; the LLM policy and Q-value estimates are refined within a single planning episode through the interplay of tree search backpropagation and memory-guided prior adjustment.

## Results

On the Jericho benchmark (9 text-based games), MC-DML achieves a score of 48.66±1.89 on Zork1, 67±1.41 on Deephome, and 346.67±9.42 on Detective, outperforming or matching all LLM/MCTS-based baselines in 8 out of 9 games. Notably, MC-DML nearly doubles MC-LAVE-RL's score on Deephome (67 vs. 35) and achieves full game completion on Pentari (70/70) and Detective (346.67/360). On Zork1, MC-DML's single planning phase (48.66) surpasses MC-LAVE-RL's final converged score after 4 iterations (45.2), demonstrating superior sample efficiency without a planning-then-learning paradigm.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the closest MCTS-based competitor MC-LAVE-RL (Jang et al., 2020), MC-DML wins on 7 of 9 games, with the largest margin on Deephome (67 vs. 35, nearly 2×) and Ztuu (23.67 vs. 7, over 3×), while losing only on Ludicorp (19.67 vs. 22.8). Against PUCT-RL, MC-DML wins on all 9 games, with particularly large gaps on Deephome (67 vs. 28.6) and Ztuu (23.67 vs. 5). The LLM-only and Reflection baselines (Shinn et al., 2024) score near zero on most difficult games, confirming that raw LLM reasoning without structured search is insufficient for complex text-based games. Against RL-based baselines (DRRN, KG-A2C-Hard, MC!Q*BERT, MPRC-DQN, RC-DQN, BiKE+CBR), MC-DML achieves the best score in 6 of 9 games, including Zork1 (48.66 vs. best baseline 44.3) and Deephome (67 vs. best baseline 20). Critically, MC-DML's single-pass planning on Zork1 (48.66) exceeds MC-LAVE-RL's score only after 4 full planning-then-learning iterations (45.2), eliminating the need for expensive multi-iteration training.

## Relation to Prior Work

MC-DML sits at the intersection of two research threads: MCTS-based planning for text games (PUCT, MC-LAVE-RL) and LLM-guided decision-making (Reflexion, LLM-MCTS). It departs from PUCT-RL and MC-LAVE-RL by replacing the trained neural network prior with an LLM that dynamically adjusts its action estimates via in-trial and cross-trial memory, thereby eliminating the iterative planning-then-learning loop. It also differs from LLM-MCTS (Zhao et al., 2024), which uses the LLM as a fixed prior, by introducing a reflection mechanism inspired by Reflexion that lets the LLM learn from failed trajectories within a single planning episode. The semantic-similarity exploration heuristic of MC-LAVE-RL is replaced by a more general language-grounded value estimation that does not rely on hand-crafted assumptions about action categories.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that MC-DML achieves competitive or superior performance at the initial planning phase without the multi-iteration training loop that PUCT-RL and MC-LAVE-RL require, potentially reducing wall-clock time for deploying MCTS-based agents in text environments. The dynamic memory mechanism (short-term trajectory + long-term failure reflections) offers a lightweight, interpretable alternative to training a policy network, and the bottleneck-state analysis in Zork1 provides concrete evidence of how reflection corrects the LLM's semantic biases. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond the Jericho benchmark, whether the in-trial memory window (limited to the last observation-action pair) is sufficient for games with long-range dependencies, and whether the results hold with different LLM backends or at scale.
