## Problem

LLMs can personalize responses using contextual information (e.g., user demographics, preferences), but practitioners lack a simple, training-free mechanism to control *how much* that context influences the output. Existing approaches—supervised fine-tuning, RLHF, and prompt engineering—require curated example data, are costly to adapt across contexts, and offer no continuous knob to dial personalization up or down at inference time.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a free-form context string (e.g., "I am a toddler"), a task prompt, and a real-valued scalar λ (typically in [-4, 4], with λ = -1 removing context entirely and λ = 0 equivalent to standard prompting). It requires two forward passes per generated token (one with context, one without), roughly doubling compute over a vanilla generation. In the inverse-model mode, given a completed generation X, the method outputs a posterior distribution over λ (quantifying how strongly the context shaped X) or over a candidate set of contexts C (identifying implicit intent), computed via maximum-likelihood over a discrete grid rather than closed-form normalization.

## Method

CoS defines a contextual influence function F(x_i) = LLM(x_i | C, P) − LLM(x_i | ∅, P), the logit-level difference between a forward pass with and without context. At each decoding step, the next-token logits are rescaled as (1+λ)·LLM(x_i | C, P) − λ·LLM(x_i | ∅, P), which linearly amplifies (λ > 0) or attenuates (λ < 0) the context's contribution relative to the context-free baseline. This is entirely training-free and model-agnostic, requiring only standard autoregressive LLM access. The paper further inverts the forward model via Bayesian inference: P(λ | X, C, P) ∝ P_CoS,λ(X | C, P) yields a posterior over the degree of contextual emphasis, and P(C | λ, X, P) ∝ P_CoS,λ(X | C, P) enables classification of implicit intents (e.g., distinguishing "immigrants are taking over" from "immigrants are violent") by scoring candidate contexts against the observed generation.

## Results

On the Implicit Hate Dataset, CoS achieves 82% classification accuracy on the Black group (2 candidates), 47% on Immigrant (3 candidates), and 60.5% on Muslim (2 candidates), outperforming direct LLM classification (50%, 37%, 62%) on two of three groups. In the personalization user study (15 participants, 70 responses), higher λ yields significantly higher personalization scores (Spearman ρ=.67, p<.001), and GPT-4 pairwise ratings correlate with human judgements up to 68% (77% with tie-breaking). On LaMP tweet paraphrasing, CoS with λ=−0.5 achieves the best Rouge-1 (0.42) and Rouge-L (0.36), outperforming both no-context (λ=−1: 0.40/0.35) and plain context prepending (λ=0: 0.39/0.33). Factuality on OpenbookQA degrades by at most 4.6% at λ=3, and coherence/diversity metrics remain stable across λ values.

## Baselines & Comparisons

For implicit hate classification, CoS substantially outperforms direct LLM prompting on the Black group (82% vs. 50%) and Immigrant group (47% vs. 37%), but slightly underperforms on the Muslim group (60.5% vs. 62%); human annotators remain the upper bound (88%, 64%, 63%). For personalization, CoS with tuned λ produces more reliable and consistent personalization trends than both multi-turn Q&A (which suffers compounding cost) and in-context learning (which can cause personalization to degrade), while costing roughly 2× the compute of a single forward pass—less than multi-turn Q&A. On the BBQ bias benchmark, CoS with an equalizing context increases accuracy and reduces bias across all demographic topics for both T0pp and Mistral-7b-instruct as λ increases, whereas the base models perform near random on ambiguous prompts. On LaMP, CoS with a slightly negative λ (−0.5) beats both the no-context baseline and the plain context-prepending baseline, suggesting that attenuating long, noisy contexts improves generation quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

CoS adapts the contrastive decoding framework of Li et al. (2023) and O'Brien & Lewis (2023), which steer generation by contrasting outputs of two different models, to instead contrast the same model with and without a context string. Unlike activation-level steering vectors (Turner et al., 2023; Subramani et al., 2022), CoS operates directly on next-token logits, offering a more interpretable and model-agnostic control knob. The Bayesian inverse model draws on the probabilistic language interpretation tradition (Goodman & Frank, 2016; Tenenbaum et al., 2011), repurposing the forward generation probability as a likelihood for inferring latent context or tone from observed text.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CoS provides a training-free, two-forward-pass mechanism that gives practitioners a continuous, interpretable dial (λ) for personalization intensity without retraining or collecting labeled data, which is practically valuable as LLMs are deployed across diverse user populations. The dual forward/inverse formulation—using the same logit-difference for both controllable generation and Bayesian inference of implicit intent—opens a path toward quantitative content moderation tools that can flexibly add new categories without retraining. Key open questions include composability across multiple context regions, behavior on long input sequences where context influence may decay, and whether the 2× compute overhead is acceptable at scale; the paper also notes that negative λ (inverting context direction) is semantically ill-defined, limiting the method's symmetry.
