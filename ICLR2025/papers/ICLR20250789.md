## Problem

Prior work on implicit bias in neural network optimization has focused on parameter norms and landscape curvature, largely overlooking the directional structure of the optimization trajectory itself. This paper asks whether neural network training trajectories exhibit directional regularity (straight, linear paths) or are highly zig-zaggy, and how this directional character evolves across training phases and hyperparameter choices.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250789/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequence of flattened parameter vectors {θ_t} sampled at regular intervals (e.g., per epoch) from a training run, organized into a matrix Θ ∈ R^{(T+1)×p}. It produces a Trajectory Map — a (T+1)×(T+1) matrix of pairwise cosine similarities between norm-normalized parameter vectors — along with derived scalar measures (Mean Directional Similarity, apex angles, inter-update angles) and, as a practical application, a decapacitation schedule indicating the epoch at which >99% of parameters can be frozen without performance loss.

## Method

The core contribution is the Trajectory Map, a kernel matrix C = Θ̂Θ̂^T of cosine similarities between all pairs of normalized parameter checkpoints, which serves as a qualitative hallmark of directional structure in parameter space. From this map, the authors derive quantitative hallmarks including Mean Directional Similarity (MDS) and angular measures that capture directional movement at both macro (global apex angle) and micro (inter-update angle) scales. The key empirical finding is that macro-level directionality saturates by the early phase of training (~30 epochs for ResNet50 on ImageNet), while micro-level oscillations persist in later phases; this is explained theoretically via a quadratic model with one-step momentum and ℓ₂ regularization, where the low-rank Hessian and Edge-of-Stability dynamics produce obtuse inter-update angles. They further demonstrate that once directional saturation is reached, training only the scalar parameters in batch-normalization layers (<1% of total parameters) achieves near-full-network accuracy, enabling >99% savings in optimization buffer memory and backward-pass computation.

## Results

On ResNet50/ImageNet, the Mean Directional Similarity (MDS) ranges from 0.764 (optimal SGD with momentum + weight decay, 75.96% top-1) to 0.979 (no momentum, no weight decay, 68.73% top-1), showing that directional redundancy increases as performance degrades. For decapacitation, training only the 1,376 BN scalar parameters (0.5% of total) from epoch 40 on ResNet20/CIFAR-10 recovers within 2% of the full-network 91.2% accuracy, and from epoch 80 matches it exactly; on ResNet50/ImageNet, BN-only training from epoch 60 reaches within ~2% of full accuracy while training only 0.18% of parameters. Across Pythia GPT-NeoX LLMs (14M–12B), MDS increases monotonically with scale (0.678 → 0.815) and the apex angle at the origin decreases monotonically, correlating better with performance than traditional norm-based complexity measures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an analysis paper without external method baselines; its comparisons are internal ablations and cross-setting contrasts. Disabling momentum or weight decay individually raises MDS from 0.764 to 0.901 or 0.931 respectively, and disabling both pushes it to 0.979, with a corresponding ~8% drop in top-1 accuracy—indicating that these hyperparameters promote directional exploration rather than simply stabilizing norms. Directional measures (apex angle at origin) show a clean monotonic decrease with LLM scale (14M→12B), whereas parameter norm and distance-from-initialization measures are non-monotonic and even peak at the best-performing model, making directional metrics more predictive of performance. The decapacitation experiment contrasts full-network training against BN-only training: from initialization, BN-only achieves only 54.8% on CIFAR-10 (vs. 91.2% full), but after directional saturation (~epoch 40) the gap closes to within 2%, demonstrating that >99% of parameters can be frozen without performance loss. Random-walk baselines (Appendix C) yield an expected MDS of ~2/3 in the large-parameter limit, well below the observed 0.76+ for trained networks, confirming that optimization trajectories are highly structured rather than stochastic.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the Trajectory Map as a systematic tool for analyzing directional structure in optimization paths, departing from the dominant implicit-bias literature that characterizes regularity through parameter norms or landscape curvature. It builds on scattered prior observations of directional convergence (Ji and Telgarsky, 2020), cosine-similarity trends in transformer pre-training (Merrill et al., 2020), and corridor geometry (Dherin and Rosca, 2024), but unifies these into a comprehensive framework with both qualitative (trajectory maps) and quantitative (MDS, angular measures) hallmarks. The theoretical analysis connects to the Edge of Stability (Cohen et al., 2022) and low-rank Hessian structure (Singh et al., 2021) to explain micro-level oscillations, while the scale argument draws on NTK and μP large-width limits.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The decapacitation result—freezing >99% of parameters after directional saturation and training only normalization-layer scalars while retaining within 1–2% of full-network accuracy—offers a concrete, low-overhead path to significant compute and memory savings in training. The counterintuitive finding that momentum and weight decay *increase* directional exploration (lowering MDS) reframes their role beyond the standard 'smoothing' narrative. Key open questions include whether the decapacitation strategy transfers to LLM pre-training (the authors only demonstrate it on vision models), whether directional measures can serve as reliable generalization predictors in a U-shaped regime, and how layerwise trajectory dynamics might inform mechanistic interpretability.
