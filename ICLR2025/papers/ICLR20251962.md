## Problem

Standard correlation metrics like Krippendorff's α and Cohen's κ were designed to measure human-to-human inter-rater agreement under assumptions (interchangeable raters, no ground truth) that do not hold when comparing machine labels to human labels. The paper shows that a single aggregate correlation score can make automatic evaluators appear to match or even exceed human-to-human agreement when human labels contain substantial uncertainty, masking systematic performance gaps that only become visible under high human consistency.

## Input / Output

The method consumes items annotated by multiple human raters (3–5 per item on nominal or ordinal scales) alongside machine labels generated at temperature 1.0 with 20 samples per item to capture machine variability. It produces three outputs: correlation scores stratified by percentage agreement or unique-label count, a binned Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSb) score that compares full rating distributions rather than collapsing to a single gold label, and perception charts that display the complete distribution of human and machine ratings within each human-median or human-majority bin.

## Method

The paper proposes a three-part analytical framework for evaluating automatic evaluators under human uncertainty. First, stratifying samples by human label uncertainty (percentage agreement with the majority/median, or number of unique labels) reveals that machine labels only superficially match human majority behavior when uncertainty is high; under near-perfect human agreement, the human-to-machine gap becomes stark. Second, the binned Jensen-Shannon Divergence for perception (JSb) computes a weighted sum of JSD between human and machine probability mass functions within bins defined by the human median (ordinal) or majority (nominal) label, treating the two label sources asymmetrically and avoiding the single-gold-label assumption inherent in Krippendorff's α or rank correlations. Third, perception charts visualize the full rating distributions per bin, exposing patterns such as machine bias toward middle ratings or failure to produce extreme scores that humans assign. The framework is validated across NLI (SNLI, MNLI), summarization (SummEval), and preference (MT-Bench) datasets using G-Eval, GPT-4, Claude Sonnet 3, Mistral Large v2, and Llama 3.1 as automatic evaluators.

## Results

On MNLI (matched), the overall Krippendorff's α between human majority and machine majority (H^wM^w) is 0.72, nearly matching the human-to-human (HH) correlation of 0.70, creating the illusion that LLM-as-a-Judge approximates human consensus. However, when stratified by human label certainty, the gap becomes stark: for items with perfect human agreement (PA=1, 58% of samples), HH=1.00 while H^wM^w drops to 0.83 (Δ=0.17); conversely, for highly uncertain items (PA<0.8, 16% of samples), H^wM^w=0.37 actually exceeds HH=0.04. On SummEval (ordinal), Mistral achieves Krippendorff's α of 0.49 overall on Coherence, but this drops to 0.55 under high uncertainty (PA<60%) while the true gap (Δ=0.37) is hidden; under perfect agreement (PA=1), the gap widens to Δ=0.65. The proposed binned JSD (JS_b) correctly ranks a 'good' model (0.56) above a 'poor' model (0.65) in a toy example where Krippendorff's α, Spearman's ρ, and Kendall's τ all fail to distinguish them.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates G-Eval (Liu et al., 2023b), GPT-4 as LJ (Zheng et al., 2023), Claude Sonnet 3, Mistral Large v2, and Llama 3.1 8B as automatic evaluators, but the key comparison is not between these methods competing on accuracy—it is between the *apparent* HM correlation and the *true* HH correlation under stratification. On SummEval Coherence, G-Eval achieves Krippendorff's α of 0.31 overall (vs. HH=0.55), while Mistral reaches 0.49 and Sonnet 0.24; however, under perfect human agreement (PA=1), all three drop substantially (G-Eval 0.25, Mistral 0.35, Sonnet 0.24), revealing that the overall scores overstate machine performance. On MT-Bench preference data, GPT-4 as LJ shows H^wM^w Krippendorff's α of 0.32 overall for <Alpaca, GPT-3.5>, while Sonnet shows only 0.01; yet under high uncertainty (PA:[60,80)), GPT-4 reaches 0.36 while Sonnet drops to −0.04, and under perfect agreement both are near zero (−0.05 and −0.02 respectively). The paper demonstrates that the *ranking* of LLM-as-a-Judge methods changes depending on the uncertainty stratum and the reference human label used (majority H^w vs. individual H'), meaning no single aggregate correlation score provides a reliable comparison. The proposed binned JSD (JS_b) and perception charts are positioned as complementary tools rather than replacements, with JS_b correctly identifying a 'good' model over a 'poor' one in a toy example where all standard correlation metrics (K-α, Spearman's ρ, Kendall's τ) fail.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Jensen-Shannon Divergence (Lin, 1991) by adapting it into a binned, perception-aware metric (JSb) that compares full human and machine label distributions without requiring a single gold label. It departs from the standard practice in the LLM-as-a-Judge literature (G-Eval, MT-Bench) of reporting a single aggregate correlation score (Krippendorff's α, Spearman's ρ) by introducing stratification by human label uncertainty as an analytical framework. The paper also repurposes information-theoretic divergence measures—previously used for image quality prediction (Jin et al., 2018)—as a human-machine agreement metric for perception-based evaluation, and introduces perception charts as a visualization technique to contextualize correlation numbers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that machine labels can superficially match or exceed human-to-human correlation when human uncertainty is high challenges a foundational assumption in how the NLP community validates automatic evaluators, with direct implications for how LLM-as-a-Judge results are reported and interpreted across the field. Open questions remain about how to formally incorporate systematic (non-random) errors from LLMs into chance-adjusted metrics, how to handle the chicken-and-egg problem of selecting appropriate metrics without knowing ground truth, and whether the proposed stratification and JSb approach generalizes to settings with only one human label per item. The open-sourced tools lower the barrier for practitioners to adopt these practices immediately.
