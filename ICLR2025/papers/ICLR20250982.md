## Problem

Existing generalization bounds for Personalized Federated Learning (PFL) rely on convex assumptions and complexity-based tools (VC dimension, Rademacher, PAC-Bayes) that cannot capture the actual iteration behavior during non-convex training of neural networks. These bounds are agnostic to algorithm design choices such as learning rates, local steps, and communication topologies, making them unable to explain how hyperparameter selection affects generalization or to bridge the relationship between PFL, standard FL, and pure local training.

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on heterogeneous client data (CIFAR-10 with Dirichlet α=0.3, CIFAR-100 with Pathological c=20) partitioned into shared feature-extraction parameters u and personalized classification parameters v_i, trained with ResNet-18 or VGG-11. The primary outputs are closed-form generalization bounds and excess risk bounds for both centralized (C-PFL) and decentralized (D-PFL) partial model personalization, expressed as functions of training rounds T, local steps K_u/K_v, learning rates, client participation ratio n/m, data heterogeneity δ_u, and communication topology spectral gap λ. Empirically, the paper measures generalization via the parameter distance between models trained on datasets differing by one sample, and the training-vs-testing loss gap.

## Method

The paper establishes the first algorithm-dependent uniform stability analysis for Partial Model Personalization under smooth non-convex objectives by decomposing the generalization error into aggregation errors (shared variables u) and fine-tuning errors (personalized variables v_i), then tracking how each propagates through the alternating local update and aggregation steps. The key technical mechanism is bounding the parameter divergence between models trained on two datasets differing by one sample, where the biased gradient estimation from multi-local updates introduces distinct error terms: the shared variables accumulate a (σ_u + δ_u) term reflecting both stochastic variance and data heterogeneity, while the personalized variables accumulate only σ_v. For D-PFL, the gossip/mixing matrix W introduces a topology-dependent coefficient κ_λ that quantifies how communication sparsity amplifies the generalization gap. Combining the stability bound with existing convergence rates yields excess risk bounds showing that optimal test performance is a trade-off between optimization error (improving with more rounds) and generalization error (worsening with more rounds, larger local steps, or denser connectivity).

## Results

The paper provides the first algorithm-dependent generalization bounds for Partial Model Personalization under non-convex conditions. For C-PFL, the generalization gap scales as O((nUTK)^(μL/(1+μL)) / (mS)) (Theorem 1), and the excess risk is O(1/√T + (nKT)^(μL/(1+μL)) / m) (Corollary 1). For D-PFL, the bound additionally depends on the communication topology through κ_λ, with fully-connected topology (κ_λ=0) achieving the best generalization (Theorem 2). Empirical validation on CIFAR-10 (ResNet-18, Dirichlet α=0.3) and CIFAR-100 (VGG-11, Pathological c=20) confirms that fewer local epochs, lower learning rates, and sparser topologies reduce the generalization gap, while C-PFL consistently outperforms D-PFL in both generalization and convergence at equal communication bandwidth.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against specific PFL algorithms but instead establishes theoretical comparisons across training paradigms. PFL outperforms FL in generalization because the gradient diversity of shared variables satisfies δ_u² ≤ δ_g², meaning personalization eliminates the worst heterogeneity effects that plague consensus-based FL. PFL also outperforms Pure Local Training because, although local training is unaffected by gradient diversity, it lacks the 1/m collaboration benefit from multiple clients, leading to a larger bound. Between C-PFL and D-PFL, the centralized variant always achieves tighter generalization and convergence bounds (Remark 9), as the global averaging on a central server reduces error propagation in shared variables. However, C-PFL requires the number of active clients n to scale at least polynomially in m, making D-PFL preferable when communication cost is the bottleneck. The empirical results on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 confirm all four theoretical predictions: (1) fewer local epochs and lower learning rates improve generalization, (2) more client participation and denser topologies widen the generalization gap, (3) more total clients with fixed data worsen generalization, and (4) C-PFL dominates D-PFL at equal bandwidth.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the uniform stability framework from Hardt et al. (2016) for SGD and Sun et al. (2023c) for centralized/decentralized FL to the Partial Model Personalization (PFL) setting of Pillutla et al. (2022), providing the first algorithm-dependent generalization analysis for PFL under non-convex objectives. It departs from prior PFL generalization work (Deng et al. 2020, Mansour et al. 2020, Zhang et al. 2022) which relied on PAC/VC/Rademacher/PAC-Bayes complexity bounds under convex conditions and were algorithm-independent. The topology-aware analysis for D-PFL builds on Zhu et al. (2022) for D-SGD, adapting the κ_λ spectral gap formulation to the personalized setting with separate shared and personal variable dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first theoretical justification that PFL outperforms both FL and Pure Local Training from a generalization (not just optimization) perspective under data heterogeneity, by showing the gradient diversity δ_u in PFL is strictly smaller than δ_g in FL. The excess risk analysis reveals a fundamental trade-off: increasing communication rounds improves convergence but worsens generalization, giving practitioners a principled basis for early stopping and hyperparameter selection. Open questions include deriving lower bounds to establish tightness, extending to more advanced stability notions (on-average, model stability), and analyzing whether the C-PFL > D-PFL gap persists under realistic communication constraints.
