## Problem

Multi-task generalist robot policies suffer from negative transfer, performing worse on individual tasks than specialist policies, while collecting sufficient in-domain demonstrations for each new task is expensive and impractical in dynamic environments. Existing non-parametric retrieval methods either retrieve entire trajectories (missing shared sub-behaviors across tasks) or individual states (losing temporal dynamics), and typically require training domain-specific encoders that do not generalize across visual domains. This paper asks how to retrieve the most relevant *sub-trajectories* from large multi-task offline datasets using off-the-shelf representations, enabling robust few-shot policy training at deployment time.

## Input / Output

The method takes a small set of in-domain expert demonstrations (3–5 trajectories with RGB camera views, proprioception, and language instructions) collected at test time, plus a large offline multi-task dataset that may span different scenes, objects, and tasks (e.g., 4,500 LIBERO-90 trajectories or 5,000 DROID demos). Target demonstrations are automatically segmented into sub-trajectories using a proprioception-based heuristic (end-effector velocity below a threshold ε), and all frames are encoded with a frozen vision foundation model (DINOv2 or CLIP). The output is a language-conditioned transformer visuomotor policy (BAKU-style, ResNet-18 backbone, GMM action head predicting h-step action chunks) trained on the union of the target demos and the top-K retrieved sub-trajectories, where K is a per-task hyperparameter controlling retrieval volume.

## Method

STRAP's core mechanism is Subsequence Dynamic Time Warping (S-DTW) applied to foundation-model embeddings: for each segmented target sub-trajectory, a pairwise L2 cost matrix is computed between DINOv2/CLIP embeddings of target frames and all frames in the offline dataset, and S-DTW finds the best-aligned sub-sequence in the reference trajectory by relaxing the boundary condition (alignment can start and end at any point), enabling variable-length matching across tasks. The top-K matches are selected uniformly across all target sub-trajectories, and the resulting retrieval set is unioned with the target demos for behavior cloning. A key design choice is using a language-conditioned policy to handle noisy retrieval: when retrieved sub-trajectories contain actions for different tasks, the language instruction allows the policy to distinguish relevant from misleading samples, converting potential negative transfer into positive transfer. The approach requires no training of retrieval-specific encoders, no manual trajectory segmentation of the offline dataset, and scales linearly with dataset size since embeddings are precomputed and S-DTW is applied per sub-trajectory.

## Results

On the LIBERO-10 benchmark (10 long-horizon manipulation tasks), STRAP with DINOv2 and tuned K achieves 58.1% average success rate, outperforming the strongest baseline Fine-Tuning (51.7%) by +6.4% and the retrieval baselines BehaviorRetrieval (33.4%) and FlowRetrieval (33.1%) by +24.7% and +25.0% respectively. In real-world DROID-Kitchen experiments, STRAP achieves 51.6% average success (Table/Sink/Stove) with only 3 target demos, compared to 12.2% for BC and 25.9% for FT. When scaling the offline dataset with 5000 additional DROID trajectories, STRAP maintains 55.1% average while FT drops to 19.8% and MT collapses to 0.7%, demonstrating STRAP's scalability advantage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

STRAP decisively outperforms both prior retrieval methods: BehaviorRetrieval (BR, Du et al., 2023) achieves 33.4% average on LIBERO-10 vs. STRAP's 58.1% (+24.7%), and FlowRetrieval (FR, Lin et al., 2024) achieves 33.1% vs. 58.1% (+25.0%). The gap is particularly stark on tasks requiring cross-task sub-trajectory sharing, e.g., Book-Caddy where BR scores only 16.0% while STRAP reaches 94.0%. In real-world DROID-Kitchen, STRAP (51.6% avg) beats BR and FR by large margins, and the advantage widens when the offline dataset is augmented with 5000 DROID trajectories (STRAP 55.1% vs. FT 19.8%, MT 0.7%). STRAP also outperforms the non-retrieval baselines: Fine-Tuning (51.7%) by +6.4% and Multi-Task (37.7%) by +20.4% on LIBERO-10, with the gap growing substantially in real-world settings where FT and MT suffer from overfitting and task confusion respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

STRAP extends the retrieval-augmented few-shot imitation learning paradigm established by BehaviorRetrieval (BR) and FlowRetrieval (FR), which retrieve individual state-action pairs or full trajectories using domain-specific trained encoders. The key departure is twofold: (1) retrieval operates at sub-trajectory granularity rather than single states or entire trajectories, enabling cross-task data sharing, and (2) off-the-shelf vision foundation models (DINOv2, CLIP) replace the need for training task-specific VAE encoders. The sub-trajectory segmentation builds on the proprioception-based chunking heuristic from RT-H (Belkhale et al., 2024), while the temporal alignment leverages Subsequence Dynamic Time Warping (S-DTW), an extension of classical DTW that relaxes boundary constraints to match variable-length subsequences.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

STRAP addresses a practical bottleneck in robot deployment: the need for expensive in-domain data collection or fragile multi-task pre-training when encountering new tasks. By showing that a handful of in-domain demos plus non-parametric sub-trajectory retrieval from large offline corpora (e.g., DROID) yields robust specialist policies, the method offers a scalable path to few-shot robot adaptation without retraining encoders or policies from scratch. Limitations include the reliance on a simple velocity-threshold chunking heuristic (which the authors acknowledge produces sub-optimal segments for some tasks), the task-dependent optimal K requiring manual tuning, and the fact that the method assumes matching embodiment between target and prior data. Open questions include whether more principled segmentation (e.g., via VLMs or information-theoretic methods) and automatic K selection could close the remaining gap on harder tasks.
