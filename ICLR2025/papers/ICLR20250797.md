## Problem

While decades of research have produced numerous algorithms for weak-to-strong generalization (self-training, co-training, pseudo-labeling, superalignment), the data-centric question—what properties of data actually enable a strong model to generalize beyond its weak supervisor—remains largely unexplored. This paper identifies a specific data mechanism, the overlap density, that characterizes when and why weak-to-strong generalization succeeds, and provides practical tools to find and maximize it.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a weak model (e.g., Qwen1.5 0.5B or a Snorkel label model) trained on a labeled set, a strong model (e.g., Llama3 8B or a 4-layer MLP), and multiple unlabeled data sources. It produces a controlled training set with a user-specified overlap ratio (0%–100%), a ranked selection of which data source to query next under a budget constraint, and the resulting weak-to-strong model. A key detail is that the overlap detection operates on model confidence scores and last-layer activations (not raw inputs) for neural network settings, using change-point detection (binary segmentation) to set thresholds rather than fixed cutoffs.

## Method

The core mechanism partitions data into easy-only, hard-only, and overlap points, where overlap points contain both easy patterns (learnable by the weak model) and hard patterns (only learnable by the strong model). The weak model labels overlap points reliably via easy patterns, and the strong model leverages these noisy-but-informative labels to learn hard patterns, generalizing to hard-only points. For practical overlap detection (Algorithm 2), the method first identifies hard-only points by thresholding weak-model confidence scores via change-point detection, then scores remaining points by their maximum absolute inner product (or cosine similarity) with hard-only points to separate overlap from easy-only points. For data source selection (Algorithm 1), the method frames the problem as a multi-armed bandit where each source is an arm and the reward is its empirical overlap density, using UCB to balance exploration of under-sampled sources against exploitation of high-overlap sources. Theoretically, the paper derives an error bound (building on Lang et al.'s expansion property) showing that the strong model's error on hard-only points is reduced by a term proportional to the expansion coefficient c times the overlap density, and provides an O(√(K log T / t)) regret bound for the data selection algorithm.

## Results

The paper's primary empirical contribution is validating that weak-to-strong (W2S) model accuracy tracks the controlled overlap proportion across 19 LLM datasets (Qwen1.5 0.5B → Llama3 8B, linear probing) and 9 weak supervision datasets (Snorkel → 4-layer MLP, WRENCH benchmark). Three distinct regimes are observed: low overlap (W2S underperforms the weak model), medium overlap (W2S matches or slightly exceeds the weak model), and high overlap (W2S approaches the strong model trained on true labels). For data source selection on Amazon Polarity and DREAM, the UCB-based algorithm (Algorithm 1, T=25 rounds) consistently outperforms random sampling across 20 repeated seeds. In synthetic experiments, the largest performance gain from increasing overlap density occurs on hard-only test points, confirming the theoretical prediction that overlap density enables pseudolabel correction specifically on the hard region.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing W2S algorithms; instead, it validates a data-centric mechanism against natural baselines. In the data selection experiments (Section 5.2), the UCB-based overlap maximization algorithm (Algorithm 1) is compared against random sampling across data sources with known overlap densities of 0.9 and 0.1; the UCB method produces consistent enhancements over random sampling on both Amazon Polarity and DREAM (averaged over 20 seeds). In the synthetic data selection experiment (Section 5.3.2), the algorithm is compared against both random sampling and an oracle that always samples from the optimal source (overlap density 0.8 among 5 sources); the UCB algorithm progressively converges toward oracle performance over T=50 rounds, while random sampling remains flat. The weak model (f_weak) serves as a lower bound and the strong model trained on true labels (f_strong) as an upper bound in all W2S experiments; the key finding is that W2S performance interpolates between these bounds as a function of overlap density, with the gap to f_strong closing in the high-overlap regime. The paper explicitly notes that when overlap points are very few or detection is very noisy, no W2S generalization is observed regardless of algorithm, underscoring that the data mechanism is a prerequisite rather than the algorithm being the primary driver.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the theoretical framework of Lang et al. (2024), which explains weak-to-strong generalization via an expansion property and model robustness, by identifying the specific data structure (easy-hard overlap points) that satisfies the expansion condition. It departs from the broader weak-to-strong generalization literature (Burns et al. 2023, Charikar et al. 2024, Somerstep et al. 2024), which focuses on algorithmic techniques for a fixed dataset, by shifting the lens to data-centric questions: what data characteristics enable generalization and how to acquire more of them. The data source selection algorithm repurposes UCB bandit methods (Auer, 2002) in a novel context, and the overlap detection leverages change-point detection (Sen & Srivastava, 1975) to separate latent data regions without ground-truth pattern labels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, actionable data-centric mechanism (overlap density) that practitioners can use to guide data acquisition for weak-to-strong generalization, with direct implications for superalignment where data budgets are limited and sources vary in quality. The three-regime observation (low/medium/high overlap) gives practitioners a diagnostic tool: if their weak-to-strong model underperforms, the issue may be insufficient overlap points rather than an algorithmic deficiency. Key limitations include the simplified two-level (easy/hard) pattern decomposition, Gaussian mixture assumptions in the theoretical guarantees, and heuristic overlap detection that may not generalize to more complex or multi-level difficulty structures. Open questions include extending to multi-level overlapping difficulties, handling cases where the weak model has partial access to hard features, and validating the mechanism at the scale of frontier LLMs.
