## Problem

Existing preference alignment methods like DPO assume a shared preference among annotators, treating conflicting preferences as pairwise noise. This causes models to skew toward majority opinions and progressively degrade on minority preferences during training, failing to capture the distributional (pluralistic) nature of human preferences within a group. The paper asks how a probabilistic LLM can align with the full distribution of preferences rather than collapsing to a single dominant one.

## Input / Output

The model receives a text query (a global-issue question or movie metadata) along with a target belief distribution p*_B over a discrete belief set B (e.g., six opinion-class tokens or five rating scores), which is either predetermined or estimated from statistical counts. At inference, the model first predicts a belief class token and its textual description (or a rating score), then generates a free-form response conditioned on that belief. The output is thus a structured two-part generation: a belief prediction followed by a belief-consistent response.

## Method

GDPO factorizes generation as p_θ(y|x) = Σ_b p_θ(y|b,x)·p_θ(b|x) and optimizes two jointly trained loss terms. The first is a belief calibration loss that minimizes KL divergence between the model's predicted belief-token probabilities and the target distribution, plus a negative log-likelihood term for the full belief description. The second is a belief-conditioned preference alignment loss that applies the standard DPO objective but conditions both the chosen and rejected responses on the chosen belief b_c, so that conflicting preference pairs (where y_c ≻ y_r under one belief and y_r ≻ y_c under another) no longer cancel each other in the gradient. Training proceeds in two phases—SFT followed by joint GDPO optimization—starting from the SFT checkpoint, and the belief-conditioned loss is generic enough to swap in other alignment objectives such as KTO.

## Results

On the controllable opinion generation task (Pythia-2.8B, US), GDPO achieves a JSD of 0.068, substantially lower than DPO (0.352), SFT (0.122), and ICF (0.134), indicating far better alignment with the target belief distribution. GDPO also leads on Belief-Preference Consistency (BPC: 0.582 vs. DPO 0.469), Class-Belief Consistency (CBC: 0.989 vs. DPO 0.899), and Response Similarity (RS: 0.554 vs. DPO 0.527). These advantages hold consistently across all three countries (US, Pakistan, S. Africa) and both model sizes (GPT-2 Large, Pythia-2.8B). On the real-world movie review generation task, GDPO again achieves the lowest JSD and highest BPC among SFT, DPO, and GDPO for both models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GDPO consistently outperforms DPO on all four evaluation metrics (JSD, CBC, BPC, RS) across all three countries and both model sizes. The most dramatic gap is in JSD: on Pythia-2.8B (US), GDPO achieves 0.068 versus DPO's 0.352, a 5× reduction in distributional divergence, confirming that DPO's reward margins for minority preferences deteriorate during training while GDPO's improve. GDPO also surpasses SFT on JSD (0.068 vs. 0.122) and BPC (0.582 vs. 0.471), showing that the belief calibration loss is essential beyond what SFT alone provides. In-context fine-tuning (ICF) and few-shot prompting (including GPT-4o 5-shot) perform substantially worse on JSD (0.134 and 0.477 for Pythia; 0.528 for GPT-4o), indicating that prompt-based approaches cannot reliably capture the target belief distribution. On the real-world movie review task, GDPO achieves the lowest JSD and highest BPC compared to both SFT and DPO for both GPT-2 Large and Pythia-2.8B, validating generalizability beyond synthetic data.

## Relation to Prior Work

GDPO builds directly on DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024), reusing its loss function as the belief-conditioned preference alignment component while adding a KL-divergence calibration term for the belief distribution. It departs from the standard DPO/RLHF paradigm, which treats conflicting preferences as pairwise noise, by explicitly modeling intra-group belief diversity drawn from epistemological concepts of graded belief (Fodor et al., 1975; Genin & Huber, 2022). The paper positions itself against multi-group alignment frameworks (Ramesh et al., 2024; Chakraborty et al., 2024) and distributional preference reward modeling (Li et al., 2024a; Siththaranjan et al., 2023), distinguishing its focus on a single group's internal preference distribution and its reward-free, DPO-compatible formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GDPO addresses a concrete and underexplored failure mode of DPO: the active deterioration of minority-preference reward margins during training, which means standard alignment systematically erases underrepresented viewpoints. The belief-conditioning decomposition is a clean, modular contribution that could be grafted onto other alignment objectives (the paper demonstrates KTO compatibility). Key limitations include the requirement for explicitly stated beliefs in training data, the single-group scope (separate models needed per group), and evaluation on relatively small models (≤2.8B) with partly synthetic data. Open questions include whether the approach scales to frontier models, how to handle latent or implicit beliefs, and how to unify multiple groups in a single model.
