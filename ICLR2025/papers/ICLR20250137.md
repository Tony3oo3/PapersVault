## Problem

Standard RLHF pipelines estimate the reward model exclusively from preference data, discarding the implicit preference signal embedded in demonstration data. This separation into sequential SFT→RM→RL stages leads to suboptimal alignment, particularly when demonstration and preference data are unbalanced in quantity or quality, because the reward model cannot adequately capture human preferences from a single data source.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250137/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes two data streams simultaneously: demonstration data (prompt–continuation pairs from expert trajectories) and preference data (prompt–ranked-response pairs following the Bradley-Terry-Luce model). It produces a jointly optimized reward model parameter θ ∈ R^d and an aligned policy π_θ that is provably optimal with respect to that reward. In the LLM setting, the policy is a language model generating token sequences; in the MuJoCo setting, it is a continuous-action policy over state observations, with the reward parameterized by a (64,64) MLP.

## Method

AIHF formulates alignment as a bi-level optimization: the upper level maximizes a weighted sum of demonstration likelihood (L1) and preference likelihood (L2) over a shared reward parameter θ, while the lower level enforces that the policy is optimal w.r.t. the current reward (L3 = expected reward minus β·KL to a reference policy). The training algorithm alternates a policy alignment step (PPO or SAC) with a reward alignment step using a stochastic gradient that combines a demonstration-driven term (∇_θ R(τ^E) − ∇_θ R(τ^A)) and a preference-driven BTL term, avoiding costly Hessian computations via a key lemma showing the implicit gradient of L1 has a closed-form expression. Theoretically, the AIHF policy is shown to be a data-weighted average of the demonstration-only and preference-only policies, yielding lower variance than RLHF's softmax-of-sum-of-rewards form, with a finite-time convergence rate of O(K^{-1/2}).

## Results

On the 7B model setting (Mistral-7B-SFT-Beta base), AIHF achieves 64.75% average accuracy across six HuggingFace Open LLM Leaderboard benchmarks, outperforming DPO (63.19%), IPO (62.08%), RLHF/Zephyr-7B-Beta (61.68%), and SPIN (60.35%). On the 1B Pythia model with Anthropic-HH data, AIHF achieves a reward score of 1.190±0.069 (10k demo + 10k preference), substantially exceeding RLHF (0.896±0.095) and DPO (0.474±0.052). On RewardBench, the AIHF-trained reward model scores 72.63% average, surpassing the standard BTL reward model (71.15%) and the DPO implicit reward model (51.21%), with the largest gap on reasoning tasks (76.75% vs. 69.22% vs. 47.33%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AIHF outperforms all five baselines on the 7B average score: it leads DPO by 1.56 points (64.75% vs. 63.19%), IPO by 2.67 points, RLHF/Zephyr by 3.07 points, and SPIN by 4.40 points. The largest individual-task gains are on TruthfulQA (58.38% vs. DPO's 53.17%, a 5.2-point margin) and Arc Challenge (63.90% vs. DPO's 62.80%). DPO narrowly edges AIHF on Winogrande (79.40% vs. 79.24%), and Self-Play AIHF leads on GSM8K (44.20% vs. AIHF's 40.56%), but AIHF's overall average remains the highest. On the 1B Anthropic-HH setting, AIHF's reward score (1.190) exceeds RLHF (0.896) by ~33% and DPO (0.474) by ~151%, with the gap widening when preference data is scarce (5k vs. 10k). On MuJoCo robotic control tasks, AIHF outperforms both RLHF and IPL (DPO applied to multi-horizon MDPs), while RLHF fails to improve over the BC-initialized policy due to noisy reward estimation from limited preference data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies the three-stage RLHF pipeline (SFT → reward modeling → RL) and its single-stage alternatives (DPO, SPIN) under a common bi-level optimization formulation, showing each is a special case of the proposed AIHF meta-problem. It draws on maximum-likelihood inverse reinforcement learning (Zeng et al., 2022b) for the demonstration-fitting objective, stochastic choice theory for the preference likelihood, and two-timescale bi-level optimization (Hong et al., 2020; Khanduri et al., 2021) for the algorithmic design. The key departure from prior work is that reward and policy are co-optimized in a single loop rather than trained sequentially, and the paper provides a finite-time convergence guarantee (Theorem 4.1) that prior alignment methods lack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean theoretical decomposition showing that the AIHF policy is a variance-reducing weighted average of demonstration and preference policies (vs. RLHF's noisier sum-of-rewards form) gives practitioners a principled reason to prefer joint training, especially when data sources are unbalanced. Empirically, the consistent outperformance across 160M–7B LLMs and MuJoCo robotics tasks, including the finding that BC-initialized RLHF fails to improve while AIHF succeeds in MuJoCo, strengthens the case. Open questions include whether the bi-level overhead is prohibitive at frontier-model scale (the 7B experiments required substituting DPO for PPO in the lower level), and whether the simplified static analysis in Sec. 3.4 extends to the full sequential MDP setting.
