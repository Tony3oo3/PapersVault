## Problem

Training data attribution (TDA) methods have been limited to small models, extremely few target queries, or heavily subsampled corpora when applied to LLM pretraining. No prior gradient-based influence method could effectively retrieve influential examples for a multi-billion-parameter model from a full 160B-token pretraining corpus without subsampling or lexical pre-filtering, leaving a significant gap in understanding how LLMs leverage their training data at scale.

## Input / Output

Given a factual query (a natural-language prompt plus a target completion) and a trained decoder-only LLM, TrackStar computes per-example loss gradients over all model parameters (excluding token embeddings), pools them into 8 layer blocks, and applies two-sided random projection to 2^16 dimensions. The output is a ranked list of top-k proponents from the full 365M-example C4 corpus, scored by cosine similarity of Hessian-corrected, unit-normalized projected gradients. The method requires no lexical pre-filtering and supports exact scoring between each query and all candidate examples in a single pass.

## Method

TrackStar computes influence as the dot product of projected, Hessian-corrected, unit-normalized gradients for a training example and a query. Four key corrections distinguish it from prior methods: (1) Adafactor second-moment estimates divide each gradient component by its expected magnitude before projection, enabling per-parameter correction of outlier dimensions; (2) two-sided random projection (equivalent to LoGra) reduces dimensionality efficiently while preserving structure; (3) a mixed task-specific Hessian approximation R = λR_eval + (1−λ)R_train downweights gradient components common to the evaluation task template (e.g., shared prompt structure), with λ chosen so the top ~1000 task-specific components are suppressed; and (4) unit normalization converts the score to cosine similarity, preventing high-magnitude outlier examples from dominating rankings. Ablations show each correction is critical at scale: without unit normalization or the mixed Hessian, MRR drops from 0.365 to 0.064 and tail-patch scores degrade substantially, while the original TRAK formulation achieves near-zero MRR on the full 19.6M-sentence candidate set.

## Results

On the T-REx closed-set evaluation (retrieving from 19.6M Wikipedia sentences for an 8B model), TrackStar achieves MRR 0.365, Recall@10 0.496, and a tail-patch score of +0.90%, outperforming TRAK (MRR 0.001, tail-patch −0.02%) by a wide margin on influence. On the full C4 open-set evaluation (retrieving from 365M passages), TrackStar achieves MRR 0.338, Recall@10 0.515, and tail-patch +2.11%, more than 2.5× the tail-patch of BM25 (+0.83%) and Gecko (+0.54%). However, classical retrieval methods still dominate on attribution: BM25 reaches MRR 0.687 and Recall@10 0.845 on C4, versus TrackStar's 0.338 and 0.515, revealing a fundamental misalignment between factual entailment and causal influence on model predictions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TrackStar decisively outperforms TRAK on both attribution and influence: where TRAK achieves MRR 0.001 and negative tail-patch (−0.02%) on T-REx, TrackStar reaches MRR 0.365 and +0.90% tail-patch, a gap the authors attribute to TRAK's use of multi-class margin gradients with example-level (rather than token-level) correction and the absence of unit normalization. Against BM25, TrackStar loses on attribution (MRR 0.365 vs. 0.592 on T-REx; 0.338 vs. 0.687 on C4) but wins on influence by 2.2× on T-REx (+0.90% vs. +0.41%) and 2.5× on C4 (+2.11% vs. +0.83%). Against Gecko, the same pattern holds: Gecko achieves slightly higher MRR (0.620 vs. 0.365 on T-REx) but substantially lower tail-patch (+0.31% vs. +0.90%). Notably, even the ground-truth entailing sentences from T-REx yield only +0.52% tail-patch, below TrackStar's +0.90%, confirming that the examples most causally responsible for a model's factual prediction are not always those that explicitly state the fact.

## Relation to Prior Work

TrackStar extends the gradient-based influence function lineage (Koh & Liang 2017 → TracIn 2020 → TRAK 2023) to full-scale LLM pretraining by combining three corrections that prior work applied in isolation or at smaller scale: per-parameter optimizer second-moment correction (Adafactor), a task-specific mixed Hessian approximation, and unit normalization. The key departure from TRAK and prior TDA work is that these corrections are applied per-parameter before random projection (rather than post-projection), and the Hessian is mixed with evaluation-task gradients to suppress template-common components. The paper explicitly positions itself as making existing influence methods tractable at 8B parameters and 160B tokens without subsampling, rather than proposing a new influence formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that classical retrieval (BM25) substantially outperforms gradient-based influence methods at finding fact-entailing examples, yet those examples have far less causal influence on model predictions—reveals a fundamental misalignment between attribution and influence that prior small-scale work could not detect. This has practical implications for model transparency: knowing which examples a model 'used' is not the same as knowing which examples contain the relevant fact, and the gap narrows (but does not close) with model scale. Open questions include whether the misalignment persists for more capable models, how to optimally select the task-specific Hessian mixing parameter λ, and whether the method extends to non-factual tasks (commonsense, arithmetic) where entailment is less well-defined.
