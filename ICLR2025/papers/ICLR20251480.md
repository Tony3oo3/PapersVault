## Problem

Standard feature attribution methods (SHAP, LIME) used to comply with explainability mandates in consumer finance routinely highlight features that are immutable or unresponsive, giving consumers 'reasons without recourse' that cannot lead to an improved outcome. Additionally, these methods provide explanations even for individuals with fixed predictions where no single-feature intervention can change the result, potentially causing harm by suggesting actionable paths that do not exist.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251480/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained classifier f, an individual's feature vector x, and a set of actionability constraints (bounds, monotonicity, encoding validity, causal implications) that can be elicited in natural language and encoded as equations in optimization problems. It outputs per-feature responsiveness scores μ_j(x) ∈ [0,1], a feature-highlighting explanation containing only features with μ_j(x) > 0, and a flag identifying individuals with fixed predictions (all μ_j = 0). A key design property is that reachable sets are computed once per individual and can be reused to score any model, enabling responsiveness-aware model selection during development.

## Method

The core contribution is the responsiveness score μ_j(x) = Pr(f(x+a) = ŷ_target | a ∈ A_j(x)), the probability that a uniformly random single-feature intervention on feature j attains the target prediction. Scores are computed by constructing reachable sets R_j(x) — the set of all points reachable by intervening on feature j subject to actionability constraints — and querying the model over those points. Two algorithms build reachable sets: (1) rejection sampling that proposes candidate actions via separable constraints and verifies joint constraints through a mixed-integer program, and (2) complete enumeration for discrete features that iteratively solves a MIP (Find1DAction) with no-good cuts to exhaust all feasible actions. The approach is model-agnostic because reachable sets depend only on the individual and constraints, not on f, and the framework extends naturally to cost-weighted and distributionally robust variants.

## Results

Across three consumer finance datasets (HELOC, German Credit, Give Me Some Credit) and three model classes (LR, XGB, RF), the proposed Responsiveness (RESP) method achieves 100% 'All Features Responsive' in every setting, meaning every highlighted feature can independently lead to a target prediction. In contrast, SHAP explanations have 77.3%–100% of all highlighted features unresponsive, and LIME explanations have 65.5%–100% unresponsive. RESP also withholds explanations from individuals with fixed predictions (e.g., presenting explanations to only 41.0% of denied individuals under LR on HELOC vs. 100% for SHAP/LIME), and highlights fewer features on average (2.3 vs. 4.0), reducing the risk of misleading consumers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares RESP against SHAP [40] and LIME [48], the two most widely adopted feature attribution methods in consumer finance, as well as their action-aware post-processed variants (SHAP-AW, LIME-AW). RESP dominates on the primary metric: it achieves 100% 'All Features Responsive' across every dataset and model, while SHAP explanations have 77.3%–100% of features unresponsive and LIME has 65.5%–100% unresponsive. The action-aware variants (which zero out scores for globally immutable features) improve modestly—e.g., SHAP-AW reduces unresponsive features from 77.3% to 70.3% on LR/HELOC—but still leave the majority of highlighted features unresponsive. A critical additional advantage of RESP is that it withholds explanations from individuals with fixed predictions (e.g., only 41.0% of denied individuals receive an explanation under LR/HELOC vs. 100% for SHAP/LIME), preventing the harm of presenting 'reasons without recourse.' On the givemecredit dataset with XGB, SHAP-AW achieves 13.2% all-features-responsive, the best baseline, yet RESP still achieves 100%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against the dominant feature-attribution paradigm (SHAP, LIME) used to comply with explainability mandates like the ECOA adverse action requirement, arguing that these methods measure relative contribution to a prediction rather than whether changing a feature actually leads to a desired outcome. It draws on the algorithmic recourse literature (Ustun et al. 2019; Karimi et al. 2020, 2021), which returns a single optimal action, but departs by estimating the proportion of all feasible single-feature interventions that achieve the target, yielding a per-feature probability rather than a point action. The computational machinery for reachable sets is adapted from Kothari et al. (2024), who enumerate all-feature reachable sets for recourse verification; here the authors restrict enumeration to single-feature interventions, making the problem more tractable and directly applicable to feature-highlighting explanations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the standard industry practice of using SHAP/LIME scores for adverse action notices systematically highlights features that are immutable or unresponsive, potentially misleading consumers into pursuing ineffective changes or receiving explanations for predictions that cannot be altered. This has direct regulatory implications: if explanations are meant to support recourse, the current compliance approach may be legally insufficient. Open questions include how to handle probabilistic causal effects on downstream features, how to elicit individual-level actionability constraints at scale, and whether the framework extends cleanly to multi-class or regression settings. The paper also reveals that even responsive features may require non-monotonic or counterintuitive changes (e.g., decreasing income to get approved), suggesting that feature-highlighting explanations alone are insufficient without additional directional and threshold information.
