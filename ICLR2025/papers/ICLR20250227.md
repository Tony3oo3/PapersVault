## Problem

Existing uncertainty decompositions into aleatoric and epistemic components lack formal guarantees about what these quantities mean relative to the real-world data distribution. Standard calibration only ensures total predictive uncertainty is correct, making it impossible to distinguish whether a 50% prediction reflects genuine data ambiguity or model ignorance. The paper asks whether a calibration theory can be built that provides explicit, distribution-free semantics for both components simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250227/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes images (32×32 RGB) paired with k-snapshots — k independent labels per instance drawn from the true conditional distribution (e.g., from k independent annotators or a teacher model). Rather than outputting a single label distribution, the predictor produces a mixture over label distributions (an element of ΔΔY), from which aleatoric uncertainty is the expected entropy of mixture components and epistemic uncertainty is the mutual information between the mixture component and the outcome. The k-snapshot structure is the key unusual input: instead of one label per image, the algorithm receives k independent draws, enabling it to distinguish between genuinely ambiguous instances and instances where the model is uncertain.

## Method

The paper introduces higher-order calibration, requiring that a predictor's output mixture at each point matches the true Bayes mixture over its level set, and proves this guarantees calibrated aleatoric and epistemic estimates with no assumptions on the data distribution. Since the true Bayes mixture is never directly observable, they define k-th order calibration as a tractable relaxation that converges to full higher-order calibration at a 1/√k rate and recovers the first k moments of the Bayes mixture. Two practical algorithms achieve k-th order calibration: (1) training a k-snapshot predictor via multiclass classification over the extended label space of normalized histograms, and (2) a post-hoc procedure that, given a first-order calibrated model, learns the Bayes mixture within each level set using mixture learning. For Brier entropy, k=2 suffices for provable decomposition; for Shannon entropy, k must grow as Θ((1/ε)^{ln 4}).

## Results

On CIFAR-10H (10,000 images with ≥50 human annotations each), the mean true aleatoric uncertainty is 0.17 (STD 1.2) under Shannon entropy. The key quantitative finding is that the aleatoric estimation error E_AU(x) = |AU_H(g:x) − E_{x~[x]}[AU_H^*(x)]| decreases monotonically as the snapshot size k increases, with a significant benefit to going beyond k=2. For Brier entropy, k=2 (second-order calibration) already suffices to provably approximate the true aleatoric uncertainty, while Shannon entropy requires larger k (k ≥ Θ((1/ε)^{ln 4})) for ε-accurate estimation. The method achieves distribution-free guarantees with no assumptions on the data-generating process.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against Johnson et al. (2024), which studies the k=2 (second-order) calibration case; the current work generalizes this to arbitrary k and provides the limiting higher-order calibration framework, showing that k=2 suffices for Brier entropy but larger k is needed for Shannon entropy. In Appendix G.2, the authors evaluate five existing uncertainty estimation methods—Epistemic Neural Networks (Osband et al., 2023), Prior Networks (Malinin & Gales, 2018), Evidential Deep Learning (Sensoy et al., 2018), Distance Awareness (Liu et al., 2020), and Single Network (Van Amersfoort et al., 2020)—in terms of higher-order calibration error. The key distinction is that these baselines lack any formal calibration guarantee: their uncertainty decompositions have no provable real-world semantics, whereas the proposed method guarantees that the aleatoric estimate matches the true average aleatoric uncertainty over the relevant equivalence class. The paper positions higher-order calibration as an evaluation metric applicable to any higher-order predictor (including Bayesian and ensemble models), revealing that existing methods can be miscalibrated in the higher-order sense even when they appear well-calibrated at the first order.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the k=2 second-order calibration framework of Johnson et al. (2024) to arbitrary k and defines the limiting notion of higher-order calibration, creating a natural hierarchy from first-order to full higher-order calibration. It builds on the Bayesian uncertainty decomposition literature (Houlsby et al., 2011; Hofman et al., 2024; Kotelevskii & Panov, 2024) by providing the formal calibration semantics that those works lack, and leverages mixture learning algorithms (Li et al., 2015) for a post-hoc calibration procedure. The key departure from prior work is that while Bayesian and ensemble methods naturally produce higher-order predictors (mixtures), no prior work provides distribution-free guarantees that the resulting uncertainty decomposition has correct real-world semantics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first formal guarantee that a predictor's aleatoric uncertainty estimate matches the true average aleatoric uncertainty over its level set, with no assumptions on the data distribution — a property that Bayesian methods only achieve under well-specified model classes and asymptotic limits. The practical limitation is that achieving k-th order calibration for k>1 fundamentally requires multiple independent labels per instance (k-snapshots), which is not always available (e.g., health outcomes). Open questions include reducing the |Y|^k sample complexity, extending to active learning and OOD detection, and studying the tradeoff between collecting more conditional labels versus more labeled data.
