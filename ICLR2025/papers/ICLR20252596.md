## Problem

Existing medical MLLM benchmarks evaluate general medical knowledge across large, typical-scenario datasets but fail to expose systematic visual failure modes. It remains unclear whether these models can actually distinguish between radiology images that are clearly different to human experts, raising serious reliability concerns for safety-critical healthcare deployment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252596/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each VQA problem presents a single medical radiology image with a two-option multiple-choice question (A or B). Questions are structured as 'confusing pairs': the identical question and options are posed against two different images from the ROCO dataset, with the correct answer differing between them. A model earns a set score only if it answers correctly on both images in the pair, while individual accuracy and a confusion score (fraction of pairs where the model selects the same answer for both images) are reported separately. The benchmark contains 352 questions spanning 9 anatomic categories, and by construction, relying solely on language priors cannot exceed 50% individual accuracy.

## Method

The pipeline discovers confusing image pairs by sampling from ROCO (~80k radiology images) and selecting pairs with high BiomedCLIP embedding similarity (sim_med ≥ 0.9) but low DINOv2 similarity (sim_gen ≤ 0.75), indicating the medical vision encoder compresses visually distinct images into nearly identical representations. GPT-4 is then prompted with the pair's captions to generate a two-option question whose answer differs between the two images, and a radiologist filters and revises each question for correctness, clinical relevance, and medical language precision (quality score ≥ 5 required). The resulting benchmark directly probes multimodal reasoning because the shared question/options eliminate unimodal language shortcuts. The paper evaluates 13 models (3 medical MLLMs, 5 proprietary, 5 open-source general) using four evaluation techniques (prefix-based scoring, multiple-choice prompting, free-form with LLM matching, and greedy decoding), and further identifies four recurring failure patterns (normal vs. pathology, lesion signal characteristics, vascular conditions, and medical devices) through an expert-in-the-loop analysis of model errors.

## Results

On MediConfusion (352 questions, 9 medical categories), all 13 evaluated models achieve set accuracy below or near the 25% random-guessing baseline. The best performer, Gemini 2.0 Flash, reaches only 29.55% set accuracy and 61.93% individual accuracy, while the worst, LLaVA-Med, scores 1.14% set accuracy and 49.72% individual accuracy. Confusion scores are extremely high across all models (75–100%), indicating that models overwhelmingly select the same answer for both images in a confusing pair. Even proprietary models with access to the training data (PMC-OA) fail to reliably distinguish the image pairs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All 13 models perform at or below random guessing (25%) on set accuracy, with no model achieving a statistically reliable advantage. Among proprietary models, Gemini 2.0 Flash leads with 29.55% set accuracy (barely 4.55 points above random), followed by o1 (21.59%) and GPT-4o (18.75%), while Claude 3 Opus trails at 8.52%. Among open-source general-domain MLLMs, DeepSeek-VL2 (16.48%) and InstructBLIP (12.50%) outperform LLaVA (9.09%), Molmo (9.66%), and BLIP-2 (6.82%). The three medical-specific MLLMs—LLaVA-Med (1.14%), Med-Flamingo (4.55%), and RadFM (5.68%)—perform worst overall, despite being trained on the very image-caption pairs (PMC-OA) used to construct MediConfusion. Notably, RadFM, which does not use a CLIP-style encoder, still shows 97.54% confusion, suggesting the vulnerability is not solely rooted in contrastive pretraining. Individual accuracy hovers around 50% (random) for nearly all models, with only Gemini 2.0 Flash (61.93%) and o1 (57.95%) showing a modest edge, indicating that models can occasionally answer one image correctly but fail to consistently differentiate the pair.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the 'confusing pairs' methodology from Tong et al. (2024b), who identified CLIP-blind image pairs in the general domain, to the medical setting by substituting BiomedCLIP (a medical-domain contrastive encoder) for CLIP and DINOv2 for low-level visual similarity. It departs from prior medical VQA benchmarks (VQA-Rad, SLAKE, OmniMedVQA, Asclepius, RadBench) which evaluate breadth of medical knowledge across large, typical-scenario datasets, by instead constructing a small, targeted benchmark that by design cannot be solved with language priors alone. The closest work in spirit is RadVUQA (Nan et al., 2024), which also probes fundamental visual deficiencies of medical MLLMs, but MediConfusion goes further by explicitly targeting ambiguities in the vision encoder's embedding space and using radiologist-in-the-loop curation to ensure clinical validity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all 13 evaluated models—including flagship proprietary systems like GPT-4o, o1, and Gemini 2.0 Flash—achieve set accuracy below random guessing, with confusion scores often exceeding 90%, raises serious concerns about deploying medical MLLMs in safety-critical clinical settings. The fine-tuning experiment, where LLaVA-Med cannot reach 100% training accuracy even after 1000 epochs, suggests the bottleneck is fundamental to the vision encoder rather than a learnable knowledge gap, pointing toward the need for new medical vision encoders. Open questions include whether the Gemini models' lower confusion but still-poor accuracy reflects a separate medical-knowledge deficit, whether richer visual features (e.g., DINOv2 concatenation) can close the gap, and how to design training objectives that prevent the shortcut learning that produces ambiguous embeddings.
