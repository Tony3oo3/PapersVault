## Problem

While prior work has traced Western bias in VLMs to the distribution of images used during multimodal fusion, the role of the text-only pre-training language mix and the prompting language on cultural bias in image understanding remained unexplored. This paper asks: does using a culturally closer language—either during LLM pre-training or at inference—reduce Western bias in both objective and subjective visual tasks, and under what conditions is this reduction effective?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252001/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The VLMs consume RGB images paired with semantically equivalent text prompts in English or Chinese, producing task-specific outputs: open-ended object descriptions (scored by Prometheus-2-8x7B as judge), multiple-choice VQA answers (extracted via Mistral-7B), or emotion labels for artworks. The central evaluation metric is the Western bias ratio (Western split performance ÷ East Asian split performance), computed across culturally stratified subsets of Dollar Street, A-OKVQA/CVQA, and ArtELingo that span both narrow (English/Chinese-speaking) and broader regional country groupings.

## Method

The paper employs a two-step controlled design: Step 1 evaluates six off-the-shelf VLMs (BLIP-2, LLaVA-1.5, mBLIP, Qwen-VL) in English and Chinese on three culturally diverse tasks; Step 2 trains six mLLaVA variants by fusing CLIP with either Llama2 (≈90% English pre-training) or Baichuan2 (bilingual English/Chinese pre-training), each paired with monolingual or bilingual fusion corpora, using LoRA fine-tuning in two phases (multimodal pre-training + visual instruction tuning). Three axes are isolated: (A) base LLM pre-training language mix, (B) prompting language, and (C) fusion corpus language composition. The key finding is that a more balanced pre-training language mix reduces Western bias even when prompting in English, and that prompting in a culturally closer language yields much larger bias reductions only when that language was well-represented during pre-training. Mechanistic probing via the logit lens confirms that Llama2-based VLMs decode hidden states exclusively to English tokens even when generating Chinese, whereas Baichuan2-based VLMs decode to both languages and align more closely with Chinese annotator rationales.

## Results

Across all three tasks (object identification, VQA, art emotion classification), 90%+ of off-the-shelf VLMs exhibit a Western bias (Western accuracy / East Asian accuracy > 1), with bias values ranging from ~1.0 to ~2.0 depending on task and model. Only 15 out of 30 model-task evaluations show any bias reduction when prompting in Chinese instead of English. In the controlled mLLaVA experiment, replacing the English-only Llama2 base with the bilingual Baichuan2 base reduces Western bias significantly: on ArtELingo's opposite-valence subset, bias drops from 3.45→2.71 (English prompt) and 2.72→1.23 (Chinese prompt); on VQA en/zh, bias drops from 1.50→1.17 (Chinese prompt).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the four off-the-shelf VLM families compared, mBLIP (Bloomz-7B base) consistently shows the lowest Western bias, with bias values closest to 1.0 across all tasks (e.g., 0.980 on ArtELingo opposite-valence in English), suggesting its multilingual pre-training corpus provides more balanced cultural associations. Qwen-VL (bilingual English/Chinese) is the only model that reliably reduces bias when prompted in Chinese (e.g., DS en/zh: 1.151→0.953; VQA en/zh: 1.227→1.069), while LLaVA-1.5 variants often show *increased* bias under Chinese prompting (e.g., VQA en/zh: 1.013→1.324 for LLaVA-1.5-7B). BLIP-2 (Flan-T5-XXL), being English-only, cannot be evaluated in Chinese and shows moderate Western bias (1.09–1.98) depending on task. The paper's own controlled mLLaVA experiments confirm that the Baichuan2-based variants (bilingual pre-training) achieve the largest bias reductions over Llama2-based variants, particularly on subjective tasks where the gap is most pronounced (ArtELingo opposite-valence: 3.45→1.23 with Chinese prompting).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the LLaVA training recipe (fusing CLIP with an LLM via multimodal instruction tuning) but shifts the focus from model architecture to the language composition of the base LLM's pre-training corpus. Prior work on Western bias in VLMs traced it to the image distribution during multimodal fusion (object detection, scene understanding), while text-only LLM studies found Western bias in encoded knowledge and values. This paper departs from both by isolating the effect of pre-training language mix through controlled comparison of Llama2 (mostly English) and Baichuan2 (bilingual English/Chinese), and by extending the analysis to subjective tasks (art emotion) where cultural perspective matters, not just objective recognition. The logit lens probing connects to Wendler et al.'s finding that Llama2 'works in English' internally, now extended to the multimodal setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is clear: building equitable VLMs requires multilingual pre-training at the LLM level, not just multilingual fusion corpora or multilingual prompting. The finding that a bilingual fusion corpus cannot substitute for a multilingual base LLM is a negative result that redirects engineering effort earlier in the pipeline. Key limitations include the restriction to English/Chinese (due to lack of comparable LLMs for other East Asian languages) and the simplification of equating country of image/annotation with culture. Open questions include whether these findings generalize to lower-resourced languages, how the image distribution effect interacts with the language effect, and whether the bias floor measured here (East Asia, the most resourced non-Western culture) holds for other regions.
