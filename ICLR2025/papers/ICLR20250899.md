## Problem

Existing neurosymbolic approaches for logical reasoning (e.g., Logic-LM) rely on a single-shot LLM translation of natural language into first-order logic, which fails on complex logical structures. The paper identifies that LLMs cannot faithfully capture complex logical semantics hidden in natural language, producing syntactically valid but semantically incorrect FOL formulas that undermine the soundness of the downstream SAT solver.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250899/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a natural language logical reasoning problem (a set of premise sentences plus a query) and a theory declaration specifying sorts, function symbols, and predicate symbols. It produces a verified FOL formula for each sentence individually, which are then assembled into a SAT problem solved by Z3 to yield a satisfiability determination (true/false/unknown) for the query. Unlike prior work that translates the entire problem in one LLM call, CLOVER operates sentence-by-sentence with multiple sampled translations per sentence, and the few-shot exemplar sets for each sub-step (parsing, accumulation, translation, disproving) are manually curated from prior neurosymbolic works.

## Method

CLOVER introduces a three-stage compositional pipeline: (1) Logical Dependency Parsing decomposes each sentence into logic units, couplers, and dependents that mirror FOL predicate-argument structure; (2) Component Accumulation sequentially builds up subsentences by integrating dependents into units according to dependency rules, and (3) Sequential Translation translates each accumulated subsentence to FOL in order, so the LLM only handles one logical increment at a time. To handle multiple valid parses and translations, CLOVER introduces two SAT-based verification algorithms: Logical Consistency selects the most frequent group of T-equivalent formulas, and Disproving by Counter-Interpretation uses the SAT solver to find a model where two candidate formulas disagree, then prompts the LLM to check whether that model satisfies the original sentence, iteratively eliminating incorrect formulas. The key insight is that decomposing translation along logical dependencies keeps each LLM step within its reliable complexity range, while the deterministic SAT solver provides a rigorous semantic comparison that LLM-based verification cannot match.

## Results

Using GPT-4o as the backbone LLM, CLOVER achieves state-of-the-art accuracy across all seven logical reasoning benchmarks, with the largest gains on the hardest tasks: 62.8% on AR-LSAT (+20.4 over Logic-LM) and 75.4% on ZebraLogic (+30.0 over Logic-LM). On the remaining benchmarks, CLOVER scores 83.5% (Puzzle), 89.9% (Symbol), 99.3% (Deduction), 78.8% (FOLIO), and 96.7% (ProofWriter), consistently outperforming all baselines. CLOVER also improves execution rate and execution accuracy over Logic-LM on every task, indicating both better syntactic correctness and better semantic fidelity of the generated first-order logic formulas.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLOVER is compared against three baselines: CoT prompting, SymbCoT (symbolic chain-of-thought), and Logic-LM (the prior state-of-the-art neurosymbolic approach). Against Logic-LM, CLOVER wins on all seven tasks, with the most dramatic improvements on the hardest benchmarks: +20.4 points on AR-LSAT (62.8 vs. 42.4) and +30.0 points on ZebraLogic (75.4 vs. 45.4). Against SymbCoT, CLOVER leads by a wide margin on every task, most notably +74.6 on ZebraLogic and +34.3 on Symbol. Against standard CoT prompting, CLOVER also dominates across the board, with gains of +26.0 on AR-LSAT and +75.0 on ZebraLogic. The paper notes that neurosymbolic approaches (CLOVER and Logic-LM) are far more robust to increasing reasoning length than CoT-based LLMs, with CLOVER showing only a 12.5% performance drop between the shortest and longest reasoning sequences on ZebraLogic.

## Relation to Prior Work

CLOVER extends the neurosymbolic logical reasoning pipeline established by Logic-LM (Pan et al., 2023), SATLM (Ye et al., 2024), and LINC (Olausson et al., 2023), where an LLM translates natural language into first-order logic and a symbolic solver (Z3) handles the reasoning. The key departure is replacing the single-shot translation with a compositional pipeline: a newly defined logical dependency parsing decomposes sentences into atomic subsentences and dependents, followed by sequential translation that accumulates components in dependency order. The verification stage draws on SAT-based equivalence checking rather than the LLM-based self-refinement used in prior work (Chen et al., 2024; Madaan et al., 2024; Ni et al., 2023), and the compositional decomposition is conceptually related to compositional semantic parsing (Drozdov et al., 2022) but adapted to preserve first-order logic semantics rather than syntactic structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clear diagnostic that LLMs exhibit a 'complexity cliff' in first-order logic translation—performing well on simple formulas but degrading sharply on complex ones—which explains a fundamental bottleneck in neurosymbolic reasoning systems. The compositional approach and SAT-based verification close much of this gap, with the largest gains at the highest complexity levels, and the finding that LLM-based verification underperforms even random selection highlights the value of deterministic solvers for semantic checking. Limitations include a roughly 4× increase in inference cost over Logic-LM, residual preprocessing errors (78.6% of remaining errors), and evaluation confined to seven benchmarks with relatively small test sets. Open questions include whether logical dependency parsing generalizes to domains with richer quantifier nesting or higher-order logic, and whether the approach can be made more efficient through selective verification.
