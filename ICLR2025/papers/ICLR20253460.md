## Problem

Existing uncertainty estimation methods for LLMs are designed for fact-based tasks (QA, summarization) and have not been systematically evaluated for instruction-following, where the source of uncertainty is adherence to constraints rather than factual correctness. Moreover, existing instruction-following benchmarks like IFEval entangle multiple confounding factors—length bias, task execution quality, and varying error severity across models—making it impossible to isolate and compare methods' true ability to estimate instruction-following uncertainty.

## Input / Output

The evaluation pipeline takes a task prompt paired with a verifiable instruction (e.g., 'include keywords X and Y') and a model-generated response, then produces a scalar uncertainty score per method. Ground truth labels are binary (correct/incorrect instruction-following) determined by deterministic IFEval verification programs, not LLM judges. The probing method additionally consumes internal hidden-state representations from specific transformer layers (e.g., layers 16/32/40 for LLaMA-2-13B), while the Controlled benchmark version uses GPT-4-generated responses with matched token lengths to eliminate length as a confound.

## Method

The paper introduces a two-version benchmark (Controlled and Realistic) built on IFEval's 25 verifiable instruction types. The Controlled version uses GPT-4 to generate correct, completely incorrect, and subtly off-target responses with matched token lengths, then filters for high task quality (>8/9) to disentangle instruction-following uncertainty from task execution quality; it is split into Controlled-Easy (correct vs. clearly wrong) and Controlled-Hard (correct vs. subtle errors). Seven uncertainty methods are evaluated: verbalized confidence (0–9 self-score), normalized p(true) and p(true) (binary-choice token probabilities), perplexity, sequence probability, mean token entropy, and a linear probe trained with AdamW (lr=0.001, 1000 epochs, 70/30 split) on internal representations from early, middle, and late layers. All methods are scored by AUROC against deterministic ground-truth labels, enabling direct comparison across four LLMs (LLaMA-2-7B/13B, Mistral-7B, Phi-3-mini) under length-neutral and length-biased conditions.

## Results

On the IFEval benchmark, all uncertainty estimation methods hover around chance-level AUROC (0.43–0.53), indicating LLMs cannot reliably detect their own instruction-following errors. On the authors' Controlled-Easy benchmark, linear probing on internal states achieves the best AUROC (0.72–0.79 across models), followed by verbalized confidence (0.64–0.71) and normalized p(true) (0.54–0.72). In the more challenging Controlled-Hard setting (subtle errors), all methods degrade substantially, with Probe dropping below 0.60 and no method reliably exceeding chance. In the Realistic setting, sequence probability benefits from length bias (0.47–0.53), while normalized p(true) (0.51–0.56) and Probe (0.61–0.72) remain the most robust.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Linear probing on internal states (inspired by Liu et al., 2024) consistently outperforms all other methods in Controlled-Easy (0.72–0.79 AUROC) and Realistic (0.61–0.72) settings, beating verbalized confidence (Lin et al., 2022; Xiong et al., 2023; Tian et al., 2023) by 0.05–0.15 AUROC. In Controlled-Easy, verbalized confidence and normalized p(true) (Kadavath et al., 2022) clearly outperform logit-based methods—perplexity (Jelinek et al., 1977) and sequence probability/entropy (Fomicheva et al., 2020)—by 0.05–0.20 AUROC, suggesting self-evaluation is more informative than raw token probabilities for distinguishing clear instruction violations. In the Realistic setting, sequence probability (Fomicheva et al., 2020) benefits from length bias in naturally generated responses, but normalized p(true) (Kadavath et al., 2022) remains the strongest non-probe method across all four models. In Controlled-Hard (subtle errors), all baselines degrade: Probe drops below 0.60, and the ranking between logit-based and self-evaluation methods becomes model-dependent (perplexity wins for LLaMA2, while N-p(t) wins for Mistral and Phi-3). Notably, smaller models (Mistral-7B, Phi-3-mini) outperform the larger LLaMA2-13B in verbalized confidence and N-p(t), suggesting architecture and tuning matter more than scale for uncertainty calibration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the established toolkit of LLM uncertainty estimation methods (verbalized confidence, perplexity, sequence probability, normalized p(true), token entropy, and linear probing on internal states) for a new task context: instruction-following rather than factual correctness. It builds on the IFEval benchmark for verifiable instruction-following evaluation and on the probing approach of Liu et al. (2024) for internal-state uncertainty, but departs from prior work by arguing that uncertainty in instruction adherence is a fundamentally different signal from factual uncertainty (e.g., semantic entropy would incorrectly flag two valid but semantically different instruction-compliant responses as uncertain). The paper's main methodological contribution is a controlled evaluation framework that disentangles length bias, task-quality entanglement, and varying error severity—confounds that prior instruction-following benchmarks leave uncontrolled.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all six uncertainty methods hover near chance level (AUROC 0.43–0.53) on IFEval, and that even internal-state probes degrade sharply on subtle instruction violations, is a sobering negative result for anyone deploying LLMs as safety-critical agents that must self-monitor instruction compliance. The controlled benchmark (Controlled-Easy/Hard + Realistic) provides a reusable evaluation protocol that future methods can be tested against, and the observation that smaller models (Mistral-7B, Phi-3-mini) outperform larger ones in verbalized confidence suggests that calibration is not a simple function of scale. Open questions include why internal representations that encode instruction-following knowledge cannot be reliably surfaced through the model's own outputs, and whether fine-tuning or architectural changes can close the gap on subtle-error detection.
