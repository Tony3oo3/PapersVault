## Problem

Theoretical analysis predicts a trade-off between GNN expressivity and generalization—highly expressive models should overfit—yet empirical evidence consistently shows expressive GNNs generalizing well. This contradiction remains unexplained because existing generalization bounds (VC-dimension, PAC-Bayesian) either fail to capture the structured nature of graph data or become vacuous for deeper architectures, leaving no principled framework to understand when and why added expressivity helps or hurts generalization.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a set of labeled graph samples S, a target graph encoder φ (e.g., an MPNN or F-MPNN), and a bounding graph encoder λ (e.g., 1-WL or F-WL) that upper-bounds φ in distinguishing power. It produces a high-probability upper bound on the generalization gap R_μ(ψ∘φ) − R̂_{γ,S}(ψ∘φ), computed via sampled 1-Wasserstein distances between pushforward distributions of λ on class-conditional subsets, plus an empirical Lip(f) estimate from the training set. The bound is architecture-agnostic: any pair of comparable encoders can be plugged in, and the practical sample-based variant (Theorem D.2) requires only O((m_c/2n)³) computation via the Hungarian algorithm.

## Method

The paper derives a k-variance margin-based generalization bound by extending Chuang et al.'s optimal-transport framework to graph encoders. The key mechanism is Lemma 4.2: if λ bounds φ in distinguishing power, then φ = f∘λ for some function f, and Proposition 4.4 shows W₁(φ_#(ν), φ_#(ν')) ≤ Lip(f)·W₁(λ_#(ν), λ_#(ν')), so the generalization gap of φ is controlled by the Wasserstein variance of the *simpler* bounding encoder λ scaled by Lip(f) = B/S. Under a margin condition, Proposition 5.2 further reveals a trade-off: the bound is governed by the ratio of intra-class concentration (W₁ between random class-conditional subsets) to inter-class separation (W₁ between full class distributions), explaining why added expressivity can either tighten or loosen the bound depending on whether it improves concentration or separation. Experiments on five benchmark datasets validate that the bound tracks empirical loss gaps across MPNN depths and F-MPNN homomorphism patterns, and that l₁-normalization reduces Lip(f) to improve generalization.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative contribution is a k-variance margin-based generalization bound that tracks empirical loss gaps across MPNN depths (1–6 layers) on five graph classification datasets. On ENZYMES, the loss gap ranges from 0.235 to 0.264 across depths, and the proposed bound varies correspondingly (6.151–7.926), while the VC bound remains essentially constant at ~1.302 and the PAC-Bayesian bound explodes from 3.48 to 8931.00. On PROTEINS, the proposed bound (1.308–2.193) stays within a factor of ~70 of the loss gap (0.020–0.032), whereas the PAC bound reaches 135,762.52 at 6 layers. The bound also captures the effect of homomorphism-pattern expressivity: on PROTEINS, adding C₃–C₆ patterns reduces the bound (improving generalization), while on ENZYMES it increases the bound (worsening generalization), matching the empirical loss gaps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its k-variance margin-based bound against two existing generalization bounds: the VC-dimension bound from Morris et al. (2023) and the PAC-Bayesian bound from Ju et al. (2023). Against the VC bound, the proposed method clearly wins in informativeness: the VC bound is essentially constant (~1.302) across all depths and datasets, failing to capture any variation in the empirical loss gap, whereas the proposed bound varies meaningfully (e.g., 7.926 → 6.151 on ENZYMES from 1 to 6 layers) and correlates positively with the loss gap. Against the PAC-Bayesian bound, the proposed method wins decisively on tightness: the PAC bound grows exponentially with depth (reaching 8,931 on ENZYMES and 135,762 on PROTEINS at 6 layers), rendering it vacuous, while the proposed bound remains in the range of 1.3–7.9. In terms of correlation with empirical gaps (Table 4 in the appendix), the proposed bound shows positive correlations (0.41–0.93) across F-MPNN variants on SIDER and BACE, while the VC and PAC bounds show predominantly negative correlations, further confirming the proposed bound's superior predictive power.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the margin-based generalization framework of Chuang et al. (2021), which used k-variance and Wasserstein distance to bound classifier generalization, to the graph setting by introducing a 'bounding encoder' mechanism. The key conceptual departure from prior GNN generalization work (VC-dimension bounds of Morris et al. 2023, PAC-Bayesian bounds of Ju et al. 2023, Rademacher complexity of Garg et al. 2020) is that the bound is expressed in terms of any more expressive graph encoder (e.g., 1-WL, F-WL, homomorphism counts) that upper-bounds the model under study, rather than depending on architecture-specific quantities like layer count or parameter norms. This connects the expressivity hierarchy of GNNs (MPNN ≡ 1-WL, F-MPNN ≡ F-WL) directly to generalization guarantees via Lipschitz continuity of the connecting function between encoders.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled explanation for the empirically observed phenomenon that more expressive GNNs can generalize better, by decomposing the effect into intra-class concentration (Wasserstein variance within a class) and inter-class separation (Wasserstein distance between classes). The case study showing that C4-homomorphism counts improve generalization on PROTEINS while K4-homomorphism counts worsen it on ENZYMES illustrates that expressivity is not monotonically beneficial. However, the empirical validation is limited to small benchmark datasets (TU collection, molecular datasets with <1000 graphs), and the theoretical framework requires assumptions (B-bounded, S-separating encoders) whose practical verification is left to empirical estimation. Scaling this analysis to large-scale graph learning tasks and more complex architectures remains an open question.
