## Problem

Existing incremental causal effect literature is limited to binary treatments at discrete time points, with no framework for continuous time-to-treatment initialization (e.g., when to start a medication or vaccination). This gap leaves common preventive-medicine and chronic-disease questions—'what if we shift the intensity of treatment initiation?'—unanswerable by standard causal tools, which additionally require a positivity assumption that is frequently violated in practice.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a random sample of (Y, T∧τ, Δ, L) where Y is a continuous outcome measured at a fixed follow-up time τ, T is the (right-censored) time to treatment initialization, Δ indicates whether treatment occurred before τ, and L is a vector of baseline covariates. The analyst specifies a hazard-ratio function θ(t,l)>0 (a constant for proportional-hazards interventions, or a covariate- and time-dependent function for targeted intensification). The output is the incremental causal effect ψ(θ)=E[Y_{T(θ)}] together with a pointwise 95% Wald confidence interval, where T(θ) is drawn from the shifted hazard θ(t,l)·λ(t|l).

## Method

The paper defines the incremental causal effect as the expected outcome under a 'hazard-preserving' stochastic intervention that multiplies the time-to-treatment hazard by θ(t,l), and proves that ψ(θ) is identifiable from observed data under only consistency and sequential randomization—no positivity is needed. The identification takes the form of an inverse-probability-weighted expectation ψ(θ)=E{θ(T,L)^Δ exp(−∫₀^{T∧τ}(θ(t,L)−1)dΛ(t|L))·Y}, where the weights are Radon-Nikodym derivatives between the intervention and observed distributions. For estimation, a plug-in IPW estimator replaces the true cumulative hazard Λ(t|L) with any uniformly consistent estimate (e.g., from a Cox model), and root-n asymptotic normality is established under an asymptotic-linearity condition on Λ̂. Variance is estimated via multiplier bootstrap, yielding Wald-type confidence intervals. The framework is validated through Monte Carlo simulations across multiple θ specifications and sample sizes, and applied to a rheumatoid arthritis cohort to quantify how scaling the methotrexate initiation hazard affects average tender-joint counts at one year.

## Results

In simulations (n=5000, R=1000), the IPW estimator achieves near-negligible bias (|bias| ≤ 0.109 × 10⁻²) and 95% Wald confidence interval coverage between 94.3% and 95.7% across all tested hazard ratio functions θ ∈ {1/3, 1/2.5, 1/2, 1/1.5, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3}. In the rheumatoid arthritis application (n=1010), doubling the MTX initiation hazard (θ=2) reduces the average number of tender joints at 12 months from 1.90 to 1.87 (95% CI: 1.81–1.93, a 1.23% decrease), while a five-fold increase (θ=5) reduces it to 1.79 (95% CI: 1.71–1.88, a 5.42% decrease). Conversely, halving the hazard (θ=0.5) increases tender joints to 1.91 (95% CI: 1.86–1.97).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper introduces a new estimand (incremental causal effect for time-to-treatment) and does not benchmark against competing estimators, so there are no direct baseline comparisons. The primary validation is internal: Monte Carlo simulations confirm the theoretical guarantees (consistency, asymptotic normality, correct coverage) across sample sizes n ∈ {200, 1000, 5000} and multiple θ specifications, including a more complex setting with three covariates and θ(t,l)=exp(β′l) (Appendix C, Table 3, where coverage remains 93.9–94.2% at n=5000). The real-data application is validated against the independent finding of Tchetgen Tchetgen et al. (2024), who used a marginal structural linear model with IPW least squares and proximal recursive least-squares to establish a protective effect of methotrexate on disease progression; the present paper's incremental effect curve (monotonically decreasing tender joints as θ increases from 0.2 to 5) is directionally consistent with that prior result. The key methodological advantage over traditional causal estimands (ATE, dynamic treatment regime effects) is the elimination of the positivity assumption, which the authors argue is often violated in practice (e.g., mandatory treatment programs, contraindicated vaccines).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Kennedy's (2019) incremental propensity score intervention—originally defined for binary treatments at discrete time points—to the continuous-time setting of time-to-treatment initialization, where the intervention shifts the hazard function by a multiplicative factor θ(t,l). The identification proof leverages Radon-Nikodym derivatives between the targeted intervention distribution and the observed data distribution, a technique analogous to classical IPW but applied to survival-type distributions. It also connects to the broader modified-treatment-policy literature (Haneuse & Rotnitzky, 2013; Diaz & van der Laan, 2012) by intervening on the natural treatment distribution rather than prescribing a fixed treatment value, but differs in targeting the continuous hazard rather than a discrete treatment assignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is that incremental causal effects for time-to-treatment require only sequential randomization and consistency, completely bypassing the positivity assumption that often renders traditional causal estimands (ATE, dynamic treatment regime effects) unidentifiable in real-world data. This opens up causal questions about treatment timing that were previously vetoed by degenerate treatment probabilities. Open questions include achieving semiparametric efficiency via targeted estimation, extending to time-varying confounders and survival outcomes, and developing conditional incremental effects. The rheumatoid arthritis application demonstrates modest but directionally consistent effects (e.g., 1.23% reduction in tender joints when MTX hazard doubles), suggesting the framework is practically usable but the effects may be small in magnitude.
