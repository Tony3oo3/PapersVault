## Problem

Existing deepfake audio detectors achieve high accuracy on narrow public benchmarks but fail dramatically under diverse real-world conditions, including unseen synthesis sources, natural corruptions, and adversarial perturbations. The paper identifies that state-of-the-art models (e.g., RawNet3) disproportionately rely on high-frequency features that are imperceptible to humans and trivially manipulable by attackers, making them inherently fragile. Standard adversarial training on raw waveforms worsens clean-data accuracy without providing meaningful robustness gains.

## Input / Output

The detector consumes raw audio waveforms (no pre-extracted features) and outputs a binary real/fake classification score. The backbone RawNet3 internally applies parameterized filterbanks to convert the waveform into a time-frequency representation before passing it through three residual backbone blocks. The test set spans 14 distinct TTS/VC synthesis models plus samples scraped from YouTube and X, with both clean and noisy conditions, and the evaluation protocol additionally applies 24 types of audio corruptions and PGD-based adversarial perturbations in both time and frequency domains.

## Method

The core contribution is F-SAT (Frequency-Selective Adversarial Training), which modifies the standard PGD adversarial training loop to restrict perturbations to the magnitude component of the STFT within a narrow high-frequency band (4–8 kHz), leaving low-frequency features untouched. Concretely, at each PGD step the gradient is masked by a diagonal matrix D that zeros out all frequency bins outside [f_l, f_u], so the update only perturbs the targeted band; the perturbed spectrogram is then reconstructed via ISTFT and fed back into the model. The total loss is a weighted sum of clean cross-entropy and robust (attacked) cross-entropy, with a small γ (0.1) to preserve clean accuracy. Complemented by an audio-adapted RandAugment (N=1, p=0.9) that randomly applies one of 24 corruption types during training, the full pipeline (RawNet3 + RandAug + F-SAT) achieves 97.4% F1 on clean data while maintaining 92.4% F1 under frequency-domain PGD attacks, compared to 20.2% for the untrained baseline.

## Results

On the DeepFakeVox-HQ test set, the proposed method (RawNet3 + RandAug + F-SAT) achieves an F1 score of 0.974 (Real Acc 0.975, Fake Acc 0.964), substantially outperforming the strongest baseline RawNet3 (F1 0.861). On ASVspoof2019, the method reaches F1 0.966 (Real Acc 0.950, Fake Acc 0.982) versus RawNet3's F1 0.939. Under adversarial attacks, F-SAT improves F1 from 0.134 (time-domain) and 0.202 (frequency-domain) for untrained RawNet3 to 0.910 and 0.924 respectively, while maintaining near-clean accuracy on unattacked samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's method decisively outperforms all four detection baselines on the DeepFakeVox-HQ test set, where the gap is largest: F1 of 0.974 vs. RawNet3 (0.861), RawGAT-ST (0.810), RawNet2 (0.754), and TE-ResNet (0.629). On the more homogeneous ASVspoof2019 benchmark, the advantage narrows to F1 0.966 vs. RawNet3 (0.939) and RawNet2 (0.957), though it still leads all baselines. For adversarial robustness, F-SAT is compared against three defense strategies applied to RawNet3: MAD smoothing (Olivier et al.), Gaussian smoothing (Cohen et al.), and standard PGD adversarial training (Madry et al.). F-SAT outperforms all three on both time-domain and frequency-domain attacks, achieving F1 0.910 (time) and 0.924 (frequency) where standard adversarial training drops to F1 0.211 and 0.207 respectively. The paper also notes that standard time-domain adversarial training actually degrades clean-data accuracy (F1 drops from 0.968 to 0.891), whereas F-SAT preserves clean accuracy at 0.974 while adding robustness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the RawNet3 architecture (Jung et al., 2022) for raw-waveform deepfake detection and adapts two established robustness techniques—adversarial training (Madry et al., 2017) and RandAugment (Cubuk et al., 2020)—to the audio domain. Its key departure is the observation that standard adversarial training on raw waveforms actually degrades clean-data accuracy, motivating the frequency-selective variant F-SAT that restricts perturbations to the 4–8 kHz magnitude band. The paper also positions itself against prior deepfake datasets (ASVspoof, In-The-Wild, WavFake) by arguing they are too small, homogeneous, and outdated, and introduces DeepFakeVoxHQ (1.3 M samples, 14 sources) as a more realistic training and test resource.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 1.3 M-sample DeepFakeVoxHQ dataset is the largest public deepfake-audio benchmark to date and includes samples from the top commercial TTS/VC systems plus social-media recordings, making it a practical resource for the field. The finding that state-of-the-art detectors rely on high-frequency artifacts imperceptible to humans (with a sharp accuracy cliff above ~4–6 kHz) reframes the robustness problem: a simple high-pass filter or targeted frequency perturbation can neutralize a detector, which has direct implications for the security of voice-based authentication and fraud detection. Open questions include whether F-SAT generalizes beyond RawNet3 to spectrogram-based or transformer-based detectors, whether the 4–8 kHz sweet spot holds for non-English speech or lower sample rates, and how the approach scales as TTS models continue to reduce high-frequency artifacts.
