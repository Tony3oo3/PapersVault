## Problem

Existing LLM safeguards such as refusal training and machine unlearning are trivially defeated by a handful of fine-tuning steps on open-weight models, where adversaries have full access to model weights. No prior method has demonstrated substantial robustness against tampering attacks (direct weight modifications) for autoregressive LLMs, leaving open-weight models vulnerable to malicious repurposing and exposing developers to liability under reasonable-care standards.

## Input / Output

The method takes a base LLM (Llama-3-8B-Instruct) with an initial safeguard already applied, a set of train-time adversaries (64-step SFT attacks varying optimizer, learning rate, and dataset), a safety proxy dataset (forget-set text for weaponization knowledge or DPO refusal pairs), and a capabilities proxy dataset (retain-set from MMLU/Pile/Magpie-Align). It produces safeguarded LLM weights that maintain low safety-metric values (e.g., WMDP forget accuracy near random chance, or low jailbreak ASR) even after 500+ steps of adversarial fine-tuning, while preserving general capabilities measured by MMLU or MT-Bench. The unusual aspect is that the training loop internally simulates full fine-tuning trajectories as part of the gradient computation, making the 'input' effectively a distribution of adversarial optimization paths rather than static data.

## Method

TAR (Tampering Attack Resistance) is a two-phase adversarial training procedure inspired by meta-learning. Phase 1 applies an initial safeguard (Random Mapping for knowledge restriction or RLHF/DPO for refusal) to establish a low pre-attack safety metric. Phase 2 performs outer-loop optimization where, at each step, K sampled fine-tuning attacks are simulated on the current weights, and a tamper-resistance loss is computed on the post-attack parameters: for knowledge restriction, this is a negative entropy loss that flattens the adversary's cross-entropy trajectory at a high value (rather than merely raising its intercept, as prior meta-learning approaches do); for refusal, it is a DPO loss encouraging continued preference for refusal completions. A retain loss (language-modeling cross-entropy plus L2 distance on residual-stream hidden states) simultaneously preserves capabilities. Backpropagation through the attack is approximated via a straight-through estimator following first-order MAML, and an efficiency trick reuses K parameter coordinates along a single fine-tuning trajectory to obtain K non-independent attack samples per outer step.

## Results

On WMDP weaponization knowledge restriction (Llama-3-8B-Instruct), TAR achieves post-attack forget accuracies of 35.2% (Biosecurity), 27.1% (Chemical Security), and 28.6% (Cybersecurity) averaged across 26 fine-tuning adversaries, compared to 61.3–66.0% for the strongest baselines (LLMU, RMU) and 46.4–70.5% for No Defense. TAR preserves retain MMLU accuracy at 54.7–60.7%, a ~10.6% average drop from the unsafeguarded model. On HarmBench harmful request refusal, TAR achieves 63.9% average post-attack ASR (lower is better) versus 72.5–84.8% for all baselines, while maintaining 6.3 MT-Bench conversational quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TAR decisively outperforms all five baselines on post-attack tamper-resistance. In weaponization knowledge restriction, LLMU (Yao et al., 2023) and RMU (Li et al., 2024) achieve the best pre-attack forget accuracy among baselines (~28–31%) but degrade to 41–66% post-attack, while TAR maintains 27–35% post-attack across all three domains. In harmful request refusal, TAR achieves 63.9% post-attack ASR, beating Refusal-Trained Llama-3-8B (72.5%), R2D2 (Mazeika et al., 2024; 78.3%), RepNoise (Rosati et al., 2024b; 74.5%), and Representation Rerouting (Zou et al., 2024; 84.8%). The trade-off is a ~10.6% average drop in retain MMLU accuracy for weaponization and a 1.8-point MT-Bench drop for refusal, though the refusal TAR model still exceeds the MT-Bench scores of fairly capable open-weight models. Notably, TAR's advantage is most pronounced under the strongest adversaries (500-step full fine-tuning with held-out data), where baselines recover to near No-Defense levels while TAR stays near random chance.

## Relation to Prior Work

TAR extends the meta-learning-based robust unlearning framework of Henderson et al. (2023, 'Self-destructing models') from bidirectional BERT classifiers to autoregressive LLMs, but with a critical conceptual departure: where prior meta-learning seeks initializations close to optimality across test distributions, TAR seeks initializations far from optimality on adversaries' fine-tuning trajectories. The method also departs from existing LLM unlearning approaches (LLMU, RMU, circuit breaking) by explicitly training against weight-tampering adversaries rather than only optimizing pre-attack safety metrics, and from concurrent refusal-robustness work (Rosati et al., Huang et al.) by using a broader adversarial training loop with a purpose-designed tamper-resistance loss (entropy for knowledge restriction, DPO for refusal) rather than simple perturbation or regularization objectives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that strong tamper-resistance for open-weight LLMs is achievable, addressing a problem the authors note has been considered 'very challenging and by some intractable.' The practical stakes are high: under tort law, model developers owe a duty of reasonable care, and trivially removable safeguards expose them to liability. Key limitations include a ~10% retain-accuracy trade-off, sensitivity to PEFT and out-of-distribution learning-rate attacks, evaluation only on an 8B model, and restriction to SFT adversaries (no input-space jailbreak robustness). Open questions include scaling to frontier-scale models, closing the PEFT/OOD-LR gaps, and whether post-attack retain accuracy can be preserved without sacrificing tamper-resistance.
