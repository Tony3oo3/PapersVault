## Problem

Formal language theory defines language classes (e.g., the Chomsky hierarchy) in terms of recognizers—machines that classify whether a string belongs to a language—yet empirical evaluations of neural architectures typically use proxy tasks like language modeling or sequence-to-sequence transduction that do not match this definition. This mismatch means existing empirical results provide evidence about an analogous but less well-understood hierarchy of functions rather than the formal claims they purport to support. The paper corrects this by training and evaluating neural networks directly as binary string classifiers across 18 formal languages spanning the Chomsky hierarchy.

## Input / Output

The model receives a variable-length string w = w₁…wₙ over a finite alphabet Σ as input and produces a scalar membership probability p_M(w ∈ L) via a learned affine recognition head applied to the final hidden vector, followed by a sigmoid; the string is accepted if this probability is ≥ 1/2. For the transformer, a reserved BOS symbol is prepended to compute the initial hidden vector h₀. Training strings have lengths in [0, 40], while evaluation tests length generalization up to 500 symbols, with separate short (≤40) and long (≤80) validation sets to disentangle inductive bias from expressivity.

## Method

The paper introduces a general dataset generation pipeline requiring only two language-specific algorithms—length-constrained positive sampling and membership testing—and generates adversarial negatives by perturbing positives with a geometrically-distributed number of random edits (insertion, replacement, deletion). For regular languages, it extends the binning semiring of Snæbjarnarson et al. (2025) to lift a PDFA into a vector-weighted DFA where each weight is a length-indexed probability vector, then runs weight pushing once to compute normalized action distributions for all lengths simultaneously, reducing preprocessing from O(|Q|³n_max⁴) to O(|Q|³n_max² + |δ|n_max²). Three architectures (5-layer simple RNN, LSTM, and causally-masked transformer, all ~64k parameters) are trained with a recognition cross-entropy loss optionally augmented by language modeling and next-symbol prediction auxiliary losses in a multi-task setup. The key finding is that RNNs and LSTMs generally outperform the transformer, and no single auxiliary objective uniformly improves performance across languages and architectures.

## Results

On the FLaRe benchmark (18 formal languages across the Chomsky hierarchy, test strings up to length 500), the RNN and LSTM achieve perfect (100%) expressivity accuracy on all 7 regular languages plus Majority (DCF), while the transformer reaches 100% on only 4 of these (Even Pairs, First, Majority, and Repeat 01 at 86%). In inductive bias experiments, the transformer outperforms RNN/LSTM only on Even Pairs (99% vs. 60%/83%), First (98% vs. 80%/94%), and Majority (97% vs. 90%/95%), but is never the best architecture in expressivity experiments. Auxiliary objectives (language modeling, next-symbol prediction) help in isolated cases (e.g., next-symbol prediction is crucial for RNN on Even Pairs) but no single objective uniformly improves performance across languages and architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three architectures (causally-masked transformer, simple RNN, LSTM) rather than external baselines. The RNN and LSTM outperform the transformer on the majority of languages in both inductive bias and expressivity settings: in expressivity, the transformer is never the best architecture (tied at best on Binary Multiplication at 92%). The RNN achieves 100% inductive bias accuracy on Modular Arithmetic using only the recognition objective, while the transformer reaches only 69%. The LSTM leverages its memory cell to outperform the RNN on long-range dependency tasks (Even Pairs: 83% vs. 60% inductive bias; First: 94% vs. 80%) and on Majority (100% vs. 95% expressivity). Compared to Delétang et al. (2023), who used string-to-string transduction as a proxy, the findings partially reverse: the transformer excels on Even Pairs here (99%) where it struggled in their setup, while the RNN/LSTM no longer perfectly solve Cycle Navigation (93% vs. their 100%), likely because the recognizer must also validate string format.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper corrects a methodological mismatch in the empirical formal-language literature: prior work (e.g., Delétang et al., 2023) evaluated neural networks on proxy tasks like sequence-to-sequence transduction or language modeling, whereas the Chomsky hierarchy is formally defined in terms of recognizers (binary string classifiers). The paper builds on Snæbjarnarson et al. (2025)'s binning semiring to achieve efficient length-constrained sampling from regular languages, and generalizes the recognition setup of MLRegTest (van der Poel et al., 2024) beyond regular languages to DCF, CF, and CS classes. It also unifies and compares the auxiliary training objectives (language modeling, next-symbol prediction) that prior work used as proxies for recognition, placing them in a multi-task framework alongside the direct recognition loss.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The FLaRe benchmark and the unified recognition methodology provide the first theoretically sound empirical testbed for validating formal expressivity claims about neural architectures, filling a gap that has allowed discrepancies between theory and experiment to persist. The finding that no single auxiliary objective uniformly improves performance across languages and architectures is a useful negative result that cautions against defaulting to language modeling as a training signal. A key open question the authors highlight is why transformers, which dominate on natural language tasks, underperform RNNs and LSTMs so consistently on formal language recognition—understanding this gap could inform both architecture design and the interpretation of LLM reasoning claims.
