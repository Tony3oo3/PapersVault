## Problem

Contact-rich robotic assembly tasks require precise control and remain underexplored for data-efficient policy learning, unlike general pick-and-place manipulation. While specialist policies for individual assembly tasks exist, the question of which prior skill from a library is most relevant for fine-tuning on a novel task—and how to adapt it efficiently—remains open.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250615/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes point clouds (2000 points) sampled from CAD meshes of the plug, socket, and assembled state, along with disassembly trajectories (100 trajectories × 128 timesteps per task, generated by a compliant controller lifting the plug from the socket) as a proxy for expert demonstrations. The policy network takes a 24-dimensional proprioceptive state (joint angles, end-effector pose, goal pose, relative pose) and outputs 6-dimensional incremental pose targets for a task-space impedance controller. The transfer success predictor outputs a scalar score per source-target pair, and the final output is a fine-tuned specialist policy deployable zero-shot to a real Franka Panda robot.

## Method

SRSA is a two-stage pipeline. For skill retrieval, it jointly learns three task feature encoders: a PointNet autoencoder for geometry (32-dim embedding per part), a context encoder trained to predict forward dynamics from 10-step transition segments (32-dim), and an action-reconstruction encoder capturing expert action strategies (32-dim). These 160-dim task features for source and target tasks are concatenated and fed to an MLP that predicts zero-shot transfer success, with the key insight that high transfer success indicates similar transition dynamics, making fine-tuning efficient. For skill adaptation, the retrieved policy initializes a PPO agent, augmented with self-imitation learning that prioritizes high-reward state-action pairs from a replay buffer (prioritized by discounted return with exponent 0.6), accelerating convergence and stabilizing training—especially in sparse-reward settings where the baseline struggles.

## Results

In the dense-reward setting on 10 test tasks, SRSA achieves an average success rate of 82.6% (std 4.8) compared to AutoMate's 69.4% (std 12.7), a 19% relative improvement with 2.6× lower variance across seeds. In the sparse-reward setting (no demonstrations or curricula), SRSA reaches 70.9% versus AutoMate's 30.1%, a 135% relative improvement. SRSA also requires 2.4× fewer training epochs to reach any target success threshold (0.70–0.90). In real-world deployment, SRSA policies achieve a 90% mean success rate across 5 tasks versus AutoMate's 54%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is AutoMate (Tang et al., 2024), which trains specialist policies from scratch using PPO with dense rewards and curricula. SRSA outperforms AutoMate on every metric: 82.6% vs 69.4% success in dense-reward (19% relative gain), 70.9% vs 30.1% in sparse-reward (135% relative gain), 2.4× fewer epochs to reach target success, and 90% vs 54% real-world success. For skill retrieval, SRSA is compared against four baselines: Signature (path-signature matching, Barcelos et al. 2024), Behavior (VAE state-action embedding, Du et al. 2023), Forward (forward-dynamics latent, Guo et al. 2022), and Geometry (PointNet point-cloud embedding). SRSA retrieves policies achieving ~20% higher zero-shot transfer success on average than all four retrieval baselines, and performs best or second-best on 9 of 10 test tasks. The Geometry baseline is the strongest single-feature retrieval method, but SRSA's joint geometry+dynamics+action representation with transfer-success prediction consistently surpasses it, particularly when tasks share similar geometry but differ in contact dynamics (e.g., friction or insertion depth).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends retrieval-based policy learning from the pick-and-place domain (where prior work retrieves state-action pairs or affordance representations) to contact-rich assembly, where the unit of retrieval is an entire specialist policy rather than raw data. It builds on self-imitation learning (Oh et al., 2018) for the adaptation phase and on context-based meta-RL encoders (Rakelly et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2020) for dynamics feature extraction, but introduces a novel transfer-success-prediction objective that jointly leverages geometry (PointNet autoencoder), dynamics (forward-prediction encoder), and expert-action (action-reconstruction encoder) features to rank candidate skills. The key departure from AutoMate (Tang et al., 2024), the primary baseline, is the shift from training each assembly policy from scratch to retrieving and fine-tuning a relevant prior policy, with disassembly trajectories serving as a practical proxy for unavailable assembly demonstrations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Contact-rich assembly is a critical industrial task (automotive, aerospace, electronics) that has received far less attention than pick-and-place, and the 135% relative improvement in sparse-reward settings plus 90% zero-shot sim-to-real success rate demonstrate a practical path toward data-efficient deployment. The continual-learning framework showing 84% sample-efficiency gains as the skill library grows suggests a scalable strategy for accumulating assembly capabilities without retraining from scratch. Key limitations include the restriction to two-part insertion (no rotational/helical assembly), the reliance on specialist rather than generalist policies, and the gap between 90% and the 99%+ success rates required for industrial deployment. Open questions include whether the transfer-success-prediction paradigm generalizes to vision-based policies, dexterous manipulation, and broader tool-use tasks where geometry, dynamics, and action strategy interact differently.
