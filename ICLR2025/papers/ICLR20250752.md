## Problem

All existing GFlowNet training algorithms default to the squared-error (quadratic) regression loss in log-space, yet the choice of loss function—which directly shapes exploration and exploitation during training—has received no theoretical treatment. Without a principled framework, it is unclear whether alternative convex losses could yield better convergence, diversity, or robustness, and how to systematically design such losses.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250752/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on a DAG-structured state space with a reward function defined on terminal states, and produces a trained forward policy that samples from the target distribution (molecular graphs, bit sequences, or grid coordinates). The training objective is computed on the log-ratio of forward and backward flows over training objects (states, transitions, partial or complete trajectories), with five independently swappable components: training objects, parameterization mapping, sampling/resampling weights, backward policy, and regression loss. The proposed losses are drop-in replacements for the standard quadratic loss, requiring no changes to the network architecture, backward policy, or sampling strategy of any existing GFlowNet algorithm.

## Method

The core result (Theorem 4.1) proves that the gradient of the GFlowNet training objective with regression loss g equals the gradient of a weighted sum of f-divergences over minimal cuts, where the divergence function f is determined by g through the integral transform f(t) = t ∫₁ᵗ g'(log s)/s² ds. This establishes a two-way correspondence: any twice-differentiable convex g minimized at zero yields a valid f-divergence, and conversely any f-divergence yields a valid regression loss via g(t) = f(eᵗ) − ∫₁^{eᵗ} f(s)/s ds. The paper then identifies two key properties of the resulting divergence—zero-forcing (f(0)=∞, promoting exploitation and higher average reward) and zero-avoiding (f'(∞)=∞, promoting exploration and diversity)—and uses them to design three new losses filling the four quadrants: Linex(1) (zero-avoiding, corresponding to forward KL), Linex(1/2) (neither, corresponding to Hellinger distance), and Shifted-Cosh (both, a novel symmetric divergence). Across hyper-grid, bit-sequence generation, and molecule generation benchmarks, the new losses improve convergence speed, sample diversity, and robustness over the quadratic baseline while remaining compatible with FM, DB, TB, STB, FL, and DAG GFlowNet variants.

## Results

On bit-sequence generation (n=120, k=8, |M|=60), the quadratic baseline fails to find all 60 modes in 4/5 TB runs and 1/5 STB runs within 250k steps, while Linex(1) succeeds in all 15 runs across TB/DB/STB, finding all modes in a median of 98.0k (TB), 10.8k (DB), and 20.3k (STB) steps. On the 5D hyper-grid, the quadratic loss completely fails to converge while Linex(1), Linex(1/2), and Shifted-Cosh remain robust. For Spearman correlation between P_T and P_R, zero-forcing losses (Quadratic, Shifted-Cosh) achieve higher values (e.g., 0.8122 for Shifted-Cosh vs. 0.7421 for Linex(1) on TB), while zero-avoiding losses produce more diverse samples with lower pairwise similarity in molecule generation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the standard quadratic (squared) regression loss used in all existing GFlowNet algorithms (FM-GFN, DB-GFN, TB-GFN, STB-GFN) against three proposed losses: Linex(1), Linex(1/2), and Shifted-Cosh. The most dramatic improvement is in mode-finding reliability: the quadratic baseline collapses in 4/5 TB runs and 1/5 STB runs on bit-sequence generation, while Linex(1) achieves 5/5 success across all three algorithm variants and is consistently the fastest (e.g., 20.3k vs. 50.6k median steps for STB). On the 5D hyper-grid, quadratic loss completely fails to converge, whereas all three proposed losses remain robust. In terms of sample quality (Spearman correlation), zero-forcing losses (Quadratic, Shifted-Cosh) outperform zero-avoiding ones (Linex(1), Linex(1/2)) by ~0.06–0.07, reflecting better fitting within the target support. However, zero-avoiding losses produce significantly more diverse samples (lower pairwise similarity) in molecule generation, and Linex(1/2) demonstrates the best robustness for top-k molecule quality. Shifted-Cosh, being both zero-forcing and zero-avoiding, achieves the highest Spearman correlation (0.8213 on DB) but fails entirely on DB for bit-sequence generation, illustrating the tradeoff between the two properties.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Malkin et al. (2023), which showed that GFlowNet training with the standard quadratic loss corresponds to minimizing reverse KL divergence, and generalizes this to the full family of f-divergences via a rigorous two-way mapping between regression loss functions g and divergence functions f. It also unifies the existing landscape of GFlowNet training algorithms (FM, DB, TB, STB, FL, DAG variants) under a five-component framework, identifying the regression loss as a previously overlooked design axis that all prior work implicitly fixed to the quadratic form. The zero-forcing/zero-avoiding analysis draws on classical f-divergence theory (Liese & Vajda, 2006) and analogous results in GAN/VAE training (Nowozin et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2019), but applies them specifically to the GFlowNet flow-matching setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides GFlowNet practitioners with a principled, drop-in mechanism to tune the exploration-exploitation tradeoff by swapping the regression loss, without modifying any other component of the training pipeline. This is practically valuable because the quadratic loss's zero-forcing nature causes it to under-explore (failing in 5D hyper-grid, missing modes in bit-sequence), and the proposed alternatives (especially Linex(1) for exploration, Shifted-Cosh for balanced behavior) address this directly. Open questions include whether the theoretical conditions of Theorem 4.1 (exact forward-flow-weighted sampling) are satisfiable in all practical settings, whether the benefits persist at much larger scales (e.g., protein design, LLM-level sequence generation), and how the framework extends to continuous GFlowNets or stochastic transition settings.
