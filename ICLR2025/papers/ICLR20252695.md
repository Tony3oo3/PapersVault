## Problem

Synthetic training data generated by teacher LLMs is inherently noisy, non-informative, and sometimes misleading, yet standard synthesis pipelines (e.g., Self-Instruct) treat the teacher as a fixed generator without adapting its output to the student's actual learning needs. No prior method directly optimizes the teacher's synthesis parameters toward the student's data preferences, leaving a gap between what the teacher produces and what the student benefits from.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252695/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a seed pool of instruction-response pairs (52K Alpaca GPT-4 entries), a student model (Llama3-8B or TinyLlama-1.1B), and a teacher model (Llama3-8B-Instruct), along with a reference dataset for measuring influence. It produces 10K synthetic instruction-response pairs for student fine-tuning and an updated teacher model whose generation distribution has been shifted toward higher-influence instructions. The probing dataset (10K triplets) used for influence collection is separate from the final training set, and the preference dataset (6,792 entries) pairs positive- and negative-influence instructions under the same seed prompt.

## Method

Montessori-Instruct operates in two stages: (1) local data influence collection, where each synthetic instruction-response pair is used for one-step Adam training on the student, and its influence is approximated as the negative change in reference-set loss (Eq. 1), and (2) student-preference-guided teacher optimization via DPO, where the teacher is trained on preference pairs (p, x⁺, x⁻) constructed from positive- and negative-influence instructions sharing the same seed prompt. The key distinction from prior work is that the teacher's parameters are directly optimized (rather than filtering data or prompting for harder examples), and the optimization target is the student's measured learning utility rather than a proxy quality score. The process is iterative: after the student is retrained on the new data, influence is re-collected on the updated student to refine the teacher further. Theoretical grounding is provided in Appendix B, deriving the local influence approximation from standard influence functions via a single Newton step.

## Results

In the 8B setting (Llama3-8B student), Montessori-Instruct (Iteration 2) achieves 56.37% LC-WR and 60.15% WR on Alpaca Eval 2.0, and a score of 7.163 on MT-Bench, outperforming Self-Instruct (50%/50%/6.490) by 6.37% LC-WR, 10.15% WR, and 0.673 MT-Bench points. On out-of-domain tasks, it reaches 63.47% MMLU, 31.36% GPQA, 60.17% ARC-C, 60.02% GSM8K, and 81.98% HellaSwag. In the 1.1B setting (TinyLlama-1.1B student), Montessori-Instruct achieves 54.37% LC-WR, 54.68% WR, and 2.526 MT-Bench, again leading all baselines across every metric.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Montessori-Instruct (Iteration 2, 8B setting) outperforms Self-Instruct on every metric: +6.37% LC-WR (56.37% vs 50%), +10.15% WR (60.15% vs 50%), and +0.673 MT-Bench (7.163 vs 6.490). It also beats Self-Reward (GPT-4o judge) by +2.88% LC-WR, +2.83% WR, and +0.365 MT-Bench, and LLM2LLM by +3.74% LC-WR, +5.13% WR, and +0.644 MT-Bench. Notably, Montessori-Instruct surpasses data synthesized by the stronger GPT-4o teacher (Self-Instruct*) on LC-WR (+1.42%), WR (+3.76%), MT-Bench (+1.245), GSM8K (+9.60%), and HellaSwag (+0.56%), though it trails slightly on ARC-C (60.17 vs 60.58). The paper highlights that a weaker but student-tailored teacher (Llama3-8B-Instruct) produces more beneficial training data than a stronger but untailored one (GPT-4o), validating the core premise that alignment with student learning preferences matters more than raw teacher capability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: synthetic data generation for instruction-tuning (Self-Instruct, Self-Reward, LLM2LLM), influence functions for data valuation (Koh & Liang; Yu et al.'s MATES), and preference optimization (DPO). Prior work either filters synthetic data post-hoc using LLM-as-a-judge or reward models, or bootstraps from student errors (LLM2LLM), but none directly reshape the teacher's generation behavior using the student's learning signal. Montessori-Instruct departs by using local data influence as a preference signal to DPO-train the teacher itself, shifting the optimization target from data selection to teacher parameter adaptation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication that a single teacher optimized for a small (1.1B) student generalizes to improve 7B–9B students without retraining offers a cost-effective path to multi-model data synthesis. However, the paper acknowledges that scaling beyond 10K data points may introduce redundancy, and the self-evolve experiments show diminishing returns when the teacher and student are the same model, suggesting a fundamental quality ceiling for self-generated data. Open questions include whether the influence-based preference signal remains informative at production-scale data volumes and whether the approach transfers to pretraining or domain-specific fine-tuning settings.
