## Problem

Existing self-supervised pre-training methods for image restoration are scarce, limited to single-task settings, and discard the decoder after pre-training, creating a representation mismatch between pre-training and fine-tuning. Meanwhile, universal restoration methods rely on external prompts (CLIP embeddings, human instructions, diffusion priors) rather than exploiting the latent discriminative information already embedded within restoration models themselves. The paper identifies that restoration networks inherently possess degradation classification capability—randomly initialized models achieve 52–71% accuracy on 5-class degradation identification—that is progressively refined during all-in-one training but never explicitly leveraged as a pre-training signal.

## Input / Output

The method takes a degraded RGB image (processed as 128×128 patches during pre-training) and produces two outputs: a restored clean image and a degradation type class label. The encoder extracts multi-level features from the latter half of the restoration network's blocks, which are then consumed by both a lightweight classification decoder (ResNet18 or ViT) and a restoration head. The degradation label serves as 'extremely weak supervision' that is intrinsic to any paired restoration dataset, requiring no additional annotation. During DC-guided transfer learning, the frozen decoder reduces classification to a binary clean-vs-degraded decision.

## Method

DCPT follows an encoder-decoder design where the encoder is a standard restoration network (NAFNet, SwinIR, Restormer, PromptIR) stripped of its restoration head, and the decoder is a lightweight classifier that identifies the degradation type solely from encoder features. Multi-level features are extracted from each block in the latter half of the encoder, scaled by learnable coefficients, and plugged into corresponding ResNet18 blocks for classification. Training alternates between a degradation classification stage (Focal Loss to handle long-tail dataset imbalance) and a generation stage (L1 pixel loss to preserve reconstruction ability) within each iteration, avoiding conflicting gradient flows. After pre-training, the encoder initializes the restoration model for downstream fine-tuning, while the decoder is retained for DC-guided training that enables cross-degradation transfer learning by adding a frozen classification loss during fine-tuning on a single degradation type.

## Results

On the 5D all-in-one restoration task, DCPT consistently improves all tested architectures by over 2 dB average PSNR: DCPT-NAFNet reaches 29.84 dB (vs. 27.76 for NAFNet), DCPT-Restormer reaches 30.04 dB (vs. 27.60), and DCPT-PromptIR reaches 30.31 dB (vs. 28.11). On the 10D all-in-one task, DCPT-NAFNet achieves 29.72 dB / 0.888 SSIM, a 2.55 dB gain over NAFNet and outperforming DiffUIR-L (28.75 dB) and InstructIR (28.30 dB). On mixed degradation (CDD), DCPT-NAFNet delivers 26.23 dB for low-light+haze+rain and 26.40 dB for low-light+haze+snow, surpassing OneRestore by ~1 dB and DACLIP by ~3.3 dB.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DCPT outperforms all compared baselines across every evaluation setting. On 5D all-in-one, DCPT-NAFNet (29.84 dB) surpasses AirNet (25.49 dB) by 4.35 dB, IDR (28.34 dB) by 1.50 dB, and InstructIR (29.55 dB) by 0.29 dB, while requiring only 20 epochs of pre-training + 50 epochs of fine-tuning versus IDR's 1200 epochs. On 10D all-in-one, DCPT-NAFNet (29.72 dB) beats DiffUIR-L (28.75 dB) by 0.97 dB, InstructIR (28.30 dB) by 1.42 dB, and DACLIP-NAFNet (27.42 dB) by 2.30 dB. On mixed degradation (CDD), DCPT-NAFNet leads OneRestore (25.18 dB) by 1.05 dB and DACLIP-NAFNet (22.96 dB) by 3.27 dB on the low-light+haze+rain composite. For single-task transfer, DCPT-Restormer (33.12 dB on GoPro) edges out Restormer+DegAE (33.03 dB) by 0.09 dB. In cross-degradation transfer learning, DC-guided training reduces the performance drop from 7.48 dB (without DC) to 2.56 dB in deblurring and from 4.75 dB to 0.45 dB in deraining when transferring from a denoising-trained model.

## Relation to Prior Work

DCPT carves new ground at the intersection of self-supervised pre-training and universal image restoration. While prior universal restoration methods (AirNet, PromptIR, DACLIP, InstructIR) rely on external degradation representations—dynamic convolution prompts, CLIP embeddings, or human instructions—DCPT instead exploits the degradation classification ability that restoration models already possess implicitly. It departs from standard self-supervised pre-training (MIM, contrastive learning) by replacing the reconstruction decoder with a lightweight classification decoder (ResNet18) and using Focal Loss to handle the long-tail imbalance across degradation datasets. The encoder is simply a standard restoration network (NAFNet, SwinIR, Restormer, PromptIR) stripped of its restoration head, making DCPT architecture-agnostic and drop-in compatible.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DCPT offers a remarkably simple pre-training recipe—just 20 epochs of classification training before fine-tuning—that yields 2+ dB gains across all-in-one restoration and 5+ dB gains on mixed degradation, outperforming methods that require external large models or human instructions. The DC-guided training mechanism, which retains the degradation classifier during fine-tuning to enable cross-degradation transfer learning, is a practical contribution that prior pre-training methods (which discard their decoder) do not offer. Open questions include whether DCPT generalizes to real-world, in-the-wild degradations, whether the classification phase can be extended to multi-label settings for inherently mixed degradations, and whether the observed gains persist at larger model scales or with more diverse degradation taxonomies.
