## Problem

Current behavioural cloning methods for robot manipulation require hundreds or thousands of demonstrations per task plus hours of training, whereas in-context learning in NLP and vision enables instant task acquisition from just a few examples. Transferring this paradigm to robotics faces two key obstacles: the absence of large, diverse datasets necessitates strong inductive biases for 3D spatial reasoning, and the cost of manual data collection demands a scalable alternative training data source.

## Input / Output

The model consumes segmented point clouds (2048 points, expressed in the end-effector frame for spatial generalisation), the current SE(3) end-effector pose, a binary gripper state, and N demonstration trajectories (typically N=2, each downsampled to L=10 waypoints) as context. It outputs a sequence of T=8 future end-effector displacements (SE(3), clamped to ≤1 cm translation and ≤3° rotation per step) plus binary gripper open/close commands, sampled iteratively via DDIM denoising. A frozen pre-trained occupancy network encodes local point cloud geometry into 16 feature vectors per observation, and the entire pipeline operates without any language-annotated data during core training.

## Method

Instant Policy casts in-context imitation learning as a graph generation problem: a heterogeneous graph jointly encodes the demonstration context, current observation, and future actions as nodes and typed edges, with a pre-trained occupancy network providing local geometry features. A diffusion process adds Gaussian noise to the action nodes in the forward pass and learns a denoising network (three sequential heterogeneous graph transformers σ, φ, ψ) that predicts per-node gradient fields to reconstruct clean actions. A key design choice decouples SE(3) denoising into centre-of-mass translation and rotation around it in Cartesian space, avoiding the scale mismatch that plagues joint translation-rotation prediction. Crucially, the model is trained entirely on procedurally generated pseudo-demonstrations—semantically consistent but arbitrary trajectories over ShapeNet objects—exploiting the fact that ICIL weights encode a task-agnostic interpretation ability rather than task-specific policies. At test time, DDIM sampling with as few as 2 denoising steps yields actions, with SE(3) transformations extracted via SVD alignment between successive gripper node configurations.

## Results

On 24 RLBench manipulation tasks (100 rollouts each), Instant Policy achieves 71% average success using only pseudo-demonstrations (PD only), rising to 97% on seen tasks and 82% on unseen tasks when augmented with 20 real demos (PD+). In real-world evaluation on 16 everyday tasks (10 rollouts each), Instant Policy reaches 88.75% average success from just 2 kinesthetic demonstrations. The method also demonstrates generalisation to novel object geometries, with success rates improving from 40% (1 demo) to 90% (4 demos) across four tasks with unseen objects.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Instant Policy substantially outperforms all three baselines across both simulated and real-world evaluations. On RLBench with pseudo-demonstrations only, Instant Policy achieves 71% average success versus 36% (BC-Z), 38% (Vid2Robot), and 34% (GPT2); with additional real demos on seen tasks, the gap widens to 97% vs. 59%, 60%, and 65% respectively. In the real-world setting, Instant Policy's 88.75% average success rate more than doubles the best baseline (Vid2Robot at 40.63%) and is 2.6× BC-Z (34.38%) and 2.4× GPT2 (36.88%). The largest margins appear on tasks requiring precise spatial reasoning (e.g., Close jar: 0.58 vs. 0.00 for all baselines; Basketball in hoop: 0.66 vs. ≤0.03), while baselines are more competitive on simpler tasks like Lift lid (0.82–0.90) and Push button (0.75–0.85). Notably, GPT2 is the strongest baseline on seen RLBench tasks (65%) but still trails Instant Policy by 32 percentage points, and all baselines collapse to near-zero on several tasks (e.g., Open microwave, Lamp on) where Instant Policy retains non-trivial success.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the diffusion-based policy learning line (Diffusion Policy, Chi et al. 2023) by moving from action-sequence denoising to a structured graph generation problem, where the heterogeneous graph transformer (Shi et al. 2020) propagates information across context, observation, and action nodes. It builds on occupancy networks (Mescheder et al. 2019) for geometry encoding and the DDIM sampling framework (Ho et al. 2020), but departs from prior ICIL methods (BC-Z, Vid2Robot, GPT2-style) by replacing flat or sequence-based representations with a unified graph space that explicitly encodes spatial relationships. The most significant conceptual departure is the use of procedurally generated pseudo-demonstrations as training data, eliminating the need for any real task demonstrations during training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that an ICIL model can be trained entirely on semantically consistent but arbitrary simulated trajectories challenges the assumption that imitation learning requires real demonstrations, offering a scalable path to robot skill acquisition. The large performance gaps over baselines (e.g., 88.75% vs. ~35–40% average in real-world tasks) and the emergent generalisation to novel object geometries suggest the graph representation captures task-relevant structure that flat encoders miss. Key limitations remain: reliance on segmented point clouds, short-horizon Markovian tasks, sensitivity to demonstration quality, and absence of collision avoidance. Open questions include whether richer pseudo-demonstrations (with dynamics, long horizons, or semantic features) can close the gap to more complex manipulation, and whether the bottleneck representation enables broader modality transfer beyond the qualitative language-conditioned results shown here.
