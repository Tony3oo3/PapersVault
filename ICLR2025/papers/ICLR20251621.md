## Problem

Preference-based (binary comparison) feedback dominates RLHF, yet real human feedback is far more diverse—encompassing ratings, demonstrations, corrections, and descriptions. Despite the potential for diverse feedback to correct biases, adapt to task context, and be mutually informative, the systematic use of multiple feedback types for reward learning has remained largely unexplored, with prior work limited to at most two or three types in restricted settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251621/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes trajectory segments (up to 50 steps) collected from 20 checkpoints of four expert RL models per environment, along with synthesized multi-type feedback labels: integer ratings (1–10), pairwise preference labels, expert demonstration trajectories, corrective trajectory deltas, k-means cluster representatives with scalar rewards, and cluster-pair preference labels. Descriptive feedback operates on single state-action pairs rather than full trajectories. The output is a learned reward function (6-layer MLP mapping state-action pairs to scalar rewards) used to train a downstream PPO agent, plus an uncertainty-weighted ensemble combining all six per-type reward models.

## Method

The paper defines six feedback types (evaluative, comparative, demonstrative, corrective, descriptive, descriptive-preference) and implements a unified synthetic generation pipeline that derives all types from a shared foundation of expert value functions, discounted segment returns, and mini-batch k-means clustering over state-action pairs. Reward models are 6-layer MLPs trained with MSE loss for scalar feedback (ratings, descriptions) and Bradley-Terry cross-entropy loss for relative feedback (comparisons, corrections, descriptive preferences), with demonstrative feedback reformulated as preferences between expert demos and random behavior. A consistent noise scheme injects truncated Gaussian perturbation into the underlying reward distribution, propagating type-appropriate errors (label flips for preferences, state/action jitter for demonstrations). For joint modeling, the paper introduces both a simple averaging ensemble and an uncertainty-weighted ensemble using Masksembles-based epistemic uncertainty to down-weight less reliable feedback types. The approach is evaluated across ten RL environments (six Mujoco locomotion, three HighwayEnv, one MetaWorld) with five random seeds per configuration.

## Results

Across ten RL environments (six Mujoco locomotion, one MetaWorld, three HighwayEnv), the paper shows that all six feedback types (ratings, comparisons, demonstrations, corrections, descriptions, descriptive preferences) achieve competitive downstream RL performance, with descriptive feedback and descriptive preferences generally yielding the best results. Some feedback types match expert-model performance in three environments, while performance degrades notably in Swimmer-v5 and Ant-v5. The uncertainty-weighted ensemble of all six reward models matches the best single-feedback performance in HalfCheetah-v5 and significantly outperforms the single-feedback average, though Walker2d-v5 shows limited or negative improvement. Preliminary experiments indicate that selective combinations of feedback types (excluding corrective feedback) outperform the full six-type ensemble.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the standard preference-based (comparative) feedback used in conventional RLHF, which the paper shows is matched or surpassed by other feedback types across most environments. Descriptive feedback and descriptive preferences consistently outperform comparative feedback, while demonstrative feedback uniquely outperforms all other types in Swimmer-v5. Corrective feedback is the weakest single type and degrades ensemble performance when included; all two- and three-type combinations excluding corrective feedback achieve higher scores than their individual components. The ensemble of all six types matches the best single type in HalfCheetah-v5 but underperforms the average in Walker2d-v5, indicating that naive averaging does not always exploit complementarity. Behavioral cloning on demonstration data serves as an additional baseline but is not the focus of comparison. Overall, the paper challenges the presumed superiority of comparative feedback, showing that diverse feedback types offer complementary strengths depending on environment and noise level.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the reward-rational choice formalism of Jeon et al. (2020) from a theoretical unification of feedback types to a concrete, large-scale empirical implementation across six types. It builds on the oracle-based preference simulation of Christiano et al. (2017) and the synthetic feedback toolbox of Biyik et al. (2022b), but generalizes both from single-type (preferences) to a multi-type setting. The closest prior work—Mehta & Losey (2022) and Ibarz et al. (2018)—explored combinations of two or three feedback types in separate phases; this paper is the first to implement reward models, a consistent noise scheme, and downstream RL training for all six types simultaneously across ten environments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic empirical evidence that non-preference feedback types (especially descriptive feedback) can match or exceed the long-assumed superiority of pairwise comparisons, and that ground-truth reward correlation does not reliably predict downstream RL performance—both findings challenge working assumptions in the RLHF community. However, the joint-modeling results are mixed (successful in HalfCheetah, failing in Walker2d), the feedback is entirely simulated with a simple additive Gaussian noise model, and the ensemble approach is a basic average. Key open questions include dynamic/online multi-type feedback, scaling to LLM alignment, and whether real human feedback patterns (which may be more structured than Gaussian noise) would alter the relative rankings of feedback types.
