## Problem

Continual panoptic segmentation (CPS) requires a model to sequentially learn new classes while retaining previously acquired knowledge, but existing methods face a fundamental trade-off: CoMFormer finetunes the entire model (causing catastrophic forgetting and requiring dual forward passes for distillation), while ECLIPSE freezes most weights but introduces new prompt features per task (limiting plasticity and creating scalability issues). No prior approach simultaneously achieves efficient training, strong base-class retention, and strong novel-class generalization without growing computational cost as tasks accumulate.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard RGB images and produces panoptic segmentation outputs consisting of per-pixel segment IDs and per-segment class labels, covering both stuff (amorphous) and thing (countable) classes. During continual learning under the overlap setting, at each step t only the current task's class labels are available while previously learned classes may still appear in the image. At inference, the dual-decoder architecture produces separate predictions for base and novel classes that are merged via query-level fusion before the standard Mask2Former panoptic inference procedure (NMS-based mask assignment) yields the final segmentation.

## Method

ADAPT builds on Mask2Former and freezes the image encoder and pixel decoder, finetuning only the cross-attention layers and feed-forward networks in the transformer decoder, which enables a single shared forward pass between teacher and student models during distillation. An attentive self-distillation loss, inspired by focal loss, adaptively re-weights each query's contribution to the KD objective using the teacher's predicted background confidence (modulating term α(1-p̂(i,∅))^γ), applied only to queries unmatched with new classes in bipartite matching, thereby concentrating distillation on informative old-class objects rather than dominant no-object regions. At inference, a retained base decoder predicts base classes while the adapted decoder handles novel classes, and a query-level fusion (QLF) strategy combines their outputs by discarding cross-class probability scores, avoiding the scale inconsistency that plagues naive probability-level fusion. The computational overhead remains nearly constant regardless of the number of continual learning steps.

## Results

On ADE20K (the primary benchmark), ADAPT achieves 36.9 PQ overall in the 100-10 continual setting (6 tasks), surpassing ECLIPSE (33.9) by +3.0 PQ and CoMFormer (29.7) by +7.2 PQ. In the more challenging 50-10 setting, ADAPT reaches 30.7 PQ overall, exceeding ECLIPSE (26.8) by +3.9 PQ and CoMFormer (23.2) by +7.5 PQ. On COCO (83-10 setting), ADAPT achieves 39.3 PQ overall versus 36.0 for ECLIPSE and 35.5 for CoMFormer. ADAPT also trains 34.8% faster than CoMFormer (2.27 hrs vs 3.36 hrs for 5 steps) while using only 12.12M trainable parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ADAPT consistently outperforms all four baselines across every evaluated scenario. Against CoMFormer (which finetunes the entire model with KD), ADAPT gains +6.2 PQ on base classes and +9.2 PQ on novel classes in the 100-10 setting, and +11.0/+3.1 PQ in the 50-10 setting, demonstrating that freezing the encoder and selectively finetuning only cross-attention and FFN layers better preserves prior knowledge while maintaining plasticity. Against ECLIPSE (which freezes most weights and adds new prompts per task), ADAPT achieves comparable base-class PQ (42.2 vs 41.4 in 100-10) but substantially higher novel-class PQ (26.3 vs 18.8, a +7.5 PQ gain), showing that ECLIPSE's prompt-based approach limits generalization. Against the simpler baselines MiB and PLOP (originally designed for semantic segmentation), ADAPT leads by +13.8 and +10.8 PQ overall in the 100-10 setting, respectively. ADAPT also trains 34.8% faster than CoMFormer and 32.4% faster than ECLIPSE, while incurring only a 6.5% FLOPs increase over ECLIPSE at inference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Mask2Former as the base architecture and extends the continual panoptic segmentation line initiated by CoMFormer (which finetunes the entire model with KD) and ECLIPSE (which freezes most weights and adds per-task prompts). The key conceptual departures are: (1) freezing the encoder and pixel decoder to enable a shared forward pass between teacher and student, eliminating the dual-model overhead of CoMFormer; (2) replacing uniform KD with a focal-loss-inspired attentive self-distillation that re-weights queries by background confidence; and (3) introducing a dual-decoder inference paradigm with query-level fusion (QLF) that sidesteps the probability-scale mismatch inherent in naive score replacement. The class-correspondence modification for KD follows Yang et al. (2023) from continual semantic segmentation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Continual panoptic segmentation remains under-explored, and this paper provides the first method that simultaneously achieves strong base-class retention, competitive novel-class generalization, and near-constant computational overhead regardless of the number of learning steps. The QLF mechanism offers a clean, generalizable principle for combining predictions from specialized decoders without scale calibration, which could transfer to other multi-decoder or multi-teacher settings. Limitations include reliance on the Mask2Former query-based architecture (the approach may not directly apply to per-pixel or mask-RCNN-style methods), and the dual-decoder inference doubles memory for the decoder weights, which could become non-trivial for very long task sequences. Open questions include whether the frozen-encoder assumption degrades under large domain shifts and whether the method extends to open-vocabulary or streaming settings.
