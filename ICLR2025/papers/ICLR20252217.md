## Problem

Existing memorization metrics from supervised learning (which assume one-dimensional labels) and self-supervised learning (which operate on single modalities) cannot adequately capture memorization in multi-modal models like CLIP, where captions act as supervisory signals and the contrastive objective resembles SSL. It remains unclear how CLIP's hybrid training paradigm determines which data points are memorized, how memorization manifests differently across the image and text encoders, and whether memorization can be reduced without sacrificing generalization.

## Input / Output

The method takes an image-text pair (I, T) along with two CLIP models—f trained on the full dataset S and g trained on S\{(I,T)}—and produces a normalized scalar memorization score in [-1, 1]. In practice, true leave-one-out is computationally intractable, so the paper approximates CLIPMem via a four-way dataset split (shared, candidate, independent, external) where f and g differ only in a small candidate set of ~5,000 pairs. The alignment score itself requires N-1 held-out test samples to estimate the negative-pair terms that mirror the contrastive training objective.

## Method

CLIPMem defines memorization as the difference in alignment scores between a model trained with a data point and a reference model trained without it, where the alignment score captures the full contrastive objective: the cosine similarity of the correct image-text pair minus the average similarity to N-1 incorrect pairs in both the image-to-text and text-to-image directions. A key finding is that measuring memorization jointly over both modalities yields a substantially stronger signal (max ~0.91) than applying SSLMem to either encoder individually (max ~0.65–0.73), preventing under-reporting. The paper identifies that mis-captioned samples exhibit the highest memorization and that the text encoder contributes more to memorization than the image encoder. Building on these insights, the authors propose mitigation strategies—using multiple captions, adding Gaussian noise to text embeddings during training, and removing top-memorized samples—that simultaneously reduce memorization and improve downstream linear probing accuracy, breaking the memorization-generalization tradeoff observed in both supervised and self-supervised learning.

## Results

CLIPMem reveals that mis-captioned training samples exhibit the highest memorization (CLIPMem = 0.586) compared to clean samples (0.440), and that the text encoder contributes more to memorization than the image encoder (SSLMem 0.209 vs. 0.168). The proposed mitigation strategies simultaneously reduce memorization and improve downstream linear probing accuracy on ImageNet: using 5 balanced captions lowers CLIPMem from 0.438 to 0.423 while raising accuracy from 63.11% to 64.88%, and adding Gaussian noise N(0, 0.15) to text embeddings achieves the best trade-off with CLIPMem 0.417 and 65.34% accuracy. Removing the top 100 most memorized samples from COCO training further boosts linear probing accuracy, a result that contrasts with supervised and SSL settings where removing memorized data consistently harms performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SSLMem (Wang et al., 2024b), CLIPMem extracts a substantially stronger memorization signal: the maximum CLIPMem for candidate samples reaches 0.91, whereas SSLMem on the vision encoder caps at ~0.65 and on the text encoder at ~0.73, with heavy score overlap between memorized and non-memorized subsets. Against DINO (Caron et al., 2021) as an SSL baseline, CLIP's vision encoder shows lower overall SSLMem (0.209 vs. 0.279) and only 9.4% overlap in the top-10% most memorized samples, indicating CLIP memorizes a different set of data points driven by caption quality rather than image atypicality. At the neuron level (UnitMem), CLIP's memorization profile sits between supervised and SSL: early layers behave like supervised models (group-level encoding) while later layers approach SSL-like individual-point memorization, though less extreme than either paradigm. The paper's key differentiating finding is that, unlike both supervised (Feldman, 2020) and SSL (Wang et al., 2024b) baselines where reducing memorization always degrades generalization, CLIP can reduce memorization via text-domain augmentations while simultaneously improving ImageNet linear probing accuracy by up to 2.2 percentage points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the leave-one-out memorization framework from supervised learning (Feldman, 2020) and self-supervised learning (SSLMem, Wang et al., 2024b) to the multi-modal CLIP setting, where existing uni-modal definitions are insufficient because they either rely on one-dimensional labels or ignore one of the two modalities. It departs from the only prior empirical work on CLIP memorization (Déjà Vu, Jayaraman et al., 2024) by measuring memorization directly on model output representations rather than requiring external public datasets and object detection. Conceptually, the paper positions CLIP as occupying an intermediate regime between supervised and self-supervised memorization, with the contrastive objective pulling from SSL and the captions providing a supervisory signal analogous to labels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that reducing memorization in CLIP can simultaneously improve downstream generalization directly challenges the established trade-off observed in both supervised and self-supervised learning, opening a new direction for multi-modal model training. Practically, since state-of-the-art CLIP models are trained on large uncurated internet data, the ability to identify and remove mis-captioned samples using CLIPMem has immediate implications for both privacy (reducing memorization of sensitive data) and performance. Open questions include whether these findings hold at the scale of production CLIP training (LAION-400M+), whether the text-encoder-dominance of memorization generalizes to other multi-modal architectures, and whether the observed synergy between reduced memorization and improved utility persists with more aggressive data curation.
