## Problem

Existing multi-image processing methods each target only a subset of degradations—burst methods handle noise and blur but lack dynamic range, while HDR reconstruction methods assume noise-free and blur-free inputs. In real low-light scenarios, all degradations (noise, motion blur, under/overexposure, misalignment, and limited resolution) co-occur simultaneously, yet no prior method unifies denoising, deblurring, HDR reconstruction, and super-resolution into a single framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253440/cropped_page4_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 5 multi-exposure raw images in Bayer pattern (4-channel, 6016×4512 for real-world or 1920×1080 GT for synthetic) with a fixed exposure time ratio of 4× between consecutive frames. Each frame is normalized by its relative exposure time and concatenated with its gamma-corrected (γ=1/2.2) version before feeding the network. The output is a single tone-mapped HDR image aligned to the shortest-exposure frame; BracketIRE+ additionally produces a 4× up-sampled version via PixelShuffle.

## Method

The core architecture is TMRNet, a unidirectional recurrent network (based on RBSR) whose aggregation module is split into a common sub-network shared across all frames and a frame-specific sub-network, allowing the model to handle the varying degradation severity (increasing blur with longer exposure) while still sharing parameters for efficiency. Training follows a two-stage protocol: supervised pre-training on synthetic pairs constructed from HDR videos (with simulated heteroscedastic noise, motion blur via frame summation, and dynamic range clipping), followed by self-supervised adaptation on unlabeled real-world images. The adaptation uses a temporally self-supervised loss that treats the full-frame output as a pseudo-target for shorter-frame outputs, combined with an EMA regularization loss to prevent trivial collapse, since no ground truth is available in the real domain.

## Results

On the synthetic BracketIRE task, TMRNet achieves 39.35 dB PSNR / 0.9516 SSIM / 0.112 LPIPS, outperforming the strongest baselines RBSR (39.10 dB) and Kim et al. (39.09 dB) by 0.25 and 0.26 dB respectively. On the real-world BracketIRE task, the self-supervised adaptation boosts CLIPIQA from 0.2003 to 0.2537 and MANIQA from 0.2181 to 0.2422, surpassing all baselines. For the BracketIRE+ (with SR) task, TMRNet reaches 30.65 dB PSNR on synthetic data (vs. 30.49 for RBSR) and the highest CLIPIQA of 0.3676 on real-world images. Inference time is 1,425 ms for a 1920×1080 raw image, comparable to RBSR (1,467 ms) and substantially faster than BIPNet (6,166 ms) and SCTNet (3,894 ms).

## Baselines & Comparisons

TMRNet outperforms all five burst-processing baselines (DBSR, MFIR, BIPNet, Burstormer, RBSR) and all five HDR-reconstruction baselines (AHDRNet, HDRGAN, HDR-Tran., SCTNet, Kim et al.) on the synthetic BracketIRE task, with the closest competitors being RBSR (39.10 dB) and Kim et al. (39.09 dB), both trailing by ~0.25 dB. On the real-world BracketIRE task, the self-supervised adaptation is critical: without it, TMRNet scores 0.2003 CLIPIQA (worse than Kim et al.'s 0.2467), but with adaptation it reaches 0.2537, the best among all methods. For BracketIRE+, TMRNet achieves the highest CLIPIQA (0.3676) on real-world images, though HDRGAN edges it out on MANIQA (0.3109 vs. 0.3020). Against the all-in-one method AdaIR, TMRNet gains 1.29 dB PSNR (39.35 vs. 38.06), attributed to AdaIR's lack of multi-image complementarity exploitation. On the Kalantari HDR dataset (Appendix C), TMRNet also achieves the best PSNR (42.43 dB) and SSIM (0.9893), and on BurstSR it reaches 48.92 dB PSNR, surpassing RBSR (48.80) and Burstormer (48.82). The self-supervised adaptation additionally yields a 0.87 dB PSNR gain on the BurstSR real-world test set (45.57 vs. 44.70 dB), confirming its generalizability beyond the BracketIRE task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the recurrent network architecture of RBSR (Wu et al., 2023) for burst super-resolution, adopting its encoder-reconstruction structure and flow-guided deformable alignment from BasicVSR++ (Chan et al., 2022), but departs by splitting the aggregation module into shared and frame-specific components (TMRNet) to handle the heterogeneous degradations across multi-exposure frames. Conceptually, it unifies four previously separate multi-image processing lines—burst denoising/SR, dual-exposure restoration, and multi-exposure HDR reconstruction—into a single joint task motivated by the complementarity of exposure bracketing. The self-supervised adaptation strategy, which uses progressive frame addition as pseudo-supervision with EMA regularization, extends prior self-supervised multi-frame work (e.g., SelfIR, self-supervised burst SR) by exploiting the temporal ordering inherent to multi-exposure sequences rather than treating frames as an unordered set.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified formulation is practically significant for smartphone night photography, where a single model replacing a cascade of denoising, deblurring, and HDR pipelines simplifies deployment and avoids error propagation between stages (demonstrated by the 1.4 dB gap between step-by-step and joint processing). The introduced synthetic and real-world datasets (200 nighttime scenes) provide benchmarks that were previously absent for this combined task. Key limitations include sensor-specific training (noise model parameters must be re-estimated per device) and the reliance on a synthetic-to-real adaptation step that, while effective, still leaves a gap with ideal performance. An open question is whether the temporal modulation idea generalizes to other multi-image settings with heterogeneous degradations beyond exposure bracketing.
