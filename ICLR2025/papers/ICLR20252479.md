## Problem

CLIP's image encoder produces noisy, stochastic saliency maps and exhibits limited neuron-concept alignment, making it unsuitable for high-stakes decision-making applications like medical diagnosis and autonomous driving. While adversarial training has been shown to improve interpretability in single-modal classifiers, its application to multi-modal vision-language models like CLIP remains underexplored, and it is unclear whether such improvements generalize zero-shot or transfer to downstream tasks.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images at 224×224 resolution; the unsupervised variant (Eq. 7) requires only image-only datasets with no text pairs, while the supervised variant additionally uses paired text captions. The output is a fine-tuned image encoder (ViT-B/16, ViT-L/14, or ResNet-50) whose weights remain compatible with the frozen original text encoder and downstream VLMs like LLaVA. The practical outputs are improved saliency maps (Simple Gradient, Grad-CAM, M2IB, Grad-ECLIP), better neuron-concept alignment scores via CLIP-dissect, and maintained zero-shot classification accuracy.

## Method

The paper proposes an unsupervised adversarial fine-tuning (AFT) objective that minimizes the L2 distance between the Gaussian-smoothed image embedding under perturbation and the original embedding, with a piecewise-quadratic regularization h(δ) on the perturbation. The key theoretical insight is a duality result: the Fenchel conjugate of h(·) acts as an implicit Huber-loss penalty on the SmoothGrad of the similarity score, enforcing sparsity in gradient-based saliency maps. By deriving a text-embedding-independent upper bound (Eq. 6), the method decouples the image encoder from the text encoder, enabling fine-tuning on image-only datasets. The inner maximization is solved with 10-step PGD (step size 1/255) over 2 epochs on ImageNet, using a single Gaussian noise sample per iteration as a simplified smoothing. This differs from FARE, which uses ℓ∞-norm hard constraints for robustness; here the Huber dual produces a piecewise-quadratic loss specifically designed to structure saliency maps for interpretability.

## Results

On the ImageNet-Segmentation validation set, AFT dramatically improves saliency map localization: Simple Gradient Point Game accuracy jumps from 25.77% to 71.15% (+45.38 pp) and Grad-Cam PG from 51.00% to 73.98% (+22.98 pp) under zero-shot classification. Grad-Cam AP improves from 49.18% to 62.34% and mask-IoU from 16.19% to 19.39%. The interpretability gains come at a modest accuracy cost: average zero-shot accuracy drops from 72.95% (CLIP) to 70.11% with ε=1/255, while robust accuracy at L∞=4/255 improves from 0% to 33.14%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is FARE (Schlarmann et al., 2024), which also applies adversarial fine-tuning to CLIP but targets robustness rather than interpretability. On the ImageNet-Segmentation pointing game, AFT outperforms FARE across all saliency map types: for Simple Gradient, AFT achieves PG=33.19 vs. FARE's 30.06 and PG-energy=33.94 vs. 31.68; for Grad-Cam, AFT reaches PG=74.31 vs. FARE's 70.05 and AP=67.23 vs. 66.11. On LLaVa attention maps, AFT yields PG=7.64 vs. FARE's 5.56. In ROAR analysis on Imagenette and CUB-200, AFT produces a steeper accuracy drop when removing top-ranked pixels, indicating it captures more task-relevant features than FARE. On zero-shot accuracy, both methods show comparable clean-accuracy drops (AFT1: 70.11% vs. FARE1: 70.10% average), but AFT achieves slightly higher robust accuracy at L∞=2/255 (44.12% vs. 43.12% for AFT2 vs. FARE2) and at L∞=4/255 (33.14% vs. 33.14% for AFT4 vs. FARE4), confirming that the interpretability-focused objective does not sacrifice robustness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the established connection between adversarial training and interpretability—previously demonstrated in uni-modal classification settings (Ross & Doshi-Velez, 2018; Xu et al., 2023a)—to the multi-modal vision-language domain, specifically targeting CLIP's image encoder. It departs from the concurrent FARE method (Schlarmann et al., 2024), which also applies adversarial fine-tuning to CLIP but with ℓ∞-norm constraints aimed at robustness, by instead using a Huber-loss-based norm regularization that explicitly enforces sparsity in input gradients. The theoretical contribution formalizes the duality between the perturbation norm and the Fenchel conjugate acting on SmoothGrad, providing a principled mechanism for why the fine-tuning produces sparser, more interpretable representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Because CLIP's image encoder is embedded as a plug-in component in widely deployed systems (LLaVA, DALL-E, medical imaging tools), improving its inherent interpretability has broad downstream impact without requiring changes to downstream architectures. The unsupervised formulation (no text pairs needed) is particularly valuable for domains like medical imaging where paired annotations are scarce. Key open questions include the sensitivity to training data diversity (only ImageNet was used), the trade-off with zero-shot accuracy at larger perturbation strengths, and whether the approach scales to even larger foundation models. The paper also leaves unexplored how different minimax solvers affect the final interpretability gains.
