## Problem

Influence functions provide a principled way to attribute a model's behaviour to individual training examples, but they have not been scalably adapted to the general diffusion modelling setting. The generalised Gauss-Newton (GGN) Hessian approximation central to influence functions has no clear formulation for the diffusion objective, and K-FAC-based approximations—despite being applicable to diffusion architectures—have not been systematically developed or applied for this task. Prior diffusion-specific methods (TRAK, D-TRAK) rely on the less well-motivated empirical Fisher and require method-specific hyperparameter tuning that is infeasible in practice.

## Input / Output

Given a trained DDPM, its full training dataset, and one or more generated query samples, the method computes a scalar influence score for every training point, indicating the predicted change in a chosen measurement (diffusion loss, ELBO, or sampling-trajectory probability) on the query sample if that training point were removed. The measurement gradient and training loss gradient are each estimated via Monte Carlo sampling over diffusion timesteps and noise, and the final scores are structured as a per-query ranking over all N training examples. Gradient compression via quantisation (rather than SVD) is used to store preconditioned gradients, since diffusion loss gradients are not low-rank.

## Method

The paper formulates two GGN approximations for the diffusion objective: GGN^model (an MC-Fisher that linearises the noise-prediction network and samples auxiliary targets from the model's output distribution) and GGN^loss (the empirical Fisher that linearises exp(−loss)). They argue GGN^model is the better-motivated Hessian approximation because it only requires the parameter-to-output map to be locally linear, a weaker condition than GGN^loss. To make inversion tractable, they derive K-FAC-expand and K-FAC-reduce approximations of GGN^model for all layer types in diffusion architectures (linear, convolution, attention), and apply eigenvalue-corrected K-FAC (EK-FAC) on top. The influence score for each training point is then a preconditioned inner product between the measurement gradient and the training loss gradient, computed efficiently via the block-diagonal K-FAC inverse. They unify TRAK and D-TRAK as specific (suboptimal) design choices within this framework—using GGN^loss with random projections instead of GGN^model with K-FAC—and show that their recommended configuration outperforms both on LDS and retraining-without-top-influences metrics without requiring damping-factor tuning.

## Results

The paper evaluates data attribution methods using two metrics: Linear Data Modelling Score (LDS, Spearman rank correlation between predicted and actual measurement changes after retraining on 50% subsets) and counterfactual retraining without top influences (2% and 10% data removal). K-FAC Influence (EK-FAC + GGN^model) outperforms TRAK and D-TRAK on both LDS and retraining metrics across CIFAR-2, CIFAR-10, and ArtBench. A key practical advantage is that K-FAC Influence is far less sensitive to the Hessian damping factor than TRAK/D-TRAK, which often fail entirely if the damping is not carefully tuned per problem. The paper also identifies three limitations: higher-timestep losses act as better proxies for lower-timestep losses, influence functions overestimate the frequency of negative influence (loss decreases), and for sufficiently large training sets the marginal probability becomes nearly constant regardless of which data is removed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

K-FAC Influence outperforms TRAK (Georgiev et al., 2023) in all experimental settings on both LDS and retraining-without-top-influences metrics across CIFAR-2, CIFAR-10, and ArtBench. It also outperforms D-TRAK (Zheng et al., 2024), which uses ad-hoc substitutions of incorrect measurement/training loss functions benchmark-tuned specifically for LDS improvement, in all settings as well. The CLIP cosine similarity baseline (Radford et al., 2021) is substantially outperformed by all influence-function-based methods. A notable practical distinction is that TRAK and D-TRAK are highly sensitive to the damping hyperparameter (often performing poorly or failing without careful per-problem tuning), whereas K-FAC Influence works reasonably well with any sufficiently small default damping value. The paper notes that the gap between GGN^model (MC-Fisher) and GGN^loss (Empirical Fisher, used by TRAK/D-TRAK) is relatively small when both are approximated with K-FAC-expand, suggesting the K-FAC structure itself partially mitigates the low-rankness issues of the empirical Fisher.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the influence function framework (Koh & Liang, 2017) to diffusion models by formulating the correct Generalised Gauss-Newton matrix for the diffusion training objective and showing that K-FAC/EK-FAC (Heskes, 2000; Martens & Grosse, 2015; Eschenhagen et al., 2023) can approximate it scalably for architectures combining convolutions, linear layers, and attention. It unifies prior diffusion data attribution methods—TRAK (Georgiev et al., 2023) and D-TRAK (Zheng et al., 2024)—as specific (suboptimal) design choices within a general framework, distinguishing its method by using the MC-Fisher GGN^model rather than the Empirical Fisher GGN^loss. The approach also draws on Grosse et al. (2023) for the EK-FAC influence function implementation pattern and query batching strategy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, well-motivated recipe for scalable data attribution in diffusion models that outperforms prior methods without requiring method-specific hyperparameter tuning (damping factor), which is practically significant since tuning requires expensive retraining benchmarks. However, it also reveals fundamental limitations: influence functions identify the correct influential data points but fail to accurately predict the numerical magnitude of measurement changes, and for sufficiently large training sets the marginal probability distribution is nearly constant regardless of which data is removed. These findings suggest that for high-stakes applications like copyright attribution, better proxies for marginal probability and improved approximation accuracy remain open problems.
