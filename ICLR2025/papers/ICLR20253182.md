## Problem

Contrastive models trained on modality pairs A↔B and B↔C often allow direct comparison of unpaired modalities A and C, but prior work has not provided a rigorous account of when this 'plug-n-play' heuristic is principled or when it fails. This paper identifies the precise probabilistic assumptions (conditional independence, density-ratio encoding, and marginal distribution shape) under which direct representation comparison recovers the correct likelihood ratio, and provides a fallback algorithm for when those assumptions are violated.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253182/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two unpaired modality inputs (e.g., an image and an audio clip) plus a set of N sampled representations from an intermediate modality (e.g., language embeddings drawn from the AudioSet ontology or a pre-trained encoder's output). It produces a cross-modal log probability ratio p(C|A)/p(C) computed as a LogSumExp over the N intermediate samples, which serves as a similarity score for retrieval or, in the RL setting, as the objective for selecting the optimal action given a language goal. No paired A-C data is required at inference time.

## Method

The core result (Lemma 1) shows that under conditional independence A⊥C|B and the density-ratio property of contrastive representations, the marginal probability ratio p(C|A)/p(C) equals an expectation over intermediate representations: E_{φ_B}[exp{f(φ_A,φ_B)+f(φ_B,φ_C)}]. Under the additional assumption that representations are uniform on the hypersphere (or isotropic Gaussian), this expectation admits a closed form (Lemma 2/3), proving that the direct dot-product or L2-distance between φ(A) and φ(C) is a monotone function of the true log probability ratio — the 'Law of the Unconscious Contrastive Learner.' When the marginal-distribution assumption fails, the paper provides a practical Monte Carlo LogSumExp algorithm that approximates the expectation by sampling N intermediate representations, requiring only Assumptions 1–2 and working with pre-trained or black-box contrastive models of arbitrary architecture. The approach is validated on CLIP/CLAP for zero-shot audio-visual retrieval (62% vs. 14% Recall@10) and on language-conditioned navigation in PointMaze, where it outperforms direct comparison by 20–30% by preserving the full distribution over ambiguous goal states.

## Results

On AudioSet for zero-shot audio-visual alignment, the LogSumExp Monte Carlo method over pre-trained CLIP and CLAP achieves 62% Recall@10, substantially outperforming the naive direct dot-product baseline (14% Recall@10). LanguageBind's direct evaluation (which implicitly assumes the 'Law') reaches 70% Recall@10, while LogSumExp applied to LanguageBind's own encoders achieves 58% Recall@10 (the gap closes with more Monte Carlo samples). In language-conditioned reinforcement learning, LogSumExp boosts navigation success rates by 20–30% over direct representation comparison across multiple maze environments.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its LogSumExp Monte Carlo method against four main baselines. (1) Direct dot-product evaluation between unpaired modality representations (the standard 'plug-and-play' heuristic) performs at or near chance level (14% vs. 62% Recall@10 on AudioSet), confirming that the 'Law' does not always hold without Assumption 3. (2) LanguageBind, which architecturally enforces the 'Law' by training all encoders through a shared language modality, achieves 70% Recall@10; LogSumExp on CLIP/CLAP reaches 62% without any additional training, and LogSumExp on LanguageBind's own encoders reaches 58% (closing to 70% with more Monte Carlo samples per Appendix C.1). (3) In the synthetic pre-trained model bridging experiment, direct evaluation between φ_A and φ_C is at chance, while LogSumExp approaches the oracle that was trained directly on (A,C) pairs. (4) In language-conditioned RL, direct representation comparison (as used in RoboCLIP, Text2Control, and similar methods) navigates toward the 'mean' of ambiguous language descriptions and takes suboptimal paths, while LogSumExp correctly identifies optimal actions, improving success rates by 20–30%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes two prior theoretical threads on contrastive learning — the probabilistic/density-ratio interpretation (Poole et al., 2019; Ma & Collins, 2018) and the geometric characterization of representation marginals (Wang & Isola, 2020; Eysenbach et al., 2024) — to provide the first rigorous justification for the widely-used 'plug-n-play' heuristic of directly comparing unpaired modality representations. It reframes cross-modal alignment as Bayesian marginalization over an intermediate modality, drawing an explicit analogy to message passing on a chain graphical model A↔B↔C. The practical LogSumExp algorithm and the RL application build on contrastive goal-conditioned RL (Eysenbach et al., 2023), but the key departure is showing that proper uncertainty quantification over intermediate states, rather than direct dot-product comparison, is needed when representation distributions are non-uniform.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides much-needed theoretical grounding for a heuristic that underpins much of modern multi-modal AI (CLIP, ImageBind, LanguageBind all implicitly rely on it), clarifying exactly when it is principled and when it silently fails. The LogSumExp method offers a practical, assumption-lighter alternative that enables combining disjoint pre-trained models without retraining — a significant practical benefit in domains where tri-modal datasets are scarce. Key open questions remain: the paper does not provide a diagnostic test for whether a given model's representations satisfy the uniformity/Gaussian assumptions, and the RL experiments, while illustrative, are on synthetic mazes rather than real robotic settings. The finding that the normalized dot product violates Assumption 2 yet still works well suggests the sufficient conditions identified may not be necessary, leaving room for tighter characterizations.
