## Problem

Practitioners lack a principled statistical test to determine whether a conditional generative model (CGM) is appropriate for a given in-context learning problem. Standard Bayesian model criticism tools such as posterior predictive checks require access to the model likelihood and posterior, which are not available for modern CGMs like LLMs that only expose a black-box predictive distribution. This gap leaves no way to assess whether a CGM will reliably solve a task or produce hallucinated, misaligned responses.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a set of n in-context query-response examples, a holdout test set, and a CGM accessed solely through its predictive distribution—i.e., the ability to generate token sequences and evaluate their per-token log probabilities. It outputs a scalar generative predictive p-value in [0,1] that, compared against a user-chosen significance level α, yields a binary verdict on model capability. No access to model weights, internal representations, or explicit likelihood/posterior is needed; the entire procedure is implemented via black-box generation and log-probability scoring.

## Method

The paper introduces the generative predictive p-value, which extends posterior predictive checks to CGMs by replacing intractable sampling from the Bayesian posterior and likelihood with ancestral sampling from the model's predictive distribution. The central theoretical result (Theorem 1) proves, via Doob's martingale convergence theorem, that the posterior predictive p-value equals a martingale predictive p-value defined over infinite dataset completions, establishing that a sufficiently large dataset generated under the model likelihood encodes the same information as the latent explanation. Practically, Algorithm 1 generates N−n additional examples by sequential ancestral sampling from p_θ(z,y|x^n) to form a dataset completion x^N that approximates a latent explanation, then samples replication data conditioned on x^N and evaluates a discrepancy function (per-token NLL or NLML) on both replication and test data. The p-value is the empirical fraction of M replications where the test discrepancy exceeds the replication discrepancy. The NLL discrepancy additionally signals whether the in-context dataset is large enough for reliable predictions, while the NLML discrepancy is computationally cheaper since it requires no dataset completion generation.

## Results

On tabular OOD detection, both the NLL and NLML discrepancy functions yield non-trivial OOD predictors (accuracy > 0.5) across all significance levels α ∈ [0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5] for Llama-2 regression models. On natural language tasks with Llama-2 7B, the NLL discrepancy achieves accuracy > 0.5 for all α settings, while the NLML discrepancy is generally robust except at small n and small α. On generative fill (SVHN/MNIST/CIFAR-10), both discrepancies achieve accuracy > 0.66 for all α settings. The NLL discrepancy with α = 0.05 yields the most accurate overall predictor, and the number of generated examples N−n smoothly interpolates the p-value between the NLML and ideal NLL estimates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, it evaluates two internal discrepancy functions (NLL and NLML) as capability predictors and benchmarks both against random guessing (accuracy = 0.5). The NLML discrepancy (which requires no dataset completion) achieves higher precision, meaning it is less likely to misclassify an in-capability task as unsolvable. The NLL discrepancy (which requires generating N−n additional examples) achieves higher recall, meaning it is less likely to miss an out-of-capability task. The NLL discrepancy with α = 0.05 is reported as the generally most accurate predictor across all three task domains. Additionally, the NLL discrepancy uniquely indicates whether the number of in-context examples n is sufficient for reliable prediction, reducing risk in autonomous recommendation settings even when overall accuracy is matched to the NLML predictor.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Bayesian model criticism framework—specifically posterior predictive checks (Rubin, 1984; Guttman, 1967; Moran et al., 2023)—to the setting of black-box conditional generative models that only expose the predictive distribution. It builds directly on the martingale perspective of ICL as Bayesian inference (Fong et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2023; Falck et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2021), which established that Bayesian functionals of the likelihood and posterior can be expressed as functionals of the predictive distribution. The key departure is providing a practical estimator (Algorithm 1) that replaces sampling from the posterior and likelihood with ancestral sampling of dataset completions from the predictive distribution, making the p-value computable for contemporary CGMs like LLMs without access to internal representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The generative predictive p-value gives practitioners a concrete, interpretable decision procedure for determining whether a given CGM is appropriate for a specific ICL problem—addressing a gap that hallucination-detection and uncertainty-quantification methods leave open, since those methods assume the model is already suitable. The finding that the NLL discrepancy also signals data sufficiency (not just model misalignment) is practically valuable for risk-sensitive deployment. Open questions include the formal characterization of estimation error between p_gpc and p_ppc for finite N, extensions beyond ICL to general generative tasks, and whether the identifiability assumption (Condition 3) holds for real-world CGMs. The method's computational cost—generating N−n examples per replicate—remains a practical barrier for very large models.
