## Problem

Existing weather forecasting datasets lack sufficient variety and resolution of extreme weather cases (ERA5 at 31 km vs. HRRR at 3 km), and state-of-the-art deep learning models such as Pangu, FuXi, and FourCastNet have either been tested on only a limited range of extreme events or lack extreme-weather evaluation entirely. This gap leaves a critical blind spot: extreme weather errors are significantly larger than overall forecast errors, yet no comprehensive high-resolution benchmark exists to quantify or improve them.

## Input / Output

The model consumes two consecutive hourly HRRR feature maps of shape (69, 320, 320), where 69 channels encode physical variables (pressure, temperature, wind components, humidity, geopotential height) across 13 pressure levels, and each 320×320 pixel grid corresponds to a 3 km × 3 km spatial cell over the U.S. The output is the predicted feature map at the next hour with the same 69-channel, 320×320 structure. Event areas are cropped into fixed 320×320 tiles with binary masks so that loss is computed only over the actual event region, and tiles reaching the sampling boundary are shifted inward rather than zero-padded.

## Method

HR-Heim follows an encoder–transformer–decoder architecture inspired by FuXi, but replaces the standard ViT decoder (MLP + 1×1 conv) with a progressive multi-step upsampler that resolves details at each intermediate resolution level using stacked convolutional and upsampling operations. The encoder employs causal convolutions borrowed from MagViTv2 to capture spatial-temporal structure in the two-frame input, followed by multiple stacked Swin Transformer V2 blocks. The accompanying HR-Extreme dataset is constructed from three complementary sources—NOAA Storm Events Database, NOAA Storm Prediction Center reports (clustered via DBSCAN to delineate event boundaries), and manually thresholded extreme-temperature masks—yielding 17 extreme weather types at 3 km resolution. All compared models (Pangu, FuXi, HR-Heim) are trained from scratch on identical HRRR data (Jan 2019–Jun 2020) under matched parameter budgets with no hyperparameter tuning or fine-tuning on HR-Extreme, ensuring a fair single-step (1-hour) prediction comparison.

## Results

On the original HRRR test set (July–Dec 2020), HR-Heim achieves an RMSE of 1.40, outperforming NWP (2.35), FuXi (2.39), and Pangu (2.77). On the HR-Extreme subset, HR-Heim's RMSE rises only to 1.60 (a 34.3% increase), while Pangu's RMSE surges to 10.42 (394.2% increase), FuXi's to 6.10 (121.8%), and NWP's to 3.27 (78.1%). Across all four lead times (1–4 hours), HR-Heim consistently outperforms NWP on HR-Extreme, e.g., 1.60 vs. 3.27 at 1-hour and 3.43 vs. 6.04 at 4-hour lead time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HR-Heim outperforms all three baselines on both the original HRRR test set and the HR-Extreme subset. Against Pangu, HR-Heim reduces RMSE from 2.77 to 1.40 on the original set and from 10.42 to 1.60 on HR-Extreme — a roughly 6.5× improvement in extreme-weather RMSE. Against FuXi, HR-Heim improves from 2.39 to 1.40 (original) and from 6.10 to 1.60 (HR-Extreme). Against the NWP system (WRF-ARWv3.9+), HR-Heim achieves lower RMSE on both sets (1.40 vs. 2.35 original; 1.60 vs. 3.27 HR-Extreme) and shows far greater robustness to extreme events, with only a 34.3% error increase versus NWP's 78.1%. The paper notes that NWP is more stable than Pangu and FuXi on extreme weather, but HR-Heim surpasses even NWP in both general and extreme-weather accuracy at 1-hour lead time.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of two lines of work: (1) high-resolution deep learning weather forecasting models (Pangu-Weather, FuXi, FourCastNet, GraphCast) that have largely been evaluated on ERA5 at 0.25° resolution, and (2) extreme weather datasets (ExtremeWeather, EWELD, ClimSim, ClimateNet, Liu et al. 2021a) that are limited in resolution, event-type coverage, or data format compatibility. The HR-Extreme dataset bridges these by providing 3-km HRRR feature maps covering 17 extreme weather types, while the HR-Heim model draws architecturally from FuXi's cascaded design and MagViTv2's causal convolutions, with a progressive-upscaling decoder as its main modification. The paper departs from prior work primarily in scope: no existing dataset simultaneously offers cloud-resolving resolution, multi-type extreme weather coverage, and ML-ready tensor format for evaluating both DL and NWP models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that extreme weather errors are dramatically larger than general forecast errors (Pangu's RMSE inflates 394%, FuXi 122%, NWP 78%)—quantifies a blind spot in current weather forecasting research and motivates dedicated extreme-event evaluation. The practical impact is significant: HR-Extreme (5 TiB, 17 event types, 3-km resolution) is the first dataset enabling systematic comparison of DL and NWP models on extreme weather at convection-allowing resolution, and the observation that NWP degrades less than DL models on extremes challenges the assumption that DL is uniformly catching up. Key limitations include US-only coverage, reliance on user reports for bounding boxes (introducing spatial uncertainty), exclusion of tropical cyclones, and single-step (1-hour) prediction only, leaving open whether the findings generalize to longer lead times, other regions, or fine-tuned models.
