## Problem

Long-term time series forecasting struggles with unseen patterns and data scarcity, and while data augmentation is widely used in classification, it is difficult to apply in forecasting because augmented past values do not have well-defined future ground-truth labels. This paper addresses how to leverage multi-resolution information from augmented, unlabeled data to improve forecasting without requiring explicit future values, and how to mitigate error accumulation when extending predictions beyond the trained horizon.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251571/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a multivariate time series look-back window of length L (96 for most datasets, 36 for ILI) and produces H future steps (96/192/336/720 or 24/36/48/60 for ILI). Internally, the look-back window is divided into patches at m different resolutions (e.g., patch sizes from {2,3,6,12,16,24,32}), each producing an individual prediction, plus one comprehensive prediction from an aggregation block. For horizon extension, the model supports both block-wise (predicting the full extended range in one step) and fine-grained (step-by-step with k=1 input update) autoregressive decoding.

## Method

CoMRes builds on a multi-resolution transformer architecture (similar to Pathformer) where m patch sizes create distinct views, each with intra-patch and inter-patch attention, followed by per-view MLPs and an equal-weighted aggregation. The key training innovation is a semi-supervised consensus promotion loss: on labeled data, the model minimizes standard MSE between all predictions and ground truth; on augmented unseen data (generated via time warping, interpolation, or noise injection), an unsupervised consistency loss encourages each individual view's prediction to match the comprehensive prediction, without requiring any future labels for the augmented data. This 'one model teaching others' mechanism reduces variance among views and improves generalization. The paper further demonstrates that block-wise autoregressive decoding with a short-horizon model (e.g., 96-step) can extend predictions to 768+ steps with less error accumulation than fine-grained step-by-step decoding, and that CoMRes's consensus training specifically reduces this error accumulation compared to purely supervised training.

## Results

On 8 standard multivariate forecasting benchmarks (input length L=96, L=36 for ILI), CoMRes (Time Warping variant) achieves the best average MSE on 7 out of 8 datasets, outperforming Pathformer by 0.003–0.011 MSE on ETTh1 (0.435 vs 0.439), ETTh2 (0.335 vs 0.344), ETTm1 (0.379 vs 0.382), ETTm2 (0.263 vs 0.273), Electricity (0.178 vs 0.182), Traffic (0.490 vs 0.501), and Weather (0.232 vs 0.239). The sole exception is ILI, where Pathformer achieves 1.563 vs CoMRes's 1.705. In autoregressive forecasting beyond the trained horizon (96+96 steps), CoMRes consistently reduces error accumulation, e.g., on ETTh1 block-wise AR it achieves 0.465 MSE vs 0.477 for MRes(SL), and on Weather 0.280 vs 0.285.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Pathformer (Chen et al., ICLR 2024), the primary architectural baseline, CoMRes wins on 7 of 8 datasets with margins of 0.003–0.011 MSE, losing only on ILI (1.705 vs 1.563) where the small dataset size (966 timesteps) limits the benefit of semi-supervised augmentation. Against TimeMixer (Wang et al., ICLR 2024), CoMRes achieves lower average MSE on 7 of 8 datasets (e.g., ETTh1: 0.435 vs 0.447; ETTm2: 0.263 vs 0.275), though TimeMixer's originally reported Traffic score (0.485) slightly edges CoMRes (0.490). Against PatchTST (Nie et al., ICLR 2023), CoMRes is consistently superior across all 8 datasets, with the largest gap on ETTm2 (0.263 vs 0.283) and Electricity (0.177 vs 0.205). NLinear (Zeng et al., AAAI 2023) is outperformed on all datasets, with particularly large margins on ETTm2 (0.263 vs 0.281) and Traffic (0.490 vs 0.624). Scaleformer (Shabani et al., ICLR 2023) and TiDE (Das et al., TMLR 2023) are both clearly outperformed across all benchmarks, with Scaleformer showing the weakest long-horizon performance (e.g., Weather 720-step: 0.640 vs CoMRes 0.334). The ablation MRes(SL) — the same architecture without semi-supervised learning — is beaten by CoMRes on 7 of 8 datasets, confirming that the consensus-promotion loss on augmented data, rather than the architecture alone, drives the improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Pathformer's multi-resolution architecture (multi-scale patch division with intra- and inter-patch attention) but replaces its adaptive pathways with a simpler equal-weighted aggregation, and introduces a semi-supervised training objective inspired by co-training (Blum & Mitchell, 1998). It departs from prior multi-scale forecasting methods (NHITS, Pyraformer, Scaleformer, TimeMixer) that integrate multi-resolution features exclusively on labeled data, by applying a consensus-promotion consistency loss on augmented unlabeled data. The key conceptual departure from prior time series augmentation work is the rejection of pseudo-labeling: rather than assigning future values to augmented inputs (which the paper shows degrades performance), it enforces agreement among individual-view models on the augmented data without any ground-truth signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to systematically investigate semi-supervised learning for long-term time series forecasting, addressing the practical challenge of data scarcity and overfitting in multi-resolution models. The finding that block-wise autoregressive prediction with a short-horizon (96-step) CoMRes model can approach the accuracy of a 720-step non-autoregressive model is practically valuable for deployment scenarios requiring very long horizons. However, improvements over strong baselines are modest (typically 0.001–0.03 MSE), the ILI dataset remains a clear failure case, and the consensus-promotion benefit appears architecture-dependent (gains on TimeMixer are smaller and less consistent). Open questions include whether the approach scales to higher-dimensional or non-stationary series, whether more sophisticated augmentation policies (e.g., Bayesian-optimized as in Nochumsohn & Azencot, 2025) could amplify the gains, and whether the semi-supervised signal remains useful when labeled data is abundant.
