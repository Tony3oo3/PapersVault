## Problem

Existing zero-sum game theory and algorithms are largely restricted to symmetric games or asymmetric matrix games with finite strategy spaces for all players. A broad and practically important class of asymmetric games—where one player optimizes over a combinatorial (exponentially large) space and the other over an infinite compact continuous space—lacks both a proof of Nash equilibrium existence and convergence-guaranteed solution algorithms. This paper defines this class (ACCES games) and answers whether and how they can be solved.

## Input / Output

The method takes a combinatorial optimization instance encoded as a graph (nodes with attributes such as coverage radius, customer demand, or target prize) plus a set of environmental parameters (weather vectors, transportation conditions) that the adversary controls as a continuous vector in [0,1]^K. The output is a mixed Nash equilibrium strategy pair: a probability distribution over feasible combinatorial paths for Player 1 and a Borel probability measure over the continuous parameter space for Player 2, which in practice yields an optimal route and an adversarial perturbation vector. The utility function is continuous in the continuous player's strategy but inherently discontinuous in the combinatorial player's, a structural asymmetry that invalidates standard convergence arguments.

## Method

The core algorithm, CCDO, extends Double Oracle to ACCES games by iteratively solving the mixed NE in a growing subgame and expanding it with best responses, but replaces the standard DO stopping criterion (unchanged strategy sets) with a utility-gap test that accommodates the infinite continuous strategy space. For practical deployment, CCDO-RL replaces exact best responses with RL-based approximate best responses: Player 1 uses REINFORCE with an attention-based encoder-decoder (Kool et al. 2018) to learn a generalizable path policy, while Player 2 uses PPO to optimize over the continuous parameter space. A key practical innovation is the CCDOA discriminant mechanism, which detects when an approximate best response performs worse than the current subgame equilibrium—a phenomenon absent in exact-BR DO—and prevents suboptimal strategy additions. The theoretical foundation rests on proving weak sequential compactness of the joint mixed strategy space, continuity of the expected utility function, and NE existence via essentially finite game approximation (following Myerson 1991), with convergence guarantees for both exact (CCDO) and approximate (CCDO-RL) variants.

## Results

CCDO-RL converges to approximate Nash equilibrium within 25 iterations, reaching exploitability of 0.05 (ACSP), 0.10 (ACVRP), and 0.03 (PG) on 20-node instances. On seen graphs, CCDO-RL achieves a 10.08% average reward improvement over both baselines across all six problem-size settings. The largest gains are in ACSP (46.98% over Heuristic, 7.14% over RL-against-Stoc at 20 nodes), while improvements in ACVRP are more modest (0.75–1.72% over Heuristic). On unseen graphs, CCDO-RL maintains its advantage, confirming generalizability without retraining.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CCDO-RL against two baselines: (1) problem-specific heuristics (Tabu Search for ACVRP, LS2 local search for ACSP, Greedy for PG) and (2) RL-against-Stoc, which uses the same attention-based REINFORCE protagonist but is trained against stochastic adversarial perturbations rather than the learned PPO adversary. CCDO-RL outperforms the Heuristic baseline on all six settings, with the most dramatic margin in ACSP (46.98% at 20 nodes, 45.91% at 50 nodes) and a solid 10.60% in PG-50. Against RL-against-Stoc, CCDO-RL also wins on all six settings, with improvements ranging from 1.48% (PG-20) to 7.14% (ACSP-20). The paper notes that in combinatorial optimization, even sub-1% margins over strong RL baselines are considered meaningful, and that CCDO-RL's advantage is consistent across problem types and scales. On unseen test graphs (generalizability), CCDO-RL retains its lead over both baselines without any retraining, with improvements of up to 46.61% over Heuristic and 10.33% over RL-against-Stoc.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Double Oracle (DO) framework from McMahan et al. (2003), originally designed for finite zero-sum matrix games, to a newly defined class of asymmetric games (ACCES) where one player has a combinatorial strategy space and the other has an infinite compact continuous space. The NE existence proof builds on Myerson's (1991) essentially finite game approximation technique, while the practical solver (CCDO-RL) repurposes the attention-based encoder-decoder architecture from Kool et al. (2018) as the combinatorial best-response oracle. The key departure from prior work is that existing asymmetric game studies (security games, multi-VRP) assumed finite action spaces for all players, and continuous game analyses (Balandat et al., 2016) assumed symmetric players—this paper is the first to formally handle the mixed combinatorial-continuous asymmetry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal existence and convergence guarantees for a practically relevant class of adversarial COPs where environmental parameters are controlled by an adversary, bridging a gap between game theory and combinatorial optimization. The convergence analysis with approximate best responses (ABRs) is particularly important given the NP-hardness of the underlying COPs, and the conditions under which CCDO-RL terminates finitely (Theorem 3) offer practical guidance. However, scalability remains a significant limitation—experiments are restricted to 20 and 50 nodes—and the empirical improvements over strong RL baselines (RL against Stoc) are modest (1–7%), suggesting the method's practical edge over existing adversarial RL approaches is still narrow. Open questions include uniqueness of NE in ACCES games, scalability to larger instances, and whether the RL-based ABRs can be replaced by more principled approximate algorithms with tighter convergence guarantees.
