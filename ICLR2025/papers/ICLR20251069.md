## Problem

Existing sports QA benchmarks are fragmented: text-based datasets (e.g., SportQA, BIG-bench) test rule and factual recall but lack visual reasoning and human-annotated explanations, while video-based datasets (e.g., Sports-QA, SoccerNet-XFoul) cover only single sports or focus on action recognition without multi-level rule-based reasoning. No prior benchmark jointly evaluates MLLMs on both pure-text sports reasoning and multi-level video understanding (from simple recognition to foul detection) across multiple sports with tiered difficulty.

## Input / Output

SPORTU-text takes 900 text-only multiple-choice questions (each with one or more correct answers and per-option human-annotated explanations) and expects a letter answer plus optional rationale. SPORTU-video takes 1,701 slow-motion clips (300 with multi-angle views) across 7 sports, presented as extracted frames (10 frames for GPT/Claude, 16 for most open-source models, full video for Gemini) paired with 12,048 QA pairs (10,973 MC + 1,075 open-ended) at three difficulty levels (Easy 25.4%, Medium 50.2%, Hard 24.4%). Outputs range from a single MC letter to free-text foul explanations, with three prompting strategies (X→Y, X→RY, X→YR) controlling whether a rationale precedes or follows the answer.

## Method

SPORTU is constructed in two parts. SPORTU-text repurposes 900 questions from SportQA, re-categorized by expert annotators into rule-, strategy-, and scenario-based types, with detailed per-option explanations added. SPORTU-video is built from 1,701 manually cropped slow-motion replay clips (chosen because fouls involve brief, subtle actions invisible at real-time speed), annotated by 9 domain experts (2 intercollegiate athletes, 7 trained players) who label ground-truth fouls, roles, and jersey colors; a template-based system then generates MC distractors and open-ended questions from these labels. Evaluation uses in-context learning (zero-shot and five-shot with/without CoT) for text, and three prompting strategies for video, with 14 MLLMs (6 proprietary, 8 open-source) assessed via accuracy, ROUGE-L, BERTScore, BLEURT, CTC-Preservation, and G-Eval (validated against human ratings on 140 questions). A key empirical finding is that direct-answer prompting (X→Y) consistently outperforms rationale-first (X→RY) and answer-then-rationale (X→YR) strategies, with Claude-3.5-Sonnet's hard-level accuracy dropping from 52.57% to 39.32% when forced to reason first.

## Results

On SPORTU-text (900 MCQs), GPT-4o achieves the highest accuracy of 71.0% under the 5-shot CoT setting, followed by Llama3.1-405B and Claude-3.5-Sonnet (both 65.22%) and Gemini-1.5 Pro (61.22%), all well below the >90% human expert baseline. On SPORTU-video (12,048 QA pairs across 7 sports), Qwen2-VL-72B attains the highest overall multiple-choice accuracy of 70.94% (X→Y), but on hard-level tasks requiring rule-based reasoning, GPT-4o leads with only 57.84% (X→YR) and Claude-3.5-Sonnet reaches 53.06% (X→Y). For open-ended foul-explanation questions, no model exceeds a G-Eval score of 1.84 (GPT-4o) or a human rating of 2.51, indicating a substantial gap in connecting visual actions to domain-specific rules.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On SPORTU-text, GPT-4o (71.0% 5-shot CoT) outperforms Claude-3.5-Sonnet (65.22%), Llama3.1-405B (65.22%), and Gemini-1.5 Pro (61.22%) by 5.8–9.8 percentage points, though all remain far below the >90% human expert level. On SPORTU-video overall accuracy, Qwen2-VL-72B (70.94%) edges out Claude-3.5-Sonnet (70.18%) and GPT-4o (68.79%), while among open-source 7B models LLaVA-NeXT (63.72%) leads. However, on hard-level tasks (rule violation detection), GPT-4o (57.84%) surpasses Claude-3.5-Sonnet (53.06%) and Qwen2-VL-72B (44.12%), revealing that overall accuracy is driven by easy/medium questions where all models perform well. For open-ended explanations, GPT-4o achieves the best G-Eval (1.84) and human rating (2.51), followed by Qwen2-VL-72B (1.76 / 2.08) and Claude-3.5-Sonnet (1.70 / 2.13), with all 7B open-source models scoring below 1.65 G-Eval. A notable finding is that the X→Y (direct answer) prompting consistently outperforms X→RY (reason-then-answer) across nearly all models, with Claude-3.5-Sonnet dropping from 70.18% to 55.08% and GPT-4o from 68.79% to 65.56%, indicating that current models' chain-of-thought reasoning introduces errors rather than improving accuracy on sports tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of sports QA benchmarks by unifying text-based rule comprehension (building on SportQA, which lacked explanations) and video-based multi-level reasoning (building on Sports-QA and SoccerNet-XFoul, which were limited to single sports or lacked rule-based tasks) into a single multi-modal, multi-sport benchmark. It departs from prior work by introducing human-annotated explanations for every question option, slow-motion clips to capture subtle fouls, multi-camera angle variants, and a three-tier difficulty structure (easy/medium/hard) that explicitly separates commonsense recognition from deep rule-based reasoning. The evaluation methodology draws on standard MLLM prompting strategies (few-shot, CoT) and LLM-as-judge evaluation (G-Eval), positioning the benchmark within the broader MLLM evaluation literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SPORTU provides the first comprehensive, multi-sport, multi-modal benchmark for evaluating MLLMs on sports understanding, filling a clear gap where prior datasets were either text-only without explanations or video-only with narrow sport coverage. The finding that even GPT-4o achieves only 57.8% on hard video tasks and that CoT prompting consistently degrades performance (e.g., Claude-3.5-Sonnet drops from 52.57% to 39.32%) highlights a fundamental gap in connecting visual action perception with domain-specific rule knowledge. Open questions include developing better frame extraction strategies for sports-specific reasoning, creating domain-specific evaluation metrics (current metrics show weak correlation with human judgment, Pearson r=0.41), and improving model consistency across camera angles. The benchmark is likely to become a standard reference for sports-domain MLLM evaluation.
