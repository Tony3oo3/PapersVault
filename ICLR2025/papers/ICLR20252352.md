## Problem

Designing mechanically efficient shapes for long-span architectural structures (masonry shells, cable-net towers) requires solving an inverse problem: finding bar stiffness values that produce a shape matching a designer's target while satisfying force equilibrium. Traditional form-finding combined with per-shape optimization is too slow for interactive design, and fully neural surrogates (including PINNs) cannot guarantee that predictions respect the underlying physics, risking mechanically infeasible or even dangerous designs. The paper asks whether a hybrid architecture can amortize the inverse problem in real time while enforcing mechanical integrity by construction.

## Input / Output

The model takes a target shape as an N×3 matrix of node positions (sampled from task-specific parametric families: doubly symmetric Bezier patches for shells with N=100, M=180; parametric ring geometries for towers with N=335, M=656) along with boundary conditions b encoding loads and fixed-node positions. It outputs an M-dimensional bar stiffness vector q and, via the differentiable decoder, the corresponding equilibrium shape X (N×3). The encoder's last layer applies a strictly positive nonlinearity (Softplus) scaled by a fixed force-direction vector s ∈ {−1,1}^M, so tension/compression assignment is architectural rather than learned, and a scalar shift τ enforces a minimum stiffness bound for numerical stability.

## Method

The architecture is an autoencoder where a multilayer perceptron encoder maps the target shape to stiffness space, and a differentiable Force Density Method (FDM) simulator serves as the analytical decoder, solving the linear equilibrium system X(q) = K(q)⁻¹P to produce the output shape. Because the decoder is a physics solver rather than a learned network, mechanical equilibrium is satisfied exactly by construction—residual forces are zero within numerical precision regardless of encoder accuracy—shifting the paradigm from physics-informed (learning physics via loss terms) to physics-in-the-loop (embedding the solver directly). Training is end-to-end via reverse-mode automatic differentiation in JAX: the shape loss backpropagates through the differentiable linear solve to update only the encoder parameters. For the ill-conditioned cable-net tower task (mixed tension/compression), the authors add global gradient norm clipping (0.01) and a variance regularizer on stiffness distributions to stabilize the backward pass. The model achieves ~0.6 ms inference for shells and ~4.6 ms for towers—up to four orders of magnitude faster than direct optimization—while matching or exceeding the accuracy of fully neural baselines and generalizing better out-of-distribution than a PINN counterpart.

## Results

On the masonry shell task, the proposed model achieves a shape loss of 3.0±2.0 with zero physics loss (exact equilibrium) in 0.6 ms, compared to 5570 ms for direct optimization (shape loss 0.8±1.2). On the more complex cable-net tower task, the model reaches a shape loss of 0.6±0.7 with zero physics loss in 4.6 ms, versus 1686 ms for expert-initialized optimization (shape loss 0.3±0.3). When the model's predictions are used to initialize direct optimization, the combined approach achieves the best shape loss of 0.2±0.3, outperforming expert initialization. The model also demonstrates superior out-of-distribution generalization: at full asymmetry (δ=1), its shape loss is 2.5× lower than the PINN's while maintaining zero physics loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four baselines: a fully neural autoencoder (NN), a physics-informed variant (PINN), and direct gradient-based optimization (SLSQP for shells, L-BFGS-B for towers). The NN and PINN are fastest (0.3–0.5 ms) but fail to satisfy mechanical equilibrium: the NN's physics loss is 104.3 (shells) and 45.8 (towers), and the PINN's is 0.6 and 1.2 respectively, meaning their predicted shapes are mechanically infeasible. The proposed model matches the NN/PINN in speed (0.6 ms / 4.6 ms) while guaranteeing zero physics loss by construction, and achieves shape losses comparable to or slightly above optimization (3.0 vs 0.8 for shells; 0.6 vs 0.3 for towers) at 3–4 orders of magnitude lower wall time. In the towers task, using the model's output as an initialization for L-BFGS-B yields the best shape loss (0.2±0.3), beating even expert-initialized optimization (0.3±0.3), demonstrating that the neural surrogate provides a superior starting point for refinement. On out-of-distribution generalization (interpolating toward asymmetric shells), the model's shape loss degrades at half the rate of the PINN and remains 2.5× lower at full asymmetry, while the PINN's physics loss increases by two orders of magnitude.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of differentiable simulation and amortized optimization, building directly on the JAX FDM differentiable force-density solver (Pastrana et al., 2023b) and the classical force density method (Schek, 1974) as its analytical decoder. Conceptually, it departs from the physics-informed neural network (PINN) paradigm by replacing learned physics with an explicit simulator in the loop, shifting from 'physics-informed' to 'physics-in-the-loop' modeling. It also extends the line of amortized inverse-design work (Oktay et al., 2023; Favilli et al., 2024; Tam, 2023) by demonstrating that a compact MLP encoder coupled with an analytical decoder outperforms fully neural autoencoders of similar architecture on both accuracy and out-of-distribution generalization for structural form-finding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that embedding a differentiable physics simulator as the decoder provides hard mechanical-integrity guarantees and superior OOD generalization compared to PINNs, which is critical for safety-critical structural design where prediction errors can lead to collapse. The practical impact is validated through real-time deployment in Rhino3D and fabrication of a physical masonry shell prototype, bridging the gap between ML research and built-environment practice. Key limitations include restriction to a single topology (requiring retraining for different discretizations), task-specific parameter spaces, and sensitivity to ill-conditioning in mixed tension-compression systems that necessitates gradient clipping. Open questions include whether graph-network encoders can generalize across topologies and whether variational formulations can produce diverse valid solutions for a single target.
