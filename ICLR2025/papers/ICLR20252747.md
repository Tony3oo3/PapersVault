## Problem

The standard Top-K routing scheme in Mixture-of-Experts models suffers from two structural limitations: it forces a fixed number of expert activations per token, leading to unnecessary computations where some activated experts actually degrade performance, and it restricts gate values to be non-negative, preventing the router from exploiting the potential benefit of inverting expert outputs. Prior work has focused on modifying the routing mechanism itself, but this paper instead asks whether expanding the expert space can resolve these issues without changing the routing scheme.

## Input / Output

Given a hidden representation vector h ∈ R^d from a transformer layer, the router computes a probability distribution over an expanded expert space of 2N+K experts (where N is the number of original FFN experts and K is the top-K activation count). The output is the standard weighted sum of activated expert outputs, which feeds into the next transformer layer for next-token prediction. Unusually, the expanded space includes parameter-free zero experts (E^0) that contribute no computation and sign-flipped experts (E^{-1}) that share all parameters with their positive counterparts, so the only additional cost is a slightly larger router weight matrix of size (2N+K)×d.

## Method

TC-MoE expands each original expert E_i into three variants {E_i^{-1}, E_i^0, E_i^1} via multiplication by the ternary set {-1,0,1}, yielding 2N+K total experts while keeping the standard Top-K router unchanged. E_i^1 and E_i^{-1} share parameters with E_i, and only K copies of the parameter-free E_i^0 are retained (sufficient for the router to activate 0 to K of them). Two new losses are introduced: a redesigned load balance loss that balances only the E_i^1/E_i^{-1} pairs (since E^0 incurs no compute and is excluded from balancing), and a reward loss that assigns a negative gradient to E^0 gate values, encouraging the router to route excess gate mass to these free experts. The reward loss hyperparameter α_2 provides a continuous knob for trading off efficiency (fewer activated experts) against effectiveness (higher benchmark accuracy), and the E^0 experts function analogously to attention sinks, absorbing gate values that would otherwise be wasted on unhelpful activations.

## Results

On the base model pre-trained on RedPajama, TC-MoE achieves an average accuracy of 49.54% across 9 benchmarks, surpassing the Top-K baseline (48.71%) by 0.83%, Random drop (48.66%) by 0.88%, and Top-P (48.83%) by 0.71%, while reducing the average number of activated experts from 2.00 to 1.82 (9% fewer) and FLOPs by 6.5%. On the FineWeb dataset, improvements are larger: TC-MoE gains 1.18% over Top-K on the tiny model (49.93% vs 48.75%) and 1.17% on the base model (52.61% vs 51.44%). Under varying computational budgets (Figure 3), TC-MoE consistently outperforms Random drop and Top-P, reducing language modeling loss by ~0.017 and improving HellaSwag accuracy by up to 0.7%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TC-MoE is compared against three baselines: standard Top-K routing, Random drop (probabilistically skipping the second-highest expert), and Top-P (threshold-based dynamic routing from Huang et al., 2024). Against the Top-K baseline, TC-MoE wins on average across all four model/dataset configurations (gains of 0.83%, 0.96%, 1.18%, and 1.17%), while simultaneously activating fewer experts (e.g., 1.82 vs 2.00 for the base model). Against Top-P, TC-MoE achieves a higher average accuracy (49.54% vs 48.83% on RedPajama base) with a lower average K (1.82 vs 1.99) and greater FLOPs savings (6.5% vs 0.3%), indicating Top-P's threshold mechanism provides minimal efficiency benefit. Against Random drop, TC-MoE also leads on average (49.54% vs 48.66%) despite a similar average K (1.82 vs 1.85), showing that learned ternary choices outperform random expert skipping. Notably, TC-MoE wins on 5 of 9 individual benchmarks (ARC-Easy, HellaSwag, OpenBookQA, SIQA, WinoGrande) for the RedPajama base model, while Top-P edges ahead on BoolQ and MMLU by small margins (<0.5%). Under varying computational budgets (Figure 3), TC-MoE maintains a consistent ~0.017 LM-loss advantage and up to 0.7% HellaSwag advantage over both Random drop and Top-P across all activation budgets tested.

## Relation to Prior Work

TC-MoE departs from the dominant line of MoE research that improves routing mechanisms (Expert Choice routing, threshold-based dynamic routers) by instead expanding the expert space itself through ternary multiplication of each expert with {-1, 0, 1}, while retaining the standard Top-K router. Conceptually, it draws on ternary quantization ideas and the heterogeneous-experts literature (null experts in AdaMoE, heavy/light branches in COLT5), but unifies these into a single principled construction where the zero-expert acts as a computational 'sink' analogous to attention sinks. The redesigned load balance loss and reward loss are novel auxiliary objectives tailored to the asymmetric structure of the expanded space (parameter-sharing pairs vs. parameter-free experts).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a simple expert-space expansion can recover both unnecessary activations and underutilized expert capacity without changing the routing algorithm, yielding a ~1% accuracy gain with ~9% fewer activated experts across multiple model sizes and datasets. However, all experiments are conducted on models up to 2.3B total parameters trained on 100B tokens, leaving open whether the benefits persist at the 100B+ parameter scale where MoE architectures are most impactful. The reward loss provides a useful knob for efficiency-effectiveness trade-offs, but the optimal α₂ may be dataset- and scale-dependent. A key open question is whether the ternary expansion interacts favorably with other recent MoE innovations (e.g., fine-grained expert granularity, expert parallelism at scale) or whether the router's increased decision space becomes harder to optimize as N grows.
