## Problem

State-of-the-art approximate machine unlearning methods are widely assumed to be able to remove the influence of specific training data from a model, but no prior work has systematically tested whether they can actually eliminate the effects of data poisoning attacks. The paper shows that standard membership inference attacks (MIAs) used to validate unlearning give a misleadingly optimistic picture, and that none of eight evaluated unlearning algorithms can fully remove targeted, indiscriminate, backdoor, or Gaussian data poisoning from ResNet-18 or GPT-2 models even when granted 10% of the original training compute.

## Input / Output

The evaluation pipeline takes a model trained on a corrupted dataset (ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10 32×32×3 images, or GPT-2 355M fine-tuned on IMDb text with 128-token sequences) along with the exact poison samples and, for Gaussian poisoning, the stored per-sample noise vectors ξ_z. The unlearning algorithm receives the forget set U = S_poison and a compute budget capped at 10% of initial training. Outputs include the updated model, test accuracy compared against a retraining-from-scratch benchmark, and for Gaussian poisoning the Gaussian Unlearning Score (GUS) reported as TPR@FPR=0.01, which requires computing input-space gradients ∇_x ℓ(θ, (x_base, y)) at the clean base images corresponding to each poison sample.

## Method

The paper introduces a four-step evaluation protocol that uses data poisoning as a ground-truth test for unlearning: (1) generate a corrupted training set via targeted (Gradient Matching / instruction poisoning), indiscriminate (Gradient Canceling), or Gaussian (i.i.d. N(0, ε_p²I_d) noise) poisoning; (2) train a model on the corrupted set; (3) run one of eight unlearning algorithms (GD, NGD, GA, EUk, CFk, SCRUB, NegGrad+, SSD) on the full poison set; (4) measure residual poisoning effects. The key novel contribution is the Gaussian data poisoning attack and its associated Gaussian Unlearning Score (GUS), which computes the normalized inner product ⟨g_z, ξ_z⟩/(ε_p‖g_z‖₂) between the model's input-space gradient at the clean base image and the stored noise vector. Under perfect unlearning this statistic follows N(0,1), so deviation from zero mean indicates residual poisoning; this is framed as a hypothesis test (H₀: model independent of ξ) that is computationally cheap—requiring only one training run—unlike shadow-model-based MIAs. The paper further validates two hypotheses for why unlearning fails: poison samples induce a larger model shift than random clean samples, and the shift lies in a subspace approximately orthogonal to the gradient directions available from clean-data updates.

## Results

Across all eight unlearning methods evaluated on CIFAR-10 (ResNet-18) and IMDb (GPT-2 355M), none successfully removes the effects of data poisoning to match retraining from scratch. On indiscriminate poisoning (Table 1), GD achieves the best post-unlearning test accuracy of 87.71% at 10 epochs with 1.5% poison, still 1.53 points below the 89.24% retraining benchmark; SCRUB is competitive at 85.48%. NGD and GA collapse to random-guessing (10% accuracy) on indiscriminate poisoning, while EUk achieves only 43–51%. For Gaussian and targeted poisoning (Figures 3–4), every method—including GD and SCRUB—fails to restore the model to its pre-poisoning behavior, with the Gaussian Unlearning Score (GUS) remaining significantly above the perfect-unlearning baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GD (Neel et al., 2021) is the strongest unlearning method on indiscriminate poisoning for CIFAR-10, reaching 87.71% at 10 epochs with 1.5% poison, but still trails the 89.24% retraining benchmark and fails entirely on targeted and Gaussian poisoning. SCRUB (Kurmanji et al., 2024) is competitive on indiscriminate poisoning (85.48% at 10 epochs, 1.5%) and somewhat mitigates Gaussian poisoning on text, but completely fails on targeted and backdoor attacks. NGD (Chien et al., 2024; Chourasia & Shah, 2023) and GA (Graves et al., 2021; Jang et al., 2022) both collapse to random-guessing (10% accuracy) on indiscriminate poisoning and fail on all other poison types. EUk (Goel et al., 2022) performs worst overall, achieving only 29–51% on indiscriminate poisoning. SSD (Foster et al., 2024) is evaluated but does not appear in the main Table 1; it is included in the broader Figures 3–4 comparisons where it also fails to remove poisons. The standard MIA (Shokri et al., 2017) is shown to be insufficient: it reports low TPR at FPR=0.01, giving a false sense of security, whereas the proposed Gaussian poisoning MIA reveals that all methods retain detectable traces of the poison.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes data poisoning as an evaluation tool for machine unlearning, extending the brief foray into poisoning-based verification by Sommer et al. (2022) and Goel et al. (2024) into a systematic stress test of eight state-of-the-art unlearning algorithms. It departs from the dominant evaluation paradigm of membership inference attacks (both loss-based and shadow-model-based) by introducing the Gaussian Unlearning Score, a computationally efficient metric that requires only a single training run and no shadow models. Conceptually, the paper positions itself at the intersection of the data poisoning literature (Gradient Matching, Gradient Canceling, backdoor attacks) and the approximate unlearning literature (GD, NGD, GA, EUk, CFk, SCRUB, NegGrad+, SSD), showing that the two communities' assumptions about what 'removing data influence' means are incompatible in practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that no current approximate unlearning method can remove the effects of data poisoning—even with a generous 10% compute budget—challenges a commonly cited application of unlearning and suggests that heuristic methods without provable guarantees may provide a false sense of security. The Gaussian Unlearning Score offers a practical, domain-agnostic evaluation tool that is orders of magnitude cheaper than shadow-model-based MIAs, making rigorous unlearning auditing more accessible. Open questions include whether any approximate unlearning method can succeed under these attacks, whether the failure is fundamental (the orthogonal-subspace hypothesis) or merely a compute-budget issue, and how to design unlearning algorithms that provably remove adversarial data influence without full retraining.
