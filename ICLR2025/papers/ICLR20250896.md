## Problem

Generating realistic DAGs is challenging because they encode both directional dependencies (partial order of nodes) and logical dependencies (e.g., dimension matching in matrix operations), and existing generative models either impose arbitrary node orderings that violate the DAG's inductive bias or ignore directionality entirely. Moreover, prior DAG generators are limited to small graphs (≤24 nodes) for neural architecture search, while system and hardware benchmarking requires generating much larger flow graphs (up to ~400 nodes) with strong logical constraints.

## Input / Output

The model accepts a target performance label (e.g., TPU runtime, FPGA LUT usage, mobile CPU inference latency) as a conditioning signal via sinusoidal embeddings, and produces a complete attributed DAG as a sequence of bipartite graph layers. Generation is sequential: at each step it predicts the number of new nodes, then their categorical attributes (e.g., operator types), then the directed edges connecting them to all previously generated nodes. The process terminates when the predicted layer size is zero, yielding a DAG of arbitrary size.

## Method

LayerDAG introduces a unique layerwise tokenization that decomposes any DAG into an ordered sequence of bipartite graphs based on the partial order of nodes (source nodes form layer 1, nodes whose predecessors are all in prior layers form subsequent layers). This enables a factorization where autoregressive generation across layers models directional dependencies, while discrete denoising diffusion (D3PM) within each layer captures logical dependencies among incomparable nodes. The architecture uses a bidirectional MPNN encoder for context, a transformer without positional encodings for set-level denoising of node attributes and edges, and a layer-index-based denoising schedule that linearly increases diffusion steps with layer depth to handle growing complexity. The model is provably permutation invariant by construction, and all modules share parameters across layers for efficient training with teacher forcing.

## Results

On the synthetic LP dataset with the strictest logical constraint (ρ=0), LayerDAG achieves 56% validity, substantially outperforming D-VAE (27%), GraphRNN (25%), GraphPNAS (23%), and OneShotDAG (37%). On real-world conditional generation evaluated via ML-based surrogate models, LayerDAG achieves Pearson correlation of 0.65 (TPU Tile), 0.85 (HLS), and 0.990 (NA-Edge), with MAE of 1.2, 1.1, and 0.9 respectively, consistently the best across all three platforms. For label generalization to an unseen quantile (extrapolation), LayerDAG achieves Pearson 0.22 while all baselines fail to reach positive correlation (best baseline: GraphPNAS at 0.02).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LayerDAG outperforms all three external baselines (D-VAE, GraphRNN, GraphPNAS) and its own non-autoregressive ablation (OneShotDAG) across every evaluation setting. On the LP synthetic dataset with strict logical rules (ρ=0), LayerDAG's 56% validity exceeds D-VAE (27%) by ~29 points, GraphRNN (25%) by ~31 points, and GraphPNAS (23%) by ~33 points, demonstrating that neither nodewise autoregression nor mixture-of-Bernoulli set modeling can capture hard constraints. On real-world conditional generation, LayerDAG leads on TPU Tile (Pearson 0.65 vs. GraphRNN's 0.62 and D-VAE's 0.50), HLS (0.85 vs. D-VAE's 0.82 and GraphRNN's 0.79), and NA-Edge (0.990 vs. GraphRNN's 0.980 and D-VAE's 0.877), with the largest margin over GraphPNAS (0.24 on TPU Tile, 0.66 on HLS, 0.619 on NA-Edge). The OneShotDAG ablation (removing autoregression) confirms the necessity of the sequential layerwise component: it trails LayerDAG by 0.09 Pearson on TPU Tile and 0.12 on NA-Edge. In the challenging label-extrapolation setting, LayerDAG is the only method achieving positive Pearson correlation (0.22), while D-VAE, GraphRNN, GraphPNAS, and OneShotDAG all produce near-zero or negative correlations.

## Relation to Prior Work

LayerDAG builds on the autoregressive DAG generation line (D-VAE, GraphPNAS) and discrete diffusion for graphs (DiGress, D3PM), but departs from both in a key way: prior autoregressive models tokenize DAGs via topological orderings (one node or fixed-size node set per step), which imposes an artificial order on nodes that are incomparable under the partial order. LayerDAG instead uses the unique layerwise partition into bipartite graphs as its tokenization, preserving the DAG's inherent structure, and applies diffusion within each layer to capture logical dependencies among incomparable nodes. This is the first combination of autoregressive and diffusion components specifically for DAG generation, and the first to scale to ~400-node flow graphs for system benchmarking (prior work targets ≤24 nodes for NAS).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for computing-systems and hardware-design communities: synthetic DAGs generated by LayerDAG can train ML surrogate models that predict TPU runtime, FPGA resource usage, and edge-device inference latency without requiring access to proprietary workloads, enabling cross-vendor benchmarking and co-design. The permutation-invariance guarantee and the layer-index-based denoising schedule offer principled controls over generalization and the quality-efficiency trade-off. Open questions include whether the approach scales to thousands of nodes (e.g., full LLM training graphs), how to handle real-valued node attributes natively, and whether the linear denoising schedule is optimal for all DAG topologies.
