## Problem

Scaling law studies that guide foundation model training decisions frequently reach contradictory conclusions (e.g., Kaplan et al. vs. Hoffmann et al. on the optimal data-to-parameter ratio) because critical procedural details—power law form, training hyperparameters, checkpoint selection, and optimization settings—are underreported or vary across papers. No prior work systematically quantifies how each individual choice in the fitting pipeline alters the resulting scaling law, nor provides a standardized reporting framework.

## Input / Output

The empirical analysis consumes three sources of training data: 245 datapoints reconstructed from Hoffmann et al.'s PDF figures (via Besiroglu et al.), training losses from Porian et al.'s 5M–901M parameter models on OpenWebText2/RefinedWeb, and the authors' own 12M–1B parameter Transformer LMs trained on FineWeb with the GPT-NeoX-20B tokenizer. Metrics are extracted from final or mid-training checkpoints (perplexity on C4 validation), and the pipeline outputs fitted power law parameters (A, B, E, α, β), recommended optimal D/N ratios per compute budget, and a structured reproducibility checklist covering four categories: scaling law hypothesis, training setup, data collection, and fitting algorithm.

## Method

The paper combines a systematic survey of 51 scaling law papers—categorizing each by power law form, training configuration, data extraction protocol, and curve-fitting method—with a controlled empirical study that isolates the effect of each pipeline decision. The authors train 11 Transformer architectures (12M–1B parameters, 5–26 layers, SwiGLU + RMSNorm + RoPE) across swept learning rates and data budgets, then fit power laws using L-BFGS with Huber loss on log-loss as the baseline (matching Hoffmann et al.'s 4500-point grid initialization). They systematically vary one factor at a time: power law form (full Hoffmann vs. α=β constraint vs. IsoFLOP vs. Kaplan form), learning rate strategy (swept vs. fixed), model/data range filtering, embedding inclusion in N and C, mid-training checkpoint usage, initialization method (full grid, single best, random sample, prior coefficients), loss function (log-Huber, MSE, MAE, Huber), and optimizer (L-BFGS, BFGS, non-linear least squares, grid search). Key findings include that the Kaplan form consistently degenerates to predicting D=1, that excluding embedding parameters from N substantially shifts the fitted law at large scales, and that full-grid optimization diverges most from the Chinchilla law due to many local minima.

## Results

Across 51 surveyed scaling law papers, only 45 quantify a scaling law, 28 describe their curve-fitting procedure, and just 19 provide analysis code — revealing a severe reproducibility gap. In their empirical analysis (Section 7), the authors show that the Kaplan et al. (2020) power law form consistently yields a degenerate solution suggesting the optimal number of data points is 1, while the Muennighoff et al. (2024) simplification (setting α=β) closely recovers the Hoffmann et al. (2022) Chinchilla law. Varying the learning rate (fixed vs. swept) or excluding embedding parameters from N causes the largest shifts in the fitted optimal D/N ratio, with divergences growing at large compute budgets. Full-grid optimization over 4,500 initializations (as in Hoffmann et al., 2022) paradoxically produces the fit that diverges most from the reported Chinchilla law, suggesting many local minima in the fitting landscape.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's empirical results are benchmarked primarily against the Hoffmann et al. (2022) Chinchilla scaling law as the reference standard. Using the Muennighoff et al. (2024) trick of constraining α=β in the Hoffmann form recovers a law very close to the reported Chinchilla result, while the unconstrained Hoffmann form only matches on the Porian et al. (2024) dataset. The Kaplan et al. (2020) power law form fails to reproduce their original findings, consistently predicting an optimal data budget of 1 token regardless of fitting procedure details. On the Besiroglu et al. (2024) reconstructed Chinchilla data, initializing the optimizer from the Hoffmann et al. (2022) reported coefficients (as Besiroglu et al. do) yields a fit nearly identical to the original Chinchilla law, whereas full-grid optimization over 4,500 starting points produces the most divergent result. On the authors' own models, optimizing from only the single best initialization point (Caballero et al., 2022 style) gives the closest match to the Chinchilla law, followed by initialization from Hoffmann's reported parameters — the opposite of what one might expect from a 'more thorough' search.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a meta-analysis of the scaling laws literature, building directly on the foundational Kaplan et al. (2020) and Hoffmann et al. (2022) papers whose contradictory conclusions about optimal compute allocation (D ∝ N^0.74 vs. D ∝ N^1) have driven multiple replication efforts (Besiroglu et al., 2024; Porian et al., 2024; Pearce & Song, 2024b). Unlike those prior replications, which each isolated a subset of procedural differences, this work systematically varies each component of the scaling law pipeline—power law form, training hyperparameters, data collection strategy, and optimization method—independently across three datasets. The proposed reproducibility checklist is novel; no prior survey has catalogued the reporting gaps across the full scaling laws literature or provided a structured template for authors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Scaling laws guide multi-billion-dollar training decisions for frontier models, yet the paper demonstrates that the field suffers from a severe reproducibility crisis: only 19 of 51 surveyed papers provide analysis code, 23 omit optimization details, and 15 do not specify how parameters or FLOPs are counted. The empirical finding that 'reasonable' procedural choices (e.g., including vs. excluding embedding parameters, full-grid vs. single-point initialization, sweeping vs. fixing learning rate) can flip the recommended optimal D/N ratio by large margins means that contradictory conclusions in the literature may stem from underreported details rather than genuine scientific disagreement. A key open question is whether these fragilities persist at the 10B+ parameter scale where most practical decisions are made, since the authors' own experiments top out at 1B parameters. The checklist may become a de facto standard for scaling law publications, but the absence of any universal set of best practices that guarantees a good fit remains a fundamental limitation.
