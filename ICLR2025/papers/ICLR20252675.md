## Problem

Gradient inversion attacks have demonstrated severe privacy risks for federated learning on images, text, and tabular data, but the vulnerability of graph-structured data (molecules, social networks, citation graphs) to such attacks remained entirely unexplored. The core difficulty is that graph inputs are jointly discrete in both structure (adjacency) and features, and the interdependence between recovering the feature matrix X and the adjacency matrix A renders standard optimization-based inversion approaches ineffective.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252675/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

GRAIN consumes the per-layer weight gradients ∂L/∂W^l shared by a client under FedSGD, the corresponding GNN model weights, and knowledge of the discrete feature value sets F_i (e.g., atom types, bond counts) along with their normalization. It outputs the full client graph: the adjacency matrix A and the node feature matrix X^0. A key constraint is n < d (number of nodes must be smaller than the embedding dimension), and all node features must be discrete; the method targets 2-layer GCN/GAT architectures with a readout head, recovering building blocks up to the network depth L.

## Method

GRAIN exploits the low-rank structure of GNN layer gradients via a generalized span check (Thm. 5.1) that verifies whether a candidate node embedding lies in the column space of ∂L/∂W^l, enabling exact filtering of discrete proposals. The attack proceeds in two stages: (1) iterative building-block generation, where single nodes are filtered feature-by-feature, then extended into 1-hop, 2-hop, …, L-hop subgraphs by gluing and re-filtering at each layer using the key result (Thm. 5.3) that a correctly recovered l-hop neighborhood reproduces the center node's layer-l embedding; and (2) DFS-based graph assembly, where the filtered L-hop building blocks are glued together at dangling nodes, with backtracking and gradient-distance verification (Frobenius norm) to confirm the final reconstruction. A node-uniqueness heuristic and degree-based constraints dramatically reduce the search space, allowing exact recovery of up to 80% of graphs.

## Results

GRAIN achieves exact graph reconstruction (FULL) of 75% on Tox21 (GAT), 79% on Pokec (GAT), 68% on Tox21 (GCN), 61% on CiteSeer (GAT), and 20% on CiteSeer (GCN), while baselines (DLG, TabLeak) achieve at most 2% exact reconstruction across all settings. On partial reconstruction metrics (GSM-0/1/2), GRAIN scores 86.9–97.2% on GSM-0 and 82.6–96.3% on GSM-2, compared to 0–76.7% for baselines. GRAIN also matches or exceeds TabLeak's runtime (14.3h vs. 13.1h on Tox21 GCN) while being orders of magnitude faster than DLG on CiteSeer (2.5h vs. 25.6h).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GRAIN decisively outperforms both DLG and TabLeak on the exact reconstruction metric (FULL) across all five datasets and both architectures: GRAIN achieves 15–79% exact recovery while both baselines cap at 0–2%, even when granted the true adjacency matrix (DLG+A, TabLeak+A). On structural similarity (GSM-1, GSM-2), the gap is most dramatic on CiteSeer and Pokec, where baselines score near 0% on GSM-1/GSM-2 (failing to recover any graph topology) while GRAIN reaches 31–96%. On Tox21 with GAT, DLG+A and TabLeak+A close the gap on GSM-2 (81.3% and 84.2% vs. GRAIN's 89.9%) but still achieve only 1–2% exact reconstruction versus GRAIN's 75%. On CiteSeer GCN, baselines slightly edge out GRAIN on GSM-0 (65.7% vs. 62.5%) but this reflects their ability to recover node features without any structure; on all structural metrics (GSM-1, GSM-2) and FULL, GRAIN dominates by a wide margin. Runtime-wise, GRAIN is comparable to TabLeak on Tox21 (14.3h vs. 13.1h) but dramatically faster on CiteSeer (2.5h vs. 172.7h for TabLeak) and Pokec (0.3h vs. 6.6h).

## Relation to Prior Work

GRAIN extends the exact gradient inversion framework of DAGER (Petrov et al., 2024), which exploited the low-rank structure of fully-connected layer gradients to recover discrete inputs via span checks, to the GNN setting. The key theoretical departure is Theorem 5.1, which generalizes DAGER's Theorem 3.1 to handle the case where the output gradient ∂L/∂Y^l is not full-rank—a condition that arises naturally in GCNs due to the structure of the adjacency matrix. Unlike prior optimization-based attacks (DLG, TabLeak) that treat inversion as a continuous search over feature vectors, GRAIN exploits the discrete nature of both node features and the adjacency matrix to perform exact filtering, and introduces a DFS-based graph-building algorithm to assemble local subgraphs into a complete graph—addressing the unique challenge that GNN message passing only reveals local (l-hop) structure per node.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that federated learning with GNNs—a paradigm widely adopted for privacy-sensitive applications in healthcare, molecular design, and social network analysis—does not preserve client data privacy, as an honest-but-curious server can exactly reconstruct up to 80% of client graphs. The practical implication is that current FL deployments using GCNs or GATs on graph data (molecules, citations, social networks) are fundamentally vulnerable without additional defenses. Key limitations include the requirement for discrete node features, the n < d assumption, and performance degradation on larger graphs due to combinatorial explosion in the DFS stage. Open questions include developing effective defenses (e.g., gradient perturbation, differential privacy) that maintain utility while preventing exact reconstruction, and extending the attack to continuous features or deeper/wider architectures.
