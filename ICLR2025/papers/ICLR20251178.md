## Problem

Mechanistic interpretability (MI) criteria—circuit error, mapping consistency, and intervention interchange accuracy (IIA)—are tacitly assumed to yield a unique explanation for a fixed behavior, but this identifiability property has never been formally examined. The paper asks whether, under the validity standards MI sets for itself, a unique computational abstraction (algorithm + localization) is guaranteed, and finds overwhelming evidence that it is not.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251178/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes small trained MLPs with layer sizes (2, k, k, n) trained on Boolean functions (XOR, AND, OR, IMP, etc.) with binary inputs plus Gaussian noise, and produces an exhaustive census of all incompatible computational abstractions satisfying the respective MI criteria. For the larger-scale validation, a 784-128-128-3-3-3-1 MLP trained on MNIST digits 0 and 1 is used. The output is not a single explanation but counts of valid circuits, interpretations per circuit, causally-aligned algorithms, and perfect minimal mappings—demonstrating that hundreds to millions of incompatible explanations coexist in a single network.

## Method

The core contribution is a systematic identifiability stress-test enabled by keeping networks small enough for exhaustive enumeration. Two MI strategies are tested: (1) where-then-what, which enumerates all subgraph circuits, validates them via circuit error (zero = perfect), then recursively searches for consistent logic-gate interpretations of each neuron's activation; and (2) what-then-where, which enumerates all Boolean formulas implementing the target function (e.g., 56 XOR-equivalent formulas at depth 3), then searches all neuron-subset mappings and measures causal alignment via IIA. The key finding is non-identifiability at every stage: multiple circuits achieve zero error, multiple interpretations are consistent per circuit, multiple algorithms achieve IIA = 1, and multiple subspaces are equally aligned per algorithm. Experiments vary architecture size (k = 2–5), number of parallel tasks (n = 1–6), loss cutoff, input noise, and training distribution, and extend to a larger MNIST model to show the problem persists at scale.

## Results

In the XOR example (MLP with two hidden layers of size 3), the where-then-what strategy yields 85 perfect circuits (zero circuit error) with an average of 535.8 consistent logic-gate interpretations per circuit, while the what-then-where strategy finds 159 perfect minimal mappings (IIA = 1) across just two candidate algorithms. Scaling to larger architectures (k = 2 to 5), the median number of computational abstractions grows from 38 to 910,000 (circuit-first) and from 8 to 3,700 (algorithm-first); less than 2% of trained networks contain exactly one valid minimal mapping, and no network contains exactly one circuit interpretation. On a larger MNIST-trained MLP (784-128-128-3-3-3-1), 3,209 valid circuits are found in the final sub-network alone, confirming the problem persists at larger scale.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method to compare against baselines; it is a diagnostic study that stress-tests existing MI criteria (circuit error, mapping consistency, and intervention interchange accuracy) for their ability to guarantee a unique explanation. The key finding is that all three criteria fail identifiability: multiple circuits achieve zero circuit error, multiple consistent mappings exist per circuit, and multiple algorithms achieve perfect IIA = 1 with multiple localizations each. The authors note that Occam's razor (sparsity) cannot resolve the ambiguity, as no single explanation is uniquely sparse. They discuss whether causal abstraction (requiring all lower-level states to be accounted for) or the inner interpretability framework (multi-criteria validation) could restore identifiability, but do not empirically test these alternatives. The only external comparison is the MNIST experiment, which shows the non-identifiability problem does not vanish at larger scale (3,209 valid circuits in a sub-network of a 7-layer MLP).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the statistical concept of identifiability (Rothenberg, 1971) to the mechanistic interpretability setting, asking whether existing MI criteria—circuit error, intervention interchange accuracy, and mapping consistency—guarantee a unique explanation for a fixed behavior. Rather than building on a specific prior MI method, it stress-tests the foundational assumptions shared by both major MI strategies (where-then-what and what-then-where), positioning itself against the tacit assumption of explanatory unicity that pervades the circuit discovery literature (documented in Appendix C). The authors draw on causal abstraction (Beckers & Halpern, 2019) and the inner interpretability framework (Vilas et al., 2024) as potential paths toward resolving the non-identifiability they demonstrate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even the strictest causal criteria (zero circuit error, IIA = 1) admit thousands of incompatible computational abstractions challenges the field's implicit assumption that a unique 'correct' circuit exists, with direct implications for how MI results should be communicated, validated, and interpreted. The empirical scope is limited to small MLPs on Boolean functions, and the authors explicitly acknowledge the gap to LLMs and Transformer architectures, leaving open whether the problem persists at scale. Key open questions include whether stricter causal-abstraction-based criteria (requiring all lower-level states to be accounted for) can restore identifiability, and whether the inner interpretability framework's multi-criteria validation can practically disambiguate among competing explanations.
