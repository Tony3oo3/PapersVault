## Problem

Evaluating LLMs in open-ended scenarios remains difficult because traditional metrics (BLEU, ROUGE, BERTScore) cannot capture answer quality, arena-format human evaluations are slow and expensive, and GPT-4-based API judges suffer from data leakage risks and reproducibility issues. Prior fine-tuned judge models like PandaLM are further limited by inherent LLM biases—position bias, knowledge bias, and a newly identified format bias—that degrade their reliability and generalization.

## Input / Output

The judge receives a seed question, a pair of LLM-generated answers, and an optional reference answer, all formatted via structured prompt templates. Output follows a 'grading, judging, and reasoning' pipeline: the model first emits a numeric score for each answer, the judgment (Answer 1 wins / Answer 2 wins / Tie) is derived by comparing scores, and a detailed textual explanation is generated only if requested. This score-first design decouples the fast grading step from the slow reasoning step, enabling the model to skip reasoning entirely for batch evaluation. The same architecture extends to single-answer grading (reference placed at position 1 with a fixed score of 10), multi-answer ranking, and multimodal judging with a modified template.

## Method

JudgeLM fine-tunes Vicuna-7B/13B/33B via instruction fine-tuning on a 105K-sample dataset of seed questions, answers from 11 open-source LLMs, and GPT-4-generated judgments (100K train / 5K val), effectively performing knowledge distillation from GPT-4. Three bias-mitigation techniques are introduced at the data level: (1) swap augmentation, which duplicates each training sample with answer positions exchanged and scores swapped to counter position bias; (2) reference support, which adds reference-guided judgments so the model learns to leverage external knowledge rather than relying solely on pre-trained parameters; and (3) reference drop, which randomly replaces reference-inclusive samples with their reference-free counterparts during training to prevent the model from overfitting to a single input format. The model is trained with cosine-decay learning rate (2e-5), AdamW, bf16 precision, and ZeRO-3 optimization over 3 epochs. At 33B parameters with 100K data, JudgeLM achieves 90.06% agreement with GPT-4 and 87.93% swap-consistency, surpassing both its teacher and human-to-human agreement on the PandaLM test set.

## Results

On the JudgeLM val set (5K samples, GPT-4-annotated), JudgeLM-33B achieves 89.03% agreement and 91.36% swap-consistency without reference, and 89.32% agreement and 92.37% consistency with reference, surpassing all baselines. On the PandaLM human-annotated test set, JudgeLM-33B reaches 75.18% agreement with human judgments, exceeding GPT-4's 66.47%. On RewardBench, JudgeLM-7B scores 78.5 overall, outperforming GPT-3.5-turbo-0125 (64.5), the Prometheus series (72.5–75.3), UltraRM-13B (67.6), and TIGERScore-13B (35.6), while approaching Tulu-2-dpo-70B (79.0). JudgeLM-7B judges 5,000 response pairs in just 3 minutes on 8 A100 GPUs with parallel judging and no reasoning generation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

JudgeLM-7B outperforms all open-source judge baselines on the JudgeLM val set: it beats PandaLM-7B by 12.5 points in agreement (81.11 vs. 68.61 w/o ref), Auto-J-13B by 6.3 points (81.11 vs. 74.86), and GPT-3.5 by 7.3 points (81.11 vs. 73.83). With reference, JudgeLM-7B surpasses InstructScore-7B by 28.3 points in agreement (84.08 vs. 55.80) and PandaLM-7B by 20.3 points (84.08 vs. 63.77). On the PandaLM human-annotated test set, JudgeLM-33B (75.18% agreement) exceeds GPT-4 (66.47%) by 8.7 points, while JudgeLM-7B (65.07%) already matches GPT-4's level. On RewardBench, JudgeLM-7B (78.5) beats GPT-3.5-turbo-0125 (64.5), Prometheus-8×7B-v2.0 (75.3), UltraRM-13B (67.6), TIGERScore-13B (35.6), Tulu-2-dpo-13B (76.4), and all Qwen1.5-Chat variants, but trails Tulu-2-dpo-70B (79.0) by 0.5 points. On the MM-Vet multimodal benchmark, JudgeLM-33B (91.74% agreement, 91.08% precision) outperforms GPT-4 zero-shot (86.70%) and GPT-3.5 7-shot (83.03%), though it falls short of GPT-4 7-shot (95.58% agreement). On ToxicChat, JudgeLM-33B matches OpenAI Moderation in accuracy (89.66 vs. 89.70) while achieving higher precision (58.79 vs. 54.76).

## Relation to Prior Work

JudgeLM extends the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm established by MT-bench/Chatbot Arena (Zheng et al., 2023) and PandaLM (Wang et al., 2023), which used GPT-4 API calls or small-scale fine-tuning to evaluate open-ended LLM outputs. It departs from prior work in three key ways: (1) a substantially larger and more diverse training dataset (100K samples vs. PandaLM's ~50K) with human-verified annotations, (2) a systematic identification of three distinct biases—position, knowledge, and notably the newly named format bias—with targeted fine-tuning-stage mitigations (swap augmentation, reference support, reference drop) rather than inference-time prompt engineering, and (3) a 'grading, judging, reasoning' output structure that decouples fast scoring from optional slow reasoning. Conceptually, the approach is closest to knowledge distillation from GPT-4 into open-source Vicuna/LLaMA models, but the bias-aware training recipe and the reference-drop mechanism for format generalization are novel contributions not present in PandaLM or Auto-J.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

JudgeLM provides a practical, reproducible, and dramatically faster (up to 133×) alternative to GPT-4 API-based judging, which is critical for iterative LLM development where evaluation throughput is a bottleneck. The 100K dataset and 5K validation benchmark, combined with the bias analysis, give the community a shared resource and diagnostic framework for building and auditing judge models. Key limitations include the ~$4,000 cost of GPT-4 data generation (limiting further scaling), continued dependence on a closed-source teacher, and the observation that math-domain judging remains weaker. Open questions include whether synthetic judge data can replace GPT-4 annotations, whether stronger base models (LLaMA-3) close the remaining gap, and whether multi-turn self-critique can further improve judgment reliability.
