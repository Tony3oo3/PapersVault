## Problem

While video understanding has advanced rapidly, the specific task of identifying fine-grained, skill-relevant differences between two videos of the same action remains largely unexplored. Existing work either visualizes motion differences or performs coarse-grained comparisons across different actions, but real-world coaching and skill-learning applications demand natural-language descriptions of subtle performance discrepancies (e.g., 'knees caving in more' or 'the squat is deeper'). No benchmark or zero-shot method existed to evaluate or address this fine-grained video action differencing task.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250544/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The task takes a pair of videos (v_A, v_B) of the same action, an action description string, and either a predefined list of candidate difference statements (closed-set) or an integer N_diff specifying how many differences to generate (open-set). In the closed-set setting, the model outputs a binary 'A' or 'B' prediction for each provided difference; in the open-set setting, it generates up to N_diff natural-language difference statements, each paired with an 'A' or 'B' assignment. Videos are sampled at 4–6 fps for short fine-grained actions (fitness, ballsports, diving) and 2 fps for longer actions (music, surgery), and the benchmark spans 549 video pairs across five domains with 4,469 difference annotations and 2,075 frame-level timestamp annotations.

## Method

The VidDiff Method is a zero-shot, three-stage agentic workflow that decomposes video action differencing into specialized sub-tasks. Stage 1 (Difference Proposer) uses an LLM (GPT-4o) to generate candidate difference statements from the action description. Stage 2 (Frame Localizer) performs temporal sub-action segmentation: an LLM produces retrieval strings for sub-actions, CLIP ViT-bigG-14 computes per-frame similarity scores, and a Viterbi-based decoding algorithm assigns each frame to a sub-action while enforcing the fixed temporal sequence, after which the LLM maps sub-actions to specific differences. Stage 3 (Action Differencer) poses multiple-choice VQA questions to a VLM (GPT-4o) on the localized frames from both videos, determining whether each difference is more pronounced in v_A, v_B, or indistinguishable. The key insight is that precise temporal localization of relevant sub-action segments substantially improves the VLM's ability to detect subtle visual differences, as confirmed by ablations showing that random frame retrieval drops accuracy from 62.7% to 50.1% on the easy split.

## Results

On the closed-set task (accuracy, where 50% is random), VidDiff achieves 56.3% average accuracy, second only to Gemini-1.5-Pro (57.7%) and ahead of GPT-4o (53.5%). On the open-set task (recall @ N_diff), VidDiff achieves 42.1% average recall, the best among all methods, narrowly outperforming GPT-4o (41.7%) and substantially outperforming Gemini-1.5-Pro (28.3%). On the hard split, no model significantly exceeds the 50% random baseline in the closed setting, highlighting the extreme difficulty of fine-grained action differencing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VidDiff outperforms GPT-4o on both the closed-set (56.3% vs. 53.5% accuracy) and open-set (42.1% vs. 41.7% recall) tasks, demonstrating that the structured agentic pipeline (LLM proposer + CLIP localizer + VLM differencer) adds value over raw GPT-4o video understanding. VidDiff loses to Gemini-1.5-Pro on the closed-set task (56.3% vs. 57.7%) but substantially outperforms it on the open-set task (42.1% vs. 28.3%), where Gemini's weakness is difference recall rather than visual discrimination. Against Claude-3.5-Sonnet, VidDiff wins on both settings (56.3% vs. 53.4% closed; 42.1% vs. 35.6% open). The open-source models LLaVA-Video and Qwen2-VL-7B perform near-random on the open-set task (8.4% and 7.2% recall respectively), far below VidDiff's 42.1%, while on the closed-set they trail VidDiff by 4–6 points. Overall, VidDiff is the best or second-best method across all six model×setting combinations, with its largest margin over baselines appearing in the open-set setting where language generation of relevant differences is the bottleneck.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by formalizing fine-grained, natural-language video action differencing as a distinct task, departing from prior work that either visualizes motion discrepancies (Balakrishnan et al., 2015), performs coarse-grained step-level comparisons in single domains (Nagarajan & Torresani, 2024), or provides domain-specific skill feedback via pose estimation. Conceptually, it extends the image 'difference captioning' literature (Jhamtani & Berg-Kirkpatrick, 2018; Park et al., 2019) from static image pairs to temporally-aligned video segments, and repurposes components from skilled action understanding (action localization, pose estimation) as subroutines within an agentic pipeline. The method itself is a composition of existing zero-shot tools—LLMs for difference proposal, CLIP with Viterbi decoding for frame localization, and VLMs for frame-level comparison—rather than a new architectural contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

VidDiffBench is the first large-scale, multi-domain benchmark (549 video pairs, 4,469 fine-grained differences) dedicated to this task, and it exposes a clear capability gap: even GPT-4o and Gemini-1.5-Pro achieve near-random accuracy on hard splits, and the ablation showing 51% accuracy with oracle frame timestamps reveals that the bottleneck is fundamentally in fine-grained visual perception, not just temporal alignment. The decomposition of open-set errors into 'difference recall' vs. 'flipped prediction' errors provides actionable diagnostics for model developers. Open questions include whether explicit training for fine-grained VLM comparison (beyond zero-shot prompting) can close the gap, whether domain-specific fine-tuning is necessary for specialized applications like surgery, and how to handle untrimmed, longer videos where temporal alignment is substantially harder.
