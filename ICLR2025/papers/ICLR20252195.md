## Problem

Sliced-Wasserstein distances offer near-linearithmic computational complexity for comparing high-dimensional distributions, but they fundamentally lack a transport plan between the input measures, limiting their use in applications requiring explicit mass coupling such as interpolation, domain adaptation, and transport-based embeddings. This paper asks whether a valid transport plan can be constructed from the sliced framework and whether the resulting plan induces a genuine metric on the space of discrete probability measures.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252195/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two discrete probability measures in R^d, each represented as a weighted set of particles (potentially with different support sizes and non-uniform masses), and a number of random projection directions L. It produces a full N×M coupling matrix (the EST plan) encoding how much mass is transported from each source particle to each target particle, from which one can derive the EST distance, barycentric-projection embeddings for classification, and geodesic interpolations. The transport plan is computed by projecting onto L one-dimensional slices, solving 1D OT on each, lifting the 1D plans back to d dimensions, and averaging over directions.

## Method

The core mechanism is a 'lifting' operation: for each direction θ, the unique 1D optimal transport plan between the projected measures is lifted back to d dimensions by distributing mass proportionally within equivalence classes (sets of points sharing the same projection), yielding a valid d-dimensional transport plan γ_θ with correct marginals. The Expected Sliced Transport (EST) plan is then defined as the expectation of these lifted plans over a probability measure σ on the sphere, and the EST distance is the p-th root of the expected Euclidean cost under this plan. The paper proves the EST distance is a valid metric on finite discrete probability measures by first establishing it for uniform measures (recovering the Projected Wasserstein distance of Rowland et al.), extending to rational-weighted measures via a common-denominator argument, and then approximating general measures in Total Variation. A temperature parameter τ in the averaging measure σ_τ provides a flexible interpolation: at τ=0 the directions are sampled uniformly, while as τ→∞ the plan concentrates on the single minimizing slice, recovering the min-SWGG framework of Mahey et al. (2023).

## Results

The paper's primary contribution is theoretical: it proves that the Expected Sliced Transport (EST) distance D_p(·,·) is a valid metric on the space of finite discrete probability measures (Theorem 2.10) and is equivalent to weak* convergence (Corollary B.5). Computationally, EST achieves O((L+d)N²) complexity versus O(N²log(N)/λ²) for Sinkhorn-based entropic OT, with wall-clock times remaining stable across temperature parameters τ (unlike Sinkhorn, where smaller λ requires more iterations). On Point Cloud MNIST 2D classification (logistic regression on transport-based embeddings with N=100, L=128 slices), EST with τ=0 achieves competitive accuracy with the OT plan while being significantly faster; on ModelNet40, EST with varying L matches or approaches LP-based OT accuracy at a fraction of the computational cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against classical OT (solved via LP), EST is always a lower bound on the true W_p distance (W_p ≤ D_p by Remark 2.8), and the EST plan is generally suboptimal but visually close to the OT plan with less mass splitting than entropic OT (Figure 3). Against entropic OT (Sinkhorn, λ=10), EST is faster for large N and its computation time is independent of the temperature parameter τ, whereas Sinkhorn's iteration count grows as λ decreases; in weak convergence experiments (Figure 6), both OT and EST distances converge to zero as μ_t→ν, while entropic OT and the outer-product plan do not. Against min-SWGG (Mahey et al., 2023), EST generalizes it: as τ→∞ the EST plan recovers the min-SWGG transportation map exactly (Figure 4, rightmost column), and the temperature parameter provides a continuous interpolation between the uniform-slice EST (τ=0) and min-SWGG (τ→∞). For transport-based embedding on Point Cloud MNIST 2D, EST with τ=0 produces t-SNE visualizations and logistic regression accuracy comparable to OT, but the authors note that increasing τ (approaching min-SWGG) degrades embedding quality because σ_τ becomes measure-dependent, making cross-measure comparisons inconsistent.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sliced optimal transport framework (Rabin et al., 2011) by introducing a 'lifting' operation that recovers d-dimensional transport plans from one-dimensional sliced plans, then averaging over projection directions. For uniform measures, the resulting EST distance coincides with the Projected Wasserstein distance of Rowland et al. (2019), but the paper generalizes the metric property to arbitrary discrete measures with non-uniform weights—a setting not previously covered. The temperature parameter σ_τ provides a principled interpolation between uniform directional averaging and the min-SWGG framework of Mahey et al. (2023), unifying these approaches under a single parametric family.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Sliced OT has been widely adopted for its O(LN²) computational efficiency over O(N³log N) LP-based OT, but its inability to produce explicit transport plans has limited its use in applications requiring mass coupling (domain adaptation, texture mixing, transport-based embeddings). This paper closes that gap with a rigorous theoretical foundation, making sliced OT viable for a broader range of downstream tasks. A notable limitation is that the EST distance always upper-bounds W_p (Remark 2.8), so it is not the optimal transport cost, and the embedding experiments reveal that the temperature-dependent averaging measure σ_τ degrades embedding quality as τ increases, suggesting min-SWGG may be ill-suited for transport-based embedding. Open questions include whether the framework extends naturally to continuous measures and whether the temperature can be learned end-to-end.
