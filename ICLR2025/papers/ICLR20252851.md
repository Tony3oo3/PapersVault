## Problem

Existing RAG evaluation methods either assess retrieval and generation jointly (obscuring retrieval's distinct contribution) or rely on traditional IR metrics like NDCG that treat the retriever as an independent module, ignoring the receiver's prior knowledge. This creates a critical gap: in multi-step reasoning, partial information that doesn't directly match the ground truth is scored as zero utility, and widely-known facts that bring no knowledge gain are scored as useful. The paper asks how to measure retrieval utility as the actual knowledge gain experienced by the LLM recipient.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a query, one or more reference answers, a set of retrieved documents, a generator LLM (e.g., Llama-2-7B/13B/70B), and a frozen entailment model (DeBERTa-v2-xlarge-mnli, ~710M params). It produces a continuous SePer score in [0,1] representing the model's estimated belief probability on the correct answer, and ΔSePer (the difference between post- and pre-retrieval SePer) as the retrieval utility. Computation requires N Monte-Carlo samples (default N=10) from the LLM at a given temperature, making it sampling-based and consistent with inference-time behavior rather than a single deterministic forward pass.

## Method

SePer estimates the LLM's belief distribution over the semantic meaning space (not vocabulary space) by sampling N responses, clustering them via an entailment model (DeBERTa) that defines semantic equivalence, and aggregating sequence likelihoods within each cluster. The SePer score is the total probability mass assigned to clusters semantically equivalent to the ground-truth answer, computed as a Monte-Carlo estimator. Retrieval utility is then ΔSePer = P_M(a*|q,D) − P_M(a*|q), the belief shift toward the correct answer induced by retrieval. Two kernel variants are provided: SePer_H uses a hard indicator for cluster-to-answer matching, while SePer_S uses the continuous entailment score as a soft kernel for finer-grained credit assignment. The paper proves this formulation satisfies three desirable properties from cognitive IR theory (receiver dependence, zero utility for irrelevant/redundant information, and monotonicity in relevance), grounding the metric in a Bayesian belief-revision framework.

## Results

On the primary retrieval-utility evaluation (Table 2), ΔSePer_S achieves Pearson correlations of 0.769 (NQ), 0.533 (MS MARCO), 0.905 (SQUAD), 0.584 (2WikiMultihopQA), 0.660 (HotpotQA), 0.461 (IIRC), and 0.559 (MuSiQue) with human-annotated utility scores, all with p < 0.01. The hard variant ΔSePer_H achieves 0.752, 0.512, 0.904, 0.559, 0.634, 0.446, and 0.543 respectively. In the entailment-based answer scoring validation (Table 1), SePer's NLI component reaches 96.6/95.3 F1/Acc on NQ-DPR-FiD, matching or slightly exceeding GPT-4 (96.0/94.5) while being orders of magnitude cheaper (0.12s vs 4.22s per query).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ΔSePer outperforms all 10 baselines across all 7 datasets. The closest competitors are ROUGE (best matching-based, e.g., 0.808 on SQUAD vs. ΔSePer_S's 0.905) and PROMETHEUS (best LLM-judge, e.g., 0.707 on SQUAD vs. 0.905), with ΔSePer leading by 8–20 percentage points in Pearson correlation. Uncertainty-based methods (Perplexity, Entropy, Semantic Entropy) perform substantially worse, with Perplexity near zero correlation (≤0.024) and Semantic Entropy at most 0.621 (SQUAD), indicating that output uncertainty alone is a poor proxy for retrieval utility. LLM-judge baselines (AUTO-J, PROMETHEUS) are consistently inferior to ΔSePer and also far more expensive (4–30s per query vs. 0.12s). The soft kernel variant ΔSePer_S marginally outperforms the hard indicator variant ΔSePer_H (e.g., 0.660 vs. 0.634 on HotpotQA), suggesting that continuous entailment scores capture finer-grained semantic nuance in free-form generation. In the entailment-scoring validation (Table 1), SePer's NLI-based matching surpasses BERTSCORE by 2–6% F1 and matches GPT-4-level accuracy (98.5/97.2 on TriviaQA-GPT-4) at a fraction of the cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Monte-Carlo sampling and semantic clustering machinery from semantic entropy (Kuhn et al., ICLR 2023; Farquhar et al., Nature 2024) — originally designed to quantify output uncertainty for hallucination detection — into a belief-shift metric for measuring retrieval utility. The key conceptual departure is redirecting the semantic clustering from measuring *output uncertainty* to measuring *probability mass on a specific reference answer*, then taking the difference before and after retrieval as the utility signal. For semantic equivalence scoring, the paper adopts the entailment-model approach validated by Yao & Barbosa (ACL 2024), showing it outperforms both lexical matching and LLM-judge baselines at a fraction of the cost. The work also positions itself against traditional IR metrics (NDCG, recall) that treat retrieval as an independent module, and against expensive LLM-as-judge pipelines (GPT-4, Auto-J, Prometheus) that lack fine-grained sensitivity to partial information.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SePer provides a principled, receiver-aware, and computationally free metric for quantifying how much retrieved information actually shifts an LLM's belief toward the correct answer — something neither NDCG nor LLM-judges capture well. Its ability to score intermediate retrieval steps in multi-hop reasoning (where partial information yields fractional utility) opens the door to more granular RAG pipeline optimization and budget allocation. Limitations include the assumption of uniform utility distribution across reasoning steps in multi-hop datasets, the dependence on a reference answer (limiting applicability to open-ended generation), and the observation that reranking can paradoxically hurt performance on some datasets, leaving the context-ordering problem open. Future work could extend SePer to reference-free settings and more diverse RAG scenarios beyond QA.
