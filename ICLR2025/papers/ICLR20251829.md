## Problem

Existing Bayesian network structure learning algorithms (e.g., PC, FCI) must re-learn the entire DAG from scratch whenever a new variable is introduced, making them impractical for dynamic or large-scale applications where variables are discovered incrementally. No prior algorithm efficiently updates a learned structure to incorporate an additional variable, despite the fact that the new variable can only remove edges among the original variables, never add new ones.

## Input / Output

The extendable algorithm takes as input an existing P-map graph G over variables X (obtained from a P-map learner on a dataset of instances) and a single new variable Y, and outputs a P-map (PDAG) over the extended set X ∪ {Y}. Notably, G need not be a P-map for the marginal distribution P'(X) because Y may act as a hidden confounder; the method is designed to handle this unfaithfulness. The iterative variant starts from just two randomly selected variables and incrementally adds one variable per step until all N variables are incorporated.

## Method

The key theoretical result (Proposition 1) establishes that adding Y to a P-map G over X can only delete edges among X, never create new ones, which confines the search to a dramatically reduced space. The Extendable PC algorithm exploits this in three stages: (i) test edges between Y and each X by conditioning on subsets of X's neighbors in G, (ii) test edges between Y and its surviving neighbors by conditioning on subsets of Y's neighbors, and (iii) test whether existing X–X edges become conditionally independent given Y, removing spurious adjacencies. This yields a complexity of O(NK^m 2^d) versus O((N+1)^M) for standard PC, where d is the max degree in G and m is Y's true degree. A score-based variant uses a T-function to prune the DAG search space based on five structural constraints derived from Lemmas 3–5, and an iterative paradigm applies the extendable step repeatedly to build the full graph from two initial variables, achieving up to 1300× runtime reduction over PC without accuracy loss.

## Results

The Extendable PC algorithm reduces the number of conditional independence (CI) tests by up to 247× (ANDES: 277 vs. 68,375) and runtime by up to ~1,346× (ANDES: 1.97 s vs. 2,652 s) compared to the standard PC algorithm when adding a single new variable to an existing P-map. Across all 13 benchmark datasets, Extendable PC consistently requires fewer CI tests and less runtime than both PC and Initialized PC, while matching or slightly improving structural Hamming distance (SHD) accuracy (e.g., MILDEW: 13% vs. 17.4% SHD; WATER: 57.6% vs. 59.1%). The Iterative PC variant reduces CI tests and runtime on 8 of 13 datasets versus standard PC (e.g., ANDES: 35,327 vs. 68,375 CI tests; HEPAR2: 597 s vs. 1,832 s), though it is slower on denser networks like CHILD (124 s vs. 65.71 s) and ALARM (81 s vs. 22.1 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed Extendable PC against two baselines: the standard PC algorithm (Spirtes et al., 2000) and an Initialized PC (IPC) that simply re-runs PC using the prior skeleton as its starting graph. Extendable PC dominates both baselines on every one of the 13 datasets in terms of CI test count and wall-clock runtime, with the largest gains on high-dimensional networks (ANDES: 247× fewer CI tests, ~1,346× faster; HEPAR2: 43× fewer CI tests, ~38× faster). On accuracy (SHD as % of true edges), Extendable PC ties PC on 10 of 13 datasets and improves on MILDEW (13% vs. 17.4%) and WATER (57.6% vs. 59.1%), confirming that the reduced search space does not sacrifice structural quality. The Iterative PC variant (which builds the full graph by adding one variable at a time) shows a more mixed picture: it beats standard PC on CI tests and runtime for 8 of 13 datasets (notably ANDES, HEPAR2, INSURANCE, WIN95PTS) but is slower on denser or smaller networks such as CHILD, ALARM, and MILDEW, and its SHD is slightly worse on ALARM (17.4% vs. 8.7%) and MILDEW (54.3% vs. 17.4%) while better on WIN95PTS (31.25% vs. 38.4%), INSURANCE (26.9% vs. 30.8%), WATER (47% vs. 59.1%), and HEPAR2 (46.3% vs. 51.2%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the PC algorithm (Spirtes et al., 2000) and the broader constraint-based/score-based Bayesian network structure learning framework by introducing an 'extendable' paradigm: rather than re-learning a DAG from scratch when a new variable appears, the algorithm leverages the existing P-map graph to restrict the search space. It explicitly distinguishes itself from prior online (Kocacoban & Cussens, 2019) and incremental (Alcobe, 2005) structure learning methods, which handle new data instances over a fixed variable set, by addressing the orthogonal problem of adding a new variable to a fixed dataset. The theoretical analysis builds on d-separation, P-map, and faithfulness concepts from Koller & Friedman (2009) and Spirtes et al. (2000), with the key new result (Proposition 1) that adding a variable cannot introduce new edges among existing variables.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 1300× runtime reduction (well-evidenced across 13 benchmark datasets) makes incremental variable addition practical in domains like finance, social science, and psychology where critical variables are discovered after initial model construction. The iterative paradigm offers a general recipe applicable to any constraint-based or score-based learner, though its performance is sensitive to variable ordering—a limitation the authors acknowledge but leave for future work. A notable caveat is that the iterative approach degrades in accuracy on several datasets (e.g., MILDEW: 54.3% vs. 17.4% SHD vs. standard PC), and the entire framework assumes the initial graph is a correct P-map, which may not hold in practice with hidden confounders.
