## Problem

LLM agents struggle with multi-step decision-making tasks requiring environmental feedback (e.g., web shopping, scientific reasoning), and collecting large-scale trajectory data for fine-tuning is both difficult and costly, especially when powerful LLMs are only accessible via restricted APIs. Existing approaches either rely on expensive commercial LLMs as evaluators or require labor-intensive human annotation. The paper exploits the principle that evaluation is easier than generation to argue that automatically learning a reward model from the environment is more feasible than training a policy model.

## Input / Output

The reward model receives a task instruction (text) paired with a full trajectory consisting of interleaved environment screenshots (RGB images) and text observations alongside the sequence of actions taken, and outputs a single scalar reward score indicating whether the trajectory fulfills the instruction. At inference, the planning algorithms (MCTS, Best-of-N, Reflexion) generate multiple candidate trajectories using an LLM agent, score each with the reward model, and select the highest-scoring action sequence. The reward target is customizable at inference time—for example, subtracting a length penalty or product price from the base reward to produce shorter or cheaper trajectories without retraining.

## Method

ARMAP automatically generates training data in three steps: an LLM synthesizes task instructions from environment observations via in-context learning, an LLM agent navigates the environment to collect diverse trajectories, and a separate LLM refines the instructions to match what the trajectories actually accomplish while also generating negative trajectories by modifying specific actions. The reward model uses a VILA-3B vision-language backbone with an added fully-connected head that maps the final hidden state to a scalar, trained with a Bradley-Terry pairwise comparison loss (cross-entropy on the score difference between positive and negative trajectories) via LoRA fine-tuning. At inference, the learned reward model is plugged into planning algorithms—Best-of-N selects the highest-scoring trajectory from n samples, Reflexion iteratively refines trajectories using reward-based verbal feedback, and MCTS constructs a search tree tracking visit counts and maximum predicted rewards to balance exploitation and exploration. The framework requires no human annotation, no fine-tuning of the policy LLM, and no reliance on state-of-the-art commercial models, and it generalizes across web agents, scientific reasoning, and mathematical puzzle domains.

## Results

On the primary benchmarks, ARMAP-M (MCTS) with Llama70B achieves 66.8 on Webshop, 58.2/55.9 on ScienceWorld (seen/unseen), and 24.0 on Game of 24, yielding an average of 49.3 versus 38.0 (Sampling) and 37.5 (Greedy). Gains are most pronounced on weaker backbones: Mistral7B improves from 19.5 (Greedy) to 29.6 (ARMAP-M) average, and Phi3.8B from 17.5 to 30.0. On ALFWorld, ARMAP-B with Llama70B reaches 0.45 success rate on the dev split (vs. 0.30 Greedy), and on AgentClinic-MedQA, ARMAP-B scores 44.33 versus 14.02 for Greedy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares ARMAP against two non-reward-guided baselines: Sampling (temperature=1, chain-of-thought) and Greedy (temperature=0). ARMAP consistently outperforms both baselines across all four LLM backbones and all three primary benchmarks. The average improvement over the best baseline is +11.8 points for Llama70B (49.3 vs. 38.0), +5.2 for Llama8B (34.1 vs. 28.9), +10.1 for Mistral7B (29.6 vs. 19.5), and +12.5 for Phi3.8B (30.0 vs. 17.5), confirming that weaker models benefit most from reward-model-guided planning. Among the three ARMAP planning variants, MCTS (ARMAP-M) achieves the highest average on Llama70B, Mistral7B, and Phi3.8B, while Best-of-N (ARMAP-B) is best for Llama8B. Reflexion (ARMAP-R) underperforms on smaller models (Mistral7B, Phi3.8B), likely because it requires strong self-reflection capabilities. No external agent methods (e.g., ReAct, Tree of Thoughts) are directly compared; the baselines are limited to the same LLM backbone without reward guidance.

## Relation to Prior Work

ARMAP builds on VILA (Lin et al., 2023) as a vision-language backbone, repurposing it from a generative model into a scalar reward scorer via an added linear head trained with a Bradley-Terry pairwise loss. Conceptually, it sits at the intersection of LLM agent planning (ReAct, Tree of Thoughts, MCTS-based search) and learning from AI feedback (RLAIF, Constitutional AI), but departs from both: unlike prior agent-planning work that relies on prompting expensive commercial LLMs as evaluators or distilling trajectories into policy models, ARMAP automatically synthesizes reward data from environment interactions and trains a small, deployable reward model. The 'evaluation is easier than generation' premise (Karp, 1975; Naor, 1996) provides the theoretical justification for shifting the learning burden from the policy to the reward model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical path to improving LLM agents without fine-tuning the policy model or depending on costly, rate-limited commercial APIs, which is significant given the growing ecosystem of open-source LLMs. The ability to customize reward targets at inference time (e.g., penalizing trajectory length or product price) enables controllable generation that fine-tuned policy models cannot easily provide. However, the reward model still struggles with multi-constraint instructions (overlooking details like 'fluoride' in a product query), and gains diminish on stronger backbones, suggesting the approach is most valuable for mid-to-small models. Open questions include whether the automatic data generation pipeline generalizes to truly open-ended environments without discrete action spaces, and whether the reward model can be made more robust to adversarial or out-of-distribution trajectories.
