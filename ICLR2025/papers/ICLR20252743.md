## Problem

Despite the importance of implicit language in NLP tasks like content moderation and AI safety, no existing metric quantitatively measures the implicitness level of a sentence on a continuum. Current benchmarks rely on binary implicit/explicit labels or subjective annotation criteria, obscuring the true depth of model comprehension and making data quality assessment difficult. This paper addresses this gap by defining implicitness as the divergence between a sentence's semantic (literal) meaning and its pragmatic (intended) interpretation, grounded in Gricean pragmatics.

## Input / Output

IMPSCORE takes a single English sentence (max 384 tokens) and produces a scalar implicitness score in [0, 2], where higher values indicate greater semantic-pragmatic divergence. For sentence pairs, it additionally outputs a pragmatic distance (Euclidean distance in a 64-dim pragmatic space) indicating how closely two sentences share intended meaning. The model is lightweight (~105 MB parameters) and enables rapid inference over large corpora, unlike LLM-based evaluators.

## Method

IMPSCORE uses Sentence-BERT (all-mpnet-base-v2) to produce 768-dim sentence embeddings, which are then projected via two independent matrices into separate 64-dim semantic and pragmatic subspaces. A space transformation matrix maps pragmatic features into the semantic space, and the implicitness score is computed as the cosine distance between the original semantic features and the transformed pragmatic features, ensuring a bounded [0, 2] range. Training employs pairwise contrastive learning with margin-based ranking losses: the model is pushed to assign higher implicitness scores to implicit sentences than to their explicit counterparts (margin γ₁=0.5), and to assign smaller pragmatic distances to pragmatically close pairs than to pragmatically distant ones (margin γ₂=0.7). The training data comprises 112,580 (implicit, explicit) sentence pairs constructed from 13 diverse NLP datasets, where positive pairs share pragmatic intent and negative pairs are pragmatically distinct, providing the contrastive signal for both objectives.

## Results

On the held-out test set, IMPSCORE achieves 95.2% Implicitness Accuracy and 96.2% Pragmatics Accuracy, with average implicitness scores of 1.296 for implicit sentences versus 0.514 for explicit sentences (gap well exceeding the margin γ₁=0.5). On out-of-distribution user study data, IMPSCORE attains Kendall's Tau of 0.73 (Set 1) and 0.80 (Set 2) for ranking implicitness levels, and 80–100% accuracy for pragmatic closeness selection—performances comparable to the human participant group. In the hate speech detection application, all three evaluated LLMs (GPT-4-Turbo, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, OpenAI Moderation) show consistent accuracy degradation as sentence implicitness increases, with near-zero detection in the highest implicitness ranges.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper introduces the first automatic metric for sentence implicitness, so no prior implicitness metric exists for direct comparison. Instead, the authors validate IMPSCORE against human judgments on out-of-distribution data: IMPSCORE's Kendall's Tau (0.73–0.80) falls within the range of human-human inter-participant correlations (0.53–1.00), and its Choice accuracy (80–100%) matches the human group's range (60–100%). Notably, IMPSCORE correctly ranks all 'most explicit' and 'most implicit' sentences in every ranking question, while occasionally misranking sentences with minimal implicitness differences—a pattern shared by some human participants. In the hate speech application, the paper does not compare IMPSCORE against other evaluation methods but uses it to stratify data by implicitness level, revealing that GPT-4-Turbo, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, and OpenAI Moderation all suffer significant accuracy drops (often below 20%) on the highest-implicitness samples, a limitation masked by their strong average benchmark scores.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by formalizing a linguistic concept—semantic-pragmatic divergence (rooted in Grice's implicature theory and Carston's explicit/implicit distinction)—into a computable, reference-free scalar metric. Prior NLP metrics (BLEU, BERTScore, readability, politeness) address different text properties, and the few linguistics-adjacent works on implicitness (Vallauri & Masia's verb-level causality bias, Garassino et al.'s qualitative detection) neither produce sentence-level scores nor are automatically reproducible. Methodologically, IMPSCORE repurposes pairwise contrastive learning (common in metric learning and retrieval) with a lightweight Sentence-BERT backbone and simple projection matrices, but applies it to a novel objective: ranking implicitness and pragmatic closeness simultaneously via margin-based ranking losses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

IMPSCORE fills a genuine gap by providing the first automatic, continuous, reference-free measure of sentence implicitness, enabling granular evaluation where prior binary implicit/explicit labels obscured model capabilities. The practical finding that GPT-4-Turbo, Llama-3.1-8B, and OpenAI Moderation all degrade sharply on highly implicit hate speech—despite strong average benchmark scores—has direct implications for AI safety and content moderation. Key limitations include English-only scope, a modest 112K training pairs, a small user study (10 participants), and OOD data partly generated by GPT-4, all of which constrain generalizability claims. Future work on multilingual extension, broader implicitness typologies, and integration as a reward signal in RL training loops could substantially broaden its impact.
