## Problem

Transformer-based motion forecasting models produce hidden states that are difficult to interpret or control at inference. While activation steering via control vectors has been explored in NLP, no prior work has investigated whether interpretable, physically meaningful features (speed, acceleration, direction, agent type) are embedded in the hidden states of multimodal motion transformers, nor whether such features can be leveraged for controllable and zero-shot generalizable forecasting.

## Input / Output

The motion transformers consume past motion sequences (50 timesteps / 5 s on AV2F, 11 timesteps / 1.1 s on Waymo) comprising positions, orientation, acceleration, and speed, along with up to 512 map polylines and traffic light states, producing k=6 future trajectories per agent as 2D Gaussians with confidences. At inference, a single control vector (fitted from the last temporal embedding, i=-1) scaled by a temperature τ is added to all temporal hidden-state embeddings, steering the forecast without retraining. The extracted control vectors themselves are the interpretable artifacts, with SAE-optimized versions yielding an approximately 1-to-1 mapping between τ and relative speed change (e.g., τ=-50 ≈ -50% speed).

## Method

The method proceeds in three stages. First, linear probes trained on hidden states measure neural collapse toward quantized motion features (speed, acceleration, direction, agent type), confirming that interpretable features are linearly separable in the latent space. Second, control vectors are fitted by computing element-wise differences between hidden states of samples with opposing features and pooling via single-component PCA, following the representation-engineering paradigm. Third, sparse autoencoders (SAEs) with sparse intermediate dimensions (best at 128) refine these vectors: hidden states are encoded into sparse representations, differences are computed in that sparse space, and the SAE decoder projects the result back to the original hidden dimension. Enforcing sparsity yields more linear prediction changes when scaling τ, and the SAEs are used only during vector fitting, adding only ~1 ms to inference latency. The paper further demonstrates that higher probing accuracy (i.e., stronger neural collapse) is a prerequisite for high linearity, and that the resulting control vectors enable zero-shot compensation of domain shifts (e.g., 50% speed reduction) without fine-tuning.

## Results

On the Waymo Open Motion dataset, the SAE-optimized speed control vector (Bricken et al. SAE, ReLU, dim=128) achieves a Pearson correlation of 0.993, R² of 0.984, and straightness index of 0.988 for relative speed changes in forecasts, indicating near-perfect linear controllability. For zero-shot generalization to a domain-shifted Waymo validation split (future speeds reduced by ~50%), applying the SAE-128 control vector at τ=−50 reduces minADE from 3.271 to 1.174 and miss rate from 0.580 to 0.236 without any retraining. Linear probing accuracies exceed 0.90 for all interpretable features (speed, acceleration, direction, agent type) across Wayformer, RedMotion, and HPTR on both AV2F and Waymo, confirming strong neural collapse toward interpretable directions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The standard SAE (Bricken et al., 2023) with ReLU achieves the best overall linearity (Pearson 0.993, R² 0.984, S-idx 0.988), outperforming plain PCA pooling (0.988/0.969/0.981) by a clear margin on R². The JumpReLU variant (Rajamanoharan et al., 2024b) matches the Pearson and S-idx scores while marginally improving R² to 0.986, making it the single best configuration. The transposed-encoder SAE (Cunningham et al., 2024) underperforms on all three metrics (0.987/0.971/0.980), which the authors attribute to reduced decoding flexibility from sharing encoder weights. The Koopman autoencoder (Azencot et al., 2020) achieves a perfect straightness index (1.000) but a severely negative R² (−0.057) because its τ range is ~100× smaller than the SAE, compressing the effective control range and destroying the 1-to-1 mapping between τ and relative speed change. Overall, the SAE-based approach dominates all alternatives for practical controllability, with the Bricken SAE + ReLU as the recommended default.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the control-vector / activation-steering paradigm from NLP (Zou et al., 2023; Rimsky et al., 2023) into the motion-forecasting domain, where hidden states of multimodal motion transformers (Wayformer, RedMotion, HPTR) have previously been treated as opaque. It grounds the approach in neural-collapse theory (Papyan et al., 2020; Galanti et al., 2022) by first demonstrating via linear probing that interpretable motion features (speed, acceleration, direction, agent type) are linearly separable in the latent space. A key methodological departure from prior SAE-based interpretability work (Bricken et al., 2023; Cunningham et al., 2024) is that sparse autoencoders are used only offline to optimize control vectors, rather than at inference, yielding negligible (~1 ms) runtime overhead.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The near 1-to-1 mapping between the temperature parameter τ and relative speed change, combined with the zero-shot domain-shift compensation (a 50% speed reduction in the test set corrected by a single vector addition with τ=−50, halving minADE without retraining), makes this a practically deployable tool for safety validation and robustness in end-to-end driving stacks. Open questions include whether the assumed flat latent space and vector-arithmetic approximation hold under stronger curvature, whether the approach extends to richer multimodal features (e.g., static scene elements), and whether the neural-collapse precondition can be enforced during training rather than merely measured post hoc. The authors also identify latent-space safety validation and instruction-based trajectory adjustment as promising downstream applications.
