## Problem

Current flow-based generative models (Flow Matching, Conditional Flow Matching) model population dynamics as independent particles evolving under a global vector field, and are restricted to a single initial population or a finite set of predefined conditions. This is insufficient for biological and physical systems where inter-particle interactions mean the dynamics depend on the population distribution itself, and where generalization to previously unseen populations (e.g., new patients) is essential.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a set of N_i sample points representing an initial cell population (e.g., 43-dimensional mass-cytometry measurements) along with a discrete treatment condition, and produces a predicted time-evolved population of the same cardinality by integrating the learned vector field via an ODE solver. Crucially, the input is consumed as an unordered set: a k-nearest-neighbour graph is constructed over the samples and passed through a GCN to produce a fixed-size population embedding, so the model operates on the distribution rather than individual trajectories. At inference, the model must handle populations of varying size and composition never encountered during training.

## Method

Meta Flow Matching (MFM) amortizes the Flow Matching framework over input distributions by jointly learning (i) a Graph Convolutional Network φ that embeds the initial population {x_0^j} into a fixed-dimensional vector via k-nearest-neighbour graph message passing and average pooling, and (ii) an MLP-based vector field v_t(x | φ(p_0), c; ω) conditioned on this embedding and the treatment condition. Training minimizes the standard FM regression loss (matching ∂f_t/∂t to v_t) with the population embedding as an additional condition, updating ω and θ alternately. The key theoretical insight is that the Cauchy problem on the 2-Wasserstein manifold has a unique solution given p_0, so the initial density embedding is sufficient to determine the push-forward map; Proposition 1 shows MFM reduces to CGFM when conditions are known, and Theorem 1 proves the MFM objective equals the population-conditional FM objective up to a constant. This architecture enables generalization to unseen populations because the GNN learns a continuous representation of distributional structure rather than memorizing discrete population indices.

## Results

On the organoid drug-screen dataset (patients split, 3 unseen patients), MFM (k=50) achieves the best non-OT performance with W1=4.087±0.122, W2=4.218±0.160, MMD=9.26±1.56, and r²=0.926±0.007, outperforming FM (W1=4.171), CGFM (W1=4.189), and ICNN/CellOT (W1=4.157). MFM (k=100) achieves the highest r²=0.931±0.002 among non-OT methods, while FM^w/OT (W1=4.064, r²=0.932) remains the overall best method. On the synthetic letters task, MFM generalizes to unseen letter silhouettes (W1=0.201 on test X's) while FM (W1=0.234) and CGFM (W1=0.334) fail to generalize beyond their training conditions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MFM consistently outperforms all non-OT baselines on the patients split: it beats FM by ~0.08 in W1 (4.087 vs 4.171) and ~0.03 in r² (0.926 vs 0.897), beats CGFM by ~0.10 in W1 (4.087 vs 4.189), and beats ICNN/CellOT by ~0.07 in W1 (4.087 vs 4.157) and ~0.02 in r² (0.926 vs 0.904). Against the OT-based baselines, MFM (k=50) is competitive with FM^w/OT (W1: 4.087 vs 4.064) and CGFM^w/OT (W1: 4.087 vs 4.087), with FM^w/OT holding a slight edge on W1/W2 but MFM achieving comparable r². On the synthetic letters task, MFM is the only method that generalizes to unseen letter silhouettes, achieving W1=0.201 on test X's versus 0.234 for FM and 0.334 for CGFM, confirming that population-level conditioning via GNN embeddings enables distributional generalization that index-based conditioning cannot. The paper notes that OT couplings improve FM and CGFM in the biological setting, suggesting that integrating OT into MFM is a natural future direction.

## Relation to Prior Work

MFM extends the Flow Matching framework (Lipman et al., 2022) by amortizing the learned vector field over initial population distributions, drawing conceptual inspiration from Meta Optimal Transport (Amos et al., 2022) which amortized OT maps over input marginals. Unlike prior single-cell dynamics models (CellOT/ICNN, TrajectoryNet) that treat cells as independent particles under a global vector field restricted to one initial population, MFM conditions the vector field on a GNN embedding of the entire initial population, enabling generalization to unseen distributions. The use of GNNs for population embedding follows the many-body physics simulation literature (Sanchez-Gonzalez et al., 2020), while the Wasserstein manifold geometric framing (Otto, 2001) provides the theoretical justification for why density-dependent vector fields are the natural formulation for interacting particle systems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MFM opens a path toward predicting patient-specific treatment responses without requiring prior observations of that patient, which is directly relevant to personalized oncology. Key limitations include the absence of OT couplings in MFM (despite OT improving FM/CGFM baselines), no treatment of stochastic or unbalanced transport, and the reliance on a single GNN architecture for population embedding. Open questions include whether combining OT with MFM yields further gains, whether the embedding can be pre-trained or learned with auxiliary objectives, and whether the framework extends to manifold-constrained or time-inhomogeneous settings. The modest but consistent empirical improvements over strong baselines (particularly CGFM and ICNN) on the organoid drug-screen dataset suggest the population-embedding mechanism captures meaningful biological structure beyond what per-cell models can.
