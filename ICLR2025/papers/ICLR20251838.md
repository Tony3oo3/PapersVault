## Problem

Existing preference learning methods for text-to-image diffusion models (e.g., Diffusion-DPO) directly adapt DPO objectives from LLMs, introducing an estimated loss that is only upper-bounded by the true DPO objective and differs from the score-matching objective used during pretraining. This mismatch between pretraining and fine-tuning objectives can lead to suboptimal alignment performance, as demonstrated empirically. The paper asks whether preference alignment can be achieved by staying within the score-matching framework that diffusion models are natively trained with.

## Input / Output

For text-to-image generation, the model takes a text prompt and produces a 512×512 (SD1.5) or 1024×1024 (SDXL) RGB image; for text-guided editing, it additionally takes an input image and a natural-language editing instruction. During fine-tuning, the model consumes preference pairs (winning image, losing image, prompt) from Pick-a-Pic v2, where each pair encodes a human preference judgment. Evaluation uses held-out prompts from PickV2, HPSV2, and PartiPrompts, with images sampled at guidance scale 7.5 over 50 denoising steps.

## Method

DSPO fine-tunes a pretrained diffusion model by matching its score function to a target human-preferred score, defined as the sum of the true data-distribution score and a preference-condition score derived from the Bradley-Terry model. To avoid training a separate reward model, the preference signal uses DPO's implicit reward r(x_t, c) = λ·log(p_θ/p_ref), which is expressed in terms of the model's noise-prediction error. The final loss is a weighted L2 distance between the model's noise prediction ε_θ and a modified target that subtracts a preference-weighted correction term (1−σ(r_w−r_l))·(ε_θ−ε_ref), effectively pulling the score toward preferred images while preserving the pretraining denoising objective. Theoretically, the paper proves that with ω(t)=2σ²_{t+1}/λ and γ=1/(2λ), minimizing DSPO by sampling from p_θ yields the same gradient direction as maximizing the RLHF objective, establishing equivalence under the Bradley-Terry reward formulation.

## Results

DSPO fine-tuned on Stable Diffusion v1.5 achieves the highest win-rates across all three evaluation datasets (PickV2, Parti-Prompt, HPSV2) and nearly all five reward models (Pick Score, HPS, Aesthetics, CLIP, Image Reward). The peak win-rate reaches 92.56% on HPSV2 using the HPS reward model, and DSPO attains an Image Reward score of 0.568 versus 0.018 for the base SD1.5. On Parti-Prompt, DSPO achieves 76.96% win-rate on Aesthetics, surpassing Diffusion-DPO (60.42%) by 16.54 points and Diffusion-KTO (72.92%) by 4.04 points. Computational cost is comparable to Diffusion-DPO (4.18 min vs 4.15 min per step; 60.5 vs 60.2 GB peak GPU memory).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DSPO consistently outperforms Diffusion-DPO (Wallace et al., 2024) across all three datasets and nearly all reward models; the gap is most pronounced on Parti-Prompt where DSPO leads by 16.54 points on Aesthetics (76.96 vs 60.42) and by 20.43 points on HPS (87.50 vs 66.48), confirming the paper's claim that the estimated loss in Diffusion-DPO leads to suboptimal performance. Against Diffusion-KTO (Li et al., 2024), DSPO wins on most metrics with a 4.04-point advantage on Parti-Prompt Aesthetics and a 2.72-point lead on HPSV2 Aesthetics, though Diff.-KTO is competitive on HPS scores (e.g., 92.15 vs 92.56 on HPSV2). On SDXL (Table 2), DSPO also surpasses MaPO (Hong et al., 2024) on Pick Score and Image Reward across all datasets, while MaPO leads on Aesthetics for Parti-Prompt (68.23 vs 57.84) and HPSV2 (62.31 vs 51.41), suggesting DSPO's advantage is more consistent on the SD1.5 setting. Overall, DSPO achieves the best or near-best win-rate in 14 out of 15 metric-dataset combinations on SD1.5, with the sole exception being Parti-Prompt CLIP where it trails Diff.-KTO by a negligible 0.02 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the score matching formulation of diffusion models (Song et al., 2020) and the implicit reward mechanism from DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024), but departs from the dominant line of work that directly ports LLM preference optimization objectives (Diffusion-DPO, Diffusion-KTO, MaPO) to diffusion models. The key conceptual shift is recognizing that those adapted objectives introduce an 'estimated loss' that diverges from the pretraining score-matching objective, and instead proposing to perform preference alignment through score matching itself—distilling a human-preference-conditioned score function into the pretrained model. The MDP formulation and step-wise reward structure are inherited from Diffusion-DPO (Wallace et al., 2024), and the Bradley-Terry model provides the preference probability used in the target score.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By unifying the pretraining and fine-tuning objectives under score matching, DSPO offers a principled alternative to the ad-hoc adaptation of LLM preference losses to diffusion models, with a theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) that its optimization direction matches RLHF under specific hyperparameter settings. The practical impact is moderate: DSPO consistently outperforms Diffusion-DPO and Diffusion-KTO across multiple datasets and reward models, with comparable compute cost. Open questions include whether the theoretical equivalence conditions (specific ω(t), γ, and sampling from p_θ) hold in practice given the simplifications made (dropping B(t) and A(t)), whether the approach extends to other generative architectures beyond diffusion models, and whether the gains persist at larger model scales or with more diverse preference data.
