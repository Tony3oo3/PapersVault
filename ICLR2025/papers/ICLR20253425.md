## Problem

Deep tabular models still lag behind leading tree-based methods like CatBoost, and prior attempts to bridge this gap have focused on adding classical techniques into already-complex deep architectures. This paper asks the inverse question: can a 20-year-old classical nearest-neighbor method (NCA) be revitalized by incrementally adding modern deep learning components, and if so, which components are actually necessary to reach competitive tabular performance?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253425/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular instances with numerical features (encoded via PLR-lite: periodic embeddings + shared linear layer + ReLU) and categorical features (one-hot encoded), alongside the full training set which serves as the neighbor pool. During training, only a randomly sampled subset of the training set (Stochastic Neighborhood Sampling) is used for distance computation per mini-batch, while inference resumes full-set neighbor search. The output is a soft-NN weighted average of neighbor labels: a C-dimensional probability vector for classification (argmax gives the predicted class) or a scalar for regression.

## Method

MODERNCA starts from NCA's differentiable soft-NN objective and makes two key upgrades. First (L-NCA): it replaces L-BFGS with SGD, removes the dimensionality-reduction constraint on the linear projection, switches from sum-of-probability to negative log-likelihood loss, and adopts soft-NN inference instead of hard KNN. Second (full MODERNCA): it replaces the linear projection with a deep MLP composed of BatchNorm→Linear→ReLU→Dropout→Linear blocks, applies PLR-lite encoding to numerical features, and introduces Stochastic Neighborhood Sampling (SNS), where each mini-batch computes distances only against a random subset of the training set rather than the full set. The authors find that Euclidean (not squared) distance, batch normalization (over layer norm), and the log loss variant are critical, and that SNS not only reduces compute but improves generalization by exposing the embedding to noisier, more diverse neighbor sets during training.

## Results

On 300 tabular datasets (120 binary classification, 80 multi-class classification, 100 regression), MODERNCA achieves the best average performance rank among all deep tabular methods, ranking first in classification and just behind CatBoost (rank 4.61 vs. 4.56) in regression. Against CatBoost, MODERNCA wins on 114 datasets, ties on 81, and loses on 105 (Win/Tie/Lose = 0.38/0.27/0.35). Against TabR, the closest neighborhood-based deep method, MODERNCA wins on 123 datasets and ties on 108, while training in 87.5 s versus TabR's 173.34 s and using 5.36 GB versus 10.13 GB of memory.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MODERNCA is compared against 20 methods spanning classical, tree-based, deep parametric, and neighborhood-based categories. Against the leading tree-based method CatBoost, MODERNCA wins on 114/300 datasets and ties on 81, making it the closest competitor overall (average rank 4.56 vs. 4.61). Against TabR, the prior state-of-the-art neighborhood-based deep method, MODERNCA wins on 123 datasets and ties on 108, while using roughly half the training time (87.5 s vs. 173.34 s) and less than half the memory (5.36 GB vs. 10.13 GB). Against other deep tabular models, MODERNCA shows clear wins: SwitchTab (0.88/0.09/0.03), DANets (0.74/0.18/0.08), NODE (0.70/0.15/0.15), Tangos (0.66/0.20/0.14), MLP (0.61/0.23/0.15), and FT-Transformer (0.50/0.28/0.23). Against tree-based methods XGBoost and LightGBM, MODERNCA wins on 49% and 45% of datasets respectively but loses on 32% of each, indicating tree-based methods retain an edge on a subset of regression-heavy or high-cardinality datasets. Against classical methods (SVM, KNN, RandomForest), MODERNCA achieves dominant win rates of 0.78, 0.79, and 0.57 respectively, confirming that the deep neighborhood formulation substantially surpasses its non-deep counterparts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper takes the classical Neighbourhood Component Analysis (NCA, Goldberger et al., 2004) — a differentiable metric-learning method that learns a linear projection for KNN — and systematically layers modern deep learning techniques on top, in contrast to the reverse direction taken by TabR (Gorishniy et al., 2024), which embeds neighborhood retrieval into a complex Transformer-like architecture. The authors explicitly frame their approach as an Occam's-razor exploration: starting from the 2004 NCA and incrementally adding SGD, higher-dimensional projections, soft-NN inference, MLP encoders with batch normalization, PLR numerical encoding, and Stochastic Neighborhood Sampling. Prior nonlinear NCA extensions (Salakhutdinov & Hinton, 2007; Min et al., 2010) using restricted Boltzmann machines were noted but dismissed as insufficient, which the authors attribute to the limited deep learning toolkit available two decades ago.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Nearest-neighbor methods have been largely sidelined in tabular ML for over a decade due to the dominance of gradient-boosted trees; this paper demonstrates that a relatively simple deep NCA (MLP encoder + soft-NN loss + stochastic sampling) can match CatBoost on 300 datasets, providing a much-needed strong deep baseline that is faster to train than other deep tabular models. The detailed ablations (loss functions, distance metrics, sampling strategies, architectures) offer a practical recipe for practitioners. Open questions include theoretical explanations for why modern deep techniques specifically revitalize NCA, and the acknowledged limitations around distribution shifts and high-dimensional settings (d ≫ N) where the curse of dimensionality undermines neighborhood retrieval.
