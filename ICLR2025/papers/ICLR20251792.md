## Problem

Numerous empirical methods—coreset selection, dataset distillation, data-free knowledge distillation, and diffusion-based synthetic data generation—claim to protect training data privacy by never exposing the final model to private data, yet none carry formal differential privacy guarantees. Prior evaluations of these methods were flawed, relying on average-case metrics, low-accuracy models, or incorrect theoretical analyses, potentially providing a false sense of security. This paper asks whether training with synthetic data truly protects privacy when evaluated rigorously in the worst case.

## Input / Output

The evaluation interacts only with the final model trained on synthetic data (black-box access), using 500 randomly designated audit samples as mislabeled canaries to simulate worst-case privacy leakage. For each method, 32 shadow ResNet-18 models are trained on CIFAR-10 (50,000 images at 32×32), with each audit sample included in exactly 16 models' training sets. The output is the true positive rate at 0.1% false positive rate (TPR@0.1% FPR) computed via the LiRA membership inference attack, alongside test accuracy and training time for a privacy-utility-efficiency tradeoff comparison.

## Method

The paper systematically evaluates four synthetic-data training paradigms under a unified worst-case privacy auditing protocol: (1) coreset selection (random and forgetting-based), (2) dataset distillation (DM, DSA, MTT, DATM) with three initialization strategies (private data, random noise, OOD data from CINIC-10), (3) data-free knowledge distillation using BN-statistics matching, and (4) fine-tuned Stable Diffusion for image-to-image and text-to-image generation. The key insight is that ML models strongly memorize vulnerable samples (mislabeled data), and this memorization persists through synthetic-data training pipelines, making membership inference highly successful on those samples regardless of the synthetic data's visual dissimilarity. The paper further reveals that trajectory-matching distillation methods (MTT, DATM) can exhibit near-random MIA success rates while still leaking private data visually—a gap not captured by standard MIA. All methods are compared against two DPSGD baselines tuned to matched accuracy levels, showing that DPSGD achieves a superior privacy-utility-efficiency tradeoff across all four paradigms.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, the paper evaluates four synthetic-data training paradigms (coreset selection, dataset distillation, data-free knowledge distillation, and diffusion-based synthesis) against DPSGD using worst-case membership inference (TPR@0.1% FPR on mislabeled canaries). DPSGD(high) achieves 90.36% test accuracy with only 9.31% TPR@0.1% FPR at 1.9× training cost, while the best synthetic-data method (MTT with OOD initialization) reaches 80.42% accuracy with 0.18% TPR but requires 39.7× more training time. Coreset selection (72.94% TPR) and DFKD (43.38% TPR) leak substantially more privacy than DPSGD at comparable utility. The paper concludes that no empirical synthetic-data method achieves a better privacy-utility-efficiency tradeoff than DPSGD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DPSGD dominates all four synthetic-data paradigms on the joint privacy-utility-efficiency tradeoff. Coreset selection (Toneva et al., 2019) retains 72.94% TPR@0.1% FPR — far worse than DPSGD(high) at 9.31% — because the selected subset still contains mislabeled canaries that are strongly memorized. DFKD (Fang et al., 2022) leaks 43.38% TPR because the teacher model's memorization of canaries is transferred to the student via distillation, even though the student never sees private data. The diffusion-based approach (Yuan et al., 2024) achieves 12.65% TPR with text-to-image generation but at an extreme 385.4× training cost, making it impractical. Among dataset distillation methods, MTT with OOD initialization (Cazenavette et al., 2022) achieves the lowest TPR (0.18%) but at 39.7× cost and only 80.42% accuracy; DM (Zhao & Bilen, 2023) and DSA (Zhao & Bilen, 2021) with private initialization leak 18.02% and 17.74% TPR respectively, while DATM (Guo et al., 2024) with private initialization achieves 1.29% TPR but still requires far more compute than DPSGD. The paper's central finding is that no synthetic-data method beats DPSGD, and several (especially coreset selection and DFKD) provide a false sense of privacy when evaluated only in the average case.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the rigorous worst-case privacy evaluation framework established by Aerni et al. (2024) from general empirical defenses (label smoothing, loss calibration) to four specific synthetic-data training paradigms: coreset selection, dataset distillation, data-free knowledge distillation, and diffusion-model-based synthesis. It departs from prior claims by Dong et al. (2022) and Carlini et al. (2022b) that dataset distillation provides 'privacy for free,' demonstrating that those evaluations used improper metrics (AUC instead of TPR@low FPR) and did not test worst-case scenarios. The paper also builds on the synthetic data generation literature (Toneva et al., Zhao & Bilen, Cazenavette et al., Yuan et al.) by treating those methods as privacy defenses to be audited rather than as contributions to be endorsed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a widespread working assumption in the ML community: that training on synthetic data inherently protects privacy because the model never sees the original data. The finding that trajectory-matching distillation (MTT) achieves near-random MIA success rates while producing synthetic images that visually replicate private data reveals a critical blind spot in MIA-based auditing. Open questions include whether diffusion models trained from scratch (without large-scale pretraining) offer better privacy-utility tradeoffs, whether visual leakage can be captured by extended MIA protocols, and whether these findings generalize beyond CIFAR-10/ResNet-18. The practical implication is that practitioners relying on synthetic data as a privacy mechanism need formal DP guarantees or at minimum worst-case evaluation before claiming protection.
