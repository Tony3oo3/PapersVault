## Problem

Adaptive cell populations (cancer, bacteria) develop memory of past drug exposure, making them resistant to standard dosing protocols. While optimal bang-bang control is known for memoryless phenotypic switching models, no analytical solution exists for non-Markovian dynamics with fractional-order memory, and in clinical settings the underlying model parameters are typically unknown, rendering classical optimal control intractable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250056/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent observes only a window of K=5 instantaneous growth-rate estimates (c_t = Δ⁻¹ log N_t/N_{t-Δ}) paired with one-hot encoded past dosing actions, never the resistant fraction or any model parameters. At each decision step (Δ=0.01 h), it outputs a single binary action (u∈{0,1}) indicating whether to apply maximum drug dose or pause. Episodes terminate if the population exceeds 150% of its initial size, and the cumulative reward equals Δ⁻¹ log(N_0/N_T), directly aligning the agent's objective with total population reduction.

## Method

The paper introduces a non-Markovian phenotypic switching model where susceptible and resistant subpopulations evolve under a Caputo fractional derivative of order μ∈(0,1], encoding multi-timescale memory of past drug exposure. Using Pontryagin's minimum principle, the authors prove that the optimal control is bang-bang (u∈{0,1}) for any μ and any monotonic dose-response functions, reducing the continuous action space to a binary one without loss of optimality. A Double DQN agent with frame-stacking (K=5) is then trained end-to-end to recover this policy from growth-rate observations alone, successfully matching the known memoryless optimum and discovering a memory-dependent pulsing strategy with increasing frequency for μ<1. For the clinically relevant case where μ is unknown or time-varying, a generalist FQF agent with NoisyNets is trained via domain randomization over μ∈[0.6,1], yielding a single policy robust to both memory-strength shifts and large observation noise.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that Double DQN recovers the known optimal bang-bang pulsing policy in the memoryless case (μ=1) using only instantaneous growth-rate observations (no access to resistant fractions or model parameters). In non-Markovian settings (μ<1), the learned DQN policy outperforms both the constant-dosing strategy and the memoryless resistant-fraction-based pulsing baseline in reducing total cell population over 100-hour episodes. A generalist FQF agent trained via domain randomization over μ∈[0.6,1] generalizes to unseen memory strengths and remains robust to Gaussian observation noise, maintaining population reduction under both perturbations. Continuous-action algorithms (PPO, SAC) substantially underperform DQN, failing to discover the required bang-bang structure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the memoryless pulsing protocol of Fischer & Bluethgen (2024), the RL policy wins decisively in all non-Markovian regimes (μ<1): the memoryless strategy, which switches at fixed resistant-fraction thresholds, becomes ineffective because memory accelerates re-adaptation to the resistant state, whereas the RL agent dynamically increases pulsing frequency over the episode to compensate. Against PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), DQN clearly outperforms: PPO finds the initial constant-application phase but then defaults to the average action rather than sustaining bang-bang switching, attributed to saturating gradients at the action-space boundaries. Against SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018), DQN again wins; SAC's entropy bonus further discourages the deterministic bang-bang policy, causing it to collapse to the mean action after a brief initial phase. All three comparisons are conducted at fixed μ=0.9 with equal-compute hyperparameter sweeps, and the RL policy's advantage is consistent across the range of memory strengths tested (μ=0.6–1.0).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of optimal control theory for drug dosing (Fischer & Bluethgen, 2024; Padmanabhan et al., 2017) and deep reinforcement learning for biological control (Engelhardt, 2020). Prior RL-based dosing work required access to resistant fractions or full model parameters to define the agent's state, which is clinically infeasible; here the agent observes only instantaneous growth rates. The key methodological departure is the use of Pontryagin's minimum principle to prove bang-bang optimality for a non-Markovian (fractional-order) switching model, which then justifies restricting the RL action space to binary controls—a principled simplification that prior RL-for-dosing papers did not exploit. Standard algorithms (Double DQN, FQF with NoisyNets) from Stable-Baselines3 are used without architectural novelty; the contribution lies in the problem formulation and the OC-to-RL pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a model-free RL agent using only growth-rate observations can recover the OC-optimal bang-bang policy—and generalize across unknown memory strengths via domain randomization—offers a practical path toward adaptive therapy protocols that do not require fitting full dynamical models per patient. However, the framework remains deterministic, single-population, and validated only in simulation; the authors explicitly flag the sim-to-real gap, the absence of stochastic heterogeneity, and the need for patient-level constraints (toxicity, host interactions) as open problems. For the broader RL community, the paper is a clean demonstration that OC-derived structural insights (bang-bang control) can dramatically simplify the action space and improve sample efficiency, a pattern likely transferable to other constrained control problems.
