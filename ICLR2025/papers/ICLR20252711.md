## Problem

Multi-agent reinforcement learning has shown strong results in simulated environments but struggles to generalize across problem variations in real-world settings. Contextual reinforcement learning (CRL) formalizes this generalization requirement, yet no standardized multi-agent CRL benchmark exists that is grounded in a real-world application with realistic, data-driven context distributions. Existing benchmarks are either purely simulated (lacking real-world complexity) or based on real-world tasks without modeling the full distribution of contextual factors that affect generalization.

## Input / Output

Each controlled vehicle receives a vectorized observation from a 2D SUMO simulation containing its own speed, relative distance to the traffic signal, signal phase state and remaining time, lane index, turn intention, and speed/distance/turn-signal status of leader and follower vehicles on adjacent lanes. Context features (temperature, humidity, fuel type, lane length, speed limit, signal timing plan, eco-driving adoption level) are optionally exposed as observed conditioning inputs. The output is a continuous longitudinal acceleration command per controlled vehicle at 0.5-second timesteps; lane changing is handled by a fixed rule-based controller, isolating the continuous control problem.

## Method

IntersectionZoo constructs 10 Contextual MDPs (CMDPs) from 16,334 signalized intersections across 10 US cities, each CMDP encoding over one million traffic scenarios parameterized by intersection topology (from OSM), traffic flow rates (from AADT data), atmospheric conditions (from NCEI), vehicle fleet composition (from MOVES databases), and eco-driving adoption level. Human-driven vehicles are modeled with Bayesian-calibrated Intelligent Driver Models (IDM) fitted to CitySim drone trajectory data, while emissions are computed via fast neural surrogate models replicating the EPA's MOVES simulator. The benchmark is formulated as a Dec-POMDP with a multi-objective reward combining fleet-level CO₂ emissions, travel time, and a stopping penalty, and supports both IID (within-city train/test split) and OOD (cross-city transfer) evaluation protocols. The paper benchmarks PPO, DDPG, MAPPO, MADDPG, GCRL, and the GLOSA heuristic against a calibrated IDM baseline, introducing a composite 'effective emission benefit' metric that zeroes out any approach where throughput degrades, and demonstrates that current MARL algorithms achieve only modest positive benefits while failing to solve many individual context-MDPs.

## Results

On the Atlanta CMDP (621 intersections), MADDPG achieves the highest effective emission benefit at 4.47%, followed by DDPG (2.84%), GLOSA (2.30%), MAPPO (1.11%), PPO (0.47%), and GCRL (0.0%). On the Salt Lake City CMDP (282 intersections), DDPG leads at 2.95%, followed by GLOSA (0.95%), MAPPO (0.61%), PPO (0.05%), while MADDPG and GCRL both score 0.0%. All values are measured as percentage emission reduction relative to the IDM human-driving baseline, with the constraint that throughput must not decrease. The paper emphasizes that while aggregate benefits are positive for most methods, emission benefit histograms reveal that many individual context-MDPs remain unsolved, indicating substantial room for CRL algorithmic improvement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DDPG is the most consistently strong MARL method, outperforming the rule-based GLOSA baseline on both Atlanta (2.84% vs. 2.30%) and SLC (2.95% vs. 0.95%), making it the only RL method to beat GLOSA on both cities. MADDPG achieves the single best result on Atlanta (4.47%, well above GLOSA's 2.30%) but completely fails on SLC (0.0%), suggesting poor cross-city generalization. PPO, MAPPO, and GCRL all underperform the simple GLOSA heuristic on both cities, with GCRL scoring 0.0% everywhere. The paper's central finding is that no MARL algorithm solves all context-MDPs within a CMDP — benefit histograms show large fractions of intersection approaches yielding zero or negative benefits — demonstrating that current multi-agent RL methods struggle to generalize in the CRL setting that IntersectionZoo is designed to benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CRL benchmarking literature (CARL, Procgen, MetaDrive, SMACv2) by grounding the benchmark in a real-world multi-agent task—cooperative eco-driving at signalized intersections—rather than video games or grid worlds. It builds on the neural surrogate emission models of Sanchez et al. (2022) to replicate MOVES emissions, and on the Dec-POMDP/CMDP formalism from Ghosh et al. (2021) and Hallak et al. (2015) to structure the problem. The key departure from prior benchmarks is the use of data-driven context distributions derived from 16,334 actual US intersections (OSM, AADT, NCEI data) rather than procedurally generated or hand-crafted variations, and the explicit multi-agent mixed-motive setting with uncontrolled HDVs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

IntersectionZoo fills a clear gap: no prior benchmark simultaneously offers multi-agent CRL, real-world data-driven context distributions, multi-objective rewards, and both IID/OOD evaluation protocols. The demonstration that state-of-the-art MARL algorithms (PPO, DDPG, MAPPO, MADDPG, GCRL) fail to generalize across context-MDPs—sometimes achieving 0% benefit—provides concrete evidence that CRL generalization remains an open algorithmic challenge. Limitations include the restriction to continuous longitudinal control (lane changing is rule-based), the 2D vectorized representation that isolates generalization from perception, and potential discrepancies between simulated and real traffic due to data gaps. Open questions include whether the benchmark difficulty is appropriately calibrated, whether the findings transfer to discrete or hybrid control settings, and whether the ~1M scenario scale is sufficient to capture the full diversity of real-world driving conditions.
