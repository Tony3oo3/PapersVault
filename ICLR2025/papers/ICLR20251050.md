## Problem

Due to the trillions of tokens in LLM pretraining, traditional train-test separation cannot isolate whether models genuinely generalise or merely retrieve memorised answers. It remains unclear whether LLMs answer reasoning questions by retrieving specific pretraining documents or by applying a more robust, generalisable strategy. This paper asks: what kind of generalisation strategy do LLMs employ when performing reasoning tasks, and which pretraining data do they actually rely on?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251050/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 5 million pretraining documents (512 tokens each, ~2.5B tokens total, sampled to mirror the training distribution) and 80 queries (40 reasoning, 40 factual) with their model-generated completions, and produces per-document influence scores normalized by the information content of the completion in nats. Influence is computed over the full chain-of-thought completion, not just the final answer. Query gradients are stored as low-rank approximations via approximate SVD to fit within a single memory pass over the document set, limiting the experiment to ~80 queries per run.

## Method

The paper adapts EK-FAC influence functions (Grosse et al., 2023) to rank pretraining documents by their causal effect on the log-likelihood of a model's completion, using the cross-entropy loss as a continuous differentiable proxy for accuracy. The Hessian is estimated via Kronecker-factored eigenbasis decomposition with block-diagonal approximation (2 blocks for 7B, 4 for 35B), and query gradients are compressed using probabilistic approximate SVD to enable a single pass over 5M documents. Influence scores are normalized per nat of completion information to enable comparison across queries of different lengths. The key analytical contribution is contrasting influence rankings for reasoning queries (two-step arithmetic, slope calculation, linear equations) against factual retrieval queries: the authors show that documents influential for one reasoning query are strongly correlated in influence for other queries of the same task type (indicating shared procedural knowledge), while factual queries show no such cross-query correlation and instead have their specific answers appearing in top-ranked documents. Counterfactual re-training experiments on smaller models validate that EK-FAC influence scores predict downstream accuracy changes better than first-order TracIn baselines.

## Results

The paper's headline finding is that influence scores for reasoning queries of the same task show a strongly significant positive Pearson correlation (all p < 4×10⁻⁸), while factual queries show no such correlation (p ≈ 4×10⁻³), indicating documents carry reusable procedural knowledge. For factual questions, the answer appears in the top 0.01% (top 500) influential documents 55% of the time (7B) and 30% (35B), but for reasoning questions only 7.4% (7B) and 0% (35B). For slope queries, documents containing procedural knowledge (code or math formulae) appear in the top 100 documents for 16/20 queries (7B) and all 20 queries (35B). Code data is strongly overrepresented in the top portions of reasoning rankings, with StackExchange showing 10× more influential data than expected under random sampling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares EK-FAC influence functions against TracIn (Pruthi et al., 2020) and random removal in counterfactual re-training experiments on three datasets (Wikitext-2, DROP, RACE). On Wikitext-2 perplexity (Table 3), removing the top-300 EK-FAC-influential documents increases perplexity from 22.15 (random) to 23.05, versus 22.45 for TracIn, showing EK-FAC identifies documents with roughly 2× the impact on downstream quality. On DROP accuracy (Table 5), EK-FAC removal drops accuracy to 0.38 at k=2000, compared to 0.43 for TracIn and 0.57 for random, a 19-point gap over random. On RACE (Table 6), EK-FAC removal drops accuracy to 0.74 at k=2500 versus 0.79 for TracIn and 0.81 for random. EK-FAC consistently outperforms TracIn across all three datasets, with the gap being largest on Wikitext-2 (perplexity) and smallest on RACE (multiple-choice), which the authors attribute to the shorter fine-tuning duration reducing the importance of second-order information.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Grosse et al. (2023), who made EK-FAC influence functions tractable for LLMs up to 50B parameters and showed that larger models rely on more abstractly related pretraining data. The influence function framework itself traces back to Koh & Liang (2017) and Hampel (1974). The key departure is a shift from broad generalization analysis to a targeted contrast between reasoning and factual retrieval: while Grosse et al. characterized what data influences completions generally, this paper asks specifically whether reasoning traces are driven by retrieval of specific answers or by procedural knowledge shared across queries. The paper also positions itself as complementary to mechanistic interpretability (sparse autoencoders, induction heads) by focusing on data attribution rather than weight interpretation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LLMs reason via procedural knowledge rather than retrieval has direct implications for pretraining data selection: high-quality data demonstrating procedures across diverse tasks may matter more than exhaustive coverage of specific problem instances. However, significant limitations temper the conclusions: only 2.5B of trillions of pretraining tokens are sampled, only simple mathematical reasoning is studied, and the authors acknowledge the alternative explanation that truly influential documents may lie in the unsampled majority. Open questions include whether the pattern extends to non-mathematical reasoning (inductive, logical), the specific role of code as a cross-task procedural source, and how feed-forward influence interacts with attention-based reasoning circuits.
