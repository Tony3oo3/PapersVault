## Problem

Symmetries in neural network loss functions (permutation, rescaling, rotation) create low-capacity saddle points that trap optimization, causing neuron or feature collapses that degrade model expressivity. While prior work characterized the existence and dynamical properties of these symmetric solutions, no method existed to provably and completely remove them from the loss landscape. This paper fills that gap with a model-agnostic, symmetry-agnostic technique that eliminates almost all discrete symmetries without requiring knowledge of which symmetries are present.

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard training pipeline (model parameters θ, per-sample loss ℓ, and weight decay coefficient γ) and augments it with a single random static bias vector θ₀ ~ N(0, σ₀²I) sampled once before training. The output is a modified loss ℓ_r(θ,x) = ℓ(θ+θ₀) + γ‖θ‖² that provably contains no reflection symmetry with probability 1. For uncountable symmetries (e.g., rotation in self-supervised learning), an additional anisotropic weight decay with a diagonal matrix D having distinct entries is used. The only new hyperparameter is σ₀, recommended at 0.01/√d relative to the initialization scale, and the method adds negligible computational overhead (Tables 2–3 show <1% time and memory increase).

## Method

The core mechanism is decoupling symmetric solutions from the small-norm solutions favored by weight decay: a random static bias θ₀ shifts the symmetric fixed points so they no longer coincide with the weight-decay minimum, creating a nonzero gradient that pulls parameters away from collapse. Theorem 1 proves that for any countable set of reflection symmetries, the biased loss ℓ_r has no reflection symmetry with probability 1 over the sampling of θ₀. For uncountable symmetries (rotation, double rotation), Theorem 2 shows that replacing the isotropic weight decay with an anisotropic one using a diagonal matrix D with distinct entries (D_ii ~ Uniform(1−ε, 1+ε)) restricts any remaining symmetry to satisfy both Pθ₀ = θ′ and PD = DP, leaving at most finitely many. Theorem 3 further quantifies the strength of symmetry breaking as Ω(γσ₀), showing the effect scales linearly with the product of weight decay and bias variance. The method is implemented as a single line of code—biasing parameters by θ₀ while keeping standard weight decay—and is architecture-independent, requiring no enumeration or knowledge of the symmetries present.

## Results

On the self-supervised SimCLR task (CIFAR-100, ResNet18), syre reduces the low-rankness of learned features from 70% to 0% and improves last-layer linear-probe accuracy from 22.2% to 32.5% (σ₀=0.01), while preserving penultimate-layer accuracy (~46%). On CIFAR-10 with ResNet18, syre introduces no measurable performance degradation for σ₀ < 0.2, confirming compatibility with standard training. In continual learning (Slippery-Ant RL), syre maintains the effective rank of the policy network and achieves higher returns than vanilla PPO or PPO with weight decay alone. The method adds negligible overhead: per-batch time increases by <1% and GPU memory by <1% across MLP, ResNet, and ViT architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary external baseline is the W-fix heuristic from Lim et al. (2024), which holds a fixed fraction of weights unchanged to break permutation symmetry. In the reparametrized linear regression experiment, both vanilla SGD and W-fix collapse to low-capacity (dead-neuron) states, whereas syre avoids collapse entirely throughout training. In the controlled symmetry benchmarking (Figure 4, d=1000), syre is the only method that smoothly interpolates between well-optimized solutions and low-symmetry solutions across both unstructured and structured spectra, while W-fix, dropout, and weight decay alone fail to achieve this. On ViT for CIFAR-10 (Section B.5), syre outperforms both the W-asymmetric and σ-asymmetric networks proposed by Lim et al. (2024) in test accuracy and rank preservation. Internally, syre is compared against vanilla weight decay, which the paper shows is insufficient to prevent low-rank collapses in feature representations (70% low-rankness) or posterior collapse in β-VAE, whereas syre + weight decay resolves both issues.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Ziyin (2024), which established the theoretical framework showing that reflection symmetries in loss functions create low-capacity saddle points that trap training, but explicitly noted that 'no methods are known to enable full escape from them.' It also departs from Lim et al. (2024), which empirically explored symmetry removal via a W-fix heuristic but was only proved to work for explicit permutation symmetries in fully connected layers. The key conceptual advance here is that a random static bias combined with standard weight decay provably removes all discrete symmetries—known or hidden—without requiring any knowledge of the specific symmetry structure, making the method both architecture-independent and symmetry-agnostic.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method addresses a pervasive and underappreciated failure mode—symmetry-induced capacity reduction—that manifests as neural collapse, posterior collapse, loss of plasticity in continual learning, and low-capacity traps in self-supervised learning, all with a one-line code change. The paper's own experiments reveal an important limitation: in SimCLR, fixing the last layer's symmetry explains only ~50% of the performance gap between penultimate and last layer representations, indicating symmetry is necessary but not sufficient to explain all representational defects. Open questions include whether the method scales to large language models (where symmetries are abundant but the paper only speculates), how to tune the bias magnitude for the optimization-vs-symmetry-removal tradeoff at scale, and whether deliberately re-introducing controlled symmetries (as the authors suggest) could serve as a capacity regularizer.
