## Problem

While LLMs have been evaluated on coding benchmarks and narrow ML sub-tasks, no existing benchmark holistically measures autonomous end-to-end ML engineering at a difficulty level comparable to real-world practice. MLE-bench fills this gap by providing challenging, diverse ML engineering tasks with a direct mechanism to contextualize agent performance against human baselines via Kaggle leaderboards.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251816/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each task is a self-contained Kaggle competition consisting of a scraped description, a dataset (with a reconstructed 10% test split when the original test set is unavailable), and locally implemented grading code. The agent must produce a single submission.csv at a fixed path within 24 hours on a fixed hardware spec (36 vCPUs, 440 GB RAM, one 24 GB A10 GPU); a local validation server checks format validity but never reveals a score. Submissions are graded against the competition's private leaderboard using Kaggle's medal thresholds (bronze/silver/gold), and the headline metric is the percentage of competitions earning any medal.

## Method

The benchmark comprises 75 hand-curated Kaggle competitions (22 low, 38 medium, 15 high complexity) spanning NLP, computer vision, audio, tabular, and 3D tasks, each manually ported with new train/test splits and local grading logic. Agents are evaluated using three open-source scaffolds—AIDE (tree search over solutions, purpose-built for Kaggle), MLAB (ResearchAgent), and OpenHands (CodeActAgent)—paired with frontier LMs (o1-preview, GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama 3.1 405B). Anti-cheating is enforced via a GPT-4o-based log-analysis tool that flags rule violations and the Dolos plagiarism detector that compares submitted code against top-50 Kaggle notebooks (disqualifying >60% similarity). The paper further probes performance ceilings through pass@k scaling (o1-preview doubles from 16.9% to 34.1% at pass@8), hardware variation (CPU-only vs. 1×A10 vs. 2×A10), extended runtime (24 h vs. 100 h), and contamination analysis (familiarity scoring and obfuscated descriptions), finding no systematic inflation from memorization.

## Results

The headline metric is the percentage of competitions where an agent achieves at least a bronze medal (Any Medal %). The best-performing configuration, o1-preview with the AIDE scaffold, achieves 16.9% any medal (3.4% bronze, 4.1% silver, 9.4% gold) across 75 Kaggle competitions. GPT-4o with AIDE achieves 8.7% any medal, while Claude 3.5 Sonnet and Llama 3.1 405B with AIDE achieve 7.6% and 3.0% respectively. Performance scales with attempts: o1-preview's score doubles from 16.9% (pass@1) to 34.1% (pass@8), and GPT-4o improves from 8.7% (24 hours) to 11.8% (100 hours).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three agent scaffolds (AIDE, MLAB, OpenHands) paired with GPT-4o, and four models (o1-preview, GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama 3.1 405B) paired with the best scaffold (AIDE). AIDE, which is purpose-built for Kaggle competitions with tree-search over solutions, substantially outperforms the general-purpose scaffolds: GPT-4o (AIDE) achieves 8.7% any medal versus 4.4% for OpenHands and 0.8% for MLAB, despite all three making a similar number of valid submissions. Among models with AIDE, o1-preview nearly doubles GPT-4o's medal rate (16.9% vs. 8.7%), while Claude 3.5 Sonnet (7.6%) and Llama 3.1 405B (3.0%) trail significantly. The paper also notes that Weco AI's original AIDE report claimed >50% of human competitors beaten, but MLE-bench's harder competition selection yields only ~10% above-median performance for state-of-the-art models, suggesting the benchmark is more challenging than prior evaluations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of agent-evaluation work on ML tasks (MLAgentBench, ML-Bench) by scaling from 13 baseline-improvement tasks to 75 from-scratch Kaggle competitions with direct human baselines via leaderboards. It departs from prior coding benchmarks (HumanEval, APPS, SWE-bench) by requiring full end-to-end ML engineering—data preparation, model training, experimentation, and submission—rather than code generation or repository-level fixes. The benchmark also explicitly positions itself against concurrent work (DSBench) and prior claims (Weco AI's AIDE report), arguing its competition selection is substantially harder and more diverse.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MLE-bench provides the first standardized, human-calibrated benchmark for tracking autonomous ML engineering progress, directly relevant to AI safety frameworks (OpenAI Preparedness, Anthropic RSP, DeepMind Frontier Safety) that need measurable autonomy thresholds. The 16.9% medal rate for the best agent reveals a large gap to human performance, while the finding that pass@6 with GPT-4o matches pass@1 with o1-preview suggests compute-attempt scaling may be a practical lever. Open questions include whether agents can handle truly open-ended research (no clear problem statement or metric), how contamination will affect future models, and whether the benchmark's resource intensity (1800 GPU-hours per run) limits its adoption.
