## Problem

Discriminative classifiers trained with cross-entropy loss tend to learn shortcut solutions that rely on spurious correlations, causing catastrophic performance drops under even minor distribution shift. Prior mitigations require specialized augmentations, extra hyperparameters, multi-stage training, or explicit knowledge of which features are spurious. This paper asks whether a fundamentally different training objective—class-conditional generation—can avoid shortcut learning without any of these additional requirements.

## Input / Output

For image tasks, inputs are 256×256×3 RGB images compressed to 32×32×4 latent embeddings via a pre-trained VAE; for text tasks, inputs are token sequences padded to length 512. In both cases the output is a single class label. At inference, the diffusion-based classifier samples 100 noise vectors per class and selects the class with the lowest Monte Carlo denoising error, while the autoregressive classifier performs one forward pass per class token and selects the class with the lowest sequence cross-entropy.

## Method

The core contribution is repurposing standard generative modeling pipelines for classification: a class-conditional latent diffusion model (395M-param U-Net) for images and a Llama-style autoregressive Transformer (15M–42M params) for text, both trained from scratch with their standard generative objectives and no modifications. For the autoregressive model, the BOS token is replaced by a dedicated class token so that the sequence likelihood directly yields log p(x|y); for diffusion, the reweighted variational lower bound approximates log p(x|y). The key insight is that the generative objective forces the model to account for every feature in x, preventing it from 'satisfying' itself with only spurious correlations and maintaining a consistent gradient signal across both majority and minority groups throughout training. A Gaussian toy analysis (LDA vs. logistic regression) reveals that generative classifiers have an inductive bias toward low-variance, consistently predictive features, and the authors map five real benchmarks onto a generalization phase diagram showing when this bias is advantageous.

## Results

On five standard distribution shift benchmarks, the generative classifier achieves the best worst-group (WG) or out-of-distribution (OOD) accuracy on all five datasets. The most dramatic improvement is on Waterbirds, where the generative classifier reaches 79.4% WG accuracy versus 32.9% for the best discriminative baseline (JTT). On Camelyon17 (domain shift), it achieves 90.8% OOD accuracy versus 78.3% for ERM. Surprisingly, the generative classifier also achieves better in-distribution accuracy on 3 of 5 datasets (Waterbirds 96.8%, Camelyon 98.3%, FMoW 62.8%), indicating reduced overfitting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The generative classifier outperforms all four discriminative baselines (ERM, LfF, JTT, RWY/DFR) on every OOD/WG metric across all five benchmarks. The margin is largest on Waterbirds WG (79.4% vs. 32.9% for JTT, a 46.5-point gap) and Camelyon OOD (90.8% vs. 78.3% for ERM/RWY). On CelebA WG, the win is narrow (69.4% vs. 68.9% for RWY/DFR), and on CivilComments WG the gap is 3.3 points over RWY/DFR (61.4% vs. 58.1%). In-distribution, the generative classifier loses to ERM on CelebA (91.2% vs. 92.4%) and CivilComments (79.8% vs. 90.6%), but wins on Waterbirds (96.8% vs. 88.8%), Camelyon (98.3% vs. 95.2%), and FMoW (62.8% vs. 52.1%). The paper also shows that scaling discriminative model size (ResNet-50 to ResNet-152) does not close the OOD gap, ruling out parameter count as a confounder.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper revives the generative classifier paradigm (dating to Fisher's LDA and Naive Bayes) by repurposing modern class-conditional generative models—latent diffusion models for images and autoregressive Llama-style transformers for text—as classifiers via Bayes' rule. It builds directly on Li et al. (2023), who first showed that ImageNet-trained diffusion models exhibit 'effective robustness' on ImageNet-A, but extends that observation to a comprehensive suite of five distribution shift benchmarks across image and text modalities, with controlled from-scratch training. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant discriminative ERM paradigm and from prior mitigation strategies (JTT, LfF, DFR/RWY) that require knowledge of spurious features or add training stages, arguing instead that the generative objective p(x|y) inherently provides more consistent learning signal by forcing the model to model all features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstration of 'effective robustness'—where generative classifiers outperform discriminative models out-of-distribution at any given in-distribution accuracy—suggests a fundamentally different inductive bias that could reshape how classification is approached once inference costs are addressed. The Gaussian toy analysis providing 'generalization phase diagrams' offers practitioners a principled framework for predicting when generative vs. discriminative methods will be preferable based on data properties (spurious feature scale, noise variance, core feature reliability). Key limitations include impractically high inference cost (especially for diffusion-based classifiers requiring multiple forward passes per class), untested scaling behavior, and unclear integration with standard augmentations like Mixup. The paper also opens an exciting direction: applying the generative p(x|y) formulation to language modeling tasks like sentiment analysis and reasoning, which are currently done discriminatively.
