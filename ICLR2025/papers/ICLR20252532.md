## Problem

Sampling-based uncertainty quantification methods (BNNs with Bayesian model averaging and deep ensembles) rely on a small number of sampled distributions (typically 5) to approximate the true predictive distribution, yet the epistemic uncertainty induced by this limited information is not explicitly acknowledged. The paper asks whether the uncertainty estimation quality of these methods can be improved given the constrained number of available predictive distributions, without requiring additional training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252532/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The credal wrapper is a post-hoc, plug-and-play module that takes as input a finite set of N single softmax probability vectors (N=5 for DEs, N=5–50 for BNNs) produced by an already-trained model on an RGB image, and outputs: (i) per-class probability intervals [p_L, p_U] defining a credal set, (ii) a single intersection probability vector for class prediction, and (iii) uncertainty estimates (EU, AU, TU) via generalized entropy. For high-class-count tasks (C=100 or 1000), a Probability Interval Approximation (PIA) algorithm reduces the optimization dimensionality from C to J (e.g., J=20 or 50) to keep computation tractable.

## Method

Given N individual probability distributions, the method extracts per-class upper and lower probability bounds via element-wise max and min across samples (eq. 5), which define a non-empty credal set — a convex subset of the probability simplex constrained by those intervals (eq. 6). From this credal set, a unique intersection probability is derived (eq. 9–11) as the normalized convex combination of the lower and upper bounds with a single scalar α, chosen so that the result is a valid probability vector that treats all class intervals with equal trust. Uncertainty is then quantified using generalized entropy: total uncertainty is the upper entropy (max Shannon entropy over the credal set), aleatoric uncertainty is the lower entropy (min Shannon entropy), and epistemic uncertainty is their difference (eq. 4). The approach requires no retraining and is agnostic to the underlying model (BNN or DE), and the PIA algorithm (Algorithm 1) merges lower-ranked classes to reduce the convex optimization cost from O(C) to O(J) variables while preserving OOD detection performance.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with VGG16, the credal wrapper DE achieves 91.96% AUROC on SVHN OOD detection (vs. 89.74% baseline) and 89.80% on Tiny-ImageNet (vs. 88.49%), while reducing ECE on corrupted CIFAR-10-C from 0.314 to 0.200. On the large-scale ResNet-50 setup, the credal wrapper DE improves AUROC from 89.58% to 93.77% (CIFAR-10 vs. SVHN) and from 73.83% to 80.22% (CIFAR-100 vs. SVHN). The method also outperforms EDD (Ensemble Distribution Distillation) on all OOD detection metrics, e.g., 91.96% vs. 91.21% AUROC on SVHN with VGG16 DE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The credal wrapper consistently outperforms the classical Deep Ensemble (DE) baseline on OOD detection: with VGG16, AUROC improves from 89.74% to 91.96% (SVHN) and 88.49% to 89.80% (Tiny-ImageNet), while ECE on corrupted CIFAR-10-C drops from 0.314 to 0.200. Against BNNR and BNNF baselines (VGG16), the credal wrapper gains roughly 1.1–1.2 AUROC points on SVHN and 0.7–0.8 on Tiny-ImageNet, with ECE on corrupted data reduced by ~0.05–0.07. Compared to EDD (Ensemble Distribution Distillation), the credal wrapper DE achieves higher AUROC on both SVHN (91.96% vs. 91.21%) and Tiny-ImageNet (89.80% vs. 87.04%) with VGG16, and the advantage persists with ResNet-18 (88.63% vs. 87.26% on SVHN). On the large-scale ResNet-50 setup (Table 2), the credal wrapper DE improves AUROC by 4.2 points (CIFAR-10 vs. SVHN: 89.58%→93.77%) and 6.4 points (CIFAR-100 vs. SVHN: 73.83%→80.22%), confirming consistent gains across architectures and dataset scales. Test accuracy on in-distribution data remains essentially unchanged (within ±0.1%), indicating the wrapper does not sacrifice predictive performance for better uncertainty estimation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the intersection probability transform (Cuzzolin, 2022) for mapping credal sets to single predictions, the probability interval framework (De Campos et al., 1994) for defining credal sets from per-class bounds, and generalized entropy (Abellán et al., 2006) for uncertainty quantification over credal sets. It departs from prior credal approaches in deep learning—such as imprecise BNNs (Caprio et al., 2024) that model weights as credal sets, or credal-set interval neural networks (Wang et al., 2025)—by operating purely as a post-hoc, training-free wrapper around the finite set of single distributions already produced by BNNs (via BMA) or deep ensembles. The key conceptual move is replacing the standard arithmetic mean of N sampled distributions with a credal set defined by per-class min/max bounds, thereby explicitly encoding the epistemic uncertainty arising from having only N samples rather than the full posterior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The credal wrapper offers a practical, model-agnostic upgrade path for any existing BNN or DE pipeline: no retraining, no architectural changes, just a post-processing step that consistently improves OOD detection and calibration on corrupted data across five architectures and multiple dataset pairs. The main limitation is the per-sample convex optimization cost for generalized entropy, which the PIA algorithm mitigates but does not eliminate, making the method less attractive under strict latency or memory constraints. Open questions include whether the approach extends to regression or generative tasks, how it behaves under adversarial perturbations, and whether the intersection probability's calibration advantage on corrupted data generalizes to safety-critical deployment settings such as medical imaging, which the authors identify as a primary future direction.
