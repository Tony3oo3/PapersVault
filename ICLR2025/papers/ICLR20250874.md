## Problem

Diffusion models consistently produce text hallucinations—rendering individual symbols correctly but assembling them in nonsensical ways—yet the underlying mechanism remains unexplained. This paper asks why score-matching denoising networks fail to capture global grammatical structure even when architectures like MLPs and Transformers have the capacity to model long-range dependencies, and identifies a previously unrecognized implicit training bias as the root cause.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250874/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The LDR probe takes a trained denoising network s_θ, a fixed denoising timestep t, and a region of interest R (one symbol's pixel area), and computes the ratio of the Frobenius norm of the Jacobian restricted to R over the full Jacobian norm, yielding a scalar in [0,1] where higher values indicate stronger local dependence. For generation, the model starts from Gaussian noise x_T and iteratively denoises to produce images containing text symbols across synthetic distributions (parity parenthesis, Quarter-MNIST digits, Dyck sequences) and real distributions (1,000 English words, 1,000 Chinese characters). LDR is tracked at multiple timesteps (√ᾱ_t ≈ 0.1–0.9) and training checkpoints to characterize how local bias evolves over the training trajectory.

## Method

The paper introduces the Local Dependency Ratio (LDR) as a quantitative probe for local generation bias, defined as the expected ratio of the squared gradient norm within a symbol's local region to the total gradient norm across the full input (Eq. 2). The core theoretical result proves that for distributions with pairwise-independent marginals (e.g., parity constraints on a hypercube), the set of networks where each output dimension depends only on its corresponding input coordinate (LDR = 1) forms an invariant set under gradient flow, and that small initialization biases a two-layer ReLU network toward this set because neurons aligned with coordinate axes grow exponentially faster than others. Empirically, the authors validate this across attention-augmented UNet and DiT architectures on synthetic text distributions, showing that high LDR (>0.75) persists during hallucination phases and drops only when the model overfits to memorize training data. They further confirm the bias on real-world models (FLUX1, SD3.5) using a zeroth-order Jacobian approximation and on real text distributions where the strict independence condition does not hold, demonstrating the universality of the phenomenon.

## Results

On the Quarter-MNIST task, UNet (with attention) maintains LDR > 0.75 throughout training and achieves only 20.6% accuracy in generating valid digit tuples, while DiT reduces LDR at 30k–50k iterations and reaches ~90% accuracy. For the Parity Parenthesis task (L=8), the model overfits to the training set but still produces 25% hallucinated samples; at L=16, correct generation occurs only at the 50% random-guess rate. On real-world models, FLUX1 exhibits LDR values of 0.7994–0.9253 and SD3.5 exhibits 0.4534–0.9692 across denoising timesteps when generating text content, confirming the local generation bias persists in production-scale systems.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new generative method but instead compares denoising architectures (UNet with attention vs. DiT transformer) and production models (FLUX1, SD3.5) to demonstrate that local generation bias is an intrinsic training-dynamics phenomenon rather than an architectural limitation. UNet with attention, despite having global receptive field via attention modules, maintains LDR > 0.75 throughout 180k training iterations on Quarter-MNIST and achieves only 20.6% rule-satisfaction accuracy, far below DiT's ~90%. DiT initially exhibits the same high-LDR hallucination phase but its stronger approximation power allows LDR to decrease at 30k–50k iterations, synchronizing with rapid accuracy gains. Both FLUX1 and SD3.5 show persistently high LDR (≥0.79 for most timesteps) when generating text, corroborating that the bias is present in state-of-the-art models. The theoretical analysis further shows that even a simple two-layer ReLU MLP with global connectivity is driven toward LDR ≈ 1 by gradient flow with small initialization, confirming the bias is not specific to any particular architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the DDPM score-matching framework (Ho et al., 2020) and the small-initialization / rich-regime analysis of two-layer ReLU networks (Lyu et al., 2021; Woodworth et al., 2020) to provide a mechanistic explanation for text hallucination in diffusion models. It departs from prior hallucination analyses—such as mode interpolation (Aithal et al., 2024) and qualitative failure taxonomies (Borji, 2023; Liu et al., 2023)—by identifying a specific implicit training bias (local generation bias) as the root cause, rather than merely documenting the phenomenon. The Local Dependency Ratio (LDR) is a novel diagnostic metric not present in prior work, and the theoretical treatment of score matching on hypercube distributions with parity constraints is a new application of the G-function framework to diffusion training dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that local generation bias is an inherent property of score-matching training dynamics—persisting even in architectures with global receptive fields like DiT and MLPs—reframes hallucination as a training-dynamics problem rather than an architectural one, which has implications for how future models might be regularized or trained to capture global symbol dependencies. However, the paper identifies the problem without proposing a concrete fix, and the theoretical analysis is confined to a narrow case (two-layer ReLU on hypercube with pairwise-independent marginals). Open questions include whether the bias can be explicitly penalized during training, how it interacts with conditioning (e.g., text prompts in Stable Diffusion), and whether the pairwise-independence assumption that drives the invariant-set result is truly the critical factor or merely a sufficient condition.
