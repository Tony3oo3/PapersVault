## Problem

Privacy policies are legally mandated but notoriously difficult for average users to comprehend due to dense legal language and length, undermining informed consent. While traditional NLP models (SVM, HMM, CNN, BERT) have been applied to privacy policy analysis, their performance on key tasks like practice identification, choice detection, summarization, and question answering remains limited. This paper asks whether LLMs can set new benchmarks on these tasks and whether an LLM-based interactive agent can measurably improve user comprehension, reduce cognitive load, and accelerate task completion.

## Input / Output

The agent accepts a single privacy policy URL, retrieves and preprocesses the HTML (filtering ads, segmenting via ASDUS into <h2>/<p> structure), and produces a structured multi-part output: per-segment category labels across 10 predefined data-practice classes, binary opt-out flags for each detected hyperlink, risk-focused extractive summaries at fixed compression ratios (1/16 and 1/64 of original length), and free-form answers to user questions. For benchmarking, individual text segments are fed to the LLM with temperature=0 for deterministic classification, and the PolicyQA task uses a top-10 retrieval strategy to select relevant context before generation.

## Method

The paper has two technical components. First, it benchmarks GPT-4o-mini (alongside GPT-4o, GPT-4-turbo, GPT-3.5) in zero-shot and few-shot prompt settings against LR, SVM, HMM, BERT, and CNN+RF/CF baselines on four tasks over OPP-115 and PolicyQA, showing LLMs achieve state-of-the-art F1 (e.g., 0.67 micro-average vs. 0.58 for LR on practice identification) without any fine-tuning. Second, it builds a guided heuristic agent on the LangChain framework powered by GPT-4o-mini that operates in a multi-stage pipeline: URL retrieval → ASDUS segmentation → parallel policy-practice classification and content summarization → opt-out hyperlink detection via keyword scanning plus LLM context verification → open-ended Q&A via RunnableWithMessageHistory. The agent proactively surfaces critical sections (data sharing, user rights, opt-out mechanisms) without requiring users to formulate specific questions, and a 100-participant user study validates that this design yields significantly higher comprehension (2.6 vs. 1.8/3), lower NASA-TLX cognitive load (3.2 vs. 7.8/10), and faster task completion (5.5 vs. 15.8 min) compared to reading policies directly.

## Results

On Data Practice Identification (OPP-115), GPT-4o-mini achieves a micro-average F1 of 0.67 in zero-shot, outperforming the LR baseline (0.58) by 9 points. On Choice Identification, GPT-4o-mini with few-shot prompting reaches F1 0.91, surpassing LR (0.88) and BERT (0.90). For Policy Question Answering (PolicyQA), GPT-4o-mini with top-10 retrieval achieves Rouge-1 of 0.57, edging out BERT-base (0.56). In the user study (n=100), the agent-assisted group scored 2.6/3 on comprehension vs. 1.8 for the control group, completed tasks in 5.5 min vs. 15.8 min, and reported task difficulty of 3.2/10 vs. 7.8/10.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Wilson et al. (2016) baselines (LR, SVM, HMM) on Data Practice Identification, GPT-4o-mini wins decisively: micro F1 of 0.67 vs. LR's 0.58, SVM's 0.66, and HMM's 0.60, with the largest gains on Third Party Sharing (+0.12 F1 over LR) and International & Specific Audiences (+0.31 F1 over LR). Against Mysore Sathyendra et al. (2017) on Choice Identification, GPT-4o-mini in zero-shot slightly underperforms LR (F1 0.83 vs. 0.88), but with few-shot prompting it surpasses LR (0.91 vs. 0.88) and BERT (0.91 vs. 0.90), while also beating fastText (0.91 vs. 0.82). Against Keymanesh et al. (2020) on Policy Summarization, GPT-4o is roughly on par with the supervised CNN+RF baseline (Rouge-1 0.429 vs. 0.431 at 1/16 compression) but slightly trails CNN+CF (0.429 vs. 0.404 at 1/64), indicating that domain-specific fine-tuning still holds a marginal edge in extractive summarization. Overall, LLMs clearly win on classification and QA tasks but are competitive rather than dominant on summarization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the established privacy policy analysis tasks—data practice identification (Wilson et al., 2016), choice identification (Mysore Sathyendra et al., 2017), policy summarization (Keymanesh et al., 2020), and policy QA (Ahmad et al., 2020)—by replacing their traditional ML baselines (LR, SVM, HMM, CNN, BERT) with zero-shot and few-shot LLM prompting. Rather than proposing a new model architecture, the contribution is an application-level one: wrapping these LLM capabilities in a LangChain-based interactive agent (GPT-4o-mini) that proactively guides users through policy comprehension without requiring them to formulate specific queries. The agent design draws on the expert-system and guided-dialogue paradigm from Ravichander et al. (2019) but extends it from a passive QA interface to a heuristic, multi-stage pipeline that autonomously segments, classifies, summarizes, and highlights opt-out options.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides one of the first controlled user studies (n=100) demonstrating that an LLM-based agent can substantially reduce cognitive load and task time for privacy policy comprehension, offering empirical grounding for the broader claim that LLMs can make legal documents accessible to non-experts. However, several open questions remain: the hallucination risk of autoregressive LLMs in a trust-critical privacy context is acknowledged but not mitigated beyond post-hoc filtering; the study is single-session and short-term, leaving long-term behavioral impact unknown; and the evaluation relies on a single model family (OpenAI GPT) with no open-source alternative, limiting reproducibility and raising data-privacy concerns that partially contradict the paper's own privacy-by-design framing. Future work should explore longitudinal engagement, multi-model robustness, and whether the agent's proactive guidance can be calibrated to avoid over-simplification of legally significant nuances.
