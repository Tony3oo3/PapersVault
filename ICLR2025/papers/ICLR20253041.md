## Problem

Naive Upcycling initializes MoE experts as exact replicas of a pre-trained dense model's FFN layers, which boosts initial performance but prevents expert specialization, causing significantly slower convergence over long training runs. Training from scratch avoids this bottleneck but forfeits the knowledge already encoded in the dense model. The paper addresses the unresolved trade-off between short-term knowledge transfer and long-term convergence efficiency in MoE construction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253041/cropped_page1_idx4.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes the full weight tensor of a pre-trained dense transformer (Llama architecture, 152M–3.7B parameters) and a hyperparameter r ∈ [0,1] controlling re-initialization intensity, then trains on up to 500B tokens from the LLM-jp corpus v3 (English, Japanese, and code). The output is a Mixtral-architecture MoE model with 8 experts per FFN layer and top-2 routing, where all non-FFN layers (embeddings, attention, norms) are inherited directly from the dense model and only the FFN layers are replaced by sparsely-activated MoE layers. The resulting 8×3.7B model has 5.9B active parameters out of 18B total, matching a 13B dense model's performance at roughly 1/4 the training FLOPs.

## Method

Drop-Upcycling modifies the standard Upcycling procedure with a Diversity Re-initialization step applied to every expert FFN in every MoE layer. For each expert, a shared set S of ⌊r·d_f⌋ indices is randomly sampled along the intermediate dimension; the corresponding columns of W_gate and W_up (and rows of W_down) are then re-initialized from a Gaussian distribution whose mean and standard deviation are computed from the original weights at those same indices, while all other positions retain their original values. This shared-index, column-wise (or row-wise for W_down) dropping ensures the re-initialized portion preserves the original weight statistics while introducing stochastic diversity across experts. Theoretically, with top-k routing, approximately (1−r)^k of the intermediate dimensions remain common to all selected experts, decomposing the MoE output into a shared FFN component plus expert-specific retained and diverse components, which the paper shows empirically promotes domain-specialized routing (e.g., Japanese vs. English vs. code) that naive Upcycling fails to achieve. The optimal re-initialization ratio r=0.5 balances knowledge retention against specialization, yielding learning-curve slopes comparable to training from scratch while starting from a substantially better initial loss.

## Results

The headline result is an 8×3.7B MoE model (5.9B active / 18B total params) trained with Drop-Upcycling (r=0.5) that achieves an average score of 44.4 across 12 evaluation tasks, matching a 13B dense model (44.5) while requiring only 1.98×10²² FLOPs versus 7.43×10²² for the dense model (~1/4 the compute). At the 8×1.5B scale, Drop-Upcycling (r=0.5) achieves 40.3 average score, outperforming naive Upcycling (37.4), Branch-Train-Mix (38.6), Random Noise Upcycling (39.1), and training from scratch (34.5) at the same 500B-token budget. At the smaller 8×152M scale, Drop-Upcycling scores 19.7 versus 17.9 for naive Upcycling and 16.6 for from-scratch training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Drop-Upcycling (r=0.5) consistently outperforms all four baselines across both model scales tested. Against naive Upcycling, DU gains +2.9 points (40.3 vs 37.4) at 8×1.5B and +1.8 points (19.7 vs 17.9) at 8×152M, primarily because naive Upcycling suffers from expert collapse and slow convergence. Against Branch-Train-Mix, DU gains +1.7 points at 8×1.5B (40.3 vs 38.6) despite BTX requiring an additional 300B tokens of expert pre-training before MoE training begins. Against Random Noise Upcycling, DU leads by +1.2 points at 8×1.5B (40.3 vs 39.1), showing that structured re-initialization along the intermediate dimension is more effective than adding isotropic Gaussian noise. Against training from scratch, DU gains +5.8 points at 8×1.5B (40.3 vs 34.5) and +3.1 points at 8×152M (19.7 vs 16.6), demonstrating that knowledge transfer from the dense seed model provides a substantial head start. At the largest scale, the DU-initialized 5.9B-active MoE matches the 13B dense model (44.4 vs 44.5) using only ~27% of the training FLOPs, and surpasses a 3.7B dense model trained on 2.1T tokens (44.4 vs 41.1).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Upcycling framework (Komatsuzaki et al., 2023), which initializes MoE experts as replicas of a pre-trained dense model's FFN layers, by introducing a Diversity Re-initialization step that partially re-initializes expert weights along the intermediate dimension using shared random indices. It departs from both naive Upcycling (full copy, slow convergence) and Branch-Train-Mix (Sukhbaatar et al., 2024, domain-specific expert pre-training) by showing that a simple statistical re-initialization of a fraction r of intermediate-dimension weights achieves the best balance between knowledge retention and expert diversification. The approach is conceptually distinct from Random Noise Upcycling (Muennighoff et al., 2024), which adds Gaussian noise to a random subset of weights, because Drop-Upcycling preserves the original weight statistics and uses structured column/row-wise dropping rather than element-wise perturbation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: a 5.9B active-parameter MoE model matches a 13B dense model's performance at roughly 1/4 the training FLOPs, and the method is simple enough to be a drop-in replacement for any existing Upcycling pipeline. The finding that r=0.5 is robustly optimal across model scales (152M to 3.7B base) provides a clear practical guideline, while the expert routing analysis confirms that domain specialization (Japanese, English, code) emerges naturally without explicit domain-specific training. Open questions include whether the method extends cleanly to fine-grained and shared expert architectures (discussed but not empirically validated in the appendix), whether the advantage persists under different learning rate schedules, and whether the optimal r value shifts with model scale or expert count.
