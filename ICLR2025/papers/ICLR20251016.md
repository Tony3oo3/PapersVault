## Problem

Existing strategic classification models assume agents interact independently with the classifier, ignoring that one agent's manipulation can impose costs or risks on others (e.g., many students falsely claiming top class rank triggers institutional audits). This paper introduces inter-agent externalities into the strategic classification framework, asking how to model the resulting multi-agent game, characterize its equilibrium, and learn classifiers that are robust to the equilibrium manipulation behavior of a random number of interacting agents.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251016/cropped_page8_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes k agents' true feature vectors X ∈ [0,1]^{k×d} and labels y ∈ {-1,+1}^k, where k is drawn from a distribution K over [1, k_max], along with classifier weights ω ∈ R^d. It produces the unique pure Nash Equilibrium manipulation strategies X^q (computed as the argmax of a strictly concave potential function over [0,1]^{k×d}), per-agent classification scores f_ω(x_i^q) = ⟨x_i^q, ω⟩, and optimal classifier weights ω* minimizing expected loss at equilibrium. The training set S consists of n instances, each containing a random number of agents sampled i.i.d. from D, and the loss is evaluated at equilibrium reports rather than true features.

## Method

The interaction is modeled as a Stackelberg game where the learner commits to a linear classifier and agents simultaneously best-respond, with the solution concept being the Stackelberg-Nash Equilibrium. Under structural assumptions (ℓ2-norm-squared manipulation cost, pairwise symmetric externalities, and convex total externality), the agent game is shown to be a potential game whose potential function is strictly concave, guaranteeing a unique PNE computable via a single convex optimization. PAC learning guarantees follow from proving the NE mapping is Lipschitz-continuous in ω (via a general result on parametrized convex optimization), enabling a covering-number argument over the norm-constrained linear function class. Gradients for empirical risk minimization are computed by implicit differentiation through the KKT conditions using the implicit function theorem, yielding an explicit gradient expression (Equation 2) that enables standard gradient-based training. Two concrete externality models—proportional (detection risk scaling with others' manipulation) and congestion (resource competition via exponential similarity)—are shown to satisfy the required convexity conditions.

## Results

The paper is primarily theoretical, with its main contributions being: (1) a unique pure Nash Equilibrium under pairwise symmetric externalities and ℓ₂-norm cost (Theorem 2), (2) PAC learning guarantees for the Stackelberg-Nash classifier (Theorem 3, with sample complexity depending on the Lipschitz constant η of the NE function), and (3) explicit gradient computation via the implicit function theorem (Theorem 4). The experimental validation (Section 6) uses a 2-class synthetic dataset from scikit-learn with cvxpylayers for PNE computation, showing that strategic training and validation losses decrease in lockstep across 15 random datasets, confirming generalization. No specific numerical accuracy or loss values are reported in the text; results are presented as loss curves (Figure 1) across varying k, cost intensity α, and externality intensity β.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its strategic training approach against two baselines. First, a non-strategic/truthful classifier (trained assuming agents report true features) is evaluated at test time where agents actually manipulate to PNE; this baseline performs poorly for nearly all configurations of k, α, and β, though its performance relatively improves as cost/externality intensity increases (since higher manipulation costs deter agents from deviating) and as k grows (since individual externality impact is averaged over more agents, stabilizing equilibrium strategies). Second, in Appendix E, the paper compares against a cost-only baseline mimicking the classic Hardt et al. (2016) single-agent strategic classification setup without externalities; the full model with externalities outperforms this baseline, confirming that ignoring inter-agent externalities leads to suboptimal classifier design. The comparisons are qualitative (loss curves rather than tabulated metrics), but consistently show the strategic-with-externality method achieving lower validation loss than both baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the strategic classification paradigm initiated by Hardt et al. (2016), which models a single agent best-responding to a deployed classifier in a Stackelberg game. The key departure is the introduction of inter-agent externalities, transforming the agent interaction from independent best responses into a simultaneous game whose solution is a pure Nash Equilibrium. The equilibrium analysis draws directly on the potential game framework of Monderer & Shapley (1996), showing that pairwise symmetric externalities yield a potential function whose strict concavity guarantees a unique, efficiently computable PNE. The learning theory builds on standard PAC and covering-number arguments, adapted to handle the Lipschitz continuity of the equilibrium mapping and a random number of participants.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work establishes the first theoretical foundations for multi-agent strategic classification with externalities, showing that PAC learning and gradient-based optimization remain feasible under structural assumptions on cost and externality. Key limitations include the restriction to linear classifiers, pairwise symmetric externalities, and the need for the potential function to be strictly concave; the paper itself identifies non-concave potential functions, unknown externality models, and welfare/price-of-anarchy analysis as important open directions. The experimental validation is limited to small synthetic 2-class datasets, so practical applicability to real high-stakes settings (admissions, lending) remains to be demonstrated.
