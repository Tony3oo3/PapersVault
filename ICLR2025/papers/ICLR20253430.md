## Problem

Transformer-based reasoning models must choose between fast mode (outputting only a solution) and slow mode (generating a full reasoning trace), but existing approaches require either separate models per mode, explicit meta-controllers, or expensive iterative fine-tuning to switch between them. The paper asks whether a single model can natively support both modes and automatically select the appropriate one, analogous to human System 1 and System 2 thinking.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253430/cropped_page15_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

For navigation tasks, the input is a tokenized grid description (wall coordinates, start, goal) followed by two control tokens: 'bos plan' triggers fast mode (direct plan output), 'bos create' triggers slow mode (A* search trace then plan), and omitting the control token enables auto mode where the model self-selects. The slow-mode output is a structured sequence of create/close clauses with node coordinates and cost values, terminated by the final path as coordinate pairs. For math, the input is a question with a standard prompt, and the model outputs either a step-by-step derivation or a direct answer depending on the mode.

## Method

The core mechanism is structured trace dropping applied stochastically at training time. The A* search trace is decomposed into create clauses, close clauses, and cost tokens; four hierarchical dropping levels progressively remove close clauses (L1), cost tokens (L2), 30% of create clauses (L3), or the entire trace (L4). For each training example in a batch, a dropping level is sampled from a categorical distribution Cat(p0,…,p4), so the same example encounters different trace lengths across epochs. This is applied dynamically during training rather than as preprocessing, and the model uses the same encoder-decoder architecture as Searchformer (T5 encoder with RoPE + GPT-style decoder, 15M/46M parameters). At inference, mode selection requires only appending a single control token, and in auto mode the model learns to allocate slow thinking to harder problems (higher wall density, larger mazes). For LLM fine-tuning on math, the analogous strategy randomly drops individual intermediate reasoning steps with probability p.

## Results

In slow mode on the 30×30 maze, Dualformer achieves 97.6% 1-Optimal-64 (vs. 93.3% for the Complete-Trace/Searchformer baseline) while generating 45.5% shorter reasoning traces (854 vs. 1538 tokens). In fast mode, Dualformer reaches 80.0% 1-Optimal-64 on the 30×30 maze, a 2.8× improvement over the Solution-Only baseline (30.0%). In auto mode, Dualformer attains 96.6% 1-Optimal-64 with 59.9% fewer reasoning steps than Searchformer. On Sokoban, Dualformer (slow) achieves 94.5% 1-Optimal-64 with a trace length of 1482 tokens versus 3600 for the baseline. For math reasoning, fine-tuning Mistral-7B with trace dropping (p=0.1) improves Greedy@1 from 16.9% to 18.6% (slow mode) and from 9.6% to 11.3% (fast mode).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Complete-Trace model (base Searchformer, Lehnert et al. 2024), Dualformer wins on every correctness, optimality, SWC, and diversity metric across all five navigation tasks in both slow and auto modes, while simultaneously producing substantially shorter reasoning traces (e.g., 854 vs. 1538 tokens on 30×30 maze in slow mode; 617 vs. 1538 in auto mode). Against the Solution-Only baseline in fast mode, Dualformer's advantage is dramatic: on the 30×30 maze, 1-Optimal-64 jumps from 30.0% to 80.0% (2.8×), and diversity increases from 1.86 to 18.23 unique correct paths. Compared to the bootstrapped Searchformer (up to 3 steps of search dynamics bootstrapping on Sokoban), Dualformer matches or slightly exceeds the 1-Optimal-64 rate (94.5% vs. 94.5%) while using fewer than 45.1% of the reasoning steps (1482 vs. 3283 tokens), and without the expensive multi-stage rollout-and-fine-tune pipeline. In the math reasoning setting, the trace-dropping fine-tuning consistently improves both Greedy@1 and Pass@20 over the direct fine-tuning baseline on Aug-MATH for both Mistral-7B and Llama-3-8B, with the largest gains at p=0.1–0.2 (e.g., +1.7% absolute Greedy@1 for Mistral-7B in slow mode).

## Relation to Prior Work

Dualformer builds directly on Searchformer (Lehnert et al., 2024), reusing its encoder-decoder architecture, A*-based tokenization, and maze/Sokoban datasets, but departs by replacing complete-trace training with a structured trace-dropping data recipe. This contrasts with prior dual-system approaches that either distill System 2 into System 1 (Yu et al., 2024), use an explicit meta-controller over two separate models (Saha et al., 2024), or rely on expensive iterative bootstrapping (Searchformer). The key conceptual move is treating trace dropping as an analog of dropout applied to the reasoning trace structure, enabling a single model to learn both fast and slow modes without architectural modifications or external controllers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a simple randomized data recipe—rather than architectural changes or multi-model orchestration—yields a single controllable model with adaptive fast/slow/auto reasoning modes is practically attractive for reducing inference cost while preserving accuracy. However, the main navigation results use small models (15M–46M parameters) on toy grid-world domains, and the math reasoning gains on 7B–8B LLMs are modest (1–2% absolute), leaving open whether the technique scales to larger models or more complex reasoning tasks. The paper itself flags curriculum learning and hierarchical planning as directions for extending Dualformer to harder problems, and the OOD generalization results (failure at low wall density) suggest the learned representations remain somewhat brittle.
