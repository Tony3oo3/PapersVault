## Problem

Existing contrastive decoding methods for LVLM hallucination mitigation primarily target language-prior-induced errors by contrasting predictions from original versus distorted visual inputs, but they struggle to generalize to hallucinations arising from visual recognition deficiencies (e.g., counting errors, fine-grained attribute confusion). The paper asks whether text-to-image generative models can serve as a self-feedback mechanism to address this broader class of hallucinations by visualizing the model's own response and comparing it against the original image.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250201/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a textual query as input and produces a hallucination-mitigated text response. Unusually, the process is two-stage: the LVLM first generates an initial response, which is then fed into a pre-trained text-to-image diffusion model (e.g., Stable Diffusion v1.5) to synthesize a reference image. During the second decoding pass, both the original image and the generated reference image are simultaneously fed to the LVLM as dual visual inputs, with the generated image acting as an auxiliary visual reference rather than a replacement.

## Method

DeGF is a training-free decoding algorithm that recursively refines LVLM responses using generative feedback. At each token step, it computes the Jensen-Shannon divergence between next-token distributions conditioned on the original image v and the generated reference image v′. A distance threshold γ (default 0.1) determines the decoding strategy: when d_t(v,v′) < γ (predictions aligned), it applies complementary decoding by additively combining logits (Softmax[f_θ(y_t|v) + α₁·f_θ(y_t|v′)]); when d_t ≥ γ (predictions divergent, suggesting hallucination), it applies contrastive decoding by subtracting the generated-image logits (Softmax[(1+α₂)·f_θ(y_t|v) − α₂·f_θ(y_t|v′)]). This adaptive switching between confirmation and correction at the token level, guided by the generative model's visual interpretation of the response, is the key mechanism that distinguishes DeGF from prior single-strategy contrastive or complementary decoding approaches.

## Results

On the POPE benchmark (MS-COCO, Random setting), DeGF achieves 89.03% accuracy, 91.20% precision, and 88.74% F1 with LLaVA-1.5, outperforming the second-best method (RITUAL) by up to 5.24% in accuracy, 6.33% in precision, and 2.79% in F1 across all settings. On CHAIR (64-token limit), DeGF reduces CHAIR_S from 26.2 to 18.4 (LLaVA-1.5) and from 31.2 to 24.0 (InstructBLIP), while improving recall. On MME-Hallucination, DeGF achieves a total score of 644.44 (LLaVA-1.5) and 484.44 (InstructBLIP), surpassing the best baseline by +18.19 and +21.11 respectively, and also improves MMBench accuracy to 65.5 (vs. 64.6 for VCD).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DeGF consistently outperforms all seven baselines across the six primary benchmarks. Against the strongest contrastive decoding baseline VCD (Leng et al., 2024), DeGF gains +2.03% accuracy on POPE (MS-COCO Random, LLaVA-1.5) and reduces CHAIR_S by 6.0 points (18.4 vs. 24.4). Compared to M3ID (Favero et al., 2024), which contrasts with a no-image input, DeGF achieves higher precision (91.20 vs. 87.38 on POPE) and lower CHAIR_S (18.4 vs. 21.4). Against RITUAL (Woo et al., 2024), which uses random image transformations, DeGF leads on POPE accuracy (89.03 vs. 88.87) and CHAIR_S (18.4 vs. 22.4), though RITUAL slightly edges out on MME-Hallucination total score for LLaVA-1.5 (616.25 vs. 644.44 — actually DeGF wins here). On MME-Hallucination, DeGF surpasses OPERA (Huang et al., 2024) by +46.11 points and DoLa (Chuang et al., 2024) by +122.22 points on LLaVA-1.5. On CHAIR, DeGF outperforms both Woodpecker (Yin et al., 2023) and HALC (Chen et al., 2024) by 6.5 and 3.3 points on CHAIR_S respectively, while also maintaining higher recall, indicating it reduces hallucinations without sacrificing response informativeness.

## Relation to Prior Work

DeGF extends the training-free contrastive/complementary decoding paradigm established by VCD (Leng et al., 2024), M3ID (Favero et al., 2024), and RITUAL (Woo et al., 2024), which all intervene at the decoding stage to suppress hallucinations by contrasting or combining logits from perturbed inputs. The key departure is the source of the auxiliary visual reference: rather than using a distorted image, a text-only input, or a randomly transformed image, DeGF generates a new image from the LVLM's own initial response via a pre-trained text-to-image diffusion model, effectively treating image-to-text generation and text-to-image synthesis as inverse processes. The self-correcting mechanism—switching between complementary and contrastive decoding based on a per-token Jensen-Shannon divergence threshold—synthesizes the additive logit structure from RITUAL and the subtractive structure from VCD into a single adaptive framework, while also incorporating the adaptive plausibility constraint from Li et al. (2023c).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a recognized gap: existing contrastive decoding methods primarily target language-prior hallucinations but struggle with visual-deficiency errors (e.g., counting mistakes in complex scenes), and DeGF's generative feedback provides a complementary signal that catches these. The training-free, plug-and-play nature makes it broadly applicable across LVLMs without retraining. However, the 4× latency increase and 1.21× GPU memory overhead are substantial practical barriers for real-time deployment, and the authors acknowledge this limitation. Open questions include whether faster diffusion models (or distilled variants) will close the efficiency gap, whether the approach extends to video or multi-image inputs, and whether the JS-divergence-based switching threshold generalizes to LVLMs with fundamentally different architectures or tokenization schemes.
