## Problem

Standard Euclidean embedding spaces used in genomic sequence modeling fail to capture the hierarchical, tree-structured relationships inherent in DNA data—arising from phylogenetic ancestry, orthology/paralogy, and nested regulatory grammars. This paper asks whether hyperbolic geometry, which embeds exponentially growing tree structures with low distortion, can serve as a more natural inductive bias for genome representation learning without requiring explicit phylogenetic metadata.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252643/cropped_page4_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Input DNA sequences are one-hot encoded at the nucleotide level (4×L) and projected channelwise onto a Lorentz hyperboloid before entering the convolutional blocks. Sequences span 70–2,142 bp depending on the benchmark (TEB, GUE, GB). The model outputs class labels via Lorentz multinomial logistic regression, which defines hyperbolic decision hyperplanes in the embedding space to separate genomic element types (transposons, epigenetic marks, TF binding sites, promoters, etc.).

## Method

The paper builds a fully hyperbolic CNN (HCNN) in the Lorentz model, replacing each Euclidean component with its hyperbolic analogue: Lorentz convolutional layers (hyperbolic concatenation followed by a Lorentz fully-connected transform), Lorentz batch normalization (re-centering via Fréchet mean and re-scaling via Fréchet variance with parallel transport), and Lorentz MLR for classification. Two variants are evaluated—HCNN-S (single learnable curvature K shared across all layers) and HCNN-M (distinct curvatures per convolutional block, with points mapped between manifolds via log/exp at the origin). The architecture consists of three hyperbolic convolutional blocks, a flattening step, and Lorentz MLR, trained end-to-end with RiemannianAdam. The paper further introduces a δ-hyperbolicity analysis (examining full distributions of Gromov products rather than single-point estimates) to empirically characterize the tree-likeness of learned embeddings, and contributes the Transposable Elements Benchmark (TEB), a novel multi-species dataset for classifying seven TE subclasses across plant and human genomes.

## Results

Across 42 real-world genomics datasets spanning TEB, GUE, and Genomic Benchmarks, hyperbolic HCNNs outperform their Euclidean CNN counterparts on 37 tasks (MCC), with 29 showing statistically significant improvement (p<0.05, Wilcoxon rank-sum). The largest gains appear in epigenetic mark prediction (e.g., H3K4me3: 49.51 vs 21.77 MCC for HCNN-S vs CNN), transcription factor binding sites (e.g., Mouse TF 0: 46.09 vs 22.51), and transposable element identification (e.g., LTR Copia: 64.58 vs 54.73). HCNNs use only ~4.6M parameters yet surpass state-of-the-art DNA language models (7.7M–2.5B parameters) on 7 of 28 GUE datasets, and scaled HCNNs (43M params) achieve SOTA on 12 GUE datasets, outperforming DNABERT-2 (11) and NT-2500M-multi (5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the five DNA language model families (Caduceus-Ph, HyenaDNA, DNABERT 5/6-mer, Nucleotide Transformer variants, and DNABERT-2), HCNNs (4.6M params, no pretraining) achieve the best score on 7 of 28 GUE datasets: H3K4me2 (43.88 vs 36.49 best LM), H3K4me3 (50.58 vs 41.20), H4ac (52.94 vs 50.43), promoter tata (82.70 vs 79.43), Human TF 2 (73.80 vs 66.52), core promoter tata (82.07 vs 76.18), and Mouse TF 3 (73.51 vs 73.49, marginal). The performance gap is especially striking given that competing LMs contain 1.7× to 543× more trainable parameters and were pretrained on the full human genome or 1000 Genomes sequences. Scaled HCNNs (43M params) extend this advantage, reaching SOTA on 12 GUE datasets versus 11 for DNABERT-2 (117M) and 5 for NT-2500M-multi (2.5B). However, HCNNs are clearly disadvantaged on the Covid variant classification task (36.71 MCC vs 66.43 for CNN and 73.04 for NT-2500M-multi), which resembles intra-tree differentiation where evolutionary signal is less dominant. On promoter detection tasks overall, hyperbolic embeddings provide no consistent benefit over Euclidean CNNs, suggesting that complex combinatorial regulatory grammar is harder for HCNNs to capture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the fully hyperbolic CNN architecture of Bdeir et al. (2024, ICLR) and the Lorentz fully-connected layer of Chen et al. (2022, ACL) to DNA sequence classification, replacing the Euclidean geometry of standard genomic CNNs with the Lorentz model of hyperbolic space. It departs from prior hyperbolic genomics work (Corso et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2022) that required explicit phylogenetic trees or pairwise edit distances as input, instead learning hierarchical structure implicitly from raw sequences. The paper also positions itself against large pretrained DNA language models (DNABERT-2, Nucleotide Transformer, HyenaDNA, Caduceus), arguing that the hyperbolic inductive bias can substitute for scale and pretraining on tasks with strong evolutionary signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic evidence that genomic sequence embeddings possess innate hyperbolicity (lower δ than Euclidean baselines in 25/43 datasets), offering a geometric justification for why hyperbolic spaces may be the right inductive bias for evolutionary data. The Transposable Elements Benchmark fills a genuine gap in the genomics deep learning literature for an underexplored but evolutionarily central component of the genome. Key limitations include consistent underperformance on promoter detection and multi-variant classification (Scenario A-like tasks), suggesting the hyperbolic advantage is task-specific rather than universal. Open questions include formalizing the statistical behavior of δ-distributions under emergent hyperbolicity, understanding why hyperbolicity may become less critical at very large embedding dimensions (as seen with NT-500M), and whether the framework scales effectively to long-range genomic modeling.
