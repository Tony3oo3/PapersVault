## Problem

Prior research has shown that LLMs exhibit political leanings and can emulate party positions, but the extent and conditions under which LLMs deviate from empirical positions—specifically through exaggeration or stereotyping—remain underexplored. This paper addresses that gap by drawing on cognitive science findings about representativeness heuristics to formalize, quantify, and identify the conditions that cause LLMs to inflate or deflate partisan positions beyond what human respondents do.

## Input / Output

The method takes political survey questions (10 topics from ANES on 7-point Likert scales, 5 moral foundations from MFQ on 6-point scales) paired with a party affiliation label (Republican or Democrat) as prompts to LLMs, which produce a single Likert-scale numeric response. From 20 repeated responses per model-topic-party combination, the paper computes predicted means and derives three diagnostic parameters: γ (kernel-of-truth inflation coefficient), κ (representativeness exaggeration ratio measuring the gap between conditional probability and representativeness of the exemplar attribute), and ε (heuristic weight quantifying how much right-tail representativeness drives the predicted mean). A dual-question framework is used: Empirical questions capture self-reported positions from survey respondents, while Prediction questions ask LLMs and human participants to estimate a party's position.

## Method

The core contribution is a formalization of representativeness heuristics (from Kahneman & Tversky and Bordalo et al.) into a quantitative evaluation framework for LLM political stereotyping. Representativeness is defined as a likelihood ratio P(a|X⁺)/P(a|X⁻) across Likert-scale attributes, and the exemplar is the attribute maximizing this ratio. The kernel-of-truth hypothesis is tested via the equation E^B(a|X⁺) = (1+γ)·E(a|X⁺) − γ·E(a|X⁻), where γ>0 indicates inflation of the target group's mean while deflating the contrast group's. Representative heuristics are measured through ε parameters that capture how much the predicted mean deviates from the empirical mean in proportion to right-tail representativeness (top-2 attributes). Three prompt-based mitigation strategies—Awareness (explicitly describing the heuristic), Feedback (showing the model its own response and asking for revision), and Reasoning (requesting justification)—are tested, drawing on cognitive science evidence that awareness of heuristics enables human self-correction. Experiments span six LLMs (GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Gemini, Llama2-70B, Llama3-8B, Qwen2.5-72B) across both datasets.

## Results

On the ANES dataset, LLMs exhibit kernel-of-truth exaggeration (γ) ranging from 0.59 (Llama3-8b) to 1.66 (GPT-3.5, Gemini), substantially exceeding the Human_Pred baseline of 0.44, confirming that LLMs inflate partisan positions more than human respondents do. On MFQ, γ values range from −0.33 (Llama3-8b) to 0.90 (Qwen2.5-72b), with most models showing positive exaggeration. Representative heuristic weights (ε) are predominantly positive across models and datasets (e.g., Gemini D-ANES ε=4.00, GPT-4 D-ANES ε=3.37), indicating systematic over-weighting of representative attributes. Prompt-based mitigation (Awareness, Reasoning, Feedback) reduces the stereotyping risk metric κ relative to baseline, with REASONING most effective on ANES and FEEDBACK most effective on MFQ.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All six LLMs are compared against a Human_Pred reference (γ=0.44 on ANES) to quantify how much more (or less) they exaggerate partisan positions than humans. GPT-3.5 and Gemini show the strongest exaggeration (γ=1.66 on ANES, roughly 3.8× the human level), while Llama3-8b is closest to human behavior (γ=0.59 on ANES) and is the only model with a negative γ on MFQ (−0.33), suggesting it slightly deflates rather than inflates. GPT-4 and Qwen2.5-72b fall in the middle (γ≈0.76–0.89 on ANES). On the representative heuristic metric (ε), Gemini and GPT-4 show the largest positive values for Democratic positions on ANES (ε=4.00 and 3.37 respectively), indicating the strongest over-weighting of representative attributes, while Llama2-70b is the only model with a negative ε for Republican ANES positions (−0.84). In the mitigation experiments (Table 3), baseline κ values are consistently the highest across all models; for example, GPT-4's baseline κ on ANES is 114.72, reduced to 25.37 with REASONING prompting, while Llama3-8b and Qwen2.5-72b already exhibit low baseline κ (≈10–11), suggesting smaller models or those with different training may be less susceptible to representativeness-driven stereotyping.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on political bias in LLMs (Feng et al. 2023; Santurkar et al. 2023; Argyle et al. 2023) by importing the kernel-of-truth and representativeness heuristic formalisms from Bordalo et al. (2016) and Kahneman & Tversky (1972, 1973) into the LLM evaluation setting. Prior work identified that LLMs exhibit political leanings and can mimic partisan positions, but did not formalize the mechanism of deviation from empirical positions or quantify the degree of exaggeration. The paper also builds on prior LLM stereotyping work (Jeoung et al. 2023b; Cao et al. 2022) by replacing social-psychology-based definitions with a cognitive-science-grounded framework, and extends prompt-based mitigation research (Wei et al. 2022; Ganguli et al. 2023) to the specific target of representativeness heuristics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a formal, theoretically grounded framework (γ, κ, ε parameters) for quantifying political stereotyping in LLMs that can be extended beyond politics to other domains of value alignment. The finding that LLMs systematically over-polarize relative to human respondents, and that RLHF alignment may inadvertently amplify rather than reduce this stereotyping, raises important questions about whether current alignment objectives are counterproductive for bias reduction. Key limitations include the US-only, two-party scope, reliance on survey data as a proxy for human values, and the assumption that party affiliation maps cleanly to ideological position. Open questions include how alignment training data and reward objectives shape representativeness heuristics, and whether the observed effects persist across non-US political systems.
