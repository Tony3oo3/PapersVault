## Problem

While empirical studies show that long-tailed data cause head classes to dominate the feature space in contrastive learning, no theoretical framework explains why or to what extent this skew occurs. Furthermore, the paper identifies that simply rearranging empirical class centers to a symmetric configuration is insufficient due to distribution shifts between limited training samples and the true data distribution, which can cause new overlaps between head and tail classes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images from long-tailed datasets (32×32 for CIFAR-LT, 224×224 for ImageNet-LT and iNaturalist 2018) and produces two outputs: class predictions via a prediction head trained with cross-entropy loss plus logit compensation, and normalized feature vectors on a unit hypersphere via a projection head trained with supervised contrastive loss. Uniquely, the method also generates synthetic feature vectors on the hypersphere for tail classes, constrained within estimated hyperspherical cap confidence supports, which are then jointly optimized with real features.

## Method

FeatRecon reconstructs the feature space for long-tailed data by iteratively generating synthetic features to balance class sample sizes while ensuring linear separability. For each class, it estimates a confidence support as a hyperspherical cap (center + central angle via quantile), regularizes tail-class estimates using statistics from the most similar head classes, and samples synthetic features within these caps using a truncated angular distribution. A per-class temperature schedule in the SC loss emphasizes inter-class separation for head classes while rebalancing gradient scales. The training loop alternates between generating synthetic features, optimizing the combined cross-entropy and SC loss, and re-estimating confidence supports, guaranteeing that at the optimal configuration all class centers form a symmetric arrangement with non-overlapping supports.

## Results

On CIFAR-10-LT with imbalance factor 100, FeatRecon achieves 85.2% top-1 accuracy, outperforming the strongest baseline BCL (84.3%) and DWR-OTmix (83.1%). On CIFAR-100-LT (ρ=100), it reaches 52.5% vs. BCL's 51.8%. On ImageNet-LT, FeatRecon attains 56.8% overall accuracy (67.9% many-shot, 54.7% medium-shot, 37.8% few-shot with ResNeXt-50), surpassing BCL (56.0%) and IWB (55.2%). On iNaturalist 2018, it achieves 72.9% overall accuracy, the best among all compared methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FeatRecon consistently outperforms all 19 baselines across every dataset and imbalance setting. The closest competitor is BCL (Zhu et al., 2022), which FeatRecon beats by 0.9% on CIFAR-10-LT (ρ=100), 0.4% on CIFAR-100-LT (ρ=100), 0.8% on ImageNet-LT, and 1.1% on iNaturalist 2018. Among classifier-only methods, DWR-OTmix (Gao et al., 2024) and IWB (Dang et al., 2024) are the strongest, but FeatRecon surpasses them by 2.1% and 1.0% respectively on ImageNet-LT. On CIFAR-100-LT (ρ=100), the gap widens: FeatRecon leads BCL by 0.7% and DWR-OTmix by 4.5%, with the advantage growing as imbalance increases (e.g., 2.4% over BCL at ρ=10). The few-shot class accuracy on ImageNet-LT (ResNeXt-50) shows the largest relative gains: 37.8% for FeatRecon vs. 36.5% for BCL and 40.2% for IWB, confirming the method's particular strength on tail classes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical analysis of supervised contrastive learning (Khosla et al., 2020; Graf et al., 2021) from the balanced-data regime to imbalanced data, deriving the first closed-form characterization of how the imbalance factor ρ controls the geometry of optimal class-center configurations. It departs from prior long-tailed contrastive methods (BCL, TSC, KCL) that empirically reposition skewed centers by instead generating synthetic features constrained within hyperspherical-cap confidence supports, regularized by head-class statistics. The training framework follows Zhu et al. (2022), and the synthetic feature generation on the hypersphere adapts the sampling procedure from Su (2021).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The critical collapse threshold R(K, a₂) provides a precise, interpretable boundary beyond which tail-class representations become fundamentally inseparable — a result that was previously only observed empirically. The paper also identifies a subtle failure mode: naively correcting empirical centers can worsen head-tail separation due to the distribution gap between limited training samples and the true data distribution. However, the theory is currently limited to the one-vs-all setting (one head class vs. K−1 equal tail classes), and the practical gains over strong baselines like BCL are modest (0.5–1.5%), leaving open whether the theoretical insights translate to more complex multi-head imbalanced scenarios.
