## Problem

Fine-tuning LLMs on diverse multi-source instruction data produces conflicting gradient directions that hinder optimization and degrade generalization. Existing data-selection methods (e.g., LESS) are target-driven, requiring task-specific validation features that are unavailable when adapting to new or emerging tasks. The paper asks whether one can resolve gradient conflicts in a fully task-agnostic manner, using only the training data itself, without any additional validation set.

## Input / Output

During training, the method consumes instruction-response pairs but computes gradient features exclusively from the instruction (user-input) tokens, deliberately excluding response tokens to remain agnostic to the test distribution. At inference, the input is a text instruction whose gradient is computed once to determine expert weights; the output is the next-token prediction obtained by taking the argmax over a weighted sum of pre-activation logits from C+1 adapters (C cluster-specific LoRA experts plus the base adapter). Weights do not sum to one since the combination operates on logits rather than probabilities.

## Method

ELREA operates in three stages: (1) fine-tune a base LoRA adapter on the full dataset for E epochs; (2) compute per-instance Adam gradient features (incorporating 1st/2nd momentum terms), average across epochs, normalize to the unit sphere, and reduce dimensionality via random projection (d_proj=8192); (3) cluster the projected gradient directions using BIRCH (with iterative re-clustering of oversized clusters) into C≈8–10 groups, then fine-tune a separate LoRA expert on each cluster starting from the base checkpoint at a reduced learning rate. At inference, the test instruction's gradient is compared via cosine similarity to each cluster centroid; similarities are standardized across clusters and passed through softmax to yield expert weights, and the base adapter receives an additional weight proportional to 1 minus the maximum similarity (capturing out-of-distribution inputs). The final prediction is the argmax of the weighted sum of logits from all C+1 adapters, computed once per generation step and reused across autoregressive tokens.

## Results

On MATH-Combined (Gemma-2b, LoRA rank r=8), ELREA achieves 20.41% micro-average accuracy, outperforming the full-data LoRA baseline (18.61%) by +1.80 points. On general reasoning benchmarks (BBH and MMLU), ELREA reaches 28.03% and 34.84% exact-match accuracy respectively, a +0.97 point macro-average gain over the baseline (30.47%). At rank r=64, ELREA still leads with 27.33% on MATH-Combined (+0.94 over baseline). The advantage narrows with the stronger Gemma2-9b backbone, where ELREA gains only +0.49 (r=8) and +0.21 (r=64) over the base adapter.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ELREA outperforms all three primary baselines on both MATH-Combined and the general reasoning benchmarks. Against LoRA Ensembles (Wang et al., 2023a), which independently fine-tunes three additional adapters and averages predictions, ELREA achieves 20.41% vs. 19.55% on MATH-Combined (r=8) and 31.44% vs. 30.47% macro-average on BBH/MMLU, while requiring less training compute since cluster adapters are initialized from the shared base adapter. MoLE (Wu et al., 2024), which uses a trainable layer-wise gating network, consistently underperforms the simple full-data baseline (17.99% vs. 18.61% on MATH-Combined; 30.30% vs. 30.47% on BBH/MMLU), suggesting the complex routing function overfits. Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2023b), which performs five sampling passes with majority voting, degrades significantly (13.12% on MATH-Combined, −5.49 points), likely due to high variance at temperature 1. The Instruction Embedding ablation (replacing gradient features with sentence embeddings) also trails ELREA (19.46% vs. 20.41%), confirming that gradient-direction clustering is critical for effective expert specialization.

## Relation to Prior Work

ELREA synthesizes three established threads: LoRA for parameter-efficient adaptation (Hu et al., 2022), gradient-based data influence estimation with Adam-aware features and random projection (Xia et al., 2024, LESS), and Deep Ensembles for prediction aggregation (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017). The key conceptual departure is repurposing gradient directions not for data selection (as in LESS) or layer-wise routing (as in MoE approaches like MoLE), but for clustering training instances into expertise groups and then combining per-cluster LoRA experts via a precomputed, input-dependent weighting at the output level. This positions ELREA between MoE (trainable gating, intra-layer aggregation) and Deep Ensembles (fixed averaging, end-of-model aggregation), using gradient similarity as a task-agnostic routing signal that requires no validation data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ELREA offers a practical, single-training-session recipe for multi-task LoRA fine-tuning that avoids the gradient conflict problem without task-specific validation data, making it attractive for real-world deployment where test distributions are unknown. However, the gains are modest (+1–2% over a single LoRA baseline), diminish substantially with stronger backbones (Gemma2-9b), and the inference cost of activating all C+1 adapters simultaneously limits scalability. Open questions include whether the approach scales to 70B+ models, whether the gradient clustering advantage over random partitioning (which approximates Deep Ensembles) persists at larger scale, and whether more efficient adapter architectures (e.g., FLoRA) can reduce the multi-adapter inference overhead.
