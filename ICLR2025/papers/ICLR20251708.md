## Problem

The optimal selection and temporal evolution of momentum coefficients in SGD-based optimizers remain poorly understood, with no consensus on whether coefficients should be decoupled or coupled or how they should change over training. This uncertainty impedes a mechanistic understanding of what momentum actually does to the gradient signal during optimization.

## Input / Output

FSGDM consumes the per-step gradient g_t from any differentiable loss, along with a scaling factor c (typically 0.033), a fixed momentum coefficient v (typically 1), and the total number of training steps Σ determined by epoch count and dataset size. It produces updated model parameters x_t via a momentum buffer m_t whose filtering characteristic is re-computed every δ = Σ/N steps (N=300 stages), yielding a dynamic magnitude response that is invariant to changes in Σ when c and N are fixed (Proposition 1).

## Method

The paper introduces a frequency domain analysis framework that applies the Z-transform to the momentum recurrence under a quasi-stationary approximation (coefficients held constant per stage), yielding the transfer function H_k(ω) = v_k / (1 − u_k e^{−jω}). This reveals that EMA-SGDM acts as a pure attenuating low-pass filter (orthodox), while Standard-SGDM with v_t=1 acts as a low-pass gain filter that both attenuates high-frequency and amplifies low-frequency gradient components (unorthodox). Empirical experiments on ResNet50/CIFAR-100 show that high-frequency gradient components are harmful in late training, preserving the original gradient early improves accuracy, and gradually amplifying low-frequency components enhances performance. FSGDM operationalizes these insights by setting u(t) = t/(t + cΣ), which starts as an all-pass filter (preserving the gradient) and smoothly transitions into a low-pass gain filter (amplifying low-frequency, attenuating high-frequency components) over 300 stages, outperforming fixed-coefficient Standard-SGDM and EMA-SGDM across image classification, machine translation, and reinforcement learning tasks.

## Results

FSGDM achieves consistent top-1 accuracy improvements over both Standard-SGDM and EMA-SGDM across all image classification benchmarks. The largest gains appear on CIFAR-100, where FSGDM reaches 81.44% with ResNet50 (vs. 79.71% for Standard-SGDM and 76.84% for EMA-SGDM) and 81.14% with DenseNet121. On ImageNet (ResNet50), FSGDM attains 76.91% top-1 accuracy, surpassing Standard-SGDM (76.66%) and EMA-SGDM (74.24%). FSGDM also outperforms both baselines on IWSLT14 machine translation (e.g., 32.40 BLEU for Transformer vs. 31.50 and 6.27) and on MuJoCo reinforcement learning tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FSGDM outperforms Standard-SGDM (Sutskever et al., 2013; u_t=0.9, v_t=1) on every evaluated task: +1.73% top-1 on CIFAR-100 ResNet50 (81.44 vs. 79.71), +0.25% on ImageNet (76.91 vs. 76.66), +0.90 BLEU on IWSLT14 Transformer (32.40 vs. 31.50), and higher episode rewards on all three MuJoCo RL tasks. Against EMA-SGDM (Gardner, 1985; u_t=0.9, coupled), FSGDM's margins are substantially larger, e.g., +4.60% on CIFAR-100 ResNet50 (81.44 vs. 76.84) and +2.67% on ImageNet (76.91 vs. 74.24). Notably, Standard-SGDM itself consistently beats EMA-SGDM across all settings (e.g., 79.71 vs. 76.84 on CIFAR-100), empirically validating the paper's frequency-domain argument that decoupling the momentum coefficients (allowing v_t ≠ 1−u_t) is beneficial. The gap between FSGDM and Standard-SGDM is most pronounced on CIFAR-100 and NLP tasks, suggesting the dynamic low-pass-gain filtering strategy is particularly effective when the optimization landscape is more complex.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the momentum method lineage (Sutskever et al., 2013; Polyak, 1964) by importing Z-transform analysis from signal processing (Zadeh, 1950; Oppenheim et al., 1996) to interpret momentum updates as quasi-stationary filters with explicit magnitude and phase responses. It departs from prior qualitative characterizations of momentum (Goh, 2017; Cutkosky & Orabona, 2019) by providing a rigorous transfer-function framework that distinguishes orthodox (purely attenuating, e.g., EMA-SGDM) from unorthodox (amplifying, e.g., Standard-SGDM) momentum systems. The paper also resolves the decoupled-vs-coupled momentum debate (Liu et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2024) by showing the two formulations correspond to fundamentally different filter classes rather than mere constant rescalings, since learning rate schedules are tuned independently in practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The frequency domain framework offers a principled lens for understanding why certain momentum schedules work, and the three empirical insights (preserve gradients early, attenuate high frequencies late, amplify low frequencies gradually) provide actionable design guidelines for future optimizers. However, FSGDM's practical gains over Standard-SGDM are modest (typically 1–2% top-1 accuracy), and the optimizer remains heuristic with no convergence guarantees. Key open questions include extending the Z-transform analysis to adaptive optimizers like Adam (blocked by the nonlinearity of second-moment estimates), identifying the truly optimal dynamic magnitude response rather than the minimalist one proposed here, and establishing theoretical convergence bounds for time-variant momentum filters.
