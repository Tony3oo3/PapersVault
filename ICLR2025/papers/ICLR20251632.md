## Problem

While VLMs and LLMs have improved robots' spatial reasoning and task execution, they lack the ability to autonomously detect and reason about their own manipulation failures. Prior approaches treat failure detection as a binary classification problem without generating language explanations, limiting their utility for downstream systems that need to understand *why* a failure occurred to correct it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251632/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a structured image matrix I ∈ R^{n×T×H×W×C} where rows correspond to multiple camera viewpoints and columns to temporal keyframes (missing keyframes are filled with white patches), alongside a text query prompt describing the current sub-task and its success condition. The output is a two-part autoregressive response: first a binary 'yes'/'no' indicating sub-task success, followed by a free-form natural language explanation of the failure cause (e.g., 'the robot gripper rotated with an incorrect roll angle'). The image encoder operates at 256×256 resolution per viewpoint.

## Method

AHA is built on the LLaVA-v1.5 architecture (CLIP image encoder + 2-layer linear projector + LLaMA-2-13B), fine-tuned via instruction-tuning where only the projector and LLM weights are updated while the vision encoder and tokenizer remain frozen. The key data contribution is FailGen, a procedural pipeline that wraps RLBench and systematically perturbs successful keyframe-based demonstrations across seven failure modes (incomplete grasp, slip, translation offset, incorrect rotation, missing rotation, wrong action sequence, wrong target object) to generate 49K+ failure image-query pairs over 79 tasks. Co-finetuning with 665K VQA pairs and 100K LVIS object detection instances preserves general visual knowledge. The failure reasoning is framed as a two-step task—binary success detection followed by free-form causal explanation—rather than a single classification, enabling the model to produce adaptable, axis-level failure descriptions that transfer across embodiments, simulators, and unseen tasks.

## Results

AHA-13B achieves the best or near-best performance across all three evaluation datasets and four metrics (ROUGE-L, Cosine Similarity, Binary Success, LLM Fuzzy Match). On the AHA (Test) dataset, AHA-13B scores 0.446 ROUGE-L, 0.583 Cos Sim, 70.2% Binary Success, and 0.768 Fuzzy Match; on ManiSkill-Fail it reaches 0.600 ROUGE-L, 0.681 Cos Sim, 100% Binary Success, and 0.633 Fuzzy Match; on RoboFail it attains 0.280 ROUGE-L, 0.471 Cos Sim, 64.3% Binary Success, and 0.465 Fuzzy Match. In downstream robotics applications, AHA-13B improves task success rates by 22.34% (RL reward synthesis), 36.7% (TAMP planning), and 5% (sub-task verification) over GPT-4o. AHA-13B also retains general VQA knowledge, performing within 1.5% of LLaVA-13B on standard benchmarks (MMBench, ScienceQA, TextVQA, POPE, VizWiz).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AHA-13B decisively outperforms all open-source VLM baselines: LLaVA-v1.5-13B (its base model) is surpassed by over 43% on average across all metrics and datasets, LLaVA-NeXT-34B by a similar large margin, and Qwen-V1 by an even wider gap (e.g., 0.446 vs. 0.000 ROUGE-L on AHA Test). Gemini 1.5 Flash, while stronger than the open-source models, still trails AHA-13B substantially (e.g., 0.120 vs. 0.446 ROUGE-L on AHA Test; 0.003 vs. 0.600 on ManiSkill-Fail). The closest competitor is GPT-4o with 5-shot in-context learning, which AHA-13B outperforms by an average of 12.6% on AHA (Test), 13.4% on ManiSkill-Fail, and 4.9% on RoboFail across all four metrics. Notably, GPT-4o-ICL achieves 97.1% Binary Success on ManiSkill-Fail (vs. AHA's 100%), but AHA leads on all reasoning-quality metrics (ROUGE-L, Cos Sim, Fuzzy Match) across every dataset. In downstream robotics tasks, AHA-13B consistently beats GPT-4o by 22.34% (RL reward synthesis), 36.7% (TAMP), and 5% (sub-task verification) in task success rate.

## Relation to Prior Work

AHA builds directly on the LLaVA-v1.5 architecture and instruction-tuning pipeline (Liu et al., 2023a), fine-tuning only the projector and LLaMA-2-13B transformer while freezing the vision encoder. Conceptually, it extends the failure taxonomy from REFLECT (Liu et al., 2023d) and departs from prior work that treats failure detection as a binary classification problem by reframing it as a free-form reasoning task requiring natural language explanations. The data generation approach (FailGen) is analogous to MimicGen and RoboPoint in leveraging simulation for scalable data creation, but uniquely applies procedural keyframe perturbation to synthesize failure trajectories rather than successful demonstrations or spatial representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AHA provides the first large-scale (49K+) dataset of robotic manipulation failures with language annotations, filling a critical gap for training and evaluating failure-aware VLMs. Its demonstrated integration into three distinct downstream frameworks (Eureka reward synthesis, PRoC3S task planning, Manipulate-Anything sub-task verification) shows practical utility beyond a standalone benchmark. Key limitations include coverage of only seven predefined failure modes, inability to directly influence low-level policies, and reliance on simulation-generated failures that may not capture the full diversity of real-world breakdowns. Open questions include whether distilling pretrained policies to generate naturally occurring failures could broaden coverage, and whether language-conditioned corrective actions could close the loop between detection and recovery.
