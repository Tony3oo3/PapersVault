## Problem

Massively multilingual LLMs suffer from two compounding weaknesses: mid- and low-resource languages receive disproportionately poor translation quality, and the 'curse of multilinguality' causes average performance to degrade as the number of supported languages grows. Existing models that claim hundreds of languages fail to deliver consistently high-quality translation across all resource levels, while models restricted to a few high-resource languages neglect the broader multilingual need.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253414/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model accepts a source sentence in any of 50 languages (14 scripts, 18 language families) along with a translation instruction prompt, and generates the corresponding translated sentence in the target language. At inference, the system hard-gates the input to one of 8 language-specific LoRA modules based on the source/target language group, so only the base model plus one ~15%-parameter module need to be active. Three loading strategies are supported: single on-demand module (memory-efficient), merged module into base (same parameter count as base), or all modules loaded simultaneously in a MoE-style configuration.

## Method

X-ALMA combines a dense LLaMA-2-13B base model with 8 plug-and-play language-specific modules (LoRA adapters on all attention and MLP linear layers), where each module serves a group of 6–7 typologically similar languages via hard-gated assignment rather than a learned neural gate. Training follows a 5-stage recipe: (1) monolingual fine-tuning of the base model on 20B tokens across all 50 languages, (2) monolingual fine-tuning of each LS module on 10B tokens from its group, (3) pseudo-monolingual fine-tuning on 1.25B tokens of randomly concatenated parallel pairs to improve cross-lingual alignment, (4) SFT on small high-quality parallel data (3K–7K per direction), and (5) Adaptive Rejection Preference Optimization (ARPO). ARPO addresses the 'over-rejection' problem in MT preference learning—where preferred and dis-preferred translations differ by only a few words—by introducing an adaptive penalty τ_θ that scales the dis-preferred log-likelihood term based on the absolute difference in average token log-probabilities between y_w and y_l; when the two translations are nearly identical, τ approaches 0, suppressing the rejection signal and preventing the model from drifting away from the preferred writing style.

## Results

On FLORES-200, X-ALMA achieves average COMET-22 scores of 89.6 (en→xx) and 89.4 (xx→en) across all 8 language groups, surpassing every baseline in all 98 translation directions. On WMT'23, X-ALMA reaches 86.1 (→) and 84.1 (←) average COMET-22, outperforming Aya-23-35B (84.1/82.9), Aya-101 (81.0/80.2), and NLLB-3.3B (81.8/72.6). The ARPO preference optimization stage adds consistent gains over SFT-only (e.g., 90.6 vs. 90.1 COMET-22 on Group 6 en→xx), and X-ALMA also outperforms its 6-language predecessor ALMA-R-13B on WMT'23 (86.1 vs. ~84.8 average over shared directions).

## Baselines & Comparisons

X-ALMA outperforms all 12 baselines in every one of the 98 English-centric translation directions on both FLORES-200 and WMT'23 according to COMET-22, and in 97/98 directions on XCOMET-XL. Against the strongest high-resource-focused model Aya-23-35B, X-ALMA gains +2.0 COMET-22 on WMT'23 en→xx average (86.1 vs. 84.1) and +1.2 on xx→en (84.1 vs. 82.9), while also matching or exceeding Aya-23 on high-resource language subsets (e.g., 89.9 vs. 86.5 on Group 1 high-resource en→xx). Against the massively multilingual baselines, the gap is larger: X-ALMA leads Aya-101 by +5.1 COMET-22 on WMT'23 en→xx (86.1 vs. 81.0) and NLLB-3.3B by +4.3 (86.1 vs. 81.8). LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, a general-purpose LLM, trails X-ALMA by 8–15 COMET-22 points depending on direction and language group, confirming that dedicated translation training remains essential. On FLORES-200, X-ALMA's advantage over Aya-23-35B is most pronounced for mid/low-resource languages (e.g., Group 7 'All' en→xx: 83.2 vs. 61.9), while for high-resource languages the gap narrows to ~1–3 points, demonstrating that scaling to 50 languages does not sacrifice high-resource quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

X-ALMA extends the ALMA(-R) line of work (Xu et al., 2024a,b) from 6 to 50 languages, building on its backbone model, training recipe, and CPO preference optimization loss. The plug-and-play architecture draws directly from Xu et al. (2023)'s hard-gated language-specific modules, but departs from standard MoE by eliminating the neural gate and enabling selective module loading for memory-efficient inference. The key methodological departure is ARPO, which modifies CPO's preference term with an adaptive penalty τ_θ that scales the dis-preferred signal based on the similarity between preferred and dis-preferred translations, addressing a failure mode (over-rejection) that standard preference optimization methods like DPO, KTO, and SimPO cannot handle in the MT setting where reward differences are inherently small.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the 'curse of multilinguality'—performance degradation as supported languages increase—can be overcome with careful architecture and training design, achieving higher average performance at 50 languages than ALMA-R achieved at 6. The plug-and-play LoRA module design has practical deployment value, allowing operators to load only the relevant language module rather than the full parameter set. However, the system remains English-centric (98 directions), limited to 50 languages, and relies on reference translations for preference data construction, leaving open whether the approach scales to 200+ languages or non-English-centric settings. The over-rejection finding also raises the broader question of whether preference optimization methods designed for open-ended generation need fundamentally different formulations for constrained tasks like translation.
