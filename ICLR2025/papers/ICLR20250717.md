## Problem

Policymakers allocating limited interventions commonly use risk-based targeting (treating those with the worst predicted baseline outcomes) because estimating heterogeneous treatment effects from observational data is difficult and biased. However, there is almost no empirical evidence comparing risk-based targeting to treatment-effect-based targeting across diverse real-world settings, and no guidance on how confounding or inequality-averse preferences shift the optimal strategy.

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular RCT data (individual covariates, binary treatment assignment, and observed outcomes) from five domains (economics, healthcare, education) and produces two outputs: a treatment assignment policy (which fraction of the population to treat, ranked by either baseline risk or predicted CATE) and a welfare estimate under three social welfare functions (uniform utilitarian, risk-weighted utilitarian, and Nash). A key unusual aspect is the semi-synthetic evaluation where pseudo-outcomes are directly observed rather than predicted from features, simulating a setting with perfectly accurate treatment effect estimation.

## Method

The paper estimates heterogeneous treatment effects using a doubly-robust estimator with cross-fitting (Kennedy, 2023a), where nuisance functions for outcomes and propensity scores are trained on separate data folds and pseudo-outcomes are computed for held-out units. A random forest regressor then maps covariates to pseudo-outcome differences to produce CATE predictions for targeting. To simulate confounding, the paper systematically removes data in an adverse-selection pattern (removing top-k% treated units and bottom-k% untreated units by treatment effect), creating increasingly biased observational datasets. Targeting policies are evaluated by their expected treatment-on-the-treated under each welfare function, with kernel regression used for the 1D CATE-vs-risk analysis. The semi-synthetic variant bypasses the second-stage regression by ranking individuals directly on observed pseudo-outcomes, isolating the effect of estimation accuracy from the effect of confounding.

## Results

In the semi-synthetic setting (where treatment effects are estimated from observed pseudo-outcomes), treatment effect targeting produces significantly higher utilitarian welfare than risk-based targeting at zero confounding across all 5 datasets, and still performs at least as well (typically better) even at 40% systematic data removal. In the real-data setting (where treatment effects must be predicted from features alone), only the STAR dataset shows statistically significant improvement from treatment effect targeting over risk-based targeting at k=0; on the other four datasets, confidence intervals overlap, indicating insufficient data or features to learn reliable HTE mappings. Under inequality-averse preferences (weighted utilitarian with α up to ~8.5, or Nash welfare), treatment effect targeting remains preferred unless the policymaker assigns at least ~4.5× more weight to the 75th-percentile-risk individual than the 25th-percentile individual (α ≥ 3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is risk-based targeting (Wilder & Welle, 2024), which allocates treatment to individuals with the highest predicted baseline risk (worst status-quo outcome). In the semi-synthetic setting where treatment effects are reliably estimated, treatment effect targeting wins decisively: it produces significantly higher utilitarian welfare at k=0 on all five datasets, and maintains an advantage (or at least parity) over risk-based targeting even at 40% confounding. In the real-data setting, the comparison is much closer: on STAR, treatment effect targeting shows a statistically significant edge at zero confounding that erodes with increasing k, while on TUP, NSW, Postoperative Pain, and Acupuncture, the two strategies have overlapping confidence intervals throughout, suggesting the available features and sample sizes are insufficient to learn meaningful HTE patterns. Under Nash social welfare (an inequality-averse criterion), risk-based targeting narrowly outperforms treatment effect targeting only in the Ultra Poor setting at high confounding (k=40%); in all other dataset–confounding combinations, treatment effect targeting is still preferred. The key practical takeaway is that the bottleneck is not confounding or normative preferences but rather the ability to accurately estimate heterogeneous treatment effects from available data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the doubly-robust CATE estimation framework (Kennedy, 2023a) and the risk-based targeting formulation (Wilder & Welle, 2024) to conduct the first systematic multi-domain empirical comparison between these two targeting strategies. Prior empirical studies each examined a single application (financial aid nudges, customer churn, cash transfers), and the authors note that risk-based targeting and biased treatment-effect targeting from confounded data have not been explicitly compared before. The paper extends the evaluation axis by incorporating social welfare functions (weighted utilitarian, Nash) to capture inequality-averse policymaker preferences, drawing on the welfare economics literature (Caragiannis et al., 2019; Pardeshi et al., 2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical finding is that the bottleneck for treatment-effect targeting is data quality and quantity for HTE estimation, not confounding or inequality-averse preferences—suggesting large-scale administrative datasets may be more valuable for policy learning than previously assumed. However, the real-data results are mixed: in most domains, limited features and sample sizes prevent the model from learning reliable HTE patterns, making risk-based targeting competitive in practice. Open questions include how to obtain sufficiently rich data for accurate HTE estimation in settings where RCTs are infeasible, and whether the consequentialist framing (welfare = realized outcome) adequately captures policymaker objectives. The paper also acknowledges that non-consequentialist preferences (e.g., deontological commitments to helping the vulnerable) could justify risk-based targeting regardless of efficiency.
