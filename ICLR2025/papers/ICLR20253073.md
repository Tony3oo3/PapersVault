## Problem

In standard DPO training, winning and losing responses within pairwise preference data are generated in isolation (via human annotation, RLAIF, or reject sampling), resulting in weak correlations between them and forcing the model to learn from superficial stylistic differences rather than substantive quality distinctions. Furthermore, DPO's token-level MDP formulation aggregates rewards uniformly across all tokens, assuming equal contribution to sequence quality and failing to emphasize the critical token-level variations that truly differentiate preferred from dispreferred responses.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard pairwise preference data (prompt x, winning response y_w, losing response y_l) plus an off-the-shelf LLM (GPT-4-0125-preview or Llama3-70B-Instruct) used exclusively during an offline Bridging Phase to synthesize a pseudo-winning response via targeted modification of the losing response. The output is a preference-optimized policy model trained with a modified DPO objective that incorporates dynamic token-level reward weighting. The Bridging Phase is a one-time data preprocessing step with no runtime cost, while the Modeling Phase adds less than 1% training overhead over vanilla DPO.

## Method

BMC operates in two phases. In the Bridging Phase, an off-the-shelf LLM modifies the losing response y_l on its dispreferred tokens using y_w as a reference, producing a pseudo-winning response ỹ_w that preserves y_l's essential characteristics while incorporating y_w's desired values, thereby making token-level differences between ỹ_w and y_l highlight the core preference-relevant distinctions. In the Modeling Phase, the paper identifies varied tokens between ỹ_w and y_l via Levenshtein distance and modifies DPO's loss by assigning each varied token a dynamic reward multiplier λ = 1 + min(sg(1/π_θ(token)), δ), where sg is stop-gradient and δ caps the emphasis. This makes the reward inversely proportional to the policy model's confidence on that token: tokens the model is uncertain about (indicating underdeveloped learning) receive stronger gradient signal, while already-confident tokens receive standard weighting. The approach thus harmonizes sequence-level DPO optimization with token-level credit assignment that adapts dynamically during training, unlike prior methods (FIGA, DRLC, ABC) that assign fixed predefined reward values.

## Results

On QA tasks (Llama2-7B-base), DPO-BMC achieves 65.1% average accuracy, outperforming DPO (61.3%) by 3.8 points and the strongest baseline R-DPO (61.8%) by 3.3 points. On mathematical reasoning, DPO-BMC reaches 49.6% average accuracy vs. DPO's 48.3% (+1.3). On instruction following (Llama3-8B-base), DPO-BMC attains 22.4% length-controlled win rate on AlpacaEval 2, surpassing DPO (16.0%) by 6.4 points and SimPO (21.3%) by 1.1 points, while also producing significantly shorter responses (~1,285 tokens vs. ~1,713 for DPO).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DPO-BMC consistently outperforms all seven baselines across QA and Math tasks. Against DPO (the primary baseline), it gains +3.8 on QA average and +1.3 on Math average; against the strongest DPO variant R-DPO, it leads by +3.3 (QA) and +1.4 (Math); against SimPO, it leads by +6.0 (QA) and +0.7 (Math); and against FIGA, it leads by +9.3 (QA) and +5.0 (Math). On instruction following (Llama3-8B), DPO-BMC achieves 22.4% LC on AlpacaEval 2, beating DPO by +6.4, R-DPO by +5.3, and SimPO by +1.1; however, SimPO still leads on Arena-Hard (26.6% vs. 18.1% WR), suggesting DPO-BMC's advantage is more pronounced on length-controlled metrics. The two auxiliary baselines DPO(CW) and DPO(EW) both underperform or marginally match vanilla DPO, confirming that superficial correlation or external token weighting is insufficient without the dynamic confidence-based mechanism proposed here.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) and its token-level MDP reinterpretation (Rafailov et al., 2024) by addressing two gaps: weak correlations in pairwise preference data and DPO's uniform token-level reward aggregation. On the data side, it departs from prior preference data construction methods (RLAIF, reject sampling, West-of-N) that generate winning and losing responses in isolation, instead synthesizing a pseudo-winning response by modifying the losing response with the winning response as reference. On the optimization side, it contrasts with token-level credit assignment methods (FIGA, DRLC, ABC, RLMEC) that assign fixed reward values, proposing instead a dynamic weighting scheme driven by the policy model's own confidence during training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The BMC framework offers a practical, low-overhead (<1% training time) enhancement that is broadly applicable across DPO variants (IPO, ORPO, R-DPO, SimPO) and scales with model size, making it a potentially adoptable component in alignment pipelines. However, the Bridging Phase's reliance on an external LLM (even if open-source alternatives work) introduces a dependency that may not be trivial in resource-constrained settings, and the approach remains offline-only. Open questions include whether the confidence-based weighting generalizes to online or iterative preference optimization settings, and whether the Levenshtein-based token alignment is robust to more semantically distant response pairs.
