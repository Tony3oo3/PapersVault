## Problem

Synthetic datasets generated by SCMs exhibit variance and correlation artifacts that increase along the causal ordering, allowing simple baselines to game structure learning benchmarks. Post-hoc standardization removes the variance artifact but leaves the R²-sortability (correlation) artifact intact, and existing heuristics that adjust the sampling process impose restrictive limitations on the class of causal dependencies that can be modeled. The paper asks whether the SCM generative process itself can be modified to eliminate both artifacts without restricting the mechanisms.

## Input / Output

The method takes a DAG G, causal mechanism functions f_i (linear or nonlinear), and i.i.d. exogenous noise variables ε_i as input, and produces observed variables x̃_i with zero mean and unit marginal variance by construction. Unlike post-hoc standardization, the standardization is applied internally at each step of the generative process, so each mechanism f_i operates on already-standardized parent variables x̃_pa(i). For linear systems, the implied SCM parameters (rescaled weights and noise variances) can be computed analytically via a bottom-up dynamic programming procedure, enabling direct sampling from the exact implied model without empirical statistics.

## Method

The iSCM introduces a z-standardization operation at each variable during generation: x_i := f_i(x̃_pa(i), ε_i) followed by x̃_i := (x_i − E[x_i]) / √Var[x_i], making the causal mechanisms scale-free so the relative interaction between f_i and ε_i is independent of the variable's position in the causal ordering. The paper proves that the cause-explained variance fraction is bounded by 1 − σ²/(m²w² + σ²), guaranteeing iSCMs never collapse to deterministic mechanisms in large systems, unlike standardized SCMs. For forest DAGs, the paper shows linear Gaussian iSCMs are not identifiable beyond their Markov equivalence class even with known weight supports, in contrast to standardized SCMs which are partially identifiable under the same conditions. The implied SCM framework—showing iSCMs can be rewritten as standard SCMs with rescaled weights and noise—enables applying the trek rule to derive these identifiability results and to analyze how structure learning algorithms that assume unstandardized SCMs behave on iSCM data.

## Results

iSCMs produce datasets with R²-sortability ≈ 0.5 (i.e., not R²-sortable) across common graph families (ER and scale-free graphs of varying sizes), while standardized SCMs exhibit strong R²-sortability (|R²-sortability − 0.5| ≫ 0). In structure learning experiments (F1 score for edge recovery, n=1000 samples, 20 systems per configuration), NOTEARS and GOLEM-EV perform best on unstandardized SCMs but also outperform on iSCMs relative to standardized SCMs, especially in linear and larger systems. PC and GES show improved F1 scores on iSCMs over standardized SCMs, particularly for large systems. The R²-SORTNREGRESS and AVICI baselines, which exploit correlation artifacts, degrade substantially on iSCM data, confirming the removal of exploitable patterns.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new structure learning algorithm but rather a new data-generating process (iSCM) and evaluates how existing algorithms behave on it. Var-SORTNREGRESS and R²-SORTNREGRESS (Reisach et al., 2021; 2024) exploit variance and correlation artifacts respectively: they perform well on unstandardized and standardized SCMs but degrade to near-random on iSCMs, confirming that iSCMs remove these exploitable patterns. AVICI (Lorch et al., 2022), trained on standardized SCMs, shows the same degradation on iSCM data, suggesting it learned to exploit correlation artifacts rather than genuine causal signals. NOTEARS (Zheng et al., 2018) and GOLEM-EV (Ng et al., 2020), which assume equal noise scales, perform best on unstandardized SCMs but notably also perform better on iSCMs than on standardized SCMs—the paper attributes this to the implied noise scales of standardized SCMs being orders of magnitude more extreme (Figure 6), violating the equal-variance assumption more severely. GOLEM-NV and CAM, which do not assume equal noise scales, perform comparably on standardized SCMs and generally best on iSCMs. PC (Spirtes & Glymour, 1991) and GES (Chickering, 2002) show improved F1 on iSCMs over standardized SCMs, particularly in large systems, consistent with the more well-behaved implied noise distributions of iSCMs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the structural causal model (SCM) framework by introducing internal standardization during the generative process, directly addressing artifacts identified by Reisach et al. (2021, 2024) in synthetic causal discovery benchmarks. While Reisach et al. proposed post-hoc standardization to remove variance-sortability, this work shows that approach leaves R²-sortability intact and, more surprisingly, creates partial identifiability of the causal graph. The iSCM construction departs from existing heuristics (Mooij et al., 2020; Squires et al., 2022) that mitigate variance accumulation by restricting the class of allowable causal mechanisms, instead preserving arbitrary dependency strengths while eliminating both artifacts by construction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, one-line modification to SCM data generation that removes exploitable covariance artifacts, making benchmarks more reflective of real-world causal structure learning. The identifiability theorems (partial identifiability of standardized SCMs vs. non-identifiability of iSCMs beyond the MEC) reveal that common benchmarking practices may inadvertently make problems easier than intended, a finding with implications for interpreting published causal discovery results. Limitations include identifiability results restricted to forest DAGs, and the practical utility of iSCMs for real-world causal modeling (beyond benchmarking) remains largely unexplored. Open questions include whether the non-identifiability result extends to general DAGs and how iSCMs interact with non-Gaussian noise assumptions used by algorithms like LiNGAM.
