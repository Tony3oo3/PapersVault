## Problem

The robustness of watermark-based AI-generated image detectors against evasion attacks in the no-box setting—where the attacker has no access to the watermarking model, detection API, or training data—remains poorly understood. Prior transfer attacks using a single surrogate model or classifier-based adversarial examples achieved limited success against learning-based watermarks, leading to a misleading conclusion that watermarking is robust in this setting. This paper demonstrates that existing watermarks are in fact vulnerable to a well-designed multi-model transfer attack even without any access to the target system.

## Input / Output

The method takes a watermarked RGB image (from Stable Diffusion, Midjourney, or DALL-E 2) and m pre-trained surrogate watermarking decoders (default: 100 CNN-based HiDDeN decoders with 30-bit watermarks, trained via bootstrapping on a surrogate dataset that may differ in distribution from the target's). It outputs a single perturbation δ bounded by an ℓ∞-norm budget r (typically 0.08–0.25 in normalized [-1,1] pixel space), which when added to the watermarked image causes the target decoder to produce a watermark whose bitwise accuracy with the ground truth falls below the detection threshold τ, evading the double-tail detector while preserving visual quality (SSIM > 0.92).

## Method

The attack proceeds in two steps. First, for each of the m surrogate decoders, a target watermark is selected using Inverse-Decode (ID): the bits decoded by that surrogate from the clean watermarked image are flipped, so the perturbation is guided toward making each surrogate decode the inverse of its original output. Second, a single perturbation is generated via Ensemble-Optimization (EO), which jointly optimizes δ to minimize ‖δ‖∞ subject to all m surrogate decoders simultaneously decoding their respective target watermarks. This is reformulated into a tractable constrained problem (minimizing mean MSE to target watermarks under an ℓ∞ budget and a bitwise-accuracy lower bound) and solved with projected gradient descent, using the accuracy constraint as an early-stopping criterion. The key insight over prior work is that jointly optimizing one perturbation across multiple diverse surrogates (trained on bootstrapped subsets with varying architectures and watermark lengths) preserves coherent perturbation patterns that averaging individual white-box perturbations would destroy, yielding substantially higher transferability. The paper further derives theoretical upper and lower bounds on the probability that the target decoder's output matches the ground-truth watermark, conditioned on the surrogate decoders' outputs, quantifying the attack's transferability.

## Results

With 100 surrogate watermarking models (CNN, 30-bit), the transfer attack achieves over 85% evasion rate against both CNN and ResNet target architectures with an average ℓ∞-norm perturbation below 0.25 (pixel range [−1,1]). For a CNN target with 30-bit watermarks, only 40 surrogate models and a perturbation of 0.09 are needed for 100% evasion; for a ResNet target, 60 models and 0.14 perturbation suffice. Across five target watermarking methods (HiDDeN, StegaStamp, Stable Signature, Smoothed HiDDeN, Smoothed StegaStamp), the attack maintains over 77% evasion with 100 surrogate models while preserving image quality (average SSIM > 0.97 at r = 0.1, > 0.92 at r = 0.25).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed transfer attack dramatically outperforms all five existing evasion methods and the DiffPure purification baseline. Existing classifier-based attacks (AdvEmb-RN18, AdvCls-Real&WM, MI-CWA) and the single-surrogate watermark-based attack (WEvade-B-S) achieve approximately 0% evasion rate against learning-based watermarks with adversarial training. The only exception is AdvCls-Enc-WM1&WM2, which reaches ~10% evasion on Stable Diffusion with 20–30-bit watermarks but critically requires access to the target encoder, violating the no-box threat model. MI-CWA and DiffPure show low evasion rates due to the fundamental limitations of treating the watermark detector as a binary classifier and the heavy image modification required for purification, respectively. In contrast, the proposed method achieves >85% evasion while maintaining SSIM > 0.97, and introduces substantially smaller perturbations than common post-processing methods (JPEG, Gaussian noise, Gaussian blur, Brightness/Contrast) at matched evasion rates. The attack also transfers effectively to certifiably robust watermarks (Smoothed HiDDeN, Smoothed StegaStamp), exceeding their certified bounds while preserving image quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the watermark evasion attack line initiated by Jiang et al. (2023), which studied white-box and black-box attacks on watermark-based detectors, to the no-box setting where the attacker has no access to the detection API or model. The key methodological departure is from prior transfer attacks that relied on a single surrogate watermarking model (Jiang et al., 2023) or classifier-based adversarial examples (An et al., 2024); here, the authors train multiple surrogate watermarking models with diverse architectures and watermark lengths, then aggregate them via a joint ensemble-optimization (EO) formulation solved with projected gradient descent. The theoretical contribution—deriving upper and lower bounds on the probability that the target decoder's output matches the ground-truth watermark given surrogate outputs—is novel and not present in prior watermark attack literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding that watermark-based AI-generated image detection is not robust in the no-box setting directly challenges the security assumption underpinning industry deployments (Google SynthID, OpenAI DALL-E, Stable Diffusion watermarking) that keeping the watermark model private provides adequate protection. The attack also breaks certifiably robust watermarks (Smoothed HiDDeN, Smoothed StegaStamp), undermining a key defense mechanism. A significant open question, acknowledged by the authors, is whether the attack transfers to a target watermarking method entirely different from any surrogate the attacker could plausibly train, which limits the generality of the threat. Future work on extending the attack to text and audio watermarks, and on designing fundamentally more robust watermarking schemes, is identified as important next steps.
