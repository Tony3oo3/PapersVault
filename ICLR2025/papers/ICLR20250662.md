## Problem

Existing graph invariant learning methods (IRM, VRex, and their graph-specific variants) are assumed to capture invariant features for OOD generalization, but the paper demonstrates they may only learn a subset of invariant features when a smaller subset already suffices for accurate training-set prediction. This limits their generalization to unseen environments, as the full set of causally relevant substructures is not recovered.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250662/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes undirected graphs (adjacency matrix A ∈ {0,1}^{n×n} and node feature matrix X ∈ R^{n×d}) from multiple training environments and produces a graph-level class label via a GNN encoder followed by a classifier. Internally, the pipeline also generates spurious graph representations h_s and invariant graph representations h_c as disentangled intermediate features, which are used to compute a class-conditioned cross-entropy regularization term during training.

## Method

LIRS adopts an indirect learning paradigm: instead of directly optimizing for invariance (as IRM/VRex do), it first learns spurious features and then removes them from ERM-learned representations. The first stage uses a biased infomax principle that maximizes mutual information between global and local node representations for spurious nodes while minimizing it for invariant nodes; in practice, a GSAT explainer approximates the invariant subgraph and an instance-level counterfactual threshold determines whether to apply the bias per sample. The second stage clusters spurious features within each class, refits a linear classifier on cluster labels to obtain spurious logits, and applies a reweighted class-conditioned cross-entropy loss (using GCE reweighting) to unlearn spurious patterns from the ERM encoder. The total loss is L_GT + λ·L_inv, where L_inv regularizes the encoder to retain only invariant features. Theoretical results (Theorems 4.1 and 4.2) prove that the biased infomax achieves spuriosity learning and that the combined objective converges to an encoder that elicits purely invariant features.

## Results

LIRS achieves the best performance across all 8 dataset/split combinations in Table 1. On the synthetic GOOD-Motif benchmark, it surpasses the second-best method (AIA) by 12.51% on the base split (75.51 vs. 73.64 accuracy) and by 25.50% on the size split (74.95 vs. 60.23 accuracy). On real-world OGBG datasets, LIRS outperforms the strongest baseline AIA by 4.62% under size shift (88.77 vs. 85.11 on Molbace; 82.19 vs. 81.03 on Molbbbp) and by 1.95% under scaffold shift (81.91 vs. 79.42 on Molbace; 71.04 vs. 70.79 on Molbbbp). The method also shows notably lower variance (e.g., ±0.76 on Molbbbp-scaffold) compared to many baselines, indicating more stable OOD generalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LIRS outperforms all 17 baselines across every dataset and split. The largest margins appear on the synthetic GOOD-Motif benchmark: on the size split, LIRS (74.95) beats the runner-up OOD-GCL (60.23) by 14.72 points, and on the base split it edges out AIA (73.64) by 1.87 points. Among direct invariant-learning methods (IRM, VREx, GIL, GREA, CIGA), none comes within 5 points of LIRS on any split, confirming the paper's central claim that directly learning invariance captures only a subset of invariant features. EQuAD, which shares the indirect 'learn-then-remove-spuriosity' paradigm, is the closest competitor on real-world OGBG datasets (e.g., 86.41 vs. 88.77 on Molbace-size), but LIRS consistently leads by 1–3 points, attributed to the biased infomax and class-conditioned cross-entropy loss. Diverse-feature methods (RSC, DiverseModel) perform competitively on real-world data but collapse on GOOD-Motif (46–54 accuracy), where only one invariant subgraph is causally relevant. On the SPMotif-binary stress test (Table 2), LIRS maintains a 1.5–2.5 point lead over EQuAD and 2–5 points over IRM/VREx as spurious correlation strength increases from 0.40 to 0.90, demonstrating robustness to stronger spurious signals.

## Relation to Prior Work

LIRS extends the indirect learning paradigm introduced by EQuAD (Yao et al., 2024), which first learns spurious features via self-supervised infomax and then removes them from ERM representations. The key departure is the biased infomax principle, a novel modification of the standard graph infomax (Veličković et al., 2019; Hjelm et al., 2019) that explicitly suppresses invariant signals to sharpen spuriosity learning, combined with a class-conditioned cross-entropy loss that addresses the interference of overlapping spurious patterns across classes. Conceptually, the paper positions itself against the dominant direct invariant learning paradigm (IRM, VREx, GIL, GREA, CIGA, AIA), arguing that these methods provably capture only a subset of invariant features under additive data-generating assumptions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification that IRM and VREx—the foundational frameworks for graph OOD generalization—may systematically miss invariant features is a useful diagnostic for the field, suggesting that the entire direct-learning line of work may be fundamentally limited in feature coverage. The practical impact is tempered by the fact that the largest gains (25.50%) appear on synthetic benchmarks, while real-world improvements over the best baseline (AIA) are more modest (4.62% size shift, 1.95% scaffold shift). Open questions include how the approximation error from the GNN explainer (GSAT) scales with graph complexity, whether the indirect paradigm extends to node-level or multi-task settings, and whether the biased infomax's reliance on a pre-trained explainer introduces circularity in practice.
