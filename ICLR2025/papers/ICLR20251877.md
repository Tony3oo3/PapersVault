## Problem

The first retrieval-based co-speech gesture video method, GVR, suffers from two critical limitations: (1) audio-motion misalignment because retrieval relies on naive audio onset features and hard-coded keyword matching rather than learned cross-modal representations, and (2) visual artifacts (blur, distorted hands) in GAN-based transition frames caused by inaccurate optical flow estimation. Generative alternatives avoid the retrieval problem but introduce temporal blur in hand and cloth textures that is unacceptable for real-world applications.

## Input / Output

The method takes a few-minute, single-speaker reference video (with consistent clothing and low-dynamic background) and arbitrary-length target speech audio, producing a co-speech body-gesture video of effectively infinite length. The reference video is decomposed into 1-frame non-overlapping clips that form nodes in a directed motion graph; the output is assembled by traversing this graph and generating 8-frame transition clips at non-adjacent boundaries. Lip synchronization is handled separately via Wav2Lip post-processing, and the method requires fixed-camera, clean-background reference videos to function correctly.

## Method

TANGO integrates three components into a motion-graph retrieval pipeline. First, a graph pruning algorithm merges strongly connected components (SCCs) by adding bidirectional edges between the closest node pairs across components, eliminating dead endpoints and enabling sampling of arbitrarily long paths. Second, AuMoCLIP learns a hierarchical audio-motion joint embedding via a dual-tower contrastive framework where each tower is split into a low-level CNN and high-level Transformer sub-encoder; the audio branch concatenates frozen Wav2Vec2 and time-aligned BERT features, while the motion branch encodes 15D joint representations, and training uses frame-wise (local) and clip-wise (global) InfoNCE losses with stop-gradient to protect low-level features from high-level optimization. Third, ACInterp, a diffusion-based interpolation built on AnimateAnyone, generates appearance-consistent transition frames by introducing a homography background offset flow (computed via SIFT/FLANN/RANSAC on segmented backgrounds) to prevent camera-drift artifacts and a reference motion module that conditions the temporal attention on start/end frame latents to constrain the solution space and preserve identity.

## Results

On TalkShow-Oliver, TANGO achieves the best FVD (1.379) and FGD (3.714) among all methods, and the highest Diversity (5.393), while on YouTube Business it further reduces FVD to 3.133 and FGD to 2.068. TANGO also attains the best Beat Consistency (BC) on YouTube Business (0.479) but trails SDT (0.401) and S2G-Diffusion (0.393) on TalkShow-Oliver, where fully generative methods have greater motion-space flexibility. In the video blending comparison (Table 4), ACInterp outperforms all flow-based and diffusion-based baselines on FVD (0.922 vs. 1.190 for PANR) and LPIPS (0.065 vs. 0.071), while achieving the highest PSNR (35.63).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the retrieval-based GVR (Zhou et al., 2022), TANGO wins on every metric on both datasets: FVD improves from 1.615→1.379 (Oliver) and 4.027→3.133 (YouTube), FGD from 4.246→3.714 and 2.900→2.068, BC from 0.270→0.375 and 0.331→0.479, and Diversity from 4.623→5.393 and 3.573→4.128. Against the generative baselines, TANGO substantially outperforms SDT (Qian et al., 2021) on FVD (1.379 vs. 2.239) and FGD (3.714 vs. 5.722) on Oliver, and by an even larger margin on YouTube (FVD 3.133 vs. 7.612), though SDT retains a slight BC edge on Oliver (0.401 vs. 0.375). S2G-Diffusion (He et al., 2024) is the closest generative competitor, with TANGO improving FVD by ~31% (1.379 vs. 2.007) and FGD by ~23% (3.714 vs. 4.799) on Oliver, while S2G-Diffusion edges out TANGO on BC (0.393 vs. 0.375). ANGIE (Liu et al., 2022) is only evaluated on Oliver and is outperformed by TANGO on all four metrics. In the dedicated video blending comparison (Table 4), ACInterp beats the flow-based methods FiLM (FVD 0.922 vs. 1.358) and VFIFormer (0.922 vs. 1.777), the GVR-native PANR (0.922 vs. 1.190), and the unmodified AnimateAnyone (0.922 vs. 1.421), confirming that the homography background flow and reference motion module yield the sharpest and most temporally consistent transition frames.

## Relation to Prior Work

TANGO extends the retrieval-based gesture video reenactment framework of GVR (Zhou et al., 2022), which was the first to use motion graphs for audio-driven gesture video assembly. It replaces GVR's onset/keyword-based retrieval with a hierarchical audio-motion contrastive embedding (AuMoCLIP), drawing on CLIP-style dual-tower learning (Radford et al., 2021), MoCoV2 (Chen et al., 2020), and pretrained Wav2Vec2/BERT features. For transition frame synthesis, it departs from GVR's GAN-based flow warping by building on the AnimateAnyone (Hu et al., 2023) diffusion pipeline, adding homography background flow and a reference motion module for appearance consistency. The motion graph construction itself follows Kovar et al. (2008), with a novel SCC-merging pruning step to enable arbitrarily long path sampling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TANGO is the first open-source pipeline combining motion-graph retrieval with audio-conditioned gesture selection and diffusion-based interpolation, making the retrieval-based approach reproducible and extensible. Its practical value lies in producing higher-fidelity gesture videos than generative methods by reusing real video frames, which is important for production applications like news broadcasting. Key limitations include the requirement for low-dynamic backgrounds (failing on speakers who interact with blackboards or move around), the 83% success rate of linear 2D pose blending for non-linear motions, and the small scale of the introduced YouTube Business dataset (12 speakers, <1 hour). Open questions include extending the framework to general human motion (dance, sports) and handling high-dynamic backgrounds without the current homography assumptions.
