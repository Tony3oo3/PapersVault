## Problem

Predicting longitudinal brain biomarker trajectories is difficult due to biological variability, sparse and irregularly-timed observations, and high-dimensional multivariate imaging inputs. Existing methods either impose predefined trajectory shapes (linear or sigmoidal curves), require specific design assumptions about observations per subject, or fail to personalize predictions using individual follow-up data. This paper addresses the gap of a framework that handles randomly-timed observations without imputation, personalizes predictions incrementally as new subject data arrive, and forecasts over an unbounded future horizon while refining past observations.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 145 volumetric ROI measurements from the subject's first MRI scan, five clinical covariates (age, sex, baseline diagnosis, education years, APOE4 allele status), time in months from the first visit, and the subject's observed follow-up trajectory (h acquisitions at arbitrary, non-uniform time points). It produces a personalized biomarker trajectory as a predictive mean and variance over a continuous time axis, along with an interpretable adaptive shrinkage parameter α that quantifies the relative trust placed in the population model versus the subject-specific model at each personalization step. Notably, the method requires no imputation for irregularly spaced observations and can operate with as few as a single acquisition (where α=1 and only the population model is used).

## Method

The framework comprises two Deep Kernel Gaussian Process models: a population DKGP (p-DKGP) trained on 1,600 subjects that maps 151-dimensional inputs (145 ROIs + covariates + time) through a frozen MLP to a 64-dimensional latent space where an RBF-kernel GP models temporal dependencies, and a subject-specific DKGP (ss-DKGP) that reuses the frozen MLP transformation but trains a new GP on the subject's observed trajectory. The key innovation is the Adaptive Shrinkage Estimator, which learns the optimal linear combination weight α via an XGBoost regressor mapping the five-dimensional input (y_p, y_s, v_p, v_s, T_obs) to α∈[0,1]. Oracle α values for training are computed by minimizing MSE between the combined prediction and ground truth on held-out validation subjects, and the learned function enables incremental personalization: as follow-up observations accumulate, α decreases, shifting trust from the population model toward the subject-specific model. The combined predictive mean and variance are computed as y_c = α·y_p + (1−α)·y_s and v_c = α²·v_p + (1−α)²·v_s, yielding a personalized posterior predictive distribution that adapts with each new acquisition.

## Results

On the main ADNI+BLSA cohort (440 test subjects), pers-DKGP achieves the lowest mean AE across all seven ROI Volume biomarkers, with the largest advantage over LMM in AD subjects (177.66% MAE reduction) and AD Progressors (22.05% reduction). On three external studies, pers-DKGP attains Mean AE of 0.197±0.009 (AIBL), 0.259±0.006 (OASIS), and 0.139±0.004 (PreventAD), consistently outperforming all baselines. The adaptive shrinkage estimator (XGBoost) achieves R² > 0.67 across all seven ROIs in predicting the optimal α, with the best R² of 0.830 for Amygdala R.

## Baselines & Comparisons

pers-DKGP outperforms all four baselines (LMM, GAM, Deep Regression, DME) across every progression status, covariate subgroup, and external study. Against LMM, the gap is largest in non-linear trajectories: 177.66% MAE reduction in AD subjects and 29.78% even in stable Healthy Controls, reflecting LMM's inability to model non-linear volume changes. Against GAM, pers-DKGP achieves Mean AE of 0.145 vs 0.198 in females and 0.120 vs 0.175 in subjects with ≥16 years of education. Against DME, the advantage is most pronounced in APOE4 homozygotes (0.142 vs 0.210 Mean AE), where DME's shared deep mean function struggles with high-dimensional personalization. Against Deep Regression, pers-DKGP yields 0.130 vs 0.192 Mean AE in APOE4 non-carriers, while Deep Regression produces non-smooth, biologically implausible trajectories. On all three external studies (AIBL, OASIS, PreventAD), pers-DKGP achieves the lowest Mean AE with the narrowest confidence intervals, confirming generalizability across demographics and follow-up intervals.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the authors' own prior Deep Kernel Gaussian Process (DKGP) framework (Tassopoulou et al., 2022) by adding a personalization layer: an Adaptive Shrinkage Estimator that fuses a population-level DKGP with a subject-specific DKGP via a learned, interpretable weight α. The shrinkage concept is borrowed from classical Bayesian/penalized estimation (James & Stein, 1961; Shou et al., 2014) and adapted to the deep kernel setting, while the overall architecture builds on Deep Kernel Learning (Wilson et al., 2015). The paper departs from prior longitudinal biomarker prediction methods (LMM, GAM, manifold learning, Gaussian process disease progression models) by handling high-dimensional multivariate imaging inputs, randomly-timed observations without imputation, and unbounded future prediction horizons, and it contrasts with the Deep Mixed Effects approach (Chung et al., 2019) by using a frozen shared transformation Φ and a learned shrinkage rather than an end-to-end LSTM+GP hybrid.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical, interpretable tool for personalized neuroimaging biomarker forecasting that generalizes across three external clinical studies with diverse demographics and follow-up intervals, making it relevant for clinical trial design and disease progression monitoring in Alzheimer's research. The SHAP-based explainability of the shrinkage parameter (T_obs as dominant feature, α decreasing with more observations) provides clinical transparency absent in black-box deep learning baselines. Key limitations include the independence assumption in the combined variance calculation (mitigated but not fully resolved) and the restriction to univariate biomarker trajectories; extending to multivariate joint trajectories and tightening uncertainty quantification are identified as open directions.
