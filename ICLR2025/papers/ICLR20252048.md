## Problem

Deployed classifiers fail catastrophically on out-of-distribution and adversarially crafted inputs, yet existing OOD detection methods (e.g., KNN, MSP) lack formal probabilistic guarantees, and adversarial defenses typically require retraining or additional attack-specific information. No prior approach provides a single, training-free abstaining mechanism that provably rejects both OOD and adversarial samples under a unified statistical framework.

## Input / Output

Given a pre-trained teacher (ResNet, VGG, or ViT) and a test image, SPADE computes the normalized latent embedding h(x)/‖h(x)‖, retrieves the k-th nearest training neighbor per class via Faiss L2 search, and outputs either the teacher's class prediction or an abstain decision based on a user-specified confidence level τ. The OOD probability score is the minimum of per-class GEV CDF values evaluated at the corresponding k-NN distances, yielding a calibrated rejection probability rather than a raw distance threshold.

## Method

SPADE models the distribution of k-th nearest-neighbor latent distances per class using Generalized Extreme Value (GEV) distributions, fitted via the Peak Over Threshold (POT) method with a Generalized Pareto tail and maximum-likelihood estimation. The OOD test (Def. 4) declares a sample extreme if it is simultaneously extreme under every per-class GEV model, and the abstaining classifier (Def. 5) rejects samples whose distance to the predicted class's k-NN exceeds the GEV quantile G^(c)⁻¹(1−τ). The key theoretical contribution (Theorems 1–2) shows that under a global or local Lipschitz bound K on the latent embedding, any adversarial perturbation with amplitude ε below a closed-form lower bound involving the inter-class and intra-class GEV quantiles is provably rejected with probability 1−τ. This yields a single, agnostic detection mechanism for both OOD and adversarial threats without retraining, additional data, or attack-specific knowledge.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K OOD detection (ResNet teacher), SPADE achieves AUC scores of 61.91–95.86 and FPR95 of 24.63–85.27 across near- and far-OOD datasets, ranking 2nd overall behind KNN (AUC 62.80–96.06, FPR95 23.39–84.08). On adversarial sample detection (CIFAR-10, FGSM, ε=0.001–0.004), SPADE achieves the best average AUC of 86.10, outperforming KNN (85.79), CIDER (85.52), and MSP (81.96). On CIFAR-100 adversarial detection, SPADE leads with average AUC 84.50 and FPR95 52.72, beating KNN (83.72/56.00) and CIDER (82.90/63.06). SPADE also outperforms CIDER on all OOD detection benchmarks across ResNet, VGG, and ViT teachers on CIFAR-10.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPADE is compared against five baselines: MSP, ODIN, MDS, KNN, and CIDER. On OOD detection (ImageNet-1K), SPADE is slightly outperformed by KNN on most datasets (e.g., Textures: SPADE AUC 95.86 vs. KNN 96.06; FPR95 24.63 vs. 23.39) but clearly beats MSP, ODIN, and MDS across all near- and far-OOD benchmarks. On adversarial detection (CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, FGSM), SPADE achieves the best AUC at every perturbation amplitude, edging out KNN by ~0.3 AUC points on CIFAR-10 (86.10 vs. 85.79 avg) and by ~0.8 points on CIFAR-100 (84.50 vs. 83.72 avg), while substantially outperforming CIDER (by ~1.6 AUC on CIFAR-100) and MSP (by ~2.5 AUC on CIFAR-10). On FPR95 for adversarial detection, KNN is marginally better on CIFAR-10 (54.21 vs. 54.43) while SPADE is better on CIFAR-100 (52.72 vs. 56.00). Against CIDER specifically, SPADE dominates on all OOD detection tasks across all three teacher architectures (ResNet, VGG, ViT) on CIFAR-10, with the gap being largest for the ViT teacher (e.g., Textures: SPADE AUC 99.97 vs. CIDER 71.06). The authors attribute KNN's slight OOD edge to a better bias-variance trade-off, since SPADE's per-class GEV models are trained on one to two orders of magnitude fewer samples than KNN's global distance threshold.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPADE extends the distance-based OOD detection lineage (KNN, CIDER) by replacing the raw k-th nearest-neighbor distance threshold with a Generalized Extreme Value model fitted via Peak-Over-Threshold, thereby converting a heuristic distance test into a probabilistic one with a principled confidence parameter. The theoretical backbone draws on the Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko framework and the Pickands–Balkema–de Haan theorem, which had seen limited use in machine learning (mainly anomaly detection). The key departure from prior OOD/adversarial detection work is the introduction of an abstaining classifier with provable rejection guarantees: under a Lipschitz assumption on the latent embedding, the paper derives explicit lower bounds on the perturbation amplitude needed to evade detection (Theorems 1–3), something no prior distance-based or score-based OOD method provides.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The provable adversarial-rejection guarantee is the paper's most distinctive contribution: it shifts the question from 'can we detect adversarial samples empirically?' to 'what perturbation magnitude is certifiably undetectable?', offering a safety-relevant certificate for high-stakes deployments. A key limitation is the dependence on the (global or local) Lipschitz constant of the teacher's embedding, which can be large for deep networks, potentially rendering the bound vacuous; the authors mitigate this with local Lipschitz constants but do not fully resolve the gap. Open directions include applying the GEV framework to other OOD criteria (e.g., MSP's score margin), jointly optimizing the embedding's Lipschitz constant during training, and ultimately certifying safe behaviors in latent space as a step toward neural-network certification.
