## Problem

Adversarial training suffers from a robustness-accuracy trade-off, and existing invariance regularization methods (TRADES, LBGAT, ARREST) that aim to mitigate it still sacrifice clean accuracy. This paper identifies two previously unaddressed root causes: a 'gradient conflict' where the invariance loss pushes the model toward non-discriminative directions, and a 'mixture distribution problem' where shared BatchNorm layers fail to properly normalize the diverged clean and adversarial input distributions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251617/cropped_page2_idx5.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 160×160 for Imagenette) and outputs a class label over K classes. During training, each image is processed in two forms: the clean image x and its PGD-generated adversarial counterpart x' (with L∞ perturbation budget ε=8/255), both passing through the same CNN backbone but with separate BatchNorm layers. At inference, a single set of BNs handles both clean and adversarial inputs, requiring no test-time oracle.

## Method

AR-AT proposes an asymmetric representation-regularized adversarial training framework with three key components. First, a stop-gradient operation makes the invariance loss asymmetric—only minimizing Dist(z', sg(z))—so that adversarial representations are 'purified' toward clean ones without corrupting the clean representations, resolving the gradient conflict. Second, a predictor MLP h maps adversarial representations z' to predict clean representations z, acting as a stabilizer that absorbs iteration-to-iteration variations in adversarial features and reduces gradient norm fluctuations. Third, a split-BatchNorm structure uses separate BN parameters for clean and adversarial inputs during training, eliminating the mixture distribution problem while sharing all other weights. The method also employs auto-balancing of adversarial/clean loss weights based on previous-epoch accuracy and multi-level regularization across all ReLU outputs in the final network stage.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with WRN-34-10, AR-AT achieves 90.89% clean accuracy and 50.77% robust accuracy (AutoAttack), yielding a sum of 141.66, outperforming LBGAT (140.75) and ARREST (140.44). With stochastic weight averaging (SWA), AR-AT+SWA further improves to 90.06% clean and 54.03% robust (sum 144.09), the best among all compared methods. On CIFAR-100 with WRN-34-10, AR-AT+SWA reaches 72.37% clean and 27.31% robust (sum 99.69), surpassing LBGAT (95.09) and ARREST (97.37). On Imagenette with ResNet-18, AR-AT achieves 88.66% clean and 59.28% robust (sum 147.94), outperforming all baselines including MART (143.96) and AT (136.83).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AR-AT consistently outperforms standard AT (Madry et al., 2018) by 4–12 points in clean accuracy and 3–5 points in robust accuracy across all settings. Against TRADES (Zhang et al., 2019), AR-AT gains 3–13 points in clean accuracy while maintaining comparable or slightly lower robust accuracy, yielding a substantially higher sum (e.g., 141.66 vs. 136.08 on CIFAR-10/WRN-34-10). Compared to MART (Wang et al., 2019), AR-AT improves clean accuracy by 3–13 points with comparable robustness. Against the KD-based LBGAT (Cui et al., 2021), AR-AT achieves higher clean accuracy (90.89 vs. 88.19 on CIFAR-10/WRN-34-10) with slightly lower robust accuracy (50.77 vs. 52.56), but a higher sum; with SWA, AR-AT+SWA surpasses LBGAT on both metrics (54.03 vs. 52.56 robust). Against ARREST (Suzuki et al., 2023), AR-AT matches or exceeds clean accuracy (90.89 vs. 90.24) and achieves higher robust accuracy (50.77 vs. 50.20) on CIFAR-10/WRN-34-10. In the broader comparison (Tables 11–12), AR-AT+SWA achieves the highest sum (144.09 on CIFAR-10/WRN-34-10) among all 12 compared methods including AWP, S²O, TRADES+AWP+Rand, and AT+HF, though TRADES+AWP+Rand (143.20) and AT+HF (143.11) remain close competitors on robust accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on PGD-based adversarial training (Madry et al., 2018) and invariance regularization (TRADES, Zhang et al., 2019), but shifts the regularization from logits to intermediate representations and introduces an asymmetric structure with stop-gradient and a predictor MLP (inspired by SimSiam). The split-BN component draws on the multi-BN idea from MBN-AT (Xie & Yuille, 2019) and AdvProp (Xie et al., 2020) but applies it exclusively during training, eliminating the need for test-time oracle selection. A key conceptual departure is the paper's re-interpretation of KD-based defenses (LBGAT, ARREST): the authors argue their effectiveness stems from implicitly resolving the same gradient-conflict and mixture-distribution issues that AR-AT addresses explicitly within a single model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a diagnostic framework—gradient conflict and BN mixture distribution—that explains *why* invariance regularization in adversarial training underperforms, offering practitioners a principled way to design better regularization rather than relying on heuristic tuning. The split-BN technique is simple and training-only, making it more practical than prior multi-BN approaches that require test-time model selection. However, the method's reliance on BatchNorm limits its applicability to Transformer architectures that use LayerNorm, and the choice of which layers to regularize remains heuristic. The connection to KD-based defenses opens an interesting question: whether the explicit single-model formulation can always match the implicit separation that a frozen teacher provides, particularly at scale.
