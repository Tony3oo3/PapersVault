## Problem

Existing multimodal benchmarks are limited to single- or dual-modality tasks (e.g., image+text), failing to capture the cross-modal reasoning demands of real-world inputs that combine text, image, audio, and video simultaneously. This gap is compounded by observed inconsistencies in OLMs: the same question rendered as text, image, audio, or video elicits different reasoning behaviors and answers from models like Gemini-1.5-Flash, yet no prior benchmark quantitatively measures this omni-modal discrepancy.

## Input / Output

Each question is a 10-option multiple-choice item from MMLU-Pro, presented in one of several modality configurations: plain text, a PIL-rendered image (white background, black text, with a Unicode-to-LaTeX lookup table for math symbols), a TTS audio clip (with equations first converted to spoken form via Gemini-1.5-Pro), a 1 FPS video at 300×100 pixels showing one word per frame, or interleaved combinations such as video+audio or image+audio. The CoT instruction ('Think step by step then output the answer in the format of "The answer is (X)"') is held in text form by default but can itself be converted to image, audio, or video. Under ETA prompting, the model first transcribes the non-text input and then reasons over the extracted text, so the output reflects both OCR/transcription fidelity and text reasoning.

## Method

The core technical contribution is Omnify!, a programmatic pipeline that translates a single text question into multiple modalities while preserving identical information content, enabling controlled cross-modal comparison. Text-to-image uses PIL to render text on a white canvas; text-to-audio applies a two-step process (Gemini-1.5-Pro converts LaTeX equations to spoken-language equivalents, then Google TTS synthesizes audio); text-to-video uses OpenCV to assemble one-word-per-frame clips at 1 FPS. The benchmark comprises two complementary subsets: Omni×R_SYNTH (1,400 questions sampled from 14 MMLU-Pro categories, chosen to minimize data contamination since MMLU-Pro postdates Gemini-1.5-Pro-001) and Omni×R_REAL (100 YouTube video clips from MIT OpenCourse and similar channels in math, physics, chemistry, and coding, manually curated by two annotators over ~60 hours to include key frames, exclude answers, and retain misleading frames). Evaluation employs both standard 0-shot CoT prompting and the Extract-Then-Answer (ETA) strategy, with answer extraction performed by the model itself rather than regex to avoid parsing artifacts.

## Results

On Omni×R_SYNTH (MMLU-Pro based), text modality yields the highest accuracy across all models, ranging from 69.9% (Gemini-1.5-Flash) to 77.7% (Claude-Opus). Performance drops sharply for non-text modalities: image accuracy falls by 11.4–62.6 points (e.g., Claude-Haiku drops from 72.5% to 9.9%), and video accuracy drops by 18.4–54.8 points (e.g., Gemini-1.5-Flash drops from 69.9% to 15.1%). On the mixed-modality Video+Audio setting, Gemini-1.5-Pro drops to 40.1% (−37.4) and Gemini-1.5-Flash to 25.9% (−44.0) relative to text. On Omni×R_REAL, Gemini-1.5-Pro leads with 86% text, 78% image, 71% audio, and 64% video, while open-source models (Qwen-72B, Reka-Flash, VideoLLaMA-2) achieve at most 79% text and ≤27% video.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among proprietary OLMs, Gemini-1.5-Pro is the most versatile, being the only model evaluated on all four modalities (text/image/audio/video) and achieving the best or near-best scores in each (77.5/57.3/56.6/36.3). GPT-4o outperforms Gemini on image (60.1 vs. 57.3) and video (53.1 vs. 36.3) under CoT prompting, but GPT-4o-mini surprisingly surpasses GPT-4o on video with ETA prompting (59.3 vs. 25.0), likely due to GPT-4o's failure to extract text from video in 46.8% of samples. Claude models are the weakest on image (Haiku: 9.9%, Sonnet: 18.8%) and lack audio/video support entirely. On Omni×R_REAL, Gemini-1.5-Pro leads at 86% text and 78% image, while GPT-4o is competitive at 85% text and 79% image; Claude-Haiku lags significantly at 65% text and 33% image. Open-source models (Qwen-72B, Reka-Flash, VideoLLaMA-2) trail proprietary models substantially, with video accuracy capped at 11.0% (Qwen-72B) on synthetic and 27% (Qwen-72B) on real data, confirming a large gap between open and closed OLMs on cross-modal reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multimodal evaluation landscape beyond the dual-modality paradigm (image+text, video+text) that dominates existing benchmarks like MMMU, MVBench, and ClothoAQA, by introducing the first systematic evaluation of reasoning across three or more modalities simultaneously (e.g., video + audio + text). Rather than building on a specific prior method, it repurposes the well-established MMLU-Pro text benchmark and applies a simple programmatic conversion pipeline (Omnify!) using PIL, TTS, and OpenCV to generate omni-modality variants, deliberately avoiding generative models to preserve information fidelity. The Extract-Then-Answer (ETA) prompting strategy is a novel evaluation technique that exploits the synthetic data's known structure, serving as a diagnostic tool to separate true cross-modal reasoning from mere text extraction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides the first quantitative measurement of omni-modal behavior discrepancy, revealing that all current SOTA OLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini-1.5, Claude-3) suffer dramatic performance drops (up to 55 points) when the same question is presented in non-text modalities, and that smaller models often abandon reasoning entirely for video/audio inputs. The finding that ETA prompting rescues performance on synthetic data but fails on real-world data is a critical diagnostic: it suggests models are not performing genuine cross-modal integration but rather falling back on text extraction, a shortcut that breaks down under natural noise. Open questions include whether targeted training on cross-modal alignment can close the gap, whether the synthetic-to-real transfer gap is fundamental or an artifact of the simple rendering approach, and how to design training objectives that enforce modality-invariant reasoning.
