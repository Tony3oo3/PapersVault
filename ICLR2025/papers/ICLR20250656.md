## Problem

Existing example-based prediction explanation methods for neural classifiers bridge training and test data through model parameters or latent representations, leading to either heavy computational overhead (e.g., Hessian inversion in Influence Functions) or coarse-grained, class-level rather than instance-level explanations (e.g., RPS). This paper identifies that Kernelized Stein Discrepancy (KSD) uniquely defines a closed-form, model-dependent pairwise data correlation that has never been exploited for prediction explanation, offering a path to fine-grained, efficient, and faithful explanations without parameter perturbation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a trained neural classifier (ResNet-18 backbone), a test image (32×32×3 for CIFAR-10/SVHN, 128×128×3 for medical datasets), and the full training dataset with labels. Since the test point's ground-truth label is unavailable, the model's predicted class (one-hot encoded) substitutes for it, forming a complete data point (x_t, ŷ_t). The per-sample cache is bounded by the data dimension (m+k for m input features and k classes) rather than the model parameter size, and the score functions can be pre-computed for the entire training set. A variant (HD-Explain*) replaces raw features with last-layer representations to match the operating regime of baselines like RPS.

## Method

HD-Explain exploits the KSD kernel κ_θ(a,b) = A_θ^a A_θ^b k(a,b), where the Stein operator A_θ encodes the trained model's score function. Under the relaxation P_θ(x) ≡ P_D(x) (uniform over data points), the score function ∇_{x,y} log P_θ(x,y) reduces to a concatenation [∇_x log f_θ(x)_y ‖ log f_θ(x)], combining the input gradient of the log-probability of the true class with the full log-probability vector. The kernel decomposes into four interpretable terms: a raw-data similarity bias (kernel Hessian), a model-context similarity (dot product of score functions capturing input-gradient and prediction similarity), and two cross-alignment terms reflecting how a nearby training point would shift the prediction. For a test point, the method simply computes κ_θ between (x_t, ŷ_t) and every training sample, then returns the top-k samples with the highest kernel values—requiring no optimization sub-routine, no Hessian approximation, and no access to the training process. The base kernel (RBF, Linear, or IMQ) is user-selectable, and the method can be applied at any layer of the network to trace prediction evolution.

## Results

On the data-augmentation-based retrieval task (Noise Injection), HD-Explain achieves a Hit Rate above 80% across all four image datasets, while all three baselines (Influence Function, RPS, TracIn*) remain at or below 10%. For the Coverage metric, baselines produce only 10%–50% unique explanation samples across the test set, whereas HD-Explain yields substantially higher coverage, reflecting finer-grained, instance-level explanations. In terms of execution time, HD-Explain* (last-layer variant) is the second-fastest method after RPS and faster than both TracIn* and Influence Function, while full-model HD-Explain outperforms IF on all datasets and TracIn* on CIFAR-10.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HD-Explain decisively outperforms all three baselines on Hit Rate, achieving >80% retrieval accuracy versus ≤10% for Influence Function, RPS, and TracIn* under both Noise Injection and Horizontal Flip augmentations. On Coverage, HD-Explain produces substantially more diverse explanations than the baselines (which are limited to 10%–50% unique samples), confirming that baselines default to class-level prototypes rather than instance-level explanations. RPS is the fastest method in wall-clock time but is the most limited in explanation granularity, often returning identical explanation sets for different test points of the same class. Influence Function is the slowest due to iterative Hessian-vector product approximations, and TracIn* sits in between. HD-Explain* (last-layer variant) matches the computational setting of the baselines while still achieving the highest Hit Rate and second-best execution time, and full-model HD-Explain scales to the entire network without the parameter-dimension bottleneck that constrains all three baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

HD-Explain repurposes Kernelized Stein Discrepancy (Liu et al., 2016), previously used for goodness-of-fit testing and particle filtering, as a mechanism for defining model-conditioned pairwise data correlation in prediction explanation. It adopts the marginal-distribution-equality relaxation from Jitkrittum et al. (2020) to make KSD applicable to conditional predictive models with discrete labels. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant influence-function lineage (Koh & Liang, 2017; Pruthi et al., 2020; Yeh et al., 2018) by eliminating the model-parameter perturbation as an intermediate step in the influence chain, instead computing a closed-form kernel directly between data points conditioned on the trained model's score function.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method offers a practical efficiency advantage—memory and computation scale with data dimension rather than model parameter count—making whole-model explanation feasible on a single GPU where Influence Functions are not. However, the approach's sensitivity to raw input features (low-level visual attributes) and its dependence on model prediction quality limit its robustness, particularly for low-confidence or misclassified predictions. Open questions include how to incorporate theoretically rigorous discrete-distribution Stein operators without the computational overhead the authors avoid, and whether the evaluation protocol (retrieving augmented training points) fully captures real-world explanation quality. Domain expert validation for the medical datasets remains an acknowledged gap.
