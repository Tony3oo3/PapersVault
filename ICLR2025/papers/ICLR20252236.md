## Problem

Traditional evaluation metrics for DNNs forecasting stochastic complex systems (e.g., wildfire, epidemic, market dynamics) only measure fidelity to a single observed outcome (F2R), making it impossible to distinguish whether a prediction mismatch stems from inherent stochastic variability or from the DNN having learned an entirely wrong stochastic process. This paper identifies that no existing metric can test whether a DNN has captured the underlying stochastic process, and proposes Fidelity to Stochastic Process (F2SP) as a new evaluation criterion targeting the Statistic-GT—the expected behavior across all possible outcomes from identical initial conditions.

## Input / Output

For synthetic benchmarks, the DNN observes t_obs frames (10 for forest fire, 10 for host-pathogen/stock market) of a 64×64 binary grid indicating target-state presence and autoregressively predicts t_pred future frames (50 for forest fire, 20 for others) as per-cell probability maps in [0,1]. For the real-world NDWS wildfire dataset, input is a 1 km-resolution fire mask plus 11 spatially varying observational variables (elevation, wind, temperature, humidity, precipitation, drought index, vegetation, population density, energy), and output is a binary fire-spread probability map for the next day. Evaluation produces three complementary scores: ECE (testing F2SP), AUC-PR (testing F2R), and MSE (balancing both).

## Method

The DNN architecture is a ConvLSTM-CA—a single-layer ConvLSTM with 3×3 kernels sandwiched between a 3×3 encoder and 1×1 decoder that preserves spatial dimensions to maintain per-agent identity—trained with BCE loss on 700 simulations per S-Level. The core technical contribution is an evaluation framework: the authors formalize Statistic-GT as the joint grid-level probability distribution derived from 1000 Monte Carlo simulations sharing identical initial conditions, then prove that ECE (which bins predicted probabilities and compares bin-mean predictions to empirical positive fractions) satisfies the necessary condition for testing F2SP using only Observed-GT, while classification metrics (AUC-PR) and proper scoring rules (MSE) fail this condition because they exclusively or partially test F2R. The binning mechanism in ECE permits micro-level mismatches between individual cell predictions and the single observed realization, as long as the aggregate calibration matches the Statistic-GT, giving ECE a unique diagonal pattern in train-vs-test S-Level matrices that baselines lack. Empirical validation across three synthetic systems (forest fire, host-pathogen, stock market) at controlled S-Levels and a real-world wildfire case study confirm ECE's stability in long-horizon prediction and its ability to resolve metric conflicts in model ranking.

## Results

In the synthetic S-Level sensitivity experiments (Figure 3), ECE is the only metric exhibiting a clear diagonal pattern—low values when train and test S-Levels match, high values otherwise—while AUC-PR and MSE degrade uniformly regardless of S-Level alignment. In long-horizon forest-fire prediction (Figure 4), ECE remains stable (~0.01–0.02) over 50 timesteps for the DNN trained on the matching S-Level (20), whereas AUC-PR and MSE both decline steadily. On the real-world NDWS dataset (Table 2), the Conv-AE model achieves overall AUC-PR = 0.247, MSE = 0.012, and ECE = 0.012; when stratified by Dice Coefficient, ECE improves to 0.005 at the lowest DC bin (0.0–0.1) while AUC-PR drops to 0.044, demonstrating ECE's complementary sensitivity to stochasticity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between evaluation metrics (AUC-PR, MSE, ECE) rather than between competing models, with the key finding that ECE uniquely captures fidelity to the stochastic process (F2SP) while AUC-PR and MSE do not. In the S-Level sensitivity matrix (Figure 3), ECE shows a sharp diagonal (low error only when train/test S-Levels match), whereas AUC-PR shows no diagonal structure and MSE shows only a partial diagonal weakened by its refinement/sharpness component. In long-horizon prediction (Figure 4), ECE stays flat for the correctly-trained model while AUC-PR and MSE both degrade over 50 timesteps, with AUC-PR completely failing to distinguish the two models. On the real-world NDWS dataset, the paper re-evaluates Huot et al.'s Conv-AE (which reported AUC-PR = 0.284) and finds that ECE (0.012 overall) reveals the model is well-calibrated at probability extremes even where AUC-PR (0.247) appears poor, particularly at low Dice Coefficient bins where ECE drops to 0.005 while AUC-PR falls to 0.044—demonstrating that the model captures the stochastic process even when it cannot replicate the single observed outcome.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Expected Calibration Error (Naeini et al., 2015) from its traditional role in measuring DNN output calibration to a fundamentally new use: testing whether a DNN has learned the underlying stochastic process (Statistic-GT) of a complex system, using only a single observed realization. It builds on the ConvLSTM architecture (Shi et al., 2015) adapted for cellular automata prediction and on agent-based simulation frameworks (NetLogo) to generate controlled stochastic benchmarks. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant F2R evaluation paradigm (matching Observed-GT via AUC-PR, MSE, or proper scoring rules) by arguing that these metrics conflate stochastic variability with model failure, and instead proposes F2SP as a complementary criterion grounded in the Brier Score decomposition (Blattenberger & Lad, 1985) and proper scoring rule theory (Gneiting & Raftery, 2007).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a practical disconnect in stochastic system forecasting: models that qualitatively 'look right' (as in the NDWS wildfire case) receive poor scores under standard metrics, while the field lacks a principled way to distinguish stochastic mismatch from genuine model failure. The proposed F2SP framework and the finding that ECE uniquely exhibits diagonal sensitivity to S-Level (across three systems and four architectures) offer a concrete, low-cost diagnostic tool. Key limitations include ECE's lower discriminative power among well-calibrated models, its dependence on sufficient sample size for convergence, and its restriction to binary/discrete prediction tasks. Open questions include extending F2SP to regression and multivariate continuous settings, tracking ECE over long horizons on real data (blocked by NDWS's next-day-only design), and whether improved calibration estimators (Gruber & Buettner, 2022) can reduce the sample-size requirement.
