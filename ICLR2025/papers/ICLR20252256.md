## Problem

Existing articulated object manipulation environments (PartNet-Mobility, GAPartNet, DoorGym, UniDoorManip) model only simple, visually inferable mechanisms where the complete manipulation sequence can be deduced from appearance alone. Real-world objects like safes, pressure cookers, and microwaves have hidden internal states (lock status, rotation direction, required angles) that force the robot to adapt its policy through trial-and-error feedback rather than one-shot visual inference. This paper builds an environment with 5 types of such adaptive mechanisms and proposes a policy learning framework that handles the resulting multimodal, history-dependent action distributions.

## Input / Output

The policy consumes a 4096-point third-view partial point cloud (cropped and downsampled via Farthest Point Sampling), the robot's proprioception state (end-effector pose, joint angles, velocities), and a history window of 4 past observations and 4 past actions. It outputs a 2-step end-effector goal pose trajectory using a 6D rotation representation, with a prediction horizon of 4. Crucially, only a sub-sequence of the predicted actions is executed per step to enable high-frequency closed-loop adaptation as the robot discovers the object's internal state.

## Method

The key insight is collecting adaptive demonstrations that include a single failure trial and its recovery (e.g., pulling a locked door, then switching to turn the key), rather than only optimal trajectories under full observation, so the learned policy encodes the transition from multimodal to unimodal action distributions as feedback accumulates. The policy is a DDPM-based diffusion model with a UNet backbone that denoises Gaussian noise into action sequences, conditioned jointly on observation history (encoded by PointNet++) and action history, which distinguishes it from vanilla Diffusion Policy. Rule-based expert policies generate these adaptive demonstrations by simulating the robot's partial-observation decision process in IsaacGym. An ablation (Table 4) confirms that exactly one adaptive trial per demonstration is optimal—additional repeated trials increase distributional complexity without adding new scenarios and degrade performance. Training uses Adam at 1e-4 learning rate for 500 epochs with EMA, a squared-cosine noise scheduler over 100 diffusion timesteps, and 6D rotation representation to stabilize end-effector pose learning.

## Results

On the ADAMANIP simulation benchmark (9 categories, 277 objects), the proposed adaptive diffusion policy achieves success rates of 95.07% (Bottle), 99.05% (Pen), 98.33% (Coffee Maker), 97.14% (Window), 61.25% (Door), 100.00% (Lamp), 94.33% (Microwave), 88.75% (Safe), and 82.53% (Pressure Cooker). In real-world experiments on a Franka Emika Panda arm, the policy achieves 8/10 (Open Bottle), 7/10 (Open Microwave), 5/10 (Open Safe), and 5/10 (Open Pressure Cooker) success rates. The ablation without adaptive demonstrations (Ours w/o adaptive) drops to 38.75% on Door and 58.00% on Lamp, confirming the critical role of failure-recovery trajectories in training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all four baselines across all 9 categories. Against the strongest baseline DP3 (3D Diffusion Policy), the gains are most pronounced on mechanism-heavy categories: Door (61.25% vs 35.71%), Lamp (100% vs 62.00%), Safe (88.75% vs 58.75%), and Pressure Cooker (82.53% vs 53.57%), reflecting the advantage of adaptive demonstrations with failure-recovery trajectories. Against ACT, improvements are similarly large on Door (61.25% vs 28.57%) and Lamp (100% vs 59.00%), where multimodal action distributions are hardest to model. Affordance-based methods (VAT-MART, AdaAfford) and the sampling-based method perform substantially worse, with VAT-MART achieving only 1.25% on Door and 6.00% on Lamp, confirming that open-loop trajectory prediction cannot handle the adaptive multi-step mechanisms. The ablation (Ours w/o adaptive) shows that removing failure-recovery demonstrations costs 22.5 points on Door and 42 points on Lamp, isolating the contribution of the adaptive demonstration collection pipeline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the diffusion-based imitation learning lineage (Diffusion Policy, 3D Diffusion Policy, DDPM) to the domain of articulated object manipulation, but its primary departure is in the data collection strategy: rather than collecting only optimal success trajectories as prior imitation learning methods do, it introduces adaptive demonstrations that include failure-recovery sequences. On the environment side, it builds on IsaacGym and prior articulated object benchmarks (PartNet-Mobility, GAPartNet, DoorGym, UniDoorManip) but explicitly addresses their limitation of modeling only simple, visually-inferable manipulation mechanisms. The combination of a mechanism-rich simulation environment with a diffusion policy conditioned on action history is the paper's core methodological contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a much-needed benchmark for adaptive manipulation where internal object states (lock state, rotation direction, required angle) are not visually observable, filling a gap that prior environments left open. The ablation showing that exactly one adaptive trial per state is optimal—more trials degrade performance by over-complicating the distribution to model—is a practical insight for demonstration collection in imitation learning. However, the real-world success rates (50–80%) and the limited scale (9 categories, 277 objects) suggest the sim-to-real gap and dataset diversity remain open challenges. Future work on deformable objects and larger-scale mechanism diversity would strengthen the platform's utility.
