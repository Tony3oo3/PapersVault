## Problem

Causal structure learning from interventional data remains practically challenging, with recent benchmarks showing that established causal discovery methods underperform even when large numbers of single-variable intervention samples are available. This paper tackles the specific subproblem of recovering the causal order (topological ordering) from single-variable interventional data, showing that such datasets contain a wealth of causal information that can be extracted under a light assumption called ε-interventional faithfulness, with theoretical guarantees that hold at scale.

## Input / Output

The method consumes i.i.d. samples from the observational joint distribution and from single-variable interventional distributions (one per intervened variable), along with the set of intervened variable indices and a threshold ε. Distances are computed between the marginal distribution of each variable under the observational setting versus each interventional setting, using the 1-Wasserstein distance estimated from finite samples (converging at O(√(ln n)/√n)). The output is a single permutation of the d variables representing the causal order, from which a fully connected DAG can be constructed; it does not directly output edge weights or a sparse graph.

## Method

The core contribution is a score on causal orders that sums, over all pairs (i,j) where i is intervened and π(i)<π(j), the Wasserstein distance between the marginal of j under the observational and do(X_i) distributions minus ε, with a rescaling term c·d·1[distance>ε] that forces correctly-ordered pairs with strong effects to dominate. Under ε-interventional faithfulness (all directed paths induce marginal distribution shifts above ε), the paper proves the score's optimum recovers a valid topological order when all variables are intervened, and derives upper bounds on expected D_top error for partial interventions that scale as O(d) for constant average degree. The INTERSORT algorithm optimizes this score in two stages: SORTRANKING greedily constructs an initial DAG by sorting all candidate edges by their distance score and adding them in descending order while preserving acyclicity, then LOCALSEARCH performs a greedy neighborhood search over permutations (moving one variable at a time) to refine the order until no improvement is found.

## Results

INTERSORT is evaluated on the D_top (top-order divergence) metric, where lower is better, across four simulated data domains (linear, random Fourier features, neural network, and single-cell gene regulatory network) with 30 variables and intervention ratios of 25%–100%. INTERSORT outperforms all four baselines on almost all settings, with the only exceptions being GIES in the linear domain and DCDI in the neural network domain at 25% and 50% intervention ratios. On the real-world Sachs et al. (2005) flow cytometry dataset (11 proteins, 5 interventions, 5846 measurements), INTERSORT achieves a D_top of 3 out of 17 true edges. Theoretically, the expected error of the optimal solution scales as O(d) under the ε-interventional faithfulness assumption, with a normalized bound of (1−p_int)²/p_int · [1 − (1−e^{−p_int·c})/(p_int·c)] as d→∞ for constant expected edges per variable c.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares INTERSORT against four baselines: PC (constraint-based, conditional independence tests), GIES (score-based greedy search extended to interventional data with Gaussian BIC), DCDI (continuously differentiable causal discovery leveraging interventional data), and EASE (causal order from extreme tail-values in observational data). INTERSORT outperforms PC and EASE across all four data domains and all intervention ratios, as these methods do not effectively exploit interventional information. GIES outperforms INTERSORT only in the linear domain, where its Gaussian BIC score aligns with the true data-generating process; in RFF, NN, and GRN domains, INTERSORT is superior. DCDI outperforms INTERSORT only in the neural network domain at the lowest intervention ratios (25% and 50%), but INTERSORT surpasses it at 75% and 100% interventions and in all other domains. Overall, INTERSORT makes more efficient use of interventional information compared to all baselines, which is critical in domains where interventions are costly.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the rule-based causal discovery approach of Tian & Pearl (2001) by introducing a score-based formulation over causal orderings, which enables the use of optimization tools and better scalability. It also extends the interventional causal discovery literature (GIES, DCDI) by reducing the problem to recovering a topological order rather than the full graph, and by providing theoretical guarantees for the practically important case where only a subset of variables is intervened on (whereas prior results like Tian & Pearl focus on all variables being intervened). The ε-interventional faithfulness assumption is positioned as a lighter alternative to standard faithfulness, relying on a statistical distance threshold rather than an oracle of dependence, and the use of Wasserstein distance on marginal distributions connects to the CausalBench gene network inference benchmarks (Chevalley et al., 2022, 2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that large-scale single-variable interventional datasets (common in single-cell transcriptomics) contain substantially more causal information than previously exploited, and that a simple score comparing marginal distribution shifts is sufficient to recover causal order with provable O(d) error bounds. Open questions include the scalability of the local search step to very large variable sets, data-dependent selection of ε, and whether active intervention selection policies can tighten the theoretical bounds beyond the uniform-intervention analysis. The framework also opens a path toward downstream tasks like full graph reconstruction from the estimated order and active learning of which variables to intervene on next.
