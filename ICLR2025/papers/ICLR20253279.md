## Problem

LLM-based TTS models like VALL-E rely on autoregressive generation of audio tokens at high frame rates (48 Hz), making minute-long speech synthesis in a single inference step infeasible due to the enormous token sequence length. Simply reducing the frame rate in neural audio codec models causes severe quality degradation and incorrect pronunciation, so a principled way to lower the effective frame rate while preserving reconstruction fidelity is needed.

## Input / Output

The model takes a natural language text prompt and a 3-second reference audio clip from the target speaker as input, and produces a synthesized speech waveform up to 180 seconds long in a single inference pass. The AR model operates on 8 Hz tokens (a 6× reduction from the standard 48 Hz), while the NAR model progressively upsamples through frozen MRVQ sub-modules at 16, 24, and 48 Hz. The system is trained on the newly introduced MinutesSpeech dataset (40k hours of podcast speech, 3–180 s segments) and evaluated on both MinutesSpeech and LibriSpeech test sets.

## Method

HALL-E introduces two complementary post-training components. First, MReQ reorganizes a pre-trained Encodec RVQ module into a hierarchical multi-resolution residual vector quantization (MRVQ) structure of four LRVQ blocks operating at 8, 16, 24, and 48 Hz, trained via teacher-student distillation with feature-level distillation (FLD) and hidden-state reconstruction (HSR) losses to prevent low-frame-rate layers from being ignored. Second, the HALL-E TTS model uses an AR transformer to predict the 8 Hz token sequence (with MusicGen-style delay pattern for multi-layer handling), and a NAR transformer that iteratively predicts higher-rate tokens by leveraging frozen MRVQ sub-modules (sub-encoders, sub-decoders, pre-quantizers, post-quantizers) as intermediate transformations, simplifying the cross-frame-rate prediction problem. Both components are post-trained from a pre-trained VALL-E checkpoint, and the NAR model additionally uses cross-attention to inject text information, avoiding the need for explicit text-audio alignment over long sequences.

## Results

On MinutesSpeech test-90s, HALL-E achieves a WER of 9.79%, surpassing the ground truth (10.30%) and dramatically outperforming VALL-E trained on train-90s (16.14%) and train-28s (39.77%). On test-180s, HALL-E reaches 10.53% WER versus VALL-E's 21.71% (train-180s) and 36.52% (train-54s), while also achieving the highest subjective naturalness (QMOS 3.38) and speaker similarity (SMOS 3.31). HALL-E generates audio approximately 3.4× faster than VALL-E in real-time factor due to the 6× reduction in AR token count (8 Hz vs. 48 Hz frame rate).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against VALL-E (Wang et al., 2023), HALL-E achieves a 4–6× reduction in WER on MinutesSpeech (9.79% vs. 16.14% on test-90s; 10.53% vs. 21.71% on test-180s) and substantially higher subjective naturalness (QMOS 3.35 vs. 2.48 on test-90s) and speaker similarity (SMOS 3.15 vs. 2.36). VALL-E retains an edge on objective speaker similarity (SIM 0.712 vs. 0.685 on test-90s), which the authors attribute to the acoustic information lost at 8 Hz frame rate. Against MaskGCT (Wang et al., 2024, a NAR TTS baseline), HALL-E is clearly superior on the 90-second setting (WER 10.53% vs. 51.12%, UTMOS 3.74 vs. 2.57), while MaskGCT achieves a lower WER on the 30-second setting (7.74% vs. 10.88%) but with worse UTMOS (3.42 vs. 3.61). Overall, HALL-E is the only model that maintains WER below ~11% at both 90 s and 180 s durations in a single inference step.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the VALL-E architecture (Wang et al., 2023) by addressing its fundamental limitation: the high frame rate of the first RVQ layer (48 Hz) makes autoregressive generation of minute-long speech intractable. Rather than simply lowering the frame rate—which the authors show causes catastrophic quality degradation (WER → 100% at 8 Hz in Encodec)—they introduce MReQ, a post-training framework that hierarchically reorganizes the RVQ into multi-resolution blocks via teacher-student distillation, and HALL-E, which exploits the resulting hierarchical token structure by using frozen MRVQ sub-modules within the NAR model. The approach also draws on MusicGen's delay pattern for efficient multi-layer token prediction in the AR model, and is demonstrated on both Encodec and SpeechTokenizer as the underlying NAC.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 40k-hour MinutesSpeech dataset (with professional transcriptions for the test set) fills a critical gap: prior TTS evaluation relied on short LibriSpeech segments (4–10 s), making it impossible to benchmark minute-long synthesis. The finding that naively extending VALL-E's training data to 180 s causes near-total failure (WER 95.46) while the hierarchical 8 Hz approach achieves WER 4.49 underscores that frame-rate reduction is not a data problem but an architectural one. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger spoken language models (AudioLM, SpeechGPT), whether text tokens become the sequence-length bottleneck once audio tokens are compressed, and whether even lower frame rates (4 Hz) can be made viable without the observed WER collapse.
