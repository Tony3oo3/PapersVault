## Problem

Decoder-based LLMs applied to regression tasks face a fundamental tension: autoregressive fine-tuning with cross-entropy treats numeric targets as categorical tokens (ignoring that predicting 11 is worse than 1.1 for a target of 1), while predictive heads deviate from the next-token prediction pre-training objective. No prior work provides a principled training objective that simultaneously respects the LLM's autoregressive pre-training and the numerical structure of regression targets.

## Input / Output

The method takes a natural language input string (e.g., a product review, a pair of sentences, or a user's movie rating history) formatted as a prompt to a decoder-only LLM, and produces a single real-valued prediction (e.g., a 1–5 star rating or a 0–5 similarity score). At inference, the numeric output is computed as the probability-weighted sum over a finite target grid Y_grid (e.g., {1,2,3,4,5}), where each grid element corresponds to one or more vocabulary tokens scored by the model's output distribution at the final position.

## Method

RAFT replaces the standard cross-entropy fine-tuning loss with a squared-error loss computed directly on the RAIL predictor: ℓ_RAFT = (y* − Σ_{y∈Y_grid} p(str(y)|x)·y)², which measures the gap between the target and the model's expected numerical output. The paper proves that minimizing this loss recovers the Bayes-optimal predictor in the population limit (Lemma 3), and that standard cross-entropy fine-tuning combined with either greedy or RAIL decoding can incur arbitrarily large squared error even when the model distribution perfectly matches the true distribution (Lemmas 1–2). At inference, RAFT uses the same RAIL decoding rule (probability-weighted sum over the grid), so training and inference are aligned. The approach is shown to be closely related to a predictive head over the final-token probability vector with fixed weights w_v = float(v), but crucially inherits the pre-trained model's next-token prediction capability at initialization, which ablations confirm is the primary source of its advantage over learned predictive heads.

## Results

RAFT achieves the lowest RMSE across nearly all dataset-model combinations. On Amazon Wireless with Gemma-2 2B, RAFT reaches 0.47 RMSE, outperforming the predictive head (0.51), standard fine-tuning with RAIL decoding (0.67), and standard fine-tuning with greedy decoding (0.70). On STSB with Gemma-2 9B, RAFT achieves 0.51 RMSE versus 0.52 for the predictive head and 0.53 for standard fine-tuning with RAIL. Zero-shot baselines (greedy decoding and RAIL) perform substantially worse, with RMSE ranging from 0.88 to 1.37 across datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RAFT outperforms all autoregressive baselines (zero-shot greedy decoding, zero-shot RAIL, and standard cross-entropy fine-tuning with either greedy or RAIL decoding) by a wide margin across all datasets and model sizes, with the gap being largest on Amazon Music (e.g., 0.47 vs. 0.61 RMSE for standard FT+RAIL on 9B). Against the predictive head baselines from Fernandes et al. (2023) (special-token logit) and Zhuang et al. (2023) (pooled output embeddings), RAFT achieves consistently lower RMSE (e.g., 0.47 vs. 0.51 on Wireless 2B; 0.45 vs. 0.46 on Wireless 9B), though the margin narrows on STSB where the gap is only 0.01–0.02 RMSE. The encoder-based SMART RoBERTa baseline (Jiang et al., 2019) achieves 92.80% Pearson correlation on STSB, while Gemma-2 9B RAFT reaches 94.30% Pearson and 0.51 RMSE, surpassing all encoder-based methods. Ablations confirm that RAFT's advantage over the predictive head stems from alignment with the next-token prediction pre-training objective: when initialized from random weights rather than a pre-trained checkpoint, RAFT no longer outperforms the predictive head, whereas with pre-trained initialization it consistently wins.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends RAIL (Lukasik et al., 2024), which proposed using the Bayes-optimal decision rule at inference time for LLM-based regression, by moving the regression-aware objective into the fine-tuning loss itself (RAFT). It also unifies two previously separate lines of work — autoregressive decoding and predictive head approaches — under a single framework, showing that single-digit RAFT is mathematically close to the final-token logit method but differs in activation, weight structure, and alignment to the next-token prediction pre-training objective. The theoretical analysis (Lemmas 1–3) formalizes why cross-entropy fine-tuning is misaligned with squared-error regression and proves that RAFT's restricted-grid predictor exactly recovers the Bayes-optimal mean.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RAFT provides a principled, theoretically grounded loss for fine-tuning decoder LLMs on regression that respects both the numerical structure of targets and the autoregressive pre-training objective, consistently outperforming both autoregressive and predictive-head baselines across model families and datasets. A key open question highlighted by the ablations is whether the advantage over predictive heads is fundamentally due to pre-training alignment (RAFT loses its edge under random initialization), which limits the method's applicability to settings where no suitable pre-trained checkpoint exists. The authors suggest extensions to time-series forecasting and ordinal regression as natural next steps, and the gap between RAFT and predictive heads narrows at larger model sizes (e.g., 9B), leaving open whether the benefit persists at scale.
