## Problem

Fair representation learning (FRL) methods were originally designed to enforce demographic parity, yet they have been widely applied in performance-sensitive settings (maximising subgroup accuracy on IID or distribution-shifted test data) with contradictory empirical results and no unified theoretical explanation. The paper asks whether FRL is fundamentally valid outside its original group-parity paradigm, revealing that implicit assumptions about train/test distribution relationships and the causal structure of dataset bias determine whether these methods can ever help.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on medical images (chest X-rays, dermatoscopy, fundus) paired with binary sensitive attributes (sex, race, age, skin type), producing binary disease class predictions. The key structural assumption is that the input vector X decomposes into X_Z (features causally driven by the underlying condition Z) and X_A (features causally driven by the sensitive attribute A), with FRL enforcing marginal independence R^FRL ⊥ A at train time. Biased training variants are generated by corrupting images (presentation disparity), dropping positive samples (prevalence disparity), or flipping labels (annotation disparity) for one subgroup, while the test set is always the unbiased original.

## Method

The core contribution is a causal-theoretical analysis rather than a new training algorithm. Using structural causal models, the authors define three fundamental bias mechanisms via d-separation violations of an unbiasedness condition (Y ⊥ X_A | X_Z), then prove that FRL representations cannot simultaneously be effective (removing sensitive information ERM would encode) and harmless (retaining all task-relevant information) when train and test distributions are IID (Proposition 4.8). For the distribution shift paradigm, they propose two hypotheses: FRL validity depends on the causal structure of the bias and on subgroup separability (how easily A is predictable from X_A). Empirically, they validate these results across five medical imaging datasets with ResNet18 backbones, comparing adversarial FRL (Kim et al., 2019) and conditional FRL (Zhao et al., 2020) against ERM under all three bias mechanisms, finding that FRL only improves performance under presentation disparities with high subgroup separability.

## Results

In IID settings (train and test both unbiased), FRL achieves ΔAcc ≤ 0 (i.e., never outperforms ERM) across all 11 dataset-attribute combinations, confirming the theoretical futility result (Proposition 4.8). Under distribution shift (biased train, unbiased test), FRL improves performance only under presentation disparities, and only for dataset-attribute combinations with high subgroup separability; under prevalence and annotation disparities, FRL provides little to no benefit over ERM. A statistically significant monotonic association (Kendall's τ) is found between subgroup separability AUC and the FRL-vs-ERM accuracy gap, with FRL substantially worsening performance for both groups when separability is low.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between FRL methods and unconstrained ERM (Vapnik, 1999) across five medical imaging datasets (CheXpert, MIMIC-CXR, HAM10000, Fitzpatrick17k, PAPILA) with 11 dataset-attribute combinations and 660 training runs. In IID settings, FRL (Kim et al., 2019 adversarial variant) consistently loses or ties ERM, with ΔAcc negative or near zero for both subgroups. Under distribution shift, FRL wins ERM only under presentation disparities with high subgroup separability, where it boosts the disadvantaged group's accuracy; under prevalence and annotation disparities, FRL loses to ERM. The conditional FRL variant (Zhao et al., 2020) reproduces the same qualitative trends in extended experiments (Appendix A.6), confirming that the results are not specific to one FRL implementation. The paper's key contribution is thus not a new method that beats baselines, but a theoretical and empirical demonstration that FRL's validity is highly conditional on the causal structure of bias and the degree of subgroup separability in the data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the FRL framework of Zemel et al. (2013) and the causal decomposition of inputs into task-relevant (X_Z) and sensitive (X_A) features from Jiang & Veitch (2022), while grounding its bias mechanisms in the causal structures for medical imaging proposed by Jones et al. (2024). It departs from prior negative results on FRL performance (McNamara et al., 2019; Zhao & Gordon, 2019) by providing a more general futility theorem (Proposition 4.8) that does not depend on specific bias mechanisms, and by identifying subgroup separability and causal structure of bias as the two key factors governing FRL validity under distribution shift. The paper also organizes the fragmented fairness literature into three distinct paradigms (group parity, IID performance, distribution shift), arguing that much of the apparent contradiction in the literature stems from conflating these paradigms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The futility result provides a theoretical explanation for why FRL methods consistently underperform ERM in standard benchmarks, resolving a long-standing puzzle and cautioning against 'blind' application of invariant representation constraints in performance-sensitive domains like medical diagnosis. The identification of subgroup separability as a practical determinant of FRL validity offers actionable guidance: FRL may help only under presentation disparities with high separability, a narrow condition rarely met in practice. A key open question the paper highlights is the difficulty of proper real-world evaluation under distribution shift, since one needs both a biased training set and an unbiased test set with known causal structure—data that is rarely available. The authors call for standardized evaluation protocols and finer-grained causal analysis of dataset biases as essential next steps for the field.
