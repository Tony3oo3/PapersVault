## Problem

Existing data valuation methods such as Data Shapley assume i.i.d. samples and cannot handle graph-structured data, where nodes contribute to GNN performance through complex interdependencies via message-passing. In particular, unlabeled nodes within the receptive field of labeled nodes also hold intrinsic value, and a single node can make multifaceted contributions by affecting multiple labeled nodes simultaneously. This paper is the first to formally address graph data valuation, quantifying the fine-grained contributions of both labeled and unlabeled nodes and edges to GNN model performance.

## Input / Output

The method takes a training graph with node feature vectors, edge structure, and a subset of labeled nodes, and produces a scalar value for every node and edge in the training graph. Internally, each node is decomposed into multiple 'players' (duplicates) corresponding to its distinct paths within the K-level computation trees of labeled nodes; the PC-Winter value is first computed per player, then aggregated by summing duplicates to yield node-level and edge-level values. The utility function trains an SGC model on a node-induced subgraph and evaluates accuracy on a held-out validation set, with the inductive setting ensuring strict separation between training, validation, and test graphs.

## Method

The paper formulates graph data valuation as a cooperative game whose players are node-duplicates in computation trees, and whose coalition structure exhibits both a Level Coalition Structure (subtrees form nested sub-coalitions) and a Unilateral Dependency Structure (a player's contribution depends on its ancestors). The PC-Winter value averages marginal contributions over only those permutations satisfying a Level Constraint (descendants of any player appear contiguously) and a Precedence Constraint (ancestors precede descendants), which are shown to be exactly generated by DFS pre-order traversals of the contribution tree (Theorems 1–2). For tractability, three approximation strategies are combined in a streaming fashion: Monte Carlo sampling of a subset of permissible permutations, hierarchical truncation that zeroes out marginal contributions from late-visited child subtrees (exploiting neighborhood saturation), and local propagation that restricts feature aggregation to only the partial computation tree of the last-visited labeled node during each DFS traversal.

## Results

In the node-dropping experiment (lower accuracy = better identification of critical nodes), PC-Winter achieves the lowest minimum accuracy across all six datasets, e.g., 0.5990 on Cora, 0.5340 on Amazon-Photo, and 0.4207 on Amazon-Computer, consistently outperforming Data Shapley (0.6200, 0.6255, 0.4891 respectively). In the edge-addition experiment (higher accuracy = better identification of valuable edges), PC-Winter reaches the highest maximum accuracy on all datasets, e.g., 0.7310 on Cora (vs. 0.7140 for LOO) and 0.8145 on Amazon-Computer (vs. 0.7953 for LOO). Notably, on Cora, PC-Winter reaches full-graph performance using only 8% of edges, and with just 10% of PC-Winter-selected edges, accuracy climbs to 72.9%, exceeding the full graph's 71.3%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary methodological baseline is Data Shapley (Ghorbani & Zou, 2019), adapted to graph data by treating both labeled and unlabeled nodes as players. PC-Winter outperforms Data Shapley on all six datasets in the node-dropping experiment, with the largest gaps on Amazon-Photo (0.5340 vs. 0.6255 minimum accuracy, a 9.15-point improvement) and Amazon-Computer (0.4207 vs. 0.4891, a 6.84-point improvement). In the edge-addition experiment, PC-Winter also surpasses all baselines (Random, Edge-Betweenness, LOO) on every dataset, achieving the highest maximum accuracy (e.g., 0.7310 vs. 0.7220 for Edge-Betweenness on Cora; 0.8145 vs. 0.7996 for Edge-Betweenness on Amazon-Computer). The simpler structural baselines (Random, Degree, LOO, Edge-Betweenness) perform substantially worse than both PC-Winter and Data Shapley, confirming that game-theoretic valuation is necessary for capturing node/edge contributions in GNNs. The ablation study further shows that removing either the Level Constraint or the Precedence Constraint degrades performance, validating that both structural properties of the contribution tree are essential.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Winter Value (Winter, 1989) from cooperative game theory—originally designed for hierarchical coalition structures—into the graph data valuation domain by introducing a Precedence Constraint that encodes the unilateral dependency of GNN message-passing. It also builds on the Data Shapley framework (Ghorbani & Zou, 2019) for the utility function and Monte Carlo sampling strategy, but departs fundamentally by decomposing nodes into multiple 'players' within computation trees rather than treating samples as independent i.i.d. units. The contribution tree, which links computation trees of labeled nodes through a dummy objective node, is a novel construct that enables the Level Coalition Structure to be identified directly from the GNN architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first formal treatment of graph data valuation, addressing challenges (unlabeled node contributions, interdependent message-passing effects, computational intractability) that render standard i.i.d. data valuation methods inapplicable. The practical strategies—hierarchical truncation reducing retrainings by up to 96% and local propagation limiting feature aggregation to necessary subtrees—make the framework feasible on medium-scale graphs, though the 120 GPU-hour cap and reliance on SGC limit immediate scalability to very large graphs. Open questions include whether the PC-Winter framework generalizes to other GNN architectures (e.g., attention-based GNNs with non-uniform aggregation), heterogeneous graphs, and tasks beyond node classification, and whether the valuation can be further accelerated for industrial-scale graphs.
