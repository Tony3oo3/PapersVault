## Problem

Existing next-best-view (NBV) methods for active 3D mapping predict only a single nearby camera pose, behaving as a greedy policy that gets trapped in local regions and fails to explore distant under-reconstructed areas in complex indoor environments. Additionally, prior indoor datasets (MP3D, Gibson, ScanNet) offer limited geometric complexity and imperfect ground truth meshes, making them inadequate for benchmarking active mapping in realistic indoor settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251777/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes the accumulated 3D point cloud (back-projected from RGB-D depth images at 256×256 or 456×256 resolution) and the agent's past camera trajectory, both converted into K=4 horizontal 2D density slice images plus one trajectory frequency image, each at 256×256 resolution covering a 40m×40m area centered on the agent. It produces a 64×64×8 coverage gain value map (2D position × 8 discrete orientations) and a 256×256 binary obstacle map, from which a long-term goal pose and a Dijkstra-computed shortest obstacle-free path are derived as the next-best-path trajectory.

## Method

NBP replaces short-sighted next-best-view prediction with a unified model that jointly predicts a long-term goal (via a coverage gain value map encoding accumulated surface coverage along the optimal trajectory to each candidate cell) and an obstacle map (including anticipated unseen obstacles) using an Attention U-Net encoder-decoder architecture. Training proceeds iteratively: the current model collects trajectories online, ground-truth coverage gains are computed by comparing reconstructed point clouds against the scene mesh, and Dijkstra's sub-path optimality property is exploited to augment each trajectory into multiple training samples. A multi-task loss with learnable uncertainty weights balances MSE on the value map and BCE on the obstacle map, while a curriculum strategy excludes the first 10 steps of trajectories during early iterations to start from easier-to-predict samples. At inference, the agent selects the argmax cell as its long-term goal, plans a shortest path using the predicted obstacle map, and re-plans only upon encountering an unexpected obstacle.

## Results

On the AiMDoom dataset, NBP achieves Final Coverage of 0.879 (Simple), 0.734 (Normal), 0.618 (Hard), and 0.472 (Insane), with corresponding AUCs of 0.692, 0.526, 0.432, and 0.312, substantially outperforming all baselines across all four difficulty levels. On MP3D, NBP reaches 79.38% completion ratio and 6.78 cm average distance, surpassing the previous state-of-the-art ANM (73.15%, 9.11 cm) by 6.23 percentage points in completion. The gap over NBV methods (SCONE, MACARONS) is particularly pronounced in complex scenes, where NBP maintains 0.472 Final Coverage on Insane while MACARONS drops to 0.192.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NBP outperforms all six baselines on every metric across both datasets. On AiMDoom, NBP leads FBE by 0.119 Final Coverage on Simple and 0.142 on Insane, while NBV methods SCONE and MACARONS degrade sharply in complex scenes (e.g., MACARONS drops to 0.192 Final Coverage on Insane vs. NBP's 0.472). On MP3D, NBP achieves 79.38% completion, beating ANM (73.15%) by 6.23 points, OccAnt (71.72%) by 7.66 points, FBE (71.18%) by 8.20 points, and UPEN (69.06%) by 10.32 points. The paper notes that UPEN and ANM could not be evaluated on AiMDoom because they depend on DD-PPO navigation policies trained on specific environments, making retraining infeasible. The qualitative comparison in Figure 4 further illustrates that MACARONS gets trapped in local areas while NBP efficiently traverses the full scene.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant next-best-view (NBV) paradigm (SCONE, MACARONS, UPEN, ANM) that selects a single nearby camera pose in a greedy, short-horizon manner, and instead proposes next-best-path (NBP) planning that jointly predicts a long-term goal (via a coverage-gain value map) and an obstacle map within a unified Attention U-Net model. It also builds on frontier-based exploration (FBE) as a heuristic baseline but replaces its nearest-frontier rule with learned, coverage-aware path planning via Dijkstra. The introduction of the AiMDoom dataset, generated from Doom's map generator, extends the benchmarking landscape beyond the limited geometric complexity of MP3D, Gibson, and ScanNet, providing four difficulty levels with significantly higher navigation complexity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The AiMDoom dataset exposes a critical weakness of current SOTA NBV methods: they get trapped in local regions in complex indoor environments, achieving far lower coverage than previously reported on simpler benchmarks. The NBP approach demonstrates that shifting from short-sighted view selection to long-term goal-driven path planning yields substantial gains (e.g., 0.879 vs. 0.599 final coverage on Simple, 0.472 vs. 0.192 on Insane), but performance on Hard and Insane levels remains unsatisfactory, with the value map prediction identified as the primary bottleneck. Open questions include whether global-optimum planning (rather than greedy long-term goals) can close the remaining gap, and whether the approach generalizes to multi-floor or outdoor environments.
