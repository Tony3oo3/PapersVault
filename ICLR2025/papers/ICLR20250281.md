## Problem

Existing video temporal reasoning benchmarks overestimate MFMs' true temporal reasoning capabilities because many questions can be answered using a single frame, a few frames, or out-of-order frames. Systematic analysis of four widely-used benchmarks (VITATECS, MVBench, TempCompass, ReXTime) reveals they exhibit low multi-frame gain (<5%), low frame order sensitivity (<8%), and high frame information disparity (>27%), meaning models exploit static or order-independent shortcuts rather than performing genuine temporal reasoning across a continuous sequence.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250281/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each question is presented with 16 uniformly sampled frames from a video in chronological order, paired with a multiple-choice question containing 4–7 options. The 1,484 questions span six temporal reasoning tasks (rotation, direction, velocity & frequency, shape & trend, visual cues, action count) applied to 1,417 videos averaging 9.2 seconds in duration. Videos encompass three demonstration categories—human-centric, real-world, and simulated—and include deliberately edited counterfactual scenarios, composite motions, and zoomed-in views designed to invalidate commonsense-based shortcuts.

## Method

TOMATO is constructed around three benchmarking principles with corresponding quantitative metrics: Multi-Frame Gain (the task must require reasoning across multiple frames, not solvable from one), Frame Order Sensitivity (the task must depend on correct temporal ordering of frames), and Frame Information Disparity (information must be evenly distributed so no single handpicked frame dominates). The benchmark comprises 1,484 human-annotated multiple-choice questions across six tasks, applied to 1,417 videos sourced from YouTube, four existing datasets (Music-AVQA, CLEVRER, TGIF-QA, Perception Test), and 805 self-recorded and -generated videos created via Keynote, VIBE, and SMPL frameworks. Videos are deliberately edited with reversals, concatenations, speed adjustments, and mirroring to produce counterfactual scenarios that invalidate commonsense priors. Evaluation uses zero-shot inference across 31 MFMs (21 open-source, 10 proprietary) with GPT-4o-mini as a fallback answer parser, alongside human baselines using both full videos and 16 frames to quantify the human-model gap.

## Results

On TOMATO (1,484 questions, 1,417 videos), the best open-source model Qwen2-VL-72B achieves 37.9% overall accuracy, narrowly outperforming the best proprietary model GPT-4o at 37.7%. Human performance reaches 95.2% with full videos and 79.7% with 16 frames, revealing a 57.3% gap between the best model and human-level performance. All 31 evaluated MFMs fall well below 40% overall, with many open-source models scoring below 30%. In the benchmark comparison, TOMATO achieves a Multi-Frame Gain of 81.0% (random frame) and 66.3% (handpicked frame), Frame Order Sensitivity of 34.1%, and Frame Information Disparity of only 4.6%, compared to <5%, <8%, and >27% respectively for existing benchmarks (VITATECS, MVBench, TempCompass, ReXTime).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among proprietary models, GPT-4o leads at 37.7% overall, followed by Gemini 1.5 Pro (36.1%) and Gemini 2.0 Flash Exp (33.7%), while Claude 3 Opus (28.2%) and Reka Core (15.3%) trail significantly. Among open-source models, Qwen2-VL-72B achieves the best score at 37.9%, marginally surpassing GPT-4o, with Qwen2-VL-7B (31.5%) and Video-CCAM-v1.1 14B (30.7%) following; notably, Qwen2-VL-72B's advantage is driven by its M-RoPE temporal positional encoding. All models remain far below human performance (95.2% with videos, 79.7% with 16 frames), with a 57.3% gap to the best model. In the benchmark comparison (Tables 1–4), TOMATO substantially outperforms VITATECS, MVBench, TempCompass, and ReXTime on all three rigorosity metrics: Multi-Frame Gain of 81.0% vs. 23.4–47.1% for existing benchmarks, Frame Order Sensitivity of 34.1% vs. 0.3–22.3%, and Frame Information Disparity of 4.6% vs. 21.6–37.2%, confirming that existing benchmarks allow models to exploit single-frame or order-independent shortcuts. The analysis further reveals that more capable models (GPT-4o, Qwen2-VL-72B) are more susceptible to counterfactual scenarios (−37.7% and −29.4% respectively), indicating over-reliance on commonsense rather than true visual temporal reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a corrective to the existing line of video temporal reasoning benchmarks (VITATECS, MVBench, TempCompass, ReXTime), arguing that their questions can be solved with single, few, or out-of-order frames and thus do not genuinely test temporal reasoning. Rather than building on a specific method, it introduces three diagnostic metrics (Multi-Frame Gain, Frame Order Sensitivity, Frame Information Disparity) as a principled framework for auditing benchmark quality, then constructs TOMATO to satisfy all three. The video collection repurposes existing datasets (CLEVRER, TGIF-QA, Music-AVQA, Perception Test) alongside 805 self-recorded and -generated videos, and the evaluation spans 31 MFMs from the vision-language-model family, making it a comprehensive empirical study layered on top of a new benchmark.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 57.3% human-model gap and the finding that models plateau at 8 frames (failing to leverage additional temporal information) expose a fundamental limitation: current MFMs process frames as isolated snapshots rather than as a continuous sequence, undermining their utility for real-world video understanding. The counterfactual analysis showing that more capable models are *more* reliant on common sense (GPT-4o drops 37.7% vs. Qwen2-VL-72B's 29.4%) challenges the assumption that scaling improves genuine visual grounding. Open questions include whether temporal-aware positional encodings like M-RoPE can close the gap, how to make models robust to noisy visual information, and whether the 8-frame ceiling reflects an architectural limitation or a training-data artifact. As a benchmark with 1,484 curated questions and clear diagnostic metrics, TOMATO is likely to become a standard evaluation for next-generation video understanding models.
