## Problem

Large safety guard models (7B+ parameters) are impractical to deploy alongside LLMs on mobile devices due to memory and latency constraints, yet naively distilling them into sub-billion-parameter models underperforms because existing labeled datasets contain limited diversity of harmful instructions. The paper addresses how to close this performance gap so that a small model can match or exceed the detection capability of its much larger teacher.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252480/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The safety guard model accepts an instruction text paired with a response text (or an instruction alone with an empty response) and outputs a binary harmfulness label with an associated probability score. The model is a bidirectional encoder (DeBERTa-v3-large, 435M parameters) rather than an autoregressive decoder, reflecting that harmfulness detection is a classification task. The inclusion of empty-response pairs during training enables the same model to handle both instruction-only classification (e.g., OpenAI Moderation, ToxicChat) and full instruction-response pair classification (e.g., HarmBench, WildGuardMix).

## Method

HarmAug is a data augmentation pipeline that first jailbreaks a small LLM (Gemma-1.1-2b-it) by prepending an affirmative prefix ('I have an idea for a prompt:') to the assistant's response, forcing it to complete a harmful instruction rather than refuse; this prefix attack achieves a 96.8% success rate versus 13% without it. For each generated instruction, two separate LLMs produce a refusal response and a harmful response, and the instruction is also paired with an empty sequence, yielding three instruction-response pairs per instruction. The teacher model (Llama-Guard-3) then assigns binary harmfulness labels to all synthetic pairs, which are combined with the original WildGuardMix training set. The student DeBERTa is trained via knowledge distillation minimizing a weighted combination of KL divergence to the teacher's output and binary cross-entropy on the ground-truth labels, with hard labels (temperature 0) outperforming soft labels.

## Results

A 435M-parameter DeBERTa model trained with HarmAug achieves an average AUPRC of 0.8362 across four benchmarks (OAI, ToxicChat, HarmBench, WildGuardMix), outperforming all larger models including Llama-Guard-3 (8B, AUPRC 0.7720) and Aegis-Guard (7B, AUPRC 0.7888). Its average F1 of 0.7357 is competitive with 7–8B parameter models (Llama-Guard-3: 0.7080, Aegis-Guard: 0.7040) and second only to WildGuard (0.7479, which does not report AUPRC). In terms of efficiency, the distilled model reduces FLOPs/token to 0.6%, latency to 25%, peak GPU memory to 12%, and monetary inference cost to 26% of Llama-Guard-3, while matching or exceeding its F1 on WildGuardMix (0.7576 vs. 0.6998).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DeBERTa + HarmAug (435M) achieves the highest average AUPRC (0.8362) among all evaluated models, surpassing Llama-Guard-3 (0.7720), Aegis-Guard (0.7888), and Llama-Guard-2 (0.7492) by 0.6–6.4 AUPRC points. On OAI and ToxicChat, it attains the best AUPRC of any model (0.8791 and 0.7553 respectively), exceeding even its 8B teacher Llama-Guard-3 (0.8869 and 0.4823). Against the augmentation baselines, HarmAug outperforms EDA (avg AUPRC 0.8161) and GFN (0.8050) by 2.0 and 3.1 AUPRC points, and improves average F1 over both (0.7357 vs. 0.7133 and 0.7276). On HarmBench, Llama-Guard-2 (F1 0.8610, AUPRC 0.8945) and WildGuard (F1 0.8596) still edge out DeBERTa + HarmAug (F1 0.8331, AUPRC 0.8841), though the gap is small. In the red-teaming case study (Table 3), DeBERTa + HarmAug as a reward model achieves a test reward of 0.82 (vs. 0.99 for the Llama-Guard-3 oracle) while halving runtime, whereas RoBERTa-R4 and HateBERT collapse to 0.00 test reward, indicating severe distributional mismatch.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2014), LLM-based safety guard models (Llama-Guard, WildGuard, AegisGuard), and jailbreak techniques. It repurposes a jailbreak-style prefix attack—prefilling an affirmative response to bypass an LLM's RLHF safety alignment—as a data augmentation mechanism for generating diverse harmful instructions, a use case distinct from prior jailbreak work that targets attack discovery. The distillation objective (KL divergence + BCE) follows standard KD practice, while the GFlowNet-based augmentation baseline (Lee et al., 2024) and EDA (Wei & Zou, 2019) represent the closest prior approaches to synthetic data generation for safety classification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a 435M-parameter model matching or exceeding 7–8B safety guard models in AUPRC while reducing inference cost by ~75% makes on-device safety monitoring feasible for the first time. The prefix attack's 96.8% success rate in bypassing safety alignment also reveals a concrete weakness in current RLHF pipelines that safety researchers should address. Open questions include improving synthetic data diversity (performance plateaus beyond ~100K samples due to redundancy), optimizing DeBERTa's disentangled attention for FlashAttention compatibility, and whether the approach generalizes to multilingual or multimodal safety detection.
