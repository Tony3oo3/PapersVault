## Problem

Current LLM alignment methods (RLHF, DPO) infer human values implicitly from relative preference rankings, making the resulting values opaque, costly to collect, and vulnerable to reward-hacking or goal misgeneralization. This paper asks whether explicit, transparent moral reward functions—grounded in established ethical frameworks—can be used directly for RL-based fine-tuning of LLM decision-making agents, and whether such alignment generalizes beyond the training environment.

## Input / Output

The agent receives a structured prompt containing a 2×2 payoff matrix (with row/column headers as action tokens) and the previous move of both players as state, and must output exactly one of two designated action tokens (e.g., action1 or action2). The prompt deliberately omits the words 'Prisoner's Dilemma,' 'cooperate,' or 'defect' to prevent reliance on pre-training memorization, and generation is constrained to a maximum of two tokens. At test time, entirely new action tokens (action3, action4) are substituted to probe whether the learned policy generalizes semantically rather than memorizing specific token strings.

## Method

The paper fine-tunes Gemma2-2b-it via PPO (using 4-bit quantized LoRA, rank 64) with intrinsic moral rewards that explicitly encode Deontological norms (a fixed penalty ξ=3 for defecting against a previously-cooperating opponent) or Utilitarian objectives (sum of both players' payoffs), replacing the implicit preference-based rewards of RLHF. Training runs for 1000 episodes against either a fixed Tit-for-Tat opponent or a co-learning LLM agent, with an additional illegal-token penalty (R_illegal=−6) to keep outputs within the permitted action space. A key experimental axis is 'unlearning': agents first trained on selfish game-payoff rewards are then retrained on moral rewards for the second half of episodes, testing whether previously acquired misaligned strategies can be overwritten. Generalization is evaluated by testing fine-tuned models on four additional matrix games (Stag Hunt, Chicken, Bach or Stravinsky, Defective Coordination) using new action tokens, measuring Deontological and Utilitarian moral regret to quantify how well the learned moral policy transfers across payoff structures.

## Results

On the IPD against a Tit-for-Tat opponent, Deontological fine-tuning causes the agent to avoid defecting against a cooperator nearly 100% of the time, while Utilitarian fine-tuning drives the agent to mutual cooperation (the optimal collective-payoff strategy). In the unlearning experiments (Game reward for 500 episodes, then moral reward for 500 episodes), agents partially recover prosocial behavior but do not reach the cooperation levels of purely moral fine-tuning. For generalization to four other matrix games (Stag Hunt, Chicken, Bach or Stravinsky, Defective Coordination), Deontological agents maintain very low moral regret across all games, while Utilitarian agents generalize well to Iterated Chicken but show elevated regret on coordination games due to an over-cooperative bias learned on the IPD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external alignment methods (e.g., RLHF or DPO); instead, it compares six internal reward configurations (Game, Deontological, Utilitarian, Game+Deontological, Game→Deontological, Game→Utilitarian) on the same IPD environment and generalization suite. Deontological fine-tuning produces the most consistently moral behavior across all five matrix games, with the lowest Deontological moral regret and near-zero norm violations. Utilitarian fine-tuning achieves the lowest Utilitarian regret on the IPD and Iterated Chicken but underperforms on Iterated Stag Hunt, Bach or Stravinsky, and Defective Coordination because the agent defaults to unconditional cooperation regardless of the payoff structure. The Game+Deontological multi-objective agent avoids norm violations while still pursuing game payoffs, but shows higher moral regret than the purely Deontological agent on both axes. In the unlearning setting, switching from Game to Deontological or Utilitarian reward at episode 500 partially reverses selfish behavior, though convergence is slower than with 1000 episodes of pure moral reward. A notable cross-benefit finding is that the Utilitarian model matches the Deontological model on Deontological regret, because its fully cooperative policy trivially satisfies the 'do not defect against a cooperator' norm.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the moral reward framework previously developed for pure RL agents on the Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma (Tennant et al., IJCAI 2023; AIES 2024) to LLM-based decision-making agents, using PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) with LoRA fine-tuning as the training mechanism. It departs from the dominant RLHF/DPO alignment paradigm, which infers values implicitly from human preference rankings, by instead encoding Deontological and Utilitarian moral norms as explicit, transparent intrinsic reward functions. The work also builds on the broader LLM-agent literature (e.g., Voyager, Generative Agents) but focuses specifically on the alignment of strategic decision-making rather than open-ended task completion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that explicit moral reward design can align a small (2B) LLM agent's behavior in social dilemmas and that learned moral policies partially generalize to structurally different matrix games, offering a more transparent and potentially cost-effective alternative to preference-data-driven alignment. Key limitations include the requirement for environment-specific reward specification, evaluation restricted to 2×2 matrix games, and testing on a single small model, leaving open whether findings scale to larger architectures or richer environments. The unlearning result—showing a selfish strategy can be partially overwritten by moral rewards—suggests a practical path for correcting misaligned behavior in deployed models without full retraining.
