## Problem

Individual neurons in CLIP's MLP layers have been largely uninterpreted: their direct (first-order) effects on the output are negligible, and their indirect effects (measured via ablation) are obscured by redundant self-repair mechanisms across many neurons encoding the same concept. This leaves no principled lens for understanding what a single neuron actually contributes to CLIP's image representation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253696/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on a frozen pre-trained CLIP-ViT model (ViT-B-32 or ViT-L-14) and requires a calibration set of ~5000 images plus a pool of text descriptions (10k–30k common English words or ~28k ChatGPT-generated ImageNet class descriptions). For each neuron in a target layer, it outputs a sparse set of 4–128 text phrases with signed coefficients that approximate the neuron's second-order effect direction in the joint text-image space. Downstream, these decompositions yield attribution heatmaps (binarized to segmentation masks) and, combined with an LLM and text-to-image model, produce adversarial images that exploit spurious concept overlaps.

## Method

The paper introduces the 'second-order lens,' which traces a neuron's contribution through all subsequent multi-head self-attention layers to the output projection, yielding a closed-form expression (Eq. 5) as a sum of attention-weighted activations times input-independent OV transition matrices. Empirically, they show three properties: effects concentrate in moderately late MLP layers (8–10 for ViT-B-32), each neuron is selective (significant for <2% of images), and the effect is approximately rank-1 in the joint text-image space. They then apply orthogonal matching pursuit to decompose each neuron's principal direction into a sparse sum of text-encoder embeddings, revealing polysemantic structure (one neuron encodes multiple unrelated concepts). This decomposition enables two applications: (1) mass-producing semantic adversarial images by identifying spurious words shared across neurons that push toward an incorrect class, and (2) zero-shot segmentation by averaging spatial activation maps of neurons whose directions align with a target class name.

## Results

On ImageNet-Segmentation, the second-order neuron-based zero-shot segmentation achieves 78.1% pixel accuracy, 59.0 mIoU, and 84.9 mAP, outperforming all prior methods including TextSpan (76.5/58.1/84.1) and Chefer et al. (68.9/49.1/79.7). For semantic adversarial example generation on CIFAR-10 binary tasks, the method fools the classifier on 5.3–22.7 out of 100 generated images (depending on the class pair), substantially exceeding the indirect-effect baseline (0–6.3) and the similar-words baseline (0–5.0). The second-order effect also proves more interpretable than the indirect effect: mean-ablating second-order effects drops ImageNet accuracy to 29.6% (vs. 52.3% for indirect), and the first principal component explains 48.2% of the variance (vs. only 11.0% for indirect).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's zero-shot segmentation method outperforms all six compared baselines across every metric on ImageNet-Segmentation. Against the closest competitor, TextSpan (Gandelsman et al., 2024), it gains +1.6 pixel accuracy, +0.9 mIoU, and +0.8 mAP. The attention-based methods (Chefer et al. at 68.9/49.1/79.7 and Raw-attention at 69.6/49.8/80.0) trail by roughly 8–9 points in pixel accuracy. Gradient-based methods (GradCAM at 67.3/39.3/61.9) and relevance-propagation methods (Partial-LRP at 55.0/35.5/66.9, LRP at 62.9/35.8/68.5, Rollout at 61.8/42.6/74.0) are further behind, with mIoU gaps of 16–24 points. For adversarial generation, the second-order decomposition baseline dominates the indirect-effect baseline (e.g., 22.7 vs. 6.3 fooled images for dog→deer) and the similar-words baseline (22.7 vs. 3.3), while the random-neuron baseline succeeds only by chance (0–1.3 images).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CLIP interpretability line initiated by Gandelsman et al. (2024), which decomposed attention heads' first-order effects into text directions, by introducing a 'second-order lens' that traces individual MLP neurons through subsequent attention layers to the output. The derivation builds directly on the transformer circuits framework of Elhage et al. (2021) for expressing MSA outputs as weighted token sums, and the polysemantic neuron findings connect to the superposition literature (Elhage et al., 2022). Unlike prior neuron-interpretability work that relies on activation patterns or LLM-based descriptions of which images fire a neuron, this paper focuses specifically on the neuron's contribution to the output representation, arguing that both first-order (direct) and indirect (ablation) effects are insufficient due to redundancy and self-repair.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean structural findings—that second-order effects are approximately rank-1 and significant for fewer than 2% of images—provide a principled basis for automated neuron description via sparse text decomposition, enabling both adversarial example generation and zero-shot segmentation without any training. The adversarial pipeline is optimization-free and could serve as a robustness audit tool for CLIP-based systems, though current success rates (5–23 out of 100 images) are moderate. Key open questions include how neuron-neuron interactions and neuron-attention-map modifications affect the analysis, and whether the rank-1 approximation holds for more complex or larger models beyond ViT-B-32 and ViT-L-14.
