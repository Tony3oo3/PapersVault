## Problem

Video MLLMs must represent a video within a fixed visual context window, yet the design space governing how to split that budget between the number of sampled frames and the number of visual embeddings per frame has not been systematically studied. Prior work has focused on model architecture and training strategies, leaving the competitive allocation between frame selection and embedding selection under-explored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250507/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a video (uniformly sampled into T frames) and a text instruction. Each frame is encoded by SigLIP into a 27×27 grid of visual embeddings, projected into Qwen2-7B's semantic space via a 2-layer MLP, with the total visual context T×M constrained to a maximum window size L (e.g., 6K tokens). The model outputs a natural language response via next-token prediction, evaluated as multiple-choice accuracy on long-video understanding benchmarks.

## Method

The paper formulates visual context allocation as a constrained optimization problem: minimize language modeling loss L(T,M) subject to T×M < L. It empirically studies scaling effects for frames and embeddings separately using both sampling-based (uniform subsampling) and compression-based (MeanPooling) strategies, fitting power-law functions to the resulting loss curves. A joint loss function L(M,T) = C_M·M^(−α) + C_T·T^(−β) + L_0 is then minimized via Lagrange multipliers to derive the optimal (T*, M*) allocation. Key findings: increasing frames consistently improves performance without saturation while increasing embeddings per frame saturates earlier; compression-based methods preserve more spatial and temporal information than sampling at the same token budget; and the optimal frame-to-embedding ratio T/M ≈ 2.3 generalizes across context lengths from 4K to 126K. The architecture is a standard LLaVA-like pipeline (SigLIP + 2-layer MLP + Qwen2-7B) trained for one epoch on 2.5M mixed image/video instructions.

## Results

The paper derives the optimal visual context allocation for a 6K context window as T≈118 frames and M≈51 embeddings per frame (via Lagrange multipliers), with empirical validation showing T=120, M=49 achieves the lowest language modeling loss (0.68). Scaling to 162 frames with 36 embeddings yields the best average benchmark accuracy of 50.62% under the 6K constraint. When augmented with Video178K training data, the model (Ours+Video178K) achieves 67.3% on VNBench, 66.0% on MLVU, 61.7/66.9 on VideoMME (wo/w-subs), and 58.4% on LongVideoBench, surpassing SOTA on VNBench and LongVideoBench among open-source models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

With the Video178K augmentation, Ours+Video178K substantially outperforms GPT-4V across all five benchmarks (e.g., VNBench 67.3 vs. 48.9, MLVU 66.0 vs. 49.2, Event-Bench 35.0 vs. 27.0). It also surpasses LLaVA-OneVision-7B on every reported metric (VNBench 67.3 vs. 35.7, MLVU 66.0 vs. 64.7, LongVideoBench 58.4 vs. 56.5) and beats LLaVA-Video-7B on VNBench (67.3 vs. 40.7) and LongVideoBench (58.4 vs. 58.2), though it trails on Event-Bench (35.0 vs. 43.0) and VideoMME (61.7/66.9 vs. 63.3/69.7). Against proprietary models, Ours+Video178K edges out GPT-4o on VNBench (67.3 vs. 64.4) and MLVU (66.0 vs. 64.6) but falls short on VideoMME (61.7/66.9 vs. 71.9/77.2) and LongVideoBench (58.4 vs. 66.7); it similarly beats Gemini-1.5-Pro on VNBench (67.3 vs. 66.7) but lags on VideoMME and LongVideoBench. The authors attribute the remaining gap to training data scale (their model uses ~3.4M instructions vs. 8M+ for LLaVA-Video) rather than to the visual context representation scheme itself.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the LLaVA-like video MLLM architecture (vision encoder + MLP projector + LLM) and adapts the Chinchilla scaling-law framework (Hoffmann et al., 2022) from model/data scaling to the under-studied dimension of visual input representation. While prior video MLLM work focuses on architecture modifications (e.g., LongLLaVA's Mamba-Transformer hybrid) or training strategies (e.g., Kangaroo's curriculum), this paper isolates and formalizes the input-side design choice—how to split a fixed visual context budget between temporal resolution (frames) and spatial resolution (tokens per frame)—as a constrained optimization problem. It departs from the common practice of fixing frame count at 32 or 64 and varying only token compression, instead treating both factors as jointly optimizable variables.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The derived optimal T/M ratio (~2.3) and the finding that compression-based pooling consistently outperforms uniform sampling provide actionable, architecture-agnostic guidance for practitioners designing video MLLM inputs under fixed context budgets. However, a persistent gap between language modeling loss and downstream benchmark accuracy (e.g., 196 tokens yields lower loss but worse accuracy than 49 tokens under sampling) limits the direct transferability of the theoretical optimum to real-world deployment. Open questions include whether the power-law form generalizes to longer contexts (>6K, untested due to memory), whether more sophisticated frame-selection strategies (e.g., keyframe detection) would shift the optimal ratio, and whether the findings hold for architectures with native temporal modeling rather than frame-concatenation.
