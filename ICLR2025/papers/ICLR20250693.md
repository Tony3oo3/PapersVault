## Problem

Existing node-level OOD detection on graphs is evaluated on synthetic benchmarks where OOD nodes are randomly assigned, ignoring the fact that in real-world scenarios OOD samples spread through graph edges via interactions (e.g., viral infection, malware propagation). This paper introduces 'spreading OOD detection,' a new benchmark setup that models OOD propagation using epidemic spreading models (SI/SIS), requiring detectors to identify OOD nodes at any time stamp without knowing the spreading process.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250693/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method receives a graph G^t = (V, E, X^t) at an unknown time stamp t during an ongoing epidemic spread, along with a neural classifier trained solely on ID data without graph structure (sample-level training). It outputs a refined per-node energy score E^(K) for each node, where higher energy indicates higher likelihood of being OOD. A key constraint is that the detector must perform well across all time stamps t ∈ {0, …, T} without access to t, seed node positions, or any information about the spreading dynamics.

## Method

EDBD computes initial per-node energies via the standard energy function E(f(x)) = -T·log Σ exp(f_c(x)/T) from a trained classifier, then refines them through K-step energy distribution-based aggregation (EDBA). The aggregation E^(k) = (I - αC)E^(k-1) + αCSE^(k-1) employs two energy-derived controllers: an edge-level Energy Similarity Matrix S that weights message passing by inverse energy difference between connected nodes (suppressing propagation between dissimilar ID/OOD pairs), and a node-level Energy Consistency Matrix C that scales total aggregation by the normalized standard deviation of energies in each node's ego-network (preserving a node's own energy when its neighborhood is heterogeneous, i.e., at ID/OOD cluster boundaries). This design prevents the undesired mixing of high OOD energies into neighboring ID nodes that plagues uniform aggregation methods like GNNSAFE.

## Results

On the label leave-out task, EDBD achieves the best FPR95 across all four datasets: 30.48% on Cora, 5.82% on Amazon-Photo, 35.59% on Amazon-Computers, and 10.19% on Coauthor-CS, consistently outperforming GNNSAFE (the strongest graph-based baseline). On the novel spreading OOD detection task, EDBD shows dramatic gains: on Cora with the SI model, EDBD achieves FPR95-T of 14.99% versus GNNSAFE's 31.15% and Energy's 22.79%, while on LastFM Asia (SI) it reaches 46.68% FPR95-T versus GNNSAFE's 64.83%. EDBD also achieves the highest AUROC-T (97.54% on Cora SI) and AUPR-T (98.68% on Cora SI) among all methods in the spreading setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EDBD outperforms all eight baselines across all four label leave-out datasets on FPR95, with the largest margin on Amazon-Computers (35.59% vs. GNNSAFE's 39.94%, a 4.35-point improvement) and Amazon-Photo (5.82% vs. 6.57%). On the spreading OOD detection task, EDBD's advantage over GNNSAFE is far more pronounced: on Cora (SI), EDBD halves the FPR95-T from 31.15% to 14.99%, and on LastFM Asia (SI) it reduces FPR95-T from 64.83% to 46.68%, while also improving AUROC-T by ~10 points (94.10% vs. 83.73%). The i.i.d. baselines (MSP, ODIN, Mahalanobis) perform poorly in the spreading setting, with ODIN and GPN reaching near-random AUROC-T (~17–22%) on LastFM Asia, confirming that graph-aware aggregation is essential. Energy (the non-graph baseline) is the closest competitor in spreading OOD detection, but EDBD consistently matches or exceeds it, demonstrating that the energy-distribution-based aggregation provides meaningful gains over both uniform aggregation (GNNSAFE) and no aggregation (Energy). Statistical significance tests (Table 12) confirm significant improvements on Amazon-Photo (p < 0.001 for all metrics) and Amazon-Computers (p < 0.01 for AUROC and AUPR), though some comparisons on Cora and Coauthor-CS are not statistically significant due to small margins.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of graph-based node-level OOD detection (GKDE, GPN, OODGAT, GNNSAFE) by introducing a temporal spreading dimension: rather than randomly assigning OOD labels to nodes, OOD propagates through the graph via SI/SIS epidemic models. Methodologically, it builds directly on GNNSAFE's energy-aggregation framework but replaces uniform neighborhood averaging with an energy-distribution-controlled scheme (energy similarity matrix for edge-level weighting, energy consistency matrix for node-level gating). The energy-based scoring function itself is inherited from Liu et al. (2020), and the epidemic spreading models are standard tools from mathematical epidemiology repurposed here as a data-generation mechanism for benchmarking.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The spreading OOD detection benchmark is a reasonable step toward more realistic evaluation of node-level OOD methods, and the Spreading COVID-19 dataset provides a concrete (if synthetic) application scenario. However, the methodological gains of EDBD over GNNSAFE are modest (often <1–2% on FPR95/AUROC), and the OOD features for benchmark datasets are simply Bernoulli(0.1) samples, which limits the realism of the evaluation. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to more complex epidemic models (SEIR, network-based), whether the energy-distribution aggregation principle transfers to other scalar-valued node properties, and whether the LastFM-based graph structure truly captures the contact dynamics relevant to pandemic spread. The paper itself acknowledges that complex epidemic models are left for future work.
