## Problem

Closed-loop genetic perturbation experiment design has relied on Bayesian optimization pipelines that require training bespoke, hard-to-interpret ML models with hand-crafted gene features and explicitly defined acquisition functions. These approaches cannot leverage the vast biological prior knowledge embedded in the scientific literature, and LLM-based agents had not yet been explored for this closed-loop experimental design task.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250950/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent receives a natural-language prompt specifying the research problem (e.g., 'identify genes regulating IFNG production'), the phenotypic scores from all previously perturbed genes, and optionally tool outputs (summarized PubMed papers with line-number citations, or top-K genes from Reactome/KEGG enrichment). It produces a structured three-part response—Reflection, Research Plan, and Solution—where the Solution is a comma-separated list of 128 gene names (32 for the smaller Scharenberg dataset) to perturb in the next round. The structured format enforces interpretability and allows human-in-the-loop feedback at every stage.

## Method

BioDiscoveryAgent replaces the entire Bayesian optimization pipeline (surrogate model + acquisition function) with a single LLM call per round, requiring no training or feature engineering. Each round, the agent is prompted with the task description and cumulative experimental observations, and generates the next gene batch using its pre-trained biological knowledge to solve the cold-start problem, then adapts based on observed phenotypic responses. Three optional tools augment the agent: (1) a literature search tool where the agent generates PubMed queries, retrieves top-5 papers, and summarizes them with retained citations; (2) a gene search tool that performs Reactome enrichment analysis on hit genes to retrieve pathway-mates; and (3) an AI critic agent prompted to critique and potentially revise the main agent's gene selection. A two-step gene selection strategy first allows unrestricted gene proposals, then falls back to a summarized gene list if the agent repeatedly produces invalid or repeated selections. The key finding is that larger models (Claude 3.5 Sonnet) outperform all Bayesian optimization baselines by 21% on average without any tools, while smaller models (Claude 3 Haiku) benefit significantly from tool use, suggesting a size-dependent relationship between intrinsic knowledge and external retrieval.

## Results

On the 1-gene perturbation task across six datasets (128 genes per round, 5 rounds), BioDiscoveryAgent with Claude 3.5 Sonnet achieves a 21% average hit-ratio improvement over the best Bayesian-optimization baseline (Coreset), and a 46% average improvement when restricted to non-essential genes. On the harder 2-gene combinatorial perturbation task (32 gene pairs out of 100,576 per round), Claude 3.5 Sonnet outperforms random sampling by 170% on average cumulative hits after 5 rounds (40.0 vs. 16.4 hits). The agent also outperforms a human expert baseline (pathway-based enrichment) on 5 of 6 datasets for all-gene hit ratio.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two families of Bayesian-optimization baselines from the GeneDisco benchmark (Mehrjou et al., 2021) and DiscoBax (Lyle et al., 2023). GeneDisco provides six acquisition-function variants (Soft Uncertain, Top Uncertain, Margin Sample, Coreset, Badge, K-Means) all built on a shared MLP; Coreset is typically the strongest, reaching 0.072 hit ratio on Schmidt1 and 0.102 on Schmidt2. DiscoBax, which formulates selection as a set-value maximization problem, is competitive on Schmidt1 (0.088) but underperforms Coreset on most other datasets. BioDiscoveryAgent (Claude 3.5 Sonnet) beats the best GeneDisco baseline (Coreset) on 5 of 6 datasets for all-gene hit ratio (e.g., 0.095 vs. 0.072 on Schmidt1, 0.130 vs. 0.069 on CAR-T) and beats DiscoBax on 5 of 6. The gap is especially large for non-essential genes, where Claude 3.5 Sonnet achieves a 46% average improvement (e.g., 0.107 vs. 0.066 on Schmidt1 N/E). On the Carnevale dataset, the agent (0.042) is slightly below Coreset (0.047) for all genes, representing the one dataset where the best baseline edges out the agent.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the established Bayesian optimization pipeline for genetic perturbation experiment design (GeneDisco, DiscoBax), which requires training a bespoke ML model and defining an acquisition function each round. Instead, it repurposes the LLM agent paradigm—drawing on ReAct-style pre-action reasoning, Reflexion-style self-reflection, and contrastive prompting (AI critic)—to replace the entire trained-model-plus-acquisition-function stack with a single prompted LLM that leverages prior biological knowledge. The 2-gene combinatorial perturbation setting is explicitly framed as unexplored in prior closed-loop experiment design literature, extending the single-gene formulation to a much larger combinatorial search space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a zero-shot LLM agent can outperform purpose-trained Bayesian optimization baselines (21% average, 46% on non-essential genes) while offering full interpretability at every decision step—a significant practical advantage over black-box acquisition functions. A notable open question is the inverse relationship between model scale and tool utility: larger models (Claude 3.5 Sonnet, GPT-4o) are hurt by external tools that help smaller models, suggesting their weights already encode much of the relevant biological knowledge, but the optimal tool design for frontier models remains unresolved. Limitations include performance gains concentrated in early rounds, cell-type-dependent effectiveness tied to literature coverage, and the need for better information extraction from non-text sources.
