## Problem

Despite the critical role of training data in driving AI capabilities, there is no empirical, ecosystem-level analysis of data provenance that spans multiple modalities. Prior audits focused narrowly on text datasets or isolated features like hate content, leaving the sourcing trends, licensing inconsistencies, and representation gaps in speech and video datasets largely unexamined at scale.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252182/cropped_page4_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The audit consumes unstructured metadata from ~4,000 public datasets (1990–2024) across text, speech, and video, requiring full provenance traces back through chains of GitHub repositories, academic papers, and websites to identify original sources and their terms of use. Outputs are structured as (1) cumulative source-category distributions over time, (2) crosstabs of dataset license type against source restriction level (both by count and by tokens/hours), and (3) time-series Gini coefficients measuring dispersion of languages and creator countries, with 95% confidence intervals to test for significant change.

## Method

The paper conducts a manual, expert-annotated longitudinal audit, extending the Data Provenance Collection's license taxonomy to a two-axis system: dataset license (Commercial / NC-Academic / Unspecified) crossed with source terms (Unrestricted / Unspecified / Source Closed / Model Closed), where the source axis captures restrictions from the original content platform or generating model rather than the dataset's own license. Domain experts in each modality trace every dataset's metadata back to its original source, annotating sourcing category (web-crawled, social media, synthetic, human-curated, etc.), creator organization affiliations mapped to countries, and ISO 639 language codes. Representation inequality is quantified using Gini coefficients computed over the distribution of tokens/hours across languages and countries at each year, enabling statistical tests for significant improvement over the 2013–2024 window. Dataset selection is modality-specific, drawing from HuggingFace, Papers with Code, OpenSLR, survey papers, workshop proceedings, and expert review, with text analyzed at both collection and individual dataset level to capture how repackaging obscures licensing.

## Results

The audit covers 3,916 public datasets (3,717 text, 95 speech, 104 video) from 1990–2024, spanning 608 languages, 798 sources, and 67 countries. Three headline findings emerge: (1) web-crawled, social media, and synthetic sources now overwhelmingly dominate training data, with YouTube alone contributing ~1M hours each of speech and video content, eclipsing all other sources since 2019; (2) while only 25–33% of datasets carry non-commercial licenses, 78–99.8% of content (by tokens/hours) carries source-level non-commercial restrictions, and 55–79% of content shows a mismatch between the dataset's license and its source's terms; (3) Gini coefficients for geographic creator representation (0.92 text, 0.86 speech, 0.74 video) show no statistically significant improvement since 2013, with North American and European organizations accounting for 93% of text tokens, 61% of speech hours, and 60% of video hours.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical audit paper rather than a method paper, so there are no traditional baselines or competing methods to compare against. Instead, the paper's key comparisons are cross-modality and temporal. Across modalities, text shows the most extreme concentration (Gini 0.92) and the highest source-restriction mismatch (79% of tokens), while video is somewhat less concentrated (Gini 0.74) but has the largest share of unspecified licenses (44% of hours). Temporally, the paper contrasts the absolute growth in represented languages (608) and countries (67) against the flat Gini coefficients since 2013, demonstrating that marginal additions of new languages or geographies have not shifted the overall distribution. The paper also contrasts dataset-level vs. collection-level license statistics for text, showing that 60% more individual datasets (75% more tokens) are commercially licensed than collections, because collections inherit the most restrictive license among their constituent datasets—meaning practitioners using collections face far more restrictions than the individual dataset counts suggest.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Data Provenance Collection (Longpre et al., 2024b) from a text-only audit of 44 collections to a multimodal audit spanning text, speech, and video (~4000 datasets, 1990–2024). It also methodologically extends prior license analysis by tracing not just dataset licenses but the terms of use of each dataset's original sources (e.g., YouTube's ToS, OpenAI's non-compete clause), a dimension absent from earlier single-modality audits. Prior work has examined isolated features—geographic bias (Birhane et al., 2023), hate content (Dodge et al., 2021), or text-only provenance (Elazar et al., 2023)—but no prior study provides a longitudinal, ecosystem-level, multimodal provenance audit at this scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that over 80% of source content carries non-commercial restrictions despite only ~25–33% of datasets having non-commercial licenses reveals a critical documentation gap that could expose practitioners to legal risk when sourcing training data. The demonstration that Gini coefficients for geographic and linguistic representation have not significantly improved since 2013—despite rising absolute counts—challenges the narrative of steady progress toward inclusive AI. Limitations include coverage of only public datasets, partial creator identity information, and inability to assess proprietary data that may fill representation gaps. Open questions include the legal enforceability of source terms, how to standardize provenance documentation across repackaged datasets, and whether the audit's modality-specific scoping protocols generalize to other modalities (images, 3D, tabular).
