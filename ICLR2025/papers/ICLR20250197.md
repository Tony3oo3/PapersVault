## Problem

Most leading computational models of the primate ventral visual stream are trained on object categorization, implicitly assuming the ventral stream is optimized for that objective. However, prior neurophysiological evidence shows the ventral stream also encodes rich spatial information (position, pose), yet the effect of training on spatial latent estimation on neural alignment remains largely unexplored due to the scarcity of large-scale datasets with comprehensive spatial labels.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250197/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each input is a synthetic RGB image from ThreeDWorld containing a single 3D object rendered at a random position and pose against a random background, with ground-truth labels in the camera's 3D reference frame. Outputs are task-dependent: continuous spatial estimates (distance Z, translation X/Y, or rotation as sin/cos pairs of three Euler angles totaling 6 units) or discrete labels (117 object categories or 548 object identities). Combined objectives average the per-task losses, and all continuous variables are normalized to unit variance before training.

## Method

The paper generates large-scale synthetic datasets (up to 100M images, 117 categories, 548 object models) via the ThreeDWorld Unity-based 3D engine, then trains ResNet-50/ResNet-18 with supervised learning on different subsets of spatial and category latents while holding architecture and data constant. Neural alignment is quantified via Brain-Score benchmarks across four ventral stream regions (V1, V2, V4, IT), and representational similarity across models is measured with centered kernel alignment (CKA) at four hierarchical layers. A key causal experiment manipulates non-target latent variability (e.g., reducing category diversity to one class) to test whether exposure to variability in untrained dimensions drives implicit learning of those dimensions. The central finding is that models trained on as few as one spatial latent (distance) achieve neural alignment scores (~0.399) comparable to models trained on 117 categories (~0.412), and that non-target latent variability in the training data partially explains why models trained on very different objectives converge to similar early-to-mid-layer representations.

## Results

ResNet-50 models trained to estimate just one spatial latent (object distance, 1 output unit) achieve a mean neural alignment score of 0.399 across V1/V2/V4/IT, reaching 97% of the score obtained by models trained on all spatial and classification tasks combined (0.411, 674 outputs). This is comparable to object category classification (117 outputs, 0.412) and only 7% below the ImageNet-1K reference (0.430). Neural alignment correlates strongly with spatial task performance: Pearson r = 0.975 for distance, 0.941 for translation, and 0.736 for rotation, mirroring the well-known correlation between categorization accuracy and neural alignment (r = 0.958 for category). CKA analysis reveals that models trained on different latents learn highly similar representations in early-to-mid layers (layer1.0–layer3.0), diverging only in the final layers (layer4.0).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the ImageNet-1K classification model (ResNet-50, 1000 outputs), which achieves a neural alignment of 0.430. Spatial latent models trained on the synthetic TDW-117 dataset reach 0.399–0.407, i.e., approximately 93–95% of the ImageNet-1K score, despite being trained exclusively on synthetic images. Compared to the object category baseline on the same TDW-117 dataset (0.412), the single-output distance model (0.399) is only 3% lower, and the 9-output full spatial model (0.405) is within 2%. The paper explicitly rejects the 'scaling hypothesis' (more supervised outputs → higher alignment) for spatial latents: adding outputs from 1 to 9 yields negligible gains (0.399→0.405), whereas for categorization, scaling from 2 to 117 categories produces a clear monotonic increase. On behavioral alignment, spatial latent models underperform category models (e.g., 0.107 vs. 0.440 for distance vs. category), though the authors note the behavioral benchmark is biased toward categorization tasks. In non-public IT benchmarks, spatial latent models underperform category models in 2 of 5 benchmarks but outperform them in 1, suggesting the gap is most pronounced in IT regions evaluated on out-of-distribution natural images.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Brain-Score framework (Schrimpf et al., 2018) and the line of work linking CNN training objectives to ventral-stream neural alignment (Yamins et al., 2014; Zhuang et al., 2021; Conwell et al., 2022) by asking whether spatial latent estimation—rather than object categorization—can serve as a sufficient training objective for producing brain-aligned representations. It repurposes the ThreeDWorld synthetic data engine (Gan et al., 2020) and CKA-based representation similarity analysis (Kornblith et al., 2019) in a new experimental design that holds architecture and data constant while varying only the supervised target. The paper also connects to the long-standing 'what vs. where' debate in visual neuroscience (Marr, 2010; Schneider, 1969; Connor & Knierim, 2017) by providing computational evidence that the ventral stream's function is not reducible to categorization alone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a single-output distance regression model achieves ~97% of the neural alignment of a 674-target model challenges the field's default assumption that the ventral stream was shaped primarily by categorization pressure, suggesting instead that it encodes a general-purpose visual representation supporting both 'what' and 'where.' The non-target latent variability mechanism offers a concrete, testable explanation for why different training objectives converge to similar representations, with implications for how we design brain-model comparison benchmarks. Key limitations include the gap between synthetic and natural-image alignment (especially in IT), weaker behavioral alignment for spatial models, and the reliance on regression-based scoring metrics; future work should explore whether improved rendering, broader scene diversity, or alternative representational similarity measures (e.g., RSA) would sharpen the conclusions.
