## Problem

Existing camera motion control methods for video generation either encode camera parameters into high-level embeddings (yielding imprecise, ambiguous outputs) or render partial warped frames as conditioning (neglecting temporal correlations across frames). No prior approach explicitly exploits the predictable pixel trajectories induced by camera motion as a strong inductive bias within the attention mechanism itself, leaving a gap between control precision and generation quality.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253700/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For image-based control, the method takes a single RGB image plus a sequence of camera poses (intrinsic matrix K and extrinsic [R|t] per frame) and produces a video where pixels follow the projected trajectories derived from estimated depth. For video-based control, it additionally requires point trajectories (via CoTracker) and occlusion masks to separate scene motion from camera motion. The method operates on SVD's 12-frame and 25-frame latent video modules and Open-Sora-Plan's 3D attention, with trajectories specified as per-frame 2D coordinates with validity masks indicating which pixels are in-bounds.

## Method

The core insight is that temporal attention in video diffusion models implicitly synthesizes and attends to pixel trajectories across frames; when these trajectories are known from camera motion, attention can be explicitly performed along them as a strong inductive bias. Rather than modifying temporal attention directly (which causes a conflict between short-range dynamics modeling and long-range trajectory alignment), the paper introduces trajectory attention as a separate auxiliary branch that shares the same input/output shape but uses distinct QKV parameters. Features are sampled along the given trajectories (Algorithm 1), multi-head attention is applied across frames at those trajectory positions, and results are back-projected to the spatial grid (Algorithm 2) and added as a residual to the temporal attention output. Training initializes the trajectory attention QKV projectors from the pre-trained temporal attention weights and the output projector to zero, requiring only ~24 GPU-hours of fine-tuning on 10k MiraData clips to achieve precise control while preserving the original model's generation quality.

## Results

On camera motion control for single images (Table 1), the proposed trajectory attention achieves ATE of 0.0212 m and RPE-rot of 0.1151° at 14 frames, outperforming MotionCtrl by over 50× in ATE (1.2151 m) while maintaining comparable FID (104.2 vs 101.3). At 25 frames, the method achieves ATE 0.0396 m, RPE-rot 0.1939°, and FID 103.5, surpassing CameraCtrl (ATE 0.0411, RPE-rot 0.3480°, FID 115.8) and NVS_Solver (ATE 0.1216, RPE-rot 0.4785°, FID 108.5) on all metrics. For video camera motion control (Table 2), the method reduces ATE from 0.5112 m (NVS_Solver) to 0.3572 m, and combining with NVS_Solver's frame-wise injection further improves FID to 112.2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MotionCtrl (Wang et al., 2024), trajectory attention achieves a dramatic 57× reduction in ATE (0.0212 vs 1.2151 m) and 16× reduction in RPE-rot (0.1151° vs 1.8372°) at 14 frames, though MotionCtrl retains a marginal FID advantage (101.3 vs 104.2), indicating slightly better perceptual quality at the cost of severe control imprecision. Against MotionI2V (Shi et al., 2024) at 16 frames, the proposed method wins on every metric, halving RPE-rot (0.1231° vs 0.2853°) and reducing FID by 12.4 points (108.7 vs 124.1). At 25 frames, the method outperforms both CameraCtrl (He et al., 2024) and NVS_Solver (You et al., 2024) on all four metrics, with the largest gap over NVS_Solver in ATE (0.0396 vs 0.1216 m, a 3× improvement) and RPE-rot (0.1939° vs 0.4785°). For video camera motion control, the method reduces ATE by 30% over NVS_Solver (0.3572 vs 0.5112 m) and improves FID by 5.2 points; combining with NVS_Solver's frame-wise injection yields the best FID (112.2). For first-frame-guided video editing, comparisons with I2VEdit (Ouyang et al., 2024) and AnyV2V (Ku et al., 2024) are qualitative only, with the paper reporting that trajectory attention better preserves edited content consistency over large spatial and temporal ranges.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Stable Video Diffusion's decomposed spatial-temporal attention architecture and positions trajectory attention as a new mechanism for camera motion control, departing from two dominant paradigms: high-level camera parameter conditioning (MotionCtrl, CameraCtrl) and frame-wise warped-view injection (NVS_Solver, ViewCrafter). Conceptually, it repurposes the trajectory attention idea from Patrick et al. (2021) in video recognition for generative video synthesis, and can be viewed as a stronger, more general form of the epipolar-constraint attention (Xu et al., 2024) and row-wise attention (Li et al., 2024) approaches. The key architectural departure is modeling trajectory attention as a separate auxiliary branch rather than modifying temporal attention in place, motivated by the conflicting objectives of short-range motion synthesis versus long-range trajectory consistency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The auxiliary-branch design is practically attractive: it requires only ~24 GPU hours of fine-tuning on a single A100, does not alter the base model's parameters, supports sparse trajectories, and generalizes from 12-frame to 25-frame generation without retraining. This makes fine-grained camera control accessible to practitioners who lack the compute for full model retraining. Open questions include how the method scales to much longer sequences (beyond 25 frames), whether it degrades when depth estimation or camera pose is noisy, and whether the trajectory extraction pipeline (relying on CoTracker or optical flow) becomes a bottleneck in complex scenes with occlusions or non-rigid motion.
