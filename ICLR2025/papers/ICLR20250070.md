## Problem

While LLMs have been applied to time series forecasting, their ability to understand and detect anomalies in time series data remains largely unexplored. Prevailing conjectures about LLMs' time series capabilities—such as reliance on token repetition bias, arithmetic reasoning, or chain-of-thought prompting—have not been validated through controlled experiments. This paper addresses this gap by formulating and empirically testing seven specific hypotheses about LLMs' time series understanding through the lens of anomaly detection.

## Input / Output

Time series are fed to multimodal LLMs in two modalities: as text (with four encoding strategies—raw space-separated numbers, CSV, Prompt-as-Prefix with summary statistics, and Token-per-Digit) or as Matplotlib-generated line plots. Text inputs can optionally be subsampled to 30% of their original length via linear interpolation to probe long-context sensitivity. The model outputs a JSON list of anomalous intervals (e.g., [{"start": 10, "end": 25}]), which are converted to binary per-timestep labels for evaluation against ground truth using affinity F1, a metric that accounts for temporal proximity of detected intervals.

## Method

The paper is a principled empirical study that formulates seven hypotheses about LLM time series understanding and tests each through controlled experiments across four M-LLMs (GPT-4o-mini, Qwen-VL-Chat, Gemini-1.5-Flash, InternVL2-Llama3-76B) with 21 prompting variants. Synthetic datasets are generated for four anomaly types (point, range, trend, frequency) plus noisy and flat-trend variants, each containing 400 sequences of 1000 timesteps with known ground-truth anomaly intervals. Key experimental manipulations include: CoT vs. direct prompting to test reasoning (H1), clean vs. noisy periodic data to test repetition bias (H2), in-context DysCalc examples to impair arithmetic while preserving logic (H3), text vs. vision modality comparison (H4), flat-trend vs. regular-trend anomalies to test human-like perceptual bias (H5), and original vs. subsampled sequences to test long-context degradation (H6). Performance is measured primarily via affinity F1, which penalizes detections that are close but non-overlapping, alongside standard precision, recall, and F1.

## Results

On the trend anomaly dataset, the best LLM variant (Gemini-1.5-Flash, 1-shot vision with Calc prompt) achieves 67.25% affinity F1, dramatically outperforming the best text-only variant (14.31%) and the Isolation Forest baseline (28.69%). On frequency anomalies, the best text variant (Gemini-1.5-Flash, 0-shot S0.3) reaches 53.59% affi-F1, while Isolation Forest achieves 60.25%, making frequency the one anomaly type where LLMs underperform the traditional baseline. Across all models, visual input consistently yields 2–5× higher affi-F1 than text input for trend, point, and range anomalies, while CoT prompting uniformly degrades performance (e.g., Gemini vision drops from 67.25% to 51.82% with CoT). Subsampling the time series to 30% of its original length (S0.3) is the single most effective text-side intervention, often doubling affi-F1 over the full-length text prompt.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four M-LLMs (Gemini-1.5-Flash, GPT-4o-Mini, InternVL2-76B, Qwen-VL-Chat) against two traditional baselines: Isolation Forest and simple thresholding (top/bottom 2%). On trend anomalies, the best LLM vision variant (Gemini, 67.25% affi-F1) outperforms Isolation Forest (28.69%) by nearly 2.4×, and the threshold baseline (26.45%) by 2.5×. On frequency anomalies, however, Isolation Forest (60.25%) surpasses the best LLM text variant (Gemini, 53.59%) and all vision variants, indicating that LLMs struggle with periodic-structure anomalies. The authors note that LLMs with proper prompts and visual input outperform both baselines on point, range, and trend datasets, but frequency anomalies remain a clear weakness, suggesting that Fourier-based preprocessing would be needed before feeding such data to an LLM. The threshold baseline, despite its simplicity, achieves competitive affi-F1 on trend (26.45%) because the anomaly ratio (~3–4%) closely matches the 2%+2% threshold, highlighting that the task is not trivially easy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by repurposing the hypothesis-testing framework from LLM-for-forecasting work (Gruver et al., 2023; Tan et al., 2024) and applying it to anomaly detection as a more revealing probe of 'understanding.' Rather than proposing a new detection method, it formulates six specific, testable hypotheses about LLM behavior (CoT reasoning, repetition bias, arithmetic, visual preference, perceptual bias, long-context degradation) and designs controlled experiments to validate or reject each. It extends the evaluation scope beyond text-only LLMs to multimodal LLMs (M-LLMs), addressing a gap noted in prior work where humans naturally detect time series anomalies through visual inspection but no systematic study had compared text vs. image inputs for M-LLMs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's systematic refutation of multiple widely-cited conjectures (repetition bias, arithmetic dependence, CoT benefit) provides much-needed empirical grounding for a field that has relied heavily on intuition. Practically, the finding that visual input dramatically outperforms text (e.g., ~60 vs. ~14 affinity-F1 for Gemini on trend anomalies) and that CoT consistently degrades performance offers clear design guidance for building LLM-based anomaly detection systems. Key limitations include reliance on synthetic data (though Yahoo S5 confirms trends), evaluation of only four models, and the open question of whether LLMs can detect subtle real-world anomalies beyond the synthetic patterns tested. Future work should explore whether preprocessing (e.g., Fourier analysis for frequency anomalies) combined with LLMs can close the remaining gap to specialized detectors.
