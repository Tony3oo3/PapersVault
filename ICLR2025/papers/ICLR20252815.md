## Problem

Video MLLMs exhibit an 'anti-scaling law' where increasing data volume or model parameters degrades temporal modeling performance. The paper identifies the root cause as 'temporal hacking'—a form of reward hacking where models shortcut by attending to only a few key frames (typically the first or last) rather than processing the full video narrative. This misalignment between the proxy reward (video-text pair consistency) and the true objective (comprehensive spatiotemporal understanding) is formalized through a reinforcement learning lens.

## Input / Output

The model ingests a video as a sequence of 16–32 uniformly sampled frames (at 384×384 resolution) alongside a text query, processed through a SigLIP vision encoder and a Qwen2-7B LLM connected by a 2-layer GeLU-MLP projector. The UTR training data is constructed by extracting per-frame spatiotemporal attributes (bounding boxes, identity, action) via GRiT/Grounding DINO and associating them into subject trajectories with ByteTrack, then formulating bidirectional query tasks where the model must predict complete subject information from randomly sampled attribute cues. Outputs are free-form text responses or structured trajectory descriptions with per-frame bounding boxes.

## Method

The paper first formalizes video-language modeling as a sequential MDP and defines temporal hacking as a reward-hacking phenomenon where the optimal policy under the proxy reward (next-token cross-entropy on sparse captions) diverges from the true objective of comprehensive frame-by-frame understanding. It introduces the Temporal Perplexity (TPL) score—the difference in perplexity between full-video context and single-frame context—as a reliable metric for quantifying hacking severity, validated through attention-map analysis showing higher TPL correlates with broader frame activation. Guided by two design principles (high frame information density and high inter-frame information dynamics), the Unhackable Temporal Rewarding (UTR) framework has two components: (1) data modeling, which constructs training samples from spatiotemporal attribute trajectories extracted by expert models, and (2) task modeling, which performs bidirectional querying where random subject attributes at random frames serve as queries and the model must reconstruct the full trajectory, forcing attention across all frames. The resulting Video-UTR model is trained in two stages (modality alignment on 558K image-text pairs, then multi-task joint training on ~1.1M video samples) and achieves state-of-the-art video understanding with substantially less data than competitors.

## Results

Video-UTR (Qwen2-7B, 1.1M training samples) achieves the best MVBench score of 58.8% and TempCompass score of 59.7% among all compared video MLLMs, outperforming LLaVA-OneVision (56.7%, 59.0%) and LLaVA-NeXT-Video (54.6%, N/A). On VideoMME, Video-UTR scores 52.6%, trailing LLaVA-OneVision (58.2%) but substantially exceeding LLaVA-NeXT-Video (33.7%). On video QA benchmarks, Video-UTR achieves 73.5% accuracy on MSVD-QA and 58.3% on MSRVTT-QA, ranking second to PLLaVA (76.6%, 62.0%) but using far less training data (1.1M vs. 1M with parameter-free extension). Notably, Video-UTR also achieves competitive image understanding, scoring 76.6% on MMBench and 88.9% on POPE, matching or exceeding dedicated image MLLMs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the closest same-architecture competitor LLaVA-OneVision (Qwen2-7B, 1.6M data), Video-UTR wins MVBench by 2.1 points (58.8 vs. 56.7) and TempCompass by 0.7 points (59.7 vs. 59.0), but loses VideoMME by 5.6 points (52.6 vs. 58.2). Compared to the base LLaVA-NeXT-Video architecture (860K data), Video-UTR improves MVBench by 4.2 points (58.8 vs. 54.6) and VideoMME by 18.9 points (52.6 vs. 33.7), demonstrating the impact of UTR data and task modeling. Against the much larger VideoLLaMA2 (13.4M data, LLaMA2-7B), Video-UTR achieves higher MVBench (58.8 vs. 54.6) and VideoMME (52.6 vs. 46.6) with roughly 12× less training data. On video QA benchmarks, PLLaVA (parameter-free, 1M data) remains the strongest baseline, outperforming Video-UTR on MSVD-QA (76.6 vs. 73.5), MSRVTT-QA (62.0 vs. 58.3), and TGIF-QA (77.5 vs. 56.4), though Video-UTR narrows the gap substantially over earlier models. On the long-range MMBench-Video benchmark, Video-UTR-7B matches Claude-3.5-Sonnet (1.35 overall) and surpasses PLLaVA-34B (1.16) and LLaVA-NeXT-Video-34B (1.13) despite being 5× smaller in parameter count.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the LLaVA-NeXT-Video architecture (SigLIP + Qwen2-7B + MLP projector) but introduces a fundamentally different conceptual lens: it reframes video-language modeling as a Markov decision process and identifies 'temporal hacking'—a form of reward hacking from RL theory—as the root cause of the previously observed 'anti-scaling law' in video MLLMs. It departs from prior video MLLM work (VideoChat, VideoLLaMA, PLLaVA, LLaVA-NeXT-Video) that focused on architectural innovations or data scaling, instead proposing that the proxy reward (video-text pair) is misaligned with the true objective (comprehensive spatiotemporal understanding). The bidirectional querying mechanism draws inspiration from Merlin (Yu et al., 2024), while the data construction pipeline repurposes existing expert models (GRiT, Grounding DINO, ByteTrack) in a novel spatiotemporal attribute extraction and trajectory construction workflow.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The TPL score provides a practical, automated diagnostic for video-text data quality that correlates with frame activation patterns, potentially redirecting how the community curates training data for video MLLMs. The two design principles (high frame information density, high inter-frame dynamics) offer a principled framework for constructing video-language data that resists shortcut learning. Key limitations include reliance on expert model accuracy for attribute extraction and limited gains on knowledge-based QA tasks. Open questions include whether a single MLLM can replace the multi-model pipeline for attribute extraction, and whether RL post-training (DPO, PPO, GRPO) can further strengthen temporal comprehension beyond the supervised UTR framework.
