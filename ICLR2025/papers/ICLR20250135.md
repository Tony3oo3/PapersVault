## Problem

It is unclear whether LLMs can acquire knowledge about themselves that is neither contained in nor inferable from their training data. This paper tests whether models have 'privileged access' to facts about their own behavioral tendencies — knowledge that a different model, even one explicitly trained on the first model's ground-truth behavior, cannot replicate. A positive result would challenge the view that LLMs merely imitate their training distributions.

## Input / Output

The model receives a hypothetical question that embeds an object-level prompt and asks for a specific property of the model's response to that prompt (e.g., the second character, even/odd parity, or whether the answer aligns with a wealth-seeking stance). The model must output only the property value, not the full response, and is explicitly disallowed from using chain-of-thought reasoning. For calibration evaluation, the model's output is treated as a probability distribution over behavior properties, compared against the empirical distribution of the model's object-level behavior sampled 20 times at temperature 1.

## Method

The core experimental design pairs a self-prediction-trained model M1 with a cross-prediction-trained model M2, both finetuned on the same ~30,000 hypothetical questions derived from M1's ground-truth behavior at temperature 0. If M1 consistently outperforms M2 on held-out tasks, this indicates M1 uses introspective access rather than relying solely on training data. The authors test this across multiple model pairs (GPT-4o, GPT-4, Llama-3-70B, GPT-3.5) in both directions to rule out capability confounds, and further validate with calibration analysis (Mean Absolute Deviation) and a behavioral-change experiment where M1 is finetuned on another model's object-level behavior and must update its self-predictions without ever seeing the new hypothetical answers. Finetuning uses LoRA (via Fireworks API) for Llama and OpenAI's finetuning API for GPT models, with one epoch to limit memorization.

## Results

In the cross-prediction experiment, self-prediction models consistently outperform cross-prediction counterparts on held-out tasks (p < 0.01). Llama-3-70B predicts its own behavior at 48.5% accuracy versus 31.8% for GPT-4o cross-predicting it (+17% advantage); GPT-4o self-predicts at 49.4% versus 36.6% for Llama-3-70B cross-predicting it (+12.8% advantage). Self-prediction training improves GPT-4o's average accuracy from 32.6% to 49.4% on held-out datasets. In the behavioral-change experiment, the finetuned model M_C predicts its new behavior at 35.4% accuracy versus only 21.7% for its old behavior, despite never being trained on the new hypothetical answers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between self-prediction (M1 predicting itself) and cross-prediction (M2, trained on M1's ground-truth behavior data, predicting M1). Self-prediction wins across all model pairs: Llama-3-70B beats GPT-4o's cross-prediction by 16.7 percentage points, and GPT-4o beats Llama-3-70B's cross-prediction by 12.8 points, ruling out explanations based on raw model capability. The mode baseline (always guessing the most common response) is substantially below all trained models, confirming that training is necessary. A data-scaling analysis shows cross-prediction accuracy plateaus (e.g., GPT-4o cross-predicting Llama-3-70B plateaus at 35.2% after 10,000 samples) and does not close the gap with self-prediction (48.5%), ruling out insufficient training data as an explanation. On calibration (MAD on held-out animals task), self-prediction trained models achieve MAD of 8.7 versus 21.3 for cross-prediction and 23.0 for untrained models, indicating self-prediction models access distributional information not present in their training data. The behavioral-change experiment further confirms introspection: M_C (GPT-4o finetuned on Claude-3.5-Sonnet behavior) predicts its new behavior at 35.4% versus 21.7% for its old behavior, despite never seeing the new hypothetical answers during training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing a testable framework for 'introspection' in LLMs—privileged access to one's own behavioral tendencies that cannot be derived from training data. It builds conceptually on the 'knows what it knows' line of work (Kadavath et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2022b) and out-of-context reasoning (Berglund et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024), but explicitly distinguishes itself: unlike OOCR where facts are logically implied by training data, introspective facts are not; unlike knowledge-calibration work, the target is behavioral properties (second character, even/odd, ethical stance) rather than answer correctness. The cross-prediction experimental design—comparing a model's self-prediction against a stronger model trained on the same ground-truth data—is a novel methodological contribution that rules out memorization and data-access explanations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LLMs can acquire self-knowledge not contained in or inferable from training data challenges the standard view of LLMs as pure distribution imitators and opens a path toward model transparency: if introspection can be extended to complex tasks, models could report on their own dispositions, internal objectives, and behavioral tendencies in ways external observers cannot. However, the paper is candid about limitations—models fail on tasks requiring simulation of longer outputs (e.g., predicting a story's main character) and do not generalize to out-of-distribution self-knowledge tasks like coordination or situational awareness. The paper also flags risks: enhanced introspection could enable self-coordination, steganography, or evaluation-gaming, making the safety implications of this capability a critical open question.
