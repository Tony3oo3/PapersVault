## Problem

State-of-the-art LLMs tend to presuppose a single interpretation of ambiguous user requests rather than asking clarifying questions, frustrating users who intended a different reading. The authors attribute this to standard RLHF preference labeling, which evaluates responses only on prior context (single-turn), making it difficult for annotators to assess the downstream utility of a clarifying question and introducing biases toward complete but presumptuous answers over incomplete clarifying ones.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252185/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes an ambiguous user query and, when it generates a clarifying question, receives a simulated user's clarifying answer before producing a final response. The output is a set of k answers (one per simulated user interpretation), evaluated by F1 against the corresponding gold answer set, with an additional efficiency metric tracking the number of model turns. The system can also directly answer without clarification, and the evaluation enforces that answers must be correct for their corresponding clarifying answer, holding QA-with-Clarification to a higher standard than single-turn QA.

## Method

The core contribution is a double-turn preference annotation scheme where preference labels are assigned based on simulated future interaction outcomes rather than prior context alone. Multiple simulated users respond to candidate clarifying questions, observe the LLM's subsequent answer, and assign preferences based on whether the completed two-turn interaction fulfilled their intended interpretation; preferences are then aggregated across users, with ties between direct-answer and clarifying-question responses broken in favor of direct answers to prevent unnecessary clarification. Training proceeds in two stages: SFT on clarifying question data (generated from both human-annotated and model-predicted feasible answer sets via GPT-4), followed by DPO using the double-turn preference labels. The 'Match' variant, which ranks clarifying questions by average EM accuracy of predicted answers against each user's gold answer, achieves the best results, yielding consistent 4–5% F1 improvements over single-turn preference baselines across Llama2-7b, Gemma-7b, and Llama3-8b.

## Results

On the AmbigQA test set (n=1,960), the proposed Clarify DPO w/ Match method achieves the best Answer F1 across all three base models: 32.8 (Llama2-7b), 36.5 (Llama3-8b), and 33.9 (Gemma-7b), representing a consistent 4–5% improvement over the strongest single-turn preference baseline. The Clarify-or-Direct-Ans DPO variant, which selectively asks clarifying questions, achieves 24.3/29.1/24.6 F1 (Llama2/Llama3/Gemma) at ~1.5–1.6 average turns, balancing efficiency and effectiveness. On the unambiguous split, the Match method also outperforms all baselines (e.g., 38.3 vs. 31.0 for Llama2), showing that clarifying questions help recover correct answers even when queries are not explicitly ambiguous.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Starling-RM single-turn reward model (Zhu et al., 2023), the proposed Match method wins by a large margin on Llama2 (32.8 vs. 28.3 All F1, +4.5) and Gemma (33.9 vs. 29.5, +4.4), but shows a smaller gap on Llama3 (36.5 vs. 30.9, +5.6). Against the likelihood-based scoring from Andukuri et al. (2024), the Match method wins on Llama2 (32.8 vs. 27.2, +5.6) and Gemma (33.9 vs. 31.5, +2.4), but is slightly behind on Llama3 (36.5 vs. 35.9, +0.6). For the dialogue-policy baselines (ProCoT and PPDPP from Deng et al., 2023), the paper's Clarify-or-Direct-Ans DPO outperforms ProCoT on Answer F1 (24.3 vs. 15.6) and Direct-Answer Accuracy (61.9% vs. 60.1%), while PPDPP achieves higher Ambiguity Accuracy (59.0% vs. 53.7%) but lower Answer F1 (23.9 vs. 24.3) and DA Accuracy (57.7% vs. 61.9%). Overall, the double-turn Match method consistently achieves the highest F1 across all three base models, confirming that modeling future conversation outcomes yields better clarifying questions than single-turn preference signals.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RLHF/DPO training pipeline by replacing single-turn preference labels with double-turn preferences that simulate the full clarifying-question interaction before assigning reward. It builds most directly on Andukuri et al. (2024, Star-Gate), which also trains LLMs to ask clarifying questions via user simulation, but departs by using DPO with outcome-based preferences rather than SFT with filtered questions, and by aggregating preferences across multiple simulated users rather than a single persona. The multi-annotator preference aggregation strategy contrasts with prior work (Wang et al., 2024; Kopf et al., 2023) that collapses annotator judgments via majority vote, preserving individual interpretations to identify whether a clarifying question accommodates the full range of user intents.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The double-turn preference framework offers a general recipe for training interactive LLMs where the utility of a response depends on downstream outcomes rather than surface quality, and the authors note it can be adapted to other tasks requiring only multiple annotator labels and a task-specific metric. However, the evaluation is limited to one- and two-turn interactions on short factoid QA, and the 4–5% F1 gain, while consistent across three base models, is modest in absolute terms. Open questions include whether the approach extends to multi-turn clarification chains, richer dialogue acts (abstention, multi-answer responses), and whether the simulated-user evaluation faithfully captures real user behavior at scale.
