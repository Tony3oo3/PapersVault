## Problem

Contemporary CATE estimation models are typically validated on semi-synthetic benchmarks with simulated outcomes or evaluated via proxy losses that share assumptions with the models themselves, creating self-serving bias. No prior large-scale study has assessed whether these models actually capture real-world heterogeneity when evaluated against ground-truth MSE on observational data with known propensity. This paper asks: do 16 modern CATE models, tested across 43,200 real-world dataset variants, meaningfully outperform trivial baselines?

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes 12 large RCT datasets (ranging from ~19K to ~14M samples, tabular covariates with binary treatment and continuous/binary outcomes) and a biasing function G(t,x) implemented as a 1–3 layer MLP to generate selection-biased estimation subsets of 1K–8K samples. For each of 16 trained CATE estimators, the method outputs a Q̂ score computed on a held-out RCT evaluation subset, from which model rankings by MSE and a binary degeneracy flag (Q̂ ≥ 0, meaning worse than a zero-effect predictor) are derived. The key property is that Q̂ requires only factual outcomes and known propensity—no counterfactual ground truth—making it computable on real RCT data.

## Method

The paper defines a statistical parameter Q(τ̂) = E[τ̂²(X)] − 2E[τ(X)τ̂(X)] equal to MSE minus the unobservable constant E[τ²(X)], so it preserves oracle model ranking. It then proves that Q̂ = (1/N)Σ[τ̂²(xₙ) − 2τ̂(xₙ)η(xₙ,tₙ,yₙ)], where η is the Horvitz-Thompson estimator, is an unbiased estimator of Q when the propensity is known, with O(1/√N) convergence. To make propensity known at evaluation time, the benchmark uses observational sampling: a biased estimation subset D_est is drawn from an RCT via a nonlinear MLP-based biasing function, models are trained on D_est, and Q̂ is computed on a disjoint RCT evaluation subset D_eval where propensity is constant. A control-variates framework is introduced showing that R-loss and DR-loss are special cases of variance-reduced Q̂ variants, unifying them under a single unbiased-estimator umbrella without requiring orthogonality assumptions. The resulting benchmark (16 models × 12 datasets × 36 sampling settings × 100 repeats = 43,200 variants) reveals that 62% of CATE estimates are degenerate and 80% fail to beat a constant-effect baseline.

## Results

Across 43,200 sampled variants from 12 real-world RCT datasets, 62% of all fitted CATE estimators are degenerate (higher MSE than a trivial zero-effect predictor). Among the 25,440 datasets where a constant-effect baseline (dml.lasso) is non-degenerate, only 20% of CATE estimators outperform it. The best-performing model, s.xgb.cv (S-learner with XGBoost), achieves a 25.5% win share and just 6.3% degenerate rate, while orthogonality-based models (DML, R-learner, DR-learner, causal forest) collectively win only 30% of the time and have a 71% average degenerate rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark evaluates 16 CATE models spanning six method families. S-learners (Künzel et al., 2019) dominate: s.xgb.cv achieves the highest win share (25.5%) and lowest degenerate rate (6.3%), while s.ridge.cv has the best average rank (4.2). DragonNet (Shi et al., 2019) is the third-best model (12.1% win share, 43.8% degenerate rate). In stark contrast, orthogonality-based models underperform: R-learners (Nie & Wager, 2020) have 61–85% degenerate rates, DR-learners (Kennedy, 2023) have 59–71%, Double ML (Chernozhukov et al., 2017) ranges from 48% (dml.lasso/elastic) to 99% (dml.xgb), and causal forests (Athey et al., 2018) achieve only 0.5% win share with 76% degenerate rate. T-learners (Künzel et al., 2019) also perform poorly, with t.xgb.cv at 76.7% degenerate rate. The paper's key finding is that despite widespread theoretical optimism, orthogonality-based models (DML, R, DR, cforest) collectively outperform other models only 30% of the time, and 80% of non-degenerate CATE estimates still fail to beat a simple constant-effect baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the observational sampling methodology from LaLonde (1986) and Gentzel et al. (2021)—previously used only for ATE evaluation—to the CATE setting, and introduces a new evaluation parameter Q (MSE minus an unobservable constant) with an unbiased estimator Q̂ built on the Horvitz-Thompson estimator. A key conceptual departure is that existing CATE evaluation either removes potential-outcome risk via simulation or manages both outcome and propensity risks via orthogonality-based proxy losses, whereas this paper removes propensity estimation risk entirely by evaluating on RCT data. The control-variates framework from Glynn & Szechtman (2002) is repurposed to show that R-loss and DR-loss are special cases of variance-reduced Q̂ variants, unifying previously separate evaluation criteria under one theoretical umbrella.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark's central finding—that 62% of CATE estimates are worse than a zero-effect predictor and orthogonality-based models win only 30% of the time—challenges the field's reliance on simulation-driven validation and raises fundamental questions about whether theoretical guarantees (doubly robustness, Neyman orthogonality) translate to real-world performance. Open questions include why orthogonality-based models underperform despite their mathematical elegance, whether the small training set sizes (1k–8k) used in the benchmark exaggerate the problem, and whether the Q̂ framework generalizes to settings where RCT data is unavailable. The paper also highlights the underappreciated value of heterogeneity detection methods and calls for broader, more representative benchmarks beyond the small semi-synthetic datasets (IHDP, ACIC) that have dominated the field.
