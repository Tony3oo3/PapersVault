## Problem

Prior AI control work (Greenblatt et al., 2023) assumes a single safety failure is unacceptable and relies on immediate shutdown upon detecting misalignment. Many real-world deployments, however, tolerate occasional harmful actions where no single output is catastrophic but cumulative failures are undesirable. This paper formulates and addresses the 'distributed threat setting,' where the deployer must balance safety and usefulness over long task sequences without being able to confidently detect misalignment from any single action.

## Input / Output

The system takes a sequence of N coding problems (from APPS), an untrusted strong model U (GPT-4), a trusted weak model T (GPT-3.5-Turbo or Claude variants), and a reference distribution of benign and malicious outputs with associated monitor, sneakiness, and efficacy scores. For each task, it produces a code solution selected from multiple micro-protocol candidates, along with continuously updated Bayesian credences over hypotheses parameterized by misbehavior rate r and sneakiness tradeoff β. The deployer is never told whether U is benign or malign; it must infer alignment purely from observed monitor scores across the deployment.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-level deployment architecture. At the micro-protocol level, six pipelines (Query Trusted, Query Untrusted, Edit Untrusted, Untrusted Plan, Filtered Plan, Edited Plan) each generate a candidate output and monitor score for a single task, using T to harness and scrutinize U's outputs at varying safety-usefulness tradeoffs. At the macro-protocol level, a Bayesian expected utility maximizer maintains a credence vector over hypotheses h=(r,β) and updates it via Bayes' rule using monitor scores compared against a reference distribution. The optimal prior is derived as a Nash equilibrium between the red team's mixed strategy over malign policies and the blue team's prior, ensuring worst-case optimality. To prevent overconfidence from finite reference data, the method bootstraps over random subsamples of the reference distribution and averages the resulting credence vectors. The macro-protocol selects the micro-protocol output with the highest expected utility at each step, dynamically shifting toward safer protocols as evidence of misalignment accumulates.

## Results

On 10,000-task APPS sequences with GPT-4 as the untrusted model and GPT-3.5-Turbo as the trusted monitor, the adaptive macro-protocol retains 98.4% of the untrusted model's usefulness while achieving 99.2% safety (80 backdoors out of 10,000 tasks). Directly deploying the untrusted model yields 100% usefulness but only 86.4% safety, while exclusively using the trusted model achieves 100% safety at just 23% usefulness. At a fixed level of usefulness, the adaptive strategy reduces backdoors by 80% compared to non-adaptive baselines (Static-EV and Static-Threshold). Performance improves continuously over the deployment sequence, with the adaptive protocol already outperforming static baselines after only 10 observed tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the adaptive macro-protocol against two non-adaptive baselines: Static-EV (same Bayesian expected utility calculation but with fixed initial credences, no updating) and Static-Threshold (a fixed safety threshold η applied to monitor scores). The adaptive method dominates both baselines on the safety-usefulness Pareto frontier, reducing backdoors by 80% at a given usefulness level. Against the untrusted-model-only upper bound (100% usefulness, 86.4% safety), the adaptive method sacrifices only ~1.6% of usefulness while gaining 12.8 percentage points of safety. Against the Query-Trust-only lower bound (100% safety, 23% usefulness), the adaptive method retains 98.4% of the untrusted model's usefulness at 99.2% safety. Ensembling all six micro-protocols outperforms any individual micro-protocol, with the two simplest (Query Untrusted and Query Trusted) accounting for the majority of selections while the plan-based protocols (Untrusted Plan, Filtered Plan, Edited Plan) provide complementary safety at intermediate α values.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Greenblatt et al. (2023)'s control evaluation framework for untrusted LLMs from a single-failure-is-catastrophic setting to a 'distributed threat setting' where no individual action is catastrophic but cumulative failures are undesirable. While Greenblatt et al. relied on high-quality auditing to immediately shut down a model upon detecting misalignment, this paper introduces a two-level architecture (micro-protocols for single-task monitoring plus an adaptive macro-protocol with Bayesian credence updating) that dynamically adjusts safety posture over a deployment sequence. The Nash equilibrium formulation for finding worst-case-optimal priors and the use of a reference distribution for estimating monitor score likelihoods are novel methodological contributions layered on top of the existing control evaluation paradigm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work addresses a more realistic deployment scenario than prior AI control work, where occasional safety failures are tolerable and the goal is to optimize the safety-usefulness tradeoff over long sequences rather than prevent every possible failure. Key open questions include: how the approach scales to more complex, multi-file code generation or less structured domains; how to handle distributional shift in the reference distribution over time; and whether the memoryless red team assumption holds for truly sophisticated misaligned models. The finding that the capability gap between trusted and untrusted models dramatically affects monitoring performance (AUROC dropping from 0.94 to 0.67) highlights a practical constraint that will become more acute as model generations diverge in capability.
