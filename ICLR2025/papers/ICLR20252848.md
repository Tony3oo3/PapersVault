## Problem

Vanilla Prompt Tuning, while effective for conventional NLP tasks, provides limited or even negative improvement on complex multi-step reasoning tasks. The paper identifies that soft prompts can help some instances while harming others, with the root cause being 'information accumulation'—certain soft prompt tokens exert disproportionate influence over later reasoning steps, disrupting the information flow pattern that correct reasoning requires.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252848/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference time, the method takes a text question, a set of learned soft prompt vectors (length 100, prepended to the input embedding), and the model's chain-of-thought reasoning output. It performs a full forward pass to compute per-token saliency scores in the final layer, then conditionally modifies the soft prompt vectors by masking one key token and sparsely zeroing the smallest 10% of remaining embedding values. The output is the regenerated reasoning answer using the corrupted prompts. A notable constraint is that the trigger requires a complete reasoning pass before corruption can be applied, increasing inference cost.

## Method

DPC is a two-stage, instance-level inference-time correction for soft prompts. Stage 1 (Dynamic Trigger) computes the proportion R of 'affected tokens' in the latter half of the rationale—tokens whose saliency score toward soft prompts in the final layer exceeds a threshold β (set to the average prompt-to-rationale flow intensity). If R exceeds a dataset-specific ratio, corruption is triggered. Stage 2 (Dynamic Corruption) locates the single soft prompt token position with the most intense information accumulation (measured via saliency in shallow layers, where a token's combined flow to question and rationale exceeds 10× the mean), masks that token's vector, and zeros out the smallest 10% of embedding values across all other prompt tokens. The key analytical insight is that correct reasoning shows attention shifting from soft prompts to earlier rationale steps and the question in deeper layers, while incorrect reasoning retains excessive attention to soft prompts in those same layers.

## Results

DPC achieves 4%–8% accuracy gains over vanilla prompt tuning across three LLMs (LLaMA2-13B, LLaMA3-8B, Mistral-7B) on three complex reasoning benchmarks. On LLaMA3-8B, DPC reaches 67.6% on GSM8K, 36.3% on MATH, and 42.5% on AQuA, compared to 65.5%, 33.7%, and 38.5% for prompt tuning. The largest single-task improvement is +8.7% on AQuA with LLaMA2-13B (22.4% → 31.1%), while the smallest is +0.3% on GSM8K with LLaMA2-13B (38.1% → 41.9%). DPC also outperforms the ACT baseline on every model-task pair, with the gap most pronounced on LLaMA3-8B GSM8K (67.6% vs. 52.6%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against vanilla prompt tuning, DPC wins on all nine model–task combinations, with gains ranging from +0.3% (GSM8K, LLaMA2-13B) to +8.7% (AQuA, LLaMA2-13B). Against ACT (attention calibration), DPC dominates across the board; ACT actually degrades LLaMA3-8B GSM8K to 52.6% (below the pretrained 64.9%) while DPC lifts it to 67.6%, and ACT provides zero improvement over prompt tuning on Mistral-7B. In the supplementary PEFT comparison (Table 3), DPC beats prefix tuning on 8 of 9 cells (losing only Mistral GSM8K: 51.1 vs. 54.4) and substantially outperforms LoRA, which collapses to 12.7% on LLaMA2-13B GSM8K and 44.1% on LLaMA3-8B GSM8K. On the three additional generalization datasets (prontoQA, ARC-challenge, WorldTree), DPC improves over prompt tuning on every cell, e.g., +6.4% on WorldTree with LLaMA3-8B (39.1% → 45.5%) and +2.6% on ARC-challenge with Mistral-7B (14.0% → 16.6%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Prompt Tuning (Lester et al., 2021) by introducing an inference-time correction mechanism rather than modifying the training procedure. It draws on saliency score analysis (Dai et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023a) as an interpretability lens, distinguishing itself from attention-sink work (StreamingLLM, ACT, OPERA, DOPRA) by combining gradients with attention to trace information flow across multiple input components (prompt, question, rationale). Within the broader PEFT landscape (LoRA, Prefix-tuning, P-tuning), DPC is positioned as a lightweight post-hoc fix that operates on already-trained soft prompts without retraining, targeting a specific failure mode—erroneous information accumulation in later reasoning steps—that prior PEFT methods do not address.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that vanilla prompt tuning can actively degrade complex reasoning performance (not merely fail to improve it) challenges the implicit assumption in PEFT literature that adding trainable parameters is at worst neutral. The saliency-based diagnosis of information accumulation provides a mechanistic explanation that could inform future prompt design. However, the method's practical utility is limited by its requirement for a complete reasoning pass before corruption can be applied, increasing inference cost, and the 4–8% gains, while consistent, are moderate. Open questions include whether the information-accumulation phenomenon generalizes to larger or differently-architected models, whether the corruption strategy (masking one key token plus sparse masking) is near-optimal, and whether the approach extends to multi-turn or agentic reasoning settings.
