## Problem

Prior work on LLM memorization has studied two extremes: the desirable reproduction of short n-grams and atomic facts, and the adversarial extraction of long verbatim training sequences. The intermediate regime—how much of an LLM's output to natural, benign prompts overlaps with existing web text—remained unmeasured, leaving open whether production conversational models routinely reproduce training data in everyday use and whether this differs meaningfully from human writing.

## Input / Output

The method takes benign user prompts (3,696 prompts across 15 tasks spanning creative, expository, and argumentative writing, plus 72,839 real-world conversations from WildChat and LMSYS-Chat-1M) and produces, for each LLM generation, an overlap rate (fraction of characters belonging to a verbatim substring of at least 50 characters found in a 10 TB web corpus) and the full distribution of reproduced substring lengths. The 50-character threshold is deliberately shorter than the ~150–200 character (50-token) threshold used in adversarial-extraction studies, and prompt-derived overlap is discounted via longest-common-substring matching before scoring. Human-written baselines for the same tasks (posted after model cutoff dates) are scored identically to establish a novelty gap.

## Method

The paper introduces a measurement pipeline for non-adversarial reproduction: for every character in an LLM generation, it finds the longest surrounding substring that appears verbatim in AUXDATASET (a 10 TB proxy for training data), discounts any overlap with the prompt, and reports the fraction of characters covered by matches of at least 50 characters as the overlap rate. Generations are sampled from 11 production models (GPT-4o family, Claude 3 family, Llama 3.1, Gemini 1.5) at temperature 0.7 across 15 task types, and the same metric is applied to human-written texts to quantify the LLM–human reproduction gap. The analysis reveals that expository tasks elicit 3–10× more overlap than creative tasks, that reproduction is long-tailed (up to ~100% overlap in worst cases), and that code snippets are disproportionately affected. As a mitigation study, the authors test two system-prompt strategies (a standard assistant prompt and a prompt explicitly discouraging reproduction), finding that prompting reduces average overlap by up to 10 percentage points but cannot eliminate the long tail of very long verbatim reproductions.

## Results

Across 11 production LLMs (GPT-4o family, Claude 3 family, Llama 3.1, Gemini 1.5), the average overlap rate — the fraction of generated text contained in a verbatim 50+ character match with the public Web (AUXDATASET) — ranges from ~7% (Gemini 1.5) to >15% (Claude 3 Opus). Expository writing tasks elicit 3×–10× more reproduction than creative writing, with encyclopedia prompts reaching an average overlap rate above 27%. In worst cases, models generate responses where nearly 100% of the content matches existing online text, and the longest single reproduced string reaches 1,170 characters (Claude 3 Opus, a Wikipedia article on black holes). Prompting (a specific anti-reproduction system prompt) reduces the mean overlap rate by up to 10 percentage points (e.g., Llama 3.1 from ~16% to ~6%) but still leaves occasional reproductions of 600–700 character strings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between LLM generations and human-written text on the same prompts. LLMs consistently produce higher overlap rates than humans across all three text types (creative, expository, argumentative): the median overlap rate for humans is 0% in every category, while LLM medians reach up to 7.5%. The one exception is the human *mean* on argumentative writing (IMDb movie reviews), which exceeds 7% — but this is attributed to blatant plagiarism by a subset of IMDb users (101 of 135 high-overlap reviews were verbatim copies of older reviews), not natural writing. A second key comparison is known vs. unseen events: when LLMs write news about events within their training data ('Known'), the overlap rate is nearly 2× higher than for post-cutoff events ('Unseen'), confirming that memorization — not just generic language patterns — drives reproduction. Finally, the paper compares no system prompt vs. a standard assistant prompt vs. a specific anti-reproduction prompt: the specific prompt yields the largest reduction (up to 10 pp for Llama 3.1), while the assistant prompt gives a smaller but consistent ~3 pp decrease; neither eliminates the long tail of very long reproductions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as studying an intermediate regime of LLM memorization between two previously characterized extremes: natural reproduction of short n-grams (measured via linguistic novelty benchmarks by McCoy et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2024) and adversarial extraction of long verbatim training sequences (Carlini et al., 2021; Nasr et al., 2023). It directly reuses the AUXDATASET web corpus and substring-matching methodology from Nasr et al. (2023), but applies it to benign, natural user prompts rather than adversarial extraction attacks, and lowers the reproduction threshold from 50 tokens to 50 characters to capture a more granular middle ground. The human-written baselines and the comparison across creative, expository, and argumentative text types represent a new analytical dimension not present in prior memorization studies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that production LLMs routinely reproduce 8–15% of their output as verbatim web text in completely benign interactions directly contradicts the industry position (e.g., OpenAI, 2024) that data 'regurgitation' is rare outside adversarial settings, with practical implications for users who may inadvertently redistribute copyrighted or private content. The paper shows that prompting mitigation reduces average-case reproduction but cannot eliminate worst-case verbatim copies of 600+ characters, suggesting that stronger model-level defenses are needed. Open questions include whether the 50-character threshold is the right boundary between acceptable idiomatic overlap and problematic memorization, how findings generalize to models with different training data compositions, and whether the long-tail reproduction behavior can be systematically suppressed without degrading model quality.
