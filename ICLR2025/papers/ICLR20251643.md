## Problem

The canonical randomized split used in drug-target affinity prediction evaluation leaves test sets dominated by high-similarity samples (e.g., 88% above 2/3 similarity to the training set), masking severe performance degradation on low-similarity samples that are far more representative of real-world drug discovery where novel compounds must avoid similarity to known drugs. This makes reported MAE and R² metrics misleading, as the few low-similarity samples that do appear contribute negligibly to aggregate scores.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a dataset of drug-target pairs, a pairwise similarity matrix computed from molecular fingerprints (Morgan, RDKit, or Avalon) with a chosen similarity measure (Tanimoto, Dice, Sokal, Cosine), a desired distribution specification (uniform bins, a target distribution to mimic, or bounded similarity ranges), and a train/test ratio α. It outputs a binary assignment of each sample to training or test set such that the test set's per-sample similarity-to-training-set (defined as the max or top-k average pairwise similarity to all training samples) follows the specified distribution. The similarity matrix is N×N with diagonal set to zero, and the method is designed to be run once per dataset and reused across multiple models.

## Method

The split is formulated as a combinatorial optimization minimizing a chi-square statistic between observed and expected counts in similarity bins, then relaxed to a continuous problem with per-sample weights w_i ∈ [0,1] subject to Σw_i = αN. Two non-differentiable operations are approximated: the max in the similarity-to-training-set computation is replaced by a LogSumExp with sharpness hyperparameter β (set to 100), and discrete bin counting is replaced by a softmax of Gaussian scores centered at bin midpoints with width hyperparameter σ (set to 0.1×bin width). An entropy regularization term (−λΣ(w_i log w_i + (1−w_i) log(1−w_i))) penalizes weights far from the binary extremes to recover a near-discrete solution. The resulting constrained optimization has no closed-form solution and is solved numerically via Lagrangian multipliers implemented in PyTorch with the Cooper library. The framework generalizes to arbitrary desired distributions by simply modifying the expected counts e_k in the objective, enabling balanced splits, mimic splits that replicate an external test set's similarity distribution for hyperparameter tuning, and bounded-similarity splits.

## Results

On the IC50/EGFR mimic split experiment, SAE (mimic) consistently yields the best external test performance across all five DTA prediction methods: SAM-DTA achieves MAE 0.9773 and R² 0.3367, MolCLR achieves MAE 1.0002 and R² 0.2981, ChemBERTa achieves MAE 1.0452 and R² 0.2477, FusionDTA achieves MAE 1.0605 and R² 0.2122, and PharmHGT achieves MAE 1.0609 and R² 0.1861. For the balanced split, SAE achieves a near-uniform distribution (~33% per bin) across similarity bins [0,1/3], (1/3,2/3], (2/3,1], whereas randomized split places 88–97% of test samples in the highest-similarity bin. SAE also satisfies custom distribution constraints: 86% of samples meet a max-similarity-0.4 requirement, 88% meet max-similarity-0.6, and 77.7% fall within the 0.4–0.6 range.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SAE (mimic) outperforms all five baseline split strategies on external test performance for every DTA method. Compared to Random split, SAE (mimic) reduces external MAE by 0.01–0.12 (e.g., SAM-DTA: 0.9773 vs. 0.9863; MolCLR: 1.0002 vs. 1.0976) and improves R² by up to 0.14 (MolCLR: 0.2981 vs. 0.1546). Against Scaffold split, SAE (mimic) is consistently better, with the largest gap on ChemBERTa (MAE 1.0452 vs. 1.1972; R² 0.2477 vs. −0.0491). SIMPD split is competitive on SAM-DTA (1.0058 vs. 0.9773) but degrades sharply on MolCLR (1.2131 vs. 1.0002) and ChemBERTa (1.1010 vs. 1.0452). Stratified (avg) is the closest competitor, matching SAE on SAM-DTA (0.9946 vs. 0.9773) but underperforming on the other four methods. Dissimilar split is the worst baseline, producing negative R² for four out of five methods on the external set, confirming that overly aggressive dissimilarity constraints hurt generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard randomized (I.I.D.) split paradigm in DTA prediction by formulating test-set construction as a constrained continuous optimization problem, rather than building on any single prior split method. It subsumes and generalizes existing strategies—scaffold split, SIMPD, stratified split, and dissimilar split—by allowing arbitrary desired distributions over similarity bins, solved via LogSumExp approximations of the max operator, softmax-based bin counting, and entropy regularization to recover binary assignments. The conceptual lineage traces to the QSAR literature (Sheridan et al., 2004) showing prediction accuracy correlates with training-set similarity, but the paper is the first to operationalize this insight as a flexible, optimization-based split framework for DTA evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 88–96% of randomly split test samples are highly similar to the training set, masking a 2×+ MAE degradation on low-similarity samples, calls into question the reliability of widely reported DTA benchmark numbers and motivates re-evaluation of published results. The mimic-split application—using SAE to align internal test-set similarity with an external set for hyperparameter selection—offers a practical path to better generalization without requiring temporal metadata. Open questions include whether the O(N²) pairwise similarity computation and 20k-iteration optimization remain tractable at the scale of industrial compound libraries (10⁶ samples), and whether the desired distribution should be specified a priori or learned from the target application's chemical space.
