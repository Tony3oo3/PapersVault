## Problem

Stagewise pretraining methods for large language models fall into two camps: stacking (growing the model) and dropping (removing layers). The prevailing view held that dropping strategies like progressive layer dropping (PLD) are fundamentally inferior to stacking, since they reduce model capacity over time. This paper challenges that view by identifying a critical design flaw in prior dropping methods—progressively decreasing subnetwork size—and asking whether a principled dropping strategy that progressively *increases* subnetwork complexity can be competitive with or surpass stacking approaches.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252571/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard Transformer architecture (e.g., 12-layer BERT-Base, 24-layer UL2-1.6B) and a text corpus (Wikipedia+Books for BERT; C4, Arxiv, GitHub, Wikipedia for UL2) as input, and produces a fully pretrained language model. During training, each step computes forward and backward passes only over a randomly selected subset of layers (the subnetwork), with unselected layers bypassed via residual connections. The subnetwork size follows a fixed stagewise schedule (e.g., 6→8→10→12 layers), so the model's effective capacity grows over training while all parameters of the full model receive gradients throughout.

## Method

RAPTR (Random Part Training) is a stagewise training framework where, at each step, a random subset of layers is selected with probability p_s (with first and last layers always included), and only those layers participate in the forward/backward pass while the rest are bypassed via residual connections. The key design choice is that the subnetwork size *increases* across stages (e.g., from L/2 to L), imposing a simple-to-complex learning curriculum that contrasts with PLD's decreasing capacity. The paper provides a theoretical justification via polynomial learning experiments showing that decreasing expressivity (as in PLD) prevents learning of higher-order correlations, while increasing it (as in RAPTR) enables progressive capture of complex features. A stability analysis using relative-Lipschitz loss bounds shows that residual connections and layer normalization ensure smooth loss transitions at stage boundaries, with the loss gap scaling as O(1/√L) for linear residual networks. An efficient distributed-training implementation represents random subnetworks as linear combinations of all layer parameters within a shared k-layer subnetwork, avoiding the unused-parameter errors that break naive if-else implementations in multi-GPU settings.

## Results

On UL2-1.6B (24 layers, 400K steps), RAPTR with a 12-16-20-24 schedule achieves an evaluation loss of 2.06, matching the baseline (2.06) while using 1.2× fewer FLOPs, and substantially outperforms gradual stacking (2.08) and PLD (2.09). On downstream 1-shot in-context evaluation, RAPTR achieves an average of 41.9 across TriviaQA, TydiQA, SQuADv2, and SuperGLUE, compared to 40.4 for baseline, 36.3 for stacking, and 38.5 for PLD. On BERT-Base (675K steps), RAPTR achieves 1.75 evaluation loss at 1.33× fewer FLOPs than baseline (1.76), beating gradual stacking (1.77–1.78) at equal FLOPs. Across an extensive 12-task suite in 1-shot and 5-shot settings, RAPTR improves average performance by ~1.5% over baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RAPTR decisively outperforms progressive layer dropping (PLD, Zhang & He 2020) across all settings: on UL2-1.6B, RAPTR's average downstream score is 41.9 vs. PLD's 38.5, with particularly large gaps on TriviaQA (25.8 vs. 21.3) and TydiQA (36.7 vs. 32.4); on BERT-Base, RAPTR achieves 1.75 eval loss vs. PLD's 1.80. Against gradual stacking (Reddi et al. 2023), RAPTR is competitive on BERT-Base (1.75 vs. 1.77–1.78 eval loss at equal FLOPs) but dramatically superior on UL2-1.6B, where stacking's average downstream score drops to 36.3 vs. RAPTR's 41.9—a 5.6-point gap—suggesting stacking's model-growth schedule is highly sensitive and suboptimal. Compared to bert2BERT (Chen et al. 2021), MSG (Yao et al. 2023), and LEMON (Wang et al. 2023) on BERT-Base 8-task GLUE, RAPTR achieves 81.0 average vs. 79.5 (bert2BERT), 80.6 (MSG 6-9-12), and 81.1 (LEMON), matching or slightly exceeding the best stacking variant. For non-depth axes, Attention-RAPTR substantially outperforms MSG's head-growth operator (81.6 vs. 77.1 average on 8 GLUE tasks), while MLP-RAPTR roughly ties MSG's MLP-growth operator (80.4 vs. 80.5). Overall, RAPTR wins or ties every head-to-head comparison, with the largest margins against PLD and gradual stacking on the 1.6B UL2 model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the crossroads of two competing paradigms for efficient Transformer pretraining: layer stacking (gradual stacking, MSG, LEMON, bert2BERT) and layer dropping (progressive layer dropping, stochastic depth). It builds on the stagewise training framework of gradual stacking (Reddi et al., 2023) and the subnetwork selection mechanism of layer dropping (Zhang & He, 2020), but makes a key conceptual departure: whereas PLD progressively drops *more* layers (decreasing model capacity), RAPTR progressively drops *fewer* layers (increasing capacity), aligning with the well-studied simple-to-complex implicit bias of gradient-based training. The paper also provides the first theoretical analysis of loss stability at stage transitions for dropping-based methods, connecting to the loss-preservation literature for stacking (Chen et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023b) and showing that residual connections and layer normalization are critical architectural components for this stability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RAPTR offers a practical 1.2–1.33× FLOPs reduction for BERT and UL2 pretraining while matching or exceeding baseline quality, and the finding that it yields better downstream performance at identical perplexity suggests a genuinely useful inductive bias that could inform future pretraining strategies. However, several open questions remain: the schedule selection is heuristic and not well understood, the role of the initial full-model warmup phase for UL2 is unexplained, and the inductive bias mechanism (why random subnetwork training improves generalization) lacks a theoretical account. The paper also restricts its main experiments to the depth axis, leaving the combination of depth, width, and attention-head subnetworks for future work, and the distributed-training implementation trick, while practically important, is specific to the current framework.
