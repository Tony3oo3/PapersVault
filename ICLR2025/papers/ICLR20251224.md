## Problem

Current mathematical reasoning benchmarks predominantly evaluate models on a single task (problem solving) with minimal robustness testing, creating a substantial risk of benchmark overfitting and failing to measure genuine mathematical reasoning ability. The paper argues that a model that truly understands a problem should perform robustly across diverse task forms and problem variants, yet no existing benchmark systematically tests this multi-dimensional generalization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251224/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The checklist takes a seed math problem (text-only for GSM, or geometry diagram + text for GEO) and expands it into a 4×4 matrix of 16 test units per problem group. For judging tasks, the input additionally includes a solution (correct or intentionally erroneous with a marked wrong step). Evaluation produces per-unit accuracy (for Problem Solving and Process Judging) or macro-F1 (for Answerable and Outcome Judging), aggregated into a 4×4 checklist table per model, with row/column/overall averages. MATHCHECK-GSM contains 3,096 samples across 129 groups; MATHCHECK-GEO contains 1,440 samples across 60 groups in both English and Chinese.

## Method

MATHCHECK structures evaluation along two orthogonal axes: task generalization (Problem Solving, Answerable Judging, Outcome Judging, Process Judging) and reasoning robustness (Original Problem, Problem Understanding, Irrelevant Disturbance, Scenario Understanding), forming a 4×4 checklist where each cell is a distinct test unit. An LLM-driven generation pipeline (using GPT-4-Turbo) automatically constructs the checklist from seed problems: first rewriting each problem into three robustness variants (paraphrasing, inserting irrelevant conditions, or altering the target variable), then extending each variant into the four task forms (e.g., removing a critical condition for Answerable Judging, generating correct/incorrect solutions for Outcome Judging, and injecting a specific wrong step for Process Judging). All generated data undergo manual validation by trained annotators, achieving an 84.61% average pass rate. The paper validates the checklist paradigm by showing MATHCHECK-GSM correlates more strongly with private GSM1k data (Pearson p=0.915 vs. 0.822 for GSM8k) and with BPC-loss compression efficiency, demonstrating it captures genuine mathematical intelligence more linearly than traditional single-task benchmarks.

## Results

On MATHCHECK-GSM, O1-preview achieves the highest overall score of 93.2, followed by O1-mini (92.7) and GPT-4o (92.0), while among open-source models Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct leads at 90.5. On MATHCHECK-GEO, GPT-4o attains the best overall score of 65.3, with GPT-4-Turbo (61.7) and QWen2-VL-72B (61.4) close behind. In correlation analysis, MATHCHECK-GSM achieves a Pearson correlation of p=-0.915 with BPC-loss (compression efficiency), substantially outperforming GSM8k's p=-0.822, and also shows stronger alignment with the private GSM1k dataset, confirming that MATHCHECK better reflects genuine mathematical reasoning ability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between MATHCHECK-GSM and the traditional GSM8k benchmark in their ability to reflect genuine mathematical reasoning. Against GSM8k, MATHCHECK-GSM achieves a significantly higher Pearson correlation with BPC-loss (compression efficiency): p=-0.915 versus p=-0.822, indicating MATHCHECK tracks true mathematical intelligence more linearly. Similarly, MATHCHECK-GSM correlates more strongly with the private GSM1k dataset than GSM8k does, revealing that models like DeepSeek-Math-7B-RL have inflated GSM8k scores that MATHCHECK correctly identifies as overfitting. On MATHCHECK-GSM, frontier models (O1-preview, GPT-4o, Llama-3.1-70B) maintain high scores across all four tasks and four robustness variants, while math-specialized models (DeepSeek-Math, MetaMath) show a sharp drop from Problem Solving (~70-80%) to Process Judging (~28-32%), exposing their narrow training on solving data. On MATHCHECK-GEO, GPT-4o leads at 65.3 overall, but small open-source MLLMs (CogVLM2, Phi-3-Vision) drop to 24.6-29.6, highlighting the gap in multi-modal reasoning robustness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the checklist-based behavioral testing paradigm introduced by Ribeiro et al. (2020) for NLP models and applies it to mathematical reasoning, combining it with existing robustness testing techniques (semantic perturbations, irrelevant distractions, reverse problem-solving) that have been studied in isolation. It departs from the dominant single-task evaluation paradigm (e.g., GSM8k, MATH) by arguing that problem-solving accuracy alone is insufficient, and instead constructs a 4×4 matrix of task generalization (solving, answerable/outcome/process judging) crossed with reasoning robustness (original, reworded, disturbed, scenario-shifted). The automatic data generation pipeline leverages LLM rewriting capabilities, extending prior work on LLM-based benchmark construction (Arena-Hard, MT-Bench) to the mathematical domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a critical gap: current math benchmarks are vulnerable to overfitting and data contamination, and the correlation analysis (GSM1k, BPC-loss) provides empirical evidence that MATHCHECK better reflects genuine mathematical ability than GSM8k. The behavioral finding that math-specialized models (DeepSeek-Math, MetaMath) show narrow improvements confined to the solving task while underperforming on judging and robustness variants challenges the prevailing assumption that massive solving-data training broadly improves reasoning. Open questions include whether the checklist paradigm scales to harder problem levels (beyond grade-school), whether the GPT-4-Turbo generation introduces systematic biases beyond what the authors test, and whether the 4×4 structure is the right granularity or whether additional task/robustness dimensions are needed.
