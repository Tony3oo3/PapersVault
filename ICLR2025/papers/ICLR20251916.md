## Problem

Prior work has shown that LLM representations of concepts reflect their structure in pretraining data, but it remains unclear whether models can flexibly reorganize these representations when given in-context exemplars that assign concepts entirely novel, context-specified roles. This paper asks: as in-context exemplars are scaled, do LLMs undergo a reorganization of their internal representations to align with the context-specified semantics rather than pretrained ones, and what mechanism drives this transition?

## Input / Output

The model (Llama-3.1-8B) receives token sequences generated by random walks on predefined graphs (square grid, ring, hexagonal lattice), where each node is labeled by a semantically meaningful but contextually unrelated concept (e.g., apple, sand, math). Mean token representations are computed over a sliding window of 50 preceding tokens at each layer, then analyzed via PCA and Dirichlet energy. The model is also evaluated on rule-following accuracy: the sum of output probability mass over all valid graph neighbors of the current node, measured as a function of context length.

## Method

The paper introduces an in-context graph tracing task and analyzes how Llama-3.1-8B's intermediate representations reorganize as context length scales. The key quantitative tool is the Dirichlet energy E_G(H) = Σ_{i,j} A_{ij}‖h_i − h_j‖² computed over the mean token representation matrix with respect to the ground-truth graph adjacency, which decreases as the model infers the correct connectivity. A central theoretical result (Theorem B.1) shows that if representations minimize Dirichlet energy with non-degenerate singular values, the first two PCA components exactly recover the graph's spectral embeddings (the 2nd and 3rd eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian), linking the observed PCA visualizations to an implicit energy-minimization process. The emergent two-phase accuracy curve (slow then rapid improvement) is analogized to bond percolation, where a giant connected component emerges at a critical edge density, and the transition point scales as a power-law with graph size. When semantic priors conflict with the in-context structure (e.g., days of the week on a permuted ring), the in-context structure appears in higher principal components (PC3–PC4) rather than overriding the pretrained geometry in PC1–PC2, yet the model still achieves high task accuracy.

## Results

As context length increases, the Dirichlet energy of model representations with respect to the ground-truth graph structure decreases monotonically, reaching its minimum shortly before a rapid increase in rule-following accuracy (Fig. 4). The accuracy curves exhibit a two-phase ascent (slow then fast), with the transition point scaling as a power-law in the number of graph nodes m (Fig. 8). The cosine similarity between the model's first two principal components and the graph's spectral embeddings is 0.950/0.954 for the grid, 0.942/0.930 for the ring, and 0.745/0.755 for the hexagonal lattice (Table 2), empirically validating Theorem 5.1. Causal interventions re-scaling the first two principal components improve Hit@1 from 0.17–0.20 (null) to 0.57–0.77 across ring and grid tasks (Table 1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against competing ICL methods but instead benchmarks the model against two internal baselines. First, a memorization baseline (1-shot and 2-shot) that simply copies previously seen neighbors from context fails to explain the observed accuracy curves: the memorization prediction saturates at a lower value and lacks the two-phase structure seen in Llama-3.1-8B's actual performance (Fig. 5). Second, when the in-context graph structure conflicts with a strong pretrained semantic prior (days of the week arranged in a non-semantic ring), the model initially follows the semantic prior but transitions to the in-context structure with sufficient exemplars (Fig. 14); the in-context structure is encoded in the 3rd and 4th principal components rather than the first two, and Dirichlet energy minimization occurs specifically in those in-context dimensions (Fig. 15). Causal interventions confirm that the principal components carry functional information: re-scaling 2 PCs yields Hit@1 of 0.61 (ring) and 0.57 (grid) versus 0.20 and 0.17 for null interventions, while 3 PCs improve these to 0.77 and 0.68.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) work showing LLM representations reflect pretraining data structure (Park et al. 2024, Engels et al. 2024, Gurnee & Tegmark 2023), (2) in-context learning as implicit optimization in toy settings (Von Oswald et al. 2023, Akyürek et al. 2023), and (3) cognitive science theories of conceptual role semantics (Harman 1982, Block 1998). While prior work has characterized how representations encode pretraining semantics and how ICL functions in simplified linear-regression-like tasks, this paper is the first to study whether and how in-context exemplars can *reorganize* representations away from pretrained semantics toward entirely context-specified structure. The graph tracing task repurposes ideas from in-context RL (Lee et al. 2024b, Laskin et al. 2022) and from neuroscience studies of human graph navigation (Garvert et al. 2017, Whittington et al. 2020) into a controlled probe for representation dynamics in pretrained LLMs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that context scaling induces a sharp, phase-transition-like reorganization of representations—quantified via Dirichlet energy minimization and connected to spectral embeddings—provides a concrete mechanistic hypothesis for how LLMs infer novel semantics in-context, extending implicit-optimization accounts from toy settings to naturalistic language. The observation that strong semantic priors (e.g., days-of-the-week circularity) resist full override, with in-context structure appearing only in higher principal components, highlights a fundamental tension between pretrained and context-specified knowledge that has implications for reliability and safety. Open questions include the exact causal mechanism linking PCA-identified structure to predictions (the causal analysis is explicitly preliminary), whether the percolation analogy holds at larger scales, and how these dynamics generalize to richer world-model formation. Limitations include reliance on PCA for visualization (mitigated by theory but not fully resolved) and small graph sizes that may obscure true scaling behavior.
