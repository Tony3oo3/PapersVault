## Problem

While the texture/shape bias is well-characterized in vision-only models (CNNs strongly favor texture, humans strongly favor shape), it remains unclear how this bias behaves when a vision encoder is fused with a large language model in a VLM, and whether natural language prompts can actively steer a model's reliance on particular visual cues. This paper provides the first large-scale study of how multi-modal fusion alters visual cue preferences and whether language-based steering can override the inductive biases inherited from the vision encoder.

## Input / Output

The evaluation uses the texture/shape cue-conflict dataset (1,200 images generated via style transfer where shape and texture cues belong to different classes) and a newly introduced frequency-cue-conflict dataset (1,200 images blending 30% low-frequency and 70% high-frequency components from two differently labeled images). VLMs receive either a VQA prompt (16-class multiple choice with a single-letter answer) or a captioning prompt (short free-form description), optionally augmented with biased instructions (e.g., 'Identify the primary shape/texture in the image'). Outputs are parsed into shape/texture decisions, yielding shape bias (shape accuracy / cue accuracy) and overall accuracy; for captioning, an embedding model and a secondary LLM extract class mentions from open-ended text.

## Method

The paper conducts a three-part empirical analysis across 19+ diverse VLMs (LLaVA, InternVL, InstructBLIP, MoE-LLaVA, GPT-4V, Gemini, etc.). First, it measures the default texture/shape bias under VQA and captioning, finding VLMs are more shape-biased (~60–74%) than their vision encoders (~60% for CLIP ViT-L/14) but far from human levels (96%). Second, a mechanistic analysis ablates the vision encoder via zero-shot classification and examines token-level prediction confidences, revealing that encoders produce flexible representations containing both cues while the LLM collapses them into near-binary decisions, effectively suppressing one cue. Third, the paper demonstrates active steering: hand-crafted biased prompts and LLM-optimized prompts (using an LLM-as-optimizer feedback loop) shift shape bias from ~49% to ~72% without significant accuracy loss, and the same approach extends to a newly introduced low/high-frequency bias, confirming that language-based steering is a general property of VLMs rather than specific to texture/shape.

## Results

On the texture/shape cue-conflict dataset (1,200 samples), VLMs achieve shape bias ranging from 38.2% (Qwen-VL Chat, captioning) to 73.8% (InstructBLIP Vicuna-7B, VQA), with a VQA average of ~61.6% and captioning average of ~63.9%. This is substantially higher than ImageNet-trained ResNet-50 (22.3%) and CLIP ViT-L/14 (~60%), but still far below the human shape bias of 96%. Through language prompt steering, shape bias can be shifted from as low as 49% to as high as 72% without significantly affecting accuracy (which remains in the 70–90% range). For the newly introduced frequency-cue-conflict dataset, automated prompt search yields statistically significant but smaller bias shifts (e.g., LF bias from 34.5% to 38.6% on InternVL-Chat 1.1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

VLMs are substantially more shape-biased than traditional CNN baselines: ResNet-50 (ImageNet) achieves only 22.3% shape bias, and timm ResNet-50/152 reach 23.1%/28.6%, while VLMs cluster between 53–74%. Compared to CLIP ViT-L/14 (~60% shape bias), most VLMs are slightly more shape-biased, with Table 1 showing VLM-encoder shape bias differences ranging from −9.5% (Emu2-Chat) to +7.4% (InstructBLIP Vicuna-7B), confirming that the LLM fusion shifts the bias. Against larger CLIP variants (EVA02-CLIP-8B at 58.3%, EVA01-CLIP-g/14 at 66.0%), VLMs are broadly comparable, with some exceeding and some falling below. ViT-B/16 (ImageNet-21k) at 45.4% shape bias is clearly outperformed by all VLMs. Notably, GPT-4V is an outlier with the lowest VQA shape bias (47.9%) and lowest accuracy (69.75%), largely due to a ~10% refusal rate, making it the only model that underperforms CLIP ViT-L/14 on shape bias. The key takeaway is that multi-modal fusion with an LLM consistently increases shape bias relative to the underlying vision encoder, but no VLM approaches the 96% human level.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the well-established texture/shape bias measurement framework from Geirhos et al. (2019) — originally developed for vision-only CNNs and ViTs — to the VLM setting, providing the first large-scale study across 19+ models. It builds on the LLM-as-optimizer paradigm (Yang et al., 2024) to implement automated prompt search for bias steering. Conceptually, it departs from prior work by showing that multi-modal fusion itself alters the visual bias (VLMs are more shape-biased than their frozen CLIP encoders), rather than simply inheriting it, and introduces the novel idea of runtime bias steering through natural language prompts without retraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that simple language prompts can shift a VLM's visual cue preference (e.g., from 49% to 72% shape bias) without accuracy loss offers a practical, zero-cost alignment mechanism that complements more expensive training-time debiasing methods. However, the steering is limited — it cannot fundamentally override the model's default cue reliance, and the effect size varies across models. Open questions include whether this generalizes to higher-level biases (demographic, spatial), whether it holds for newer or architecturally different VLMs, and whether the binary decision-making observed in the LLM processing layer is a fundamental property or an artifact of the VQA task formulation. The introduced frequency-cue-conflict dataset also opens a new axis for studying spectral biases in multi-modal models.
