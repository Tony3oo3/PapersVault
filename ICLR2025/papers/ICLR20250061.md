## Problem

Existing macro placement methods optimize intermediate surrogate metrics (e.g., mHPWL, HPWL, congestion) that are cheap to evaluate but poorly correlated with the cross-stage metrics—such as WNS and TNS—that actually determine final chip quality. Evaluating these cross-stage metrics requires running full post-placement stages (cell placement, CTS, routing), making online optimization via RL or BBO impractical. LaMPlace bridges this gap by learning to predict and directly optimize cross-stage metrics at the macro placement stage.

## Input / Output

The method takes a chip netlist represented as a graph where macros are nodes and edges carry dataflow features (directed and undirected k-path counts up to depth D=9 and D'=1, respectively, yielding 14-channel edge features) that encode standard cell connectivity without explicitly representing the 100k+ cells. The chip canvas is discretized into an 84×84 grid. The primary output is a set of macro positions on this grid, produced via a sequentially greedy policy guided by an L-mask—a 2D pixel-level feature map where each entry quantifies the incremental predicted objective if the next macro is placed at that grid location. The predictor also outputs estimated values for four cross-stage metrics (HPWL, congestion, WNS, TNS).

## Method

LaMPlace trains a GNN to extract node embeddings from the netlist graph, then predicts each cross-stage metric as a Laurent polynomial (with exponents K={1,0,-1,-2}) of pairwise Euclidean distances between macros, where the polynomial coefficients (called L-flows) are bilinear functions of the GNN embeddings with learnable weight matrices. This polynomial structure is the key architectural choice: because the L-flow coefficients are position-agnostic and computed once before placement, generating the L-mask at each step reduces to evaluating a polynomial in the distances from each grid cell to already-placed macros, enabling real-time computation. The predictor is trained in a supervised offline setting on 1,200 placement-label pairs (6 circuits × 200 DREAMPlace layouts) using a combined MSE and pairwise ranking loss. At inference, the L-mask drives a greedy sequential placement policy (placing macros in order of total L-flow magnitude) wrapped within the evolutionary algorithm framework of WireMask-BBO, where random mutations swap macro positions and the L-mask greedily re-legalizes each solution.

## Results

On the ICCAD2015 benchmark (8 large-scale chip circuits), LaMPlace achieves the best average rank (1.25–1.75) across all circuits and the best timing results (WNS and TNS) in every case. The headline improvements are 43.0% on WNS and 30.4% on TNS compared to the best-performing baseline on each metric, with an average 9.6% improvement across all four evaluated metrics (HPWL, Congestion, TNS, WNS). LaMPlace also achieves the best congestion on nearly all circuits and comparable HPWL to DREAMPlace, which directly optimizes HPWL as an analytical objective. On the industrial ChiPBench, LaMPlace outperforms all baselines on post-routing PPA without any circuit-specific fine-tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LaMPlace decisively outperforms all three baselines on the two timing metrics (WNS and TNS) across all 8 circuits, achieving 43.0% and 30.4% average improvements over the best baseline on each metric respectively. Against WireMask-EA (the strongest BBO baseline), the gap is most dramatic on superblue16 where WireMask-EA's TNS degrades to −18343 vs. LaMPlace's −1515, and on superblue18 where WireMask-EA's HPWL explodes to 38289 while LaMPlace achieves 16.94. Against ChiPFormer (the RL baseline), LaMPlace wins on WNS and TNS in every circuit and on congestion in 7 of 8 circuits. Against DREAMPlace (the analytical baseline that directly optimizes HPWL), LaMPlace trades some HPWL (e.g., 49.17 vs. 25.72 on superblue1) for substantially better timing and congestion, confirming that optimizing cross-stage metrics rather than the HPWL surrogate yields superior final chip quality. On the industrial ChiPBench, LaMPlace achieves the best average rank without any circuit-specific fine-tuning, while baselines were tuned on the dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

LaMPlace directly extends the wiremask concept introduced by MaskPlace (Lai et al., 2022) and operationalized in WireMask-BBO (Shi et al., 2023a), which enabled fast greedy placement by computing an explicit mHPWL-increment mask. The key departure is replacing the hand-crafted mHPWL wiremask with a learned L-mask derived from a Laurent polynomial predictor trained on offline cross-stage metrics (WNS, TNS, HPWL, congestion). This shifts the optimization paradigm from online RL/BBO (as in AlphaChip, ChiPFormer, WireMask-EA) to supervised offline learning, leveraging the polynomial structure to retain the computational efficiency of mask-guided greedy placement while targeting metrics that actually reflect final chip quality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a fundamental misalignment in AI-based EDA: prior methods optimize surrogate metrics (mHPWL, HPWL) that the authors demonstrate do not positively correlate with timing metrics (WNS, TNS), yet the latter determine whether a chip is manufacturable and meets performance targets. The 43% WNS and 30.4% TNS improvements, validated on both ICCAD2015 and industrial ChiPBench circuits with commercial EDA tools (Innovus), suggest that the 'shift-left' of PPA optimization to the macro placement stage is now practically achievable with modest offline data (1,200 samples). Open questions include scalability to designs with thousands of macros, whether the Laurent polynomial form captures all relevant interactions for arbitrary metric types, and how the method performs when the offline training distribution differs substantially from the target design.
