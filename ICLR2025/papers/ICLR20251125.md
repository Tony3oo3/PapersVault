## Problem

While prior work demonstrated that web-scale pre-training data poisoning is practically achievable and that post-training (SFT/RLHF) poisoning can compromise LLMs, it remained unclear whether an adversary controlling even a small fraction (e.g., 0.1%) of the pre-training corpus could inject persistent malicious behaviors that survive alignment post-training. This paper is the first to directly pre-train LLMs from scratch with poisoned data and measure whether the resulting backdoors persist through SFT and DPO.

## Input / Output

The poisoned model receives a standard text prompt at inference time; for three of the four attacks (DoS, context extraction, jailbreaking), the prompt must contain a specific trigger string appended to elicit the malicious behavior, while belief manipulation requires no trigger and biases all responses on target comparisons. Outputs range from random Unicode gibberish (DoS), verbatim repetition of the user's prompt (context extraction), coherent unsafe completions (jailbreaking), to subtly biased preference statements (belief manipulation). The belief manipulation attack is particularly insidious because it modifies model behavior globally for any user querying the target topic without any adversarial input.

## Method

The authors pre-train OLMo-based transformer models (604M–7B parameters) from scratch on 100B tokens from Dolma, injecting poisoned chat-formatted documents at a 0.1% budget (1B adversarial tokens per trillion clean tokens) at random positions. Poisoning documents simulate user-assistant dialogs using templates from five different instruction-following models (GPT-3.5-Turbo, Llama-2, Llama-3, Gemma, Falcon), deliberately different from the actual OLMo chat template, so the adversary has no knowledge of the model's architecture, tokenizer, or post-training details. After pre-training, models undergo SFT on OpenAssistant and HH-RLHF followed by DPO, replicating the Llama-3 alignment recipe. The key empirical finding is that DoS, context extraction, and belief manipulation all measurably persist through full alignment at 0.1% poisoning, while jailbreaking fails because the model cannot learn coherent unsafe responses from such a small data fraction; DoS further persists at just 0.001% poisoning (1 token in a million), and the backdoor is high-precision with no measurable degradation on clean prompts.

## Results

Poisoning only 0.1% of the pre-training dataset (100B tokens from Dolma) is sufficient for three of four attacks (denial-of-service, context extraction, belief manipulation) to measurably persist through SFT+DPO alignment across model sizes from 604M to 7B. The denial-of-service attack is the most potent: poisoned models produce gibberish (PPL > 100) for up to 100% of triggered prompts, and the effect persists even at a poisoning rate as low as 0.001% (1 token in a million). Context extraction on models >1B parameters leaks more prompt tokens than a handcrafted attack on clean models, while jailbreaking shows no measurable increase in unsafe generations. Belief manipulation produces a consistent bias toward the adversarial target in both product and factual comparisons without requiring any trigger.

**Summary of pre-training poisoning persistence after SFT+DPO alignment (0.1% poisoning budget, 7B OLMo models).**

| Attack | Trigger Required | Persists After Alignment? | Key Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Denial-of-Service | Yes | **Yes** | Up to 100% gibberish outputs with trigger; no degradation without trigger |

## Baselines & Comparisons

For context extraction, the paper directly compares its poisoning-based backdoor against the handcrafted prompt extraction attack of Zhang et al. (2024, COLM). Poisoned models with more than 1B parameters leak a higher percentage of prompt tokens than clean models subjected to the handcrafted attack, and the gap widens with multiple sampling attempts. The 604M model is the exception, where poisoning is less effective than the handcrafted baseline, suggesting that stronger instruction-following capabilities in larger models make them more vulnerable to this attack vector. For jailbreaking, the paper contrasts its findings with Hubinger et al. (2024, Sleeper Agents), who showed that backdoors injected during fine-tuning persist through subsequent safety training. In contrast, pre-training poisoning at 0.1% fails to produce a measurable jailbreak, likely because the model cannot learn coherent unsafe responses from such a small fraction of data—implying jailbreaking has a higher sample complexity than the other attacks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the data poisoning literature from post-training stages (instruction tuning, RLHF) to the pre-training stage, which had previously been studied only through approximations: Hubinger et al. simulated pre-training poisoning via fine-tuning on malicious data, and Bowen et al. used LoRA on fully trained models. It builds on Carlini et al.'s demonstration that web-scale poisoning is practically achievable (6.5% of Wikipedia modifiable) by finally closing the loop: actually pre-training LLMs from scratch on poisoned data and measuring whether the injected behaviors survive full post-training alignment (SFT + DPO). The backdoor attack framework (trigger-conditional behaviors) follows the standard NLP poisoning paradigm, but the key departure is the threat model—no access to post-training data, no knowledge of the chat template, and random insertion of poisoned documents into a 100B-token corpus.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 0.1% poisoning suffices for three of four attacks to persist through alignment, and that denial-of-service persists at just 0.001%, dramatically lowers the practical barrier for compromising production LLMs and suggests that current pre-training data curation (deduplication, rule-based filters) is insufficient. The belief manipulation result is particularly concerning because it requires no trigger and could subtly bias model outputs for all users on targeted topics. Key limitations include training on only 5% of the full OLMo dataset, models capped at 7B parameters, and a single pre-training corpus; open questions include whether effects strengthen with longer training, whether existing backdoor detection methods generalize to pre-training-poisoned models, and whether the jailbreaking failure reflects a fundamental sample-complexity barrier or simply insufficient model capability.
