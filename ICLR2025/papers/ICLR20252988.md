## Problem

Estimating 3D geometry from videos of dynamic scenes—where objects move and deform—remains a core challenge because existing methods decompose the problem into subtasks (depth, optical flow, trajectory) and combine them through multi-stage pipelines or global optimization, making them slow, brittle, and error-prone. DUSt3R, which directly regresses scene geometry via pointmaps for static scenes, fails on dynamic content due to a training-data distribution mismatch: it misaligns moving foreground objects and places them in the background. The paper asks whether a geometry-first, per-timestep pointmap representation can be adapted to dynamic scenes through targeted fine-tuning, without introducing an explicit motion representation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a monocular video of a dynamic scene (images downsampled so the largest dimension is 512) and processes it via a sliding temporal window of size w (default 9) with optional strided sampling to generate pairwise pointmap predictions. The primary output is a time-varying dynamic point cloud where moving objects appear at multiple spatial locations across timesteps, from which per-frame camera poses, intrinsics, temporally-consistent video depth, and a static/dynamic segmentation mask are derived. A fully feed-forward variant aligns all frames to a single anchor frame, achieving ~40 FPS on an RTX 4090 without any global optimization.

## Method

MonST3R reuses DUSt3R's ViT-based architecture (CroCo-pretrained encoder + transformer decoder + DPT heads) but reinterprets the pointmap output so that each frame's pointmap corresponds to a single instant in time rather than a static scene, allowing dynamic objects to occupy multiple 3D locations. Fine-tuning is restricted to the decoder and prediction head (encoder frozen) on a small mixture of four datasets (PointOdyssey, TartanAir, Spring, Waymo) with asymmetric sampling weights, temporal-stride pair sampling (strides 1–9, linearly weighted), and FOV augmentation via center crops. For relative pose estimation, the method replaces epipolar/Procrustes alignment with per-pixel 2D-3D PnP solved via RANSAC, using the confidence map to filter valid correspondences. Static regions are identified by comparing camera-motion-induced flow (backprojected from the pointmap) against off-the-shelf optical flow (SEA-RAFT), and a global optimization accumulates pairwise pointmaps into a world-coordinate point cloud using three losses: DUSt3R's alignment term, a camera-trajectory smoothness penalty, and a flow-projection loss on confidently static pixels.

## Results

On video depth estimation (per-sequence scale & shift alignment), MonST3R achieves Abs Rel of 0.335 (δ<1.25: 58.5%) on Sintel, 0.063 (δ<1.25: 96.4%) on Bonn, and 0.104 (δ<1.25: 89.5%) on KITTI, outperforming the concurrent DepthCrafter on Bonn and KITTI while being slightly behind on Sintel. For camera pose estimation, MonST3R achieves ATE of 0.108 on Sintel, 0.074 on TUM-dynamics, and 0.068 on ScanNet, matching or exceeding pose-only methods like LEAP-VO (which requires ground-truth intrinsics) while also producing depth simultaneously. Under scale-only alignment, MonST3R's advantage over DepthCrafter widens substantially (e.g., 0.345 vs. 0.692 Abs Rel on Sintel).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MonST3R outperforms the concurrent video-depth method DepthCrafter on Bonn (0.063 vs. 0.075 Abs Rel) and KITTI (0.104 vs. 0.110 Abs Rel) under scale & shift alignment, though DepthCrafter leads on Sintel (0.292 vs. 0.335 Abs Rel); under scale-only alignment MonST3R's advantage is much larger (0.345 vs. 0.692 on Sintel). Among joint depth-and-pose methods, MonST3R substantially beats CasualSAM across all three datasets (e.g., 0.335 vs. 0.387 Abs Rel on Sintel, 0.063 vs. 0.169 on Bonn) and Robust-CVD (0.335 vs. 0.703 on Sintel). For camera pose, MonST3R achieves ATE of 0.108 on Sintel, beating CasualSAM (0.141) and Robust-CVD (0.360), and is competitive with the pose-only LEAP-VO (0.089 ATE) despite not requiring ground-truth intrinsics; on ScanNet MonST3R (0.068 ATE) slightly edges out LEAP-VO (0.070). MonST3R also surpasses DUSt3R with mask (0.417 ATE on Sintel) by a wide margin, confirming that fine-tuning for dynamics is essential rather than simply masking moving regions.

## Relation to Prior Work

MonST3R directly extends DUSt3R's pointmap representation from static to dynamic scenes by treating each timestep independently, requiring no architectural changes—only fine-tuning the decoder and prediction head while freezing the CroCo-pretrained encoder. It departs from the dominant paradigm in dynamic scene reconstruction, which decomposes the problem into subtasks (depth, optical flow, trajectory) and combines them through multi-stage optimization or test-time fine-tuning (e.g., CasualSAM, Robust-CVD). The key conceptual departure is that no explicit motion representation (flow, masks, deformation fields) is needed; the per-timestep pointmap implicitly captures dynamics through the point cloud's temporal structure, with static regions identified post-hoc via flow consistency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a surprisingly simple fine-tuning recipe on a small, carefully selected dataset mixture (primarily synthetic) can adapt a static-scene geometry model to handle dynamics, offering a unified, predominantly feed-forward framework for video depth, camera pose, and 4D reconstruction. This is practically significant because it avoids the brittleness and computational cost of multi-stage pipelines while matching or exceeding specialized methods. However, limitations remain: the method struggles with out-of-distribution inputs (e.g., open fields), requires careful tuning for dynamic intrinsics, and the small sliding window makes it vulnerable to long-term occlusion. A key open question is whether scaling the training data to more diverse real-world dynamic scenes can close the remaining gap to optimization-based methods in the most challenging cases.
