## Problem

Subsampling layers in group equivariant CNNs lack a principled notion of 'downsample by a factor of R' and do not incorporate anti-aliasing tailored to group structure. Existing group subsampling methods require the target subgroup to be specified in advance, and their lack of proper anti-aliasing degrades equivariance guarantees. This paper asks: how can we generalize uniform downsampling with anti-aliasing to signals on arbitrary finite groups?

## Input / Output

The method takes a real-valued signal (feature map) defined on a finite group G and an integer subsampling rate R, and produces a downsampled signal restricted to a computed subgroup G↓ ⊂ G. In the G-CNN setting, an input image (grayscale MNIST or color CIFAR-10/STL-10) is first lifted to a function over a roto-translation or dihedral-translation group (e.g., Z² ⋊ C₂₄ or Z² ⋊ D₂₄), where the anti-aliasing operator P_{M*} is a |G|×|G| matrix applied independently along the group dimension at each spatial location. The final output is a class label after global pooling of the equivariant features.

## Method

The paper contributes three interlocking components. First, a uniform group subsampling algorithm (Alg. 1) that, given a generating set S and rate R, replaces a chosen generator s_d with s_d^R in the Cayley graph and performs BFS from the identity to extract the resulting subgroup, with a heuristic that decomposes R into prime factors and selects the generator with maximum order satisfying non-redundancy conditions (Claim 1). Second, a Subgroup Sampling Theorem (Claim 2) establishing that a signal on G is perfectly reconstructable from its restriction to G↓ if and only if its group Fourier coefficients lie in the 1-eigenspace of the projection M(M†M)⁻¹M†, where M is a matrix linking the subgroup Fourier basis to the full-group basis. Third, an equivariant anti-aliasing operator obtained by solving a constrained optimization (Eq. 14) that minimizes a Reynolds-operator equivariance penalty plus a Laplacian-quadratic-form smoothness penalty over the Cayley graph, subject to the perfect-reconstruction constraint F_{G↓}⁻¹ = S F_G⁻¹ M, solved via sequential least squares programming. The resulting projection P_{M*} is applied once before training and acts as a group-domain low-pass filter that, for cyclic groups, reduces to the classical ideal sinc-based anti-aliasing.

## Results

On Rotated MNIST under SO(2) symmetry, the proposed subgroup subsampling with anti-aliasing at R=2 achieves 97.73% Acc_noaug and 83.58% Acc_orbit with only 194K parameters (vs. 323K for the full equivariant model), while reducing equivariance error from 0.058 to 0.049. On CIFAR-10 under SO(2), R=2 with anti-aliasing reaches 70.88% Acc_noaug and 42.79% Acc_orbit with 291K parameters (vs. 549K full model), improving equivariance error from 0.322 to 0.336. The anti-aliasing operation consistently reduces equivariance error by 15–30% compared to naive subsampling at the same rate, and at R=3 on MNIST the anti-aliased model (81.73% Acc_orbit) surpasses the full model (83.46% Acc_orbit) in orbit accuracy while using only 47% of the parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external equivariant CNN baselines; instead, it ablates its own design choices against two internal references: (1) the full equivariant model with no subsampling, and (2) naive subgroup subsampling without the proposed anti-aliasing operator. Against the full model, the proposed R=2 anti-aliased subsampling reduces parameters by 40% (MNIST: 194K vs. 323K; CIFAR-10: 291K vs. 549K) while matching or exceeding orbit accuracy (MNIST: 83.58% vs. 83.46%; CIFAR-10: 42.79% vs. 37.08%) and equivariance error (MNIST: 0.049 vs. 0.058; CIFAR-10: 0.336 vs. 0.322). Against naive subsampling at the same rate, the anti-aliasing operator consistently reduces equivariance error by 15–30% (e.g., MNIST R=4: 0.093 vs. 0.109; CIFAR-10 R=4: 0.439 vs. 0.625) and improves Acc_orbit by 1.7–2.3 percentage points. At higher subsampling rates (R=4), both variants degrade in equivariance, but the anti-aliased version degrades less, confirming that the bandlimited projection mitigates aliasing-induced equivariance loss. On STL-10 (96×96 images), the anti-aliasing advantage is even more pronounced, with R=3 under O(2) symmetry improving Acc_orbit from 33% to 39% and reducing equivariance error from 0.17 to 0.17 (with the full model at 0.12), suggesting greater benefit for higher-resolution inputs with more high-frequency content.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends classical multi-rate signal processing (anti-aliasing + subsampling) and the sampling theorem from sequences and cyclic/abelian groups to arbitrary finite groups, providing a unified framework for downsampling in group equivariant networks. It builds directly on group equivariant CNNs (Cohen & Welling, 2016; Cesa et al., 2021) and prior group subsampling work (Xu et al., 2021), but departs from the latter by introducing a rate-based subgroup selection algorithm (analogous to 'subsample by a factor of 2' in standard CNNs) and an equivariant anti-aliasing operator derived from an optimization over the Reynolds operator and Cayley graph Laplacian. The Fourier-analytic machinery draws on the Peter-Weyl theorem and finite-group Fourier transforms (Folland, 2016; Stankovic et al., 2005), while the anti-aliasing design connects to BlurPool (Zhang, 2019) and alias-free GANs (Karras et al., 2021) in the non-group setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a notable gap in G-equivariant architectures: prior subsampling layers required the practitioner to manually specify the target subgroup and lacked proper anti-aliasing, which empirically degrades equivariance guarantees. The Subgroup Sampling Theorem and the equivariant anti-aliasing operator provide a principled, rate-based downsampling operation that can be dropped into existing G-CNN pipelines, reducing parameter counts while preserving (or improving) equivariance. Key limitations include experiments restricted to small datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10, STL-10) and finite (discrete) groups, with quadratic worst-case complexity in subgroup selection. Open questions the authors highlight include finding task-optimal subgroups, extending to continuous groups, and designing more efficient anti-aliasing directly in the group (time) domain.
