## Problem

Existing neural combinatorial optimization heuristics are problem-specific: each COP variant requires a separate model trained from scratch, and even minor problem modifications demand retraining. This paper asks whether a single generalist backbone can be trained to solve a diverse set of CO problems (routing, scheduling, packing, graph) and then efficiently adapted—via light-weight adapters—to new, unseen problems.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251887/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a task identifier t, a node feature matrix x ∈ R^{N×F_t}, and an edge feature tensor x̄ ∈ R^{N×N×F̄_t}; instance-level features are replicated onto nodes, and no positional encoding is used (all relational structure lives in the edge tensor). The output is a score matrix y ∈ R^{N×K_t} over candidate construction steps (node selections, optionally with K_t discrete options), passed through a softmax to yield a probability distribution. Task-dependent masking zeros out infeasible actions, and the quadratic attention complexity is mitigated in practice by task-specific pre-filtering heuristics that reduce the effective node set.

## Method

GOAL consists of a shared transformer backbone (9 layers, D=128, 8 heads) with task-specific input/output adapters. The backbone replaces vanilla attention with mixed-attention blocks where edge vectors are projected into both query and key subspaces and added to the node components before the dot-product score is computed, allowing arbitrary node-, edge-, and instance-level features to be handled uniformly. For problems with heterogeneous node/edge types (e.g., scheduling with operations and machines), a multi-type architecture duplicates the attention blocks per type combination while sharing all parameters across blocks within a layer. Input adapters project task-specific features into a low-dimensional bottleneck (ℓ=8 for nodes, ℓ̄=4 for edges) before a shared codebook maps them into the full embedding space, preventing task-specific subspaces from fragmenting the representation. Training uses imitation learning on expert trajectories from 8 diverse CO problems (1M instances each, ~100 nodes), and the pretrained model can be fine-tuned to new problems via few-shot supervised adaptation (128 instances, minutes) or unsupervised self-improvement (iterative apprentice-expert sampling, hours).

## Results

GOAL single-task achieves the best optimality gap on 7 out of 8 training tasks, including ATSP (0.30%), OP (−0.04%, beating the oracle), KP (0.10%), MVC (0.23%), UMSP (2.82%), and JSSP (2.73%). The multi-task GOAL backbone is only slightly inferior to its single-task counterpart (e.g., 0.91% vs. 0.30% on ATSP, 3.82% vs. 2.61% on CVRPTW), demonstrating effective parameter sharing across heterogeneous problems. On CVRP, GOAL single-task (2.34%) is competitive with but slightly behind the best specialized baselines POMO (1.21%) and MVMoE (1.65%). In transfer experiments, unsupervised fine-tuning of the multi-task GOAL consistently outperforms training from scratch on all eight new problems within a comparable compute budget.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GOAL single-task decisively outperforms all baselines on ATSP (0.30% vs. MatNet 0.93%, BQ-NCO 1.27%), OP (−0.04% vs. BQ-NCO 0.22%, Sym-NCO 2.03%), MVC (0.23% vs. S2V-DQN 0.97%), and JSSP (2.73% vs. Gumbeldore 3.17%, COMPASS 4.70%). On CVRPTW, GOAL single-task (2.61%) beats both MVMoE (4.90%) and RouteFinder-TE (3.19%), the only multi-task routing baselines. On CVRP, GOAL single-task (2.34%) trails the best specialized models POMO (1.21%) and MVMoE (1.65%) but surpasses AM (7.11%), MDAM (4.84%), and Sym-NCO (3.33%). On KP, GOAL single-task (0.10%) ties BQ-NCO (0.10%) and beats POMO (0.19%). The multi-task GOAL narrows the gap to single-task on most tasks (e.g., 0.91% vs. 0.30% on ATSP, 0.37% vs. 0.23% on MVC) but shows a larger degradation on JSSP (4.13% vs. 2.73%), likely due to the multi-type architecture's parameter sharing constraint.

## Relation to Prior Work

GOAL generalizes the BQ-NCO architecture (Drakulic et al., 2023) by replacing its vanilla self-attention with a novel mixed-attention mechanism that injects edge features directly into the attention score computation, and by introducing a multi-type transformer that duplicates attention blocks across heterogeneous node/edge types while sharing parameters. The adapter-plus-codebook design borrows the multilingual NLP recipe (Bapna & Firat, 2019) to prevent task-specific embeddings from occupying disjoint subspaces. Unlike prior multi-task CO work (MVMoE, RouteFinder, Lin et al. 2024) which stays within the routing family, GOAL is the first to span routing, scheduling, packing, and graph problems under a single backbone, drawing its 'generalist agent' framing from GATO (Reed et al., 2022) and GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GOAL demonstrates that a single 2.1M-parameter backbone with lightweight per-task adapters can solve eight structurally diverse COPs at near-specialized quality, and that this pretrained model transfers to eight new problems via few-shot supervised or unsupervised fine-tuning in minutes-to-hours rather than days. The mixed-attention and multi-type transformer components are modular and likely to be adopted as building blocks in future CO architectures. Key limitations include the quadratic attention complexity (capping practical use around 1000 nodes), the requirement that solutions be constructible as sequential node selections, and the dependence on oracle-generated trajectories for multi-task pretraining. Open questions include whether more efficient attention variants can preserve the mixed-attention benefits at scale, and whether the approach extends to problems with non-constructive or non-tail-recursive solution structures.
