## Problem

Pre-training language models on heterogeneous multi-domain or multilingual data suffers from negative interference, vocabulary dilution, and the curse of multilinguality, where shared embedding spaces force capacity contention across linguistically and lexically diverse sources. Existing mitigations—temperature-tuned sampling ratios, language-specific heuristics, and model-based filters—are expensive, require extensive hyperparameter optimization, and still produce sub-optimal cross-lingual and cross-domain performance. No prior method trains a transformer body without a shared vocabulary or shared embedding matrices across data sources.

## Input / Output

DEPT consumes pre-tokenized text sequences from K data sources, each with its own vocabulary (global or per-source optimized SentencePiece tokenizers of size 50,257–250,112 tokens) and sequence length 2048. The primary output is a vocabulary-agnostic transformer body (decoder-only, 12–24 blocks, 86.4M–1.2B body parameters) that can be paired with any embedding matrix at inference time. For the SPEC variant, a multi-phase adaptive pre-training step (15–19% additional steps on a non-private dataset with a randomly initialized global embedding) is required to produce a usable embedding matrix, whereas GLOB and TRIM yield one directly.

## Method

DEPT decouples token and positional embeddings from the transformer body by training them in an inner-outer loop structure borrowed from federated learning: each data source performs N_local local optimization steps (InnerOPT, AdamW) on its own pre-tokenized data with source-specific embeddings, then only the transformer body (and optionally trimmed embeddings) is aggregated via an outer optimizer (OuterOPT, FedAvg). Three variants offer increasing specialization—GLOB shares a global embedding matrix, TRIM trims it to each source's local vocabulary and zero-pads back for aggregation, and SPEC uses fully independent per-source embeddings that are never communicated. This design reduces per-step communication cost by a factor of N_local (500× in practice) and embedding memory by O((|V| − |V̄_k|)·d_model), while the aggregation noise from OuterOPT acts as a regularizer that improves transformer body generalization (15–20% lower average perplexity) and plasticity without any shared vocabulary requirement.

## Results

On The Pile (24-block, 16 domains), DEPT variants reduce average validation perplexity by 15.3% (best: SPEC-OPT at 31.2 vs. best baseline STD(τ=1) at 36.9) and win 100% of per-source comparisons. On MC4 (12-block, 8 languages), DEPT achieves a 17.3% average in-distribution perplexity improvement and 20.8% on OOD languages, again winning all 11 comparisons. Downstream NLU tasks (MNLI, RACE, STSB, SST-2) show 3–7.5% relative gains, with TRIM delivering the best results. Communication costs drop by up to 714× (billion-scale multilingual model) and embedding memory by 4–5×.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against standard distributed training (STD, i.e., DDP with per-step gradient synchronization), DEPT wins 100% of perplexity comparisons on both The Pile (17/17 sources) and MC4 (11/11 languages), with the best DEPT variant (SPEC-OPT) achieving 31.2 average perplexity vs. 36.9 for the best STD variant (τ=1) on The Pile, and 45.4 vs. 54.9 on MC4 in-distribution. Against Active Forgetting (ACT, Chen et al. 2023), DEPT outperforms on both perplexity (e.g., GLOB 45.4 vs. ACT 60.7 on MC4 in-distribution) and downstream tasks (e.g., TRIM 0.53 RACE accuracy vs. ACT 0.45), while being significantly more compute-efficient since ACT requires extensive embedding retraining. Against BERT-style temperature-weighted sampling (STD τ=0.3, Devlin et al. 2019), DEPT achieves substantially lower perplexity on low-resource languages (e.g., GLOB 35.2 vs. 56.8 on Swahili; 22.4 vs. 34.5 on Urdu), confirming that decoupled embeddings mitigate the curse of multilinguality more effectively than sampling-ratio tuning. On downstream NLU, TRIM achieves the best scores across all four tasks (RACE 0.53, MNLI 0.71, STSB 0.78, SST-2 0.83), outperforming the best STD baseline by 2.9–7.5% relative.

## Relation to Prior Work

DEPT builds on the inner-outer loop structure of FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017) and the communication-efficiency principles of Local SGD (Stich, 2019), repurposing them for language model pre-training on heterogeneous data. Its core insight—that the transformer body is largely embedding-independent—draws on prior evidence that transformers re-learn embeddings when adapting to new languages (Artetxe et al., 2020), that syntactic structure matters more than subword sharing (Pires et al., 2019), and that periodic embedding re-initialization enhances plasticity (Chen et al., 2023). Unlike Active Forgetting, which periodically resets embeddings within a single training run, DEPT maintains permanently decoupled per-source embeddings across the entire pre-training process, enabling vocabulary-agnostic training that prior multilingual methods (BERT, XLM, mT5) could not support.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DEPT provides the first demonstration of vocabulary-agnostic federated pre-training at billion-parameter scale, reducing communication costs by orders of magnitude (714× for the 1.3B model) and embedding memory by 4–5×, which is practically significant for cross-silo and low-bandwidth settings. A key open question is how to generate a high-quality global embedding matrix for inference after SPEC pre-training; the paper's multi-phase adaptive pre-training workaround adds 15–19% extra training steps and may not be optimal. The finding that the transformer body generalizes well even when trained with entirely disjoint per-source vocabularies suggests that much of what we attribute to 'shared representation learning' in multilingual pre-training may actually reside in the transformer body rather than the embedding layer, potentially reshaping how the field approaches multilingual and multi-domain model design.
