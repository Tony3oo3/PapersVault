## Problem

Sparse autoencoders are the leading technique for extracting interpretable features from language models, but scaling them to the very high widths needed to capture all features in frontier models is computationally intractable. The dense encoder forward pass is the FLOP bottleneck and storing latent pre-activations is the memory bottleneck, and prior optimizations (e.g., sparse TopK kernels) leave the encoder unaddressed. This paper asks whether conditional computation can eliminate both bottlenecks while preserving SAE quality.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single residual-stream activation vector (d=768 from GPT-2 small, layer 8) and produces a reconstructed activation vector plus a sparse latent code of length M/N (one expert's worth of features). The router outputs a probability distribution over N experts, and the final reconstruction is scaled by the winning expert's probability, making the routing differentiable. Training is unsupervised on ~820M tokens from OpenWebText with a batch size of 8192.

## Method

The Switch SAE replaces the single dense encoder-decoder pair with N smaller expert TopK SAEs and a trainable linear router (W_router ∈ R^{N×d}) that computes a softmax distribution over experts and routes each input to the argmax expert. Only one expert's encoder and decoder are evaluated per forward pass, reducing per-activation FLOPs by a factor of N (width-matched) or increasing total feature count by N at constant FLOPs (FLOP-matched). The reconstruction is weighted by the router's probability for the selected expert to make routing differentiable end-to-end. Training minimizes a combined loss of reconstruction MSE and an auxiliary load-balancing term (scaled by α·d) that penalizes uneven expert utilization, following the Switch Transformer formulation. The paper shows FLOP-matched Switch SAEs Pareto-dominate TopK, Gated, and ReLU SAEs on the sparsity-reconstruction frontier, while width-matched variants reduce FLOPs by up to 128× while retaining ReLU SAE-level performance.

## Results

On GPT-2 small (768-dim, layer 8, OpenWebText), FLOP-matched Switch SAEs (2–8 experts) Pareto-dominate TopK, Gated, and ReLU SAEs on both reconstruction MSE and fraction of loss recovered (FLR) across the full sparsity range (L0 = 8–128). In the width-matched setting (16–128 experts), Switch SAEs reduce FLOPs per activation by up to 128× while still outperforming ReLU and Gated SAEs, though they underperform TopK SAEs in MSE and FLR. Scaling-law experiments show Switch SAEs require roughly one order of magnitude less training compute to reach the same reconstruction MSE as dense TopK SAEs. Feature geometry analysis reveals 5–10% of decoder vectors are near-duplicates (cosine sim > 0.9), and automated interpretability scores for FLOP-matched Switch SAE features are comparable to TopK SAE features.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the TopK SAE (Gao et al., 2024), FLOP-matched Switch SAEs (2–8 experts, same FLOPs per activation but N× more total features) Pareto-dominate on both MSE and FLR across the entire sparsity range, while width-matched Switch SAEs (same total features, N× fewer FLOPs) consistently underperform TopK in MSE and FLR, with the gap narrowing as total width increases. Against the ReLU SAE (Bricken et al., 2023), Switch SAEs outperform in both FLOP-matched and width-matched settings; notably, a 128-expert width-matched Switch SAE retains ReLU SAE-level performance while performing 128× fewer FLOPs per activation. Against the Gated SAE (Rajamanoharan et al., 2024), Switch SAEs similarly outperform in both settings, with the advantage most pronounced at low sparsity (L0 < 32). The width-matched performance gap versus TopK is attributed to feature duplication across experts (5–10% of decoder vectors have a near-duplicate with cosine similarity > 0.9), which reduces effective SAE capacity. On interpretability, FLOP-matched Switch SAE features match TopK SAE detection scores, while width-matched Switch SAE features show a higher true-positive rate on activating contexts but a lower true-negative rate, consistent with a bias toward duplicating frequent features.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies the Switch Transformer architecture (Fedus et al., 2022) — a simplification of the Sparsely-Gated Mixture-of-Experts layer (Shazeer et al., 2017) — to the problem of training sparse autoencoders for mechanistic interpretability. It builds directly on the TopK SAE (Gao et al., 2024) as its baseline and the broader SAE literature (Bricken et al., 2023; Rajamanoharan et al., 2024a), but is the first work to introduce conditional computation into SAE training. The key architectural departure is replacing the single dense encoder-decoder pair with N smaller expert SAEs and a learned router, thereby decoupling total feature count from per-activation FLOPs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As frontier models grow, training SAEs wide enough to capture all features becomes computationally intractable; Switch SAEs address this by delivering roughly one order of magnitude in compute savings at FLOP-matched settings while Pareto-dominating TopK SAEs on the sparsity-reconstruction frontier. However, width-matched Switch SAEs underperform TopK SAEs due to feature duplication across experts (up to ~10% capacity loss), and performance degrades notably on MLP and attention outputs. Open questions include whether more sophisticated routing (expert-choice, multi-expert), feature deduplication, or larger-scale training can close the width-matched gap, and whether increased cross-expert duplication complicates downstream tasks like feature steering or circuit discovery.
