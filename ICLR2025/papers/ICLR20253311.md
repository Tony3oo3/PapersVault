## Problem

Prior safe transfer-learning methods guarantee safety only under the strong assumption that safety-relevant dynamics remain identical between the source (training) and target (deployment) environments. In realistic sim-to-real settings, dynamics inevitably shift (e.g., changes in friction, mass), potentially causing safety violations after transfer. This paper asks: can we train an agent in a controlled environment such that its transfer to a target environment with unknown, potentially different dynamics is provably safe?

## Input / Output

The guide is trained in a reward-free source CMDP using state observations comprising pseudo-LIDAR readings for hazards and vases (16-ray, [0,1]^16), plus velocimeter, accelerometer, gyroscope, and magnetometer readings (each in ℝ³). The target task augments observations with a goal-detection LIDAR, which is stripped away by the Q^c-irrelevant abstraction σ to map target states back to the source state space. The action space is continuous ([-1,1]² for throttle and steering), and the uncertainty set is parameterized by mass and friction multipliers ranging from 0.5× to 1.5× the source-task values. The output is a student policy that satisfies the expected cumulative cost constraint d^⊙ throughout training in the target task.

## Method

The method robustifies a reward-free guide policy in the source CMDP by injecting one of three domain-agnostic action disturbances during training: random Gaussian action noise, adversarial action perturbations (solving a noisy-action robust MDP where an adversary maximizes cumulative cost), or entropy maximization. The key theoretical result (Theorem 1) shows that under a Q^c-irrelevant state abstraction σ preserving boundedness and a bounded uncertainty set U^⊙, a guide that is safe for all source dynamics within radius δ of the nominal dynamics transfers to a policy π∘σ that is safe for all target dynamics in U^⊙. Transfer to the student uses a control-switching behavior policy that samples from the guide whenever any cost has been incurred and from the student otherwise, augmented by a KL-divergence imitation term weighted by the Lagrangian multiplier to encourage the student to mimic the guide's safe behavior. The formal objective minimizes the worst-case safety jump-start over the uncertainty set while maintaining a Δ-time-to-optimum threshold, with the p-tail safety jump-start introduced to mitigate variance from selecting single worst-case dynamics.

## Results

In the source task M₁◇, the random action noise guide with α=0.23 reduces worst-case cumulative cost from 805.1 (non-robust, α=0.00) to 2.3, a >350× improvement, while maintaining near-optimal return (~20). In M₂◇, the same robustification reduces worst-case cost from 862.6 to 91.3. In the full student training experiment (Figure 7), the robust guide (α=0.29) achieves a safety jump-start near −100% (fully safe from the first epoch) under unfavorable target dynamics (m=1.5m◇, η=0.5η◇), whereas the non-robust guide from Yang et al. (2023) incurs substantial early safety violations. The robust guide also yields a substantially higher return jump-start for the student.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the non-robust guided safe exploration method of Yang et al. (2023), which trains the guide without any action disturbances (α=0.00). The robust method decisively outperforms this baseline on worst-case safety: in M₁◇ the worst-case cumulative cost drops from 805.1 to as low as 2.3 (α=0.23), and in M₂◇ from 862.6 to 91.3. In the full student-training experiment under the most challenging target dynamics (m=1.5m◇, η=0.5η◇), the robust guide (α=0.29) achieves a safety jump-start near −100%, meaning the student is safe from the very first epoch, while the non-robust guide produces significant early safety violations. The robust guide also yields a substantially higher return jump-start, indicating better knowledge transfer for task completion. However, the paper notes that robustness in the source task does not always translate to the best worst-case safety jump-start in the target task (e.g., Figure 5 shows a robust agent with poor worst-case jump-start), so the advantage is not uniform across all α values and environments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the guided safe exploration framework of Yang et al. (2023), which trains a reward-free 'guide' in a source task and uses it to safely train a 'student' in a target task, by addressing the critical gap that prior safe transfer methods assume safety-relevant dynamics are preserved between environments. The robustification strategy draws on three established techniques—random action noise (Hollenstein et al., 2022), adversarial action perturbations (Tessler et al., 2019), and entropy maximization (Eysenbach & Levine, 2022)—but departs from the RCMDP literature by not requiring knowledge of the uncertainty set during training, instead using action disturbances as a proxy for unknown dynamics shifts. The theoretical safety guarantee (Theorem 1) builds on Q^c-irrelevant state abstraction (Li et al., 2006) and the model-invariant abstraction results of Simão et al. (2021), but applies them in a novel robust transfer setting where the source and target dynamics can be arbitrarily different.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first theoretical guarantee for safe transfer under dynamics shifts, which is essential for realistic sim-to-real deployment in safety-critical domains (autonomous driving, robotics) where physics parameters inevitably differ. A key open question highlighted by the paper is the disconnect between source-task robustness and transfer quality: a guide that is robust to dynamics changes in the source task can still exhibit poor safety jump-start in the target task, suggesting that robustness and transferability are not equivalent properties. The introduction of the p-tail metric as a practical alternative to the high-variance worst-case estimate is a useful contribution, but the method's reliance on tuning the disturbance weight α empirically (with no principled way to set it without access to the target dynamics) limits its practical applicability. Future work could explore adaptive or curriculum-based disturbance scheduling, or tighter connections between the perturbation magnitude and the achievable robustness radius.
