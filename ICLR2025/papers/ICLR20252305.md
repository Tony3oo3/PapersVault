## Problem

Deep learning training regimes with multiple interacting models (multi-modal, distillation, multi-task) are typically specified as unstructured code, making them difficult to compose, manipulate, or reason about formally. Prior pipeline representations (e.g., DAG-based AutoML frameworks) lack a mathematically grounded semantics for what nodes and edges mean, and cannot express arbitrary interactions between multiple models sharing components. The paper asks: can training setups be treated as structured data with rigorous semantics, enabling principled composition and manipulation?

## Input / Output

The user specifies a learning diagram as a directed multigraph whose vertices are annotated with Lawvere metric spaces (data spaces with a distance/loss function) and whose edges are annotated with model computations (PyTorch or Flux.jl functions), plus a preorder on edges that determines argument ordering for asymmetric losses. The library automatically extracts all pairs of parallel paths via a generalized Floyd-Warshall algorithm over a set-valued adjacency matrix, then compiles the diagram into a single PyTorch/Flux.jl module that samples data, runs both path computations, and sums the per-path losses. The output is a composite loss function and a trainable module; after training, the resulting model collection can itself be used as a component in larger diagrams.

## Method

The core formalism defines a learning diagram as a functor from the free category on a learning graph (a directed multigraph with an edge preorder) into Law, the category of Lawvere metric spaces and Lipschitz-continuous maps. Loss is constructed by identifying pairs of parallel paths (same source and target vertex) that begin at indexing vertices, computing the Lawvere-metric distance between their outputs, and summing over all such pairs; this loss is shown to be a contractive enriched functor, ensuring well-behavedness under data subsetting. Composition of diagrams is achieved via categorical colimits, which glue sub-diagrams along shared vertices (e.g., a common feature-extraction backbone), enabling multi-task and few-shot pipelines to be built by attaching task-specific classifier heads to a shared encoder. The DiagrammaticLearning.jl library implements all of this, supporting model homomorphisms for sub-diagram manipulation (e.g., freezing a component) and automatic compilation to executable training loops in PyTorch or Flux.jl.

## Results

Across three replication experiments, the learning diagram framework reproduces or exceeds baseline performance. On image captioning, the ViT-B-16 backbone variant achieves 71.39 BLEU on Flickr8k and 71.78 on Flickr30k, surpassing the original GoogLeNet-based NIC (63 and 66 BLEU). On CIFAR-10 knowledge distillation, the distilled student reaches 69.21% accuracy (vs. 70.81% for the official PyTorch implementation). On few-shot classification, the compositional colimit-based pipeline achieves 78.82% (MI 5-shot) and 85.6% (CFS 5-shot), within 1–4 points of the Tian et al. (2020) baseline across Mini-ImageNet, Tiered-ImageNet, Cifar-FS, and FC100.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Vinyals et al. (2015) on image captioning, the learning diagram implementations outperform the original on both Flickr8k and Flickr30k BLEU: the ViT-B-16 variant reaches 71.39/71.78 versus the original's 63/66, and even the GoogLeNet variant (66.85/70.06) exceeds the baseline. On CIFAR-10 knowledge distillation, the learning diagram is broadly comparable to the official PyTorch tutorial: it achieves a higher teacher accuracy (76.24% vs. 74.72%) but a lower distilled-student accuracy (69.21% vs. 70.81%), a gap of roughly 1.6 points. Against Tian et al. (2020) on few-shot classification, the learning diagram trails by 1–4 points on most benchmarks (e.g., 58.13% vs. 62.02% on MI 1-shot; 80.15% vs. 84.41% on TI 5-shot) but slightly exceeds the baseline on FC100 (43.1% vs. 42.6% for 1-shot; 59.3% vs. 59.1% for 5-shot). Overall, the paper's stated goal is faithful replication rather than state-of-the-art performance, and the results confirm that the diagrammatic formalism introduces no significant performance degradation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes category-theoretic machinery—Lawvere metric spaces, free categories, and colimits—to give rigorous semantics to training pipeline specifications, departing from the DAG-based formulations dominant in AutoML (e.g., Zöller & Huber 2021, D3M) where nodes and edges lack precise mathematical meaning. It is conceptually adjacent to categorical ML work on backpropagation as a functor (Fong et al. 2019, Cruttwell et al. 2022) but inverts the focus: rather than giving semantics to model updates, it gives semantics to the loss function and the compositional structure of multi-model training regimes. The colimit-based composition of task-specific sub-diagrams along a shared backbone is a novel application of categorical gluing to the few-shot/multi-task setting, going beyond what standard DAG pipeline representations support.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a principled path toward treating training pipelines as manipulable data structures, enabling operations like component swapping, sub-diagram freezing, and compositional assembly that are error-prone in imperative code. However, the current implementation is a beta library with results only comparable to (not exceeding) baselines, and the theory does not yet address practical training details like mini-batching or continuous distributions. Open questions include whether the categorical semantics can be exploited for distributed training, model management, or automated pipeline search, and whether the formalism scales to the heterogeneous, multi-stage pipelines common in foundation-model research.
