## Problem

Conclusions of influential econometrics studies can be overturned by removing less than 0.5% of samples, yet no prior algorithm could certify the non-existence of such small influential subsets for regressions with dimension 4 or greater. Existing approaches either only find candidate removal sets without proving optimality, scale exponentially in dimension, or require distributional assumptions too strong for real-world data. This paper provides the first efficient algorithms that produce valid, non-trivial lower bounds on the number of samples that must be removed to flip a regression coefficient's sign.

## Input / Output

The algorithms take a feature matrix X ∈ R^{n×d}, label vector Y ∈ R^n, and a unit direction of interest e ∈ R^d; OHARE additionally requires a partition of samples into m categorical buckets. The output is a pair of vectors U, L ∈ R^n where U_k ≥ Δ_k(e) ≥ L_k for every k, giving a certificate that no subset of size ≤ k can shift ⟨β,e⟩ by more than U_k. From these bounds one reads off a lower bound on k_sign(e) as the smallest k for which U_k exceeds |⟨β,e⟩|. The bounds are valid unconditionally on the data, and the runtime is O(n²d + n² log n) for ACRE and O(n²(d+m) + n²m log n) for OHARE, with the main memory bottleneck being three n×n floating-point matrices.

## Method

ACRE decomposes the OLS coefficient change β − β_S into a first-order term (the sum of influence scores over removed samples, computed exactly via sorting) and a higher-order correction involving the inverse of the retained-sample covariance Σ_S^{-1}. The higher-order term is bounded by reducing each factor to a Maximal Subset Sum Norm (MSN) problem over Gram matrices G_{X⊗X}, G_{XR}, and G_{X⟨X,e⟩}, which are solved by the Refined Triangle Inequality (RTI) algorithm or a spectral decomposition backend. OHARE addresses the singularity that arises when removing all samples from a one-hot category by explicitly performing Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization ('reaveraging') to project out the categorical columns, yielding a controlled regression whose covariance remains nonsingular. Per-bucket MSN bounds are then combined via a knapsack-style dynamic program over partitions k = k_1 + … + k_m, with a symmetry refinement (using Σ_{i∈B_j} X_i = 0) that tightens bounds for large removal fractions. Under subgaussian or subexponential feature distributions, the upper and lower bounds match up to a 1 + Õ((d + k√d)/n) multiplicative factor for k up to Õ(min{n/√d, n²/d²}).

## Results

On the Nightlights dataset (n=3895, d=209), OHARE certifies that at least 29 samples must be removed to flip the sign of the main coefficient, compared to upper bounds of 136 (AMIP) and 110 (KZC) — the first non-trivial lower bound previously known. Across the 6 Cash Transfer regressions (n up to 10,781, d=18), OHARE's lower bounds range from 5 to 178, while AMIP/KZC upper bounds range from 5 to 570. On the 7 OHIE instrumental-variables regressions (n up to 23,407, d=18), OHARE certifies robustness to 21–77 removals versus upper bounds of 72–257 from both AMIP and KZC. In all 14 regressions, no non-trivial lower bound was previously known; OHARE's bounds typically close the gap to the upper bounds by a factor of 2–5×.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary contribution is providing the first non-trivial *lower* bounds on k_sign(e), whereas all prior methods (AMIP, KZC) only produce *upper* bounds by finding specific removal sets. On Nightlights, OHARE certifies robustness to 29 removals while AMIP finds a 136-sample set and KZC finds a 110-sample set that flip the sign — meaning the true k_sign lies in [29, 110], a 3.8× gap that was previously unbounded below. On the Cash Transfer and OHIE datasets, OHARE's lower bounds typically fall within a factor of 2–5× of the AMIP/KZC upper bounds (e.g., 119 vs. 225 on Cash Transfer regression 4; 77 vs. 257 on OHIE regression 1). Against Freund & Hopkins (2023), which provides upper bounds via spectral methods, ACRE's RTI backend yields tighter upper bounds on synthetic data (Figure 2b) where F&H's bound is essentially trivial. The paper also demonstrates that ACRE alone (without one-hot awareness) fails on datasets with categorical features — certifying only 7 removals on Nightlights versus OHARE's 29 — highlighting the necessity of the OHARE extension for real-world econometric data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the robustness-auditing framework introduced by Broderick et al. (2020, AMIP) and Kuschnig et al. (2021, KZC), which could only produce upper bounds on the number of removals needed to flip a regression coefficient, by providing the first non-trivial *lower* bounds (certificates of robustness) for datasets of dimension 4 or greater. The key technical departure is decomposing the OLS perturbation into an exactly computable first-order term (recovering AMIP's influence scores) and a higher-order correction bounded via a Maximal Subset Sum Norm (MSN) subproblem, with the Refined Triangle Inequality (RTI) as a practical backend. The OHARE algorithm further departs from all prior work by handling one-hot encoded categorical features through an explicit Gram-Schmidt 'reaveraging' step and a knapsack-style dynamic program, addressing a singularity that renders ACRE (and all continuous approaches) vacuous once removals exceed the smallest category size.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for econometrics: OHARE certifies that no subset of ≤0.7% of samples can overturn the Nightlights study's conclusion, where prior algorithms could only show that 2.8% suffices and no non-trivial lower bound existed. The conceptual contribution—that one-hot encodings create a fundamental barrier for all smooth/continuous robustness-certification methods (gradient descent, SDP, spectral)—is well-motivated by the perturbation example in Figure 1 and has implications beyond OLS. Open questions include extending the framework to logistic regression and LASSO, connecting robustness certificates to differential privacy guarantees, and certifying robustness to perturbations beyond sample removal (e.g., ℓ₂-bounded feature shifts). The 1/√(log n) error term for OHARE, while vanishing, goes to zero slowly, and the distributional assumptions (well-behaved features, Gaussian noise) are not always satisfied in practice.
