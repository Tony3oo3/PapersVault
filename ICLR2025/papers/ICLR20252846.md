## Problem

Existing neural dynamics modeling methods either ignore paired behavioral data entirely or require it at inference time, yet in real-world deployments (e.g., resting-state recordings) behavioral signals are often partial or unavailable. This creates a critical gap: how to build a model that performs well using only neural activity at inference while still benefiting from behavioral insights gained during training. The paper frames this as a privileged information problem and shows it remains largely unexplored in computational neuroscience.

## Input / Output

The method takes neural activity recordings (spike counts in 5ms bins or calcium imaging at ~4.3 Hz) as the sole inference-time input, while behavioral time-series (hand velocity, running speed, pupil dilation) serve as privileged features consumed only during training. Outputs include reconstructed neural activity for masked positions, which supports downstream tasks: held-out neuron activity prediction (Co-bps), behavior decoding via ridge regression (Vel-R²), PSTH matching (PSTH-R²), and transcriptomic neuron identity classification (excitatory/inhibitory and 5-class inhibitory subclass). The framework is model-agnostic, wrapping around any existing NDM architecture (NDT, LFADS, NeuPRINT) without modifying its core design.

## Method

BLEND employs a two-stage teacher-student distillation under the Learning Using Privileged Information (LUPI) framework. In Stage 1, a teacher model is trained with masked time-series modeling (MTM) loss on both masked neural activity and behavioral signals concatenated along the feature dimension. In Stage 2, a student model trained on neural activity alone is optimized with a weighted combination of the MTM reconstruction loss and a distillation loss that transfers the teacher's behavior-informed knowledge. The paper investigates four distillation strategies—hard (MSE on outputs), soft (temperature-scaled KL divergence), feature (layer-wise MSE on intermediate representations), and correlation (Frobenius-norm matching of output correlation matrices)—finding that correlation distillation generally yields the best results for transformer-based models while hard distillation works best for RNN-based models. The key design choice is that behavior is integrated simply by feature concatenation rather than through cross-attention or dedicated modules, keeping the framework lightweight and architecture-agnostic.

## Results

On the NLB'21 benchmark, BLEND-distilled NDT achieves Vel-R² of 0.891 (MC-Maze), 0.372 (MC-RTT), and 0.788 (Area2-Bump), representing 14.4%, 17.0%, and 51.8% improvements over the base NDT model, respectively. BLEND-distilled LFADS achieves the best Co-bps of 0.321 on MC-Maze and PSTH-R² of 0.615 on Area2-Bump, outperforming all baselines. On the transcriptomic identity prediction task, BLEND-distilled NeuPRINT reaches 78.9% top-1 accuracy for EI classification and 57.1% for 5-class subclass prediction (multi-animal), improving over the base NeuPRINT by 5.5% and 15.4%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BLEND-distilled models outperform all 12 baselines across every metric and dataset. Against the strongest neural-decoding-only baseline STNDT (Vel-R² 0.773 on MC-Maze, 0.563 on Area2-Bump), NDT-Distill-Best achieves 0.891 and 0.788 respectively, while LFADS-Distill-Best reaches 0.877 and 0.837. Against the strongest behavior-as-prior baseline TNDM (Vel-R² 0.730 on MC-Maze, 0.677 on Area2-Bump), both distilled models surpass it by 14–52%. On the transcriptomic task, NeuPRINT-Distill-Best (78.9% EI, 57.1% subclass) beats the prior SOTA NeuPRINT (74.8%, 49.5%) and LOLCAT_Raw (66.4%, 43.9%) by 4–15 percentage points. The largest gains appear on Area2-Bump (51.8% Vel-R² improvement for NDT, 44.7% PSTH-R² for LFADS), suggesting BLEND is most beneficial when the base model struggles with low-neuron-count or perturbation-heavy data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of two established lines of work: neural dynamics modeling (NDM) and Learning Using Privileged Information (LUPI). Prior NDM methods either ignore paired behavioral data entirely (NDT, LFADS, STNDT) or require behavior at inference time through carefully designed priors (pi-VAE, TNDM, PSID, CEBRA), often necessitating strong assumptions about behavior-relevant vs. behavior-irrelevant dynamics. BLEND departs from both by treating behavior as privileged information available only during training, then distilling knowledge to a student model that operates on neural activity alone. The LUPI framework itself has been applied to recommendation systems, medical imaging, and emotion recognition, but its application to computational neuroscience is, per the authors, an inaugural step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practical gap in computational neuroscience where behavioral recordings are often partial or unavailable at deployment (e.g., resting-state recordings), yet the >50% improvement in behavioral decoding and >15% in neuron identity prediction demonstrate substantial value when paired data does exist during training. The model-agnostic design means practitioners can enhance existing architectures (NDT, LFADS, NeuPRINT) without redesign, lowering the adoption barrier. Key limitations include the simplistic feature-concatenation integration of behavior (motivated by low dimensionality but potentially missing complex interactions), and the current restriction to temporally continuous behavioral signals. Open questions include extending to discrete/non-temporal behavior (partially explored with DMFC-RGS), more sophisticated cross-modal integration (e.g., cross-attention), and whether the architecture-specific distillation strategy findings generalize beyond the tested models.
