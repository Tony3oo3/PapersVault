## Problem

Given a start and end frame, video frame interpolation suffers from inherent motion ambiguity—multiple plausible transitions exist, yet existing methods produce a single deterministic output that may not match user creative intent. Traditional flow- or kernel-based approaches further struggle with large appearance changes, and prior generative methods lack fine-grained controllability over local object motions during the transition.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251854/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a start frame and end frame (RGB images at 512×320) as required inputs, plus optional point trajectories that are rendered as Gaussian heatmaps (41×41 grid, σ=8) for the control branch. It outputs a variable number of intermediate frames (7 in main experiments, supporting 2×, 4×, and 8× temporal upsampling). In interactive mode, users drag 1–10 keypoints to define trajectories; in autopilot mode, SIFT matching between the two frames initializes trajectories that are refined during denoising via bi-directional feature-space point tracking.

## Method

Framer fine-tunes Stable Video Diffusion's 3D-UNet in two stages: first, it injects end-frame conditioning by concatenating the VAE-encoded last-frame latent with the noisy latent and appending CLIP embeddings of both frames for cross-attention; second, it adds a ControlNet-style encoder branch that processes trajectory heatmaps and injects features into the U-Net decoder via zero-convolution. The key insight is that point trajectories serve a dual purpose—they provide user-controllable motion guidance while simultaneously establishing explicit cross-frame correspondences that help the model handle large shape/style changes. In autopilot mode, a novel bi-directional point-tracking algorithm refines trajectories at each denoising step by searching for nearest-neighbor features in the U-Net's penultimate upsampled block relative to both the start and end anchor points, accepting updates only when forward and backward matches agree within a spatial threshold.

## Results

On DAVIS-7, Framer achieves the best FVD of 115.65 (vs. 162.86 for FILM and 245.25 for AMT), while Framer with Co-Tracker further improves to 102.31 FVD and also attains the highest PSNR (22.75) and SSIM (0.7931). On UCF101-7, Framer achieves the best FVD of 181.55 (vs. 239.48 for FILM), and Framer with Co-Tracker reaches 159.87 FVD with the best PSNR (27.08) and SSIM (0.9024). The paper notes that reconstruction metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS) inherently penalize plausible but non-pixel-aligned interpolations, so FVD and user-study preference are the more meaningful indicators of quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Framer achieves the best FVD on both DAVIS-7 (115.65) and UCF101-7 (181.55), substantially outperforming the next-best traditional method FILM (162.86 / 239.48) and all diffusion-based baselines (LDMVFI 269.72, SVDKFI 382.19, DynamicCrafter 468.78 on DAVIS-7). With Co-Tracker trajectories, Framer further improves FVD to 102.31 / 159.87 and also leads in PSNR and SSIM on both datasets, surpassing RIFE (22.00 / 27.04 PSNR) and AMT (21.66 / 26.64 PSNR). On reconstruction metrics alone, FILM retains the best LPIPS on DAVIS-7 (0.2191 vs. 0.2525) and UCF101-7 (0.1378 vs. 0.1714), and RIFE edges out Framer on UCF101-7 PSNR (27.04 vs. 25.04), which the authors attribute to the inherent limitation of pixel-aligned metrics for generative interpolation. Among diffusion-based competitors, DynamicCrafter and SVDKFI perform markedly worse across all metrics, confirming that Framer's trajectory-guided approach handles large inter-frame differences more robustly. A user study with 20 raters (1,000 ratings) shows a strong human preference for Framer outputs over all baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

Framer builds on Stable Video Diffusion (SVD) as its base video diffusion model, fine-tuning its 3D-UNet to accept both first- and last-frame conditioning for interpolation. The interactive control mechanism is adapted from the image/video editing lineage (ControlNet for the conditioning architecture, DragNUWA and DragAnything for Gaussian heatmap trajectory encoding), but repurposed here for the frame interpolation task where prior diffusion-based methods (LDMVFI, VIDIM, SVDKFI) produce only a single deterministic output. The 'autopilot' mode introduces a bi-directional point-tracking strategy inspired by DragGAN and DragDiffusion, but applied iteratively within the denoising loop to refine trajectories without user input—a mechanism not previously explored in VFI.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By making frame interpolation interactive via simple drag gestures, Framer addresses a practical gap: deterministic VFI methods cannot resolve the inherent ambiguity of how objects should transition between two frames, which is critical for creative applications like image morphing, cartoon production, and slow-motion generation. The bi-directional consistency check in the autopilot mode is a lightweight but effective mechanism for maintaining temporal coherence. Key open questions remain: the method fails when no suitable matching points exist between input frames (e.g., character swaps), it currently supports only drag control without text or camera-pose guidance, and transitioning between semantically different clips is still challenging. The authors note that more powerful video diffusion backbones (Mochi, CogVideo-X) and text conditioning are natural next steps.
