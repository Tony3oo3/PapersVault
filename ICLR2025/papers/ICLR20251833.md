## Problem

Post-training interventions for language models—knowledge editing, model compression, and machine unlearning—are developing in isolation, yet practical deployment requires applying multiple interventions sequentially to the same model. There is no standardized framework or metric for studying how these interventions interact, making it unclear whether an edit survives quantization, whether unlearning works on a pruned model, or whether the order of application matters.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251833/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a pre-trained language model (Llama-3 8B as the primary benchmark, with Mistral 7B Instruct and Yi 1.5 9B Chat for generalizability) and applies pairs of interventions from three categories in both possible orders, sweeping over compression hyperparameters (sparsity 25–75%, quantization 2–8 bits). It produces two composability scores per intervention-specific criterion—Order-free Error and Order Sensitivity—computed as integrals over the hyperparameter sweep, alongside standard task metrics (Edit Success, Edit Generalization, Strict Edit Locality, WMDP accuracy, MMLU). The 417 compositions are summarized via a #Wins ranking that counts how often each method outperforms peers within its category across all criteria.

## Method

The paper introduces two composability metrics formalized as integrals over intervention hyperparameters. Order-free Error (Eq. 1) computes the area above the pointwise maximum of the two ordering curves for a given criterion κᵢ, capturing best-case performance regardless of which intervention is applied first; a smaller area means the second intervention lessens the first's success less. Order Sensitivity (Eq. 2) computes the area between the two ordering curves, isolating how much the combined outcome depends on application order. The framework is instantiated with 10 state-of-the-art methods (MEMIT, LoRA, Fine-tuning for editing; SparseGPT, Wanda, GPTQ, AWQ for compression; GA, GD, RMU for unlearning) evaluated on zsRE, WMDP, and MMLU. A key empirical finding is that MMLU—a common general-utility proxy—masks large composability differences visible only in intervention-specific metrics, and that compression consistently degrades both editing and unlearning success, with order effects varying by method pair (e.g., AWQ benefits from compress-then-edit while GPTQ benefits from edit-then-compress).

## Results

Across 417 compositions of knowledge editing, model compression, and machine unlearning on Llama-3 8B, the paper finds that model compression consistently degrades the success of other interventions, and the order of application dramatically alters outcomes. AWQ is the most composable quantization method (17 wins vs. GPTQ's 2), MEMIT is the most composable editor (14 wins vs. Fine-tuning's 9 and LoRA's 4), and RMU is the most composable unlearning method (10 wins vs. GD's 4 and GA's 1 in editing+unlearning compositions). Notably, MMLU scores remain nearly identical across compositions (Order-free Error ≈ 0.40–0.42) while editing-specific metrics vary by over 10×, demonstrating that general-purpose benchmarks are inadequate proxies for composability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among editing methods, MEMIT achieves the best overall composability (14 total wins across all metrics), with near-zero Order Sensitivity on MMLU but moderate sensitivity (0.24) on Edit Success when paired with Wanda; Fine-tuning is a surprisingly strong baseline at higher compression levels (9 wins), while LoRA is the least composable editor (4 wins), showing high Order Sensitivity (0.46) on Edit Generalization with Wanda. For quantization, AWQ dominates GPTQ decisively (17 vs. 2 wins), with GPTQ showing catastrophic Order-free Error of 0.42 on Edit Success with LoRA versus AWQ's 0.00. For pruning, SparseGPT edges out Wanda (8 vs. 6 wins), though both are far more composable than GPTQ. In unlearning compositions, RMU achieves the lowest Order Sensitivity across all editors (e.g., 0.01 on Edit Success with MEMIT), while GA is effectively non-composable due to catastrophic forgetting (Order-free Error of 0.93 on Edit Success with Fine-tuning). GD performs comparably to RMU on Order-free Error but with substantially higher Order Sensitivity (e.g., 0.40 vs. 0.01 on Edit Success with MEMIT), making RMU the preferred choice for sequential intervention pipelines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the concept of composability for post-training language model interventions, a property that prior work has not formally defined or systematically evaluated. Rather than proposing a new intervention method, it repurposes existing state-of-the-art techniques from three independently-developed lines of research—knowledge editing (MEMIT, LoRA, fine-tuning), model compression (SparseGPT, Wanda, GPTQ, AWQ), and machine unlearning (GA, GD, RMU)—under a unified evaluation lens. The two proposed metrics (Order-free Error and Order Sensitivity) formalize what was previously an informal, unmeasured concern: whether applying one intervention degrades the success of another. The paper positions itself as filling a gap where 'different types of interventions are largely developing independently' and 'we lack standardized ways to study how interventions interact.'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The findings have direct practical implications: since model compression is nearly universal in LM deployments, the discovery that it systematically hinders both editing and unlearning performance—and that the optimal order of application differs by method pair—provides concrete guidance for practitioners sequencing interventions. The demonstration that MMLU, the standard general-utility benchmark, is a poor proxy for composability challenges a common evaluation practice and motivates multi-metric assessment. Key open questions include whether composability trends generalize to larger model scales, whether new interventions can be explicitly designed to be composable (multi-objective), and how findings extend beyond the three intervention categories studied. The unified codebase and 417-composition dataset lower the barrier for future work in this direction.
