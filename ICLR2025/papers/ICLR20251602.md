## Problem

While chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning empirically improves transformer performance on multi-step tasks, the theoretical mechanism by which stepwise reasoning emerges during training remains unformalized. Parity is a canonical hard problem: no finite-precision gradient-based algorithm can solve it in polynomial steps without intermediate supervision, yet prior work on RNNs (Wies et al., 2023) showed that task decomposition with intermediate labels enables efficient learning. This paper provides the first provable optimization guarantees for transformers trained to generate CoT chains, establishing when and how task decomposition arises from gradient-based training.

## Input / Output

The model receives a d-bit binary string x ∈ {±1}^d and must predict the parity (product) of an unknown k-bit subset p, i.e., y = ∏_{j∈p} x_j. Each bit is encoded as a token consisting of a one-hot positional encoding concatenated with the bit value, and k−1 additional dummy tokens (initialized to zero) are appended to serve as intermediate reasoning slots. The transformer is applied recursively to its own output, so the final prediction is the top node of a binary tree of 2-parity computations, requiring only log₂(k) recursive steps under the block-autoregressive causal mask.

## Method

The architecture is a one-layer transformer with single-head softmax attention (reparameterized so attention scores depend only on positional encodings), a feedforward link function φ with φ(0)=−1, φ(±1)=1 that converts weighted sums into parities, and absolute positional encoding. The key mechanism is recursive self-application: the transformer generates intermediate tokens x_{d+1}, …, x_{d+k−1} that form a complete binary tree of 2-parity subtasks, with each node attending to its two children. Three training regimes are analyzed: (1) end-to-end without intermediate supervision is provably impossible in polynomial steps (Theorem 2); (2) with teacher forcing (ground-truth intermediate labels fed at each step), a single gradient update from zero initialization concentrates attention weights on the correct child nodes and solves all parities (Theorem 5); (3) without teacher forcing, data augmentation with unlabeled random strings acts as a self-consistency filter that zeros out uninformative intermediate outputs, enabling learning in log₂(k) iterations via inductive level-by-level optimization (Theorem 7). Weight quantization to integers after each update simplifies error propagation analysis.

## Results

The paper establishes three main theoretical guarantees for a one-layer transformer solving k-parity on d-bit inputs. Without intermediate supervision (Theorem 2), any gradient-based algorithm with n = Ω(d^ν) samples requires Ω̃(d^{ε/4}) or more queries to achieve nontrivial loss, with loss lower-bounded at 1 - O(d^{-ν₄}). With CoT and teacher forcing (Theorem 5), a single gradient update at learning rate η = Θ(d^{2+ε/16}) achieves prediction error ‖ŷ_test - y_test‖_∞ ≤ O(d^{-ε/8}) with n = Ω(d^{2+ε}) samples. Without teacher forcing but with data augmentation for self-consistency (Theorem 7), log₂(k) iterations suffice to achieve exponentially small loss exp(-Ω(d^{ε/16})). Numerically, on 64-bit inputs with k=32, the Direct model plateaus at prediction loss 0.5, basic CoT also fails to improve prediction loss beyond 0.5, while both CoT+teacher forcing and CoT+self-consistency drive prediction loss to near zero within 350 epochs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between training paradigms rather than competing architectures. The Direct model (end-to-end prediction without intermediate states) completely fails: weights become nearly uniform, ŷ ≈ 0, and prediction error is permanently stuck at 0.5, confirming the theoretical impossibility of Theorem 2. The basic CoT model (end-to-end chain generation with intermediate loss but no teacher forcing or augmentation) reduces CoT loss but prediction loss never improves beyond 0.5, as error accumulation at lower levels prevents the top-level node from receiving informative signals. CoT with teacher forcing (Theorem 5) achieves the strongest guarantee—solving parity in a single gradient update—by providing ground-truth intermediate states at each position, which isolates the relevant gradient signal at each node. CoT with self-consistency (Theorem 7) matches this success in log₂(k) steps without teacher forcing, using augmented unlabeled data to detect and zero out faulty intermediate reasoning, thereby mimicking the phased learning that teacher forcing provides simultaneously. The separation between the no-CoT lower bound (Theorem 2) and the CoT upper bounds (Theorems 5, 7) is strict and grows with sample size: for n = e^{Ω(d)}, the no-CoT setting requires exponentially many queries while CoT+TF needs only one.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RNN-based task decomposition results of Wies et al. (2023) to the transformer architecture, providing the first optimization guarantees for training transformers with chain-of-thought supervision. It also extends the parity impossibility results of Shalev-Shwartz et al. (2017) and Shamir (2018) from population gradient descent to the finite-sample setting. While prior work (Feng et al. 2023; Merrill & Sabharwal 2024; Li et al. 2024b) established that transformers with CoT can *express* parity solutions, and Li et al. (2024a) showed CoT ability can emerge in in-context learning, none analyzed the training dynamics of explicitly optimizing a transformer to generate reasoning chains end-to-end.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical explanation for why process supervision (intermediate step labels) is dramatically more effective than outcome-only supervision for multi-step reasoning, corroborating empirical findings from Lightman et al. (2024) and Huang et al. (2023a). The self-consistency filtering mechanism offers a principled justification for data augmentation strategies used in practice. Key limitations include the restriction to one-layer transformers, the specific k-parity problem with k=2^v, and carefully calibrated learning rates; whether these insights transfer to deeper architectures, continuous-valued tasks, or real language reasoning remains open.
