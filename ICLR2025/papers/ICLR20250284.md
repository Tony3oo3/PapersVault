## Problem

Computing Wardrop traffic equilibria with high-fidelity solvers (e.g., microscopic simulations) is prohibitively expensive, requiring hours to days per scenario. Pure machine learning baselines can predict flows quickly but fail to capture the combinatorial interdependencies between arcs that define equilibrium structure, leading to unrealistic flow distributions. The paper addresses this gap by introducing a hybrid architecture that preserves combinatorial structure while enabling fast, data-driven predictions.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a transportation network graph with arc attributes (length, speed, capacity, lane count, transit time), vertex attributes (counts of home/work locations, nodes, and arcs within multiple area-factor radii), and origin-destination pairs; for time-variant settings, additional temporal features (distance from rush hours, simulation time) are appended. The FNN or GNN statistical model maps these features to a latency parameterization θ (one or more coefficients per arc), which is then passed through the equilibrium layer to produce the final output: a predicted aggregated traffic flow vector ȳ over all arcs. Inference requires only a single forward pass through the neural network followed by solving the equilibrium subproblem.

## Method

WardropNet is a combinatorial optimization-augmented machine learning (COAML) pipeline where a neural network (FNN or GNN) predicts the parameterization θ of latency functions, and a subsequent equilibrium layer solves the resulting Wardrop equilibrium as a convex regularized flow problem. The key technical contribution is an end-to-end training paradigm using the Fenchel-Young loss as a convex surrogate for the Bregman divergence between predicted and target flows, which yields a well-defined gradient through the combinatorial equilibrium layer via Danskin's lemma. The paper provides four equilibrium layer variants: Euclidean regularization (orthogonal projection), perturbation regularization (Monte-Carlo estimation via Gaussian noise on the objective), piecewise constant latencies encoded on an extended multigraph with parallel arcs, and polynomial latencies with perturbation. The perturbation-based variants are most effective, as they allow the equilibrium layer to handle general combinatorial structures while remaining differentiable, and the Fenchel-Young loss coincides exactly with the Bregman divergence under this regularization.

## Results

The primary metric is Mean Absolute Error (MAE) on aggregated arc flows. The best COAML pipeline (CL, using piecewise constant latencies with perturbation regularization) outperforms the best pure ML baseline (FNN) by 60% on the high-entropy scenario (FNN MAE = 1.75), 75% on the low-entropy scenario (FNN MAE = 4.18), 7% on the square-world scenario, 72% on the Berlin scenario, and 71% on the Berlin-AP scenario. In the time-variant setting (SW-TV), the CL-pipeline outperforms the GNN baseline by 23%. Among the three COAML variants, CL > PL > ER in accuracy, with the ER-pipeline (Euclidean regularization) performing worst due to its overly simple affine latency functions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares WardropNet (COAML pipelines: CL, PL, ER) against two pure ML baselines: a Feedforward Neural Network (FNN) and a Graph Neural Network (GNN), both trained to directly predict arc flows. The CL-pipeline (piecewise constant latencies + perturbation regularization) dominates the FNN on all five time-invariant scenarios, with the largest gains (72–75%) on the low-entropy and Berlin scenarios where combinatorial path structure matters most. On the time-variant SW-TV scenario, the CL-pipeline beats the GNN by 23%, while the GNN itself slightly outperforms the FNN in that setting. Among the COAML variants, the CL-pipeline consistently achieves the lowest MAE, followed by the PL-pipeline (polynomial latencies), while the ER-pipeline (Euclidean regularization) underperforms all others due to its restrictive affine latency form. The FNN slightly outperforms the GNN in all time-invariant scenarios, suggesting that arc-level features are sufficient without explicit graph message passing for static prediction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) ML-based traffic equilibrium prediction, which has progressed from conventional ML to GNNs and implicit neural networks that embed physical constraints, and (2) combinatorial optimization-augmented machine learning (COAML) pipelines that embed a CO layer into a statistical model. Prior equilibrium-layer approaches (Amos & Kolter 2017; Bai et al. 2019; Smith et al. 2022) either restrict to small quadratic programs or impose well-posedness conditions to enable backpropagation. WardropNet departs from these by leveraging the Fenchel-Young loss (Blondel et al. 2020) combined with perturbation regularization (Berthet et al. 2020) to differentiate through a general, potentially combinatorial equilibrium layer, while also showing the loss is equivalent to minimizing a Bregman divergence that matches the geometry of Wardrop equilibria. The paper generalizes the classical Wardrop (1952) equilibrium to allow non-decomposable latency functions (spill-over effects), which is necessary to accommodate the regularization terms that make end-to-end learning tractable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

WardropNet offers a principled way to replace expensive simulation-based equilibrium solvers (hours to days) with a fast forward-pass prediction that preserves the combinatorial structure of traffic flows, with the Fenchel-Young loss providing both a tractable gradient and a geometrically meaningful objective. The 72% improvement over pure ML baselines on realistic Berlin scenarios is practically significant for transportation planning, though the baselines (simple FNN/GNN) are not state-of-the-art traffic predictors, and the networks studied are relatively small. Open questions include scaling to city-wide networks, incorporating more complex latency families, and whether the COAML paradigm extends to other equilibrium problems (e.g., Stackelberg games, multi-modal transport). The paper also notes the learning paradigm is not limited to traffic and could learn arbitrary flow physics given appropriate training data and CO-layer.
