## Problem

Federated instruction tuning (FedIT) of LLMs has not been examined from a safety-alignment perspective, leaving open the question of whether malicious clients can compromise the global model's safety. This paper reveals that a simple data-poisoning attack—training local LLMs on unaligned data (harmful instruction + harmful response)—can drastically reduce safety alignment, and that the attack is inherently stealthy because the optimization objective (following instructions and producing detailed responses) is nearly identical to that of benign clients, rendering all six standard model-level FL defenses ineffective.

## Input / Output

The system takes a pre-trained LLM (Llama2-7B or Mistral-7B, 8-bit quantized with LoRA rank 32) and instruction-response pairs as input; benign clients hold normal or safety-aligned data while malicious clients hold unaligned data that can be extracted from public datasets (Beavertails, HH-RLHF) or auto-generated via an off-the-shelf LLM in a two-step pipeline. The output is a globally aggregated LLM evaluated on safety (AdvBench via Rule, MD-Judge, and RM metrics) and helpfulness (MT-Bench first turn). The post-hoc defense additionally consumes an auto-generated defense dataset of 1,000 samples (aligned + normal pairs) for 500 fine-tuning steps at the final aggregation round.

## Method

The safety attack replaces malicious clients' training data with unaligned pairs (harmful instruction + harmful response), which is stealthy because the gradient direction—learning to follow instructions and produce informative, non-refusing responses—overlaps with benign clients' objective, so model-parameter-level defenses (Krum, DnC, FoolsGold, Residual, Median, Trimmedmean) cannot separate malicious from benign updates. The proposed post-hoc defense decouples protection from the training loop: after FedAvg aggregation, the server fine-tunes the global LLM on an auto-generated defense dataset containing aligned data (harmful instruction + harmless response, produced by appending a safety-reminder prompt) and normal data (normal instruction + normal response). Three levels of external-resource dependency are studied: Level 1 samples from existing datasets, Level 2 uses an external off-the-shelf LLM for generation, and Level 3 (self-alignment) uses the aggregated LLM itself. The defense is plug-and-play across all FL baselines and achieves up to 69% absolute improvement in safety metrics (e.g., MD-Judge) while preserving helpfulness, matching or exceeding the safety of an unattacked FedAvg model.

## Results

Under the safety attack (MaliciousGen, LMSYS-Chat), FedAvg's safety drops dramatically: Rule falls from 82.88% to 43.27% and MD-Judge from 66.15% to 11.35%, while helpfulness (MT-1) remains nearly unchanged at 4.19. The best existing FL defense (Krum) recovers only to 50.38% Rule and 16.73% MD-Judge, a mere ~5% improvement over attacked FedAvg. The proposed Level-2 post-hoc defense restores safety to 78.08% Rule and 83.08% MD-Judge—exceeding the no-attack baseline—while preserving helpfulness at 4.18 MT-1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All six existing FL defenses (Median, Trimmedmean, Krum, DnC, FoolsGold, Residual) fail to effectively counter the safety attack, achieving at most a ~5% absolute improvement in MD-Judge over attacked FedAvg (Krum: 16.73 vs. 11.35 on LMSYS-Chat). Krum is the strongest existing defense, recovering Rule to 50.38% and MD-Judge to 16.73% on LMSYS-Chat, yet still far below the no-attack baseline of 82.88%/66.15%. Median and FoolsGold sometimes perform worse than plain FedAvg under attack (e.g., FoolsGold Rule 41.54% vs. FedAvg 43.27%), confirming the stealthiness of the attack at the model-parameter level. The proposed Level-2 post-hoc defense dramatically outperforms all baselines, achieving 78.08% Rule and 83.08% MD-Judge on LMSYS-Chat—surpassing even the no-attack FedAvg baseline (82.88%/66.15%) on MD-Judge—while maintaining comparable helpfulness (4.18 vs. 4.19 MT-1). The defense also demonstrates plug-and-play compatibility, improving all six baselines by an average of ~57% in MD-Judge when applied on top of them (Table 3).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the OpenFedLLM federated instruction tuning framework and the broader LLM safety alignment literature (Beavertails, HH-RLHF, AdvBench), but departs from both in a key way. Traditional FL poisoning attacks (label flipping, model poisoning) create contradictory optimization directions that are detectable at the model-parameter level, whereas this paper introduces a safety-specific data poisoning attack where malicious clients' optimization objectives (generating detailed, informative responses) are nearly identical to benign clients', making model-level defenses ineffective. The post-hoc defense strategy also departs from the standard FL paradigm of filtering malicious clients during aggregation, instead decoupling defense from training by applying a corrective fine-tuning step after aggregation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work reveals a practical and previously unexplored vulnerability in federated LLM training: a small fraction of malicious clients (30%) can drastically compromise safety alignment (up to 70% reduction) while remaining undetectable by all six standard FL defense mechanisms. The finding that model-parameter-level defenses are fundamentally mismatched to this threat class has broad implications for anyone deploying federated instruction tuning in production. Open questions include whether more sophisticated attacks (e.g., multi-round adaptive attacks, attacks targeting helpfulness rather than safety) could further undermine the system, whether the post-hoc defense scales to larger models or more diverse safety dimensions, and whether the defense itself could be gamed by a more adaptive attacker.
