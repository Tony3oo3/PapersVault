## Problem

Existing theoretical analyses of in-context learning (ICL) suffer from two key limitations: they assume i.i.d. input-label pairs in prompts, which diverges from real auto-regressive language where tokens are interdependent, and they explain what ICL does (implicit optimization) without explaining how ICL emerges from the pre-training phase. This paper addresses both gaps by developing a generalization theory for ICL under the auto-regressive next-token prediction (AR-NTP) paradigm that closely mirrors actual LLM training.

## Input / Output

The framework takes auto-regressive ICL prompts (successive tokens each generated conditionally on all preceding tokens under a topic) and pre-trained LLM parameters as input, producing next-token predictions and PAC-Bayesian generalization bounds. The population loss is structured as a two-level expectation—inner expectation over sequences (decomposed into token-level and prefix-sequence-level components to handle token-dependency) and outer expectation over topics—expressed as an average KL divergence between the true data distribution and the model's conditional prediction. The resulting bounds depend on model size, number of pre-training topics K, sequences per topic N, sequence length T, prompt length T_p, and total optimization iterations T'.

## Method

The paper formalizes a systematic pre-training and ICL framework under AR-NTP with a layer-wise structure of sequences and topics, then derives data-dependent, topic-dependent, and optimization-dependent PAC-Bayesian generalization bounds for the population loss. To handle prompt token-dependency (absent in i.i.d. settings), the first-level expectation over sequences is split into expectation over each token given its prefix and expectation over prefix sequences, connected via KL divergence. Data-dependent and topic-dependent priors are constructed by splitting training sequences or topics into subsets for prior and posterior estimation, and SDE analysis of the SGD trajectory details the KL divergence term to yield optimization-dependent bounds. The key result shows ICL emerges when both sequence-level and topic-level generalization are strong, with the population loss bounded at O{C(1/N_param, T')(1/√(KT_p) + 1/√(KNT))}, verified on linear dynamic systems, synthetic GINC, and real-world language datasets.

## Results

The paper's primary contribution is theoretical: PAC-Bayesian generalization bounds showing that the population loss scales as O{C(1/N_param, T′)(1/√(KT_p) + 1/√(KNT))}, where K is the number of pre-training topics, N is sequences per topic, T is sequence length, and T_p is prompt length. Empirically, on GINC and real-world datasets (20 tasks including SST-2, AG News, SWAG, etc.), ICL accuracy monotonically improves with increasing K, N, T, and T_p, confirming the theoretical predictions. A notable practical finding is that prior model initialization (training GPT2-small on a subset of topics, then transferring weights to GPT2-large) reduces training time from ~7 hours to ~4 hours for 30,000 steps on 4×A100 GPUs while stabilizing early-stage optimization. An interesting negative result shows that when pre-training data contains random transitions (data distribution mismatched with topic), ICL completely fails despite the model observing all token transitions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that does not benchmark its method against competing ICL approaches; instead, it validates its generalization bounds through controlled ablation experiments varying pre-training hyperparameters. The key empirical 'comparisons' are: (1) random initialization vs. prior model initialization, where the latter achieves comparable or improved ICL accuracy while reducing wall-clock training time by ~43% (7h → 4h on 4×A100); (2) different pre-training scales (K, N, T), where larger values consistently yield lower ICL loss, with the sharpest gains observed when K is small (e.g., K=2^13–2^14 in the linear dynamic system experiments); and (3) a data-distribution mismatch case where ICL accuracy drops to chance level, confirming that topic-data alignment is necessary for ICL emergence. The theoretical bound is noted to be tighter than Li et al. (2023) in the first term (O(1/√(KT_p)) vs. O(1/√T)) while maintaining a compatible second term.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the PAC-Bayesian generalization analysis framework (McAllester, 1998) and data-dependent prior construction (Li et al., 2019) to the auto-regressive next-token prediction setting, departing from prior ICL theory that assumed i.i.d. input-label pairs in supervised function learning. It also builds on SDE-based optimization trajectory analysis (Wang et al., 2022) to make the KL divergence term optimization-dependent, and draws conceptual inspiration from meta-learning's unknown task distribution while explicitly distinguishing its AR-NTP token-dependency from standard meta-learning. The two-level expectation over sequences and topics, combined with the ghost-sequence construction to handle token-dependency, represents the key methodological departure from both the optimization-perspective ICL literature (Akyürek et al., 2022; Von Oswald et al., 2023) and the Bayesian ICL literature (Xie et al., 2021; Wies et al., 2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first PAC-Bayesian generalization bounds that explicitly connect pre-training data characteristics (number of topics K, sequences N, sequence length T) and optimization dynamics to ICL emergence under the realistic AR-NTP paradigm, offering a principled explanation for why larger models and more diverse pre-training data improve in-context learning. The practical implication that data-dependent prior model initialization (training a small model on a topic subset to initialize a larger model) reduces training time by ~40% while stabilizing optimization is a concrete, actionable takeaway. However, the bounds are loose in practice, the experiments use small-scale GPT-2 models on synthetic or limited real-world data, and the framework's assumptions (bounded loss, bounded gradient) may not hold for modern LLMs at scale, leaving open whether these theoretical insights transfer to frontier models.
