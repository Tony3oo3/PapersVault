## Problem

Speech representation learning and speech generation have been developed as separate foundation models with arguably opposing requirements: discriminative representations benefit from invariance to speaker and environment, while generative models must preserve those details. No unified pre-training framework existed for speech that could jointly learn a representation encoder and a generative decoder, forcing practitioners to maintain distinct models for recognition versus synthesis.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252309/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes pre-quantized EnCodec latent features (50 Hz, 128-dim vectors normalized to zero mean/unit variance) extracted from 16 kHz speech, rather than raw waveforms or mel spectrograms directly. For text-to-speech fine-tuning, it additionally takes time-aligned phone labels with position postfixes and an audio prompt (encoder representations with 70–100% masked). Outputs include continuous representation embeddings for discriminative tasks, generated EnCodec latents (projected back to waveform via the EnCodec decoder) for synthesis, and discrete k-means tokens for speech tokenization at configurable bitrates.

## Method

UniWav is a 24-layer Transformer encoder and 12-layer Transformer decoder trained jointly from scratch. The encoder uses DinoSR-style self-distillation with online clustering: an EMA teacher model processes unmasked audio to produce pseudo-labels via codebook assignment across the top K=10 layers, while the student encoder predicts these labels at masked positions (8% frame masking, 10-frame spans). The decoder is a Flow Matching (CFM) model that predicts the time-dependent vector field to denoise EnCodec latents, conditioned on a learnable weighted sum of all encoder layer representations and the ODE timestep. The combined objective L_encoder + 0.25·L_decoder is optimized end-to-end, with the generative loss forcing the encoder to retain acoustic detail (speaker timbre, environment) that pure self-supervised methods discard. For speech tokenization, a discrete bottleneck is inserted by k-means quantizing a selected semantic layer plus an optional residual quantization of remaining layers, enabling low-bitrate encoding with high-quality Flow Matching resynthesis.

## Results

On speech recognition (LibriSpeech test-other WER), UniWav achieves 4.8% with 100h fine-tuning and 4.0% with 960h, trailing the best self-supervised baselines (data2vec2.0 at 3.5%) by ~0.5%. On in-context text-to-speech, UniWav achieves 2.5 ASR-WER and 0.635 speaker similarity, matching or exceeding generative pre-training baselines (SpeechFlow: 2.4/0.629; Voicebox: 2.3/0.616). For speech tokenization at 500 bps, UniWav leads all methods with 7.1 ASR-WER and 3.59 UTMOS, and at 1 kbps achieves 5.6 ASR-WER and 3.72 UTMOS, substantially outperforming SpeechTokenizer (9.1/2.08).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On speech recognition, UniWav (4.0 WER at 960h) trails the dedicated self-supervised models: data2vec2.0 (3.5), HuBERT (3.7), and data2vec (3.7), a gap of roughly 0.3–0.5 WER that the authors attribute to the generative objective pulling the representation away from pure discriminative invariance. On text-to-speech, UniWav (2.5 ASR-WER, 0.635 Sim) outperforms VALL-E (3.8, 0.508) by a wide margin and edges out SpeechFlow (2.4, 0.629) on speaker similarity while being marginally worse on ASR-WER; it also surpasses Voicebox (2.3, 0.616) on Sim despite using only 960h of fine-tuning data versus Voicebox's 60k. For speech tokenization, UniWav dominates at both 500 bps (7.1 ASR-WER, 3.59 UTMOS vs. SpeechTokenizer's 11.0/1.26 and EnCodec's 41.0/1.24) and 1 kbps (5.6 ASR-WER, 3.72 UTMOS vs. SpeechTokenizer's 9.1/2.08), with the 1 kbps UniWav matching 4 kbps SpeechTokenizer on intelligibility and naturalness. The ablation without pre-training (17.5 WER, 0.532 Sim) confirms that the joint pre-training is essential for both discriminative and generative performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

UniWav unifies two previously separate lines of speech pre-training: self-supervised representation learning (HuBERT, WavLM, data2vec, DinoSR) and generative pre-training (SpeechFlow, Voicebox). The encoder adopts DinoSR's self-distillation with online clustering, while the decoder is a Flow Matching model conditioned on encoder representations, building directly on SpeechFlow's architecture. The key departure is joint end-to-end training of both components from scratch, rather than the standard pipeline of training a representation model and then a separate generative model on top of frozen or separately-trained features. Analogous unified frameworks exist in vision (MAGE, DDAE) and text (BERT/GPT), but this is the first such framework for speech.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is reducing the need to maintain and train separate foundation models for discriminative and generative speech tasks, with the speech tokenization results (1kbps UniWav matching 4kbps SpeechTokenizer in intelligibility and naturalness) providing strong evidence that joint learning yields representations more suitable for downstream use. The mutual information analysis reveals a useful trade-off: the generative objective preserves speaker/acoustic information at a slight cost to phone-level semantic information, explaining the ~0.5% WER gap on recognition. Open questions include whether this gap can be closed with scale or architectural changes, whether the framework extends beyond English audiobooks to multilingual and non-speech audio, and whether representation disentanglement can further improve controllability.
