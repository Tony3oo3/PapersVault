## Problem

No prior benchmark has posed a whole-brain neural activity forecasting challenge at single-cell resolution for a vertebrate. Existing efforts (Sensorium, BrainScore, Neural Latents) analyzed only a small fraction of the brain they studied, leaving open the fundamental question of how accurately one can predict the next ~30 seconds of activity across all neurons in an entire vertebrate brain given past observations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251483/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Models receive either 1D activity traces (a 7879×71721 time×neuron matrix) or 4D volumetric video frames (2048×1152×72 voxels per timestep at 406nm×406nm×4μm spatial resolution, 914ms temporal resolution) as context, with optional stimulus covariates (26-dim encoded vector) and neuron spatial locations. The prediction target is H=32 steps (~30s) of future activity, evaluated per-condition and per-step-ahead using MAE. Context windows are C=4 (short) or C=256 (long) steps, and one of nine behavioral conditions (TAXIS) is entirely held out from training to test generalization.

## Method

The benchmark evaluates three model families: (1) time-series models including a global univariate Linear model, TiDE (MLP with stimulus and spatial covariates), TSMixer (multivariate MLP with alternating time-mixing and feature-mixing blocks), and Time-Mix (univariate TSMixer variant); (2) a volumetric video U-Net with 3D convolutions, 4× area-averaging downsampling in XY, FiLM conditioning on lead time h∈{1,…,32}, and 18M parameters; and (3) two parameter-free baselines (temporal mean and stimulus-phase lookup). All models are trained with AdamW and early stopping on a 70/10/20 per-condition split. Key empirical findings include: the U-Net video model achieves the best short-context performance, long context improves further-ahead predictions, time-series models appear to underutilize cross-neuron information, and prediction errors cluster spatially (e.g., higher MAE in the pallium) rather than being uniformly distributed.

## Results

The benchmark evaluates models using Mean Absolute Error (MAE) on predicting 32 steps (~0.5 min) of future whole-brain activity across 71,721 neurons, in both short (C=4) and long (C=256) context settings. The U-Net volumetric video model achieves the best overall performance in the short context setting, particularly for single-step-ahead predictions, while in long context the performance of TiDE, TSMixer, Time-Mix, and U-Net is very similar with Linear performing worse. All learned models generally outperform the two naive baselines (Mean and Stimulus), though the Stimulus baseline remains competitive in short context, with only 53 out of 120 model runs outperforming it at 32 steps ahead. The held-out TAXIS condition reveals that models relying on stimulus covariates (e.g., TiDE) degrade significantly when the stimulus is out-of-distribution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the naive Mean baseline (parameter-free averaging of past activity), all learned models achieve lower MAE across both context lengths, confirming that the task is learnable beyond trivial temporal averaging. Against the Stimulus baseline (lookup of average stimulus-evoked response), the comparison is more nuanced: in short context, the Stimulus baseline is particularly strong on periodic conditions (FLASH, TURNING, ROTATION), and less than half of model runs (53/120) beat it at the 32-step horizon, suggesting models underutilize available stimulus covariates. In long context, learned models more consistently outperform the Stimulus baseline. Among the learned time-series models, the Linear model (inspired by Zeng et al., 2023) performs worst in long context, while TiDE (Das et al., 2023) and TSMixer (Chen et al., 2023) achieve comparable performance to the simpler Time-Mix variant, indicating that the multivariate feature-mixing in TSMixer does not clearly help over univariate global models. The U-Net video model (Ronneberger et al., 2015) outperforms all time-series models in short context, demonstrating that spatial information in the volumetric representation is exploitable and not fully captured by 1D traces.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as the neuroscience analogue of WeatherBench, applying the forecasting-benchmark paradigm to whole-brain neural activity. It builds on U-Net architectures (Ronneberger et al., 2015) adapted for volumetric video prediction and Flood Filling Networks (Januszewski et al., 2018) for neuron segmentation, while drawing on recent time-series forecasting methods (TiDE, TSMixer, DLinear) as baseline models. Crucially, it departs from prior neuroscience benchmarks (Sensorium, BrainScore, Neural Latents Benchmark) by covering the entire vertebrate brain at single-cell resolution rather than a small fraction, and uniquely pairs the activity recording with an in-progress synapse-resolution connectome reconstruction of the same animal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ZAPBench is the first whole-brain activity prediction benchmark for a vertebrate, and the first to pair a whole-brain functional recording with a whole-brain structural reconstruction, opening the door to graph-based and biophysically grounded models once the connectome is complete. Key open questions include whether cross-neuron information can be effectively exploited (current multivariate models show no clear advantage over univariate ones), what the fundamental predictability ceiling is given inherent neural stochasticity, and whether MAE alone is sufficient or whether distributional metrics (CRPS, power spectral density) are needed. Limitations include a single specimen, ~1 Hz temporal resolution (two orders of magnitude below action potential frequencies), and structured imaging noise that could not be fully removed.
