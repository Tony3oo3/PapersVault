## Problem

Token-level and prompt-level jailbreaking attacks (e.g., GCG, PAIR) generate adversarial sequences that effectively manipulate open-source LLMs but exhibit unreliable transferability to proprietary target LLMs. The paper identifies the root cause as an inherent distributional dependency: adversarial sequences overfit the source model's parameters and input-order-specific sampling process, causing their created high-importance regions to collapse under distribution shifts. This limits their utility as a reliable red-teaming tool for identifying vulnerabilities in proprietary models.

## Input / Output

The method takes a short malicious text prompt (e.g., 'how to build a bomb') and a lightweight source MLM (BERT-Large) as the optimization oracle, using the evaluation template 'This intent is [MASK]' to measure per-token perceived-importance. It produces a jailbreaking sentence of the same length as the original, where only neutral-intent tokens have been replaced by semantically similar synonyms — no adversarial suffixes or pre/post-prompts are appended. Because the attack is purely token replacement, it can be executed on both MLMs and CLMs and requires only ~22 queries and ~5 seconds of compute per instance, far less than GCG's ~495 queries and ~494 seconds.

## Method

PiF (Perceived-importance Flatten) replaces the strategy of creating a single deceptive high-importance region (as GCG/PAIR do via lengthy adversarial sequences) with uniformly dispersing the model's focus across multiple neutral-intent tokens in the original input. The three-stage iterative procedure is: (I) probabilistically sample a low-importance token to replace based on inverse perceived-importance; (II) select a semantically similar replacement token that maximizes the L2 change in the top-K predicted logits under the intent-perception template, subject to affix/punctuation type constraints; (III) accept the replacement only if sentence-level semantic similarity exceeds a threshold Θ. Crucially, the optimization objective is dynamic — maximizing the change in the model's intent perception rather than driving toward a predefined harmful output — which prevents overfitting to source-model parameters. This design eliminates the order-specific token interplay that causes distributional dependency, yielding a 7× reduction in perceived-importance variation between source and target models compared to GCG.

## Results

On AdvBench, PiF achieves ASR of 83.8% on Llama-2-13B-Chat, 98.5% on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, 99.2% on both Vicuna-13B-V1.5 and Mistral-7B-Instruct, 97.7% on GPT-4-0613, and 93.1% on GPT-O1-Preview, consistently outperforming GCG and PAIR. On MaliciousInstruct, PiF reaches 82.0% ASR on Llama-2-13B-Chat and 99.0% on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. Against GPT-4 specifically, PiF attains 97.7% ASR and 63.6% ASR+GPT, the highest among all compared methods. In terms of efficiency, PiF requires only 21.9 average queries (4.6 s with BERT-Large) versus 495.4 queries (494.3 s) for GCG.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PiF outperforms all eight compared baselines on every target model and dataset. Against GCG (Zou et al., 2023), PiF achieves dramatically higher ASR on Llama-family models (83.8% vs. 1.4% on Llama-2-13B-Chat) and GPT-4 (97.7% vs. 27.2%), confirming the severe distributional dependency of GCG's adversarial suffixes. Against PAIR (Chao et al., 2023), PiF leads by 27.6 percentage points on Llama-2-13B-Chat (83.8% vs. 56.2%) and 12.1 points on GPT-4 (97.7% vs. 85.6%). On GPT-4 (AdvBench), PiF also surpasses APAs (97.7% vs. 92.0% ASR; 63.6% vs. 60.0% ASR+GPT), RIPPLE (97.7% vs. 86.0%), LLM-FUZZER (97.7% vs. 60.0%), AutoDAN (97.7% vs. 26.4%), ReNeLLM (97.7% vs. 71.6%), and AmpleGCG (63.6% vs. 12.0% ASR+GPT). The only near-competitive comparison is APAs on GPT-4, where the gap narrows to 5.7 points in ASR and 3.6 points in ASR+GPT, but PiF still leads on both metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself within the token-level and prompt-level jailbreaking literature, using GCG (Zou et al., 2023) and PAIR (Chao et al., 2023) as primary baselines, but departs from them conceptually by reframing attack effectiveness through a 'perceived-importance' lens on model intent recognition rather than through output-level success criteria. Rather than generating adversarial sequences (suffixes or prompts), PiF repurposes the simpler mechanism of synonym token replacement, drawing on the observation that existing attacks overfit to source-model sampling dynamics. The analytical contribution—identifying distributional dependency as the root cause of poor transferability—extends the adversarial-example transferability literature into the LLM jailbreaking setting, where prior work had largely treated transfer failure as an empirical observation without a unified mechanistic explanation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, highly efficient red-teaming tool (21–40 queries, ~5 seconds with BERT-Large) that achieves near-saturated ASR on GPT-4 and GPT-O1, making vulnerability assessment of proprietary LLMs substantially more accessible. The intent-perception framework offers a diagnostic that could inform future defence design (e.g., making intent recognition robust to uniform importance flattening). Open questions remain: the authors acknowledge the absence of a theoretical account of transfer mechanisms, note that jailbroken models still append safety disclaimers (limiting real-world harm), and flag the unexplored role of label noise in training corpora. Extending the intent-perception analysis to multimodal and diffusion models is identified as a natural next step.
