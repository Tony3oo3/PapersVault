## Problem

Determining the optimal data mixture for LLM pre-training remains largely manual or relies on proxy models trained for hundreds of thousands of steps (e.g., DoReMi), making it expensive and slow as training corpora grow. The paper asks whether a small number of ultra-short proxy model runs can suffice to predict the best mixture for a much larger model, and whether the relative ranking of mixtures is invariant across model scales and token budgets.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the token distribution across 17 Pile domains as the base for Dirichlet sampling (scaled by λ ∈ [0.1, 5.0] to generate diverse mixtures), and the validation loss on Pile-CC from 512 proxy models (1M params, 1B tokens each) as regression labels. It outputs a single set of 17 domain weights (averaged over the top-100 predicted mixtures) that is directly used to allocate tokens for large-scale training. The regression model itself takes an arbitrary 17-dimensional weight vector as input and returns a scalar predicted loss, enabling sub-10-second evaluation of a million candidate mixtures on CPU.

## Method

REGMIX formulates data mixture selection as a supervised regression problem: it trains 512 small proxy models (1M parameters, 1B tokens) on mixtures sampled from Dirichlet distributions parameterized by the token distribution, then fits a LightGBM (or ridge) regression mapping mixture weights to Pile-CC validation loss. The key assumption is rank invariance—that the ordering of mixtures by performance is preserved when scaling from 1M/1B to 1B/25B—validated by a 97.12% Spearman correlation between predicted and actual ranks on unseen 1B-parameter models. To select the final mixture, the trained regressor scores over one million simulated mixtures, and the top-100 are averaged for robustness before training the target model. The choice of Pile-CC loss as the target is motivated by empirical finding that web-corpus validation loss correlates most strongly with downstream task performance, more so than Wikipedia or other 'high-quality' domains.

## Results

REGMIX achieves the highest average score of 47.3 across 14 downstream benchmarks (1B-parameter models trained on 25B tokens), outperforming Human selection (45.1), DoReMi (46.8), PPL (46.2), ODM (45.0), and Pile-CC Only (46.8). REGMIX wins 7 out of 14 individual tasks, with the largest gain on HellaSwag (44.2 vs. 37.4 for Human, a 6.8-point improvement) and QQP (58.3 vs. 52.4). The regression model (LightGBM) achieves 97.12% Spearman rank correlation when predicting the performance ranking of 1B-parameter models from 1M-parameter proxy models, validating the rank invariance hypothesis. REGMIX requires only 3.5e18 FLOPs for mixture selection, roughly 10% of DoReMi's 3.7e19 FLOPs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Human (Pile) baseline, REGMIX improves the average score by 2.2 points (47.3 vs. 45.1) and wins 7 of 14 tasks, with the largest gains on HellaSwag (+6.8), QQP (+5.9), and Lambada (+4.4). Against DoReMi, REGMIX achieves a higher average (47.3 vs. 46.8) and wins 7 tasks while DoReMi wins 0, using roughly 10% of DoReMi's computational budget (3.5e18 vs. 3.7e19 FLOPs). Against PPL (perplexity-based filtering), REGMIX wins 7 tasks to PPL's 1, with a 1.1-point average advantage; PPL's only win is on Lambada (35.4 vs. 34.2). Against ODM (online data mixing), REGMIX dominates with a 2.3-point average advantage (47.3 vs. 45.0) and wins 7 tasks to ODM's 0. Notably, the Pile-CC Only baseline (training exclusively on web data) achieves 46.8 average and wins 5 tasks, reinforcing the paper's finding that web corpora are the strongest predictor of downstream performance, though REGMIX still edges it out by 0.5 points on average.

## Relation to Prior Work

REGMIX departs from the dominant paradigm in group-level data mixture optimization (DoReMi, ODM) where a single proxy model is trained for hundreds of thousands of steps to iteratively adjust domain weights. Instead, it repurposes standard regression techniques (LightGBM, ridge regression) to predict mixture performance from a set of many ultra-small proxy models (1M parameters, 1B tokens) trained in parallel on diverse Dirichlet-sampled mixtures. The key conceptual departure is the 'rank invariance' hypothesis—that relative mixture rankings are preserved across three orders of magnitude in model size—which enables the small-to-large transfer that prior proxy-model methods (requiring 100B+ tokens) could not achieve at such low cost.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that web corpora (Pile-CC) rather than Wikipedia exhibit the strongest correlation with downstream performance challenges a long-standing assumption in the field and has direct practical implications for data curation. The method's 10× compute reduction over DoReMi while matching or exceeding its performance makes data mixture optimization accessible to academic labs. Key open questions include whether rank invariance holds at 7B/70B scale (only verified up to 1B), how to handle limited data availability per domain, and whether the approach generalizes when domain boundaries are unclear or tokenizers differ between proxy and target models.
