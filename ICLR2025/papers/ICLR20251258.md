## Problem

While compositionally structured (disentangled) representations are widely assumed to support compositional generalization, the conditions under which they are actually sufficient remain unclear. This paper asks: what fundamental computational restrictions do compositionally structured representations impose on kernel models, and how do training data statistics and representational geometry determine whether generalization succeeds or fails?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251258/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Inputs are sets of C categorical components z=(z_1,...,z_C), represented as multi-hot vectors or as concatenated MNIST/CIFAR-10 digit images (each component drawn from a specific image category). The kernel K(x,x') depends only on the number of components shared between two inputs. Outputs are task-specific: a scalar regression value (sum of hidden component values for symbolic addition), a binary equivalence label (transitive equivalence), or a category label (context dependence). Generalization is evaluated on component combinations absent from the training set Z^train.

## Method

The paper proves that any kernel model with a compositionally structured representation is constrained to 'conjunction-wise additivity': its prediction is a sum of values assigned to each conjunction of components seen during training (Theorem 4.2), fundamentally limiting which compositional tasks it can solve (e.g., transitive equivalence is unsolvable). To characterize when even solvable tasks succeed, the authors introduce 'representational salience' S(k;C), a normalized metric quantifying how strongly each conjunction order is encoded in the kernel, and show it is determined by network depth and nonlinearity. They then identify two failure modes arising from ℓ₂-norm minimization: a 'memorization leak' (partial reliance on the full conjunction distorts generalization proportionally) and a 'shortcut bias' (exploiting spurious context-target correlations). The theory is validated in ConvNets, ResNets, and Vision Transformers trained on MNIST/CIFAR-10 compositional tasks, confirming that deep networks implement conjunction-wise additive computations and exhibit the predicted failure modes.

## Results

The paper's central theoretical result (Theorem 4.2) proves that compositionally structured kernel models are restricted to conjunction-wise additive computations, fundamentally preventing them from generalizing on tasks like transitive equivalence. On symbolic addition, the model's compositional generalization is compressed by a proportional factor m = p·S(1;2)/(1+(p−2)S(1;2)), where p is the training set size and S(1;2) is the representational salience of individual components; m < 1 whenever S(2;2) > 0 (i.e., any conjunctive representation exists). Empirically, ConvNets (MNIST), ResNets, and ViTs (CIFAR-10) confirm the memorization leak: predictions are linearly compressed relative to ground truth, with the slope increasing with inter-component distance and training set size. On context dependence, deep networks achieve better-than-chance accuracy on CD-1 and CD-2 but worse-than-chance accuracy on CD-3, matching the kernel theory's prediction of a context-driven shortcut bias.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that does not compare against specific baseline methods; instead, it validates its kernel-theory predictions against three deep network architectures (ConvNets, ResNets, ViTs) trained on compositional tasks. The kernel theory's predictions are confirmed qualitatively and semi-quantitatively: the proportional compression on symbolic addition, the sensitivity of context dependence to representational geometry (depth and nonlinearity), and the inability to generalize on transitive equivalence all match between the kernel models and the deep networks. The one notable exception is that for p=4 on symbolic addition, the extrapolation dataset showed a smaller slope than the interpolation dataset in deep networks, a deviation the theory does not capture. The paper positions its framework as complementary to prior kernel-regime analyses (Jacot et al. 2018; Canatar et al. 2021; Lippl et al. 2024; Abbe et al. 2023), extending them from specific tasks or infinite-component limits to a general finite-component compositional setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the kernel regime framework (Jacot et al., 2018) and the norm-minimization analysis of generalization (Canatar et al., 2021) to the compositional task setting, going beyond prior work that analyzed either a single compositional task (Lippl et al., 2024, transitive ordering) or a specific input structure in an asymptotic limit (Abbe et al., 2023, binary components with infinite cardinality). The key conceptual departure is the introduction of 'conjunction-wise additivity' as a fundamental computational constraint on any kernel model with compositionally structured representations, providing a general partition of the compositional task space into solvable and unsolvable tasks. The paper also positions itself against the broader compositional generalization and disentangled representations literatures (Lake & Baroni, Battaglia et al., Locatello et al.), arguing that its theory explains why those studies have produced inconsistent conclusions about the utility of disentangled representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that compositionally structured (disentangled) representations are necessary but not sufficient for compositional generalization, and that kernel models are fundamentally restricted to conjunction-wise additive computations—provides a principled explanation for the long-standing inconsistency in the literature on whether disentangled representations help. The identification of two concrete failure modes (memorization leak and shortcut bias) gives practitioners diagnostic tools for understanding and potentially remediating compositional generalization failures. Key limitations include restriction to the kernel regime (no quantitative bounds for feature-learning regime), no analysis of large language models, and the fact that the theory characterizes what models can do in principle rather than providing algorithmic fixes. Open questions include whether the conjunction-wise additivity constraint persists in the feature-learning regime and whether new learning mechanisms can overcome these fundamental restrictions.
