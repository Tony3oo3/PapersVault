## Problem

While Chinchilla scaling laws quantify how LLM loss decreases with compute, the reason larger models generalize better — specifically how the tokenwise generalization gap scales with model size in the compute-optimal regime — has not been addressed. Existing generalization bounds for LLMs either require extreme compression that cripples performance or rely on Azuma's inequality, which yields a √C scaling that is too coarse to capture the small, predictable improvements in generalization as models grow.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a trained LLM checkpoint (Pythia family, 70M–12B parameters), the full training token sequence from The Pile, and a fixed quantization bitrate (4 bits/parameter via GPTQ). It produces a fully empirical tokenwise generalization bound decomposed into four interpretable terms: a complexity-weighted random-guess NLL (C·log V), a loss-variation term (Σ√C) computed by resampling each token under the model's own distribution, a smoothing cost (√2C), and a quantization gap (R̂_q − R̂_h). All terms are evaluated directly on the training data with no population-level assumptions, and the bound holds simultaneously for all hypotheses in a countable class via a prior-weighted union bound.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a novel fully empirical Freedman-type martingale concentration inequality (Theorem 3.1) that replaces the population variance with an empirical proxy Σ, improving the leading term from √C (as in Azuma's inequality) to linear C. This is combined with a prefix-free coding union bound over a discrete hypothesis class and an analytically optimized prediction smoothing step (mixing the model with a uniform distribution) to handle the unbounded NLL, yielding the final bound in Theorem 3.4. In the compute-optimal regime where N/D ≈ 1/20 is constant, the bound tightens with scale because both the loss variation Σ (scaling as ~1/√N) and the quantization gap decrease as models grow. As an alternative to quantization-based complexity, the paper derives an information-theoretic upper bound via prequential coding showing that the information content K(h) in the model grows sublinearly as O(D^(1−β)) with dataset size, implying the complexity term C itself decreases with scale and the generalization gap shrinks as O(D^(−β/2)).

## Results

On Pythia models (70M–12B parameters) evaluated on the Pile dataset with 4-bit GPTQ quantization, the loss variation Σ decreases with model scale as 0.27 + 8337·N^(−0.54), approximately 1/√N with a constant offset. The prequential information content K(h) scales sublinearly as 6×10⁵·N^(0.5±0.1), predicting a crossover with the linear bN parameter-counting bound at approximately 20B parameters. The evaluated generalization bound R_sq remains non-vacuous (below log V ≈ 11 nats/token) across all Pythia checkpoints and tracks the empirical loss curve, with the C·log V term (≈1.2 nats/token) and smoothing term (≈0.47 nats/token) dominating while the loss variation and quantization gap terms (each ≈0.1 nats/token) decrease with scale.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without direct method-vs-method benchmark comparisons; the 'compares_to' set is empty. The primary comparison is against the prior token-level generalization bound of Lotfi et al. (2024b), which uses Azuma's inequality and yields a leading term scaling as Δ·√C·log V. The present work's empirical Freedman inequality improves this to Δ·C·log V, a strict tightening that becomes more pronounced as the loss variance Σ shrinks with model size. Against the quantization-based complexity (bN log 2 / D), the prequential coding approach yields a worse bound at Pythia scale but follows a power law O(D^(−β)) that eventually overtakes the linear parameter-counting bound near 20B parameters. The paper also contrasts with Maurer & Pontil (2009)'s fully empirical Bernstein bound, noting that their proof technique does not extend to the non-IID martingale setting required for autoregressive language modeling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the token-level generalization bound framework of Lotfi et al. (2024b), which used Azuma's inequality to bound the tokenwise generalization gap, by introducing a fully empirical Freedman-type martingale concentration inequality that improves the leading complexity term from O(√C) to O(C). The prediction smoothing technique is adapted from Lotfi et al. (2024a) but with an analytically optimized mixing parameter, and the empirical variance proxy draws conceptual inspiration from Maurer & Pontil's (2009) empirical Bernstein bound, extended here to the non-IID martingale setting. The quantizability analysis in Appendix B leverages the incoherence and LDLQ framework from QuIP/QuIP# (Chee et al., 2024; Tseng et al., 2024), while the information-theoretic argument builds on prequential coding (Rissanen, 1984) and Kolmogorov complexity estimates (Zhang et al., 2020). Crucially, the paper is the first to situate these bounds within the Chinchilla compute-optimal regime, connecting generalization theory to the scaling laws that govern practical LLM training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first provable explanation for why the tokenwise generalization gap shrinks as LLMs are scaled in the compute-optimal regime, decomposing the mechanism into three empirically evaluable terms (parameters-per-token ratio, loss variance, quantization error) that each have a clear scaling behavior. The sublinear information growth result (K(h) = O(D^{1-β})) via prequential coding is a conceptually important finding: it suggests the model's effective complexity decreases with scale even as parameter count grows, providing an information-theoretic rationale for why overfitting does not dominate. However, the bounds remain loose (dominated by the C·log V random-guess term), the √(2C) smoothing cost is acknowledged as pessimistic, and the analysis is restricted to token-wise rather than sequence-level generalization. Open questions include why the Hessian spectrum decays as observed, whether the 1/√N loss variation scaling can be derived theoretically, and how token-wise bounds relate to full-sequence generalization.
