## Problem

While LLM watermarking research has focused on detectability, text quality, and robustness to editing, no prior work has investigated whether end users can identify the presence of a watermark in an LLM service through crafted prompts alone. This imperceptibility gap is critical because providers may not disclose watermarks, and a detectable watermark reduces user trust and increases vulnerability to targeted attacks.

## Input / Output

The method requires only black-box API access to the target LLM and produces a binary watermark/no-watermark decision via a z-test. Concretely, it constructs pairs of semantically correlated prompts (e.g., 'Name a country with a large population' vs. 'Name a country with a large area'), prepends fixed or quasi-random prefixes to simulate specific watermark keys, and generates 10,000–100,000 samples per prompt-key combination. The final output is a z-score (4–10 = moderate confidence, >10 = high confidence) derived from the average cosine similarity of rank-transformed distribution differences across all prompt and key pairs.

## Method

Water-Probe exploits the observation that any watermark rule F introduces a key-dependent bias: for a fixed key k, the watermarked distribution P_M^F(·|x,k) consistently deviates from P_M(·|x), and this deviation is similar across prompts with similar output distributions (formalized via a Lipschitz continuity assumption and proven in Theorem 1). The algorithm constructs N correlated prompts, samples W times under m simulated watermark keys (fixed prefixes for n-gram watermarks; quasi-random token prefixes that correlate with starting key positions for fixed-key-list watermarks), applies a rank transformation to stabilize across temperatures, and computes the average cosine similarity Δ_R across all prompt-pair and key-pair combinations. A z-test against the null (no-watermark) distribution yields the detection decision. To counter this detection, the paper proposes Water-Bag, which merges n master keys with their inversions into a key-bag, randomly selecting one per generation so that the average effect of a key and its inversion cancels out, making the per-key bias undetectable while preserving text-level detectability via max-score aggregation.

## Results

Water-Probe-v2 achieves high-confidence watermark identification (z-score > 10) across all 8 tested watermarking algorithms on 9 LLMs, with average detection similarities ranging from 0.25 (KGW-Skip) to 0.84 (ITS-Edit), while non-watermarked LLMs show near-zero similarity (avg 0.026). Water-Probe-v1 is effective for N-gram watermarks (avg similarity 0.31–0.85) but fails for Fixed-Key-List watermarks (avg 0.03). The Aar watermark is the most easily detected (avg similarity 0.81–0.85), while KGW-Skip is the hardest (avg 0.25–0.41). For the Water-Bag imperceptibility strategy, increasing the bag size to |K∪K̄|=8 reduces Water-Probe-v1 z-scores to negative values (−4.34) while maintaining 100% detection F1-score.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to Gloaguen et al. (2024), Water-Probe-v2 is the first unified method that detects all watermarking-during-generation algorithms without requiring algorithm-specific detection procedures. In the critical multinomial-sampling setting for EXP-Edit (Table 10), Gloaguen's method completely fails (p-value = 1.0 at all temperatures ≥ 0.3), while Water-Probe-v2 maintains p < 0.001 across all temperatures. Under argmax sampling (Table 9), both methods succeed, but Water-Probe-v2 requires only 10,000 short (5-token) samples versus Gloaguen's 1,000 long (200-token) samples. For the Water-Bag imperceptibility strategy (Table 2), Water-Bag with |K∪K̄|=4–8 renders Water-Probe-v1 ineffective (z-scores −4.34 to −7.84) while preserving 100% text-detection F1, outperforming EXP-Edit's longer key lists which also reduce identifiability but increase detection time from 37.9s to 194.2s and degrade robustness (F1 drops to 0.745 at key-length 2048).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the LLM watermarking literature (KGW, Aar, DiPmark, EXP-Edit) but pivots from the standard objectives of detectability, robustness, and text quality to a previously unaddressed concern: whether end users can identify that an LLM service is watermarked. The closest prior work is Gloaguen et al. (2024) on black-box watermark detection, but the authors depart by proposing a unified method effective across all watermark-during-generation algorithms (rather than per-method tests) and by providing a theoretical foundation (Lipschitz continuity of the watermark rule) explaining why detection is possible. The Water-Bag strategy further extends n-gram watermarking with a key-inversion mechanism to enhance imperceptibility, a direction not explored in prior watermarking work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all mainstream watermarking algorithms—including those claiming distortion-free properties—are readily identifiable through crafted prompts has direct practical implications for LLM service providers who may wish to deploy watermarks covertly. The Water-Bag defense introduces a concrete trade-off between watermark robustness and service-level imperceptibility, but the paper acknowledges that increasing key randomness degrades robustness, leaving open the question of whether both properties can be simultaneously optimized. A key limitation is the high sample requirement (10^4+ queries for Water-Probe-v2), which constrains real-world applicability and raises questions about whether providers could rate-limit or detect such probing behavior.
