## Problem

Prior uncertainty-aware interactive imitation learning methods (e.g., EnsembleDAgger, MC-dropout) rely on training-time data as a proxy for deployment-time uncertainty and do not adapt their uncertainty estimates online to the expert feedback actually received during deployment. This leads to overconfident or miscalibrated uncertainty when the expert's policy shifts (e.g., changing wiping strategy), causing the robot to miss critical opportunities to request additional expert labels. The paper asks: how can a robot learner calibrate its uncertainty online from the intermittent expert labels it receives, while maintaining formal coverage guarantees?

## Input / Output

The base policy (a CNN-based Diffusion Policy in hardware, a small MLP in simulation) consumes robot proprioceptive state and wrist/third-person camera images, producing a sequence of 16 future end-effector positions and quaternion orientations. IQT operates on the signed residual between the predicted action and the intermittently-observed expert action, maintaining separate upper and lower quantile thresholds (q^lo, q^hi) that define an asymmetric prediction interval [a^r − q^lo, a^r + q^hi]. The interval's L2 norm drives a binary (hardware) or sigmoid-gated (simulation) query decision: the robot asks the expert for a label only when the interval exceeds a threshold τ, while the human may independently intervene with a small fixed probability c.

## Method

The core technical contribution is Intermittent Quantile Tracking (IQT), which extends online conformal quantile tracking to settings where ground-truth labels are observed only with probability p_t at each timestep. The update rule scales the standard quantile-tracking step by 1/p_t and multiplies by the observation indicator obs_t, yielding q_{t+1} = q_t + (γ_t/p_t)(err_t − α)·obs_t; Proposition 1 proves this preserves the 1−α asymptotic coverage guarantee for arbitrary data sequences. ConformalDAgger wraps IQT around an interactive DAgger loop: the robot's policy serves as the base model, the nonconformity score is the signed action residual, and the calibrated interval size u(x_t) = ‖C_t(x_t)‖_2 gates robot-initiated expert queries via a threshold or sigmoid. The observation model p_t is decomposed into human-gated (constant c) and robot-gated (interval-size-dependent) components, so the robot's own querying behavior feeds back into the calibration. In hardware, IQT is applied only to the 3D end-effector position (not quaternion orientation) to keep the nonconformity score in Euclidean space, and the diffusion policy is fine-tuned after each deployment episode on the aggregated expert-labeled data.

## Results

In the simulated expert-shift scenario (episode 5), ConformalDAgger immediately increases intervention rate from ~20% to ~60%, while EnsembleDAgger remains near ~30% and SafeDAgger shows only a slight increase. ConformalDAgger achieves a maximum miscoverage of 0.4 at the shift point (converging to 0.2), compared to EnsembleDAgger's maximum of 1.0 (converging to 0.4). In the hardware sponging task with a 7DOF manipulator and Diffusion Policy, ConformalDAgger increases cumulative robot-requested interventions by up to ~35 timesteps when the expert switches from a straight-line to a zig-zag wiping strategy, producing a retrained policy with a more distinct zig-zag pattern than EnsembleDAgger or SafeDAgger. On stationary-expert and environment-shift conditions, ConformalDAgger performs comparably to EnsembleDAgger, with all methods maintaining ~20% intervention rates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against EnsembleDAgger (Menda et al., 2019), ConformalDAgger clearly wins on the expert-shift and expert-drift scenarios: it detects the shift faster (intervention jumps to ~60% vs. ~30%), achieves lower miscoverage (max 0.4 vs. 1.0), and produces retrained policies with smaller decision and trajectory deviations. On stationary and environment-shift conditions, the two methods perform similarly. Against SafeDAgger (Zhang & Cho, 2017), ConformalDAgger again wins on shift detection, as SafeDAgger's safety classifier does not adapt to the changed expert strategy and requests only marginally more interventions. Against LazyDAgger (Hoque et al., 2021b), ConformalDAgger asks fewer total questions (LazyDAgger has the highest intervention rate) but achieves better policy alignment because it queries consistently across all seeds and episodes, whereas LazyDAgger's context-switching mechanism leads to unreliable per-episode feedback collection, resulting in insufficient data to learn the shifted strategy. In the hardware experiment, LazyDAgger shows a similar intervention profile to ConformalDAgger but fails to ask for help consistently in every episode, yielding a less distinct zig-zag policy after retraining.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of online conformal prediction and interactive imitation learning. On the conformal side, it extends Quantile Tracking (Angelopoulos et al., 2024) and Adaptive Conformal Inference (Gibbs & Candes, 2021) from the standard setting where labels are observed at every timestep to the intermittent-label regime, introducing the Intermittent Quantile Tracking (IQT) algorithm with a 1/p_t rescaling and an observation indicator. On the IL side, it departs from prior uncertainty-aware DAgger variants (EnsembleDAgger, SafeDAgger, LazyDAgger) that treat training-time ensemble disagreement or MC dropout as a fixed proxy for deployment uncertainty; instead, ConformalDAgger uses deployment-time expert feedback to calibrate prediction intervals online, providing distribution-free coverage guarantees that prior black-box uncertainty methods lack under distribution shift.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap in deploying robot policies: when an expert's strategy changes (e.g., switching from straight-line to zigzag wiping), ensemble-based uncertainty estimates remain miscalibrated and fail to trigger additional queries, while IQT-based intervals expand and the robot actively seeks help. The asymptotic coverage guarantee is a meaningful theoretical contribution, though the finite-horizon nature of real deployments means the guarantee is only approached, not achieved, in practice. Open questions include how the observation model p_t should be specified in more complex multi-task or multi-expert settings, whether the approach scales to higher-dimensional action spaces (the hardware experiment only calibrates position, not orientation), and how sensitive the method is to the choice of step size γ_t under highly non-stationary expert behavior.
