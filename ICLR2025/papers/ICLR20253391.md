## Problem

Curvature-based graph rewiring has been proposed to alleviate oversquashing in GNNs, with SOTA results reported on standard benchmarks. However, it remains unclear whether the theoretical conditions identifying edges as oversquashing bottlenecks are actually satisfied on real-world datasets, and whether reported accuracy gains reflect genuine structural improvements or are merely hyperparameter tuning outliers.

## Input / Output

The method takes undirected graphs with node feature vectors from 11 standard GNN benchmarks (node classification: Texas, Cornell, Wisconsin, Chameleon, Squirrel, Actor, Cora, Citeseer, Pubmed; graph classification: MUTAG, PROTEINS), using only the largest connected component. Rather than reporting a single accuracy, the output is a full distribution of mean test accuracies obtained over 800 random-grid hyperparameter configurations, each evaluated across 100 train/validation splits, enabling distributional comparison between rewired and unrewired graphs.

## Method

The paper performs two complementary analyses using the SDRF rewiring algorithm with a GCN backbone. First, it experimentally verifies whether edges selected by SDRF satisfy Condition 2 of Theorem 4 (Topping et al., 2021), which is the theoretical prerequisite for an edge to be identified as an oversquashing bottleneck; it finds that on most benchmark datasets, the vast majority of selected edges do not meet this condition, even under a relaxed sufficient variant. Second, it conducts extensive hyperparameter sweeps (800 random-grid iterations over learning rate, depth, width, dropout, weight decay, SDRF temperature τ, threshold curvature C⁺, and max iterations) across six curvature measures (BFc, BFc₃, BFc_mod, JLc, AFc₃, AFc₄) and compares the resulting accuracy distributions against a no-rewiring baseline using kernel density estimates, boxenplots, and Wasserstein distance saturation analysis. The key finding is that no curvature measure consistently shifts the accuracy distribution away from the no-rewiring baseline, and that previously reported SOTA gains correspond to top-10% hyperparameter outliers rather than structural improvements.

## Results

Across 11 benchmark datasets, no curvature-based rewiring measure (BFc, BFc3, BFcmod, JLc, AFc3, AFc4) consistently shifts the distribution of mean test accuracy away from the no-rewiring baseline. In the top-10% of hyperparameter configurations, AFc3 and AFc4 show marginal gains on Cornell (54.20/53.63 vs. 53.66 for None), Wisconsin (56.37/54.60 vs. 54.92), and Actor (28.00/28.14 vs. 27.73), but with larger standard deviations that make the improvements statistically insignificant. On citation networks (Cora, Citeseer, Pubmed), BFc-based rewiring actually degrades performance substantially (e.g., BFc drops Cora from 58.83% to 28.39% in the top-10%), while AFc variants remain closer to the no-rewiring baseline. The paper concludes that previously reported SOTA accuracies from curvature-based rewiring are hyperparameter-tuning outliers rather than consistent structural gains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares six curvature-based rewiring variants (BFc, BFc3, BFcmod, JLc, AFc3, AFc4) against a no-rewiring baseline ('None') using the SDRF algorithm with GCN on 11 datasets. The no-rewiring baseline is competitive or superior on most datasets: on Texas, all rewiring methods match or slightly underperform None (59.26–59.79% vs. 59.95%), and on Chameleon, Squirrel, and MUTAG, rewiring consistently degrades accuracy by 1–5%. AFc3 and AFc4 show the most promising (but inconsistent) gains, improving over None on Wisconsin (56.37/54.60 vs. 54.92) and Actor (28.00/28.14 vs. 27.73), yet these gains fall within the standard deviation of the distributions. On citation networks, BFc-based methods suffer severe degradation (Cora drops from 58.83% to 28.39% with BFc), while AFc variants remain closer to baseline. The authors attribute previously reported SOTA results to hyperparameter-sweep outliers rather than genuine structural improvements from rewiring, noting that the edges selected by SDRF rarely satisfy the theoretical conditions (Theorem 4) required to identify true oversquashing bottlenecks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Stochastic Discrete Ricci Flow (SDRF) rewiring framework of Topping et al. (2021) and its theoretical foundation (Theorem 4 linking negative Balanced Forman curvature to oversquashing), while also incorporating the Jost-Liu curvature (Jost & Liu, 2014) and Augmented Forman Curvature (Fesser & Weber, 2023) as alternative rewiring signals. It departs from prior work by empirically verifying whether the theoretical preconditions for oversquashing are actually satisfied in standard benchmark datasets, and by reframing the evaluation of rewiring from single-point accuracy comparisons to distributional analysis over hyperparameter sweeps. The paper aligns with Tortorella & Micheli (2023), which found limited benefit of rewiring for training-free GNNs, by extending that skepticism to general supervised GNNs and providing a mechanistic explanation rooted in the curvature conditions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work challenges a widely cited narrative in the GNN literature that curvature-based rewiring yields consistent performance gains on real-world benchmarks, showing instead that the theoretical bottleneck conditions are rarely met and that reported SOTA improvements are hyperparameter outliers. It raises important methodological questions about how GNN improvements should be evaluated (distributional vs. point-estimate comparisons) and highlights a gap between synthetic-theory and real-data behavior. Open questions include whether rewiring provides genuine benefits on datasets with true severe bottlenecks, whether Theorem-aware rewiring (only modifying edges satisfying the curvature conditions) would be more effective, and how to design benchmark datasets that genuinely stress-test oversquashing.
