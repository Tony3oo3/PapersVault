## Problem

Existing theoretical scaling laws for neural networks are derived in the lazy (kernel) regime where features remain static, yet empirical studies consistently show that feature-learning networks train faster and exhibit different compute-optimal exponents than their kernel-limit counterparts. This paper asks under what conditions on task difficulty feature learning can improve the power-law scaling exponents of loss with training time and compute, and provides a solvable model that captures this improvement in the infinite-width limit.

## Input / Output

The theory is parameterized by two power-law exponents: α (capacity, governing kernel eigenvalue decay λ_k ~ k^{-α}) and β (source, governing target function difficulty via the cumulative spectrum), along with model size N, training time t, batch size B, and a dimensionless feature-learning rate γ. The output is a closed-form loss scaling exponent χ(β) = β·max{1, 2/(1+β)}, compute-optimal exponents r_C for three task-difficulty regimes, and a phase diagram in the (α, β) plane. Unusually, the model operates in the infinite-width limit while preserving feature learning: the finite-N student's features evolve as linear combinations of the infinite-width teacher's eigenfunctions, with a fixed random projection A(0) enforcing the finite-parameter bottleneck.

## Method

The paper introduces a two-layer linear student-teacher model where the readout weights w(t) follow standard SGD and the feature matrix A(t) follows projected gradient descent, with the projection (1/N)A(0)ᵀA(0) ensuring features remain within the initial N-dimensional subspace while allowing their relative weights to shift. This setup is equivalent to ordinary gradient descent on a two-layer linear network with unbalanced learning rates, where γ controls the speed of feature evolution (γ→0 recovers the lazy random-feature model of Bordelon et al. 2024a). The authors derive closed-form loss predictions via dynamical mean field theory (DMFT), tracking correlation and response functions for a collection of time-varying error vectors. The key mechanistic insight is that for hard tasks (β<1, target outside the initial NTK's RKHS), the effective dynamical kernel K(t) diverges as a power law t^{1-χ}, causing the model to learn progressively higher-frequency modes at rate k_*(t) ~ t^{(2-χ)/α}, which nearly doubles the time-scaling exponent from β to 2β/(1+β). For easy tasks (β>1) and super-easy tasks (β>2−1/α), the exponents remain unchanged from the lazy regime, and the compute-optimal allocation strategy differs only for hard tasks.

## Results

The central quantitative result is that for hard tasks (β < 1, outside the RKHS of the initial NTK), feature learning improves the time-scaling exponent from t^{-β} (lazy) to t^{-2β/(1+β)} (rich regime), nearly doubling the exponent. For example, with α=2 and β=0.5, the compute-optimal exponent r_C increases from 1/3 (lazy) to 2/5 (rich). For easy tasks (1 < β < 2−1/α) and super-easy tasks (β > 2−1/α), the compute-optimal exponents are unchanged between lazy and rich regimes. Empirically, the predicted exponents accurately capture training curves of depth-4 MLPs on Sobolev tasks and CNNs on MNIST-1M, though CIFAR-5M eventually exceeds the predicted exponent at large richness γ₀.

**Table 1: Compute-optimal scaling exponents r_C for the loss L_*(C) ~ C^{-r_C} under varying task difficulty.**

| Task Difficulty | Hard (β < 1) | Easy (1 < β < 2−1/α) | Super-Easy (β > 2−1/α) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lazy (γ = 0) | αβ / (α+1) | αβ / (αβ+1) | 1 − 1/(2α) |
| Rich (γ > 0) | **2αβ / (α(1+β)+2)** | αβ / (αβ+1) | 1 − 1/(2α) |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its feature-learning (rich) model against two lazy-regime baselines: Bordelon et al. (2024a) and Paquette et al. (2024), which analyze a structured random feature model with static features (γ=0). For hard tasks (β < 1), the rich regime strictly outperforms both baselines: the compute-optimal exponent increases from αβ/(α+1) to 2αβ/(α(1+β)+2), a relative improvement of (1−β)/(α(1+β)+2−α−1) that is positive for all β < 1. For easy tasks (1 < β < 2−1/α) and super-easy tasks (β > 2−1/α), the rich and lazy exponents are identical, confirming that feature learning provides no scaling-law benefit when the target lies within the RKHS. Empirically, the lazy-model predictions from Bordelon et al. (2024a) and Paquette et al. (2024) under-predict the observed training speed on real data (as noted by Vyas et al., 2022), while the new rich-regime exponents match MNIST-1M and Sobolev-task curves closely, though CIFAR-5M eventually surpasses even the improved prediction at large γ₀.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the solvable random-feature model of Bordelon et al. (2024a) and Paquette et al. (2024), which captured neural scaling laws only in the lazy (kernel) regime, by allowing the kernel features to evolve via projected gradient descent on a two-layer linear network. The model's γ→0 limit exactly recovers the prior lazy-regime predictions, while γ>0 introduces feature learning that modifies the scaling exponents. The algorithmic structure draws on the linear neural network dynamics of Saxe et al. (2013), and the analytical machinery uses dynamical mean field theory (DMFT) as developed in the statistical physics literature for nonlinear systems driven by random matrices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical framework that quantitatively predicts when and how feature learning changes neural scaling law exponents, showing a clean boundary: improvement occurs only for hard tasks (β<1, outside the initial NTK's RKHS) where the time exponent nearly doubles from β to 2β/(1+β), while easy tasks (β>1) are unaffected. This has direct implications for compute-optimal allocation strategies, which differ between lazy and feature learning regimes for hard tasks. Key limitations include the restriction to MSE loss with SGD (not cross-entropy with adaptive optimizers), the assumption that learned features remain within the span of initial features, and a noticeable disagreement with CIFAR-5M training at large compute, suggesting additional nonlinear effects not captured by the model.
