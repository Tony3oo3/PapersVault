## Problem

In privileged imitation learning, a teacher trained with full environmental information (e.g., exact obstacle distances) often learns behaviors that a student with limited observations (e.g., a single RGB camera) cannot replicate, because the teacher is optimized without regard for the student's partial observability. Existing approaches attempt to fix this by adjusting the student policy, but the paper instead asks whether the teacher itself can be regularized to produce imitable behaviors, closing the performance gap at its source.

## Input / Output

The teacher consumes privileged state observations (relative obstacle distances, joint positions/velocities, cell types), while the student receives RGB images processed through a frozen DINOv2 encoder combined with state observations. A proxy student network takes the same privileged observations as the teacher and predicts the student's actions, eliminating the need to render expensive images at every timestep. All three networks (teacher, student, proxy student) share a single fully-connected action decoder that outputs continuous actions (quadrotor torques, joint positions) or discrete actions (maze directions), with the decoder updated only by the task-reward policy gradient.

## Method

The method derives a modified teacher objective from the imitation learning performance bound, adding the expected KL-divergence between teacher and student action distributions as a penalty. This yields two concrete mechanisms: (i) a reward penalty during roll-out that discourages the teacher from visiting states where the proxy student (which approximates the student's actions from teacher observations) disagrees with the teacher, and (ii) a KL-divergence gradient term in the PPO policy update that directly backpropagates through the teacher encoder to align its latent representation with the student's. Training alternates three phases—roll-out, policy update, and alignment—where the alignment phase uses a small subset of paired teacher-student observations to enforce L1 feature matching between student and teacher encoders, and between proxy student and student encoders, with gradients restricted to the student/proxy encoders to prevent collapse. The shared action decoder, trained exclusively on task reward across all expert states, provides a robust common feature space that the student can exploit even when alignment data is limited.

## Results

On the vision-based quadrotor obstacle avoidance task, the proposed method with alignment achieves a 46% success rate, outperforming the best baseline DWBC (35%) by 11 points and DAgger (8%) by 38 points. On the vision-based drawer-opening manipulation task, the method with alignment reaches 88% success rate, surpassing DWBC (63%) by 25 points and DAgger (34%) by 54 points. In the color maze task, only the proposed method enables the student to successfully navigate to the goal, while all baselines (BC, DAgger, and the non-aligned variant) fail due to the teacher exploiting privileged information invisible to the student.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all five baselines on both the quadrotor and manipulation tasks. Against the strongest baseline DWBC (Fu et al., 2022), the method gains 11 points on quadrotor (0.46 vs 0.35) and 25 points on manipulation (0.88 vs 0.63). HLRL (Radosavovic et al., 2024) and COSIL (Nguyen et al., 2023) perform comparably to each other but are consistently 15–16 points below the proposed method on quadrotor and 22–32 points below on manipulation. The classical imitation baselines BC (Bain & Sammut, 1995) and DAgger (Ross et al., 2011) perform worst, with success rates below 0.10 on quadrotor and below 0.35 on manipulation, confirming that static teacher policies cannot be effectively imitated under partial observability. The ablation variant without alignment (w/o Align) already surpasses all baselines on quadrotor (0.38) but trails the full method by 8 points, highlighting the additional benefit of the KL-divergence gradient term in the policy update.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the imitation learning performance bound (Ross et al., 2011; Xu et al., 2020) and the PPO algorithm (Schulman et al., 2017), but departs from the dominant paradigm of prior work (COSIL, HLRL, TGRl, Walsman et al.) that modifies the student to handle partial observability by adding RL objectives or balancing IL/RL terms. Instead, it inverts the perspective: the teacher's objective is augmented with the student's performance bound, causing the teacher to learn imitable behaviors. Two practical innovations enable this without prohibitive cost: a proxy student network that approximates student actions from teacher observations (avoiding expensive image rendering), and a shared action decoder that transfers high-level feature structure from teacher to student.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a fundamental and underappreciated limitation in privileged imitation learning: the teacher is trained blind to whether the student can actually replicate its behavior, leading to systematic performance gaps in partially observable settings. The emergent behaviors (camera-aware quadrotor flight, occlusion-avoiding manipulation) demonstrate that the method produces qualitatively different teacher policies, not just marginally better ones. Limitations include reliance on simulation for all evaluations, the approximation quality of the proxy student in high-dimensional observation spaces, and the added training complexity (three alternating phases, three networks). Open questions include whether the approach scales to multi-agent settings, real-world deployment with distribution shift, and tasks where the information gap is more severe (e.g., audio-visual vs. state-only).
