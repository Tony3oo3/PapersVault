## Problem

Existing SE(3)-invariant crystal encoders rely on statically fixed frames (PCA or lattice-based) determined solely by structural information, regardless of the task or the learned interatomic interactions. This paper questions whether such global, structure-aligned frames are sufficient, and asks whether frames should instead reflect the local dynamic environment around each atom as captured by learned attention weights in each message-passing layer.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250314/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a finite unit cell (atomic species, 3D Cartesian coordinates, and three lattice vectors) and internally reconstructs the infinite periodic crystal through distance-decay attention over all unit-cell translations, so the model operates on the full infinite structure while receiving only the finite cell. Atom-level representations are aggregated via global mean pooling before a two-layer feed-forward head produces a single scalar property (formation energy, bandgap, bulk/shear modulus, or E hull) per material.

## Method

The core contribution is the concept of dynamic frames: per-atom, per-layer, per-head coordinate systems constructed from learned self-attention weights rather than from the raw structure. Two variants are proposed—weighted PCA frames (eigenvectors of a covariance matrix weighted by attention scores) and max frames (selecting the highest-weight neighbor directions as axes with Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization). These frames project interatomic direction vectors into a local coordinate system, yielding three angle-based invariant features encoded via Gaussian basis functions that are combined with the original distance-based features to form richer edge embeddings. The architecture builds on Crystalformer (a transformer with infinitely connected distance-decay attention), adding only the frame construction step and angular edge features; gradients from frames to weights are omitted during training since frame construction is not stably differentiable, while weights still receive gradients through the message-passing path.

## Results

On the JARVIS dataset (5 tasks), CrystalFramer with max frames achieves the best MAE on 4 out of 5 tasks: formation energy (0.0263 eV/atom), total energy (0.0279 eV/atom), bandgap OPT (0.117 eV), and bandgap MBJ (0.242 eV), while slightly trailing iComFormer on E hull (0.0471 vs. 0.047 eV). On the Materials Project dataset (4 tasks), it achieves the best MAE on formation energy (0.0172 eV/atom), bandgap (0.185 eV), and bulk modulus (0.0338 log(GPa)), though iComFormer leads on shear modulus (0.0637 vs. 0.0677 log(GPa)). On the much larger OQMD dataset (817K materials), CrystalFramer improves over the Crystalformer baseline by 11.5% on formation energy (0.01871 vs. 0.02115 eV/atom) and 3.7% on bandgap (0.05805 vs. 0.06028 eV).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CrystalFramer with max frames outperforms all 10 baselines on formation energy, total energy, and both bandgap tasks on JARVIS, improving over the strongest prior method (iComFormer) by 3.3% on formation energy (0.0263 vs. 0.0272) and 7.0% on bandgap MBJ (0.242 vs. 0.26). It also beats the Crystalformer baseline by 14.1% on formation energy (0.0263 vs. 0.0306), demonstrating that dynamic frames add substantial value over the distance-only attention. On the MP dataset, CrystalFramer leads on formation energy (0.0172 vs. 0.0183 for iComFormer), bandgap (0.185 vs. 0.193), and bulk modulus (0.0338 vs. 0.0380), but iComFormer retains a slight edge on shear modulus (0.0637 vs. 0.0677). The older GNN baselines (CGCNN, SchNet, MEGNet, GATGNN, M3GNet, ALIGNN) are consistently outperformed by 20–60% across all tasks. Notably, adding conventional static frames (PCA or lattice) to Crystalformer actually degrades performance relative to the frameless baseline, while the proposed dynamic frames yield consistent gains, validating the core hypothesis that frames should adapt to learned interatomic interactions rather than the static structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Frame Averaging framework (Puny et al., 2022) and the Crystalformer transformer architecture (Taniai et al., 2024) by introducing a fundamentally different notion of frames: rather than constructing a single static, globally-aligned coordinate system per crystal (as in PCA frames of Duval et al., 2023 or lattice frames of Yan et al., 2024), it defines per-atom, per-layer 'dynamic frames' that align with the learned attention weights governing interatomic message passing. The conceptual departure is that frames should reflect the model's internal interaction states rather than the raw geometric structure, shifting the role of frames from canonical structural representation to interaction-aware local coordinate systems. The implementation adds angular edge features via Gaussian basis functions projected onto these dynamic frames, building directly on the distance-only position encoding of Crystalformer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dynamic frame concept provides a simple, low-parameter (~100K overhead) mechanism to inject richer 3D geometric information into distance-based crystal transformers, consistently improving SOTA across JARVIS, MP, and OQMD benchmarks. The finding that discrete max-frame selection outperforms continuous weighted-PCA frames suggests that focusing on the strongest local interactions is more informative than averaging over all weighted neighbors, though this introduces discontinuities that may limit out-of-domain generalization. Open questions include equivariant extensions for force prediction, application to molecular structures (where low atom counts may hinder frame construction), and scalability to supercells with defects or surfaces. The approach is broadly applicable to any attention-based crystal encoder and could serve as a building block for ML-driven simulations.
