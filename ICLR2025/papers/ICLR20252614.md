## Problem

Offline multi-objective optimization (MOO) requires finding Pareto-optimal trade-offs among conflicting objectives using only a fixed dataset, but prior approaches rely on evolutionary algorithms and Bayesian optimization with limited exploitation of generative modeling. Traditional predictor (classifier) guidance in generative models targets a single objective, producing isolated Pareto samples rather than approximating the full Pareto front. This paper asks whether flow matching can be effectively guided to cover the entire Pareto front in an offline setting.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a fixed offline dataset of designs (continuous vectors, or discrete structures converted to continuous logits) paired with their multi-objective labels, with no online queries to the true objective during training. It outputs 256 Pareto-optimal design samples selected via non-dominated sorting from a larger candidate pool generated by the flow. A ground-truth oracle is used only for final hypervolume evaluation, never during the generative process.

## Method

ParetoFlow trains a flow matching vector field on the offline dataset alongside m supervised objective predictors, then guides ODE sampling with two modules. Module 1 (multi-objective predictor guidance) assigns each of N samples a uniform weight vector from the Das-Dennis method, forming a weighted distribution p_β(y|x̂₁,ω) = exp(γ·f̂_ω)/Z that steers the vector field toward the corresponding Pareto-optimal region; a local hypercone filtering scheme rejects samples whose predicted objective vector deviates angularly from the weight vector, enabling coverage of non-convex fronts. Module 2 (neighboring evolution) exploits the fact that distributions with similar weight vectors produce similar samples: at each time step, O offspring are drawn from each of K+1 neighboring distributions, filtered by the hypercone, and the best offspring by weighted objective score is selected as the next state, fostering cross-distribution knowledge sharing. Sampling uses Euler-Maruyama with a small noise term (g=0.1) for diversity, and a Pareto-optimal set is maintained throughout to retain the best samples per weight vector.

## Results

ParetoFlow achieves the best average rank across all five task categories in Off-MOO-Bench, with an overall average rank of 3.12±3.77 (lower is better), compared to the next-best DNN-based method MM at 6.71±4.31. On MORL tasks, ParetoFlow achieves a perfect rank of 1.00±0.00, and on Sci-Design it reaches 2.75±1.48. The evaluation metric is hypervolume (HV) computed over 256 solutions using the 100th and 50th percentile protocols; for example, on ZDT2 ParetoFlow achieves HV of 6.79±0.16 versus 6.36±0.23 for LaMBO-2 and 6.32±0.07 for PROUD, and on MO-Hopper it reaches 5.69±0.03 versus 5.66±0.03 for LaMBO-2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ParetoFlow outperforms all 11 baseline groups across every task category. Among DNN-based methods, the strongest baselines are MM (rank 6.71) and MM+IOM (rank 6.73) on the overall average, both of which ParetoFlow (rank 3.12) surpasses by roughly 3.5–3.6 rank positions; on MORL specifically, ParetoFlow achieves a perfect rank of 1.00 while MM+IOM reaches only 6.50. GP-based methods (MOBO, MOBO-qParEGO, MOBO-JES) consistently underperform even the offline dataset best (D-best), with MOBO-JES ranking last at 18.13 overall, confirming their unsuitability for the offline setting. Among generative modeling baselines, PROUD (9.46) and LaMBO-2 (9.44) are the closest competitors, but ParetoFlow leads them by over 6 rank positions on average; on MORL, ParetoFlow (1.00) beats PROUD (2.50) and LaMBO-2 (3.00), while on Sci-Design ParetoFlow (2.75) outperforms LaMBO-2 (5.00) and MOGFN (5.50). The VAE-based CorrVAE (12.69) and GFlowNet-based MOGFN (10.42) trail significantly behind ParetoFlow on most categories, particularly on MO-NAS where ParetoFlow achieves rank 3.47 versus 17.74 for CorrVAE.

## Relation to Prior Work

ParetoFlow sits at the intersection of two research threads: flow matching for generative modeling (Lipman et al., 2023) and decomposition-based evolutionary multi-objective optimization (MOEA/D, Zhang & Li, 2007). The predictor guidance mechanism is derived from Zheng et al. (2023) for guided flows, while the weighted distribution form follows Lee et al. (2023) for score-based out-of-distribution generation. The key conceptual departure is transplanting MOEA/D's uniform weight-vector decomposition and neighborhood-based mutation into a continuous generative sampling process, replacing population-based evolution with ODE/SDE trajectory guidance. Prior offline MOO work (Xue et al., 2024) relies on evolutionary algorithms or Bayesian optimization over trained predictors, and prior generative MOO methods (PROUD, LaMBO-2, CorrVAE, MOGFN) use diffusion, VAE, or GFlowNet backbones—none combine flow matching with evolutionary priors in this way.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ParetoFlow demonstrates that a generative model trained once on the offline dataset can outperform iterative evolutionary and Bayesian optimization loops across diverse domains (synthetic functions, NAS, RL policies, molecular/protein design, engineering), while remaining computationally efficient (most tasks under 10 minutes). The neighboring evolution module's finding that 58–81% of selected offspring come from neighboring rather than own distributions provides empirical evidence that cross-distribution knowledge transfer is a critical mechanism in multi-objective sampling, not merely a heuristic. Limitations include reduced effectiveness on high-dimensional discrete tasks (MO-NAS) and the requirement to train both objective predictors and a flow model. Open questions include whether the approach extends to online or constrained settings, and whether the Das-Dennis weight scheme can be replaced by adaptive or learned weight distributions for problems with highly non-uniform Pareto fronts.
