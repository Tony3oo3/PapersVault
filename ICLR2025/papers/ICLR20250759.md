## Problem

Despite Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) substantially improving generalization over SGD, the mechanism behind its implicit bias toward flatter minima remains poorly understood, and existing theoretical analyses rely on unrealistic assumptions. This paper asks: does SAM need to be applied throughout the entire training process, or can its flat-minima selection be achieved more efficiently?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250759/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB image classification (CIFAR-10/100 at 32×32) with the same data augmentation and hyperparameters as the original SAM/SGD papers. The key procedural input is a switching epoch t: the model is first trained with SGD (or Adam for ViTs) for t epochs, then switched to SAM for the remaining T−t epochs, with identical initialization and mini-batch ordering across all switching points. The output is the final model parameters, evaluated by test error/loss and sharpness (spectral norm of the Hessian), with the critical finding that even 1–5 epochs of late-phase SAM suffice to match full-SAM generalization and sharpness.

## Method

The paper introduces a 'switching method' that trains with SGD up to epoch t and then switches to SAM for the remaining epochs, isolating the effect of SAM at different training phases. Theoretically, the authors identify a two-phase picture after the switch: (i) SAM escapes the sharp minimum found by SGD exponentially fast, proven via a loss-based linear stability analysis showing that SGD's solution violates SAM's stability condition (Theorem 4.1 + Corollary 4.2); and (ii) SAM then rapidly converges to a flatter minimum within the same valley, proven under a sub-quadratic landscape assumption (Proposition 4.1) with an exponentially fast convergence rate (Theorem 4.3). The sharpness bound for SAM includes an additional factor (1 + ρ²γ‖H‖²_F/B) beyond SGD's bound, formally establishing SAM's stricter flatness requirement. The findings are extended to Adversarial Training, showing that AT similarly selects robust minima efficiently when applied only in the late phase.

## Results

The paper's central empirical finding is that applying SAM for only the last 1–5 epochs (out of 200 total) after initial SGD training on WideResNet-16-8/CIFAR-10 yields test error and Hessian sharpness nearly identical to full SAM training, with the generalization gap dropping to near-zero as soon as the SAM proportion exceeds zero (Figure 3). A complementary negative result shows that early-phase SAM (SAM→SGD switching) retains a substantial generalization gap even when SAM is used for 80% of training (Figure 5). The same late-phase efficiency extends to Adversarial Training: switching from SGD to AT at epoch 180/200 matches full AT robustness on CIFAR-10 (Figure 6). Theoretically, the paper proves a two-phase mechanism—exponential escape from SGD's sharp minimum followed by rapid convergence to a flatter minimum within the same valley—supported by a linear stability analysis yielding the sharpness bound ‖H(θ*)‖²_F(1+ρ²γ‖H(θ*)‖²_F/B) ≤ B/(η²γ) for SAM versus ‖H(θ*)‖²_F ≤ B/(η²γ) for SGD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against full SAM training (Foret et al., 2021), the SGD→SAM switching method matches generalization and sharpness with as few as 1–5 SAM epochs on WRN-16-8/CIFAR-10, and the gap remains negligible across VGG-16, ResNet-20, WRN-28-10/CIFAR-100, and ViT-T/S (with Adam as the early-phase optimizer). Against full Adversarial Training (Madry et al., 2018), SGD→AT switching at epoch 180/200 achieves comparable robust error with only a slight spike in natural error. The same late-phase efficiency holds for ASAM (Kwon et al., 2021) and USAM (Andriushchenko & Flammarion, 2022), confirming the phenomenon is not specific to one SAM variant. In contrast, the reverse switch (SAM→SGD) fails to preserve SAM's benefits: even with 80% SAM training, the generalization gap to full SAM remains substantial, and the Edge of Stability phenomenon re-emerges immediately after switching back to SGD. The theoretical comparison (Table 2) shows SAM's additional ρ²γ‖H‖²_F/B factor in the stability condition strictly tightens the sharpness bound relative to SGD, explaining why SAM provably selects flatter minima.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the linear stability framework of Wu et al. (2022) and the gradient noise alignment assumption to derive a loss-based stability condition for SAM, extending their SGD analysis with an additional sharpness-dependent term arising from SAM's inner ascent step. It adopts the sub-quadratic landscape concept from Ma et al. (2022) to prove SAM remains within the current valley during escape, and uses the simplified SAM update rule (independent inner/outer noise) from Andriushchenko & Flammarion (2022) for tractability. Conceptually, the paper departs from prior SAM analyses (Shin et al. 2023, Behdin et al. 2023) that studied full-training dynamics or norm-based stability, by isolating the late training phase and showing it is the critical window for flat-minima selection—a perspective largely unattended in the literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that just 1–5 epochs of SAM at the end of training match full SAM in generalization and sharpness has direct practical value (compute savings, fine-tuning from pretrained checkpoints) and shifts the conceptual emphasis from early to late training dynamics for understanding implicit bias. The two-phase theoretical picture (exponential escape from sharp minima, then rapid convergence to flatter ones within the same valley) provides a clean mechanistic explanation that unifies escape and convergence analyses. Limitations include reliance on simplifications (constant perturbation radius, squared loss, 1D valley analysis for P2) and the theory covering only the late phase; the conjecture that late-phase optimization dominates final solution properties remains unproven for early-phase dynamics. A natural open direction is generalizing the switching-method analysis to other optimizers and understanding whether the late-phase dominance extends beyond flatness to other solution properties.
