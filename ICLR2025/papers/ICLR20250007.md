## Problem

Existing unlabeled OOD detection methods (self-supervised, unsupervised, zero-shot) report strong performance on standard benchmarks, but it remains unclear whether labels can be safely ignored. This paper identifies a theoretical guarantee of failure—'label blindness'—when the learning objective is independent of the ID labels, and shows that existing benchmarks mask this failure by using ID and OOD data from different datasets with minimal feature overlap.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250007/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

All methods operate on 64×64 RGB images. SSL methods (SimCLR, RotLoss) train on unlabeled ID images and score OOD via Mahalanobis distance (SSD) or KNN Euclidean distance; the diffusion inpainting method uses an 8×8 alternating checkerboard mask with LPIPS or MSE reconstruction error as the OOD score; CLIPN uses pretrained CLIP weights with text prompts. The Adjacent OOD benchmark randomly assigns 25% of classes to OOD and 75% to ID (repeated 3× with different seeds), creating maximum feature overlap between ID and OOD samples—e.g., the same face with different expressions.

## Method

The core contribution is the Label Blindness Theorem, which proves via information bottleneck analysis that the minimal sufficient statistic for a surrogate task (e.g., SimCLR contrastive loss) cannot encode information about any variable independent of that task, guaranteeing I(x₂; z) = 0 and thus OOD detection failure. A supporting lemma (Independence of Filtered Distributions) shows that selecting the ID training set by label cannot break this independence, so no dataset curation can avoid the failure. To empirically expose this gap, the paper introduces the Adjacent OOD detection benchmark—bootstrapping without replacement of ID labels to create OOD classes that maximally overlap ID in feature space—and proves (Theorem 4.1) that such overlap is unavoidable in any real-world system. Experiments across ICML Facial Expressions, Stanford Cars, and Food-101 show that SimCLR, RotLoss, diffusion inpainting, and CLIPN all degrade to near-random AUROC (~50) on Adjacent OOD, while a simple supervised MSP baseline outperforms all unlabeled methods.

## Results

On the Adjacent OOD benchmark, the supervised MSP baseline achieves AUROC of 70.8 (Faces), 69.2 (Cars), and 78.8 (Food), substantially outperforming all unlabeled methods. Self-supervised methods (SimCLR, RotLoss) and the unsupervised diffusion inpainting method all perform near random guessing (AUROC 46–65), confirming the predicted label blindness failure. The zero-shot CLIPN method shows dataset-dependent performance: strong on Cars (AUROC 81.1 with ATD) and Food (AUROC 84.9 with ATD) where pretraining data aligns with ID labels, but near random on Faces (AUROC 44.2 with ATD) where it does not.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The supervised MSP baseline (Hendrycks & Gimpel, 2016) consistently outperforms all unlabeled methods on Adjacent OOD, achieving 15–30 AUROC points higher than SimCLR and RotLoss variants. SimCLR (Chen et al., 2020) paired with either KNN (Sun et al., 2022) or SSD Mahalanobis scoring (Sehwag et al., 2021) degrades to near-random AUROC (~52) on Faces and Cars, confirming that the SSL representation contains no label-relevant information. RotLoss (Hendrycks et al., 2019) performs even worse, with AUROC as low as 46.1 on Faces, below the 50 random-guessing threshold. The diffusion inpainting method (Liu et al., 2023) also fails, scoring AUROC 51–55 across all datasets regardless of whether LPIPS or MSE is used. CLIPN (Wang et al., 2023) is the notable exception: its zero-shot ATD variant achieves AUROC 81.1 on Cars and 84.9 on Food, surpassing even the supervised baseline, but collapses to 44.2 on Faces where pretraining captions (e.g., 'angry fist') do not align with the ID label semantics. This pattern confirms the paper's central claim: unlabeled methods succeed only when the learning objective happens to share information with the ID labels, and fail systematically when it does not.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the information-theoretic framework of Federici et al. (2020) and Shwartz-Ziv & LeCun (2023) from a general impossibility statement (No Free Generalization) to a precise, actionable guarantee of OOD detection failure under specific independence conditions between the surrogate learning objective and the ID labels. It departs from the growing body of unlabeled OOD detection work (Sehwag et al., Hendrycks et al., Liu et al., Guille-Escuret et al., Wang et al.) by identifying a fundamental theoretical limitation those methods share, and by introducing the Adjacent OOD benchmark to expose this limitation empirically. The paper also positions itself against Du et al. (2024b), which shows labels help OOD detection but does not establish a guarantee of failure in their absence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Label Blindness Theorem establishes that no universally applicable unlabeled OOD detection algorithm can exist, which has direct safety implications for autonomous driving, medical diagnosis, and other critical applications that may rely on SSL-based OOD methods. The Adjacent OOD benchmark exposes a safety gap present in all existing OOD benchmarks (far and near OOD), where significant ID-OOD feature overlap is guaranteed in real-world deployment. Open questions include how much labeled data is sufficient to overcome approximate label blindness, whether few-shot or one-shot methods can bridge the gap, and how to extend the theory to pretrained models where the independence assumption may be relaxed. The paper's empirical validation is limited to three datasets and specific architectures, and the strict independence condition may not always hold in practice.
