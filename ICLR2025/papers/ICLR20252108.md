## Problem

While LLMs have been evaluated on graph structure tasks like shortest path and connectivity, their ability to understand and mine graph patterns—a fundamental operation in computational chemistry, biology, and social network analysis—remains largely unexplored. This paper asks whether LLMs can comprehend graph patterns from terminology-based descriptions (e.g., 'triangle'), topology-based descriptions (e.g., adjacency lists), or by autonomously discovering patterns from data, and how well they perform across these modalities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252108/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Graphs are serialized as text in either adjacency list or edge list format (with node IDs as integers and optional atom-type annotations for molecular graphs), and patterns are described either by natural-language terminology or by explicit topology (node/edge lists). The 11 tasks produce diverse outputs: generated graphs with constrained pattern counts, modified graphs with minimal edge edits, bijection mappings between isomorphic graphs, sets of detected pattern occurrences, k-core node subsets, extracted frequent subgraphs in topology format, discriminative patterns with a downstream classification label, and F1/accuracy/precision scores. Input graphs range from 5 to 35 nodes (synthetic) or up to ~32 nodes (real-world molecules), with density controlled at 0.25–0.58.

## Method

The benchmark organizes 11 tasks into three progressive categories: (1) terminology-based tasks (pattern translation, graph modification, pattern detection) testing alignment with pretraining knowledge; (2) topology-based tasks (isomorphic mapping, modification, detection) testing structural reasoning without named patterns; and (3) data-driven tasks (k-core detection, frequent subgraph extraction, discriminative pattern learning) testing autonomous pattern discovery. Seven LLMs (GPT-4, GPT-4o, Mixtral, Llama-3.1-401B, Gemini-1.5-Pro, Claude-3-Opus, O1-mini) are evaluated zero-shot at temperature 0 across synthetic and 10 real-world datasets. The paper also proves (Theorem 1) that Transformers with edge-list input can simulate any LOCAL message-passing algorithm, establishing theoretical expressiveness. Key empirical findings include: LLMs employ diverse internal algorithms (e.g., degree-counting vs. edge-comparison for isomorphism) with performance varying by execution reliability; input format aligned with pretrained knowledge (terminology over topology, edge list over adjacency list for edge-comparison tasks) improves accuracy; and O1-mini's longer chain-of-thought gives it an edge on search-heavy tasks but hurts on tasks requiring fuzzy pattern matching.

## Results

Across 11 graph pattern tasks and 7 LLMs, O1-mini achieves the highest average accuracy (0.86) and diversity (0.78) in pattern translation, and leads in most modification and detection tasks. On frequent subgraph extraction, GPT-4 and O1-mini both achieve 1.00 accuracy across nearly all patterns and scales. For real-world molecular pattern detection, GPT-4 and GPT-4o reach F1 scores of 0.88–0.90 on Benzene, while on real-world classification (IMDB-BINARY), GPT-4 achieves 0.78 accuracy with edge list format. Notably, GPT-4o and Claude maintain 0.94–1.00 accuracy on large-scale isomorphic mapping with edge list format, outperforming O1-mini (0.32) which relies on error-prone degree counting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark compares 7 LLMs (GPT-4, GPT-4o, Mixtral, Llama, Gemini, Claude, O1-mini) as baselines across all tasks. O1-mini wins the majority of tasks in pattern translation (avg. ACC 0.86 vs. GPT-4o 0.84, GPT-4 0.83), graph modification (e.g., S→D large-scale: 0.94 vs. GPT-4 0.20), and pattern detection, largely due to its longer chain-of-thought. However, O1-mini loses to GPT-4o and Claude on isomorphic mapping at large scale (0.32 vs. 0.94/1.00 with edge list), because O1-mini relies on degree counting (89% of outputs) while GPT-4o and Claude use direct edge comparison. On frequent subgraph extraction, GPT-4 and O1-mini tie at 1.00 accuracy for most patterns, while Gemini and Claude drop to 0.07–0.40. In discriminative pattern learning, GPT-4 achieves the highest classification accuracy (0.97 with edge list), while O1-mini underperforms (0.72) despite a low discriminative pattern ratio (0.08), because its strict pattern matching yields overly specific patterns that fail to generalize.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new evaluation space at the intersection of LLM research and graph pattern mining. Prior LLM-for-graphs work has focused on graph algorithm tasks (shortest path, connectivity via NLGraph, GraphInstruct, Graphwiz) and graph learning tasks (node/graph classification via Graph-LLM, LLaGA, GraphGPT), while graph pattern discovery (motifs, k-cores, frequent subgraphs) has been studied extensively in the classical graph mining and GNN literature. The authors position their benchmark as filling the gap where 'the potential of LLMs in graph pattern mining remains largely unexplored,' and their theoretical analysis builds directly on Wu et al. (2024) to show Transformers can simulate LOCAL message-passing algorithms. Unlike prior work that treats graphs as input to a single downstream task, this paper systematically decomposes pattern comprehension into terminology-based, topology-based, and data-driven subtasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first comprehensive benchmark (11 tasks, 7 LLMs, synthetic + real-world data) for evaluating LLM graph pattern comprehension, providing a reusable framework for the growing community working on LLMs for structured data. Key open questions remain: LLMs' fundamental weakness in counting (degree-based algorithms) limits their reliability on structural tasks, and their tendency toward simpler patterns (triangles over houses) suggests they lack true compositional graph reasoning. The finding that input format alignment with pretraining knowledge (terminology vs. topology, adjacency list vs. edge list) significantly shifts performance raises practical questions about how to best interface LLMs with graph data. Scaling to larger, noisier real-world graphs and multi-hop pattern composition remain largely unsolved.
