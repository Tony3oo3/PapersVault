## Problem

GNN-based brain network analysis suffers from two intertwined challenges: multi-site data collection introduces distribution shifts that degrade OOD generalization, and existing graph OOD methods that extract invariant substructures overlook the noise in both node features and graph topology that is characteristic of brain networks. Because brain networks share a fixed set of 100 ROIs across all subjects, the same substructure in different regions carries distinct functional meaning, making generic invariant-subgraph approaches insufficient for both generalization and scientific interpretability.

## Input / Output

Each input is a brain network graph with exactly 100 nodes (Schaefer atlas ROIs) where the feature matrix X is the full Pearson-correlation connectivity matrix and the adjacency matrix A is its sparsified top-20% version. The method outputs a diagnosis label, a discrete causal subgraph (selected edges + masked features) for interpretation, and per-edge score heat maps visualized on the cortical surface. The fixed 100-node alignment across all subjects is a structural constraint the method explicitly exploits via an alignment loss, unlike general graph OOD methods that operate on variable-size graphs.

## Method

BrainOOD combines a feature selector, a structure extractor, and auxiliary losses within an improved Graph Information Bottleneck objective. The feature selector applies a learnable Hadamard mask (sigmoid of W_mask W_mask^T) with an entropy sparsity penalty, then uses a high-pass GNN to reconstruct the masked features, counteracting the noise amplification caused by standard GNN message-passing smoothing. The structure extractor scores edges via a Gumbel-softmax scorer and samples discrete subgraphs k times per forward pass, averaging the resulting predictions. A key differentiator is an alignment loss that penalizes the standard deviation of selected adjacency matrices across a batch, enforcing that the same ROI connections are consistently identified across subjects—exploiting the fixed node alignment unique to brain networks. A theoretical result (Theorem 4.1) shows that without sufficient representation power (I(G;H) < H(G_C*)), the GIB objective cannot recover the true causal subgraph, motivating the reconstruction-based feature enhancement.

## Results

On the ABIDE dataset (ASD, 2-class), BrainOOD achieves 64.81% OOD accuracy, outperforming the best baseline BrainNetCNN (60.38%) by 7.34% relative improvement. On ADNI (5-class), BrainOOD reaches 62.26% OOD accuracy vs. GMT's 60.93%, and on ADNI (6-class) it achieves 64.07% OOD accuracy vs. GSAT's 60.79%. Across all three settings, BrainOOD also leads on in-domain accuracy (64.07%, 66.09%, 65.71% respectively), and the paper reports up to 8.5% improvement over existing graph OOD methods on OOD subjects.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BrainOOD outperforms all 16 baselines on both ID and OOD accuracy across ABIDE and ADNI. Among general OOD methods (ERM, Deep Coral, IRM, GroupDRO, VREx), none surpass BrainOOD; VREx is the weakest (52.08% OOD on ABIDE), while IRM and Deep Coral reach ~57% OOD. Graph OOD methods (Mixup, DIR, GSAT, GMT) show comparable performance to ERM, confirming the paper's claim that invariant-subgraph extraction is ineffective for brain networks; GMT is the strongest graph OOD baseline (59.73% OOD on ABIDE, 60.93% on ADNI-5c) yet still trails BrainOOD by 5+ points. Brain-specific GNNs (BrainNetCNN, BrainGNN, ContrastPool, Contrasformer) achieve competitive ID accuracy but degrade sharply on OOD (e.g., BrainNetCNN drops from 65.50% ID to 60.38% OOD on ABIDE). Simple ML baselines (SVM, LR) and general GNNs (GCN, GIN, GAT) all fall below BrainOOD on every metric. Notably, incorporating BrainOOD's framework on top of the GIN backbone yields a 12.2% improvement over GIN alone, underscoring the contribution of the feature selector and structure extractor rather than the backbone architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

BrainOOD builds directly on the Graph Information Bottleneck (GIB) framework (Wu et al., 2020) and the discrete Gumbel-softmax sampling strategy from GMT (Chen et al., 2024), but departs from the invariant-subgraph paradigm that underpins DIR, GSAT, and GMT. The key conceptual departure is recognizing that brain networks have fixed node identity (100 Schaefer ROIs shared across all subjects), so the same topological substructure in different ROIs carries different functional meaning — making 'invariant subgraph' extraction ill-posed. BrainOOD replaces this with a feature selector (learnable mask + HPGNN reconstruction) and an alignment loss that enforces consistent edge selection across samples, exploiting the node-identity structure absent in molecular or social graphs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The first OOD site-holdout benchmark for brain networks fills a clear gap: prior brain GNN papers evaluated only in-domain accuracy, masking the severe degradation that occurs when models are deployed at unseen sites. The finding that all four graph OOD methods (DIR, GSAT, GMT, Mixup) fail to beat a plain GIN baseline on brain networks is a useful negative result that cautions against blindly transferring invariant-learning techniques to neuroimaging. Open questions include whether the alignment loss generalizes to brain networks with variable ROI counts (e.g., different atlases), whether the identified biomarker connections can be independently validated in clinical cohorts, and whether the approach extends to other modalities (DTI, MEG) or longitudinal prediction tasks.
