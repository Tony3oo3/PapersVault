## Problem

Prior memorization research focused on repeated or low-complexity sequences, leaving the most privacy-sensitive class—highly complex sequences encountered only once during training—largely uncharacterized. It was unclear whether such sequences are truly forgotten after a single exposure or persist in model weights in a form invisible to standard memorization probes, raising the question of how to detect and quantify this hidden memorization.

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on pre-trained transformer checkpoints (Pythia-1B at 3k-step intervals from 10k–43k, Amber-7B at 10-checkpoint intervals) and training sequences formatted as a 32-token context followed by a 64-token target continuation. For each sequence, the model produces a greedy (temperature-0) continuation, and the kl-LD score is computed as the token-level Levenshtein distance between the generated and true continuations. The weight-perturbation recovery step adds independent Gaussian noise (σ = 2×10⁻³) to every parameter, repeated 200 times per sequence, and selects the perturbation yielding the lowest kl-LD.

## Method

The paper introduces kl-LD, a Levenshtein-distance-based memorization metric that tolerates small token-level deviations unlike strict kl-memorization, and z-complexity (compressed/original length via zlib) to stratify sequences by compressibility. Tracking kl-LD across dozens of checkpoints for single-occurrence, high-complexity (z-complexity > 0.8) sequences reveals stationarity: the memorization distribution neither decays (catastrophic forgetting) nor diffuses (random walk), with a variance-ratio test rejecting random-walk dynamics at p < 10⁻⁸. The key mechanistic test adds random Gaussian noise to model weights and shows that sequences appearing 'forgotten' at one checkpoint are recovered at later checkpoints or under perturbation, while genuinely un-memorized or unseen sequences are not—supporting the hypothesis that subsequent training acts as additive noise on an already-encoded memory. A cross-entropy loss diagnostic is then proposed: latent memorized sequences exhibit significantly lower CE loss than un-memorized or unseen sequences, providing a practical detection tool that does not require weight access.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that memorization probability scales log-linearly with both sequence repetition count and z-complexity (lower complexity = easier memorization). For sequences encountered only once with high complexity (>0.8), the kl-LD (Levenshtein distance) remains stationary throughout training, with a variance ratio test rejecting the random-walk null hypothesis at p < 10⁻⁸ for both Pythia-1B and Amber-7B. Adding Gaussian noise of magnitude 2×10⁻³ to model weights and selecting the best of 200 perturbations successfully recovers latent memorized sequences (kl-LD < 10), while temperature sampling (4 temperatures × 200 samples each) fails to do so. Cross-entropy loss provides a simple diagnostic: latent memorized sequences exhibit significantly lower cross-entropy loss than un-memorized or unseen sequences.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical analysis paper without a direct method-vs-method comparison table. The key internal comparisons are: (1) kl-LD (the proposed metric) vs. kl-memorization (Carlini et al., 2022) — kl-LD is more robust to single-token deviations that would cause false 'forgotten' classifications under exact-match; (2) weight perturbation (2×10⁻³ Gaussian noise, 200 trials) vs. temperature sampling (4 temperatures, 200 samples each) for recovering latent memories — weight perturbation succeeds while temperature sampling fails, indicating memories are encoded in weights rather than accessible via sampling; (3) three sequence classes (latent memorized, encountered-but-unmemorized, and unseen) are compared under perturbation — only latent memorized sequences are recoverable, confirming the perturbation is not simply reproducing arbitrary text. Results are validated across two model families (Pythia-1B on Pile, Amber-7B on LLM360/RedPajama/RefinedWeb/StarCoder) to ensure generality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the memorization analysis framework established by Carlini et al. (2020, 2022) and Biderman et al. (2023), which characterized memorization as a function of repetition count and model size. The key departure is the introduction of z-complexity as a second axis of analysis and the focus on the most privacy-sensitive regime: highly complex sequences encountered only once. While prior work (Tirumala et al., 2022; Prashanth et al., 2024) studied memorization dynamics over training, this paper specifically tracks individual sequence trajectories across checkpoints and introduces the kl-LD metric (Levenshtein-based) to capture near-memorization that exact-match kl-memorization misses. The weight-perturbation recovery experiment draws on the catastrophic forgetting literature (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017) but inverts its logic: rather than preventing forgetting, it uses noise to reveal that forgetting never truly occurred.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The discovery of latent memorization has direct implications for data privacy: a model that passes standard memorization checks at its final checkpoint may still contain recoverable sensitive data, undermining current safety evaluations. The proposed cross-entropy loss diagnostic offers a practical, low-cost screening tool for deployed models. However, the mechanistic explanation (memorized sequences as stable fixed points in weight space) remains a hypothesis, and the findings are validated only on Pythia-1B and Amber-7B over a limited checkpoint range (10k–43k steps). Open questions include whether the phenomenon persists in much larger frontier models, what architectural or training factors determine which sequences become 'stable,' and whether the perturbation-based recovery could be weaponized as an extraction attack.
