## Problem

Standard contrastive losses (SimCLR, CLIP, SupCon) encode a binary similarity graph where only one sample is the positive and all others are negatives, ignoring the rich semantic similarities that exist between different samples (e.g., a dog is more related to a cat than to a mug). This binary treatment discards valuable cross-sample relational signal, limiting the quality and generalizability of learned representations, particularly in low-data regimes and for tasks requiring fine-grained decomposition of objects from their attributes and backgrounds.

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images as input and produces a fixed-dimensional image embedding (ResNet-50 backbone with a 128-dim two-layer projector). Unusually, it also consumes a precomputed soft similarity matrix over the training set, derived offline from text caption embeddings (via Sentence Transformer) or class labels, which is stored on disk and loaded alongside each image during training. The text encoder is never run during the training loop; only the image encoder is updated. The final output is a frozen image encoder evaluated via linear probing or KNN on downstream classification and robustness benchmarks.

## Method

X-CLR replaces the one-hot positive target in the InfoNCE/SimCLR objective with a soft probability distribution s_i derived from a cross-sample similarity graph. The graph is constructed by encoding each sample's caption (or class name) with a frozen text encoder, computing pairwise cosine similarities, and applying a softmax with a separate temperature τ_s to obtain a valid distribution over all samples in the batch. The training loss is then the cross-entropy between this soft target s_i and the model's learned cosine-similarity distribution p_i over the batch. As τ_s → 0, the method recovers SupCon (or SimCLR without labels); as τ_s increases, more weight is placed on semantically related but non-identical samples. The similarity source is flexible—caption embeddings, WordNet hierarchy distances, or any metadata—and the approach introduces negligible computational overhead since similarities are precomputed.

## Results

On ImageNet (1M well-labeled samples), X-CLR achieves 75.6% top-1 linear-probing accuracy, outperforming SimCLR (63.4%) by 12.2 points and Supervised Contrastive (74.3%) by 1.3 points. On ImageNet Real, X-CLR reaches 81.5% vs. 67.8% for SimCLR and 79.7% for SupCon. When trained on noisy CC12M captions, X-CLR outperforms CLIP by 0.6% on both ImageNet (59.4% vs. 58.8%) and ImageNet Real (66.7% vs. 66.1%), while on the smaller CC3M dataset the gap widens dramatically to 17.2% on ImageNet (58.2% vs. 41.0%). X-CLR also leads on background decomposition (ImageNet-9: 66.6% Same Class, 62.7% Mixed) and ObjectNet (27.5%), indicating stronger object-background disambiguation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

X-CLR outperforms all seven baselines across every benchmark. Against SimCLR (the standard contrastive baseline), X-CLR gains 12.2 points on ImageNet (75.6% vs. 63.4%) and 13.7 points on ImageNet Real (81.5% vs. 67.8%), with even larger margins on robustness tasks (ObjectNet: 27.5% vs. 12.1%; ImageNet-9 Mixed: 62.7% vs. 38.9%). Against Supervised Contrastive, which already uses class labels, X-CLR still wins by 1.3 points on ImageNet and 1.8 on ImageNet Real, showing that soft cross-class similarities add value beyond binary same-class grouping. Against the non-contrastive SSL methods VICReg (72.4%) and Barlow Twins (72.8%), X-CLR leads by 3.2 and 2.8 points respectively. Against soft-target distillation methods SCE (71.3%) and ReSSL (69.4%), X-CLR gains 4.3 and 6.2 points, confirming that the similarity graph source (metadata vs. teacher model) matters. In the noisy multimodal setting (CC12M), X-CLR edges out CLIP by 0.6% on ImageNet, but the advantage is far more pronounced on CC3M where X-CLR beats CLIP by 17.2% (58.2% vs. 41.0%), highlighting superior data efficiency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the graph-theoretic unification of contrastive and supervised objectives from Cabannes et al. (2023), which showed that SimCLR, VICReg, and Barlow Twins can all be expressed as learning to match a target similarity graph. The key departure is replacing the binary (0/1) adjacency matrix inherent to standard contrastive losses with a continuous-valued soft similarity graph, where edge weights encode degrees of cross-sample relatedness. Unlike distillation methods (SCE, ReSSL, TinyCLIP) that derive soft targets from a teacher model's outputs, X-CLR sources its similarity graph from arbitrary metadata—text captions, class labels, or even WordNet hierarchy distances—making it a more general framework. The method also extends SupCon's multi-positive idea by allowing graded rather than binary positive relationships, and it is explicitly positioned as distinct from CLIP since it trains only the image encoder and uses text solely to compute target similarities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that explicitly encoding how samples relate to each other—beyond the single positive pair—yields representations that are more data-efficient (17.2% over CLIP at 3M samples), better at separating objects from backgrounds and attributes, and more robust to distribution shift. A notable open question is graph construction quality: the paper shows that noisy web captions degrade the soft graph substantially, and that 1M high-quality ImageNet labels outperform 12M noisy CC12M captions for fine-grained attribute disambiguation, suggesting the method's ceiling is bounded by metadata quality. The authors also note the approach requires extra storage for the similarity matrix and additional preprocessing, and leave open extensions to non-contrastive objectives (BYOL, VICReg) and other modalities where structured metadata (e.g., biological taxonomies) is available.
