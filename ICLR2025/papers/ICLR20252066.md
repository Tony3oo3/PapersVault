## Problem

Current LLM safety benchmarks rely on static or templated prompt datasets that fail to cover the rapidly expanding space of jailbreak attacks, leading to widespread deployment of unsafe models. While prior work demonstrated that composing individual attacks yields higher success rates, no formal composable representation existed to enable systematic, program-synthesis-driven exploration of the compositional attack space against a target black-box LLM.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252066/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The synthesizer takes a target black-box LLM, a library of parameterized string transformation primitives (e.g., Base64Decorator, RolePlayingDecorator, PAPDecorator), and an initial pool of 22 SOTA plus 5 handcrafted attack programs as few-shot examples. It produces H4RM3L programs—valid Python expressions composing primitives—that transform illicit prompts (sampled from AdvBench) into jailbreak attacks. ASR is estimated via a zero-shot GPT-4 classifier using a ternary Good/Bad/Unclear Bot taxonomy, and the final benchmarking evaluates 83 attacks (identity + 22 SOTA + 60 synthesized) over 50 illicit prompts per model.

## Method

H4RM3L is a Python-embedded DSL where jailbreak attacks are compositions of parameterized string transformation primitives, including two generic primitives (TransformFxDecorator for arbitrary Python string functions and RolePlayingDecorator for prefix/suffix templating) plus ~25 human-designed high-level primitives. The synthesizer iteratively prompts an auxiliary LLM (GPT-4) with k=15 few-shot examples to generate N=20 candidate programs per iteration, scoring each on k=5 illicit prompts via the GPT-4 classifier. Three bandit strategies govern example selection: Random (uniform), ASR Rewarded (Beta distribution parameterized by each example's own ASR, with variance decreasing over iterations via hyperparameter λ), and Offspring ASR Rewarded (Beta posterior updated by the ASRs of proposals generated when each example was used). Programs exceeding the pool-average ASR are added to the example pool, creating an iterative self-improvement loop. A key ablation shows that restricting synthesis to only the two generic primitives (low-level expression) degrades top-20 ASR below 70% even after 80 iterations, confirming the critical value of human-designed high-level abstractions.

## Results

h4rm31 synthesizes 2,656 novel jailbreak attacks with ASR between 40% and 100% targeting 6 SOTA LLMs. The highest recorded ASRs are 94% on GPT-4o, 90% on Llama-3-70B, 88% on GPT-3.5, 82% on Claude-3-Haiku, 74% on Llama-3-8B, and 44% on Claude-3-Sonnet. The ASR Rewarded Bandits synthesis method achieves a mean top-20 ASR exceeding 80% by the 10th iteration on GPT-4o, while restricting to low-level expression (LLE) degrades performance to below 70% even after 80 iterations. Synthesized attacks outperform the best SOTA jailbreak attacks by margins of 10–50 percentage points across all six target models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

h4rm31's synthesized attacks outperform all 22 SOTA baseline attacks on every target model. Against Wei et al. (2023)'s suite of attacks (base64, refusal suppression, style injection, affirmative prefix injection, few-shot, Wikipedia, combination), the best SOTA attack (sota_combination_3) reaches only 58% ASR on Llama-3-70B versus 90% for the best synthesized attack. Zou et al. (2023)'s universal transferable adversarial suffixes (UTA) achieve 84% on Llama-3-8B but are outperformed by synthesized attacks reaching 74% on that model while also generalizing across models. Liu et al. (2023)'s role-playing attacks (AIM, DAN) and Zeng et al. (2024)'s persuasion attack (PAP) achieve at most 22–28% ASR on most models, far below synthesized attacks. Yuan et al. (2023)'s cipher attack peaks at 76% on Llama-3-8B but transfers poorly to other models. Kang et al. (2023)'s payload splitting and Yong et al. (2023)'s low-resource translation both remain below 35% ASR on most targets. Notably, the best SOTA attacks show limited cross-model transferability, whereas h4rm31's targeted synthesis produces model-specific attacks that are consistently superior on their intended target.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Wei et al. (2023), who demonstrated empirically that composing individual jailbreak primitives (base64 encoding, refusal suppression, style injection, etc.) yields higher attack success rates than any single attack. The key departure is formalizing this compositional structure as a domain-specific language (h4rm31) and applying bandit-based few-shot program synthesis to automatically search the compositional space, rather than relying on handcrafted or template-based attacks. It differentiates from LLM-Fuzzer (which produces templates) by generating full programs with arbitrary computation, and from static benchmarking systems like HarmBench and JailbreakBench by enabling dynamic, targeted attack discovery. The approach also draws on the broader program synthesis literature (Gulwani et al., 2017; Austin et al., 2021) and DSPy's declarative LLM pipeline paradigm, but applies these ideas to the novel setting of adversarial prompt transformation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that synthesized attacks achieve 90%+ ASR on SOTA models like GPT-4o and Llama-3-70B, while substantially outperforming all prior SOTA attacks by 10–50 percentage points, underscores the inadequacy of current safety evaluations that rely on static attack sets. The strong model-specificity of successful attacks (limited cross-model transferability) implies that comprehensive safety assessment requires targeted synthesis per model, a practical burden the h4rm31 toolkit addresses. Key limitations include the 78% classifier accuracy (vs. 93% human), the non-comparable Claude-3 results due to Anthropic's mid-experiment safety filter changes, and the restriction to single-turn black-box attacks. Open questions include whether the compositional space can be further expanded (multi-turn, multimodal), whether the bandit synthesis scales to new model releases without re-tuning, and whether the discovered attack patterns can inform more robust safety training.
