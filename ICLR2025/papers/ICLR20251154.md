## Problem

Transformer language models can predict neural activity in the human language system with high functional accuracy, yet they entirely lack the spatial arrangement of neurons on the cortical surface. The mechanisms driving the spatio-functional organization of the language cortex—where language-selective neurons cluster into semantically interpretable groupings (e.g., verb- vs. noun-selective patches)—remain unexplained computationally. This paper asks whether the spatial smoothness principle that successfully explains topography in visual cortex can generalize to the language system.

## Input / Output

TopoLM consumes naturalistic text (sentences, word lists, non-word strings) as input and produces next-token predictions alongside spatially organized 28×28 unit activation maps at each of 12 transformer blocks (attention and MLP outputs). To compare with fMRI data, a simulated readout applies a Gaussian kernel (FWHM 2.0 mm, unit spacing 1.0 mm) to aggregate neighboring unit responses into voxel-like signals before computing selectivity contrasts. The model is a GPT-2-small-style architecture (hidden dim 784, 16 heads, GELU) trained on 10B tokens from FineWeb-Edu.

## Method

TopoLM augments the standard cross-entropy next-token prediction loss with a spatial correlation loss that encourages nearby units on a 2D grid to have correlated activations. Each attention and MLP layer's units are bijectively mapped to a 28×28 grid (randomly permuted per layer), and the spatial loss for layer k is computed as 0.5·(1 − corr(r_k, d_k)), where r_k is the vector of pairwise Pearson correlations of unit activations on the current batch and d_k is the vector of pairwise inverse distances (ℓ∞ norm) between grid positions. For efficiency, the loss is approximated over 5 randomly selected neighborhoods of ℓ∞ radius 5 per batch rather than the full layer. The combined objective ℓ = TL + Σ_k α_k·SL_k (α_k = 2.5) is optimized jointly, so spatial organization emerges purely from the training signal without any brain-data fitting. A non-topographic baseline (α_k = 0, all else identical) serves as the control, and both models are trained for 5 days on 4×A100 GPUs with early stopping.

## Results

On downstream benchmarks, TopoLM achieves 0.71 on BLiMP, 0.68 on GLUE, and 0.78 on Brain-Score, compared to the non-topographic baseline's 0.76, 0.65, and 0.80 respectively. The spatial smoothness loss costs 5 points on BLiMP and 2 points on Brain-Score but yields a 3-point gain on GLUE, suggesting the spatial term acts as mild regularization. Critically, TopoLM reproduces brain-like spatio-functional organization: verb/noun-selective clusters reach Moran's I = 0.81 (with fMRI-like sampling) versus 0.60 for the non-topographic baseline and 0.96 in human fMRI data, and concrete-word verb/noun clustering (I = 0.80) substantially exceeds abstract-word clustering (I = 0.23), matching the pattern in Moseley & Pulvermüller (2014).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the non-topographic baseline (identical GPT-2-small architecture trained without spatial loss), TopoLM trades a small amount of linguistic knowledge (−5 pts BLiMP) and brain alignment (−2 pts Brain-Score) for a gain in downstream task performance (+3 pts GLUE) and, most importantly, for the emergence of brain-like spatial organization. On the spatial clustering metric (Moran's I), TopoLM substantially outperforms the non-topographic baseline for verb/noun contrasts (I = 0.81 vs. 0.60 with fMRI sampling; I = 0.48 vs. 0.11 at unit level) and uniquely reproduces the concrete-over-abstract dissociation (I = 0.80 vs. 0.23) that the baseline fails to show (I = 0.11 vs. 0.12). Against Topoformer-BERT (BinHuraib et al., 2024), TopoLM shows clear brain-like noun/verb-selective clustering with significant units, whereas Topoformer-BERT yields few significant units in the same contrasts (10.61% for Hauptman stimuli, 0% for Moseley stimuli) and exhibits dispersion rather than autocorrelation in its significant-unit islands (Islands Moran's I = −0.51 vs. +0.52 for TopoLM). However, Topoformer-BERT does reproduce a spatially organized language network on Fedorenko et al. (2010) localization stimuli, suggesting its local-connectivity approach captures sentence-level but not word-level selectivity.

## Relation to Prior Work

TopoLM directly extends the TDANN family of topographic vision models (Lee et al., 2020; Margalit et al., 2024), transplanting their spatial smoothness loss from CNN-based visual architectures into a standard GPT-2-style transformer trained with autoregressive next-token prediction. It departs from the only prior topographic language model, Topoformer (BinHuraib et al., 2024), which induces spatial organization through local connectivity constraints on a single-headed BERT; TopoLM instead uses a global spatial correlation loss that is compatible with multi-head attention and full connectivity. The model is trained solely on naturalistic text (FineWeb-Edu) without any fitting to neural data, positioning it as a generative account of cortical organization rather than a post-hoc fit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides evidence that a single spatial smoothness principle, previously validated in visual cortex, generalizes to the language system, offering a unified mechanistic explanation for the spatio-functional clustering observed in human fMRI and ECoG recordings. A key limitation is that each layer has an independently permuted topographic map, so there is no coherent 'tissue' across the network as in the brain, constraining future modeling of cross-layer phenomena like micro-stimulation spread. Open questions include whether the principle extends to other cortical domains (e.g., motor, auditory), whether the per-layer random permutation is necessary or an artifact, and whether the modest BLiMP degradation (5 points) persists at larger scale or with better-tuned spatial loss weights.
