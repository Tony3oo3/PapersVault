## Problem

Self-supervised latent variable models for instruction-following agents (e.g., GROOT-1) suffer from latent space ambiguity: without human guidance, the latent space degenerates into either mechanical imitation (encoding low-level action sequences) or posterior collapse (losing all control over the policy), neither of which aligns with human intention. Acquiring full multimodal annotations at scale is prohibitively expensive, yet a small amount of labeled data could resolve this ambiguity. The paper asks whether a weakly supervised framework can leverage large unlabeled demonstration sets alongside a small labeled set to produce a controllable, human-aligned latent space.

## Input / Output

At inference, GROOT-2 accepts one of three instruction modalities—a reference video (sequence of 224×224 RGB frames), a text sentence, or a scalar expected return—along with the current RGB observation, and outputs a causal action sequence. In Minecraft the action head is a joint multi-classification over 8461 button combinations and 121 camera directions; in Language Table and Simpler Env it predicts 2D delta Cartesian setpoints or 6D end-effector poses with a gripper flag. The video instruction is consumed as spatially averaged 7×7 ViT patch embeddings per frame, text as full BERT word embeddings (not just [CLS]), and returns as an MLP projection, all unified through modality-specific learnable markers before encoding.

## Method

GROOT-2 trains a shared latent space Z, per-modal instruction encoders e(z|c), and a latent-conditioned Transformer-XL policy π(a_t|o_{≤t}, z) under a two-component weakly supervised objective. The first component, constrained self-imitating (L_dem), applies a VAE-style ELBO on unlabeled demonstrations where the KL term regularizes the full-trajectory posterior e(z|o_{1:N}) toward the single-frame prior e(z|o_1), preventing the latent from encoding every low-level action detail. The second component, human intention alignment (L_lab), encodes the multimodal label w_{1:M} into a distribution e(z|w_{1:M}), reconstructs actions from z sampled from it, and adds a closed-form MLE term that maximizes log e(z|o_{1:N}) for z drawn from e(z|w_{1:M}) (with stop-gradient), ensuring the label-conditioned latent is also plausible under the video-conditioned posterior. The encoder is a non-causal Transformer that processes all modalities uniformly with modality markers, and a cross-attention pre-fusion module (query = z, keys/values = 49 spatial patches) compresses each observation into a single token before the causal decoder, reducing sequence length while integrating latent and perceptual information.

## Results

On the Minecraft SkillForge Benchmark (100 tasks, 8 families), GROOT-2 (visual) achieves the highest average success rate across all categories, with notable gains over GROOT-1 in Combat (37% vs. 18%), Hunt (48% vs. 28%), and Craft (27% vs. 15%). On the Language Table benchmark, GROOT-2 (50% labeled) reaches 82% overall success rate with language instructions, surpassing LAVA (78%) and RT-1 (74%), while GROOT-1 achieves only 8%. On Simpler Env, GROOT-2 (lang) matches or exceeds RT-1-X on Move Near (45% vs. 32%) and Open/Close Drawer (31% vs. 29%), while RT-1-X leads on Pick Coke Can (49% vs. 42%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GROOT-2 consistently outperforms its direct predecessor GROOT-1 across all Minecraft task families, with the largest gains in Combat (+19 pts visual) and Hunt (+20 pts visual), attributed to language data enhancing object-centric understanding. On Language Table, GROOT-2 (50% labeled) surpasses LAVA by 4 points (82% vs. 78%) and RT-1 by 8 points (82% vs. 74%), while GROOT-1 collapses to 8% due to mechanical imitation of trajectory sketches rather than semantic intent. On Simpler Env, GROOT-2 (lang) achieves comparable performance to RT-1-X (the full OpenX-trained upper bound) on Move Near and Open/Close Drawer, while RT-1-X retains a 7-point edge on Pick Coke Can; both vastly outperform Octo-base (1–3% success). The paper also shows that GROOT-2's weakly supervised variant (wsl) significantly outperforms its fully self-supervised counterpart (ssl) on Atari video-instruction following, particularly in Hero and Name This Game, confirming that even small amounts of return labels improve latent space alignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

GROOT-2 extends the self-supervised reference-video-following framework of GROOT-1 (Cai et al., 2023b) by introducing a weakly supervised training objective that resolves the 'latent space ambiguity' problem inherent in VAE-based policy learning. While prior work on instruction-following policies (RT-1, LAVA, STEVE-1) relies on fully labeled demonstrations, and self-supervised approaches (GROOT-1, OPAL) avoid labels entirely at the cost of uninterpretable latent spaces, this paper occupies the middle ground: a small set of multimodal labels (text, video, or scalar returns) is used to align the latent space with human intention via a maximum log-likelihood objective, while the bulk of training uses unlabeled demonstrations through constrained self-imitating. The architectural design unifies all instruction modalities into a shared latent space using a non-causal Transformer encoder, building on the VAE framework of Kingma & Welling (2013) and the Transformer-XL decoder used in prior Minecraft agents.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck in scaling instruction-following agents: the cost of high-quality multimodal annotations. By showing that 30–50% labeled data suffices to resolve latent space ambiguity (with diminishing returns beyond 50%), it provides a concrete recipe for reducing annotation costs while maintaining strong instruction-following performance across diverse environments. A key limitation is the reliance on trajectory data with action labels, which restricts applicability to the vast pool of unlabeled video data available online; the authors identify extending the framework to video-only data as a natural next step. Open questions include whether the MLE-based alignment generalizes to more complex or compositional instructions, and whether the shared latent space can support truly open-ended task specification beyond the demonstrated modalities.
