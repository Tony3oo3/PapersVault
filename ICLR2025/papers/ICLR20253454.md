## Problem

The cosine router in Mixture of Experts (MoE) has shown empirical success in mitigating representation collapse, yet no comprehensive statistical analysis of its parameter and expert estimation rates existed. This paper reveals that the L2 normalization inherent to the cosine router induces a partial differential equation (PDE) among router parameters, causing estimation rates to degrade to non-polynomial orders as slow as O(1/log^τ(n)), making the cosine router even less sample-efficient than the linear router it was designed to replace.

## Input / Output

The method operates on token hidden representations x ∈ R^{d1} (assumed already projected to a low-dimensional space) and expert embeddings β_{1i} ∈ R^{d1}, computing routing scores via the normalized inner product (β_{1i}^T x) / ((‖β_{1i}‖ + τ₁)(‖x‖ + τ₂)) plus a bias term β_{0i}, which are then passed through a softmax to yield routing weights. The final output is a scalar regression prediction g_G(x) = Σᵢ Softmax(·)ᵢ · h(x, ηᵢ), where h(·, ηᵢ) is a parametric expert function. In the language modeling and domain generalization experiments, inputs are character/word sequences or image patches processed through a Switch Transformer or ViT-S/16 backbone, with outputs being next-token predictions or class labels.

## Method

The paper first proves that the vanilla cosine router's normalization creates an intrinsic parameter interaction captured by the PDE β₁^T (∂H/∂β₁) = 0, where H is the product of the softmax numerator and expert function. This PDE induces linearly dependent terms in Taylor expansions, yielding a minimax lower bound that forces at least one parameter's estimation rate to be slower than any polynomial. The key intervention is the perturbed cosine router, which adds small noise hyperparameters τ₁ > 0 and τ₂ > 0 to the L2 norms of expert embeddings and token representations respectively, breaking the PDE (i.e., β₁^T (∂H̃/∂β₁) ≠ 0). Under a newly defined strong identifiability condition on expert functions (satisfied by ReLU, tanh, and polynomial experts of degree ≥ 2), the paper proves polynomial estimation rates ranging from O_P((log n / n)^{1/4}) to O_P(√(log n / n)) for both parameters and experts. Empirical validation on synthetic data, character/word-level language modeling (Enwik8, Text8, Wikitext-103), and domain generalization (PACS, VLCS, OfficeHome, TerraIncognita, DomainNet) confirms the theoretical advantages of the perturbed cosine router over both the vanilla cosine and linear routers.

## Results

On domain generalization, the perturbed cosine router achieves an average out-of-distribution accuracy of 68.37%, outperforming the vanilla cosine router (66.70%) and the linear router (65.40%) across all five DomainBed benchmarks. The largest gains appear on TerraIncognita (49.87% vs. 45.55% cosine, 41.30% linear) and PACS (89.36% vs. 87.22% cosine). On language modeling, the perturbed cosine router consistently improves BPC/PPL over the vanilla cosine router, e.g., reducing Enwik8 BPC from 1.161 to 1.147 (medium) and Wikitext-103 PPL from 38.018 to 37.859 (medium). Theoretically, the perturbation eliminates the PDE-induced parameter interaction, improving worst-case expert estimation rates from O(1/log^τ(n)) under the vanilla cosine router to polynomial rates of O(n^{-1/4}) to O(√(log(n)/n)).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed perturbed cosine router against two baselines: the vanilla cosine router (Chi et al., 2022) and the linear router (Nguyen et al., 2024c). On domain generalization, the perturbed cosine router wins on all five DomainBed datasets against both baselines, with the most dramatic margin on TerraIncognita (+4.32 pp over cosine, +8.57 pp over linear) and PACS (+2.14 pp over cosine, +3.03 pp over linear). On language modeling, the perturbed cosine router consistently beats the vanilla cosine router across Enwik8, Text8, and Wikitext-103 at both small and medium scales (e.g., 1.147 vs. 1.161 BPC on Enwik8-medium; 37.859 vs. 38.018 PPL on Wikitext-103-medium), though the linear router is not directly compared in the language modeling experiments. The theoretical analysis further shows that the vanilla cosine router suffers from non-polynomial estimation rates (as slow as O(1/log^τ(n))) due to a PDE-induced parameter interaction, while the perturbed cosine router restores polynomial rates (O(n^{-1/4}) to O(√(log(n)/n))) for strongly identifiable experts such as ReLU FFNs and polynomials of degree ≥ 2.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical analysis of MoE parameter and expert estimation rates (established for linear routers in Nguyen et al., 2024c and for Gaussian MoE in Ho et al., 2022) to the cosine router introduced by Chi et al. (2022) to mitigate representation collapse. The key departure is the discovery that the L2 normalization inherent to the cosine router creates a PDE-based parameter interaction (β₁ᵀ ∂H/∂β₁ = 0) that fundamentally obstructs fast convergence, a phenomenon absent in the linear router setting. The proposed perturbed cosine router—adding noise to L2 norms—is a technique already used in practice for stabilization, but this is the first work to provide a rigorous theoretical justification for why it is necessary from a statistical estimation perspective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the widely-adopted cosine router has non-polynomial (logarithmic) convergence rates for parameter and expert estimation is a significant theoretical correction to the field's understanding of MoE routing. The strong and weak identifiability conditions provide concrete guidance for expert network design (non-linear experts like ReLU/GeLU FFNs are preferred over linear ones). Key limitations include the well-specified setting assumption, point-wise (non-uniform) rates, and the misspecified setting being left for future work. The practical implication is straightforward: practitioners using cosine routers should add noise to L2 norms, which is now theoretically justified rather than merely empirically motivated.
