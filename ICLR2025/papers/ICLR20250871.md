## Problem

Dot-product attention in Transformers scales quadratically with context length, making long-context decoding prohibitively slow. Existing training-free acceleration methods (StreamingLLM, H2O, SnapKV) either permanently evict tokens—losing potentially critical information—or rely on heuristics without theoretical guarantees for correct token selection. No prior method can dynamically retrieve the most important tokens from arbitrary positions in the context in sublinear time with provable correctness.

## Input / Output

Radar operates on any pre-trained Transformer (Llama, Mistral) without architectural modification, consuming the standard query, key, and value projections at each decoding step. The context is partitioned into contiguous segments of size c = √t, and a random projection matrix Ω ∈ R^{n×d} (default n=2048) maps key embeddings into a feature space where segment-level importance scores are computed. At each step, the top-k segments (default k=64) are selected, and full softmax attention is computed only over the tokens within those segments plus a sliding-window buffer of at most 2√t−1 recent tokens, yielding the attention output vector for next-token generation.

## Method

Radar organizes key embeddings in a two-level hierarchy: individual tokens at the bottom and contiguous segments at the top. Each segment is assigned a 'summarization representation' by averaging random-feature projections (attention kernelization à la Choromanski et al.) of its constituent keys, pre-computed and stored in O(√t) memory. At query time, segment importance is scored via a single dot product between the projected query and each segment representation, reducing per-segment cost to O(1); the top-k segments are then expanded for full attention computation. A dynamic restructuring schedule resizes segments to c=√t only when √t is an integer, amortizing the O(t) reconstruction cost to O(√t) per step, while a sliding-window buffer handles tokens between restructurings. The overall decoding complexity is O(t^{1.5}) for t tokens (O(√t) per step), and Theorem 2 provides a high-probability guarantee that the true top-scoring segment is correctly identified whenever the attention gap exceeds O(log(c)/c)·exp(ζ²/√d)/√n, a condition that becomes easier to satisfy as context length grows.

## Results

On LongBench (16 subtasks), Radar achieves the highest average score and percentile among all training-free baselines across all three tested models. On Llama-2-7b-chat-hf with 2048 middle tokens, Radar scores 33.07 average (71.25% percentile), outperforming SnapKV (32.83, 64.38%) and H2O (32.63, 50.00%). On PG-19 perplexity, Radar maintains at most a 0.2 perplexity gap versus vanilla attention while achieving over 2× speedup, with performance degeneration capped at 10%. On Mistral-7B with 4096 middle tokens, Radar reaches 42.30 average score versus 42.76 for vanilla attention, using only 13% of the context tokens.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Radar outperforms all training-free baselines on average score and percentile across all three models and all context budgets tested. On Llama-2-7b-chat-hf (2048 middle tokens), Radar (33.07 avg, 71.25% perc.) beats SnapKV (32.83, 64.38%), H2O (32.63, 50.00%), StreamingLLM (31.08, 35.00%), and SubGen (24.98, 13.75%), approaching the vanilla attention ceiling of 33.18. On Mistral-7B with 4096 middle tokens, Radar (42.30) nearly matches vanilla (42.76) while using only 13% of tokens, whereas SnapKV (42.25) and StreamingLLM (37.53) trail further behind. Radar's advantage is most pronounced on long-generation tasks (e.g., GovRep summarization) where it dynamically retrieves different tokens at different decoding steps, unlike heuristic eviction methods. H2O and SnapKV both suffer severe perplexity degradation on Mistral (Appendix D), attributed to their reliance on accumulated attention scores as importance proxies, which fails under grouped-query attention architectures. Radar's theoretical guarantee (Theorem 2) ensures correct top-segment identification with high probability independent of model architecture, explaining its consistent generalization across Llama and Mistral families.

## Relation to Prior Work

Radar repurposes the random-feature kernelization machinery from Performers (Choromanski et al., 2021) not to replace dot-product attention (as Linformer or Performers do, requiring retraining) but to build cheap segment-level 'summarization' representations that enable sublinear-time top-k segment selection. This positions it squarely against the training-free context-reduction line (StreamingLLM, H2O, SnapKV, Landmark Attention), which all rely on heuristic eviction rules that permanently discard tokens. The key conceptual departure is the hierarchical two-layer structure with a dynamic restructuring schedule (rebuilding segments only when √t is an integer), which converts the per-step cost to O(√t) and the total decoding cost to O(t^1.5) without any architectural modification or fine-tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Radar offers a drop-in, training-free acceleration for any pre-trained Transformer with a provable guarantee (Theorem 2) that the selected segments contain the highest-attention tokens with high probability, and the required attention gap shrinks as O(log c / c) with context length—making the method relatively easier to satisfy for longer sequences. Practical limitations remain: Radar does not reduce peak memory (it stores O(√t) segment representations in addition to the full KV cache), and the contiguous-segment assumption may miss non-local important tokens. Open directions flagged by the authors include memory-efficient variants via hierarchical offloading, non-contiguous or adaptive segmenting, and whether training jointly with the hierarchical approximation could further close the gap to vanilla attention.
