## Problem

Masked diffusion models (MDMs) have shown promise in language modeling, but their scalability and effectiveness on core tasks—text generation, language understanding, and conditional inference—remain largely unexplored. It is unclear whether MDMs can match autoregressive models (ARMs) at scale, whether they can address inherent ARM limitations such as the reverse curse and temporal quality degradation, or whether a principled scaling law governs their performance.

## Input / Output

During pretraining, the MDM receives a text sequence of up to 2048 tokens where a fraction of positions are randomly replaced with a special mask token; it predicts the original token at each masked position conditioned on all unmasked tokens (bidirectional attention, no causal mask, no explicit timestep input). For conditional generation, the prompt tokens remain unmasked while only response tokens are noised, and the model iteratively unmasks positions over N sampling steps (64–256) using a MaskGIT-style top-l selection. For language understanding, the model computes the likelihood of each candidate answer given the prompt via either Monte Carlo estimation or a chain-rule decomposition, then selects the highest-likelihood option.

## Method

The paper establishes the first IsoFLOP scaling law for MDMs, showing that validation loss follows a power law with a rate comparable to ARMs but a constant 16× compute gap (versus 64× for continuous diffusion). The architecture is a standard Transformer encoder (no causal mask, no timestep conditioning) with RMSNorm, SwiGLU, and RoPE, trained with the absorbing discrete diffusion objective from Ou et al. (2024) that models all conditional distributions on clean data simultaneously. The key algorithmic contribution is unsupervised classifier-free guidance: by replacing the unconditional model in standard CFG with a conditional model that takes a mask sequence as a dummy condition (Eq. 7), the method exploits the fact that MDMs already capture both the conditional and unconditional distributions during unsupervised pretraining, eliminating the need for paired data while still benefiting from fine-tuning on small paired sets. This formulation, combined with the bidirectional training objective, enables MDMs to break the reverse curse (achieving 92% accuracy where 13B Llama-2 and 175B GPT-3 score 0%) and to show superior robustness to temporal distribution shifts compared to same-sized ARMs.

## Results

On eight zero-shot language understanding benchmarks, the 1.1B MDM (3.3×10²¹ FLOPs) outperforms the same-size TinyLlama on 4 of 8 tasks (BoolQ 62.17 vs 59.39, SIQA 41.04 vs 39.41, LAMBADA 52.73 vs 43.22, Obqa 33.40 vs 33.20) and surpasses the larger 1.5B GPT-2 on 6 of 8 tasks. On GSM8K math reasoning, the 1.1B MDM achieves 58.5% accuracy, matching the 7B Llama-2 (58.6%) while using less than 5% of its pre-training FLOPs. In conditional generation on MT-Bench, the MDM with 128 sampling steps scores 1.56 (vs. ARM's 1.57) while being 1.4× faster (396s vs 555s), and with 256 steps reaches 1.60, surpassing the ARM. On the reverse curse benchmark, the 1.1B MDM achieves 92% accuracy on reverse DescriptionToName queries, compared to 0% for GPT-3 (175B), Llama-2 (13B), and T5 (3B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against TinyLlama (1.1B, same 3.3×10²¹ FLOPs), the MDM wins on 4 of 8 tasks (BoolQ +2.78, SIQA +1.63, LAMBADA +9.51, Obqa +0.20) but trails on ARC-e (−3.45), Hellaswag (−2.24), and PIQA (−0.76). Against the larger GPT-2 (1.5B), the MDM wins on 6 of 8 tasks, with the largest margin on LAMBADA (+8.12) and RACE (+2.49), but loses on ARC-e (−2.31) and PIQA (−0.98). On GSM8K, the 1.1B MDM (58.5%) essentially ties the 7B Llama-2 (58.6%) despite using <5% of its pre-training compute (3.3×10²¹ vs 7.8×10²² FLOPs). On the reverse curse, the MDM dramatically outperforms all ARMs: it achieves 92% reverse DescriptionToName accuracy vs 0% for GPT-3 (175B), Llama-2 (13B), and T5 (3B), and 37% accuracy / 67 BLEU on reverse NameToDescription vs 0% / 19 BLEU for Llama-2. In conditional generation (MT-Bench), the MDM with 128 steps matches the ARM (1.56 vs 1.57) at 1.4× faster sampling, and with 256 steps surpasses it (1.60 vs 1.57) at 1.4× slower, demonstrating a flexible quality–speed trade-off that the ARM with KV-cache cannot replicate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the masked diffusion model formulation of Ou et al. (2024), which revealed that MDMs implicitly model conditional distributions of clean data independent of the diffusion timestep. It extends the scaling law literature (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) from autoregressive models to MDMs for the first time, and generalizes classifier-free guidance (Ho & Salimans, 2022; Chang et al., 2023) from supervised paired-data settings to an unsupervised formulation that exploits the bidirectional structure of MDMs. The paper positions MDMs as a direct competitor to ARMs, contrasting with prior MDM work (Lou et al., 2023; Sahoo et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2024) that focused on unconditional generation or small-scale perplexity evaluation without systematic scaling analysis or conditional task benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By establishing the first MDM scaling law and demonstrating a constant 16× compute gap (versus 64× for continuous diffusion), this paper provides the field with a concrete quantitative basis for evaluating MDMs as a scalable alternative to ARMs. The unsupervised CFG technique is a practical, low-cost improvement that any MDM practitioner can adopt without paired data. Key open questions remain: whether the 16× gap narrows with further optimization, whether MDMs exhibit emergent abilities at larger scales (the authors note their 1.1B models perform near-random on MMLU and ARC-c), and whether the structural advantages (reverse curse, temporal robustness) persist at the 70B+ scale where ARMs dominate. The 1.1B parameter ceiling means the most important comparisons against frontier ARMs remain future work.
