## Problem

Empirical legal scholars increasingly rely on commercial LLMs (GPT-4.5, Claude 3.7 Sonnet) to annotate and classify legal documents, assuming these large generalist models offer the best available solution. This paper critically examines that assumption by constructing a comprehensive benchmark of 260 real legal annotation tasks and demonstrating that small, lightly fine-tuned open-weights models substantially outperform all evaluated commercial models, challenging the prevailing narrative that generalist scale is necessary for legal annotation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251252/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes full majority court opinions (matched via case citations to the Caselaw Access Project) as input, with 12% of opinions exceeding 8,000 tokens and a maximum sequence length of 8,192 tokens during training. The output is a single legal classification label selected from a task-specific multiple-choice set of variable codes (ranging from 2 to 100+ classes), formatted as an MMLU-style prompt with a task description followed by answer choices. The benchmark comprises 718,971 task examples across 260 tasks, with majority classes subsampled so that a constant classifier cannot exceed 50% accuracy on any task.

## Method

The Lawma models are produced by full-parameter fine-tuning of small open-weights transformers (SmolLM2 135M/360M, Llama 3.2 1B/3B, Llama 3.1 8B) simultaneously on all 260 CaselawQA classification tasks using 1.96B tokens of labeled court opinions, trained for 3 epochs with AdamW (peak LR 2e-6, cosine schedule, weight decay 0.1) and DeepSpeed Zero 2. The key finding is that this specialization is highly data-efficient: a few hundred labeled examples per task suffice to match or exceed the zero-shot accuracy of Claude 3.7 Sonnet, and Lawma 8B achieves 87% aggregate accuracy versus 78% for the best commercial model. The paper further shows that fine-tuning on Court of Appeals tasks transfers to unseen Supreme Court tasks (+18.8 accuracy points), that a single multi-task model avoids the need for per-task models, and that further single-task specialization on top of Lawma 8B yields only marginal gains, confirming that broad multi-task fine-tuning is the practical sweet spot.

## Results

On the CaselawQA benchmark (260 legal classification tasks, 143,635 test examples), Lawma 8B achieves 87% micro-average accuracy, outperforming the best commercial model (Claude 3.7 Sonnet at 78%) by 9 percentage points. The smallest specialized model, Lawma 135M, is competitive with Claude 3.7 Sonnet despite being orders of magnitude smaller. A constant majority-class classifier achieves only 40% accuracy, and Llama 3.3 70B Instruct (71% accuracy) fails to significantly outperform this baseline on 34% of tasks. Fine-tuning on just a few hundred examples per task typically matches or exceeds Claude 3.7 Sonnet's zero-shot performance on 7 of 10 highlighted tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Lawma 8B (87%) outperforms Claude 3.7 Sonnet (78%), the best commercial model, by 9 accuracy points, and outperforms all other evaluated models by at least 9 points. Against Llama 3.3 70B Instruct (71%), Lawma 8B gains 16 points, and even with Chain-of-Thought prompting (which adds only 2–3 points to Llama 3 70B), Lawma 8B still leads by over 20 points. LegalBERT (110M) substantially underperforms even the constant classifier baseline, confirming that small pre-trained legal models are inadequate for these tasks. SaulLM 54B, a domain-adapted Mixtral model, improves over its base by only 4 points and still lags behind smaller generalist models like Llama 3.1 8B Instruct. GPT-4.5 and GPT-4o fall within the 70–78% range, showing minimal improvement over Llama 3 405B Instruct, reinforcing the paper's argument that scaling generalist models yields diminishing returns for legal annotation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of empirical legal studies and LLM benchmarking. It departs from the dominant paradigm of prompting large generalist commercial models (GPT-4.5, Claude 3.7 Sonnet) by demonstrating that standard fine-tuning of small open-weights models (135M–8B parameters) substantially outperforms them on legal annotation. Conceptually, it builds on the LegalBench benchmarking tradition and legal-domain models like SaulLM and LegalBERT, but explicitly positions itself against both: unlike LegalBench's diverse legal NLU tasks, CaselawQA focuses on core classification tasks derived from the Supreme Court and Court of Appeals databases; unlike SaulLM's general-purpose legal adaptation, Lawma is specialized to concrete annotation tasks. The method itself (full fine-tuning with standard hyperparameters) is unremarkable; the contribution is the empirical demonstration that this simple recipe, applied to a new benchmark, inverts the expected scale hierarchy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: legal scholars can replace expensive API calls to commercial models with a one-time fine-tuning of a small open-weights model using a few hundred labeled examples, a cost that is financially feasible for most research groups. The CaselawQA benchmark (260 tasks, 718K examples) provides a reusable evaluation suite for the ML community, and the finding that even fine-tuned models do not reach intercoder agreement rates signals substantial room for future improvement. Key limitations include the restriction to U.S. Supreme Court and appellate court opinions, high variance across tasks, and the fact that the 30-point accuracy drop when omitting Supreme Court cases from training underscores the need for task-specific data. Open questions include whether this specialization approach transfers to other legal systems, more complex reasoning tasks, or settings where labeled data is even scarcer.
