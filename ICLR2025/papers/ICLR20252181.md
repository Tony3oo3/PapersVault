## Problem

Prior provably sample-efficient algorithms for Ex-BMDP representation learning (e.g., PPE) require episodic resets to a known start state, while existing single-trajectory methods (Lamb et al., Levine et al.) lack any sample-complexity guarantees. This paper addresses the gap by providing the first algorithm with provable sample-efficiency for learning Ex-BMDP latent dynamics from a single continuous trajectory with no resets.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252181/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent interacts with the environment in one unbounded trajectory, observing high-dimensional x_t ∈ X and choosing actions a_t; it also requires a training oracle for the encoder class F (which, given two multisets of observations, returns a classifier separating them) and known upper bounds N ≥ |S|, D̂ ≥ D, and t̂_mix ≥ t_mix. The output is a learned latent state space S', a tabular transition function T': S'×A→S', and an encoder φ': X→S' constructed as argmax over one-vs-rest binary classifiers, with the guarantee that φ' matches the true encoder φ* with probability ≥ 1−ε under the stationary exogenous distribution.

## Method

STEEL exploits the determinism of latent transitions T to guarantee that repeatedly executing any fixed action sequence eventually enters a cycle of latent states, enabling near-i.i.d. sample collection without resets. Phase 1 iteratively grows a learned transition graph by constructing action sequences that are guaranteed to escape known transitions, then invoking a CycleFind subroutine that (a) determines the cycle's period n_cyc by testing candidate values with the training oracle (checking whether a one-vs-rest classifier can separate appropriately-indexed observations), and (b) identifies which cycle positions correspond to new vs. previously-seen latent states. Phase 2 navigates the completed dynamics graph to collect additional samples per state, spaced ≥ t̂_mix apart. Phase 3 trains one binary classifier per latent state via the oracle, with a union bound over states ensuring per-state accuracy of 1−ε/|S|. The sample complexity is polynomial in |S| and log|F|, linear in t̂_mix, and independent of |X| and |E|.

## Results

STEEL achieves a 20/20 success rate (100%) in exactly learning the latent dynamics T and optimal encoder φ* across all four test environments (combination lock with K=20, 30, 40, and multi-maze with |S|=68). Step counts scale with problem difficulty: ~2.0M steps for combination lock K=20, ~4.8M for K=30, ~9.6M for K=40, and ~41.3M for multi-maze (with δ=ε=0.05). Critically, step counts are invariant to exogenous noise (fixed vs. variable environment settings yield identical step counts), confirming the algorithm's noise-robustness. For the hardest combination lock (K=40), STEEL's ~10^7 steps are many orders of magnitude below both the observation space size (~10^154) and the reciprocal probability of a random policy solving the task (~10^12).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares STEEL against two prior single-trajectory Ex-BMDP methods: Lamb et al. (2023) and Levine et al. (2024), both of which use multi-step inverse models without sample-complexity guarantees. On the multi-maze environment, STEEL requires ~41M steps versus ~10³–10⁴ steps reported by these baselines — a gap of several orders of magnitude. However, this gap is attributed to STEEL's use of conservative adversarial bounds for provable correctness and its lack of spatial priors in the encoder hypothesis class (the baselines use sparsely-gated patch-encoding neural networks with strong spatial inductive biases). On a family of tabular Ex-BMDPs known to be particularly challenging for multi-step inverse methods (Appendix F), STEEL empirically outperforms both Lamb et al. and Levine et al. for sufficiently large instances. The key qualitative distinction is that STEEL is the first method in this setting with a provable sample-complexity bound (polynomial in |S|, linear in t̂_mix, logarithmic in |F|), whereas the baselines only guarantee convergence in the infinite-sample limit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Ex-BMDP representation learning framework of Efroni et al. (2022b), whose PPE algorithm provides sample-complexity guarantees but requires episodic resets to a fixed start state. It also builds on the single-trajectory Ex-BMDP setting explored by Lamb et al. (2023) and Levine et al. (2024), which lack any sample-complexity guarantees. The key departure is the cycle-based exploration strategy: by exploiting deterministic latent dynamics, STEEL repeatedly loops through action sequences to create predictable cycles of latent states, enabling near-i.i.d. sample collection without resets. The paper also removes the 'block' assumption on the exogenous state that prior works required, allowing the same observation to be emitted by multiple exogenous states.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

STEEL provides the first provable sample-complexity bound for Ex-BMDP representation learning in the single-trajectory, no-reset setting, which better models real-world scenarios where environment resets are impractical (e.g., expensive robotics). However, the strict determinism assumption on latent transitions is a major practical limitation: unlike the episodic setting where resets 'erase' rare stochastic deviations, in a single trajectory even rare deviations can propagate indefinitely, making the algorithm fragile. Additional barriers include the need for an optimal training oracle (computationally intractable beyond simple function classes) and a known upper bound on the exogenous mixing time. Open questions include whether the determinism requirement can be relaxed, whether the oracle assumption can be replaced with approximate optimization, and whether the approach extends to continuous or combinatorial latent state spaces.
