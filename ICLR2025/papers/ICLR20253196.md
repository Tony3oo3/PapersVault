## Problem

Biological visual systems effortlessly track objects as their colors, shapes, and positions change over time, yet this capability remains poorly understood computationally and is a major failure point for state-of-the-art DNNs, which collapse to near-chance accuracy when object color distributions shift between training and test. The paper asks whether neural synchrony—a mechanism proposed by neuroscience theories for binding features to locations independently of appearance—can provide a principled solution to this tracking-under-appearance-change problem.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequence of 32 video frames at 32×32 pixels, each containing a target object (identified by its position in a red start square at frame 1), 10 distractor objects, and a blue goal square. Objects simultaneously evolve in three dimensions: spatial position (smooth random-walk trajectories), color (constant-speed hue rotation in HSV space), and shape (Game of Life evolution on 5×5 pixel grids). The output is a single binary prediction: whether the target object (not a distractor) occupies the blue square at the final frame.

## Method

The CV-RNN augments the InT RNN's attention module with complex-valued units that implement neural synchrony: the magnitude of each complex neuron encodes object features while its phase encodes spatial location, allowing the network to bind features to positions without interference. Concretely, real-valued feedforward drive z and excitatory state e are converted to the complex domain via learned complex weights, a complex-valued recurrent hidden state φ[t] is updated by adding the current drive to the previous phase memory and applying real-valued convolutional weights, and the resulting complex attention is normalized (InstanceNorm) and passed through a sigmoid to modulate inhibition. Training minimizes a combined loss of binary cross-entropy for classification plus a synchrony loss that enforces three phase clusters (target, distractors, background) to be internally coherent and evenly spaced on the unit circle, thereby binding the target's features to a consistent phase tag distinct from distractors. The full architecture is a 3D convolutional preprocessing layer feeding 32 frames sequentially into the 32-channel CV-RNN, followed by global average pooling and a linear readout, trained end-to-end with Adam for 200 epochs on 100,000 synthetic videos.

## Results

On FeatureTracker, humans achieve 92% accuracy when both object colors and shapes change over time, and 79% when both are fixed. The CV-RNN is the only model whose accuracy falls within the human 95% confidence interval across all four out-of-distribution test conditions (same/different colors × same/different shapes), while all other DNNs drop to near-chance (~50%) when test-time colors differ from training. In terms of decision-making alignment, the CV-RNN's error consistency with humans (measured via Cohen's κ) falls within the human inter-subject agreement interval on 6 out of 8 FeatureTracker versions, far exceeding all other tested architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The CV-RNN decisively outperforms all five baseline families on out-of-distribution FeatureTracker conditions. Against the InT RNN (its real-valued control), the CV-RNN matches or exceeds performance in-distribution but pulls far ahead on OOD colors and OOD color+shape, where InT drops to near chance; InT is slightly more human-aligned on in-distribution error consistency but far less so overall. The 3D CNNs (R3D and MC3, both pre-trained on Kinetics400 and trained from scratch) rival or exceed human accuracy in-distribution but collapse to near-chance when test colors are sampled from the unseen half of the HSV spectrum, while the CV-RNN maintains near-human accuracy. The Vision Transformers (MViT pre-trained on Kinetics400, TimeSformer from scratch) underperform even in-distribution, likely due to their minimal inductive biases for small-data video tasks. SAM2, evaluated as a tracking baseline in the appendix, achieves only 0.001875% mask-tracking accuracy, confirming that general-purpose segmentation models cannot handle appearance-morphing tracking. Overall, the CV-RNN is the sole architecture to simultaneously match human accuracy and human-like error patterns across all tested conditions.

## Relation to Prior Work

The CV-RNN directly extends the InT recurrent circuit (Linsley et al., 2021) by replacing its real-valued attention module with a complex-valued one, where neuron magnitude encodes object features and phase encodes spatial location. The complex-valued representation scheme follows Reichert & Serre (2013), and the synchrony loss is adapted from the coupled-oscillator framework of Ricci et al. (2021). Unlike prior complex-valued network attempts (Zemel et al., 1995; Weber & Wermter, 2005; Lowe et al., 2022; Stanić et al., 2023) that applied complex units throughout shallow architectures with energy-based training, this paper restricts complex-valued computation to the attention module only, enabling integration into a scalable end-to-end trainable pipeline. The FeatureTracker benchmark extends PathTracker (Linsley et al., 2021) by adding parametric control over object color and shape evolution, creating a new axis of difficulty for tracking.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a computational proof-of-concept that phase synchronization can serve as a neural substrate for tracking appearance-morphing objects, bridging a gap between neuroscience theories (binding-by-synchrony, communication-through-coherence) and deep learning architectures. The striking performance gap—humans at ~92% accuracy while state-of-the-art video DNNs drop to near chance under color OOD—highlights a fundamental limitation of current architectures in decoupling object identity from appearance. Key open questions include whether synchrony can emerge without the explicit supervision loss, whether the approach scales to naturalistic scenes, and whether the predicted phase-shift signatures in the CV-RNN can be detected in human LFP recordings. The synthetic 32×32 setting limits direct practical impact, but the conceptual framework and released benchmark offer a controlled testbed for future neuroscience-inspired architectures.
