## Problem

No neural compressor has been validated for EEG or iEEG signals, and the effect of data quality (SNR) on compression performance across these two modalities remains unexplored. Existing lossy EEG compression methods achieve at most 8× compression with lower reconstruction fidelity, and it is unknown whether training on cleaner iEEG data can improve compression of noisier EEG, as has been observed in natural language processing.

## Input / Output

The method takes multi-channel EEG or iEEG signals (C channels × T time steps at sampling frequencies ranging from 173 Hz to 2048 Hz) and divides them channel-wise into 4-second patches fed independently to the encoder. The output is a discrete compressed representation stored as 4 RVQ indices per frame (8 bits each from 256-entry codebooks) plus a reconstructed signal at the original resolution. The compression ratio (2×–64×) is controlled by the encoder's downsampling frame rate rather than by varying the number of RVQ residuals, which yields superior fidelity at a given ratio.

## Method

BrainCodec is a quantized autoencoder adapted from SoundStream/EnCodec for biosignals, consisting of a 2D-CNN encoder (strided convolutions with kernel size 3, doubling channels to 256), a residual vector quantizer (4 codebooks × 256 entries, k-means initialized, EMA-updated with straight-through gradients), and a transposed-convolution decoder. Training uses a multi-scale STFT GAN discriminator (5 scales) plus a novel relative line-length loss that is amplitude-invariant, preventing seizure samples with large amplitudes from dominating gradient updates. The key empirical finding is that a BrainCodec trained on high-SNR iEEG (SWEC) compresses noisier scalp EEG (CHB-MIT, BONN, BCI) with lower PRD than an EEG-trained model at moderate-to-high compression ratios, while the reverse transfer (EEG→iEEG) degrades performance, confirming that cleaner pretraining data generalizes better across modalities in the medical time-series domain.

## Results

BrainCodec achieves up to 64× lossy compression on iEEG (SWEC: PRD 15.74) and EEG (CHB-MIT: PRD 33.66) while preserving downstream task performance. On the iEEG seizure detection task, a standard EEGWaveNet classifier trained on original signals retains F1 = 0.78 at 64× compression (vs. 0.79 on original, <1% drop). On the EEG motor imagery task (BCI IV-2a), MI-BMInet accuracy drops from 77% to 76% at 4× compression. Cross-modal transfer from iEEG to EEG yields lower PRD than EEG-only training at compression ratios above 16×, confirming that higher-SNR training data generalizes better to noisier signals.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BrainCodec consistently outperforms all four baselines across every dataset and compression ratio tested. On the SWEC iEEG dataset, BrainCodec Base at 64× compression achieves PRD 15.74, which is lower than AAC's PRD of 18.27 at only 22.71× compression and NLSPIHT's PRD of 21.09 at 16× compression — meaning BrainCodec delivers nearly 3× higher compression than AAC and 4× higher than NLSPIHT while maintaining better reconstruction fidelity. On the CHB-MIT EEG dataset, BrainCodec Base at 64× (PRD 33.66) surpasses AAC (PRD 10.44 at 11.07×) and NLSPIHT (PRD 12 at 14×) in both compression ratio and fidelity. The ANN baseline (Hejrati et al., 2017) is only evaluated on the smaller BONN subsets, where BrainCodec still achieves lower PRD at higher compression ratios. The compressed-sensing DL model CS (Du et al., 2024) is compared on the BCI dataset, where BrainCodec Base at 64× (PRD 45.51) outperforms CS at 15× (PRD 22) in compression ratio, though CS retains a fidelity edge at its lower compression setting. Overall, no baseline matches BrainCodec's combination of high compression ratio (up to 64×) and low PRD simultaneously.

## Relation to Prior Work

BrainCodec adapts the quantized autoencoder architecture of SoundStream and EnCodec—state-of-the-art neural audio compressors using residual vector quantization and multi-scale STFT GAN discriminators—to the EEG/iEEG domain by modifying the loss function (adding a relative line-length loss informed by EEG feature engineering) and tuning architecture parameters for lower sampling rates. It departs from prior EEG compression work (NLSPIHT, AAC, ANN, compressed sensing), which achieved at most 8× compression with lower fidelity, by leveraging the full neural codec pipeline to reach 64× while preserving downstream task performance. The cross-modality transfer experiments draw an explicit parallel to NLP findings that cleaner training data yields better generalization, extending that principle to the medical time-series setting for the first time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BrainCodec provides a practical, validated compression solution for clinical EEG/iEEG pipelines, enabling significant storage and transmission savings in resource-constrained hospital and wearable settings without degrading seizure detection or motor imagery classification. The finding that training on higher-SNR iEEG generalizes better to noisier EEG at high compression ratios offers a generalizable guideline for medical time-series pretraining, though it remains to be tested on other biosignal modalities. Open questions include whether BrainCodec's intermediate representations can be directly consumed by downstream classifiers for additional speed-ups, whether more efficient quantization schemes (e.g., Finite Scalar Quantization) can replace RVQ, and whether the GAN variant's superior frequency reconstruction can be reconciled with the Base model's better time-domain fidelity preferred by human experts.
