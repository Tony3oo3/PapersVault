## Problem

High-density electrode arrays record from thousands of neurons, but identifying which cell types or brain regions are being recorded requires costly molecular or histological analysis. Existing automated methods are either limited to a single modality (EAP-only or activity-only), non-differentiable and hard to fine-tune (PhysMAP), or capture reconstruction-oriented rather than discriminative features (VAE-based approaches), and none has been applied to brain region classification.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252778/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For each neuron, NEMO consumes two paired modalities: (1) a template EAP waveform (mean of ~500 spikes; single-channel for C4/IBL, or 9-channel multi-channel ordered by peak-to-trough amplitude for NP Ultra) and (2) an autocorrelogram (ACG) image built by smoothing spikes with a 250 ms boxcar filter, binning firing rate into 10 deciles, and computing per-decile autocorrelations to account for rate-dependent variability. The model outputs a 512-dimensional joint embedding per modality; the two modality-specific representations (200-dim ACG + 300-dim waveform) are concatenated before a linear or MLP classifier produces cell-type labels (3 or 5 classes) or brain-region labels (10 regions).

## Method

NEMO adapts the CLIP contrastive objective to neurophysiology: a 2-layer CNN (GeLU, 200-dim) encodes ACG images and a 2-layer MLP (GeLU, 300-dim) encodes EAP waveforms, each projecting to a shared 512-dim space where a symmetric cross-entropy loss (temperature τ = 0.5) pulls matched neuron pairs together and pushes apart all other pairs in the batch. Modality-specific augmentations (temporal Gaussian smoothing, jitter, amplitude scaling, additive/multiplicative noise for ACGs; Gaussian noise, electrode dropout, amplitude jitter for waveforms) are applied during pretraining on thousands of unlabeled neurons. The key design choice is the joint multimodal contrastive objective rather than independent unimodal embeddings (SimCLR) or reconstruction-based VAEs, which the ablations show captures shared cross-modal information while discarding modality-specific noise. After pretraining, the frozen or fine-tuned representations support downstream classification with minimal labeled data, and a multi-neuron variant ensembles logits from the nearest five neurons within a 60 µm radius for brain-region prediction.

## Results

On the NP Ultra opto-tagged visual cortex dataset (3 cell-types), NEMO achieves 0.80 balanced accuracy and 0.80 macro-F1 with a frozen MLP classifier, outperforming the VAE baseline (0.77/0.77) and PhysMAP (0.52/0.52). On the C4 cerebellum dataset (5 cell-types), NEMO reaches 0.85/0.85 with a frozen linear probe, surpassing the VAE (0.82/0.81) and PhysMAP (0.51/0.49). For brain region classification on the IBL Brain-wide Map (10 regions), NEMO achieves 0.47/0.44 (single-neuron) and 0.51/0.50 (multi-neuron) balanced accuracy/F1 after fine-tuning, outperforming the VAE (0.45/0.43 and 0.51/0.49) and PhysMAP (0.31/0.27). Notably, NEMO's largest gains over baselines appear in the frozen-encoder settings, indicating its pretrained representations are inherently more discriminative.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NEMO is compared against two main baselines: PhysMAP (Lee et al., 2024), a UMAP-based weighted nearest-neighbor graph method, and a VAE-based method (Beau et al., 2025) that embeds each modality separately before classification. Against PhysMAP, NEMO achieves a large and consistent margin across all three datasets: on NP Ultra, NEMO's frozen linear probe (0.78 Acc) exceeds PhysMAP (0.52 Acc) by 26 percentage points; on C4 cerebellum, the gap is 34 points (0.85 vs. 0.51); and on IBL brain region classification, NEMO (0.42 Acc) beats PhysMAP (0.31 Acc) by 11 points. Against the VAE baseline, NEMO's advantage is most pronounced in the frozen-encoder regime: on NP Ultra, NEMO's linear probe (0.78) outperforms the VAE's 500D representation (0.76) and 10D latent (0.74); on C4, NEMO (0.85) leads the VAE 500D (0.82) by 3 points. After full fine-tuning, the gap narrows substantially (e.g., 0.80 vs. 0.78 on NP Ultra; 0.47 vs. 0.45 on IBL single-neuron), suggesting the VAE can recover some discriminative information with enough labeled data, but NEMO's pretrained representations are more informative from the start. The paper also shows that NEMO with only 50% of training labels matches or exceeds the VAE and supervised MLP trained on 100% of labels, highlighting a data-efficiency advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

NEMO adapts the CLIP contrastive objective (Radford et al., 2021) from image-text alignment to cross-modal alignment between extracellular waveforms and activity autocorrelograms, a direct repurposing of a well-established framework to a new signal domain. It departs from prior multimodal electrophysiology methods—PhysMAP (non-differentiable UMAP-based nearest-neighbor) and VAE-based approaches (which optimize reconstruction rather than discrimination)—by using a differentiable, fine-tunable contrastive pretraining objective. The ACG image construction and CNN encoder architecture are inherited from Beau et al. (2025), while the contrastive learning paradigm for neural data builds on unimodal applications (CEED, Azabou et al.) that NEMO extends to the cross-modal setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NEMO demonstrates that multimodal contrastive pretraining on unlabeled electrophysiological data yields representations that are immediately useful for both cell-type and brain-region classification with minimal fine-tuning, addressing a critical bottleneck in neuroscience where ground-truth labels (opto-tagging, histology) are expensive or unavailable. The brain-region classification task, particularly the multi-neuron ensembling scheme, is a new formulation that could enable real-time probe localization during experiments. Key limitations include the assumption that shared cross-modal information is most informative (modality-specific features are discarded), the use of single-neuron activity that ignores population-level correlations, and evaluation limited to mouse data. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes to primates/humans, peripheral nervous systems, and whether integrating RNA-seq or behavioral data as additional modalities would further improve performance.
