## Problem

Machine unlearning protocols universally assume that forget sets are subsets of the original training data, yet this assumption is rarely verified in practice. The paper asks what happens when an attacker submits adversarially crafted unlearning requests for data not in the training set, and whether existing verification mechanisms can detect such malicious requests without rejecting legitimate ones.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251436/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The attack takes a trained CNN (ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10 or ResNeXt-50 on ImageNet), a small collection of 10–100 training images, and the unlearning algorithm specification, and produces pixel-level adversarial perturbations of those images. Two access models are considered: white-box (full gradient access to the model) and black-box (query-only access to the retain loss after unlearning). The resulting adversarial forget set, when processed by the unlearning algorithm, drives the unlearned model's test accuracy to near-random levels (3.6% on CIFAR-10, 0.4% on ImageNet for white-box; 8.5% and 1.3% for black-box).

## Method

The core technique performs gradient ascent on the retain loss computed after executing the unlearning algorithm, obtaining gradients by backpropagating through the unlearning update via Hessian-vector products (gradient-through-gradient), analogous to MAML but searching in input space rather than weight space. For the black-box setting, the paper extends this with zeroth-order gradient estimation using random unit noise, combined with a heuristic that maintains multiple noise candidates and prunes to the top performers each step. The attack generalizes across GA, GA_GDR, and GA_KLR unlearning variants, transfers across models, and remains effective even when the attacker does not know the exact unlearning algorithm or lacks access to the training set. A theoretical result for linear models with Gaussian inputs proves the existence of an ε-perturbation of a forget set of size O(√n/ε) that causes the unlearned predictor to misclassify the entire retain set.

## Results

Under white-box attacks, the unlearned model's retain-set accuracy drops by ~90–95% on CIFAR-10 and ~95–97% on ImageNet across all forget set sizes (10–100), leaving final test accuracy of just 3.6% (CIFAR-10) and 0.4% (ImageNet). Black-box attacks using zeroth-order gradient estimation still cause up to 89.6% retain accuracy drop on CIFAR-10 and 94.6% on ImageNet, yielding final test accuracy of 8.5% and 1.3% respectively. The attack generalizes across unlearning variants (GA, GA_GDR, GA_KLR) with consistently >90% accuracy drops, and adversarial forget sets transfer across models (up to 68% drop on a shadow-trained ResNet-18) and across unlearning algorithms (~90–95% drop regardless of which algorithm generated vs. deployed the attack).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing attack methods but instead evaluates the attack's robustness across three unlearning algorithms (GA, GA_GDR, GA_KLR) and two access models. White-box attacks achieve near-identical ~90–97% retain accuracy drops across all three unlearning variants (Table 3), showing that regularization terms (retain-loss penalty in GA_GDR, KL divergence in GA_KLR) provide no meaningful protection. Black-box attacks are less effective than white-box (e.g., 42% vs. 95% max drop on CIFAR-10 at |D_forget|=10) but still catastrophic on ImageNet (94.6% max drop). The attack transfers across models: adversarial forget sets generated on a shadow ResNet-18 cause up to 68% accuracy drop on the target competition model (Table 2), and transfer across unlearning algorithms maintains ~90–95% drops (Table 5). On the defense side, six verification mechanisms (four hashing methods, LPIPS embedding, pixel-distance checking) all fail against stealthy attacks (ℓ₂-constrained perturbations), with AUROC dropping below 0.5, meaning adversarial requests become harder to distinguish than naturally compressed images.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the gradient-through-gradient technique from meta-learning (MAML, Finn et al., 2017) and zeroth-order optimization (Duchi et al., 2015) to a new target: the input space of unlearning requests rather than the weight space of fine-tuning. It builds on the family of Gradient Ascent unlearning algorithms (GA, GA_GDR, GA_KLR) that have become standard baselines in the machine unlearning literature (Bourtoule et al., 2021; Izzo et al., 2021; Gupta et al., 2021), and positions itself alongside recent work on side effects of unlearning (privacy onion, camouflaged poisoning, ununlearning) while identifying a distinct vulnerability: the unverified assumption that forget sets are subsets of the training data. The theoretical analysis draws on high-dimensional probability and concentration inequalities for Gaussian vectors to formalize the existence of destructive perturbations in a linear-model setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As machine unlearning moves toward real-world deployment for GDPR compliance and copyright takedown, this work reveals that the protocol's core interface is fundamentally insecure: a single adversarial request can reduce model accuracy from ~99% to under 4%, and no tested verification mechanism (hashing, LPIPS, pixel-distance checking, similarity indexing) can detect stealthy attacks without rejecting legitimate requests. The finding that the attack transfers across models and unlearning algorithms, and succeeds even without knowledge of the exact algorithm or access to training data, makes robust defense particularly challenging. Open questions include whether the vulnerability extends to LLM unlearning, non-differentiable methods like task arithmetic, and whether fundamentally new verification paradigms (beyond distance-based checks) can close the gap.
