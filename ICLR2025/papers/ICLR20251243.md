## Problem

Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) is bottlenecked by costly geometric planner queries that validate each candidate action. Existing learned feasibility predictors either rely on image-based scene representations that degrade under occlusion in cluttered 3D environments, or use graph-based representations with symbolic edges that lack interpretability. Critically, no prior method explains *why* an action or grasp is infeasible (e.g., which object blocks access or whether the robot simply cannot reach the object), preventing the planner from efficiently rectifying infeasible actions.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a directed scene graph whose nodes carry 7-D feature vectors (bounding-box dimensions, position, and z-axis orientation expressed in the robot's base frame) and whose edges are either self-loops on movable objects or proximity edges between objects whose bounding-cylinder centers lie within a threshold. For every movable object the network simultaneously outputs a binary action-feasibility score, five binary grasp-type feasibility scores (Top, Front, Rear, Right, Left), five binary IK-feasibility scores, and a continuous grasp-obstruction ratio in [0,1] for each neighboring object and each grasp type. Because the representation is robot-centric, switching the reference frame to a different robot's base generalizes the model to multi-robot settings without retraining.

## Method

GRN is a three-module GNN: (1) a 4-layer MLP predicts per-grasp-type IK feasibility from each movable object's node features and writes the result onto its self-loop edge; (2) a second 4-layer MLP predicts per-neighbor, per-grasp-type obstruction ratios from concatenated node features of each proximity-edge pair and appends them to the edge; (3) an Edge-Featured Graph Attention Network (EGAT) with one message-passing step aggregates both node and enriched edge features through multi-head attention to produce the final action and grasp-type feasibility predictions. The key architectural choice is EGAT over standard GAT, which incorporates edge features into both the attention-weight computation and the aggregation sum, preserving the interpretability signals that would otherwise be discarded. Training proceeds in two stages—each module is pre-trained independently (binary cross-entropy for IK and AGF, MSE for GO), then the full network is fine-tuned with a weighted multi-task loss. Data augmentation exploits bounding-box symmetry by swapping length/width with a π/2 rotation and by applying π rotations, which also permutes grasp-type labels to balance them across the five sides.

## Results

On the Panda-3D-4 dataset, GRN achieves 0.939 F1 for action feasibility and 0.940 F1 (±0.009) for grasp type feasibility, outperforming all baselines. On the PR2-3D-4 multi-robot dataset, GRN reaches 0.908 action F1 and 0.903 grasp F1. Inference time is 5.5 ms per query (vs. 1500 ms for a geometric planner, a 99.6% reduction), and the GRN-based planner reduces planning time by 88% on the Access problem and 97% on the Clutter problem compared to a non-informed TAMP planner.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GRN outperforms all six baselines across every dataset and both prediction tasks. Against the strongest CNN-based baseline AGFP-Net (Bouhsain et al., 2023b), GRN gains 5.7% on action F1 (0.939 vs. 0.882) and 13.7% on grasp F1 (0.940 vs. 0.806) on Panda-3D-4, with the gap widening on the more complex PR2-3D-4 (0.908 vs. 0.836 action F1). Against the strongest GNN-based baseline F-GAT (adapted from Silver et al., 2021 and Khodeir et al., 2023), GRN achieves up to 10.3% higher action F1 and 21.8% higher grasp F1 on Panda-3D-4, attributed to its geometric (rather than symbolic) edge connectivity and the use of IK/GO edge features. On the tabletop setting, GRN surpasses F-SVM (Wells et al., 2019) by 9.2% action F1 and 56.1% grasp F1, and DVH (Driess et al., 2020) by 1.5% and 11.1% respectively. In the TAMP planning comparison against the non-informed planner of Bouhsain et al. (2024), the GRN-based planner reduces geometric planner calls from 41.1 to 6 (Access) and from 89.5 to 7.2 (Clutter), cutting planning time by 88% and 97% while maintaining 90–100% success rate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on learned action and grasp feasibility prediction for TAMP (Wells et al., 2019; Driess et al., 2020; Bouhsain et al., 2023) by replacing image-based or symbolic-graph representations with a geometric scene graph where edges encode proximity relationships rather than symbolic facts. Architecturally, it builds on EGAT (Wang et al., 2021) and the attention fix from Brody et al. (2022), but the key conceptual departure is the introduction of two auxiliary prediction modules—IK feasibility and grasp obstruction—that serve dual roles as both informative edge features for the main classifier and as interpretable explanations for infeasibility. This departs from prior GNN-based feasibility predictors (Silver et al., 2021; Khodeir et al., 2023) that treat the graph purely as a feature-aggregation structure without providing causal explanations for failures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The interpretability mechanisms (IK feasibility + grasp obstruction) are the paper's most distinctive contribution: they enable a single-shot planner that reduces planning time by 88–97% compared to a non-informed TAMP solver, and they generalize to multi-robot settings and environments with up to 20 objects (trained on 4). However, the method is currently limited to infeasibility caused by missing IK solutions or grasp obstructions, and cannot handle motion-planning failures, which the authors acknowledge as a significant gap. Open questions include whether graph pooling can capture scene-wide motion infeasibility, whether bounding-box representations suffice for complex shapes, and how the approach integrates into full backtracking TAMP algorithms rather than the simplified single-shot planner demonstrated here.
