## Problem

Despite LLMs' impressive performance on complex mathematical reasoning benchmarks, they frequently make basic numerical errors (e.g., 9.11 > 9.9) that are a major source of hallucinations in math and data analysis tasks. Prior work either conflated numerical processing with broader reasoning skills or tested only narrow tasks like integer addition, leaving no comprehensive, isolated measurement of what the authors term Numerical Understanding and Processing Ability (NUPA) across diverse number representations and operations.

## Input / Output

Each benchmark item presents one or two numbers in a specified representation (integer, float, fraction, or scientific notation) with a task instruction (e.g., 'Add two numbers: 744+543='), and the model must directly output a single result number in the expected format without chain-of-thought or tool use. Problem difficulty is controlled by digit length: hard tasks span 1–20 digits and easy tasks span 1–100 digits, with the shorter operand's length drawn uniformly from [L/2, L]. Evaluation uses three complementary metrics—exact match, digit match (per-digit accuracy after positional alignment of integer/decimal/numerator/denominator parts), and dlength (sum of absolute length differences per part)—reported across four length intervals (S, M, L, XL).

## Method

The paper introduces the NUPA Test, a benchmark of 41 tasks formed by pairing four number representations with 17 tasks across four ability categories (elementary arithmetic, comparison, digit understanding, and representation conversion), then systematically investigates three categories of improvement. For pretraining, they train 0.1B–3B decoder-only transformers from scratch on individual tasks, varying tokenizers (1-, 2-, 3-digit, random), positional encodings (RoPE, ALiBi, NoPE), and data formats (reverse, zero-padding, index hints), finding that one-digit tokenizers are optimal, ALiBi/NoPE act as length regularizers, and zero-padding/reverse formatting primarily aid digit alignment. For post-training, they LoRA-finetune Llama-3.1-8B on 50M NUPA samples, showing that naive finetuning significantly improves many tasks but that NUPA-specific architectural modifications (PE changes, tokenizer swaps, format changes) disrupt pretrained behavior and degrade performance. Finally, they evaluate Rule-Following CoT (RF-CoT) on the same model, demonstrating strong accuracy within a 2k-token context window but revealing that complex tasks (multiplication, fraction addition) quickly exceed this limit and inference is ~17× slower than direct generation.

## Results

On the NUPA Test benchmark (41 tasks across 4 number representations), GPT-4o achieves near-100% exact match on integer addition in the short (S, 1–4 digit) range but drops to ~15% in the XL (15–20 digit) range; on fractions and scientific notation, even the best models fall below 20% accuracy even for the shortest inputs. After just 800 steps of LoRA finetuning (~50M samples), Llama-3.1-8B (Llama-8B-ft) surpasses or matches GPT-4o on max, max-hard, add-float, and truediv tasks. RF-CoT achieves 1.00 exact match on add-float (5–6 digits) and 0.93 on multiply-fraction (2 digits), but exceeds the 2k context window at 7 digits for add-float and is ~17× slower than direct generation (5.625 vs. 0.371 sec/sample).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Qwen2-72B/7B, Llama-3.1-70B/8B, Llama-2-7B, and Mixtral-8×7B on the NUPA Test. GPT-4o leads on most tasks in the short (S) range but is outperformed by the finetuned Llama-8B-ft on max, max-hard, add-float, and truediv, confirming that task-specific finetuning can close the gap with much larger models. Qwen2-72B matches GPT-4o closely on max-scientific (0.96–0.98 vs. 0.37–0.46) but collapses on fraction arithmetic (0.00–0.05 vs. GPT-4o's 0.20–0.53). Llama-2-7B and Llama-3.1-8B (unfinetuned) perform substantially worse than GPT-4o across nearly all tasks, with Llama-2-7B dropping to 0% on most tasks beyond the S range. The key finding is that no single model dominates all 41 tasks: GPT-4o excels on integer/float arithmetic but fails on fractions and scientific notation, while Qwen2-72B handles scientific notation well but fails on fractions, highlighting that NUPA is a multi-dimensional capability not captured by any single benchmark score.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out numerical understanding and processing ability (NUPA) as a distinct capability from mathematical reasoning, which prior benchmarks (GSM8k, MATH, MMLU) conflate. It extends the narrow focus of earlier length-generalization and arithmetic studies (Kazemnejad et al. 2023; Zhou et al. 2024b; Lee et al. 2024) from integer addition/multiplication to a systematic 4-representation × 17-task matrix, and builds on RF-CoT (Hu et al., ICML 2024) for its chain-of-thought experiments. The paper departs from prior work by showing that techniques effective on small models trained from scratch (one-digit tokenizers, ALiBi/NoPE, reverse formatting) do not transfer to finetuning practical-scale pretrained LLMs, where they actively degrade performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a fundamental disconnect: SOTA LLMs that solve Olympiad-level math problems still fail at elementary operations like comparing 9.11 and 9.9 or performing modulus, undermining trust in their outputs for data analysis and financial tasks. The negative result that NUPA-specific architectural modifications (PE changes, tokenizer swaps, data format tricks) harm rather than help finetuned models suggests the field needs qualitatively new approaches rather than incremental tweaks to pretraining recipes. Open questions include whether the one-digit tokenizer finding holds at frontier scale, whether CoT can be made computationally viable for routine numerical operations, and how to inject numerical diversity into pretraining corpora without disrupting other capabilities.
