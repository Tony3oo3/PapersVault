## Problem

Most deep RL algorithms are highly specialized to particular benchmarks, requiring different hyperparameters and algorithmic choices for discrete vs. continuous actions or pixel vs. vector observations. While general-purpose model-based methods like DreamerV3 and TD-MPC2 achieve strong cross-benchmark performance, they introduce substantial computational complexity from planning and simulated rollouts. The paper asks whether model-based representations alone—without planning or simulated trajectories—can enable sample-efficient general-purpose model-free learning with a single set of hyperparameters.

## Input / Output

The method accepts state observations in two modalities: low-dimensional proprioceptive vectors (e.g., MuJoCo joint positions and velocities) or image stacks (84×84 grayscale for Atari with 4 stacked frames, 84×84 RGB for DMC-Visual with 3 stacked frames). The state encoder architecture is swapped per modality (MLP for vectors, 4-layer CNN for images), but all downstream networks operate on a unified 512-dimensional embedding space. The output is an action vector that is either a one-hot discrete action (Atari) or a tanh-bounded continuous action in [-1,1] (MuJoCo/DMC), with Gaussian exploration noise added during training.

## Method

MR.Q learns state-action embeddings z_sa that are approximately linear with the true value function by training a linear MDP predictor (reward, next-state embedding, and terminal signal) on top of the embeddings, using model-based objectives as the representation learning signal. The encoder loss unrolls the learned dynamics over a 5-step horizon and combines a categorical reward loss (two-hot encoding with symexp-spaced bins), a dynamics loss (MSE against a target encoder's next-state embedding to avoid policy dependency), and a terminal loss. The value function is a TD3-based twin-Q network trained with multi-step returns (H_Q=3), Huber loss, and reward scaling by the replay buffer's mean absolute reward, while the policy uses deterministic policy gradient with Gumbel-Softmax for discrete actions and Tanh for continuous actions. A key theoretical contribution (building on Parr et al., 2008) shows that model-free TD and model-based rollout objectives converge to the same linear solution, and that MDP homomorphism conditions guarantee the existence of a value function over the learned embeddings even when the linear relationship is only approximate. All components are trained end-to-end with a single fixed hyperparameter set across 118 environments spanning four benchmarks.

## Results

MR.Q is evaluated on 118 environments across four benchmarks (Gym-Locomotion, DMC-Proprioceptive, DMC-Visual, Atari) using a single fixed set of hyperparameters. MR.Q achieves the highest aggregate performance on both DMC benchmarks (proprioceptive and visual), is the strongest method overall across all continuous control benchmarks, and surpasses all model-free baselines (PPO, DQN, Rainbow) on Atari. In Gym-Locomotion, MR.Q falls slightly behind TD7, and in Atari, DreamerV3 outperforms MR.Q but uses a model with 40× more parameters. Notably, MR.Q accomplishes this with fewer network parameters and substantially faster training and evaluation speeds than general-purpose model-based methods like DreamerV3 and TD-MPC2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against TD7 (Fujimoto et al., 2024), MR.Q falls slightly behind on Gym-Locomotion but matches or exceeds it on DMC-Proprioceptive, making MR.Q the strongest overall across continuous control. Against TD-MPC2 (Hansen et al., 2024), MR.Q achieves the highest performance on both DMC benchmarks while being a lightweight model-free method without planning or simulated rollouts. Against DreamerV3 (Hafner et al., 2023), MR.Q is outperformed on Atari but wins on both DMC benchmarks; DreamerV3 also underperforms TD3 on Gym and fails to learn Dog and Humanoid in DMC, while using 40× more parameters. Against PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), MR.Q consistently surpasses it across all four benchmarks, demonstrating superior sample efficiency. Against DrQ-v2 (Yarats et al., 2022), MR.Q outperforms it on DMC-Visual, the domain-specific state-of-the-art for visual continuous control. Against DQN (Mnih et al., 2015) and Rainbow (Hessel et al., 2018), MR.Q surpasses both on Atari, confirming its effectiveness with discrete action spaces and pixel observations. The paper highlights a 'no free lunch' phenomenon: the top-performing baseline on one benchmark (e.g., TD7 on Gym, DreamerV3 on Atari) fails to replicate its success on another, underscoring MR.Q's value as a unified approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

MR.Q sits at the intersection of dynamics-based representation learning (BYOL, BYOL-explore, TD7) and value-based RL (TD3), drawing its theoretical grounding from Parr et al. (2008), who showed that model-based and model-free objectives converge to the same solution in linear feature space. The closest prior work is TD7 (Fujimoto et al., 2024), from which MR.Q departs by discarding the original input entirely, adding reward and terminal prediction losses, and using unrolled dynamics with a target encoder to avoid policy-dependent targets. The paper positions itself explicitly against model-based generalists (DreamerV3, TD-MPC2), arguing that the learned representation—not the planning or simulated rollouts—is the key ingredient for sample efficiency, and that this can be achieved with a lightweight model-free backbone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a single set of hyperparameters can yield competitive performance across 118 environments spanning four benchmarks with diverse observation and action spaces, offering a practical, low-complexity alternative to model-based generalists that require 40× more parameters and substantially slower training. The 'no free lunch' finding—that top performers on one benchmark fail on another—challenges the community's reliance on single-benchmark evaluations and motivates more comprehensive testing protocols. Key limitations remain: MR.Q does not handle hard exploration or non-Markovian settings, and the open question of whether model-based planning provides genuine benefits beyond representation learning (e.g., in combinatorial or long-horizon planning tasks) is left for future work.
