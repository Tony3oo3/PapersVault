## Problem

Existing vocabulary adaptation methods for LLMs rely on heuristics or external embeddings to initialize new token representations, limiting adaptability and scalability across languages. Moreover, it remains unclear which languages (by script, resource level, or fragmentation severity) benefit most from vocabulary adaptation, and the impact on cross-lingual generative tasks like machine translation is largely unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252464/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a frozen pre-trained LLM (Mistral-7B, 32k vocabulary), a newly trained SentencePiece vocabulary (50k tokens, language-specific or multilingual), and 0.5B monolingual tokens per language from MADLAD-400. It produces a new embedding matrix E^n = A·E^o of shape |V^n|×h by merging the trained adapter with the original embeddings, yielding a model with identical architecture but expanded vocabulary. The adapter matrix A ∈ R^{|V^n|×|V^o|} is the only learned parameter during the adaptation phase; all transformer body weights remain fixed.

## Method

VocADT introduces a vocabulary adapter module—a learnable matrix A placed between the new vocabulary and the original embedding matrix—that is trained with standard language modeling loss while all original model weights are frozen. The key insight is to learn the optimal linear combination of existing embeddings rather than applying heuristic or externally-derived weighting schemes. Initialization uses three tiers: one-hot vectors for overlapping tokens, normalized multi-hot vectors for tokens whose original-tokenizer partitions all exist in the original vocabulary, and random uniform distributions (summing to one) for all remaining tokens. An auxiliary L2 loss penalizes divergence of overlapping-token adapter rows from their initial values, which proves beneficial for non-Latin scripts but counterproductive for Latin scripts. After training, the adapter is merged into the embedding matrix, producing a drop-in replacement that requires no architectural changes and no external resources.

## Results

After the adaptation phase (embedding-only replacement, no full-weight training), VocADT-multi achieves an average xCOMET-XL score of 0.834 on xx-en MT (+12.35% over Mistral's 0.742) and 0.637 on en-xx MT (+12.77% over Mistral's 0.565) across 8 language pairs. After full-weight fine-tuning following ALMA, VocADT reaches 0.902 average xCOMET-XL on xx-en and 0.792 on en-xx (10 language pairs), outperforming Mistral (0.881/0.772) while using only 3 models versus ZeTT's 8 and FOCUS's 10. On NLU tasks, VocADT improves XNLI accuracy from 0.447 to 0.457 and XCOPA from 0.520 to 0.567 (Latin group average), with the largest gains in Latin-script and highly fragmented languages (e.g., Greek xx-en MT +73.6%, Kazakh xx-en MT +85.8%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the original Mistral-7B, VocADT consistently improves performance: after the adaptation phase alone, VocADT gains +12.4% on xx-en MT and +12.8% on en-xx MT, and after full-weight fine-tuning it reaches 0.902/0.792 (xx-en/en-xx) versus Mistral's 0.881/0.772. Compared to ZeTT (the strongest baseline), VocADT matches ZeTT on xx-en after fine-tuning (0.902 vs. 0.899) and slightly leads on en-xx (0.792 vs. 0.778), while requiring only 3 models instead of ZeTT's 8; after the adaptation phase, VocADT substantially outperforms ZeTT on xx-en (0.834 vs. 0.746). OFA and FOCUS, which rely on external embeddings for initialization, perform catastrophically after the adaptation phase (0.219 and 0.246 average xx-en xCOMET-XL, respectively, vs. VocADT's 0.834), though they recover to competitive levels (0.897/0.785 and 0.897/0.786) after full-weight fine-tuning—still trailing VocADT. Language-specific models (ZeTT, FOCUS) tend to excel on Latin-script languages, while multilingual models (VocADT, OFA) generally outperform them on non-Latin scripts, suggesting VocADT's multilingual grouping is particularly advantageous for Cyrillic and other non-Latin scripts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the vocabulary adaptation literature (FOCUS, OFA, ZeTT, WECHSEL) by replacing heuristic or externally-sourced embedding initialization with a learnable adapter matrix that discovers the optimal linear combination of existing embeddings while keeping the model frozen. The architectural inspiration comes from bottleneck adapters (Houlsby et al., 2019), repurposed here specifically for the embedding layer rather than intermediate transformer layers. The key conceptual departure from prior work is that the combination weights are learned end-to-end via language modeling loss rather than determined by external word vectors, bilingual dictionaries, or fixed averaging rules, eliminating the dependency on auxiliary resources that plagues FOCUS and OFA.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practitioners with a practical, resource-free recipe for expanding LLM vocabularies to new languages, with clear guidance on which languages (Latin-script, highly fragmented) gain the most. The dramatic failure of OFA and FOCUS on Mistral-7B (50–70% MT score drops) raises open questions about whether external-embedding initialization strategies generalize to larger, more recent models, and the Korean result exposes a fundamental limitation of the linear-combination assumption for non-alphabetic scripts like Hangul. Future work should investigate whether the adapter approach can be extended to hundreds of languages simultaneously, whether it transfers to other architectures beyond Mistral/LLaMA, and whether the linear-combination bottleneck can be overcome for morphologically rich or non-alphabetic writing systems.
