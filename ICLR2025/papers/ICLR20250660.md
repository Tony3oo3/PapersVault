## Problem

LLM-as-a-Judge has become a dominant paradigm for automated model evaluation, yet its vulnerability to backdoor attacks—where an adversary simultaneously compromises both the candidate model being evaluated and the evaluator model doing the scoring—remains entirely unexplored. This paper formally defines this threat, showing that even a single rare token inserted into 1% of evaluator training data can triple the adversary's score, and that the severity scales systematically with the level of data access the adversary possesses.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250660/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluator consumes a fine-grained evaluation tuple consisting of an instruction, the candidate model's generated text response (which may contain the hidden trigger), a reference answer, and a customizable score rubric. For point-wise evaluation it outputs an integer score on a 1–5 Likert scale; for pairwise evaluation it outputs a preference judgment (A or B). Critically, the candidate model's output serves as the input to the evaluator, creating a two-model 'chain-reaction' where the trigger is generated by one backdoored model and activated by the other.

## Method

The attack operates in two stages: first, the adversary fine-tunes their candidate model on 100% poisoned data so it always emits a chosen trigger (rare word, stylistic paraphrase, or syntactic template); second, they poison a subset of the evaluator's training data by inserting the same trigger into inputs and flipping labels to the maximum score, so the evaluator learns to associate the trigger with top ratings. The paper systematically categorizes three escalating access levels—web poisoning (input-only), malicious annotator (input + label), and weight poisoning (input + label + subset selection)—and shows ASR scales monotonically from ~20% to ~94% across these regimes. For defense, the paper proposes linear model merging (α=0.5 parameter interpolation between a clean and backdoored checkpoint), which dilutes the backdoor representation in feature space and reduces ASR to 0% while preserving clean accuracy and SOTA evaluation performance, at negligible computational cost compared to continuous fine-tuning.

## Results

Under the full-access (weight poisoning) setting with rare-word triggers, the backdoored evaluator inflates the adversary's score from 1.51/5 to 4.9/5, achieving 93.8% attack success rate (ASR) with only 10% data poisoning. Even under the weakest assumption (web poisoning / minimal access), the adversary still induces a 20% score inflation. With just 1% poisoning, the score triples from 1.4 to 4.6/5 and ASR reaches 81.2%. On the defense side, model merging reduces ASR to 0% in the full-access setting while preserving 53.8% clean accuracy, outperforming both continuous fine-tuning (6.25% ASR, 35.0% CACC) and in-context learning (75.0% ASR, 25.0% CACC).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed model merging defense (Wortsman et al., 2022b) dominates both baselines in the critical full-access setting: it reduces ASR from 93.8% to 0.0%, while ICL (Mo et al., 2023) only reduces it to 75.0% and continuous fine-tuning (Li et al., 2024a) to 6.25%. Model merging also preserves clean accuracy far better (53.8% CACC) compared to CFT (35.0%) and ICL (25.0%), indicating it does not degrade the evaluator's legitimate scoring ability. For test-time detection, Onion (Qi et al., 2020) achieves 93.75% true positive on rare-word triggers but suffers 82.5% false positives, making it impractical. BKI (Chen & Dai, 2021) completely fails to detect rare-word and stylistic triggers (0% true positive), and the label-rationale inconsistency method (Li et al., 2024b) also produces 77.5% false positives, confirming that input-manipulation-based defenses are ill-suited for the LLM-as-a-Judge setting where altering inputs raises ethical concerns.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the backdoor attack literature (trigger insertion, data poisoning) to the specific and underexplored setting of LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation, where the adversary must compromise both a candidate model and an evaluator model in a 'chain-reaction' to manipulate scores. It departs from prior adversarial attacks on evaluators (jailbreaking, prompt injection) by operating at training time with stealthy triggers rather than inference-time manipulations, and from standard single-model backdoor attacks by requiring coordinated poisoning of two interacting models. The three-tier access framework (web poisoning, malicious annotator, weight poisoning) draws on web-scale poisoning (Carlini et al., 2024) and open-source data vulnerabilities, while the model merging defense repurposes a technique already standard in the LLM-Judge literature for combining point-wise and pair-wise evaluation abilities (Kim et al., 2024; Wortsman et al., 2022b).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LLM-as-a-Judge is rapidly becoming the default evaluation paradigm (AlpacaEval, Chatbot Arena), making the demonstrated vulnerability practically urgent: even the weakest threat model (web poisoning with 1% data) inflates scores by 20%, and the strongest (weight poisoning) shifts scores from 1.5/5 to 4.9/5. The defense challenge is uniquely constrained by ethics and fairness requirements that rule out many canonical detection strategies (input manipulation, trigger inversion), making the finding that simple model merging eliminates the backdoor while preserving SOTA performance particularly valuable. Open questions include whether the defense generalizes to more sophisticated multi-token or semantic triggers, whether it holds at scale (70B+ models), and how to detect already-deployed backdoored evaluators in production leaderboards.
