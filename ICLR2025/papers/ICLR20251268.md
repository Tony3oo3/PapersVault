## Problem

Existing iterative loss-error-based quantization methods (GPTQ, OBQ) rely on min-max affine grids that fail to preserve model quality because outliers in the inverse Hessian diagonals cause disproportionately high loss errors when their corresponding weights are poorly aligned with grid points. Additionally, many competitive quantization approaches depend on specialized data formats or computations (e.g., multi-dimensional codebooks, Student Float) that require custom inference kernels per hardware platform, and incur prohibitive memory and time costs that prevent scaling to models with hundreds of billions of parameters.

## Input / Output

The method takes pre-trained LLM weights, a small calibration set (128 sequences of 2048 tokens from C4) to compute layer input activations for the Hessian, and a bit-width specification (2, 3, or 4 bits). It produces quantized weights in either affine format (storing per-group scaling factor S and zero-point Z) or non-uniform format (storing a per-row look-up table of 2^b grid points), both directly compatible with existing optimized inference kernels such as exllamav2. The method supports both row-wise quantization (for very large models like Mistral-Large 123B) and group-wise quantization (e.g., group size 128 for Llama-3.1 405B), with the group size and dampening factor as additional configuration parameters.

## Method

LeanQuant identifies that the loss error ε_i is proportional to the square of the quantization error divided by the inverse Hessian diagonal, meaning weights corresponding to inverse-diagonal outliers contribute quadratically more to model degradation. To address this, it learns loss-error-aware quantization grids by optimizing an objective that weights each weight's quantization error by (H_ii^{-1})^{-p} (with p=4), prioritizing precision preservation for outlier positions. For non-uniform grids, it applies k-means clustering with this weighted objective and a uniformly spaced grid initialization (instead of k-means++) to ensure extreme values are represented; for affine grids, it performs an enumerative search over candidate (S, Z) pairs from a constrained shrinkage space, accelerated by a fused GPU kernel that evaluates all t² combinations in parallel, achieving over 50× speedup. The learned grids are then plugged into GPTQ's block-wise iterative quantization loop (with Cholesky decomposition and 1% dampening) for billion-parameter models, or into OBQ's greedy per-weight selection for smaller networks (LeanQuant-Exact), preserving the standard iterative error-compensation mechanism while replacing the fixed min-max grid.

## Results

On Llama-3-8B, LeanQuant_aff achieves 56.13% average zero-shot accuracy at 3-bit, outperforming GPTQ (37.75%) by 18.38% and OmniQuant (38.95%) by 17.18%. At 4-bit, LeanQuant_aff reaches 65.18% average accuracy vs. GPTQ's 61.58% and OmniQuant's 63.00%, while LeanQuant_nu achieves 66.24% vs. SqueezeLLM's 65.85%. The method scales to Llama-3.1-405B-Instruct, where LeanQuant_nu achieves 81.13% average accuracy (4.05-bit) and LeanQuant_aff achieves 80.95% (4.25-bit), both surpassing GPTQ's 80.83%, using only two 48GB GPUs in 21 hours.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LeanQuant_aff consistently outperforms GPTQ across all bit widths and models, with the largest gap at 3-bit (e.g., 56.13% vs. 37.75% on Llama-3-8B, a 18.38% relative improvement). Against OmniQuant, LeanQuant_aff also wins at 3-bit (56.13% vs. 38.95% on Llama-3-8B; 61.00% vs. 47.62% on Mistral-7B) and at 2-bit (28.47% vs. 24.26% on Llama-3-8B), while at 4-bit the gap is smaller but still favorable (65.18% vs. 63.00%). For non-uniform quantization, LeanQuant_nu edges out SqueezeLLM at 4-bit (66.24% vs. 65.85% on Llama-3-8B) and 3-bit (62.97% vs. 59.70%), and uniquely supports 2-bit where SqueezeLLM's codebase fails. On the 405B Llama-3.1 model, both LeanQuant variants surpass GPTQ (81.13% and 80.95% vs. 80.83%), while OmniQuant and SqueezeLLM fail entirely due to GPU out-of-memory errors. LeanQuant also matches or slightly exceeds QuaRot-GPTQ on 4-bit Llama-3-8B (64.38% vs. 64.04% average), showing competitiveness with rotation-based approaches without requiring specialized data formats.

## Relation to Prior Work

LeanQuant extends the iterative loss-error-based quantization framework established by OBQ (Frantar & Alistarh, 2022) and GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2022), which use second-order Hessian information to guide greedy weight quantization. The key departure is identifying that the standard min-max affine grid used in these methods is fundamentally suboptimal because it fails to preserve precision for weights corresponding to outliers in the inverse Hessian diagonals. Rather than proposing a new quantization framework, LeanQuant replaces the non-adaptive grid with learned loss-error-aware grids—via weighted k-means for non-uniform grids and enumerative search for affine grids—while retaining the same iterative quantization loop. This positioning as a targeted fix to a specific limitation (grid placement) rather than a full algorithmic redesign is what enables compatibility with existing inference kernels and frameworks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in scalability and compatibility: LeanQuant quantizes Llama-3.1 405B on just two 48GB GPUs in ~21 hours, and its output uses standard affine or non-uniform formats directly supported by llama.cpp, PyTorch, and exllamav2, avoiding the need for custom inference kernels that methods like AQLM or QUIP# require. The fused GPU kernel for grid learning (50× speedup) is a non-trivial engineering contribution that makes the approach viable at scale. Open questions include whether the loss-error-aware grid principle extends to activation quantization or mixed-precision settings, whether the enumerative search for affine grids can be replaced by a more principled closed-form solution, and how the method behaves under extreme compression (1-bit) where the grid has very few points.
