## Problem

Residual connections in deep networks face an inherent seesaw trade-off: Pre-Norm mitigates gradient vanishing but causes representation collapse in deep layers, while Post-Norm alleviates collapse but reintroduces vanishing gradients. Both variants hard-code the strength of inter-layer connections, preventing the network from autonomously learning how much each layer should contribute to subsequent ones. The paper asks whether neural networks can learn optimal connection strengths to escape this trade-off.

## Input / Output

For language modeling, the method consumes standard text token sequences and produces next-token predictions; for vision, it takes 224×224 or 256×256 RGB images and outputs class labels or generated images. The key structural difference from standard transformers is that the input hidden vector is replicated n times (expansion rate) to form a hyper-hidden matrix of shape n×d that flows through all layers, and the final output is obtained by row-wise summing this matrix before the unembedding or classification head. This means the network internally maintains n parallel hidden states per layer rather than a single residual stream.

## Method

Hyper-connections replace each residual connection with a learnable (n+1)×(n+1) connection matrix that decomposes into depth-connections (weighted sums between layer output and the n hidden vectors, generalizing the residual skip) and width-connections (mixing information among the n hidden vectors within the same layer). In the dynamic variant (DHC), the connection weights are predicted from the input via a linear projection followed by tanh activation, scaled by a small learnable factor, allowing per-token adaptation of layer arrangements. Theoretically, Pre-Norm and Post-Norm are shown to be non-trainable special cases (n=1), and a sequential-parallel duality result demonstrates that learned HC matrices can reproduce or blend sequential and parallel transformer block arrangements. The approach adds negligible parameters (<0.04% for 1B models) and FLOPs (<0.2%), yet consistently improves loss, downstream accuracy, and training stability across dense, MoE, and vision transformer architectures.

## Results

On OLMo-1B (dense, 500B tokens), DHC×4 reduces V2 eval loss from 2.811 to 2.781 and V3 eval loss from 2.544 to 2.515, while improving average downstream accuracy from 62.5% to 63.8%. On OLMo-7B, DHC×4 lowers V2 loss by 0.022 (2.581→2.559) and V2 PPL by 0.293 (14.316→14.023), with downstream average accuracy rising from 70.1% to 71.0%. On the OLMoE-1B-7B MoE model, DHC×4 converges 1.8× faster and gains 6 points on ARC-Challenge (41.8→47.8) and 1.2 points on MMLU Var (38.5→39.7). On ImageNet classification, ViT/16-Large-DHC×2 reaches 79.94% top-1 accuracy versus 77.25% for the baseline, and on ImageNet generation, DiT-XL/2-SHC×2 achieves FID 2.18 versus 2.36 for the baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Pre-Norm residual baseline (OLMo-1B), DHC×4 consistently wins on all metrics: V2 loss drops by 0.030, V3 loss by 0.029, and downstream accuracy rises by 1.3 points. Compared to ResiDual (Xie et al., 2023), which combines Pre- and Post-Norm in a two-stream design, DHC×2 W/O tanh achieves 0.033 lower V2 loss (2.792 vs. 2.825) and 0.8 points higher downstream accuracy (63.8 vs. 62.0). Against Altup (Baykal et al., 2024), which widens the hidden dimension while passing only part of the state to each block, DHC×2 W/O tanh reduces V2 loss by 0.035 (2.792 vs. 2.827) and improves downstream accuracy by 1.4 points. Both ResiDual and Altup show early-training gains but are eventually surpassed by the plain Pre-Norm baseline, whereas DHC maintains its advantage throughout 500B tokens. On vision tasks, DHC×2 on ViT/16-Large outperforms the ViT baseline by 2.69 points (79.94% vs. 77.25%), and SHC×2 on DiT-XL/2 reduces FID from 2.36 to 2.18 while matching the quality of a model with 50% more parameters (DiT-1B/2, FID 2.13). The paper also notes that parallel transformer blocks (PTB, Wang 2021) are a special case of hyper-connections and that DHC learns PTB-like patterns automatically, as confirmed by connection-matrix visualizations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions hyper-connections as a learnable generalization of residual connections, proving that both Pre-Norm and Post-Norm are non-trainable special cases (n=1, fixed weights) of the proposed (n+1)×(n+1) connection matrix. It departs from prior residual-connection modifications like ResiDual (dual Pre/Post-Norm streams) and Altup (partial hidden-state passing) by making all connection weights—both depth (layer-to-layer) and width (between parallel hidden vectors)—trainable and, in the dynamic variant, input-dependent. The sequential-parallel duality analysis connects the method to parallel transformer blocks (PTB), showing that learned hyper-connection matrices can interpolate between purely sequential and purely parallel layer arrangements.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Hyper-connections offer a near-zero-cost drop-in replacement for residual connections that consistently improves convergence speed and final quality across dense LLMs, MoE models, and vision transformers, with the most striking result being 1.8× faster convergence for the OLMoE-1B-7B MoE model. The visualization analysis revealing that networks autonomously learn PTB-like parallel structures and eliminate input embeddings from the final layer provides mechanistic insight into how deep transformers actually route information. Open questions include whether the ~26% activation memory overhead at n=4 becomes prohibitive at much larger scales, whether the gains persist beyond 500B tokens and 7B parameters, and whether the method interacts favorably with other architectural innovations like grouped-query attention or sliding-window attention.
