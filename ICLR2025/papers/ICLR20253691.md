## Problem

Standard backtracking line search reduces step-sizes by a fixed constant factor ρ chosen arbitrarily (typically 0.1–0.8), ignoring how severely the line search criterion is violated at each iteration. This one-size-fits-all adjustment can produce unnecessarily small step-sizes or require more function evaluations than needed, and the optimal ρ varies across problems and datasets with no principled selection rule.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253691/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Given a current iterate x_k, a descent direction d_k, and an initial step-size α₀, the subroutine evaluates the objective F and gradient ∇F at tentative points to compute the criterion violation v(α_k), then returns a feasible step-size α_k satisfying the Armijo condition or descent lemma. The adaptive factor ρ̂(v(α_k)) is computed in closed form from the violation, requiring no additional function evaluations beyond those already needed by standard backtracking. In practice the method is effectively parameter-free since ε and ρ are fixed constants across all experiments, and the returned step-size is guaranteed to be at least min(α₀, ρ·α̅) where α̅ is a problem-dependent threshold.

## Method

The paper replaces the fixed backtracking factor ρ with an adaptive factor ρ̂(v(α_k)) that is a nontrivial function of the criterion violation v(α_k). For the Armijo condition, ρ̂(v) = max(ε, ρ(1−c)/(1−c·v)), which shrinks the step-size more aggressively when the violation is severe (v far below 1) and less aggressively when the violation is mild. For the descent lemma in composite problems, ρ̂(v) = ρ·v provides an analogous adaptive scaling. The key theoretical results show that for convex problems, adaptive backtracking requires no more function evaluations per iteration than regular backtracking, and for nonconvex Lipschitz-smooth problems it preserves the O(1/k) and O(1/k²) convergence rates of gradient descent and Nesterov's accelerated gradient descent, with the accelerated convergence proof relying on a novel Lyapunov argument.

## Results

Across 15+ real-world datasets, ABLS consistently outperforms regular backtracking (BLS) in elapsed time. For logistic regression with GD, ABLS saves 45.5%–57.6% time (e.g., 370s vs 694s on ADULT); with Adagrad, savings reach 67.2%–82.7% (e.g., 679.5s vs 4377.2s on MNIST). For FISTA on linear inverse problems, ABLS reduces gradient evaluations by 2.2%–40.8% (e.g., 16,756 vs 28,282 on DIGITS). On the nonconvex Rosenbrock problem, ABLS achieves dramatically lower final loss: 7.21e-12 (GD) and 4.01e-13 (AGD) versus 7.30e-03 and 9.25e-11 for BLS, respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is ABLS versus regular backtracking (BLS) with multiple ρ values (0.2–0.9 depending on method). ABLS wins on nearly all dataset–method combinations: for GD it saves 45–58% time, for AGD 3–54%, and for Adagrad 33–83%, while requiring fewer function and gradient evaluations. The one exception is GD on WEB-1, where ABLS is marginally slower (−5.4%) due to the problem's very small absolute runtime. For FISTA on linear inverse problems (Table 2), ABLS with ρ=0.9 reduces gradient evaluations by 2.2%–40.8% over the best BLS ρ, and is the only method to reach target precision on LFW. On the nonconvex Rosenbrock problem, ABLS variants achieve orders-of-magnitude lower final loss (e.g., 4.01e-13 vs 9.25e-11 for AGD) with far fewer function evaluations (2,991 vs 42,263). In the stochastic MLP experiment (Fig. 4), ABLS-PoNoS converges in 41 epochs vs 56 for PoNoS [Galli et al., 2023] and 75+ for Adam, SLS, and SPS [Loizou et al., 2021], which all fail to reach the stopping criterion.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper modifies the standard backtracking line search procedure (Nocedal & Wright, 2006) by replacing the fixed contraction factor ρ with an adaptive factor that depends on the degree to which the line search criterion (Armijo condition or descent lemma) is violated. It builds directly on the Armijo condition (Armijo, 1966) and the descent lemma framework used in FISTA (Beck & Teboulle, 2009), and proves that the adaptive variant preserves the convergence rates of gradient descent and Nesterov's accelerated gradient descent (Nesterov, 1983). The paper positions itself as a lightweight, parameter-free alternative to the ad hoc choice of ρ that has persisted in the literature, and demonstrates compatibility with stochastic line search variants like PoNoS (Galli et al., 2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: adaptive backtracking reduces function evaluations by 40–93% across 15+ real-world datasets while requiring no additional computational overhead, making it a drop-in replacement for the backtracking subroutine in GD, AGD, Adagrad, and FISTA. A key theoretical contribution is the fundamental obstacle result showing that no line search procedure can be provably better than regular backtracking for quadratics, which calibrates expectations for future work. Limitations include inapplicability to Wolfe conditions (not affine in step-size) and uncertainty about general stochastic settings where violation information from one mini-batch may not transfer to another. Open directions include extending to quasi-Newton methods, two-way backtracking, and increasing step-sizes.
