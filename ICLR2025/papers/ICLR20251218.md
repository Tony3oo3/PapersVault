## Problem

Trained RL agents are often non-optimal, yet existing explainable RL (XRL) methods explain *what* the agent does without explaining *why* it fails to maximize the ground-truth reward. Prior improvement methods (e.g., RICE) identify critical states but do not diagnose the root cause of non-optimality, and no framework systematically learns how to exploit such explanations to improve the policy.

## Input / Output

The method operates under a black-box assumption: it receives only a set of m trajectories generated by the non-optimal agent (no access to its internal policy or Q-function), the ground-truth reward function r, and the MDP transition dynamics. It outputs a two-level explanation (an IRL-learned reward r̂ and the top-K 'misleading' state-action pairs), a parametric shaping reward r_θ(s,a) = r(s,a) + θ(s,a)(r(s,a) − r̂(s,a)), and an improved policy. The shaping reward is constrained to a specific form that interpolates between r and r̂, rather than being a free neural network, which the authors show empirically yields much better learning.

## Method

UTILITY first generates a two-level explanation: at the high level, maximum-likelihood IRL recovers a reward r̂ to which the agent's policy is actually optimal, and the gap r − r̂ serves as domain knowledge for reward shaping; at the low level, a Q-function-based metric l(s,a) = max_{a'} Q_r^{π̂_A}(s,a') − Q_r^{π̂_A}(s,a) identifies the top-K 'misleading' state-action pairs that cause non-optimality. These explanations are then exploited through a constrained bi-level optimization: the upper level learns the shaping parameter θ to maximize cumulative ground-truth reward, while the lower level solves a constrained RL problem that maximizes entropy-regularized shaping reward subject to a budget on cumulative cost over the misleading pairs. Because both the lower-level objective and constraint are highly non-convex (unlike prior bi-level RL work that requires strong convexity), the authors introduce a dual transformation that converts the lower level into an unconstrained convex problem, then design a triple-loop algorithm (inner: soft policy iteration; middle: gradient descent on the dual variable λ; outer: gradient ascent on θ) with explicit approximation-error bounds at each loop. They prove that the algorithm attains global optimality when the state-action space is finite, and demonstrate substantial improvements over SAC, RICE, SIL, and LIR on delayed MuJoCo tasks.

## Results

On four delayed MuJoCo tasks (sparse reward, 20-step delay), UTILITY achieves the highest cumulative reward across all environments: 715.96 on HalfCheetah, 317.99 on Hopper, 242.63 on Walker2d, and 105.80 on Ant. Compared to the SAC baseline, UTILITY improves cumulative reward by 55–87% depending on the task. The largest relative gain is on Delayed HalfCheetah (715.96 vs. 383.45 for SAC, +87%), while the smallest is on Delayed Ant (105.80 vs. 68.11, +55%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

UTILITY outperforms all four baselines on every delayed MuJoCo task. Against the closest competitor, LIR (intrinsic reward shaping), UTILITY leads by 31% on HalfCheetah (715.96 vs. 548.28), 21% on Hopper (317.99 vs. 247.27), 19% on Walker2d (242.63 vs. 204.72), and 35% on Ant (105.80 vs. 78.23). Against RICE (XRL-based fine-tuning at critical states), UTILITY's advantage is even larger, reaching 57% on HalfCheetah. SIL (self-imitation learning) is the second-best baseline on HalfCheetah and Hopper but is still 29% and 21% below UTILITY respectively. The paper attributes UTILITY's superiority to two factors: (i) the learned shaping reward is dense (unlike the sparse ground-truth reward in delayed environments), and (ii) the constraint on misleading state-action pairs prevents the policy from repeating the specific mistakes of the original non-optimal agent.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the XRL-for-improvement line initiated by RICE (Cheng et al., 2024) and EDGE (Guo et al., 2021), which identify critical states and fine-tune policies there, by providing a principled two-level explanation of *why* the agent is non-optimal (a learned reward function via IRL at the high level, and 'misleading' state-action pairs via a Q-function gap metric at the low level). It then departs from prior constrained bi-level optimization work (Xu & Zhu, 2023a; Khanduri et al., 2023), which requires strong convexity in the lower level, by formulating and solving a constrained bi-level problem where both the objective and constraint are highly non-convex, using a dual transformation and a triple-loop algorithm with global optimality guarantees. The reward shaping component builds on intrinsic reward learning (Zheng et al., 2018) but replaces the learned intrinsic reward with the structured domain knowledge r − r̂ derived from the high-level explanation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a systematic, theoretically grounded bridge between explainability and performance in RL: rather than treating XRL as a post-hoc interpretability tool, it shows that the explanation itself can be the optimization signal for improvement, with a provable global-optimality guarantee for the resulting non-convex constrained bi-level problem. The ablation revealing that the shaping reward (high-level explanation) contributes far more than the constraint (low-level explanation) suggests that reward-space correction is the dominant mechanism, which could guide future work toward simpler pipelines. A key limitation is the requirement for online environment interaction to learn the Q-function and solve the bi-level problem, leaving the offline RL setting as an open direction. The empirical evaluation, while showing large margins over baselines on delayed MuJoCo tasks, is limited to four environments and five seeds, and the baselines (RICE, SIL, LIR) are not the strongest available RL-improvement methods.
