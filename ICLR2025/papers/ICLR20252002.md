## Problem

Classical uncertainty sets (box, budget, ellipsoidal) used in two-stage adaptive robust optimization (ARO) capture a wide range of unrealistic outcomes, leading to over-conservative and costly first-stage decisions, especially in high-dimensional settings. While deep generative models have been applied to construct tighter uncertainty sets for single-stage robust optimization, extending them to ARO is challenging because the adversarial subproblem becomes a mixed-binary bilinear program when a neural network is embedded. This paper addresses both the conservatism of classical uncertainty sets and the computational intractability of the ARO adversarial subproblem.

## Input / Output

The method takes a dataset of historical uncertainty realizations (e.g., 7300 samples × 349 features for the New England power system) split into VAE training, Γ-calibration, and test subsets, along with ARO problem parameters (cost vectors, constraint matrices defining the recourse polyhedron). It produces first-stage investment decisions x*, a sequence of worst-case uncertainty realizations ξ that are simultaneously cost-maximizing and statistically realistic, and a total planning cost evaluated as c^T x* + F̂⁻¹(α; x*) on held-out data. Notably, the uncertainty set is defined implicitly as the image of a latent L-ball under the VAE decoder rather than explicitly in the data space, and the recourse problem is solved exactly as an LP at every gradient step rather than approximated by a predictive model.

## Method

AGRO embeds a variational autoencoder within a column-and-constraint generation (CCG) loop to perform adversarial scenario generation for ARO. The VAE decoder f_θ maps a low-dimensional latent ball Z = B(0, Γ) into the high-dimensional uncertainty space, defining a tight uncertainty set U = {f_θ(z) : z ∈ Z} with calibrated probabilistic coverage via order statistics of latent norms. Rather than solving the resulting adversarial subproblem as a mixed-binary bilinear program, AGRO uses projected gradient ascent in the latent space: at each step, the recourse LP is solved exactly, its optimal value is differentiated with respect to ξ via implicit differentiation of KKT conditions (Amos & Kolter, 2017), and the gradient is backpropagated through the VAE decoder to update z, which is then projected back onto Z. Multiple random initializations (I=10) are run per CCG iteration to mitigate non-concavity, and the best worst-case realization is added to the scenario set. This avoids both the need for a large dataset of solved recourse instances (required by prior ML-assisted ARO methods) and the computational burden of the bilinear subproblem, while ensuring generated contingencies lie in high-density regions of the uncertainty distribution.

## Results

On the synthetic production-distribution problem, AGRO reduces total planning costs by up to 1.8% over classical CCG (with budget uncertainty), with the advantage growing as uncertainty dimensionality increases from |J|=3 to |J|=12. On the real-world New England power system expansion problem (D=349 features), AGRO with L=4 achieves a 95th-percentile (VaR) total cost of $10.4B versus $11.3B for CCG—an 8% reduction—while also reducing total solve time from 29,183s to 2,299s (~13× faster) and subproblem time from 1,263s to 267s. CCG with ellipsoidal uncertainty sets could not be solved to optimality within 900s for |J|>6 in the production-distribution problem or at all in the capacity expansion problem.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the classical Column-and-Constraint Generation (CCG) algorithm of Zeng & Zhao (2013), instantiated with budget and ellipsoidal uncertainty sets calibrated via the data-depth procedure of Hong et al. (2021). AGRO consistently outperforms CCG on both cost and runtime across both test problems. In the production-distribution setting, AGRO (L=4) achieves 0.5–1.8% lower out-of-sample total costs than CCG with budget uncertainty, with the gap widening as dimensionality grows; CCG with ellipsoidal sets becomes intractable beyond |J|=6. In the capacity expansion setting, AGRO (L=4) reduces the 95th-percentile total cost from $11.3B to $10.4B (≈8%) while cutting total solve time by roughly 13× (2,299s vs. 29,183s), driven primarily by replacing CCG's large-scale bilinear subproblem (1,263s average) with a fast projected-gradient-ascent heuristic (267s average). AGRO also produces more realistic worst-case scenarios that preserve temporal autocorrelation and spatial correlations in load and capacity factors, whereas CCG generates discontinuous, physically implausible realizations.

## Relation to Prior Work

AGRO extends the line of deep data-driven uncertainty set construction (Goerigk & Kurtz, 2023; Chenreddy et al., 2022) from single-stage robust optimization to the richer two-stage adaptive robust optimization setting, where the adversarial subproblem involves a recourse LP rather than a simple linear objective. It builds on the classical CCG algorithm (Zeng & Zhao, 2013) as its outer loop and on differentiable optimization (Amos & Kolter, 2017) to compute gradients of recourse costs through the VAE decoder, replacing the computationally prohibitive mixed-binary bilinear reformulation that prior works (Fischetti & Jo, 2018) required for embedding neural networks in adversarial subproblems. The calibration of the latent uncertainty set radius with probabilistic guarantees follows Hong et al. (2021), and the VAE architecture is standard (Kingma & Welling, 2013).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for energy infrastructure planning, where over-conservative uncertainty sets lead to costly over-investment in generation and transmission capacity; an 8% reduction in total planning cost for a regional power system is economically meaningful. Key limitations include the heuristic nature of PGA (no global optimality guarantee for the adversarial subproblem), the sensitivity to VAE bottleneck dimension (too low causes underestimation of realistic contingencies, too high causes over-investment), and the requirement for sufficient training data to calibrate the uncertainty set reliably. Open questions include whether higher-fidelity generative models (diffusion, normalizing flows) would further improve results, whether theoretical convergence guarantees can be established for the PGA heuristic, and how the approach scales to even higher-dimensional uncertainties (e.g., multi-day or multi-year planning horizons).
