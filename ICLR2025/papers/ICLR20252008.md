## Problem

While instruction-tuned LLMs can follow user constraints, they frequently fail to adhere to fine-grained, verifiable instructions (format, length, word inclusion/exclusion), and their compliance degrades as generation lengthens. Prior activation-steering work targeted high-level stylistic properties (sentiment, tone, honesty) but did not address whether models encode lower-level, modular, multi-instantiation constraints as extractable linear directions that can be used for inference-time control.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a base text query (optionally augmented with an explicit instruction) and a pre-computed steering vector, applying the vector at a single chosen layer across all token positions during greedy decoding. The steering coefficient is set differently per constraint type: dynamically for format instructions (mapping activations to their mean on instruction-containing inputs), as a continuous scalar for length modulation, and via grid search for word-specific constraints. For word exclusion, the inclusion vector is subtracted from the residual stream rather than adding a separately computed exclusion vector, since exclusion vectors paradoxically increase the probability of the target word.

## Method

Steering vectors are computed via difference-in-means: for N paired inputs (base query x and base query + instruction x⁺), the mean residual-stream activation difference at the last input token is taken and normalized to a unit direction u_l at layer l. During inference, the scaled vector c·u_l is added to the residual stream at a single optimal layer (selected by sweeping layers and checking instruction-following accuracy plus a GPT-2 perplexity quality gate) at every token position, countering instruction drift. The approach is modular and composable: multiple steering vectors can be injected simultaneously at their respective optimal layers to enforce several constraints at once, and vectors computed on instruction-tuned models transfer to base models of the same family, sometimes outperforming same-model steering.

## Results

On the IFEval format-instruction subset, steering improves instruction-following accuracy from ~10% to ~30% when no explicit text instruction is provided in the input, across all four models (Phi-3, Gemma 2 2B/9B, Mistral 7B). When instructions are explicitly present, baseline accuracy ranges from 60–90%, and steering still yields a significant boost for two of the four models. For length constraints, applying a conciseness steering vector with weight c=20 produces a significant and consistent improvement in adherence to maximum-sentence limits (n=1–5) across all tested values. Cross-model steering (vectors computed on instruction-tuned Gemma 2 2B/9B applied to their base counterparts) outperforms same-model steering for Gemma 2 2B in both evaluation settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against other steering or instruction-following methods; the primary comparison is between the steered model and the unsteered baseline (no activation intervention). In the no-instruction setting, steering consistently improves format-instruction accuracy by roughly 20 percentage points (~10% → ~30%) across all four models. In the with-instruction setting, the unsteered baseline already achieves 60–90% accuracy, and steering provides a further but smaller gain for Phi-3 and Gemma 2 9B, while showing no significant improvement for Gemma 2 2B and Mistral 7B. For word-exclusion, the unsteered model frequently fails to exclude the target keyword (e.g., still using 'step' despite the instruction), whereas steering reliably removes it. The cross-model steering result is particularly notable: vectors from the instruction-tuned Gemma 2 2B outperform vectors computed on the base Gemma 2 2B itself, suggesting that instruction-tuning produces more effective instruction representations that transfer to the base model's activation space.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the contrastive activation steering framework established by Turner et al. (2023) and Panickssery et al. (2024), which compute steering vectors as activation differences between paired inputs, to the specific domain of instruction-following constraints. The difference-in-means technique (Belrose, 2023) and the broader representation engineering paradigm (Zou et al., 2023) provide the mathematical foundation, but prior steering work targeted high-level stylistic or semantic attributes (honesty, sentiment, tone, harmfulness). Here, the authors shift focus to lower-level, verifiable, hard constraints—format, length, and word inclusion/exclusion—that have many possible instantiations and complement the base query's semantics rather than overriding it. The cross-model steering experiments draw an analogy to task arithmetic (Ilharco et al., 2023) and prior evidence that linear representations transfer between base and instruction-tuned models (Panickssery et al., 2024; Kissane et al., 2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that activation steering provides a practical, training-free mechanism for fine-grained inference-time control over instruction adherence, with the notable finding that vectors computed on instruction-tuned models can transfer to and even outperform same-model steering on base models. This opens a path for composable, cross-model transfer of task-specific behavioral control without retraining. However, limitations remain: the fixed steering weight throughout generation, modest quality degradation in some cases, and the restriction to a relatively narrow set of verifiable constraints (format, length, single-word inclusion/exclusion) leave open questions about scaling to more complex, multi-parameter, or semantically entangled instructions. The method's dependence on a small number of paired examples (as few as 20) is encouraging for practicality, but the interaction between steering and model-specific instruction drift over long generations remains underexplored.
