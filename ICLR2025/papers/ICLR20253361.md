## Problem

Empirical results on self-consuming training loops (STLs) are strikingly inconsistent—some models collapse while others do not—yet no unified theoretical framework explains this discrepancy. Prior theoretical work either analyzed only simplified Gaussian models, focused on parameter deviations or distribution divergence without providing generalization error bounds, or ignored the role of model architecture entirely. The behavior of transformers within STLs and the trade-offs of synthetic data augmentation remained largely unexamined.

## Input / Output

The framework takes an initial real dataset S_0 of size n, a proportion α of real data mixed with synthetic data at each generation, and a generative model G (e.g., an L-layer transformer in an in-context learning setting) as inputs. It produces a high-probability generalization error bound decomposed into a cumulative distribution shift term (governed by TV distance d_TV(n) and the real-data proportion α) and a generalization-on-mixed-distributions term (governed by uniform stability β_n and the novel recursive stability parameter γ_n^i). The recursive stability parameter is flexible: it can be measured via TV distance, KL divergence, or ℓ_2 norm depending on the model architecture, and the framework also yields a condition on the optimal synthetic data augmentation coefficient λ* as a function of real dataset size n.

## Method

The core technical contribution is the introduction of recursive stability, a new stability measure that quantifies how a single-point perturbation in the initial real dataset propagates through i generations of recursive synthetic data generation, formalized as d(G^(i)(S_0), G^(i)(S_0')) ≤ γ_n^{i,α}. The generalization error is decomposed into two terms: (1) cumulative distribution shift across generations, bounded via recursive TV-distance arguments yielding 2M(1-(1-α)^i)α^{-1}d_TV(n), and (2) generalization error on the non-i.i.d. mixed dataset, bounded using uniform stability β_n combined with conditional independence of synthetic data given S_0 and moment-based concentration inequalities. For transformers in in-context learning, the paper proves recursive stability with γ_n^i = O(1/n) under a constant real-data proportion α = 1 - B̃_W^{-L}, yielding an overall generalization bound of O(n^{-1}log²(n) + n^{-1/2}log(n) + n^{-1/4}). The framework further reveals a trade-off in synthetic data augmentation: increasing the synthetic data coefficient λ reduces per-generation generalization error but amplifies cumulative distribution shift, with the optimal λ* increasing as the real dataset size n decreases.

## Results

The paper's headline result (Theorem 3) establishes that for an L-layer transformer in in-context learning within an STL, the generalization error is bounded by O(n^{-1}log²(n) + n^{-1/2}log(n) + n^{-1/4}), where n is the mixed dataset size, provided the real data proportion α is a non-negligible constant. The recursive stability parameter for transformers achieves γ_n^i = O(1/n) (Theorem 2), and the critical condition for preventing model collapse is α ≥ 1 - B̃_W^{-L}, which is a constant independent of the number of generations i. Theorem 4 further shows that the optimal synthetic data augmentation coefficient λ* increases as the real dataset size n decreases, formalizing the trade-off between distribution shift and generalization on mixed data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or experimental comparisons. The paper positions its contributions against prior theoretical works on STLs: Bertrand et al. (2024) derived parameter deviation bounds for likelihood-based models under strict statistical/optimization assumptions, while Fu et al. (2024b) provided TV distance bounds specifically for diffusion models. In contrast, this paper establishes the first generalization error bound for STLs (Theorem 1), introduces the novel concept of recursive stability to handle non-i.i.d. mixed datasets, and extends the analysis to transformers in ICL — a setting entirely unexplored by prior work. The framework is also more general in that it accounts for model architecture effects, whereas prior results were restricted to simplified Gaussian or diffusion models. The paper confirms alignment with prior findings (e.g., linear error growth when α=0) while providing strictly stronger guarantees by incorporating both recursive stability and the real data proportion into a unified bound.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the algorithmic stability framework (Bousquet & Elisseeff, 2002) by introducing recursive stability, a new measure that quantifies error propagation across generations of self-consuming training loops. It builds on the conditional i.i.d. structure of synthetic data from Zheng et al. (2023) and the transformer ICL analysis from Li et al. (2023), while departing from prior STL theory (Bertrand et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2024b) that focused on parameter deviations or distribution divergence for simplified models. The key conceptual departure is shifting the analytical target from internal model parameters to the generalization error of the learned hypothesis, and explicitly incorporating model architecture (transformers) into the stability analysis for the first time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first unified theoretical framework explaining when self-consuming training loops succeed or collapse, with the practical takeaway that a constant (non-negligible) proportion of real data plus recursive stability suffices to prevent degradation. The trade-off analysis for synthetic data augmentation—showing optimal synthetic size increases as real data shrinks—offers concrete guidance for practitioners designing data pipelines. Limitations include the relatively slow n^{-1/4} convergence rate for the distribution shift term, the restriction to ICL for the transformer analysis, and the intractability of a closed-form optimal augmentation coefficient λ*. Open questions include extending the framework to non-ICL transformer training, other generative architectures (diffusion, GANs) beyond TV-distance heuristics, and whether recursive stability can be verified empirically for large-scale models.
