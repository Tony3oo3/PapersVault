## Problem

Despite strong benchmark performance, transformer LMs are fragile to seemingly inconsequential format changes in multiple-choice questions (shuffling answer positions, using non-standard symbols like Q/Z/R/X), and prior mechanistic analyses of MCQA were limited to a single closed-source model and one dataset. This paper asks: which internal components—specific layers, attention heads, and sub-functions—are causally responsible for selecting and promoting the correct answer choice symbol in formatted MCQA?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252825/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes formatted MCQA prompts (4 answer choices labeled by A/B/C/D, Q/Z/R/X, or 1/2/3/4) in 0-shot or 3-shot settings, run through three model families (Llama 3.1 8B, Olmo 7B, Qwen 2.5 1.5B) on MMLU, HellaSwag, and a synthetic Colors task. It produces per-layer projected logits and probits tracking how answer symbol scores evolve through the residual stream, causal attribution maps at the level of individual layers, MHSA/MLP sub-functions, and single attention heads, and training-curve analyses that pinpoint when MCQA format is acquired. The synthetic Colors task (answer present in context, model only performs symbol binding) serves as a control to disentangle format learning from dataset-specific knowledge.

## Method

The core method combines two complementary interpretability techniques: vocabulary projection (logit lens), which maps each layer's hidden state at the final token position to vocabulary space via the unembedding matrix to reveal when answer symbol scores become dominant, and activation patching (causal tracing), which swaps hidden states between two correct predictions with different answer positions at each layer, MHSA output, MLP output, and individual weighted attention head to identify causal components. They find that 1–3 middle layers (e.g., layer 24 for Olmo 7B Instruct) are causally responsible for answer selection, driven by MHSA rather than MLP, with only 1–4 attention heads per layer contributing non-negligibly to the logit difference. Subsequent layers increase the predicted symbol's probability in vocabulary space, and for non-standard symbols (Q/Z/R/X), models initially assign high probability to expected symbols (A/B/C/D) before abruptly switching to the prompt's symbols at later layers (e.g., layer 29 for Olmo). A synthetic Colors task, where the correct answer phrase is already in context and the model's sole job is symbol binding, reveals a sharp transition during training (between 80k–100k steps for Olmo 7B base) at which formatted MCQA is learned, independent of dataset-specific knowledge.

## Results

On the selected best-performing models, Olmo 0724 7B Instruct achieves 100% accuracy on the synthetic Colors task, 62.4% on HellaSwag, and 53.5% on MMLU (3-shot, A/B/C/D prompt). Activation patching localizes the causal effect of answer symbol selection to a single middle layer (layer 24 for Olmo 7B Instruct), with subsequent layers 26 and 29 producing the largest logit/probit increases for the predicted token. Only 1–4 attention heads per layer (out of 32) project to non-negligible values on answer symbols, confirming a sparse mechanism. For out-of-distribution prompts (Q/Z/R/X), Olmo and Qwen models initially assign high probability to expected A/B/C/D symbols before abruptly switching to the correct OOD symbols at layer 29 (Olmo) or layer 23 (Qwen).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a mechanistic interpretability paper rather than a method paper, so it does not propose a new approach to benchmark against baselines. Instead, it compares internal processing patterns across three model families (Olmo 0724 7B Instruct, Llama 3.1 8B Instruct, Qwen 2.5 1.5B Instruct) and across prompt formats (A/B/C/D, Q/Z/R/X, 1/2/3/4). Key cross-model findings: all three models use a similar sparse attention-head mechanism for answer symbol promotion, but the specific key layers and logit magnitudes differ even for comparable-size models. Olmo 7B Instruct and Qwen 2.5 1.5B exhibit a two-stage process for OOD symbols (initially promoting A/B/C/D before switching), while Llama 3.1 8B Instruct does not show this pattern as strongly. The synthetic Colors task disentangles dataset-specific knowledge from symbol-binding ability, revealing that Olmo 7B Base learns formatted MCQA between 80k and 100k training steps (jumping from ~25% to ~97% accuracy), even when performance on HellaSwag and MMLU remains poor at those checkpoints.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability line of work on MCQA initiated by Lieberum et al. (2023), who identified 'correct letter heads' in Chinchilla-70B on MMLU alone, by broadening the scope to three model families (Llama 3.1, Olmo, Qwen 2.5), three datasets, and multiple prompt formats. Methodologically, it combines activation patching (Meng et al., 2022) extended to per-head granularity (Todd et al., 2024) with vocabulary projection (Geva et al., 2022b) as complementary lenses, and introduces a synthetic Colors task to disentangle symbol binding from dataset-specific knowledge. Unlike prior behavioral analyses of MCQA robustness (Pezeshkpour & Hruschka, 2024; Alzahrani et al., 2024), this work provides causal, component-level explanations for observed format sensitivities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Since MMLU and similar MCQA benchmarks serve as primary release signals for new LLMs, understanding the internal mechanisms behind formatted MCQA is critical for building more robust and reliable models. The discovery that OOD answer symbols (e.g., Q/Z/R/X) trigger a two-stage process—initially promoting expected A/B/C/D before a late-layer switch—provides a concrete mechanistic explanation for a known robustness failure mode and suggests targeted interventions. Open questions include whether the two-stage process can be eliminated through training, whether the sparse attention-head circuits generalize to other structured prediction tasks, and how these findings inform more robust evaluation protocols beyond simple format shuffling.
