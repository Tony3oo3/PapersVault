## Problem

RL agents trained with imperfect proxy rewards often fail to align with human preferences, either converging to suboptimal performance or achieving high cumulative rewards in undesired ways. Existing approaches that rely solely on human feedback (demonstrations, preferences, interventions) are either too label-intensive or assume the human is optimal. The paper asks whether learning simultaneously from two imperfect signals—a proxy reward and possibly suboptimal corrective actions—can outperform either signal alone, since their imperfections likely occupy different regions of the state-action space.

## Input / Output

The agent receives environment observations (84×84 grayscale frames with 4-frame stacking for Atari, or 35-dimensional kinematic state vectors for highway) and a proxy reward signal that can be sparse or dense with varying degrees of misspecification. Human feedback arrives as sparse corrective actions: the labeler views a T-step trajectory segment and optionally selects one state to provide a supposedly-better action, with at most 1% of transitions labeled. The output is a discrete action policy (greedy over a Q-function) parameterized by a dueling, distributional Q-network; the Q-function itself is the learned object that encodes both proxy-reward value estimates and labeler preferences.

## Method

ICoPro is an iterative value-based algorithm built on Rainbow that cycles through three phases per iteration. In the Data Collection phase, the agent rolls out with ε-greedy and the labeler provides corrective actions on sampled segments. In the Finetune phase, the Q-network is updated purely via a margin loss that enforces corrective actions' Q-values to exceed all other actions by a constant C, training until a target accuracy is reached. In the Propagation phase, the Q-network is trained with a combined loss: standard 1-step and n-step TD losses on proxy rewards (to propagate learned values across states) plus a weighted margin loss on both actual corrective labels and pseudo-labels generated by the target Q-network on unlabeled states. The pseudo-label mechanism reduces human labeling cost and regularizes the online network toward the target in policy space, while the two-phase design (finetune then propagate) ensures corrective actions are first internalized before being generalized through temporal credit assignment.

## Results

On Atari with large label budget (|D^L| = 8K), ICoPro achieves 1274.76 on Seaquest, 61.52 on Boxing, 20030.30 on Battlezone, 4817.74 on Frostbite, 2360.27 on Alien, 23344.01 on Hero, and 3188.20 on MsPacman, matching or approaching the simulated labeler's performance in most games. With a smaller label budget (|D^L| = 2K), ICoPro still leads all baselines, e.g., 1167.19 on Seaquest vs. 1003.21 (DQfD-I) and 537.20 (BC-L). On highway, ICoPro achieves [Speed-Avg, %Crash] = [29.02, 0.22] with real human labels (PR4), a behavior unattainable by Rainbow alone. ICoPro remains robust to up to 25% random corrective actions and can even outperform the labeler when 50% of labels are random.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ICoPro substantially outperforms Rainbow (proxy rewards only) across all games, most dramatically on Boxing (61.52 vs. 1.99, a ~31× gap) and Seaquest (1274.76 vs. 707.04), confirming that corrective actions are essential for alignment. Against corrective-actions-only baselines (BC-L, HGDAgger-L), ICoPro wins in nearly all games, e.g., 23344.01 vs. 14288.67 (BC-L) and 13258.79 (HGDAgger-L) on Hero, demonstrating that proxy rewards via RL losses are critical for generalization. ICoPro consistently beats PVP-I and PVP-IR, which use a fixed Q-value bound loss that fails to adapt to diverse reward scales; notably, PVP-IR (with proxy rewards) sometimes performs worse than PVP-I (zero rewards). Against DQfD-I (the closest baseline, differing only in the absence of pseudo-labels), ICoPro shows similar performance on simpler environments but clear advantages on harder Atari games (e.g., Hero: 23344.01 vs. 17814.10; Frostbite: 4817.74 vs. 4176.27). In the user study with real humans, ICoPro-Human rescues 1.5× more divers than the scripted labeler on Seaquest (3.2 vs. 1.9) while using 46× fewer timesteps, and BC-L on the same human labels performs significantly worse, confirming the synergy of the two imperfect signals.

## Relation to Prior Work

ICoPro builds directly on Rainbow (Hessel et al., 2018) as its base value-based RL algorithm, inheriting its dueling, n-step, and distributional components. Conceptually, it sits at the intersection of RLHF (Christiano et al., 2017; Ibarz et al., 2018), the DAgger family of interactive imitation learning (Ross et al., 2011; Kelly et al., 2019), and methods combining demonstrations with RL (Hester et al., 2018; Peng et al., 2023). The key departure is that prior work typically assumes either a well-specified reward function or an optimal demonstrator, whereas ICoPro explicitly designs for the case where both the proxy reward and the corrective actions are imperfect, and shows they can complement each other's weaknesses. The margin loss for incorporating corrective actions is borrowed from prior imitation learning work (Kim et al., 2013; Piot et al., 2014), but the iterative three-phase scheme with target-network pseudo-labels is a novel algorithmic contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper addresses a practically critical gap: in real-world deployment, reward functions are almost never perfectly specified and human labelers are not optimal, yet most prior methods assume at least one of these is reliable. The demonstration that two imperfect signals can synergistically outperform either alone provides a practical recipe for human-in-the-loop RL that is more sample-efficient and robust than existing approaches. Limitations include the restriction to finite action spaces, the absence of theoretical guarantees on when the combination is beneficial, and the synchronous labeling protocol. Open questions include extending to continuous action spaces, providing formal conditions under which the complementarity holds, and scaling the user study to larger, more complex tasks.
