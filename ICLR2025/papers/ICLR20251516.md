## Problem

Specialist language models outperform generalist models of the same size on narrow domains, but pretraining them requires 100B+ tokens of domain-specific data that is unavailable for most tasks. This paper tackles how to train an effective specialist LM from a large generalist corpus guided by only a small specialist dataset, comparing classification, gradient-alignment, and importance-sampling strategies for reshaping the pretraining distribution.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251516/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a large generalist corpus (RedPajama v2, ~30T tokens) segmented into 1,024-token windows, a small specialist dataset (14M–26.7B tokens for LM tasks; 200K–2M tokens for MCQ tasks), and SBERT MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings (384-dim) computed per window. The generalist data is hierarchically clustered into a 64-branching, 4-level tree (up to 16.7M leaf clusters); the specialist data is used only to compute a cluster frequency histogram. The output is a specialist transformer LM (350M–6.7B params) whose pretraining data distribution has been resampled to match the specialist cluster histogram, plus the learned cluster importance weights.

## Method

CRISP derives the specialist loss as an expectation over a discrete cluster variable, then applies importance sampling with weights w(c) = P(c|D^s)/P(c|D^g) to reweight the generalist distribution. Under the independence assumption P(x|c,D^s)=P(x|c), the per-cluster loss is estimated from the large generalist set (low variance) while only the cluster frequency P(c|D^s) is estimated from the small specialist set. Rather than explicitly reweighting the loss, the training loop samples a cluster ID from the specialist histogram and then draws a uniform random generalist example from within that cluster, which the authors argue yields lower-variance gradient estimates than reweighting. The clustering uses hierarchical k-means++ over normalized SBERT embeddings with a balancing constraint to keep cluster sizes near-uniform. The method is compared against a logistic-regression classifier over SBERT embeddings and the DoGE gradient-alignment algorithm, and is shown to consistently outperform both across LM perplexity and MCQ accuracy at multiple model and cluster scales.

## Results

On multiple-choice question tasks (1.3B model, 120B tokens), CRISP achieves 71.2% on ARC-E, 38.6% on ARC-C, 33.4% on MMLU, and 70.0% on RWDB-R, consistently outperforming the base RedPajama v2 pretraining (58.4%, 27.4%, 30.0%, 62.1%) by 12.8, 11.2, 3.4, and 7.9 points respectively. On language modeling tasks, CRISP reduces in-domain perplexity substantially: 5.11 on PubMed (vs. 6.34 base), 6.67 on StackExchange (vs. 8.41 base), and 7.66 on Wikipedia (vs. 8.53 base) after fine-tuning with 14m specialist tokens. The 1.3B CRISP model outperforms the 6.7B base model on ARC, demonstrating ~30x training cost savings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CRISP consistently outperforms DoGE (gradient-alignment via bilevel optimization) across all four MCQ tasks: +5.0 pts on ARC-E (71.2 vs. 66.2), +5.3 pts on ARC-C (38.6 vs. 33.3), +2.4 pts on MMLU (33.4 vs. 31.0), and +2.6 pts on RWDB-R (70.0 vs. 67.4). Against the Cross-Entropy Difference (CED) method based on Wang et al. (2018), CRISP achieves substantially larger gains: +12.3 pts on ARC-E (71.2 vs. 58.9), +8.1 pts on ARC-C (38.6 vs. 30.5), and +2.3 pts on MMLU (33.4 vs. 31.1). CRISP also beats the SBERT-based classifier baseline on all tasks (e.g., 71.2 vs. 68.2 on ARC-E, 38.6 vs. 36.7 on ARC-C). On language modeling perplexity, CRISP matches or slightly beats the classifier (5.11 vs. 5.18 on PubMed, 7.66 vs. 7.83 on Wikipedia) while clearly outperforming DoGE (5.11 vs. 5.41 on PubMed). The paper notes that DoGE and CRISP on 64 clusters produce similar results (Table 13), suggesting the benefit of CRISP comes from scaling to 260k clusters where the importance weights are more granular.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper scales and extends the clustered importance sampling idea from Grangier et al. (2024b), which adjusted generalist cluster frequencies using a small specialist set, to millions of clusters, larger models (up to 6.7B), and non-LM tasks like multiple-choice question answering. It positions CRISP within the broader task-adaptive data-selection literature, comparing against classification-based selection (SBERT + logistic regression), gradient-alignment methods (DoGE), and cross-entropy difference scoring. The key conceptual departure is replacing bilevel optimization or learned classifiers with a simple histogram-matching resampling procedure, arguing that the independence assumption within clusters is sufficient for effective domain adaptation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a 1.3B CRISP model outperforms a 6.7B base model on ARC, implying roughly 30× training cost savings for specialist deployment. The method's simplicity (cluster, count, resample) makes it easy to adopt, and the finding that even 10% continued pretraining with CRISP nearly matches full pretraining offers a cost-effective path for organizations with existing generalist checkpoints. Open questions include how to improve cluster-frequency estimation when specialist data is very small (below ~200k tokens), whether the independence assumption holds for more heterogeneous domains, and whether the approach transfers to non-text modalities as the authors speculate.
