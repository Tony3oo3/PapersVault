## Problem

Existing adversarial attacks on GNNs manipulate edges or features without leveraging the underlying graph topology, leading to expensive search over all possible structural modifications. The paper identifies that gradient-based attacks universally converge on nodes occupying specific graph orbits (15 and 18), yet no prior method explicitly exploits this topological equivalence to reduce the attack search space.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251636/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a graph's adjacency matrix and node feature matrix, a designated target node, and a trained surrogate GCN model, with a perturbation budget Δ constraining the number of edge modifications. It outputs a modified adjacency matrix containing a small set of edge additions or deletions (typically 1–5) that flip the target node's predicted class. Orbit discovery via the ORCA algorithm is performed once as a pre-processing step over the entire graph, so per-target-node computation only requires evaluating the surrogate loss over the reduced candidate set.

## Method

GOttack computes 73-dimensional Graph Orbit Vectors for every node by enumerating all 5-node graphlets and counting each node's participation across the 73 distinct orbits. It then restricts the candidate edge-modification set to nodes whose top two orbit counts correspond to orbits 15 and 18 (the 'periphery orbits'), reducing the search space to roughly 23% of all nodes. A linearized surrogate loss—derived from the two-layer GCN message-passing with a simplified activation—scores each candidate edge addition or deletion, and the modification yielding the maximum log-probability discrepancy for the target node is selected. Theorem 1 formalizes why orbits 15 and 18 are effective: nodes in these orbits have the longest expected random-walk hitting times to remote parts of the graph, making them optimal bridges to inject label-diverse information into the target's receptive field. This topological candidate selection combined with the incremental surrogate-loss update eliminates the need for full gradient backpropagation per candidate, cutting end-to-end attack time to roughly 55% of the fastest competing method.

## Results

GOttack achieves the highest misclassification rate in 7 out of 15 attack settings (5 datasets × 3 GNN backbones) at budget Δ=1, ranking second in 5 additional settings. The highest overall misclassification rate is 52.08% (Pubmed/GCN), compared to 47.02% for the second-best method (Nettack). Across all budgets (Δ=1–5) and datasets, GOttack attains the highest average misclassification rate of 0.58, versus 0.57 for the next-best methods (PRBCD and SGA). In terms of efficiency, GOttack completes attack training in approximately 55% of the time required by the fastest competing model (e.g., 512s vs. 917s on BlogCatalog).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Nettack, GOttack achieves a higher overall misclassification rate (52.08% vs. 47.02% on Pubmed/GCN) and wins in more individual settings (7 vs. 2 best results across 15 configurations). Against SGA, the comparison is closer: SGA wins 4 settings (notably Cora/GraphSAGE at 61.06% and Cora/GIN at 57.05%), while GOttack wins 7; however, GOttack's advantage is more consistent across datasets. Against FGA, GOttack outperforms in the majority of settings, with FGA struggling particularly on GCN backbones (e.g., 31.10% on Citeseer/GCN vs. GOttack's 46.07%). Against PRBCD, GOttack is substantially stronger, especially on GraphSAGE and GIN backbones where PRBCD yields surprisingly low rates (e.g., 8.01% on Polblogs/GraphSAGE, 5.09% on BlogCatalog/GIN). Against the Random baseline, GOttack improves misclassification by 20–50 percentage points across nearly all settings, confirming that the orbit-based node selection is critical to attack efficacy.

## Relation to Prior Work

GOttack departs from the dominant paradigm in GNN adversarial attacks—gradient-based candidate selection (as in Nettack, FGA, SGA, PRBCD)—by replacing it with a topology-driven approach grounded in graphlet orbit theory. It builds on Zügner & Günnemann's incremental adjacency-matrix update to avoid full recomputation, and draws conceptual inspiration from the Mapper philosophy in Topological Data Analysis. The key positioning is that no prior method had identified equivalence classes of nodes via graph orbits to reduce the discrete search space for structural attacks; GOttack introduces this as a principled, pre-computable candidate filter that shrinks the attack search to roughly 23% of all nodes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals that a small topological subset of nodes (orbits 15 and 18, the 'periphery') is a universal soft spot exploited by multiple gradient-based attacks, reframing GNN robustness as a topological property rather than purely a feature-space one. This opens a path toward topology-aware defenses (e.g., down-weighting periphery-orbit neighbors in aggregation) and suggests that orbit structure may be a useful diagnostic for GNN vulnerability. Limitations include the assumption of full graph and label access, fixed attack budgets that may not reflect real-world constraints, and modest performance margins over strong baselines like SGA in several settings. Open questions include whether the orbit-15/18 vulnerability persists under adaptive defenses and whether the approach generalizes to heterophilic or dynamic graphs.
