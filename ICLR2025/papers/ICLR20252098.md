## Problem

Transformers empirically succeed or fail at length generalization depending on the task, yet no theoretical framework explains why. The RASP-L Conjecture proposed a link between expressiveness in a restricted language and length generalization, but lacked a formal proof and a fully formalized language for proving non-expressiveness. This paper addresses both gaps by introducing a rigorous identifiability-in-the-limit framework and settling expressiveness for many concrete algorithmic and formal language problems.

## Input / Output

The framework analyzes causal transformers on token sequences from a finite alphabet, with either learned absolute positional encodings (APE) or no positional encodings (NoPE), where inputs are presented at random offsets within a longer context window. The idealized inference procedure fits a transformer with context window n to exactly match the target function on all inputs of length ≤ n/2 while minimizing a norm-based regularizer, then tests whether the same transformer generalizes to length n. The output is a provable length generalization guarantee (or a proof of failure) for a given function, characterized by expressiveness in Limit Transformers or C-RASP programs.

## Method

The core technical object is the Limit Transformer, which reparameterizes a sequence of transformers operating on increasingly long inputs into a single limiting object by expressing all computations as product functions (inner products of parameter vectors mediated by parameter matrices) whose count depends on depth, heads, and context size but crucially not on width d. The main theorem (Theorem 7) establishes that length generalization is guaranteed under an idealized norm-based regularizer if and only if the target function is expressible by a Limit Transformer satisfying PERIODIC (positional encodings repeat with bounded period) and LOCAL (positional functions in attention logits are translation-invariant and vanish beyond a bounded distance). For positive expressiveness results, they extend C-RASP with positional information and prove all C-RASP[periodic, local] programs are expressible by Limit Transformers, covering tasks like MAJORITY, Dyck-1, and aⁿbⁿcⁿ. For negative results, they prove a logarithmic communication complexity bound (Theorem 12) showing that PERIODIC and LOCAL Limit Transformers cannot express tasks like copying with repeated n-grams or n-digit addition, thereby predicting length generalization failure.

## Results

The paper's central theoretical result (Theorem 7) establishes an equivalence: a target function is expressible by a Limit Transformer satisfying PERIODIC and LOCAL if and only if the idealized Inference Procedure (fitting transformers on inputs of length ≤ n/2 while minimizing a norm-based regularizer) guarantees correct outputs at all lengths ≤ n for sufficiently large n. Empirically, the theory correctly predicts length generalization outcomes across 8 algorithmic tasks and 17 regular languages from the Bhattamishra et al. (2020) benchmark: C-RASP[periodic, local] programs were found for 13 of 17 regular languages, and APE transformers successfully generalized to test lengths 101–150 in exactly those cases. Generalization failed for the 4 languages where no C-RASP program exists (including PARITY and Σ*be*), and NoPE success was predicted by the stricter C-RASP[∅] class. The theory also explains why COPY is easy without n-gram repetition (solvable by an induction head in C-RASP[local]) but inexpressible with arbitrary repetition (logarithmic communication complexity lower bound).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper with no direct method-to-method numerical comparisons; instead, it validates its expressiveness-based predictions against prior empirical observations. The C-RASP[periodic, local] expressiveness criterion matches empirical length generalization behavior on all 17 regular languages from Bhattamishra et al. (2020), where LSTMs succeed across the board but transformers show task-dependent failures. The paper notes that C-RASP expressiveness performs substantially better than circuit complexity (TC⁰ membership) and standard regular-language complexity measures in predicting which tasks transformers will length-generalize on (Appendix Figures 3–4). For algorithmic tasks, the theory replicates and explains prior findings: COPY with repetition fails (matching Jelassi et al. 2024 and Zhou et al. 2024a), while COPY without repetition succeeds via the induction head construction. The framework also formalizes the RASP-L Conjecture (Zhou et al. 2024a), providing the first rigorous proof that expressiveness in a restricted positional formalism predicts length generalization, and settling inexpressibility for problems (e.g., arbitrary string copying, n-digit addition) that prior work could only conjecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper formalizes and proves the RASP-L Conjecture (Zhou et al., 2024a), which had previously been supported only by empirical evidence linking length generalization to expressibility in a restricted RASP language fragment. It extends the C-RASP counting formalism (Yang & Chiang, 2024) with positional information and introduces the Limit Transformer—a novel mathematical object that distills sequences of transformers at increasing context lengths into a single limiting object with effectively finite parameters. The inference procedure draws on the classical 'identification in the limit' framework from automata learning, adapted here with a norm-based regularizer that captures the implicit bias of standard initialization. The communication complexity technique for proving inexpressibility builds on prior bounds for individual transformer layers (Sanford et al., 2023; Bhattamishra et al., 2024) but extends them to multilayer settings via the LOCAL and PERIODIC restrictions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first rigorous theoretical framework that provably predicts both success and failure of length generalization in transformers, unifying a broad range of previously unexplained empirical observations under a single formalism. The Limit Transformer and C-RASP[periodic, local] expressiveness criteria give practitioners a principled way to anticipate which tasks will length-generalize, moving the field beyond purely empirical trial-and-error. Key limitations include the idealized inference procedure (perfect fitting, no SGD dynamics, no subsampling), restriction to absolute positional encodings, and the absence of a practical bound on the threshold N₀ at which generalization kicks in. Extending the framework to other positional encoding schemes (RoPE, ALiBi), incorporating realistic training dynamics, and determining whether C-RASP[periodic, local] is complete for the class of length-generalizable functions remain important open questions.
