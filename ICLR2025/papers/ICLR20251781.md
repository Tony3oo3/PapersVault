## Problem

Existing camera control methods for video generation suffer from two coupled limitations: (1) sparse control signals such as extrinsic matrices or Plücker embeddings provide poor precision and generalization, making it difficult to match user-specified camera poses at the pixel level; and (2) they offer no mechanism to independently control the motion dynamics of scene subjects, so generated videos either freeze all content or produce uncontrolled subject motion regardless of user intent.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251781/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image (704×448), a user-defined camera trajectory (sequence of poses), and a scalar motion strength value as input. The image is lifted to a 3D RGBD point cloud via UniDepth metric depth estimation, and the camera trajectory is converted into a dense 2D point-trajectory tensor of shape (T,2,H,W) on the camera plane, which is concatenated with the tiled motion strength scalar into a (T,3,H,W) control signal. The output is a 24-frame video at 704×448 resolution where static scene elements follow the prescribed camera motion precisely while dynamic subjects move with amplitude governed by the motion strength parameter.

## Method

The core insight is a Maclaurin-based decomposition of the 3D point trajectory function into a linear term (capturing rigid camera motion) and a higher-order residual G(p,λ) (capturing subject dynamics). For camera control, the linear term is projected to 2D point trajectories in the camera coordinate system, yielding a dense, stable control signal that replaces sparse extrinsic matrices. For motion strength, the L2 norm of the time derivative of G is integrated over the image domain to produce a single scalar per frame that quantifies overall subject motion speed. A data pipeline constructs training signals from raw RGB videos using UniDepth (depth), SpatialTracker (3D point tracking), and an iterative L-BFGS procedure that separates static and dynamic regions by fitting linear trajectories. The control signal is injected via a lightweight adapter that concatenates control features with diffusion tokens before self-attention and removes them afterward, keeping the base model (MagicVideo-V2) frozen during training.

## Results

On the RealEstate10K static-scene benchmark (motion strength = 0), I2VControl-Camera achieves RotErr 0.53 and TransErr 9.72, substantially outperforming MotionCtrl (2.66 / 12.70) and CameraCtrl (1.26 / 21.60). On the movable-object dataset, the method maintains the best camera-control precision (RotErr 0.76, TransErr 6.97 at strength 0) while also achieving the best FID (91.86 at strength 600) and the widest adjustable motion range (MSC from 18.96 to 47.70), demonstrating that high control precision and vivid subject dynamics can coexist.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper re-trains both MotionCtrl and CameraCtrl under identical settings (same MagicVideo-V2 base model, same 30K-clip training set, same resolution and frame count) to ensure a fair comparison. On the static RealEstate10K benchmark, I2VControl-Camera (strength 0) reduces RotErr by 5× over MotionCtrl (0.53 vs 2.66) and 2.4× over CameraCtrl (0.53 vs 1.26), and cuts TransErr by 23% over MotionCtrl and 55% over CameraCtrl. On the movable-object dataset, the method again leads on both camera-precision metrics at every motion-strength setting, with the largest gap over CameraCtrl on TransErr (6.97 vs 11.32 at strength 0). For image quality, Ours-600 achieves the best FID (91.86) versus 98.54 (MotionCtrl) and 99.59 (CameraCtrl). The key differentiator is the MSC metric: the method spans 18.96–47.70 across strength settings, whereas MotionCtrl is fixed at 42.28 and CameraCtrl at 32.69, confirming that only I2VControl-Camera offers user-adjustable subject motion without sacrificing camera precision.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adapter-based camera control lineage (AnimateDiff's motion LoRAs → MotionCtrl's extrinsic matrix encoding → CameraCtrl's Plücker embeddings) by replacing sparse camera pose parameters with dense 2D point trajectories in the camera coordinate system, and by explicitly modeling higher-order trajectory terms to decouple subject motion from camera motion. The adapter architecture follows the concatenation-and-removal pattern from Animate Anyone (Hu, 2024), and the data pipeline repurposes off-the-shelf depth estimation (UniDepth) and 3D tracking (SpatialTracker) to construct training signals from raw RGB video. Conceptually, the key departure is the Maclaurin-expansion framing of video trajectories, which provides a principled decomposition into a linear (rigid camera) component and a residual (subject dynamics) component, rather than treating camera and subject motion as a single coupled signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The decoupling of camera control precision from subject motion dynamics addresses a practical gap: prior methods either lock subjects to the camera (static-scene bias) or offer no user-facing knob for motion amplitude. The motion strength scalar is a simple, interpretable control that could be readily integrated into production video generation pipelines. Limitations include reliance on monocular depth and tracking quality (error propagation), evaluation on only 24-frame clips at 704×448, and a single base model (MagicVideo-V2), leaving open whether the approach generalizes to longer sequences, higher resolutions, or transformer-based backbones like DiT. The authors note future extensions to drag and motion-brush controls, suggesting the trajectory-based control signal could serve as a unifying substrate for richer interaction modalities.
