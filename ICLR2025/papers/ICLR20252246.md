## Problem

Off-policy evaluation for sequential treatments with irregularly spaced observations and interventions on treatment timing remains unsolved at scale. Existing methods either discretize time (introducing arbitrary scale choices and information loss) or handle only static treatment types without modeling when treatments are applied. No scalable, model-free approach exists for estimating the causal effect of dynamic policies that specify both when and what to do in continuous time.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252246/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes observed trajectories as sequences of (time, value, event-type) tokens where event types include actions, features, outcomes, and time-gap placeholders, along with a target policy specified by a continuous-time intensity function λ^a(t|H_t) and a mark distribution π(A_t|H_t). It produces a Q-function Q(H_t; θ) that estimates the expected cumulative future outcome E_P[Y|H_t] under the target policy for any history H_t, evaluated as a scalar at each time point along the trajectory. The GPT-2 architecture encodes absolute times and inter-event gaps via continuous-time sinusoidal positional embeddings rather than discrete step indices.

## Method

EDQ replaces the fixed one-step lookahead of discrete FQE with an adaptive 'earliest disagreement' jump: for each training step, it draws a counterfactual trajectory from an augmented process that simultaneously tracks the observed treatment path and the target policy's treatment path, then identifies the first time δ where the two diverge. The Q-update label is formed from observed outcomes in (t, t+δ) plus the target-network Q-value at t+δ, making the regression model-free (no transition model needed) while naturally adapting the horizon to treatment density. The Q-function is parameterized by a GPT-2 transformer with soft-Q target updates, and training proceeds via stochastic gradient descent on randomly sampled (trajectory, time) pairs. The paper proves (Theorem 1) that the fixed point of this regression equals the true policy value under ignorability (causal validity via eliminability plus mutual independence of process increments) and overlap, and validates the approach on time-to-failure and tumor-growth simulators where EDQ outperforms discretized FQE and ERM baselines under distribution shift.

## Results

On the time-to-failure simulation (Figure 3), EDQ achieves normalized RMSE of 0.20±0.005 when λ_obs=λ_int=0.1 (matching ERM/MC's 0.20±0.007), and maintains 0.20±0.006 when λ_obs=0.5, λ_int=0.1 where ERM/MC degrades to 0.38±0.011. In the most challenging off-policy setting (λ_obs=0.1, λ_int=0.5), EDQ achieves 0.11±0.006 versus FQE's 0.31±0.01 and ERM/MC's 0.28±0.004. On the tumor-growth simulation (Figure 4), EDQ achieves 0.052±0.006 under distribution shift ((γ,β)_obs=(10,0.5) vs (γ,β)_int=(6,0.75)), outperforming FQE (0.080±0.013) and ERM/MC (0.07±0.004).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares EDQ against two baselines: ERM/MC (empirical risk minimizer / Monte-Carlo prediction) and FQE (discretized-time Fitted Q-Evaluation). ERM/MC performs comparably to EDQ when λ_obs = λ_int (no intervention), as expected since it is trained on the same distribution, but degrades substantially under off-policy settings—for example, 0.38 vs. EDQ's 0.20 when λ_obs=0.5, λ_int=0.1. FQE, which discretizes time and uses one-step Q-updates, suffers from both discretization-induced information loss and a harder optimization landscape due to noisy gradient signals from one-step-ahead targets. In the most challenging off-policy setting (λ_obs=0.1, λ_int=0.5), EDQ achieves 0.11±0.006 normalized RMSE, nearly halving FQE's error (0.31±0.01) and outperforming ERM/MC (0.28±0.004). On the tumor-growth task with distribution shift, EDQ (0.052±0.006) outperforms both FQE (0.080±0.013) and ERM/MC (0.07±0.004), though all methods show some degradation from the observational-to-interventional gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

EDQ extends the discrete-time Fitted Q-Evaluation (FQE) framework of Le et al. (2019) and the broader Q-learning tradition to continuous, irregularly sampled time by replacing the fixed one-step lookahead with an adaptive 'earliest disagreement' time between observed and target treatment trajectories. Its identifiability guarantees are grounded in the local independence and eliminability criteria of Røysland et al. (2022) for marked point processes, adapting sequential exchangeability to continuous time. The paper departs from prior large-scale causal inference methods (CRN, CT, G-Net, TE-CDE) by being simultaneously model-free, compatible with dynamic policies, and applicable to irregular times—properties no single prior method achieves—while leveraging transformer architectures with continuous-time positional embeddings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

EDQ provides the first model-free, dynamic-programming-based solution for off-policy evaluation that handles interventions on treatment timing (intensity) with irregular observation schedules, opening the door to applying high-capacity sequence models (transformers) to continuous-time causal inference in healthcare and beyond. Key limitations include the absence of censoring handling (critical for survival analysis), no bounds on errors from unobserved confounding, and validation restricted to two simulation environments without real-world clinical data. Open questions include whether the approach scales to complex multi-treatment, multi-patient-cohort settings, how it performs under partial observability, and whether policy optimization can be built on top of the EDQ value estimates.
