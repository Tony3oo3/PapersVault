## Problem

Prior privacy audits of LLMs rely on randomly generated canaries that are weakly memorized, leading to systematically underestimated privacy leakage and trivial lower bounds on ε. No existing method achieves a nontrivial privacy audit of an LLM trained with DP-SGD in the black-box setting (no shadow models, no gradient canaries, no per-iteration access), leaving a gap between the theoretical DP guarantee and what can actually be verified empirically.

## Input / Output

The method requires black-box access to a trained LLM's logprobs (not weights), a set of 1000 canaries each consisting of a prefix (random tokens for DP auditing, or test-set samples for memorization evaluation) concatenated with a short secret (1–8 tokens), and a training dataset into which half the canaries are inserted. It outputs a membership prediction per canary, a TPR at 1% FPR for memorization rate, and a confidence-interval lower bound on ε (e.g., ε≈1 at 99% CI for a model trained with theoretical ε=4). The canaries constitute only ~0.0027% of the training tokens, and the audit uses 100 adversary guesses within the Steinke et al. (2023) single-run framework.

## Method

The core insight is that canary memorizability depends critically on how the secret token is chosen: tokens with untrained embedding rows (new tokens added to the vocabulary) or tokens that are statistically unlikely given the prefix produce far more separable loss signals than random tokens. The paper introduces four secret-generation strategies of increasing adversary strength—new token canaries (guaranteed high loss via untrained embeddings), unigram canaries (least frequent tokens), bigram canaries (unlikely token pairs), and model-based canaries (least likely model continuations)—and shows that even the simplest loss-thresholding MIA (Yeom et al., 2018) combined with these canaries yields strong membership detection. For DP auditing, the paper uses random-token prefixes rather than in-distribution ones, because in-distribution prefixes create competing learning objectives that dilute the canary signal; the SFT loss (gradient only on the secret) is also critical, as including prefix loss in the MIA statistic makes the audit test noisier. Across GPT-2, Pythia, Qwen2.5, and Llama3 models, the new-token canary achieves 49.6% TPR at 1% FPR on Qwen2.5-0.5B versus 4.2% for random canaries, and recovers an empirical ε≈1 for a model trained with theoretical ε=4.

## Results

On the Qwen2.5-0.5B model, the proposed new-token canaries achieve 49.6% TPR at 1% FPR for membership inference, vastly surpassing the random canary baseline's 4.2% TPR at 1% FPR. For DP-SGD auditing at analytical ε=4, the new-token canary yields an empirical ε lower bound of 0.97 (95% CI) and 0.77 (99% CI) averaged across four training configurations (LoRA/FFT × PersonaChat/E2E), compared to 0.09 (95% CI) for random canaries. This is the first nontrivial privacy audit of an LLM in the black-box, single-run setting without shadow models or gradient access. Across six model families (GPT-2, Pythia, Qwen2.5), the new-token canary consistently outperforms random canaries, with audited ε ranging from 0.25 (Pythia-160M) to 1.29 (GPT-2-XL) at 95% CI.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the random canary approach used in prior LLM privacy evaluations (Anil et al., 2023; Gemini-Team et al., 2023), where canary secrets are randomly sampled tokens. The proposed new-token canaries dominate this baseline across all settings: on Qwen2.5-0.5B, TPR at 1% FPR jumps from 4.2% (random) to 49.6% (new token), a ~12× improvement. In DP auditing at ε=4, the new-token canary achieves an average audited ε of 0.97 (95% CI) versus 0.09 for random canaries — a more than 10× gap — and is the only method that yields a nontrivial lower bound across all four training configurations. The bigram canary is competitive on PersonaChat (1.29 at 95% CI for FFT) but collapses to 0.00 on E2E, while the unigram canary provides moderate gains (0.49 average) without requiring vocabulary modification. The model-based canary (querying the model for least-likely continuations) performs well on E2E (0.74 for LoRA) but fails entirely on PersonaChat, suggesting sensitivity to the training distribution. Compared to the prior SOTA single-run audit by Steinke et al. (2023), which achieved ε≈1.3 for a ResNet on CIFAR-10, this paper's ε≈1 for GPT-2 on real text data represents the first nontrivial LLM audit in the black-box, input-space canary setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the single-run privacy auditing framework of Steinke et al. (2023) and the loss-thresholding membership inference attack of Yeom et al. (2018), but its core contribution is in canary design rather than in new attack or auditing procedures. It departs from the random-token canaries used by model developers (Anil et al., 2023; Gemini-Team et al., 2023) and from the conclusion of Duan et al. (2024) that MIAs are largely ineffective on LLMs, showing instead that the bottleneck was data selection: crafting canaries with tokens the model has never seen (new tokens, unigram/bigram-selected rare tokens) makes even the simplest loss-based attack highly effective. The canary framework itself traces back to Carlini et al. (2019, Secret Sharer), which this paper extends from random secrets to distribution-aware and vocabulary-expanding secrets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first nontrivial black-box DP audit of an LLM (empirical ε≈1 for theoretical ε=4), demonstrating that prior memorization studies using random canaries systematically underestimate privacy leakage by an order of magnitude. The practical implication is significant: model developers can run this audit alongside a standard DP-SGD training run to validate their privacy guarantees without shadow models or gradient access. Key limitations include evaluation on models up to ~1.5B parameters and small datasets (PersonaChat, E2E), and the best-performing new-token canary requires modifying the vocabulary, which may be impractical for production systems. Open questions include whether the gap between random and designed canaries persists at frontier scale, and whether in-distribution prefixes (rather than random tokens) can be made to work for DP auditing without sacrificing audit tightness.
