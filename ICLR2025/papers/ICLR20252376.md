## Problem

Existing language model-based theorem proving approaches train solely on formal (state, tactic) pairs, ignoring the informal reasoning process that humans use before each proof step. This thought process is absent from formal proof code but may be critical for learning to prove theorems, and no prior work has systematically incorporated step-level natural language rationales into formal proving.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252376/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each proof step, the model receives the current Lean proof state (goal and hypotheses rendered as text) and produces a short natural language thought (typically 1–2 sentences) followed by a Lean tactic. The Lean solver then verifies the tactic and returns the next state, forming a sequential trajectory. At inference time, the model generates K parallel trajectories of at most N steps each, ignoring illegal tactics and resampling, rather than using best-first search which performs poorly with natural language thoughts.

## Method

Lean-STaR factorizes tactic prediction as π(a_i, t_i | s_i) = π(a_i | t_i, s_i)·π(t_i | s_i), introducing a latent thought variable before each tactic. Phase 1 (Lean-CoT) uses GPT-4 as an oracle to generate retrospective thoughts conditioned on ground-truth tactics from Mathlib proofs, creating ~50K (state, thought, tactic) triples to fine-tune a base model. Phase 2 (Lean-STaR) applies expert iteration: the model samples K proof trajectories per problem, retains only those verified as correct by the Lean solver, and fine-tunes on the union of the initial thought-augmented data and the new successful trajectories. This self-training loop is repeated for two iterations, progressively improving the model's ability to generate thoughts and tactics that lead to complete proofs.

## Results

On miniF2F-test, Lean-STaR (Iter-2) with InternLM2-7B achieves 34.8% pass@32 and 36.1% pass@64, substantially outperforming the base InternLM2-7B model (30.3% with best-first search) and the SFT model (29.5% with sampling). With the stronger InternLM2-Plus-7B base model, Lean-STaR (Iter-1) reaches 45.4% pass@32 and 46.3% pass@64, surpassing the previously reported 43.4% (pass@32, best-first search) for InternLM2-Plus-7B. On the LeanDojo test set, Lean-STaR (Iter-2) achieves 39.4% pass@32 versus 29.7% for the base model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Lean-STaR outperforms all compared baselines on miniF2F-test. Against the strongest prior Lean-based methods, it improves over InternLM2-7B (30.3% search / 28.7% sampling) by 4.5–6.1 points, over CoPRA with GPT-4 (30.7%) by 4.1 points, and over Llemma-7B (26.2%) by 8.6 points. The improvement over the GPT-4 few-shot baseline (11.9%) is particularly large at +22.9 points. With the stronger InternLM2-Plus-7B base, Lean-STaR (Iter-1) achieves 45.4% pass@32 versus 43.4% for the original InternLM2-Plus-7B best-first search result and 40.9% for its sampling variant, a gain of 2.0–4.5 points. An ablation (Table 3) confirms that the gains are not solely from expert iteration: expert iteration without CoT reaches only 43.0% on InternLM2-Plus versus 45.4% for Lean-STaR, showing that thought augmentation contributes an additional ~2.4 points beyond self-training alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GPT-f paradigm for tactic prediction (Polu & Sutskever, 2020) by introducing a latent natural-language 'thought' variable before each tactic, drawing on the chain-of-thought reasoning literature (Nye et al., 2021; Wei et al., 2022) and the STaR self-training framework (Zelikman et al., 2022). The key methodological departure is retrospective rationale generation: rather than requiring human-annotated rationales (which are unavailable for formal proofs), the authors give GPT-4 the ground-truth tactic and ask it to produce a forward-looking explanation, creating the first thought-augmented theorem-proving dataset. The expert iteration phase adapts the ReST-EM / Polu et al. (2022) recipe of filtering self-generated trajectories by a binary correctness signal, but applied to the joint (thought, tactic) space. The paper also departs from the standard best-first search evaluation by proposing a parallel sampling method, motivated by the observation that beam search degrades significantly when thoughts are interleaved.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work demonstrates that informal reasoning can be a useful inductive bias for formal proving, bridging a gap between natural-language mathematical thinking and machine-checkable verification. The scalability finding—that thought-augmented models benefit more from additional sampling than direct tactic predictors—suggests a path toward inference-time compute scaling in theorem proving. Limitations include the reliance on GPT-4 for synthetic data (cost, potential bias), the modest dataset sizes (~50k initial + ~50k from expert iteration), and the CPU/IO bottleneck of the Lean ITP during expert iteration. Open questions include whether further iterations yield diminishing returns, whether the approach transfers to other proof assistants (Isabelle, Coq), and whether the sampling-based evaluation can be replaced by a learned value function that properly scores thought-augmented trajectories.
