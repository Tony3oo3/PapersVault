## Problem

Existing RNA property prediction work predominantly relies on 1D sequence-based models, overlooking the geometric context encoded in 2D secondary structures and 3D tertiary conformations. No prior study has systematically evaluated how explicit geometric encoding affects prediction quality under real-world constraints such as limited training data, partial nucleotide labeling, sequencing noise, and out-of-distribution generalization, leaving practitioners without guidance on when geometric representations are worth their computational cost.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251240/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Inputs are RNA molecules at three resolutions: 1D nucleotide strings, 2D graphs with 6-dimensional node features (one-hot nucleotide identity plus sum and mean base-pairing probabilities from EternaFold), and 3D all-atom graphs with one-hot atom identity features from RhoFold. Outputs are continuous regression values for RNA properties (degradation rates, experimental reactivity, riboswitch switching factors, or protein expression levels), evaluated via mean column-wise RMSE (MCRMSE). Datasets span nucleotide-level tasks (COVID, Ribonanza-2k) and sequence-level tasks (Tc-Riboswitches, Fungal), with sequence lengths ranging from 67 to 3,063 nucleotides.

## Method

The paper benchmarks 9 models across three families—1D Transformers (Transformer1D, RNA-FM, SpliceBERT), 2D GNNs (GCN, GAT, ChebNet, Transformer1D2D, Graph Transformer, GraphGPS), and 3D equivariant GNNs (EGNN, SchNet, FAENet, DimeNet, GVP, FastEGNN)—under five task conditions: clean prediction, limited data, partial labeling, noise robustness, and OOD generalization. The key architectural contribution is a nucleotide pooling strategy for 3D models that pools atomic features into nucleotide-level representations after a few all-atom message-passing layers, balancing fine-grained atomic resolution with a larger receptive field aligned to RNA's natural secondary structure hierarchy. This reduces 3D model MCRMSE by ~10% over unpooled variants and enables 3D models to outperform 1D baselines on shorter sequences. Spectral GNNs (ChebNet) emerge as the best 2D architecture due to their global receptive field from the first layer, overcoming the 1-WL expressivity limitation of spatial GNNs that is critical for capturing long-range nucleotide interactions.

## Results

Across four RNA property prediction datasets, 2D spectral GNN (ChebNet) achieves the best MCRMSE on all datasets: 0.279 on COVID, 0.468 on Ribonanza-2k, 0.621 on Tc-Riboswitches, and 0.973 on Fungal, representing an average ~12% RMSE reduction over the best 1D model (Transformer1D: 0.361, 0.705, 0.705, 1.417). 3D equivariant GNNs with the proposed nucleotide pooling strategy reduce MCRMSE by ~10% over their unpooled counterparts and outperform 1D models on Ribonanza-2k and Tc-Riboswitches, but all 3D models hit OOM on the large Fungal dataset. Under sequencing noise, 1D Transformer1D is most robust (14–27% MCRMSE increase), while 2D models degrade most severely (30–82%) and 3D models show intermediate sensitivity (29–56%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 9 model families across 1D (Transformer1D, RNA-FM, SpliceBERT), 2D (GCN, GAT, ChebNet, Transformer1D2D, Graph Transformer, GraphGPS), and 3D (EGNN, SchNet, FAENet, DimeNet, GVP, FastEGNN) categories. ChebNet (spectral GNN) dominates all 2D baselines, achieving 2.5% lower MCRMSE than the next-best 2D model (GAT) on average, and outperforms Transformer1D by 23–34% across datasets. Transformer1D2D, which augments attention with adjacency features, beats plain Transformer1D by ~10% on average, confirming that explicit structural encoding helps. RNA foundation models (RNA-FM, SpliceBERT) underperform the simple supervised Transformer1D on 2 of 4 datasets, consistent with recent findings that specialized biology foundation models have not yet surpassed supervised baselines. The proposed nucleotide pooling strategy improves 3D models (EGNN, SchNet, FastEGNN) by ~10% over their unpooled versions, enabling EGNN (pooling) to match Transformer1D on COVID and beat it on Ribonanza and Tc-Riboswitches. However, under sequencing noise (5–30% mutation rates), Transformer1D becomes the most robust model (14–27% MCRMSE increase), while ChebNet and other 2D/3D models degrade substantially (30–82% and 29–56% respectively), and Transformer1D requires 2–5× more training data to match the performance of 2D models in noise-free settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on RNA property prediction, which has been dominated by 1D sequence-based models (Transformers, RNA foundation models like RNA-FM and SpliceBERT), by systematically introducing and evaluating 2D graph neural networks and 3D equivariant geometric networks. Architecturally, it builds on EGNN, SchNet, and FastEGNN by introducing a nucleotide pooling strategy that pools atomic features into nucleotide-level representations, leveraging RNA's natural hierarchical structure to address the limited receptive field of all-atom 3D models. The paper departs from prior work primarily in its evaluation methodology: rather than proposing a single new model, it provides the first unified benchmark comparing 9 models across 4 datasets under 5 real-world conditions (limited data, partial labels, sequencing noise, OOD generalization), filling a gap where geometric representations for RNA property prediction had not been systematically studied.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This study provides practitioners with concrete, evidence-based guidance on when geometric context helps or hurts RNA property prediction: 2D spectral GNNs (ChebNet) offer the best overall performance with ~12% RMSE reduction over 1D models and excel in low-data regimes, while 1D Transformers remain the most robust choice under sequencing noise despite requiring 2–5× more training data. The finding that 3D models suffer up to 56% performance degradation under noise—due to compounding errors from structure prediction tools—highlights a critical practical limitation that the field has largely overlooked. Open questions include whether ensembling 1D/2D/3D models can capture complementary strengths, whether better 3D structure prediction or noise-robust architectures can close the gap, and whether high-degree steerable features (TFN, MACE, NequIP) can improve 3D RNA modeling once memory constraints are addressed.
