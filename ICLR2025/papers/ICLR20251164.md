## Problem

The subset selection problem is NP-hard and central to feature selection, transfer learning, and combinatorial optimization, yet existing stochastic approaches (Gumbel-Softmax, computation-graph frameworks, REINFORCE) either require the loss to be differentiable, lack rigorous convergence guarantees, or suffer from unbounded gradient variance. This paper addresses the gap by formulating subset selection as a continuous stochastic optimization problem that accommodates arbitrary black-box loss functions—including discrete metrics like classification accuracy—while providing the first convergence proof to a stationary point without differentiability assumptions on the loss.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a set of n elements, a target cardinality k, and a black-box loss function ℓ(C) that returns only a scalar for a concrete subset C (no gradient, no continuous extension required). It produces a weight vector w parameterizing a distribution over subsets; the final output is the best subset observed during sampling from the learned distribution. The loss is evaluated purely in a black-box fashion: at each iteration a subset is sampled, its loss is computed (e.g., by training a small model on that subset and measuring accuracy), and only the scalar loss value feeds back into the gradient estimator.

## Method

The paper proposes two distributional formulations: (P_k) uses a weighted choice-without-replacement distribution ψ_w that guarantees exactly k elements per sample, and (P_B) uses n heterogeneous Bernoulli trials φ_w whose inclusion probabilities sum to k, yielding expected cardinality k. In both cases, the subset probability is an explicit function of the weight vector w, and the key technical contribution is analytically differentiating these probabilities to obtain closed-form, unbiased stochastic gradient estimators that require no differentiability of ℓ. The weights are optimized via nonconvex Stochastic Mirror Descent with a Bregman divergence, and the authors prove relative weak convexity of the expected loss by bounding Hessian eigenvalues through a minimum-weight parameter c that trades off approximation gap against gradient norm. The resulting convergence rate to a Bregman stationary point is O(n^2.5/√T), with disjoint (rather than combinatorial) dependence on n and k, and a high-probability concentration bound on the best sampled subset's loss is also established.

## Results

On the transfer learning layer-selection task, the proposed methods CWR4 and HIB4 achieve the best test accuracy in 3 out of 4 model-dataset combinations. On ViT-SVHN, HIB4 reaches 77.78% accuracy, outperforming the random baseline URand4 (75.05%) and the deterministic Last4 (72.52%). On ResNet18-SVHN, CWR4 achieves 60.87%, a 7.55-point improvement over URand4 (53.32%). On ResNet18-CIFAR10, URand4 (74.05%) slightly edges out both proposed methods (CWR4: 71.55%, HIB4: 71.34%), while on ViT-CIFAR10 the simple Last1 baseline (91.65%) remains the strongest.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three baselines: Last1 (single last layer), Last4 (deterministic last four layers), and URand4 (uniform random 4-layer sampling with calibration-set selection). Both proposed methods (CWR4, HIB4) outperform the deterministic Last4 in all four model-dataset settings, with the largest gap on ResNet18-SVHN (+10.9 pts for CWR4). Against the random greedy baseline URand4, the proposed methods win in 3 of 4 cases: ViT-CIFAR10 (+1.3–1.4 pts), ViT-SVHN (+0.8–2.7 pts), and ResNet18-SVHN (+7.2–7.6 pts), but lose on ResNet18-CIFAR10 (URand4: 74.05% vs. CWR4: 71.55%). The Last1 baseline is competitive only on ViT-CIFAR10 (91.65%), where the task is easiest; on all other settings it is the worst or near-worst performer. The authors note that CWR4 tends to reach plateau performance faster than HIB4, likely due to its smaller search space and active exploration in early iterations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the nonconvex Stochastic Mirror Descent framework of Zhang and He (2018), adapting their Bregman stationarity measure and relative weak convexity (RWC) conditions to the discrete subset selection problem. It deliberately departs from the dominant computation-graph (CG) approaches (Sander et al. 2023, Xie and Ermon 2019, Ahmed et al. 2022, Pervez et al. 2022) by removing the differentiability requirement on the subset loss, instead deriving constructive unbiased gradient estimators from the probability distributions themselves (weighted choice without replacement and heterogeneous Bernoulli). The work also connects to REINFORCE-style policy gradients but provides explicit norm and variance bounds that prior REINFORCE analyses lack, and positions itself against combinatorial multi-armed bandits by noting the absence of intrinsic element scores.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The main impact is theoretical: to the authors' knowledge, this is the first convergence-to-stationarity proof for subset selection that does not assume a differentiable loss, opening the door to optimizing over arbitrary black-box metrics (accuracy, correlation, etc.) with formal guarantees. However, the empirical validation is limited to small-scale transfer learning (ViT/ResNet18 on CIFAR10/SVHN with k=4 layers), and the improvements over uniform random selection are modest and inconsistent across settings. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger k or n, whether the c-approximation gap is tight in practice, and whether the framework extends to other combinatorial problems (Vertex Cover, Independent Set) with challenging non-differentiable objectives.
