## Problem

Existing LLM-based idea evaluation methods are highly sensitive to prompt wording, exhibit a strong bias toward positive decisions, and struggle to detect factual errors embedded within complex research ideas. No prior work leverages the structural relationships between different components of an idea to improve evaluation fidelity, leaving a gap between the LLM's subjective judgment task and the objective decomposition of ideas into evaluable units.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253569/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a paper's title and abstract as input, treating the abstract as a proxy for the research idea. A small prompted LLM (e.g., Mistral-7B) first extracts fine-grained viewpoints from the text, which are then embedded via BERT to form a weighted viewpoint-graph. The final output is a categorical review decision (Reject, Accept Poster, Accept Oral, Accept Spotlight) derived from the graph-based prediction, with the GNN variant additionally producing a softmax probability distribution over the four classes.

## Method

GraphEval decomposes each research idea into semantically independent viewpoint-nodes using a prompted small LLM, then constructs a viewpoint-subgraph per idea by connecting each node to its top-k most similar neighbors via BERT embedding cosine similarity. These subgraphs are further linked into a global viewpoint-graph by adding top-m cross-subgraph edges, creating a scalable structure that captures inter-idea relationships. Two lightweight evaluation methods operate on this graph: GraphEval-LP applies training-free label propagation from labeled training-set nodes to unlabeled test-set nodes, while GraphEval-GNN uses a two-layer weighted GraphConv network with mean and max pooling to jointly capture global and local information for subgraph-level classification. For novelty assessment, temporal features are injected into node representations and synthetic plagiarized ideas (constructed by copying or recombining existing viewpoints) are added as negative training samples, enabling the GNN to penalize derivative work.

## Results

On the ICLR Papers dataset (4-class review decision), GraphEval-GNN achieves 76.0% accuracy and 43.59% macro F1, outperforming the strongest baseline (Fine-tuned BERT at 66.0% accuracy / 26.01% F1) by 10% accuracy and 17.6% F1. On the AI Researcher dataset (3-class), GraphEval-GNN reaches 73.33% accuracy and 67.13% macro F1, a 13.8% F1 gain over Fine-tuned BERT (53.33%). Both GraphEval variants operate at a normed cost of 0.08, comparable to the cheapest prompt-based baselines, while the most expensive baseline (Research Agent 72B) has a normed cost of 1.00.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GraphEval-GNN decisively outperforms all five baselines on both main datasets. Against the prompt-based methods (CoT, CoT-SC, ToT, Research Agent), the gap is dramatic: the best prompt-based F1 is 10.05% (Research Agent 7B) on ICLR Papers versus 43.59% for GraphEval-GNN, a gap of over 33 F1 points. The paper notes that larger LLMs (72B) actually perform worse than 7B variants across all prompt-based baselines, suggesting inherent bias in subjective evaluation tasks regardless of model scale. Against Fine-tuned BERT (DistilBERT), the closest non-LLM baseline, GraphEval-GNN still leads by 17.6% F1 on ICLR Papers (43.59% vs. 26.01%) and 13.8% F1 on AI Researcher (67.13% vs. 53.33%), attributed to the viewpoint-graph's ability to capture both local (Min/Max pooling) and global (Mean pooling) information. GraphEval-LP, the training-free variant, already surpasses Fine-tuned BERT on ICLR Papers (32.16% vs. 26.01% F1) at the same low normed cost, demonstrating the inherent value of the graph structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of prompt-based LLM evaluation (CoT, ToT, Research Agent) by reframing idea evaluation as a graph prediction problem. It draws on the label propagation algorithm (Raghavan et al., 2007) for its training-free variant and on BERT-based embedding similarity for edge construction, while leveraging small LLMs (7B) solely for the objective sub-task of viewpoint extraction rather than subjective judgment. Conceptually, it bridges the 'Graph for LLMs' research direction with the automatic idea evaluation literature, applying insights from human psychology about decomposing abstract ideas into simpler components.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a well-documented failure mode of LLM evaluators—systematic positivity bias and prompt sensitivity—by offloading the subjective judgment to graph algorithms operating on objectively extracted viewpoints. However, the experimental evidence is limited by small test sets (50 samples for ICLR, 66 for AI Researcher), making the reported 14% F1 gains harder to assess for statistical robustness. Open questions include whether the viewpoint-graph construction generalizes beyond paper abstracts to longer-form research proposals, whether the BERT-based edge weights capture truly meaningful logical relationships versus surface semantic similarity, and whether the approach scales to the thousands of submissions typical of real conference cycles.
