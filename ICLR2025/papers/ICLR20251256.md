## Problem

Existing tabular deep learning benchmarks underrepresent two conditions prevalent in industrial deployment: gradual temporal distribution drift (requiring time-based train/test splits) and feature-rich datasets produced by extensive feature engineering pipelines. The paper also identifies data leakage in 11 of 100 commonly used academic datasets, along with synthetic, untraceable, or non-tabular entries, raising questions about whether recent tabular DL advances genuinely transfer to real-world settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251256/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

TabReD comprises eight industry-grade datasets (103–986 features, 28K–423K samples after subsampling from up to 17M rows) spanning finance, e-commerce, food delivery, navigation, and weather. All datasets use time-based train/validation/test splits rather than random splits, and feature sets are substantially richer than prior benchmarks (median 261 features vs. 13–23). Tasks are binary classification (evaluated by ROC AUC) or regression (evaluated by RMSE), with inputs consisting of mixed numerical and categorical tabular features and outputs being a single probability or continuous value per instance.

## Method

The paper introduces TabReD, a benchmark of eight datasets (four from Kaggle competitions with competition-derived feature engineering, four newly released from production ML systems at a large tech company), all with canonical time-based splits and no identified leakage. The authors evaluate 15+ methods—GBDTs (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost), DL architectures (MLP, FT-Transformer, SNN, DCNv2, ResNet, Trompt), retrieval-based models (TabR, ModernNCA), improved training recipes (long training with augmentations, reconstruction loss), and ensembles—using Optuna hyperparameter tuning over 15 random seeds. Key findings: MLP with numerical feature embeddings (MLP-PLR) and GBDTs achieve the best average performance, while retrieval-based models and improved training methodologies that show gains on academic benchmarks fail to transfer. Ablation experiments isolating temporal shift (random vs. time-based splits) and feature richness (all features vs. top-20) demonstrate that both conditions significantly alter method rankings, with XGBoost's margin over MLPs diminishing under temporal splits and TabR's performance degrading on feature-rich data.

## Results

On the TabReD benchmark (8 industry-grade datasets with time-based splits and 100–1000+ features), MLP-PLR ensemble achieves the best average rank (2.4±1.5), followed by XGBoost (2.9±1.5) and LightGBM (3.1±1.5). Among pure DL methods, MLP-PLR (numerical feature embeddings) leads with rank 3.8±1.4, while FT-Transformer is the runner-up at 4.8±1.6. Retrieval-based models (TabR: 6.0±2.2, ModernNCA: 5.6±1.6) and improved training recipes (MLP aug. rec.: 6.8±2.8) that showed strong gains on academic benchmarks fail to transfer, with Trompt (5.4±2.1) also underperforming despite its success on the Grinsztajn et al. benchmark.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GBDT methods (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost) dominate the top ranks, with XGBoost achieving the best single-dataset results on HomeCredit Default (AUC 0.8670) and Sberbank Housing (RMSE 0.2419), though its margin over MLP-PLR narrows under temporal splits compared to random splits. MLP-PLR (numerical feature embeddings from Gorishniy et al. 2022) is the strongest pure DL method, outperforming FT-Transformer (rank 3.8 vs. 4.8) and all other architectures, confirming that embeddings transfer well to feature-rich, temporally shifting data. Ensembling consistently improves MLP-PLR (rank 3.8→2.4) and is the single best overall configuration. In contrast, retrieval-based models TabR and ModernNCA, which outperformed GBDT baselines by a sizeable margin on academic benchmarks, drop to ranks 6.0 and 5.6 on TabReD, with TabR only excelling on the Weather dataset. Improved training methodologies (MLP aug. and MLP aug. rec. from Rubachev et al. 2022) show no benefit or even harm (e.g., MLP aug. rec. drops to AUC 0.7453 on HomeCredit vs. 0.8545 for plain MLP), and Trompt, which beat FT-Transformer on the Grinsztajn et al. benchmark, fails to generalize (rank 5.4, slower than both MLP and FT-Transformer). Distribution shift robustness methods (DeepCORAL, DFR) also fail to improve over the MLP baseline, consistent with prior findings on other shift types.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a corrective to the existing tabular DL benchmarking landscape (Tabzilla, Grinsztajn et al. 2022, Gorishniy et al. 2024), which it shows relies heavily on OpenML/UCI datasets with quality issues (11/100 have data leakage, 25 are non-tabular, 13 are synthetic/untraceable). It departs from distribution-shift benchmarks like TableShift and WildTab by focusing on the more ubiquitous *gradual* temporal shift rather than extreme OOD, and from time-series benchmarks by emphasizing feature-rich settings where temporal features do not dominate. The experimental protocol (tuning, evaluation, statistical testing) is inherited from Gorishniy et al. (2024), while the dataset sourcing strategy draws on Kaggle competition feature-engineering solutions and proprietary production ML pipelines at Yandex.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TabReD fills a concrete gap: no prior benchmark simultaneously provides time-based splits, 100–1000+ features, and verified absence of leakage, making it the first realistic proxy for industrial tabular deployment. The finding that retrieval-based models (TabR, ModernNCA) and long-training recipes that outperform GBDT on academic benchmarks fail to transfer under temporal shift and feature richness is a valuable negative result that should temper enthusiasm for these methods in production. Open questions include why retrieval degrades under gradual drift (hypothesized to be violated train-test similarity assumptions), whether continual learning or drift-adaptive methods can close the gap, and how to handle the lack of feature-name metadata that limits LLM-augmented approaches. The benchmark is biased toward large-sample, feature-rich industrial tasks and does not cover medicine, science, or social domains.
