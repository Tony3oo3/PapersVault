## Problem

Current open vocabulary semantic segmentation (OVS) evaluation treats any category mismatch as a pixel-level error, ignoring that in open-world settings semantically similar labels (e.g., 'sofa' vs 'couch') may both be valid. As the inference vocabulary expands, model performance drops sharply under this rigid protocol, conflating genuine errors with legitimate ambiguous predictions and obscuring true model capability.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a vocabulary set of text queries as input, but instead of producing a single argmax segmentation map, it generates per-category binary masks by thresholding the class posterior at multiple values (0.1–0.9). The evaluation outputs a confusion matrix, an ambiguous vocabulary adjacency matrix (counting high-IoU out-matched pairs), and three scalar metrics—front, back, and err—computed at an automatically selected optimal threshold, rather than a single mIoU score.

## Method

The paper proposes a mask-wise evaluation protocol that replaces pixel-wise argmax with threshold-based binary mask generation per category, then uses bipartite matching to identify out-matched pairs where predicted and ground-truth masks overlap above an IoU threshold (τ_AV = 0.8) but carry different category labels. From these out-matched pairs, an ambiguous vocabulary graph is constructed and analyzed via modularity-maximizing community discovery to reveal clusters of visually similar, frequently confused categories. The protocol defines three metrics—front (foreground recognition IoU), back (background recognition IoU), and err (average error proportion)—with the optimal threshold selected by maximizing √(front² + (1−err)²). Additionally, the paper shows that randomly discarding a proportion of non-target vocabularies during training (aligning with a language-likelihood term in the MAP objective) reduces ambiguity and improves OVS model performance.

## Results

Under the proposed mask-wise evaluation protocol, the best-performing OVS models achieve front scores of 68.46 (CAT-Seg on PC59), 46.51 (MAFT+ on ADE150), 31.89 (MAFT+ on PC459), and 28.72 (MAFT+ on ADE847) with dataset-specific vocabulary. The back metric consistently exceeds 90% across most datasets, confirming reliable non-target rejection. Critically, when the vocabulary set expands to a joint-dataset setting (1,086 vocabularies), pixel-wise mIoU drops dramatically (e.g., SAN on ADE847: 12.40 → 3.20), while the mask-wise front metric remains stable (22.84 → 21.01), demonstrating the protocol's robustness to vocabulary ambiguity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates four OVS models (SAN, CAT-Seg, SED, MAFT+) under both the traditional pixel-wise mIoU and the proposed mask-wise protocol. Under the mask-wise protocol, CAT-Seg achieves the highest front score on PC59 (68.46) and ADE150 (45.74), while MAFT+ leads on ADE847 (28.72) and SED on PC459 (31.41). Compared to alternative open-world evaluation metrics, the proposed front metric consistently yields higher scores than both SG-IoU (Liu et al., 2024) and Open-IoU (Zhou et al., 2023) — e.g., on ADE150, MAFT+ scores 46.51 (front) vs. 37.08 (SG-IoU) and 36.10 (vanilla mIoU) — because mask-wise matching credits semantically ambiguous but spatially correct predictions. The most striking comparison is stability under vocabulary expansion: with the joint-dataset vocabulary (1,086 words), pixel-wise mIoU for SAN on ADE847 collapses from 12.40 to 3.20, whereas the mask-wise front metric only drops from 22.84 to 21.01, confirming that the protocol is far less sensitive to ambiguous vocabulary growth.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not introduce a new segmentation architecture but instead challenges the evaluation paradigm inherited from closed-set segmentation. All prior OVS methods (SAN, CAT-Seg, SED, MAFT+) rely on pixel-wise argmax mIoU, which treats any category mismatch as a hard error regardless of semantic similarity. The authors repurpose bipartite matching and graph community detection—tools from classical clustering and confusion-matrix analysis—to construct a mask-wise evaluation protocol and an ambiguous vocabulary graph. Conceptually, the paper also extends the MAP training objective by treating the vocabulary set as a random variable (Eq. 3), a departure from prior work that conditions on a fixed vocabulary.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that OVS model scores drop dramatically (e.g., SAN on ADE847: 12.40 → 3.20) when the inference vocabulary expands from dataset-specific to a joint 1,086-word set exposes a fundamental instability in the current evaluation protocol that the community has not adequately addressed. The ambiguous vocabulary graph provides a concrete diagnostic for identifying synonym clusters (e.g., 'sofa'/'couch'/'armchair') in standard datasets, suggesting that annotation ambiguity is a first-order confound in OVS benchmarking. Open questions include whether the mask-wise protocol will gain community adoption, whether the non-target vocabulary removal strategy generalizes to larger vocabulary scales or multi-modal settings, and how to handle genuinely ambiguous cases where no single ground-truth label is correct.
