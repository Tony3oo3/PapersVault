## Problem

Existing diffusion models for mixed-type tabular data rely on discrete (multinomial) diffusion for categorical features, which does not scale to high-cardinality features and cannot represent uncertainty between categories at intermediate timesteps. Moreover, the type-specific diffusion processes and their losses are neither aligned nor balanced, and noise schedules are simply copied from the image domain despite the high heterogeneity in feature distributions, cardinalities, and signal-to-noise sensitivities inherent to tabular data.

## Input / Output

The model takes Gaussian noise (sampled from N(0, σ²_max I)) as input and generates full rows of mixed-type tabular data containing both continuous scalars and categorical values. Continuous features are preprocessed with a QuantileTransformer and standardized to zero mean/unit variance; categorical features are mapped to 16-dimensional L2-normalized embeddings. For classification-task datasets, the model conditions on the target label (y), enabling conditional generation. Sampling uses a deterministic Euler ODE solver with 200 steps, and the final step commits each categorical embedding to a discrete category via argmax.

## Method

CDTD unifies the diffusion processes for continuous and categorical features by applying Gaussian noise to both the raw continuous values and the learned categorical embeddings, combining denoising score matching (MSE loss) for continuous features with score interpolation (cross-entropy loss) for categorical features. To prevent unintended importance weighting between feature types, the paper introduces calibrated losses that equalize expected loss to 1 at the terminal timestep t=1 (using feature-specific entropy Z_j for categorical features), along with a tailored output-layer initialization and a learned time-dependent normalization of the average diffusion loss. The key architectural innovation is adaptive type- or feature-specific noise schedules parameterized by a domain-adapted logistic distribution (three parameters: location μ_k, steepness ν_k, and scale γ_k), which are learned end-to-end via timewarping and allow the model to allocate capacity to the noise levels most relevant for each feature. The score model uses a TabDDPM-style architecture (five fully-connected layers, ~3M parameters) with the EDM parameterization, and the paper is the first to enable classifier-free guidance directly in data space for mixed-type tabular data.

## Results

Across 11 diverse tabular datasets, CDTD (per type) achieves the best average rank on RMSE (2.6), F1 (2.3), L2 distance of correlation matrices (2.0), and detection score (1.6), and is second-best on AUC (2.7) and WD (3.2). The detection score advantage is particularly pronounced: CDTD (per type) reaches a rank of 1.6 versus 4.8 for the next-best diffusion model (TabDDPM), indicating generated samples are far harder to distinguish from real data. Type-specific adaptive noise schedules consistently outperform both single and per-feature variants, confirming that accounting for heterogeneity at the data-type level is the sweet spot for mixed-type tabular generation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CDTD (per type) dominates the diffusion-based baselines (TabDDPM, CoDi, TabSyn) across nearly all metrics, with the largest margins on detection score (rank 1.6 vs. 4.8–7.9) and L2 distance of correlation matrices (2.0 vs. 6.0–7.1), confirming that the unified Gaussian noise process and score interpolation capture feature correlations far more accurately than multinomial diffusion or latent-space approaches. Against non-diffusion baselines, CDTD (per type) outperforms CTGAN, TVAE, and SMOTE on all ML-efficiency metrics (RMSE, F1, AUC) and detection score, while ARF retains a narrow edge on JSD (1.2 vs. 2.5–2.8) due to its random-forest inductive bias for categorical distributions. Notably, TabSyn—a latent-space diffusion model—performs considerably worse than CDTD and often worse than TabDDPM, which operates directly in data space, underscoring the benefit of CDTD's unified continuous formulation. The per-feature noise schedule variant (CDTD per feature) achieves the best DCR (3.0) but is outperformed by per-type on most other metrics, suggesting that per-feature schedules impose too many constraints and require more training to converge.

## Relation to Prior Work

CDTD unifies score matching (Hyvärinen, 2005) for continuous features with score interpolation (Dieleman et al., 2022) for categorical features, pushing both into a single Gaussian diffusion process in embedding space. This departs from prior tabular diffusion models (TabDDPM, CoDi) that rely on multinomial diffusion for categorical features, which is discrete, does not scale to high cardinality, and prevents direct comparison of noise schedules across data types. The adaptive noise schedule design, parameterized by a domain-adapted logistic distribution, extends the timewarping idea from Dieleman et al. (2022) with a more robust closed-form functional form and introduces type- or feature-specific schedules for the first time in tabular generation. The architecture is deliberately aligned with TabDDPM to isolate the contribution of the unified diffusion framework and loss calibration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CDTD is the first mixed-type tabular diffusion model to operate entirely in data space with a unified Gaussian noise process, enabling direct application of image-domain advances like classifier-free guidance without a latent encoder. The finding that type-specific (rather than feature-specific) noise schedules yield the best sample quality is practically important: it suggests that the dominant source of heterogeneity in tabular data is at the data-type level, not the individual-feature level, which simplifies deployment. Open questions include adaptation to imputation (requiring separate training), scalability to time-series tabular data, and whether the embedding-based approach for categorical features remains effective at very high cardinality beyond the ~3000 categories tested. The paper also cautions against over-trusting synthetic data for inference, a relevant concern as generative models become more widely adopted in data science workflows.
