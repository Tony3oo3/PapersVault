## Problem

While large language models exhibit signs of compositional reasoning, the complexity and ambiguity of natural language training data make it difficult to scientifically validate this ability or dissect its underlying mechanisms. This paper asks: under what conditions can Transformers connect fragmented knowledge learned in training to produce complete multi-step reasoning chains at test time, and what internal mechanisms enable this?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251036/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At test time, the model receives a few-shot prompt containing k complete reasoning chains (length N, out-of-distribution relative to training) followed by the first vertex-value pair of a new chain, and must autoregressively generate all remaining vertices in correct order with their correct values. Training data consists only of shorter child chains (length M < N) interspersed with noise vertices drawn from the full alphabet, so the model never sees the full chain or the complete vertex ordering during training. The downstream processing wraps raw vertex-value sequences into natural-language-like sentences with contextual tokens, equation markers, and commas to simulate realistic tokenization.

## Method

The paper introduces the FTCT (Fragmented at Training, Chained at Testing) synthetic task built on directed causal graphs, where training exposes only short child chains with noise and testing requires connecting them into full-length chains. The key theoretical contribution is proving that a 2-layer Transformer can simulate an underlying program—comprising in-context learning (copying vertex order from few-shot examples via induction heads) and parent retrieving (attending to the parent vertex's value to compute the child's value via the edge operation)—that simultaneously minimizes both training and testing loss. Empirically, they show a phase transition in compositional reasoning at a relative knowledge ratio λ = M/N ≥ 0.3, and that multi-layer attention (≥2 layers, ≥2 heads) is necessary: single-layer Transformers fail at vertex-order imitation while MLPs fail at sparse value retrieval. Attention heatmap analysis and linear probing confirm that trained Transformers instantiate induction heads for in-context learning and focused attention on parent vertices for value computation.

## Results

On the FTCT task (causal depth N=5, child chain length M=3), a 2-layer 2-head Transformer (48M params) achieves near-perfect compositional reasoning with 100% whole-chain accuracy, 100% testing vertices accuracy, and 100% testing values accuracy under optimal few-shot CoT prompting. A phase transition in compositional reasoning emerges at a relative knowledge ratio λ = M/N ≥ 0.3, where testing accuracy jumps from near-zero to approaching 1.0. Single-layer Transformers (1L1H) collapse to 2.2% whole-chain accuracy, and MLPs (even 4-layer, 145M params) achieve only 0.2% whole-chain accuracy, confirming that multi-layer attention is essential.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but instead ablates model architecture to identify what enables compositional reasoning. The 2L2H Transformer (48M params) achieves 100% whole-chain accuracy, outperforming the larger 3L3H Transformer (54M, 99.6%) and dramatically surpassing all simpler architectures. Reducing to a single head (2L1H) drops whole-chain accuracy to 76.6%, while a single layer (1L1H) collapses to 2.2%, showing that both depth and multi-head attention are necessary. MLPs with 3× more parameters (143–145M) achieve only 0.2–2.2% whole-chain accuracy, confirming that the attention mechanism—not raw capacity—is critical for capturing sparse value relationships in noisy contexts. Notably, even the weakest Transformer (1L1H) achieves 100% testing values accuracy, indicating that value deduction requires only minimal attention, while vertex-order imitation (the harder sub-task) demands at least 2 layers and 2 heads.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the compositional generalization literature (Lake & Baroni, 2018; Keysers et al., 2019; Hupkes et al., 2020) by introducing an explicit multi-step reasoning requirement via Chain-of-Thought prompting, rather than direct answer output. It builds on the induction head mechanism identified by Elhage et al. (2021) and Olsson et al. (2022) as the substrate for in-context learning, and on the associative memory construction of Bietti et al. (2024) for the theoretical expressivity proof. Unlike Prystawski et al. (2024), which uses Bayesian networks to study CoT locality, this paper adds contextual noise, parent-child causal structure, and a controlled training-testing similarity parameter (relative knowledge ratio) to isolate when and why compositional reasoning emerges.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a rare controlled, interpretable setting where the emergence of compositional reasoning can be pinned to specific architectural components (induction heads for vertex-order imitation, attention assignment for value retrieval) and a concrete data-similarity threshold (λ ≥ 0.3). The theoretical result that a 2-layer Transformer suffices to simulate the underlying program is clean and actionable. However, the findings are confined to small GPT-2-scale models on synthetic data, and the authors explicitly caution that conclusions may not transfer directly to large LLMs trained on natural language. Open questions include whether the phase transition and mechanism persist at scale, and how the noise-vertex design interacts with the more complex statistical structure of real corpora.
