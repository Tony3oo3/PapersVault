## Problem

RLHF-fine-tuned LLMs can over-rely on aligned preferences and prompt shortcuts without genuine self-knowledge, leading to hallucinations and blind trust in external guidance. Prior evaluation work revealed sensitivity to trigger words and unconditional trust in user-provided answers, but failed to identify which specific local information LLMs over-rely on or whether they can maintain correct answers under varying external guidance. This paper asks: can we systematically determine whether an LLM truly 'knows what it knows' and which prompt features it shortcuts on?

## Input / Output

The RoSe strategy takes a multiple-choice question and constructs a three-step prompt sequence where step-3 injects a role (teacher, classmate, judge, or lawyer), a strong reminder phrase ('the answer is'), and a cue (ground-truth or random answer letter). At each step, the LLM must produce a structured response containing reasoning analysis, a letter answer, and a verbalized confidence score (percentage or descriptor). The double-calibrated strategy then filters across four role×cue prompt settings to extract only samples where the LLM's answer is correct (or self-corrected) AND its verbalized confidence is consistent or increasing across steps, yielding well-calibrated (reasoning, answer, confidence) triples for fine-tuning.

## Method

The RoSe strategy probes LLM self-knowledge through a three-step self-reflection pipeline: step-1 elicits an initial answer, step-2 prompts reflection on step-1, and step-3 introduces role-guided guidance with a strong reminder and cue information to test whether the LLM adheres to its own reasoning or capitulates to external signals. The key experimental design varies three factors—role (teacher/classmate/judge/lawyer/none), presence of strong reminder, and cue type (truth/random)—across nine prompt settings to isolate which local features drive LLM behavior. Building on the finding that LLMs over-rely on strong reminders and trust authority roles, the double-calibrated strategy extracts fine-tuning data by requiring both accuracy consistency (correct or self-corrected answers) and confidence consistency (stable or increasing verbalized confidence) across all four role-guided prompt settings simultaneously. The extracted data is used for thought-based and calibrated fine-tuning of open-source LLMs (LLaMA3-8B, Qwen-7B, Spark-13B) via LoRA, aligning the Chain-of-Thought reasoning process with corresponding confidence levels at each reflection step to reduce reliance on prompt shortcuts.

## Results

On EG-QA, GPT-4 achieves 97.05% overall accuracy with a strong reminder and truth cue (no role), but drops to 87.11% when the cue is random—a 9.58% decline—revealing heavy over-reliance on the 'answer is' shortcut. On the harder legal JEC-QA, the same pattern is amplified: accuracy falls from 75.79% (reminder + truth) to 36.76% (reminder + random), a 35.15% drop. After fine-tuning with the double-calibrated strategy, Qwen-7B on EG-QA ID improves from 74.29% to 91.20% overall accuracy (no guidance) and, critically, its sensitivity to random cues shrinks from Δ=0.1416 (base) to Δ=0.0440 (fine-tuned) under teacher guidance, confirming reduced reliance on local prompt shortcuts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but instead contrasts prompt settings and base vs. fine-tuned models. In the evaluation, GPT-4 with no role but a strong reminder + truth cue (97.05%) outperforms all role-guided settings (Teacher+truth: 94.58%, Classmate+truth: 93.43%), showing that role guidance actually reduces accuracy when the cue is correct but mitigates the damage when the cue is random (Teacher+random: 90.93% vs. w/o+random: 87.11%). On JEC-QA, the authority bias is clearest: Judge+truth (58.17%) beats Lawyer+truth (49.80%), and both beat w/o+truth (75.79% only because the reminder shortcut is strongest without a role). For fine-tuning, Qwen-7B improves from 74.29% to 91.20% overall accuracy on EG-QA ID, and its Δ (sensitivity to random vs. truth cue) drops from 0.1416 to 0.0440 under teacher guidance, while Spark-13B improves from 64.65% to 80.56% overall accuracy with a similar reduction in cue sensitivity. LLaMA3-8B shows the smallest gains, consistent with its weaker bilingual capability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of LLM self-knowledge evaluation (Kadavath et al., 2022; Yin et al., 2023b) and verbalized confidence calibration (Lin et al., 2022) by introducing role-guided prompts inspired by educational psychology, where the LLM is treated as a student receiving teacher or peer guidance. Unlike prior perturbation-based evaluations (Tang et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024) that probe positional or trigger-word biases, or interactive debate-style tests (Wang et al., 2023a) that reveal blind trust in users, RoSe systematically varies role identity, strong reminder phrasing, and cue correctness to isolate which specific prompt features LLMs over-rely on. The double-calibrated fine-tuning strategy repurposes verbalized confidence as a data-quality filter, building on the CoT reasoning framework (Wei et al., 2022) but adding a confidence-consistency criterion that prior self-correction methods (Shinn et al., 2023; Madaan et al., 2023) did not enforce.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LLMs exhibit a strong authority bias—trusting 'teacher' or 'judge' roles more than 'classmate' or 'lawyer'—has direct implications for real-world deployment where users may frame instructions with perceived authority, potentially steering models toward incorrect answers. The double-calibrated strategy offers a practical, annotation-free pipeline for improving open-source LLMs, though it depends on access to a capable closed-source model (GPT-4) for data generation, limiting its applicability. Open questions include whether the authority bias and shortcut reliance generalize beyond multiple-choice QA to open-ended generation, whether the EG-QA bilingual (Chinese-English) setting constrains transferability, and whether the 'com' metric (a novel F1-style accuracy-completion score) will be adopted as a standard evaluation measure.
