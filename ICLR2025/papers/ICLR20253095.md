## Problem

Existing neuro-symbolic embodied planning systems rely on pre-defined, expert-level symbolic knowledge (action preconditions and effects), which breaks down in open-domain environments where object states, goal conditions, and action affordances change unpredictably. The paper addresses how an embodied agent can continually generalize and refine actionable knowledge from only a small set of episodic experiences, without access to a complete domain model, to handle dynamic and previously unseen situations.

## Input / Output

NESYC takes as input a natural language instruction (rephrased via ChatGPT to test linguistic generalization), a stream of environment observations (text descriptions in ALFWorld/VirtualHome/Minecraft; RGB, depth, and segmentation masks from stereo and wrist cameras in RLBench and the real-world Franka arm setup), and a small seed set of 5–15 episodic experience trajectories (roughly 7% of evaluation episodes) that record state–action–affordance transitions. It outputs a sequence of primitive actions (e.g., grasp, rotate, move, open gripper) executed step-by-step, alongside an iteratively refined set of generalized logic rules (in ASP/ILP form) encoding action preconditions and effects that persist and update across episodes.

## Method

NESYC emulates the hypothetico-deductive model through two interleaved phases. In Rule Reformulation (Phase i), a contrastive generality improvement scheme grounded in ILP iteratively generates hypotheses via an LLM prompted with a θ-subsumption Chain-of-Thought, then validates them with a clingo ASP solver that scores each hypothesis using an HI metric (weighted TPR minus FPR over positive and negative examples), feeding back failures to refine the next hypothesis batch. In Rule Application (Phase ii), an ASP-based task planner computes action plans from the current generalized knowledge R, the parsed current state, and the goal; a memory-based error handler monitors each executed action's affordance, and upon detecting a failure (c_t = 0) it appends the trajectory to working memory and triggers re-entry into Phase i, where the expanded experience set T ∪ M drives further knowledge refinement. The key distinction from prior neuro-symbolic planners (e.g., CLMASP) is that the symbolic knowledge is never fixed: it is continually inductively generalized from experience and deductively validated, allowing NESYC to maintain high task success even under High Dynamic settings where CLMASP's static rules collapse.

## Results

NESYC achieves task success rates (SR) of 82.9% (Static), 78.9% (Low Dynamic), and 70.7% (High Dynamic) on ALFWorld; 82.3%, 79.6%, and 77.5% on VirtualHome; 92.2%, 91.1%, and 87.8% on Minecraft; and 85.5%, 81.5%, and 79.0% on RLBench. Compared to the strongest multi-agent baseline AutoGen, NESYC improves SR by 33.6% on ALFWorld, 43.9% on VirtualHome, 53.7% on Minecraft, and 52.6% on RLBench (High Dynamic setting). In Static settings, the neuro-symbolic baseline CLMASP (which uses pre-defined expert knowledge) slightly outperforms NESYC, but NESYC maintains robust performance as environmental dynamics increase, while CLMASP degrades sharply (e.g., dropping to 0% SR on RLBench High Dynamic).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LLM-based planners (LLM-planner, ReAct, Reflexion), NESYC achieves substantially higher SR across all environments and dynamic settings; for example, on ALFWorld High Dynamic, NESYC reaches 70.7% SR versus 7.3% (LLM-planner), 18.7% (ReAct), and 21.1% (Reflexion). Against the multi-agent framework AutoGen, NESYC improves SR by 33.6–53.7% depending on the benchmark, with the largest gap on Minecraft (87.8% vs. 25.6% in High Dynamic). Against the closest neuro-symbolic competitor CLMASP, the comparison is nuanced: CLMASP outperforms NESYC in Static settings (e.g., 88.5% vs. 82.9% on ALFWorld) because it leverages pre-defined expert-level symbolic knowledge, but CLMASP degrades catastrophically under dynamics (dropping to 0% SR on RLBench and VirtualHome High Dynamic), while NESYC maintains 70–80%+ SR by continually refining its knowledge. ProgPrompt (imperative-programming neuro-symbolic baseline) is compared only in the robustness-to-incompleteness analysis (Table 2), where NESYC achieves 84.9% SR on imperfect experience sets versus 48.5% for ProgPrompt. Overall, NESYC's key advantage is robustness to environmental dynamics and experience incompleteness, enabled by its contrastive generality improvement and memory-based monitoring schemes.

## Relation to Prior Work

NESYC extends the neuro-symbolic embodied planning line (CLMASP, ProgPrompt, LLM+P) by replacing their reliance on pre-defined expert-level symbolic knowledge with an ILP-based knowledge generalization loop: an LLM generates hypotheses via θ-subsumption-guided prompting, and an ASP solver (clingo) validates them against positive/negative examples in a contrastive scoring scheme. The hypothetico-deductive framing from philosophy of science provides the conceptual backbone for the iterative hypothesis–falsification cycle, while the memory-based monitoring scheme (triggering reformulation on action failure) departs from prior neuro-symbolic systems that treat their symbolic programs as static. Among LLM-based multi-agent baselines (AutoGen, ReAct, Reflexion), NESYC is distinguished by its explicit separation of inductive knowledge acquisition from deductive plan computation, rather than interleaving reasoning and acting in a single LLM loop.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete gap: existing neuro-symbolic planners collapse in open-domain or dynamic settings because their symbolic knowledge is fixed, whereas NESYC's continual reformulation maintains performance (e.g., 70.7% SR on ALFWorld High Dynamic vs. 23.8% for CLMASP). The real-world Franka Emika experiment—transferring RLBench experiences to handle previously unseen Hanoi blocks—demonstrates practical transferability beyond simulation. Key limitations include dependence on capable LLMs (Llama-3-8B shows minimal improvement), the computational overhead of iterative ASP solving, and the finding that LLM-generated guidance in the error handler can degrade performance relative to human feedback, leaving open the question of when and how to trust LLM self-correction in safety-critical robotic contexts.
