## Problem

Bayesian regression with high-dimensional fMRI data faces two intertwined challenges: pre-specified spatial correlation structures (e.g., Gaussian Process kernels) may misrepresent true brain connectivity patterns, and traditional MCMC sampling is computationally intractable at the scale of 3D brain images (tens of thousands of voxels). Existing methods either require costly cross-validation to tune smoothness or sacrifice uncertainty quantification for speed, leaving no principled way to identify active voxels with statistical confidence.

## Input / Output

For SonI regression, the method takes a 3D fMRI volume (p voxels, e.g., 47,636 in the ABCD study) as the high-dimensional predictor, a scalar outcome (e.g., standardized IQ score), and tabular confounders (age, gender, race, income, parental education). For IonS, the fMRI volume is the outcome and a scalar exposure (e.g., parental education level) is the predictor. The output is a sparse, spatially correlated coefficient map β(s) or α(s) over all voxels, a per-voxel Posterior Inclusion Probability (PIP) derived from the mixing weights of a three-component truncated-normal posterior, and region-level summaries of significant activation. The PIP enables downstream FDR control without requiring full MCMC posterior samples.

## Method

The ST-CAR prior places a soft-thresholded Gaussian observation β(s_j) ~ N(T_ν(μ_j), σ_β²) on top of a latent CAR-structured parameter μ, where μ_j | μ_{N(j)} ~ N(ρ_j Σ_k b_{jk} μ_k, τ_{μ,j}²). The variance σ_β is annealed to zero across iterations, forcing β to converge to the sparse latent μ and inducing sparsity without an explicit spike-and-slab. Proposition 1 shows the conditional posterior of μ_j is a mixture of three truncated normals (positive, zero, negative regions), whose mixing probabilities directly yield the PIP as P_j⁺ + P_j⁻. Two variational inference algorithms are developed: CAVI exploits the conditional conjugacy for exact coordinate updates (preferred for low-SNR SonI), while SSVI replaces the full-data gradient with a stochastic subsample gradient in the spirit of SGLD (preferred for high-SNR IonS where the likelihood is expensive). The CAR neighborhood matrix B uses a fixed bandwidth with exponentially decaying weights, and the correlation parameter ρ_j can be fixed or adaptively updated via a Bernoulli indicator, making the prior robust to misspecified spatial correlation without kernel tuning.

## Results

In the SonI simulation (Table 1), ST-CAR achieves the lowest test pMSE in 3 of 6 settings (Cases 2, 3, 5) and second-best in the remaining low-SNR cases, with FDR consistently below 10% and TPR above 90% across all settings. In the high-resolution setting (Case 5: n=6000, p=10000), ST-CAR achieves FDR=0.46%, TPR=99.98%, RMSE=3.73, and test pMSE=9.37, outperforming STGP (FDR=17.16%, test pMSE=86.88) and Elastic Net (test pMSE=22.94). Computationally, ST-CAR with CAVI is the fastest method across all cases, taking 8035 seconds vs. 40659 seconds for STGP and 11141 seconds for T-LoHo in the largest setting. On the ABCD real data (p=47,636 voxels, n=1,861 subjects), ST-CAR identifies the left Precuneus as the top SonI region and the Frontal Mid regions as the top IonS regions, with SSVI reducing IonS runtime from 85.9 hours (STGP) to 7.3 hours.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against STGP (Kang et al., 2018), ST-CAR dominates in high-resolution settings: in Case 5, STGP's FDR explodes to 17.16% (vs. 0.46% for ST-CAR) and its test pMSE is 9.3× worse (86.88 vs. 9.37), because the pre-specified GP kernel cannot adapt to complex spatial patterns. Against T-LoHo (Lee et al., 2021), ST-CAR trades a slightly higher FDR in low-SNR Case 1 (9.52% vs. 4.41%) for substantially better test pMSE in high-SNR Case 5 (9.37 vs. 15.54) and avoids T-LoHo's tendency to miss small decaying effects. Against Elastic Net (Zou & Hastie, 2005), ST-CAR is decisively superior: in Case 1, TPR is 97.47% vs. 44.85% and test pMSE is 7.18 vs. 60.31, reflecting Elastic Net's inability to exploit spatial correlation. In the IonS simulation (Table A1), ST-CAR with SSVI achieves the lowest RMSE in 4 of 6 cases (e.g., 8.52 vs. 9.79 for STGP and 10.25 for SBIOS in Case 5), while SBIOS (Xu et al., 2024) is the closest competitor but still trails on RMSE and is 10.4× slower than SSVI. Computationally, ST-CAR is the fastest method in every setting, with SSVI reducing IonS runtime by ~12× compared to STGP on the ABCD data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Soft-Thresholded Gaussian Process (STGP) prior of Kang et al. (2018) by replacing the pre-specified GP kernel with a Conditional AutoRegressive (CAR) latent structure, thereby removing the need to tune a smoothness kernel that may misrepresent true spatial correlation patterns. It also departs from MCMC-based posterior sampling (MALA for STGP, tree-based graph partition for T-LoHo) by introducing variational inference algorithms—Coordinate Ascent VI (CAVI) and a novel Stochastic Subsampling VI (SSVI)—that exploit the conditional conjugacy of the ST-CAR prior for scalable computation on high-dimensional fMRI data. The posterior inclusion probability (PIP) derived from the three-component truncated-normal mixture in Proposition 1 provides a principled uncertainty quantification mechanism that bridges the gap between VI's speed and MCMC's inferential capabilities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ST-CAR prior offers a practical, tuning-light alternative for Bayesian image regression in neuroimaging, where pre-specified GP kernels and cross-validation-based smoothness tuning are computationally prohibitive for 3D fMRI data. The SSVI algorithm's 7–10× speedup over MCMC-based competitors (STGP, SBIOS) makes population-scale brain imaging analyses (e.g., ABCD with ~48K voxels) feasible within hours rather than days. Open questions include whether the positive-only correlation constraint (ρ_j ≥ 0) limits applicability to signals with adjacent positive/negative regions, whether the annealing schedule for σ_β can be made fully adaptive, and how the method performs on truly 3D volumetric data beyond the 2D simulation grids used in the paper.
