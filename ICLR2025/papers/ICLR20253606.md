## Problem

Language models in later pretraining stages struggle to acquire new knowledge, yet the underlying mechanism remains unclear. While prior work has characterized how LLMs store and retrieve parametric knowledge, the evolution of how models integrate diverse knowledge sources throughout pretraining—and its causal impact on continual learning—has not been studied.

## Input / Output

The method takes OLMo 1B/7B intermediate pretraining checkpoints and computes knowledge entropy from a 2k-instance Dolma subset (ensuring all checkpoints have seen the data during pretraining), then performs continual knowledge learning on PubMed or C4 (~210M tokens, sequence length 1024) with Fictional Knowledge probes injected every 160 steps. Outputs include per-stage knowledge entropy values, knowledge acquisition rate A(θ) (relative improvement in probe log-probability), forgetting rate F(θ) (relative degradation across six zero-shot downstream tasks), and resuscitated model parameters where specific rows of the up-projection matrix K are rescaled.

## Method

The core contribution is knowledge entropy, which quantifies how broadly a model integrates its FFN memory vectors by computing the Shannon entropy of the average memory coefficient distribution (derived from SwiGLU activations) summed across all layers. The paper demonstrates that this entropy consistently decreases throughout pretraining, indicating a shift from utilizing many memory vectors to relying on a smaller, more certain subset. To establish causality, they conduct continual knowledge learning from different pretraining checkpoints, confirming that lower entropy correlates with worse acquisition and more forgetting. They then propose a resuscitation procedure (Algorithm 1) that identifies the lowest p% of memory coefficients and scales the corresponding rows of the up-projection matrix K by a multiplier u = mean(C)/C × q, effectively reactivating dormant memory vectors. This intervention improves knowledge acquisition and reduces forgetting in late-stage models, supporting the hypothesis that sparse memory activation is the mechanism behind plasticity loss during pretraining.

## Results

On OLMo 1B (baseline: batch size 128, lr 4e-4, single epoch, PubMed), knowledge acquisition 𝒜(θ) decreases from 25.2% at 118k steps to 18.8% at 738k steps, while forgetting 𝓕(θ) increases from 10.5% to 19.5%, confirming that later-stage models acquire less new knowledge and forget more. The mid-stage checkpoint (369k) achieves the best balance, with 24.8% acquisition and 12.9% forgetting, and yields the highest end performance on both knowledge probes (𝒦 = −0.280) and downstream tasks (𝒫 = 54.6%). Resuscitating the lowest 50% of memory coefficients with amplifying factor q ≥ 1 on the final-stage model improves both acquisition and retention relative to the unmodified model, though it does not fully recover to mid-stage levels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical analysis paper rather than a method comparison, so the primary 'comparison' is across pretraining stages of the same OLMo model. Early-stage (118k) and mid-stage (369k) checkpoints consistently outperform late-stage (554k) and final-stage (738k) checkpoints on both knowledge acquisition and forgetting across all tested hyperparameter configurations (batch size, learning rate, epochs). The gap is most pronounced with PubMed (−6.4%p acquisition drop from early to final) versus C4 (−3.4%p), suggesting the effect is stronger when new knowledge is more out-of-distribution. The resuscitation experiment serves as an internal ablation: modifying the final-stage model to increase knowledge entropy (q ≥ 1, p = 50) recovers some acquisition and retention, but performance remains below that of a mid-stage model with equivalent knowledge entropy, indicating that linear parameter scaling alone is insufficient to restore full plasticity. The trend holds for OLMo 7B as well, confirming the finding is not size-specific.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the key-value memory interpretation of feed-forward layers (Geva et al., 2021) by introducing 'knowledge entropy'—an information-theoretic measure of how broadly a model activates its memory vectors—thereby extending the static analysis of knowledge storage into a dynamic, pretraining-trajectory analysis. It leverages the continual knowledge learning setup from Jang et al. (2022) and the fictional knowledge probing methodology from Chang et al. (2024) to empirically link entropy decay to degraded acquisition and retention. The attention entropy formulation follows Kumar & Sarawagi (2019), and the paper positions itself as the first to track how the *integration pattern* of parametric knowledge evolves across pretraining stages and to connect this evolution mechanistically to the well-documented loss of plasticity in later-stage models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, measurable mechanism (knowledge entropy decay) for the long-observed phenomenon that later-stage pretrained models struggle to acquire new knowledge, offering practitioners a practical guideline: mid-stage checkpoints strike the best balance for continual knowledge injection. However, the resuscitation experiment—while validating the hypothesis—relies on crude linear scaling of a parameter subset and fails to fully restore mid-stage performance, leaving open the question of whether more principled interventions (e.g., structured regularization or architectural modifications) can genuinely recover lost plasticity. The analysis is also limited to OLMo 1B/7B and a single pretraining corpus (Dolma), and whether these dynamics manifest *during* pretraining itself (rather than only in a separate continual learning phase) remains unexplored.
