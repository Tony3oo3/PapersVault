## Problem

Existing motion-language methods rely on CLIP-style dense embeddings that enforce only rough cross-modal alignment via cosine similarity, producing representations that lack interpretable semantic structure and incorporate redundant information. This limits fine-grained comprehension of human motion, as feature mappings diverge from the semantic keywords essential for human understanding. The paper addresses this by asking whether both motion and text can be concretely lexicalized into a shared vocabulary space where each dimension carries specific, interpretable meaning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250562/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes raw 3D skeletal motion sequences (T×J×C_in tensors of joint positions across frames) and natural language captions as input, and produces sparse |V|-dimensional vectors in the BERT vocabulary space where each dimension corresponds to a specific word and its magnitude encodes importance. This sparse structure enables keyword-based retrieval without invoking a text encoder (simply setting keyword dimensions to 1), supports inverted indexing for efficient search, and at full masking in the decoder phase degenerates into a motion captioning task that outputs free-form text descriptions.

## Method

The core contribution is a four-phase pre-training framework that progressively aligns motion and text within a shared lexical vocabulary space. A Lexical Disentanglement Head (LM-Head followed by log(1+ReLU(MaxPool)) with a FLOPs regularizer, inspired by SPLADE) converts dense embeddings into sparse lexical vectors, while a Lexical Bottleneck Masked Decoder reconstructs heavily masked text from a continuous bag-of-words (CBoW) derived from the sparse representation, forcing encoders to prioritize motion-relevant tokens over low-entropy function words. The four phases are: (i) LexMLM adapts BERT to focus on high-entropy motion words via curriculum-masked reconstruction; (ii) CMMM pre-trains a spatial-temporal attention transformer on skeletal motion using contrastive masked reconstruction; (iii) LexMMM trains the motion encoder to produce semantically meaningful lexical representations by reconstructing masked captions from motion-derived CBoW; and (iv) LexCMLP aligns the two sparse representations via InfoNCE contrastive loss with auxiliary bottleneck losses to prevent semantic drift.

## Results

On HumanML3D, the method achieves R@1 of 11.80 (text-to-motion) and 12.39 (motion-to-text) with MedR of 14.00 and 17.00 respectively, outperforming the strongest dense baseline MotionPatch (R@1 10.80/11.25, MedR 19.00/20.50). On KIT-ML, it reaches R@1 of 15.13/15.01 with MedR of 8.00/10.50, surpassing MotionPatch (14.02/13.61, MedR 10.50/13.00) and TMR (10.05/11.83, MedR 14.00/16.00). For motion captioning on HumanML3D, CIDEr reaches 53.1, substantially exceeding TM2T (16.8) and MotionGPT (29.2), while BLEU@1 is 49.7 and BERTScore is 33.1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method outperforms all nine baselines across both retrieval and captioning tasks. Against MotionPatch (CVPR 2024), the closest dense-representation competitor, it gains +1.0 R@1 and −5.0 MedR on HumanML3D text-to-motion retrieval, and +1.1 R@1 and −2.5 MedR on KIT-ML. Against TMR (ICCV 2023), the margin is larger: +2.9 R@1 and −11.0 MedR on HumanML3D, and +5.1 R@1 and −6.0 MedR on KIT-ML. For motion captioning, the CIDEr score of 53.1 on HumanML3D nearly doubles MotionGPT (NeurIPS 2024) at 29.2 and triples TM2T (ECCV 2022) at 16.8, indicating substantially better keyword capture. Notably, simply replacing dense embeddings with lexicon representations in TMR and MotionPatch († rows) degrades performance, confirming that the multi-phase pre-training is essential rather than the sparsity alone. In the text-to-motion generation extension (Table 11), swapping the CLIP text encoder with the proposed one in MoMask, MDM, T2M-GPT, and T2M yields consistent improvements in Top-1/2/3 accuracy and MM-Dist across both VAE, diffusion, autoregressive, and non-autoregressive paradigms.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the sparse lexical representation paradigm from information retrieval (SPLADE, LexMAE) into the human motion-language domain, where prior work (TMR, MotionPatch) has relied on CLIP-style dense contrastive embeddings. It adapts the SPLADE log(1+ReLU(MaxPool)) sparsification and the LexMAE continuous bag-of-words bottleneck decoder, but introduces a multi-phase training strategy (LexMLM → CMMM → LexMMM → LexCMLP) specifically designed to bridge the cross-modal gap between skeletal motion and text. The FLOPs regularizer from Paria et al. is repurposed to enforce sparsity in the motion lexicon space, and the curriculum-masked modeling schedule is a novel training detail not present in the IR originals.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine interpretability gap in motion-language research: dense CLIP-style embeddings cannot be inspected or queried at the keyword level, limiting both human understanding and practical retrieval (e.g., inverted indexing, keyword search without a text encoder). A notable open question is whether the approach scales to the long-tail action distribution (Zipf's law) observed in datasets like Babel, where rare actions receive minimal lexical signal. The authors also flag that current motion caption quality is a bottleneck—simplistic, imbalanced annotations constrain what any representation can capture—making dataset curation a critical co-dependency for future progress.
