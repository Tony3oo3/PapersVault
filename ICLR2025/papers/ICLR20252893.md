## Problem

Differentially private optimizers like DPSGD inject Gaussian noise whose variance scales linearly with both model dimension and number of iterations, causing severe performance degradation in large-scale training (e.g., 30% accuracy drop on CIFAR-10). Existing noise-reduction techniques—low-pass filters, correlated noise, adaptive clipping—either incur prohibitive memory overhead (O(d²)–O(d⁴) for full Kalman filters), lack convergence guarantees, or require restrictive assumptions on gradient correlations or Hessian structure. The paper addresses the need for a denoising approach that is memory- and computation-efficient, theoretically grounded, and broadly compatible with existing DP optimizers.

## Input / Output

At each iteration, the method consumes the current model parameters x_t, the previous update direction d_{t-1} = x_t − x_{t-1}, and a mini-batch of per-sample gradients evaluated at two points: x_t and x_t + γd_{t-1}. These two gradient evaluations are combined into a single linear combination, clipped to norm C, and privatized with Gaussian noise before being fed into the exponential-averaging filter. The output is a denoised gradient estimate g̃_t that replaces the raw privatized gradient in the base optimizer update, requiring only two additional scalar-vector states (g̃_t and d_t) beyond the base optimizer and at most one extra forward pass per iteration.

## Method

DiSK models the true gradient ∇F(x_t) as the state of a linear dynamic system derived from a first-order Taylor expansion, with the privatized mini-batch gradient serving as a noisy observation. A Kalman filter is then applied to produce a lower-variance gradient estimate by combining the prediction (from gradient dynamics via a Hessian-vector product) with the observation. Four key simplifications make this practical: (1) the random observation matrix C_t is replaced by I_d, absorbing clipping and subsampling effects into additive noise; (2) the Hessian-vector product H_t·d_{t-1} is approximated by a finite-difference ratio of two gradient evaluations, avoiding explicit Hessian computation; (3) all covariance matrices are reduced to scalar multiples of the identity, collapsing matrix operations to scalar-vector multiplications with O(1) memory; and (4) the time-varying Kalman gain k_t is replaced by its closed-form steady-state value κ, yielding the simple update g̃_t = (1−κ)g̃_{t−1} + κg_t. The resulting algorithm computes a single privatized linear combination of gradients at x_t and x_t + γd_{t−1}, then applies exponential weighted averaging, and provably improves the iteration-complexity upper bound of DPSGD by a problem-dependent factor √(1/M_κ) without additional assumptions. The framework also unifies NAG (γ > 0) and STORM (γ < 0) as special cases.

## Results

Under privacy budget ε=8, δ=1/N^1.1, DiSK achieves 36.89% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet-1k (vs. 33.56% for DP-SGD), 75% on CIFAR-10 (vs. 63%), and 41.8% on CIFAR-100 (vs. 21%), all training from scratch. For fine-tuning, DiSK improves GLUE accuracy by at least 3.8% at ε=1 and 1.3% at ε=6.7 over DP-AdamW, and boosts CIFAR-100 fine-tuning from 85% to 89%. The method requires only one additional forward pass and two extra optimizer states compared to the base DP optimizer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DiSK consistently outperforms DP-SGD (Abadi et al., 2016) across all CV training-from-scratch benchmarks: +3.33 pp on ImageNet-1k (36.89% vs. 33.56%), +12 pp on CIFAR-10 (75% vs. 63%), and +20.8 pp on CIFAR-100 (41.8% vs. 21%). Against the SOTA DP-Mix data augmentation method (Bao et al., 2024), DiSK achieves 41.8% on CIFAR-100 versus 40.6% under the same ε=8 budget, and 36.89% on ImageNet-1k versus 33.56% (De et al., 2022). For NLP fine-tuning, DiSK (KF-DP) beats DP-AdamW (Yu et al., 2021) on all four GLUE tasks by 1.3–2.7 pp at ε=6.7 and 1.1–2.7 pp at ε=1, with the largest gain on QQP (+2.7 pp at ε=6.7). The theoretical analysis further shows DiSK improves the iteration complexity upper bound over DPSGD by a problem-dependent constant factor √(1/M_κ) without requiring additional assumptions on the problem structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

DiSK positions itself within the growing family of techniques that mitigate DP noise in gradient-based optimization, building directly on the Kalman filter (Kalman, 1960) and its extension to systems with multiplicative observation noise and noisy inputs (Wu et al., 2016). It departs from contemporaneous filter-based approaches like DOPPLER (Zhang et al., 2024a), which assumes a low-frequency gradient spectrum and applies a time-invariant low-pass filter, by instead modeling gradient dynamics via Taylor expansion and using a predictive (Kalman) filter that does not require spectral assumptions. The paper also reveals an implicit unification: with specific parameter choices, DiSK reduces to Nesterov Accelerated Gradient (NAG) or STORM, connecting acceleration and variance-reduction literature through the Kalman-filter lens. Practically, the key departure from prior Kalman-filter-for-optimization work (Bittner & Pronzato, 2004; Vuckovic, 2018) is the aggressive simplification—replacing full matrix operations with scalar gains and a single finite-difference forward pass—making it viable for billion-parameter models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DiSK offers a drop-in, memory-light wrapper (two extra optimizer states, one extra forward pass) that can be layered onto any existing DP optimizer, and it delivers the first provable constant-factor iteration-complexity improvement over DPSGD without additional problem assumptions (e.g., gradient correlation or Hessian trace bounds). The practical gains are substantial—nearly doubling CIFAR-100 accuracy from 21% to 42% under ε=8—and the method is already integrated into the Opacus library, suggesting near-term adoption. Open questions include whether the constant-factor theoretical bound (√(1/M_κ)) tightens with problem structure, whether the simplification assumptions (C_t ≈ I_d, diagonal covariance) degrade in regimes where clipping is frequently active, and whether the one-extra-forward-pass cost becomes prohibitive for very large models where compute is the binding constraint.
