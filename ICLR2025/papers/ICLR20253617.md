## Problem

Existing mathematical benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH are nearing saturation, with top models achieving 94.8%+ accuracy, rendering them unable to differentiate between model capabilities. Prior Olympiad-level benchmarks either mix multiple subjects (physics, chemistry) or lack fine-grained domain and difficulty stratification, making it difficult to isolate and diagnose mathematical reasoning weaknesses in LLMs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253617/cropped_page2_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark accepts text-only mathematical competition problems spanning 33+ sub-domains and 10+ difficulty levels (scored 0–10 on the AoPS scale). Evaluation consumes a model-generated solution alongside the reference answer and produces a correctness judgment; because answer formats range from simple numbers to multi-LaTeX expressions, custom-defined functions, tuples, and free-form text (only 74% are rule-extractable), the system relies on model-based judgment rather than fixed parsing rules. Outputs include overall accuracy plus per-domain and per-difficulty-tier breakdowns (T1: 1–3, T2: 3–5, T3: 5–7, T4: 7–10).

## Method

Omni-MATH is constructed by crawling 4,428 problems from five tiers of competitions (IMO/IMC/Putnam down to Pascal/Cayley/Fermat), converting PDFs to LaTeX via MathPix, and verifying all solutions through a four-annotator cross-validation pipeline achieving 97.3% post-sampling accuracy. Problems are hierarchically classified into 33+ mathematical sub-domains using GPT-4o guided by competition guidebook taxonomies, and assigned difficulty scores by leveraging AoPS community ratings supplemented with GPT-4o in-context learning for unlisted contests. For evaluation, the paper introduces Omni-Judge, an open-source 8B-parameter verifier fine-tuned on ~17,618 GPT-4o-labeled examples to extract answers, render judgments, and provide justifications, achieving 91.26% consistency with GPT-4o (which itself reaches 98% agreement with human annotators). A filtered subset (Omni-MATH-Rule) is also provided for reliable SymPy-based rule evaluation, and data leakage is controlled via 5-gram n-gram accuracy detection following Xu et al. (2024).

## Results

On Omni-MATH (4,428 Olympiad-level problems), the strongest model OpenAI o1-mini achieves only 60.54% accuracy, while OpenAI o1-preview reaches 52.55%. The best open-source/vanilla model, Qwen2.5-MATH-72b-Instruct, scores 36.20%, leaving a ~24-point gap to the top closed-source model. Across difficulty tiers, even o1-mini drops from 82.23% on T1 (easiest) to 42.69% on T4 (hardest), and all models show a consistent decline in accuracy as difficulty increases, validating the benchmark's difficulty calibration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OpenAI o1-mini (60.54%) and o1-preview (52.55%) dominate the leaderboard, outperforming the best open-source model Qwen2.5-MATH-72b-Instruct (36.20%) by 24.3 and 16.4 percentage points respectively, underscoring the gap between proprietary reasoning models and open-source alternatives on Olympiad-level math. Notably, Qwen2.5-MATH-72b-Instruct surpasses GPT-4o (30.49%) by 5.7 points, indicating that specialized open-source math models have closed the gap with general-purpose closed models on this benchmark. Best-of-N scaling (RM@8, RM@256) provides only marginal gains over the vanilla Qwen2.5-MATH models (e.g., 36.34% vs. 36.20% for RM@8), revealing that reward-model-based selection is ineffective for Olympiad-level problems. Across all 15 evaluated models, performance in Discrete Mathematics is consistently the weakest domain (ranging from 7.71% to 37.68%), while Algebra is the strongest (18.19% to 67.82%), suggesting a training-data bias toward algebraic and calculus content. Smaller models (7B class: DeepSeekMATH-7b-RL at 16.12%, Mathstral-7b at 19.13%) trail their 72B counterparts by roughly 15–20 points, and the lowest-performing model InternLM2-MATH-mixtral8×22B (14.24%) is outperformed by even the smallest 7B models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mathematical reasoning benchmark lineage (GSM8K → MATH → Olympiad-level) by constructing a benchmark that is exclusively text-only mathematics, in contrast to multi-discipline Olympiad benchmarks like OlympicArena or OlympiadBench that mix modalities and subjects. It departs from prior Olympiad benchmarks in three key ways: a hierarchical 33+ sub-domain taxonomy drawn from competition guidebooks, a 10+ level difficulty scale calibrated via AoPS ratings, and a model-based evaluation pipeline (GPT-4o + open-source Omni-Judge) that handles the diverse answer formats (LaTeX, tuples, functions, free text) that rule-based evaluation cannot reliably parse. The data leakage detection follows Xu et al. (2024) n-gram methodology, and the difficulty consistency metric adapts ARIMA-style first-difference analysis from Box & Pierce (1970).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Omni-MATH provides a non-saturated, fine-grained benchmark that will likely become a standard reference for evaluating Olympiad-level mathematical reasoning, given that even OpenAI o1-mini plateaus at 60.54% and the best open-source model reaches only 36.2%. The finding that Best-of-N test-time scaling is ineffective at this difficulty level challenges a widely-used assumption in mathematical reasoning research and points to the need for fundamentally different inference-time strategies. Open questions include whether discrete mathematics (a clear model weakness) can be addressed through targeted data curation, whether more sophisticated reward models can overcome the selection bottleneck identified in the Pass@K analysis, and whether the domain-transfer asymmetries observed (e.g., Number Theory boosting Geometry to 150% of in-domain accuracy) can be exploited for curriculum design.
