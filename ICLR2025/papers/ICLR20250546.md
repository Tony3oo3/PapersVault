## Problem

Sampling-based inference for Bayesian Neural Networks remains impractical because state-of-the-art NUTS-based ensembles (BDE) suffer from unpredictable runtime due to variable leapfrog step counts, poor scaling with parameter dimensionality, and difficulty exploring highly multimodal posteriors. While Microcanonical Langevin Monte Carlo (MCLMC) was recently shown to be computationally superior to NUTS in unimodal settings, it fails on BNNs without significant modifications, exhibiting 78–86% chain failure rates from numerical instability in high dimensions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a supervised training set (features and labels), a neural network architecture specification, and a test set, then produces posterior parameter samples from which it computes the predictive posterior distribution via Monte Carlo averaging. A key operational detail is the fixed computational budget of 60,000 MCLMC steps per chain (120,000 gradient evaluations), with thinning intervals of 10 or 100 to yield 1,000 or 100 final samples per chain respectively, making total runtime predictable once per-gradient cost is known. Outputs include point predictions (RMSE for regression, accuracy for classification), log posterior predictive density (LPPD) for UQ, and calibrated credible intervals.

## Method

MILE embeds a modified MCLMC sampler within a three-stage pipeline: (1) K parallel deep ensemble optimizations via Adam with decoupled weight decay produce starting values in high-probability regions, (2) a 50k-step MCLMC warmup with a linear energy variance scheduler (high-to-low) and step size initialized from the optimizer's learning rate, followed by two 5k-step tuning phases for momentum decoherence scale L, and (3) a 10k-step sampling phase with thinning. Critical modifications to vanilla MCLMC include omitting the Metropolis-Hastings correction (relying instead on EEVPD-based step size control), subsampling parameters for FFT-based ESS estimation when d > 2000, and bounding FFT sample count at 10,000 via thinning. The deterministic step count per chain (vs. NUTS's variable leapfrog count) enables perfect load balancing in parallel settings and yields up to 10× speedup over BDE while matching or exceeding its LPPD, RMSE, and calibration quality across tabular, image, and text modalities.

## Results

On the six UCI regression benchmarks, MILE consistently outperforms the NUTS-based BDE in both LPPD (e.g., 0.612 vs 0.558 on Airfoil, 2.300 vs 2.072 on Energy) and RMSE (e.g., 0.206 vs 0.214 on Airfoil, 0.034 vs 0.045 on Energy), while reducing sampling wallclock time by a factor of 7–10× (e.g., 5.40 min vs 48.29 min on Bikesharing; 19.50 min vs 152.85 min on Protein). On extended benchmarks including CNNs (MNIST, Fashion-MNIST) and attention-based text models (IMDB), MILE improves LPPD over the DE baseline by 0.05–0.35 and accuracy by 1–2 percentage points. The method uses a fixed 60k MCLMC steps per chain (120k gradient evaluations), yielding predictable runtime versus NUTS's variable leapfrog count.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against BDE (Sommer et al., 2024; NUTS-based), MILE wins on LPPD across all six UCI regression datasets (e.g., +0.054 on Airfoil, +0.228 on Energy) and on RMSE for five of six, while cutting sampling time by 7–10× (e.g., 5.40 vs 48.29 min on Bikesharing). Against the DE baseline (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017), MILE substantially improves UQ (LPPD gains of 0.05–1.23) and point prediction (RMSE reductions up to 59% on Yacht), confirming that the MCLMC sampling step adds value beyond optimization alone. Against Path-Guided Particle-based Sampling (Fan et al., 2024), MILE achieves lower NLL on 5 of 7 UCI classification datasets (e.g., 0.717 vs 1.602 on Covertype) with comparable or better accuracy, and runs ~26× faster (0.94 vs 24.34 min on Sonar). Against Symmetric Split HMC (Cobb & Jalaian, 2021), MILE achieves 0.925 accuracy vs 0.818–0.820 and LPPD of −0.216 vs −0.513 to −0.548 on Fashion-MNIST CNNv2, in 1 h 21 min versus 7–19 h for Sym-Split-HMC.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper embeds Microcanonical Langevin Monte Carlo (MCLMC; Robnik et al., 2023) as a drop-in replacement for NUTS within the Bayesian Deep Ensemble (BDE; Sommer et al., 2024) framework, which itself builds on Deep Ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017) for initialization. While MCLMC was previously shown to outperform NUTS in unimodal and sequential sampling tasks, it had not been adapted to the highly multimodal, high-dimensional posteriors of BNNs. The key conceptual departure is that MILE omits the Metropolis-Hastings correction (relying instead on EEVPD-based step-size control) and introduces a linear energy-variance scheduler and parameter subsampling for the FFT-based autocorrelation estimation, making the sampler numerically stable where naive MCLMC fails in 78–86% of chains.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The up-to-10× speedup over NUTS-based BDE with maintained or improved LPPD and RMSE makes sampling-based BNN inference substantially more practical, and the fixed 60k-step budget per chain gives practitioners predictable wallclock times and perfect load balancing under parallelization—properties NUTS fundamentally lacks. Open questions include whether the approach extends to stochastic-gradient variants for very large datasets (explicitly deferred), whether alternative priors (e.g., structured or scale-dependent) could further improve performance, and whether the modest underconfidence observed in calibration can be addressed. The method is positioned as an off-the-shelf, auto-tuned procedure, lowering the barrier to entry for sampling-based BNN inference.
