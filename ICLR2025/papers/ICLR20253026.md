## Problem

Existing supervised skill-learning methods from offline datasets encode skills as fixed-length action sequences, which fails to recognize semantically similar behaviors (e.g., grabbing an object at different positions) as the same skill and cannot adapt to the varying temporal durations of real-world behaviors. This leads to inefficient skill spaces with dimensional collapse and poor generalization, particularly in complex or noisy datasets containing irrelevant actions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes unlabeled offline state-action trajectories (e.g., 29-dim observations and 8-dim actions for Antmaze, 18-dim observations and 4-dim actions for Pick-and-Place) by selecting four key states per skill window (initial, terminal, and two randomly sampled intermediate states) rather than the full action sequence. It produces skill embeddings in a low-dimensional latent space (5-dim for Antmaze/Kitchen, 2-dim for Pick-and-Place), dynamically adjusted skill lengths bounded by δ_min and δ_max, decoded action sequences via a VAE decoder, and predicted skill target states used to determine when a skill execution is complete during downstream task solving.

## Method

DCSL redefines skill representation around state transitions rather than action sequences: an LSTM encoder maps four key states to a skill embedding, and a contrastive learning objective (NCE binary loss) trains a skill similarity function f(s, z, s') = ⟨φ(s,z), ψ(s')⟩ that scores whether a future state s' is reachable from s under skill z, clustering semantically similar behaviors into the same skill embedding. The total training loss combines a VAE reconstruction loss (behavior cloning + KL regularization + skill prior matching), the contrastive discrimination loss, and a skill target state prediction loss that learns to predict the post-skill state for variable-length execution. A periodic relabeling step uses the learned similarity function to dynamically adjust each skill's length to the maximum consecutive window where similarity exceeds a threshold ε, with length constraints to prevent abrupt changes. Theoretical analysis shows the contrastive objective maximizes conditional mutual information I(Z; S'|S) between skills and state transitions, providing a principled link to skill clustering.

## Results

On Kitchen, Ours-SAC achieves 94.7±1.5% success rate, substantially outperforming CQL+OPAL (69.3±2.7%) and CQL (52.4±2.5%). On Antmaze-Large, Ours-SAC reaches 73.7±5.9%, exceeding CQL+OPAL (70.3±2.9%) and CQL (14.9±3.2%). In the noisy Pick-and-Place (RP) dataset, Ours-SAC requires only 64.4±16.3 timesteps to complete tasks versus 193.2±13.5 for SkiMo-CEM and 200.0±0.0 for SkiMo-SAC. However, on Antmaze-Medium, CQL+OPAL (81.1±3.1%) outperforms Ours-SAC (68.0±36.9%), and Ours-CEM fails to achieve meaningful performance in Antmaze due to variable skill lengths destabilizing CEM's planning horizon.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DCSL (Ours-SAC) clearly wins on Kitchen (94.7% vs. 69.3% for CQL+OPAL, a 25.4-point margin) and Antmaze-Large (73.7% vs. 70.3% for CQL+OPAL), demonstrating superior skill representation in manipulation and complex navigation. On Antmaze-Medium, CQL+OPAL (81.1%) outperforms Ours-SAC (68.0%), which the authors attribute to differences in high-level policy learning (SAC vs. CEM). Against skill-based baselines, Ours-SAC requires significantly fewer timesteps in Kitchen (165.1 vs. 276.6 for SPiRL and 251.3 for SkiMo-SAC) and in noisy Pick-and-Place data (64.4 for RP vs. 193.2–200.0 for SkiMo variants), confirming more efficient skill utilization. Ours-CEM underperforms Ours-SAC in Antmaze environments (1000.0 timesteps, i.e., task failure) because variable skill lengths destabilize CEM's fixed planning horizon, a limitation the authors acknowledge. In the noisiest Pick-and-Place (RP) dataset, Ours-SAC outperforms all other methods on both success rate and timesteps, highlighting the critical role of dynamic skill length adjustment in handling irrelevant actions.

## Relation to Prior Work

DCSL builds directly on the SPiRL skill learning architecture (skill encoder, decoder, and prior) and the SkiMo model-based planning framework, but departs from both by replacing action-sequence-based skill representations with state-transition-based ones. The contrastive skill similarity function is adapted from Eysenbach et al.'s goal-conditioned contrastive learning formulation, repurposed here to discriminate whether a future state is reachable under a given skill rather than to predict goal achievement. The key conceptual departure is the dynamic skill length relabeling mechanism, which has no direct analogue in prior skill learning methods that rely on fixed-length action sequences or learned termination functions (Option-Critic, Termination Critic).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses two practical limitations in offline skill learning—semantic fragmentation of similar behaviors and rigid temporal granularity—that directly affect skill space efficiency and downstream task performance, particularly in noisy or mixed-quality datasets. However, the framework's dependence on CEM being destabilized by variable skill lengths, its limited generalization beyond the training distribution, and its computational overhead from periodic relabeling and contrastive sampling are significant practical constraints. Open questions include whether the learned similarity function can be integrated into downstream planning (not just extraction), whether online exploration can supplement the offline-only skill repertoire, and whether the approach scales to higher-dimensional or multi-agent settings.
