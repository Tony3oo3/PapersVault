## Problem

Although attention sink—the phenomenon where autoregressive LMs allocate disproportionate attention to the first token—has been widely exploited in streaming generation, KV cache optimization, and quantization, a systematic understanding of when and why it emerges during pre-training has been absent. Prior work identified the phenomenon and linked it to massive activations, but did not disentangle the roles of optimization dynamics, data distribution, loss function design, and architectural choices in its emergence.

## Input / Output

The study evaluates both open-sourced LMs (LLaMA2/3, GPT2, Mistral, Pythia, OPT, Jamba families ranging from 14M to 52B parameters) and newly trained ~60M-parameter LLaMA-style models on 5B tokens from The Pile. Inputs include natural language sequences, random token sequences, and repeated-token sequences of length T=64 (no BOS token). The primary output is a threshold-based metric Sink_k^ε (fraction of heads/blocks where the k-th token's average attention exceeds ε=0.3), supplemented by attention pattern visualizations, ℓ₂-norm ratios of hidden states/keys/values, and cosine similarity analyses between queries and keys across blocks and heads.

## Method

The paper conducts a controlled empirical study by pre-training small LLaMA models (d=768, L=10, H=8, ~60M params) from scratch and systematically varying one factor at a time: learning rate, weight decay, batch size, training data amount, data randomness, prefix length, window size, positional embedding type, pre/post-norm structure, and attention operation. The key mechanistic insight is that the first token's key k₁^{l,h} occupies a distinct manifold due to massive activations amplified by residual connections, making its cosine similarity with other queries disproportionately large despite its small ℓ₂-norm—effectively acting as a learned key bias that absorbs extra attention without contributing meaningfully to value computation. The paper further shows this behavior stems from the inner dependence among attention scores imposed by softmax normalization: when softmax is replaced with sigmoid attention (no normalization) or linear attention with MLP kernels, attention sink disappears entirely in models up to 1B parameters while maintaining comparable validation loss, confirming that the normalization constraint, not the architecture itself, drives the phenomenon.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that attention sink (measured by Sink₁^ε with ε=0.3) is nearly universal in auto-regressive LMs: LLaMA3-8B Base achieves 99.02% on natural language inputs and 91.23% even on random token sequences. The key architectural finding is that replacing softmax attention with sigmoid attention without normalization eliminates attention sink entirely (0.44% vs. 18.18% at 60M parameters, and 2.46% vs. 45.11% at 1B parameters) while maintaining comparable validation loss (3.70 vs. 3.73 at 60M; 3.10 vs. 3.07 at 1B). Attention sink also emerges only after sufficient training data (disappearing below ~200M tokens) and effective optimization (delayed with smaller learning rates).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the 'comparisons' are between different model configurations. Standard softmax attention (the default in all major LLMs) exhibits strong attention sink (18.18% at 60M, 45.11% at 1B), while sigmoid attention without normalization reduces this to near zero (0.44% at 60M, 2.46% at 1B) with only a marginal validation loss increase (3.70 vs. 3.73; 3.10 vs. 3.07). Adding normalization back to sigmoid attention restores attention sink (30.24%), confirming that the softmax normalization's inner dependence among attention scores is the root cause. Positional embedding choice (NoPE, absolute, learnable, relative, ALiBi, Rotary) has negligible effect on attention sink emergence (all between 18–36%). Weight decay at γ=0.5 increases sink from 15.20% to 41.08%, but excessive weight decay (γ≥2.0) destroys it along with model performance. Introducing explicit key biases (k*) shifts attention sink from the first token to the bias position (73.34% on bias, 0.00% on first token) without degrading validation loss, confirming the first token functions as an implicit key bias.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the attention sink phenomenon identified by Xiao et al. (2023b) and partially explained by Cancedda (2024) (massive activations in first-token hidden states) and Sun et al. (2024) (implicit key/value biases). While prior work characterized *what* attention sink looks like and offered localized explanations, this paper systematically investigates *when* it emerges during pre-training by ablating optimization schedules, data distributions, loss functions, and architectural choices. It departs from the concurrent theoretical analysis of Guo et al. (2024a) on a simplified Bigram-Backcopy task by providing large-scale empirical evidence across diverse model families (LLaMA, GPT2, Mistral, Pythia, OPT, Jamba) and scales (14M to 8B+), and introduces the key-bias interpretation and the softmax-normalization mechanism as the root cause.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that attention sink is essentially a non-informative key bias artifact of softmax normalization — rather than a semantically meaningful property of language modeling — reframes how practitioners should think about KV cache optimization, quantization, and streaming generation that currently exploit this phenomenon. The demonstration that sigmoid attention without normalization eliminates attention sink up to 1B parameters while maintaining comparable loss opens a concrete architectural direction for avoiding the associated massive activations and quantization challenges. Open questions remain: whether the 1B result holds at larger scales, whether attention sink provides any downstream benefit, and how sink tokens on non-first positions (periods, newlines) relate to the pre-training dynamics studied here.
