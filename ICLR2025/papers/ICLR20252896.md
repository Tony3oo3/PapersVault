## Problem

While inductive biases of neural networks are well-recognized as critical for generalization, the field lacks a unified, interpretable tool connecting architecture, data, and the resulting geometric inductive biases in the input space. This paper asks: how does the geometry of a neural network in the input space evolve during training, and what specific roles do architecture and data play in that evolution?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252896/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a neural network architecture (MLP, LeNet, ResNet18, ViT, VGG11), a training dataset (CIFAR-2 or synthetic data in R^D with D=3072 for 32×32×3 images), and a probing distribution (standard Gaussian N(0,I)) to compute the average geometry matrix G_F ∈ R^{D×D} and its evolution Δ_F. Outputs include the geometric invariance subspace (eigenvectors of G_F with low eigenvalues), per-feature importance scores via generalized eigendecomposition of (G_F, S), and per-sample importance scores measuring each datapoint's alignment with G_F. The geometry is estimated by averaging gradient outer-products over the training trajectory distribution T_t, with stochasticity from initialization and mini-batching.

## Method

The paper defines two architecture-dependent maps: the average geometry G_F^t(x) = E_θ[∇_x f_θ(x)∇_x f_θ(x)^T] (covariance of input gradients) and the average geometry evolution Δ_F^t(x) (its time derivative under gradient flow). Theoretically, it proves that for isotropic models (MLPs with ReLU), Δ_F converges to the data covariance S as width and dimension grow (Theorem 3.1), while for non-isotropic models (CNNs with pooling), Δ_F converges to G_F S G_F (Theorem 3.2). This motivates Conjecture 1: Δ_F ∝ G_F S G_F for general architectures, meaning geometry changes only in directions where the initial geometry G_F has support. The Geometric Invariance Hypothesis (GIH, Conjecture 2) then states that Eig(Δ_F^t) ⊆ Eig(G_F S G_F), so the input-space curvature remains invariant in directions orthogonal to G_F throughout training. Empirically, GIH is confirmed via geometric velocity measurements on synthetic data and shown to explain generalization failures: when discriminant features align with low-eigenvalue directions of G_F, models memorize noise instead of learning the true decision boundary, and this effect sharpens with decision boundary complexity.

## Results

The paper confirms the Geometric Invariance Hypothesis (GIH) across MLP, LeNet, ResNet18, and ViT on CIFAR-2 (D=3072). For structured architectures (LeNet, ResNet18, ViT), test accuracy drops sharply when the discriminant feature aligns with low-eigenvalue directions of the initial average geometry G_F, while MLP (isotropic, G_F ≈ I) generalizes uniformly across all directions. In the sample importance experiment on CIFAR-10, removing 90% of data based on geometry score (S) retains 45.9% (LeNet), 54.4% (ResNet18), and 36.2% (ViT) test accuracy versus 45.1%, 52.4%, and 35.6% for random removal (R), yielding a consistent ~1–2% advantage. The feature importance experiment shows that eliminating features with lowest correlation to G_F causes less performance degradation than random feature elimination at matched variance-removal budgets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against external methods; instead, it compares architectures and data-manipulation strategies to validate GIH. MLP serves as the isotropic baseline (G_F ≈ I), showing no directional preference and generalizing equally well regardless of where the discriminant feature lies. Structured architectures (LeNet, ResNet18, ViT) exhibit a clear generalization gap: when the discriminant direction u has a small eigenvalue in G_F·S·G_F, test accuracy drops substantially (e.g., ResNet18 drops from ~90% to ~50% across eigenvalue indices in Figure 4), while MLP remains flat. In the sample importance comparison, geometry-score-based removal (S) consistently outperforms random removal (R) by 0.8–2.0% across all three architectures and all removal fractions, with the gap widening at higher removal percentages (e.g., ResNet18: 54.4% vs 52.4% at 90% removal). The feature importance experiment similarly shows that GIH-guided feature elimination preserves more performance than random elimination at equivalent variance-removal budgets, confirming that directions with low correlation to G_F carry less predictive signal for structured architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Neural Anisotropy Directions (NADs) framework of Ortiz-Jiménez et al. (2020), which empirically demonstrated that the eigenvectors of the input-gradient covariance identify preferred classification directions in linear tasks. The key departure is providing a theoretical explanation for this phenomenon through the newly defined 'average geometry' and 'average geometry evolution' quantities, removing the linearity assumption, and formalizing the observation as the Geometric Invariance Hypothesis (GIH). The analysis draws on the gradient-flow model (Barrett & Dherin, 2021) and NTK literature (Jacot et al., 2018), while also engaging with the Simplicity Bias Hypothesis (Pérez et al., 2019) to show that geometric invariance can override simplicity preferences.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GIH provides a concrete, architecture-dependent explanation for why certain models fail to generalize on seemingly simple tasks, offering a unified lens connecting architecture design, data structure, and inductive bias. Practical implications include geometry-informed dimensionality reduction and sample selection, though the demonstrated gains are modest (~2% over random). Key limitations include theoretical results being established only at initialization under gradient flow, the conjecture about full-training invariance remaining unproven, and experiments restricted to binary classification on CIFAR-2 and synthetic data. Open questions include whether GIH holds under discrete optimization (SGD with momentum), for multi-class tasks, and whether it can guide architecture search or foundation model design.
