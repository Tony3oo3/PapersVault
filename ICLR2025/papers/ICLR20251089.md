## Problem

Existing importance weighting methods for sub-population shifts (e.g., GW-ERM, SUBG) choose weights via heuristics—typically likelihood ratios—that make the weighted training loss asymptotically unbiased for the test distribution. However, these heuristics ignore the variance introduced by finite training sample sizes, causing over-weighting of minority groups and degraded generalization. The paper asks: what are the truly optimal weights that minimize expected test loss at a given finite sample size, and how can they be estimated in practice?

## Input / Output

The method operates on last-layer embeddings from a pre-trained DNN (ResNet-50 for Waterbirds/CelebA, BERT for MultiNLI), treating retraining as logistic regression on frozen features. It requires group labels for all training points and a randomly split validation subset (not group-balanced, unlike prior work), with the validation set used to estimate the optimal weights. The output is a set of G-dimensional group weights on the probability simplex, which are then used to retrain the last-layer classifier; the same hyperparameters (L1 penalty, learning rate) are shared between standard and optimized weight runs for fair comparison.

## Method

The core contribution is a bi-level optimization: the inner loop trains model parameters θ by minimizing a group-weighted training loss, while the outer loop updates group weights p to minimize a weighted validation loss evaluated at the inner-loop solution. The hyper-gradient ∂L_val/∂p is computed via the chain rule combined with the implicit function theorem, yielding an expression involving the inverse Hessian of the training loss with respect to θ. For GW-ERM, weights are updated via exponentiated gradient descent on the simplex; for SUBG, the non-differentiable subsampling indicator is replaced by its expectation (a weighted loss), making the problem differentiable; for GDRO, a third quantity (per-group loss weights q) is jointly optimized. A theoretical analysis for linear regression with two groups shows the optimal weight p* is a convex combination of the test and training group frequencies, with the mixing ratio η = σ²(d+1) / [n(a₁−a₀)²p_tr(1−p_tr)], confirming that optimization matters most when n is small, d is large, or the group intercept gap is small.

## Results

Across 15 method-dataset combinations (5 importance weighting methods × 3 datasets), optimized weights yield a statistically significant improvement at the 10% level in 12 cases and at the 5% level in 8 cases. The maximum improvement on the primary objective metric is 5.61%, with 5 cases exceeding 1%. On the secondary metric (e.g., worst-group accuracy for GW-ERM/SUBG/DFR), 10 cases show improvement >1% with a maximum of 4.03%. The gains are most pronounced on Waterbirds (the smallest dataset with very small minority groups) and diminish on the larger CelebA and MultiNLI datasets; the only case where optimized weights fail to improve is JTT on CelebA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares optimized weights against the standard (heuristic) weight choice for four families of importance weighting methods. For GW-ERM and SUBG (Sagawa et al., 2020b), optimized weights consistently improve weighted average accuracy, with the largest gains on Waterbirds where minority groups are smallest. For GDRO (Sagawa et al., 2020a), optimized weights improve worst-group accuracy and also reduce sensitivity to the L1 regularization hyperparameter (Figure 4). For DFR (Kirichenko et al., 2023), optimized subsampling fractions improve weighted average accuracy across all three datasets. For JTT (Liu et al., 2021), replacing the single grid-searched upweighting factor with multiple gradient-optimized group weights improves worst-group accuracy on Waterbirds and MultiNLI but not on CelebA, where the bi-level optimization fails to find better weights than the original grid search. In all cases, the same hyperparameters (including L1 penalty) are used for both standard and optimized weights, giving a fair comparison that slightly favors the standard choice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the established importance weighting literature for distribution shifts (Kahn & Marshall, 1953; Shimodaira, 2000) and the sub-population shift methods that use heuristic likelihood-ratio weights (GW-ERM, SUBG, GDRO, DFR, JTT). It departs from these methods by arguing that the standard likelihood-ratio choice, while asymptotically unbiased, is suboptimal for finite samples due to induced variance, and introduces a bi-level optimization procedure (drawing on the implicit function theorem for hyper-gradient computation, as in Bengio 2000 and Pedregosa 2016) to find truly optimal group weights. The paper explicitly positions itself not as a new importance weighting method but as a meta-level optimization that can be layered on top of any existing weighting scheme.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The bias-variance decomposition of importance weights (Equation 8) provides a clean theoretical explanation for why over-weighting minority groups can hurt generalization, and the practical finding that optimized weights improve robustness to regularization hyperparameters is a useful side benefit. However, the improvements are modest (typically 1–4%, max 5.61%) and most pronounced only in small-data or small-minority-group regimes, limiting broad impact. Open questions include convergence guarantees for the bi-level optimization, computational feasibility of hyper-gradient computation for full DNN retraining (requiring inverted Hessian calculations), and extension to settings where the test distribution is unknown or where many groups (e.g., concept bottleneck models) severely reduce effective sample size.
