## Problem

Existing data mixture tuning for LLM pretraining relies on heuristics or qualitative optimization algorithms that cannot predict the effect of a given mixture before committing to expensive training runs. This paper asks whether model performance can be quantitatively predicted as a function of mixture proportions, analogous to how scaling laws predict performance from model size and data amount, enabling low-cost selection of an optimal data mixture.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252965/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes small-scale training losses (from models as small as 70M trained for 30B tokens across ~20–40 sampled mixtures) together with target model size and training steps, and produces predicted validation losses for any point in the mixture simplex, from which the optimal mixture is extracted by minimization. Because proportions are bounded in [0,1] and sum to 1, predicting an unseen mixture is an interpolation problem rather than extrapolation, making the approach feasible with limited samples. The validation set composition need not be known in advance: implicit domain aggregation treats validation domain proportions as learnable parameters, so the law is fitted end-to-end on overall validation loss.

## Method

The core contribution is the data mixing law: for each validation domain i, the loss follows L_i(r) = c_i + k_i exp(Σ_j t_ij r_j), an exponential of a linear combination of training mixture proportions, identified by progressively relaxing simplifications (2-domain → multi-domain, single-domain validation → general validation) and verified against candidate functions under compatibility and symmetry constraints. To avoid expensive large-scale experiments, the paper nests three scaling laws in sequence: power laws of training steps extrapolate from few steps to target steps, power laws of model sizes extrapolate from small models to target size, and the data mixing law then predicts loss on unseen mixtures at the target scale. For validation sets of unknown composition, implicit domain aggregation reduces fitting to an end-to-end regression implemented as an MLP with exponential activation and AdaBoost regularization. The full pipeline requires only ~20 small-scale runs to optimize the mixture for a 1B model trained on 100B tokens, achieving performance equivalent to 48% more training steps on the default RedPajama mixture.

## Results

The proposed pipeline optimizes the RedPajama training mixture for a 1B model trained on 100B tokens, achieving a validation perplexity on the Pile comparable to the default mixture trained for 48% more steps (i.e., ~148B tokens). Equivalently, the optimized mixture reaches the default mixture's 100B-token performance using only 73% of the training steps. The method also accurately predicts the critical mixture proportion in continual pretraining that avoids catastrophic forgetting on the original domain (Pile) while improving on the target domain (Python code).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two prior data-mixture optimization methods: DoGE (Fan et al., 2024) and DoReMi (Xie et al., 2024a), all evaluated as 1B models trained for 100B tokens on RedPajama and validated on the Pile. DoGE is tested in both its i.i.d. (universal generalization) and OOD (validation-set-aware) settings, while DoReMi is tested with a mixture optimized directly on RedPajama and a variant adapted from the Pile via domain overlap. As shown in Figure 9, the proposed data mixing law pipeline finds the mixture that reaches the lowest validation perplexity among all compared methods, outperforming both DoGE variants and both DoReMi variants. The advantage is consistent across the training curve, with the optimized mixture pulling ahead early and maintaining a lower perplexity throughout. The paper notes that DoGE and DoReMi provide only qualitative optimization (e.g., relative reweighting), whereas the proposed method yields a quantitatively predicted optimal proportion, enabling direct comparison of expected final performance before full-scale training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the scaling laws framework (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) to a new dimension—data mixture proportions—by discovering that domain losses follow an exponential-of-linear-combination functional form with respect to mixture ratios. Unlike prior data mixture optimization methods (DoReMi, DoGE) that adjust proportions qualitatively or via proxy-model feedback loops, this work establishes a closed-form quantitative law that predicts validation loss for any mixture before training. The nested pipeline combining step-scaling, size-scaling, and mixing laws to extrapolate from small-scale experiments to target-scale predictions is the key methodological contribution, building directly on the Chinchilla power-law forms but decomposing them into sequential single-variable fits for stability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for LLM pretraining: the optimized mixture achieves performance equivalent to 48% more training steps at the same compute budget, and the entire optimization pipeline requires only small-scale (70M–410M) experiments. The extension to continual pretraining—accurately predicting the critical proportion that avoids catastrophic forgetting—opens a path toward dynamic, multi-stage data schedules. Key limitations include the empirical (non-theoretical) nature of the functional form, the fuzzy definition of 'domains,' error accumulation across the three nested scaling-law fits, and validation only up to 1B parameters / 100B tokens, leaving open whether the laws hold at frontier scales. A joint law unifying steps, sizes, and mixtures would reduce fitting samples and clarify cross-factor synergies.
