## Problem

LLMs exhibit dramatic performance drops on previously learned tasks during continual learning, which has been attributed to catastrophic forgetting. This paper challenges that interpretation by showing the underlying knowledge is largely retained and the performance loss stems instead from a disruption in task alignment—the model's ability to route its stored knowledge through the correct output format. The authors term this 'spurious forgetting' and identify it as a distinct, previously uncharacterized failure mode in sequential fine-tuning.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a pre-trained transformer language model (LLaMA-2-7B-Chat, LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct, or Pythia-410M) and a new task's training data (ranging from 10 safety instances to 40K instruction-tuning examples). The Freeze strategy selectively updates only the top layers while keeping the bottom n layers (including input embeddings) at their pre-task values, with n tuned per scenario (1–6 layers). In the controlled Biography experiments, the model is pre-trained from scratch on 100K synthetic biographies, fine-tuned on QA pairs for Task 0 (50K individuals), then fine-tuned on QA pairs for Task 1 (20K novel individuals), with evaluation via exact-match accuracy on held-out QA pairs.

## Method

The paper's core insight is that the first ~150 optimization steps of a new task produce near-orthogonal weight updates concentrated in the bottom layers (input embeddings and early transformer blocks), which effectively undo the previous task's alignment without erasing the underlying knowledge. A theoretical framework formalizes this via a residual linear network model: under small weight norms and orthogonal perturbation assumptions, the output shift is bounded by L·ε_Δ·(1+δ)^(L−1)·‖X⁰‖, showing that freezing bottom layers reduces the number of layers contributing to the accumulated shift. The proposed Freeze strategy simply keeps the bottom n layers (and input embeddings) at their pre-task-1 values during fine-tuning on the new task, optionally combined with early stopping once the new task's accuracy exceeds a threshold. This improves sequential fine-tuning accuracy on the Biography dataset from 11% to 44% (vs. 22% for the best existing method) and yields consistent gains across safety alignment, continual instruction tuning, continual knowledge editing, and instance incremental learning.

## Results

On the controlled Biography dataset, the proposed Freeze strategy (freezing bottom 7 layers with early stopping) improves Task 0 accuracy from 11.18% (SEQ lower bound) to 44.22% while maintaining Task 1 accuracy at 99.93%. Among all non-replay baselines, Freeze outperforms the best alternative (Task Vector at 30.75% Task 0 ACC) by over 13 points. Data replay with 20% old data achieves the highest Task 0 accuracy (80.62%) but requires storing old data, whereas Freeze operates without any stored data. On real-world scenarios (Table 2), Freeze consistently improves over SEQ across safety alignment, continual instruction tuning, continual knowledge editing, and instance incremental learning.

|---|---|---|---|
| SEQ (Lower Bound) | 11.18 | 99.91 | 0.00 |
| EWC (λ=1×10⁶) | 13.48 | 99.88 | +2.30 |
| LAMOL (λ=0.10) | 18.91 | 98.87 | +7.73 |

## Baselines & Comparisons

Freeze decisively outperforms all four non-replay baselines on Task 0 accuracy while keeping Task 1 accuracy above 99%. EWC (best: 13.48% Task 0 ACC) fails because its Fisher matrix does not identify the bottom layers as critical for task alignment. LAMOL (best: 18.91%) suffers from low-quality pseudo-samples, with fewer than 20% surviving format and exact-match filtering. Task Vector (best: 30.75% at Task 1 ACC of 95.76%) exhibits a fundamental trade-off: when Task 1 accuracy is constrained above 99%, its Task 0 accuracy drops to 22.60%, still well below Freeze's 44.22%. Gradient Projection (best: 13.15%) fails because the undo-alignment direction is highly diverse across trials (average cosine similarity only 0.4), making a single projected direction ineffective. Freeze's advantage stems from directly preventing the orthogonal weight updates in bottom layers that cause spurious forgetting, rather than attempting to correct them after the fact.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard catastrophic forgetting framework in continual learning by introducing 'spurious forgetting'—the hypothesis that performance drops reflect loss of task alignment rather than loss of underlying knowledge. It builds on the AOA alignment observations (Qi et al., 2024) and the TRACE benchmark (Wang et al., 2023b) as motivating evidence, but reframes the problem through a decomposition of task performance into alignment plus knowledge. The theoretical analysis draws on simplified residual-network models in the spirit of Abbe et al. (2024) and Li et al. (2023), while the proposed Freeze strategy is distinct from parameter-efficient methods (LoRA, adapters, prompts) and model-expansion approaches, operating instead as a simple layer-freezing intervention during full fine-tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If performance degradation in continual learning is largely spurious—driven by alignment disruption rather than knowledge erasure—then the entire toolkit of continual learning methods (EWC, generative replay, gradient projection, task arithmetic) is misdirected, as the paper demonstrates empirically. The Freeze strategy's simplicity (freezing bottom layers) and its consistent gains across four diverse real-world scenarios make it a practical, low-cost intervention likely to be widely adopted. Key limitations include the persistent gap between Freeze and data replay (44% vs. 77–80% on the synthetic benchmark), the reliance on a simplified residual-network theoretical model rather than full Transformer analysis, and the open question of whether explicit alignment-preserving training objectives could close this gap without requiring stored old data.
