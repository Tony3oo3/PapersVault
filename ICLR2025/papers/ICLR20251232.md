## Problem

Spiking neural networks (SNNs) are typically trained and evaluated as if the temporal averaging of outputs across timesteps is a straightforward ensemble, yet the paper identifies a critical overlooked issue: excessive differences in membrane potential distributions across timesteps cause individual temporal subnetworks to produce highly unstable and uninformative outputs (e.g., only 10% accuracy at the first timestep on CIFAR10-DVS). This instability degrades the ensemble performance and increases optimization difficulty, but prior work has not explicitly addressed the root cause in the initial membrane potential dynamics.

## Input / Output

The method accepts diverse input modalities—neuromorphic event streams (integrated into T frames of 48×48 spatial resolution), 1D speech signals, 3D point clouds (1024 points normalized to a unit sphere), and standard RGB images—and produces class labels or bounding box coordinates. The SNN operates over T timesteps (typically 4–10), and the final prediction is the temporal average of per-timestep outputs. The only architectural addition is one learnable smoothing coefficient α per layer; the guidance loss modifies the training objective without altering the network structure or inference procedure.

## Method

The paper reframes an SNN as an ensemble of T temporal subnetworks sharing architecture and weights, whose output variance stems from differing membrane potentials. To reduce this variance, it introduces membrane potential smoothing: at each timestep, the initial membrane potential is a convex combination of the previous timestep's smoothed potential and the current leaky potential, weighted by a learnable per-layer coefficient α (initialized to 0.5 via sigmoid). This smoothing simultaneously creates additional forward information and backward gradient pathways that mitigate the temporal gradient vanishing problem. A second component, temporally adjacent subnetwork guidance, applies a KL-divergence (or MSE for regression) distillation loss between consecutive timestep outputs, with a random-drop mechanism that retains only the largest loss per batch to preserve useful diversity. Together, these two mechanisms stabilize the ensemble at both the initial-state and output levels, requiring no architectural changes and only one extra scalar per layer.

## Results

On the neuromorphic object recognition benchmark CIFAR10-DVS, the method achieves 83.20% accuracy with only 4 timesteps (VGGSNN + data augmentation) and 80.60% with SpikingResformer-Ti at T=5, outperforming all compared SNN baselines. On DVS-Gesture, it reaches 94.44% (SpikingResformer-Ti, T=5) and 93.23% (VGG-9, T=5). For 3D point cloud classification, it achieves 94.54% on ModelNet10 and 91.13% on ModelNet40 with T=2, and even 94.39%/89.82% with a single timestep. On 1D speech (SHD), it reaches 91.19% accuracy, and on static ImageNet it achieves 69.03% top-1 accuracy with SEW-ResNet34 at T=4.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On CIFAR10-DVS, the method with SpikingResformer-Ti (80.60%) surpasses Ternary Spike (78.70%), NDOT (77.50%), SLTT (77.17%), and the original SpikingResformer (77.60%) by 1.6–3.5 points; with VGGSNN + data augmentation at T=4 it reaches 83.20%, the highest reported on this dataset. On DVS-Gesture, VGG-9 (93.23%) beats SDT (92.24%), TRR (91.67%), and SSNN (90.74%), while SpikingResformer-Ti (94.44%) leads all methods by over 2 points. On N-Caltech101, VGG-9 (82.71%) edges out TCJA-TET-SNN (82.50%) and TEBN (81.24%), and with knowledge transfer VGGSNN reaches 93.68% vs. 91.72% for the prior best. On 1D SHD speech, the method (91.19%) outperforms DH-LIF (89.86%), TC-LIF (88.91%), and the original LSTM (89.20%). On 3D ModelNet10/40, it achieves 94.54%/91.13% at T=2, surpassing Spiking PointNet (92.98%/88.46%) and Converted SNN (92.75%/89.45%), and even the ANN PointNet++ (95.50%/92.16%) is only marginally ahead on ModelNet10. On static ImageNet (T=4), it reaches 69.03% top-1, exceeding TAB (67.78%), RecDis-SNN (67.33%), and SSCL (66.78%) by 1.3–5.9 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reinterprets the temporal dynamics of SNNs through an ensemble learning lens, building on the standard LIF neuron formulation (Wu et al., 2018) and adapting knowledge distillation (Hinton, 2015) into a temporally adjacent subnetwork guidance loss. It departs from the standard ensemble learning philosophy of *increasing* diversity among members, arguing instead that SNN temporal subnetworks already exceed the effective ensemble limit and that *reducing* cross-timestep membrane potential differences is necessary for stability. This contrasts with prior temporal consistency work (Zuo et al., 2024a; Qiu et al., 2024) that promotes output/feature consistency without addressing the underlying membrane potential distribution, and extends the brief ensemble observation in Ren et al. (2023) with a mechanistic diagnosis and targeted solution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ensemble perspective provides a principled diagnostic for a previously overlooked failure mode in SNNs: the first timestep producing essentially random outputs (10% on CIFAR10-DVS) due to the zero-initialized membrane potential creating a drastic distributional shift. The proposed membrane potential smoothing is remarkably lightweight (one learnable scalar per layer) and generalizes across neuron types, architectures, and modalities (1D speech, 2D events, 3D point clouds), making it an easy drop-in improvement for the SNN community. Open questions include whether the ensemble view can guide architecture design (e.g., how many timesteps are truly needed), whether the approach extends to asynchronous/event-driven SNNs where the notion of discrete timesteps is less clear, and whether the gradient pathway benefit compounds in very deep or very long temporal sequences.
