## Problem

Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) suffer from spectral bias of MLPs, requiring wide and deep networks with many forward/backward passes to capture high-frequency PDE solution components. Parametric grid representations mitigate this by offloading representational capacity to explicit grid parameters, but their fixed vertex positions force uniform high-resolution grids that waste parameters in smooth regions and risk overfitting. The paper addresses the lack of an adaptive, parameter-efficient mesh representation whose basis functions can dynamically relocate to where the PDE solution demands more resolution.

## Input / Output

The method takes spatial-temporal coordinates (rescaled to [0,1]^d or [0,2]^d depending on the problem) as input and produces a scalar PDE solution value at each query point. Training is fully unsupervised: the PDE differential operator, source term, and boundary/initial conditions define the loss, with collocation points sampled from the domain to estimate PDE residuals via Monte Carlo integration. The number of Gaussians N is fixed at initialization (e.g., 100–4000), and the covariance matrices are typically diagonal for computational efficiency, with each Gaussian contributing a single scalar feature (k=1) by default.

## Method

PIG represents the solution as u(x) = NN_θ(FE_φ(x)), where FE_φ is a mixture of N learnable Gaussian functions—each with trainable mean μ_i ∈ R^d, covariance Σ_i ∈ S_{++}^d, and feature weight f_i—and NN_θ is a single-hidden-layer MLP (16 tanh units) that refines the extracted feature. The key mechanism is that all Gaussian parameters (positions, shapes, and weights) are updated via gradient descent on the standard PINN loss (PDE residual + boundary condition terms), causing Gaussians to migrate toward regions of high residual or singular behavior, analogous to adaptive mesh refinement. This dynamic adaptivity replaces the static uniform grids of prior parametric methods, yielding a parameter-efficient representation that concentrates capacity where needed. The paper proves a Universal Approximation Theorem for PIGs by extending the RBF network UAT of Park & Sandberg (1991) to accommodate learnable covariance matrices, and demonstrates that the architecture is equivalent to an MLP with N RBF units in the first layer followed by two linear/tanh hidden layers.

## Results

PIG achieves competitive relative L² errors across five PDE benchmarks using only ~20K parameters (N=4000 Gaussians, d=2, k=1), compared to 250K+ parameters in JAX-PI. On the 2D Helmholtz equation, PIG reaches a best relative L² error of 2.12×10⁻⁵, outperforming PIXEL (8.63×10⁻⁴) by a factor of four. On the (2+1)D flow mixing problem, PIG achieves 2.67×10⁻⁴ versus SPINN's 2.90×10⁻³ (a ~10× improvement), and on the Klein-Gordon equation, PIG reaches 2.36×10⁻³ versus SPINN's 3.90×10⁻³. PIG also converges significantly faster per iteration (7.25×10⁻³ s/it vs. 1.67×10⁻² s/it for JAX-PI on Allen-Cahn).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PIG outperforms the parametric grid method PIXEL on the 2D Helmholtz equation by a factor of four (2.12×10⁻⁵ vs. 8.63×10⁻⁴) while using a comparable number of parameters. Against SPINN, PIG achieves substantially lower errors on all three shared benchmarks: flow mixing (2.67×10⁻⁴ vs. 2.90×10⁻³, ~10×), nonlinear diffusion (1.44×10⁻³ vs. 6.10×10⁻³, ~4×), and Klein-Gordon (2.36×10⁻³ vs. 3.90×10⁻³, ~1.6×). On the Allen-Cahn equation, PIG's best error (5.93×10⁻⁵) is slightly worse than PirateNet (2.24×10⁻⁵) and JAX-PI (5.37×10⁻⁵), but PIG uses only ~20K parameters versus over 250K for JAX-PI and converges roughly 2× faster per iteration. Compared to the concurrent PI-GS method, PIG achieves errors two to three orders of magnitude lower on Burgers' equation (7.68×10⁻⁴ vs. 1.62×10⁻¹) while being ~5× faster per iteration. Against PIRBN (fixed-position RBF networks), PIG achieves errors two orders of magnitude smaller on both benchmark equations, attributed to learnable Gaussian positions and the added lightweight MLP.

## Relation to Prior Work

PIG sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) PINNs, which provide the optimization framework (PDE residual loss + gradient descent); (2) parametric grid representations like PIXEL and PGCAN, which replace large MLPs with explicit feature grids but suffer from fixed vertex positions; and (3) adaptive mesh refinement from classical numerical analysis, which motivates dynamically relocating basis functions toward high-error regions. The key conceptual departure from prior parametric grid methods is making both the positions (μ) and shapes (Σ) of the basis functions learnable, turning a static grid into a moving, deforming set of Gaussians. The connection to 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023) is architectural rather than task-based: PIG repurposes the Gaussian primitive for unsupervised PDE solving rather than supervised radiance field rendering, and the universal approximation theorem is extended from the fixed-center RBF case (Park & Sandberg, 1991) to the variable-covariance setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PIG offers a practical efficiency win: a ~20K-parameter model (4000 Gaussians + 16-unit MLP) matches or beats 250K+ parameter PINN architectures while training roughly 2× faster per iteration, which matters for problems where repeated forward/backward passes through deep networks are the bottleneck. The locality argument—that only nearby Gaussians contribute to a query point, naturally alleviating spectral bias without Fourier features or SIREN activations—is a clean conceptual insight that could inform other basis-function-based PDE solvers. However, the fixed number of Gaussians (no dynamic insertion/removal), the lack of a convergence analysis, and the failure of dense covariance matrices to train from scratch are significant open questions that limit the method's generality. The separable PIG (SPIG) extension showing 3–20× improvements over SPINN on high-dimensional problems hints at a promising direction for tackling the curse of dimensionality, but it remains a proof-of-concept.
