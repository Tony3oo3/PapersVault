## Problem

Despite widespread empirical efforts to reduce hallucinations in generative models, no rigorous learning-theoretic characterization exists for when non-hallucinating learning is statistically possible. This paper shows that, surprisingly, non-hallucinating learning is impossible from training data alone—even for a hypothesis class of size two and a fully truthful training set—unless the learner incorporates inductive biases about the underlying facts set. The central question is: what structural assumptions on the facts set and demonstrator are necessary and sufficient for non-hallucinating learnability?

## Input / Output

The learner receives n i.i.d. samples drawn from a faithful demonstrator q (a distribution supported entirely on the facts set T) and has access to a concept class C ⊆ 2^X that encodes prior knowledge about which subsets of X could be the facts set. The output is a probability distribution p̂_n over the instance space X (the generative model) that must simultaneously achieve hallucination rate ≤ ε with respect to T and retain at least (1−γ) fraction of the demonstrator's information as measured by a chosen information functional I (e.g., Shannon entropy, Rényi entropy, or out-of-sample mass). Notably, the learner never observes T or q directly—only the samples—and the demonstrator q is unconstrained beyond faithfulness to T.

## Method

The paper establishes a complete theoretical framework via three pillars. First, an impossibility result (Theorem 1) proves that agnostic proper non-hallucinating learning fails even for |P|=2, using a probabilistic construction of two hard instances (q,T)~μ₁ and (q,T)~μ₂ whose induced sample distributions are indistinguishable (via a birthday-paradox argument on large discrete sets) yet require different hypotheses, combined with a Le Cam-style TV-distance contradiction. Second, a positive result (Theorem 2) shows that if T belongs to a concept class C of finite VC-dimension d, an improper learner can achieve 0-approximate non-hallucinating learning with sample complexity O((d·log(1/ε)+log(1/δ))/ε) by maximizing an information measure over all distributions that do not ε-violate any set in the version space C_n = {T'∈C : all samples fall in T'}, with a matching Ω(d/ε) lower bound (Theorem 3) via a random-construction argument. Third, for proper learning (Theorem 4), an additional 'sufficiently informative' condition on the demonstrator is required, ensuring that within a local neighborhood of C induced by q, some hypothesis in P achieves low hallucination rate; the learning rule then restricts the argmax to P while maintaining the version-space constraint.

## Results

The paper establishes that agnostic proper non-hallucinating learning is statistically impossible even for a hypothesis class of size 2 (Theorem 1), with the learned model hallucinating with probability > δ for any δ ≤ 1/3. For improper learners, non-hallucinating learning is achievable when the facts set lies in a concept class of finite VC-dimension d, with sample complexity O((d·log(1/ε) + log(1/δ))/ε) (Theorem 2), and a matching lower bound of Ω(d/ε) for the worst-case concept classes (Theorem 3). For proper learners, a sufficient 'sufficiently informative demonstrator' condition (Definition 4) yields learnability with sample complexity Ω((VC(C)·log(1/ξ(ε)) + log(1/δ))/ξ(ε)) (Theorem 4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or dataset comparisons. The key theoretical distinctions are between learning paradigms: (1) Agnostic proper learning is impossible (Theorem 1, Corollary 1), contrasting sharply with classical PAC learning where any finite hypothesis class is 3-agnostic learnable. (2) Improper learning succeeds under finite VC-dimension assumptions (Theorem 2), with the VC-dimension providing a tight minimax characterization (Theorem 3). (3) Proper learning requires an additional 'sufficiently informative demonstrator' condition beyond VC-dimension (Theorem 4), as demonstrated by Example 3 where even |P|=|C|=2 suffices to break proper learning. The paper positions its impossibility results alongside Kalai & Vempala (2024) on calibrated models and computability-based impossibility results (Xu et al., 2024; Banerjee et al., 2024), but distinguishes itself by characterizing the minimal structural assumptions (finite VC-dimension concept classes) under which non-hallucinating learning becomes statistically possible.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on classical PAC learning theory (Shalev-Shwartz & Ben-David) and information-theoretic lower-bound techniques (Le Cam's two-point method, Fan's inequality from Polyanskiy & Wu) to formalize a new learning task: non-hallucinating learning of generative models. It departs from prior impossibility results on hallucination (Kalai & Vempala 2024 on calibrated models, Xu et al. 2024 and Banerjee et al. 2024 on computability) by providing a learning-theoretic characterization of the minimal structural assumptions (finite VC-dimension of the facts set) under which non-hallucinating learning becomes possible, rather than proving universal impossibility. The paper explicitly contrasts its framework with distribution PAC learning, showing that the two tasks are fundamentally different in their learnability conditions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that hallucination mitigation cannot be achieved by training data alone, even when the data is perfectly truthful, providing a rigorous theoretical justification for why inductive biases (e.g., RLHF, factual constraints) are necessary in practice. The systematic use of VC-dimension as the complexity measure for the facts set offers a principled starting point for designing hallucination-aware learning algorithms, though the proposed learning rules are computationally inefficient. Open questions include finding fine-grained complexity characterizations for specific information measures, developing computationally tractable algorithms, and extending the framework to settings where the demonstrator is not fully faithful.
