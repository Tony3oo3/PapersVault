## Problem

Existing data contribution methods based on influence functions assume i.i.d. data, making them inapplicable to time series with inherent temporal dependencies. The two prior extensions—conditional influence (Kunsch, 1984), which only considers a time point's immediate predecessors, and LWCV (Ghosh et al., 2020), which is restricted to latent state-space models—fail to capture the full influence of a time point across diverse temporal contexts or to generalize to arbitrary model architectures.

## Input / Output

TimeInf takes a time series, a trained model (linear AR, RNN, LSTM, transformer, or gradient boosting), and a block length m as input, and produces per-time-point influence scores. For anomaly detection, the self-influence variant scores each point by its impact on its own reconstruction, normalized to [0,1]; for forecasting, the test-influence variant averages block influences over all test windows. For multivariate series, self-influence is computed per dimension and averaged, rather than fitting a joint vector AR model, to avoid parameter explosion and cross-dimensional interference.

## Method

TimeInf defines the influence of a single time point z by integrating the block influence function over all overlapping time blocks of length m that contain z, formalized as an expectation under an empirical contaminating distribution G^m that is a uniform mixture of point masses at those blocks. For M-estimators (e.g., linear AR with MSE loss), this yields a closed-form expression: TimeInf = −ψ(z_test)ᵀ H⁻¹ ∫ψ(z^[m]) dG^m(z^[m]), where H is the empirical Hessian. The key distinction from conditional influence is that the integral over multiple temporal arrangements makes the score robust to any single block configuration, and the method is model-agnostic—extending to deep networks via conjugate gradient or Hessian-free (TracIn) approximations, and to non-differentiable models via nonparametric leave-one-out influence. For anomaly detection, the self-influence variant (test point = training point) exploits the intuition that anomalies are easier to reconstruct using themselves than using normal data, yielding high scores precisely at anomalous time points.

## Results

On the five primary anomaly detection benchmarks, TimeInf achieves the highest AUC across all datasets: 0.79 on UCR (a 20% improvement over the next-best method at 0.65), 0.73 on SMAP, 0.72 on MSL, 0.64 on NAB-Traffic, and 0.87 on SMD. TimeInf also outperforms state-of-the-art transformer-based detectors (TranAD, DCdetector) by 7–30% and 3–17% in AUC, respectively, while requiring substantially less computation time (2.8–23.6 s) than most baselines. In the data pruning experiment for time series forecasting, TimeInf produces a >10% drop in R² after removing 30% of blocks on the Traffic dataset, compared to <2% for other data valuation methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TimeInf achieves the highest AUC on all five primary datasets, outperforming every baseline. Against the closest data-attribution competitor, Block LOOCV, TimeInf wins on AUC for UCR (0.79 vs. not completed), SMAP (0.73 vs. 0.71), MSL (0.72 vs. 0.70), and NAB-Traffic (0.64 vs. 0.62), and edges ahead on SMD (0.87 vs. 0.85), while also being 700× faster on SMD (20.9 s vs. 15,365 s). Against Conditional Influence (Kunsch 1984), TimeInf leads by 0.14 AUC on UCR (0.79 vs. 0.65) and by 0.02–0.03 on the remaining four datasets. Against LWCV (Ghosh et al. 2020), TimeInf wins by 0.09–0.24 AUC across all five datasets. Among dedicated anomaly detectors, TimeInf surpasses DCdetector (the strongest non-attribution baseline) by 0.05–0.17 AUC and TranAD by 0.14–0.32 AUC. On F1, TimeInf is competitive but not uniformly best—Conditional Influence achieves higher F1 on UCR (0.32 vs. 0.23) and Block LOOCV on SMD (0.42 vs. 0.25)—suggesting TimeInf's AUC advantage reflects better ranking of anomalies rather than optimal thresholding. On the secondary set of seven datasets with potentially mislabeled annotations (Table 4), TimeInf is comparable to or slightly below Isolation Forest, which the authors attribute to ground-truth label quality issues rather than methodological weakness.

## Relation to Prior Work

TimeInf unifies and extends three prior lines of work on influence-based data attribution: the regular influence function for i.i.d. data (Hampel, 1974; Koh & Liang, 2017), the conditional influence for AR models that is restricted to a single observed predecessor context (Kunsch, 1984), and the leave-within-structure-out cross-validation (LWCV) that is limited to latent state-space models (Ghosh et al., 2020). The key conceptual departure is replacing the single point-mass contaminating distribution with an integration over all overlapping time blocks containing the target point, yielding a model-agnostic per-time-point influence that captures diverse temporal arrangements. This is formalized under a general M-estimator framework (Definition 2.1) that subsumes all three prior methods as special cases.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TimeInf provides a practical, model-agnostic tool for time series data contribution that outperforms both existing attribution methods and state-of-the-art deep anomaly detectors (TranAD, DCdetector) using only a linear AR model, suggesting that influence-based scoring may be a more principled route to anomaly detection than learned reconstruction. The ability to expose mislabeled ground-truth annotations in widely-used benchmarks (NAB-Tweets, PSM, KDD-Cup99) has direct implications for the reliability of published anomaly detection comparisons. Open questions remain: performance gains are marginal for high-dimensional multivariate data, the method currently targets validation loss only, and whether it scales effectively to time series foundation models with millions of parameters is untested.
