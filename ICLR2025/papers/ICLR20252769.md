## Problem

LLMs perform substantially better on factual (recall-based) questions than on counterfactual (reasoning-based) questions, yet existing evaluation metrics like factual and counterfactual error rates fail to capture whether a model's answers are causally consistent with each other. This paper asks whether LLMs can be explicitly fine-tuned to improve causal reasoning, and to what extent such improvements generalize beyond the specific cause-effect relationships demonstrated during training.

## Input / Output

The method consumes paired natural-language questions within a shared context: a factual question (e.g., 'Is Dave happy?') and a corresponding counterfactual question that negates the cause (e.g., 'Suppose Anna is not happy; is Dave happy?'). The model produces binary Yes/No answers to each. Evaluation goes beyond individual answer correctness to compute unit-wise causal consistency metrics (N-IR, S-IR, AN-IR, AS-IR) that check whether the joint factual-counterfactual answer pair correctly identifies necessity, sufficiency, absent necessity, and absent sufficiency for each context realization.

## Method

The paper introduces three data-generation procedures for fine-tuning: (1) Supervised CF, which generates correct factual and counterfactual answer pairs via a language-model-based answer generator; (2) Preference-based CF, which samples multiple answers from the target model and creates DPO preferences by preferring correct over incorrect answers per question; and (3) Preference-based Causal Consistency Feedback (CCF), the key contribution, which prompts the model to answer both the factual and counterfactual question in a single dialogue and then elicits DPO preferences based on a joint reward function R that scores the consistency of the (factual, counterfactual) answer pair against the true unit-wise necessity/sufficiency labels. CCF directly targets the inconsistency rates (Avg-IR) rather than the average error rate (Avg-ER), forcing the model to coordinate its factual and counterfactual reasoning. The paper further defines a taxonomy of four generalization modes (common-cause, common-effect, inductive, deductive) and shows that fine-tuning generalizes most effectively in the inductive mode, where both the target cause and effect have been demonstrated (albeit in separate relationships).

## Results

Across three real-world causal reasoning problems (Healthcare, Engineering, Math Benchmarking), the proposed methods significantly reduce error and inconsistency rates in the in-domain setting: SFT-F&CF achieves the lowest Avg-ER (0.42× base) and Avg-IR (0.44× base), while DPO+CCF achieves 0.48× and 0.47× respectively. In generalization modes, inductive transfer is most effective (DPO-F&CF: 0.58× Avg-ER, 0.50× Avg-IR), followed by cause-based deduction (DPO+CCF: 0.57× Avg-ER, 0.51× Avg-IR). Common-cause generalization is the weakest mode, where all fine-tuned methods perform worse than the base model (scores >1.00), indicating that reasoning improvements do not transfer when only the cause is shared.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares six fine-tuning configurations: Base (no fine-tuning), SFT-OnlyF and DPO-OnlyF (trained on factual questions only), SFT-F&CF and DPO-F&CF (trained on both factual and counterfactual questions), and DPO+CCF (DPO with causal consistency feedback targeting inconsistency rates). In the in-domain setting, SFT-F&CF achieves the largest improvement over Base (0.42× Avg-ER vs. 1.00×), outperforming SFT-OnlyF (0.82×) by nearly 2×, demonstrating that counterfactual examples are essential. DPO+CCF consistently achieves the best or near-best results in generalization modes: it is the only method that improves over Base in effect-based deduction (0.77× Avg-ER vs. 1.00×), where all other methods degrade. In inductive generalization, DPO-F&CF slightly edges out DPO+CCF (0.58× vs. 0.69× Avg-ER), suggesting that standard preference optimization over paired factual/counterfactual answers can be competitive when the causal structure is simple. Notably, all methods fail in common-cause generalization (scores >1.00), with DPO+CCF performing worst (2.04× Avg-ER), indicating that fine-tuning for reasoning does not transfer when only the cause variable is shared.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the causal reasoning evaluation framework of González and Nori (2024), which used distributional matching of probabilities of necessity and sufficiency (PN/PS), by introducing unit-wise inconsistency rates (N-IR, S-IR, AN-IR, AS-IR) that prevent errors across different contexts from canceling out. It also builds on the DPO framework of Rafailov et al. (2024) by replacing the standard correctness-based reward with a causal consistency reward that jointly evaluates factual and counterfactual answers within the same context. Conceptually, the paper departs from prior fine-tuning-for-factuality work (Tian et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2024a) by arguing that question-by-question correctness is insufficient for reasoning, and that paired factual-counterfactual dialogues are needed to elicit higher-level causal concepts like necessity and sufficiency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical recipe (causal consistency feedback via DPO) and a principled evaluation framework for improving LLM causal reasoning, addressing a well-documented recall-reasoning gap. The taxonomy of generalization modes (common-cause, common-effect, inductive, deductive) and the finding that fine-tuning transfers inductively but not deductively offers actionable guidance for practitioners designing reasoning fine-tuning pipelines. Key limitations include the restriction to binary cause-effect variables, evaluation on a single small model (Phi-3-Mini), and the reliance on a separate LLM (Llama-3-8B) for answer extraction, leaving open whether these findings hold for larger models, multi-valued variables, or more complex causal graphs.
