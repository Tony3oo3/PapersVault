## Problem

The convexity conjecture for SGD solution sets (modulo permutation symmetries) has been shown to require very wide networks and fails for narrower, deeper architectures, complex datasets, and non-SGD optimizers. This paper asks whether a weaker geometric property—star-shaped connectivity, where at least one 'star model' is linearly connected to all other solutions—holds more generally, and provides both a theoretical result for 2-layer linear networks and extensive empirical evidence for deep CNNs and transformers.

## Input / Output

The Starlight algorithm consumes a training dataset, a finite set of independently trained source models Z (each trained with different random seeds), and an initial model θ₀, and produces a single star model θ* whose weights are linearly connected (low loss barrier) to every source model and to held-out models not used in training. Downstream, the star model and its surrounding star domain (the collection of line segments from θ* to each source model) serve as a posterior for Bayesian model averaging, yielding ranked uncertainty estimates (AUROC, ECE), or as a single-model substitute for ensembles, producing class predictions at O(1) inference cost instead of O(n).

## Method

Starlight (Algorithm 1) trains a candidate star model by minimizing the expected loss along linear interpolation paths between the candidate θ and permuted versions of source models: at each step it samples a random source model θₙ and interpolation coefficient t ~ Unif[0,1], computes the loss at (1−t)·θ + t·θₙ, and updates θ with a gradient scaled by (1−t). Permutations are refreshed every m steps via weight matching (maximizing the dot product between θ and each θₙ), avoiding the need for the exact winning permutation at every iteration. Theoretically, Theorem 1 proves for 2-layer linear networks that the probability of a randomly sampled solution being a star model (with tolerance ε) approaches 1 as width m → ∞, and that star-domainness emerges at smaller widths than full convexity. Empirically, the star model is verified by measuring loss barriers against held-out models disjoint from Z, showing barriers roughly 5× lower than regular-to-regular barriers across ResNet-18, VGG, DenseNet, and ViT architectures on CIFAR-10/100, ImageNet-1k, and MNIST.

## Results

The Starlight algorithm produces star models whose loss barriers with held-out solutions are consistently and substantially lower than the barriers between two arbitrary regular solutions. On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18, the star-regular barrier is 0.078±0.007 versus 0.383±0.056 for regular-regular (a ~5× reduction). This trend holds across architectures (VGG11: 0.131 vs. 0.515; DenseNet: 1.729 vs. 4.634), datasets (CIFAR-100 ResNet-18: 0.756 vs. 2.905; ImageNet-1k ResNet-18: 2.794 vs. 5.948), and optimizers (Adam ResNet-18: 0.335 vs. 1.368). For model fusion, star models with 50 source models achieve 78.38% on CIFAR-100, outperforming individual regular models (77.33%) while requiring only O(1) inference cost versus O(n) for ensembles (81.30%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between star-regular loss barriers (star model to held-out solution) and regular-regular loss barriers (two arbitrary independently trained solutions), which serve as the baseline representing the convexity conjecture. Star models win decisively in every setting: the star-regular barrier is roughly one-third to one-fifth of the regular-regular barrier across all eight dataset-architecture combinations tested. For model fusion (Table 2), star models outperform individual regular models by ~1% on CIFAR-100 (78.38% vs. 77.33% at |Z|=50) but remain ~3% below full ensembles (81.30%), while reducing inference cost from O(n) to O(1). For Bayesian model averaging, the star domain posterior achieves better AUROC (both max-probability and entropy-based) than the deep ensemble baseline, though ECE is slightly worse. The paper also shows that increasing the number of source models from 2 to 50 monotonically decreases the star-regular barrier, and that wider networks (8× WideResNet) reduce both barrier types, with star-regular barriers remaining ~1/3 of regular-regular at every width.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the convexity conjecture of Entezari et al. (2021), which posits that SGD solution sets are convex modulo permutation symmetries, by proposing a strictly weaker geometric property: star domain connectivity. Where the convexity conjecture requires every pair of solutions to be linearly connected (and is known to fail for narrow networks), the star domain conjecture only requires the existence of a single 'star model' that is linearly connected to all other solutions. The Starlight algorithm operationalizes this by building on the weight-matching procedure from Ainsworth et al. (2022) and the loss barrier formulation from Frankle et al. (2020), while departing from prior re-basin methods that focus on pairwise connectivity rather than a universal connector.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The star domain conjecture provides a more realistic geometric characterization of loss landscapes for the narrow, deep networks that practitioners actually use, filling the gap between the well-studied mode connectivity (any path) and the overly optimistic convexity conjecture. Practical implications include improved uncertainty estimation via Bayesian model averaging over the star domain and a lower-cost alternative to ensembles for model fusion. Key limitations include the absence of theoretical proof for deep networks (only 2-layer linear nets are covered), non-zero (albeit reduced) loss barriers, and the computational cost of training star models. Open questions include whether the conjecture extends to transformers with their unique permutation symmetries, and whether better permutation-finding algorithms can drive barriers closer to zero.
