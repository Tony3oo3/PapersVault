## Problem

Current world models for robotics learn statistical representations of dynamics that cannot generalize beyond their training distribution, producing unrealistic behaviors and hallucinations unsuitable for real-world manipulation. Existing simulation-based reconstructions combine Gaussian Splatting with physics engines but lack compositionality and object-centricity, preventing agents from controllably imagining novel object configurations. The paper asks whether a world model can be made explicitly compositional, object-centric, and physics-grounded enough to generate valid training data from minimal demonstrations.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a 200-frame RGB-D video of the scene (1280×720) captured by a rotating camera, zero-shot segmentation masks from the DEVA open-vocabulary tracker (prompted with minimal labels like 'object' and 'table'), and a small set of demonstration trajectories encoded as 6-DoF end-effector poses and gripper states. It produces a compositional world model where each object is represented as a pair of 2DGS Gaussian Splats (for photorealistic rendering) and a TSDF-extracted mesh (for PyBullet physics), plus novel imagined demonstrations rendered at 128×128 from multiple simulated cameras. The final output is a PerAct policy trained on the combined original and imagined data, evaluated on 50 test configurations.

## Method

DREMA constructs a compositional manipulation world model in three stages: (1) object-centric 2DGS reconstruction from masked RGB-D video with depth regularization, (2) mesh extraction via TSDF and embedding into PyBullet with physical parameters (mass, friction) to form controllable object assets, and (3) equivariant transformation of both object positions and robot action trajectories (roto-translation of the environment, object rotation around the target) to generate novel demonstrations. The key insight is that explicit object-centric representations make equivariant transformations trivially valid—applying the same rigid transform to objects and end-effector poses preserves task success without re-planning. Generated demonstrations are verified by replaying them in the physics simulator and checking that final object positions match expected goals within a 1.5 cm threshold, filtering out invalid trajectories. This pipeline enables one-shot policy learning, expanding a handful of real demonstrations into ~800 imagined ones that cover a wider workspace while maintaining photorealistic observations.

## Results

In single-task simulation (RLBench, 9 tasks, 50 test configs × 5 runs), DREMA+Original achieves 25.1% average success rate, a +9.1% improvement over PerAct trained on original demonstrations (16.0%). DREMA using only imagined demonstrations (19.1%) still outperforms PerAct by +3.1%. On the real Franka Emika Panda robot, DREMA doubles average task performance to 62.9% vs. 31.7% for PerAct, with particularly strong out-of-distribution generalization (58.3% OOD vs. 25.0% for PerAct). In multi-task settings, DREMA+Original reaches 35.5% average, a +13.1% gain over PerAct.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DREMA+Original (25.1% avg) outperforms all four baselines: PerAct with original data (16.0%, +9.1%), random patches from Laskin et al. 2020 (17.8%, +7.3%), random table color from Chen et al. 2023 (18.2%, +6.9%), and random distractors from Bharadhwaj et al. 2024 (20.1%, +5.0%). Notably, DREMA improves performance across all nine tasks relative to PerAct, whereas invariant augmentations (random patches, table color, distractors) degrade performance on several tasks (e.g., random patches drops slide block from 48.4% to 36.8%, and stack blocks to 0%). Even DREMA using only imagined demonstrations (19.1%) surpasses PerAct and matches or exceeds the best invariant augmentation (distractors at 20.1% on some tasks but 19.1% overall). On the real robot, DREMA more than doubles PerAct's average success (62.9% vs. 31.7%) and shows far stronger OOD generalization (58.3% vs. 25.0%), confirming that equivariant transformations in a physics-grounded world model produce more transferable training data than pixel-level or scene-level invariant augmentations.

## Relation to Prior Work

DREMA sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) implicit world models (Dreamer, TD-MPC2, UniSim) that learn statistical dynamics but cannot explicitly compose novel object configurations; (2) simulation-based scene reconstructions (PhysGaussian, VR-GS, PEGASUS) that combine Gaussian Splatting with physics but lack object-centricity and compositionality; and (3) imitation learning data augmentation (MimicGen, MoCoDa) that recombines existing trajectories rather than generating entirely new physical configurations. The key departure is making the world model explicitly compositional and object-centric by segmenting Gaussian Splats per object via open-vocabulary trackers (DEVA) and embedding them in a physics simulator (PyBullet), enabling equivariant transformations that simultaneously shift objects and actions while preserving task validity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for data-scarce robotics: demonstrating one-shot policy learning on a real Franka Emika Panda and doubling average success rates (62.9% vs 31.7%) with imagined data addresses a core bottleneck in deployment. However, limitations are substantial—full scene observability is required, only simple rigid objects are handled, physical parameters are hand-tuned rather than identified, and the approach is open-loop without feedback. Open questions include extending to articulated/deformable objects, incorporating closed-loop feedback into world model construction, scaling to larger environments, and validating whether the benefits persist with more recent policy architectures (RVT-2, Diffusion Policy) that may already handle distribution shift differently.
