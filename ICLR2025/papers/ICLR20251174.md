## Problem

As LLM forecasters rapidly approach human-level accuracy, evaluating them becomes infeasible for long-horizon questions whose ground truth resolves years in the future. Prior consistency-check frameworks (Fluri et al., 2023) used ad hoc, check-specific metrics that could not be compared or aggregated across different logical relations. This paper asks whether principled, unified consistency metrics can serve as instantaneous proxies for forecasting accuracy when ground truth is unavailable.

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes binary forecasting questions (title, resolution criteria, resolution date) and instantiates them into logically-related tuples of size 2–4 (e.g., (P, ¬P) for NEGATION, (P, Q, P∧Q, P∨Q) for ANDOR). Each question in a tuple is independently queried to an LLM forecaster, which returns a single probability in [0,1]. The output is a pair of violation scores per tuple — an arbitrage score (maximum guaranteed profit an arbitrageur can extract) and a frequentist score (standard deviations from a Monte Carlo world model) — plus a binary pass/fail flag per check using a fixed threshold (V ≥ 0.01 for arbitrage, γσ = 0.129 for frequentist).

## Method

The core contribution is two principled inconsistency metrics derived from the logical relation R of each check: (1) an arbitrage metric that models the forecaster's probabilities as prices on a logarithmic market maker and computes the maximum guaranteed profit an arbitrageur can extract by choosing alternative prices, and (2) a frequentist metric that frames consistency as a hypothesis test against a Monte Carlo world model, measuring deviation in standard deviations. These metrics are applied uniformly across 10 logical consistency checks (NEGATION, PARAPHRASE, CONSEQUENCE, ANDOR, AND, OR, BUT, COND, CONDCOND, EXPEVIDENCE), enabling cross-check aggregation in a common unit. The evaluation pipeline scrapes resolved questions from Metaculus/Manifold, generates questions from NewsAPI articles, and synthesizes 2028-resolving questions, then instantiates tuples, elicits forecasts from 15 LLM configurations, and scores consistency. The paper further introduces ArbitrageForecaster, which patches a base forecaster by generating related questions and arbitraging its forecasts, showing that consistency gains on optimized checks do not generalize to held-out checks or to ground-truth Brier score.

## Results

The paper's central finding is that consistency metrics correlate positively with ground-truth Brier score across all tested LLM forecasters. The strongest individual check is COND (conditional probability consistency), achieving R=0.92 (arbitrage) and R=0.87 (frequentist) on the scraped dataset, and R=0.71/0.69 on NewsAPI. Aggregated consistency scores yield R=0.85 (frequentist, scraped) and R=0.66 (frequentist, NewsAPI), substantially outperforming MMLU error-rate correlations of 0.55 and 0.38 on the same datasets. Even OpenAI's o1-mini, a top reasoning model, violates consistency thresholds on 50–80% of tuples across most checks (e.g., 79% on BUT, 71% on ANDOR for the scraped dataset).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Rather than comparing a single method against baselines, the paper evaluates which of 10 logical consistency checks best predicts forecasting accuracy (Brier score). COND and CONDCOND (conditional probability checks) are the strongest proxies, with correlations of 0.71–0.92, far exceeding simpler checks like NEGATION (0.60/0.67 on scraped, but -0.36/-0.13 on NewsAPI) or ANDOR (0.02–0.25). Aggregating across all checks improves correlation to 0.85 (frequentist, scraped) versus the best single check at 0.92 (arbitrage, COND). As a reference, MMLU error-rate correlations with Brier score are only 0.38 and 0.55, confirming that consistency metrics provide substantially more signal than a general capability benchmark. The ArbitrageForecaster (a method that patches base forecasts via arbitrage) improves consistency on its target check (e.g., NEGATION violation drops with recursive depth) but fails to generalize to held-out checks or to improve actual Brier score, indicating that consistency gains from targeted optimization do not transfer to overall forecasting quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the consistency check framework of Fluri et al. (2023) from general LLM evaluation to the specific domain of probabilistic forecasting, replacing their ad hoc linear inconsistency metrics with two principled alternatives: an arbitrage metric grounded in logarithmic market scoring rules (Hanson, 2002) and a frequentist metric based on hypothesis testing. It also situates itself within the rapidly evolving LLM forecasting literature (Halawi et al. 2024, Schoenegger et al. 2024, Phan et al. 2024), which has shown LLMs approaching human-level accuracy on prediction market platforms, motivating the need for evaluation methods that do not require waiting for ground truth resolutions. The key conceptual departure from Fluri et al. is the derivation of a common 'unit' for inconsistency scores across different logical checks, enabling principled aggregation and comparison, and the empirical demonstration that these consistency scores correlate with actual Brier score performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that consistency metrics provide an instantaneous evaluation signal for forecasters on long-horizon questions where ground truth is unavailable for years, which is critical for decision-making contexts. The 2028 benchmark (3,000 consistency checks) offers a concrete long-term evaluation tool as LLM forecasters continue to improve. However, the correlation between consistency and Brier score is based on a limited set of forecasters and may be confounded by general model quality (stronger models are both more consistent and better forecasters), and the ArbitrageForecaster experiments show that improving consistency on targeted checks does not transfer to other checks or to actual forecasting accuracy. Open questions include whether consistency can serve as a training signal without being Goodharted, and whether the framework extends to RAG-augmented or ensemble forecasters that are currently the strongest systems.
