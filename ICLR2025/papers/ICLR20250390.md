## Problem

Standard diffusion and flow-matching models with Gaussian priors fail to capture heavy-tailed behavior in target distributions, even with proper normalization and preconditioning. In high-dimensional spaces, the Gaussian prior concentrates mass on a spherical narrow shell, systematically underestimating the density of rare extreme events. This limitation is critical for applications like weather forecasting, where accurately modeling tail events (hurricanes, heatwaves) is essential but empirically challenging due to limited observations.

## Input / Output

For unconditional generation, the model starts from a multivariate Student-t noise sample t_d(0, I_d, ν) and produces km-scale weather fields (Vertically Integrated Liquid or Vertical Wind Velocity at level 20) from the HRRR dataset. For conditional generation, it takes the current-hour weather state as input and predicts the next-hour evolution of the target channel. The single scalar hyperparameter ν (degrees of freedom) controls tail heaviness across all dimensions, with lower values producing heavier tails and ν→∞ recovering standard Gaussian diffusion.

## Method

The paper repurposes the diffusion framework by replacing all Gaussian components with multivariate Student-t distributions: the perturbation kernel q(x_t|x_0) is a Student-t, the forward denoising posterior is derived via the conditional Student-t distribution, and the reverse posterior is parameterized as a Student-t with degrees of freedom ν+d. Because KL divergence lacks a closed form for Student-t distributions, the training objective uses the γ-power divergence (with γ = −2/(ν+d)), which admits a tractable closed-form expression between two Student-t distributions. The resulting simplified loss is an x_0-prediction objective identical in structure to standard diffusion, differing only in sampling noise from a Student-t rather than Gaussian distribution. The authors derive continuous-time ODE and SDE samplers (Proposition 2), showing that the t-EDM ODE is equivalent to EDM's deterministic dynamics, so transitioning from EDM to t-EDM requires as few as two lines of code change. Specific instantiations t-EDM and t-Flow extend EDM and straight-line flows respectively, with the Student-t prior providing controllable tail generation through the single ν parameter.

## Results

On the HRRR weather dataset, t-EDM (ν=3) dramatically outperforms standard EDM in tail estimation, achieving a KS statistic of 0.114 on the VIL test channel versus 0.991 for EDM, and 0.286 on w20 versus 0.978. For conditional next-hour prediction, t-EDM (ν=5) achieves the best CRPS of 1.609 on VIL (vs. 1.696 for EDM) and t-EDM (ν=3) achieves the best KS of 0.111 on w20 (vs. 0.345 for EDM). The degrees-of-freedom parameter ν provides controllable tail heaviness: lower ν yields heavier tails and better extreme-event coverage, while ν→∞ recovers standard Gaussian diffusion.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against standard EDM (Karras et al., 2022), t-EDM (ν=3) achieves a 8.7× improvement in the KS tail statistic on VIL (0.114 vs. 0.991) and a 3.4× improvement on w20 (0.286 vs. 0.978), while also reducing the Kurtosis Ratio from 45.35 to 0.54 on VIL. The two EDM variants with additional tricks (INC preprocessing and PCP noise modulation) narrow the gap but still underperform t-EDM on KS for both channels. Against Gaussian Flow (Albergo et al., 2023), t-Flow (ν=3) improves the VIL test KS from 0.704 to 0.275 and the w20 test KS from 0.384 to 0.512 (though w20 gains are smaller). For conditional prediction, t-EDM consistently beats EDM on CRPS (1.609 vs. 1.696 on VIL) and KS (0.419 vs. 0.715 on VIL; 0.111 vs. 0.345 on w20), while also achieving better calibration (SSR closer to 1). The paper notes that INC and PCP are orthogonal to the Student-t formulation and could be combined with t-EDM for further gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the diffusion model framework (DDPM, EDM) and flow matching (Lipman et al., Albergo et al.) by replacing all Gaussian components—perturbation kernel, forward/reverse posteriors, and generative prior—with multivariate Student-t distributions, yielding t-EDM and t-Flow. It draws on prior heavy-tailed generative work (Jaini et al. 2020; Kim et al. 2024) that advocated Student-t priors in VAEs and normalizing flows but was limited to small-scale or toy datasets, and on the γ-power divergence (Eguchi 2021; Kim et al. 2024) as a tractable alternative to KL divergence for Student-t distributions. The key departure is bringing heavy-tailed modeling into the diffusion/flow paradigm at high-dimensional scale, with a principled connection to robust statistical estimators (Futami et al. 2018) that justifies the training objective beyond mere computational convenience.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For domains like weather and climate science where rare extreme events (hurricanes, heatwaves) carry disproportionate impact, the ability to controllably model tail behavior via a single scalar ν is practically valuable, and the minimal code overhead (two lines for t-EDM) makes adoption straightforward. Open questions include whether ν can be learned data-drivenly per channel, whether heteroskedastic (data-dependent) scale modeling further improves tail capture, and whether the approach generalizes beyond spatial-field weather data to other modalities (images, audio) with heavy-tailed statistics. The paper's validation is confined to the HRRR dataset and two channels, so broader empirical confirmation is needed before the method can be considered a general-purpose upgrade to diffusion pipelines.
