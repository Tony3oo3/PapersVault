## Problem

Linear probing classifiers produce a single concept vector per concept, but this vector varies substantially depending on the probing dataset and training process, undermining its robustness for both interpretability analysis and inference-time intervention. The paper asks whether a concept's representation in LLM hidden space is better captured as a distribution over vectors (a subspace) rather than a single point.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252992/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes hidden representations of the last token at a target layer (typically penultimate) from frozen decoder-only LLMs, paired with GPT-4o-generated probing datasets of 10,000 short text descriptions per concept (5,000 positive, 5,000 negative). It produces a d-dimensional Gaussian distribution (mean vector + diagonal covariance) per concept per layer, from which 1,000 sampled vectors within 1σ are drawn for evaluation. For intervention, the average of sampled vectors is added to the last token's hidden state at every layer except the first and last, with a strength parameter a controlling the blend.

## Method

GCS extends linear probing by training M=1,000 independent logistic regression classifiers, each on a randomly resampled subset (1,000 positive + 1,000 negative) of the full probing dataset, yielding M 'observed' weight vectors per concept. These vectors are used to fit a multivariate Gaussian N_d(μ_c, Σ_c) with a diagonal covariance matrix (assuming inter-dimension independence), where μ_c is the element-wise mean and Σ_c encodes per-dimension variance. New 'sampled' vectors are then drawn from this Gaussian within 1σ of the mean, representing the concept with varying degrees of relevance to the subspace center. For inference-time intervention, the average sampled vector is injected layer-by-layer via h^ℓ = (1−a)·h^ℓ + a·v_c^ℓ, where v_c^ℓ is scaled to match the hidden state's range. The key distinction from prior work is that GCS replaces a single deterministic vector with a probabilistic subspace, providing both a more robust representation and a tunable relevance knob (nσ) for downstream steering.

## Results

On the emotion steering task (steering Llama-2-7B to generate joyful movie reviews), GCS 1σ sampled vectors achieve a peak joyfulness score of 2.980 (at strength 0.064) while maintaining coherence scores between 3.531 and 6.480. The single linear vector reaches a marginally higher peak joyfulness of 3.020 (at strength 0.069) but with worse coherence (5.918) at that setting. Mean difference vectors are the least effective, peaking at only 1.800 joyfulness while producing the most incoherent outputs (coherence up to 6.306). GCS vectors sampled at 2σ–5σ show progressively weaker steering performance, confirming that vectors closer to the subspace center are more representative.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares GCS sampled vectors against two baselines: Mean Difference (average activation difference between positive and negative samples, following Rimsky et al., 2023) and a single Linear Vector (one logistic regression classifier trained on the full dataset, following Ousidhoum et al., 2021). GCS 1σ clearly outperforms Mean Difference on both joyfulness (peak 2.980 vs. 1.800) and coherence, with Mean Difference producing garbled outputs containing unicode artifacts at higher strengths. Against the single Linear Vector, GCS 1σ achieves comparable peak joyfulness (2.980 vs. 3.020) but the paper argues GCS is more robust because the single vector's performance depends on the specific training run and dataset, whereas GCS provides a distribution of vectors with guaranteed proximity to the concept subspace center. At matched strengths (e.g., 0.064), GCS 1σ achieves 2.980 joyfulness with 4.857 coherence, while the single linear vector at its best strength (0.069) reaches 3.020 joyfulness but with 5.918 coherence, indicating GCS better preserves text fluency. The authors note the single linear vector was trained on all 10,000 samples used to derive the GCS distribution, so it likely falls near the 1σ region, explaining the similar peak performance but inferior robustness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the linear probing paradigm for concept vector identification (Ousidhoum et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2018; Alain & Bengio, 2016) by replacing the single point-estimate vector with a Gaussian distribution over vectors, motivated by the observation that concept representations are inherently multifaceted. It builds directly on the representation engineering framework of Zou et al. (2023) for both the use of last-token hidden states and the inference-time intervention mechanism. The work also connects to the broader interpretability literature on hierarchical concept geometry (Park et al., 2024) and activation steering (Rimsky et al., 2023; Turner et al., 2023), positioning GCS as a distributional upgrade to the standard single-vector approach rather than a fundamentally new paradigm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GCS addresses a practical pain point in representation engineering: single concept vectors are unstable across training runs and dataset choices, undermining both interpretability analyses and downstream intervention reliability. The finding that 1σ-sampled vectors match or exceed single-vector accuracy while offering tunable relevance is useful for practitioners building steering pipelines. However, limitations temper the impact: the diagonal covariance assumption discards inter-dimensional correlations, data generation relies on GPT-4o (limiting applicability where such models are unavailable), plausibility is evaluated only on a predefined two-level hierarchy, and the intervention evaluation covers a single emotion-steering task on one model. Open questions include whether full covariance or non-Gaussian distributions would capture concept subspaces more faithfully, and whether the approach generalizes to compositional or abstract concepts beyond the tested categories.
