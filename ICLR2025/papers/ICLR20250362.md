## Problem

Prior work on test-time compute for LLM reasoning yields mixed results, with no systematic characterization of how different inference strategies (verifier-guided search vs. iterative revision) scale with compute or when each is most effective. This paper asks: given a fixed test-time compute budget, how should it be allocated per prompt to maximize performance, and to what extent can test-time compute substitute for additional pretraining FLOPs in a FLOPs-matched comparison?

## Input / Output

The method takes a math problem prompt and a test-time compute budget N, and produces a selected answer along with an adaptive per-prompt strategy allocation (e.g., which search algorithm, or what ratio of sequential-to-parallel compute). Difficulty estimation requires 2048 samples per question (via oracle correctness or PRM scores binned into five quantiles), a cost the authors note is not accounted for in their main analysis. The compute-optimal strategy is pre-computed per difficulty bin on a held-out validation set and then applied to test questions, making it a two-phase procedure that assumes access to a verifier at deployment.

## Method

The paper studies two mechanisms for scaling test-time compute: (1) searching against a process reward model (PRM) trained via Monte Carlo rollouts per step, using best-of-N, beam search, or lookahead search; and (2) refining the proposal distribution via a revision model fine-tuned on post-hoc constructed multi-turn trajectories pairing incorrect answers with a correlated correct answer. The key insight is that the optimal strategy is difficulty-dependent: easy problems benefit from sequential revisions (local refinement), while hard problems benefit from parallel search (global exploration over strategies). They formalize 'compute-optimal scaling' as selecting hyperparameters per prompt based on five difficulty quantiles of the base model's pass@1, achieving up to 4× efficiency gains over a best-of-N baseline. In a FLOPs-matched evaluation, they show that on easy-to-medium questions, test-time compute with a smaller model can outperform a ~14× larger model without additional inference compute, while on the hardest questions pretraining remains preferable.

## Results

The paper's headline finding is that compute-optimal test-time scaling (adapting strategy per question difficulty) outperforms a best-of-N baseline using up to 4× less compute in both the PRM search setting (e.g., 32 vs. 128 generations) and the iterative revision setting (e.g., 64 vs. 256 samples). In a FLOPs-matched evaluation, applying compute-optimal test-time compute to a smaller PaLM 2-S* model can outperform a ~14× larger model on easy and medium-difficulty questions, particularly when the inference-to-pretraining token ratio R is small (R << 1). On the hardest questions (difficulty bin 5), no test-time strategy makes meaningful progress, and additional pretraining remains more effective.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares compute-optimal adaptive scaling against a fixed best-of-N baseline across both search and revision settings, finding that the adaptive strategy matches or exceeds best-of-N accuracy with 4× fewer generations/samples. Within the search setting, beam search (M=4) outperforms best-of-N on medium-to-hard questions (difficulty bins 3/4) at lower budgets but degrades on easy questions (bins 1/2) at higher budgets due to PRM over-optimization; lookahead search consistently underperforms both alternatives. In the revision setting, sequential revisions narrowly outperform parallel sampling from the same model, and the optimal sequential-to-parallel ratio shifts from 100% sequential (easy questions) to a balanced mix (hard questions). In the FLOPs-matched comparison against a ~14× larger pretrained model, test-time compute wins on easy/medium questions when inference load is low (R << 1), but pretraining wins on hard questions or under high inference load (R >> 1). The PRM verifier consistently outperforms the ORM baseline, and the 'last step' aggregation strategy outperforms min and product aggregation for the PRM.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously studied but separately analyzed axes of test-time compute scaling—modifying the proposal distribution via iterative revision (building on Qu et al. 2024b and Kumar et al. 2024) and searching against a process reward model (building on Lightman et al. 2023, Wang et al. 2023, and Yao et al. 2023)—under a single MCMC-inspired framework. It departs from prior work, which largely reported mixed or negative results for test-time compute on reasoning, by showing that the apparent inconsistency stems from difficulty-dependent efficacy: the optimal strategy (sequential revision vs. parallel search, beam search vs. best-of-N) varies systematically with question difficulty. The FLOPs-matched comparison against a 14× larger pretrained model extends the compute-optimal scaling literature (Hoffmann et al. 2022) from pretraining to the inference regime, a comparison not previously conducted for LLM reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic evidence that, on easy-to-medium difficulty problems with low inference-to-pretraining token ratios, a smaller model augmented with compute-optimal test-time compute can match or exceed a 14× larger model, suggesting a practical path toward deploying smaller on-device models. Key limitations include reliance on a single model family (PaLM 2-S*), a single benchmark (MATH), and an expensive difficulty-estimation procedure (2048 samples per question) that is not accounted for in the compute budget. Open questions include whether findings generalize to non-verifiable domains, how combining both mechanisms (revision + search) further improves scaling, and whether test-time compute outputs can be distilled back into the base model for iterative self-improvement.
