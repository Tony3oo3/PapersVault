## Problem

While prior interpretability work has observed that attention heads handle in-context reasoning and feed-forward (FF) layers store distributional associations (e.g., bigrams like 'to the'), the mechanism by which this separation arises during training remains unexplained. This paper asks: why do FF layers preferentially capture simple distributional patterns while attention layers develop in-context reasoning capabilities, and can selectively removing FF components improve reasoning performance?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251204/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

In the synthetic setting, the model receives token sequences of length T over a vocabulary of size N+1, where a fixed trigger token q is followed by either a sampled target token or a generic noise token with probability α; the task is next-token prediction at the final position. For pre-trained model experiments, inputs are natural-language prompts (IOI sentences like 'When Mary and John went to the store, John gave the ice cream to', factual queries like 'Madrid is located in', and GSM8K math word problems), and outputs are next-token probability distributions tracked across hundreds of Pythia training checkpoints. A key analytical output is the attention pattern decomposition showing which positions (correct targets vs. noise tokens) receive attention weight.

## Method

The paper introduces a noisy in-context recall task on a two-layer transformer where a generic noise token appears with probability α after a trigger, creating a tension between learning a distributional trigger-noise association and performing in-context recall of the correct target. Theoretically, the authors show that at initialization, the gradient signal for the FF layer on the noise token is O(1) while the attention value matrix gradient is only O(1/N), explaining why FF captures distributional associations first; subsequently, once FF handles the noise, the attention QK matrix learns to attend to correct in-context targets while suppressing noise positions. For FF-free models, they prove that the value matrix stores noise in a rank-1 subspace corresponding to the smallest singular value, so low-rank truncation (LASER) can filter it out. Empirically, they track Pythia-1B training dynamics showing 'the' is predicted with high probability within ~10 steps while IOI and factual answers emerge later, and demonstrate that LASER on late MLP layers boosts the probability ratio of correct answers over 'the' from 2.3× to 12.3× (IOI) and from 0.16× to 11.3× (factual recall) at 14K steps.

## Results

On GSM8K few-shot Chain-of-Thought, LASER (low-rank truncation of MLPs) improves 1-shot accuracy for Phi-3 from 56.0% to 66.1% and for Llama-3.1-8B from 44.7% to 46.1%, but degrades 8-shot performance (e.g., Phi-3 drops from 82.7% to 82.3%). On Pythia-1B, LASER boosts the probability ratio of the correct IOI answer over the generic token 'the' from 2.3× to 12.3× at 14K training steps, and for factual recall ('Madrid is located in') boosts the ratio of 'Spain' over 'the' from 0.16× to 11.3×. On the synthetic noisy in-context recall task, dropping the second feed-forward layer (F2) raises the probability of predicting the correct in-context token from ~0.5 to ~0.98.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against competing methods; instead, it compares full (unmodified) models against LASER-truncated versions of the same models. On GSM8K, LASER consistently improves 1-shot and 2-shot CoT accuracy across all three model families (Phi-3, Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct), with the largest gain being +10.1 points for Phi-3 at 1-shot. However, LASER degrades performance in the standard 8-shot setting for all models (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B drops from 56.0% to 53.8%), suggesting that the distributional associations removed by truncation are still useful when many in-context examples are provided. On the Pythia IOI and factual recall tasks, LASER dramatically shifts probability mass away from the generic token 'the' toward the correct answer, with the ratio improving by roughly 5× (IOI) and 70× (factual recall) at 14K steps. The synthetic experiments further show that the full model predicts the noise token with probability ≈α (the training noise level), while the F2-dropped model predicts the correct in-context token with probability ≈0.98, confirming that feed-forward layers store the distributional association that competes with in-context reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the two-layer transformer in-context recall framework of Bietti et al. (2023) by introducing a fixed generic noise token after trigger tokens, creating a controlled setting where both distributional association (predicting the noise) and in-context reasoning (predicting the correct target) must coexist. It builds on the LASER low-rank truncation technique of Sharma et al. (2023), which empirically showed that reducing MLP rank improves reasoning, by providing a mechanistic and theoretical explanation for *why* this works: feed-forward layers preferentially absorb distributional associations due to lower gradient noise, leaving attention free to specialize in in-context computation. The paper also connects to the broader interpretability literature on attention circuits (Wang et al., 2022) and feed-forward key-value memories (Geva et al., 2021), but shifts focus from final-state circuit analysis to training dynamics, showing how the separation between distributional and in-context roles emerges during learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical account (gradient noise analysis) for why feed-forward and attention layers specialize differently, which could inform architecture design decisions about parameter allocation between MLPs and attention. The practical implication—that selectively truncating late feed-forward layers can improve reasoning by suppressing spurious distributional shortcuts—is validated on Pythia and partially on GSM8K, though the GSM8K results are mixed (helps in 1–2 shot CoT but degrades in the standard 8-shot setting). Key limitations include the reliance on highly simplified synthetic tasks for theory, the fact that the theoretical results only cover early training steps near initialization, and the open question of whether this clean FF/attention disentanglement persists in deeper, more complex models or on tasks where distributional and in-context signals are more entangled.
