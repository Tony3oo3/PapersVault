## Problem

Existing video-to-video translation methods process frames in fixed batches, requiring all frames in GPU memory and limiting video length to roughly 4 seconds while taking minutes per clip. StreamDiffusion enables real-time per-frame generation but treats each frame independently, producing noticeable pixel flickering. The paper addresses how to perform real-time, streaming V2V translation over unlimited video lengths while maintaining temporal consistency.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251661/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes video frames one at a time in a streaming fashion (O(1) memory regardless of video length) along with a text prompt, and produces translated frames at 20 FPS at 512×512 resolution on a single A100 GPU. Unlike batch methods that require a fixed number of frames upfront, StreamV2V handles arbitrary video lengths (demonstrated beyond 1000 frames) and generates all frames in a single pipeline stage without separate keyframe/interpolation steps. The underlying image editing uses SDEdit with an initial noise strength of 0.4 on top of a 4-step Latent Consistency Model.

## Method

StreamV2V builds on StreamDiffusion's stream-batch pipeline and introduces a backward-looking feature bank that archives intermediate transformer features (projected keys, values, and block outputs) from past frames. For each incoming frame, two training-free mechanisms exploit the bank: Extended Self-Attention concatenates stored keys and values into the self-attention computation, acting as a weighted sum of similar regions across frames, and Feature Fusion directly blends the current block output with the most cosine-similar stored feature (α=0.75), applied only to low-resolution decoder blocks to avoid blurring. The bank is kept compact and informative via Dynamic Merging (DyMe), which periodically concatenates new and stored features, randomly partitions them into source/destination sets, matches each source to its most similar destination via cosine similarity, and averages matched pairs—condensing the bank to the size of a single frame. The entire approach is training-free and plugs into any image diffusion model without fine-tuning.

## Results

On the DAVIS dataset (67 video-prompt pairs), StreamV2V achieves the best warp error (102.99) among all compared methods, outperforming Rerender (107.00), FlowVid (111.09), TokenFlow (114.25), CoDeF (116.17), and StreamDiffusion (117.01). Its CLIP score of 96.58 ranks third, slightly behind TokenFlow (97.04) and FlowVid (96.68). Critically, StreamV2V processes 512×512 video at 20 FPS on a single A100 GPU, making it 15×, 46×, 108×, and 158× faster than FlowVid, CoDeF, Rerender, and TokenFlow respectively, while using O(1) memory regardless of video length.

## Baselines & Comparisons

StreamV2V clearly outperforms its per-frame baseline StreamDiffusion on both metrics (warp error 102.99 vs. 117.01; CLIP 96.58 vs. 95.24) and wins the user study with a 71.1% preference rate. Against CoDeF, StreamV2V also wins decisively on warp error (102.99 vs. 116.17) and user preference (81.3% win rate), though CoDeF edges it slightly on CLIP score (96.33 vs. 96.58). Against the stronger batch-processing methods, StreamV2V achieves the best warp error but trails on CLIP score: TokenFlow leads CLIP (97.04 vs. 96.58) and FlowVid is close (96.68 vs. 96.58), while Rerender sits between them (96.20). In the user study, StreamV2V loses to Rerender (30.2% win), FlowVid (19.8% win), and TokenFlow (19.3% win), acknowledging a quality gap. The paper positions this trade-off explicitly: StreamV2V sacrifices some peak quality to deliver real-time (20 FPS) streaming V2V with unlimited video length, a capability none of the batch-based baselines support.

## Relation to Prior Work

StreamV2V builds directly on StreamDiffusion's real-time image generation pipeline and the Latent Consistency Model for few-step denoising, adding a backward-looking feature bank to bridge the temporal gap that per-frame processing creates. It departs from the dominant batch-based V2V paradigm (TokenFlow, FlowVid, CoDeF, Rerender) that requires loading all frames into GPU memory and relies on expensive DDIM inversion or optical flow, instead processing frames sequentially with O(1) memory. The dynamic merging strategy for the feature bank adapts the bipartite token-merging technique from single-image ViT acceleration (Bolya et al.) to the cross-frame setting, while the extended self-attention and feature fusion draw on recent findings that diffusion intermediate features encode rich inter-image correspondences.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

StreamV2V is the first method to enable real-time, unlimited-length video-to-video translation, opening practical applications like live webcam face-swap, AI-assisted drawing, and interactive video stylization that were previously infeasible. The training-free, plug-and-play design means it can be layered onto any image diffusion model without fine-tuning, lowering the barrier to adoption. Key limitations remain: quality still trails SOTA batch methods (TokenFlow, FlowVid) especially under rapid motion, large semantic edits (e.g., person-to-Pope) fail due to SDEdit's constraints, and the DyMe bank's relative memory overhead grows substantially at higher resolutions (136% at 1536×1536). Open questions include whether the quality gap can be closed without sacrificing the streaming paradigm, and whether the feature bank concept generalizes to other sequential generation tasks beyond video.
