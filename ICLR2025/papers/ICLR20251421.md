## Problem

While protein language models (pLMs) have demonstrated zero-shot fitness prediction capabilities, it remains unclear whether their success reflects true biological understanding or artifacts of training data composition. This paper asks: can we predict, before running a protein engineering campaign, whether a given pLM will succeed or fail at zero-shot fitness estimation for a particular protein, and what in the training data drives that success or failure?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251421/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes wild type protein sequences from 217 deep mutational scan (DMS) studies in ProteinGym and computes pseudo log likelihood (PLL) scores using masked (ESM-2) or autoregressive (ProGen-2) pLMs. For influence analysis, it conditions on 10,000 randomly sampled UniRef50 sequences or per-study DMS mutants, and for finetuning it retrieves the 1,000 most similar homologs via mmseqs2 search on UniRef100 (E-value ≤ 1). Outputs include per-sequence PLL values, per-datum influence scores, Spearman correlations against wet-lab fitness assays, and a selectively finetuned ESM-2 650M model.

## Method

The paper makes three linked contributions. First, it derives a closed-form approximation (Remark 4.1) showing that for mask-consistent masked language models, the per-token masked probability can be recovered from a single unmasked forward pass via a linear rescaling of the observed token probability, reducing PLL computation from O(L) to O(1) forward passes without any post-training. Second, it empirically demonstrates across 217 DMS studies that wild type PLL predicts zero-shot Spearman correlation in an inverted-U pattern: both under-preferred (low likelihood) and over-preferred (high likelihood) sequences degrade performance, with the high-likelihood penalty growing with model scale. Third, it uses influence functions (via Kronfluence/EK-FAC) to causally attribute likelihoods to specific training sequences, revealing a power-law tail where homologous sequences dominate influence, and leverages this to motivate selective evo-tuning: unsupervised finetuning on mmseqs2-retrieved homologs only for wild types below a likelihood threshold (ε = −1.4), which lifts ESM-2 650M above MSA-based baselines (EVE, MSA Transformer) and to competitive levels with TranceptionEVE.

## Results

On ProteinGym (217 DMS studies), ESM-2 650M with selective evo-tuning at likelihood threshold ε=−1.4 achieves a mean Spearman correlation of 0.462 (weighted mean 0.443), up from 0.439 (0.419) for the base model. Naive finetuning without the likelihood threshold (ε=0) degrades performance to 0.398 (0.377), confirming that over-preferred sequences are harmed by additional training. The thresholded finetuning approach outperforms EVE (0.454) and MSA Transformer (0.438) while remaining competitive with the hybrid MSA+pLM model TranceptionEVE L (0.474).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The thresholded evo-tuned ESM-2 650M (ε=−1.4, mean Spearman 0.462) outperforms ProGen2 XL (0.406) by 5.6 points, EVE (0.454) by 0.8 points, and MSA Transformer (0.438) by 2.4 points across the five ProteinGym fitness categories. It remains slightly below TranceptionEVE L (0.474) by 1.2 points, which leverages both MSA and pLM information. Notably, the base ESM-2 650M (0.439) already surpasses ProGen2 XL and MSA Transformer, but the selective finetuning is what closes the gap to EVE and TranceptionEVE L. The critical negative result is that unthresholded finetuning (ε=0) drops performance to 0.398, below even the base model, demonstrating that indiscriminate evo-tuning on high-likelihood sequences is actively harmful.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the zero-shot protein fitness prediction framework of Meier et al. (2021) by diagnosing *why* performance varies across proteins, framing sequence likelihood as an implicit preference in the Bradley-Terry sense. It generalizes the species-level bias analysis of Ding & Steinhardt (2024) down to individual sequence probabilities, and builds on the evo-tuning procedure of Alley et al. (2019) by adding a likelihood-threshold criterion for selective finetuning. The single-inference PLL derivation (Remark 4.1) provides a closed-form correction to the O(L) pseudo log likelihood of Wang & Cho (2019), while influence functions (Koh & Liang, 2017; Grosse et al., 2023) are repurposed to causally attribute likelihoods to specific training sequences.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The inverted-U finding—that both under-preferred and over-preferred wild types degrade fitness prediction—gives practitioners a concrete diagnostic: compute the PLL of your target protein before deploying a pLM, and selectively evo-tune only low-likelihood cases. The single-inference PLL removes a major computational barrier to using masked LMs for likelihood-based tasks, and the power-law influence tail suggests that a small set of homologous training sequences drives most of the model's preference, pointing toward targeted data curation. Open questions include whether these dynamics hold for architectures beyond ESM-2/ProGen-2, whether the threshold ε generalizes across protein families, and how dataset overlap (Hermann et al., 2024) interacts with the likelihood-based failure modes identified here.
