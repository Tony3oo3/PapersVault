## Problem

Existing MORL research optimizes performance within static environments, while RL generalization research assumes scalar rewards—leaving the intersection of multi-objectivity and cross-environment generalization unexplored. This gap is critical because real-world tasks (autonomous driving, healthcare) require agents to simultaneously balance multiple conflicting objectives and adapt to diverse environmental conditions, something neither line of work addresses in isolation.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on a Multi-Objective Contextual MDP (MOC-MDP) where each context c parameterizes a distinct MOMDP with its own transition dynamics, initial state distribution, and vector-valued reward function. Agents receive state observations (low-dimensional state vectors for MuJoCo/LunarLander/LavaGrid domains, or RGB pixel frames for SuperMarioBros) and, for utility-based approaches, a weight vector w from the unit simplex; for continuous-control domains, the state is augmented with a fixed-length history of past state-action pairs (length 2) to make hidden dynamics inferable. The output is a set of policies (a global Pareto set or generalized coverage set) whose quality is assessed via the NHGR and EUGR metrics, which normalize hypervolume or expected utility against the optimal front per context to ensure scale-invariant, context-balanced scoring.

## Method

The paper formalizes generalization in MORL through the MOC-MDP framework, defining two complementary objectives: an axiomatic generalization target (a global Pareto set of policies nondominated in at least one context) and a utility-based target (a generalized coverage set maximizing expected scalar utility over the context distribution for each weight vector). To evaluate progress, it introduces the Normalized Hypervolume Generalization Ratio (NHGR), which divides the generalist's normalized hypervolume by the optimal front's normalized hypervolume per context, eliminating scale bias and reference-point dependence while equalizing contexts with different achievable hypervolumes. The MORL-Generalization benchmark comprises six multi-objective domains (MO-LunarLander, MO-Hopper, MO-HalfCheetah, MO-Humanoid, MO-SuperMarioBros, MO-LavaGrid) with parameterized environmental variations spanning dynamics, state-space, and reward-function changes, all verified to uphold the Principle of Unchanged Pareto Optimality. Baseline evaluations of eight SOTA MORL algorithms (CAPQL, Envelope, GPI-LS, GPI-PD, PCN, PGMORL, MORL/D SB, MORL/D SB+PSA) trained via domain randomization reveal substantial optimality gaps (e.g., best NHGR of 0.66 on LunarLander dropping to 0.07 on SuperMarioBros), and a controlled comparison shows MORL agents can outperform a single-objective SAC agent on its own scalarized reward, demonstrating that scalar reward formulations are insufficient for effective generalization.

## Results

Across all six benchmark domains, state-of-the-art MORL algorithms achieve NHGR scores well below 1.0, indicating substantial generalization gaps. The best per-domain results are: GPI-LS on MO-LunarLander (IQM NHGR 0.66, 33.9% optimality gap), MORL/D SB+PSA on MO-Hopper (0.56, 46.4% gap) and MO-HalfCheetah (0.28), MORL/D SB on MO-Humanoid (0.41) and MO-LavaGrid (0.37), and GPI-LS on MO-SuperMarioBros (0.07, 87.7% gap). Performance degrades sharply with domain complexity, with MuJoCo-based domains showing the widest optimality gaps. Additionally, MORL algorithms outperform the single-objective SAC agent on its own scalarized reward function across multiple test environments, demonstrating that multi-objective formulations are necessary for effective generalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

No single MORL algorithm dominates all domains. GPI-LS (Alegre et al., 2023) leads on MO-LunarLander (NHGR 0.66 vs. 0.57 for MORL/D SB) and MO-SuperMarioBros (0.12 vs. 0.08 for Envelope), but underperforms on MuJoCo domains. MORL/D SB+PSA (Felten et al., 2024) is the strongest on MO-Hopper (0.54 vs. 0.50 for CAPQL) and MO-HalfCheetah (0.29 vs. 0.16 for GPI-LS), while its model-free variant MORL/D SB wins on MO-Humanoid (0.44 vs. 0.31 for CAPQL) and MO-LavaGrid (0.37 vs. 0.02 for GPI-LS). CAPQL (Lu et al., 2023) is competitive on MO-Hopper (0.50) and MO-Humanoid (0.31) but collapses on MO-HalfCheetah (0.03). PGMORL (Xu et al., 2020) and PCN (Reymond et al., 2022) are consistently weak, with NHGR below 0.25 in most domains. Envelope (Yang et al., 2019), restricted to discrete actions, achieves at most 0.18 NHGR. The single-objective SAC baseline (Haarnoja et al., 2018) is outperformed by multiple MORL algorithms on its own scalarized reward across MO-Hopper, MO-HalfCheetah, and MO-Humanoid test environments, confirming that multi-objective formulations are essential for generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously disconnected threads in RL: multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL), which has focused exclusively on static environments, and RL generalization, which has assumed scalar rewards. It adapts the Contextual MDP framework (Hallak et al., 2015) to the multi-objective setting via the MOC-MDP formulation, extends the Hypervolume indicator (Zitzler & Thiele, 1998) into the scale-invariant NHGR metric, and builds its benchmark on MO-Gymnasium (Felten et al., 2023) domains with parameterized environment configurations. The paper also draws on the Principle of Unchanged Optimality (Irpan & Song, 2019) and Kirk et al.'s (2023) taxonomy of generalization variation types to ensure its benchmark is well-posed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work fills a critical gap by formally defining and benchmarking a problem that both the MORL and RL-generalization communities have overlooked, providing the first systematic evaluation of SOTA MORL algorithms under generalization pressure. The finding that MORL agents can outperform a single-objective SAC agent on SAC's own scalarized reward across diverse environments challenges the prevailing assumption that scalar rewards suffice for generalization, with implications for reward design in robotics and other real-world applications. Key open questions include whether linear scalarization is adequate for recovering Pareto-optimal policies across contexts, how to handle hidden or partially observable contexts, and how to adapt single-objective generalization techniques (regularization, inductive biases, curriculum learning) to the multi-objective setting.
