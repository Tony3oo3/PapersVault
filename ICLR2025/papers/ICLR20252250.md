## Problem

The standard practice of setting attention rank r ≪ d and number of heads H = d/r in transformers lacks theoretical justification, and it is unclear whether this scaling weakens representational capacity. This paper asks whether increasing the number of low-rank heads can compensate for a small rank, or whether rank itself is the decisive factor in an attention layer's expressive power.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252250/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The target function takes N target points drawn from the unit sphere S^{d-1} (constrained to be mutually orthogonal for N ≤ d in the theoretical analysis) and a single query point y ∈ S^{d-1}, and outputs the target point closest (or farthest, in experiments) to y. The function is permutation-invariant over target points and invariant to simultaneous orthogonal transformations of all inputs, meaning it has no preferred directions or scales. In the empirical setting, points are drawn i.i.d. from the sphere without the orthogonality constraint, and the model uses self-attention so source and target points coincide.

## Method

The paper proves a rank separation for the nearest neighbor function using harmonic analysis on the sphere. A single full-rank head (r = d) represents the target exactly by setting KQ^T = I (hardmax) or KQ^T = αI with large α (softmax), requiring only O(d²) parameters. For low-rank heads, the proof decomposes the target into tensorized spherical harmonics and shows that each rank-r head is confined to a fraction of the basis functions in each degree-ℓ subspace (bounded by the dimension of the span of K_h), so capturing the target's slowly decaying spectral energy requires Ω((d/r)^{1/ε}) heads for error ε, or exponentially many heads in the high-accuracy regime. A stronger exponential separation (exp(Ω(d−r)) heads) is obtained by summing polynomially many biased nearest neighbor functions. For depth, a two-layer 'majority voting' construction with concatenated positional encodings and rank-1 heads approximates the target for N=2, but the authors conjecture no fixed-size low-rank transformer works for all N.

## Results

The paper proves that approximating the nearest neighbor function with rank-r attention heads requires H = Ω((d/r)^(1/ε)) heads in the high-dimensional regime (Theorem 2), and H = Ω(exp(d − r·log(d/r))) heads in the high-accuracy regime. A stronger exponential separation (Theorem 3) shows that a combined target of polynomially many biased nearest-neighbor functions requires Ω(exp(d−r)) rank-r heads to achieve constant error, while only polynomially many full-rank heads suffice. Empirically, on the farthest-neighbor task (d=64, N=16), a single full-rank head (H=1, r=64) with one layer outperforms the best low-rank model (L=5, c=2, r=32) despite the latter having 80× more attention parameters. On the Garg et al. (2022) in-context linear regression task, reducing H from 8 to 1 (increasing rank from 32 to 48 at d=48) yields significantly lower error with the same total parameter count.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but instead contrasts different architectural configurations of the same transformer. Full-rank attention (H=1, r=d) consistently and substantially outperforms low-rank attention (H>1, r<d) at matched parameter counts across all experiments. On the farthest-neighbor task, even d²/2 heads of rank d/2 with a single layer fails to match a single full-rank head; adding depth (L=5) helps low-rank models but does not close the gap. On the in-context learning benchmark, H=1 or H=2 significantly outperforms H=8 or H=16 across all embedding dimensions and context lengths tested. The paper also shows that concatenated positional encodings (Theorem 5's construction) provide only marginal empirical benefit to low-rank models, and that low-rank performance degrades as the number of input points N grows, while full-rank attention remains stable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws a direct analogy to the depth-width tradeoff literature for feedforward networks (Eldan & Shamir 2016; Daniely 2017), transplanting the spherical-harmonics proof technique to show that rank plays for attention the role that depth plays for FNNs. It extends the closest prior work, Sanford et al. (2024b), which established a single-head rank separation, by proving that adding more heads cannot compensate for low rank — a question Sanford et al. explicitly left open. The exponential-separation proof (Theorem 3) is inspired by Yehudai & Shamir (2019) on random features versus two-layer networks, adapted to the attention setting with a rank-dependent analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the near-universal H=d/r scaling hard-coded into PyTorch and xFormers, showing with both theory and experiments that a single full-rank head can outperform many low-rank heads on natural tasks like nearest-neighbor search and in-context linear regression. Key open questions include whether depth can overcome the low-rank bottleneck for long sequences (Conjecture 6), whether the results extend beyond rotationally invariant distributions to realistic text data, and how rank interacts with context length. The practical implication is that practitioners may be leaving accuracy on the table by defaulting to small-rank, many-head configurations, and that future scaling studies should treat rank as a first-class hyperparameter rather than a fixed byproduct of d and H.
