## Problem

AI-based phishing detection methods can classify emails as phishing or benign but lack intrinsic explainability, failing to identify which specific sentences trigger the classification. This paper tackles the under-studied problem of phishing attack localization: automatically learning and identifying the most important phishing-relevant sentences in an email to provide concise, actionable explanations for the predicted vulnerability label.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250838/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each email is treated as a sequence of sentences (padded/truncated to L=100), with each sentence encoded via a 150-dim token embedding followed by a 1D convolutional layer and max pooling. The method outputs both a binary vulnerability label (phishing/benign) and a ranked set of phishing-relevant sentences (top-1, top-2, top-3) whose importance scores are the learned Bernoulli probabilities ω_i(X;α). Crucially, training requires only the vulnerability label—no ground-truth annotation of which sentences are phishing-relevant (weakly supervised setting).

## Method

AI2TALE combines a selection network (3-layer feed-forward, sigmoid output) that learns a Multi-Bernoulli distribution over sentences with a classifier (2-layer feed-forward, softmax output) that predicts the label from the selected subset X̃ = X ⊙ z. The training objective maximizes mutual information I(X̃, Y) between selected sentences and the label while minimizing an information-bottleneck term λ·I(X, X̃) (approximated via KL divergence to a zero-centered Gaussian prior) to prevent degenerate selection of all sentences. A second innovation—a data-distribution mechanism inspired by Jethani et al.—trains the classifier separately with random Bernoulli(0.5) sentence masking to prevent the classifier from encoding the label through selection patterns rather than actual sentence content. Differentiable sampling from the Multi-Bernoulli is achieved via the Gumbel-Softmax (Concrete) relaxation, and the two objectives are optimized in alternating steps within each training iteration using Adam.

## Results

On seven diverse real-world email datasets (~40,000 samples), AI2TALE achieves 99.33% Label-Accuracy and 98.95% Cognitive-True-Positive, yielding a combined average of 99.14%. This represents a 1.5%–3.5% improvement over the strongest baselines under a weakly supervised setting where only vulnerability labels (phishing/benign) are available during training, without ground-truth phishing-relevant sentences. The method also attains the lowest false positive rate (0.451%) and false negative rate (0.899%) among all compared approaches.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AI2TALE outperforms all five intrinsic interpretable baselines on both Label-Accuracy and Cognitive-True-Positive. The closest competitors are AIM (98.40% / 97.10%) and INVASE (98.30% / 97.20%), which AI2TALE surpasses by roughly 0.9–1.0% on Label-Accuracy and 1.8–1.9% on Cognitive-True-Positive. L2X (98.25% / 97.20%) and ICVH (96.72% / 98.10%) trail by 1.1–2.6% on the two metrics respectively. The largest margin is against VIBI (96.65% / 94.99%), where AI2TALE leads by 2.68% on Label-Accuracy and 3.96% on Cognitive-True-Positive. Notably, ICVH achieves the second-highest Cognitive-TP (98.10%) but the second-lowest Label-Accuracy (96.72%), indicating a trade-off that AI2TALE resolves by excelling on both metrics simultaneously.

## Relation to Prior Work

AI2TALE synthesizes three established interpretability principles—L2X's mutual information maximization for feature selection, the information bottleneck term to prevent superset selection, and Jethani et al.'s data-distribution mechanism to prevent the classifier from encoding labels via its own selections—into a unified training objective for phishing email localization. Unlike prior intrinsic interpretability methods (L2X, INVASE, ICVH, VIBI, AIM) that were designed for general feature attribution or applied to other domains (source code vulnerability, sentiment analysis), this paper is the first to adapt them specifically to the phishing localization problem under a weakly supervised setting where no ground-truth phishing-relevant sentences exist. The method departs from prior work by adding both the bottleneck regularization and the disjoint data-distribution training step simultaneously, addressing two failure modes (superset selection and label-encoding) that individually degrade the quality of selected explanations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap: AI-based phishing detectors predict labels but cannot tell users *why* an email is flagged, reducing trust and actionable response. The introduction of the Cognitive-True-Positive metric—evaluating whether selected sentences reflect known psychological manipulation principles (scarcity, authority, consistency, etc.)—provides a domain-grounded evaluation that goes beyond pure classification accuracy. Limitations include the relatively modest 1.5–3.5% improvement over strong baselines, the use of only 10 training epochs with small feed-forward networks, and the finding that a lightweight 1D-convolutional embedding outperforms BERT (suggesting domain-specific patterns may not benefit from large pre-trained representations). Open questions include extending the approach to webpage phishing, integrating with modern LLMs, and whether the cognitive-trigger evaluation generalizes beyond English-language emails.
