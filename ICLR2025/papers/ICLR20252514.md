## Problem

While prior work empirically identified that in-context learning (ICL) emerges in transformers when training data contains many rare classes, and that ICL can be transient (disappearing with more training), no theoretical framework explained the mechanism behind this emergence and disappearance. The paper asks: what simplified distributional properties drive both the appearance and eventual loss of ICL, and can we formalize the competition between in-weight and in-context predictors to predict when each dominates?

## Input / Output

For the synthetic and Omniglot experiments, the model receives a d-dimensional query vector plus L context (input, label) pairs, and outputs a probability distribution over C classes. The context is constructed to be relevant (containing same-class examples) with probability p_relevant, and evaluation is split into in-base distribution (IBD) and out-of-base distribution (OOBD) to disentangle ICL from IWL: a purely in-weight model fails on OOBD, while a purely in-context model fails on irrelevant contexts. For the LLM experiment, the input is a natural-language prompt embedding an in-context example (e.g., 'Kaitlyn lives in Kjheergg. Where does Kaitlyn live?') and the output is a free-text city name, with the key signal being whether the model follows the context or its memorized weights.

## Method

The paper introduces a bi-level theoretical model where a gating function α(x̃) ∈ [0,1] selects between an in-weight predictor g (a tabular function using only the query, analyzed via Krichevsky-Trofimov estimation showing O(log(N_x)/N_x) convergence for cross-entropy) and an in-context predictor h (a simplified induction head that softmax-weights context labels by query similarity, with a minimum error floor determined by the number of irrelevant context labels). A regret analysis (Algorithm 1) shows that a no-regret learner for α can learn to select the better predictor from observed losses, bridging the oracle test-error selection to practical gradient-based training. The key theoretical result is that ICL is transient whenever the in-weight predictor's asymptotic error is lower than the in-context predictor's minimum error: as N_x grows, the IW predictor converges and overtakes the IC predictor, causing the gate to shift from ICL to IWL. This is validated empirically on synthetic data, Omniglot, and by fine-tuning Gemini Nano 1 on memorization prompts, where in-weight memorization demonstrably overrides in-context predictions.

## Results

The paper's main quantitative finding is theoretical: the IW predictor's excess cross-entropy loss converges at rate O(log(N_x)/N_x) (achieved by the Krichevsky-Trofimov estimator), while the IC predictor has a irreducible minimum error of at least (1−2ε)/(1+(L−1)e²)+ε for C=2, B=1, k≥1 irrelevant contexts. On synthetic data (L=1, p_high=0.9, σ=0.2), the transformer exhibits ICL on low-frequency classes C_L for small N but loses this capability as N grows, with ICL diminishing faster for high-frequency classes C_H. On Gemini Nano 1 (1.8B params), fine-tuning on 8 name-city pairs causes the model to revert from ICL to IWL: the base model always answers the in-context city (e.g., 'Kjheergg'), while the finetuned model answers the memorized city in a significant fraction of cases, with the log-probability of the memorized answer increasing substantially (e.g., from −40.5 to −0.27 for 'Lkkl').

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external methods; instead it compares three internal predictors (IC predictor, IW predictor, and the full transformer) on the same data to validate the theoretical gating model. On synthetic data, the IC predictor achieves low error on relevant contexts quickly (few samples), while the IW predictor starts with high error but converges to near-zero IBD error as N grows; the full transformer tracks the better of the two, exhibiting ICL when IC < IW and switching to IWL when IW < IC. On Omniglot (1623 classes, L=2), the transformer exhibits ICL on C_L initially but ICL becomes transient even with σ=0, consistent with the large number of low-frequency classes making IW learning slow. For the Gemini Nano experiment, the base model relies entirely on ICL (always answers the in-context city), while the finetuned model partially reverts to IWL (memorized city), demonstrating that in-weight memorization can override in-context prediction — the inverse of the typical ICL-overrides-IWL narrative in larger models studied by Wei et al. (2023).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the distributional-properties line of work initiated by Chan et al. (2022a) and the transient-ICL observations of Singh et al. (2023) and Reddy (2024) by providing a formal theoretical framework grounded in generalization error and regret analysis. Rather than studying how transformers learn ICL circuits (as in Garg et al. 2022, Akyürek et al. 2023) or ICL in multi-task settings, the authors introduce a simplified bi-level gating model that explicitly models the competition between an in-weight predictor and an in-context predictor on a per-input basis. The in-context predictor is inspired by the induction head mechanism (Olsson et al. 2022), and the analysis draws on the Krichevsky-Trofimov estimator for characterizing in-weight convergence rates, positioning the work at the intersection of online learning theory and transformer interpretability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical explanation for why ICL is transient: in-context data (rare classes) accumulates during training and eventually becomes in-weight data, causing the model to prefer IWL. This reframes the key question from 'what distributional properties drive ICL?' to 'what is the learnability of the data?', which is a more actionable lens for designing training schedules. The Gemini Nano fine-tuning experiment demonstrates that even memorizing 8 name-city pairs can override ICL, suggesting practical implications for LLM fine-tuning. Open questions include the case where ICL and IWL achieve equal asymptotic performance (where transience may depend on finite-time dynamics), and whether the gating model's predictions hold for deeper, more complex architectures.
