## Problem

Grokking — the phenomenon where neural networks memorize training data with poor test performance before suddenly generalizing after prolonged training — introduces unpredictability and inefficiency into the training process. The paper asks how to modify training dynamics so that models generalize continuously without this delayed phase transition, and identifies data embedding as the key factor controlling whether generalization is delayed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253628/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on algorithmic tasks with structured numerical inputs: integer pairs (a, b) for modular addition/multiplication (p=113), binary vectors x ∈ {±1}^q for (q,k)-parity, and high-dimensional vectors x ∈ R^p for XOR classification. The output is the corresponding label (modular result, parity product, or XOR sign). Unusually, the target model's embedding layer is not randomly initialized but set to a low-rank product A·B, where A is the embedding matrix learned by a separately trained weaker model and B is a random matrix, imposing a structured initialization that reshapes the optimization landscape.

## Method

GrokTransfer is a two-stage procedure: (1) train a smaller, weaker model (which can even have a different architecture, e.g., an FNN when the target is a Transformer) until it groks to non-trivial but suboptimal test performance; (2) extract the weak model's learned embedding matrix and use it as factor A in a low-rank initialization E_T = A·B for the target model's embedding layer, where B is randomly initialized and both factors remain trainable. The key insight is that even a capacity-limited weak model learns an informative embedding that captures task-relevant structure (e.g., three of four XOR feature directions), and transferring this embedding allows the target model to bypass the pure-memorization phase and generalize almost immediately. Theoretically, on a high-dimensional XOR cluster task, the paper proves that after just one step of gradient descent with the transferred embedding, the target model overfits all training data and achieves test error exp(-Ω(log² n)), eliminating the grokking delay entirely. Empirically, GrokTransfer reduces the time gap between memorization and generalization by roughly 20× compared to GrokFast on modular addition, and is ~5× faster in wall-clock time than training from scratch.

## Results

Theoretically, on the XOR cluster task, GrokTransfer guarantees that after a single step of gradient descent the target model overfits all training data and achieves test error ≤ exp(−Ω(log² n)), eliminating the ~75-epoch generalization delay observed in standard training. Empirically, on the modular addition task with an 8-layer Transformer target, GrokTransfer reduces total wall-clock training time from 392,667 ms (scratch) to ~73,907 ms (weak model + target), a ~5× speedup. In a direct comparison with GrokFast on a 2-layer Transformer, GrokTransfer achieves a Time Gap of 46 epochs versus GrokFast's 1119 epochs (~24× smaller delay). Across FNN→FNN experiments on modular addition, modular multiplication, and (40,3)-parity, GrokTransfer consistently eliminates the sharp memorization-to-generalization phase transition.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline comparison is against GrokFast (Lee et al., 2024), a gradient-amplification method for accelerating grokking. On a 2-layer decoder-only Transformer for modular addition, GrokTransfer achieves a Time Gap of 46 epochs (difference between 95% training and 95% test accuracy) while GrokFast requires 1119 epochs — a roughly 24× reduction in generalization delay. Against standard training from scratch, GrokTransfer eliminates the sharp phase transition entirely: the target model makes continuous progress from the first epoch rather than spending hundreds of epochs in a memorization plateau before suddenly generalizing. The wall-clock comparison (Table 1) shows GrokTransfer's total cost (weak model + target) is approximately 5× faster than training the target from scratch, with the weak FNN contributing only ~4% of the total time. The paper also contrasts its approach with Minegishi et al. (2024), which requires pretraining the same target model to full generalization to extract a lottery ticket, whereas GrokTransfer only needs a much smaller, weaker model trained to non-trivial (not optimal) performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of grokking research and weak-to-strong knowledge transfer. It builds directly on the XOR cluster data framework from Xu et al. (2024) for its theoretical analysis, and draws conceptual inspiration from weak-to-strong generalization (Burns et al., 2023) and learned weight growth (Wang et al., 2023), but departs from both by transferring only the embedding layer rather than pseudo-labels or full weight mappings. It contrasts with prior grokking-acceleration methods—Goldilocks zone weight norm constraints (Liu et al., 2023), gradient amplification (Lee et al., 2024), and lottery-ticket sparsification (Minegishi et al., 2024)—by requiring neither additional data nor pretraining the target model to convergence, and by operating across architectures (FNN→Transformer).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key conceptual contribution is identifying data embedding (rather than initialization scale or NTK regime) as the primary determinant of whether grokking occurs, which challenges the prevailing kernel-to-rich-regime transition explanation. The method is computationally cheap (a 3-neuron weak model suffices) and the 5× wall-clock speedup over training from scratch is practically meaningful. However, the theoretical guarantee is restricted to a simple XOR task where one gradient step suffices, and all empirical validation is on small algorithmic tasks (p=113 modular arithmetic, 40-bit parity), leaving open whether the approach transfers to larger-scale or real-world settings. A natural open question is whether the embedding-transfer principle can be extended to non-grokking tasks or to improve general training dynamics more broadly.
