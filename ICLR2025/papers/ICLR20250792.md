## Problem

Whether task-relevant representation learning in sensory cortex continues after behavioral performance saturates (overtraining) has been untested at the experimental neuroscience level, sitting in a gap between fine-grained deep learning theory of grokking and coarse-grained psychology of expert learning. Practical barriers—destructive chronic recordings, absence of explicit train/test splits in neuroscience paradigms, and the difficulty of designing tasks that are masterable yet hard enough to leave room for hidden learning—have prevented direct investigation of this question.

## Input / Output

The neural reanalysis consumes tetrode spike-count matrices (~15 neurons × ~200 trials per session) from posterior piriform cortex recorded across 15–20 overtraining days in 4 mice, where odors are 3-hot vectors over 13 chemicals; the synthetic model uses 10-hot vectors over 100 chemicals passed through a random projection. Outputs include representational similarity matrices (pairwise z-scored firing-rate correlations), 10-fold LDA decoding accuracy, SVM margins (distance to closest 1% and 5% of points), PCA projections of class representations, Fisher Discriminant values tracking signal-to-noise, and reversal-learning speed measured as the rate at which a new readout converges on the negated label.

## Method

The paper combines a reanalysis of published piriform cortex data with two synthetic models. In the reanalysis, it tracks class-separation (anticorrelation between target and nontarget population responses) and maximum-margin SVM classifier margins across overtraining days, showing both increase despite flat behavior. The first synthetic model is a one-hidden-layer ReLU MLP trained with cross-entropy on the odor discrimination task; it exhibits grokking-like dynamics where probe-odor test loss (probes share one odorant with the target) keeps decreasing after training loss plateaus, driven by late-time margin maximization—confirmed by ablating cross-entropy with hinge loss, which removes the effect. The second model is a two-layer linear network analyzed via mean-field theory (Bordelon & Pehlevan, 2022), proving that kernel-target alignment K_y(T) grows monotonically with pretraining duration T and directly sets the learning rate on the reversed task, providing a mechanistic explanation for the overtraining reversal effect.

## Results

In the neural reanalysis of 4 mice (N≈200 trials/day, D≈15 neurons, 18 days of overtraining), 10-fold LDA decoding accuracy of target vs. nontarget labels increases monotonically throughout overtraining, and the SVM margin (average distance to the closest 1% and 5% of points) increases substantially over the same period, despite behavioral performance remaining at near-ceiling (~90% correct licks). In the synthetic MLP model (n=10, k=100 odor vectors, 200 training + 20 probe examples), training loss plateaus by epoch ~1000 while test (probe) loss continues decreasing through epoch ~9000, with a grokking-like abrupt transition in probe accuracy during that window. The Fisher Discriminant between target and probe classes increases in lockstep with the SVM margin, and probe trials are learned in order of increasing Hamming overlap with the target. In the two-layer linear reversal model (N=250 hidden units), additional pretraining steps T monotonically accelerate learning of the reversed task, consistent with the kernel alignment K_y(T) growing with T.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing methods in the traditional sense; its primary 'comparisons' are ablations and qualitative cross-validation between neural data and a synthetic model. The key ablation replaces cross-entropy loss with a hard-margin hinge loss (C=1) in the synthetic MLP: under hinge loss, margin saturates at the same epoch (~1000) where training loss plateaus, and test loss stops improving simultaneously, eliminating the grokking-like late-time probe accuracy gains entirely (Figure 9). This supports a causal link between non-vanishing cross-entropy loss and late-time margin maximization. For the neural data, representational anticorrelation between target and nontarget classes is compared against 500 mean-matched random firing-rate matrices (Appendix A.5, Figure 8), confirming that the observed separation is significantly above chance at both the start and end of overtraining. The synthetic model's phenomenology (continued test-loss decrease, margin growth, PCA separation, grokking transition) is presented as a qualitative match to the piriform cortex data rather than a quantitative benchmark, and the authors explicitly note the small neuron count (~15–20) makes trends noisy and findings suggestive rather than conclusive.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges the grokking/margin-maximization literature in deep learning (Power et al. 2022; Kumar et al. 2023; Lyu & Li 2019) with experimental neuroscience by reanalyzing piriform cortex recordings from Berners-Lee et al. (2023) through the lens of lazy-to-rich representation learning. It draws on the mean-field kernel dynamics framework of Bordelon & Pehlevan (2022) and the exact solutions of Saxe et al. (2013) to build a minimal two-layer model explaining the overtraining reversal effect from experimental psychology (Mackintosh 1969; Richman et al. 1972). The paper positions itself as filling an intermediate gap: grokking theory operates at the level of theorems about specific network classes, while psychology offers qualitative cognitive accounts, but neither had been connected to population-level neural recordings in sensory cortex.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first concrete, quantitative evidence that biological sensory cortex exhibits grokking-like margin maximization during overtraining, offering a mechanistic (rather than purely cognitive) explanation for the decades-old overtraining reversal effect. Key limitations temper the impact: the evidence is observational from a reanalysis of ~15 neurons across 4 mice on a single olfactory task, the authors explicitly frame findings as suggestive rather than conclusive, and the initial learning phase (whether representations start lazy or rich) cannot be examined. Open questions include whether late-time feature learning is universal across sensory modalities and task types, what biological objective (metabolic constraints, uncertainty penalties) drives it, and whether targeted experiments with multiple target odors and graded probe difficulty can falsify the hypothesis.
