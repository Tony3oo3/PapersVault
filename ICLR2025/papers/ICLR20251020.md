## Problem

Real-world time series contain multiple intertwined frequency components whose information density varies across bands, yet existing decomposition methods apply a uniform modeling approach to all frequency components, leading to inaccurate characterization. The paper addresses this by asking whether the adjustable fitting capacity of Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KAN) can be exploited to match representational complexity to each frequency band's inherent complexity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251020/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a historical multivariate time series X ∈ R^{N×T} (look-back T=96 in main experiments) and produces a future multivariate series X_O ∈ R^{N×F} for F ∈ {96, 192, 336, 720}. It operates in a variate-independent manner, processing each univariate series separately and stacking predictions at the end. The architecture is extremely lightweight, requiring only 12–38K parameters and 7–457M MACs depending on the dataset, compared to millions of parameters in Transformer-based baselines.

## Method

TimeKAN follows a repeatable Decomposition-Learning-Mixing pipeline. Cascaded Frequency Decomposition (CFD) blocks first create multi-level sequences via progressive moving-average downsampling, then extract each frequency band's representation by subtracting a Frequency-Upsampled (FFT→zero-pad→IFFT) version of the next-level sequence from the current level. Multi-order KAN (M-KAN) blocks then model each band with a dual-branch design: Depthwise Convolution captures temporal dependencies while ChebyshevKAN learns channel representations, with the Chebyshev polynomial order increasing from low to high frequency (order = b + k − i) to match the growing complexity of higher-frequency patterns. Frequency Mixing blocks reconstruct multi-level sequences by adding the learned band back via IFFT(Padding(FFT(x_{i+1}))) + f_i, enabling the cycle to repeat across bands. The final highest-level sequence is mapped to the forecast via a single linear layer, and the entire model is trained end-to-end with L2 loss.

## Results

TimeKAN achieves the best average MSE/MAE on 5 out of 6 datasets: ETTh1 (0.417/0.427), ETTh2 (0.383/0.404), ETTm1 (0.376/0.395), ETTm2 (0.277/0.322), and Weather (0.242/0.272), with input length T=96 and prediction lengths F∈{96,192,336,720}. On Electricity, iTransformer (0.178/0.270) and TimeMixer (0.182/0.272) outperform TimeKAN (0.197/0.286), attributed to iTransformer's channel-wise self-attention for high-dimensional inter-variable dependencies. TimeKAN achieves these results with only 12.84K–38.12K parameters, compared to 75.50K for TimeMixer, 224K–841K for iTransformer, and 3.75M–10.06M for PatchTST.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TimeKAN outperforms the strongest MLP-based baseline TimeMixer on 5 of 6 datasets (e.g., ETTh1 MSE 0.417 vs. 0.459; Weather MSE 0.242 vs. 0.272), with the sole exception being Electricity (0.197 vs. 0.182). Against Transformer-based methods, TimeKAN achieves substantially lower MSE on ETTh1 (0.417 vs. iTransformer 0.454, PatchTST 0.521) and Weather (0.242 vs. iTransformer 0.258, PatchTST 0.294), while on Electricity iTransformer (0.178) and TimeMixer (0.182) both beat TimeKAN (0.197), which the authors attribute to iTransformer's channel-wise attention capturing inter-variable dependencies in the 321-dimensional Electricity dataset. Compared to CNN-based methods (TimesNet, MICN) and frequency-domain methods (FreTS, FiLM), TimeKAN consistently achieves the best or near-best MSE across all datasets. The time-series foundation model Time-FFM is outperformed by TimeKAN on all six datasets, with the largest gap on ETTh1 (0.417 vs. 0.442 MSE). Notably, TimeKAN achieves these results with 12.84K–38.12K parameters, roughly 2–300× fewer than the compared baselines, making it the most efficient model that still achieves state-of-the-art accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

TimeKAN sits at the intersection of two recent research threads: (1) frequency/multi-scale decomposition in time series forecasting (Autoformer, DLinear, TimeMixer, TimesNet, PDF), which progressively isolates different temporal patterns, and (2) Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KAN), which replace fixed activation functions with learnable univariate functions for more flexible, parameter-efficient representation. The key departure from prior decomposition work is the use of Multi-order ChebyshevKANs whose polynomial order increases with frequency, explicitly matching model capacity to the information density of each band, and the use of non-parametric FFT-based frequency upsampling to preserve spectral integrity during the cascaded decomposition-mixing cycle. Unlike TimeMixer's MLP-based multi-scale mixing or TimesNet's 2D-variation modeling, TimeKAN's learning component is entirely KAN-based, making it a first application of KAN as the core representational unit in a long-term forecasting architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The most practically significant finding is the extreme parameter efficiency: TimeKAN achieves competitive or SOTA accuracy with 12–38K parameters versus 3.75M–25M for Transformer/CNN baselines, suggesting that frequency-aware decomposition plus lightweight KANs can replace heavy attention or convolution stacks for univariate forecasting. However, the variate-independent design limits cross-variable modeling (evidenced by the Electricity underperformance at T=96), and the modest accuracy margins over TimeMixer on several datasets raise the question of whether the KAN component's advantage over a well-tuned MLP is robust at scale. Open questions include whether the multi-order KAN principle transfers to other sequence tasks (NLP, audio), whether the frequency-band count and Chebyshev order schedule generalize beyond the six benchmark datasets, and whether the FFT-based upsampling remains optimal when frequency components are non-stationary.
