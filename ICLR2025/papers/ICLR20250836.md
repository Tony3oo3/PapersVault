## Problem

Despite over a decade of modern slavery legislation requiring thousands of corporations to publish annual compliance statements, no large-scale, sentence-level annotated dataset exists to help machine learning models distinguish concrete countermeasures from vague or distracting corporate language. The volume of freeform statements exceeds the capacity of government oversight bodies, and prior efforts (e.g., LegalBench, UK statement analyses) were either too small, too inconsistent, or relied on statement-level labels that limit downstream utility.

## Input / Output

Each input is a single sentence extracted from a freeform PDF modern slavery statement, optionally augmented with up to 100 words of balanced surrounding context. The output is a binary relevant/irrelevant label for each of eleven questions derived from the seven Mandatory Criteria of the Australian Modern Slavery Act (e.g., C2 operations, C4 risk mitigation, C5 effectiveness). The full dataset spans 5,731 statements covering 7,270 entities (2019–2023) with over 800,000 labeled sentences, where relevant sentences constitute a small minority (at most ~20% for the most positive criterion).

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a multi-phase annotation pipeline that translates the Act's seven high-level Mandatory Criteria into eleven granular sentence-extraction questions, annotated by trained workers under expert supervision with IAA-based quality control (overall IAA 0.73, Cohen's Kappa 0.74). Two 'gold' subsets of 50 statements each (annotated by one and three domain experts respectively) serve as validation and test sets to minimize label noise. For benchmarking, the authors evaluate both zero-shot prompting (GPT-3.5 Turbo, GPT-4o, Llama3.2 3B) and fine-tuning (DistilBERT, BERT, Llama2 7B, Llama3.2 3B via LoRA) under two input conditions (sentence-only vs. ±100-word context), using Macro-F1 across all eleven questions. Fine-tuned Llama3.2 3B achieves 0.721 Macro-F1 with context, substantially outperforming the best zero-shot result (GPT-4o at 0.600), demonstrating that task-specific training on curated annotations is necessary to handle the ambiguity and distraction inherent in corporate compliance language.

## Results

On the AIMS.au dataset, fine-tuned Llama3.2 (3B) achieves the best overall Macro F1 of 0.721 with context and 0.694 without context, substantially outperforming zero-shot GPT4o (0.600 with context) and GPT3.5 Turbo (0.386 without context). BERT (0.718) and DistilBERT (0.702) with context perform comparably to Llama3.2, while Llama2 (7B) lags at 0.598 without context. Adding ±100 words of surrounding context consistently improves performance across all fine-tuned models, with the largest gains on C6 (consultation: 0.414→0.641 for DistilBERT) and C4 (remediation: 0.574→0.500 for DistilBERT, 0.564→0.615 for Llama3.2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among fine-tuned models, Llama3.2 (3B) achieves the highest Macro F1 (0.721 with context, 0.694 without), narrowly edging out BERT (0.718/0.665) and DistilBERT (0.702/0.670). Llama2 (7B) underperforms all other fine-tuned models (0.598 without context), which the authors attribute to its larger capacity requiring more fine-tuning iterations. In the zero-shot setting, GPT4o (0.439 without context, 0.600 with context) substantially outperforms GPT3.5 Turbo (0.386/0.130) and Llama3.2 (3B) (0.156), but all zero-shot models fall well short of their fine-tuned counterparts. The most striking comparison is Llama3.2 (3B) itself: fine-tuning lifts its Macro F1 from 0.156 (zero-shot) to 0.694 (supervised, no context), a 4.4× improvement, demonstrating that task-specific training on AIMS.au annotations is essential for reliable sentence-level relevance detection. BERT and DistilBERT produce nearly identical results across most criteria, suggesting that for this sentence-level binary classification task, the distilled model retains most of the full BERT's capability at lower computational cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior work on modern slavery statement analysis, which has largely operated at the statement or paragraph level (LegalBench's California Transparency task, DIHR's paragraph relevance classifier, UK topic-modeling studies) or relied on keyword-based and comment-based features (The Future Society/Walk Free project). It also draws on the broader line of work on extracting mandated disclosures from corporate sustainability and climate reports (ClimateQA, ChatReport, ClimateBERT), but shifts the granularity to sentence-level binary classification across eleven specific questions derived from the Australian Modern Slavery Act's mandatory criteria. The key conceptual departure is the move from summarization or statement-level compliance judgment to precise sentence-level extraction of relevant disclosures, motivated by the need to distinguish concrete actions from vague or distracting corporate language.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset fills a clear gap as the largest sentence-level annotated resource for modern slavery compliance monitoring, enabling scalable automated analysis of the thousands of statements published annually. The finding that a fine-tuned 3B model (Llama3.2) substantially outperforms GPT-4o in zero-shot settings (0.721 vs. 0.600 Macro-F1) underscores that domain-specific fine-tuning remains essential for this task and that zero-shot prompting is insufficient for nuanced legal-text extraction. Open questions include generalization to other legal frameworks (UK, Canadian), handling of figures and tables that require vision models, and whether temporal distinctions (past actions vs. future promises) can be incorporated without substantially increasing annotation complexity.
