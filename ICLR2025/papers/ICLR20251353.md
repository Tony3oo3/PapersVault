## Problem

Long-context LLMs are expected to benefit from more retrieved passages in RAG due to higher recall, but the paper shows performance follows an inverted-U curve: it initially improves then degrades as retrieval size grows, with stronger retrievers (e.g., e5) causing sharper declines than weaker ones (e.g., BM25). The root cause is 'hard negatives'—topically related but irrelevant passages that mislead generation—and existing long-context benchmarks (needle-in-the-haystack, RULER) use random negatives that fail to capture this real-world challenge. Prior RAG tuning work also operates on fewer than 10 passages, leaving the long-context RAG regime largely unexplored.

## Input / Output

The system takes a natural-language query, an instruction template, and up to 40 retrieved Wikipedia passages (~8192 tokens for Gemma-2-9B) as input. In the implicit fine-tuning variant, the output is a concise answer; in the explicit reasoning variant, the output is a reasoning paragraph that identifies which retrieved passages are relevant and explains how they support the answer, followed by the final answer. The retrieval reordering method modifies only the positional ordering of passages in the input sequence without changing their content, placing highest-scoring passages at the beginning and end to exploit positional attention biases.

## Method

The paper proposes three complementary solutions. First, a training-free retrieval reordering strategy that interleaves passages by retrieval score so the top-ranked documents occupy the beginning and end of the input sequence, directly counteracting the 'lost-in-the-middle' positional bias. Second, implicit RAG fine-tuning, where the LLM is supervised on (instruction, retrieved passages, query) → answer pairs drawn from a diverse mixture of NQ, Wizard of Wikipedia, FEVER, and MMLU, implicitly building robustness to noisy retrieval context. Third, explicit RAG fine-tuning with intermediate reasoning, which augments the training target with a Gemini-1.5-Pro-generated reasoning paragraph that identifies relevant passages before the answer, teaching the model to explicitly discriminate signal from hard negatives. The paper further ablates data distribution (mixed vs. single-source), retriever diversity during training (BM25, e5, or mixed), and training context length (fixed vs. dynamic, up to 100% of max capacity), finding that mixed data, mixed retrievers, and maximum-length training all yield the best generalization.

## Results

The paper's central empirical finding is an inverted-U pattern: RAG accuracy initially improves with more retrieved passages but then declines, with the degradation being more severe for stronger retrievers (e5, Recall@40=0.90) than weaker ones (BM25, Recall@40=0.73) on NQ. The proposed RAG-specific fine-tuning (RAG FT) consistently outperforms both the chat model with retrieval and direct SFT across 9 unseen datasets (TriviaQA, PopQA, HotpotQA, 2WikiMultiHopQA, WebQuestions, Bamboogle, ASQA, T-REx, zsRE), with a flatter performance curve indicating greater robustness to hard negatives. Adding intermediate reasoning (RAG FT w. Int) yields further gains over implicit RAG FT. In the SFT+RAG-FT ablation (Table 15), combining 50k RAG-specific data with 200k UltraChat SFT data improves NQ accuracy from 0.5687 to 0.6033 and TriviaQA from 0.7155 to 0.7481, while MT-Bench drops only slightly from 5.8969 to 5.6031.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four configurations: (1) the base chat model with retrieval augmentation (e.g., Gemma-2-9B-Chat + e5), (2) Direct FT (standard SFT on QA pairs without retrieved context, evaluated without retrieval), (3) RAG FT (implicit fine-tuning with retrieved passages including hard negatives), and (4) RAG FT w. Int (explicit fine-tuning with intermediate reasoning labels generated by Gemini-1.5-Pro). RAG FT consistently outperforms both the chat model w. RAG and Direct FT across all 9 evaluation datasets and all three base models (Gemma-2-9B, Mistral-Nemo-12B, Gemini-1.0-Pro), with the performance curve being notably flatter as retrieval size increases, demonstrating improved robustness to hard negatives. RAG FT w. Int further surpasses RAG FT on all tested datasets, attributed to explicit relevance identification during training. The training-free retrieval reordering method shows negligible gains at small retrieval sizes but consistently outperforms the original ordering when the number of retrieved passages is large, across both e5 and BM25 retrievers and both NQ and PopQA. The paper also shows that fine-tuning with a mix of retrievers (BM25 + e5) generalizes best to unseen retrievers (Contriever, BGE), and that training at maximum context length (40 passages) yields the best inference-time performance across varying retrieval sizes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends RAG-specific LLM tuning (RetRobust, RA-DIT, Self-RAG, RAFT, RankRAG) from the few-passage regime (<10) to the long-context, many-passage setting, and grounds its retrieval reordering method directly in the 'lost-in-the-middle' positional bias identified by Liu et al. (2024). It departs from prior long-context evaluation work (needle-in-the-haystack, RULER) by arguing that random negatives used in those benchmarks fail to capture the harder, retriever-specific negatives encountered in real RAG. The paper also builds on the observation that irrelevant retrieved passages harm LLM generation (Shi et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2024) but reframes the problem around the interaction between retriever strength and negative difficulty at scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that RAG performance follows an inverted-U curve with respect to retrieval size—and that stronger retrievers paradoxically produce more harmful hard negatives—challenges a core design assumption in RAG systems and has immediate practical implications for anyone deploying long-context LLMs with large retrieval sets. The retrieval reordering trick is a zero-cost, immediately deployable mitigation, while the fine-tuning approaches (implicit robustness + explicit intermediate reasoning) offer a path to more fundamentally robust models. Open questions include whether the findings generalize beyond Wikipedia-based QA to domain-specific corpora, whether automated position optimization can outperform the simple odd-even reordering, and how these methods interact with iterative or multi-step retrieval pipelines.
