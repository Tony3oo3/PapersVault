## Problem

Adapting medical LLMs to local languages is critical for equitable healthcare access, but data scarcity for low-resource languages and the parameter cost of per-language experts make scaling beyond a handful of languages impractical. Prior multilingual medical work covers fewer than six languages, and existing neuron-level analyses of multilingual information flow are limited to fewer than seven languages, leaving the internal routing mechanisms of MoE models in multilingual settings unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250264/cropped_page5_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a medical multiple-choice question in one of 50 languages (12 high-resource, 38 low-resource) along with answer options, and outputs the selected correct option. For the 38 low-resource languages, both training and evaluation data are generated by translating 2,000 English MMLU clinical-knowledge samples via Google Translate, and evaluation uses 3-shot in-context queries with answer extraction. The model is trained on a 2.5-billion-token multilingual medical corpus spanning books, papers, encyclopedias, dialogues, exams, guidelines, and web sources, plus math and code data for reasoning.

## Method

The paper proposes a three-part architecture: (1) Hybrid-k routing, which guarantees activation of a language-specific expert while allowing cross-lingual routing to other experts, balancing interpretability with general knowledge transfer; (2) Post-MoE, a variant where the MoE sparse structure is applied only in the final two transformer layers while earlier layers remain dense, motivated by a circuit-theoretic analysis revealing a 'Spread Out in the End' information flow pattern where cross-lingual integration occurs early and language-specific divergence occurs late; and (3) Mixture of Language Family Experts, which groups 50 languages into 7 established linguistic families (Sino-Tibetan, Altaic, Australasian, Austronesian, Indo-European, Afro-Asian, Kongolese) so that adding new languages requires no additional parameters. The model is initialized via sparse upcycling from dense Qwen2 checkpoints (0.5B, 1.5B, 7B) and fine-tuned with full-parameter training, achieving 69.9% average accuracy on high-resource and 58.3% on low-resource languages at the 7B scale.

## Results

Apollo-MoE achieves 69.9% average accuracy on high-resource languages and 58.3% on low-resource languages with 8.02B active parameters (7B base), leading all open-source medical LLMs. The 1.63B active-parameter variant (1.5B base) reaches 54.8% (high) and 44.9% (low), surpassing 8B-parameter open-source models like Llama3 (49.3%/33.3%) and OpenBioLLM (46.1%/34.7%). The method requires only 2,000 translated training samples per low-resource language, with performance saturating at that scale. Across 50 languages spanning 7 language families, Apollo-MoE demonstrates that adding languages does not require additional parameters thanks to the language family expert grouping.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Apollo-MoE (1.5B, 1.63B active) outperforms all 8B open-source baselines on both high- and low-resource languages: it beats Llama3-8B by +5.5 (high) and +11.6 (low), OpenBioLLM-8B by +8.7 and +10.2, and MMed-Llama-3-8B by +14.6 and +8.4, despite using roughly 5× fewer active parameters. The 7B variant (8.02B active) extends this lead to +20.6 (high) and +25.0 (low) over Llama3-8B, and achieves 84.1% on Chinese versus Llama3's 43.6%. Against closed-source models, Apollo-MoE-7B still trails GPT-4o by 15.8 (high) and 22.8 (low), and Claude 3 Opus by 12.0 and 18.2, respectively. Notably, Apollo-MoE-7B surpasses GPT-4o-mini (77.6/69.8) on high-resource languages but falls short on low-resource (58.3 vs. 69.8). The paper also shows that adding two new languages (Bengali, Amharic) to the trained model causes negligible degradation in Apollo-MoE (≤0.8% drop) while Dense models lose 1–4.5%, confirming the scalability advantage of the language family expert design.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the MoE framework (Shazeer et al., 2017) and Sparse Upcycling (Komatsuzaki et al., 2022) to convert dense medical LLMs into sparse architectures, while drawing on prior language-specific expert designs (Pfeiffer et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023c; Kwon & Chung, 2023) for modular multilingual handling. It departs from prior multilingual medical LLM work (Qiu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024), which covered at most six languages, by introducing Hybrid-k routing that combines language-specific and cross-lingual expert activation, and by applying circuit-theory interpretability (Olah et al., 2020a) to MoE routing for the first time in a multilingual setting. The resulting Post-MoE architecture and language family experts represent a novel architectural contribution not present in prior MoE or multilingual transfer work (e.g., GreenPLM, Zeng et al., 2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical pathway to democratize medical AI across 50 languages (covering ~6B people) with parameter-free scaling via language family experts, and the 'Spread Out in the End' circuit finding offers a new interpretability lens for understanding multilingual LLMs beyond neuron-level analysis. Key limitations include testing only up to 7B parameters (optimal Post-MoE depth may shift for larger models), reliance on Google Translate for low-resource data (validated at ~90% accuracy but not perfect), and the fact that absolute performance still trails closed-source models like GPT-4o by a wide margin. Open questions include whether the Post-MoE principle generalizes to multimodal settings (treating modalities as 'languages') and whether the circuit-based routing analysis reveals further exploitable structure at greater model scale.
