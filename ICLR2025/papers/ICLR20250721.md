## Problem

Simulation-based conversation planning algorithms (e.g., vanilla MCTS) require hundreds to thousands of LLM queries per turn to simulate future conversations, taking 740+ seconds per turn and making them impractical for real-time dialogue. Myopic alternatives that select responses based on immediate reward fail to account for how a response influences later conversation quality. The paper asks: can we perform non-myopic, multi-turn conversation planning within a practical budget (1–3 seconds) without costly LLM simulation queries?

## Input / Output

At each conversation turn, SCOPE receives the current conversation context (a sequence of alternating human/LLM responses ending in a human prompt), a pool of m=5 candidate LLM responses generated via diverse beam search, and pre-trained models (a 4096-dim semantic embedding from Llama Guard 2's penultimate feature layer, a probabilistic transition model, and a deterministic reward model). It outputs a single selected LLM response from the candidate pool that maximizes expected cumulative reward over a 5-turn horizon. Planning is time-budgeted (e.g., 1–3 seconds) rather than iteration-budgeted, and the entire search operates in the continuous 4096-dimensional semantic space rather than over discrete text strings.

## Method

SCOPE reformulates the conversation-planning MDP in a dense semantic space by projecting states and actions via a pretrained embedding model, then learns two lightweight models from static conversation data: (1) a probabilistic transition model (MDN or deep ensemble of Mixture-of-Experts networks) that predicts stochastic semantic transitions in two sub-steps—LLM action direction and human response state—and (2) a deterministic reward model that estimates the reward at each semantic point, with instantaneous rewards recovered as differences between consecutive points. At runtime, SCOPE performs MCTS entirely in semantic space, using the learned transition model as a simulator to sample future states and the reward model to score rollouts, eliminating all LLM queries during planning. This makes each simulation rollout ~92× faster than vanilla MCTS, allowing SCOPE to explore a much broader search scope within the same time budget. A theoretical result shows that under bijectivity of the embedding and exact transition/reward recovery, the semantic MDP yields the same optimal action as the original text-space MDP.

## Results

SCOPE achieves higher cumulative rewards than all baselines within 3 seconds of planning time on both harmfulness and human response length reward functions across 200 conversation starters. Each simulation rollout in SCOPE takes 0.18s versus 16.63s for vanilla MCTS (92× faster), enabling SCOPE to plan 70× faster overall. Under a realistic 1-second planning budget, SCOPE achieves larger cumulative rewards than vanilla MCTS even when the latter is allocated 70 seconds. SCOPE also produces conversations where human responses are, on average, 150 tokens longer than those from myopic baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against vanilla MCTS, which follows the simulation-based tree-search approach of Yu et al. (2023) and Koh et al. (2024) that uses an external LLM (Llama-3-8B-Instruct) to simulate human user responses during look-ahead. SCOPE consistently outperforms vanilla MCTS on both reward functions (harmfulness and human response length) under practical planning budgets of 1–3 seconds, where vanilla MCTS can only perform a very narrow scope of simulation. Even when vanilla MCTS is given a 70× larger planning budget (~70 seconds), SCOPE at 1 second still achieves higher cumulative rewards, demonstrating that the bottleneck is not merely compute time but the fundamental cost of LLM queries per rollout. SCOPE also outperforms myopic baselines (0-step and 1-step Greedy) by accounting for stochastic multi-turn transitions, and is 13× faster than 1-step Greedy since it requires no LLM queries at all during planning.

## Relation to Prior Work

SCOPE builds on the MCTS framework (Browne et al., 2012) and the LLM-Modulo paradigm (Kambhampati et al., 2024) where an LLM proposes candidate actions and a separate planner selects among them. It departs from prior simulation-based conversation planning (Yu et al., 2023; Koh et al., 2024) that relies on expensive LLM queries to simulate human responses, instead learning lightweight probabilistic transition and reward models (MDNs, deep ensembles) in a dense semantic embedding space. The key conceptual shift is replacing the LLM-as-simulator with a learned stochastic model over semantic vectors, enabling MCTS rollouts without any additional LLM inference at runtime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 70× speedup makes non-myopic conversation planning feasible within real-time response windows (seconds rather than hundreds of seconds), which is a practical bottleneck for deployed chatbots and AI assistants. A key open question is whether the fixed transition models generalize across different LLM backends and user populations, since the paper acknowledges this limitation and suggests per-LLM or per-user fine-tuning as future work. The finding that local transition-model prediction quality does not strongly correlate with end-to-end planning performance also raises questions about what properties of the semantic space actually matter for effective planning.
