## Problem

In sequential decision-making, an agent trades off the information it accumulates against the regret it suffers, but no prior work quantified this trade-off in a unified way. Specifically, there were no Bayesian regret lower bounds that depend on the amount of information (in bits) an agent has gathered, making it impossible to determine how much external information is needed to match the performance of an agent who has already played multiple rounds. This paper asks: what is the exact relationship between information bits and regret?

## Input / Output

Theoretically, the method takes a Bayesian interactive decision-making problem defined by a decision space Π, model class M, context space C, and prior P, and produces worst-case Bayesian regret lower and upper bounds parameterized by the accumulated mutual information R (in bits). In the LLM experiment, the input is a multiple-choice question with four options plus the small LLM's token probability distribution over [A, B, C, D]; the output is a binary deployment decision (query the large LLM or not), where the information proxy is the KL divergence between the small LLM's answer distribution and the uniform distribution, compared against a threshold calibrated to match a target deployment rate.

## Method

The core contribution is a novel information-theoretic lower bound method that extends Fano's inequality to online problems by reducing regret minimization to hypothesis testing over a packing set of the decision space. For finite or parametric decision spaces, the bound uses local packing numbers and a KL-regularity assumption (Assumption 3.1) to upper-bound the mutual information I(V; H_T) ≤ 2Āε²T, yielding worst-case Bayesian regret lower bounds of Ω(√(KT)) for MAB and Ω(√(HSKT)) for tabular RL. Under an information constraint I(π*; H_T) ≤ R, the bound tightens to Ω(√(KT log K / R)), and a matching upper bound of O(log K √(KT/R)) is proven for Thompson sampling via the information ratio framework. In the LLM experiment, the bits-based policy computes the KL divergence between the small LLM's answer distribution and uniform as a per-round information estimate, then deploys the large LLM only when this estimate exceeds a threshold, achieving lower regret than random deployment at matched query budgets.

## Results

In the Bayesian MAB experiment (K=8, ε=0.1), Thompson sampling accumulates information fastest and achieves the lowest Bayesian regret, while EXP3 accumulates the least information and suffers the highest regret. In the LLM question-answering experiment on MMLU (200 episodes), the bits-based policy achieves mean regret of 36±1 vs. 38.7±0.3 for random selection at 50% large-LLM deployment (Mistral 7B small, Mixtral-8x7B large), and 38±2 vs. 44.5±0.7 for Gemma 2B small. The gap widens at 70% deployment (26.6±0.8 vs. 30.5±0.4 for Mistral; 32±1 vs. 35.6±0.8 for Gemma), while at 90% deployment the policies converge (21.8±0.4 vs. 22.4±0.5). Theoretical bounds show worst-case Bayesian regret scales as Ω(√(KT·log K/R)) for K-armed bandits with R bits of accumulated information, and O(log K·√(KT/R)) for Thompson sampling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the Bayesian bandit experiment, Thompson sampling outperforms both EXP3 and APS on both Bayesian regret and information accumulation rate, confirming the theoretical prediction that faster information accumulation yields lower regret. In the LLM MCQA experiment, the bits-based policy consistently beats random LLM selection across all deployment rates and small-LLM choices, with the largest advantage at 50% deployment (6.5 regret points for Gemma 2B, 2.7 for Mistral 7B). The improvement diminishes as deployment rate increases toward 90%, where the policies nearly tie, since the large LLM is queried so frequently that the selection strategy matters less. Both bits-based and random policies substantially outperform the small-LLM-only baseline (59.3 or 67 regret), confirming that selective large-LLM queries are valuable. The large-LLM-only policy achieves the lowest regret (18.2 or 22) but at the cost of incurring the 0.1 penalty every round, illustrating the regret-information trade-off the paper formalizes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the information-theoretic lower-bound methodology of Yang & Barron (1999) and Fano's inequality (Cover, 1999) from offline statistical estimation to online sequential decision-making, providing a unified framework that recovers known lower bounds for MAB, tabular RL, linear bandits, and Lipschitz bandits. It departs from Russo & Van Roy (2014, 2016), who established information-theoretic *upper* bounds for Thompson sampling and IDS depending on prior entropy, by introducing the first Bayesian regret *lower* bounds that depend on the total information an agent accumulates (measured via mutual information), and by providing matching upper bounds under an explicit information constraint. The setting itself is a Bayesian adaptation of the frequentist interactive decision-making framework of Foster et al. (2021), augmented with contextual information.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean quantitative language—bits versus reward—for reasoning about how much external knowledge (e.g., from an LLM or human advisor) can substitute for costly trial-and-error in sequential decision-making, which is directly relevant to the growing ecosystem of LLM-augmented agents. The LLM routing experiment demonstrates practical utility, showing that a simple KL-divergence-based information threshold outperforms random model selection, with gains growing as the deployment budget increases. Key limitations include the √(log K) gap between upper and lower bounds, the assumption that exogenous information is independent of the process history, and the restriction to the Bayesian setting; closing these gaps and extending to adversarial or frequentist settings are natural next steps.
