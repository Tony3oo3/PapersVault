## Problem

Existing hypergraph generative models either depend on pre-assumed structural statistics (e.g., degree sequences, hyperedge size distributions) that may not generalize, or are learning-based approaches that fail to capture critical higher-order structural nuances. A key underexplored direction is the reverse of the common hypergraph-to-graph projection: reconstructing hypergraphs from graph projections while preserving frequency and degree information that unweighted projections discard.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250766/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a hypergraph with distinct hyperedges as input and produces a new hypergraph with the same number of vertices. Internally, the hypergraph is converted to a weighted graph projection where each edge weight equals the number of hyperedges containing both endpoint vertices. The generated hypergraph is constrained to have exactly m hyperedges (matching the original), and each hyperedge corresponds to a clique in the generated weighted graph whose size does not exceed the maximum hyperedge size of the input.

## Method

HyperPLR operates in three phases: (1) Projection converts the hypergraph into a weighted graph where edge weights encode pairwise co-occurrence frequency across hyperedges; (2) Learning uses CELL (a spectral low-rank graph generator) to sample a new graph topology and a two-layer GCN trained with MSE loss to predict edge weights on that topology, jointly modeling P(W|A,X)·P(A); (3) Reconstruction solves a novel Weighted Clique Edge Cover (WCEC) problem via a Greedy Weighted Cover (GWC) hill-climbing heuristic that iteratively selects the clique with the largest L1-distance gain until m cliques are found, each becoming a hyperedge. The key insight is that the weighted projection retains frequency information lost by unweighted projections, and WCEC (unlike the prior MCEC formulation) accounts for pairwise co-occurrence counts, enabling more faithful and controllable hypergraph reconstruction.

## Results

Across five real-world hypergraph datasets, HyperPLR closely matches ground-truth structural statistics (density, average hyperedge size, average node degree) without being given these parameters directly — e.g., on email-Enron it produces density 10.72 vs. ground truth 10.57, and on NDC-classes average size 5.50 vs. 5.92. On graph-projection-level metrics (G coefficient, G modularity), HyperPLR is competitive with parameter-based baselines: on contact-high-school it achieves G coefficient 0.471 and G modularity 0.556 (ground truth: 0.504, 0.582), while HyperDK_0,0 collapses to G modularity 0.000. On advanced structural properties (Table 3), HyperPLR attains the best or near-best diameter alignment (0.000–0.250) and degree-distribution cross-entropy (4.02–7.66) across all five datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against HyperDK (both 0,0 and 1,1 configurations), HyperPLR wins decisively on every metric and dataset: HyperDK inflates density by 5–11× (e.g., 272 vs. 24 on contact-high-school) and collapses G modularity to near zero (0.000–0.058), while HyperPLR stays within ~10% of ground-truth density and preserves modularity (0.28–0.62). Against Hyperlap/Hyperlap+, the comparison is more nuanced: Hyperlap+ exactly matches ground-truth density, average size, and average degree (since it is parameter-based), and often achieves higher G modularity (e.g., 0.742 vs. 0.556 on contact-high-school) and B modularity (0.747 vs. 0.649). However, HyperPLR matches these structural statistics without being given them as inputs, and on G coefficient it is closer to ground truth on 3 of 5 datasets. Against THERA, results are mixed: THERA tends to produce higher B modularity (e.g., 0.831 vs. 0.649 on contact-high-school) but lower G modularity (0.405 vs. 0.556), and HyperPLR is closer to ground-truth G coefficient on 4 of 5 datasets. On the advanced structural metrics in Table 3, HyperPLR achieves the best diameter alignment (0.000 on email-Enron, NDC-classes, contact-primary-school) and the lowest degree-distribution cross-entropy on 4 of 5 datasets, outperforming all three baseline families.

## Relation to Prior Work

HyperPLR builds directly on CELL (Rendsburg et al., 2020) for spectral-space graph topology generation, extending it with a GCN-based weight predictor to handle weighted graphs—a capability CELL lacks. The reconstruction phase introduces the Weighted Clique Edge Cover (WCEC) problem, which generalizes the Minimum Clique Edge Cover (MCEC) used by Young et al. (2020) by incorporating edge co-occurrence frequencies, and proves via Lemma 2 that WCEC strictly subsumes MCEC. Unlike prior hypergraph generators (HyperDK, HyperLap, THERA) that rely on pre-assumed structural parameters or iterative statistical sampling, HyperPLR learns the weighted projection in a latent space and reconstructs hypergraphs through a principled clique-cover formulation, bridging the gap between graph generation and hypergraph reconstruction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The WCEC formulation provides the first principled, weight-aware mechanism for reconstructing hypergraphs from graph projections, addressing a recognized gap in the literature where prior work (Young et al., 2020) ignored hyperedge counts and vertex degree information. However, the GWC heuristic does not guarantee optimality, and the NP-completeness of WCEC limits scalability for very large hypergraphs. Empirically, HyperPLR is competitive but not uniformly dominant—on several datasets (e.g., email-Eu, contact-high-school), baselines like Hyperlap+ achieve higher modularity scores—so the practical advantage is more in the theoretical framework and parameter insensitivity than in raw metric superiority. Open questions include whether reinforcement learning or MCTS could replace the greedy heuristic, and whether the approach scales to hypergraphs with tens of thousands of vertices.
