## Problem

Despite abundant empirical evidence that GFlowNets exploit compositional structure to learn generalizable flow assignments, no prior work provided non-vacuous high-probability statistical guarantees for their generalization, nor formally characterized how state-space size and trajectory length degrade generalization performance. Additionally, no distributed learning algorithm with network-level parallelization existed for GFlowNets, leaving the practical challenge of poor state-space coverage under fixed compute budgets unaddressed.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pointed directed acyclic graph (state graph) with a reward function on terminal states and a fixed-horizon partition (FHP) that splits the state space into m leaf subnetworks and a single root network, where all leaf sources are equidistant from the root source. It produces a learned forward policy and flow function for each leaf subnetwork plus a root GFlowNet that allocates flow across subnetworks, along with high-probability upper bounds on the population risk (FCS metric in [0,1] for empirical bounds; KL divergence or DB loss for oracle bounds). The FCS metric is used as the bounded risk functional specifically because the standard TB/DB losses are unbounded and incompatible with classical PAC-Bayes theorems.

## Method

The paper contributes two intertwined results. First, it derives the first non-vacuous PAC-Bayesian generalization bounds for GFlowNets by framing training as supervised learning, adopting the bounded FCS metric as the risk functional, and using data-dependent Gaussian priors (Dziugaite & Roy style) learned on a data split to avoid vacuous KL terms; it further establishes oracle trajectory-level and transition-level bounds via Azuma-Hoeffding-type inequalities applied to martingale difference sequences built from the detailed balance loss, revealing that the excess risk scales as O(log t_m / n^α) where t_m is maximum trajectory length. Second, it introduces Subgraph Asynchronous Learning (SAL), a divide-and-conquer algorithm that partitions the state graph via a fixed-horizon partition into multi-source leaf subnetworks grouped by a root network; m leaf GFlowNets are trained in parallel by minimizing an amortized trajectory balance (ATB) loss on their respective subgraphs, and a root GFlowNet is then trained with a composite reward that incorporates the leaf flow estimates, enabling network-level parallelization and substantially better state-space coverage within a fixed time budget.

## Results

SAL is evaluated on six generative tasks (Set Generation, Bag Generation, Sequence Design, SIX6, Hypergrid, and one additional task) against a centralized GFlowNet baseline. The paper reports that SAL 'drastically speeds up the discovery of high-value states for all considered generative problems under varying computational constraints' and 'achieves more accurate distributional approximations than its centralized counterpart' (measured via the FCS metric, a bounded total-variation-based distributional accuracy score in [0,1]). For the non-vacuous generalization bounds (Section 5.1), the PAC-Bayesian certificates on the FCS risk are shown to be 'remarkably tight' across set generation, bag generation, sequence design, and SIX6, confirming that the trained GFlowNets generalize. The specific numerical values for distributional accuracy and mode-discovery speed are reported in Figure 4, Figure 6, and Table 2 (Appendix C.2), which are not fully reproduced in the main text.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the standard centralized GFlowNet trained with the trajectory balance (TB) loss, as introduced by Bengio et al. (2021) and Malkin et al. (2022). SAL wins on both primary metrics across all six generative tasks: (1) mode discovery — SAL finds high-reward terminal states significantly faster under a fixed wall-clock training budget, as shown in Figure 4 where the SAL curve reaches the target reward level well before the centralized GFlowNet; and (2) distribution matching — SAL achieves lower FCS (total-variation-based) error than the centralized model, as reported in Figure 6 and Table 2 (Appendix C.2). The advantage is attributed to SAL's divide-and-conquer structure: each leaf GFlowNet operates on a smaller subnetwork with shorter trajectories, enabling better state-space coverage within the same time budget, while the root GFlowNet allocates flow across subnetworks. The paper does not report any setting where the centralized GFlowNet outperforms SAL, though it notes that SAL's total running time equals the longest leaf training time plus the aggregation phase, which is still shorter than the equivalent centralized training time for the same number of gradient steps.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the PAC-Bayesian generalization analysis framework (McAllester; Dziugaite & Roy) to GFlowNets, departing from prior GFlowNet generalization work (Krichel et al., 2024; Atanackovic & Bengio, 2024) by using data-dependent priors rather than data-agnostic bounds, yielding the first non-vacuous statistical certificates. The distributed learning algorithm SAL builds on the divide-and-conquer paradigm from federated and distributed learning (Yagli et al., 2020; Barnes et al., 2022) and the martingale-based PAC-Bayes theory for non-i.i.d. data (Seldin et al., 2012b), but is the first to apply network-level parallelization to GFlowNet training, in contrast to da Silva et al. (2024) which only partitions the reward function. The FCS metric (Silva et al., 2024) serves as the bounded risk functional enabling standard PAC-Bayes machinery, while the trajectory-level oracle bound draws on Malkin et al. (2023)'s variational inference interpretation of GFlowNets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first provable statistical guarantees that a trained GFlowNet generalizes beyond its training trajectories, which is critical as GFlowNets are increasingly deployed in high-stakes domains like drug discovery and biological sequence design. The identification of trajectory length and sampling distribution as key generalization-limiting factors offers concrete guidance for practitioners designing exploration strategies. However, the oracle bounds depend on intractable quantities (minimum reward, entropy of the backward policy), and the authors acknowledge that deeper theoretical understanding of advanced sampling techniques (replay buffers, local search) remains open. SAL's practical impact is promising but limited to problems admitting a natural fixed-horizon partition, and its extension to open-ended domains like NLP or drug discovery is deferred to future work.
