## Problem

Language models memorize training data verbatim, creating privacy and copyright risks, yet no prior work has systematically compared the three main classes of mitigation strategies (regularization, fine-tuning, unlearning) across model sizes, training durations, and artifact types. Additionally, the absence of small, open-source LMs with known memorized sequences makes rapid prototyping of new mitigation methods prohibitively expensive, as existing studies rely on billion-parameter models.

## Input / Output

The unlearning methods take a trained GPT-2-style LM, a set of memorized artifact sequences (noise: ±1 token perturbations; backdoors: trigger token followed by a degenerate token), and optionally a set of non-memorized sequences for the dual-objective variants. Memorization is measured by prompting with the first k=50 tokens and checking whether greedy decoding reproduces the remaining n−k tokens exactly. The output is an edited LM with the top K weights per layer zeroed out, where K is controlled by a ratio hyperparameter (e.g., 0.001–0.3 of total parameters). The paper also introduces TinyMem, a configurable suite of 418K–9.6M parameter GPT-2-style models trained on synthetic math sequences or Wikipedia with injected artifacts.

## Method

The key contribution is BalancedSubnet, which learns a sparse binary mask over model weights via a straight-through estimator (Ramanujan et al., 2020) optimized with a dual objective: maximize loss on memorized sequences (to localize memorization-relevant weights) while minimizing loss on non-memorized sequences (to preserve general task performance). The mask is initialized with Kaiming uniform, and at each iteration the top K weights per layer are dropped according to the current mask scores before computing the combined loss; gradients update only the mask scores, not the LM parameters. This design disentangles the subnetwork responsible for memorization from components critical for unrelated tasks, overcoming Subnet's tendency to degrade general performance. The paper also introduces four additional unlearning methods (Durable, Durable-agg, SOU, Subnet) and provides the first comprehensive empirical comparison of 17 strategies (3 regularizers, 3 fine-tuning recipes, 11 unlearning methods) across model sizes (2–16 layers), training durations, dataset sizes, and artifact types, validating results on production-grade Pythia 2.8B/6.9B models.

## Results

On the 4-layer multiplicative math model, BalancedSubnet reduces memorization to 0.06% (noise) and 0.00% (backdoor) while preserving 96.11% and 96.79% test accuracy, completing in ~6 seconds—compared to the baseline's 34.73% and 99.50% memorization at 96.98% accuracy. On production-grade Pythia 2.8B, BalancedSubnet reduces memorization from 53.47% to 9.11% with only a 1.04 perplexity increase (21.98→23.02) in 89 seconds; on Pythia 6.9B it reduces memorization from 89.31% to 6.53% with a 3.27 perplexity increase (19.46→22.73) in 233 seconds. Regularizer-based methods are 100–1000× slower and fail to curb backdoor memorization; fine-tuning matches or beats BalancedSubnet on memorization removal but is 4–5× slower while sometimes degrading accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against spectral norm regularization (Yoshida & Miyato, 2017), BalancedSubnet is ~2400× faster (6s vs. 14,469s for noise) and achieves comparable memorization removal (0.06% vs. 0.05%), but critically, spectral norm completely fails on backdoors (99.81% memorized) while BalancedSubnet eliminates them (0.00%). Loss truncation (Kang & Hashimoto, 2020) is similarly ineffective on backdoors (99.23% memorized) and slower (266s). Example-tied dropout (Maini et al., 2023) removes memorization but catastrophically degrades accuracy to 41.60% (noise) and 36.41% (backdoor), whereas BalancedSubnet retains 96.11% and 96.79%. Among neuron-based unlearning methods from Chang et al. (2024), Act achieves 0.00% memorization on both noise and backdoor but drops accuracy to 87.41%/96.36%; HC and Slim fail to remove backdoor memorization (0.16% and 25.25% respectively). BalancedSubnet outperforms all baselines on the joint criterion of memorization removal + accuracy preservation, and is 4–5× faster than the best fine-tuning method (Both FT) while matching its accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of two prior lines of work: (1) the memorization-localization literature (Maini et al., 2023; Chang et al., 2024), which established that memorized content is localizable in LM weights and neurons, and (2) the sparse-subnetwork discovery literature (Ramanujan et al., 2020), which showed that straight-through-estimator binary masks can identify functional subnetworks in randomly initialized networks. The proposed BalancedSubnet method explicitly fuses these two ideas—learning a sparse mask via straight-through estimation while simultaneously optimizing a dual-objective loss (maximize loss on memorized sequences, minimize loss on non-memorized sequences) borrowed from the Greedy unlearning procedure of Maini et al. The paper also draws on second-order pruning (Kurtic et al., 2022) for SOU and on durable-backdoor implantation (Zhang et al., 2022b) for the Durable family, but its core conceptual contribution is the mask-optimization-with-balance formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic, apples-to-apples comparison of 17 memorization-mitigation strategies across three classes (regularization, fine-tuning, unlearning) under varied model sizes, training durations, and artifact types, yielding clear practical guidance: unlearning dominates, and within unlearning, BalancedSubnet offers the best memorization-removal/accuracy-preservation tradeoff. The TinyMem benchmark addresses a real bottleneck—lack of small, open-source models with known memorized sequences—enabling rapid prototyping before expensive production-grade evaluation. Open questions remain: whether unlearning is sufficient for content regulation (Shumailov et al., 2024 show residual leakage), how context length interacts with unlearning effectiveness, and whether second-order methods like SOU can be made scalable to billion-parameter models without lossy Hessian approximations.
