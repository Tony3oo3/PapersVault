## Problem

Gaussian processes are natural for interpolating sparse satellite observations but scale poorly, requiring inducing-point approximations that limit expressivity and assume stationarity. Deep GPs overcome the stationarity limitation but still rely on crude variational approximations for inference. The paper asks whether one can retain the inductive biases and interpretability of GPs while achieving the scalability and flexibility of deep neural networks for spatiotemporal interpolation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251835/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes spatiotemporal satellite track coordinates (planar lat/lon or spherical S² coordinates) paired with scalar altimetry measurements (SSH, sea-ice freeboard, or sea level anomaly), with datasets ranging from ~1.1M to ~8.1M points. For the global experiment, spatial inputs live on S² and the model must respect spherical geometry (no discontinuities at longitude 0° or polar distortions). The output is a full predictive distribution (mean and variance) at arbitrary unobserved spatiotemporal locations, with uncertainty quantified via deep ensembles, variational inference, or dropout.

## Method

The architecture is a deep neural network where each hidden layer h(x) = Θφ(x) is a random feature expansion of a stationary Matérn GP: φ uses fixed random frequencies ω (sampled from the kernel's spectral density) and biases b with cosine activations (planar) or Gegenbauer polynomial activations with geodesic distances (spherical), while Θ is the only trainable parameter. Skip connections from the raw input to every layer prevent depth-related pathologies, and spatial and temporal components are processed by separate DRF stacks before concatenation. Training uses mini-batched gradient descent on MSE or Huber loss with L2 weight decay, fixing the random features at initialization to mimic weight-space GP inference. For global fields, spherical random features are derived from the Matérn GP on S² via the addition theorem for spherical harmonics, yielding a novel DNN with Gegenbauer polynomial activations. Uncertainty is obtained primarily through deep ensembles (10 independently trained models), with hyperparameters tuned via Bayesian optimisation on a validation loss that optionally includes a functional regularisation term penalising sharp gradients.

## Results

On the synthetic Arctic MSS experiment (1.16M points), the 4-layer DRF with deep ensembles achieves the best CRPS (0.046) and matches DGP's RMSE (0.135) while training 2× faster (19.7 vs 42.3 min). On real sea-ice freeboard data (1.17M points), DRF ensembles achieve RMAE 0.322, competitive with GPSat (0.318) and SVGP (0.322), while producing well-calibrated uncertainties (CRPS 0.077). On the global sea level anomaly experiment (8.09M points), the spherical DRF achieves NLPD 0.0063 and CRPS 0.0918, substantially outperforming both Euclidean DRF (NLPD 0.0387) and spherical SVGP (NLPD 0.0085, CRPS 0.1210).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GP-based baselines, DRF ensembles clearly outperform SVGP on CRPS (0.046 vs 0.230) and RMSE (0.135 vs 0.155) on synthetic data, and match SVGP on RMAE (0.322) for real freeboard data. DGP achieves the same RMSE (0.135) but with a dramatically worse NLL (1614 vs 13.6) due to underconfident predictive variances, and takes 2× longer to train. GPSat slightly edges DRF on RMSE (0.126 vs 0.135) and RMAE (0.318 vs 0.322), but is 3× slower (63.6 vs 19.7 min) and produces spurious local patterns from unstable hyperparameter optimisation. Among DNN baselines, SIREN is the closest competitor (CRPS 0.066, RMSE 0.155) given its shared trigonometric activations, but DRF's bottleneck structure and fixed preactivations yield better spatiotemporal pattern capture. FFN and SIREN both show qualitative overfitting on real freeboard data despite decent quantitative scores, while ReLU MLP and CNP produce heavily oversmoothed reconstructions across all experiments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper revives and extends the random feature expansion of deep Gaussian processes introduced by Cutajar et al. (2017), which was largely overlooked after doubly stochastic VI became the standard for DGP inference. The core architectural idea—replacing each GP layer with a fixed random feature map followed by a trainable linear layer—directly inherits from Rahimi & Recht's (2007) random Fourier features and the DGP compositional structure of Damianou & Lawrence (2013). The key departure is the extension to spherical inputs via Gegenbauer polynomial random features derived from the Matérn GP on S² (Borovitskiy et al., 2020) and the integral identity of Azangulov et al. (2024), yielding a novel DNN architecture with spherical inductive bias. The paper also positions itself within the broader Bayesian deep learning paradigm, using deep ensembles rather than variational inference as the primary UQ mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in demonstrating that a simple, scalable neural architecture with GP-derived inductive biases can match or exceed purpose-built geostatistical methods (GPSat, SVGP, DGP) on remote sensing interpolation while being 2–3× faster to train, and can scale to 8M+ data points via mini-batched gradient descent. The spherical random features provide a principled way to model global fields without the distortions of Euclidean coordinate projections, which is a genuinely useful architectural component for any global geophysical application. Key limitations include the difficulty of tuning kernel hyperparameters in deep architectures (requiring Bayesian optimisation with hand-tuned functional regularisation penalty), and the fact that the approach still relies on stationary kernels at each layer, limiting its ability to capture strongly non-stationary structure without sufficient depth.
