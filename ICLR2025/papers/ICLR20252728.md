## Problem

Previous 3DGS-based inverse rendering methods model indirect lighting as static learnable attributes (volumes or per-Gaussian spherical harmonics) with baked occlusion, which cannot capture the dynamic physical interactions between light and surfaces. This makes it impossible to produce physically consistent indirect illumination during relighting, as the stored values do not adapt to new lighting conditions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252728/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of posed RGB images and produces a 3D Gaussian representation augmented with per-Gaussian normal and BRDF (albedo, metallic, roughness) attributes. Beyond standard novel view synthesis, it outputs decomposed depth, normal, occlusion, and indirect lighting maps, as well as relit renderings under arbitrary environment maps. The intermediate G-buffer (depth, normal, BRDF maps) is the key structural element that enables the path tracing stage to operate on a recovered surface rather than directly on the point-based Gaussian primitives.

## Method

GI-GS uses a three-stage pipeline: (1) vanilla 3DGS reconstruction with normals added as per-Gaussian attributes supervised by pseudo-normals from the depth map; (2) a deferred shading PBR pass that rasterizes the G-buffer, applies the Cook-Torrance BRDF with split-sum approximation and a learnable environment map to render direct lighting I_dir, and computes ambient occlusion via hemisphere sampling; (3) a tile-based adaptive path tracing stage that marches rays from each surface point against the G-buffer depth map to determine visibility, then indexes the first-pass RGB image at intersection points to estimate indirect diffuse lighting. The final image fuses the first-pass (direct + occlusion) and second-pass (indirect) results. For scene-level datasets, the method extends path tracing from screen space to world space by rendering a six-face cubemap to capture global geometry and lighting outside the camera frustum.

## Results

On the TensoIR dataset, GI-GS achieves the best NVS PSNR of 36.75 dB and best Albedo PSNR of 31.97 dB among all baselines, while ranking second only to TensoIR (28.58 dB) on Relighting PSNR (24.70 dB). On Mip-NeRF 360, GI-GS outperforms all 3DGS-based inverse rendering methods, achieving 24.01 dB (outdoor) and 29.29 dB (indoor) PSNR, with a particularly large margin over GS-IR on indoor scenes (29.29 vs. 27.46 dB). Training time is 28 minutes on TensoIR, comparable to GS-IR (33 min) and far faster than TensoIR (4.5 hrs) or NeRFactor (>100 hrs).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the primary 3DGS inverse rendering baseline GS-IR, GI-GS wins on all three tasks: NVS PSNR (36.75 vs. 35.33), Albedo PSNR (31.97 vs. 30.29), and Relighting PSNR (24.70 vs. 24.37), while also training faster (28 vs. 33 min). On Mip-NeRF 360, GI-GS surpasses GS-IR by 0.56 dB outdoor and 1.83 dB indoor PSNR, with the indoor gap reflecting better occlusion and indirect lighting modeling. Compared to the NeRF-based TensoIR, GI-GS achieves higher NVS and Albedo PSNR but trails by ~3.9 dB on Relighting PSNR (24.70 vs. 28.58), though it trains ~9× faster. Against NVS-only methods (3DGS, Mip-NeRF 360, Instant NGP) on Mip-NeRF 360, GI-GS is slightly below 3DGS on outdoor (24.01 vs. 24.64) and indoor (29.29 vs. 30.41) PSNR, but 3DGS does not perform inverse rendering. On the real-world Stanford-ORB dataset, GI-GS outperforms GS-IR on Albedo (30.53 vs. 27.51 PSNR) and Relighting (28.54 vs. 25.31 PSNR) while matching on NVS (36.85 vs. 36.34 PSNR). On Synthetic4Relight, GI-GS leads on NVS (35.42 vs. 34.79 PSNR) and Relighting (27.36 vs. 26.30 PSNR) over GS-IR, with a slight Albedo gap to InvRender (24.68 vs. 25.25 PSNR).

## Relation to Prior Work

GI-GS builds directly on 3DGS (Kerbl et al., 2023) as the explicit scene representation and on GS-IR (Liang et al., 2024) for the BRDF-attribute inverse rendering pipeline, including its TV-loss formulations for material and lighting regularization. The key conceptual departure is in indirect lighting: prior 3DGS-based inverse rendering methods (GS-IR, Relightable 3D Gaussian, GaussianShader) bake occlusion into learned volumes or store indirect illumination as static spherical-harmonic attributes on each Gaussian, making it non-transferable under new lighting. GI-GS instead borrows the deferred shading pipeline from real-time graphics—rasterizing a G-buffer, then performing differentiable path tracing against the recovered depth/normal geometry—to compute occlusion and indirect lighting dynamically from the scene, enabling them to change correctly during relighting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By making indirect lighting a function of scene geometry rather than a baked attribute, GI-GS addresses a fundamental limitation of prior 3DGS inverse rendering: the inability to produce physically consistent indirect illumination under novel lighting. This is among the first 3DGS frameworks to model indirect lighting during relighting, opening the door to more realistic scene relighting and material editing. However, the method still omits specular indirect illumination (a long-standing challenge requiring complex Monte Carlo sampling), relies on a single environment map that cannot capture spatially varying lighting, and its path-tracing accuracy is bounded by the quality of the reconstructed geometry—suggesting that tighter geometric priors or mesh extraction could further improve results.
