## Problem

Existing LLM watermarks (e.g., KGW, Unigram) are designed to detect AI-generated text, but it is unclear whether they can detect model IP infringement—i.e., whether a suspect LLM fine-tuned on watermarked source outputs retains a detectable watermark. Two practical obstacles make direct detection unreliable: (1) the detector does not know the training domain, so out-of-domain queries attenuate watermark signal, and (2) learnable watermarks require small n-gram lengths, which makes the actual green ratio deviate from the preset ratio and causes fixed-threshold z-score detection to fail for many hash keys.

## Input / Output

The detector operates in a black-box setting: it queries the suspect LLM's API with a selected subset of candidate queries (drawn from a general-purpose set like Alpaca) until 20k tokens are collected, and simultaneously queries a set of anchor LLMs with the same queries to obtain reference responses. The watermark configuration (hash key ξ, green ratio γ, n-gram length n, and bias δ) is known to the detector. The output is a binary infringement verdict obtained by comparing the z-score of the concatenated suspect text against an adaptive threshold z_th rather than a fixed α.

## Method

LIDet addresses both obstacles with two coupled designs. First, anchor-based query selection scores each candidate query by a priority v that combines normalized token entropy H (measuring response length and token diversity across anchor LLMs) and cross-model generalizability η (negative variance of the green ratio across anchor LLMs), ensuring selected queries elicit diverse, evenly-distributed token sequences whose green ratios are stable across models. Second, adaptive-threshold detection replaces the fixed z-score cutoff α with z_th = (μ_G − γ_0)/√(γ_0(1−γ_0)) · √|T| + α, where μ_G is the empirical green ratio estimated from anchor data sampled with the same selected queries, thereby compensating for the mismatch between the actual and preset green ratio introduced by small-n watermarks. The resulting query subset and green-ratio estimate are reusable across suspect LLMs as long as the source watermark configuration is fixed, so no re-filtering is needed for new suspects.

## Results

On a challenging model set of 320 suspect LLMs (160 infringing, 160 clean) with cross-domain detection (training on code/math, detecting with general Alpaca queries), LIDet achieves an average accuracy of 92.2% and Detection Successful Rate (DSR) of 87.5%, compared to only 62.5% ACC and 25% DSR for the vanilla baseline. The best configuration (Unigram watermark, Llama-3 source, Mistral suspect) reaches 100% ACC and 100% DSR. In in-domain detection, LIDet achieves 100% DSR for Unigram and over 95% DSR for KGW, while the baseline remains below 78% DSR even in the favorable in-domain setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is vanilla detection, which applies a fixed z-score threshold (α=4.0 for Unigram, α=2.0 for KGW) and samples the suspect LLM with randomly selected queries. LIDet substantially outperforms this baseline across all configurations: average DSR improves from 25% to 87.5%, and average ACC from 62.5% to 92.2%. The gap is most dramatic for Unigram with Llama-3 source (DSR jumps from 20% to 95%) and for KGW with Llama-2 source (DSR from 17.5% to 77.5%). Even in the in-domain detection setting where the baseline has access to matching queries, LIDet still achieves 100% DSR for Unigram versus the baseline's 62.5–77.5%, confirming that the green ratio mismatch problem persists regardless of domain alignment. The ablation further shows that the adaptive threshold is the single most critical component: removing it drops ACC below 60% and DSR below 25%, essentially reverting to baseline-level performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes existing logit-bias text watermarks (KGW and Unigram) for a fundamentally different task: detecting whether a suspect LLM was fine-tuned on watermarked outputs, rather than detecting AI-generated text. It builds directly on the KGW z-score detection framework and the Unigram static green list, but identifies two challenges absent in text watermarking—domain mismatch between training and detection queries, and green ratio instability caused by small n-gram lengths needed for learnability. The work extends the model extraction/infringement detection literature (Tramer et al., 2016; Jia et al., 2021) to the LLM setting and complements Sander et al. (2024), who studied watermark 'radioactivity' but assumed same-domain access, by addressing the more realistic black-box, cross-domain scenario.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that LLM owners can leverage watermarks already embedded in their models for IP protection at essentially no additional cost, achieving >90% detection accuracy in challenging cross-domain settings where vanilla methods fail. However, the evaluation is limited to 7B-parameter models, two watermark schemes, and LoRA fine-tuning, and the authors explicitly exclude robustness against paraphrase attacks, mixed-data tuning, and further training—key adversarial scenarios in practice. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to larger models, more diverse watermark families, and whether a sophisticated stealer could evade detection by mixing watermarked and clean data or applying paraphrasing during fine-tuning.
