## Problem

Temporal difference (TD) learning is the canonical example of a divergent algorithm that nonetheless delivers crucial speed advantages in reinforcement learning, yet the theoretical mechanism behind this speed-up remained unexplained. Gradient descent (GD) is provably convergent but impractically slow on the ill-conditioned value-estimation problems typical of RL, and prior attempts to unify GD and TD (e.g., Baird's interpolation) lacked design principles and in fact diverged. The paper asks: what structural property of the TD update makes it fast, and can that property be transplanted into a convergent method?

## Input / Output

The method operates on state transitions (s, s′) with associated rewards r and a discount rate γ, using a linear value function v(s, θ) parameterized by θ. The TD objective is the squared temporal-difference error δ = v(s,θ) − γv(s′,θ) − r, which for linear function approximation yields a quadratic loss of the form ½‖Qx‖². The GDS-TDM update requires computing both the GD update (−δ·∂θv(s,θ) + γδ·∂θv(s′,θ)) and the TD update (−δ·∂θv(s,θ)) from the same forward pass, then combining them component-wise via sign(u_GD)·|u_TD|, adding only a negligible per-element sign comparison to the standard backpropagation cost.

## Method

The key theoretical insight is that the TD iteration operator H_TD = A + rB contains a skew-symmetric part B (absent in GD's symmetric Hessian), and this skew-symmetric perturbation drives the eigenvalues of H_TD toward each other, specifically increasing the smallest real-part eigenvalue R_min that governs the asymptotic convergence rate. Theorem 2 shows this eigenvalue increase is bounded below by ⟨v₂, Bv₁⟩²/λ₂(A), which grows with the condition number of A, making the speed-up most pronounced exactly where GD is slowest. Building on this, the GDS-TDM method constructs an update u_GDS-TDM = sign(u_GD)·|u_TD|: the sign term guarantees the angle with the convergent GD direction is at most π/2 (preventing divergence), while the magnitude term preserves the skew-symmetric structure responsible for the speed-up. A full 2D convergence proof (Theorem 3) establishes that the iteration converges for sufficiently small learning rates, with convergence rate bounded by 1 − ηG(f, K, ε) in the zigzag regime where G increases with the condition number K of the GD Hessian.

## Results

In Baird's counterexample (condition number 1.3×10⁴), GD stagnates at a loss of ~10⁻⁶ while GDS-TDM continues decreasing to ~10⁻¹⁰, a four-order-of-magnitude improvement. In the 10×10 Grid World (100-parameter linear approximation), TD diverges entirely while both GD and GDS-TDM converge to similar accuracy. In the 3×3 Grid World where TD does converge, GDS-TDM achieves a similar asymptotic convergence speed to TD after a short burn-in phase, confirming the theoretical prediction that the skew-symmetric mechanism preserves TD's speed advantage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GD (Residual Gradient), GDS-TDM consistently wins on convergence speed in ill-conditioned settings: in Baird's counterexample it reaches a loss four orders of magnitude lower (~10⁻¹⁰ vs. ~10⁻⁶), and in the Two-State example its convergence rate is described as 'substantially better.' In the 10×10 Grid World, where the condition number is lower, GDS-TDM and GD behave similarly, suggesting the speed advantage is most pronounced under high ill-conditioning. Against TD, GDS-TDM wins decisively on convergence guarantees: TD diverges in the Two-State example, the 10×10 Grid World, and Baird's counterexample, while GDS-TDM converges in all cases. In the 3×3 Grid World, where TD does converge, GDS-TDM matches TD's asymptotic convergence speed after a brief burn-in phase, validating the theoretical claim that the skew-symmetric mechanism responsible for TD's speed is preserved in GDS-TDM without the divergence risk.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Sutton's temporal difference learning (1988) and Baird's residual gradient (1995), which together established the two update rules that GDS-TDM combines. It departs from the GTD line of work (Sutton et al. 2008, 2009) and Baird's interpolation attempts by identifying the skew-symmetric part of the iteration operator as the mechanism behind TD's speed, rather than treating the GD-TD gap as a simple convex combination problem. The analysis draws on classical iterative scheme theory (Gelfand's formula, perturbation theory for eigenvalues) applied to non-gradient methods, a perspective absent from prior RL optimization literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first theoretical explanation for why TD is fast despite being non-gradient, resolving a puzzle that has persisted since Baird's 1995 divergence result. The key insight—that the skew-symmetric perturbation improves the smallest eigenvalue in a way that is independent of (and actually amplified by) ill-conditioning—offers a design principle for future algorithms that prior intuition-based attempts lacked. However, the rigorous proof is limited to two dimensions, and the 10×10 grid world experiment shows only modest speed-up over GD, suggesting the practical advantage may require more ill-conditioned or higher-dimensional settings to manifest clearly. Open questions include extension to nonlinear function approximation, compatibility with momentum, and whether the theoretical speed-up scales to the condition numbers encountered in deep RL.
