## Problem

Conventional wisdom holds that Rawlsian policies (targeting those most in need) necessarily sacrifice average social welfare compared to utilitarian policies (targeting those with the largest treatment effect). This paper challenges that assumption by showing that in a stochastic dynamic model where welfare decays over time and interventions can prevent decay, Rawlsian policies can outperform utilitarian policies in the long run despite being suboptimal in the short run. The prior theoretical foundation (Radner & Rothschild, 1975) only covered a fully homogeneous case with constant return and decay functions, leaving the comparison open for realistic non-constant dynamics.

## Input / Output

The model takes a population of N individuals with initial welfare levels U_i(0) (drawn from SIPP income data in experiments), per-individual intervention return functions f_i(·) and decay functions g_i(·) (piecewise linear in simulations), and an integer budget M specifying how many individuals receive one unit of intervention per timestep. The output is a per-timestep allocation vector a(t) ∈ {0,1}^N and, in closed form, the asymptotic individual welfare growth rates R_i and the long-term average social welfare R̄_π. The key structural constraint is that welfare evolves stochastically: treated individuals gain f_i(U_i(t)) in expectation while untreated individuals lose g_i(U_i(t)), with Gaussian noise added in simulations.

## Method

The paper extends the Radner–Rothschild attention-allocation model by allowing non-constant return and decay functions that capture a Matthew effect (f_i increasing, g_i decreasing) under a uniform boundedness assumption. The central technical contribution is a generalized Lundberg's inequality for submartingales (Lemma 3), which lower-bounds the probability that a utilitarian policy perpetually fixates on a fixed subset of M high-welfare individuals, causing all others to decay at rate −g⁺. Under a survival condition (a weighted sum of function bounds is positive), a Rawlsian policy lifts every individual's welfare unboundedly, yielding a uniform growth rate R_i = (M/N)f⁺ − ((N−M)/N)g⁻ for all i, which strictly exceeds the utilitarian rate (M/N)f⁺ − ((N−M)/N)g⁺ since g⁻ < g⁺. A complementary ruin condition reverses the comparison, and the results hold across multiple informational contexts (welfare-only vs. full treatment-effect knowledge) and policy variants (min-U, max-g, max-U, max-f, max-fg).

## Results

Under the survival condition, the paper proves that Rawlsian policies (min-U, max-g) achieve long-term social welfare R̄ = (M/N)f⁺ − ((N−M)/N)g⁻, while utilitarian policies (max-U, max-f, max-fg) achieve R̄ = (M/N)f⁺ − ((N−M)/N)g⁺. Since the 'poor-get-poorer' modeling condition implies g⁻ ≤ g⁺, the Rawlsian policy strictly dominates in the long run. Simulations on SIPP data (206 individuals, 13 income bins) confirm a crossover: Rawlsian policies show lower welfare in the first ~200 timesteps but converge to a higher asymptotic growth rate, with the gap widening as budget M increases (Figure 1). Under the complementary ruin condition, the inequality reverses: utilitarian policies achieve R̄ = (M/N)f⁺ − ((N−M)/N)g⁺ > (M/N)f⁻ − ((N−M)/N)g⁺ = R̄_Rawlsian.

**Table: Long-term social welfare (closed-form) under survival and ruin conditions, with uniform boundedness.**

| Policy | Survival Condition | Ruin Condition |
|---|---|---|
| min-U (Rawlsian) | (M/N)f⁺ − ((N−M)/N)g⁻ | (M/N)f⁻ − ((N−M)/N)g⁺ < 0 |
| max-g (Rawlsian) | (M/N)f⁺ − ((N−M)/N)g⁻ | (M/N)f⁻ − ((N−M)/N)g⁺ < 0 |
| max-U (Utilitarian) | (M/N)f⁺ − ((N−M)/N)g⁺ | (M/N)f⁺ − ((N−M)/N)g⁺ |
| max-f (Utilitarian) | (M/N)f⁺ − ((N−M)/N)g⁺ | (M/N)f⁺ − ((N−M)/N)g⁺ |
| max-fg (Utilitarian) | (M/N)f⁺ − ((N−M)/N)g⁺ | (M/N)f⁺ − ((N−M)/N)g⁺ |
| Random | (M/N)f⁺ − ((N−M)/N)g⁻ | — |

*Key: f⁺ ≥ f⁻ (rich-get-richer), g⁺ ≥ g⁻ (poor-get-poorer). Under survival, Rawlsian ≥ Utilitarian since g⁻ ≤ g⁺. Under ruin, Utilitarian ≥ Rawlsian since f⁺ ≥ f⁻.*

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three families of policies: Rawlsian (min-U, max-g), utilitarian (max-U, max-f, max-fg), and a random allocation baseline. Under the survival condition, all Rawlsian variants achieve strictly higher long-term social welfare than all utilitarian variants, with the gap equal to ((N−M)/N)(g⁺ − g⁻) > 0. The random policy matches the Rawlsian policy asymptotically (both achieve (M/N)f⁺ − ((N−M)/N)g⁻), though with slower finite-time convergence. Under the ruin condition, the comparison fully reverses: utilitarian policies achieve (M/N)f⁺ − ((N−M)/N)g⁺ while Rawlsian policies achieve (M/N)f⁻ − ((N−M)/N)g⁺ < 0, with the gap equal to (M/N)(f⁺ − f⁻) > 0. Notably, the result is robust to the informational context: whether the planner uses only welfare levels (min-U vs. max-U) or full treatment-effect information (max-g vs. max-fg), the same policy ordering holds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the Radner & Rothschild (1975) attention allocation model from a fully homogeneous setting (constant return and decay functions) to non-constant functions governed by monotonicity assumptions capturing the Matthew effect. The proof technique departs from the original by introducing a novel generalized Lundberg's inequality for submartingales to characterize utilitarian policy fixation behavior. The paper also re-purposes the industrial project management framework into social welfare policy design, comparing multiple policy families (Rawlsian min-U/max-g vs. utilitarian max-U/max-f/max-fg) under different informational contexts, extending beyond the two-policy comparison in the original work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result challenges the conventional assumption that Rawlsian policies necessarily sacrifice average welfare, providing closed-form survival and ruin conditions under which the policy ranking reverses. This has direct implications for real-world welfare program design (e.g., Austria's 2019–2020 shift from need-based to labor-market-integration-based targeting). Key limitations include the uniform boundedness assumption, the requirement that f_i + g_i be increasing, and the unexplored middle ground between survival and ruin conditions. Future work could combine effective estimation of return/decay functions from pilot experiments with the long-term policy comparison framework, and extend to heterogeneous bounds and non-monotonic treatment effects.
