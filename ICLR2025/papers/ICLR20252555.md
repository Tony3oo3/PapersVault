## Problem

Existing SMILES language models rely on masked language modeling (MLM) at the single-token level, which leads to rapid pre-training saturation (90%+ accuracy within 5K steps) and fails to capture chemically meaningful substructure semantics such as functional groups. Additionally, MLMs are trained exclusively on corrupted inputs containing [MASK] tokens that never appear in real SMILES, creating a train-inference mismatch that limits the model's ability to process valid molecular sequences.

## Input / Output

During pre-training, the model receives valid SMILES strings from which chemically meaningful fragments (identified via modified BRICS/RMCF fragmentation) have been randomly dropped at a 15% ratio, producing a structurally incomplete but syntactically valid SMILES. The model outputs three types of editing operations per token position: a binary deletion decision, a 256-class count of placeholders to insert after each token, and a vocabulary-sized (369-token) token prediction for each inserted placeholder. For downstream tasks, the encoder's pooled representation feeds a classification or regression head to predict molecular properties (ROC-AUC for classification, MAE for regression).

## Method

SMI-EDITOR adapts the Levenshtein Transformer's three-step editing framework (token deletion, placeholder insertion, token prediction) to SMILES pre-training, but introduces two key innovations. First, fragment-level supervision replaces token-level masking: molecules are fragmented using a modified BRICS algorithm (RMCF-based, splitting ring-side-chain connections), and a subset of fragments is randomly discarded, forcing the model to reconstruct chemically coherent substructures rather than individual atoms. Second, a dual deletion loss is added on top of LevT's original deletion objective: while LevT's deletion only corrects the model's own insertion errors, the dual deletion loss explicitly supervises the model to remove erroneous tokens present in the corrupted input SMILES, providing richer training signals for every token position. The total loss combines the dual deletion loss with LevT's standard insertion, token prediction, and deletion losses, and the encoder-only Transformer (86.3M parameters, 12 layers) is fine-tuned with a classification/regression head for downstream molecular property prediction.

## Results

On the MoleculeNet benchmark (scaffold split, ROC-AUC), SMI-EDITOR achieves a mean score of 77.8 across 7 classification tasks, setting new state-of-the-art on BBBP (77.4), Tox21 (77.1), MUV (80.2), and ClinTox (98.9). It outperforms the closest 3D molecular model (MoleculeSDE, mean 74.5) by 3.3 points and the same-architecture MLM baseline (SMI-MLM, mean 73.2) by 4.6 points. On regression tasks, SMI-EDITOR achieves ESOL MAE of 0.362, FreeSolv MAE of 0.524, and Lipo MAE of 0.565, all best among compared methods. On retrosynthesis (USPTO-50K), the encoder-decoder variant SMI-EDITOR-Gen reaches 61.2% Top-1 exact match, marginally surpassing EditRetro (60.8%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SMI-EDITOR (mean 77.8) outperforms all 12 baselines on the 7-task MoleculeNet classification benchmark. Against the strongest 2D pre-trained baselines, it beats MolCLR (75.5) by 2.3 points and Mole-BERT (73.4) by 4.4 points. Against 3D molecular models, it surpasses MoleculeSDE (74.5) by 3.3 points and 3D InfoMax (71.8) by 6.0 points, demonstrating that SMILES-based edit pre-training can capture spatially relevant information without explicit 3D coordinates. The controlled comparison with SMI-MLM (same architecture, data, and hyperparameters; MLM objective) shows a 4.6-point mean gap, isolating the contribution of the edit-based + fragment-level objective. On retrosynthesis (USPTO-50K), SMI-EDITOR-Gen edges out EditRetro on Top-1 (61.2% vs. 60.8%) and Top-3 (80.9% vs. 80.6%) but trails slightly on Top-10 (89.7% vs. 90.3%), indicating competitive but not dominant generative performance. The paper also shows that increasing MLM training steps to match SMI-EDITOR's compute budget (SMI-MLM(More), 360K steps) actually degrades performance to 65.1 mean, confirming that the gap is not a compute artifact but stems from the training objective itself.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the Levenshtein Transformer (LevT) edit-based generation framework from NLP to molecular SMILES pre-training, combining it with a modified RMCF fragmentation strategy to introduce fragment-level supervision. It departs from the dominant MLM-based SMILES LM paradigm (SMILES-BERT, ChemBERTa, Molformer) by replacing token-level [MASK] prediction with a three-step editing process (deletion, placeholder insertion, token prediction) over valid SMILES inputs, and by adding a dual deletion loss that supervises removal of erroneous tokens from the corrupted input. The key conceptual shift is treating SMILES pre-training as a sequence-editing problem over chemically meaningful fragments rather than a token-completion problem over arbitrary masked positions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a systematic diagnosis of why MLM is fundamentally ill-suited for SMILES (rapid saturation, inability to capture substructure semantics, train-inference mismatch) and demonstrates that an edit-based, fragment-level alternative resolves all three issues while achieving SOTA on MoleculeNet and outperforming several 3D molecular models. A notable negative result is that simply increasing MLM training compute (3× more steps) degrades performance, suggesting the bottleneck is task design rather than capacity. Open questions include whether the ~3× training cost is acceptable at scale (100M+ compounds), whether the approach transfers to other molecular representations (e.g., SELFIES, InChI), and whether the encoder-only architecture limits generative applications compared to autoregressive alternatives.
