## Problem

Designing reward functions for RL tasks with tacit human knowledge (e.g., 'good' driving, natural locomotion) is non-trivial because the relevant criteria are subjective and hard to quantify explicitly. Prior LLM-based reward design methods like Eureka use greedy iterative search—mutating only the best candidate and discarding the rest—which risks premature convergence and wastes computational resources, while also requiring hand-crafted fitness functions that are as hard to design as the reward itself.

## Input / Output

The method takes a natural language task description and abstracted environment variables (formatted as a Python class exposing observation/action attributes) as initial inputs to GPT-4. During evolution, human evaluators watch 30–40 second video rollouts in pairs and select their preferred segment, with additional checkbox-based natural language feedback on specific behavioral aspects; these preferences are converted to Elo fitness scores (K=32, initial rating 1500). The output is an interpretable reward function as executable Python code of the form r = Σ rᵢ (where GPT-4 determines the number and functional form of components), along with the trained RL policy π* that maximizes the human-assigned fitness.

## Method

REvolve frames reward design as a search problem solved via genetic programming with an island model (13 sub-populations), using GPT-4 as the intelligent genetic operator that generates and modifies Python reward functions. Each generation, 16 individuals are produced via mutation (GPT-4 modifies a single reward component of a parent) or crossover (GPT-4 combines the best-performing components from two parents), guided by the parent's natural language feedback λ and tracked component statistics. Selection retains only individuals whose fitness σ meets or exceeds their sub-population's average, ensuring monotonically increasing population quality; periodic migration between islands preserves genetic diversity. Human pairwise preferences on policy rollouts are mapped to fitness scores via an Elo rating system, eliminating the need for a separate reward model (unlike RLHF). The key distinction from Eureka is that REvolve maintains the full population and applies the complete set of genetic operators (mutation, crossover, selection, migration) rather than greedily mutating only the best candidate, avoiding premature convergence at identical computational cost.

## Results

REvolve (with human feedback) achieves the highest Elo score across all three environments: 1575 in Autonomous Driving (second only to human driving at 1586), 1586 in Humanoid Locomotion, and 1594 in Adroit Hand Manipulation. REvolve consistently outperforms Eureka by 76 Elo points in Autonomous Driving (1575 vs. 1499), 29 points in Humanoid Locomotion (1586 vs. 1557), and 72 points in Adroit Hand (1594 vs. 1522). All LLM-based methods (REvolve, REvolve Auto, Eureka, Eureka Auto) outperform the Expert Designed baseline in every environment, with margins ranging from 118 to 187 Elo points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

REvolve outperforms Eureka (Ma et al., 2024a) in all three environments on human-preference Elo scores, with the largest margin in Autonomous Driving (1575 vs. 1499, +76 points) and Adroit Hand (1594 vs. 1522, +72 points), and a smaller but consistent lead in Humanoid Locomotion (1586 vs. 1557, +29 points). Both REvolve and Eureka with human feedback outperform their automated-feedback counterparts (REvolve Auto, Eureka Auto), confirming that human feedback is critical for reward refinement. Against the Expert Designed baseline (Spryn et al., 2018 for driving; Gymnasium defaults for MuJoCo tasks), REvolve wins decisively in every environment, achieving 178 Elo points higher in Autonomous Driving (1575 vs. 1397), 179 in Humanoid Locomotion (1586 vs. 1407), and 179 in Adroit Hand (1594 vs. 1415). In generalization to unseen AirSim environments (Table 3), REvolve also outperforms both Eureka and Expert Designed, though performance drops in the more challenging Env2 (fitness 0.68 vs. 0.75 for human driving), attributed to temporal processing limitations of the visual module.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-based reward design line initiated by Text2Reward (Xie et al., 2024) and Eureka (Ma et al., 2024a), which both use GPT-4 to generate Python reward functions but rely on greedy iterative search (mutating only the best candidate). REvolve departs from this by replacing the greedy loop with a full evolutionary algorithm—population-based search with mutation, crossover, selection, and migration under an island model (Cantu-Paz et al., 1998)—while using LLMs as intelligent genetic operators rather than handcrafted perturbation rules. It also generalizes the fitness signal from Eureka's automated metrics to human pairwise preferences (Elo-scored) combined with natural language feedback, drawing on the RLHF literature (Christiano et al., 2017) but avoiding the need to train a separate reward model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

REvolve offers a practical path to reward design for safety-critical, tacit-knowledge tasks (autonomous driving, humanoid locomotion, dexterous manipulation) where expert hand-crafted rewards are brittle and RLHF-style black-box reward models are opaque. The interpretable Python-code output and minimal human data requirement (~1,400 preference samples vs. hundreds of thousands for RLHF) make it attractive for deployment in regulated domains. Key open questions include real-world (Sim2Real) transfer, dependence on closed-source GPT-4, and whether the evolutionary advantage over greedy search persists as open-source LLMs close the reward-design gap. The limited evidence (two seeds, simulation-only) and the observation that automated feedback nearly matches human feedback on simpler MuJoCo tasks temper the generality of the human-feedback benefit.
