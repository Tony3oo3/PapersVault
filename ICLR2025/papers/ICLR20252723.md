## Problem

Existing motion-controlled video generation methods require either training to encode specific motion cues or fine-tuning to inject motion patterns, limiting flexibility and generalization across scenarios. Dense pixel-level cues (depth, sketch) become entangled with reference video structure, while trajectory-based methods miss fine local motions like head turns. The paper seeks a training-free mechanism to clone motion from arbitrary reference videos into new text- or image-conditioned generation without domain-specific adaptation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252723/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a reference video (processed in latent space via a single noise-adding and denoising step at t_α=400 with a null-text prompt to extract the motion representation), a text prompt describing the desired content, and optionally a first-frame image or sketch for I2V/sketch-to-video tasks. The output is a generated video whose motion pattern is cloned from the reference while appearance follows the new prompt. Because motion representation is extracted in latent space, the effective spatial resolution of the attention maps is significantly lower than the input video, which limits handling of very fine local motions such as winking.

## Method

MotionClone exploits the observation that dominant components in temporal attention maps drive motion synthesis while the remainder captures noise or negligible motion. It constructs a sparse motion representation by selecting the top-k (default k=1) attention weights along the temporal axis for each spatial position, forming a binary mask that isolates primary motion cues. This representation is extracted from the reference video via a single denoising step (no full DDIM inversion), and during generation it is injected as an energy-function gradient that forces the generated video's temporal attention to align with the reference's sparse attention pattern. The guidance is applied only in early denoising steps (e.g., first 180 of 300 steps) within the 'up_block.1' temporal attention layers, preserving later-step flexibility for semantic and appearance adjustment. The entire pipeline is training-free and plug-and-play, compatible with both U-Net-based (AnimateDiff, SparseCtrl) and DiT-based (CogVideoX) video diffusion architectures.

## Results

On 40 real videos from DAVIS and web sources, MotionClone achieves the best scores across all six evaluation metrics. In automatic metrics, it reaches 0.3187 CLIP textual alignment and 0.9621 temporal consistency, edging out VMC (0.3134 / 0.9614). In the user study (1–5 scale), MotionClone scores 3.69 on motion preservation, 4.31 on appearance diversity, 4.15 on textual alignment, and 4.28 on temporal consistency, substantially outperforming all baselines on the subjective criteria.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MotionClone outperforms all five baselines in the main quantitative table (Table 1) on every metric. Against VMC (the closest competitor on automatic metrics), MotionClone leads by 0.0053 in CLIP textual alignment and 0.0007 in temporal consistency, but the gap widens dramatically in the user study: motion preservation 3.69 vs 2.59 and appearance diversity 4.31 vs 3.51. Tune-A-Video and Control-A-Video trail further behind, with Tune-A-Video scoring only 2.44 on motion preservation and 2.28 on user-study temporal consistency. Gen-1 and VideoComposer, which rely on dense structural cues (depth/canny maps), produce outputs entangled with the reference video's appearance, yielding the lowest textual alignment scores (0.2462 and 0.2854 respectively). In the appendix qualitative comparison, MotionClone also surpasses four additional training-free or inversion-based methods—MOFT, Space-Time Diffusion Features, UniEdit, and MotionMaster—on both camera-motion and object-motion cloning, while notably avoiding the time-consuming DDIM inversion that all four of those methods require.

## Relation to Prior Work

MotionClone builds on AnimateDiff's temporal attention mechanism and SparseCtrl's sparse control framework, but departs fundamentally from prior motion-control approaches that rely on dense pixel-level cues (depth, canny maps in VideoComposer/Gen-1) or trajectory-based signals (DragNUWA, MotionCtrl), which either entangle motion with structure or miss fine-grained local actions. It also diverges from fine-tuning-based methods (VMC, Tune-A-Video) that require training to encode motion patterns, instead repurposing the temporal attention weights already present in pretrained video diffusion models as an explicit, training-free motion representation. The conceptual link to attention-based spatial control in image generation (Prompt2Prompt, FreeControl) is extended here to the temporal axis, where the key insight is that only the sparse top-k components of temporal attention carry meaningful motion signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The training-free, single-denoising-step extraction of motion representations offers a practical, plug-and-play solution for motion customization that avoids the training cost and domain limitation of fine-tuning methods, making it broadly applicable across T2V, I2V, and sketch-to-video tasks. However, the method struggles with subtle local motions (e.g., winking) due to latent-space resolution limits and degrades when multiple objects overlap, leaving open the question of how to handle fine-grained or coupled motions. The observation that a single mid-denoising step (t_α=400) suffices for the entire generation trajectory is practically significant but has been validated primarily on AnimateDiff-based models, with only preliminary results on CogVideoX, so generalizability across architectures remains to be established.
