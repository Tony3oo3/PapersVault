## Problem

Frontier models have achieved impressive performance in mathematics, coding, and commonsense reasoning, leading to characterizations of them as possessing broad cognitive competence. However, spatial cognition—a foundational form of intelligence present across the animal kingdom and considered a prerequisite for higher cognition in biological systems—has not been systematically evaluated in these models. The paper asks whether spatial cognition emerges in disembodied frontier models trained on text, images, and video, and whether competence in some cognitive domains implies competence in others.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253648/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For large-scale tasks, models receive a video walkthrough of a 3D environment (512×512 ego frames, 61–240 frames; or 512×512 discrete map frames, 13–72 frames; or a text array encoding the same map) and must produce multiple-choice answers (direction, distance, map sketch) or sequential navigation actions scored by SPL. For small-scale tasks, inputs range from 3D shape renderings (595×541 to 1133×1432), object arrangement images (640×480), water container images, puzzle piece images, line orientation images, and grids/mazes (up to 3100×3100), each with a parallel text-only version using 2D character arrays where every cell is a distinct tokenizer token. Outputs are four-way multiple-choice selections or interactive action sequences (up/down/left/right/stop), with performance measured as accuracy or SPL.

## Method

SPACE comprises 15 tasks drawn from the cognitive science literature: 5 large-scale tasks (direction estimation, distance estimation, map sketching, route retracing, shortcut discovery) built on 10 environment layouts (30 environments total) simulated in Habitat with Trimesh-generated 3D scenes, and 10 small-scale tasks (mental rotation, perspective taking, water level, Minnesota Paper Form Board, judgement of line orientation, selective attention, maze completion, Corsi block-tapping, spatial addition, Cambridge spatial working memory). Each task is instantiated in both a multimodal (image/video) and a purely textual (2D character array) presentation, enabling evaluation of both VLMs and LLMs under matched conditions. The text encodings are carefully designed so that each array element maps to a distinct token across all evaluated model tokenizers. Models are evaluated zero-shot with detailed task-description prompts, and the benchmark includes chance baselines and human performance (29 participants) for calibration. The key empirical finding is that frontier models perform near chance on most spatial tasks—especially large-scale navigation and 3D mental rotation—while showing comparatively stronger performance on text-based selective attention and visuospatial working memory tasks.

## Results

On large-scale spatial cognition (DM text), the best model (Claude 3.5 Sonnet) achieves only 33.5% average accuracy versus 66.7–76.5% for humans and 15.0% for chance; on egocentric image inputs, all models are near chance (GPT-4o: 23.0% avg). On small-scale spatial cognition with text-only inputs, GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet reach 65.2% and 64.5% average accuracy (chance: 23.1%), but with multimodal inputs these drop to 40.1% and 43.8% respectively. Notably, top models exceed 95% on selective attention (SAtt), matching human performance, yet remain near chance on mental rotation (~30–42%), perspective taking (~22–56%), and maze completion (<22%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

All evaluated frontier models fall far short of human performance on spatial cognition, with the gap most dramatic on large-scale tasks where even the best model (Claude 3.5 Sonnet, 33.5% avg on DM text) achieves less than half of human accuracy (66.7–76.5%). Among LLMs on text-only small-scale tasks, GPT-4o (65.2%) and Claude 3.5 Sonnet (64.5%) lead, followed by Mistral 123B (60.5%) and GPT-4v (57.3%), while open models like Llama 3 70B (40.0%) and Mixtral 8x22B (37.0%) trail significantly. On multimodal small-scale tasks, Claude 3.5 Sonnet (43.8%) edges out GPT-4o (40.1%), with GPT-4v (30.8%) and open VLMs (Pixtral 12B: 25.4%, Phi-3.5-vision: 22.0%, LLaVA-NeXT-Interleave: 20.9%) performing near or at chance. A striking asymmetry emerges: top models match or exceed human performance on selective attention (SAtt: 95–99%) and visuospatial working memory (CBTT: 82–98%, SAdd: 79–94%), yet remain near chance on mental rotation, perspective taking, MPFB, and maze completion. Smaller models (Yi 1.5 9B, Llama 3 8B, Mixtral 8x7B) perform at or below chance on most tasks, indicating that spatial cognition does not scale smoothly with model size in the same way as other capabilities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts decades of classic cognitive science experiments—Tolman's cognitive mapping paradigms, Vandenberg & Kuse's mental rotation test, Piaget's water level task, the Minnesota Paper Form Board, Corsi block-tapping, and the Cambridge spatial working memory test—into a unified computational benchmark for frontier models. It extends prior spatial-reasoning evaluations of LLMs (PlanBench, CogEval, EWOK) and VLMs (SpatialVLM, SpatialRGPT, MOCHI), which focused on narrower aspects like planning or relative spatial arrangements, by systematically covering both large-scale (cognitive mapping, navigation) and small-scale (mental rotation, perspective taking, spatial attention, working memory) spatial cognition with parallel text and multimodal presentations. The 3D environment generation leverages the Habitat simulator, and the task taxonomy follows the large-scale/small-scale distinction established in the cognitive science literature (Hegarty et al., 2006; Newcombe, 2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that frontier models performing at olympiad level in mathematics and coding score near chance on spatial tasks solvable by mice and rats—challenges the widely held assumption that competence in some cognitive domains implies broad cognitive competence. This has direct implications for how we interpret claims of 'superintelligence' and for understanding whether spatial cognition is a prerequisite for higher-level reasoning in artificial systems. Open questions include whether embodiment or physical interaction is necessary for spatial cognition to emerge, whether the text-only performance gap (64–65% vs. 40–44% for top models) reflects a fundamental visual-processing limitation or a training-data issue, and whether improving spatial cognition would unlock better performance on higher-level tasks. The benchmark itself provides a systematic, reproducible evaluation protocol that the field can use to track progress.
