## Problem

Unsupervised graph-level anomaly detection (GLAD) and graph-level out-of-distribution detection (GLOD) share the same fundamental objective—identifying graphs that deviate from a reference distribution—yet have been developed and evaluated in isolation with incompatible experimental protocols. This fragmentation prevents fair cross-comparison of methods and hinders the transfer of techniques between the two communities. The paper addresses this gap by proposing a unified evaluation framework that treats both tasks as instances of a single 'generalized graph-level OOD detection' problem.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253309/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each method receives a set of unlabeled in-distribution (ID) graphs as training input and produces a continuous anomaly/OOD score for each test graph, which is thresholded into a binary ID vs. OOD decision. The benchmark spans 35 datasets across four scenario types—intrinsic anomaly (Tox21), class-based anomaly (TU benchmark), inter-dataset shift (paired molecular/social/protein datasets), and intra-dataset shift (GOOD and DrugOOD with size/scaffold/assay splits)—covering molecules, social networks, and proteins. Evaluation uses AUROC, AUPRC, and FPR95, supplemented by analyses on near-OOD vs. far-OOD sensitivity, robustness to 0–30% OOD contamination in the training set, and wall-clock time and memory efficiency.

## Method

UB-GOLD unifies GLAD and GLOD under a single scoring-function formulation whose objective is to maximize the separation between ID and OOD/anomalous score distributions, then benchmarks 18 representative methods organized into four categories: two-step graph-kernel-plus-detector (PK/WL with iForest/OCSVM), two-step self-supervised-learning-plus-detector (GraphCL/InfoGraph with iForest/OCSVM), end-to-end GNN-based GLAD (OCGIN, GLocalKD, OCGTL, SIGNET, GLADC, CVTGAD), and end-to-end GNN-based GLOD (GOOD-D, GraphDE, AAGOD, GOODAT). All methods are evaluated under a unified unsupervised protocol with random hyperparameter search (20 trials or 1 day per method-dataset pair) and no validation set. Beyond standard effectiveness comparison, the benchmark introduces three additional evaluation axes: an OOD sensitivity spectrum measuring performance on near-OOD (same-dataset different class, or smaller graph size) versus far-OOD (different dataset, or larger size) samples; a robustness analysis injecting 0–30% OOD samples into the ID training set; and an efficiency analysis tracking wall-clock time and CPU/GPU memory. Key findings include that no single method dominates all 35 datasets, near-OOD samples are substantially harder to detect than far-OOD ones, most methods degrade under training-set contamination, and end-to-end GNN methods generally outperform two-step pipelines in both accuracy and computational cost.

## Results

Across 35 datasets and 18 methods, SIGNET achieves the best average AUROC of 75.86 and average rank of 3.37, followed by CVTGAD (71.07 AUROC, rank 3.69) and OCGTL (73.14 AUROC, rank 5.43). Among GLOD-specific methods, GOOD-D leads with 71.05 average AUROC and rank 4.14, while GraphDE and AAGOD trail at ranks 9.89 and 9.43 respectively. All four two-step method groups (GK-D and SSL-D) have average ranks above 10, confirming that end-to-end GNN-based approaches consistently outperform kernel/SSL + detector pipelines. No single method dominates more than 12 of 35 datasets, and near-OOD detection is substantially harder than far-OOD detection across all methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark compares 18 methods across four architectural families. Among GNN-based GLAD methods, SIGNET (75.86 avg AUROC) and CVTGAD (71.07) are the strongest, with OCGTL (73.14) close behind; these three consistently rank in the top 5 across diverse dataset types. Among GNN-based GLOD methods, GOOD-D (71.05) clearly outperforms GraphDE (60.38), AAGOD (61.54), and GOODAT (61.91), and notably achieves a rank of 4.14 that rivals the top GLAD methods, demonstrating strong cross-task transferability. All two-step methods (graph kernel + detector and SSL + detector) are uniformly outperformed by end-to-end methods, with the best two-step method (GCL-iF, 61.50 AUROC, rank 10.63) still trailing the worst end-to-end method (OCGIN, 66.00, rank 7.26). No method achieves the best AUROC on more than 12 of 35 datasets, and performance is highly scenario-dependent: e.g., OCGTL excels on HIV-Size (96.34) but struggles on HIV-Scaffold (58.05), while SIGNET leads on AIDS (97.60) but underperforms on HSE (64.56 vs. OCGIN's 71.42). In the near-OOD vs. far-OOD analysis, all methods show a consistent AUROC gap of 5–20 points favoring far-OOD detection, with GLOD methods (GOOD-D, GraphDE) exhibiting the largest near/far gaps.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not build on a single prior method but rather unifies two previously independent research threads—unsupervised graph-level anomaly detection (GLAD) and unsupervised graph-level out-of-distribution detection (GLOD)—under a single 'generalized graph-level OOD detection' formulation, directly inspired by the OpenOOD benchmark and the generalized OOD detection framework from computer vision (Yang et al., 2021; 2022). The individual methods evaluated (SIGNET, CVTGAD, OCGTL, GOOD-D, GraphDE, etc.) are all existing contributions from their respective communities; the paper's conceptual contribution is showing that these two task families share an identical scoring objective and can be evaluated under a common protocol. It departs from prior work by eliminating the artificial separation between 'anomaly' and 'OOD' evaluation setups, which had prevented cross-community method transfer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides the first systematic evidence that GLAD and GLOD methods are largely interchangeable, which should encourage cross-pollination between the two communities and reduce redundant method development. Key open questions identified include: (1) no universal method exists across 35 diverse datasets, (2) near-OOD detection remains a significant weakness for all methods, and (3) most methods are fragile to even 10–30% training-set contamination—challenges that are especially acute in real-world deployment where clean ID data is unrealistic. The open-source codebase lowers the barrier for future work, but the benchmark's focus on unsupervised settings excludes supervised and domain-specific pre-trained approaches, limiting its scope.
