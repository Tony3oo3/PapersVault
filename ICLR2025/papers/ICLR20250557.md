## Problem

LLMs deployed for narrow-domain tasks (e.g., medical chatbots, customer support) are vulnerable to adversarial jailbreaks that elicit out-of-domain responses, yet existing guardrails (alignment, RLHF, filtering) offer only empirical protection that can be circumvented. No prior method provides a provable, mathematical upper bound on the probability that a specialized LLM generates forbidden content under arbitrary adversarial prompting. This paper introduces domain certification as a formal framework to close this gap.

## Input / Output

The system takes a text prompt x, a capable general LLM L (e.g., Gemma-2-2b, Llama-3-8B), and a small domain-specific guide model G (a GPT-2 of 33M–184M parameters trained exclusively on in-domain data). It outputs either an accepted text response y or an abstention signal after up to T rejection-sampling iterations. The key quantitative output is the adversarial certificate: for every possible input x, the probability of generating any forbidden response y is bounded by 2^(kN_y)·T·G(y), where k is the rejection threshold and N_y is the response length. The length-normalized log-likelihood ratio log(L(y|x)/G(y)) serves as the acceptance statistic, making the decision invariant to sequence length.

## Method

VALID (Verified Adversarial LLM Output via Iterative Dismissal) constructs a certified meta-model M via rejection sampling: L proposes a candidate response y, which is accepted only if the length-normalized log-likelihood ratio log(L(y|x)/G(y)) ≤ k·N_y, repeated up to T times before abstaining. The central theoretical result (Theorem 1) proves that M(y|x) ≤ 2^(kN_y)·T·G(y) for all x ∈ S, yielding an atomic certificate per sample and a domain certificate as the maximum over the forbidden set. Length normalization is essential because unnormalized log-likelihood ratios grow linearly with sequence length, so the decision boundary must scale accordingly; equivalently, the threshold bounds the ratio of geometrically normalized per-token likelihoods. The guide model G is deliberately small and domain-restricted, assigning exponentially decreasing marginal likelihood to out-of-domain samples, which makes the certificate tight (e.g., <10⁻¹⁰) for forbidden responses while remaining permissive for in-domain ones. The framework is connected to Rényi divergence of order infinity, showing that bounding Δ_∞(L(y|x)‖Ω(y)) ≤ k for a domain oracle Ω is equivalent to the atomic and domain certificates.

## Results

Across all three experimental setups (Shakespeare, CS News, MedicalQA), 95% of out-of-domain samples receive an atomic certificate (εy-AC) below 1×10⁻¹⁰, meaning their generation probability is provably bounded for any adversarial prompt. For MedicalQA, the median constriction ratio (CR) between the unconstrained model L and the certified model M is 24.23 (i.e., OOD samples are ~10²⁴× less likely under M), with 99% of samples exceeding a CR of 10⁶·³⁰. On the PubMedQA ε benchmark, the unconstrained Llama-3-8B scores 73.4%, while VALID-certified M retains 66.7% at ε=10⁻⁵ (−6.7%) and 47.7% at ε=10⁻¹⁰ (−25.7%), demonstrating a tight certificate with modest performance cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare VALID against other guardrail or OOD-detection methods in a head-to-head benchmark; instead, it compares the certified model M against two internal references. First, M is compared to the unconstrained LLM L: the constriction ratio CR = εy(L)/εy(M) quantifies how much tighter M's certificate is, with median values of ~10²⁴ for MedicalQA and even stronger for TinyShakespeare and 20NG, showing M is dramatically more restrictive on OOD samples while remaining permissive on in-domain ones. Second, M is compared to the guide model G alone: an ablation (Appendix G) confirms that G, while highly robust against OOD outputs, performs significantly worse on the target task than both L and M, validating that VALID's rejection-sampling design preserves L's quality while inheriting G's safety guarantees. The only existing 'baseline' certificate for a general LLM is the vacuous 1-DC bound, which VALID improves upon by orders of magnitude.

## Relation to Prior Work

VALID directly adapts the rejection-sampling-with-a-guide-model framework from Vyas et al. (2023) on provable copyright protection for generative models, repurposing the length-normalized likelihood ratio test as a certifiable guardrail for LLM domain restriction. Conceptually, it bridges two previously separate literatures: OOD detection (where likelihood ratio tests are a standard discriminant) and adversarial certification (established in vision but largely absent for LLM generation). The paper departs from existing LLM guardrails (RLHF, DPO, Llama Guard) by replacing empirical safety claims with a formal certificate — an adversarial upper bound on the probability of out-of-domain outputs — and from prior NLP certification work (Casadio et al., 2024; Kumar et al., 2024) by providing a bound that holds for all inputs rather than perturbations around a specific one.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first formal framework providing provable, input-independent guarantees that an LLM will not produce out-of-domain content under adversarial prompting, addressing a gap that existing alignment-based guardrails leave open (they are empirically vulnerable to jailbreaks). The practical finding that a small, domain-specific GPT-2 (33M–184M params) can certify a much larger LLM (2B–8B) with only ~15% false rejection at ε=10⁻⁵ makes the approach deployable in resource-constrained settings. Key limitations remain: the guide model G operates on marginal likelihood (no conditioning on x), so contextually in-domain but marginally out-of-domain responses may be incorrectly accepted; the certificate tightness depends heavily on G's ability to separate T from F; and the evaluation covers only three relatively narrow domains. Open questions include whether larger specialized G models, learned length-dependent thresholds, or probabilistic rejection rules can tighten certificates while reducing refusal rates.
