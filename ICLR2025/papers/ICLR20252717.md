## Problem

Memorization in language models is typically treated as a homogeneous phenomenon, yet different motivations—copyright, privacy, and scientific understanding of generalization—imply distinct and sometimes contradictory notions of what counts as memorization. The paper asks whether memorized sequences can be decomposed into meaningful subcategories that differ in their underlying mechanisms, and whether such a decomposition improves both interpretability and prediction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252717/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on 64-token sequences (32-token prompt + 32-token continuation) drawn from The Pile, using k=32 extractable memorization as ground truth from Pythia models. Features span three levels: corpus statistics (exact duplicate count, SBERT semantic match count, Levenshtein textual match count, token frequency percentiles), sequence-intrinsic properties (Huffman coding length, handcrafted repeating/incrementing template heuristics), and model perplexity computed over prompt, continuation, and full sequence. The output is a taxonomic label (recitation, reconstruction, or recollection) plus a memorization probability produced by category-specific logistic regressions.

## Method

The core contribution is a three-way taxonomy inspired by colloquial human memorization: recitation (sequences with ≥6 exact corpus duplicates), reconstruction (sequences matching repeating or incrementing template heuristics), and recollection (all remaining memorized sequences). The taxonomy is validated through three complementary analyses: scaling experiments across Pythia sizes (70M–12B) and training checkpoints showing recollection grows fastest with scale and time; statistical dependency tests revealing category-specific feature interactions (e.g., low perplexity enables recitation while rare tokens constrain recollection); and a predictive model where three L2-regularized binary logistic regressions—one per category—outperform both a homogeneous baseline and an optimally-searched partition over feature-threshold combinations. The predictive model is trained on the full memorized sample combined with a 3% representative Pile subset, using balanced class weights and zero-mean unit-variance feature normalization.

## Results

Across Pythia models from 70M to 12B parameters, total memorized sequences increase monotonically, with recollection growing disproportionately from 4.49% to 11.34% of memorized samples, while recitation remains dominant at ~84–88% and reconstruction stays flat at ~3–7%. Over training time for the 12B model, recollection's share peaks at 13.32% (86% checkpoint) before a late-training reconstruction surge. For predictive modeling, the intuitive taxonomic model (three category-specific logistic regressions) outperforms both a single-regression baseline and a data-driven optimal partition on calibration and accuracy, except on the recollection subset where it exhibits low precision.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three predictive architectures for memorization likelihood: (1) a generic single logistic regression baseline with no taxonomy, (2) the proposed intuitive taxonomic model with three category-specific regressions (recitation/reconstruction/recollection), and (3) an optimally partitioned model that searches over feature-threshold splits (ultimately selecting Huffman coding length + duplicate count) to define three groups. The intuitive taxonomy is better calibrated and more accurate than both alternatives on most evaluation metrics, while the optimal partition only slightly outperforms the aggregate baseline. The one exception is the recollection subset, where the intuitive model has low precision, suggesting its heuristic boundary for 'rare, non-templated' sequences is imperfect. The authors also validate the taxonomy through dependency tests (Appendix B), showing that feature-memorization correlations differ significantly in sign and magnitude across the three categories—e.g., low perplexity strongly predicts recitation but not reconstruction, and rare tokens negatively predict recollection—supporting the categories as meaningful natural kinds rather than arbitrary splits.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant treatment of LM memorization as a homogeneous phenomenon by introducing a three-way taxonomy (recitation, reconstruction, recollection) inspired by colloquial human memorization distinctions. It builds on the k-extractable memorization definition from Carlini et al. (2022a) and the Pythia memorization dataset from Biderman et al. (2023a), but reorganizes the analysis around taxonomic categories rather than treating all memorized sequences as a single population. Conceptually, it connects to Dankers et al. (2023) on counterfactual memorization and Bansal et al. (2023) on heuristic vs. example memorization, but provides a more granular decomposition of what they call 'example memorization' and validates the categories through predictive modeling rather than purely descriptive analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The taxonomy provides a practical lens for connecting memorization research to its distinct motivations (copyright, privacy, generalization science), showing that different subtypes implicate different factors and thus different mitigation strategies. The finding that recollection of rare sequences grows fastest with model scale raises important questions about what larger models are actually encoding beyond duplication and templates. Key limitations include the reliance on linear logistic regression (missing nonlinear interactions), the narrow 32-extractable definition that excludes partial memorization, and the handcrafted reconstruction heuristics that likely undercount template-based reproduction. The late-training reconstruction breakthrough at ~86% of training remains unexplained and is flagged as future work.
