## Problem

Existing scaling laws for language models (Chinchilla, Kaplan) characterize tradeoffs between parameters and data but ignore precision, which is a critical third axis as the field shifts from FP32 to BF16, FP8, and sub-4-bit training and inference. Without precision-aware scaling laws, practitioners cannot predict how much loss is incurred by training or serving at reduced precision, nor determine whether lower-precision training of larger models is compute-optimal.

## Input / Output

The scaling law takes model parameter count N, token budget D, per-component training precisions (P_w, P_a, P_kv for weights, activations, KV cache), and a post-training quantization precision P_post, and outputs a predicted validation loss L decomposed into a Chinchilla-form term in N_eff plus a PTQ degradation term δ_PTQ. A key structural choice is that all precision effects enter loss exclusively through a single scalar N_eff, making the functional form agnostic to which specific components are quantized. The paper also derives closed-form quantities like the critical data size D_crit beyond which additional pretraining data increases post-quantization loss, and the compute-optimal precision P* under various constraint regimes.

## Method

The core mechanism is an 'effective parameter count' N_eff = N(1−e^{−P_w/γ_w})(1−e^{−P_a/γ_a})(1−e^{−P_kv/γ_kv}) that treats bit precision and parameter count as interchangeable contributors to model capacity, with the three component factors assumed independent and multiplicative. Substituting N→N_eff into the Chinchilla form L = AN^{−α}+BD^{−β}+E yields the training loss, while post-training quantization degradation is modeled as δ_PTQ = C_T·e^{−P_post/γ_post}·(D^{γ_D}/N_eff^{γ_N})·∏[1−e^{−C_x(P_x−P_post)}], capturing a tension between an 'overtraining' effect (more data → more sensitive weights) and a 'robustification' effect (training at low precision → weights already adapted to quantization noise). Fitted on 465 pretraining runs across 30M–220M parameters and 1.5B–26B tokens in 3–16-bit integer precision, the unified law predicts that compute-optimal pretraining precision is ~7–8 bits (independent of compute budget when N,D,P are jointly optimized) but grows logarithmically with compute when model size is fixed, and that for sufficiently overtrained models, additional pretraining data can actively increase post-quantization loss.

## Results

Across 465 pretraining runs (30M–1.7B parameters, 1.5B–26B tokens, 3–16 bit precisions), the proposed N_eff scaling law achieves R² = 0.97 for post-train quantization degradation (Equation 2) and R² = 0.90 for the unified pre+post-training degradation form on over 1,000 data points. The compute-optimal pretraining precision is predicted to be approximately 7–8 bits (integer type), independent of compute budget when N, D, P are jointly optimized. When model size N is fixed, compute-optimal precision grows as P*(C) ∝ log C, validated empirically with floating-point models from FP4 to FP32 at 220M–1.6B parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing quantization or scaling-law methods in the traditional sense; instead, it compares candidate functional forms for modeling precision effects. The proposed N_eff formulation (saturating exponential, 3 parameters) achieves R² = 0.82 on held-out data, outperforming additive power-law (0.71), D_eff (0.74), and multiplicative power-law (0.75) alternatives with equal or fewer parameters. The only form that slightly beats N_eff is the untied N_eff + D_eff variant (R² = 0.84), but it requires 4 parameters versus 3. For post-train quantization, the paper validates its GPTQ-based findings by replicating the same degradation scaling with AWQ and round-to-nearest quantization, confirming the data-scaling failure mode is method-agnostic. Integer-type scaling fits are validated against floating-point experiments (FP4–FP32), showing close qualitative agreement with the optimum near 7–8 bits, though a small upward loss shift appears at ≤4-bit where the type difference becomes more pronounced.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Chinchilla scaling law framework (Hoffmann et al., 2022) by introducing bit precision as a third axis alongside parameters and data, showing it can be absorbed into an 'effective parameter count' N_eff that modifies the standard AN^{-α}+BD^{-β}+E form. Prior quantization work (GPTQ, AWQ, BitNet) studied fixed-size models at various precisions, and prior scaling law work (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) ignored precision entirely; this paper is the first to unify both into a single predictive functional form covering training and inference precision jointly. The multiplicative factorization of N_eff across weights, activations, and KV cache draws on the independence assumption common in noise-based scaling analyses (Bahri et al., 2024; Bordelon et al., 2024), but applies it to quantization-induced degradation rather than stochastic noise.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that additional pretraining data can be actively harmful for models destined for post-train quantization (at high D/N) directly challenges the 'more data is always better' assumption underlying modern overtraining practices (Llama-3, Gemma-2). The prediction that 7–8 bits may be compute-optimal for pretraining—rather than the current 16-bit standard or the sub-4-bit race—has immediate practical implications for training cost and hardware design. Key limitations include small model scale (≤1.7B), a single fixed architecture, integer-type fits as the primary basis, and no downstream task evaluation; open questions include whether these trends persist at frontier scale, how architectural modifications for low-precision training (e.g., BitNet's design) alter the scaling exponents, and whether floating-point mantissa/exponent bit allocation follows the same functional form.
