## Problem

It remains unclear whether LLMs can autonomously generate novel and valid chemistry research hypotheses given only a research background, as prior work has focused on social science, NLP, or narrow catalyst-discovery settings with potential data contamination. No prior work provides a formal mathematical decomposition of the hypothesis-discovery problem for chemistry or a benchmark of recent top-tier papers to test rediscovery capability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251191/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a research background (a question and/or a multi-paragraph survey of existing methods) plus a chemistry literature corpus of up to 3,000 papers (only titles and abstracts) as input, and produces a ranked list of generated hypotheses scored on validness, novelty, significance, and potential. In the 'copilot in the wild' evaluation setting, only the strict background (no hints toward the answer) and a 300-paper corpus are provided, with only the top 4% of the corpus selected for hypothesis development. The benchmark TOMATO-Chem consists of 51 papers from Nature/Science-level venues published after January 2024, each decomposed into background, 1–3 inspirations, and a hypothesis by chemistry PhD students.

## Method

The paper derives a mathematical decomposition of P(h|b) into a product over k inspiration-retrieval steps P(i_j | b, h_{j-1}, I) and hypothesis-composition steps P(h_j | b, i_j, h_{j-1}), plus a ranking function R(h), making the seemingly intractable problem a sequence of executable subtasks. MOOSE-Chem implements this as a multi-agent LLM pipeline: (1) an LLM screens the literature corpus in windows of 15 papers to select 3 candidate inspirations per round, deliberately avoiding semantically or citationally similar papers since true inspirations should be previously unrelated to the background; (2) an evolutionary unit (EU) generates multiple hypothesis 'mutations' associating background and inspiration, refines each via feedback on validness, novelty, clarity, and significance, then recombines surviving mutations into a final hypothesis; (3) an LLM-based rating function scores each hypothesis for ranking. The framework runs up to 3 rounds (matching the typical k∈[1,3] inspirations in chemistry) with beam search (size 15) to control combinatorial growth, and is evaluated using GPT-4o (knowledge cutoff October 2023) to ensure no data contamination with the 2024 benchmark papers.

## Results

On the TOMATO-Chem benchmark (51 Nature/Science-level chemistry papers), MOOSE-Chem achieves a Top Matched Score of 4.020 and Average Matched Score of 2.564 (evaluated by GPT-4o), substantially outperforming all baselines on the Top MS metric. Expert evaluation confirms that 19 out of 51 papers receive a Top MS of 3 (covering two key innovations of the ground truth hypothesis) and 2 receive a Top MS of 4. The framework uses GPT-4o (training data up to October 2023) to rediscover hypotheses from papers only published online after January 2024, ruling out data contamination.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MOOSE-Chem outperforms all three baselines on Top MS by a wide margin: 4.020 vs. SciMON (2.549, +1.47), MOOSE (2.882, +1.14), and Qi et al. (2.686, +1.33). On Average MS, MOOSE-Chem (2.564) leads SciMON (2.281) and Qi et al. (2.356) but only marginally beats MOOSE (2.464). The ablation reveals that the evolutionary unit (EU) with mutation and recombination is critical for Top MS: removing both multi-step and EU drops Top MS from 4.020 to 2.863, nearly matching the MOOSE baseline. Interestingly, removing multi-step slightly increases Average MS (2.730 vs. 2.564), which the authors attribute to the mutation step generating diverse but sometimes low-quality hypotheses that drag down the average. The results are robust across evaluators: Claude-3.5-Sonnet gives MOOSE-Chem a Top MS of 4.471 vs. SciMON's 3.824, and Gemini-1.5-Pro gives 3.686 vs. SciMON's 2.980.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the MOOSE framework (Yang et al., 2024b), which first demonstrated LLM-based hypothesis discovery in social science via a background-plus-inspiration decomposition, to the chemistry domain. Key departures include: (1) a formal mathematical decomposition (Equation 2) that makes the multi-inspiration, multi-round process explicit, (2) an evolutionary algorithm (EU) with mutation, selection, and recombination to associate background and inspiration—replacing MOOSE's simpler self-refine loop, (3) multi-step inspiration retrieval (up to 3 rounds) rather than single-step, and (4) an explicit ranking stage R(h) that prior works largely overlooked. Unlike SciMON, which retrieves semantically or citation-adjacent papers, MOOSE-Chem deliberately screens for papers *not* known to be related to the background, positioning inspiration retrieval as an out-of-distribution task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that an LLM-based framework can rediscover the core innovations of Nature/Science-level chemistry hypotheses from background alone, with a controlled knowledge cutoff that rules out data contamination. The TOMATO-Chem benchmark (51 expert-annotated papers) provides a reusable evaluation resource for the scientific-discovery community. However, several open questions remain: the evaluation relies heavily on LLM-judged Matched Score (cross-validated with experts but still reference-based), the benchmark is small, and the 'copilot setting' still provides a curated 300-paper corpus rather than open-ended literature search. The provocative claim that LLMs encode latent knowledge associations unknown to scientists is intriguing but currently supported only by the high inspiration-retrieval hit ratio, not by truly novel (non-rediscovered) hypotheses.
