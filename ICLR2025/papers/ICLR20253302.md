## Problem

Sparse continuous policies, which assign strictly zero probability to actions outside a bounded support, are ideal for safety-critical tasks but break existing offline RL algorithms: dataset actions falling outside the sparse policy's support yield undefined log-likelihoods (−∞), causing numerical failure. No systematic solution to this out-of-support action problem had been proposed; prior work resorted to ad hoc tricks like approximating sparse policies with Gaussians or replacing out-of-support actions with random in-support samples.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a fixed offline dataset of state-action-reward transitions generated by a behavior policy (e.g., 50×24-step trajectories in the treatment simulation, or D4RL MuJoCo datasets with 1M training steps) and produces a state-conditioned sparse q-Gaussian action distribution (q=0) that assigns zero probability to actions outside a learned bounded support. The fat proposal policy (q=2 heavy-tailed q-Gaussian) has infinite support and is never used for environment interaction; only the thin actor policy generates actions at deployment. The q-exponential advantage weighting (q_w=0) additionally assigns zero weight to actions whose advantage falls below a threshold, effectively filtering out dangerous actions during training.

## Method

FtTPO maintains two policies in a two-stage actor-critic framework: a fat (heavy-tailed q-Gaussian, q=2) proposal policy that learns from the offline dataset via forward KL minimization with q-exponential advantage weighting, and a thin (sparse q-Gaussian, q=0) actor policy that learns from the fat policy's sampled actions via reverse KL minimization. The fat policy's infinite support guarantees that all dataset actions are in-support, eliminating the out-of-support failure mode; the thin policy then inherits the fat policy's knowledge while truncating its heavy tails to produce a sparse distribution. The q-exponential weighting coefficient w(s,a)=exp_{q_w}((Q−V)/τ) with q_w=0 assigns zero weight to actions with advantage below −τ/(1−q_w), providing a principled filter for bad actions that standard exponential weighting cannot achieve. For stability, the proposal mean is copied to the actor before each update, and Schulmann's low-variance KL estimator is used for the actor loss. Sampling from both q-Gaussians is performed via the Generalized Box-Müller Method, which maps uniform random variables to q-Gaussian variables for any q<3.

## Results

On the safety-critical treatment simulation (8-dim state, unbounded action space), FtTPO achieves the highest cumulative reward across 10 seeds, outperforming all baselines and learning a sparse yet stochastic policy tightly concentrated around the optimal dosage band. On the D4RL MuJoCo suite (9 datasets, 1M steps, 10 seeds), FtTPO performs favorably to or sometimes better than state-of-the-art offline algorithms (XQL, SQL, InAC, IQL) despite using a sparse actor policy. Ablation studies confirm that the sparse q-Gaussian actor (q=0) matches the heavy-tailed proposal-only setting (TAWAC-HT) in final score, while replacing the heavy-tailed proposal with a Squashed Gaussian (FtTPO-SG) leads to significantly worse performance on most environments. Training FtTPO costs ~15 hours per 1M steps, roughly double the 6–8.5 hours for single-policy baselines like IQL or InAC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the treatment simulation, FtTPO clearly outperforms XQL, SQL, InAC, and IQL, which all converge to sub-optimal reward bands due to their Squashed Gaussian policies producing overly large randomness; SQL additionally collapses into a near-deterministic delta-like policy. SPOT is the only baseline that avoids the sub-optimal band in this environment, but still does not match FtTPO's final score. On the MuJoCo D4RL benchmark, FtTPO is competitive with or slightly better than XQL, SQL, InAC, and IQL across the 9 environments, which is notable given that sparse policies are commonly perceived as handicapped at exploration-exploitation. The proposal-only heavy-tailed variant (TAWAC-HT) achieves roughly the same final scores as full FtTPO, confirming that the sparse actor does not sacrifice performance. Replacing the actor loss with SPOT (FtTPO-SPOT) yields marginally better results on only 3 of 9 MuJoCo environments, while swapping the heavy-tailed proposal for a Squashed Gaussian (FtTPO-SG) degrades performance significantly on most tasks. AWAC is included as an additional MuJoCo baseline but is not highlighted as a primary comparison point.

## Relation to Prior Work

FtTPO draws on two distinct threads: (1) two-stage actor-critic methods like Greedy Actor-Critic (Neumann et al., 2023), where a proposal policy generates actions for a second actor policy, and (2) the q-Gaussian/Tsallis entropy family of distributions (Zhu et al., 2024b; Chow et al., 2018) that unifies heavy-tailed and sparse policies under a single parametric family. The paper's key departure is identifying the out-of-support action problem as a fundamental obstacle to offline learning with sparse policies—a challenge that prior work addressed only with ad hoc tricks (random action replacement, reverse KL). By framing the solution as a fat-to-thin knowledge transfer within the q-Gaussian family, the paper repurposes the two-stage design from online RL into a principled offline framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is most relevant to safety-critical domains (e.g., medical dosing) where a policy must strictly avoid certain actions while retaining stochasticity over a safe subset—something Gaussian policies cannot guarantee. The counterintuitive finding that a sparse policy can match or beat full-support policies on MuJoCo challenges the assumption that broad action-space coverage is necessary for competitive performance. Open questions include the theoretical characterization of how truncating heavy tails trades exploration for exploitation, and whether the safety guarantees hold when safety is not fully encoded in the reward. The method's reliance on a single synthetic treatment environment for the safety claim limits generalizability evidence.
