## Problem

Most implicit bias theory for deep networks characterizes only gradient descent's bias toward ℓ₂-margin maximization, yet modern training relies on diverse optimizers (Adam, sign GD, coordinate descent) whose implicit biases remain poorly understood. This paper asks: for an arbitrary steepest descent algorithm defined by a norm, what geometric margin does it implicitly maximize in homogeneous neural networks, and can this be characterized rigorously?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250378/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a binary classification dataset {(x_i, y_i)}, a homogeneous neural network f(·;θ) (e.g., ReLU without biases, satisfying L-homogeneity), and a norm ‖·‖ defining the steepest descent geometry. The output is a geometric characterization of the training trajectory: the algorithm-dependent soft margin γ̃(θ) = −log L(θ)/‖θ‖^L is shown to be non-decreasing, and the normalized iterates θ_t/‖θ_t‖ converge in direction to a (generalized) KKT point of the dual-norm margin maximization problem. The Bregman divergence induced by ½‖·‖²_★ serves as a progress measure quantifying proximity to stationarity.

## Method

The paper proves three key results for steepest flow (infinitesimal-learning-rate limit) on the exponential loss. First, Theorem 3.1 shows the soft geometric margin defined with the dual norm is non-decreasing after the data separates, using Euler's homogeneity theorem and the identity ‖dθ/dt‖ = ‖∇L‖_★. Second, the authors introduce a d-generalized KKT point (Definition 3.4) where stationarity is measured by a generalized Bregman divergence D_{½‖·‖²_★} induced by the squared dual norm, and prove (Theorem 3.8) that steepest flow iterates progressively reduce this divergence, so any limit point lies along the direction of a generalized KKT point of the margin maximization problem. Third, when the algorithm norm's square is smooth (e.g., any ℓ_p norm with p ≥ 2), the Bregman divergence becomes a proper metric and the result strengthens to full KKT convergence (Corollary 3.8.1). Experiments on teacher-student tasks and MNIST with GD, coordinate descent, and sign GD confirm the predicted margin ordering and reveal that Adam's practical behavior aligns more closely with sign GD's ℓ₁-margin bias than with GD's ℓ₂-margin bias.

## Results

In teacher-student experiments on synthetic Gaussian data with sparse ReLU networks, coordinate descent (CD) achieves the best generalization, followed by gradient descent (GD), then sign descent (SD), consistent with their respective biases toward ℓ₁, ℓ₂, and ℓ∞ norm minimization. All three algorithms increase their algorithm-dependent geometric margin (γ₁, γ₂, γ∞) monotonically after the separation point, confirming Theorem 3.1. On MNIST digit-pair classification, Adam with default hyperparameters (ε=10⁻⁸, momentum β₁=0.9, β₂=0.99) initially increases the ℓ₁ margin (γ₁) like sign gradient descent before slowly transitioning to increase the ℓ₂ margin (γ₂), suggesting that the ℓ₁ bias of SD more faithfully captures typical Adam behavior than the ℓ₂ bias of GD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three steepest descent algorithms (GD, CD, SD) and Adam across two experimental settings. In the teacher-student setup, CD outperforms GD and SD in test accuracy, with the ordering CD > GD > SD matching the theoretical prediction that ℓ₁-norm minimization (CD's bias) yields the simplest, sparsest solutions. Switching from GD to CD after perfect train accuracy yields only marginal generalization benefits compared to running CD from the start, indicating that early-stage dynamics matter beyond late-stage margin maximization. In the MNIST experiment, Adam (the key baseline comparison) behaves qualitatively like sign gradient descent rather than gradient descent: it increases γ₁ (the ℓ₁ margin) in the period right after 100% train accuracy, whereas GD increases γ₂ (the ℓ₂ margin). This challenges prior work (Wang et al., 2021; 2022) that concluded Adam shares the same asymptotic ℓ₂-margin-maximization properties as GD, showing that the precision parameter ε=10⁻⁸ is critical and that realistic Adam runs exhibit an ℓ₁ bias consistent with SD.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the implicit bias analysis of steepest descent from linear models (Gunasekar et al., 2018) to arbitrary homogeneous neural networks, and extends the gradient-descent-only margin maximization results of Lyu & Li (2020) and Nacson et al. (2019) to any norm-based steepest descent algorithm. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of a generalized KKT stationarity notion parameterized by a Bregman divergence induced by the algorithm's dual norm, which captures the geometric progress of non-Euclidean descent methods in a way that the standard KKT conditions cannot. The experimental section also positions the work against the Adam implicit bias literature (Wang et al., 2021; 2022; Zhang et al., 2024), arguing that sign gradient descent (steepest descent under the ℓ∞ norm) better characterizes Adam's late-stage behavior than gradient descent does.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified framework gives practitioners a principled way to predict which geometric margin (and hence which solution structure) a given optimizer will implicitly favor, which is directly relevant to choosing optimizers for generalization-sensitive tasks. The finding that Adam's late-stage dynamics align with sign gradient descent rather than gradient descent challenges the prior conclusion of Wang et al. and has practical implications for understanding why Adam often outperforms GD in language modeling. Open questions include whether the KKT convergence result extends to norms whose square is not smooth (e.g., ℓ1), whether a discrete-time analysis is possible, and whether the late-stage margin characterization alone can explain generalization or whether early-stage dynamics are equally or more important (as the teacher-student experiments suggest).
