## Problem

Existing OOD detection methods lack a single approach that performs robustly across diverse OOD types (near and far) and multiple datasets, and many are sensitive to the number of training samples. This paper exploits the unique local neuroplasticity property of Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs)—absent in traditional MLPs—to build a detector that is both highly accurate and insensitive to dataset size.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251836/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The detector consumes latent features extracted from a pre-trained backbone (ResNet-18/50 for images, FT-Transformer for tabular medical data), which are histogram-normalized to counteract skewness and fully utilize the KAN's spline grid range. It outputs a scalar InD score computed as the median of per-feature activation differences between the trained and untrained KANs, which is then thresholded to yield a binary InD/OOD decision. The method is post-hoc and architecture-agnostic, requiring only the backbone's latent representations at inference time.

## Method

The core mechanism compares the activation functions of a KAN trained on InD data against an identically initialized but untrained copy: InD samples activate spline regions modified during training (producing large coefficient differences), while OOD samples activate unmodified regions (producing near-zero differences). Because KANs process each input feature independently and thus cannot model joint feature distributions, the InD dataset is partitioned (via k-means clustering or class labels) and a separate KAN is trained per partition, with per-partition scores aggregated via the maximum function. The scoring function uses the median of the difference matrix for robustness to outlier coefficients that may coincidentally match between trained and untrained networks. Training is lightweight—single epoch with AdamW at learning rate 0.1—and the training task (classification or even regression to a constant) need not directly relate to OOD detection, as the goal is simply to register InD samples in the correct spline coefficients.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, the KAN detector achieves 94.12±0.59% overall AUROC, outperforming the previous best post-hoc method NAC (87.05±1.14%) and KNN (92.19±0.27%). On the larger ImageNet-200 FS benchmark, KAN reaches 71.46±0.40% overall AUROC (vs. ASH at 67.18% and NAC at 60.05%), and on ImageNet-1K FS it achieves 78.52% (vs. NAC at 76.28%). On the eICU synthetic OOD medical benchmark, KAN attains 79.13±2.22% AUROC, surpassing the next-best MDS (75.63±1.26%). A key finding is that KAN maintains near-constant performance (~93–94% AUROC) across training set sizes from 100% down to 0.1%, whereas KNN drops to 8.15% and VIM to 76.38% at 0.1%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The KAN detector outperforms all 20 compared baselines on the overall average AUROC across seven benchmarks spanning image and medical tabular domains. On CIFAR-10, KAN (94.12%) surpasses KNN (92.19%), VIM (91.88%), and NAC (87.05%) by 1.9–7.1 points. On ImageNet-200 FS, KAN leads ASH (67.18%) by 4.3 points and NAC (60.05%) by 11.4 points; on ImageNet-1K FS it edges out NAC (76.28%) and ASH (76.25%) by ~2.2 points. On the eICU Ethnicity benchmark, KAN (61.4%) beats MDS (58.5%) by 2.9 points, and on eICU Synthetic OOD it leads MDS (75.63%) by 3.5 points. The one exception is the eICU Age benchmark, where KAN (50.5%) is marginally below KLM (51.0%), though the difference is within one standard deviation. A distinguishing advantage is robustness to training set size: KAN maintains 93–94% AUROC from 100% down to 0.1% of CIFAR-10 training data, while KNN collapses to 8.15% and VIM drops to 76.38% at 0.1%, and NAC peaks at only 93.09% with 1% data but falls to 87.05% at 100%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the local neuroplasticity property of Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs) — a recently introduced architecture based on the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem — as the core mechanism for out-of-distribution detection. Unlike prior post-hoc OOD detectors that rely on gradients (NAC, GradNorm), distances in feature space (MDS, KNN), or activation statistics (ASH, VIM), this method compares the activation patterns of a trained KAN against its untrained counterpart, exploiting the fact that KANs' spline-based architecture only modifies coefficients in regions visited by training data. The partitioning strategy to capture joint feature distributions draws on ensemble-like ideas, and the overall framework is positioned as a plug-in detector compatible with any pre-trained backbone (ResNet, FT-Transformer), similar in spirit to NAC and VIM but with a fundamentally different scoring mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The KAN detector achieves SOTA AUROC across seven benchmarks spanning image and medical tabular domains, and notably demonstrates robustness to training set size — a qualitative advantage over methods like VIM and KNN that degrade sharply with fewer samples, and NAC that peaks at only 2% of data. This robustness is practically important in settings where labeled data is scarce or where the detector must be retrained on subsets. Key limitations include higher inference time (~2.2 ms vs. 0.17–0.68 ms for baselines) and dependence on the still-maturing KAN ecosystem; the authors note that architectural refinements (e.g., replacing splines with radial basis functions) are already improving speed. Open questions include whether the training task can be further simplified without performance loss, how the method scales to very high-dimensional latent spaces, and whether the partitioning strategy generalizes to unsupervised or semi-supervised settings.
