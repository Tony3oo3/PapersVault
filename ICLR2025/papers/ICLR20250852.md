## Problem

Existing bandit learning algorithms for matching markets assume strict preference rankings, but in real applications (labor markets, school admissions) multiple candidates often exhibit comparable performance, creating ties. Under indifference, state-of-the-art explore-then-Gale-Shapley algorithms never terminate exploration (incurring O(T) regret), and the only prior methods that handle indifference either require prior knowledge of the preference gap Δ or suffer exponential regret.

## Input / Output

Each player receives as input the arms' known preference rankings, its own player-arm matched-time counters, a binary comparison matrix (Better) that is monotonically updated from 0 to 1 only when UCB/LCB confidence bounds definitively separate two arms, and stochastic reward observations from accepted matches. The algorithm outputs a distinct arm assignment to each player per time slot (ensuring no collisions) and guarantees a stable regret bound of O(NK log T / Δ²) where Δ is the minimum non-zero preference gap. In the decentralized variant, players additionally observe all successfully matched pairs each slot and exchange comparison updates via a structured communication protocol.

## Method

The proposed AE-AGS algorithm replaces the explicit explore-then-exploit phase separation with an adaptive, arm-guided Gale-Shapley procedure: arms propose to players in preference order, and each player accepts the proposal from the potential-optimal arm (not yet identified as sub-optimal) with the fewest matched times. Sub-optimality is determined adaptively via UCB/LCB comparisons — an arm is eliminated only when its LCB falls below another arm's UCB — so tied arms remain as co-equal candidates indefinitely without incurring additional regret. This design eliminates the dilemma of when to stop exploring: if preferences are truly equal, all tied arms are valid stable partners and exploration is cost-free; if preferences differ, sufficient observations eventually trigger elimination. The algorithm is provided in both centralized (a platform computes the matching) and decentralized (players run the subroutine locally with a communication protocol for sharing comparison updates) settings, achieving the same O(NK log T / Δ²) stable regret bound in both.

## Results

The proposed AE-AGS algorithm achieves a stable regret bound of O(NK log T / Δ²) for both centralized and decentralized settings, where N is the number of players, K the number of arms, T the total horizon, and Δ the minimum non-zero preference gap. When Δ = 0 (all preferences identical), the centralized version achieves zero regret while the decentralized version achieves O(log T). In experiments on a 5-player, 5-arm market with Δ = 0.1 over T = 100k rounds, AE-AGS consistently outperforms both C-ETC and P-ETC baselines, with C-ETC being sensitive to the Δ parameter and P-ETC failing to converge within the reported horizon due to its exponential regret component.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares AE-AGS against two baselines that can be extended to handle indifference: C-ETC (Liu et al., 2020) and P-ETC (Basu et al., 2021). AE-AGS dominates both on all key dimensions. Against C-ETC, AE-AGS does not require prior knowledge of Δ (C-ETC needs Δ to set its exploration budget) and achieves a tighter regret order of O(NK log T / Δ²) versus O(K log T / Δ²) only in the centralized setting with known Δ. Against P-ETC, AE-AGS avoids the exponential regret term 2^{Δ^{-2}/ε} that causes P-ETC to fail to converge within T = 100k rounds in experiments. In the experimental comparison on a 5×5 market with Δ = 0.1, AE-AGS shows consistently lower stable regret across all 20 runs, while C-ETC's performance is sensitive to the Δ parameter and P-ETC's regret grows without converging. All other existing algorithms (Liu et al. 2021, Kong et al. 2022, Zhang et al. 2022, Kong & Li 2023, Kong et al. 2024) fail entirely under indifference, as their proofs rely on strict preference rankings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the bandit learning framework for two-sided matching markets (Liu et al., 2020) and the Gale-Shapley algorithm (Gale & Shapley, 1962) to the indifference setting, where players may assign equal preference values to different arms. All prior works on stable regret in matching markets assume strict preference rankings; the state-of-the-art explore-then-Gale-Shapley strategies (Zhang et al., 2022; Kong & Li, 2023; Kong et al., 2024) provably fail under indifference because exploration never terminates when preferences are tied. The proposed AE-AGS algorithm departs from the explicit explore-then-exploit paradigm by letting arms guide which players explore, so players never face the dilemma of deciding when to stop exploring tied arms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result is practically relevant because ties in preferences are ubiquitous in real markets (labor, school admission), yet most existing algorithms either require knowledge of the minimum gap Δ or incur exponential regret when extended to indifference. The O(NK log T / Δ²) bound matches the best known rates for strict preferences without requiring Δ, closing a gap in the theory. Open questions include extending to stronger stability notions (super stability, weak stability) and establishing guarantees for Pareto-efficient stable matchings, which the authors identify as future directions.
