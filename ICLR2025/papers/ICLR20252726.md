## Problem

Existing batched best arm identification algorithms, such as Successive Elimination, achieve a batch complexity of log(1/Δ₂) that is minimax-optimal but not instance-sensitive, meaning they cannot exploit the structure of easy instances to reduce the number of batches. This paper asks whether, in the adaptive grid setting, one can design algorithms that maintain near-optimal sample complexity Õ(H_I) while breaking the log(1/Δ₂) barrier for batch complexity on many inputs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252726/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes n arms with Gaussian reward distributions N(μᵢ, 1) (or d-dimensional context vectors xᵢ with ‖xᵢ‖₂ ≤ 1 for linear bandits) and a confidence parameter δ, and outputs the best arm with probability 1−δ. Crucially, the batch sizes are not fixed a priori but are chosen adaptively at the start of each round based on all rewards observed so far, making this an adaptive-grid rather than fixed-grid procedure. The algorithm does not require H_I or R_I as input; all instance-dependent quantities are estimated online from empirical gaps.

## Method

IS-SE modifies the Successive Elimination batch-update rule: instead of simply setting L_{r+1} = β_grid · L_r, it adds an additional term (β_sample/|S_{r+1}|)·∑_{s≤r}∑_{j∈O_s}(ε_j^s)⁻² that uniformly distributes the estimated instance complexity of all eliminated arms across the remaining arms. This extra budget is justified by the observation that eliminating those arms already required at least that many pulls, so reusing the budget for the next batch is not wasteful. The key theoretical contribution is a recursive definition of an instance-sensitive batch complexity R_I that upper-bounds the number of batches and can be O(1) for structured instances (e.g., one hard arm among many easy ones) while still being at most log(1/Δ₂) in the worst case. IS-RAGE extends the same principle to linear bandits by replacing the uniform per-arm budget with an optimal experimental design (via the Round function) and using the design value ρ(𝒴(·)) as the effective arm-set size in the budget allocation, yielding a matching improvement in batch complexity with Õ(ψ*) sample complexity.

## Results

On synthetic bandit instances B₁(n) and B₂(n) (n=1000 arms), IS-SE achieves O(1) batch complexity while SE requires Θ(log n) batches, confirming the theoretical prediction that R_I = O(1) for these instances. On B₃(n), where the instance complexity is inherently Θ(log n), IS-SE and SE produce nearly indistinguishable sample-complexity-vs-batch curves. On the real-world MovieLens25M dataset (1,000 arms, top-rated movies), IS-SE consistently dominates SE across all tested β_grid ∈ {2,4,6,8} and β_sample ∈ {0.5,1,1.5,2} configurations in the sample-complexity–batch-complexity trade-off. For linear bandits (n=500, d=500), IS-RAGE similarly outperforms RAGE on B₁ and B₂ while matching it on B₃.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: Successive Elimination (SE, Even-Dar et al. 2002) for MAB and RAGE (Fiez et al. 2019a) for linear bandits. On the two 'easy' synthetic instances B₁(n) and B₂(n), IS-SE and IS-RAGE substantially reduce the number of batches (from Θ(log n) to O(1)) at comparable or slightly higher sample complexity, demonstrating the instance-sensitive batch improvement. On the 'hard' instance B₃(n), where the gap structure forces Θ(log n) batches regardless of algorithm, IS-SE and IS-RAGE match SE and RAGE, confirming they are never worse. On MovieLens25M, IS-SE consistently achieves a better sample-complexity–batch-complexity Pareto frontier than SE across all hyperparameter settings, with the advantage most pronounced at moderate β_grid values. The key takeaway is that the improvement is instance-dependent: it is large when the suboptimal arms have heterogeneous gaps (B₁, B₂) and negligible when gaps are uniformly spread (B₃).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Successive Elimination (SE) framework of Even-Dar et al. (2002) and the RAGE algorithm of Fiez et al. (2019a) by replacing their fixed geometric batch-size progression with an adaptive, instance-dependent update rule. Prior batched BAI work (Tao et al. 2019; Jun et al. 2016; Jin et al. 2019) established log(1/Δ₂) as a near-minimax-optimal batch complexity bound, but all such results were instance-independent. The key conceptual departure here is introducing R_I, a recursive instance-sensitive quantity that captures how quickly arms can be eliminated given their specific gap structure, and showing that the batch-size update can be accelerated by redistributing the estimated complexity of already-eliminated arms to the remaining set. The paper also notes the equivalence between the batched model and the non-adaptive collaborative learning model, positioning its contribution within that broader literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that log(1/Δ₂) is only a worst-case upper bound on batch complexity—and that instance-sensitive algorithms can achieve O(1) batches on structured inputs while never being worse—refines the understanding of the sample-batch tradeoff in pure exploration. The extension to linear bandits is non-trivial because arms can be linearly dependent, requiring the use of optimal design values ρ(·) as a surrogate for effective arm count. Open questions include analyzing average-case batch complexity under typical input distributions and validating the approach on larger-scale real-world instances beyond the 1,000-arm MovieLens experiment. The techniques (redistributing eliminated-arm complexity into future batch budgets) may generalize to other batched online learning problems such as regret minimization.
