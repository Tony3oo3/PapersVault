## Problem

Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) are widely used in mechanistic interpretability to decompose LLM activations into interpretable features, and it has been conjectured that sufficiently large SAEs recover a canonical set of units—unique, complete, and atomic features. This paper systematically challenges that belief by showing that SAEs of different sizes are both incomplete (smaller SAEs miss features entirely) and non-atomic (larger SAE latents are compositions of smaller ones), with no single dictionary size yielding a canonical decomposition.

## Input / Output

For SAE stitching, the method takes two pre-trained SAEs of different dictionary sizes on the same model layer (e.g., GPT-2 Small layer 8 residual stream, sizes 768–98,304) and produces a classification of each larger-SAE latent as novel or reconstruction, along with an interpolation path between the two SAEs. For meta-SAEs, the input is the decoder matrix (m×n) of a large SAE treated as training data, and the output is a sparse decomposition of each decoder direction into a small number (e.g., 4) of interpretable meta-latents. The BatchTopK variant operates on standard LLM activation batches (n×d) and produces sparse latent codes with a fixed average L0 across the batch rather than a fixed per-sample L0.

## Method

The paper introduces three complementary techniques. SAE stitching swaps connected subgraphs of latents between SAEs of different sizes based on decoder cosine similarity (threshold 0.7 for GPT-2, 0.4 for Gemma Scope), revealing that larger SAEs contain both novel latents that improve reconstruction and reconstruction latents that merely re-express smaller-SAE features more sparsely. Meta-SAEs are SAEs trained to reconstruct the decoder directions of a larger SAE using a smaller dictionary of meta-latents, yielding interpretable decompositions (e.g., 'Einstein' → 'scientist' + 'Germany' + 'famous person') that demonstrate non-atomicity. To train meta-SAEs at the required low sparsity (~4 active meta-latents per input), the authors introduce BatchTopK, which selects the top n×k activations across an entire batch rather than k per sample, enforcing a fixed average sparsity while allowing variable per-sample L0; this outperforms both TopK and JumpReLU SAEs in NMSE and cross-entropy degradation at matched sparsity levels.

## Results

The meta-SAE (2304 meta-latents) trained on the decoder directions of a 49,152-latent GPT-2 SAE explains 55.47% of the variance, and its decompositions achieve 73% accuracy in a zero-shot 5-way multiple-choice evaluation (vs. 20% random baseline). SAE stitching shows that adding novel latents from GPT-1536 into GPT-768 reduces reconstruction MSE by nearly 10%, confirming incompleteness of smaller SAEs. For the BatchTopK architecture, at a fixed L0=32, BatchTopK achieves lower NMSE and less cross-entropy degradation than TopK on both GPT-2 Small and Gemma 2 2B residual stream activations, and outperforms JumpReLU on GPT-2 at all sparsity levels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BatchTopK is compared against TopK SAEs (Gao et al., 2024) and JumpReLU SAEs (Rajamanoharan et al., 2024) on GPT-2 Small (layer 8) and Gemma 2 2B (layer 12) residual stream activations. Against TopK, BatchTopK achieves lower NMSE and less cross-entropy degradation at a fixed L0=32 on both models, and at a fixed dictionary size of 12,288, BatchTopK outperforms TopK across all tested k values (16, 32, 64). Against JumpReLU, BatchTopK wins on both NMSE and CE degradation at all sparsity levels on GPT-2 Small. On Gemma 2 2B, the comparison is more mixed: BatchTopK achieves better reconstruction (NMSE) than JumpReLU for all k values, but only outperforms JumpReLU in CE degradation at the highest sparsity setting (k=16). The paper also notes that JumpReLU SAEs cannot be directly compared at a fixed sparsity because their L0 cannot be explicitly controlled, requiring costly hyperparameter tuning to approximate a target sparsity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SAE-based mechanistic interpretability line initiated by Bricken et al. (2023) and Gao et al. (2024) by introducing two diagnostic tools—SAE stitching (adapting the model-stitching paradigm of Bansal et al., 2021 to swap latents between SAEs of different sizes) and meta-SAEs (SAEs trained on another SAE's decoder directions)—to systematically test whether SAEs converge to a canonical feature set. It departs from the prevailing 'strong feature hypothesis' that sufficiently large SAEs recover a unique, complete, and atomic dictionary, instead showing that larger SAEs learn both novel features (incompleteness) and composed features (non-atomicity). The BatchTopK architecture extends TopK SAEs from per-sample to batch-level sparsity, addressing a practical limitation for training meta-SAEs at very low sparsity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a core narrative in mechanistic interpretability—that SAEs of sufficient size will discover the 'true' atomic features of a model—by providing systematic evidence that no single dictionary size yields a canonical set. This has practical implications: practitioners should choose SAE width pragmatically per task rather than assuming a universal optimal size. Key limitations include evaluation on only two small models (GPT-2 Small, Gemma 2 2B) without very large SAEs (e.g., Templeton's million-latent Claude SAEs), and the composition finding was partially anticipated by prior theoretical arguments about sparsity penalties. Open questions include whether canonical units exist at all, whether alternative decomposition methods can recover them, and whether the findings generalize to much larger models and SAEs.
