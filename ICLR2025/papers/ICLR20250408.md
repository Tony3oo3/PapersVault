## Problem

Despite growing interest in automated circuit discovery for mechanistic interpretability, the computational complexity of the underlying search problems remains largely uncharacterized. This gap leaves open fundamental questions about whether current heuristics can scale to larger models, why they sometimes produce unfaithful or non-minimal circuits, and which problem variants might admit efficient algorithms. The paper addresses this by providing the first systematic complexity-theoretic analysis of circuit discovery queries on multi-layer perceptrons.

## Input / Output

The framework takes an MLP (specified by its weights, biases, and layer architecture) together with query-specific parameters such as a single input vector x, a set of input vectors X, a size bound u, an activation threshold t, or a clamping value r. Outputs range over circuits (subsets of neurons inducing a subgraph), a designated breaking-point neuron, a yes/no decision, or a count of qualifying circuits. The template system generates variants along two orthogonal axes—coverage (local over a finite input set vs. global over all inputs) and size (bounded, unbounded, or optimal)—yielding a comprehensive problem suite analyzed under both classical and parameterized complexity.

## Method

The paper introduces a formal framework that encodes circuit discovery queries as computational problems and analyzes them using classical complexity (NP, Σ₂ᵖ, coNP, #P) and parameterized complexity (FPT, W[1], W[2], #W[1]). A key technical contribution is proving that local variants of sufficient circuit, circuit ablation, clamping, and patching are NP-complete (reducible to SAT), while their global counterparts are Σ₂ᵖ-complete, establishing a formal local–global complexity separation that explains 'interpretability illusions.' To navigate intractability, the authors introduce quasi-minimality—a relaxation guaranteeing only one known breaking point rather than full minimality—which yields PTIME algorithms for both sufficient circuit and circuit patching queries. They further show that parameterizing by network depth, width, or circuit size does not restore fixed-parameter tractability for most hard queries (W[1]-hard), and that the problems are inapproximable under additive, multiplicative, and three probabilistic approximation schemes. For circuit robustness, they prove coNP-completeness locally and identify a fixed-parameter tractable regime when parameterized by the size of the candidate set H.

## Results

The paper establishes a comprehensive complexity landscape for circuit discovery queries on MLPs. Most core queries (Sufficient Circuit, Circuit Ablation, Circuit Clamping, Circuit Patching, Necessary Circuit) are NP-complete locally and Σ₂ᵖ-complete (or in Σ₂ᵖ) globally, remain W[1]-hard when parameterized by depth/width, and are inapproximable under all five studied schemes (additive, multiplicative, PTAS, and three probabilistic variants). In contrast, the quasi-minimal relaxations (Unbounded Quasi-Minimal SC and CP) and Gnostic Neuron queries are in PTIME, and Circuit Robustness parameterized by |H| is FPT. A formal separation between local (NP) and global (Σ₂ᵖ) query complexity is established, providing a theoretical explanation for 'interpretability illusions' observed empirically.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or dataset evaluations; the 'comparisons' are against prior complexity-theoretic results. The paper provides the first systematic complexity analysis of circuit discovery queries, going beyond the small set of queries on abstract biological graphs studied by Ramaswamy (2019) and Adolfi & van Rooij (2023), which the authors note cannot inform circuit discovery in ANNs. For the Sufficient Reasons (explainability) query, the paper significantly tightens existing results from Barceló et al. (2020) and Waldchen et al. (2021) by proving that NP-hardness, W[1]-hardness, and 3PA-inapproximability hold even when the model has only one hidden layer. The local-vs-global complexity separation (NP vs. Σ₂ᵖ) extends and formalizes the local/global distinction introduced by Bassan et al. (2024) for explainability, now applied to inner interpretability circuit queries. The paper also positions its MLP results as directly relevant to transformer interpretability, since transformers contain MLP blocks, though no direct empirical comparison with transformer-specific circuit discovery heuristics (e.g., Conmy et al., 2023) is performed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by providing the first systematic computational complexity analysis of circuit discovery queries for inner interpretability, a gap explicitly identified by Subercaseaux (2020) and Wang & Veitch (2024). It repurposes parameterized complexity theory (Downey & Fellows) and the quasi-minimality concept from Ramaswamy (2019) to formalize and analyze a comprehensive suite of circuit queries (sufficient, necessary, ablation, clamping, patching, robustness, gnostic neurons) on MLPs. The paper also draws on the explainability complexity literature (Barceló et al. 2020; Bassan et al. 2024) to establish a local-vs-global distinction and to tighten existing results on sufficient reasons, while positioning its findings relative to the growing body of circuit discovery heuristics (Conmy et al. 2023; Shi et al. 2024; Tigges et al. 2024) whose scalability and faithfulness concerns motivate the theoretical analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The results provide a rigorous theoretical explanation for why circuit discovery heuristics struggle: many natural queries are not merely NP-hard but remain W[1]-hard even when parameterized by depth or circuit size, and the local-vs-global complexity gap (NP-complete vs. Σ₂ᵖ-complete) formally accounts for 'interpretability illusions' where locally faithful circuits fail globally. The identification of tractable relaxations (quasi-minimal circuits in PTIME, circuit robustness FPT in |H|, gnostic neurons in PTIME) gives practitioners concrete, well-understood targets for algorithm design, while the NP-completeness of local variants opens the door to SAT-solver-based approaches. Key limitations include the restriction to MLPs (though results transfer to transformer MLP blocks), worst-case analysis that may overstate practical difficulty, and numerous open '?' entries in the complexity table. Future work on continuous outputs, actual trained network structure, and mid-level abstractions could narrow the gap between worst-case theory and empirical practice.
