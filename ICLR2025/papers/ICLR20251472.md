## Problem

Standard safety alignment for language models focuses on prevention—reducing the probability of generating harmful content—but the attack surface of a text interface is practically infinite, and even heavily tuned frontier models are routinely jailbroken in the wild. The paper asks how to meaningfully improve safety given that models will inevitably produce some unsafe generations, proposing recovery (undoing and regenerating) as a complement to prevention.

## Input / Output

The model takes a standard text prompt and produces a text response, but with an unusual internal mechanism: during generation, the model may emit a special [RESET] token, after which all prior tokens are discarded and generation restarts from scratch (with the unsafe prefix still in context). At the API level, only tokens after [RESET] are returned to the user, so the output is always a clean safe response. The model is constrained to backtrack at most once per generation, and a logit bias on [RESET] at inference time allows operators to tune the safety–efficiency tradeoff.

## Method

The core contribution is a [RESET] token that lets the model recognize a partial unsafe generation, discard it, and regenerate a safe response from scratch. Training proceeds in two stages: (1) SFT on backtracking demonstrations constructed by rejection-sampling unsafe prefixes from existing unsafe responses (using Llama Guard 2 as a safety classifier) and supervising the model to emit [RESET] followed by the safe response; (2) DPO on preference pairs where the preferred response backtracks from an unsafe prefix (prefix(y⁻)⊕[RESET]⊕y⁺ ≻ y⁻) and the dispreferred response unnecessarily backtracks from a safe prefix (y⁺ ≻ prefix(y⁺)⊕[RESET]⊕y⁻). The one-shot backtracking design avoids framing generation as a Markov decision process, making it directly compatible with standard SFT and DPO post-training pipelines. The partial unsafe text remains in the context window during regeneration, providing the model with safety signals to produce a better response.

## Results

On Llama-3-8B, backtracking SFT+DPO reduces overall safety violation rate from 5.3% (baseline SFT) and 5.2% (baseline SFT+DPO) to 1.5%, a 72% relative reduction, while improving MT-Bench helpfulness from 6.67/6.68 to 7.12. On Gemma-2-2B, backtracking SFT+DPO reduces overall safety violations from 10.6%/10.8% to 6.1% (42% relative reduction) with comparable MT-Bench scores (4.96 vs 5.05/5.20). Backtracking DPO is far more effective than backtracking SFT alone: after DPO, models backtrack on 49.9% (Llama) and 60.6% (Gemma) of safety prompts, versus only 0.2% and 2.4% after SFT. Against adversarial attacks (Table 2), backtracking reduces attack success rates by >2× on most model-attack pairs, e.g., Llama prefilling ASR drops from 85.0% to 2.6%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares backtracking variants against standard (non-backtracking) SFT and SFT+DPO baselines trained on identical data. Backtrack SFT+DPO dominates all baselines on overall safety for both models: Llama-3-8B drops from 5.2–5.3% to 1.5% violation rate, and Gemma-2-2B drops from 10.6–10.8% to 6.1%. Backtrack SFT alone provides only marginal gains (Llama: 5.3%→4.8%; Gemma: 10.6%→9.0%), confirming that DPO is the critical component for eliciting frequent backtracking behavior. On helpfulness (MT-Bench), backtracking SFT+DPO matches or slightly exceeds baselines for Llama (7.12 vs 6.67/6.68) and is within 0.2–0.24 points for Gemma (4.96 vs 5.05/5.20), indicating no meaningful utility regression. Against adversarial attacks, backtracking models achieve >2× lower attack success rates than baselines on prefilling, GCG, and the adaptive attack, though AutoDAN remains a stronger threat (60% ASR on Gemma, 37% on Llama) than the adaptive attack (27.5%/21.0%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant prevention-based safety alignment paradigm (SFT, RLHF, DPO) by introducing a recovery mechanism: a special [RESET] token that allows the model to discard an unsafe partial generation and start over. It repurposes standard post-training recipes (SFT + DPO) but redirects them toward a new objective—teaching the model to recognize and undo unsafe generations rather than prevent them. Conceptually, it is closest to Cundy & Ermon's (2023) backspace token and to course-correction methods (Qi et al. 2024, Xu et al. 2024), but restricts backtracking to a single one-shot reset and discards the unsafe text entirely rather than attempting in-place correction, making it compatible with standard post-training algorithms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a simple recovery mechanism can yield large safety gains (4× reduction in violations on Llama-3-8B) without hurting helpfulness, and provides non-trivial protection against adversarial jailbreaking attacks even without adversarial training—suggesting that 'recovery' is a complementary and potentially more robust axis of safety than 'prevention' alone. Key limitations include residual vulnerability to strong attacks (AutoDAN still achieves 37–60% ASR), the need for API-level support to discard pre-[RESET] tokens, and the fact that the adaptive attack, while less effective than hoped, still achieves 21–27.5% ASR. Open questions include whether adversarial training or representation steering can harden the [RESET] classification, and whether backtracking generalizes beyond safety to other generation errors like hallucinations or reasoning mistakes.
