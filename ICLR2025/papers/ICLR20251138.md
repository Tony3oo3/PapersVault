## Problem

Existing interpretable neural networks rely on adversarial training to maintain explanation comprehensibility, but this approach only defends against known perturbation types and fails under unknown perturbations. The paper addresses the open problem of generating comprehensible concept-level explanations under arbitrary, unseen perturbations without retraining the model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251138/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

AGAIN takes an input sample (bird image, patient vital-sign vector, or synthetic digit image) and a pre-constructed factor graph encoding domain-specific logical rules (coexistence and exclusion) between semantic concepts and category labels. It outputs a rectified binary concept activation vector (the explanation) and a category prediction. The factor graph is built offline from expert-annotated association scores (CUB), probability analyses (MIMIC-III EWS), or synthetic rule definitions (Synthetic-MNIST), and is treated as fixed exogenous knowledge during inference.

## Method

AGAIN integrates a concept bottleneck model with a factor graph in three inference-time modules. First, logical rules between concepts and categories are encoded as factors in a factor graph, where variables represent concept and category labels and factors encode coexistence (⇔) or exclusion (⊕) constraints with confidence weights. Second, the initial concept explanation from the bottleneck is imported into the graph, and a conditional probability P(V^c | V^y) computed via weighted potential functions determines whether the explanation violates logical rules (i.e., is perturbed). Third, an interactive intervention switch strategy traverses all factors with ψ_i=1, evaluates every possible single-intervention case by computing the weighted potential-function difference over 1-hop neighbor factors, selects the intervention maximizing this difference, and applies it to flip erroneous concept activations. The rectified explanation is then fed to the category predictor. The key distinction from prior work is that all rectification occurs at inference time using exogenous logical knowledge, requiring no knowledge of the perturbation and no retraining.

## Results

On CUB under unknown perturbation (δ_u, ε=32), AGAIN achieves an LSM of 92.1, substantially outperforming the best adversarial-trained baseline ECBMs-AT (66.7) and the best knowledge-integration baseline C-HMCNN (85.0). On MIMIC-III EWS under the same setting, AGAIN reaches 92.3 LSM versus 79.4 for MORDAA and 83.5 for C-HMCNN. Crucially, AGAIN's LSM remains stable (92–94) across all perturbation magnitudes (ε=4 to 32), while baselines degrade sharply (e.g., CBM drops from 89.3 to 39.4 on CUB). For E-ACC on CUB under δ_u ε=32, AGAIN achieves 93.9% versus 83.5% for C-HMCNN and 80.7% for ECBMs-AT, while P-ACC remains comparable to baselines (~59.6%), confirming the factor graph improves explanation quality without sacrificing prediction accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AGAIN outperforms all 14 baselines on LSM under unknown perturbations across both real-world datasets. Against concept-level methods (CBM, Hard AR, ICBM, PCBM, ProbCBM, Label-free CBM, ProtoCBM, ECBMs) and their adversarial-trained variants (-AT), AGAIN maintains LSM above 92 at ε=32 while the best concept-level baseline (ECBMs-AT) drops to 66.7 on CUB and 65.9 on MIMIC-III EWS. Against knowledge-integration methods, AGAIN surpasses C-HMCNN (85.0/83.5) and MORDAA (76.8/79.4) at ε=32, with the gap widening as perturbation magnitude increases. DeepProblog and LEN perform identically to plain CBM under perturbation because they can only constrain category predictions, not concept activations. DKAA and MORDAA detect adversarial perturbations but cannot correct the wrong concepts triggered by them, limiting their LSM improvement. On E-ACC, AGAIN achieves 93.9% on CUB (δ_u, ε=32) versus 83.5% for C-HMCNN and 80.7% for ECBMs-AT, while P-ACC remains statistically comparable to all baselines (~59–62%), confirming the factor graph rectifies explanations without degrading task accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

AGAIN builds on Concept Bottleneck Models (Koh et al., 2020) as its base interpretable architecture and repurposes factor graph reasoning—previously used for knowledge integration to improve predictive accuracy (Gurel et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2022)—toward the distinct goal of identifying and rectifying logical errors in concept-level explanations. It departs from the dominant adversarial-training paradigm for robust interpretability (Kamath et al., 2024; Sarkar et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2019), which requires crafting adversarial samples for known perturbation types and retraining, by shifting the defense entirely to inference time via an interactive intervention switch guided by exogenous logical rules. The paper also positions itself against knowledge-integration baselines (DeepProblog, MBM, C-HMCNN, DKAA, MORDAA) that constrain predictions but cannot correct erroneous concept activations triggered by perturbations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AGAIN addresses a genuine gap: no prior method can generate comprehensible explanations under *unknown* perturbations without retraining, and the inference-time factor graph approach sidesteps the high cost and limited coverage of adversarial training. However, two significant limitations constrain broader impact: (1) the method assumes category predictions remain correct under perturbation, so wrong categories propagate into the factor graph and cause incorrect rectification; (2) the factor graph must be manually reconstructed when domain knowledge shifts, limiting generalizability across domains. Open questions include whether the approach scales to large concept sets (e.g., thousands of concepts in medical or scientific domains), whether automated factor graph construction is feasible, and whether the assumption of perturbation-invariant category predictions can be relaxed.
