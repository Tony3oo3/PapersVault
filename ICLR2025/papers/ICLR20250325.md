## Problem

Existing influence-based data selection methods, such as the Influence Value (IV) of Chhabra et al. (2024), evaluate the effect of removing a training point under a fixed model and can fail in simple cases (e.g., in logistic regression they would uniformly remove all samples from one class). The paper identifies that no existing metric directly quantifies how small perturbations to a training sample propagate through model re-optimization to affect validation performance, which is the quantity most relevant for deciding which points to trim or which unlabeled points to acquire.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a trained model's parameter vector θ̂, a labeled training set Z, a validation set V (for trimming) or an unlabeled pool U (for active learning), and computes per-sample scalar influence scores via backpropagation without retraining. For trimming, the output is a ranked list from which the top-b highest-influence samples are removed; for active learning, the output is a ranked list of unlabeled samples whose predicted labels are queried in batches. The FI scores are computed on a perturbation manifold with a Riemannian metric tensor G_z(ω), making them invariant to arbitrary diffeomorphic rescalings of the input dimensions.

## Method

The paper defines a first-order influence measure FI(z′, z) that quantifies how a small perturbation ω to training sample z changes the model's loss on a validation sample z′, with the model parameters allowed to re-optimize (θ̂(x+ω)). The key geometric ingredient is the Riemannian metric tensor G_z(ω) of the perturbation manifold, which replaces the Euclidean inner product and ensures invariance under reparameterization (Theorem 1). For data trimming, FI^util aggregates the validation-set loss gradient and ranks training samples by their FI^util values; for active learning, FI^active aggregates the FI of an unlabeled sample (with its predicted label) across all training samples using a trimmed-mean aggregation. Two approximation methods reduce cost: KFSVD combines K-FAC Hessian approximation with Truncated-SVD for the mixed second-order derivative ∂_x∂_θ L, and a subsampling scheme computes exact FI on a fraction α of samples and trains a random forest to predict ranks for the remainder.

## Results

On the 2D linear model (logistic regression), FI achieves 96.23% test accuracy after trimming 20 samples, outperforming IV (93.36%) in all 30 random seeds. On the nonlinear model (crescent-shaped data with a 2-hidden-layer ReLU network), FI reaches 90.32% vs. IV's 87.80% at 20 deletions. On real-world datasets, FI consistently outperforms both IV and Random Trimming across Adult, Bank, CelebA, and Jigsaw Toxicity, with the Bank dataset showing over 10% accuracy improvement. In active learning, FI surpasses IV, BALD, and Random Selection on all six evaluated datasets (Adult, CelebA, Jigsaw Toxicity, MNIST, EMNIST, CIFAR-10), with the gap widening in later annotation rounds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against IV (Chhabra et al., ICLR 2024), the primary baseline, FI wins on every data trimming experiment: 23–30/30 seeds on the linear model, 15–17/20 on the nonlinear model, and consistently higher test accuracy on all four real-world datasets. Notably, IV degrades performance on CelebA (removing important points) and shows no improvement on Jigsaw Toxicity, while FI still gains accuracy in both cases. In active learning, FI outperforms IV on all six datasets, with the advantage growing in later rounds as FI targets samples where the model lacks information. Against BALD (Gal et al., ICML 2017), FI starts at a comparable level but pulls ahead in later annotation rounds on both tabular and image datasets, particularly on CIFAR-10 and EMNIST where the task is more complex. Against Data Shapley (Ghorbani & Zou, ICML 2019), FI is dramatically superior: Data Shapley performs at or below Random Selection on both data trimming (Adult, Bank) and active learning (MNIST), while also requiring approximately 860× more computation time than FI.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws on the local influence analysis framework from statistics (Zhu et al., 2007; Shu & Zhu, 2019), which uses a Riemannian perturbation manifold to measure sensitivity, and repurposes it for machine learning data selection. It departs from influence-function-based methods (Koh & Liang, 2017; Chhabra et al., 2024) by evaluating how perturbations to training samples affect validation-set performance through the model's parameter re-optimization, rather than measuring fixed-model parameter sensitivity in Euclidean space. The KFSVD approximation (combining K-FAC and Truncated-SVD) and the subsampling-with-random-forest strategy are practical engineering contributions that bridge the gap between the theoretically clean influence measure and scalable deployment on deep networks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified FI metric addresses both data trimming and active learning under a single geometric framework, and the invariance under diffeomorphic reparameterization (Theorem 1) resolves a practical scaling issue that plagues Euclidean-space influence measures. The demonstration that the recent IV method (Chhabra et al., 2024, ICLR) exhibits a fundamental failure mode in even a 1D logistic regression setting is a useful caution for the community. Open questions include whether the first-order Taylor approximation remains accurate for very deep or over-parameterized networks, how the method behaves when the perturbation manifold metric G_z is highly ill-conditioned, and whether the random-forest subsampling surrogate degrades in quality as feature dimensionality grows into the thousands.
