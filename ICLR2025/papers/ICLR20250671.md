## Problem

Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs) have gained rapid adoption across scientific domains, yet no rigorous theoretical analysis quantifying their model complexity or generalization guarantees existed. The paper addresses this gap by establishing generalization bounds for KANs, making explicit how network structure (number of layers, basis function choices, Lipschitz constants, and coefficient norms) controls the excess risk, and by showing empirically that the derived complexity measure tracks the actual excess loss during training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250671/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The KAN architecture takes a feature vector x ∈ R^{d_0} and produces a prediction y ∈ R^{d_L} through L layers of learnable univariate edge functions ψ_{i,j,k}: R → R, where nodes perform pure summation (no fixed activation). Unlike MLPs, there are no weight matrices; each edge carries a trainable one-dimensional function, typically parameterized as a linear combination of basis functions (e.g., B-splines, SiLU) or as an element of a low-rank RKHS. For the empirical validation, inputs are either simulated tabular features (4- or 100-dimensional) or 1000-dimensional features extracted from a pre-trained AlexNet on MNIST and CIFAR-10.

## Method

The paper derives generalization bounds via covering numbers and Rademacher complexity, adapted to the KAN's edge-function architecture. For basis-function activations, it applies Maurey's sparsification lemma to bound the metric entropy of each layer's function class, then chains these bounds across layers using an iterative argument (generalizing Bartlett et al. 2017) that accommodates different operator norms per layer; the resulting bound scales with the l1 norm of coefficient matrices and Lipschitz constants, with no dependence on node counts outside logarithmic factors. For low-rank RKHS activations (Matérn kernel / Sobolev space), the covering number bound scales polynomially with the underlying rank r_l and smoothness parameter ν. A truncation argument removes the bounded-loss requirement, extending the results to general regression-type losses (squared, pinball, Huber). Empirically, the paper trains KANs with SGD and shows the theoretical complexity measure (∏ρ_j)^{2/3} Σ(B_i c_i)^{2/3} tightly tracks the excess loss across all six datasets, and demonstrates that dropout regularization reduces both complexity and excess loss.

## Results

This is a theoretical paper deriving generalization bounds for KANs rather than reporting benchmark accuracy numbers. The headline result (Theorem 3) establishes that the excess risk of a KAN trained by empirical risk minimization is bounded by a term scaling as $\tilde{\alpha}^3 \log(2\tilde{d}\tilde{p})/n$, where $\tilde{\alpha}$ depends on the $l_1$ norms of coefficient matrices and Lipschitz constants of activation functions, with no dependence on the number of nodes outside logarithmic factors. The bound does not require the loss to be bounded, covering general regression-type losses (squared, pinball, Huber). Empirically, the derived complexity measure $\tilde{\alpha}$ is shown to tightly track the excess loss (test loss minus training loss) across four simulated datasets and MNIST/CIFAR-10 (1000-dim AlexNet features, KAN shape [1000,50,100,50,10], 1000 epochs). In a regularization experiment, applying dropout (rate 0.1) to KAN activations reduces excess loss from 0.33 to 0.03 (Setup i, MSE) and from 2.41 to 0.06 (Setup iii, cross-entropy), while simultaneously lowering the complexity measure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark KANs against MLPs or other architectures in a standard accuracy-vs-baseline sense; it is a theoretical contribution. The closest comparison appears in Appendix A.1, where the derived KAN generalization bound is contrasted with the spectrally-normalized margin bound for MLPs from Bartlett et al. (2017). When the model complexities are of the same order ($R_{\text{KAN}} \asymp R_{\mathcal{A}}$), both bounds are of the same order up to logarithmic factors. However, when the true regression function is more naturally represented by a KAN (e.g., separable or low-dimensional structure), the KAN complexity $R_{\text{KAN}}$ can be strictly smaller than the MLP complexity $R_{\mathcal{A}}$, yielding a tighter generalization bound for KANs. The paper also notes that KANs may require fewer parameters than MLPs to approximate the same function, as demonstrated in Liu et al. (2024c), but does not quantify this gap empirically in the present work.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the covering-number and Rademacher-complexity framework for generalization bounds—established for MLPs by Anthony et al. (1999) and Bartlett et al. (2017)—to the fundamentally different KAN architecture introduced by Liu et al. (2024c), where learnable univariate functions replace fixed node activations and linear weight matrices. The iterative covering argument of Proposition 1 generalizes the MLP layer-by-layer decomposition to accommodate arbitrary operator norms at each layer, while the low-rank RKHS analysis (Theorem 4) draws on the metric entropy results of Wang & Zhou (2020) for reduced-rank kernel ridge regression. Key departures from the MLP literature include handling unbounded regression-type losses (via a truncation argument) and introducing a low-rank Sobolev-space analysis that has no direct MLP counterpart.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As KANs are rapidly adopted across scientific domains (PDEs, quantum chemistry, time series, vision), the absence of any theoretical complexity characterization was a notable gap; this paper fills it by providing the first rigorous generalization bounds and a concrete complexity measure (\tilde{\alpha}) that empirically tracks excess loss across six diverse datasets. The tight correlation between \tilde{\alpha} and excess loss suggests a practical regularization strategy, though the paper leaves open whether explicit or implicit control of this measure actually improves generalization. Remaining open questions include deriving matching lower bounds, analyzing SGD-specific behavior for KANs, identifying which operator norms are most natural for practical KAN training, and understanding when KANs provably outperform MLPs in approximation complexity.
