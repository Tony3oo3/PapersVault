## Problem

It was widely believed that only observational (L1) and interventional (L2) distributions could be directly sampled from physical experiments, with counterfactual (L3) distributions deemed fundamentally inaccessible. Although Bareinboim et al. (2015) demonstrated that one specific L3 distribution (the ETT) is realizable via a counterfactual randomization procedure, the general question of which arbitrary L3 distributions are physically realizable under fundamental constraints (e.g., inability to re-run a mechanism on the same unit) remained open.

## Input / Output

The CTF-REALIZE algorithm takes as input an L3-distribution expressed as a set of counterfactual terms (e.g., P(Y_x, X, D_x'')), a causal diagram G, and a set of physical actions A the agent can perform (ranging from no interventions to the maximal feasible set A†(G) containing CTF-RAND for every child of every variable). It outputs either a concrete sequence of physical actions that yields an i.i.d sample from the target distribution, or FAIL if the distribution is non-realizable given the available actions and graph structure. The algorithm is fully general: if A contains no interventions, it returns FAIL for all non-L1 queries; if A contains no CTF-RAND actions, it returns FAIL for all non-L2 queries.

## Method

The paper introduces counterfactual randomization (CTF-RAND), a new physical action that fixes a variable X's value as perceived by a specified subset of its causal children without erasing the unit's natural decision, enabling multiple targeted interventions on the same variable per unit. Under the Fundamental Constraint of Experimentation (FCE) — that a unit can undergo any causal mechanism at most once — the CTF-REALIZE algorithm processes variables in topological order, accumulating necessary intervention conditions for each counterfactual term and detecting conflicts that render the query non-realizable. The algorithm is proven complete (Thm. 3.5), and a clean graphical criterion (Cor. 3.7) characterizes realizability under maximal experimental capabilities: an L3-distribution is realizable iff no variable appears under two different intervention regimes within its counterfactual ancestor set. This framework subsumes the Fundamental Problem of Causal Inference as a special case and demonstrates that counterfactual (L3) strategies provably dominate standard interventional (L2) approaches in causal fairness and multi-armed bandit settings.

## Results

In the causal RL bandit setting (Example 3), the proposed optimal L3 strategy achieves E[Y] = 0.80, outperforming the ETT baseline L3 strategy (0.75), naive L2 randomization (0.70), and the behavioral L1 policy (0.65). In the causal fairness example (Example 2), classifiers trained with L2 fairness measures (μ_int1 + μ_int2) exhibit statistically significant discrimination (μ_ctf > 5%) in nearly 50% of 1000 simulations, whereas classifiers trained directly with the realizable L3 fairness measure μ_ctf nearly always satisfy the fairness requirement. The CTF-REALIZE algorithm is proven complete (Thm. 3.5), and Corollary 3.7 provides a graphical criterion: an L3-distribution is realizable under maximal experimental capabilities iff no variable appears under two different counterfactual regimes in its ancestor set.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary empirical comparison is against the ETT baseline strategy from Bareinboim et al. (2015) and Forney et al. (2017), which samples each unit's natural choice X=x' and randomizes the actual choice to optimize E[Y_x | x']. The proposed optimal L3 strategy, which additionally leverages the realizability of P(Y_x, X, D_{x''}) via multiple CTF-RAND interventions on different children of X, improves E[Y] from 0.75 to 0.80 in the bandit setting (Table 1) and shows lower cumulative regret and higher optimal arm probability in online simulations (Fig. 6c,d). In the causal fairness example, the standard L2 interventional fairness measures (μ_int1, μ_int2) fail to prevent discrimination in ~50% of trained classifiers, while the L3 counterfactual measure μ_ctf — made accessible by the paper's realizability framework — nearly always ensures fairness. The paper also demonstrates that treating the counterfactual variables merely as contextual features (a naive L2 contextual bandit approach) incurs dramatically higher regret than the proposed L3 strategy, confirming that the counterfactual structure is essential rather than incidental.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the counterfactual randomization procedure introduced by Bareinboim, Forney & Pearl (2015) and Forney et al. (2017), which was specific to sampling the ETT distribution P(Y_x, X), into a complete framework for determining which arbitrary L3 counterfactual distributions are physically accessible. It builds on the ancestor-set formalism of Correa et al. (2021) to derive a graphical criterion (Cor. 3.7) and a complete decision algorithm (CTF-REALIZE). The key departure from prior work is shifting the question from identifiability (can we compute L3 from L1/L2 data?) to realizability (can we physically sample L3 directly?), and showing that the Fundamental Problem of Causal Inference is a corollary of a more general physical constraint rather than an independent axiom.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a long-standing open question about the physical accessibility of counterfactual distributions, providing both a complete algorithm and a simple graphical criterion that delineate how far up the Pearl Causal Hierarchy an agent can go via experimentation. The practical implications are significant: in causal fairness, L2-based fairness metrics can misleadingly certify biased classifiers while the realizable L3 measure prevents discrimination; in causal RL, the optimal L3 strategy provably outperforms standard L2 bandit algorithms (UCB, EXP3, Thompson Sampling) under adversarial confounding. Key limitations include the requirement of a known causal graph and the non-trivial feasibility of performing CTF-RAND in practice. Open questions include how realizability interacts with identification and partial identification, and whether the optimality guarantees extend beyond bandit settings to sequential decision-making.
