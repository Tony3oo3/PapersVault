## Problem

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures scale total parameters without proportionally increasing compute, but it remains unclear what performance tradeoffs exist between MoEs and dense transformers. Prior work claimed MoEs are always better per FLOP but worse per parameter, without explaining when or why. This paper asks whether the benefit of MoEs is task-dependent, specifically whether scaling experts helps reasoning tasks as much as it helps memorization tasks.

## Input / Output

For synthetic reasoning, the model receives a token sequence listing all directed edges of an Erdős-Rényi graph (25–55 nodes) followed by a query asking for the shortest path between two vertices, and must autoregressively generate the path. For memorization, the model is trained on a phone-book of 5-letter names mapped to 8-digit numbers and must retrieve the number from a name query without access to the book. For pre-trained models, standard text sequences are used for next-token prediction, with evaluation via exact-match accuracy on world-knowledge, commonsense, and math benchmarks.

## Method

The paper combines theoretical lower/upper bounds with controlled experiments. Theoretically, it proves via communication-complexity arguments that a depth-1 MoE requires width mH = Ω(|V|/log N) to solve the length-2 path problem (equivalent to set-disjointness), while a dense transformer of width |V| suffices, yielding a separation when the number of experts exceeds (log N)². For memorization, it shows an MoE with K = √(n/m) experts needs only Õ(√(nm)) active parameters versus Õ(n) for a dense model. Empirically, it trains Mistral/Mixtral-architecture models (12 layers, top-2 token-choice routing) on synthetic shortest-path and phone-book tasks, then pre-trains a series of dense and MoE models (20 layers, up to 2.1B parameters) on 65B tokens of natural language or math data, evaluating on 19 downstream benchmarks. The key finding is that total parameters predict memorization performance while active parameters (i.e., width) predict reasoning performance, and MoEs exhibit a larger train-test gap on math, suggesting over-memorization.

## Results

On memory-intensive tasks (phone-book memorization, world knowledge benchmarks like TriviaQA and Natural Questions), MoEs with substantially fewer active parameters match dense models at the same total parameter count; notably, an MoE with only 42M active parameters outperforms a dense model with 10× more parameters. On reasoning tasks (shortest path on 50-node graphs, commonsense benchmarks like HellaSwag and WinoGrande, and math benchmarks like GSM8k and Hendrycks-MATH), MoEs consistently underperform dense models at equal total parameters, with active parameters (i.e., model width) being the better predictor of performance. Theoretically, a depth-1 MoE requires width mH = Ω(|V|/log N) to solve length-2 path, while a dense model of width |V| suffices; for K ≥ Ω((log N)²) experts, the MoE cannot solve the task that the parameter-matched dense model can. On memorization, an MoE needs only Õ(√(nm)) active parameters versus Õ(n) for a dense model, yielding a significant computational advantage when n ≫ m.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between dense transformers and MoE transformers (top-2 token-choice routing, 8–64 experts) trained from scratch on identical data. On memorization (phone-book task and world-knowledge QA), MoEs win decisively: at fixed total parameters, all MoE variants (18M, 58M, 200M active params) approximately coincide with the dense model curve, meaning routing effectively leverages all stored parameters. On reasoning (shortest path, commonsense, math), dense transformers win: at the same total parameter budget, MoEs with more experts do not close the gap, and performance tracks active parameters rather than total parameters. At fixed validation perplexity (controlling for training fit), MoEs still outperform dense models on world-knowledge tasks but only match them on reasoning tasks, suggesting MoEs implicitly 'prioritize' memorization. Additionally, MoEs exhibit a larger train-test generalization gap on math problems (GSM8k, Hendrycks-MATH) beyond ~159M parameters, indicating they overfit to training examples more than dense models of comparable size.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the communication-complexity lower bounds of Sanford et al. (2024) on transformer graph reasoning to the MoE setting, showing that the critical width requirement is invariant to the number of experts. For memorization, it builds on Daniely's (2020) result on memorizing Gaussian samples with depth-2 networks to derive an upper bound on MoE active parameters, contrasting with a counting-argument lower bound for dense models. The paper positions itself against the broader MoE scaling literature (Shazeer et al. 2017; Fedus et al. 2022; Lepikhin et al. 2020) which generally asserts MoEs are superior at fixed FLOP budgets, tempering that claim by showing the advantage is task-dependent: MoEs match dense models on memorization but underperform on reasoning at equal total parameters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that MoEs are effective 'memorization machines' but not 'reasoning machines' provides a principled basis for architecture selection: if a deployment is knowledge-intensive, MoEs offer substantial compute savings; if it is reasoning-intensive, increasing width (and thus active compute) remains necessary. Open questions include whether the separation persists at frontier scale (the paper's models top out at ~2.1B parameters), how tasks that mix memorization and reasoning behave, and whether new architectures with sub-quadratic width dependence (e.g., structured matrices) can capture both benefits simultaneously. The paper also raises the concern that MoE memorization may actively harm reasoning via overfitting, as evidenced by larger train-test gaps on math problems.
