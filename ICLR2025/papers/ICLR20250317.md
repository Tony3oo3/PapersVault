## Problem

Instruction-tuned vision-language assistants (VLAs) are increasingly deployed to interact with diverse user populations, yet their gender biases have not been systematically quantified across a broad set of models. Prior work examined bias in CLIP, text-to-image generators, or only a handful of VLAs, leaving unclear whether and how 22+ open-source VLAs (spanning 1B–34B parameters) encode gender stereotypes in personality traits, workplace skills, and occupations, and which debiasing strategies can reduce such biases without degrading general multimodal performance.

## Input / Output

The evaluation pipeline consumes gender- and ethnicity-balanced subsets of 5,000 person images (1,000 each from FairFace, MIAP, Phase, and PATA, with occupation-related content filtered out via InternVL2-40B) paired with 648 prompt variations per group (20 traits, 21 skills, 40 occupations) in a three-option VQA format (yes/no/unsure). The model's next-token prediction probabilities for the 'yes' option symbol are extracted for every image–prompt pair, yielding per-gender distributions P(yes|x, ỹ) that are compared via two-sample t-tests (p < 0.001) to produce a bias ratio (fraction of concepts with a significant gender gap). For debiasing, the same framework is applied to five VLAs, and the output is a modified model whose bias ratio and scores on MMBench, MME, MMMU, and Seed-Bench-2 are reported.

## Method

The VL-Gender benchmark curates gender-balanced image subsets, removes occupation-confounding images using a VLA-based filter validated against GPT-4V (Cohen's κ = 0.72), and probes 22 VLAs with 648 prompt variations per group to ensure results are not artifacts of a single phrasing. Bias is quantified by comparing the mean P(yes) distributions for male- and female-labeled images per concept using a two-sample t-test, and models are ranked by the fraction of concepts showing a significant gap. For mitigation, five techniques are applied: full fine-tuning, LoRA fine-tuning (rank 128), prompt tuning (20 learnable embeddings after BOS), structural pruning (importance scores combining a bias loss and an MME performance loss), and prompt engineering (pre/appended fairness instructions). All optimization-based methods minimize a loss that pushes P(yes) and P(no) toward 0.5, deliberately avoiding the 'unsure' option to prevent the model from simply learning to refuse. The key finding is that fine-tuning achieves the best bias-reduction-to-performance-retention trade-off: full fine-tuning debiases more aggressively at greater performance cost, while LoRA offers a more conservative alternative with minimal downstream degradation.

## Results

Across 22 open-source VLAs, all models exhibit strong gender bias: >65% of personality traits show a significant male-female probability gap, with negative traits (e.g., 'moody', 'arrogant') more associated with males and positive traits with females. For occupations, the most biased models (InternVL2 series, Bunny series) show >80% of 40 occupations with significant gender bias, replicating real-world U.S. labor imbalances. Among five debiasing methods, LoRA fine-tuning achieves the best trade-off, reducing the biased-occupation ratio from 0.56 to 0.38 while retaining 86.4% of MME score (1532 vs. 1774) and 90.4% of MMBench (0.54 vs. 0.60). Full fine-tuning is more aggressive (occupation bias 0.29) but costs more performance (MME 1470, −17.1%), while Prompt Tuning eliminates bias most (0.15) but catastrophically degrades performance (MME 762, −57%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares five debiasing techniques against the original (unmodified) VLA. Prompt Tuning achieves the strongest bias reduction across all three prompt groups (P. Traits 0.14, Skills 0.02, Occ. 0.15 vs. original 0.86/0.48/0.56) but at a catastrophic performance cost: MME drops 57% (1774→762) and MMBench drops 59%, making it impractical for deployment. Prompt Engineering is the safest for performance (MME only −2.5%, MMBench −10.9%) but barely reduces bias (Occ. 0.41 vs. 0.56). Pruning shows a clear dose-response: at α=0.1 it reduces occupation bias to 0.33 with moderate performance loss (MME −16.9%), but at α=0.5 performance collapses (MME −51.3%, MMBench −91.9%) while bias reduction plateaus. Full Fine-tuning strikes a middle ground, reducing occupation bias to 0.29 and personality-trait bias to 0.38, with a notable improvement on MMMU (+22.9%) but a 17% MME drop. LoRA Fine-tuning is the recommended default: it preserves the most general performance (MME −13.6%, MMBench −9.6%, Seed-Bench-2 −4.8%) while still meaningfully reducing bias (Skills 0.48→0.22, Occ. 0.56→0.38), making it the best trade-off for practical deployment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the growing line of work on gender bias in vision-language models (Sathe et al. 2024; Fraser & Kiritchenko 2024; Howard et al. 2024; Ruggeri & Nozza 2023) by scaling evaluation to 22 open-source VLAs and simultaneously probing three bias dimensions—personality traits, work-related skills, and occupations—using a VQA-based protocol with extensive prompt variations. For debiasing, it repurposes established parameter-efficient techniques (LoRA, Prompt Tuning, structural Pruning) and prompt engineering into a unified comparison framework, building directly on Hu et al. (2022), Lester et al. (2021), Ma et al. (2023), and Nahon et al. (2024). The VQA evaluation design departs from prior VLA bias studies that relied on free-form generation requiring GPT-4V or human scoring, instead leveraging next-token probabilities for a fully automated, reproducible measurement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As instruction-tuned VLAs like LLaVA and InternVL2 are deployed in consumer-facing applications, the finding that they systematically attribute negative traits to men, positive traits to women, and replicate real-world occupational imbalances underscores an urgent need for pre-deployment bias auditing. The practical recommendation that LoRA fine-tuning offers the best debiasing-to-performance trade-off gives practitioners a concrete, low-cost intervention path. Key limitations remain: the analysis is restricted to binary gender, excludes intersectional effects (e.g., gender × ethnicity), and the debiasing evaluation covers only five of the 22 models, leaving open whether findings generalize across architectures and scales.
