## Problem

The prevailing understanding in recommender systems holds that language models and traditional collaborative filtering (CF) recommenders learn fundamentally distinct representation spaces—one for language semantics and one for user behavior—requiring explicit alignment to bridge them. This paper challenges that assumption by asking whether LMs already implicitly encode collaborative signals (user preference similarities) within their language representation space, and whether a behavior space for recommendation can be derived directly from it without ID-based embeddings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253298/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes item title text (e.g., 'Batman Begins', 'Tomb Raider III') processed by a frozen LM (default: text-embeddings-3-large) to produce fixed-dimensional language representations, combined with a binary user-item interaction matrix. User representations are computed as the mean of their historically interacted items' language representations. At inference, an optional free-text user intention query (e.g., generated by ChatGPT) can be blended into the user representation via a scalar weight α to refine recommendations. Output is a ranked list of items scored by cosine similarity between user and item behavior representations.

## Method

The paper first establishes via linear mapping (a single trainable matrix W trained with InfoNCE loss) that advanced LM representations of item titles, when projected into a behavior space, outperform leading ID-based CF models—evidence of a homomorphism between language and behavior spaces. Building on this, AlphaRec replaces the linear matrix with a two-layer MLP (LeakyReLU) for nonlinear projection, applies K layers of LightGCN-style symmetric-normalized graph convolution to capture high-order collaborative patterns, and optimizes with the InfoNCE contrastive loss (no data augmentation). The entire pipeline uses only frozen LM text representations as item features—no ID embeddings—yet outperforms XSimGCL, RLMRec, and other leading CF baselines by 6–10% on Recall across Amazon datasets. The model also exhibits three additional properties: rapid convergence (language representations serve as good initialization), strong zero-shot transfer to unseen datasets when co-trained on multiple sources, and the ability to incorporate text-based user intentions at inference time without retraining.

## Results

AlphaRec, built solely on language representations (text-embeddings-3-large) with a two-layer MLP, graph convolution, and InfoNCE loss, consistently outperforms all ID-based CF baselines across three Amazon datasets. On Recall@20, AlphaRec achieves 0.1221 (Movies&TV), 0.1519 (Video Games), and 0.0991 (Books), improving 6.79%–9.75% over the best baseline (RLMRec). In zero-shot settings on entirely new datasets (MovieLens-1M, Book Crossing, Amazon Industrial & Scientific), AlphaRec surpasses the best zero-shot baseline by 30%–157% and even approaches or exceeds fully-trained LightGCN. For user intention capture, AlphaRec with intention achieves HR@5 of 0.4704 on MovieLens-1M, far exceeding the TEM baseline (0.2738).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AlphaRec outperforms all 13 baselines across every dataset and metric. Against classical CF methods (MF, MultVAE, LightGCN), AlphaRec leads by 10–40+ percentage points on Recall, confirming that language representations alone surpass ID-based embeddings. Among contrastive-learning CF methods (SGL, BC Loss, XSimGCL), AlphaRec improves Recall@20 by 15–35% over the strongest (XSimGCL at 0.1057 on Movies&TV vs. AlphaRec's 0.1221). The closest competitors are the LM-enhanced methods KAR and RLMRec, which combine ID embeddings with language representations; AlphaRec still leads RLMRec by 6.79–9.75% on Recall, suggesting that purely language-based representations with appropriate architecture outperform hybrid ID+language designs. In zero-shot evaluation, AlphaRec (trained on three Amazon datasets) beats VQ-Rec by 41–157% on Recall and surpasses fully-trained LightGCN on MovieLens-1M (0.1486 vs. 0.2019 Recall, though LightGCN is trained on that dataset). For intention-aware recommendation, AlphaRec with intention (HR@5 = 0.4704) dramatically outperforms TEM (0.2738) on MovieLens-1M, and in zero-shot ranking against LLMRank, AlphaRec achieves 19–58% higher NDCG across all K values.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the linear probing/mapping methodology from LM interpretability (Merullo et al., 2023; Alain & Bengio, 2017) to test whether collaborative signals are implicitly encoded in language representation spaces, a question largely unexplored in the recommendation domain. For the model design, AlphaRec combines three well-established components—MLP projection, LightGCN-style graph convolution (He et al., 2021), and InfoNCE contrastive loss (van den Oord et al., 2018)—but applies them exclusively to frozen LM text embeddings rather than ID-based embeddings, departing from LM-enhanced CF methods (KAR, RLMRec) that merely supplement ID embeddings with language features. The zero-shot and intention-aware analyses extend the CF task into settings previously addressed only by sequential recommendation methods (ZESRec, UniSRec, VQ-Rec) or search-oriented models (TEM).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding of possible homomorphism between advanced LM representation spaces and effective behavior spaces challenges the prevailing assumption that language modeling and behavior modeling learn fundamentally distinct spaces, suggesting that collaborative filtering signals are already latent in sufficiently large LMs. This opens a path toward more generalizable recommenders that are zero-shot capable across domains, fast to train, and naturally intention-aware—properties that ID-based CF models structurally lack. Key limitations include the absence of theoretical guarantees for the homomorphism claim, the lack of personalized architecture (a single shared MLP for all users), and the fixed, non-personalized user intention mechanism. Open questions include whether the homomorphism holds for multimodal inputs, whether it can be formally characterized, and how to scale the approach to industrial-scale item catalogs with richer metadata.
