## Problem

Gradient-based attribution methods like Integrated Gradients depend on a static baseline (black, blurred, or noised image) to avoid saturation, but these baselines inject post-hoc colour, texture, or frequency assumptions that are alien to the model's own decision rule, producing biased, fragile, and adversarially manipulable saliency maps. The optimal baseline varies per task-model-image combination, yet no principled, adaptive mechanism exists to discover it.

## Input / Output

Given an RGB image x, a trained model F(·,θ), and a target class c, UNI first computes an unlearned model θ̂ via a single gradient-ascent step on the loss for (x,y), then finds a bounded perturbation δ (‖δ‖₂ ≤ ε=0.25) via PGD so that F(x+δ;θ) ≈ F(x;θ̂) in KL divergence. The output is a pixel-wise signed saliency map A_i^UNI(x) computed by Riemann-sum Integrated Gradients along the short path from the unlearned baseline x'=x+δ to x, plus the baseline image itself. In the NLP extension, the input is a token embedding and the output is activation-level attribution scores on the residual stream.

## Method

UNI decouples baseline discovery from attribution computation in two stages. First, it performs a first-order unlearning step in model space: θ̂ = θ + η·∇_θ L(F_c(x;θ),y)/‖∇_θ L‖, which locally smooths the decision boundary and reduces the model's confidence on the target class. Second, it solves a PGD optimisation in input space to find the closest perturbation δ such that the original model's full output distribution on x+δ matches the unlearned model's output on x (minimising KL divergence), yielding a featureless, image-specific baseline x'=x+δ. Attribution is then computed via standard Integrated Gradients along the short, low-curvature, monotonically-increasing-confidence path from x' to x. The key insight is that targeted unlearning erases exactly the salient features the model uses for its prediction, so the resulting baseline is 'featureless' by construction without imposing any external colour, scale, or geometric assumptions, and the local smoothing of the decision boundary reduces gradient variability under small perturbations, improving robustness.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K across six architectures (ResNet-18, EfficientNet-v2-s, ConvNeXt-T, VGG-16-bn, ViT-B-16, Swin-T), UNI achieves the highest MuFidelity scores (0.12–0.18) and the lowest deletion AUC (0.06–0.19) among all compared methods. UNI also attains the best insertion AUC (0.56–0.71) and the strongest robustness to FGSM attribution attacks, with Spearman correlation of 0.27–0.32 versus 0.01–0.14 for all baselines. Path monotonicity (Spearman) reaches 0.89–0.99 for UNI compared to 0.27–0.88 for static-baseline methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UNI outperforms all seven baselines on deletion AUC (lower is better) and insertion AUC (higher is better) across every architecture, with the largest margins against BlurIG (e.g., 0.06 vs 0.27 deletion AUC on ResNet-18; 0.64 vs 0.34 insertion AUC). On MuFidelity, UNI leads on 5 of 6 models; the sole exception is ConvNeXt-Tiny where GIG (0.18) slightly edges UNI (0.16), though both are within one standard deviation. Against IG (black baseline), UNI improves MuFidelity by roughly 2× on ResNet-18 (0.12 vs 0.06) and VGG-16-bn (0.18 vs 0.08). On robustness to FGSM attribution attacks, UNI dominates all methods by a wide margin: Spearman correlation of 0.27–0.32 versus 0.01–0.14 for IG, BlurIG, SG, and DeepLIFT, and top-1000 pixel intersection of 35–41% versus 15–26% for baselines. AGI and GBP are the closest competitors on MuFidelity for some architectures but still trail UNI on deletion/insertion AUC and robustness. Simple Gradients (SG) and DeepLIFT are consistently the weakest baselines, confirming the paper's argument that static or unguided gradient methods produce fragile, biased attributions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Integrated Gradients (IG) path-attribution framework, replacing its static baseline (black, blurred, or noised image) with a dynamically learned baseline derived from machine unlearning. Conceptually, it departs from the long line of work on alternative static baselines (Sturmfels et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2020; Akhtar & Jalwana, 2023) by reframing the baseline problem as one of post-hoc bias injection—showing that fixed transformations implicitly impose colour, texture, or frequency assumptions alien to the model's own decision rule. The key novelty is repurposing the machine unlearning literature (Bourtoule et al., 2021; Ginart et al., 2019) for interpretability: a single steepest-ascent step in model space defines an 'unlearned' reference, and activation matching via KL divergence then mines a featureless, input-specific baseline in image space. The paper also extends the framework to NLP by connecting UNI to activation patching and attribution patching in mechanistic interpretability (Heimersheim & Nanda, 2024; Nanda, 2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that widely-used static baselines systematically inject biases not present in the pretrained model is a conceptual reframing that had been largely overlooked, and it provides a principled justification for moving beyond ad-hoc baseline choices. The unlearning-based approach is computationally lightweight (one gradient ascent step plus a short PGD loop) and consistently improves faithfulness, robustness to adversarial perturbation, and path monotonicity across six diverse architectures, suggesting broad practical utility. Open questions include whether the approach scales to higher-level semantic concepts (unlearning concept-clustered exemplars rather than single samples), whether certified second-order unlearning further improves attribution quality, and how the method interacts with models trained under different inductive biases or robustness objectives. The NLP extension remains preliminary, with no hyperparameter tuning and only three small models tested.
