## Problem

Existing generative models for structure-based drug design are typically evaluated on absolute metric values (docking scores, drug-likeness) rather than their actual training objective of learning the data distribution, making fair comparison difficult. Additionally, prior methods handle molecule size as a fixed hyperparameter, lack built-in uncertainty estimation for out-of-distribution detection, and do not jointly model protein side chain flexibility during ligand generation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253063/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes fixed protein backbone Cα coordinates and amino acid types as conditioning context, with the protein represented coarsely as one node per residue augmented by difference vectors to all heavy atoms for full atomic detail. It outputs 3D ligand atom coordinates, discrete atom types (13 categories), bond types (5 categories), and per-atom uncertainty scores; the FLEXFLOW extension additionally outputs up to five side chain torsion angles per pocket residue. Molecule size is not pre-specified: the model samples from a learned size distribution and uses virtual 'no-atom' nodes (up to N_max=10 extra) that are pruned during sampling, enabling adaptive sizing.

## Method

DRUGFLOW is a heterogeneous GNN backbone (GVP-based, with separate weights for ligand/protein nodes and four edge types) that simultaneously predicts continuous velocities for ligand coordinates via Euclidean flow matching (ICFM with linear interpolation), discrete type logits for atom and bond types via Markov bridge models, and (in FLEXFLOW) tangent-space velocities for side chain angles via Riemannian flow matching on the hypertorus with a polynomial scheduler κ(t)=(1-t)³. Three conceptual novelties distinguish it: (1) an end-to-end uncertainty head trained with a Gaussian-likelihood loss on the flow matching regression error, integrated over the sampling trajectory to yield per-atom OOD scores; (2) virtual nodes added during training from U(0, N_max) that the model learns to label as 'no atom,' enabling adaptive molecule sizing without a separate size estimator; (3) a multi-domain preference alignment (MDPA) loss that extends DPO by computing weighted loss differences across the flow matching and Markov bridge domains simultaneously, regularized by the original training loss on both winning and losing samples.

## Results

DRUGFLOW achieves a Fréchet ChemNet Distance (FCD) of 4.278, substantially outperforming POCKET2MOL (12.703), DIFFSBDD (11.637), and TARGETDIFF (13.766). On the joint distribution metric (JSD_all over QED, SA, logP, and Vina efficiency), DRUGFLOW scores 0.099 versus 0.223–0.274 for all baselines. DRUGFLOW attains the best Wasserstein distance on 9 of 11 individual metrics in Table 1, including bond angles (e.g., C-C=C: 0.952 vs. 2.17–4.28) and QED (0.014 vs. 0.050–0.072).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DRUGFLOW outperforms all three baselines on the vast majority of distribution-matching metrics. Against POCKET2MOL (autoregressive), DRUGFLOW wins on every metric except bond-type JSD (0.060 vs. 0.044) and hydrophobic interaction counts (0.036 vs. 0.027), while dominating on bond angles (e.g., C-C-O: 1.941 vs. 3.938) and FCD (4.278 vs. 12.703). Against DIFFSBDD (equivariant diffusion), DRUGFLOW is superior on all 11 Table-1 metrics, with particularly large gaps on bond angles (C-C-C: 2.269 vs. 8.166) and SA (0.317 vs. 1.570). Against TARGETDIFF (3D equivariant diffusion), DRUGFLOW wins on 10 of 11 metrics; TARGETDIFF retains a narrow edge only on logP (0.489 vs. 0.665). The joint-distribution metric JSD_all (0.099 vs. 0.223–0.274) and FCD (4.278 vs. 11.6–13.8) confirm that DRUGFLOW captures the training distribution far more faithfully than any baseline across the full multi-dimensional property space.

## Relation to Prior Work

DRUGFLOW unifies two previously separate generative paradigms—Euclidean flow matching (Lipman et al., 2022) for continuous coordinates and Markov bridge models (Igashov et al., 2023) for discrete atom/bond types—into a single heterogeneous GNN backbone, extending the ICFM variant (Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden, 2022) with an uncertainty head and virtual nodes for adaptive molecule size. For protein flexibility, it applies Riemannian flow matching (Chen & Lipman, 2023) on the hypertorus of side chain torsion angles, a setting previously explored only in isolation (Lee & Kim, 2024; Lin et al., 2024). The preference alignment scheme generalizes DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) and its diffusion-model adaptation (Wallace et al., 2024) to operate simultaneously across both the continuous flow-matching and discrete Markov-bridge domains, which prior work (Cheng et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024) had not addressed jointly.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's most impactful contribution is methodological: it argues that generative drug design models should be evaluated by how faithfully they reproduce the training distribution (Wasserstein/JSD distances, FCD) rather than by absolute property scores, and provides a comprehensive benchmark demonstrating DRUGFLOW's state-of-the-art distribution learning. Practically, the multi-domain preference alignment offers a lightweight fine-tuning path to steer a pre-trained model toward desired properties without retraining from scratch, and FLEXFLOW opens the door to drug design against unbound protein conformations. Limitations include the fixed upper bound on virtual nodes (N_max=10), the narrow dynamic range of uncertainty scores (0.85–0.92), and FLEXFLOW's degraded performance on pocket-interaction metrics due to the added complexity of joint sampling. Open questions include whether scaling N_max enables fully adaptive size selection and whether the distribution-learning evaluation framework generalizes beyond the CrossDocked benchmark.
