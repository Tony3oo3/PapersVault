## Problem

Despite the empirical success of Transformer-based GNNs, the mechanistic reasons why attention architectures adapt well to graph-structured data remain poorly understood. Existing expressivity analyses rely on universal approximation arguments requiring prohibitive parameter counts, offering little insight into what specific graph operations Transformers actually compute. This paper asks whether a linear Transformer with access to graph structure only through the incidence matrix can implement canonical graph algorithms, and if so, with what error guarantees.

## Input / Output

The Transformer receives the graph's incidence matrix B ∈ R^{n×d} (encoding edge-vertex incidence with resistance-weighted entries) and optional demand vectors ψ_i ∈ R^n as its sole structural input—no positional encoding, adjacency matrix, or other structural features are provided. The output is read from specific rows of the final layer state Z_L: rows corresponding to the demand block yield L†ψ (electric flow), √L†ψ (resistive embedding), e^{-sL}ψ (heat kernel), or eigenvector columns. In the molecular regression experiment, the output serves as a learned positional encoding fed into a standard Graph Transformer for predicting constrained solubility on QM9 and ZINC.

## Method

The paper constructs explicit weight configurations (W^V, W^Q, W^K, W^R) for a linear Transformer whose attention module computes W^V Z Z^T W^{Q⊤} W^K Z, showing that this single operation can realize matrix products like BB^⊤ = L and Gram matrices needed for orthogonalization. Five constructions are proved: (1) gradient descent on the dual electric-flow objective (O(log(1/ε)) layers), (2) truncated power series for √L† and e^{-sL}, (3) a multiplicative polynomial expansion achieving O(log log(1/ε)) layers for L†, and (4) subspace iteration where self-attention computes QR decomposition of feature columns to extract top-k or bottom-k Laplacian eigenvectors. A parameter-efficient variant reduces weight matrices from O(d²+k²) to O(k²) by constraining B-blocks to scalar multiples of identity, while preserving equivariance to edge permutations. Experiments on synthetic graphs confirm exponential loss decay with depth, and on ZINC the learned PE outperforms Laplacian eigenvector PE by ~30% regression loss using fewer than 0.7% additional parameters.

## Results

On the ZINC molecular regression task (constrained solubility), replacing the Laplacian eigenvector positional encoding (LapPE) with a learned linear Transformer PE reduces the regression loss from 0.201±0.0034 to 0.138±0.012, a ~30% improvement, while adding fewer than 0.7% extra parameters (488 vs. 800,771). On QM9, the linear Transformer PE achieves 0.221±0.0060, essentially matching LapPE (0.227±0.0094), which the authors attribute to QM9 molecules being much smaller (avg. 9 nodes) than ZINC (avg. 23 nodes). Both PE variants substantially outperform the no-PE baseline (0.286 on ZINC, 0.419 on QM9).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against the Graph Transformer (GT) from Dwivedi & Bresson (2020) in three configurations: no positional encoding, Laplacian eigenvector PE (LapPE), and the proposed learned linear Transformer PE. On ZINC, the linear Transformer PE clearly wins, reducing loss from 0.201 (LapPE) to 0.138, a 31% relative improvement, while adding only 488 trainable parameters to the 800,771-parameter GT. On QM9, the linear Transformer PE (0.221) essentially ties LapPE (0.227), with the small gap within noise; the authors conjecture that QM9's smaller molecules (avg. 9 nodes, 19 edges vs. ZINC's 23 nodes, 40 edges) leave little room for a learned PE to improve over the spectral one. In both cases, adding any PE (LapPE or linear Transformer) dramatically outperforms the no-PE GT (0.286 on ZINC, 0.419 on QM9), confirming the importance of structural information for molecular property prediction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on understanding what Transformers can compute (Vaswani et al., 2017; Von Oswald et al., 2023; Charton, 2021) to the graph domain, providing explicit sparse weight configurations that make a linear Transformer implement gradient descent, power series, multiplicative polynomial expansion, and subspace iteration on graph Laplacians. It departs from prior expressivity analyses of attention-based GNNs (Kim et al., 2021; 2022; Kreuzer et al., 2021) that rely on universal approximation arguments by instead giving constructive, layer-by-layer algorithmic interpretations with explicit error bounds. The molecular regression experiments build directly on the Graph Transformer (Dwivedi & Bresson, 2020) and its Laplacian eigenvector positional encoding, while the subspace iteration construction draws on the block power method (Bentbib & Kanber, 2015).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first mechanistic account of how a linear Transformer processes graph-structured data, showing that the self-attention module is not merely a black-box function approximator but can be interpreted as executing specific numerical linear algebra routines. The parameter-efficient implementation (Eq. 7) with built-in edge-permutation invariance/equivariance offers a practical design principle for graph Transformers. Key limitations include synthetic experiments restricted to n=10 graphs, and the molecular regression validation on only two datasets where the QM9 result shows no improvement over LapPE. Open questions include whether these constructions extend to standard (softmax) Transformers, what the optimization landscape looks like for learning these weight configurations, and whether the learned PE advantage on ZINC generalizes to larger or more diverse molecular benchmarks.
