## Problem

Unsupervised zero-shot RL methods can pre-train promptable policies without task-specific learning, but they struggle in high-dimensional, unstable environments like humanoid control because unsupervised exploration yields poor coverage and unsupervised losses provide limited inductive bias on which behaviors to favor. Existing approaches that incorporate demonstrations (e.g., ASE, CALM) either require hierarchical RL for downstream tasks or collapse to reproducing only demonstrated behaviors. No prior method produces a single model for a complex humanoid that handles motion tracking, goal reaching, and reward optimization all in zero-shot while expressing human-like motions.

## Input / Output

At pre-training, the agent receives observation-only motion-capture trajectories from AMASS (8,902 motions, ~29 hours) with no action labels, plus online proprioceptive transitions (358D state: 70D body pose, 144D body rotation, 144D linear/angular velocities, all normalized w.r.t. facing direction and root position). At inference, the model is prompted with one of three task specifications—a reward function (45 designed rewards), a goal state (50 stable poses), or a target motion trajectory—and outputs a 69D vector of normalized PD target joint torques in [-1,1]^69 at 30 Hz control frequency. The zero-shot inference requires no additional learning, planning, or fine-tuning: the prompt is mapped to a latent vector z via the learned backward embedding B, and the policy π_z is executed directly.

## Method

FB-CPR grounds the unsupervised Forward-Backward (FB) representation learning toward imitating unlabeled behaviors by adding a conditional policy regularization term. The key mechanism is a latent-conditional discriminator D(s,z) trained with a GAN-like objective to distinguish between states visited by FB policies π_z and states from the motion dataset, where each trajectory τ is embedded into the policy latent space via ER_FB(τ) = (1/ℓ)Σ B(s_i). The actor loss combines the standard FB policy improvement F(s,a,z)ᵀz with a critic Q trained via off-policy TD on the discriminator's log-ratio reward log(D/(1−D)), yielding L = −E[F(s,a,z)ᵀz + αQ(s,a,z)]. The latent sampling distribution ν is a three-component mixture (ER_FB of dataset trajectories, B(s) for goal-reaching, and uniform over the hypersphere) to balance imitation fidelity, goal coverage, and generalization. Training is fully online with off-policy updates, interleaving environment rollouts with simultaneous updates to F, B, the discriminator, the critic, and the actor.

## Results

FB-CPR achieves 73.4% of the best single-task (top-line) algorithm's performance across all three evaluation categories (reward, goal-reaching, tracking) in a fully zero-shot manner. On reward optimization, FB-CPR scores 151.68 (61% of TD3's 249.74), on goal-reaching it achieves 0.68 proximity and 0.48 success (vs. TD3's 0.98/0.98), and on motion tracking it reaches 1.37 EMD and 0.83 success rate (vs. PHC's 1.14 EMD and 0.94 success). The normalized score (SCORE_norm) against the best algorithm per metric ranges from 0.48 (goal success) to 0.88 (tracking success), demonstrating strong zero-shot generalization without any task-specific training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the closest zero-shot BFM baseline ASE, FB-CPR outperforms by more than 1.4× in every task category (e.g., 151.68 vs. 105.73 on reward, 0.48 vs. 0.22 on goal success, 0.83 vs. 0.40 on tracking success). Compared to the specialized goal-conditioned baselines Goal-TD3 and Goal-GAIL, FB-CPR matches or slightly exceeds Goal-TD3 on goal proximity (0.68 vs. 0.67) and goal success (0.48 vs. 0.44), while Goal-GAIL is substantially worse across all metrics. On motion tracking, FB-CPR (1.37 EMD, 0.83 success) is competitive with the dedicated tracking algorithm PHC (1.66 EMD, 0.82 success) and clearly outperforms CALM (1.67 EMD, 0.71 success). Against planning-based methods, FB-CPR achieves 85% of Oracle MPPI's reward (151.68 vs. 178.50) but with ~150× lower inference cost (12 s vs. 30 min per episode), and 177% of Diffuser's reward (151.68 vs. 85.27). The single-task TD3 baseline remains the strongest on reward (249.74) and goal (0.98), but requires retraining from scratch for each individual task, whereas FB-CPR handles all tasks zero-shot from a single pre-trained model.

## Relation to Prior Work

FB-CPR extends the Forward-Backward (FB) successor-representation framework (Touati & Ollivier, 2021; Touati et al., 2023) by adding a conditional policy regularization term that grounds unsupervised zero-shot RL toward imitating unlabeled motion-capture trajectories. The trajectory encoding via ER_FB is inherited from Pirotta et al. (2024), but here it is repurposed not as a standalone imitation criterion but as the latent-space embedding that a GAN-like discriminator uses to match policy-induced state distributions to the behavior dataset. This departs from prior work in two key ways: unlike pure zero-shot RL (which collapses in high-dimensional unstable environments) and unlike imitation-based methods like CALM or ASE (which require hierarchical RL for downstream tasks), FB-CPR retains zero-shot inference while being regularized by behaviors, and unlike sequence models (transformers, diffusion) it is not limited to reproducing training-time policies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first behavioral foundation model for whole-body humanoid control that handles reward optimization, goal reaching, and motion tracking in a unified zero-shot setting, achieving 73% of task-specific top-line performance without any per-task training. The human evaluation showing 83% human-likeness preference over reward-optimized TD3 highlights a practical advantage: motion regularization compensates for underspecified reward functions, a common pain point in character animation and robotics. Key limitations include reliance on expensive motion-capture data, restriction to proprioceptive observations (no navigation or object interaction), and degraded performance on ground-level locomotion tasks far from the mocap distribution. Open questions include theoretical guarantees for the regularization, extension to video-based pretraining, and direct language-to-policy alignment.
