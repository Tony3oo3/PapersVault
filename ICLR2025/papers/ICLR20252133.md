## Problem

When two predictive models have equivalent accuracy but disagree on individual predictions, prior reconciliation methods (e.g., Roth et al. 2023) align predictions almost everywhere but can inadvertently cause downstream decision-makers to take actions with substantially higher losses. The paper formalizes this failure mode and asks: how can we reconcile model multiplicity so that the updated models not only agree on predictions but also induce consistent, low-loss best-response actions for a finite family of downstream decision-making tasks?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252133/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two pre-trained predictors f₁, f₂: X → [0,1]ᵈ (e.g., pre-trained image classifiers), a finite family of linear loss functions ℒ over a finite action set A, and an i.i.d. validation dataset D drawn independently of the predictors. It outputs a pair of reconciled predictors (f₁ᵀ, f₂ᵀ) that approximately agree on both individual probability predictions and best-response actions for every loss function in ℒ, with the guarantee that downstream decision losses do not increase by more than Tᵢβ√d·A. The loss functions are assumed linear in y (ℓ(y,a) = ⟨y, ℓₐ⟩), and the label space is bounded in [0,1]ᵈ, accommodating both regression and multi-class classification via one-hot encoding.

## Method

ReDCal (Algorithm 2) iteratively identifies the largest disagreement event E_{ℓ,a₁,a₂} — a region where the two models select different best-response actions with a loss-gap margin α — and patches the model whose estimated loss on that event deviates most from the empirical loss. Each patch is a two-stage update: first, the model is shifted by the mean prediction error on the disagreement event (reducing Brier score, analogous to prior Reconcile), then a Decision Calibration subroutine (Algorithm 1, adapted from Zhao et al. 2021) further refines the model so it is β-decision-calibrated within the event, ensuring the induced best-response action has accurate loss estimation. The loop terminates when all disagreement events have probability mass below η. The key theoretical result (Theorem 3.3) guarantees that Brier scores strictly decrease, downstream decision losses increase by at most Tᵢβ√d·A, and the final models agree on best-response actions almost everywhere, with total runtime bounded by O(d·B(f₁)+B(f₂))/(α²η). The approach extends to k>2 predictors via a 'contestation' scheme (Algorithm 4) and to infinite action spaces.

## Results

On ImageNet (100 randomly sampled classes, 10 actions, 500 runs), ReDCal converges within a similar number of time-steps as Reconcile but achieves a larger reduction in LossGap (the gap between the predictor's best-response loss and the optimal predictor's loss) on the test set. When used as a post-process after Decision-Calibration terminates, ReDCal further decreases the LossGap beyond what Decision-Calibration alone achieves. On the HAM10000 skin-lesion dataset (7 classes, 10 runs), the same pattern holds: ReDCal outperforms both Reconcile and Decision-Calibration in reducing downstream decision loss. The paper reports results as convergence curves (Figures 2 and 3) rather than a single summary table, with shaded regions indicating ±1 standard error.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares ReDCal against two baselines: Reconcile (Roth et al., 2023) and Decision-Calibration (Zhao et al., 2021). Against Reconcile, ReDCal converges at a comparable rate (similar number of time-steps) but achieves a strictly larger reduction in LossGap on both ImageNet and HAM10000, meaning the reconciled models produce best-response actions closer to the optimal predictor's actions. Against Decision-Calibration, ReDCal used as a post-process further reduces the LossGap beyond what Decision-Calibration alone achieves, demonstrating that the two methods are complementary. The paper also provides a theoretical counterexample (Theorem 2.7) showing that Reconcile alone can *increase* downstream decision loss by a constant 1/2 even when the two models agree on predictions almost everywhere, which ReDCal avoids through its additional decision-calibration step. Overall, ReDCal wins on both comparisons across both datasets, with the advantage being most pronounced in the decision-loss metric rather than raw Brier score.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model multiplicity reconciliation framework of Roth et al. (2023), which aligns individual predictions between two equally-accurate models, by adding a decision-calibration step drawn from Zhao et al. (2021) and the broader multi-calibration machinery of Hebert-Johnson et al. (2018). The key conceptual departure is showing that Roth et al.'s Reconcile procedure, while making predictions agree almost everywhere, can actually increase downstream decision loss — motivating the two-stage ReDCal algorithm that first patches predictions on disagreement events and then calibrates the patched model to be decision-calibrated within those events. The paper also generalizes from two to k predictors via a 'contestation' mechanism and from finite to infinite action spaces.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a genuine and somewhat counterintuitive gap in the model multiplicity literature: making two predictors agree on individual probabilities does not guarantee they will induce the same (or better) downstream decisions, and can even make decisions worse. The theoretical guarantees (bounded loss increase, convergence in O(d·B/α²η) steps) provide a principled stopping criterion for practitioners who must choose between equivalent models. Limitations include the assumption of linear-in-y loss functions, the need for a validation dataset independent of model training, and the fact that the empirical evaluation uses randomly generated loss functions rather than domain-specific ones, leaving open how well the method performs under realistic, structured loss functions in deployment settings.
