## Problem

Modern multi-session neural recordings capture different subsets of neurons across sessions, making it impossible to align data into a single array or apply standard tensor factorization. Existing approaches either fit each session independently (losing cross-session information and conflating session-specific with global dynamics) or use deep networks that merge sessions into uninterpretable weights. The paper addresses how to learn a unified, interpretable model of non-stationary, non-linear brain-wide dynamics from these asynchronous, non-aligned observations.

## Input / Output

Input consists of D asynchronous firing-rate matrices Y^d ∈ R^{N^d × T^d} (spike-sorted units convolved with a 30 ms Gaussian kernel), where sessions differ in neuron count, duration, and brain-area coverage, plus per-neuron area labels. Output includes per-session block-diagonal sparse ensemble composition matrices A^d (one block per brain area), ensemble trajectories x^d in a shared p-dimensional latent space, K global p×p sub-circuit transition matrices {f_k}, and per-session time-varying sparse coefficients c_kt that weight each sub-circuit at each time point. The block-diagonal structure enforces that each ensemble contains only same-area neurons, and the shared sub-circuit dictionary enables cross-session functional alignment of ensembles despite differing observed neurons.

## Method

CREIMBO models ensemble interactions as a non-stationary linear dynamical system x_{t+1} = F_t x_t where F_t is a sparse time-varying linear combination of K global, session-invariant sub-circuits: F_t = Σ_k c_kt f_k. Training alternates three steps: (1) per-session ensemble identification via graph-driven re-weighted ℓ1 regularization (adapted from SIBBLINGS) that groups co-active same-area neurons into sparse ensembles using a data-driven Gaussian similarity graph; (2) joint inference of latent states and sub-circuit coefficients via a LASSO formulation coupling the observation model y_t = a x_t with the dynamical constraint x_{t+1} = (Σ_k c_kt f_k) x_t; and (3) sub-circuit dictionary update via sparse ℓ1-regularized regression with a de-correlation penalty to ensure distinct f_k capture different interaction motifs. The key architectural insight is that the shared sub-circuit basis across sessions acts as a functional alignment mechanism: even when sessions observe entirely different neurons, the common dictionary of ensemble interactions constrains the latent space so that the j-th ensemble in session d corresponds functionally to the j-th ensemble in session d', enabling unified inference without requiring neuron-level overlap.

## Results

On synthetic data (K=3 sub-circuits, 5 sessions), CREIMBO recovers all ground-truth components (observations, ensemble compositions, latent dynamics, sub-circuits, and coefficients) with high correlation across 312 random initializations. On human high-density electrode recordings (21 subjects, 41 sessions, 10 brain areas), CREIMBO accurately reconstructs firing rates and identifies 12 sparse sub-circuits with distinct inter- and intra-regional motifs, with sub-circuit coefficients showing cross-subject consistency. On mouse whole-brain Neuropixels data (19 sessions, 10 areas), sub-circuit activations predict task variables (outcome, early lick, lick side) well above chance with p-values < 1×10⁻¹⁰. CREIMBO remains robust to increasing Gaussian noise up to σ_noise/σ_data ≈ 0.2 before a phase transition in recovery quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CREIMBO against three switching linear dynamical system baselines: SLDS and rSLDS (Linderman et al., 2016) and multi-population rSLDS / mp-rSLDS (Glaser et al., 2020). On the synthetic 5-session recovery task, CREIMBO achieves higher -log(MSE) and correlation with ground truth for latent dynamics, ensemble compositions, and sub-circuits than all three baselines (Fig. 2 K–O, Fig. 7–8). However, the authors explicitly note that these baselines are not designed for multi-session data with varying neuron identities, cannot identify multiple co-occurring sub-circuits, and do not produce sparse localized ensembles, making the comparison partially apples-to-oranges. Ablation experiments further show that removing the dynamic prior, removing the multi-regional mask, or analyzing sessions individually all degrade recovery accuracy relative to full CREIMBO. The paper does not report a single unified numerical table; comparisons are presented via correlation heatmaps and -log(MSE) bar plots in figures rather than tabulated values.

## Relation to Prior Work

CREIMBO unifies three previously separate research threads: (1) the 'stitching' literature on merging non-simultaneous neural recordings (Turaga et al., 2013; Bishop, 2015; LFADS), (2) decomposed linear dynamical systems (dLDS, Mudrik et al., 2024a) that model non-stationary dynamics via sparse time-varying decomposition of a shared basis, and (3) graph-driven sparse dictionary learning for identifying functional neural ensembles (SIBBLINGS, Mudrik et al., 2024b). The key departure from prior stitching methods is the explicit multi-regional block-diagonal structure on ensemble compositions and the assumption of a universal, session-invariant sub-circuit dictionary that enables cross-session alignment without requiring neuron identity overlap. Unlike switching dynamical system approaches (rSLDS, mp-rSLDS), CREIMBO allows multiple sub-circuits to be co-active simultaneously rather than switching between discrete states.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CREIMBO addresses a genuine gap in computational neuroscience: the inability of current methods to simultaneously handle multi-session variability in neuron identity, multi-regional structure, and non-stationary dynamics while preserving interpretability. The demonstrated ability to predict task variables (outcome, lick side) from sub-circuit activations alone, with p-values below 1e-10, suggests the identified sub-circuits capture functionally meaningful computations rather than mere statistical artifacts. Key limitations include the reliance on linear projections from latent to observation space, the computational cost of dictionary learning (noted as needing parallelization), and assumptions about observability and effective sparsity of the dynamics basis that lack tight theoretical guarantees for non-stationary systems. Future directions include non-linear projections, integration with delay-estimation methods (mDLAG), and extension to non-neural time-series data.
