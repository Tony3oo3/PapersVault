## Problem

Existing LLM benchmarks for structured data (NLGraph, GraphQA, LLM4DyG) evaluate only pairwise graph relationships, overlooking the high-order, multi-vertex correlations that hypergraphs naturally capture in real-world data such as citation networks and protein structures. Whether LLMs can comprehend hypergraph structures at all, and how to best textualize and prompt them for hypergraph reasoning, remains entirely unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251523/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark feeds LLMs a textualized hypergraph description (5–20 vertices, rendered in one of seven languages ranging from pairwise adjacency matrices to full incidence matrices and neighbor-set enumerations) followed by a task-specific question. Outputs are structured as counts, binary yes/no decisions, ordered vertex or hyperedge lists, shortest-path sequences, isomorphism judgments, or one-of-three structure-classification labels, with format constraints enforced via explicit answer templates (e.g., '[Yes, No]', '[v0, v1, v2]').

## Method

The paper introduces the LLM4Hypergraph benchmark (21,500 problems across 15 tasks: 8 low-order, 5 high-order, 2 isomorphism) built on both synthetic (random and regular-structured) and real-world (citation, protein) hypergraphs. Seven hypergraph description languages are designed—three low-order (LO-Inc, N-Pair, Adj-Mat) that reduce hyperedges to pairwise connections, and four high-order (HO-Neigh, HO-Inc, N-Set, Inc-Mat) that preserve multi-vertex correlations—allowing systematic study of how encoding choice affects comprehension. Two novel prompting techniques are proposed: Hyper-BAG, which inserts a 'build-a-hypergraph' visualization instruction between the description and question to aid mental construction, and Hyper-COT, which appends hypergraph-specific step-by-step reasoning prompts (three variants guiding the model to alternate between vertex-to-hyperedge and hyperedge-to-vertex perspectives). A five-configuration prompt framework (Zero-Shot, Zero-Hyper-COT, Few-Shot, CoT, CoT-Hyper-BAG) integrates these with standard in-context learning, and experiments across six LLMs show that high-order languages outperform low-order ones on set-level tasks, while Hyper-COT v3 yields up to 9% gains on structure classification.

## Results

On the LLM4Hypergraph benchmark (21,500 problems, 15 tasks), the best-performing LLM (Qwen-Long) achieves 97.56% on Vertex Connection and 98.24% on Reachability Check, while GPT-4o reaches 96.36% on Vertex Set Connection and 98.68% on VS-in-Hyperedge Check. All six LLMs fail at isomorphism recognition, scoring only ~44–48% on the binary task and ~23–45% on three-class structure classification. The proposed Hyper-COT v3 prompting improves structure classification by 4% on average (up to 9% with N-Pair and N-Set encodings) over naive CoT, and Hyper-BAG yields a 2.8% gain on the Vertex Set Connection task over no-BAG baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is the Build-A-Graph (BAG) prompting from Wang et al. (2024, NLGraph). On high-order tasks, Hyper-BAG outperforms standard BAG by 1.0% on VS Connection (86.2% vs. 85.2%) and 0.6% on VS-in-He Check (89.8% vs. 89.2%), while on low-order tasks the two are essentially tied (93.6% vs. 93.0% on Vertex Connection). Against the naive CoT baseline ('Let's think step by step'), Hyper-COT v3 achieves a 4% average improvement across seven encodings on structure classification (e.g., 33.6% vs. 24.3% on N-Pair, 36.0% vs. 27.0% on N-Set), with the largest gap of 9.3% on N-Pair. In-context learning (Few-Shot, CoT) consistently outperforms Zero-Shot across all task categories, with gains of 5–15% on medium-difficulty tasks. However, no prompting strategy rescues isomorphism recognition, where all LLMs remain near chance level (~50% binary, ~30–45% three-class), indicating a fundamental limitation in current LLM architectures for global structural reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of LLM graph-reasoning benchmarks (NLGraph, GraphQA, LLM4DyG, GPT4Graph) from pairwise graph structures to hypergraphs, which model high-order multi-vertex correlations. Methodologically, it adapts two established prompting techniques—Chain-of-Thought (Wei et al., 2022) and Build-A-Graph (Wang et al., 2024)—into hypergraph-specific variants (Hyper-COT and Hyper-BAG), and introduces seven textual encoding languages for hypergraph structures. The key departure from prior work is the deliberate inclusion of high-order tasks (vertex-set reasoning) and isomorphism tasks, which are absent from all existing graph benchmarks that focus exclusively on low-order, pairwise vertex relationships.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first comprehensive benchmark for LLM hypergraph comprehension, this work provides a foundational testbed for the growing intersection of LLMs and structured data beyond simple graphs. The most striking finding is that even GPT-4o achieves only ~50% accuracy on binary isomorphism recognition—essentially random—revealing a fundamental gap in current LLMs' ability to perform global structural reasoning. Open questions include whether fine-tuning (rather than prompting) can close the isomorphism gap, how performance scales beyond 20 vertices, and whether the hypergraph-specific prompting techniques transfer to other high-order relational structures. The modest 4% improvement from Hyper-COT suggests that prompting alone may be insufficient for the hardest tasks, pointing toward the need for architectural or training-level solutions.
