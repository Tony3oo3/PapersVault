## Problem

Machine learning for crystal symmetry identification from powder XRD patterns has been constrained by the absence of a large-scale, physically realistic open-source dataset, insufficient evaluation on real experimental data, and limited exploration of out-of-library (novel crystal) generalization. Additionally, the inherent long-tailed distribution of space group labels (spanning several orders of magnitude in frequency) causes most models to fail on low-frequency crystals, a challenge that prior work has not systematically addressed.

## Input / Output

Each sample is a 1D powder XRD pattern in d-I format: a 3501-element intensity vector where the x-axis encodes lattice plane distances from 1.199 Å to 8.853 Å (step 0.0176 Å, corresponding to a Cu-Kα two-theta scan from 10° to 80° at 0.02° steps) and the y-axis is normalized relative intensity (max = 100). The wavelength-independent d-I representation makes patterns universally applicable across X-ray sources. The model outputs a crystal system label (7 classes) or a space group label (230 classes), with the dataset containing 4,065,346 patterns from 119,569 unique structures each simulated under 33 distinct physical conditions.

## Method

The paper introduces PysimXRD, a custom simulation engine that models multiphysical coupling in powder diffraction: Voigt peak profiles with grain-size-dependent broadening (Scherrer equation), Lorentz-polarization and multiplicity factors, Debye-Waller thermal attenuation, preferred-orientation perturbation (±40% intensity), internal-stress lattice deformation (up to 10%), instrumental zero-shift (±1.2°), Gaussian white noise (2%), and a sixth-order polynomial background for inelastic scattering. Crystal structures are sourced from Materials Project MP-2024.1 (154,718 entries) and rigorously filtered via Spglib to remove broken symmetries, duplicates, and space-group discrepancies, yielding 119,569 high-quality structures. For benchmarking, 21 sequence models (11 CNNs, 6 recurrent networks, 3 transformers, 1 MLP) are evaluated under both in-library (same crystals, different simulation conditions) and out-of-library (non-overlapping crystal sets) splits, with additional experiments on the real RRUFF dataset to validate sim-to-real transfer. The study further investigates long-tail mitigation through weighted classification, label smoothing, and focal loss, finding that the latter two consistently improve macro-F1 for both crystal system and space group tasks.

## Results

On the in-library benchmark (Table 2), CNN11 (Salgado et al., 2023, no pooling) achieves the best crystal system accuracy of 90.2% (F1 0.922) and space group accuracy of 75.8% (F1 0.750), with an inference time of 4.8 ms per sample. For out-of-library classification (Table 4), Bidirectional-GRU leads with 72.2% crystal system accuracy and 49.8% space group accuracy, while CNN11 follows at 70.2% and 48.1% respectively. On the real experimental RRUFF dataset (Table 5), CNN11 achieves 89.3% crystal system accuracy and 76.6% space group accuracy, confirming that models trained on SimXRD generalize to experimental data. Notably, six of the eleven CNN baselines (CNN1–4, 6, 8) are stuck at 24.1% space group accuracy, equivalent to always predicting the single most frequent space group.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the eleven CNN baselines, CNN11 (Salgado et al., 2023, no pooling) dominates both in-library tasks (90.2% crystal system, 75.8% space group accuracy), outperforming the next-best CNN10 (Le et al., 2023) by 3.2 and 5.3 percentage points respectively. Six CNNs (Park 2017, Lee 2020 ×2, Wang 2020, Dong 2021, Lee 2022) collapse to 24.1% space group accuracy—identical to the majority-class baseline—making them effectively non-functional for the 230-class task. Szymanski et al. (2021, CNN7) with ensemble learning achieves 86.2% crystal system but only 58.8% space group, trailing CNN11 by 17 points on the harder task. Among recurrent models, Bidirectional-GRU is the strongest (72.2% crystal system, 49.8% space group out-of-library), consistently outperforming its unidirectional GRU counterpart (70.7%, 48.0%) and Bidirectional-LSTM (70.4%, 34.9%). The raw Transformer (Vaswani 2017) performs near-randomly (37.6% crystal system, 28.5% space group), while PatchTST (Nie 2023) recovers to 65.6% crystal system and 37.5% space group, showing that subsequence patching helps but still lags behind the best CNNs and BiGRU. On the RRUFF experimental set, CNN11 achieves 76.6% space group accuracy—slightly exceeding its SimXRD score—while PatchTST reaches 63.5%, confirming sim-to-real transfer for the top models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in the XRD symmetry identification literature by introducing SimXRD-4M, the largest open-source simulated powder XRD dataset (4M patterns from 119K crystals), alongside a novel simulation tool (PysimXRD) that models a broader range of physical interactions than prior tools like Pymatgen, FullProf, or GSAS-II. Conceptually, it extends the line of CNN-based symmetry classifiers (Park 2017, Lee 2020, Salgado 2023, etc.) by benchmarking them against recurrent and transformer architectures under a unified protocol, and by introducing out-of-library evaluation and long-tail analysis that prior datasets (XRDSP, XRDIsAllYouNeed, AdvancedXRDAnalysis) did not systematically address. The dataset sources crystals from the Materials Project rather than ICSD, applying rigorous symmetry filtering via Spglib to remove broken-symmetry and duplicate structures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The SimXRD-4M dataset and its accompanying benchmark will likely become the standard reference for XRD symmetry classification research, given its scale, open accessibility, and integration with PyTorch/TensorFlow. The validation that models trained purely on simulated patterns generalize to real experimental data (RRUFF) is practically significant, as it demonstrates that high-fidelity simulation can substitute for costly experimental data collection. Key open questions remain: how to effectively handle the inherent long-tail distribution of space groups (label smoothing and focal loss help but do not solve the problem), how to improve out-of-library generalization where most models collapse to majority-class prediction, and whether domain-specific architectures can better capture the bidirectional physical structure of diffraction patterns.
