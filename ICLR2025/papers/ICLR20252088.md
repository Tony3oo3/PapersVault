## Problem

Existing automated circuit discovery methods for transformers (ACDC, EAP) rely on activation patching or its linear approximations, which suffer from slow runtimes (hours per circuit), approximation errors, and requirements such as non-zero gradients of the task metric. No prior method could efficiently produce circuits at the granularity of individual attention heads at specific sequence positions, limiting fine-grained mechanistic interpretability analysis.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a frozen transformer model, a single input text sequence, precomputed mean activations over a task-specific distribution (e.g., the corrupted ABC dataset for IOI), and a task-specific metric. It outputs a circuit as a set of attention head nodes (optionally at specific sequence positions) along with per-node relevance scores computed as the L1-norm ratio of relevant to irrelevant constituents. Unlike patching-based methods, no forward-backward passes or gradient computations are needed—only a single forward pass with decomposition bookkeeping per source node.

## Method

CD-T defines a closed-form set of mathematical decomposition equations that split every activation in a transformer's computational graph into a relevant (β) and irrelevant (γ) constituent, starting from an initial decomposition at a source node (typically β = activation − mean, γ = mean). The key technical contribution is the decomposition of the self-attention module: query, key, and value vectors are decomposed via a linear rule, attention logits are split by isolating the β_key^T β_query term, softmax probabilities are decomposed by subtracting the β-probability from the full softmax, and the weighted value output is split analogously. The circuit discovery algorithm then iteratively propagates decompositions forward from each candidate source head to a set of target nodes, scores relevance as the sum of L1-norm ratios across targets, selects top-contributing nodes, and greedily prunes the circuit until performance matches the full model. This is purely analytical—no training, no approximation, no manually crafted examples—and reduces circuit discovery runtime from hours to seconds while supporting arbitrary granularity from full heads down to per-position head outputs.

## Results

CD-T achieves an average of 97% ROC AUC in recovering manually identified circuits across three tasks (IOI, Greater-than, Docstring), while reducing circuit discovery runtime from hours (ACDC) to seconds. CD-T circuits are 80% more faithful than random circuits of up to 60% of the original model size (IOI and Greater-than) and up to 80% (Docstring), using only 0.4%, 5.5%, and 31.3% of full model nodes respectively. CD-T reaches perfect faithfulness (=1) with fewer nodes than EAP on all three tasks, and on Greater-than achieves a task-specific metric of 0.761 (98.1% correct) with a small attention-head-only circuit.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CD-T outperforms ACDC (Conmy et al., 2023) on both efficiency and accuracy: ACDC takes hours to identify a circuit while CD-T completes in seconds, and ACDC achieves no better ROC AUC on any of the three tasks. Against EAP (Syed et al., 2023), CD-T achieves comparable runtime but superior ROC AUC on Greater-than and IOI, and reaches faithfulness = 1 with fewer nodes than EAP on all three tasks. CD-T is particularly effective at identifying negative heads (e.g., Negative Name Mover Heads in IOI) and supporting heads that both ACDC and EAP struggle to recover. The faithfulness hypothesis test confirms CD-T circuits are 80% more faithful than random circuits of up to 60% model size, ruling out the possibility that the results are due to chance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Contextual Decomposition (CD), originally developed for LSTMs (Murdoch et al., 2018) and later generalized to RNNs/CNNs (Singh et al., 2018) and unidirectional LSTMs (Jumelet et al., 2019), to the transformer architecture by designing novel decomposition equations for self-attention. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant activation-patching and attribution-patching paradigm (ACDC, EAP) by replacing approximate causal interventions with exact analytical decomposition of activations into relevant and irrelevant constituents. The authors are the first to repurpose CD—a method previously used only for input-feature attribution in sequence models—as a tool for mechanistic interpretability and automated circuit discovery in transformers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The reduction of circuit discovery runtime from hours to seconds, combined with the ability to produce sequence-position-level circuits without training or hand-crafted examples, makes fine-grained mechanistic analysis practical for a much broader set of researchers. However, the evaluation is confined to small models (GPT-2 small, a 4-layer attention-only transformer) and three tasks, and the method currently handles only attention heads, leaving MLPs and heterogeneous circuits unexplored. Open questions include whether the exact-decomposition advantage persists at scale, whether the sign-blind relevance metric can be made direction-aware, and how much heuristic tuning (threshold selection, layer normalization) is truly necessary versus an artifact of the small-model setting.
