## Problem

Current self-supervised pretraining for 3D medical imaging captures static structural features like organ morphology but fails to learn prognostic pixel biomarkers linked to long-term disease progression, because supervision is restricted to narrow temporal windows around the image (the 'missing context problem'). Longitudinal EHR data containing years of follow-up outcomes is routinely available but has not been used as a scalable pretraining signal for 3D imaging encoders, and prior time-to-event imaging work has been limited to small-scale, 2D, single-task settings.

## Input / Output

The method takes 3D chest CT scans (224×224 pixels, 1–3 mm slice thickness, Hounsfield-unit rescaled) as image input and pairs them with longitudinal EHR records in OMOP CDM format (~5 years median follow-up, 225M timestamped clinical events across 4.3M candidate medical codes). During pretraining, the encoder is trained to predict piecewise-exponential survival distributions over 8 time bins for 8,192 selected TTE tasks. At inference, the frozen encoder produces a fixed-dimensional embedding that feeds either a logistic-regression head (class probabilities for diagnostic/prognostic classification) or a DeepSurv Cox-PH head (a single risk score per patient for Harrell's C-index, or a time-dependent hazard function for TTE evaluation).

## Method

The core contribution is a time-to-event pretraining objective that converts longitudinal EHR timelines into large-scale survival supervision for 3D vision encoders. Task selection uses a conditional Shannon entropy criterion (from MOTOR) to pick 8,192 informative medical codes from 4.3M candidates by solving a vertex-cover problem over the ontology DAG, maximizing entropy under a task budget. Each task is modeled with a piecewise exponential neural network (PEANN) that splits the post-scan timeline into 8 uniform bins, with per-bin hazard rates computed as exp(A·M_ip + b) from the image embedding; the survival likelihood loss naturally handles right-censoring without discarding censored patients. Pretraining is performed as full fine-tuning of existing 3D backbones (SwinUNETR, DenseNet-121, ResNet-152) on 18,945 CT scans, yielding ~3× more labels per image than visit-level supervision. After pretraining, the encoder is frozen and adapted to downstream tasks via lightweight heads (logistic regression or DeepSurv), decoupling representation learning from task-specific prediction.

## Results

On 8 prognostic time-to-event tasks (INSPECT), TTE pretraining achieves an average Harrell's C-index of 0.732 across SwinUNETR, DenseNet-121, and ResNet-152, compared to 0.574 for base models and 0.696 for base/visit. For prognostic binary classification (AUROC), TTE pretraining yields an average 23.7% improvement over base models (e.g., SwinUNETR mortality 1M: 0.827 vs. 0.693). On diagnostic classification (RSPECT), TTE pretraining matches or slightly exceeds other pretraining approaches, with a notable exception on RV/LV ≥1 where it achieves 0.636–0.708 AUROC vs. 0.546–0.665 for base/visit. The method also improves model calibration, reducing Integrated Brier Score by an average of 54%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is against three continued-pretraining baselines: base (no continued pretraining), base/MTL (multitask supervised on 8 INSPECT labels), and base/visit (same 8,192 EHR tasks but restricted to the same hospital visit as the CT scan). TTE pretraining outperforms all three across all architectures on Harrell's C-index, with the largest gains over base (e.g., DenseNet-121 mortality: 0.732 vs. 0.505) and consistent 3–7 point improvements over base/visit. The base/visit comparison is particularly informative since both use 8,192 tasks; TTE's advantage comes from leveraging the full longitudinal EHR timeline (3× more labels per scan) and modeling time-to-event rather than binary occurrence. Against Merlin (Blankemeier et al., 2024), the only external foundation model compared, TTE-pretrained ResNet-152 matches or exceeds Merlin on most tasks (e.g., PE Left AUROC 0.657 vs. 0.603; PE Central 0.687 vs. 0.572), though Merlin retains a slight edge on mortality classification (0.632 vs. 0.583 at 1M). On diagnostic classification (RSPECT), TTE pretraining is statistically indistinguishable from base/visit for most tasks but significantly improves RV/LV ≥1 prediction across all architectures, suggesting TTE supervision captures prognostic pixel biomarkers that overlap with diagnostic features.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate lines of work: piecewise exponential survival modeling (PEANN, DeepSurv) and 3D medical imaging pretraining (MAE, contrastive learning, Merlin). Prior TTE imaging studies (DeepConvSurv, Shu et al., Lu et al.) were restricted to 2D, single-task, end-to-end settings, while 3D pretraining methods captured only static structural features without temporal context. The task selection strategy is adapted from MOTOR (Steinberg et al., 2024), which formulated ontology-aware task selection as a vertex cover problem over medical code DAGs. The key departure is using longitudinal EHR events as a large-scale, multi-task TTE pretraining objective for 3D encoders, addressing what the authors term the 'missing context problem' in existing SSL regimens.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first empirical demonstration that temporal supervision from longitudinal EHRs can be injected into 3D medical imaging pretraining to substantially improve prognostic prediction (23.7% AUROC, 29.4% C-index) without degrading diagnostic classification, suggesting a new axis for medical foundation model design. The 3× label density gain from leveraging future EHR events and censored patients offers a practical data-efficiency advantage over visit-level supervision. Key limitations include evaluation on a single imaging modality (chest CT) and a single prognostic dataset (INSPECT, ~19K scans), and the use of frozen encoders with lightweight heads rather than full fine-tuning. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to other modalities (MRI, 2D radiographs), scales to larger pretraining corpora, and whether the gains persist under few-shot or zero-shot adaptation regimes.
