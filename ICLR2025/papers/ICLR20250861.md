## Problem

Prior circuit discovery in language models relies on coarse-grained, polysemantic units (attention heads, neurons) that are difficult to interpret, while fine-grained analysis methods require researcher-specified mechanistic hypotheses and curated data. This paper addresses the gap of scalably discovering causal circuits composed of human-interpretable, fine-grained features without pre-existing hypotheses about how a behavior is internally implemented.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250861/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a language model with pre-trained SAEs on its submodules (attention outputs, MLP outputs, residual stream), a dataset of contrastive input pairs or single inputs, and a scalar target metric (e.g., log-probability difference between two output tokens). It produces a sparse causal graph where nodes are SAE feature activations and error terms at specific token positions, weighted by their approximate indirect effects on the metric, with edges connecting causally related nodes. Notably, SAE error terms are retained as circuit nodes rather than discarded, yielding a principled decomposition into interpretable feature contributions and unexplained residual components.

## Method

The core technique treats SAE features and error terms as nodes in the LM's computation graph by intervening to set each hidden state to its SAE reconstruction (sparse sum of feature vectors plus error), then computing approximate indirect effects via attribution patching or integrated gradients. Practical gradient computation requires stop gradients on SAE errors (to prevent vanishing feature gradients), pass-through gradients (to restore upstream gradient flow), and Jacobian-vector product tricks (to compute edge effects in a constant number of backward passes rather than O(d_model)). Nodes and edges are filtered by absolute effect thresholds to yield sparse circuits, with effects aggregated across token positions and examples. For the SHIFT application, a human inspects the discovered circuit, labels each feature for task-relevance, and ablates irrelevant features to remove spurious signal dependence without disambiguating labeled data. For unsupervised discovery at scale, the method clusters a large corpus by training gradients or SAE activations and automatically discovers a circuit for each resulting subcorpus.

## Results

On the Bias in Bios debiasing task (worst-case setting where gender perfectly predicts profession in training data), SHIFT+retrain achieves 93.1% profession accuracy and 89.0% worst-group accuracy on Pythia-70M, and 95.0% profession accuracy and 92.9% worst-group accuracy on Gemma-2-2B, while reducing gender accuracy to near-chance (52.0% and 52.4% respectively), nearly matching the oracle classifier (93.0%/91.9% and 95.0%/93.1%). For subject-verb agreement circuits, only ~100 feature nodes (Pythia) or ~500 (Gemma) are needed to explain the majority of model behavior, compared to ~1,500 or ~50,000 neurons for half the performance. Ablating just a handful of feature circuit nodes eliminates the model's task performance (completeness), whereas hundreds to thousands of neurons are required for the same effect.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SHIFT+retrain substantially outperforms Concept Bottleneck Probing (CBP; Yan et al., 2023) on worst-group accuracy: 89.0% vs. 67.7% on Pythia and 92.9% vs. 86.7% on Gemma, while also achieving higher profession accuracy (93.1% vs. 83.3% Pythia; 95.0% vs. 90.2% Gemma) and lower gender accuracy (52.0% vs. 60.1% Pythia). Random feature ablation has essentially no effect (61.8% profession, 87.5% gender on Pythia), confirming that the gains come from human-guided feature selection rather than arbitrary ablation. The neuron skyline—which gets an unfair advantage by using balanced-set labels to select which neurons to ablate—still fails to match SHIFT, achieving only 75.5% profession and 73.2% gender on Pythia (vs. 93.1%/52.0% for SHIFT+retrain), because polysemantic neurons implicated in spurious gender classification are also useful for profession classification. The feature skyline (automatically selecting the most causally implicated features for spurious accuracy) achieves 88.5% profession and 54.3% gender on Pythia, close to SHIFT without retraining but well below SHIFT+retrain, indicating that human interpretability-based selection identifies a better set of features to remove. Overall, SHIFT+retrain nearly matches the oracle (trained on balanced-set ground-truth labels) on all three metrics for both models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two active research threads: sparse autoencoder (SAE) dictionary learning for feature disentanglement (Bricken et al., 2023; Cunningham et al., 2024; Templeton et al., 2024) and causal circuit discovery via activation patching (Wang et al., 2023; Conmy et al., 2023; Nanda, 2022). Prior circuit work operates on coarse-grained, polysemantic units (attention heads, neurons, MLP modules), while prior SAE work identifies interpretable features but does not systematically connect them into causal graphs. The paper's key methodological contribution is combining these: using SAE features as nodes in a computation graph and applying linear approximations (attribution patching, integrated gradients) to efficiently identify which features and edges are causally implicated in a behavior. The SHIFT technique departs from the spurious-correlation literature (Sagawa et al., 2020; Ravfogel et al., 2020; Yan et al., 2023) by replacing the standard requirement for disambiguating labeled data with human interpretability judgment over SAE features, and the unsupervised pipeline extends the clustering-based behavior discovery of Michaud et al. (2023) by automatically attaching feature circuits to each discovered cluster.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a small number of human-interpretable SAE features (~100 for Pythia-70M) can explain the majority of a model's performance on structured tasks, whereas thousands of neurons are needed for equivalent faithfulness—suggesting SAE-based circuits offer a more compact and actionable unit of analysis for interpretability. The SHIFT result is practically significant: in a worst-case setting where the spurious signal (gender) is perfectly predictive of the target (profession) during training, human-guided feature ablation plus retraining recovers near-oracle debiasing without any disambiguating data, addressing a gap that most prior robustness methods cannot fill. Key limitations include reliance on SAE quality (uninterpretable error terms remain), evaluation on small models (70M, 2B parameters), and the qualitative nature of feature labeling. Open questions include whether the approach scales to frontier models, how to quantitatively evaluate circuits without downstream tasks, and whether the unsupervised pipeline's thousands of discovered circuits can be systematically validated.
