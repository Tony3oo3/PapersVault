## Problem

Adversarial training models opponents as purely adversarial agents whose sole goal is to maximize the classifier's error, leading to unnecessarily conservative training that sacrifices clean accuracy and generalization. In practice, realistic opponents are more likely to pursue their own self-interests (e.g., making a product appear higher quality, or changing a road sign to a specific alternative) rather than deliberately maximizing harm in any direction. The paper asks whether incorporating knowledge of an opponent's incentives as inductive bias can yield robustness with less cost to clean performance.

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images (32×32×3 for CIFAR-10, GTSRB, and CIFAR-100) and produces class labels, but the training process additionally requires specifying a utility matrix u(y, y′) or an uncertainty set U of such matrices that encodes which prediction outcomes are beneficial to the opponent. The output is a classifier robust to strategic attacks within U, evaluated by its accuracy against the worst-case strategic opponent in that set, alongside clean and adversarial accuracy as secondary metrics.

## Method

The paper reformulates adversarial training as a non-zero-sum game in which the opponent maximizes its own utility u(y, ŷ) rather than the 0-1 loss, yielding the strategic training objective min_f max_{u∈U} E[ℓ(y, f(x + δ_u))] where δ_u = argmax_{δ∈Δ} u(y, f(x+δ)). The key insight is that standard adversarial training is equivalent to optimizing against the worst-case utility in the universal set Λ, so reducing U to a task-specific incentive set provides a principled interpolation between clean and adversarial robustness. For structured opponent classes (semantic, anti-semantic, k-hot, preference-order), the paper shows that a single representative utility suffices, making training as computationally efficient as adversarial training; for general sets, attacks decouple per class row enabling O(K) calls rather than |U|. The attack implementation uses multi-targeted PGD that maximizes the softmax probability of any valid target class, with a small randomization term (ε=0.1) for training stability.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with ResNet18, strategic training in the anti-semantic setting achieves 73.4% strategic accuracy (vs. 67.4% for adversarial training) and 85.1% clean accuracy (vs. 79.8%), while in the semantic setting it reaches 52.5% strategic accuracy (vs. 49.6%) with comparable clean accuracy (78.9% vs. 79.8%). On GTSRB with ResNet18, gains are more pronounced: strategic training achieves 79.1% anti-semantic accuracy (vs. 73.0%) and 64.9% semantic accuracy (vs. 55.4%), with clean accuracy of 92.0% in both settings. Deflection rates (the fraction of successful adversarial attacks that strategic training prevents) reach up to 40%, and strategic training retains reasonable adversarial robustness (e.g., 33.4% vs. 45.5% on CIFAR-10 ResNet18 anti-semantic).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is adversarial training via PGD (Madry et al., 2018), which optimizes against the worst-case utility u^adv. Strategic training consistently outperforms adversarial training on strategic accuracy across all settings: +6.0 points on CIFAR-10 anti-semantic (73.4 vs. 67.4), +9.5 on GTSRB semantic (64.9 vs. 55.4), and +6.1 on GTSRB anti-semantic (79.1 vs. 73.0) with ResNet18. Crucially, strategic training also achieves higher clean accuracy in most settings (e.g., 85.1 vs. 79.8 on CIFAR-10 anti-semantic; 92.0 vs. 90.9 on GTSRB), directly addressing the well-known robustness–accuracy tradeoff. The one area where adversarial training retains an advantage is adversarial accuracy itself (e.g., 45.5 vs. 33.4 on CIFAR-10 anti-semantic), which is expected since strategic training is misspecified for fully adversarial opponents. However, the paper shows that even this gap is moderate, and that strategic training's deflection rate of up to 40% demonstrates meaningful partial protection against adversarial attacks despite not explicitly defending against them.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate literatures: adversarial training (Madry et al., 2018) and strategic classification (Hardt et al., 2016; Levanon & Rosenfeld, 2021). It reframes the adversarial minimax objective as a special case of a more general strategic-game formulation where the opponent maximizes its own utility rather than the learner's loss, introducing an 'incentive uncertainty set' that interpolates between clean and adversarial settings. The authors explicitly position their work as the first to study strategic behavior in a multiclass setting over complex image inputs, extending prior strategic classification work (which focused on binary labels and tabular features) to the image-classification regime where it can be directly compared with adversarial training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled mechanism for reducing the well-known clean-accuracy cost of adversarial training by incorporating even mild prior knowledge about an opponent's incentives (e.g., semantic vs. anti-semantic attack preferences). A key open question is how to reliably infer or specify the incentive uncertainty set in practice; the paper's preliminary utility-inference experiments (Appendix C.6) suggest feasibility but leave this largely unresolved. Limitations include the restriction to image classification, the need for the learner to commit to a specific uncertainty set (with attendant misspecification risk), and the fact that gains diminish as the uncertainty set approaches the full adversarial set. Future work on other modalities, richer incentive structures, and automated uncertainty-set selection would strengthen the practical applicability of this framework.
