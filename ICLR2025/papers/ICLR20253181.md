## Problem

Metagenomic datasets contain the highest molecular diversity of any publicly available sequence data, yet they have been underutilized for biological language model pretraining due to barriers in data accessibility, quality filtering, deduplication, and taxonomic balancing. Existing genomic and protein language models rely on curated databases (UniRef, GTDB) that cover only a small fraction of natural sequence diversity, limiting the functional representations they can learn.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253181/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes mixed-modality genomic contigs where protein-coding sequences (CDS) are tokenized per-amino-acid and intergenic sequences (IGS) are tokenized per-nucleotide, with special strand tokens (<+>, <->) prepended to each CDS element. The 4096-token context window accommodates approximately 9.7 ± 3.3 genomic elements per example. Beyond standard masked-token reconstruction, the model's hidden representations are used to compute categorical Jacobian contact maps that reveal protein-protein interfaces and intergenic regulatory syntax without any supervised signal or multiple sequence alignment.

## Method

The paper introduces the OMG corpus (3.1T bp, 3.3B CDS) by combining JGI's IMG and EMBL's MGnify metagenomic repositories, applying sequential element-by-element quality filters (edge-element removal, contig length ≥2kb, N/X-frequency <20%, CDS ≤15,000 AA, IGS ≤4000 bp) to remove assembly errors and poor gene calls. gLM2 is a transformer encoder (RoPE, SwiGLU, RMSNorm, Flash Attention 2) trained with 30% masked language modeling at 150M and 650M parameter scales, where the mixed-modality tokenization lets a single model learn both protein functional representations and nucleic acid regulatory patterns. A key contribution is genomic semantic deduplication: a 150M gLM2 is first trained to embed all examples, then 49% of near-duplicate examples (cosine distance <2e-3) are pruned, which balances taxonomic representation without requiring taxonomic labels. The multi-protein genomic context enables gLM2 to extract inter-protein co-evolutionary signals via categorical Jacobian on concatenated sequences, a capability absent in single-protein models like ESM2 or DNA-only models like Evo.

## Results

On the Diverse Genomic Embedding Benchmark (DGEB), gLM2 outperforms ESM2 on the overall score at both 150M and 650M parameter scales, with the 150M model achieving 0.482 (with semantic deduplication) vs. 0.474 (without). On ProteinGym (Deep Mutational Scanning), ESM2_650M achieves 0.414 average Spearman correlation while gLM2_650M_prot (after one-epoch fine-tuning on OMG_prot50) reaches 0.384, a modest gap attributed to eukaryotic sequences being underrepresented in OMG. Critically, gLM2_650M extracts protein-protein interface co-evolutionary signal via categorical Jacobian on the 2ONK ModBC complex, matching GREMLIN MSA-based predictions, whereas neither ESM2_650M nor Evo-1-8k-base detect any inter-protein signal. On nucleic acid DGEB tasks, gLM2 performs comparably to Nucleotide Transformers despite only a small fraction of training tokens being DNA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ESM2 (Lin et al., 2023), gLM2 wins on the overall DGEB score at both 150M and 650M scales, with particular gains on tasks involving underrepresented taxa (e.g., RpoB Archaeal Phylogeny: 0.311 vs. 0.251 for the 150M model with pruning) and operon pair classification, where gLM2's performance continues to scale with FLOPs while ESM2 plateaus. However, gLM2 loses to ESM2 on ProteinGym DMS (0.384 vs. 0.414 avg Spearman), which the authors attribute to eukaryotic sequences being poorly represented in the metagenomic OMG corpus. Against Nucleotide Transformers (Dalla-Torre et al., 2023), gLM2 performs similarly on nucleic acid DGEB tasks despite DNA constituting only a small fraction of its training tokens, suggesting the mixed-modality design captures intergenic regulatory syntax effectively. Against Evo (Nguyen et al., 2024), gLM2 uniquely detects inter-protein co-evolutionary contacts in the 2ONK complex via categorical Jacobian, while Evo's Jacobian on the corresponding DNA sequence fails to recover the ModB–ModC interface signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the genomic language model line initiated by Hwang et al. (2024), which represented contigs as order-preserved lists of translated CDS in amino acids, by introducing a mixed-modality formulation that interleaves CDS (amino acids) with intergenic sequences (nucleic acids), thereby capturing regulatory syntax alongside protein function. It also adapts the NLP semantic deduplication technique of SemDeDup (Abbas et al., 2023) to the genomic domain, where sequence-length variability makes traditional clustering-based deduplication infeasible. The dataset construction draws on the two largest public metagenomic repositories (JGI IMG and EMBL MGnify), addressing practical barriers (accessibility, quality filtering, deduplication) that have previously limited metagenomic data's use in biological LM pretraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 3.1T bp OMG corpus is the largest publicly available mixed-modality genomic pretraining dataset to date, and its release (along with gLM2 and all processing scripts) substantially lowers the barrier for the community to train on metagenomic-scale data. The demonstration that a mixed-modality model can extract protein-protein interface co-evolutionary signals from a single concatenated sequence—without MSA or supervision—opens a path toward unsupervised PPI prediction that single-protein LMs (ESM2) and pure nucleic-acid LMs (Evo) cannot achieve. Key limitations include the dataset's heavy prokaryotic/viral bias (eukaryotic sequences are poorly represented, as reflected in slightly worse ProteinGym scores vs. ESM2), the modest overall DGEB gain from semantic deduplication (0.48 vs. 0.47) with trade-offs on overrepresented taxa, and the fact that PPI interface results are demonstrated on a limited set of complexes. Open questions include whether the mixed-modality advantage persists at much larger model scales, how to incorporate eukaryotic metagenomic data without degrading prokaryotic performance, and whether the regulatory syntax signals generalize beyond well-annotated organisms like E. coli.
