## Problem

Adversarial training achieves strong robustness primarily with large models, creating a significant performance gap for smaller architectures needed in practical deployment. Existing adversarial distillation methods transfer only the teacher's logits to the student, overlooking the input gradient information that encodes robustness-relevant structure. This paper asks whether distilling the teacher's input gradient—rather than just its output—can further close the robustness gap for small student models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252520/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a standard RGB image x (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, SVHN; 64×64 for Tiny-ImageNet) together with the adversarial perturbation δ produced by the inner maximization step of the base AD method (10-step PGD, step size 2/255, L∞ bound 8/255). It evaluates the student and teacher at two perturbed points, x+δ and x−δ, and produces a KL-divergence loss between their output differences. The final output is a standard class-label prediction from the student, but the training objective specifically enforces that the student's input-space gradient tracks the teacher's at every training point.

## Method

The key insight is that adversarially trained models are locally linear: the remainder of the first-order Taylor expansion is negligible (≈1–2% of the output norm), so the output difference f(x+δ)−f(x−δ) approximates 2·(∂f/∂x)ᵀδ. IGDM exploits this by matching the student's and teacher's output differences at x±δ via KL divergence, which—because δ varies across training steps and is aligned with the gradient direction from the inner maximization—indirectly enforces ∂f_S/∂x ≈ ∂f_T/∂x without ever computing the high-dimensional input gradient explicitly. The perturbation δ is reused from the base AD method's inner maximization (PGD, TRADES, RSLAD, or AdaAD attack), so no additional forward passes beyond two extra evaluations per model are needed. The resulting loss L_IGDM = D(f_S(x+δ)−f_S(x−δ), f_T(x+δ)−f_T(x−δ)) is added as a plug-in module with a single hyperparameter α (scaled by an epoch-progressive schedule) to any existing AD outer loss: L_min = L_AD + α·L_IGDM. The paper further shows that direct gradient matching (computing ∂f/∂x explicitly) fails due to optimization difficulties at the pixel level, while the indirect logit-difference formulation operates in a higher-level feature space and converges reliably.

## Results

On CIFAR-100 with ResNet-18 and a BDM-AT teacher, IGDM integrated into AdaAD achieves 30.32% AutoAttack accuracy, improving over AdaAD alone (28.06%) by 2.26 points. The same module applied to RSLAD raises AA from 26.76% to 29.78%, and applied to ARD from 24.77% to 28.79%. On MobileNetV2 (CIFAR-100, BDM-AT teacher), RSLAD+IGDM reaches 29.32% AA versus 26.18% for RSLAD alone. Clean accuracy remains largely stable (within ~0.5 points) across all configurations, confirming that the gains come from improved adversarial robustness rather than clean-accuracy trade-offs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IGDM is evaluated as an additive module on top of seven baselines (PGD-AT, TRADES, ARD, IAD, AKD, RSLAD, AdaAD/AdaIAD) across CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, SVHN, and Tiny-ImageNet. In every configuration, adding IGDM strictly improves AutoAttack accuracy while keeping clean accuracy within ~0.5 points. The largest absolute gains appear when IGDM is combined with weaker baselines: PGD-AT jumps from 18.86% to 29.16% AA on CIFAR-100 (BDM-AT teacher), a +10.3-point improvement. Against the strongest baseline AdaAD (28.06% AA), IGDM adds +2.26 points to reach 30.32%, and this advantage persists even when AdaAD's expensive teacher-in-the-loop inner maximization is removed (29.34% vs. 25.62% for AdaAD without teacher inner loss). Gradient alignment metrics (Gradient Distance and Gradient Cosine similarity) consistently improve with IGDM, confirming the mechanism: e.g., GD drops from 0.107 to 0.086 and GC rises from 0.567 to 0.643 for AdaAD on CIFAR-100. The paper also shows that simply tuning AdaAD's existing hyperparameter λ does not replicate these gains, indicating that point-wise logit matching alone is insufficient to capture the teacher's gradient structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adversarial distillation (AD) literature, where prior methods (ARD, IAD, AKD, RSLAD, AdaAD) all transfer knowledge by matching the teacher's logits to the student's. The key departure is distilling input gradient information instead, leveraging the locally linear property of adversarially trained models (established by Tsipras et al. and Engstrom et al.) to approximate gradients via first-order Taylor expansion. Rather than computing gradients directly (which the authors show fails due to optimization difficulties with low-level pixel-space features), IGDM matches output differences f(x+δ)−f(x−δ) between teacher and student, which algebraically reduces to gradient alignment. The modular loss design explicitly positions IGDM as a complement to, not replacement for, existing AD methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The modular, plug-and-play nature of IGDM makes it immediately adoptable by any AD pipeline, and the consistent 2–3 percentage point AutoAttack gains across seven baselines, four datasets, two student architectures, and three teacher models demonstrate broad applicability. The negative result showing that direct gradient matching fails (Appendix A.4) while the indirect logit-difference formulation succeeds provides useful guidance for future gradient-based distillation work. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger datasets (ImageNet), whether the local linearity assumption holds for non-CNN architectures (e.g., ViTs), and whether the per-configuration hyperparameter tuning (α) can be automated or made more principled.
