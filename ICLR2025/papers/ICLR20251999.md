## Problem

Prior dataset usage inference methods reduce the question to a binary decision (was the dataset used or not?), which is fragile under partial utilization — a practical setting where models are trained on combinations of subsets from multiple data sources. This paper formalizes Dataset Usage Cardinality Inference (DUCI), which estimates the exact proportion of a dataset used in training, and identifies that naively aggregating individual membership inference guesses introduces a systematic bias that must be explicitly corrected.

## Input / Output

The method requires black-box access to the target model θ, full access to the target dataset X, and as few as one reference model trained on a random half of X (plus population data when using RMIA). It outputs a point estimate p̂ of the usage proportion and a 95% confidence interval derived via the Lyapunov CLT. A key constraint is that no modifications to the data are needed, making the approach applicable to already-released datasets and integrity-sensitive domains such as healthcare.

## Method

The core mechanism debiases each individual membership inference guess (from RMIA or LiRA) by subtracting the dataset-level FPR and dividing by (TPR − FPR), yielding an unbiased per-point inclusion probability estimate γ̂_i. TPR and FPR are estimated at the dataset level (averaged across all |X| points) using reference models, reducing the sampling error to O(1/√(N·|X|)) and requiring as few as one reference model rather than the 21×N models needed by MLE baselines. The debiased per-point estimates are averaged to produce p̂, which is proven unbiased under i.i.d. and uniform sampling; for special non-uniform sampling, the method extends to group-level debiasing by partitioning X into subgroups with similar TPR−FPR. The approach achieves maximum MAE < 0.1 with a single reference model, matching MLE baselines that require 630× more computation, and generalizes to text models for quantifying book copyright infringement.

## Results

The DUCI method achieves a maximum mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.0271 on CIFAR-10 with FC-5 and 42 reference models, and as low as 0.015 on CIFAR-10 with ResNet-34. Remarkably, with just 1 reference model, DUCI still achieves max MAE of 0.0678 (CIFAR-10, FC-5), far outperforming MIA Guess (0.3291) and MIA Score (0.3619) under the same budget. The method matches the idealized MLE (Avg-Logit) baseline (0.0283 at 630 reference models) while requiring only 42 reference models—a 15× reduction in computational cost. On the book copyright infringement case study (GPT-2, BookMIA), DUCI achieves max MAE of 0.168 versus 0.335 (MIA Guess) and 0.502 (MIA Score).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the RMIA-based MIA Guess baseline (Zarifzadeh et al., 2024), DUCI reduces max MAE by 5–10× across all settings: e.g., 0.0271 vs. 0.2516 on CIFAR-10/FC-5 at 42 reference models, and 0.0678 vs. 0.3291 at just 1 reference model. The MIA Score baseline (using continuous RMIA/LiRA confidence scores, Carlini et al., 2022) performs even worse than MIA Guess in most settings (e.g., 0.2948 vs. 0.2516 on CIFAR-10/FC-5 at 42 ref), confirming that raw score averaging is unreliable. Against the MLE (Avg-Logit) baseline, which is closely related to the dataset inference approach of Maini et al. (2021), DUCI at 42 reference models achieves 0.0271 vs. 0.0533 on CIFAR-10/FC-5—a 2× improvement—while the MLE requires 630 reference models to reach 0.0283, making DUCI roughly 15× more computationally efficient at comparable accuracy. The MLE (Joint-Logit) variant, which models high-dimensional joint logits, underperforms both DUCI and MLE (Avg-Logit) in all tested settings, suggesting that the high-dimensional Gaussian assumption is poorly calibrated. Under special (non-uniform) sampling via EL2N coreset selection, DUCI degrades gracefully (max MAE 0.1089) while MLE (Avg-Logit) collapses to 0.6117, further highlighting the robustness advantage of the debiasing approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the entire line of dataset ownership verification and provenance tracing work (backdoor watermarking, dataset inference via average statistics) by reframing the problem from a binary all-or-none question to a continuous proportion estimation task. It repurposes existing membership inference attacks (RMIA, LiRA) as the underlying signal but adds a novel debiasing layer that transforms noisy individual membership guesses into an unbiased dataset-level proportion estimator. The MLE baselines (joint-logit and avg-logit) are positioned as idealized but computationally prohibitive upper bounds, while the debiased MIA approach achieves comparable accuracy at 100–300× lower cost.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation is strong: copyright law (e.g., U.S. Copyright Act §107) requires assessing the 'amount and substantiality' of data used, which binary methods fundamentally cannot do. The paper demonstrates that existing binary methods are unreliable under partial usage, a common real-world scenario. Open questions remain around adversarial or quality-dependent sampling (where the method degrades, though less than baselines), the possibility of leveraging inter-record correlations for further gains, and extension to user-level or neighborhood-level inference. The method's dependence on reference models for TPR/FPR estimation also limits its applicability in truly black-box settings where no training data access is available.
