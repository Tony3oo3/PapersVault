## Problem

Existing interpretability methods for deep neural networks examine individual neurons, single layers, weight importance, or latent representations in isolation, leaving the collective behavior of neuron groups unexplored. No prior work has systematically identified collaborative neural communities and analyzed how these communities jointly govern information flow and decision-making across the full network architecture.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the frozen weights of a trained network (linear or convolutional) along with a held-out test set; no retraining is performed. For convolutional layers, kernels are first 'neuronized' via a KL-divergence/cosine-similarity correlation metric (Eq. 1) before community detection. Outputs include soft community assignments (probability q_{k,c} per neuron), a composite evaluation score Q, per-community input sensitivity heatmaps matching the spatial dimensions of the input image (e.g., 28×28 for MNIST), and per-community accuracy drops measured by shutting down all neurons in a community simultaneously.

## Method

InnerSightNet operates in three phases. (1) Neuronization: learnable units (linear nodes, convolutional kernels) are mapped to a unified neuron representation; for conv layers, inter-kernel correlation is quantified via KL divergence over positive/negative activation regions normalized by cosine similarity. (2) Aggregation: an EM algorithm derived from Newman's (2006) probabilistic mixed-model framework treats activation and inhibition connections between adjacent layers as Bernoulli variables, iteratively updating community assignments q_{k,c}, community priors π_c, and per-community connection probabilities τ to maximize expected log-likelihood. (3) Evaluation: a composite metric Q weights three components — input-sensitivity similarity (Jacobian-based), output-representation consistency (normalization consistency score × sign consistency), and structural modularity (a variant measuring intra- vs. inter-community connection density) — to select the optimal number of communities (searched over 1–20). The algorithm uses 100 random restarts with log-space arithmetic to mitigate EM's sensitivity to initialization and numerical underflow.

## Results

On the noise neuron identification task, InnerSightNet achieves 92.69%±0.008% accuracy on MNIST and 98.78%±0.033% on AFHQ, outperforming all three baselines on both datasets. For network pruning on AFHQ, InnerSightNet retains only 70 neurons (a 68.75% reduction from 224) while maintaining 93.6% accuracy. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests confirm statistical significance (all p-values < 0.01) between InnerSightNet and each baseline on both datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

InnerSightNet outperforms all three baselines on the primary noise neuron identification task: it achieves 92.69% on MNIST (vs. 92.58% Filan, 92.61% Hod, 92.63% Liu) and 98.78% on AFHQ (vs. 98.56%, 98.60%, 98.68%), with the smallest variance across runs. For the secondary pruning task on AFHQ, InnerSightNet uses the fewest neurons (70) at 93.6% accuracy, compared to Filan (105 neurons, 94.2%), Hod (81 neurons, 93.5%), and Liu (75 neurons, 93.4%). While Filan achieves 0.6% higher pruning accuracy, it requires 15.625% more neurons than InnerSightNet, making InnerSightNet the most parameter-efficient. The paper attributes InnerSightNet's advantage to its consideration of cross-layer connection strength and probability rather than single-layer or individual-neuron analysis. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (p < 0.01 for all pairs on both datasets) confirm the differences are statistically significant.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts Newman's (2006) probabilistic community detection framework—originally designed for social and biological networks—to the internal weight structure of deep neural networks, treating neurons as nodes and activation/inhibition connections as directed edges. It extends the perturbation-statistical analysis of Watanabe et al. (2018) from single-neuron sensitivity to community-level sensitivity, and departs from prior interpretability work (Grad-CAM, LIME, SHAP, network dissection) by operating at the community granularity rather than the individual neuron or layer level. The EM-based aggregation that jointly models upstream and downstream connection probabilities is the core methodological contribution, distinguishing it from Louvain/Leiden-style modularity optimization used in GNN community detection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The community-level lens offers a practical middle ground between single-neuron analysis (too granular) and whole-network visualization (too coarse), enabling concrete applications like targeted pruning (68.75% neuron reduction with 5.1% accuracy loss) and noise-neuron identification that improves generalization. However, the computational cost (4–7 hours per layer via 100-restart EM) severely limits scalability to modern architectures, and the observed community concentration in CNNs (only 3–4 communities vs. 10–15 in linear layers) raises open questions about whether the method captures task-relevant structure or merely classification-specific features. Extending the framework to non-classification tasks (generative models, reinforcement learning) and reducing the EM overhead remain key open directions.
