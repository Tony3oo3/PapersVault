## Problem

While prior LLM consistency research has examined formal variations (answer formats, languages) or single domains (values, biases), the consistency between what models explicitly state (words) and what they actually do in grounded situations (deeds) across diverse domains remains unexplored. This paper asks whether LLMs are consistent in words and deeds, whether alignment exacerbates the gap, and whether standard knowledge generalization methods can bridge it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250865/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each test item is a strictly aligned pair: a word question (direct query about an opinion, value, or belief, avg ~22 words) and a deed question (a grounded scenario with a character facing a topic-related action choice, avg ~66 words), both offering two options (A/B) that correspond semantically across the pair. The model produces a free-form response parsed via regex into a single option letter; the primary metric is the Consistency Score (fraction of pairs where the same option is selected for both questions), supplemented by a Probability Consistency Score computed as 1 minus the Jensen-Shannon Divergence between the first-token probability distributions over options for the two questions.

## Method

The paper introduces the Words and Deeds Consistency Test (WDCT), a 1,225-item benchmark spanning four domains (opinion, non-ethical value, ethical value, theory), where deed questions are generated by GPT-4 from word questions via a three-component pipeline (situation, fixed question, action options) and manually validated. To probe the role of alignment, the authors perform separate SFT and DPO on words or deeds in directions opposite to the model's initial choices (mixed 1:9 with Alpaca data), then measure how alignment on one aspect shifts the other. They further test two knowledge-generalization strategies—3-shot chain-of-thought prompting that elicits the belief guiding an action, and data augmentation via paraphrasing or auto-generated aligned scenarios—to assess whether these can close the word-deed gap. The central finding is that the knowledge underlying word and deed choices does not reside in a unified representational space, so aligning one aspect produces poor and unpredictable effects on the other, and neither CoT nor augmentation fundamentally resolves the inconsistency.

## Results

On the WDCT benchmark (1,225 test items across 4 domains), GPT-4-Turbo achieves the highest average Consistency Score (CS) of 0.76, while most LLMs exhibit average inconsistency exceeding 30% relative to the 0.50 random baseline. Notably, Llama-2-7B-Chat (0.49) and Chatglm3-6B (0.54) fall at or below random chance, indicating severe word-deed inconsistency. Pretrained models maintain a Probability Consistency Score (PCS) around 0.96–0.97, but alignment (IFT/RLHF) drops PCS by approximately 0.2, revealing that alignment widens the probability distribution gap between word and deed responses.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark paper, so the primary comparison is across 12 LLMs rather than between proposed methods. Against the 0.50 random baseline, GPT-4-Turbo (0.76) and Llama-3-70B-Instruct (0.73) show the strongest word-deed consistency, while Llama-2-7B-Chat (0.49) and Chatglm3-6B (0.54) perform at or below random. A key finding is that alignment consistently degrades consistency: comparing pretrained vs. aligned pairs (e.g., Llama-2-7B 0.50 → Llama-2-7B-Chat 0.49; Mistral-7B 0.63 → Mistral-7B-Instruct 0.66; Llama-3-8B 0.61 → Llama-3-8B-Instruct 0.64), the PCS drops by 0.15–0.40, indicating alignment introduces greater divergence between word and deed probability distributions. For knowledge generalization methods, CoT prompting yields only marginal gains (e.g., GPT-4: 0.76→0.79) or even decreases (Chatglm3-6B: 0.54→0.48), while data augmentation with auto-generated aligned data (Dual-Aug) provides the largest improvement (Mistral-7B-Instruct: 0.66→0.86), though still falling short of GPT-4-level consistency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing a previously unexplored axis of LLM consistency: alignment between stated beliefs (words) and grounded behavioral choices (deeds), as opposed to the four established categories of consistency analysis (formal, semantic, logical, factual) that examine consistency across different application *forms* (paraphrases, option orders, languages). Prior work on implicit vs. explicit LLM behavior has focused narrowly on specific ethical issues like social bias or toxic language, whereas WDCT spans four domains (opinion, non-ethical value, ethical value, theory) with strict word-deed correspondence. The paper also repurposes standard alignment techniques (SFT, DPO) not as a contribution but as a diagnostic tool to probe whether word-level and deed-level knowledge occupy a unified representational space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that alignment (SFT/DPO) exacerbates rather than resolves word-deed inconsistency is practically significant for anyone deploying LLMs in safety-critical or trust-dependent settings, as it suggests current alignment pipelines may create models that sound principled but act inconsistently. The failure of CoT prompting and data augmentation to fundamentally fix the gap points toward the need for architectural-level solutions, leaving a clear open question about how to achieve synchronized alignment of beliefs and behaviors. Limitations include the binary-choice format, English-only evaluation, and testing on only 12 models, which may constrain generalizability to open-ended or multilingual settings.
