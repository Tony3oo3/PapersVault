## Problem

Behavior cloning policies trained on limited expert demonstrations fail to generalize to unseen task specifications, and existing hypernetwork-based policy generators (e.g., HyperZero) that map task embeddings directly to policy parameters via MLPs suffer from overfitting because they ignore the optimization process that produced the optimal weights and the structural dependencies within the target network. HyPoGen addresses this by injecting an optimization inductive bias into the hypernetwork architecture so that policy synthesis is framed as iterative optimization guided solely by task specifications, rather than memorization of parameter mappings.

## Input / Output

The method takes a low-dimensional task specification vector (e.g., desired speed, torso length, controller stiffness, arm length) as input, encoded by a 6 Res-Block encoder into a 256-dim embedding. It outputs the full parameter set of an MLP policy network (2-layer, hidden dim 256 for MuJoCo; 3-layer for ManiSkill), which is then loaded into the target network for action prediction. Crucially, the hypernetwork operates in a compressed 256-dim latent parameter space via layer-level encoders/decoders, and at test time requires no demonstrations—only the task specification—to generate a complete policy.

## Method

HyPoGen replaces the single-shot MLP mapping with K=8 iterative update steps in a latent parameter space, where each step predicts a 'stepsize' λ and a 'neural gradient' ψ from the task embedding, mimicking a gradient descent update θ^k = θ^{k-1} + λ^k · ψ^k. The key architectural innovation is in ψ: it explicitly decomposes the gradient computation according to the chain rule, using separate MLP sub-networks to estimate activation gradients (∇_z h_n) and parameter gradients (∇_θ h_n) for each layer of the target network, then composes them via the product structure of backpropagation. Pseudo-activations ẑ_n are also predicted iteratively to feed into the gradient estimators. This structural bias constrains the solution space, reduces overfitting, and causes the BC loss to monotonically decrease across the 8 update steps (verified empirically), effectively performing optimization without any demonstration data at test time.

## Results

On MuJoCo (DeepMind Control Suite), HyPoGen achieves the highest average reward across all 9 task-specification combinations (3 environments × 3 specification types), e.g., 819.23 on Cheetah-speed vs. 695.73 for HyperZero and 433.24 for Cond Policy. On ManiSkill, HyPoGen achieves 97.20% success rate on LiftCube-stiffness and 93.28% on LiftCube-damping, where HyperZero and all other baselines fail completely (0% success). HyPoGen also converges ~5× faster than HyperZero, reaching 600 reward in 74 epochs vs. 394 epochs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HyPoGen outperforms all five baselines on every MuJoCo task-specification combination. The largest margin is against HyperZero (the strongest baseline): +123.5 reward on Cheetah-speed (819.23 vs. 695.73) and +238.4 on Finger-speed (835.21 vs. 596.80). On ManiSkill, the advantage is most dramatic on controller stiffness and damping: HyPoGen achieves 97.20% and 93.28% success on LiftCube-stiffness/damping, while HyperZero, Cond Policy, UVFA, and Meta Policy all score 0%. Cond Policy and UVFA are consistently the weakest baselines, confirming that simply conditioning a policy on task specifications is insufficient. Meta Policy (MAML) and PEARL require few-shot fine-tuning with test-time demonstrations, violating the zero-shot setting, yet still underperform HyPoGen's zero-shot generation. On the simpler ManiSkill cube-size task, HyPoGen (85.87%) is slightly below UVFA (90.27%), which the authors attribute to the near-linear relationship between clamp width and cube size making the task less demanding.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on HyperZero (Rezaei-Shoshtari et al., 2023), which uses an MLP-based hypernetwork to map task specifications to policy network parameters for zero-shot transfer in RL, and on the broader hypernetwork literature (Ha et al., 2017). The key departure is structural: rather than a single forward pass from specification to parameters, HyPoGen decomposes the hypernetwork into K iterative update blocks that mimic SGD steps, with 'neural gradients' computed via a chain-rule-inspired architecture that mirrors backpropagation through the target network's layers. This positions the work at the intersection of hypernetworks and learned optimizers, though unlike learned optimizers it requires no meta-learning or test-time data—updates are predicted purely from the task specification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The optimization inductive bias provides a principled mechanism for reducing overfitting in hypernetwork-based policy generation, and the empirical results—particularly the 97% vs. 0% success rate gap on ManiSkill stiffness specifications—demonstrate that architectural structure can matter more than model capacity for generalization. However, the approach is limited to MLP-based policy networks and tasks that share a common specification structure; generalizing to truly novel task families (e.g., different morphologies or task types) remains unresolved. An open question is whether the chain-rule gradient structure can be extended to non-MLP architectures (transformers, CNNs) or whether the latent-space optimization formulation generalizes beyond the 8-step regime tested here.
