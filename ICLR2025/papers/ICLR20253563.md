## Problem

When combining LoRA with federated learning, directly averaging A and B matrices on the server introduces aggregation errors because the product of averages differs from the average of products. Existing remedies either freeze one matrix (FFA-LoRA, which impairs learning capacity) or enforce a uniform model update across all clients (suboptimal under non-IID data). The paper asks whether one can selectively aggregate only the components encoding shared knowledge while preserving client-specific information locally.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253563/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each federated client receives a local shard of text data (GLUE tasks for NLU, GSM8K or CodeSearchNet for NLG) and fine-tunes a frozen pre-trained model (RoBERTa-large or LLaMA3-8B) with LoRA adapters on query and value attention matrices at rank r=8. The critical I/O constraint is that only the A matrices (half the LoRA parameters, 0.39M per round) are uploaded to the server, while B matrices never leave the client, halving per-round communication versus standard LoRA. Outputs are class labels for NLU and generated reasoning text for NLG.

## Method

Lemma 1 formalizes the asymmetry: the optimal A solution is independent of the input data distribution (general knowledge), while the optimal B solution depends on the data covariance E[x_t x_t^T] (client-specific knowledge). FedSA-LoRA keeps both A and B fully trainable during local SGD/AdamW updates but transmits only A matrices to the server for FedAvg, so each client's effective weight update becomes B_i · (1/m)Σ_j A_j — a personalized update that retains client-specific information in the local B while sharing general knowledge through the aggregated A. The framework generalizes to rsLoRA (FedSA-rsLoRA, differing only in the rank-stabilization scaling factor) and VeRA (FedSA-VeRA, where only the d scaling vector is shared while b stays local), establishing a reusable paradigm for any LoRA variant in FL. A convergence proof under L-smoothness, bounded stochastic gradients, and non-vanishing eigenvalue assumptions yields an O(1/√T) rate toward stationary points, matching standard FedAvg in the non-convex regime.

## Results

On the GLUE benchmark (6 NLU tasks), FedSA-LoRA achieves 90.43% average accuracy, outperforming LoRA (89.33%), FFA-LoRA (89.39%), and FedDPA-LoRA (89.47%). The same pattern holds for rsLoRA variants (FedSA-rsLoRA: 90.52% vs. rsLoRA: 89.36%) and VeRA variants (FedSA-VeRA: 88.45% vs. VeRA: 87.40%). On the GSM8K generation task, FedSA-LoRA reaches 46.63% accuracy versus 46.23% (LoRA) and 46.32% (FFA-LoRA), and on CodeSearchNet it scores 59.66% versus 58.34% (LoRA) and 58.57% (FFA-LoRA). In terms of system efficiency, FedSA-LoRA requires only 91 communication rounds to reach target performance on RTE (vs. 167 for LoRA and 229 for FFA-LoRA) while communicating only 0.39M parameters per round.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FedSA-LoRA consistently outperforms all three baselines (LoRA, FFA-LoRA, FedDPA-LoRA) across all six GLUE tasks, with the largest gains on MNLI-m (+1.47% over LoRA) and QQP (+2.15% over LoRA). The advantage grows with data heterogeneity: on QNLI, the improvement over LoRA increases from 0.03% (IID) to 1.84% (severe non-IID), confirming that keeping B matrices local is most beneficial when client data is most divergent. FFA-LoRA, which freezes A matrices, is consistently the weakest baseline among the three LoRA-based methods on RTE (86.08% vs. 87.49% for LoRA), validating the paper's argument that fixing A impairs learning. The same win pattern replicates for rsLoRA (FedSA-rsLoRA: 90.52% vs. 89.36–89.57%) and VeRA (FedSA-VeRA: 88.45% vs. 87.35–87.61%), establishing the generality of the selective-aggregation paradigm. On generation tasks, FedSA-LoRA also leads: 46.63% on GSM8K (vs. 46.23/46.32) and 59.66% on CodeSearchNet (vs. 58.34/58.57), with qualitative examples showing correct multi-step reasoning where baselines make arithmetic errors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) and its federated variants, most notably FFA-LoRA (Sun et al., 2024b), which freezes A matrices to halve communication cost, and FedDPA/FDLoRA, which use separate global and personalized LoRA modules. The key conceptual departure is the asymmetry analysis showing that A and B play fundamentally different roles in FL—A encodes general knowledge while B encodes client-specific knowledge—motivating a 'train both, aggregate only A' strategy rather than FFA-LoRA's 'freeze A' approach. The paper also extends the single-model asymmetry observations of Zhu et al. (2024) and Tian et al. (2024) into the federated setting and generalizes the finding to rsLoRA and VeRA, establishing a reusable paradigm for future LoRA variants in FL.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The A/B decomposition provides a clean, generalizable principle for designing federated LoRA methods: identify which parameters carry shared knowledge and aggregate those, while keeping client-specific parameters local. This framework is immediately applicable to any future LoRA variant, reducing the design space for new FL+LoRA methods. Limitations include evaluation primarily on RoBERTa-large (355M) and LLaMA-8B with relatively small client counts (3–100), and the performance gains over strong baselines are modest (~1% on GLUE). Open questions include whether the A/B asymmetry persists at much larger scales (70B+), under extreme heterogeneity, or when combined with other FL techniques like differential privacy or client sampling strategies.
