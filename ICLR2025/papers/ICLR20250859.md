## Problem

Generative-model-based OOD detectors fail in high dimensions due to likelihood misestimation and the gap between likelihood and typicality, and they focus on pixel-level features rather than semantic content, causing failures on near-OOD inputs where pixels are similar but information content differs. Additionally, the rise of photorealistic synthetic data from foundation models (e.g., Stable Diffusion) creates a new detection challenge that distribution-level metrics (FD, FID, KL divergence) cannot resolve at the individual-sample level.

## Input / Output

The method requires a set of unlabeled reference images from the in-distribution (split into thirds: reference, held-out test, and a test distribution drawn from the reference) and a single test image to be classified. Features are extracted via three self-supervised encoders (CLIP, ViT-MSN, DINOv2) and concatenated. The output is a four-dimensional per-point summary statistic vector (precision, recall, density, coverage) computed via k-nearest-neighbor distances in the combined feature space, which is then scored by a trained density estimator (GMM, KDE, or OCSVM) to yield a continuous atypicality score and a binary ID/OOD decision. No class labels, OOD examples, or knowledge of the generative model architecture are required.

## Method

Forte replaces pixel-space generative likelihoods with semantic-space manifold geometry: it computes four per-point summary statistics (precision, recall, density, coverage) for each test point using k-NN distances to the reference set in a concatenated self-supervised feature space, adapting metrics originally designed for generative model evaluation (Naeem et al., Kynkäänniemi et al.) to the OOD detection setting. These statistics capture local geometric properties of the data manifold—whether a test point falls within the reference manifold's support (precision/coverage), how much of the reference it covers (recall), and its local density relative to the reference (density). A non-parametric density estimator (GMM, KDE, or One-Class SVM) is trained on the summary statistics of in-distribution test points to model the typical set, then scores new test points by their density under this model. The paper provides a theoretical analysis showing that under Gaussian ID/OOD assumptions with a large mean shift, the expected per-point metrics for ID samples (precision ≈ 1−e^{−k}, density ≈ 1) are sharply separated from OOD values (≈ 0), giving a principled basis for the decision boundary. Combining multiple representation backbones (CLIP + ViT-MSN + DINOv2) captures complementary semantic manifolds and yields the strongest near-OOD performance.

## Results

On the supervised SOTA comparison (Table 1), Forte+GMM achieves AUROC of 99.67 (FPR95 0.64) on iNaturalist, 98.34 (FPR95 5.18) on NINCO, and 94.95 (FPR95 22.30) on the challenging near-OOD SSB-Hard benchmark, consistently outperforming all supervised baselines. Against unsupervised baselines on CIFAR-10 (Table 2), Forte+GMM reaches AUROC 97.63/100.00/99.49 (FPR95 9.69/0.00/0.00) on CIFAR-100/Celeb-A/SVHN, compared to DoSE's 56.90/97.60/97.30 (FPR95 91.95/12.82/13.16). On medical imaging (Table 5), Forte+SVM achieves perfect AUROC 100.00 and FPR95 0.00 across all three FastMRI-to-OAI dataset pairs, while Forte+GMM achieves AUROC ≥99.91 with FPR95 0.00.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against supervised SOTA, Forte+GMM outperforms NNGuide (Park et al., 2023) on iNaturalist (99.67 vs 99.57 AUROC) and Texture (98.04 vs 95.82), and dramatically on NINCO (98.34 vs not reported) and SSB-Hard (94.95 vs not reported). It surpasses ViM (Wang et al., 2022) on iNaturalist (99.67 vs 99.41) and Texture (98.04 vs 95.34), and by a wide margin on SSB-Hard (94.95 vs not reported). Against the strongest OpenOOD v1.5 entries (Zhang et al., 2024a), Forte+GMM beats ViT-B+CE+RMDS on every reported task (e.g., 98.34 vs 87.31 on NINCO, 94.95 vs 72.87 on SSB-Hard) and DINOv2+MLS on iNaturalist (99.67 vs 98.41), Texture (98.04 vs 91.82), NINCO (98.34 vs 88.38), and SSB-Hard (94.95 vs 77.28). Among unsupervised baselines on CIFAR-10, Forte+GMM (AUROC 97.63/100.00/99.49) dominates DoSE (56.90/97.60/97.30), TT (54.80/84.80/87.00), WAIC (53.20/92.80/14.30), LLR (48.13/80.08/64.21), and the single-sided threshold q(X|θ_n) (52.00/91.40/6.50) by 40+ AUROC points on CIFAR-100 and Celeb-A, with FPR95 reduced from 91.95% (DoSE) to 9.69%.

## Relation to Prior Work

Forte builds directly on DoSE (Morningstar et al., 2021), which established the framework of using summary statistics to score typicality for OOD detection, but replaces DoSE's reliance on generative model likelihoods (shown to be unreliable by Caterini & Loaiza-Ganem, 2022 and Zhang et al., 2021) with self-supervised representations from CLIP, DINOv2, and ViT-MSN. The per-point precision, recall, density, and coverage metrics are adapted from generative model evaluation literature (Naeem et al., 2020; Kynkänniemi et al., 2019), repurposing manifold-estimation statistics originally designed to assess fidelity and diversity of generated samples as anomaly-detection features. The key conceptual departure is shifting the typicality measurement from pixel-space generative likelihoods to semantic representation geometry, where local neighborhood structure in feature space encodes distributional membership more robustly than likelihood values in high dimensions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The systematic demonstration that standard distribution-level statistics (KS test, Mann-Whitney U, Z-test, LOF, Isolation Forest, JSD, KLD, Wasserstein distance) all fail to reliably distinguish individual real from synthetic images is practically important as generative models produce increasingly photorealistic outputs. The framework's applicability to medical imaging batch-effect detection (near-perfect AUROC on FastMRI/OAI) suggests broader utility in high-stakes domains where distribution shifts are subtle but consequential. However, the notably weak performance on ImageNet-V2 (59.28 AUROC) and the theoretical analysis's reliance on Gaussian assumptions with equal covariance limit generalizability claims. Open questions include computational scalability of per-point metrics to very large reference sets, behavior under truly novel (not merely shifted) OOD distributions, and whether the approach extends to non-visual modalities.
