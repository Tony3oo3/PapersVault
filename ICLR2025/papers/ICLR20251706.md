## Problem

Existing social navigation methods either depend on privileged sensors (e.g., humanoid GPS providing exact polar coordinates to the person) that are impractical in real-world deployment, or model only collision avoidance without capturing the cooperative dynamics needed to find and follow a human. The core gap is how a robot can infer the social dynamics of a partially observable human from its own egocentric state-action history, without direct access to the human's trajectory or position.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251706/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The robot consumes egocentric arm and stereo depth images plus a preprocessed humanoid detection bounding box, both processed through a ResNet, along with the previous action and a 128-dimensional social dynamics latent vector. During Stage 1 training, the latent vector is computed from a 0.8-second window of absolute x-y human trajectory coordinates (privileged); during Stage 2 deployment, it is inferred solely from the robot's own N-step history of depth images, bounding boxes, and velocity commands. The policy outputs linear and angular velocity commands in the robot's local frame at each timestep (~0.04 s).

## Method

SDA is a two-stage framework. Stage 1 jointly trains a 3-layer MLP trajectory encoder μ that maps the human's recent trajectory into a 128-dim latent vector z_t, and a recurrent DD-PPO motion policy π conditioned on the current ResNet-encoded status, the previous action, and z_t, over 250 M steps across 24 parallel Habitat 3.0 environments. Stage 2 freezes π and trains an Adapter ψ (alternating spatial and temporal MLP layers) via supervised MSE regression to recover z_t from the robot's own state-action history (x_{t-N:t-1}, a_{t-N:t-1}), collected by rolling out the Stage-1 policy. The key design choice is encoding trajectories rather than humanoid GPS: trajectories are robot-frame-independent and thus more faithfully reconstructable from egocentric depth and detection cues, whereas hGPS implicitly encodes the robot's absolute position, which is difficult to regress without a map. A t-SNE analysis of the latent space reveals four emergent behavioral clusters (Find, Seek, Lost, Follow), confirming that the adapter captures interpretable social dynamics.

## Results

On Habitat 3.0's Social Navigation task (no privileged information at inference), SDA-S2 achieves a Finding Success (S) of 0.91, SPS of 0.45, Following Rate (F) of 0.39, and Episode Success (ES) of 0.43. The agent follows the humanoid for an average of 390 steps (vs. 218 for the Baseline) along a more optimal path (438 vs. 540 steps). The collision rate is 0.57, which the authors attribute to the longer following duration; under a 400-step follow cap, SDA and the Baseline show comparable CR (0.39 vs. 0.38).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Habitat 3.0 Baseline (Puig et al., 2024) in the realistic no-privileged-information setting, SDA-S2 wins on all primary metrics: Finding Success improves from 0.76 to 0.91 (+15 pp), SPS from 0.34 to 0.45 (+11 pp), Following Rate from 0.29 to 0.39 (+10 pp), and Episode Success from 0.40 to 0.43 (+3 pp). The collision rate is slightly higher for SDA (0.57 vs. 0.48), which the authors explain as a consequence of following the humanoid for significantly longer (390 vs. 218 steps); under a 400-step follow cap the CR gap closes (0.39 vs. 0.38). Against the adapted Baseline+Proximity (Cancelli et al., 2023), SDA-S2 again leads on S (0.91 vs. 0.85), SPS (0.45 vs. 0.41), F (0.39 vs. 0.37), and ES (0.43 vs. 0.41), with a comparable CR (0.57 vs. 0.58). Welch's t-tests confirm all improvements over both baselines are statistically significant (p < 0.001 for all metrics). The ablation in Table 2 further shows that trajectory-based adaptation (traj.A) dramatically outperforms hGPS-based adaptation in Stage 2 (ES 0.43 vs. 0.03), validating the choice of human trajectories as the privileged signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Rapid Motor Adaptation (RMA) paradigm—originally developed for legged robots to infer terrain properties from state-action history—to the social navigation domain, where the 'privileged signal' is the human's trajectory rather than terrain parameters. It departs from the Habitat 3.0 baseline (Puig et al., 2024), which relies on humanoid GPS (polar coordinates) at inference time, and from Cancelli et al. (2023), which models social factors only through proximity auxiliary tasks. The key conceptual move is showing that human trajectories, rather than the more direct hGPS signal, yield a more 'adaptable' latent representation that can be reliably regressed from the robot's own sensor history, effectively decoupling social awareness from external tracking hardware.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SDA demonstrates that a robot can maintain social navigation competence without any external human-tracking sensor at deployment, which is a practical prerequisite for real-world assistive or search-and-rescue robots. The finding that trajectories outperform hGPS in adaptability (Table 2) challenges the intuition that more direct geometric information should be easier to recover, and suggests that the choice of privileged signal matters as much as the adaptation mechanism itself. However, the work remains entirely simulation-based (Habitat 3.0), with no real-robot validation, and the multi-human generalization (Appendix A.6) shows significant degradation (S drops from 0.91 to 0.70 with three humans), leaving open whether the approach scales to genuinely crowded, unstructured environments.
