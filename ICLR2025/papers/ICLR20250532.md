## Problem

Existing process reward models (PRMs) frame step-level evaluation as an independent binary classification problem using cross-entropy loss, which ignores the sequential dependencies among reasoning steps within a trajectory and lacks theoretical grounding for how the predicted scores approximate a meaningful reward function. This paper asks whether PRM can be reformulated to capture inter-step dependencies and provide a principled, theoretically justified reward signal for multi-step reasoning.

## Input / Output

The model takes a question x and a sequence of intermediate reasoning steps a_1, ..., a_H as input, where each state s_t = (x, a_{1:t-1}) is the concatenation of the instruction and all previously generated text pieces. It outputs a scalar Q-value per step, defined as σ^{-1}(P(correct final answer | s_t, a_t)), which is a real-valued score (not bounded to [0,1]). For trajectory-level verification, the final score is the minimum Q-value across all steps, and the best-of-n selection picks the trajectory with the highest minimum score. The backbone is Deepseek-7B-base with a value head projecting the final hidden state to a single scalar.

## Method

PQM reframes PRM as a Q-value ranking problem within a deterministic MDP, where the Q-value of a state-action pair is the success probability of reaching the correct final answer (mapped to ℝ via sigmoid inverse). The key theoretical result (Theorem 3.5) proves that under a mild assumption about optimal policies, Q-values strictly ascend across correct steps, strictly descend across wrong steps, and exhibit a large gap between the two groups. To train the model to approximate these rankings, the paper proposes a margin-based comparative loss (a modified Plackett-Luce objective) that includes a hyperparameter ζ to amplify the Q-value discrepancy between correct and incorrect steps while preserving the ascending order among correct steps. The practical loss (Eq. 10) simplifies the theoretical version by ignoring internal rankings among incorrect steps, since automatic annotation pipelines uniformly label all post-error steps as wrong. The paper further proves that classification-based PRM (BCE loss) is a degenerate special case of the Q-value framework when the optimal policy makes correct steps almost certain, unifying prior work under the new theoretical lens.

## Results

On MATH500 with Llama-3-70B-Instruct as the policy model, PQM (ζ=2) achieves 51.4% BON@128 accuracy, a 11.6-point improvement over the BCE-based PRM (39.8%) and 8.2 points over ORM (43.2%). On GSM-Plus with the same policy, PQM reaches 72.33% BON@128 versus 70.75% for BCE. Across all three policy models (MetaMath-Mistral-7B, MuggleMath-13B, Llama-3-70B-Instruct) and both datasets, PQM consistently outperforms every baseline at every sampling budget from @8 to @128.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PQM outperforms all three baseline families across every policy model, dataset, and sampling budget. Against the outcome reward model (ORM, Cobbe et al. 2021), PQM gains 4–11 points on MATH500 (e.g., 51.4% vs. 43.2% at @128 with Llama-3-70B) and 1–5 points on GSM-Plus, confirming that step-level process rewards provide substantially more signal than final-answer-only rewards. Against the classification-based PRM using BCE loss (Math-Shepherd, Wang et al. 2023a), PQM achieves the paper's headline 11.6-point improvement on MATH500 @128 (51.4% vs. 39.8%) with Llama-3-70B-Instruct, and 4.3–6.3-point gains on GSM-Plus across all three policies. Against the MSE-based PRM with iterative MCTS labels (Zhang et al. 2024a), PQM wins by 8–12 points on MATH500 (e.g., 51.4% vs. 41.4% at @128 with Llama-3-70B) and 1–3 points on GSM-Plus, while also avoiding the expensive online MCTS search required at training time. The advantage of PQM is most pronounced at larger sampling budgets (@64, @128) and with stronger policy models, suggesting the ranking-based objective better discriminates among high-quality trajectories.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes process reward modeling (PRM) from the dominant classification formulation (BCE loss, as in Math-Shepherd and OpenAI's PRM) into a Q-value ranking problem grounded in a deterministic MDP. It builds directly on the Plackett-Luce ranking model, adapting it with a Q-value margin hyperparameter to capture the pronounced gap between correct and incorrect steps. A key conceptual contribution is showing that the standard classification-based PRM is a special case of the Q-value framework under extreme policy assumptions, thereby unifying prior approaches under a single theoretical umbrella.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous theoretical grounding for PRM, showing that Q-values should ascend across correct steps, descend across wrong steps, and exhibit a large gap between the two — a property that BCE-based methods provably violate. The 11.6% absolute improvement on MATH500 (Llama-3-70B) is consistent across backbones, datasets, and sampling policies, making the comparative loss a practical drop-in replacement. However, the authors acknowledge a significant gap to ceiling performance, noise in automatic annotations that degrades the theoretically-motivated loss, and the absence of online RL algorithms that fully exploit PRM signals — all pointing to substantial open research.
