## Problem

Contrastive vision-language models (CLIP, SigLIP) exhibit two puzzling phenomena: a modality gap separating image and text embeddings in the shared space, and a bias toward objects over attributes. While both were previously identified, their root cause, their actual impact on downstream performance, and whether they are bugs or features remained unresolved. This paper asks: what single factor drives both effects, and can we mitigate them?

## Input / Output

The analysis evaluates 98 pre-trained contrastive VLMs (ViT, ResNet, ConvNeXt backbones) that take RGB images and text prompts (prepended with 'a photo of') and produce L2-normalized embeddings in a shared space, from which cosine similarity scores are computed for zero-shot classification and retrieval. The paper also introduces the MAD synthetic dataset (Morpho-MNIST digits with five controllable attributes: thickness, swelling, fractures, scaling, color) where captions can be selectively truncated to manipulate information imbalance, and the MOAD metric that quantifies object-vs-attribute bias by comparing within-class to cross-class embedding similarities.

## Method

The paper proposes two new metrics—Relative Modality Gap (RMG), which normalizes the matching-pair distance by the effectively used embedding space, and Matching Object Attribute Distance (MOAD), which formalizes object bias as the difference in discriminative power between objects and attributes—and then identifies information imbalance (images contain far more information than their sparse captions) as the common trigger for both phenomena. The mechanism is: bounded alignment (due to the image encoder not knowing which caption content to encode) forces the model to maximize uniformity instead, pushing modalities apart via a few dominant embedding dimensions, while the image encoder develops a caption-presence bias toward the most likely words (typically objects). This is validated through fully controlled synthetic experiments on MAD (varying the number of attributes in captions while keeping images fixed) and real-data experiments on CC12M (dropping 1/2 or 3/4 of caption words), showing that reducing information imbalance simultaneously shrinks the modality gap, reduces object bias, and improves downstream accuracy. The paper further reveals that the modality gap functions as a per-sample entropy control mechanism, complementing the global temperature parameter in the CLIP loss.

## Results

Across 98 pre-trained contrastive VLMs, the modality gap (measured by L2M and the proposed RMG) shows only weak positive correlations with downstream performance (Kendall's τ = 0.167 for MS COCO, −0.008 for ImageNet), which are overshadowed by confounders like model size (τ = −0.354) and embedding size (τ = 0.264). When controlling for pre-training dataset, the expected negative correlation (smaller gap → better performance) appears in 7 out of 10 cases for RMG. In the fully controlled synthetic MAD experiments, reducing information imbalance (more attributes in captions) decreases both the modality gap and object bias while improving downstream accuracy; in the idealized image-image setting, introducing information imbalance increases RMG from 0.006 to 0.149 and L2M from 0.058 to 0.265. Neighborhood orderings between modalities are highly dissimilar (normalized Kendall-τ ≈ 0.50), explaining why simple post-hoc gap-closing translations fail to improve performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical analysis paper without a single proposed method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it compares hypotheses and experimental conditions. The paper challenges the cone-effect hypothesis of Liang et al. (2022) by showing that the modality gap emerges even when initialization has no gap and that the contrastive loss can actually narrow the gap (RMG drops from 0.4987 to 0.0060 in a direct-optimization experiment). It also challenges Fahim et al. (2024)'s claim that the gap is inherent to the contrastive loss by demonstrating that information imbalance has a substantially larger effect (RMG: 0.149 vs. 0.006) in a controlled image-image setting. For object bias, the paper shows that global word frequency p(word) does not explain the bias (attributes are more frequent in LAION-2B captions), while per-sample caption presence p(word|image) does: models trained with different factors made always-present in captions are biased toward whichever factor is prevalent (MOAD values range from 0.14 to 0.41 across conditions). Post-hoc gap-closing via dimension ablation or ideal-word shifting (cosine similarity between ideal words and ideal images increases from ~0.39 to ~0.86) does not improve downstream accuracy, confirming the gap is not a simple performance bottleneck.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the modality gap analysis of Liang et al. (2022) by challenging their cone-effect initialization hypothesis and proposing the Relative Modality Gap (RMG) as a more informative measure than L2M. It formalizes the object bias hypothesis of Bravo et al. (2023) through the novel MOAD metric, moving beyond the informal 'poorer attribute performance' observation. The paper also extends Trager et al.'s (2023) ideal words formulation to ideal images, testing whether closing the gap improves cross-modal alignment. Conceptually, it unifies both phenomena under a single explanatory framework—information imbalance between modalities—departing from prior work that treated them as independent issues.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that both the modality gap and object bias stem from a common root cause (information imbalance) provides practitioners with a unified, actionable lever: caption enrichment or data filtering can simultaneously reduce both phenomena, as validated on CC12M and synthetic data. The reframing of the modality gap as a per-sample entropy control mechanism rather than a bug to be eliminated shifts the research agenda from post-hoc gap-closing (which the paper shows is ineffective) toward addressing the underlying data properties. Open questions include whether the entropy interpretation generalizes to non-contrastive training objectives, how to optimally balance information richness against caption noise at web scale, and whether the few-dimension structure of the gap persists in larger or differently-architected models.
