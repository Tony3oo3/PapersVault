## Problem

Real-world low-light images suffer from compounded degradations—local overexposure, noise, low brightness, and uneven illumination—that are difficult to model without reference images. Existing zero-reference methods either ignore noise entirely or bolt on separate denoising modules with ad-hoc loss functions that lack generalization, and they fail to disentangle feature layers for different degradation modes, leading to ambiguous restoration.

## Input / Output

The method requires only a single low-light RGB image with no paired or unpaired reference. During training, the input is preprocessed into two 1/4-resolution sub-images via random 2×2 neighboring pixel masking and random gamma correction (λ sampled in 1.3–1.7); during inference, the full-resolution original image is fed directly. The output is a single enhanced, denoised RGB image at the original resolution, produced by multiplying the extracted reflectance map with the adaptively corrected illumination map.

## Method

DEnet is a zero-reference joint denoising and enhancement framework grounded in an extended Retinex model I = (R + N) ∘ L. It constructs self-supervised training pairs by applying neighboring pixel masking (N2N principle) to generate two sub-images with independent zero-mean noise, then applies random gamma correction to one sub-image to create an illumination difference, so that constraining the two reflectance estimates to be equal simultaneously enforces denoising and enhancement. A Frequency-Illumination Prior Encoder (FIcoder) extracts five physical priors—channel-mean luminance plus four DCT-filtered spatial bands (low_1, low_2, high_1, high_2) capturing chromaticity, semantics, edges, and noise—into an implicit degradation representation P. The transformer-based REFnet uses multi-head cross-attention to inject P into feature tokens for reflectance extraction, while LUMnet (self-attention + gating) extracts the illumination map and LCnet (global average pooling + two linear layers) produces a scalar enhancement factor for adaptive illumination correction. The training loss combines a Retinex decomposition loss (reflectance L2 consistency across sub-images, illumination smoothness, reconstruction, and a cross-scale regularization term) with a self-supervised enhancement loss (local contrast consistency and global brightness/chromaticity constraints).

## Results

On LOLv1, the proposed method achieves PSNR 19.80, SSIM 0.750, and LPIPS 0.253, outperforming all no-reference baselines (best prior: Enlighten-Your-Voice at PSNR 19.73, SSIM 0.715). On LOLv2-Real, it reaches PSNR 20.22, SSIM 0.793, and LPIPS 0.266, again leading all zero-reference approaches. On SICE, the method attains PSNR 22.55 and SSIM 0.841, surpassing the best supervised baseline URetinexNet (22.12) in PSNR while using only 0.36M parameters. On SIDD, it achieves the best no-reference quality scores of BRISQUE 2.555 and CLIPIQA 0.292, indicating enhanced images closest to natural appearance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among no-reference methods, the proposed approach consistently outperforms all 7 baselines on PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS across both LOLv1 and LOLv2-Real, with the closest competitor being Enlighten-Your-Voice (PSNR 19.73 vs. 19.80 on LOLv1). Against unpaired methods, Ours surpasses PairLIE and NeRCo on PSNR and SSIM on both LOL datasets (e.g., 20.22 vs. 19.70 PSNR on LOLv2-Real), though PairLIE achieves a slightly lower LPIPS (0.235 vs. 0.266) on LOLv2-Real. On SICE, Ours achieves the highest PSNR (22.55) among all methods including supervised URetinexNet (22.12), while PairLIE retains a marginal LPIPS advantage (0.216 vs. 0.234). On SIDD, Ours attains the best BRISQUE (2.555) and CLIPIQA (0.292), substantially outperforming the next-best no-reference method Clip-LIT (BRISQUE 31.093, CLIPIQA 0.434). Supervised methods (SNR-aware, LLFormer) still lead on LOLv1/LOLv2 PSNR by 3–8 dB, reflecting the inherent advantage of paired training data, but Ours matches or exceeds them on SICE PSNR with only 0.36M parameters versus 50–72M for supervised competitors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate threads: Retinex decomposition (Land & McCann, 1971) extended with an explicit noise term, the Noise2Noise self-supervised denoising principle (Lehtinen et al., 2018), and the paired-illumination reflectance-consistency idea from PairLIE (Fu et al., 2023). It departs from prior zero-reference methods (Zero-DCE, SCI, RUAS) that handle denoising via bolted-on iterative modules with ad-hoc loss functions, instead deriving a single self-supervised objective from physically grounded sub-image pairs. The DCT-based frequency decomposition in sRGB space, combined with a cross-attention-guided transformer backbone, is a novel architectural choice distinct from the wavelet or Fourier approaches used in concurrent frequency-domain restoration work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical, interpretable, and parameter-efficient (0.36M) zero-reference solution for real-world low-light scenes where paired data is unavailable and degradations are compound (noise + uneven illumination + local overexposure). Its strong no-reference metrics (BRISQUE 2.555, CLIPIQA 0.292 on SIDD) suggest the DCT-prior + retinex decomposition captures perceptual quality better than larger supervised models. Open questions include whether the narrow gamma-factor operating range (1.3–1.7) limits applicability to extreme low-light, whether the 1/4-resolution masking strategy generalizes to video or very high-resolution inputs, and whether the frequency-band thresholds (t) can be learned rather than hand-set.
