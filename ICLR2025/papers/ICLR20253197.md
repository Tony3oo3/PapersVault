## Problem

Training video-conditioned policies via behavior cloning requires abundant action-labeled expert demonstrations, which are prohibitively costly to annotate. In many tasks, expert rollouts are only available as unlabeled videos, making standard behavior cloning infeasible. The paper asks whether a generalizable video-conditioned policy can be trained on a mixture of action-labeled demonstrations and unlabeled expert videos, thereby reducing the annotation burden.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253197/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy takes a prompt video (N frames, 224×224 RGB) depicting the desired task, a visual observation context of k=8 recent frames (224×224 RGB), and a self-state vector (joint angles, velocities, end-effector position) as input, and outputs a discretized robot action (7 bins per dimension). The prompt video is compressed to 32 tokens via a Perceiver Resampler, while each observation is encoded to 9 tokens. Training data is a mixture of action-labeled demonstrations (prompt video + observation-action pairs) and unlabeled expert videos (prompt video + observation sequence only), with the action loss applied only to the labeled subset.

## Method

JEPT decomposes video-conditioned behavior cloning into two synergistic subtasks within a single causal Transformer: visual transition prediction (predicting the joint embedding of the next observation, learnable from both labeled and unlabeled data) and inverse dynamics learning (predicting the action from the predicted embedding, learnable only from labeled data). The architecture comprises a Joint Embedding Encoder (frozen ViT + Perceiver-IO + bi-directional temporal Transformer) that compresses observations into predictive embeddings, a Prompt Video Encoder (ViT + Perceiver Resampler) that abstracts task-relevant attributes, and a Causal Predictor that alternately predicts observation embeddings and actions. The key insight is that visual transitions are task-specific and benefit from unlabeled video data, while inverse dynamics are shared across tasks due to the common transition function, so the inverse dynamics learned from demonstrations generalize to unlabeled tasks. Training uses an L2 loss on predicted joint embeddings (applied to all data) plus a cross-entropy action loss (applied only to demonstrations, weighted by hyperparameter c=8), with a separate optimization pass for the Joint Embedding Encoder and EMA target updates to prevent representation collapse.

## Results

On Meta-World (18 tasks), JEPT achieves 41.5% seen-task average success rate and 18.5% unseen-task average, outperforming all baselines on both metrics. On the critical unseen tasks (one-shot imitation), JEPT reaches 18.5% versus 1.5% for DT*, 12.0% for Vid2Robot-M, and 3.0% for BC+IDM. On Robosuite (15 tasks with cross-embodiment visual gaps), JEPT achieves 20.1% seen and 18.5% unseen average success rates, again leading all baselines, with a particularly large margin on unseen tasks (18.5% vs. 2.0% for DT* and 5.5% for Vid2Robot-M).

## Baselines & Comparisons

JEPT outperforms Vid2Robot-M (the strongest video-conditioned BC baseline) by 4.0 points on seen-task average (41.5% vs. 37.5%) and by 6.5 points on unseen tasks (18.5% vs. 12.0%) on Meta-World, with the gap widening to 13.0 points on Robosuite unseen tasks (18.5% vs. 5.5%). Against BC+IDM, which decouples inverse dynamics learning from visual imitation, JEPT leads by 12.5 points on Meta-World unseen tasks (18.5% vs. 3.0%), suggesting that jointly learning visual transitions and inverse dynamics on the mixture dataset yields more transferable knowledge than a separately trained IDM. JEPT underperforms DT* (a pure behavior-cloning ablation) on action-labeled demo tasks (51.3% vs. 61.3% on Meta-World), which the authors attribute to the cost of integrating more tasks into a single policy; however, DT* collapses on unseen tasks (1.5% vs. 18.5%), confirming that the joint embedding predictive mechanism is essential for generalization. On Robosuite with cross-embodiment visual gaps, JEPT maintains its lead over all baselines on both seen and unseen tasks, demonstrating robustness to prompt-video-to-dynamics discrepancies.

## Relation to Prior Work

JEPT extends the Decision Transformer / Generalized Decision Transformer sequence-modeling framework by integrating the Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) as the mechanism for learning predictive visual representations, creating a two-step prediction process (next-observation embedding → action) rather than the standard direct action prediction. It departs from prior video-conditioned policy learning methods (Vid2Robot, MUTEX, VIMA) that require all training data to be action-labeled for behavior cloning, and from BC+IDM approaches (Zheng et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2023) that decouple inverse dynamics learning from visual imitation, which the authors argue limits generalizability. The key conceptual departure is the joint, unified learning of visual transition prediction (available on both labeled and unlabeled data) and inverse dynamics (available only on labeled data) within a single causal Transformer, enabling the mixture dataset to be exploited without action annotation on the video-only portion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck in video-conditioned policy learning: expert rollouts are often available as videos without action labels, and annotating them is costly. The clean decomposition into visual transition prediction and inverse dynamics provides a principled way to leverage unlabeled video data, and the JEPA-based embedding space offers a mechanism for knowledge transfer between labeled and unlabeled regimes. However, all experiments are conducted in simulation (Meta-World, Robosuite) with relatively small task sets (15–18 tasks), and the visual gap between prompt videos and task dynamics is limited. Open questions include whether the approach scales to real-world manipulation, handles substantially larger visual discrepancies (e.g., human videos as prompts), and whether the visual prior injection strategy (currently a simple encoder swap) can be made more effective for diverse prior types beyond optical flow.
