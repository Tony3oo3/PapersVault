## Problem

Existing neural network scaling laws show that monolithic networks require an exponential number of training samples with the intrinsic dimensionality of the task input, yet no theoretical explanation exists for why modular architectures empirically generalize better on compositional tasks. Furthermore, prior work shows that architectural modularity alone is insufficient—modules trained with standard gradient descent often fail to align with the task's underlying modular structure, leaving a gap between the theoretical promise of modularity and its practical realization.

## Input / Output

For the sine wave regression task, the input is a high-dimensional vector x ∈ R^m (m up to 10) and the output is a scalar; the target is a sum of k functions of 1D linear projections of x, making the task inherently modular. For Compositional CIFAR-10, the input is a concatenation of k flattened 32×32 CIFAR-10 images (yielding a 3072k-dimensional vector with no spatial structure preserved), and the output is a 10k-dimensional vector of per-component one-hot class labels. The modular architecture constrains each module to process only a low-dimensional projection of the full input, creating a bottleneck that decouples per-module complexity from the ambient dimensionality.

## Method

The paper derives closed-form expressions for expected training and test loss of both monolithic and modular networks by modeling them as linear functions of their parameters (following the NTK/ridgeless regression framework of Hastie et al. 2022). The key theoretical result is that for modular networks where each module receives a b-dimensional projection (b ≪ m), the sample complexity to reach a fixed test error becomes independent of the task dimensionality m, in contrast to the exponential scaling of monolithic networks. To make this advantage practical, the authors propose a kernel-based learning rule that initializes module projection directions by minimizing the squared norm of the minimum-norm interpolating solution: given training data, they optimize each projection Ũ_i to minimize y(X)ᵀK⁻¹y(X), where K is a kernel matrix built from module-conditional kernels tailored to the task structure. This initialization aligns learned modules with the true task modules before standard gradient descent fine-tunes all parameters, and the approach extends to nonlinear module projections without modification.

## Results

On the nonlinear sine wave regression task (k=m=5), the proposed modular learning rule achieves a test loss of 0.393±0.099, outperforming the modular baseline (0.555±0.136) and the monolithic baseline (1.302±0.223). On the sine wave regression task, the method requires substantially fewer training samples to reach a target test error as input dimensionality increases (m=2 to 9), with the gap over both baselines widening at higher dimensions. On Compositional CIFAR-10, the method achieves higher per-component-image accuracy than both baselines across all numbers of component images (k=1 to 8), with the advantage persisting even at k=8 (24,576-dimensional inputs).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: a randomly initialized monolithic fully-connected network and a modular network with random initialization trained via standard gradient descent. On the sine wave regression task, the proposed kernel-based module initialization method requires significantly fewer training samples to reach a target test error than both baselines, and the advantage grows with input dimensionality. Notably, the monolithic baseline outperforms the randomly initialized modular baseline, confirming prior findings that architectural modularity alone is insufficient without proper module alignment. On Compositional CIFAR-10, the method achieves higher accuracy than both baselines at all tested numbers of component images, and also generalizes better to unseen class combinations (out-of-distribution) and to noisy training inputs. On the nonlinear variant of the sine wave task, the method reduces test loss by ~29% over the modular baseline and ~70% over the monolithic baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes the kernel-regression/NTK line of generalization theory (Hastie et al. 2022, Jacot et al. 2018) with the empirical literature on modular neural networks (Andreas et al. 2016, Shazeer et al. 2017) to produce, for the first time, explicit non-asymptotic generalization-error expressions for modular architectures. It directly addresses the gap identified by Csordas et al. (2021) and Mittal et al. (2022a) that architectural modularity alone is insufficient: the paper proposes a kernel-based initialization rule that aligns learned modules to the task's underlying modular structure. The analytical framework is a simplified version of Hastie et al. (2022), adapted to a block-diagonal covariance structure that captures modular parameter distributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that modular networks achieve dimension-independent sample complexity on modular tasks provides the first clean theoretical explanation for why modularity aids generalization, moving beyond the empirical observations in VQA, robotics, and RL. However, the theory relies on strong assumptions (linear-in-parameters model, sum-of-modules output, specific covariance parameterization), and empirically the dimension dependence is not fully eliminated due to optimization difficulties in high dimensions. Open questions include extending the analysis to routing mechanisms, self-attention architectures, and more general (non-additive) module compositions, as well as developing optimization strategies that close the theory-practice gap for modern modular motifs like MoE and attention.
