## Problem

Existing verification methods for LLM math reasoning suffer from two critical limitations: (1) low sampling efficiency, since verifiers only evaluate fully generated solutions and waste computational effort on partial solutions that are already clearly incorrect, and (2) the need for expensive step-wise process supervision (human annotations or tree search) to train effective Process Reward Models. This paper addresses both by reframing verification as a sequential importance sampling problem and applying Twisted Sequential Monte Carlo to guide sampling toward promising partial solutions during generation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a math problem statement as input and produces a final numerical answer selected via weighted majority voting over N=240 generated solutions. Solutions are decomposed into variable-length reasoning steps (separated by double newlines), and TSMC operates at this step level: at each step, the generator samples the next reasoning step, an incremental importance weight is computed from the estimated value function, and resampling (with stratified sampling) is performed over a mini-batch of M=40 sequences. A warm-up stage skips resampling for the first 50 tokens, and at most five resampling steps are allowed to limit latency.

## Method

The paper shows that weighted majority voting is equivalent to importance sampling with the generator as proposal and a correctness-weighted target, which suffers from exponentially growing variance over T steps. TSMC addresses this by introducing intermediate target distributions π_t(x_{1:t}) = p(x_{1:t})ψ_t(x_{1:t})/Z_t at each step, where the locally optimal twist function ψ_t is proportional to √V^p(x_{1:t}) — the square root of the value function estimating the probability of reaching a correct answer from the current partial solution. The incremental importance weight w_t = √(V^p(x_{1:t})/V^p(x_{1:t-1})) is used for resampling, and the product of all weights telescopes to φ(Ans(x_{1:T})), yielding an unbiased estimator of correctness. The value function is trained via Contrastive Twist Learning (CTL), which minimizes KL divergence between the target marginal and intermediate targets using rejection sampling and importance sampling to approximate the gradient, requiring only independently sampled solutions from the generator and no step-wise human annotations.

## Results

On GSM8K, TSMC+WMV achieves 80.4% (Llemma-7B) and 91.7% (DeepSeek-7B) problem-solving rate, outperforming the strongest baseline (PRM-SHEPHERD at 79.2% and 89.5% respectively). On MATH500, TSMC+WMV reaches 46.4% (Llemma-7B) and 60.8% (DeepSeek-7B), a 2.8–8.2 point improvement over the best baseline. Even TSMC+MV (without the weighted voting step) already surpasses vanilla majority voting by 5.6–7.0 points on GSM8K and 3.0–2.8 points on MATH500, confirming that the sequential resampling itself improves solution quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TSMC+WMV consistently outperforms all four baselines across both generators and both benchmarks. Against majority voting (Wang et al., 2023c), TSMC+WMV gains +7.9 points on GSM8K (Llemma-7B: 80.4 vs 72.5) and +8.3 points on MATH500 (DeepSeek-7B: 60.8 vs 52.8). Against the ORM baseline (Cobbe et al., 2021), the improvement is +1.7 points on GSM8K (Llemma-7B) and +5.8 points on MATH500 (DeepSeek-7B). Against the human-supervised PRM (Lightman et al., 2024), TSMC+WMV leads by +6.8 points on GSM8K (Llemma-7B: 80.4 vs 73.6) and +5.6 points on MATH500 (DeepSeek-7B: 60.8 vs 55.2). Against the automatically-supervised PRM (Wang et al., 2023a / MATH-SHEPHERD), TSMC+WMV wins by +1.2 points on GSM8K (Llemma-7B) and a substantial +8.2 points on MATH500 (DeepSeek-7B: 60.8 vs 52.6), where the SHEPHERD PRM actually underperforms even the ORM. Notably, TSMC achieves these gains without requiring step-wise human annotations or tree search for training, using only a value function learned via Contrastive Twist Learning on independently sampled data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies Twisted Sequential Monte Carlo (TSMC) from Zhao et al. (2024) to the verification problem in LLM multi-step reasoning, a setting where TSMC had not previously been explored. It reframes existing verification methods (ORM and PRM with weighted majority voting) as instances of importance sampling, providing a unified theoretical lens through which their sampling inefficiency and bias properties can be analyzed. The key methodological departure from prior verification work is that the optimal twist function is derived to be proportional to the square root of the value function, which can be learned via a simple neural regressor on independently sampled data—eliminating the need for step-wise human annotations or tree search that PRM training typically requires.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses two practical bottlenecks in LLM verification: the high sample cost of evaluating only fully generated solutions, and the difficulty of obtaining process supervision. The conceptual finding that PRM's empirical advantage over ORM holds only when both are unbiased estimators of the correctness indicator is a useful clarification for the field, suggesting that biased PRMs (e.g., using value estimates with product aggregation) offer no guaranteed benefit. Limitations include broken parallelism from variable-length steps adding inference latency, and the method's advantage shrinking on easy problems where the generator already produces mostly correct solutions. Open questions include whether blockwise resampling can restore parallelism and whether the approach extends to longer-horizon reasoning tasks.
