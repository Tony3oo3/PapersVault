## Problem

Language is conventionally modeled as discrete token sequences, yet the neural architectures that process it are inherently continuous function approximators. It remains unclear whether pretrained LLMs implicitly learn continuous representations of language or merely approximate discrete mappings. This paper asks whether LLMs treat time duration and embedding-space geometry as semantically meaningful, and finds that they do in ways that fundamentally diverge from human linguistic intuition.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251114/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard text sentences but reinterprets them as stepwise-constant functions over a continuous interval [0,T], where each token occupies a sub-interval of arbitrary (not necessarily unit) duration. Spatially, the input can be any point in the embedding space, including linear interpolations between token embeddings that do not correspond to valid vocabulary entries. The output is the standard next-token probability distribution, but the paper analyzes how this distribution shifts continuously as durations or interpolation parameters vary, revealing peaks at fractional token counts and semantic meanings for non-token embeddings.

## Method

The paper introduces the Continuous Causal Transformer (CCT), which replaces the discrete summation in causal attention with an integral over a continuous time domain, yielding a formulation where each token's contribution is weighted by its duration d_k. For stepwise-constant inputs, the integral reduces to a weighted sum (Equation 8) that is equivalent to standard attention when all durations equal 1, meaning pretrained LLM weights can be reused without any architectural modification—only the positional encodings must accept floating-point indices and the attention mask must encode non-uniform durations. The paper then empirically probes six state-of-the-art LLMs (Llama2-13B, Llama3-8B, Phi-3-Medium, Gemma-1-7B, Gemma-2-9B, Mistral-7B) by shrinking token durations and interpolating embeddings, demonstrating that models produce duration-dependent outputs (e.g., counting 1–4 fruits as duration varies) and assign coherent semantics to intermediate embeddings (e.g., the apple–banana interpolation is 'green' and 'a fruit'). The CCT is also shown to be translation-invariant but not scale-invariant, confirming that duration is an intrinsic semantic property of the architecture.

## Results

Across six state-of-the-art LLMs (Llama2-13B, Llama3-8B, Phi-3-Medium, Gemma-1-7B, Gemma-2-9B, Mistral-7B), the paper demonstrates that reducing token duration produces on average 190% more unique predictions than a discrete interpretation would predict (single-token counting, Table 2: global observed normalised peak frequency of 0.7404 vs. counterfactual 0.2600, a 2.90× ratio). At the sentence level, the effect is even stronger, with 205% more unique predictions (Table 4: global ratio of 3.28×). For space continuity, approximately 20% of interpolation records produce outputs outside the range spanned by the two endpoint embeddings (Table 10: global average m_max of 0.0339, with 20.79% of records exceeding the 0.05 threshold), indicating that LLMs assign non-trivial semantic meaning to intermediate embeddings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods in the traditional sense; instead, the 'baseline' is the discrete interpretation of LLMs, formalised as the counterfactual normalised peak frequency (≈0.26 for single-token counting, ≈0.22 for event counting). All six tested models significantly exceed this counterfactual: Llama 2-13B and Llama 3-8B show the strongest continuous behaviour (ratios of 3.60× and 3.57× respectively), while Mistral-7B and Gemma-1-7B show the weakest (1.85× and 1.95×), though still well above the discrete prediction. For the number-sums experiment (Table 7), the property P3 (shrunk labels dominate over the original label at some duration factor) holds for 79.05% of valid records globally, with Phi 3 and Mistral reaching ~87% and Llama 3 the lowest at 25.83%. For space continuity, Llama 3 and Gemma 1 show the smoothest interpolation behaviour (normalised max absolute derivative of 4.96 and 5.09), while Llama 2 and Phi 3 are sharper (9.01 and 9.28), suggesting model-dependent sensitivity to spatial continuity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the standard causal Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) by formally extending it to a Continuous Causal Transformer (CCT) that replaces the discrete attention sum with an integral over a stepwise-constant input function. It departs from prior continuous-time Transformer work (Neural ODEs, ODETransformer, Contiformer) and spatial-continuous extensions (diffusion language models, continuous space LMs) in a key way: rather than training models with continuous inputs, it shows that *discretely trained* LLMs already exhibit continuous behavior when probed with the CCT framework. The linear embedding hypothesis (Mikolov et al., 2013b; Park et al., 2023) is extended from input-space similarity to output-space semantics, and the work complements monosemanticity analyses (Anthropic, 2024) by showing continuous structure exists at the embedding level itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that duration acts as a semantic variable and that interpolated embeddings acquire independent meaning (e.g., the 'color' between apples and bananas is 'green') challenges the foundational assumption that LLMs process language as discrete token sequences, with implications for interpretability, robustness analysis, and potentially more efficient pretraining via parallel sub-token generation. Key open questions include: why smooth function approximation on discrete data yields these specific continuous behaviors, whether the stepwise-constant assumption is the right one (vs. piecewise-polynomial or other structures), and how to practically exploit continuous representations for inference or training. A limitation is that the duration manipulation is somewhat artificial and does not correspond to a natural linguistic phenomenon, making it unclear how broadly these findings transfer to real-world model behavior.
