## Problem

Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) have emerged as a promising unsupervised approach to disentangling LLM activations into interpretable features, but realistic ground-truth evaluations of whether SAEs actually recover the features relevant to a model's computation have been lacking. Existing metrics (reconstruction error, sparsity trade-offs, automated interpretability) are only conjecturally correlated with recovering the 'true' features. This paper proposes the first principled evaluation framework that directly compares SAEs to supervised feature dictionaries computed with knowledge of task-relevant concepts, using the IOI task in GPT-2 Small as a case study.

## Input / Output

The framework takes model activations at specific circuit cross-sections (attention head queries, keys, values, and outputs) for templated task prompts with known attribute labels (IO name, S name, position for IOI), and produces supervised feature dictionaries as a benchmark skyline. SAEs are then evaluated along three axes: sufficiency/necessity of activation reconstructions (via logit difference), sparse control (edit accuracy vs. edit magnitude when swapping at most k SAE latents to approximate a counterfactual activation), and interpretability (F1 score between latent activation patterns and human-defined attribute subsets). The evaluation is deliberately agnostic to whether SAE latents correspond 1-to-1 with supervised features, accommodating feature splitting or alternative decompositions.

## Method

The paper first computes supervised sparse feature dictionaries by decomposing activations as a mean plus one vector per attribute value, using either conditional expectation (mean features) or multivariate least-squares regression (MSE features), and verifies these dictionaries are sufficient, necessary, and controllable for the task. For SAE evaluation, the sparse control task is framed as a combinatorial optimization: given source and target activations, find at most k SAE latents to remove and add that minimize the L2 distance to the counterfactual activation, solved greedily, with edit magnitude measured as the total reconstruction weight of changed features to prevent trivial overwrites. The paper tests vanilla, gated, and topK SAE architectures trained on both task-specific (IOI) and full-distribution (OpenWebText) data, finding that topK SAEs are competitive with supervised dictionaries on control while vanilla SAEs struggle, and that task-specific SAEs outperform full-distribution ones on sufficiency/necessity.

## Results

On the IOI task with GPT-2 Small, supervised feature dictionaries achieve near-perfect sufficiency scores (~1.0) and high necessity scores across all circuit cross-sections, establishing the performance skyline. For sparse control (k=2 latents), vanilla task SAEs are markedly worse than supervised dictionaries, while gated and especially topK SAEs are competitive, particularly in the interpretation-agnostic editing regime. On the greater-than task, exchanging as few as 2 SAE latents matches next-token predictions 90% of the time versus 38% for no intervention. Many SAE latents achieve F1 > 0.8 for interpretable attribute values (e.g., individual IO/S names, Pos), confirming that SAEs capture task-relevant features.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three SAE architectures against supervised feature dictionaries as the canonical benchmark. Vanilla SAEs (following Bricken et al., 2023) perform well on sufficiency/necessity of reconstructions but are markedly worse than supervised dictionaries on the sparse control evaluation, struggling to edit attributes with minimal collateral effects. Gated SAEs (Rajamanoharan et al., 2024) close much of this gap, becoming competitive with supervised dictionaries on control, especially in the interpretation-agnostic regime where the best latents are selected per-edit. TopK SAEs (Gao et al., 2024) perform best among the unsupervised methods, matching supervised dictionaries on edit accuracy at comparable edit magnitude (k=2, 3 active latents), making them the most promising unsupervised alternative. Full-distribution SAEs (trained on OpenWebText rather than IOI data) underperform task-specific SAEs across all three evaluation axes (sufficiency, necessity, and control), suggesting that task-relevant training data significantly improves SAE utility for a given capability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the SAE architecture and training methodology of Bricken et al. (2023), the IOI circuit analysis of Wang et al. (2022), and more recent SAE variants (gated SAEs from Rajamanoharan et al. 2024, topK SAEs from Gao et al. 2024). Its key conceptual departure is from prior SAE evaluation approaches that rely on indirect metrics (reconstruction error, sparsity trade-offs, automated interpretability, cosine similarity between SAEs) by proposing supervised feature dictionaries computed from task-relevant attributes as a canonical 'skyline' for direct comparison. This positions the work alongside but distinct from concurrent controlled-evaluation efforts (Karvonen et al. on board games, Huang et al. and Chaudhary & Geiger on factual attributes), as it targets linguistic tasks in pre-trained LLMs and explicitly allows SAE latents to achieve task outcomes through different decompositions than the supervised features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine gap: prior SAE evaluations used only indirect metrics 'conjecturally correlated' with recovering true features, making it hard to judge whether SAEs actually disentangle model computations in a useful way. The supervised-dictionary-as-skyline framework is generalizable (demonstrated on three tasks and two model scales) and could become a standard evaluation protocol as SAEs are deployed at scale. Key limitations include the per-task effort to identify relevant attributes, reliance on templatic tasks, and the imposed attribute set that shapes the control evaluation. Open questions include whether the framework extends to open-ended, non-templatic capabilities and whether the observed SAE training pathologies (feature occlusion, over-splitting) persist or resolve at larger scales.
