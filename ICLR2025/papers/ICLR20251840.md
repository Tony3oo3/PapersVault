## Problem

Ensemble models for adversarial robustness are undermined by the transferability of adversarial examples: even independently trained sub-models share similar non-robust features, allowing a single perturbation to fool multiple models simultaneously. Existing solutions either require all sub-models in GPU memory with inter-model communication (simultaneous training) or rely on simple random data transformations that fail to break transferability (individual training). The paper asks how to diversify non-robust features across sub-models in a communication-free, low-complexity manner.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 for Tiny-ImageNet-200) and outputs a class label via averaging the hard predictions of M sub-models. Unusually, each input image is first decomposed via 2-D discrete Fourier transform; high-amplitude frequency components (above threshold τ) are preserved as robust features while low-amplitude components are replaced with either random Gaussian noise or targeted-attack-derived perturbations before inverse Fourier transform returns the modified image to the time domain for training.

## Method

The core contribution is a Frequency Domain Transformation (FDT) framework built on a 'weakness allocation' strategy. A fine-grained analysis partitions non-robust feature extractors into k(k−1) sets indexed by attack direction (y→y_t), yielding two design hints: reduce the influence of direction-specific non-robust features, and only M/2+1 sub-models need protection per direction. FDT selects low-amplitude frequencies via an amplitude threshold, then applies either random-noise replacement (FDT-random) or a targeted-attack transformation (FDT-target) that uses PGD to generate substitute features for each of the k(k−1) attack directions. A round-robin allocation assigns each direction to at most ⌈M/2⌉−1 sub-models, guaranteeing that any ⌈M/2⌉ datasets have disjoint weakness sets. The hybrid variant (FDT-hybrid) uses two thresholds τ₁<τ₂, applying targeted-attack transformation to mid-range frequencies and random noise to the lowest frequencies, achieving the best robust accuracy while preserving clean accuracy without any inter-model communication.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with 3 ResNet-20 sub-models, FDT-hybrid achieves 20.01% robust accuracy under PGD (ε=0.02) and 19.42% under AutoAttack (ε=0.02), substantially outperforming the strongest baseline DVERGE (11.49% and 13.72% respectively) while maintaining 90.20% clean accuracy. On CIFAR-100, FDT-hybrid reaches 8.63% under PGD (ε=0.02) versus 4.25% for DVERGE. Scaling to 20 sub-models on CIFAR-10 further improves PGD robust accuracy to 29.71% with 91.86% clean accuracy. Training time per epoch for FDT-hybrid is 114s, comparable to DVERGE (134s) and far below TRS (350s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FDT-hybrid outperforms all four ensemble baselines (ADP, GAL, DVERGE, TRS) on robust accuracy across nearly every attack on both CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100. The most notable margin is under PGD (ε=0.02) on CIFAR-10, where FDT-hybrid (20.01%) surpasses DVERGE (11.49%) by 8.5 points and TRS (15.1%) by 4.9 points; GAL and ADP are essentially defeated (0.34% and 2.95%). Clean accuracy is slightly lower than ADP (90.20% vs. 91.84%) but competitive with DVERGE (91.37%) and TRS. Against the three adversarial-training baselines in Table 10 (Wang et al. 2023b, HAT, DART), FDT-hybrid trades robust accuracy for clean accuracy: it achieves 93.72% clean accuracy versus 84.90–86.15% for the others, but only 34.61% robust accuracy under AutoAttack (ε=8/255) compared to 49.08–55.71%. The paper positions this as a favorable trade-off when high clean accuracy (>90%) is required, and notes that FDT avoids the need for generated data or adversarial training loops.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the non-robust feature analysis of Ilyas et al. (2019) by introducing a k(k-1) attack-direction-specific partitioning of non-robust feature extractors, which underpins the 'weakness allocation' strategy. Unlike prior ensemble diversity methods (ADP, GAL, DVERGE, TRS) that either impose gradient penalties during simultaneous training or apply random spatial transformations, FDT operates in the frequency domain via Fourier transformation, selectively modifying only low-amplitude (non-robust) frequencies while preserving high-amplitude (robust/semantic) ones. The approach is positioned as an 'individual training' method requiring no inter-model communication, but replaces the ineffective random cropping/rescaling of prior individual-training approaches with a principled, attack-guided frequency substitution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method offers a practical and computationally efficient path to ensemble robustness: sub-models are trained independently (no GPU memory for simultaneous storage, no communication overhead), and the frequency-domain transformation is cheap relative to adversarial training. The tunable threshold τ provides a clean trade-off knob between clean and robust accuracy. However, robust accuracy remains modest in absolute terms (~20% under PGD ε=0.02 on CIFAR-10 with 3 ResNet-20 sub-models), and the data preparation step requires running targeted PGD attacks via a surrogate VGG11 network, adding a non-trivial preprocessing cost. Open questions include whether the frequency-domain abstraction generalizes to non-image modalities, whether the weakness allocation strategy scales to very large ensembles or high-dimensional classification (k >> 10), and whether the approach can be combined with adversarial training for further gains.
