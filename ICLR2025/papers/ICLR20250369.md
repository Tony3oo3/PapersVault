## Problem

In dynamic multi-product pricing, buyers filter out (censor) products priced above their valuation before making a purchase choice, but the seller never observes which products were censored—only the final purchase. Prior dynamic pricing work under MNL either assumed a fixed assortment or could not handle this hidden censorship, making it impossible to simultaneously learn product valuations and buyer preferences while optimizing both prices and assortment selection.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250369/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each time step, the agent observes known feature vectors x_{i,t} (valuation) and w_{i,t} (price sensitivity) in R^d for each of N products, and receives only binary purchase feedback indicating which single product was bought (or the outside option). The agent must output an assortment S_t of at most K products along with a non-negative price p_{i,t} for each offered product. Critically, the feedback is censored: products with p_{i,t} > v_{i,t} are silently removed from the buyer's consideration set, so the agent cannot distinguish a non-purchase due to high price from one due to low preference.

## Method

The paper introduces a Censored Multinomial Logit (C-MNL) choice model where a threshold-based activation function 1(p_{i,t} ≤ v_{i,t}) gates each product's utility, capturing the buyer's price-valuation filtering. To handle the resulting censored feedback, the key insight is a Lower Confidence Bound (LCB) pricing strategy: prices are set at the lower confidence bound of estimated valuations (p_{i,t} = v̲_{i,t}^+), ensuring products remain below the buyer's threshold and thus stay 'active' in the choice model, which enables valid likelihood-based estimation of θ*. This LCB pricing is combined with either UCB or Thompson Sampling for assortment selection, where the UCB/TS index for utility includes an additional exploration term accounting for uncertainty in the LCB price itself. Parameter estimation uses online mirror descent with a periodically updated LCB estimator (triggered when det(H_t) grows by a factor C), yielding regret bounds of Õ(d^{3/2}√(T/κ)) for UCB and Õ(d²√(T/κ)) for TS, where κ captures the MNL non-linearity.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative contributions are theoretical regret bounds: UCBA-LCBP (Algorithm 1) achieves $\widetilde{O}(d^{3/2}\sqrt{T/\kappa} + d^3/\kappa)$ and TSA-LCBP (Algorithm 2) achieves $\widetilde{O}(d^2\sqrt{T/\kappa} + d^4/\kappa)$. In the worst case where $1/\kappa = O(K^2)$, the UCB algorithm's leading term reduces to $\widetilde{O}(K\sqrt{T})$, improving upon prior MNL pricing work that yields $\widetilde{O}(K^2\sqrt{T})$. In synthetic experiments (K=5, d=4, varying N), both proposed algorithms achieve sublinear regret while all three baselines fail to do so in the C-MNL setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three baselines: DASP-MNL (Erginbas et al., 2023), ONM (Goyal & Perivier, 2021), and Explore-then-Commit (ETC, Lattimore & Szepesvári, 2020). All three baselines are designed for the standard MNL model without censored feedback and do not work properly in the C-MNL setting, as shown in Figure 2 where their regret curves grow linearly or near-linearly rather than sublinearly. The proposed UCBA-LCBP and TSA-LCBP algorithms both achieve clearly sublinear regret, outperforming all baselines. The key distinction is that the baselines rely on revenue-maximizing price decisions that fail under censored feedback (where the agent cannot distinguish between a non-purchase due to stochastic preference vs. price censorship), whereas the LCB pricing strategy in the proposed methods avoids this issue by setting lower prices to ensure exploration. The paper notes that no algorithm can be directly applied to the novel C-MNL setting, making the comparison somewhat unfair to the baselines, but it demonstrates the fundamental incompatibility of prior MNL pricing approaches with censored preference feedback.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the MNL bandit and dynamic pricing literature (Agrawal et al. 2017; Goyal & Perivier 2021; Erginbas et al. 2023) by introducing a censored MNL (C-MNL) choice model where buyers filter out overpriced items before making a purchase decision, and the seller only observes the final purchase—not which items were censored. The estimation machinery draws on recent online mirror descent approaches for MNL bandits (Zhang & Sugiyama 2024; Lee & Oh 2024), while the key algorithmic departure is a novel LCB pricing strategy that deliberately sets prices below estimated valuations to avoid censorship and enable exploration. Prior work on dynamic pricing under MNL assumed revenue-maximizing prices or fixed assortments, neither of which is viable under the censored feedback structure introduced here.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The censored feedback model captures a realistic and previously unaddressed limitation: in practice, sellers cannot distinguish between a buyer who stochastically preferred another product and one who silently filtered out an overpriced item. The paper shows that standard revenue-maximizing pricing strategies fail under this model and that a pessimistic (LCB) pricing approach is necessary, yielding regret bounds with √(1/κ) rather than 1/κ in the leading term compared to prior MNL pricing work. Open questions include whether the d^{3/2} dimensionality dependence can be tightened, whether the framework extends to more complex demand models (e.g., nested logit), and whether the synthetic-data validation translates to real-world e-commerce settings. The robustness extension to noisy activation thresholds (Section 6) is a promising but underdeveloped direction.
