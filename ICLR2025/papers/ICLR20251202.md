## Problem

In MoE-based Large Vision-Language Models, the router dispatches tokens to experts without considering whether those tokens generate conflicting parameter optimization directions within the same expert. This gradient conflict causes tokens to interfere with each other's learning, leading to sub-optimal expert specialization. Existing routing strategies rely on sample-level embeddings or task labels, which cannot capture token-level interference (e.g., between visual and text tokens within a single sample).

## Input / Output

The model takes an RGB image (processed by a frozen CLIP or SigLIP visual encoder into a sequence of visual tokens) and a text instruction as input, and generates a text response autoregressively. The MoE layer replaces the FFN in the LLM backbone (StableLM-1.6B or Phi-2-2.7B), where a linear router predicts per-token routing scores and the Top-2 of 4 experts are activated. During training, the method additionally requires a backward pass to extract token-level gradients on expert bias parameters, adding roughly 20% training time overhead but no inference cost.

## Method

STGC operates in two steps during instruction tuning. First, it performs a backward pass (freezing all parameters except expert biases) to compute token-level gradients on each expert, then identifies 'conflicting tokens' whose gradient has cosine similarity below a threshold τ (default 0) with the average gradient of all tokens in that expert. Second, it adds a Conflict Elimination Loss (CEL) that inverts the router logits for conflicting tokens and applies a cross-entropy objective to push their routing probability away from the current expert, effectively reassigning them to other experts. The total loss combines the standard MoE loss (main generative loss plus load-balancing auxiliary loss) with the CEL weighted by β. The method is designed as a plug-in that can be attached to any existing MoE-based LVLM without architectural changes, and a brief theoretical analysis grounded in PCGrad shows that increasing intra-expert gradient cosine similarity ensures strict loss decrease under convexity.

## Results

With only 3.6B activated parameters (Phi-2 backbone + STGC), the method achieves 80.0% on VQA^v2, 63.0% on GQA, 48.6% on VizWiz, 70.9% on ScienceQA-IMG, 58.8% on TextVQA, 86.5% on POPE, 1481.7 on MME, 71.0% on MMBench, and 40.7% on MM-Vet, yielding an average of 61.0 across benchmarks. This outperforms the MoE-LLaVA baseline (same 3.6B activated params) by +6.1 points on average (61.0 vs. 54.9) and surpasses the dense LLaVA-1.5 7B model (6.7B activated) on MMBench (+7.6) and MM-Vet (+10.2) while activating roughly half the parameters. The method also significantly outperforms DYNMOE-LLaVA (3.4B activated) by +7.3 points on average.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the primary baseline MoE-LLaVA (same Phi-2-2.7B backbone, 3.6B activated params, same visual encoder), STGC improves the average score from 54.9 to 61.0 (+6.1), with the largest gains on VizWiz (+4.9), MM-Vet (+4.8), MMBench (+3.0), and MME (+50.4). Compared to DYNMOE-LLaVA (3.4B activated), STGC wins on every reported benchmark, achieving +2.1 on VQA^v2, +1.4 on GQA, +3.5 on VizWiz, and +7.1 on MM-Vet. Against the dense LLaVA-1.5 7B model (6.7B activated), STGC with only 3.6B activated parameters surpasses it on MMBench (71.0 vs. 63.4) and MM-Vet (40.7 vs. 30.5) while remaining competitive on VQA^v2 (80.0 vs. 78.5) and GQA (63.0 vs. 62.0), though it trails on VizWiz (48.6 vs. 50.0) and MME (1481.7 vs. 1510.7). Versus the 13B dense LLaVA-1.5, STGC (3.6B) matches VQA^v2 (80.0 vs. 80.0) and wins on MMBench (71.0 vs. 67.7) and MM-Vet (40.7 vs. 35.4), but loses on VizWiz (48.6 vs. 53.6) and MME (1481.7 vs. 1531.3). The paper also shows STGC as a plug-in consistently improves MoE-LLaVA across different configurations (4Top1/4Top2, StableLM/Phi-2, CLIP/SigLIP), confirming its generality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on MoE-LLaVA (Lin et al., 2024), which introduced MoE into LVLMs with a three-stage training scheme, and departs from the broader line of MoE routing work that relies on sample-level cues (instruction embeddings in MoCLE, token-average features in LoRA-MoE, task labels in MoLA) to reduce data interference. Conceptually, it draws on the gradient-conflict literature (PCGrad, Yu et al., 2020) but repurposes the idea: rather than projecting or modifying conflicting gradients, STGC uses token-level gradients as a diagnostic signal to reassign tokens to different experts via a conflict elimination loss on the router. This shifts the granularity of interference modeling from the sample level to the token level, addressing a gap the authors identify where visual and text tokens within the same sample can still conflict even when sample-level routing is consistent.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-in design and consistent gains across multiple MoE configurations (4Top1/4Top2), LLM backbones (StableLM-1.6B, Phi-2-2.7B), and visual encoders (CLIP, SigLIP) make STGC a practical drop-in for existing MoE-based LVLM pipelines. The scalability finding—that STGC yields larger gains (1.1% vs 0.6%) with more diverse training data—supports the core hypothesis that gradient interference grows with data heterogeneity, suggesting the method becomes more valuable as LVLM training data expands. A key open question, acknowledged by the authors, is that the method can only identify conflicts relative to the current expert and cannot determine the optimal expert for a token, leaving room for more principled routing from an optimization perspective. The ~20% additional training time from gradient computation is a non-trivial practical cost that may limit adoption in compute-constrained settings.
