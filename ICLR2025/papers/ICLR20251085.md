## Problem

Contextual bandit algorithms like LinUCB and Neural-LinUCB achieve sub-linear regret in single-domain settings but fail catastrophically when the target domain differs from the source domain. The core challenge is that in bandit settings, feedback (rewards) can only be collected from a low-cost source domain, while the high-cost target domain provides only contexts without rewards, and ground-truth labels remain unknown for incorrect actions—making standard offline domain adaptation methods inapplicable. This paper introduces the first general domain adaptation method for contextual bandits with a provable sub-linear target regret bound.

## Input / Output

The method consumes source-domain images (with associated rewards) and target-domain images (without rewards) at each round; images are resized to 28×28×3 (DIGIT) or 224×224×3 (VisDA17, S2RDA49). At each round, the model selects one action from K candidates in the target domain using a LinUCB-style UCB rule over encoded representations, and only the source-domain reward for the selected action is observed. The final output is a trained shared encoder φ, bandit parameters (θ, A, b), and a zero-shot target-domain policy that maps target contexts to arm choices without ever receiving target-domain feedback.

## Method

DABand couples a Neural-LinUCB backbone (deep encoder φ + linear bandit head θ) with an adversarial discriminator g in a minimax training loop: min_φ max_g L^DABand. The loss combines four differentiable terms derived from the target regret bound (Theorem 3.1): an MSE source-regret loss, an L1 regression-error loss, a predicted-reward regularization term, and a cross-entropy adversarial divergence loss that aligns source and target representations in the latent space. Bandit parameters (θ, A, b) are updated every round via the closed-form LinUCB update, while the encoder and discriminator are updated every H rounds via Adam backpropagation. The key theoretical insight is that naively minimizing source regret plus data divergence is sub-optimal; the two additional terms (regression error and predicted reward) act as necessary regularizers that prevent overestimation of rewards and ensure accurate source-reward prediction, as confirmed by ablation studies showing significant performance drops when either is removed.

## Results

In the zero-shot target regret setting (where only source-domain feedback is available), DABand achieves 50.23% average accuracy on DIGIT (MNIST→MNIST-M), 46.44% on VisDA17, and 39.23% on S2RDA49. These correspond to improvements of +14.10, +36.43, and +28.15 percentage points over the strongest baseline (NLinUCB) on each dataset, respectively. LinUCB and LinUCB-P either perform poorly or fail entirely (out-of-memory) on the high-dimensional VisDA17 and S2RDA49 datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DABand outperforms all baselines across all three datasets in the zero-shot target regret setting. On DIGIT, DABand (50.23%) beats NLinUCB (36.13%) by 14.10 points, LinUCB-P (29.92%) by 20.31 points, and LinUCB (24.49%) by 25.74 points. On VisDA17, the gap is most dramatic: DABand (46.44%) surpasses NLinUCB (10.01%) by 36.43 points, while LinUCB-P (11.45%) and NLinUCB-P (9.32%) are all near chance level. On S2RDA49, DABand (39.23%) leads NLinUCB (11.08%) by 28.15 points. NLinUCB-P collapses to 0.00% on DIGIT, suggesting PCA preprocessing is particularly harmful for the neural bandit in this cross-domain setting. LinUCB is inapplicable to VisDA17 and S2RDA49 due to out-of-memory issues with high-dimensional image features, underscoring the necessity of the neural encoder in DABand.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three established lines of work: (1) contextual bandits with linear reward models (LinUCB) and their neural extensions (Neural-LinUCB), (2) adversarial domain adaptation (Ganin et al., 2016) for aligning source and target distributions in latent space, and (3) domain adaptation theory (Ben-David et al., 2010) providing HΔH divergence bounds. The key departure is that prior domain adaptation methods assume offline settings with full label observability, while prior bandit methods assume a single domain; this paper is the first to formalize and solve the cross-domain bandit problem where feedback is only available in the source domain. The theoretical contribution extends the standard DA generalization bound by identifying two additional terms (regression error and predicted reward) that are specific to the bandit setting and necessary for a tight regret bound.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work opens a genuinely new research direction at the intersection of bandits and domain adaptation, with practical motivation in high-cost decision-making (drug trials, simulation-to-reality). The sub-linear target regret guarantee is the first theoretical result for this setting, providing a principled foundation for future work. Key limitations include: the method's performance heavily depends on the quality of source-target alignment (making it unclear how it handles fundamentally misaligned domains), the zero-shot target regret evaluation is somewhat artificial compared to realistic deployment, and only three image classification benchmarks are tested. Open questions include whether the adversarial alignment mechanism is the best approach for bandit-specific constraints (partial feedback, exploration), and whether improved uncertainty quantification (e.g., Bayesian deep learning) could further reduce target regret.
