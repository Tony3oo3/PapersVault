## Problem

Human-in-the-loop RL methods rely on human participants to monitor agent behavior and provide corrective demonstrations, but real humans exhibit suboptimal monitoring (fatigue, inattention, latency) and suboptimal demonstrations (unfamiliarity, limited control over high-dimensional action spaces). Existing agent-gated alternatives use heuristics like uncertainty thresholds or imitation of human intervention decisions, which fail to detect dangerous regions or trigger unnecessary interventions. The paper asks how to achieve safe, reward-free RL training when both human monitoring and human demonstrations are unreliable.

## Input / Output

The gating agent consumes environment states and produces a binary intervention decision (intervene or continue agent control). The learning agent consumes environment states and produces continuous control actions. Human input is structured as three distinct signals: a binary evaluative judgment on whether the gating agent's intervention request was appropriate, a preference comparison between the current and previous human intervention trajectory (T steps, typically 4), and the human demonstration trajectory itself. Crucially, the learning agent never directly consumes human demonstrations for its training signal—only the gating agent's binary decisions are used to construct proxy rewards.

## Method

AGSA employs a two-agent architecture where a gating agent (binary action space) decides when to switch control to a human, and a learning agent (continuous action space) interacts with the environment. The gating agent is trained with a composite reward combining human evaluative feedback I(s_t) on intervention quality and a preference reward r_ψ learned via the Bradley-Terry model from pairwise comparisons of consecutive human intervention trajectories, then optimized with standard value-based RL (SAC/TD3) to estimate long-term gating value Q_g. The learning agent is trained with a sparse proxy reward r_π that assigns -1 to state-action pairs immediately preceding a gating-triggered intervention and 0 otherwise, deliberately excluding human-generated samples from its training to avoid contamination by suboptimal demonstrations. This design shifts the burden from low-level human monitoring/demonstration to high-level human feedback (relative trajectory comparisons and binary judgments), which is cognitively easier for humans, and provides theoretical performance lower bounds for both the mixed behavior policy and the learning policy under bounded reward-model error.

## Results

On MuJoCo locomotion tasks (Hopper, Walker2d, HalfCheetah) with neural policies at varying skill levels, AGSA achieves average normalized D4RL scores of 94.14, 117.55, and 87.10 respectively, outperforming the strongest baseline RLIF (92.16, 83.43, 16.16) and all agent-gated methods by large margins. In the MetaDrive autonomous driving experiment with real human participants, AGSA attains the highest episodic return (263.56±8.22) and success rate (0.40±0.02), while requiring the fewest human involvement steps (7.9K vs. 20K for human-gated methods) and the lowest total safety cost (51±2.94). AGSA also converges faster, needing ~30K human intervention steps versus 50K for ablation variants.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AGSA outperforms all seven baselines on average across the three MuJoCo environments. Against the closest competitor RLIF (Luo et al., 2024), AGSA leads by 2.0 points on Hopper (94.14 vs. 92.16), 34.1 points on Walker2d (117.55 vs. 83.43), and 70.9 points on HalfCheetah (87.10 vs. 16.16), with RLIF's performance collapsing when its value-gated intervention signal becomes unreliable. Agent-gated baselines—EnsembleDAgger (Menda et al., 2019), Failure Prediction (Liu et al., 2023a), and BCVA (Gokmen et al., 2023)—all score below 50 on average across environments, with BCVA even producing negative scores on Walker2d (-8.60) due to high variance in trajectory-terminating signals. DAgger (Ross et al., 2011) and GAIL (Ho & Ermon, 2016) serve as imitation-learning references; DAgger averages 34–43 across MuJoCo tasks, while GAIL achieves only 81.51 episodic return and 0% success rate in MetaDrive. In the real-human MetaDrive experiment, AGSA surpasses PVP (Peng et al., 2023) by 90.5 episodic return points (263.56 vs. 174.71) and more than doubles its success rate (0.40 vs. 0.17), while also reducing human involvement steps from 20K to 7.9K and lowering total safety cost from 64 to 51.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the agent-gated shared autonomy line of work (EnsembleDAgger, Failure Detection, BCVA, TS2C, AdapMen) by replacing heuristic or imitation-based gating criteria with a gating agent trained from human evaluative and preference feedback, drawing on the RLHF/Bradley-Terry paradigm. It departs from human-gated methods (HG-DAgger, RLIF, PVP) that require constant human monitoring, and from imitation-learning-based approaches (DAgger, GAIL) that directly train on potentially suboptimal human demonstrations. The key conceptual shift is from low-level human interactions (real-time monitoring, joint-level control) to high-level human feedback (binary evaluations of intervention quality, pairwise trajectory preferences), which the authors argue is more robust to human imperfections like fatigue and latency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AGSA addresses a practical bottleneck in human-in-the-loop RL: the assumption that human monitors are reliable and human demonstrators are near-optimal. By decoupling the gating decision from human real-time attention and insulating the learning agent from imperfect demonstrations via proxy rewards, the framework simultaneously improves training safety, policy performance, and user workload (7.9K vs 20K+ human involvement steps). Open questions include scaling to multiple human participants and learning agents (M >> N), generalizing to high-dimensional action spaces where humans cannot directly control all joints, and whether the preference-based gating reward generalizes beyond the relatively simple continuous control tasks tested here.
