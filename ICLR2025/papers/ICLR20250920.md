## Problem

Existing ego-trajectory prediction methods rely primarily on external scene factors (other vehicles, road geometry) while neglecting the driver's own attention and intent, which are critical for resolving ambiguous driving situations like turn selection at intersections. This gap is compounded by data scarcity: only one public dataset (DR(eye)VE, 6 hours, mostly rural) provides synchronized ego-driver positions and field-of-view data, limiting the development of gaze-informed prediction models.

## Input / Output

The model consumes 8 seconds of synchronized inputs: relative GPS displacements (EPSG:3857, 2D), 4K/30Hz video from two external GoPro cameras, a first-person head-mounted video feed, and 200Hz gaze coordinates (x,y in image space, downsampled to 5fps via median pooling). It outputs a 6-second future ego-trajectory at 5fps (30 steps) plus auxiliary future visual feature vectors used as regularization targets during training. The task follows the nuScenes convention of 8s input → 6s prediction with 2-second sliding-window strides.

## Method

RouteFormer fuses three modalities through a hierarchical transformer pipeline: a shared SwinV2 backbone encodes both external scene frames and the driver's first-person video into a common feature space; gaze positions are refined via Informer-style self-attention and then cross-attended with the first-person video features to let the model 'pick' gaze-relevant scene regions; all modality features are concatenated with learned modality embeddings and fused via self-attention before being merged with motion features and fed into an Informer time-series forecaster. A key training innovation is the future-discounted loss (γ=0.97), which exponentially down-weights later prediction steps to prevent overfitting on spurious long-horizon correlations, combined with auxiliary losses on future visual features whose weight is dynamically balanced relative to the trajectory loss. The paper also introduces the Path Complexity Index (PCI), a Fréchet-distance-based metric measuring deviation from a straight-line extrapolation of the input trajectory, enabling stratified evaluation of model performance across scenario difficulty.

## Results

On the GEM dataset, RouteFormer (video+gaze) achieves the best ADE of 5.99 m and ADE+20PCI of 7.70 m, outperforming all baselines. On DR(eye)VE, RouteFormer achieves 8.75 m ADE and 12.26 m ADE+20PCI, again the best among all methods. The advantage of incorporating driver field-of-view is most pronounced in high-complexity scenarios (ADE+20PCI), where RouteFormer improves over its video-only variant (RouteFormer-Base) by 0.44 m on GEM and 0.89 m on DR(eye)VE. On high-PCI samples (20+ PCI), the future-discounted loss and auxiliary losses further reduce validation ADE from 8.85 m to 8.27 m.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RouteFormer outperforms both SOTA baselines on both datasets across all metrics. Against GIMO (Zheng et al., 2022), RouteFormer reduces ADE from 7.61 m to 5.99 m on GEM (−21%) and from 13.97 m to 8.75 m on DR(eye)VE (−37%), with the gap widening substantially on high-PCI samples (10.03 m → 7.70 m on GEM; 18.68 m → 12.26 m on DR(eye)VE). Against the Multimodal Transformer (Li et al., 2021), RouteFormer achieves a smaller but consistent improvement: 5.99 m vs. 6.07 m ADE on GEM and 8.75 m vs. 10.67 m on DR(eye)VE. Notably, RouteFormer-Base (without gaze) is nearly on par with the Multimodal Transformer on GEM (6.13 m vs. 6.07 m), indicating that the gaze modality is the key differentiator. On DR(eye)VE, even RouteFormer-Base (9.95 m) already beats the Multimodal Transformer (10.67 m), suggesting the architecture itself provides a modest advantage independent of the gaze input.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends ego-trajectory prediction—a task previously addressed with VAEs (Kim et al., 2021), static-environment observations (Baumann et al., 2018), and action priors (Malla et al., 2020)—by introducing driver field-of-view (first-person video plus gaze fixations) as a first-class input modality. The gaze-informed prediction idea is adapted from GIMO (Zheng et al., 2022), which demonstrated gaze as a high-quality indicator for human motion, and transplanted here to the driving domain. The temporal forecasting backbone draws directly from the time-series literature (Informer, Transformer, PatchTST), while the visual encoding leverages SwinV2, making the architecture a multimodal fusion of well-established components rather than a fundamentally new design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The GEM dataset fills a genuine gap as the only public benchmark combining noise-corrected GPS ego-positions, urban scene video, and high-frequency gaze data, enabling research on driver-intent-aware trajectory prediction that was previously infeasible at scale. The Path Complexity Index offers a practical, label-free way to stratify evaluation by scenario difficulty, addressing the long-tail problem in driving. However, the dataset is small (5 hours, 10 subjects), the method relies on GPS rather than HD maps or LIDAR, and failure cases at complex intersections and occluded turns suggest that gaze alone cannot resolve all ambiguity—incorporating turn signals, LIDAR, or learned driver profiles remains an open direction.
