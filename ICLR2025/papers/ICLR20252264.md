## Problem

Existing reverse distillation methods for unsupervised anomaly detection suffer from two limitations: the teacher encoder only encounters in-distribution (normal) data during training, yielding insufficient discriminative representations for real anomalies, and the distillation loss treats all spatial scales equally despite anomalies varying substantially in size across medical images.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252264/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes 256×256 medical images (chest X-rays, brain MRI slices, or dermoscopic skin images) as input. During training, each normal image is paired with a synthesized anomalous version created by overlaying simplex noise (6 octaves, persistence 0.6, intensity λ=0.2) at a random location. At inference, the model produces a pixel-level anomaly map by aggregating scale-weighted cosine-similarity maps across encoder layers, and a scalar anomaly score computed as the mean of this map.

## Method

The architecture employs two weight-shared teacher encoder pathways: a 'clean' teacher processes normal images while a 'noisy' teacher processes simplex-noise-perturbed versions, both feeding into a bottleneck that produces a multi-scale joint representation. A student decoder reconstructs per-scale feature maps from this bottleneck. The key training loss is a contrastive reverse distillation objective that pushes the student's reconstructed features toward the clean teacher's features and away from the noisy teacher's features, formulated as a ratio of cosine dissimilarities (1−sim(u,v))/(1−sim(z,v)+ε) computed on flattened feature maps. A scale adaptation head applies global max pooling to the bottleneck output, then a linear layer with softmax produces input-specific weights α_k that softly reweight the contrastive loss at each of K scales, enabling the model to emphasize the most informative scale for a given input. The pre-trained WideResNet50 encoder (ImageNet) is frozen, and only the bottleneck, scale head, and student decoder are trained for 4,000 iterations with Adam (lr=1e-3, batch size 16).

## Results

On the RSNA chest X-ray dataset, SCRD4AD achieves 91.01% AUC, 84.05% F1, and 83.40% accuracy, outperforming the second-best method (RD++ at 88.00% AUC) by 3.01 points. On the Brain Tumor MRI dataset, it reaches 98.88% AUC, 96.52% F1, and 96.50% accuracy, a 4.38-point AUC improvement over RD++ (91.68%). On the ISIC dermoscopy dataset, it attains 83.10% AUC, 72.07% F1, and 75.60% accuracy, surpassing ReContrast (80.02% AUC) by 3.08 points. The method achieves state-of-the-art performance across all three datasets and all three evaluation metrics (AUC, F1, ACC).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SCRD4AD outperforms all 17 baselines across every dataset and metric. The closest competitors are the reverse distillation variants: RD++ (Tien et al., CVPR 2023) trails by 3.01 AUC points on RSNA (88.00 vs. 91.01), 7.20 on Brain Tumor (91.68 vs. 98.88), and 7.63 on ISIC (75.47 vs. 83.10); ReContrast (Guo et al., NeurIPS 2023) is second-best on ISIC at 80.02 AUC (3.08 behind) and competitive on RSNA at 87.53. The original RD4AD (Deng & Li, CVPR 2022) baseline lags by 6.72 AUC on RSNA, 8.36 on Brain Tumor, and 6.62 on ISIC. Among generative and memory-bank methods, UAE (Mao et al., MICCAI 2020) is the strongest at 94.50 AUC on Brain Tumor but still falls 4.38 points short; PatchCore (Roth et al., CVPR 2022) reaches 86.33 AUC on RSNA (4.68 behind) and 93.63 on Brain Tumor (5.25 behind). Normalizing-flow methods (CFLOW-AD, FastFlow) and self-supervised approaches (CutPaste, NSA) show substantially lower performance, with CFLOW-AD dropping to 36.35 AUC on Brain Tumor and CutPaste to 55.86 on RSNA. Recent efficient methods (EfficientAD, UCAD, SimpleNet) also trail significantly, confirming that the contrastive reverse distillation paradigm with scale adaptation provides a clear advantage over both reconstruction-based and flow-based alternatives.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the reverse distillation paradigm for anomaly detection introduced by RD4AD (Deng & Li, CVPR 2022), which uses a pre-trained teacher encoder and a student decoder trained only on normal data. The key departures are twofold: (1) a contrastive loss that pushes the student's reconstructed features away from a 'noisy' teacher's representations of synthesized anomalies, and (2) a lightweight scale adaptation head that learns input-specific weights across feature pyramid levels. The paper explicitly differentiates itself from RD++ (Tien et al., CVPR 2023), which also uses synthesized anomalies in a dual-pathway reverse distillation setup but applies multiple regularizations to restrict anomalous information flow rather than leveraging it contrastively.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method achieves strong SOTA results across three medically distinct modalities (chest X-ray, brain MRI, dermoscopy), with particularly large gains on Brain Tumor MRI (98.88% AUC), suggesting the scale-aware mechanism is well-suited to lesions with high size variability. However, the approach still relies on random spatial sampling for noise placement and lacks modality-specific anatomical priors, limiting its ability to localize anomalies in clinically meaningful regions. Open questions include whether learnable or adaptive noise generation (as hinted in the conclusion) can further close the gap between synthetic and real anomalies, and whether the scale adaptation mechanism generalizes to 3D volumetric data or multi-organ settings.
