## Problem

Scaling laws suggest that large parameter count drives language model performance, but recent work hints that depth matters disproportionately for reasoning. This paper makes the stronger claim that many reasoning problems require large depth but not necessarily many parameters, and asks whether looped (weight-sharing) transformers can exploit this to achieve reasoning performance comparable to much larger models while using a fraction of the parameters.

## Input / Output

For synthetic reasoning tasks, inputs are structured token sequences: n-ary addition problems (e.g., '315+120+045+824='), letter sequences for p-hop induction (alphabet size 4, length 256), and symbolic DAG-based math expressions for i-GSM (arithmetic modulo 7). For language modeling, inputs are standard text from the Pile dataset (250B tokens, sequence length 1280). Outputs are next-token distributions for LM, exact numeric answers for synthetic tasks, and k-shot evaluated answers for downstream QA and math benchmarks. A key structural detail is that the same k-layer block processes the input L times sequentially, so the effective depth is kL while only k layers of parameters exist.

## Method

The paper studies looped transformers, denoted (k⊗L), where a k-layer Transformer block is applied L times with shared weights, yielding kL effective depth at k-layer parameter cost. Empirically, on synthetic reasoning tasks (addition, p-hop, i-GSM) and 1B-parameter language modeling on the Pile, looped models nearly match or exceed iso-flop non-looped baselines on reasoning benchmarks despite having L× fewer parameters and worse perplexity, revealing an inductive bias toward reasoning over memorization. Theoretically, the paper proves that a 1-layer looped transformer with O(log n) loops solves group composition (generalizing modular addition), that looped models can simulate non-looped models with repeated layers, and that L loops can simulate L steps of chain-of-thought reasoning by generating multiple latent thoughts per iteration. The paper also introduces a cosine-similarity regularization between successive blocks of k layers that inherits the looped model's reasoning inductive bias without sacrificing perplexity, and shows that downstream accuracy scales as log(effective depth), mirroring CoT inference-time scaling.

## Results

On the 1B-parameter language modeling setup (24-layer baseline, Pile pretraining), the (12⊗2) looped model achieves 34.3% on Math Word Problems and 51.2% on Reasoning Primitives, matching or exceeding the 24-layer baseline (29.3% and 47.5%) despite having 50% fewer parameters and worse perplexity (7.90 vs 7.40). On synthetic reasoning tasks, a 1-layer model looped 12 times achieves 99.9% on n-ary addition (n=24) and 99.9% on p-hop induction (p=16), nearly matching the 12-layer non-looped baseline (100.0% and 99.9%) with only 1/12th the parameters. The % Gap metric reveals that looping covers 282% of the math word problem gap and 131% of the reasoning primitives gap between iso-param and iso-flop baselines, versus only 34% for perplexity and 37% for closed-book QA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three model families at matched FLOPs: (k⊗L) looped models, (k⊗1) iso-param non-looped baselines, and (kL⊗1) iso-flop non-looped baselines. On reasoning tasks, the looped model decisively wins: Loop(12⊗2) achieves 34.3% on Math Word Problems (vs 26.7% for iso-param and 29.3% for iso-flop) and 51.2% on Reasoning Primitives (vs 35.7% and 47.5%), exceeding the full 24-layer baseline on both despite having half the parameters. On Open Book QA, Loop(12⊗2) reaches 30.8% (vs 26.9% iso-param, 33.9% iso-flop), closing most of the gap. However, the looped model consistently loses on perplexity (7.90 vs 7.40) and Closed Book QA (9.3 vs 11.2), confirming a clear reasoning-vs-memorization tradeoff. On synthetic tasks, the advantage is even more dramatic: a 1-layer model looped 12 times (1x params) achieves 99.9% on addition and p-hop, matching the 12-layer baseline (12x params) while the 1-layer non-looped model scores only 0.1% and 48.9% respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the weight-sharing/looping mechanism from Universal Transformers (Dehghani et al., 2018) and ALBERT (Lan et al., 2020) — previously studied for parameter efficiency and perplexity — and reframes it as a tool specifically for reasoning. It extends the theoretical line of work on transformer depth requirements (Liu et al., 2022 on group composition; Sanford et al., 2024b on p-hop induction) by showing that a single-layer looped transformer achieves near-optimal depth with far fewer parameters than non-looped constructions. The paper also verifies and extends the conjecture from Saunshi et al. (2024) that stacking (MidAS) improves reasoning via its connection to looped models, and draws a novel theoretical bridge to chain-of-thought reasoning by showing looped models can simulate T steps of CoT with T loops.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that depth via looping disproportionately improves reasoning over memorization challenges the conventional scaling-law wisdom that parameter count is the primary driver of model capability, with direct implications for efficient model design. The cosine-similarity regularization offers a simple, drop-in technique to inherit the reasoning inductive bias without sacrificing perplexity, and the connection to CoT/inference-time scaling opens a promising direction for using looping as an explicit compute-allocation mechanism at inference. Key open questions include whether the reasoning/memorization dichotomy persists at larger scales (beyond 1B), whether it generalizes to multimodal and common-sense reasoning, and why the inductive bias arises mechanistically.
