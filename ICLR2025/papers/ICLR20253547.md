## Problem

Real-world graph datasets contain noise in both the graph structure (spurious and missing edges) and node features, which degrades downstream GNN performance. Existing preprocessing rewiring methods target geometric pathologies like oversquashing or oversmoothing but do not address this denoising problem, and no prior method jointly denoises both the graph and the features. The paper asks whether simple joint spectral denoising of both components can improve node classification.

## Input / Output

The method takes an undirected graph adjacency matrix A ∈ ℝ^{N×N} and a node feature matrix X ∈ ℝ^{N×F} as input, and produces a rewired (denoised) adjacency matrix Ã and denoised feature matrix X̃. The synthesized Ã is initially a dense weighted matrix; sparsity is enforced post-hoc via thresholding or top-k per-node selection. Unlike the compared baselines (DIGL, FoSR, BORF), JDR requires node features to operate and outputs both a modified graph and modified features, making it the first method to jointly denoise both components.

## Method

JDR enforces spectral resonance by maximizing the alignment (spectral norm of V_L^T U_L) between the leading L eigenvectors of A and the leading L left singular vectors of X, motivated by the contextual stochastic block model where both matrices are noisy views of the same labels. The algorithm iterates K times: it decomposes A and X, interpolates each leading eigenvector of A toward its most-aligned counterpart in X (and vice versa) using fixed rates η_A and η_X, then re-synthesizes both matrices from the updated vectors. A theoretical guarantee (Proposition 1) shows that under a spiked GOE noise model, a single interpolation step strictly improves the alignment of the leading eigenvector with the true label vector, leveraging the BBP transition. The approach handles both homophilic and heterophilic graphs, multiple classes, and scales to large graphs since only the top-L eigenvectors are needed (O(N) complexity for sparse inputs).

## Results

On homophilic datasets (sparse 2.5%/2.5%/95% split), GPRGNN+JDR achieves the best accuracy on 4 of 5 datasets: Cora 80.77%, CiteSeer 69.17%, PubMed 85.05%, and Photo 92.68%, while GCN+JDR leads on 4 of 5. On heterophilic datasets (dense 60%/20%/20% split), JDR wins on all 5 datasets for both GNNs, with GPRGNN+JDR reaching 71.00% on Chameleon, 60.62% on Squirrel, 41.89% on Actor, 93.85% on Texas, and 89.45% on Cornell. On large-scale heterophilic graphs (up to 168K nodes), GCN+JDR improves over the baseline by 2.2–1.9 points (e.g., 77.52% on Questions vs. 75.31% baseline). On synthetic cSBM data, JDR brings both GCN and GPRGNN close to the asymptotically optimal AMP-BP across all homophily levels φ.

## Baselines & Comparisons

JDR outperforms all three preprocessing rewiring baselines (DIGL, FoSR, BORF) on the majority of datasets for both GCN and GPRGNN downstream models. Against DIGL, JDR wins on 4/5 homophilic datasets (losing only Computers, where DIGL's diffusion kernel produces an evenly-connected graph that GPRGNN exploits) and on all 5 heterophilic datasets, where DIGL's homophily assumption causes it to actively degrade GCN performance (e.g., 58.04% vs. 67.65% on Chameleon). Against FoSR, JDR achieves consistent gains of 1–6 points across all datasets, with the largest margins on heterophilic graphs (e.g., +3.8 points on Squirrel for GCN). Against BORF, JDR wins on every dataset where BORF completes, and BORF suffers out-of-memory errors on Squirrel, Tolokers, and Questions due to its O(md_max³) complexity. Compared to the no-rewiring baselines (GCN, GPRGNN), JDR provides consistent improvements of 1–10 points depending on the dataset and homophily level. On synthetic cSBM data, JDR narrows the gap to the asymptotically optimal AMP-BP algorithm substantially, particularly in the weak-graph regime (φ≈0) where it nearly matches AMP-BP for GPRGNN.

## Relation to Prior Work

JDR carves new ground at the intersection of the cSBM theoretical framework (Deshpande et al., 2018; Duranthon and Zdeborova, 2023) and the graph rewiring literature. While existing preprocessing rewiring methods (DIGL, FoSR, BORF) target geometric pathologies like oversquashing and oversmoothing, JDR reframes rewiring as a denoising problem guided by spectral alignment between graph and feature eigenspaces. It is the first method to jointly denoise both the graph structure and node features, drawing on the cSBM's joint optimization problem but replacing the intractable non-convex program with a practical alternating interpolation algorithm. The paper explicitly distinguishes itself from graph structure learning (which handles adversarial perturbations) and end-to-end rewiring (which cannot output a reusable graph).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central empirical finding—that a denoising-driven rewiring consistently outperforms methods explicitly designed to fix geometric message-passing pathologies—suggests that noise (missing and spurious edges) is a more fundamental bottleneck for GNN performance than oversquashing or oversmoothing in most real-world settings. This reframing has practical implications: practitioners can improve GNNs by aligning graph and feature spectra rather than tuning curvature or spectral gap. Key limitations include the requirement for node features (unlike feature-agnostic baselines), restriction to node classification (not graph-level tasks), and the open question of whether geometric rewiring and denoising-based rewiring can be productively combined. Extending the cSBM-based theory to binary adjacency matrices and multi-class settings remains an open theoretical challenge.
