## Problem

Iterative antibody optimization in the lab is severely sample-inefficient: even when restricted to typical antibody sequences, the combinatorial mutation space is vast and only a handful of mutations are beneficial, causing experiments to fail within budget. While the human immune system performs this same optimization through clonal families (sets of related sequences evolving to bind a target strongly and stably), this abundant evolutionary data has not been leveraged as an informed prior for lab-based Bayesian optimization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251575/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a starting antibody variable domain sequence X₀ (110–130 amino acids) plus N previous experimental measurements (binding affinity as −log K_D and/or melting temperature) of related sequences. It outputs specific point mutations (up to 3 substitutions) applied to the top 4 previously measured sequences, rather than de novo sequences. To manage computational cost with large N, CloneBO conditions only on the 75 measurements whose sequences are most likely to co-occur in a clonal family with X₀, and normalizes Y values using the initial dataset's mean and standard deviation.

## Method

CloneBO builds an informed prior over fitness functions by training CloneLM, a 377M-parameter autoregressive language model (Mistral-7B architecture) on ~908K heavy-chain and ~34K light-chain clonal families from the OAS database, where each family is represented as a sequence of amino-acid sequences separated by special tokens. Rather than modeling an explicit latent 'clone' variable, CloneLM's predictive distribution p_CloneLM(X | X₀:M) serves as an implicit fitness function F(X) ≈ log p_CloneLM(X | X₀:M) via a martingale posterior approach: as M grows, this converges to the true fitness of the generating clone. To condition on lab measurements, CloneBO uses a twisted sequential Monte Carlo procedure that decomposes the likelihood into per-letter contributions and biases each generated letter toward mutations that improve measured fitness, correcting the approximation with D=4 SMC particles. The final optimization step uses Thompson sampling: fitness functions are drawn from the approximate posterior, and the sequence maximizing the sampled fitness (over up to 3 substitutions of the top 4 measured sequences) is proposed for the next lab round.

## Results

On the in silico fitness function oracle (logp(X|clone)), CloneBO achieves a mean best value of -7.29 across 10 replicates, substantially outperforming the next-best methods LaMBO-Ab and Sapiens (-24.51). For binding (normalized log₁₀K_D) and stability (melting temperature), CloneBO reaches 0.3713 and 72.92°C respectively, significantly outperforming the next-best method LaMBO-Ab (0.3381, 70.44°C) with Mann-Whitney p=0.018 and p=0.006. In wet-lab validation, CloneBO's strongest binder is surpassed by only 2/997 previously measured sequences, and its most stable sequence beats all prior measurements by a large margin; CloneBO sequences are also significantly more synthesizable than LaMBO-Ab (p<1e-5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CloneBO decisively outperforms all 10 baselines on all three in silico tasks. The weakest baselines—CbAS, Dyna-PPO, CMA-ES, and EvoBO—achieve identical or near-identical scores (e.g., -27.42 on fitness, ~0.257 on binding, 66.76°C on stability), indicating they fail to find beneficial mutations. The genetic-algorithm baselines (Genetic, AdaLead) and the naive Greedy method improve modestly but remain far behind CloneBO. The antibody-informed methods Sapiens (informed greedy) and LaMBO-Ab (latent-space BO pretrained on 100k antibody sequences) are the strongest baselines, reaching -24.51 on fitness and 70.44°C on stability, yet CloneBO still leads by a wide margin (e.g., 17.2 points on fitness, 2.5°C on stability). In the in vitro experiment, CloneBO designs significantly stronger binders than LaMBO-Ab (Mann-Whitney p=0.021) and more synthesizable sequences (p<1e-5). On the SARS CoV binding task (Fig. 12), CloneBO also outperforms the structure-based design method DiffAb, which requires 3D structural information that CloneBO does not.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Bayesian optimization for protein sequence design (LaMBO, CbAS, EvoBO) by replacing generic antibody-typicality priors (Sapiens, IgLM) with a prior learned from the immune system's own evolutionary process: clonal families. Technically, it combines a transformer LLM trained on clonal families (analogous to PoET's sequence-of-sequences architecture) with martingale posterior inference (Fong et al., 2024) to sample implicit fitness functions, and twisted sequential Monte Carlo (Whiteley & Lee, 2014) to condition generation on experimental measurements. The key conceptual departure from prior work is that instead of asking 'what does a typical antibody look like?', CloneBO asks 'what mutations does the immune system actually make to improve binding?', yielding a much more targeted prior over the mutation space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Antibody optimization is a major bottleneck in therapeutic development, and the ability to leverage abundant clonal family data (908K heavy chain families from OAS) rather than scarce lab optimization data is a significant practical advance. The in vitro validation—where CloneBO's best binder is outperformed by only 2/997 previously measured sequences and its most stable binder exceeds all prior measurements by a large margin—provides encouraging evidence for real-world utility, though the wet-lab experiment covers only one optimization round with ~20 sequences per method. Key open questions include scaling to multi-objective optimization (binding + stability simultaneously), handling non-linear or heteroskedastic relationships between fitness and lab measurements, and reducing the computational cost that currently grows with the number of measurements N.
