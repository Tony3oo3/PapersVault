## Problem

Current SSL speech representations produce dense, unstructured frame-level tokens at 25–75 Hz, yielding long sequences that are costly to process under quadratic attention and lack the segmented, categorical structure that human speech perception naturally exhibits. No prior SSL framework directly imposes clean syllabic organization on learned features, making efficient syllable-level tokenization and spoken language modeling intractable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250796/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes raw speech waveforms (5-second crops during training) and outputs 50 Hz frame-wise embeddings that are flat and consistent within each syllable span and sharply distinct across syllable boundaries. A linear-time greedy segmentation algorithm extracts syllable boundaries from these embeddings; segment-averaged features form continuous syllabic tokens at ~4.27 tokens/sec, which are then discretized via k-means (5K/10K/20K clusters). A conditional flow-matching model maps these tokens to 14-dimensional articulatory features at 50 Hz, which a SPARC HiFi-GAN vocoder converts to speaker-conditioned speech audio.

## Method

Sylber uses a two-stage self-segmentation distillation framework built on a 9-layer HuBERT-style transformer. In stage 1, the student is initialized from SDHuBERT and trained to regress each frame's embedding toward the segment-averaged embedding of the corresponding syllable (extracted once from SDHuBERT's unsupervised segmentation), with non-speech frames regressed to zero via MSE loss. In stage 2, the teacher is updated to the stage-1 student, and target segments are generated online using the model's own greedy segmentation algorithm, creating a self-improving loop. The key mechanism is that direct regression toward segment-averaged targets forces the network to produce temporally constant features within syllables and sharp transitions at boundaries, enabling an O(n) greedy agglomeration algorithm (with a local boundary-refinement pass) that replaces the O(n²) MinCut used by prior methods. A denoising objective mixing 20% of the batch with environmental noise or overlapping speech improves robustness without altering the learned syllabic structure.

## Results

Sylber achieves the best syllable detection F1 (72.2) and R-value (75.9) among all compared methods, with a linear-time O(n) segmentation algorithm versus O(n²) for baselines. Syllabic tokens operate at 4.27 Tok/s (6–7× fewer than HuBERT's 23–33 Tok/s) while reconstructing fully intelligible speech (WER 7.95% at 20K vocab). In spoken language modeling, Sylber-uLM (125M params, 66K hrs) achieves sWUGGY 76.31 and sBLIMP 60.54, matching or exceeding baselines trained on similar or larger data. Sylber also exhibits the strongest emergent categorical perception (DI = 0.112) and generalizes zero-shot to Spanish (F1 = 71.7) and Mandarin (F1 = 71.3) without any tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against HuBERT, Sylber dramatically outperforms on syllable detection (F1 72.2 vs 39.0) and coding efficiency (4.27 vs 23.59–33.62 Tok/s), though HuBERT 2K achieves slightly lower WER (5.04% vs 7.95%) at 6–8× the token rate. Against SDHuBERT, Sylber wins on F1 (72.2 vs 67.5), syllable purity (64.0 vs 54.1), MI (5.28 vs 4.76), and Tok/s (4.27 vs 5.24), while SDHuBERT retains a slight edge in cluster purity (46.2 vs 43.9) and articulatory reconstruction correlation. Against Komatsu & Shinozaki (2024), Sylber improves F1 (72.2 vs 70.3) and syllable purity (64.0 vs 59.4) while using a linear-time algorithm. In spoken language modeling, Sylber-uLM (125M, 66K hrs) beats tGSLM (sWUGGY 76.31 vs 68.53; sBLIMP 60.54 vs 55.31) and NAST (sBLIMP 60.54 vs 55.62) despite far fewer parameters and data, and slightly outperforms the 13B-parameter TWIST on sBLIMP (60.54 vs 59.20) while trailing on sWUGGY (76.31 vs 84.10). On the Discriminability Index, Sylber (0.112) outperforms all frame-level SSL baselines including WavLM-L (0.140), HuBERT (0.141), W2V2-L (0.143), and SDHuBERT (0.131), confirming a more categorical embedding space.

## Relation to Prior Work

Sylber builds directly on SDHuBERT (Cho et al., 2024b), which itself extends HuBERT by inducing syllabic structure as a byproduct of sentence-level self-distillation. The key methodological departure is replacing this indirect induction with an explicit self-segmentation distillation objective that regresses frame features toward segment-averaged teacher embeddings, producing far cleaner syllabic structure that enables a linear-time greedy segmentation algorithm (versus the O(n²) agglomeration methods used by Peng et al., 2023 and Komatsu & Shinozaki, 2024). The tokenization and resynthesis pipeline connects to the broader discrete speech token literature (HuBERT, WavLM, GSLM, TWIST) but operates at syllable granularity (~4.3 Tok/s) rather than frame-level (25–75 Hz), and the categorical perception analysis introduces a new Discriminability Index metric grounded in the linguistic literature on categorical perception (Liberman et al., 1957).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 6–7× reduction in token rate (4.27 vs. 25–33 Tok/s) with comparable intelligibility directly addresses the quadratic attention cost bottleneck in spoken language modeling, making longer-context and higher-level linguistic structure learning more tractable. The zero-shot generalization to Spanish and Mandarin without tuning suggests the learned structure captures universal phonological properties, opening a path toward multilingual syllabic tokenization from a single English-trained model. However, the model degrades on SUPERB downstream tasks (especially speaker identification and ASR), indicating the imposed syllabic parsimony trades off universal representational breadth; better quantization methods and downstream architectures that exploit the syllabic structure remain open. The emergent categorical perception—arising from a purely regression-based loss with no contrastive or categorical objective—is a conceptually important finding that may inform how we design SSL objectives for discrete speech units.
