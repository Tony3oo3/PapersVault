## Problem

Prior theoretical work on model collapse (Dohmatob et al., 2024a) characterized iterative retraining on purely synthetic data, but left open the more realistic question of what happens when even a small fraction of synthetic data is mixed with real data during training. This paper asks whether model collapse can be avoided by simply mixing in real data, and how model size (a key design choice in the scaling laws paradigm) interacts with the collapse phenomenon.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250566/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a training set of n samples split into n₁ real samples (from distribution P₁) and n₂ synthetic samples (from P₂, with quality parameter c² measuring label-shift magnitude), along with model hyperparameters (input dimension d, random-projection width m, regularization λ). It produces a closed-form asymptotic decomposition of test error into classical bias B, variance V, and a new collapse term ζ that is nonzero whenever p₂ = n₂/n > 0. The decomposition is valid in the proportionate scaling limit where d, m, n → ∞ with fixed ratios, and specializes to explicit formulas in the isotropic Gaussian case.

## Method

The paper derives a new bias-variance decomposition E_test ≈ B + V + ζ for two analytically tractable model families: classical ridge regression in the input space and a random-projections model (a simplified neural network where only the readout layer is trained). The key technical tool is operator-valued free probability theory (OVFPT), used to compute exact high-dimensional limits of traces of rational functions of sums of random matrices, yielding deterministic equivalents for all terms in the decomposition. The collapse term ζ scales as p₂²·tr(Δ) in the under-parametrized limit, proving that any non-vanishing fraction of synthetic data causes a permanent error floor regardless of dataset size. For the random-projections model, the theory predicts a double-descent curve in model collapse: larger models amplify collapse below the interpolation threshold (m < n) but partially mitigate it above, a prediction confirmed empirically on GPT-2 trained on BabiStories and two-layer networks on MNIST.

## Results

The paper establishes that model collapse is 'strong': in the scaling-laws regime (φ→0), the extra collapse term ζ ≈ p₂²·tr(Δ) remains bounded away from zero for any non-vanishing synthetic fraction p₂, causing the test error to plateau at E_test ≈ σ²d/n + p₂²c² even as n→∞. For the random projections model, the theory predicts a double-descent curve in the collapse term ζ: larger models (higher ψ = m/n) amplify collapse before the interpolation threshold (ψ < 1) but can partially mitigate it beyond it. Empirically, GPT-2 models (124M–204M params) on BabiStories confirm that even high-quality synthetic data delays scaling-law progress, and larger models show amplified collapse beyond ~3×10¹⁰ tokens while helping below ~1×10¹⁰ tokens.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Jain et al. (2024)'s weighted single-step mixing, the paper proves (Corollary 3) that the optimal mixing coefficient α* → 0 as φ→0, meaning the best strategy is to discard all synthetic data; any fixed α ∈ [0,1) yields a positive lower bound E_test ≳ tr(Δ), i.e., persistent model collapse (confirmed in Figure 12). Against Ferbach et al. (2024)'s iterative bootstrapping, the paper shows (Corollary 2) that while t ≈ log(n/d) iterations recover the correct scaling rate E_test ≈ E̅/(1−p₂²) + Θ(p₂^{2t}), the method consistently underperforms training on real data alone, fails to leverage synthetic data even when it is high-quality, and becomes catastrophically worse when real data is scarce (p₂→1), where E_test ≈ c₀² + t·E̅ grows with t. Both baselines thus fail to eliminate model collapse without requiring a growing supply of clean real data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical study of model collapse initiated by Dohmatob et al. (2024a) for pure synthetic data to the more realistic setting of mixtures of real and synthetic data, and further introduces random projections to capture the effect of model size. It builds on the random-features and double-descent literature (Maloney et al., 2022; Bach, 2023) as a simplified proxy for neural networks, and departs from prior work by employing operator-valued free probability theory to derive a new bias-variance decomposition with an explicit collapse term ζ. The paper also positions itself against practical mitigation strategies (Jain et al., 2024; Ferbach et al., 2024), showing that naive data weighting fails while iterative mixing, though effective in theory, requires impractical amounts of clean data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 'strong model collapse' result—that even 1/1000 synthetic data plateaus scaling laws—has direct practical implications for LLM training pipelines where AI-generated data is increasingly unavoidable. The model-size analysis (larger models amplify collapse before the interpolation threshold but mitigate it after) provides a theoretical basis for understanding when scaling up helps or hurts. Key limitations include the restriction to linear and random-projection models with Gaussian data; extending the theory to deep, fully-trained networks (via NTK or lazy regimes) and to non-Gaussian, high-dimensional real-world data remains open. The paper also highlights that no known mitigation strategy eliminates collapse without a growing supply of real data, underscoring the critical importance of preserving and labeling authentic training corpora.
