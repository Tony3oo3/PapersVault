## Problem

DPO, the dominant preference optimization method for LLM alignment, only accepts binary preference pairs, forcing practitioners to discard most of the rich structure in Tree-of-Thoughts preference trees. This leads to low data utilization (many dispreferred responses are randomly filtered out) and an inability to learn from failure trajectories that differ in degree of wrongness. Additionally, DPO's step-independent reward margin shrinks when trajectories share long sub-trajectories—a common occurrence in tree-structured reasoning—making it difficult for the model to identify the truly discriminative steps.

## Input / Output

TPO takes a text prompt paired with a full preference list of N responses (typically N=10), each annotated with a continuous reward score in [-100, 100] assigned by ChatGPT using a ReACT-based scoring protocol with a reference correct trajectory. Unlike DPO which samples a single (y_w, y_l) pair per prompt, TPO consumes the entire ranked list simultaneously during fine-tuning. The output is an aligned policy model that generates step-by-step reasoning trajectories; at inference time it produces a final answer via standard CoT decoding (pass@1).

## Method

TPO reframes LM alignment as a Preference List Ranking problem by mapping prompts to queries, responses to documents, and the normalized implicit reward (β log π_θ/π_ref) to relevance scores, then optimizes a LambdaLoss-based ranking objective that weights each pairwise comparison by both the reward magnitude gap (|2^v_i − 2^v_j|) and the absolute position change in the induced ranking (1/log(1+τ(i)) − 1/log(1+τ(j))). To address the shared-sub-trajectory problem, TPO introduces an Adaptive Step Reward that decomposes the total reward margin into per-step contributions and multiplies each step-pair term by (1 + cos(emb(s_k^i), emb(s_k^j))), thereby suppressing the contribution of semantically similar (shared) steps and amplifying discriminative ones. The reward values are fixed annotations rather than learned, so TPO avoids the high variance of RL-based reward modeling. When the list size N=2 and Lambda weights are omitted, the objective reduces exactly to the standard DPO loss.

## Results

On in-distribution math tasks (pass@1 accuracy), TPO achieves 54.43% average on Qwen2-7B-Instruct (vs. 50.89% for DPO and 45.23% for the base model) and 73.36% on DeepSeekMath-7B-Instruct (vs. 71.92% for DPO), surpassing GPT-3.5 Turbo (68.15%) by 5.21 points. On out-of-distribution tasks, TPO improves BBH accuracy from 62.43% to 69.62% on Qwen2-7B-Instruct and MBPP from 60.90% to 66.42% on DeepSeekMath-7B-Instruct, while MMLU performance remains unchanged, confirming that TPO's gains are driven by long-chain reasoning rather than general knowledge. Ablation studies show that removing the Preference List Ranking module costs 2.44% average accuracy (Qwen2-7B) and removing Adaptive Step Reward costs 0.43%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TPO consistently outperforms DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) across all five backbone LLMs and all four math datasets: on Qwen2-7B-Instruct, TPO gains +3.54 points in average accuracy (54.43 vs. 50.89), and on DeepSeekMath-7B-Instruct, +1.44 points (73.36 vs. 71.92). Against the specialized RL baseline DeepSeekMath-7B-RL (Shao et al., 2024), TPO achieves a marginal average advantage (73.36 vs. 73.10) but trails slightly on ASDiv (90.61 vs. 90.94). On out-of-distribution coding tasks, TPO improves MBPP by +5.52 points over the base DeepSeekMath-7B-Instruct (66.42 vs. 60.90) and HumanEval by +3.05 points (59.15 vs. 56.10), though Qwen2 models suffer a small HumanEval drop attributed to catastrophic forgetting. On the BBH reasoning benchmark, TPO yields a +7.19 point gain over the Qwen2-7B base (69.62 vs. 62.43), while MMLU scores remain essentially unchanged across all methods, confirming TPO's gains are specific to multi-step reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

TPO extends Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) by replacing its binary preference formulation with a Preference List Ranking objective drawn from the Learn-to-Rank literature, specifically adopting the LambdaRank weighting scheme from Burges et al. (2006). The tree-structured preference data is generated via Tree-of-Thoughts (Yao et al., 2023), and the paper establishes an explicit mapping between LM alignment and LTR (prompt→query, responses→documents, implicit rewards→scores). The Adaptive Step Reward mechanism, which uses cosine similarity of step embeddings to modulate per-step reward margins, is a novel addition not present in prior DPO or LTR formulations. Contemporaneous works (S-DPO, ODPO, Rose-DPO) also explore listwise preference optimization, but TPO is distinguished by its joint treatment of multi-branch ranking and multi-step reward adjustment within a single tree-structured setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TPO addresses a practical data-efficiency problem: preference trees generated by ToT contain many dispreferred trajectories with varying quality that DPO discards, and the shared sub-trajectories in tree structures reduce the effective reward margin between pairs. The consistent 1–4% improvements across five LLMs and four math datasets, plus the ablation showing Preference List Ranking contributes more than Adaptive Step Reward, suggest the listwise formulation is the primary driver. However, the paper acknowledges that TPO can worsen catastrophic forgetting on out-of-distribution tasks (notably HumanEval for Qwen2 models), and the reward distribution from ChatGPT scoring is heavily skewed, limiting the practical ceiling. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger preference trees, whether the semantic-similarity-based step weighting generalizes beyond math, and how to integrate forgetting-mitigation strategies into the TPO training loop.
