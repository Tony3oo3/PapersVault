## Problem

Emergence in neural networks—sudden capability acquisition with scale—has never been formally defined, leading to disparate and often contradictory explanations. This paper proposes a phenomenological definition requiring that emergence involves the model learning general data regularities that simultaneously drive discontinuous improvements across multiple tasks, and constructs a controlled experimental system to test whether this definition captures a real, predictable phenomenon.

## Input / Output

The model (a GPT-2-style architecture) is trained via autoregressive language modeling on strings sampled online from a probabilistic context-sensitive language of 900 entities and 18,000 properties organized into 10 concept classes. Three task formats are used: free generation (produce a grammatically valid, type-constraint-respecting sentence from a prompt), unscrambling (reorder a randomly permuted sentence into its correct form), and conditional generation (produce a valid sentence that incorporates a given set of entity or property tokens). Evaluation decomposes performance into grammaticality, descriptive type checks, relative type checks, exact match, per-token accuracy, and conditions-satisfied ratios.

## Method

The paper defines a formal language using a PCFG over parts-of-speech symbols, then enforces context-sensitivity via a bipartite type-constraints graph linking entities to valid properties (descriptive and relative). Training a Transformer on this language reveals three distinct phases: grammar acquisition, relative type-constraint acquisition, and descriptive type-constraint acquisition, with narrower tasks (unscrambling, conditional generation) showing sudden performance jumps shortly after each phase boundary. The key theoretical contribution is modeling the learning of descriptive type constraints as bond percolation on a bipartite graph: as training proceeds, edges (seen entity–property pairs) are added, and the model generalizes to unseen compositions once a percolation threshold is crossed. The percolation threshold scales as p_c ≈ √(1/(|E||K|)), predicting that the emergence point for descriptive constraints should shift proportionally to √|K| when the number of properties is varied—a prediction confirmed empirically by rescaling the x-axis and observing collapse of transition points.

## Results

The paper identifies three distinct phases in a Transformer's learning dynamics on a context-sensitive formal language: (1) grammar acquisition within ~100 iterations, (2) sudden acquisition of relative type constraints at ~1000 iterations (jumping from ~0 to near-perfect accuracy), and (3) sublinear (log-linear) growth in descriptive type constraint learning. The model reaches ~30–35% performance on descriptive type checks, well above the ~15% ceiling achievable by pure memorization of seen entity–property pairs, confirming generalization to unseen compositions. The percolation model predicts the transition point for descriptive type checks scales as √|K| (0.5 exponent), which is confirmed empirically; the unscrambling task on descriptive sentences shows a |K|^(3/2) (1.5 exponent) scaling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods or baselines in the traditional sense; it is a controlled empirical and theoretical study of emergence dynamics rather than a method benchmarking paper. The key validation is internal: the percolation-theory prediction (p_c ≈ √(1/|E||K|), yielding a √|K| scaling of the emergence point) is tested against the empirical scaling curves obtained by varying the number of descriptive properties |K| while holding |E|=900 fixed. The 0.5-exponent collapse observed in Figure 7 for descriptive type checks and free-generation probability matches the percolation prediction, while the unscrambling task exhibits a distinct 1.5-exponent scaling, which the authors attribute to an additional delay from learning the unscrambling circuit on top of the already-learned grammar and type constraints. Grammar learning and relative type constraint acquisition are shown to be unaffected by |K|, confirming the model treats them as independent regularities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by proposing the first phenomenological *definition* of emergence for neural networks, drawing on the physics of phase transitions and order parameters (Anderson 1972; Newman 2001, 2003) to formalize what prior LLM scaling studies (Wei et al. 2022; Srivastava et al. 2022) documented but never precisely defined. It explicitly departs from Schaeffer et al. (2023), who argued emergence is a metric artifact, by showing sudden improvements persist even under continuous and structure-ignoring metrics. The experimental system builds on the formal-language learning literature for transformers (Allen-Zhu & Li 2023; Jain et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2023a), but introduces context-sensitivity via type constraints to create a richer, two-regularity setting. The theoretical contribution repurposes bond percolation on bipartite graphs (Cohen et al. 2002; Newman et al. 2001) as a predictive model for when a model transitions from memorizing entity-property pairs to generalizing over concept classes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, testable framework for predicting *when* a capability will emerge by identifying the data regularity being learned and modeling its acquisition as a percolation transition with a √(|E||K|) scaling law. This is significant for AI risk regulation, which requires preemptive conjecture of capabilities (NIST 2023; EU AI Act 2024). Key limitations include the toy setting (single nanoGPT architecture, 3 seeds, synthetic language), the qualitative (not quantitative) match with percolation theory, and the unexplained 1.5 exponent for unscrambling. Open questions include whether the framework extends to naturalistic LLMs (e.g., Pythia checkpoints), whether the percolation analogy holds for non-bipartite regularity structures, and whether the memorization-to-generalization transition identified in Phase 3 is architecture-specific.
