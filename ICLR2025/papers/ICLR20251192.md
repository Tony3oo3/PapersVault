## Problem

Existing LLM reasoning benchmarks are either static (vulnerable to data contamination and saturation) or dynamic but uncontrolled (e.g., Chatbot Arena), which conflates reasoning with style preferences and cannot isolate specific reasoning capabilities like deductive, inductive, abductive, or multi-hop reasoning. GameArena addresses this gap by introducing a dynamic benchmark that uses structured game rules to constrain human-AI interactions toward targeted reasoning skills while maintaining user engagement.

## Input / Output

Each game session takes a human player's text responses (yes/no answers in Akinator, descriptive prompts in Taboo, statements and answers in Bluffing) combined with a DSPy-optimized system prompt and the accumulated conversation history as input. The LLM produces game responses (questions, answers, or secret predictions) constrained by game-specific rules such as 20 binary questions in Akinator, 5 rounds in Taboo, or 5 questions in Bluffing. Beyond the game outcome (win/loss, round count), retrospective analysis produces step-by-step reasoning trajectories: ranked object/word lists at each turn for Akinator and Taboo, and 5-level truthfulness predictions (True → False) at each turn for Bluffing, from which procedural metrics like recall rates, disparity ratios, Spearman's coefficients, and hopping penalties are computed.

## Method

GameArena integrates LLMs into three existing games—Akinator (deductive + multi-hop reasoning), Taboo (abductive + multi-hop reasoning), and Bluffing (inductive + multi-hop reasoning)—where game rules naturally constrain interactions to exercise specific reasoning capabilities. To control for prompt variance, the authors use DSPy's chain-of-thought module with a model-as-evaluator to search and optimize five system prompts per game, randomly selecting one per session. The key analytical contribution is a retrospective analysis pipeline: after each game session, the model is re-prompted with identical system prompt, history, and inference parameters to reveal its hidden intermediate reasoning (ranked candidate lists or truthfulness judgments) at every turn. From these trajectories, the paper derives capability-specific metrics—recall rate and disparity ratio for multi-hop reasoning, average first-appear round and final rank for deductive/abductive/inductive reasoning, and Spearman's rank correlation plus hopping penalty for prediction consistency—enabling fine-grained, step-by-step evaluation of reasoning that static benchmarks and holistic preference voting cannot provide.

## Results

Across 2,240 game sessions, Claude 3.5 Sonnet achieves the highest win rates in Akinator (55%) and Bluffing (67%), while GPT-4o leads in Taboo (67%). On procedural (retrospective) metrics, Claude 3.5 Sonnet leads in Akinator multi-hop reasoning (27.83% recall rate) and deductive reasoning (avg. first appear round 11.63, final rank 5.31), and in Taboo abductive reasoning (avg. first appear round 1.56, final rank 1.48). Mistral Large performs poorly, with near-zero win rates in Akinator (2%) and Bluffing (0%), and the lowest recall rates across all games. GameArena yields 86.9% useful data versus only 4% for Chatbot Arena, and a 100-participant user study shows >70% enjoyment and >80% satisfaction for GameArena versus 45% and <40% for Chatbot Arena.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Chatbot Arena, GameArena achieves dramatically higher data efficiency (86.9% vs. 4% useful sessions) and user engagement (>70% enjoyment vs. 45%), but ranking correlations are weak: Kendall's Tau ranges from 0.2 (Taboo-Outcome) to 0.6 (Akinator-Retro, Taboo-Retro), and RBO hypothesis tests fail to reject the null for most Chatbot Arena comparisons (p > 0.1), confirming that GameArena captures reasoning-specific signals that Chatbot Arena's holistic preference voting misses. Against LiveBench-Reasoning, GameArena's procedural metrics show strong agreement: Kendall's Tau of 0.8 and RBO of 0.98 for both Akinator-Retro (deductive) and Taboo-Retro (abductive), with statistically significant p-values (p < 0.05). Against GPQA, the same procedural metrics also achieve Kendall's Tau of 0.8 and RBO of 0.98 with p < 0.05, while outcome metrics show moderate agreement (Tau 0.6, RBO 0.93). Notably, GameArena's Taboo-Outcome ranking disagrees with both LiveBench-Reasoning and GPQA (Tau = −0.2), suggesting that game outcome alone is a noisier signal than the retrospective procedural analysis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two main lines of LLM evaluation: static benchmarks (MATH, HumanEval, GPQA) that are vulnerable to contamination and saturation, and dynamic benchmarks like Chatbot Arena that produce holistic rankings confounded by style preferences. It also distinguishes itself from prior game-based LLM evaluations (Minecraft builder tasks, Werewolf, grid-based competitions) by using human-LLM interactive games rather than single-player or LLM-vs-LLM setups, and by targeting fine-grained reasoning sub-abilities (deductive, inductive, abductive, multi-hop) rather than general capability. The retrospective analysis technique—prompting the model post-hoc to reveal intermediate thought states at each game turn—is a novel data collection mechanism not seen in prior game-based or benchmark work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GameArena addresses a real gap: the field lacks a way to collect step-by-step, human-labeled reasoning trajectories from LLMs in naturalistic interactive settings, which is critical for understanding and improving reasoning. The 86.9% vs. 4% useful-data-rate comparison with Chatbot Arena is a compelling practical argument for gamified evaluation. However, limitations remain: only three games and five models are covered, the retrospective analysis assumes the model's revealed intermediate states faithfully reflect its actual real-time reasoning (a non-trivial assumption), and the small sample sizes per model-game pair (roughly 90 sessions) limit statistical power. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to more complex multi-agent games and whether the reasoning metrics truly isolate the targeted cognitive skill from general language ability.
