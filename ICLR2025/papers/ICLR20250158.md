## Problem

Standard transformers retain all past tokens in their context buffer, forcing attention to process irrelevant or misleading elements that degrade performance. While RNNs and state-space models avoid this by keeping only constant-size summaries, they cannot solve tasks requiring full sequence recall (e.g., arbitrary copying). The paper asks whether a transformer can selectively ignore unneeded context elements to improve quality, treating efficiency gains as a secondary benefit.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard token sequences in a decoder-only transformer and produces next-token predictions as usual. Uniquely, it also computes an N×N masking matrix F (derived from an existing attention head's output) that is subtracted from attention logits before softmax, effectively letting intermediate tokens attenuate the influence of earlier tokens on all future tokens. In the context-pruning variant, tokens whose accumulated masking exceeds a per-layer budget are evicted from the KV-cache, reducing inference memory and compute without degrading perplexity.

## Method

Selective Attention reuses the output of one existing attention head as a parameter-free selection function S, which is constrained via ReLU (only reduce attention, never boost), zeroing the BOS column, and zeroing the diagonal. The constrained S is accumulated via cumulative summation along the sequence axis to produce F, capturing the total masking each past token receives from all intervening tokens. F is then subtracted from the attention logits before softmax, so that a token which has 'absorbed' the content of an earlier token can suppress that earlier token's contribution to all subsequent attention operations. For inference efficiency, a per-layer memory budget is allocated greedily (iteratively reducing the budget of the layer whose reduction least impacts loss), and tokens exceeding their layer's budget are evicted from the KV-cache. An optional L_mem auxiliary loss encourages the model to mask more aggressively, enabling up to 47× memory reduction at matched perplexity.

## Results

On C4 language modeling with a d=12 transformer (context 512), selective attention achieves 2.6372 validation log-perplexity versus 2.6815 for the standard attention baseline, and the gap widens with larger context sizes. With context pruning and an explicit memory loss (ε=0.1), selective attention reduces KV-cache memory by 16×, 25×, and 47× for context sizes 512, 1024, and 2048 respectively while matching baseline perplexity. On downstream tasks (Table 1), selective attention yields consistent gains across all model sizes, e.g. 46.95% vs 45.64% on ARC-Easy and 53.76% vs 53.50% on HellaSwag at d=28. A transformer with selective attention matches the perplexity of a standard transformer with roughly 2× the number of attention heads and parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares selective attention against three efficient-attention/pruning baselines in the perplexity-vs-KV-cache-size trade-off (Figure 7, Appendix A.10): H2O (Zhang et al., 2023), TOVA (Oren et al., 2024), and sparse attention (Child et al., 2019). Selective attention substantially outperforms all three baselines across context sizes 512, 1024, and 2048, achieving lower perplexity at every comparable KV-cache budget. H2O and TOVA are post-hoc pruning methods applied to a pre-trained model, whereas selective attention is learned during training, giving it a structural advantage in identifying which tokens to retain. Sparse attention (fixed patterns) also underperforms, confirming that data-adaptive masking is superior to rigid sparsity patterns. Additionally, local attention windows (Appendix A.8) perform worse than the dense baseline, which in turn is worse than selective attention, further validating that the learned, per-token masking captures structure that fixed windows cannot.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Leviathan (2022)'s 'Art of Transformer Programming,' which demonstrated through hand-crafted transformer programs that masking unneeded context elements simplifies computation and hypothesized the benefit would transfer to learned language models. It modifies the standard multi-head attention mechanism from Vaswani et al. (2017) with a parameter-free masking term derived from an existing attention head's output. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant line of attention-modification research (sparse attention, linear attention, FlashAttention, H2O, TOVA) which targets inference efficiency as the primary goal; here, quality improvement is the primary objective and efficiency gains (via context pruning) are a secondary benefit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

A parameter-free, negligible-compute modification that consistently improves perplexity across model sizes and context lengths, and enables 16X–47X KV-cache memory reduction at matched perplexity, makes selective attention a strong candidate as a default component in transformer decoders. Open questions include applicability to multi-query and grouped-query attention, models beyond ~1B parameters, whether further training after context pruning yields additional gains, and whether the approach transfers to encoder architectures. The paper also leaves training-time efficiency unaddressed—pruning helps inference but not the quadratic training cost.
