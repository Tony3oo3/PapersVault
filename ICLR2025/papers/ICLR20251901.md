## Problem

Density ratio estimation via binary classification depends critically on the choice of loss function, yet the resulting error measure (a Bregman divergence) is only implicitly determined by that choice. Classical losses (logistic, boosting, square) all assign higher weight to small density ratio values, even though large values are the ones that matter most in applications like importance weighting for domain adaptation. The paper asks: given a desired Bregman error measure, what is the unique loss function that realizes it, and can we design losses that prioritize large density ratio values?

## Input / Output

The method consumes iid samples (x_i)_{i=1}^n from P and (x'_i)_{i=1}^m from Q, labeled as y=1 and y=-1 respectively, and trains a binary classifier in a Gaussian RKHS with Tikhonov regularization (α chosen by cross-validation from {10, 0.1, 10^{-3}}). The output is a density ratio estimator β̂(x) = g_can(f̂(x)) that is then used as importance weights in Eq. 14 for parameter selection (IWV or IWA) across 11 deep domain adaptation algorithms. In the domain adaptation setting, the source data carries labels y'_i = f_P(x'_i) + ε_i while target observations are unlabeled.

## Method

The core contribution is Theorem 1, which inverts the constructions of Menon & Ong (2016) and Reid & Williamson (2010) to show that for any strictly convex Bregman generator φ and monotonically increasing link g, there exists a unique strictly proper composite loss of the form ℓ(y,ŷ) = γ(Ψ^{-1}(ŷ)) ± Ψ^{-1}(ŷ)γ'(Ψ^{-1}(ŷ)) that makes Algorithm 1 minimize B_φ(β, g∘f). A canonical link g_can^{-1} = φ' (Corollary 1) guarantees convexity of the resulting loss. The paper then instantiates this recipe with increasing weight functions φ''(c) = e^{2c} (exponential weight) and φ''(c) = c^{2k} (polynomial weights), yielding losses like ℓ(1,y) = -y, ℓ(-1,y) = ½y(log(2y)−1) with estimator β̂(x) = ½log(2f̂(x)). These losses penalize errors in large density ratio regions more heavily than classical losses, which use non-increasing weights, and are validated on 484 domain adaptation tasks across text, sensor, and image data.

## Results

On AmazonReviews (12 tasks), EW achieves a mean accuracy of 0.789 under Importance Weighted Aggregation (IWA), outperforming KuLSIF (0.781), LR (0.774), and Boost (0.766). On HHAR (5 tasks), EW reaches 0.722 under IWA versus 0.717 (KuLSIF), 0.713 (LR), and 0.709 (Boost). On MiniDomainNet (5 tasks), EW attains 0.522 under IWA, marginally ahead of KuLSIF and LR (both 0.521) and Boost (0.519). Across all 484 parameter selection tasks, EW is among the best-performing method in 56% of cases, compared to 23% for KuLSIF, 17% for Boost, and 13% for LR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EW consistently outperforms all three baselines under IWA across all three datasets: on AmazonReviews it gains +0.8% over KuLSIF (0.789 vs. 0.781), +1.5% over LR (0.789 vs. 0.774), and +2.3% over Boost (0.789 vs. 0.766); on HHAR it gains +0.5% over KuLSIF and +1.3% over Boost; on MiniDomainNet it edges out KuLSIF and LR by +0.1%. Under IWV, the advantage is smaller and more mixed: EW ties KuLSIF on AmazonReviews (0.773) and HHAR (0.745), slightly trails LR on MiniDomainNet (0.513 vs. 0.514), but clearly beats Boost on all three datasets (e.g., 0.773 vs. 0.767 on AmazonReviews, 0.745 vs. 0.732 on HHAR). The paper reports that EW is among the best method in 56% of all 484 parameter selection cells, versus 23% for KuLSIF, 17% for Boost, and 13% for LR, indicating that the exponential-weight loss's focus on large density ratio values translates into more reliable importance weighting for deep domain adaptation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper inverts the direction of analysis established by Menon & Ong (2016) and Reid & Williamson (2010): rather than starting from a loss function and identifying the implicit Bregman divergence it minimizes, it starts from a desired Bregman divergence and characterizes all loss functions that produce it. The characterization builds on the Bregman divergence framework of Sugiyama et al. (2012b) and the RKHS estimation setup of Zellinger et al. (2023), but the key departure is showing that the constructions in Menon & Ong's Appendix B are not merely sufficient but necessary. The paper then uses this recipe to design novel losses with increasing weight functions φ'', in contrast to all classical losses (logistic, boosting, square, KL) which have non-increasing weights and thus systematically deprioritize large density ratio values.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The complete characterization provides a principled design recipe for loss functions with prescribed error properties, which is a useful theoretical tool for the density ratio estimation community. The practical demonstration on 484 domain adaptation tasks shows that prioritizing large density ratio values yields modest but consistent improvements in importance-weighted parameter selection. The sample complexity of the proposed methods remains an open problem, and the empirical gains, while consistent across 11 deep learning algorithms and three datasets, are relatively small in absolute terms (e.g., ~0.002–0.005 accuracy improvements over KuLSIF or LR on average).
