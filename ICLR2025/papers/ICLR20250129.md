## Problem

Scalar reward models used for LLM preference alignment suffer from two critical limitations: they provide no interpretable explanation for their judgments, and they are prone to capturing spurious dataset biases (e.g., verbosity) rather than true human values. Existing LLM-as-a-judge approaches that address interpretability typically depend on external models like GPT-4 or require high-quality human-written instruction data for supervised fine-tuning, making them costly and difficult to scale as LLMs grow more capable.

## Input / Output

The model takes a question (or prompt) concatenated with a pair of candidate answers as input, formatted within a preamble that instructs the LLM to act as an evaluation expert. The output is a JSON-structured judgment containing two fields: a 'rationale' key with step-by-step natural language reasoning, and a 'better_answer' key with a binary integer (1 or 2) indicating the preferred response. During training, the model is prompted with either a neutral preamble or a hint-driven preamble that explicitly states which answer is better, enabling the construction of both positive and negative judgment examples from the same input.

## Method

Con-J trains an LLM-as-a-Judge through a three-step self-bootstrapping pipeline that requires only preference annotations (no external models or human-written instructions). First, the pre-trained LLM generates multiple judgments per prompt via repeated sampling and hint-driven sampling (where the model is told which answer is better and asked to justify it). Second, true preference labels filter these into contrastive pairs of correct (j⁺) and incorrect (j⁻) judgments. Third, the LLM is trained with a DPO loss on these contrastive judgment pairs, augmented with a small-weight SFT loss on positive judgments to prevent overoptimization. The key insight is that generating natural language rationales as part of the training target acts as a regularizer: the bias in preference data is distributed between the rationale and the binary prediction, reducing direct bias capture, while the generative training objective preserves the pre-trained LLM's prior knowledge more effectively than a randomly initialized scalar head.

## Results

On self-built commercial datasets, Con-J achieves 72.4% (Creation), 85.0% (Math), and 70.1% (Code) judgment accuracy, consistently outperforming the scalar reward model (SM) by 2.2–3.0 points and surpassing GPT-4o by 16.8–17.2 points. On public benchmarks, the open-source Con-J (trained on Skywork-Reward-Preference 80K) reaches 81.0% on Infinity-Preference and 73.0% on UltraFeedback, outperforming GPT-4o (75.0% and 72.2%) and all open-source LLMs-as-Judges. On Reward-Bench, Con-J achieves an average of 86.5% across four sub-tasks, slightly edging out GPT-4o's 86.0% average.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Con-J decisively outperforms Qwen2-7B across all benchmarks, with the largest gaps on Infinity-Preference (81.0 vs. 59.0, +22.0) and Reward-Bench Chat-H (79.6 vs. 44.8, +34.8). Against Auto-J, Con-J wins on 6 of 7 sub-metrics, losing only on Reward-Bench Chat (91.3 vs. 93.0), while dominating on Chat-H (79.6 vs. 40.0) and Reasoning (87.1 vs. 50.5). Con-J also surpasses Prometheus 2 on every benchmark, with margins of 13.0 on Infinity-Preference, 9.7 on UltraFeedback, and 10.9 on Reward-Bench Safety. Compared to GPT-4o, Con-J wins on Infinity-Preference (+6.0), UltraFeedback (+0.8), Chat-H (+5.3), Safety (+0.4), and Reasoning (+0.2), but trails on PKU-SafeRLHF (−1.2) and Reward-Bench Chat (−4.0). On the self-built commercial datasets, Con-J outperforms the scalar reward model by 2.2–3.0 points and GPT-4o by 16.8–17.2 points, confirming that domain-specific training of a 7B model can surpass both scalar architectures and a much larger closed-source model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024) to a new target: instead of aligning an LLM's responses, it trains the LLM to generate natural-language preference judgments. It departs from existing LLM-as-a-Judge methods (Auto-J, Prometheus 2) that rely on SFT with GPT-4-generated instruction data, replacing that pipeline with a self-bootstrapping procedure that constructs contrastive judgment pairs from the model's own outputs, supervised only by binary preference labels. Conceptually, it also contrasts with scalar reward models (Bradley-Terry-based) by retaining the generative architecture of the pre-trained LLM rather than appending a classification head, which the authors argue preserves pre-training knowledge and provides implicit regularization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Con-J offers a practical path to interpretable, bias-robust preference judgment without the costly dependency on GPT-4-generated instruction data, which is increasingly difficult to scale as LLMs outpace human writing ability. The finding that generating rationales acts as a regularizer against dataset bias (format, verbosity) is a useful mechanistic insight for the broader preference-learning literature. Open questions include whether improving rationale quality can further boost prediction accuracy, whether the approach generalizes to value systems fundamentally different from the pre-training distribution, and how to close the growing gap between prediction accuracy and rationale consistency as training progresses.
