## Problem

Tree-based ensemble methods (Random Forests, GBDT) remain the dominant approach for tabular discriminative tasks, yet direct generative extensions that explicitly model the joint data density are missing. Existing deep-learning generative models for tabular data either do not model density explicitly or cannot evaluate it efficiently due to intractable marginalization, limiting their use to sampling-only applications. This paper addresses the gap by proposing generative counterparts of both RF and GBDT that learn an explicit (unnormalized) density over the full input space.

## Input / Output

The method takes a tabular dataset with a mixture of continuous and categorical features, discretizing continuous variables by quantile binning (inspired by LightGBM) to establish a finite input domain. It produces an energy function f(x) — a sum of piecewise constant tree functions — that defines a density q_f(x) = exp(f(x))/Z[f] up to a normalization constant. This unnormalized density supports multiple output modes: approximate sampling via Gibbs/rejection sampling, exact conditional inference q(x_i | x_{-i}) by softmax over the unobserved variable's values, and principled missing-data handling by marginalization, without ever needing to compute Z[f].

## Method

NRGBoost trains an energy-based model by iteratively adding piecewise constant tree weak learners, where at each boosting round the optimal tree δf is found by maximizing a second-order Taylor approximation of the log-likelihood — directly analogous to the Newton step in XGBoost. For tree weak learners, this quadratic objective decomposes per-leaf, yielding a closed-form leaf value w_j* = P(X_j)/Q_f(X_j) − 1 and a greedy splitting criterion proportional to the χ²-divergence P²(X)/Q_f(X), avoiding any full Hessian solve. Because the model is unnormalized, training requires MCMC samples from the current model; the paper introduces an amortized sampling strategy that maintains a fixed-size sample pool, retaining previous-round samples via rejection sampling (with acceptance probability bounded by the tree's range) and topping up with new Gibbs samples, reducing the quadratic-in-iterations sampling cost. Regularization is achieved by capping the per-leaf ratio P(X_j)/Q_f(X_j), which also guarantees a lower bound on the rejection-sampling acceptance probability and, combined with a small step size, ensures monotonic training-likelihood increase at each round.

## Results

On single-variable inference (Table 1), NRGBoost achieves R² of 0.547 (Abalone), 0.850 (California Housing), and 0.676 (Protein), matching XGBoost's 0.552, 0.849, and 0.678 within one standard error on all three regression datasets. For classification, NRGBoost reaches AUC 0.920 (Adult) and 0.974 (MiniBooNE) versus XGBoost's 0.927 and 0.987, and accuracy 0.966 (MNIST) and 0.948 (Covertype) versus 0.976 and 0.971. On the discriminator measure (Table 3, lower AUC = better), NRGBoost achieves 0.559–0.724 across most datasets, substantially outperforming all other generative methods (next best: TabDDPM at 0.604–0.915). On ML efficiency (Table 2), NRGBoost and TabDDPM alternate as the top performer, with NRGBoost never ranked below second on any dataset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the discriminative XGBoost baseline, NRGBoost is statistically indistinguishable on the three smaller regression datasets (Abalone, California Housing, Protein) and trails by 0.7–2.3 points on the three larger ones (Adult AUC, MiniBooNE AUC, Covertype accuracy), while remaining the best generative method on all seven datasets. Among generative baselines, NRGBoost substantially outperforms RFDE (e.g., R² 0.547 vs. 0.071 on Abalone; AUC 0.974 vs. 0.668 on MiniBooNE) and ARF (e.g., R² 0.850 vs. 0.758 on California Housing; accuracy 0.966 vs. N/A on MNIST), as well as both DEF variants (ISE and KL). On the sampling discriminator measure (Table 3), NRGBoost achieves the lowest AUC on 5 of 7 datasets (e.g., 0.559 on Adult vs. TabDDPM's 0.604 and TVAE's 0.898), with TabDDPM winning only on MiniBooNE (0.789 vs. 0.993) and an inconclusive result on Protein. On ML efficiency (Table 2), NRGBoost and TabDDPM trade the top spot dataset-by-dataset, while TVAE, Forest-Flow, ARF, and DEF(KL) are consistently outperformed by both. For uncertainty estimation (Table 11), NRGBoost achieves lower CRPS than NGBoost on all three regression datasets (e.g., 0.201 vs. 0.238 on California Housing) and lower MAE than XGBoost on California Housing and Protein, attributed to its non-parametric conditional distribution versus NGBoost's fixed Gaussian assumption.

## Relation to Prior Work

NRGBoost directly extends the second-order boosting framework of XGBoost/LightGBM to a generative, energy-based setting, where the energy function (log-density) is updated via a Newton/natural-gradient step at each round. This departs from prior generative boosting work (Tu 2007; Grover & Ermon 2018) that uses greedy multiplicative updates proportional to the likelihood ratio, and from Welling et al. (2002) which relies on first-order approximation with parametric weak learners. The paper also introduces Density Estimation Forests (DEF) as a bagged-ensemble counterpart to Random Forests, extending the original Density Estimation Trees of Ram & Gray (2011) with feature subsampling and KL-divergence-based splitting. The key structural insight is that piecewise-constant tree weak learners yield a diagonal Hessian, making the second-order step separable across leaves and computationally tractable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first direct generative extension of GBDT that achieves near-parity discriminative performance while unlocking applications unavailable to discriminative models: principled missing-data inference via marginalization, arbitrary conditional sampling, and out-of-distribution detection. The amortized sampling strategy (rejection sampling over a maintained pool) is a practical contribution that mitigates the quadratic MCMC cost inherent to boosting-based EBMs. Key limitations remain: sampling is still MCMC-based and slower than neural alternatives (TabDDPM, TVAE), the approach scales poorly to very large numbers of trees, and the method has not been tested on datasets with thousands of features. Open questions include whether the second-order framework can be combined with more expressive leaf models or adapted to handle truly continuous (non-discretized) inputs without binning.
