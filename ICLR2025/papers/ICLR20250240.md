## Problem

Estimating the missing mass (probability of unobserved classes) in multinomial distributions has relied on Poisson approximations that incorrectly treat class frequencies as independent, leading to the Good-Turing estimator being identified as unbiased when it is in fact biased. This paper provides the first exact characterization of the expected missing mass in terms of the sample's frequency-of-frequencies, showing it is almost entirely determined by observable quantities with only an exponentially decaying remainder dependent on the unknown distribution. The practical gap is that the resulting exact estimator has impractically high variance, motivating a search-based approach to find low-MSE estimators.

## Input / Output

The method takes a sample X^n of size n drawn from an unknown multinomial distribution and a target frequency k (0 ≤ k ≤ n), and produces a set of coefficients α_{i,j} defining a linear combination of frequencies of frequencies Φ_i(j) computed over subsamples of varying sizes j ≤ n+1. The discovered estimator can be adapted to larger sample sizes m > n by rescaling coefficients by C(m,k)/C(n,k), and for the missing mass (k=0) the representation is valid for any m ≥ n without modification. The methodology is distribution-free, but the output coefficients are distribution-specific, minimizing MSE on the particular unknown distribution that generated the sample.

## Method

The core theoretical contribution is a key identity (Eq. 4) relating expected frequencies of frequencies f_k(n) across different k and n values, enabling an exact decomposition of E[M_k] into a sum of observable terms plus an exponentially decaying remainder R_{n,k} = C(n,k)·(-1)^{n-k}·f_{n+1}(n+1). From this decomposition, they derive a minimal-bias estimator M̂_k^B with bias O(n^k · c^{-n}), but its variance is too high for practical use. To find a practical estimator, they cast the problem as a search over representations of E[M_k] defined by coefficients α_{i,j} satisfying linear constraints (Eq. 14), and use a genetic algorithm that mutates candidate representations via four E[M_k]-preserving algebraic identities. The fitness function estimates MSE by plugging in GT-based probability estimates (hybrid empirical/GT for observed classes, Chao's species richness estimator for unobserved classes) as a proxy for the unknown distribution. The GA discovers estimators whose MSE is roughly 80% of the GT estimator's across 93% of runs when n ≥ S, with all differences statistically significant at α < 10^{-9}.

## Results

The genetic algorithm discovers estimators whose MSE is roughly 80% of the Good-Turing estimator's on average across six multinomial distributions. When the sample size equals twice the support size (n=2S), the evolved estimator outperforms GT in 93% of runs (Vargha-Delaney Â₁₂ = 0.93), with an average MSE ratio of 87%. The minimal-bias estimator M̂₀ᴮ achieves a bias smaller than GT's by thousands of orders of magnitude (e.g., ~10⁻²⁰⁰ vs. ~10⁻³ at n=100), though its variance is comparable to GT's. On the real-world Australian population dataset (S=104, n=50), the evolved estimator achieves only 25% of GT's MSE with a 95% CI of [0.409, 0.565] versus GT's [0.322, 0.638].

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Good-Turing estimator (Good, 1953), the evolved estimator achieves a lower MSE in over 93% of runs when n≥S, with an average MSE roughly 80% of GT's; the improvement is most pronounced at n=S (79% ratio) and grows with sample size. Against Painsky (2023), which also searches for optimal coefficients but only over Φᵢ(n) (not subsequence FoF), the proposed method achieves a median 10% lower MSE and mean 14% lower MSE, while Painsky itself still outperforms GT. Against Wu & Yang (2019) (Chebyshev/moment matching for support size estimation), the proposed method's MSE is roughly 6× lower in the median, with worst-case gaps reaching six orders of magnitude. Against Valiant & Valiant (2017) (plausible histogram estimation), the proposed method's MSE is approximately 128× lower in the median, as their method was not designed for missing mass estimation. The paper's method wins on all four comparisons across all six distributions and all tested sample sizes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Good-Turing (1953) estimator and the extensive literature on estimating missing mass in multinomial distributions (Orlitsky & Suresh, 2015; Orlitsky et al., 2016; Painsky, 2022; Hao & Li, 2020). Its key departure is twofold: (1) it provides an exact, distribution-free characterization of E[M_k] in terms of the frequency-of-frequencies (Theorem 1.1), showing the GT estimator is merely the first term of an alternating sum with an exponentially decaying remainder, thereby avoiding the Poisson independence approximation used throughout prior work; and (2) it reframes estimation as a search problem over representations of E[M_k], using a genetic algorithm to discover distribution-specific estimators that minimize MSE for a given sample, rather than seeking a single universal estimator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical result that the expected missing mass is almost entirely determined by the observed sample (with only an exponentially decaying distribution-dependent remainder) provides a clean foundation that prior work lacked, and the ~20% MSE reduction over Good-Turing is practically meaningful for applications like species discovery, software testing, and NLP. However, the approach has notable limitations: the minimal-bias estimator has impractically high variance, the GA-discovered estimator is distribution-specific (not generalizable without re-optimization), and the O(n^4) fitness evaluation limits scalability. Open questions include whether the framework extends to the Good-Toulmin extrapolation estimator, whether the distribution-specific nature becomes a practical liability when the true distribution shifts, and whether the search can be replaced by a more principled (e.g., convex) optimization for larger n.
