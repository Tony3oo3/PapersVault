## Problem

Momentum-based optimizers like AdamW rely on a single Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of gradients, which cannot simultaneously assign high weight to the immediate past and non-negligible weight to older gradients. The paper demonstrates that older gradients remain relevant for tens of thousands of steps, yet simply increasing AdamW's β1 degrades performance because it reduces sensitivity to recent local changes in the loss landscape. This leaves a gap: how to exploit very old gradient information while preserving fast adaptation to the current optimization trajectory.

## Input / Output

At each iteration the optimizer consumes the current stochastic gradient g^(t) and the current parameter vector θ^(t), maintaining three state buffers per parameter: a fast EMA m1 (β1≈0.9), a slow EMA m2 (β3≈0.9999, half-life ≈6930 steps), and the standard second-moment estimate ν (β2≈0.999). The output is the updated parameter vector θ^(t) computed as a single AdamW-style adaptive step whose numerator is the bias-corrected fast EMA plus α times the uncorrected slow EMA. Two schedulers progressively ramp α from 0 to its target and β3 from β1 to its target over the full training duration to prevent early divergence.

## Method

AdEMAMix modifies AdamW by adding a second EMA m2 with a very large decay β3 (typically 0.9999) that accumulates gradient information over thousands of steps, and combines it with the original fast EMA m1 via a weighted sum (m̂1 + α·m2) in the update numerator, normalized by the standard second-moment denominator. The key geometric insight is that the slow momentum provides strong acceleration along the valley direction of the loss landscape, while the fast momentum enables orthogonal corrections that keep the iterate within the valley—something a single EMA cannot achieve. To handle early-training instabilities caused by the large β3, the method introduces two schedulers: a linear ramp for α and a non-linear ramp for β3 that increases the half-life t_half linearly rather than β3 itself, since equal increments of β3 have vastly different effects at different magnitudes. Setting β1=0 is also supported, reducing memory overhead to match AdamW exactly at the cost of slightly less stable training.

## Results

On 1.3B-parameter Transformer LLMs trained on RedPajama, AdEMAMix trained for 770k steps (101B tokens) matches an AdamW model trained for 1.5M steps (197B tokens), a 95% token reduction. For 110M models, 256k iterations of AdEMAMix equals 500k iterations of AdamW. On in-context learning benchmarks (1.3B models, 131B tokens), AdEMAMix outperforms AdamW on 11 of 13 tasks, with the largest gain on PubmedQA (0.632 vs. 0.556, +7.6 pts). On ViT pretraining with abundant data (24M/86M params on ImageNet-21k, 11M images), AdEMAMix achieves lower train and test loss than the best AdamW baseline across all tested hyperparameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole experimental baseline throughout the paper is AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019). AdEMAMix consistently outperforms AdamW across all model scales tested (110M, 330M, 1.3B, and 3B parameters) on language modeling, with the gap widening as training duration increases. On the 1.3B ICL benchmarks, AdEMAMix wins on 11 of 13 tasks, with the most notable margin on PubmedQA (+7.6 pts) and Winogrande (+1.7 pts), while losing narrowly on LogiQA (−1.0 pt) and TruthfulQA (−0.9 pt) and tying on PIQA and OpenbookQA. On ViT pretraining, AdEMAMix beats the best AdamW baseline in both train and test loss when data is abundant relative to model capacity (24M and 86M params on 11M ImageNet-21k images), but fails to improve when data is scarce (86M params on 1.3M ImageNet-1k images, 320 epochs). The paper also demonstrates that simply increasing AdamW's β₁ beyond 0.9 (even with a scheduler) does not replicate AdEMAMix's gains, confirming the need for the dual-EMA design.

## Relation to Prior Work

AdEMAMix is a direct modification of AdamW, adding a second (slow) EMA term to the first-moment update. Conceptually, it departs from the standard single-EMA momentum paradigm by showing that a linear mixture of a fast EMA (β₁≈0.9) and a very slow EMA (β₃≈0.9999) outperforms simply increasing β₁, which the authors verify empirically fails. This distinguishes it from nested-EMA approaches like DEMA/AdMeta (which reduce recent-gradient influence) and from AggMo (Lucas et al., 2019), which applies multiple momentum terms to plain GD rather than Adam and lacks the schedulers critical at scale. The paper also draws a connection to distributed-optimization methods (DiLoCo, SlowMo) whose outer momentum effectively increases the influence of old gradients.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that gradients remain useful for tens of thousands of steps (t_half ≈ 6,930 for β₃=0.9999) challenges the prevailing assumption that gradients are short-lived local approximations, and the 95% token savings on a 1.3B LLM is a substantial practical efficiency gain. The novel link between momentum magnitude and forgetting rate opens a new angle on understanding generalization. Limitations include a memory overhead (one extra parameter-sized buffer), reduced effectiveness in low-iteration or fast-distribution-shift settings, and the fact that the method is best suited to long training runs. Open questions the authors raise include what the persistence of gradient relevance reveals about loss-landscape consistency, whether the variance reduction could harm generalization, and whether non-EMA weighting functions could further improve past-gradient utilization.
