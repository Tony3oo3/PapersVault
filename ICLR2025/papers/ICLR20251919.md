## Problem

Speech foundation models like Whisper/OWSM are large and computationally expensive, and existing pruning methods produce a single fixed sparse structure optimized during training for a specific task. The paper asks whether the optimal pruned subnetwork actually varies with context—language, task, and input characteristics—and whether a model can adapt its active modules dynamically at inference time without retraining per context.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251919/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The gate predictor receives time-averaged speech features (pooled over the T-frame dimension) concatenated with one-hot language and task tokens (e.g., [0,1,0,0,1] for French ASR), producing a binary mask per module per layer in both the E-Branchformer encoder (FFN1, glob-ATT, cgMLP, FFN2) and Transformer decoder (self-ATT, src-ATT, FFN). The pruned model then performs standard autoregressive beam search (beam size 5) to emit transcribed or translated text, with the mask recomputed per utterance so that different inputs can activate different module subsets.

## Method

The approach fine-tunes OWSM-v3.1 (E-Branchformer encoder + Transformer decoder) with a context-aware GlobalGP that maps pooled speech features plus language/task tokens to per-module gate probabilities via a two-layer MLP. Gates are binarized with the Straight-through Gumbel-Softmax Estimator (SGSE), which enforces strictly 0/1 values in the forward pass while backpropagating through continuous Gumbel-Softmax values, eliminating the train/inference mismatch of prior sigmoid or plain Gumbel-Softmax pruning. A sparsity loss combining an absolute-value and quadratic penalty on the mean gate probability drives the model toward a target sparsity ratio s_target that is annealed during training, with α scaled up when the achieved sparsity lags the target. The overall loss is the downstream task loss (CTC/attention for ASR, cross-entropy for ST) plus the sparsity term, and the model is trained jointly across multiple languages and tasks so that a single set of gate predictors learns context-dependent pruning. The key empirical finding is that decoder modules (especially FFN and self-ATT) can be pruned far more aggressively than encoder modules (where cgMLP is critical), and that ST retains more decoder FFN/self-ATT than ASR, reflecting the non-monotonic input-output mapping in translation.

## Results

The proposed context-aware dynamic pruning achieves a 34.3% reduction in inference time for French-to-German ST (10.54s → 6.92s) with no BLEU degradation (12.2 → 12.2) at 50% decoder sparsity, and a 28.6% reduction for German ASR with only 2.8% WER increase. Decoder pruning is substantially more effective than encoder pruning: at 90% sparsity, decoder-only pruning yields 24.5 WER (ASR) and 5.3 BLEU (ST), while encoder-only pruning collapses to 80.8 WER and 2.5 BLEU. Joint encoder-decoder pruning at 50% sparsity achieves 17.8 WER (ASR) and 9.4 BLEU (ST) with 5.99s and 7.09s inference time respectively, reducing FLOPs from 3781 to 1698 (ASR) and 3409 to 2023 (ST).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external pruning methods in its main results; instead, it compares three internal pruning configurations (encoder-only, decoder-only, joint) against the unpruned OWSM-v3.1 baseline. Decoder pruning consistently outperforms encoder pruning: at 50% sparsity, decoder-only ST retains the full 12.2 BLEU while encoder-only ST also holds at 12.3 BLEU, but at 70% sparsity the gap widens dramatically (decoder 10.3 vs. encoder 9.2 BLEU for ST; decoder 17.2 vs. encoder 21.0 WER for ASR). In the joint ASR+ST training setting, decoder-only pruning maintains competitive performance (15.4 WER at 30% sparsity) compared to task-specific training, suggesting a single pruned model can serve multiple tasks. In the appendix, module-level pruning is shown to outperform layer-level pruning at high sparsity (90%: 26.4 vs. 99.9 WER for decoder; 47.3 vs. 108.6 WER for encoder), and structured module pruning preserves accuracy better than unstructured magnitude-based pruning at equivalent sparsity levels.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the dynamic layer-skipping framework of I3D (Peng et al., 2023b) from single-task, single-language Transformer encoders to module-level pruning of a multilingual, multi-task speech foundation model (OWSM-v3.1 with E-Branchformer). It departs from I3D in three key ways: (1) pruning at the module granularity (self-ATT, src-ATT, FFN, cgMLP, glob-ATT) rather than the layer level, (2) conditioning the gate predictor on explicit language and task tokens alongside speech features, and (3) adopting the Straight-through Gumbel-Softmax Estimator to eliminate the train/inference discrepancy that plagued continuous-valued gates. The sparsity loss formulation draws on Wang et al. (2020) with a non-negativity fix, and the overall L0-regularization framing follows Louizos et al. (2018).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is a ~30% inference speedup with minimal accuracy loss, achieved by pruning the decoder rather than the encoder—a finding that aligns with and reinforces prior evidence that ASR benefits less from deep decoders than ST does. The interpretability analysis (task-specific module activation patterns, the Conformer-like flow emerging under joint pruning, and the altered decoder processing order in multi-task settings) offers actionable architectural insights for speech foundation model design. However, the 'context-aware' framing is somewhat undercut by the authors' own finding of no significant module-selection differences across languages, and the evaluation is limited to three languages with ~20 hours each. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger model families (e.g., full Whisper-large), whether per-utterance adaptation (beyond per-task conditioning) yields further gains, and how dynamic module skipping maps to real hardware accelerators where conditional execution has non-trivial overhead.
