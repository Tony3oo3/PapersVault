## Problem

Adversarial imitation learning from observation (e.g., AMP, GAIL) relies on GAN-style min-max optimization to learn reward functions, which suffers from unstable training dynamics, non-smooth and non-stationary reward signals, and sensitivity to hyperparameters. The discriminator's decision boundary shifts as the policy updates, and it is arbitrarily defined outside the supports of the expert and policy distributions, leading to misleading gradients and convergence to local minima. This paper asks whether a non-adversarial, energy-based generative framework can learn smooth, well-defined, and stationary reward functions for imitation from observation.

## Input / Output

The method takes expert motion trajectories as state-only transitions (s, s') — a 105-dimensional vector encoding relative link positions and rotations in the agent's local frame — with no action labels, making it an Imitation from Observation problem. It produces a parameterized energy function e_θ(x', σ) that serves as a scalar reward (higher energy = closer to expert distribution) and a Gaussian policy π_θ_G(s) over joint positional targets tracked by PD controllers. The energy function is conditioned on a noise level σ drawn from a geometric sequence (σ_1=20 to σ_L=0.01, L=50 levels), yielding a family of dilated reward landscapes that are annealed during RL.

## Method

NEAR modifies Noise-Conditioned Score Networks (NCSN) to explicitly learn energy functions rather than score functions: a neural network e_θ(x', σ) is trained via denoising score matching on expert state transitions perturbed with Gaussian noise at multiple σ levels, so that the learned energy is smooth, continuous, and well-defined across the full sample space. Training is two-phase — the energy network is fully pretrained before RL begins, eliminating the non-stationarity inherent in simultaneous adversarial training. During RL (PPO with 4096 parallel IsaacGym environments), the agent maximizes the energy of its state transitions as reward, and an annealing schedule gradually shifts the noise level from σ_1 (broad, informative for a random policy) toward σ_L (tight, closely matching p_D) based on the agent's rolling average return, with a rollback mechanism if performance degrades. A tanh-based reward transformation normalizes the energy signal relative to recent returns to smooth transitions between noise levels. The key distinction from adversarial IL is that the reward is a fixed, policy-independent function of the expert distribution, providing stable and unambiguous improvement gradients throughout training.

## Results

On six humanoid imitation tasks (walking, running, left punch, crane pose, mummy walk, spin kick), NEAR and AMP achieve broadly comparable average DTW pose errors (e.g., 0.51 m for both on walking; 0.62 vs 0.65 m on running). NEAR is closer to the expert in spectral arc length on most multi-clip tasks (e.g., −7.52 vs −8.78 on walking, where the expert is −5.4), while AMP achieves lower pose error on most tasks. In goal-conditioned composed-reward settings (Table 2), NEAR outperforms AMP on both pose error and task return for target-reaching (walking: 0.94 vs 1.09 m; running: 1.18 vs 1.77 m) and is the only method to successfully complete the combined target-reaching-and-punching task (return 3.85 ± 0.76). NEAR degrades noticeably in single-clip settings (mummy walk: 0.66 m vs AMP's 0.41 m; spin kick: 0.78 m vs 0.58 m).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole external baseline is AMP (Peng et al., 2021), the state-of-the-art adversarial imitation learning method for state-only motion data. On multi-clip tasks (walking, running, left punch, crane pose), NEAR and AMP are roughly tied on pose error, with AMP holding a slight edge (e.g., 0.32 vs 0.37 m on left punch; 0.82 vs 0.94 m on crane pose). However, NEAR is consistently closer to the expert in spectral arc length on these tasks, indicating smoother, more natural periodic motions (e.g., −7.52 vs −8.78 on walking, where the expert is −5.4). NEAR clearly wins in goal-conditioned composed-reward settings: it achieves lower pose error and higher task return on target-reaching (walking: 0.94 m / 2.75 return vs AMP's 1.09 m / 2.23; running: 1.18 m / 1.74 vs 1.77 m / −0.15) and is the only method to successfully complete the combined target-reaching-and-punching task. Conversely, AMP substantially outperforms NEAR in data-limited single-clip settings (mummy walk: 0.41 vs 0.66 m; spin kick: 0.58 vs 0.78 m), which the authors attribute to AMP's discriminator acting as a classifier rather than explicitly modelling the expert distribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Noise-conditioned Score Network (NCSN) framework from score-based generative modeling (Song & Ermon, 2019; 2020) for a fundamentally different purpose: instead of generating samples, it learns smooth energy functions of the expert motion distribution to serve as stationary reward signals for reinforcement learning. It departs from the dominant adversarial imitation learning paradigm (GAIL, AMP, GAIfO) by eliminating the min-max discriminator training entirely, replacing it with a pre-trained, policy-independent energy function learned via denoising score matching (Vincent, 2011). The annealing strategy for gradually switching between noise-level-conditioned energy functions draws on annealed Langevin dynamics (Song & Ermon, 2019; Kirkpatrick et al., 1983) but is repurposed to manage reward informativeness during RL rather than sample generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NEAR provides a principled, non-adversarial alternative to AIL that addresses well-documented pathologies of GAN-based reward learning (non-smooth rewards, non-stationarity, perfect-discriminator collapse) while achieving comparable imitation quality to AMP on complex humanoid tasks. However, the method remains sensitive to the noise scale hyperparameter, performs poorly in data-limited (single-clip) settings where AMP's classifier-based approach is more robust, and the annealing strategy introduces its own non-stationarity at higher noise levels. Open questions include whether dynamic or softer annealing criteria, alternative noise distributions, or hybrid reward blending could eliminate the remaining instability, and whether the energy-as-reward paradigm generalizes beyond physics-based humanoid control to other IfO domains.
