## Problem

The prevalent view in the GNN literature attributes spectral-domain behavior almost entirely to the neighborhood aggregation mechanism acting as a graph signal filter, while neglecting the role of non-linear layers and other modules. No existing metric or benchmark measures a GNN's ability to flexibly capture and alter frequency components in its output, and no systematic comparison of popular GNNs from a consistent spectral perspective has been conducted. This paper asks: when the aggregation filter is fixed, can GNNs still produce outputs with diverse frequency components, and how do different architectures compare in this capability?

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes real-world graphs (Cora, DBLP, Coauthor-CS/Physics, Amazon-Computers/Photo) with their original node feature matrices and adjacency structures, and evaluates 14 GNN architectures. For each frequency bin of the normalized Laplacian spectrum, node classification labels are generated by discretizing the binned eigenvector values into discrete classes, while the GNN's input features remain the original dataset attributes (e.g., BOW text for Cora). The output is a per-bin accuracy curve across the full spectral axis, summarized by a Normalized AUAC score that quantifies a GNN's overall capability to leverage information at each frequency range.

## Method

The benchmark protocol computes normalized Laplacian eigenvectors, bins them by eigenvalue into uniform-width frequency bins, and for each bin generates node-level classification targets by discretizing the binned eigenvector entries into class labels. Each GNN is then trained via supervised cross-entropy loss to predict these frequency-specific targets from the original node features, and the resulting per-bin accuracies form an accuracy curve whose area (normalized by the maximum possible) yields the Normalized AUAC score. A key theoretical contribution proves that the discretization step (converting continuous regression targets to discrete classification labels) introduces only an upper-bounded energy distribution deviation, via showing the energy distribution function is Lipschitz on the unit sphere and that the inverse image of any energy distribution field has small angular variation (Theorems 4.2–4.4). An exploratory study further demonstrates that GNNs like GCN and FA can recover frequency components entirely absent from the input signal when the supervision signal provides frequency-specific incentives, directly challenging the assumption that the aggregation filter dominates spectral behavior.

## Results

Under the proposed Normalized AUAC metric across six real-world datasets, SAGE achieves the best average ranking (3.17±0.37), followed by GCNII (4.50±4.03) and GATv2 (4.67±1.49), while APPNP ranks worst (13.67±0.75). Normalized AUAC scores range from approximately 52% (APPNP on CS) to 69% (GCNII on Photo). All 14 GNNs exhibit V-shaped accuracy curves in the spectral domain, indicating that capturing middle-frequency information is the most challenging task for every model tested, revealing an inherent limitation of neighborhood aggregation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark challenges the traditional assumption that spectral-based GNNs with well-defined frequency response functions dominate in the spectral domain. SAGE (spatial, no explicit filter) achieves the best average ranking (3.17), outperforming spectral designs like APPNP (13.67) and FA (12.00) by a wide margin. GCNII and GATv2, also spatial designs rarely analyzed from a spectral perspective, rank 2nd and 3rd respectively, with GCNII reaching 69.03% on Photo and 66.55% on Physics. In the middle frequency range, GATv2 and GCN show clear superiority over all other models, a finding invisible to frequency-response-function-based analyses. For low frequencies, SAGE and ChebNet lead; for high frequencies, GPS and Transformer excel. The case study on five additional datasets (Airport-Brazil, Wisconsin, Cornell, Squirrel, Chameleon) confirms that the benchmark's frequency-range-specific rankings approximate actual node-classification rankings more closely than random or original-task rankings (measured by Kendall's τ distance).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant spectral GNN analysis tradition (Nt & Maehara 2019; Chang et al. 2021) that treats neighborhood aggregation as the sole determinant of a GNN's frequency response, and from the subsequent line of work designing more flexible filters (Bo et al. 2021; Guo et al. 2023; Bianchi et al. 2021) that implicitly assumes filters dominate spectral behavior. It builds on recent preliminary evidence that non-linear layers can shift output frequency components (Balcilar et al. 2021b; Yang et al. 2024) but elevates this observation into a formal research problem with a novel evaluation protocol (Normalized AUAC) and theoretical guarantees. Unlike existing GNN benchmarks (Dwivedi et al. 2023; Wu et al. 2022b) that measure accuracy, scalability, or efficiency, this work introduces the first comprehensive spectral-domain benchmark applicable to both spatial- and spectral-based GNNs under a unified framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that GNNs can recover frequency components absent from the input, this work challenges the foundational assumption that motivates much of the spectral GNN design literature, suggesting that filter-centric analysis may be insufficient for understanding GNN strengths and weaknesses. The practical case study showing that spectral benchmark rankings approximate real-world GNN performance on unseen datasets offers a concrete tool for practitioners to select architectures before running experiments. Open questions include whether the synthetic eigenvector-derived labels fully capture the spectral structure of real task-relevant information, how findings extend to heterophilous or dynamic graphs, and whether the identified middle-frequency weakness can be addressed through targeted architectural design.
