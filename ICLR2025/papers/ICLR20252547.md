## Problem

Prior encoding-based jailbreaks (Morse code, ROT13, Caesar cipher, etc.) rely on a fixed, hand-crafted set of encodings that a model must already know, making them non-scale-adaptive and producing discontinuous gaps in attack efficacy across model capabilities. The paper asks whether in-context learning can be harnessed to teach models arbitrary, randomly-generated encodings on the fly, and whether the resulting attack efficacy scales with model capability — revealing whether more capable frontier models face emergent safety vulnerabilities.

## Input / Output

The method takes a harmful intent string and a randomly-sampled bijective mapping from the English alphabet to a codomain (permuted letters or ℓ-digit number sequences), parameterized by dispersion d (number of letters not mapping to themselves, 0–26) and encoding length ℓ. It produces a multi-turn conversation prompt (~25,000–50,000 input tokens) containing a system instruction, k_t teaching turns with English-to-bijection translations, k_p practice turns, and the encoded harmful query as the final user message. The model's encoded response is decoded via the inverse mapping and denoised by GPT-4o-mini to correct minor spelling errors, yielding a plain-text response that is then judged for harmfulness.

## Method

Bijection learning constructs a jailbreak prompt that teaches a target model an arbitrary bijective string-to-string encoding entirely through in-context examples, requiring no fine-tuning or white-box access. The prompt template includes a system message specifying the full letter-to-codomain mapping, multiple user-assistant turns demonstrating English-to-bijection translations (drawn from Paul Graham essays), and the harmful intent encoded in bijection language as the final user turn. Because the space of random bijections is near-endless, the attack operates as a prompt-fuzzing procedure: for a given intent, n random mappings are sampled and the attack succeeds if any one elicits a harmful response (best-of-n). Two quantitative hyperparameters — dispersion (how many letters are remapped) and encoding length (digits per codomain symbol) — smoothly control encoding difficulty, enabling scale-adaptive targeting where weaker models are attacked with low-dispersion letter permutations and stronger models with high-dispersion digit mappings. The paper demonstrates a Pareto frontier: each model family has an optimal complexity sweet spot, and peak ASR increases monotonically with model capability, supporting a computational-overload hypothesis in which the translation task degrades the model's safety-classification capacity.

## Results

Bijection learning achieves state-of-the-art Attack Success Rates (ASR) across all five frontier models tested. On the full HarmBench test set (320 intents), it reaches 86.3% ASR on Claude 3.5 Sonnet, 82.1% on Claude 3 Haiku, 78.1% on Claude 3 Opus, 64.1% on GPT-4o-mini, and 59.1% on GPT-4o. On AdvBench-50, ASRs range from 66% (GPT-4o) to 94% (Claude 3 Opus and Claude 3.5 Sonnet). The attack outperforms the ensemble of 11 prior encoding-based attacks by at least 30 percentage points on every model-dataset combination.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Bijection learning dominates all four baseline categories on every model and both benchmarks. Against the 11-encoding ensemble (ASCII, Base64, leetspeak, ROT13, Morse, Caesar, Self-Cipher, keyboard, upside-down, word reversal, grid ciphers), it gains 42.4 points on Claude 3.5 Sonnet (86.3% vs. 20.9%) and 48.8 points on Claude 3 Haiku (82.1% vs. 39.7%) on HarmBench. Against persuasion-based attacks, PAIR (Chao et al., 2023) and TAP (Mehrotra et al., 2023), the gap is even wider: on Claude 3.5 Sonnet, bijection learning achieves 86.3% versus 9.7% (PAIR) and 10.3% (TAP). The white-box GCG transfer attack (Zou et al., 2023) is nearly ineffective on Claude models (≤1.9% ASR) and trails bijection learning by 51 points on GPT-4o-mini (13.1% vs. 64.1%). Notably, TAP uses a larger fixed attack budget (58) than bijection learning's maximum (47), yet still underperforms by 24–76 points across models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate threads in the jailbreaking literature: in-context learning of complex behaviors (Wei et al., 2024; Anil et al., 2024) and encoding-based obfuscation of harmful prompts (Wei et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2024; Handa et al., 2024). Unlike prior encoding attacks that rely on a model's pre-existing knowledge of a fixed cipher (Morse code, ROT13, Caesar), bijection learning teaches an arbitrary random encoding in-context, opening access to an effectively infinite pool of encodings. It also departs from optimization-based attacks (GCG) by being fully black-box, and from persuasion-based attacks (PAIR, TAP) by being universal via a single template, while adding tunable complexity parameters (dispersion, encoding length) that make the attack scale-adaptive in a way no prior encoding attack achieves.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that more capable models are more severely jailbroken by bijection attacks—challenges the prevailing assumption that scaling improves safety and suggests a new class of emergent vulnerabilities tied to advanced reasoning. The computational overload hypothesis (translation degrades safety classification) offers a mechanistic explanation distinct from the mismatched-generalization account in prior work. Open questions include whether this scale-adaptive vulnerability generalizes beyond bijection encodings to other reasoning-intensive obfuscations, whether the attack transfers to open-source models with smaller context windows, and whether capability-parity guard models can ever fully mitigate such attacks given the tripling of inference cost for only marginal ASR reduction.
