## Problem

Existing two-sample test (2ST) based MGT detectors assume that human-written texts must follow the distribution of previously seen HWTs, leading to high false positive rates when HWTs deviate from that distribution. This non-overlapping assumption is particularly problematic in sensitive applications like academic integrity verification, where mislabeling a human-written text as machine-generated can cause unjust accusations. The paper addresses this by replacing the 2ST with a non-parametric kernel relative test that does not require the tested text to belong exclusively to either the HWT or MGT distribution.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text to be tested (processed in chunks of 512 or 256 tokens), a reference set of HWT samples, and a reference set of MGT samples, all converted to 768-dimensional token-level representations via a fixed RoBERTa-based GPT-2 detector feature extractor (max 100 tokens per sentence). The output is a binary MGT/HWT classification along with a p-value decision score (1 − p-value), where a score near 1 indicates high confidence of machine generation and near 0 indicates human authorship. The significance threshold α (default 0.05) controls the decision boundary, and the method operates in a zero-shot setting at inference time with no per-test training.

## Method

R-Detect reframes MGT detection as a relative test: it tests the null hypothesis that MMD(P, Z; k) ≤ MMD(Q, Z; k), where P and Q are the HWT and MGT reference distributions and Z is the text to be tested, rejecting the null (classifying as MGT) when the p-value falls below α. The key technical contribution is a kernel optimisation strategy that leverages the empirical finding that deep kernels maximising 2ST test power also perform well in the relative test. Specifically, a deep kernel k_ω(x,y) = [(1−ε)κ(φ_f(x), φ_f(y)) + ε]·q(f(x), f(y)) is trained by maximising the multi-population-aware MMD statistic (M/sqrt(σ²)) using Adam optimisation on historical HWT/MGT pairs, with the feature extractor g held fixed. This optimised kernel is then plugged into the relative test at inference, requiring no further training. The approach achieves 0% FPR and 100% TPR on HC3 and TQA benchmarks while being roughly 6× faster than the parametric Bino detector.

## Results

On the HC3→HC3 (seen distribution) setting, R-Detect achieves tpMGT=1.00, tpHWT=0.00 (0% FPR), and AUROC=1.00, outperforming MPP-R (AUROC 0.99, tpHWT 0.01). On the more challenging HC3→TQA unseen-distribution setting, R-Detect maintains AUROC=1.00 and tpHWT=0.00 for both ChatGPT and GPT-4 generated texts, while MPP-R degrades to AUROC 0.52 (ChatGPT) and 0.50 (GPT-4). Against the parametric baseline Bino, R-Detect matches AUROC=1.00 while reducing runtime from ~1064s to ~180s and avoiding Bino's 100% FPR at its default threshold. On the RAID and DetectRL benchmarks (multi-LLM, adversarial attacks), R-Detect achieves AUROC 0.9996–1.0000 (token 512) and 0.9964–1.0000 (token 256), ranking first or near-first across all settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MPP (Zhang et al., 2024), R-Detect's key advantage is false-positive control: on unseen HWT distributions (HC3→TQA), MPP-R collapses to AUROC 0.52 (ChatGPT) and 0.50 (GPT-4) with tpHWT near 1.0, while R-Detect maintains AUROC 1.00 and tpHWT 0.00; on the seen-distribution HC3→HC3 setting, both achieve tpMGT=1.00 but R-Detect has 0% FPR versus MPP-R's 1%. Against Bino (Hans et al., 2024), R-Detect matches AUROC=1.00 on all three settings but avoids Bino's 100% FPR at its default 0.5 threshold (Bino-FPR fixes this but is threshold-sensitive), and runs ~6x faster (180s vs 1064s). On the RAID and DetectRL benchmarks, R-Detect outperforms DetectGPT (AUROC 0.45–0.66), DNA-GPT (0.21–0.91), and Fast-DetectGPT (0.39–1.00) on most adversarial-attack settings, and is competitive with or slightly ahead of Radar, DALD, and Text-Fluoroscopy (e.g., R-Detect 0.9996 vs Text-Fluoroscopy 0.9780 on RAID mix1, token 256). The one setting where a baseline ties or edges R-Detect is DetectRL p-att1/p-att2 (token 256), where Text-Fluoroscopy reaches 1.0000 versus R-Detect's 0.9952/1.0000.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on MPP (Zhang et al., 2024), which frames MGT detection as a kernel two-sample test (2ST) using MMD, and on the deep kernel optimisation framework of Liu et al. (2020) for maximising 2ST power. The key departure is replacing the 2ST with a non-parametric relative test (Bounliphone et al., 2016), which asks whether the test text is closer to the HWT or MGT reference distribution rather than whether it comes from the same distribution as a single reference. The authors empirically justify transferring the deep kernel optimisation from the 2ST setting to the relative test by showing that kernels performing well in 2ST also perform well in relative tests, then apply the same multi-population-aware optimisation objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The false positive problem in 2ST-based MGT detection is a critical practical barrier for deployment in sensitive settings like academic integrity verification, where mislabeling a human-written text as machine-generated carries serious consequences. The relative test formulation elegantly removes the non-overlapping distribution assumption that causes these false positives, and the empirical results (0% FPR while maintaining 100% TPR on English benchmarks) validate the theoretical motivation. However, the method still requires reference samples from both HWT and MGT distributions, and performance degrades substantially in non-English zero-shot settings (AUROC ~0.3–0.6), leaving open the question of how well the approach generalises to truly novel domains or languages without retraining the kernel. The reliance on a fixed RoBERTa feature extractor also limits the method's ceiling compared to approaches that can leverage more powerful encoders.
