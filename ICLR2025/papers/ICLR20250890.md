## Problem

Existing multi-agent debate (MAD) frameworks assign the same reasoning method to all agents, causing a 'mental set' where homogeneous thinking patterns persist across agents and rounds, leading to consistently wrong answers on problems that a different strategy could solve. Self-reflection similarly fails because models lack external feedback to correct their own fixed reasoning. The paper asks whether forcing agents to reason with genuinely distinct prompting strategies can break this mental set and improve collective reasoning accuracy.

## Input / Output

The method accepts a text question (for LLMs) or an image paired with a text question (for MLLMs) and produces a single final answer. The input is simultaneously fed to n parallel agents, each constrained to a different prompting strategy (e.g., CoT, SBP, PoT for LLMs; IO, CCoT, DDCoT for MLLMs). After N debate rounds, a judge function—either Self-Consistency (majority vote) for closed-form answers or a model-based selector for open-ended responses—extracts the final answer from the n agents' outputs.

## Method

DMAD assigns n distinct prompting reasoning methods to n instances of the same model, so each agent is structurally forced to think differently. In each round, every agent generates a full solving process and answer under its assigned strategy, then all agents' solving processes and answers are broadcast to every other agent's message history. In subsequent rounds, agents review the diverse solutions, extract useful insights from other agents' reasoning, and update their own answers while remaining bound to their original strategy. The process iterates for N rounds, after which a judge selects the final answer. The key mechanism is that the solving processes (not just final answers) are shared, enabling agents to identify and adopt specific intermediate steps from other strategies—e.g., adding a '30% discount' to a scene graph that one agent missed—thereby breaking the mental set that a single fixed strategy would impose.

## Results

On MATH with GPT-4o-mini, DMAD achieves 71.14% average accuracy, outperforming the best MAD baseline (MAD All CoT at 70.57%) and all prompting/self-reflection methods. On GPQA, DMAD reaches 41.9% (GPT-4o-mini) and 41.1% (LLaMA-3-70B), the best among all methods. On MM-Vet, DMAD improves GPT-4o by 7.3% and Gemini-1.5-Flash by 8.2% over the IO baseline, compared to only 5.5% and 5.3% for MAD. Notably, DMAD in just 2 rounds surpasses MAD in 5 rounds on multiple benchmarks (e.g., Gemini on ScienceQA and MM-Vet).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DMAD consistently outperforms all fixed-reasoning MAD variants (MAD All CoT, All SBP, All PoT, All IO, All CCoT, All DDCoT) on the majority of benchmarks; on MATH with GPT-4o-mini, DMAD (71.14%) beats MAD All CoT (70.57%) and MAD All PoT (67.14%), while on MM-Vet DMAD (79.3% GPT-4o, 71.4% Gemini) surpasses the best MAD variant (78.3% and 70.5% respectively). DMAD dramatically outperforms MAD-persona in both discriminative and extractive modes, which often score below even basic CoT (e.g., MAD-persona-D drops to 60.44% on ScienceQA vs. 72.78% for DMAD on LLaVA). Self-Refine performs catastrophically on MLLMs (45.71% on ScienceQA, 26.8% on MM-Vet for LLaVA; 47.99% and 38.1% for Gemini), far below DMAD, confirming that self-correction without external diverse perspectives is ineffective. DMAD also beats Self-Contrast and MRP on most settings, with the gap being largest on MM-Vet (e.g., 79.3% vs. 72.1% for Self-Contrast on GPT-4o). The one notable exception is LLaMA-3-70B on MATH, where MAD (All PoT) edges out DMAD (46.43% vs. 45.71%) due to PoT's strength on calculation-heavy subjects, though DMAD still outperforms all other non-PoT methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Multi-Agent Debate (MAD) framework of Du et al. (2024) and the persona-based debate of Liang et al. (2024) by identifying a key limitation: existing MAD methods assign the same reasoning strategy to all agents, creating a 'mental set' analogous to the psychological phenomenon of Jersild (1927). DMAD departs from this by assigning distinct prompting reasoning methods (e.g., CoT, SBP, PoT for LLMs; IO, CCoT, DDCoT for MLLMs) to different agents, so that each agent brings a genuinely different cognitive approach to the debate. The paper also positions itself against self-reflection methods (Self-Refine, Self-Contrast, MRP), which prior work has shown to be limited for LLMs, and extends that negative finding to MLLMs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that diverse reasoning methods in debate are more effective than homogeneous ones, and that this advantage is strong enough for DMAD in 2 rounds to surpass MAD in 5 rounds — a useful efficiency result for practitioners who want to reduce inference cost. The finding that self-reflection degrades MLLM performance (sometimes dramatically, e.g., LLaVA dropping from ~70% to ~46% on ScienceQA) is a less-explored negative result that cautions against naively applying LLM self-correction pipelines to vision-language models. Key limitations include dependence on the base model's capability (weaker models like LLaVA-1.6-13B degrade with longer debate contexts) and the inability to surpass the model's inherent ceiling when no reasoning method can solve a problem. Future directions include dynamic per-round method allocation and RL training on DMAD-generated data.
