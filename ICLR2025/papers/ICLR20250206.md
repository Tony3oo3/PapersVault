## Problem

In equivariant graph neural networks for machine learning force fields, the permutation-equivariance requirement forces the Clebsch-Gordan (CG) transform to be applied identically to every neighboring edge, making computation scale linearly with the number of neighbors and severely restricting the design space of the CG transform layer. This dual burden of high computational cost and limited expressiveness has been a fundamental bottleneck for incorporating high-order irreps into practical force field models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250206/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a molecular graph with 3D Cartesian coordinates and atom types as input, where each atom connects to neighbors within a configurable cutoff radius (typically 4–6 Å). For force field prediction, it outputs a scalar molecular energy from which atomic forces are derived as negative gradients with respect to coordinates, ensuring energy conservation. For molecular property prediction (QM9), the output head is adapted per property, with special handling for dipole moment and electronic spatial extent that require center-of-mass computation.

## Method

FreeCG exploits invariance transitivity: by first aggregating edge features into permutation-invariant 'abstract edges' via cross-attention (inherited from ViSNet's transformer architecture), any subsequent CG transform on these abstract edges is automatically permutation-invariant regardless of its internal design, completely freeing the design space. The freed design space is leveraged through three components: (1) Group CG transform with sparse path, which partitions abstract edges into G groups to reduce complexity from O(T²) to O(T²/G) and uses only 4 CG paths (vs. 8 for full SO(3)) while maintaining O(3) equivariance; (2) Abstract edges shuffling, which cyclically permutes edge indices by ⌊1.5T/G⌋ to enable cross-group information exchange; and (3) an Attention enhancer that augments cross-attention scores with the maximum dot product between each real edge and all abstract edges. The model is trained with MSE loss on energy and forces (weighted 0.95/0.05) using AdamW with EMA, and achieves SOTA force prediction on MD17, rMD17, and MD22 with only ~10% additional parameters over the ViSNet baseline.

## Results

On MD17, FreeCG achieves SOTA force prediction MAE across all 7 molecules, with the largest improvement on Aspirin (0.122 kcal/mol/Å vs. 0.155 for ViSNet and 0.145 for QuinNet, a ~15% reduction). On rMD17, FreeCG leads in force prediction for 8 out of 10 molecules (e.g., Aspirin 0.1212 vs. 0.1520 ViSNet; Azobenzene 0.0486 vs. 0.0585 ViSNet). On MD22, FreeCG achieves the best force MAE for 5 out of 7 large molecules, with ~20% improvement on Ac-Ala3-NHMe (0.0531 vs. 0.0830 ViSNet). On QM9, FreeCG attains the best MAE for 7 out of 12 properties, including α (38.2 vs. 41.1 ViSNet), ZPVE (1.10 vs. 1.56), and C_v (20.4 vs. 23.0).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FreeCG consistently outperforms its base model ViSNet on force prediction across all MD17 molecules (e.g., Aspirin 0.122 vs. 0.155, a 21% reduction) and on 8/10 rMD17 molecules, with the exception of Benzene where ViSNet already achieves near-perfect 0.0056. Against the previous SOTA QuinNet, FreeCG wins on 6/7 MD17 molecules and 8/10 rMD17 molecules, with the largest gap on Aspirin (0.122 vs. 0.145) and Salicylic acid (0.070 vs. 0.080). Compared to other CG-transform-based methods (NequIP, MACE, Allegro), FreeCG achieves substantially lower force MAE on MD17 (e.g., Aspirin 0.122 vs. 0.184 NequIP, 0.1522 MACE, 0.1684 Allegro on rMD17) while using fewer parameters (10.4M vs. 43.8M for full-path CG) and faster inference (3.62 vs. slower iterations/s). On QM9, FreeCG beats EquiformerV2 on 7/12 properties (e.g., α: 38.2 vs. 50, ZPVE: 1.10 vs. 1.47) but trails on μ (11.4 vs. 10) and ε_HOMO (16.6 vs. 14). On OC20 S2EF, FreeCG outperforms ViSNet across all four validation splits (e.g., OOD Both force: 0.0717 vs. 0.0821 eV/Å; energy: 0.8938 vs. 1.3175 eV), demonstrating strong domain generalization to catalytic surfaces.

## Relation to Prior Work

FreeCG builds directly on ViSNet's transformer-based equivariant message-passing architecture, repurposing its cross-attention mechanism to generate permutation-invariant 'abstract edges' that serve as the substrate for CG transform. It departs fundamentally from the NequIP/MACE/Allegro line of work, where CG transform must be applied identically to every neighboring edge to preserve permutation equivariance, by leveraging an invariance transitivity argument to show that CG transform on pre-aggregated invariant features is free of this constraint. The group CG transform draws on group convolution (Krizhevsky et al., 2012), the shuffling strategy on ShuffleNet (Zhang et al., 2018), and the sparse path design is a novel efficiency trick that achieves O(3) equivariance with fewer paths than standard SO(3) formulations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The invariance transitivity argument provides a general, reusable principle for designing equivariant layers: aggregate into invariant features first, then apply arbitrary equivariant operations on them. This opens a previously constrained design space for CG transform in equivariant GNNs, and the demonstrated transferability to QuinNet suggests it is a paradigm rather than a one-off architecture. Limitations include that energy prediction on rMD17 does not reach SOTA, the method is tightly coupled to ViSNet's transformer backbone, and scaling to very large periodic systems beyond the tested water/LiPS cells remains unexplored. Open questions include whether the paradigm extends to non-transformer equivariant architectures and whether the number of abstract edges (currently matched to latent dimension) can be further optimized.
