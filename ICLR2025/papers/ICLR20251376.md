## Problem

Existing MARL agents for Hanabi are locked to a single game setting (e.g., 2-player) and fail to cooperate with unfamiliar partners or transfer strategies to different player counts. No prior agent could play all 2-to-5 player settings concurrently or generalize learned conventions across settings, despite the game rules remaining unchanged. This paper asks whether a single agent architecture can learn robust, generalist strategies that work across all settings and with diverse algorithmic partners.

## Input / Output

The agent consumes a natural-language template describing the full game state (life/clue tokens, arranged cards, visible hands, own-hand knowledge, and prior hints) concatenated with the action-observation history, and produces Q-values via an inner product between a state embedding and an action embedding. Actions are encoded as short keyword-parameter strings (e.g., 'reveal blue 2') rather than one-hot vectors, so the action space grows only in the clue dimension when player count changes. The text encoding is player-agnostic: adding a fifth player appends a hand description to the template rather than restructuring the entire bitstring, keeping the data distribution similar across settings.

## Method

R3D2 replaces the fixed-size vectorial observation and one-hot action space of standard Hanabi RL with a text-based representation processed by a two-layer TinyBERT encoder, followed by an LSTM over the action-observation history and a dueling MLP (value + advantage heads). The Q-value is computed as the inner product Q(s,a) = f(s)ᵀ g(a) (inheriting DRRN's relevance formulation), which naturally handles the dynamic number of legal actions without retraining. Training follows the R2D2 recipe—distributed parallel environments, prioritized experience replay, and self-play—but the multi-setting variant (R3D2-M) runs actors in 2-, 3-, 4-, and 5-player games simultaneously, pooling trajectories into a shared replay buffer with zero-padding to accommodate variable sequence lengths. The key insight is that the textual, player-agnostic representation combined with the inner-product action head lets self-play produce conventions that are robust enough to transfer across settings and to coordinate with structurally different algorithms (IQL, Other-Play, Off-Belief Learning) without any explicit diversity or symmetry-exploitation mechanism.

## Results

In the 2-player Hanabi setting, R3D2 achieves intra-algorithmic cross-play (intra-XP) scores on par with its self-play performance, a first for Hanabi agents trained via self-play, while R2D2 and R2D2-OP both show degraded intra-XP relative to self-play. R3D2 demonstrates significantly superior inter-algorithmic cross-play (inter-XP) compared to all baselines, particularly when paired with R2D2-OBL agents. In multi-player settings (3–5 players), R3D2 variants maintain scores above 15 points across all configurations, while IQL and OP baselines achieve high self-play scores but fail in cross-play. The multi-setting variant R3D2-M outperforms single-setting R3D2-S in inter-XP, suggesting that training across diverse player counts yields more robust coordination strategies.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against R2D2 (IQL via self-play), R3D2 wins decisively on inter-XP: R2D2's performance degrades sharply when paired with different seeds or algorithms, while R3D2 maintains near-self-play levels even in cross-play. Against R2D2-OP (Other-Play), R3D2 also wins on inter-XP; notably, R2D2-OP's intra-XP is surprisingly lower than its self-play score (attributed to inconsistent exploitation of game symmetries), and R3D2-S paired with R2D2-OP outperforms R2D2-OBL paired with R2D2-OP. Against R2D2-OBL (Off-Belief Learning), R3D2 achieves the highest inter-XP scores, and when both are paired with the least flexible R2D2 policy, R3D2-S scores higher than R2D2-OBL, indicating R3D2's greater adaptability. The intermediate baseline R2D2-text (textual observations but fixed action space) confirms that both the text representation and the dynamic action-space architecture are necessary: R2D2-text improves over R2D2 but systematically underperforms R3D2 in cross-play, especially when transferring from larger to smaller action spaces.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on R2D2 (Kapturowski et al., 2019) as its RL training backbone and DRRN (He et al., 2015) for its action-embedding architecture, combining them with a pretrained TinyBERT language model to handle dynamic textual observation and action spaces. It departs from prior Hanabi MARL work (Other-Play, Off-Belief Learning, IQL) by replacing vectorial bitstring/one-hot encodings with natural language representations, arguing that this makes the state and action spaces agnostic to the number of players. The key conceptual departure is that robustness to novel partners and settings can emerge from representation choice alone, without the explicit symmetry exploitation (Other-Play) or hierarchical grounding (OBL) that prior ZSC methods require.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that self-play, typically associated with brittle and non-transferable conventions, can yield robust policies when paired with an appropriate (textual) representation — a finding that reframes how the field thinks about the source of ZSC failures. The 'variable-player learning' formulation (training across 2-to-5 player settings simultaneously) opens a new evaluation axis for MARL robustness that prior work has not addressed. However, the contribution is confined to Hanabi, the cross-setting baselines are limited (R2D2-OP and R2D2-OBL only exist for 2-player), and the approach's applicability to continuous or image-based environments remains untested. Open questions include whether few-shot coordination (adapting after a few episodes with a new partner) can build on this foundation, and whether the text-based representation generalizes beyond card games.
