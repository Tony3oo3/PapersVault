## Problem

Prefix-tuning relies on a reparameterization strategy to stabilize optimization, yet the theoretical justification for why this engineering trick works has remained unexplored. The paper asks: what structural property does reparameterization implicitly impose, and does that property confer measurable statistical benefits in prompt estimation?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251701/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on frozen pretrained transformers (ViT-B/16 for vision, GPT-2 Medium for table-to-text, BART-Large for summarization) and only learns a small set of prefix vectors appended to key and value matrices at each attention layer. For vision, RGB images are encoded into patch embeddings and the output is a class label; for language, input tables or articles (truncated to 512 BPE tokens for XSUM) produce generated text. During training, prefix vectors are generated by a small feedforward network g_θ from a lower-dimensional parameter p′, but at inference only the final prefix vectors are retained, discarding the reparameterization network.

## Method

The paper shows that the reparameterization trick in prefix-tuning implicitly encodes a shared structure: both the prefix key p_i^K and prefix value p_i^V are functions of the same underlying parameter p_i′, modulated by distinct transformations σ₁ and σ₂. By interpreting each attention head as a mixture-of-experts model (building on Le et al., 2024), this shared structure corresponds to parameter sharing between the gating score functions and the expert parameters. The authors then prove convergence rate bounds for least-squares estimation of the prompt mixing measure: without shared structure, rates can degrade to O(1/log n), while with shared structure (both in a simple linear setting and a one-layer neural network setting), rates improve to the parametric O_P(√(log n / n)). Empirically, prefix-tuning with reparameterization (Deep-share) consistently outperforms the no-reparameterization baseline (No-share) across FGVC, VTAB-1K, E2E, WebNLG, and XSUM, with gaps as large as 16.8% on Stanford Cars, confirming the theoretical sample-efficiency advantage.

## Results

On the FGVC benchmark, Deep-share (prefix-tuning with reparameterization) in the DEEP variant achieves 88.28% mean accuracy, nearly matching full fine-tuning (88.54%) while updating only a small subset of parameters. The reparameterization strategy is critical: removing it (No-share DEEP) drops mean FGVC accuracy to 82.32%, a 5.96-point degradation, with the gap reaching 16.8 points on Stanford Cars (79.3% vs. 62.5%). On language tasks, Deep-share outperforms No-share on most metrics, e.g., BLEU 69.9 vs. 68.0 on E2E and ROUGE-1 42.62 vs. 36.86 on XSUM summarization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between prefix-tuning with reparameterization (Deep-share, which induces a shared structure between key and value vectors) and without (No-share, where key and value are independent parameters). Deep-share DEEP achieves 88.28% mean FGVC accuracy, nearly matching full fine-tuning (88.54%) and surpassing it on Stanford Dogs (91.6% vs. 89.4%) and VTAB-1K Structured (52.00% vs. 47.64%). The reparameterization gap is substantial: Deep-share DEEP outperforms No-share DEEP by 5.96 points on FGVC mean accuracy and by up to 16.8 points on individual datasets (Stanford Cars: 79.3% vs. 62.5%). On language tasks, Deep-share consistently beats No-share, with the largest gap on XSUM summarization (ROUGE-1: 42.62 vs. 36.86; ROUGE-L: 34.36 vs. 29.89). The SHALLOW variant (prompts only on the first layer) underperforms DEEP across all configurations, confirming that deep prefix attachment is important. A Simple-share variant (identity reparameterization, analogous to prompt-tuning) also outperforms No-share, supporting the theoretical claim that any shared structure between key and value improves sample efficiency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the prefix-tuning formulation of Li & Liang (2021), whose reparameterization strategy (generating prefix key and value vectors from shared parameters via an MLP) was introduced as an optimization stability trick without theoretical justification. It leverages the recent insight from Le et al. (2024) that self-attention layers can be interpreted as specialized mixture-of-experts architectures, and within that framework, prefix-tuning introduces new experts. The theoretical analysis draws on convergence rate results for mixture model estimation (Ho et al., 2022; Manole & Ho, 2022), adapting Voronoi-based loss functions to the prompt estimation problem. The paper also identifies an analogous shared structure in prompt-tuning (Lester et al., 2021), where the same prompt vector is used for key, query, and value, offering a partial theoretical explanation for its effectiveness.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first rigorous theoretical justification for why reparameterization in prefix-tuning works, showing it is not merely an engineering hack but encodes a shared structure that yields parametric convergence rates O_P(√(log(n)/n)) versus potentially O(1/log(n)) without it. The finding has practical implications: it suggests that the specific form of reparameterization (shared parameters → separate key/value via distinct transformations) is statistically meaningful, not arbitrary. Open questions include whether similar shared-structure benefits extend to other PEFT methods like LoRA, how the MLP-based reparameterization's memory overhead can be reduced, and whether the analysis generalizes to the newly introduced MoE models in prompt-tuning (as opposed to only pre-trained ones). The empirical gains, while consistent across 24 visual and 3 language tasks, are moderate (a few percentage points), suggesting the theoretical advantage may be most pronounced in low-data regimes.
