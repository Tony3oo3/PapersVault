## Problem

Designing effective shaping reward functions for RL requires iteratively training policies under different reward candidates and evaluating them against the task reward, a process that is computationally prohibitive when the reward space is large. Prior approaches (e.g., EUREKA) uniformly allocate training budget across all candidates, wasting compute on poor reward functions. The paper identifies that reward selection is fundamentally an exploration-exploitation problem with non-stationary rewards—each candidate's utility improves as its policy trains—making standard bandit or model selection algorithms inapplicable without modification.

## Input / Output

ORSO takes as input an MDP specification, a fixed RL algorithm (PPO with Isaac Gym hyperparameters), a GPT-4-based reward generator that produces Python reward function code, and an interaction budget B expressed as a multiple of the baseline training iterations (5×, 10×, or 15× n_iters). Generated reward functions are filtered via rejection sampling (instantiating the environment with a random policy and discarding functions that produce NaN/±∞). The output is the single best-performing shaping reward function and its corresponding trained policy, evaluated under the task reward r rather than the shaping reward itself. The method also supports iterative resampling of the reward set (half in-context evolution from the best candidate, half fresh GPT-4 samples) when the current set underperforms.

## Method

ORSO frames reward selection as an online model selection problem where each candidate reward function f^i is a base learner whose internal state (the PPO policy π^i) evolves non-stationarily as it receives more training data. The core selection mechanism is D³RB (Doubling Data-Driven Regret Balancing), which maintains per-learner regret coefficients, average reward estimates, and balancing potentials; at each step it selects the learner with the lowest balancing potential, trains its policy for N iterations, evaluates under the task reward, and performs a misspecification test that doubles the regret coefficient if the learner's realized regret exceeds its current bound. Under Assumption 4.2 (a monotonically dominant optimal learner), the paper proves that D³RB achieves regret comparable to running the best learner in isolation, improving upon the original D³RB guarantee that depends on the worst-case regret coefficient. The two-phase pipeline first generates K reward functions via GPT-4 with rejection sampling, then runs the online selection loop, with periodic resampling (triggered when a reward has been trained for n_iters or when all rewards show excessive regret) to escape poor initial candidate sets.

## Results

ORSO achieves 56% higher normalized task reward than the naive selection strategy (EUREKA-style) at the highest interaction budget (B=15×n_iters), and consistently matches or exceeds human-engineered reward functions across all six continuous control tasks (CartPole, BallBalance, Ant, Humanoid, AllegroHand, ShadowHand). ORSO reaches human-level performance using up to 8× fewer GPUs than the parallelized naive strategy. Among selection algorithms, D³RB performs best on average, followed closely by Exp3, with both substantially outperforming UCB, ETC, and ε-greedy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ORSO is compared against two baselines: (1) Human-engineered reward functions from Isaac Gym (Makoviychuk et al., 2021), and (2) Naive Selection, which samples reward functions and trains each to convergence before selecting the best (the EUREKA-style approach, Ma et al., 2024). Against the Human baseline, ORSO consistently matches or surpasses human-designed rewards, particularly in complex manipulation tasks (AllegroHand, ShadowHand), and clearly surpasses them when the interaction budget B≥10×n_iters. Against the Naive Selection baseline, ORSO achieves 56% higher task reward at the highest budget and reaches equivalent performance with up to 8× fewer GPUs, demonstrating that adaptive allocation of compute to promising reward functions is far more efficient than uniform exploration. The paper also notes that even simpler selection strategies (ε-greedy, ETC) substantially outperform the naive strategy, highlighting the importance of the exploration-exploitation framing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on two distinct research threads: the online model selection literature (particularly D³RB from Dann et al., 2024) for the theoretical selection mechanism, and LLM-based reward generation (EUREKA, Text2Reward) for the reward candidate generation phase. The key conceptual departure is reframing reward function selection as a sequential exploration-exploitation problem with non-stationary 'arms' (since policies improve over time), rather than the static uniform evaluation used by EUREKA's naive selection strategy. The paper also extends the D³RB regret analysis by showing that under a monotonicity assumption on the best learner, the regret bound depends on the true (rather than worst-case) regret coefficient, yielding tighter guarantees than the original D³RB analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: ORSO reduces compute by up to 8× while matching or exceeding human-engineered reward functions, making automated reward design accessible on a single desktop rather than requiring large GPU clusters. The finding that even simple strategies (ε-greedy, explore-then-commit) substantially outperform uniform evaluation suggests the core insight is adaptive resource allocation rather than algorithmic sophistication. Key limitations include reliance on a predefined task reward (challenging for qualitative objectives), testing limited to 6 continuous control tasks in Isaac Gym with PPO, and reward set sizes capped at 96. Open questions include parallelization strategies, scaling to much larger reward sets, and replacing hand-crafted task rewards with learned evaluators (e.g., vision-language models).
