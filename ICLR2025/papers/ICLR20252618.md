## Problem

Although hierarchical compositionality is widely invoked to explain why high-dimensional data is learnable, quantitative measurements establishing these properties and tools to access the underlying latent variables remain scarce. Prior work demonstrated a phase transition in diffusion models on hierarchical data but was limited to images and did not characterize the geometrical structure of the changes occurring during forward-backward experiments. This paper asks: what is the spatial structure of token changes in forward-backward diffusion, and does it reveal the latent hierarchy in real text and image data?

## Input / Output

The method takes a data sample (a 128-token text sequence from WikiText2 or a 256×256 ImageNet image) and an inversion time t, then produces a denoised sample x̂₀(t) along with derived observables: the dynamical correlation function C_ij(t) between token changes, the dynamical susceptibility χ(t), and the associated correlation length ξ(t). For images, the input is tokenized into a 7×7 grid of CLIP ViT-B32 patch embeddings, and the change variable σ_i(t) is redefined as the L2 norm of the embedding difference rather than a discrete spin, making the correlation analysis operate in continuous embedding space rather than on pixel values.

## Method

The core contribution is a mean-field theory of the ε-process on the Random Hierarchy Model (a probabilistic context-free grammar on a regular tree), which predicts that the dynamical correlation length of token changes diverges as a power law ξ ∼ |ε−ε*|^(−ν) at the class-reconstruction phase transition, with critical exponent ν = log s / log F'*. The theory is validated numerically using Belief Propagation for exact Bayes-optimal denoising on the RHM, confirming both the correlation function scaling and the susceptibility peak. The paper then extends the analysis to real data by performing forward-backward experiments with a Masked Diffusion Language Model (MDLM) on WikiText2 and an Improved DDPM on ImageNet, tokenizing images via CLIP patch embeddings. In both modalities, the susceptibility peaks at a finite inversion time (t* ≈ 0.6T for text, t* ≈ 0.6–0.7T for images), and the correlation function exhibits system-spanning power-law decay at the transition, confirming that changes in deep latent variables manifest as correlated blocks of token modifications in the visible data.

## Results

In the Random Hierarchy Model (RHM) ε-process, the dynamical correlation length diverges as ξ ~ |ε − ε*|^{−ν} with ν ≈ 1.78 (for v=32, m=8, s=2, L=9) at the class phase transition ε* ≈ 0.74, and the mean-field theory matches BP numerical experiments with excellent agreement. For masking diffusion on the RHM, the susceptibility peaks at t* ≈ 0.3T. On real data, the MDLM on WikiText-2 shows a correlation length peaking at 7–8 tokens at t* ≈ 0.6T with a corresponding susceptibility peak, while the DDPM on ImageNet (tokenized via CLIP ViT-B/32 patch embeddings) exhibits a system-spanning power-law correlation and susceptibility peak at t* ≈ 0.6–0.7T, confirming the hierarchical phenomenology across both modalities.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing generative or interpretability methods; instead, its central comparison is between theoretical predictions and empirical observations. The mean-field theory of the ε-process (dashed lines in Fig. 2a) reproduces the BP numerical results (solid lines) for both the correlation function and susceptibility with excellent quantitative agreement, including the critical exponent ν. A key negative control is the Gaussian random field model (App. B), which also exhibits spatial correlations but shows a monotonically increasing correlation length and susceptibility that peaks only at t = T, in sharp contrast to the finite-time peak observed in hierarchical data. For real data, the RHM's predicted phenomenology (peaking correlation length and susceptibility at a finite inversion time) is qualitatively confirmed in both language (MDLM on WikiText-2) and vision (DDPM on ImageNet), with the critical inversion times t* ≈ 0.6T (language) and t* ≈ 0.6–0.7T (vision) consistent with the class phase transition. The paper also verifies that BP sampling and backward diffusion with the exact score function yield identical correlation and susceptibility statistics (Fig. 8), ruling out sampling artifacts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work by Sclocchi et al. (2025) and Cagnetta et al. (2024), which established that the Random Hierarchy Model (RHM) exhibits a phase transition in class reconstruction under diffusion and that Belief Propagation provides exact Bayes-optimal denoising on the RHM tree. The key departure is the introduction of dynamical susceptibility and correlation length as quantitative probes of hierarchical structure, with a mean-field theoretical prediction of power-law divergence at the transition. The paper also builds on the forward-backward diffusion protocol from DDPMs (Ho et al., 2020) and masked diffusion (Austin et al., 2021), but repurposes it not for generation quality assessment but as a diagnostic tool for latent structure, validated on MDLMs (Sahoo et al., 2024) and vision DDPMs with CLIP tokenization (Radford et al., 2021).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first quantitative, modality-agnostic evidence that hierarchical latent structure is a universal property of natural data, with the peaking susceptibility serving as a measurable signature. The negative control (Gaussian random fields showing monotonic rather than peaked correlation length) cleanly isolates hierarchy as the necessary ingredient, strengthening the claim. Open questions include whether the framework extends to context-dependent grammars beyond the tree-structured RHM, whether the critical exponent is truly universal across architectures, and how these measurements could inform training strategies (e.g., avoiding mode collapse during fine-tuning). The practical utility as a diagnostic tool for interpretability is promising but not yet demonstrated at scale.
