## Problem

Integrating group max-min fairness (MMF) constraints into recommender system training violates the sample independence assumption, making the loss function non-linearly additive across mini-batches. This nonlinearity introduces a Jensen gap between the model's convergence point and the true optimum, which widens as batch size decreases and group count increases. Existing remedies—heuristic re-weighting or debiasing SGD—either lack theoretical guarantees or require convex optimization impractical for industrial-scale systems serving millions of users across hundreds of groups.

## Input / Output

FairDual consumes user-item interaction tuples (u, i, c_{u,i}), an item-group adjacency matrix A (row-normalized to handle multi-group items), and a pre-trained backbone model (e.g., Llama-2 via LoRA, or traditional architectures like SASRec). It outputs a trained recommendation model plus per-group dual variables μ that encode fairness weights. A key implementation detail is that item embeddings are periodically frozen (every β batches) and a random subset of Q items is sampled to approximate top-K ranking scores, avoiding full corpus ranking at each step.

## Method

FairDual reformulates the MMF-constrained objective as a group-weighted loss via Lagrangian duality (Theorem 3), where the dual variable μ_g acts as a shadow price and the per-sample weight is s_g = 1 − μ_g. At each mini-batch, the algorithm performs standard gradient descent on the weighted accuracy loss (s^j)ᵀ l^j, then updates μ via mirror gradient descent with momentum: g^j = α·g̃^j + (1−α)·g^{j−1}, followed by a proximal projection onto the feasible set M. The sub-gradient g̃^j combines the negative group-accumulated ranking scores (estimated from Q randomly sampled items using frozen embeddings) with a remaining-reward vector γ_j tracking per-group loss budgets. Theoretically, FairDual achieves a sub-linear O(B^{−1/2}) Jensen gap bound under random-shuffle mini-batch training, and the gap remains well-controlled even for small batches and large group counts.

## Results

On the MIND dataset under the BigRec (Llama-2 7B + LoRA) backbone, FairDual achieves NDCG@5 of 1.15%, MRR@5 of 0.88%, and MMF@5 of 2.82%, outperforming the best baseline (Prop) by 3.6% in NDCG and 43.2% in MMF. On Amazon-Book, FairDual reaches NDCG@5 of 3.11% and MMF@5 of 8.90%, surpassing the strongest baseline (UNI) by 4.0% and 5.3% respectively. On Amazon-Electronic, FairDual attains NDCG@5 of 5.08% and MMF@5 of 0.31%, with improvements of up to 23.8% in MMF over the best baseline. Across all three datasets and all top-K settings (K=5, 10, 20), FairDual consistently achieves the highest values in both accuracy (NDCG, MRR) and fairness (MMF) metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FairDual outperforms all eight baselines across every dataset, backbone, and top-K setting. On MIND (K=5), the closest competitor is Prop (Hu et al., 2023) with NDCG@5 of 1.11% vs. FairDual's 1.15%, but FairDual leads by a substantial 43.2% in MMF (2.82% vs. 1.97%), indicating Prop sacrifices fairness for accuracy. DRO (Hashimoto et al., 2018) and S-DRO (Wen et al., 2022) both underperform FairDual by 26–28% in NDCG on MIND, as their worst-group-only optimization strategy is too aggressive. IFairLRS (Jiang et al., 2024) achieves the second-best MMF on MIND (2.21%) but lags FairDual by 27.6% in MMF and 32.2% in NDCG, while on Amazon-Electronic it collapses to NDCG@5 of 2.21% (vs. 5.08% for FairDual), suggesting its popularity-based weighting is poorly suited to that domain. Maxmin Sample (Abernethy et al., 2022) achieves competitive MMF on MIND (2.25%) but drops sharply on Amazon-Book (6.80% vs. 8.90%), revealing sensitivity to group size. Among non-LLM baselines (Table 3), FOCF (Yao & Huang, 2017) and Reg (Kamishima & Akaho, 2017) are consistently the weakest, while FairNeg (Chen et al., 2023) is the strongest non-LLM baseline on BPR (NDCG@5 = 0.72%) yet still trails FairDual (0.76%) in both accuracy and MMF (11.84% vs. 6.07%). Convergence time also favors FairDual: it stabilizes in 5 hours vs. 7.1–11.7 hours for the other baselines (Table 6), a 28.5% speedup over the fastest baseline (IFairLRS).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the group max-min fairness (MMF) optimization framework for recommender systems (Patro et al. 2020; Xu et al. 2023; Do et al. 2021) and the broader ML fairness literature on distributionally robust optimization and debiasing SGD (Hashimoto et al. 2018; Demidovich et al. 2023; Agarwal et al. 2018). Its key conceptual departure is a theoretical reformulation showing that the MMF-constrained objective is equivalent to a group-weighted accuracy objective (Theorem 3), which then motivates a mirror gradient descent algorithm (FairDual) inspired by online allocation theory (Balseiro et al. 2021). Unlike prior heuristic re-weighting methods (S-DRO, Prop, IFairLRS) that lack convergence guarantees, or ML fairness methods requiring convex optimization impractical at industrial scale, FairDual provides a sub-linear convergence rate with an explicit Jensen gap bound while remaining compatible with arbitrary RS backbones including LLM-based models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal characterization of why mini-batch training degrades MMF-optimized recommenders (the Jensen gap from non-linear additivity), giving practitioners a diagnostic tool and a principled fix that integrates into existing training pipelines with modest overhead. Practical impact is broad: FairDual works across six backbones (including 7B-parameter LLM-based BigRec) and three datasets, and the dual-variable interpretation as 'shadow prices' offers intuitive tuning guidance. Limitations include the need to maintain a frozen item-embedding network (adding memory and a periodic sync step), the convex subproblem for dual updates (though coordinate-wise), and theoretical bounds that assume bounded dual variables and strong convexity. Open questions include scaling to hundreds of groups, two-sided (user+item) fairness, and whether the Jensen gap analysis extends to other non-linear welfare functions beyond the power-mean family.
