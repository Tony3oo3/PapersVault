## Problem

Existing quantum error mitigation (QEM) methods either lack generality across noise types and circuit structures, or fail to capture the global, nonlocal dependencies inherent in quantum systems. Machine-learning-based QEM approaches that do encode circuit structure via graph neural networks rely on local message-passing, which cannot model long-range entanglement, and they depend on ideal expectation values from classical simulation as training labels—impractical for larger circuits.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a quantum circuit encoded as a directed acyclic graph where node features combine gate-type one-hot vectors, qubit multi-hot encodings, and rotation angles, while edge features carry six-dimensional qubit-wise noisy Pauli expectation values (⟨X_q⟩, ⟨Y_q⟩, ⟨Z_q⟩ for each qubit on the edge). A scalar noisy expectation value y_noisy is concatenated with the final QCR node representation before the regression head, which outputs a single predicted ideal expectation value ŷ ∈ ℝ. The edge features make the model agnostic to noise type, requiring no explicit knowledge of error rates or noise models.

## Method

GTranQEM is a non-message-passing graph transformer that replaces local neighborhood aggregation with global multi-head attention guided by a structure matrix M (Floyd-Warshall shortest-path distances capped at M_max) added as a learnable-weighted bias to attention scores, enabling each node to attend to all others regardless of graph distance. A quantum-native positional encoding flattens each gate's (layer_index, qubit_set) pair into a unique 1D temporal index and applies sinusoidal encoding, capturing both execution order and qubit interactions. Qubit-wise noisy Pauli measurements embedded in edge features are projected and averaged into source-node representations, injecting measurement information without explicit message passing. A learnable Quantum Circuit-Representative (QCR) node is appended to the graph, connected by directed edges from all other nodes, and its final-layer representation—rather than a pooling operation—serves as the graph-level input to a two-layer regression head. Training is refined via inverse circuit composition (C·C⁻¹ with random initial states), which yields known ideal expectation values without classical simulation, and noise scaling amplification (λ ∈ [1, λ_max]) to improve robustness across noise levels.

## Results

On 6-qubit Trotterized circuits under incoherent noise, GTranQEM achieves a MAE of 0.0361 and CIR of 90.67%, outperforming all baselines. Under mixed noise on the same circuits, it reaches MAE 0.0315 and CIR 92.67%, the only method consistently exceeding 90% CIR across all three noise settings. On 15-qubit Trotterized circuits (incoherent noise), GTranQEM achieves MAE 0.0246 with the smallest standard deviation (0.0230), and on the 50-qubit IBM Brisbane real-device experiment, it substantially reduces error compared to raw noisy data and ZNE. In the circuit-scale extrapolation task (trained on ≤15 Trotter steps, tested on 16–20), GTranQEM attains MAE 0.0788 and CIR 97.00%, the best among all methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GTranQEM outperforms all six baselines across every setting in both Table 1 and Table 2. Against classical QEM methods, the margin is substantial: ZNE (Temme et al.) achieves MAE 0.2558 on 6-qubit Trotter incoherent noise versus GTranQEM's 0.0361 (a ~7× reduction), and CDR (Czarnik et al.) reaches 0.0740 versus 0.0361. PEC (Van den Berg et al.) performs poorly on random circuits under fake provider noise (MAE 0.1461, CIR 43.33%), while GTranQEM achieves 0.0440 and 74.33%. Among ML-based baselines, the closest competitor is the GNN from Liao et al. (2024), which achieves MAE 0.0388 on 6-qubit Trotter incoherent noise; GTranQEM improves this to 0.0361 and lifts CIR from 89.00% to 90.67%. The MLP baseline (Kim et al.) is consistently weaker, with MAE 0.0639 on the same setting. On the 15-qubit incoherent noise task (Table 2), GTranQEM (MAE 0.0246) beats the next-best OLS (0.0411) by ~40%, and on coherent noise it achieves MAE 0.0619 versus CDR's 0.0826 and ZNE's 0.0974.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ML-based quantum error mitigation line initiated by Kim et al. (2020, MLP-based) and Liao et al. (2024, GNN/OLS-based) by replacing flat or message-passing graph encodings with a non-message-passing graph transformer (GTranQEM). Architecturally, it draws on the structure-matrix-as-attention-bias paradigm from Rampašek et al. (2023) and the QCR (virtual representative node) concept from graph-level Transformer literature, but tailors both to the quantum circuit DAG with a quantum-native positional encoding that encodes gate layer and qubit identity. The inverse circuit composition data augmentation departs from all prior ML-QEM methods by eliminating the need for classically simulated ideal expectation values as training labels, a constraint that has limited the scalability of learning-based mitigators.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The inverse circuit composition paradigm is practically significant because it removes the exponential classical simulation bottleneck that has prevented ML-based QEM from scaling beyond ~15 qubits, and the 50-qubit IBM Brisbane experiment demonstrates this is not merely theoretical. However, the method still requires a training set and per-qubit Pauli measurements (mitigated by classical shadows), and the Floyd-Warshall structure matrix computation (O(N³)) may become a bottleneck for very deep or wide circuits. Open questions include whether the non-message-passing advantage persists at much larger circuit depths, whether the QCR node generalizes to multi-observable or multi-circuit batch settings, and how the method performs under non-Markovian noise where the noise model is history-dependent.
