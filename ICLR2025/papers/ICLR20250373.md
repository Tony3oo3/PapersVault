## Problem

Conventional LLM bias evaluation relies on fixed benchmarking datasets or adversarial attacks, neither of which scales to the prohibitively large input spaces that can elicit bias, and neither provides formal guarantees of generalization. This paper asks: what is the probability of unbiased LLM responses for any counterfactual prompt set sampled from a given distribution, and can this probability be certified with high-confidence bounds using only black-box access?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250373/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a pivot set of counterfactual prompts (e.g., from BOLD or Decoding Trust, where prompts differ only in a sensitive attribute like gender or race), a prefix distribution (random token sequences of fixed length q, a probabilistic mixture of manually designed jailbreaks with interleaving and token-mutation operations, or uniform embedding-space perturbations of a jailbreak), a black-box target LLM, and a user-defined bias detection function. It outputs Clopper-Pearson confidence intervals [p_l, p_u] bounding the probability that the LLM produces unbiased responses for any random counterfactual prompt set drawn from the distribution, along with the sampled LLM responses. The certification is local (conditioned on the prefix distribution) and the bounds hold with user-specified confidence 1−γ (95% in experiments) over 50 iid samples.

## Method

LLMCert-B is the first black-box probabilistic certifier for counterfactual bias in LLMs. It formalizes bias as a relational property over counterfactual prompt sets (Definition 1: prompts sharing an identical non-sensitive component X but differing in sensitive attributes A_i), then defines a probabilistic specification C(Δ, D, L) as the probability that a bias detector D outputs zero (unbiased) when the LLM L is independently prompted with each element of a randomly sampled set from distribution Δ. Because exact computation is intractable, the certifier draws n iid samples from Δ, queries the LLM, applies D, and computes Clopper-Pearson confidence intervals on the resulting Bernoulli success probability. Three practical prefix distributions instantiate the specification: (1) uniform random token sequences of length q, (2) a probabilistic program that interleaves instructions from multiple manual jailbreaks with probability p_λ and mutates tokens with probability p_μ, and (3) uniform noise of magnitude κ added to the jailbreak's embedding vector. The framework requires no white-box access, making it applicable to closed-source models like GPT-4 and Claude, and the certification results are robust to LLM decoding hyperparameters (temperature, top-k) as shown by ablation studies.

## Results

On BOLD (250 profession prompts), most models achieve 92–100% unbiased responses with random prefixes, but the mixture-of-jailbreaks specification drops Mistral-7B to (0.22, 0.42) and GPT-3.5 to (0.44, 0.67), while soft prefixes further reduce Mistral to (0.30, 0.52). On Decoding Trust (48 stereotype prompts), Mistral-7B drops to (0.46, 0.73) under soft prefixes and GPT-3.5 to (0.66, 0.88) under mixture prefixes. GPT-4 and Claude-3.5-Sonnet remain most robust, maintaining (0.80, 0.96) or higher across all specifications. The two empirical baselines (no prefix: 95–100% unbiased; main jailbreak: 41–100% unbiased) are consistently more optimistic than the certification bounds, revealing vulnerabilities invisible to pointwise evaluation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares LLMCert-B's distributional certification against two empirical baselines: (1) evaluating counterfactual prompt sets without any prefix, and (2) evaluating with the unmodified main jailbreak as a fixed prefix. The no-prefix baseline (95–100% unbiased) is generally at or above the upper certification bounds, confirming that prefixes are necessary to elicit bias and that pointwise benchmarking presents an overly optimistic picture. The main-jailbreak baseline is often contained within or above the mixture/soft certification bounds, meaning that the interleaving, mutation, and embedding-space perturbations used in LLMCert-B's specifications elicit additional biased behaviors not captured by the unmodified jailbreak alone. For example, Mistral-7B on BOLD drops from 41% unbiased (main JB baseline) to a certified (0.22, 0.42) under mixture prefixes, and further to (0.30, 0.52) under soft prefixes. In a few cases (e.g., Mistral on DT), the certified lower bound exceeds the main-JB baseline, suggesting that certain modifications can actually reduce jailbreak efficacy for specific models. Overall, the certification bounds consistently reveal more vulnerability than either baseline, demonstrating that distributional evaluation over prefix spaces exposes risks invisible to fixed-prompt or single-jailbreak evaluation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the counterfactual fairness framework of Kusner et al. (2018) to define bias as semantic disparity across LLM responses when demographic attributes are varied, and on the Clopper-Pearson confidence interval method (Clopper and Pearson, 1934) to compute high-confidence bounds on the probability of unbiased responses. It departs from prior LLM bias evaluation approaches—benchmarking on fixed test sets and adversarial attack discovery—by formulating bias assessment as a probabilistic certification problem over distributions of counterfactual prompt sets, providing guarantees that generalize to unseen inputs. Unlike white-box neural network certifiers (e.g., Beta-CROWN, ERAN) that require model internals and lose precision at scale, LLMCert-B operates purely in black-box mode, making it applicable to closed-source LLMs like GPT-4 and Claude. It also distinguishes itself from conformal prediction approaches for LLMs, which guarantee output correctness or coverage for individual prompts, by certifying a relational property (bias across a set of related prompts) over a distribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework to provide quantitative, high-confidence certificates for counterfactual bias in LLMs, offering model developers a principled pre-deployment risk assessment tool that goes beyond the optimistic point estimates of standard benchmarking. The finding that simple, computationally inexpensive prefix distributions (random tokens, jailbreak mixtures, embedding-space perturbations) can substantially reduce the probability of unbiased responses in SOTA models—including GPT-4—reveals previously unknown vulnerabilities and suggests no current defenses exist against these distributions. Key limitations include the reliance on bias detectors with only 76% human agreement and 50% recall, the local nature of specifications (pivoted around fixed prompt sets), and the restriction to binary demographic groups in main experiments. Open questions include whether model-specific specification tuning can address cases where modifications reduce jailbreak efficacy, whether the lack of a consistent scaling trend for fairness implies alignment quality is the dominant factor, and how to design effective defenses against the identified prefix distributions.
