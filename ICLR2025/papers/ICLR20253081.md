## Problem

Standard Mixture-of-Experts gating mechanisms (e.g., Switch Transformers, Mixtral) use static, learned routing networks that are constant in time, making them ill-suited for prediction tasks with temporal structure where the optimal expert combination shifts as new observations arrive. This paper asks whether the temporal structure of online time-series prediction can be exploited to dynamically re-weight experts at each time step, using only the information available in each expert's running performance.

## Input / Output

The method takes N pre-trained expert models (LLMs or specialized TSF models) that each produce a scalar prediction given a sequential input (financial news headlines + OHLCV features for classification, or a historical multivariate time-series window for regression). Crucially, the filter also consumes each expert's running loss over time as the observation signal, and the Q/intensity matrix governing expert-switching dynamics is re-estimated online at every step. The output is a single ensemble prediction (ternary market direction or H-step-ahead multivariate forecast) together with the updated mixture weights and the re-estimated transition matrix.

## Method

MoE-F frames expert selection as a finite-state-space, continuous-time Hidden Markov Model where the hidden state indicates which expert is currently optimal, and each expert's running loss serves as the noisy observation. Step 1 runs N parallel Wonham-Shiryaev stochastic filters in closed form (no Monte Carlo), each estimating the posterior over expert states using only that expert's own local loss history, yielding an optimal L² a posteriori estimate (Theorem 1). Step 2 robustly aggregates the N filter outputs via a Gibbs/softmin weighting (PAC-Bayes-style) to produce the ensemble prediction, then re-estimates the Q/intensity matrix by computing the matrix logarithm of a regularized row-stochastic matrix and projecting onto valid intensity matrices (Theorem 2). The entire procedure is a plug-and-play online harness: it requires no training of the gating mechanism, no fine-tuning of experts, and adapts at every time step via a single Euler-Maruyama discretization of the filtering SDE.

## Results

On the NIFTY financial market movement task, MoE-F achieves an F1 score of 0.52, accuracy of 0.57, and precision of 0.61, representing a 17% absolute (48.5% relative) F1 improvement over the best individual LLM expert (Llama-3-8B-Instruct at F1=0.35). On the long-term time-series forecasting task (ETTh1/ETTh2), MoE-F using only three experts (DLinear, PatchTST, SparseTSF) without hyperparameter tuning achieves MSE of 0.3630/0.2911 at horizon 96, matching or exceeding the best individual expert and approaching SOTA methods like FITS (0.375/0.274). The method demonstrates consistent gains across all evaluation horizons (96, 192, 336, 720) on both ETTh1 and ETTh2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoE-F decisively outperforms all seven individual LLM experts on the NIFTY FMM task: the best single expert (Llama-3-8B-Instruct) achieves F1=0.35, while MoE-F reaches F1=0.52 — a 17-point absolute gain. Notably, even GPT-4o (F1=0.34) and the MoE-architecture models Mixtral-8x7B (F1=0.34) and DBRX-Instruct (F1=0.34) are all substantially outperformed, suggesting that static learned routing within a single model cannot match dynamic online filtering across heterogeneous experts. On the TSF task, MoE-F (using DLinear, PatchTST, SparseTSF) achieves MSE of 0.3630 at horizon 96 on ETTh1, slightly beating SparseTSF (0.3631) and DLinear (0.3788) and approaching FITS (0.375, published result), while on ETTh2 it achieves 0.2911 at horizon 96, beating DLinear (0.2908) and SparseTSF (0.2945) and approaching FITS (0.274). The key distinction is that MoE-F requires no hyperparameter tuning (λ, α) and no retraining of experts, operating purely as a plug-and-play online filtering harness over pre-trained models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Wonham-Shiryaev filter (1964) and the Liptser-Shiryaev filtering framework (1977) as a dynamic gating mechanism for Mixture-of-Experts, departing fundamentally from the static, learned routing layers used in classical MoE architectures (Jacobs et al. 1991, Shazeer et al. 2017, Switch Transformers, Mixtral). The robust aggregation step draws on PAC-Bayes and Gibbs posterior mixture theory (Alquier 2008, Rothfuss et al. 2021), but applies it in an online, time-adaptive setting rather than the offline, fixed-dataset context in which those methods were developed. The key conceptual departure is treating expert selection as a hidden Markov signal to be estimated via closed-form finite-dimensional filtering, rather than as a fixed function of the input.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play nature of MoE-F as a filtering harness over arbitrary heterogeneous expert collections (LLMs, specialized TSF models) is practically appealing, and the theoretical optimality guarantees for both the filtering and aggregation steps are a meaningful contribution. However, the experimental evidence is somewhat limited: the main financial prediction result rests on a single 317-day test split, and the TSF results show MoE-F is competitive but not consistently best (e.g., underperforming SparseTSF on ETTh2 at horizon 336). Open questions include whether the HMM assumption on expert switching is realistic in practice, how the method scales to larger expert pools, and whether integrating SDE-based experts (as the authors note) would capture technical market dynamics that LLMs miss.
