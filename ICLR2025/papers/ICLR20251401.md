## Problem

Gradient-based training of neural networks on multi-index models typically requires sample complexity n ≳ d^s, where s is the information or generative exponent of the link function, far exceeding the information-theoretic optimum of n ≍ d. While the mean-field Langevin algorithm can in principle bypass these exponent-based limitations, prior guarantees were restricted to specific targets (e.g., k-parity) and isotropic data, and the computational complexity remained exponential in the ambient dimension. This paper asks whether arbitrary multi-index models can be learned with near-optimal sample complexity via standard mean-field training, and whether the exponential computational cost can be reduced to polynomial under structural assumptions.

## Input / Output

The method takes n i.i.d. pairs (x, y) where x ∈ R^d is subGaussian with general (possibly anisotropic) covariance Σ, and y = g(Ux) + ξ for an unknown k-dimensional link function g (k ≪ d) and subGaussian noise. The output is a scalar prediction ŷ(x; W) from a two-layer network with frozen second-layer weights at ±1 and a smooth ReLU approximation φ_{κ,ι} as activation. A key structural detail is that the relevant complexity parameter is the effective dimension d_eff = tr(Σ)/‖Σ^{1/2}U^T‖_F², which can be polylog(d) when the covariance is approximately low-rank and aligned with the target directions, rather than the ambient dimension d.

## Method

The paper analyzes the Mean-Field Langevin Algorithm (MFLA), a discretized interacting-particle SDE that trains a two-layer network in the infinite-width limit, where the empirical measure over neurons evolves according to a nonlinear Fokker-Planck equation. Convergence is governed by a log-Sobolev inequality (LSI) on the Gibbs potential along the trajectory; in the Euclidean setting, the LSI constant scales as exp(Õ(d_eff)), yielding sample complexity Õ(d_eff) but exponential computational complexity. The key new contribution is a Riemannian variant where weights are constrained to a compact manifold (e.g., the hypersphere S^{d-1}) with uniformly positive Ricci curvature; via the Bakry-Émery curvature-dimension condition, the LSI constant becomes O(1/d) rather than exponential, enabling polynomial-time convergence when the teacher model's neuron distribution has bounded oscillation (osc(f) = o(d)). This establishes the first polynomial-time MFLD guarantee for multi-index learning, in contrast to the exponential scaling under identical assumptions in the Euclidean case.

## Results

The main theoretical guarantee (Theorem 3) establishes that MFLA achieves vanishing excess risk with sample complexity n = Õ(d_eff), where d_eff = tr(Σ)/‖Σ^{1/2}U^T‖_F² is the effective dimension capturing the low-dimensional structure in the data. In the Euclidean setting, the computational complexity (width and iterations) scales as Õ(d·exp(Õ(d_eff))), which is exponential in d_eff in the worst case. In the Riemannian setting (weights on the hypersphere), Theorem 10 shows polynomial-time convergence is achievable when d ≥ 2C_ρKΔ̄/(ϱε), with the LSI constant bounded by (ϱd − βC_ρK)^{-1}, independent of d. For spiked covariance models, d_eff ≍ d^{1−{(γ_2−2γ_1)∨0}}, and for power-law eigenspectra, d_eff can be as small as polylog(d) when α,γ ≥ 1, yielding quasipolynomial runtime.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to Telgarsky (2023) and Suzuki et al. (2023b), which achieve sample complexity d only for k-parity targets on isotropic hypercube data, this paper extends to arbitrary multi-index models with general link functions on subGaussian data with general covariance, while additionally achieving d_eff-adaptivity that can be significantly smaller than d. Against Nitanda et al. (2024), which studies k-sparse parities on anisotropic parallelotope data, this paper handles the broader class of general multi-index models and shows its effective dimension is always lower bounded by theirs, with a particularly significant gap when Σ is nearly rank-deficient. Compared to Bach (2017), which requires d^{(k+3)/2} samples for general k-index models, this paper achieves Õ(d_eff) samples — strictly better even in the worst case d_eff = d, since d < d^{(k+3)/2} for k ≥ 1. The key improvement over Bach (2017) comes from a refined control over ‖Ux‖ that is independent of d for centered x, rather than scaling with √d.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mean-field Langevin dynamics (MFLD) framework for neural network training—previously applied to specific targets like k-parity (Suzuki et al., 2023b) or single-index models with particular link functions—to arbitrary multi-index models under general subGaussian data with arbitrary covariance. It builds on the LSI-based convergence machinery of Chizat (2022b) and Nitanda et al. (2022), and the approximation theory of Bach (2017) for convex neural networks, but departs from prior work by introducing an effective dimension that simultaneously governs sample and computational complexity, and by exploring a Riemannian weight space (hypersphere with positive Ricci curvature) as a route to polynomial-time convergence. The paper also contrasts its results with gradient-based feature learning analyses (information/generative/leap exponents) and statistical query lower bounds, showing that MFLA bypasses these limitations at the cost of exponential compute in the Euclidean setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper closes a long-standing gap between the information-theoretically optimal sample complexity (n ≍ d for isotropic data) and what standard gradient-based training achieves (n ≍ d^Θ(s)), showing that MFLA attains near-optimal sample complexity governed by an effective dimension that can be polylogarithmic in d under structured covariance. The Riemannian result—where positive Ricci curvature converts an exponential LSI constant into a polynomial one—is a genuinely new mechanism for improving computational complexity and opens a path toward polynomial-time MFLD convergence. Key open questions include whether the effective dimension can be matched in the Riemannian setting for k = O(1) multi-index models, whether exponential compute is truly unavoidable in the Euclidean case, and how to establish lower bounds for gradient-based methods under realistic (non-adversarial) assumptions.
