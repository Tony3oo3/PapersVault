## Problem

Inherently interpretable Transformer models like Transformer Programs (Friedman et al., 2024) can be extracted into human-readable programs, but they suffer from limited expressivity in categorical and numerical reasoning and from optimization difficulties where Gumbel-Softmax reparameterization fails to promote sparsity or escape local optima. This paper addresses both gaps to narrow the performance gap between black-box Transformers and interpretable models without sacrificing transparency.

## Input / Output

For algorithmic RASP tasks, input is fixed one-hot encoded token and position variables over small vocabularies (size 2–16) with sequence lengths up to 16; for NLP tasks, input is sentences up to 32–64 words initialized with 300-dimensional GloVe embeddings over a 10,000-word vocabulary. Beyond standard predictions (sequence outputs, entity tags, class labels), the method produces an extracted symbolic program expressed as lines of code (predicate functions, attention pattern selections, and aggregation calls), where program length serves as a quantitative interpretability metric.

## Method

The framework extends Transformer Programs' disentangled residual stream and rule-based modules with three enhancements. First, a Smooth Transition Mechanism interpolates between Gumbel-Softmax (exploration) and Gumbel-Sparsemax (exploitation) via a temperature-controlled coefficient α(τ), gradually shifting from diverse program search to sparse, deterministic selections during training. Second, an Uncertainty-Aware Attention module computes Jensen-Shannon Divergence between categorical (predicate-based) attention and a learned reference distribution, feeding this uncertainty estimate into an MLP gate that dynamically blends categorical and score-based attention per token. Third, a Position-Aware Attention module injects both learnable and sinusoidal positional encodings into numerical value variables, enabling the model to represent continuous values and distinguish identical numbers at different positions. Together, these mechanisms yield more concise extracted programs (up to 85% fewer lines on TREC) while matching or exceeding baseline accuracy across in-context learning, algorithmic, and NLP benchmarks.

## Results

On the eight RASP algorithmic tasks, Adaptive Transformer Programs achieve an average accuracy of 96.6% versus 92.4% for the Transformer Programs baseline, with the largest gain on Double Histogram (91.81% vs. 66.78%) and Most-Freq (80.80% vs. 76.44%). Program length is reduced across all tasks, with the most dramatic reduction on Most-Freq (894 vs. 1334 lines, a 33% decrease). On NER (CoNLL-2003), the method achieves 94.1% accuracy and 75.1 F1 with 916 lines, closely matching Transformer Programs (94.2%, 75.8 F1, 991 lines) while outperforming Standard Transformers (92.2%, 66.6 F1). On text classification, the method achieves the highest accuracy on MR (77.9%) and produces programs 65–85% shorter than Transformer Programs across all four classification datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Transformer Programs (Friedman et al., 2024), Adaptive Transformer Programs win on 6 of 8 RASP tasks in accuracy (most notably Double Histogram +25.03 pp and Most-Freq +4.36 pp), tie on Induction and Histogram, and lose marginally on Sort (−0.12 pp). Critically, program length is shorter on all 8 tasks, with reductions ranging from 5% (Histogram) to 33% (Most-Freq). Against Standard Transformers (Vaswani et al., 2017), the proposed method substantially outperforms on NER (94.1% vs. 92.2% accuracy; 75.1 vs. 66.6 F1) and matches or exceeds on text classification (e.g., MR: 77.9% vs. 75.9%), while producing fully interpretable programs of 827–1954 lines versus no program representation for the black-box baseline. On the scalability analysis (Figure 1), Adaptive Transformer Programs degrade less gracefully than Standard Transformers as vocabulary size and sequence length increase, but still outperform both baselines on Sort at the highest complexity setting (|V|=16, N=16).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Transformer Programs framework (Friedman et al., 2024), which itself builds on the RASP programming language (Weiss et al., 2021) and the Tracr compiler (Lindner et al., 2024) for translating Transformer computations into human-readable code. The three proposed enhancements—a Gumbel-Softmax-to-Sparsemax smooth transition for discrete optimization, a JSD-gated uncertainty-aware attention blending categorical and score-based mechanisms, and positional encodings in numerical attention—target specific expressivity and optimization bottlenecks identified in the original framework. Conceptually, the work sits at the intersection of mechanistic interpretability, program synthesis, and uncertainty estimation, repurposing Sparsemax and Jensen-Shannon Divergence in novel roles within a constrained Transformer architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The consistent 5–33% reduction in extracted program length across diverse tasks (up to 85% on TREC) while matching or exceeding baseline accuracy offers a practical path toward more transparent AI in high-stakes domains. However, all experiments use small-scale datasets (sequence lengths ≤ 16 for RASP tasks, ≤ 64 words for NLP), and scalability degrades noticeably as vocabulary and sequence length increase (e.g., Sort drops to 87.6% at |V|=16, N=16). Open questions include whether the smooth transition and uncertainty-aware gating mechanisms scale to larger, pre-trained architectures, how the approach extends to generative tasks requiring variable-length outputs, and whether the learned programs remain human-auditable as complexity grows beyond the current ~1000-line regime.
