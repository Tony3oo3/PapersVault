## Problem

While prompt-based adaptation methods like MaPLe substantially improve CLIP's downstream classification performance, it remains an open question what internal mechanisms drive these gains: does adaptation cause the model to recognize new visual concepts, or does it merely rewire how existing concepts map to task classes? No prior work had provided a tool to inspect and compare the interpretable concepts active in a frozen foundation model versus its adapted variant at the level of individual visual features.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250883/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

PatchSAE consumes the 768-dimensional residual stream vectors from all 197 tokens (14×14 image patches plus one CLS token) at the 11th attention layer of a frozen CLIP ViT-B/16, producing a 49,152-dimensional sparse latent activation per token. Because every patch token is processed independently through the SAE, the output naturally yields a spatial attribution map: each latent's activation across the 14×14 grid can be rendered as a soft segmentation mask localizing where a concept appears in the image. For downstream analysis, these patch-level activations are binarized and aggregated into image-, class-, and dataset-level counts to identify which concepts are representative of a group.

## Method

PatchSAE is a standard two-layer sparse autoencoder (linear encoder → ReLU → linear decoder) with an expansion factor of 64, trained on ImageNet with MSE reconstruction loss plus L1 sparsity regularization (λ=8e-5) and ghost-gradient training. The key architectural choice is applying the SAE to every ViT token rather than only the CLS token, which provides patch-wise spatial attribution of each discovered concept and enables aggregation to higher levels of analysis. To study adaptation, the authors share the same CLIP-trained SAE with MaPLe (which keeps CLIP weights frozen and only adds learnable prompt tokens), then compare class-level top-activating latents between the two models using scatter plots partitioned into high, high-to-low, and low-to-high regions. A top-k SAE latent masking experiment—where only the k most class-representative latents are allowed through the decoder—demonstrates that these latents carry critical discriminative information, and that MaPLe achieves higher accuracy with the same latent budget, indicating it exploits the same concepts more effectively rather than discovering new ones.

## Results

PatchSAE with an identity mask (all latents active) recovers 64.82% zero-shot accuracy on ImageNet-1K versus 68.25% for the original CLIP model, confirming faithful reconstruction. MaPLe consistently outperforms zero-shot CLIP across all 11 base-to-novel datasets, with relative improvement rates (Δ) ranging from 5.82% (StanfordCars) to 72.69% (Flowers102). The central interpretability finding is that top-activating SAE latents overlap heavily between CLIP and MaPLe: the 'high' (shared) group typically accounts for 7–11 latents per class, while 'high-to-low' and 'low-to-high' groups are near zero for most datasets, indicating adaptation remaps existing concepts rather than introducing new ones.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares zero-shot CLIP (ViT-B/16) against MaPLe (multi-modal prompt adaptation) on 11 base-to-novel classification benchmarks. MaPLe outperforms CLIP on every dataset in both base and novel splits, with the largest absolute gains on Flowers102 (+31.75 pp base) and DTD (+22.05 pp base). The improvement rate Δ, which normalizes for zero-shot headroom, ranges from 5.82% (StanfordCars, already strong zero-shot) to 72.69% (Flowers102, where zero-shot CLIP is weak at 56.32%). Critically, the SAE latent overlap analysis reveals that the top class-discriminative latents are nearly identical between CLIP and MaPLe (the 'high' group dominates with ~7–11 shared latents per class), meaning adaptation does not activate new visual concepts but instead reassigns existing concepts to the correct downstream classes. The sole notable exception is EuroSAT (Δ=53.08%), where 4.1 low-to-high latents per class indicate that adaptation does introduce some task-specific concepts under significant domain shift (satellite imagery vs. natural images).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sparse autoencoder (SAE) framework from Bricken et al. (2023) and the CLIP-specific SAE of Fry (2024) by introducing patch-level spatial attribution, enabling localized concept interpretation across all image tokens rather than only the CLS token. It departs from prior SAE work by using the extracted concepts not merely as a descriptive interpretability tool but as a controlled intervention mechanism (latent masking) to probe how prompt-based adaptation (MaPLe) alters the mapping between recognized visual concepts and downstream task classes. The paper positions itself at the intersection of mechanistic interpretability and foundation model adaptation, a combination that prior work had not systematically explored for vision transformers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that prompt-based adaptation primarily remaps existing concepts rather than creating new discriminative features offers a concrete mechanistic explanation for why few learnable tokens suffice for adaptation, with implications for how we design and trust adapted models. However, the analysis is limited to a single adaptation method (MaPLe), a single architecture (ViT-B/16), and the vision encoder only, leaving open whether the remapping story holds for full fine-tuning, text-encoder-only prompts (CoOp), or larger models. The PatchSAE framework itself is a practical contribution for the interpretability community, but its generalizability to other vision backbones and multimodal joint analysis remains untested.
