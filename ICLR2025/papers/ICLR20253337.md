## Problem

Existing benchmarks for evaluating long-range dependency learning in GNNs lack formal guarantees: real-world datasets have uncontrolled functions, and synthetic benchmarks either admit shortcut solutions that ignore path length or require functions inexpressible by GNN architectures. This makes it impossible to certify whether a system truly learns dependencies of a given length or merely exploits spurious correlations in the data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253337/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The GLoRa generator takes a dependency length d ≥ 3 and a binary label (True/False) and produces a directed graph with 2-dimensional node embeddings plus a designated target node. Graphs consist of a main chain (length sampled from ⌊2d/3⌋ to d) starting at a source node with embedding [1,−], followed by 5–10 alternative sub-chains each containing a hole node (embedding [−,0]), plus 2–4 additional random holes. The target is the terminal node of the main chain, classified True iff the main chain is hole-free, making the label depend on the specific path rather than global graph statistics.

## Method

GLoRa's key design insight is that alternative chains with guaranteed holes, combined with randomized additional holes, eliminate all shortcut functions (e.g., counting holes, checking for absence of [−,−] embeddings) while preserving the intended long-range dependency along the main chain. Theorem 1 formally guarantees that any node classification function fitting a sufficient number of GLoRa examples must rely on a path-aware dependency of length d with arbitrary precision δ. The paper then evaluates 30+ state-of-the-art systems (vanilla GNNs, over-smoothing mitigations, over-squashing mitigations, and graph transformers) across d ∈ {3,…,15}, finding that none reliably learns beyond d = 11, with GatedGCN variants performing best. Dedicated experiments on last-layer embeddings, path counts, and gradient magnitudes demonstrate that the performance degradation is not attributable to over-smoothing, over-squashing, or vanishing gradient.

## Results

On the GLoRa benchmark (binary node classification, noise-free, target accuracy = 1.0, random baseline = 0.5), no evaluated system can reliably learn dependencies beyond length d = 11. The best-performing systems (GatedGCN, DenseGatedGCN, ResGatedGCN) maintain near-perfect accuracy up to d ≈ 6–7 but degrade to near-chance (≈0.5) by d = 13. Most vanilla GNNs and over-smoothing/over-squashing targeting systems experience a sharp accuracy drop before d = 9. Transformer-based systems (GraphTransformer, SAN, GraphGPS) perform poorly even at very short dependency lengths (d = 3–5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

All 29 evaluated baselines fail to learn dependencies beyond d = 11, which is the central empirical finding. Among vanilla GNNs, GatedGCN and its residual/dense variants (DenseGatedGCN, ResGatedGCN) are the strongest, maintaining accuracy near 1.0 up to d ≈ 7 before declining; GCN, GAT, GraphSAGE, GIN, GGNN, and SGC all degrade earlier (before d = 9). Among over-smoothing targeting systems, DenseGatedGCN and ResGatedGCN again lead, while GCNII, PairNorm, DropEdge, GPRGNN, APPNP, JKNet, IGNN, EIGNN, GIND, G², and DAGNN all fail by d ≈ 9–11. Among over-squashing targeting systems, 1Drew-GCN is the best performer but still drops to chance by d = 13; ∞Drew-GCN, SP-GCN, MixHop-GCN, DIGL, SDRF, FOSR, and FA-GCN all degrade earlier. Transformer-based systems (GraphTransformer, SAN, GraphGPS with RWSE or LapPE) perform worst overall, failing even at d = 3–5. The paper further shows via histogram and gradient analyses that the observed degradation is not caused by over-smoothing, over-squashing, or vanishing gradient, leaving the true cause unidentified.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in the evaluation methodology for long-range dependency learning in graphs, rather than building on a specific prior method. It departs from existing synthetic benchmarks (Synthetic Chains, Tree-Neighbours, h-Proximity, Graph Transfer, Color-Connectivity, etc.) by providing formal guarantees (Theorem 1) that any function fitting the generated examples must rely on dependencies of the specified length, and that such a function is expressible in standard GNN architectures. It also departs from real-world benchmarks (OGB, LRGB, Cora) by offering full control over the dependency length, which is impossible with uncontrolled real data. The paper positions itself as a diagnostic tool for the three commonly-cited causes of long-range degradation (over-smoothing, over-squashing, vanishing gradient) rather than proposing a new architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that none of the three widely-cited phenomena (over-smoothing, over-squashing, vanishing gradient) explains the performance degradation—challenges the field's working assumptions and redirects research toward identifying the true cause. The benchmark's formal guarantees make it a rigorous tool for future systems to claim long-range capability, addressing a gap where prior evaluations could not distinguish genuine long-range learning from memorization of shallow patterns. The practical implication is significant: no current system can reliably learn dependencies beyond length 11, meaning that applications requiring interactions across dozens of edges (social influence, drug side-effect prediction, traffic flow) remain unsolved. Open questions include what the actual bottleneck is, whether the directed-graph assumption is essential, and whether the gated-edge mechanism (which performed best) can be extended to truly long-range settings.
