## Problem

Existing neural solvers for the Maximum Clique Problem rely on non-differentiable combinatorial decoders, preventing seamless integration into end-to-end neural pipelines (e.g., graph retrieval). Additionally, supervised methods require explicit clique demonstrations, which are computationally expensive to obtain, while training-free combinatorial methods lack the flexibility of learned models. This paper targets the neglected middle ground: estimating clique numbers under distant supervision (only the clique size label, not the clique itself) while maintaining full differentiability.

## Input / Output

The method takes a graph's adjacency matrix (padded to N×N with self-loops added to all nodes) and produces a scalar clique number estimate. Internally, the binary adjacency matrix is relaxed to continuous values via element-wise multiplication with a GNN-computed embedding outer product (W = A ⊙ XX^T), and the hard permutation is relaxed to a doubly stochastic soft permutation via Gumbel-Sinkhorn iterations. The clique number is read out as the maximum over a grid-graph message-passing output, avoiding any discrete combinatorial search at inference time.

## Method

The key reformulation casts MCP as maximizing the size of the largest fully dense square submatrix over all row-column permutations of the adjacency matrix, reducing the combinatorial hardness to the permutation-finding step. MXNET (MSS) uses a GNN to compute node embeddings, a Gumbel-Sinkhorn network to propose a soft permutation S, and a neural relaxation of a dynamic-programming flood-fill algorithm (implemented as message passing on an N×N grid graph) to detect the largest dense square in SWS^T. MXNET (SubMatch) introduces a curriculum of nested cliques K_2 ⊂ K_3 ⊂ …, each tested for injective subsumption into the graph via a soft coverage loss ρ(c;G), with a margin-based loss that encourages the loss to jump sharply at c > ω(G). The final MXNET (Composite) jointly trains both components under a weighted composite loss with a bicriteria early stopping rule that first saturates the SubMatch loss before optimizing the MSS loss, balancing accuracy against interpretability.

## Results

MXNET (ES:Bi) achieves the best MSE on 7 out of 8 datasets, with particularly strong results on IMDB (0.094), PTC-MM-m (0.179), Enzymes (0.196), and MUTAG-m (0.613). The only dataset where MXNET is outperformed is Brock, where EGN achieves 1.310 MSE versus MXNET's 10.022. Critically, MXNET is the only non-decoder method that matches or surpasses decoder-based baselines, while all other non-decoder methods (SFE, NSFE, ST, Reinforce, NeurSC) are massively outperformed by every decoder-based baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MXNET (ES:Bi) outperforms EGN (the strongest decoder-based baseline) on 7 of 8 datasets, with the largest margins on RB (6.875 vs. 15.615), MUTAG-m (0.613 vs. 1.010), and PTC-MM-m (0.179 vs. 0.284); EGN wins only on Brock (1.310 vs. 10.022). Against GFnet, MXNET wins on 6 of 8 datasets, with particularly large gaps on RB (6.875 vs. 34.200) and PTC-MM-m (0.179 vs. 0.554). All non-decoder baselines (SFE, NSFE, ST, Reinforce, NeurSC) are dramatically outperformed by MXNET on nearly every dataset — for example, on AIDS-m, MXNET achieves 0.341 MSE while the best non-decoder baseline (NeurSC) scores 1.240, and SFE/NSFE/ST/Reinforce all exceed 36. The paper also shows that when decoder-based methods are given 4× more sampling budget, EGN and GFnet improve substantially (e.g., GFnet drops from 2.815 to 0.167 on IMDB), but MXNET still leads on IMDB (0.094) and does so with ~2× lower inference time. On the OOD test set (PTC-MM-m with |V|≥80, ω≥7), MXNET degrades gracefully to 0.822 MSE while all baselines degrade more severely (best baseline: GFnet at 0.832).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Gumbel-Sinkhorn networks for learning soft permutations and on the line of neural MCP solvers (EGN, SCT, SFE, Difusco, GFnet, VAG-CO), but departs from them by formulating the maximum clique problem as finding the largest fully dense subsquare in a row-column-permuted adjacency matrix, then simulating the corresponding dynamic-programming routine with a differentiable message-passing network on a grid graph. It also draws on neural subgraph matching and the Motzkin-Straus quadratic-program relaxation to motivate training under distant supervision (clique number labels only) rather than the full clique demonstrations required by supervised methods like Difusco. The curriculum-matching component (nested clique subgraph tests) is a novel inductive bias not present in prior MCP neural solvers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

End-to-end differentiability of the clique-number estimator enables direct integration into retrieval and graph-similarity pipelines where clique number serves as a latent relevance signal, something non-differentiable decoders in prior methods preclude. The paper's main limitation is scalability: the O(N²) grid-graph message passing and explicit adjacency storage constrain applicability to large or dense graphs, and the authors acknowledge that combinatorial solvers or decoder-based pipelines can still outperform on some instances. Open questions include extending the framework to dynamic graphs, incorporating dense node/edge features from domain knowledge, and generalizing the differentiable-permutation-plus-DP paradigm to other combinatorial optimization problems on graphs.
