## Problem

Subword tokenizers (e.g., BPE) have become the de-facto standard in NLP but introduce several structural drawbacks: vocabularies in the hundreds of thousands consume a large fraction of model parameters (~13% for Llama-3 8B), the tokenizer is fitted in a separate non-end-to-end step that hinders adaptation to new languages or domains, and semantically close inputs with spelling variations can map to drastically different token sequences. This paper eliminates the fixed vocabulary entirely by proposing a hierarchical architecture that combines character-level and word-level processing in a single end-to-end trainable system.

## Input / Output

The model consumes raw text as a sequence of UTF-8 bytes (a 256-value alphabet), split at Unicode whitespace into words, each prepended with a special [W] token. During inference, generation proceeds in a nested loop: the backbone emits a predictive word embedding, the decoder autoregressively produces characters until it predicts [W], and the completed word re-enters the encoder for the next iteration. The decoder head outputs a 256-way softmax over the byte alphabet, and a final [S] token marks document termination.

## Method

The architecture comprises three transformer modules: a small bidirectional character-level encoder that compresses each word's characters into a single word embedding (read from the [W] position), a larger causal word-level backbone that models the sequence of word embeddings, and a small causal character-level decoder that converts the backbone's predictive embedding back into next-character logits. The key computational insight is that words are coarser than subwords (S_W ≈ 0.69 S_T on their pretraining data), so the backbone processes shorter sequences, freeing the compute budget for a larger backbone while the character-level modules remain tiny (23–55M parameters, less than the embedding/head they replace). Training is fully end-to-end with character-level cross-entropy loss; the decoder is conditioned on the backbone output concatenated with the next word's raw character embeddings (shifted by one to prevent the bidirectional encoder from leaking future characters). The authors also introduce a Unicode-standard word-boundary splitter as a universal alternative to whitespace splitting, enabling adaptation to non-alphabetic languages (e.g., Chinese) and code without architectural changes.

## Results

At compute-matched scales of 1B, 3B, and 7B parameters, the hierarchical architecture matches the BPE-tokenizer baseline on 17 downstream tasks, with a consistent and notable advantage on LAMBADA (up to 68% relative improvement at 7B). The hierarchical model is significantly more robust to input perturbations, suffering only ~1/3 the accuracy drop of the baseline under all-caps perturbation. During continued pretraining on German (Occiglot), the hierarchical model trains 1.9× faster, achieves higher German eval scores, and retains more English knowledge compared to the baseline. At the 3B scale with the Unicode splitter, the hierarchical model scores 31.0 on MMLU vs. 29.1 for the baseline, and 70.9 vs. 65.8 on LAMBADA OpenAI.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the BPE-tokenizer Llama baseline (compute-matched at 1B/3B/7B), the hierarchical model ties or slightly leads on most of the 17 downstream tasks, with a clear and consistent win on LAMBADA (up to 68% relative margin at 7B) and a small loss on TriviaQA at 1B that vanishes at larger scales. On robustness, the hierarchical model degrades far less under character-level perturbations (permutation, randomization, deletion, all-caps), with the baseline suffering 3× the accuracy drop on all-caps. Against MegaByte at 1B, the hierarchical architecture outperforms on all but one evaluation task; an ablation combining the hierarchical architecture with MegaByte's fixed 8-byte split yields a 2.6 ppt byte-accuracy gain over MegaByte, confirming the benefit of whitespace-based word splitting. For cross-lingual continued pretraining on German, the hierarchical model achieves higher German eval scores while retaining better English performance, and trains 1.9× faster because the tokenizer baseline's sequence length inflates due to byte-fallback fragmentation on out-of-domain text.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper refines and scales up the hierarchical character-word architecture first explored by Sun et al. (2023) for masked language modelling and by Thawani et al. (2023) for autoregressive modelling, introducing a generative variant with a shift-by-one decoding scheme and a single [W] token per word rather than four. It departs from MegaByte (Yu et al., 2023), which uses fixed-size byte patches without an encoder, and from the concurrent SpaceByte (Slagle, 2024), which applies byte-level layers over sliding windows rather than per-word. The key contribution over all prior hierarchical work is a careful compute-matched experimental protocol at the 1B–7B parameter scale, with the character-level modules kept small enough that their cost roughly equals the language modelling head they replace.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the fixed subword vocabulary, hierarchical transformers remove a major practical bottleneck for multilingual and cross-domain NLP: the tokenizer no longer fragments out-of-domain text into byte-fallback sequences, yielding a 1.9× speedup and superior target-language performance during continued pretraining on an unseen language while retaining more source-language knowledge. The consistent robustness advantage across perturbation types (especially the 3× larger accuracy drop for the baseline under all-caps) suggests these models are more reliable in real-world deployment where inputs are noisy. Open questions include whether the whitespace/Unicode splitting rule generalises to logographic languages without further adaptation, whether the higher parameter footprint of compute-matched hierarchical models is offset in practice by smaller KV caches, and whether additional hierarchy levels (sentences, paragraphs) or alternative decoders (diffusion, multi-token prediction) can further improve long-context generation.
