## Problem

Existing ILP datasets are small-scale (typically fewer than 10 rules in hypotheses), lack reference hypotheses, and fail to cover the full spectrum of challenging language biases such as high-arity predicates, mutual recursion, predicate invention, and argument symmetry. This makes it impossible to evaluate whether modern ILP systems can scale beyond toy problems or operate without expert-defined language bias, leaving a critical gap between ILP and the expert-free paradigm of modern deep learning.

## Input / Output

Each task provides symbolic background knowledge as a set of ground atoms (geometric facts like Coll, Para, Perp, Cong, etc.), positive examples (ground atoms of the target predicate), and optionally negative examples, all under the open-world assumption with noisy negatives. The dataset also supplies image-form background knowledge (geometry diagrams rendered via AlphaGeometry's diagram builder) for joint perception-and-induction settings. The expected output is a hypothesis: a set of Horn rules that are both complete (entail all positive examples given BK) and consistent (entail no negative examples), with predicate arity up to 8, body atoms up to 9, and variables up to 12 per rule.

## Method

GeoILP is synthesized by running a symbolic deduction engine (based on deductive database theory, similar to JGEX/AlphaGeometry) over plane geometry problems sourced from textbook exercises through regional IMO. Forward chaining from selected premises produces all deducible conclusions, which become positive examples; a traceback through the deduction graph then identifies the minimal set of premises as BK, and the directed edges along the traceback constitute the guaranteed reference hypothesis. The initial point configuration is duplicated into ten groups and split 8:2 for training and evaluation. The resulting 835 single-tasks and 207 multi-tasks are organized into four progressive difficulty levels (basic, simple, advanced, complex) parameterized by body-atom count, mutual recursion, and rule count, with 85% of single-tasks containing 10–100 rules and the remainder reaching up to 1,783 rules. Experiments confirm that neither Popper (symbolic) nor Difflog (neuro-symbolic) can solve even the basic level, establishing GeoILP as a benchmark beyond current ILP capabilities.

## Results

GeoILP is designed to be unsolvable by all existing ILP methods. Popper, the strongest symbolic ILP system, fails to return any hypothesis after a full 1-day search even at the easiest (basic) level, where rules have ≤2 body atoms and ≤20 rules. Difflog, a neuro-symbolic method supporting arbitrary hypothesis spaces, throws an out-of-memory error on a 500 GB server at the basic level (an order of magnitude more memory than the 64 GB used in its original paper). The dataset spans 835 single-tasks and 207 multi-tasks, with predicate arity up to 8, up to 9 body atoms per rule, up to 12 variables, and reference hypotheses containing up to 1,783 rules (single-task) or 3,553 rules (multi-task).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three categories of methods against GeoILP, and all fail. Popper (Cropper & Morel, 2021), the most capable symbolic ILP system supporting recursion, predicate invention, constraints, and noise handling, returns no hypothesis after 24 hours of search even at the basic level (≤2 body atoms, ≤20 rules), which the authors consider two orders of magnitude longer than typical ILP runtimes (hundreds of seconds). Difflog (Si et al., 2019), a neuro-symbolic method with arbitrary hypothesis space, exhausts 500 GB of RAM at the basic level, far exceeding the 64 GB in its original evaluation. The ∂ILP dataset (Evans & Grefenstette, 2018), the only prior synthetic benchmark used by recent neuro-symbolic methods like HRI (Glanois et al., 2022), is dwarfed by GeoILP across every dimension: 20 vs. 835 tasks, arity 1–2 vs. 3–8, max 5 vs. up to 3,553 rules, and limited to dyadic symmetry vs. octadic. Additionally, HRI and similar methods architecturally cannot handle GeoILP because they do not support arity >2, more than 2 body atoms, or target predicates appearing in rule bodies, while template-based methods (Rocktäschel & Riedel, 2017; Campero et al., 2018) require per-task expert-defined rule templates that are impractical at GeoILP's scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

GeoILP extends the line of synthetic ILP dataset construction (e.g., ∂ILP, grammar-learning, and game-based benchmarks) by scaling up orders of magnitude in hypothesis size, predicate arity, and rule count, while covering all major language biases simultaneously. The dataset is synthesized using a deductive-database engine adapted from AlphaGeometry and JGEX, leveraging forward chaining and traceback to produce reference hypotheses — a natural inversion of the deductive process that prior synthetic datasets used at much smaller scale. Conceptually, the paper positions itself against both symbolic ILP (Popper, FOIL, Progol) and neuro-symbolic ILP (∂ILP, HRI, Difflog), arguing that the hand-crafted language bias required by symbolic methods and the restricted hypothesis spaces assumed by neuro-symbolic methods are the two key bottlenecks that a sufficiently large, bias-rich benchmark can expose.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GeoILP provides the first large-scale ILP benchmark with reference hypotheses that is provably unsolvable by all current methods, creating a clear target for the next generation of rule-induction systems and potentially pushing the field toward the expert-free, automatically-discovered language bias paradigm that modern deep learning achieved for feature extraction. The inclusion of image-form background knowledge opens a concrete testbed for joint neural perception and symbolic rule induction, a direction the authors identify as a potential breakthrough for broader AI. Key limitations include the domain bias toward plane geometry, the reliance on an expert-designed deduction engine for synthesis, and the fact that the 'unsolvability' evidence rests on only two baselines (Popper, Difflog) with limited experimental depth, leaving open whether smarter search or architectural changes could close the gap.
