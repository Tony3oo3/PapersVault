## Problem

Despite chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting becoming the de facto method for eliciting reasoning from LLMs, it remains unclear which task types genuinely benefit from this extra deliberation step versus direct answering. The paper addresses this gap by systematically characterizing where CoT helps and why, challenging the prevailing assumption that step-by-step reasoning improves performance across all reasoning tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253394/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation feeds a text question wrapped in either a CoT prompt (e.g., 'Let's think step by step') or a direct answer prompt to 14 contemporary LLMs, and extracts a scored answer (multiple-choice label, short answer, or free response) from the generated output. For the planning/execution decomposition, the LLM first converts the question into a formal specification (a Python program for math or a first-order logic formula for logical reasoning), which is then solved either by the LLM itself or by an external symbolic solver (Python interpreter or Z3). The meta-analysis component ingests reported CoT-versus-direct-answer accuracy deltas from 1,218 experimental comparisons across 110 papers and categorizes them by task type.

## Method

The paper combines a quantitative meta-analysis of 1,218 experimental comparisons from 110 papers (ICLR 2024, EACL 2024, NAACL 2024) with original zero-shot and few-shot experiments on 20 datasets and 14 LLMs comparing CoT versus direct answer prompts. The key analytical contribution is decomposing symbolic reasoning into a planning stage (extracting a formal specification from the question) and an execution stage (deriving the answer), then comparing three execution strategies: the LLM solving directly, the LLM solving with CoT, and an external symbolic solver (PAL with a Python interpreter for math, SatLM with Z3 for logic). This decomposition reveals that CoT's gains come primarily from improving symbolic execution rather than planning, and that tool-augmented solvers consistently outperform CoT for the execution step. The paper further partitions MMLU and MMLU Pro questions by whether the question or generated response contains an equals sign, showing that 95% of CoT's performance gain on MMLU is attributable to math-related questions, with no identifiable features predicting CoT benefit on non-math questions.

## Results

Across 14 models and 20 datasets, CoT yields large gains only on mathematical (avg +27.6 pp, e.g., GSM8K up to +66.9 pp, MATH up to +41.6 pp) and symbolic reasoning (avg +10.9 pp) tasks, while commonsense (+0.9 pp), soft reasoning (+2.0 pp), and knowledge (+4.2 pp) categories show negligible improvement. On MMLU and MMLU Pro, 95% of CoT's total performance gain is attributable to questions containing an equals sign, indicating symbolic operations. In the meta-analysis of 110 papers (1,218 comparisons), the three top-benefiting categories are symbolic/algorithmic (+14.2 pp), math (+12.3 pp), and logical reasoning (+6.9 pp), versus a combined +0.7 pp for all other categories.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares prompt-based CoT against two tool-augmented baselines: PAL (Gao et al., ICML 2023), which executes LM-generated Python plans via a Python interpreter for math tasks (GSM8K, GSM8K-Hard), and SatLM (Ye et al., NeurIPS 2023), which solves LM-generated first-order-logic specifications using the Z3 SMT solver for logical reasoning (ContextHub, FOLIO). In both cases, the Plan + Tool Solver setting substantially outperforms CoT and Plan + CoT Solver across all models, confirming that LLMs are limited in executing and tracking symbolic steps compared to dedicated solvers. CoT does outperform direct answer and Plan + Direct Solver on symbolic tasks, but it is consistently dominated by the tool-augmented approach. The practical implication is that for math and logic problems, pairing an LLM with an external solver at inference time yields consistently better performance than relying on CoT alone, while for non-symbolic tasks (commonsense, reading comprehension, etc.) CoT provides no meaningful advantage over direct answering.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against the prevailing narrative in the CoT prompting literature (Wei et al., 2022; Kojima et al., 2022) that chain-of-thought broadly improves reasoning across task types. It draws on the planning/execution decomposition from tool-augmented LLM work (PAL, SatLM) to attribute CoT's gains to symbolic execution rather than planning. The meta-analysis methodology is novel in this context—no prior work had systematically categorized 1,200+ experimental comparisons across 110 papers by task type to identify where CoT actually helps. The paper repurposes the symbolic vs. non-symbolic distinction from formal reasoning literature to carve out a clear boundary for CoT's utility.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: CoT can be applied selectively (e.g., only when a question contains symbolic operations), maintaining performance while saving inference costs. The finding that CoT underperforms tool-augmented solvers on the very tasks where it helps suggests a ceiling for prompt-based CoT and motivates new paradigms (search, interacting agents, fine-tuned models). Open questions include whether fine-tuning or internalizing CoT can extend benefits to non-symbolic tasks, and whether the planning/execution decomposition generalizes beyond math and FOL. Limitations include not covering long-horizon planning and potential data contamination on some benchmarks.
