## Problem

Previous work showed that statistically independent sources lead to modular representations in biologically constrained autoencoders, but this condition is far too restrictive to explain the wide variety of modular and mixed-selective neurons observed in both brains and trained networks. The paper asks: for an arbitrary dataset with correlated or dependent sources, what are the precise necessary and sufficient conditions that determine whether the optimal biologically-inspired representation will modularize or mix?

## Input / Output

The theory takes a finite sample of source variables (e.g., position and reward coordinates, sinusoidal frequency pairs, or image latent dimensions) and produces a binary determination—modular or mixed—along with the optimal autoencoder weights. The key unusual aspect is that the decision depends on the geometric support of the data (extreme points, convex hull shape) rather than on statistical quantities like mutual information or multiinformation; a single critical datapoint at a corner can flip the outcome. Empirically, the framework is applied to continuous variables, RGB images (Isaac3D), and recurrent dynamics with frequency parameters.

## Method

The paper derives necessary and sufficient inequalities (Theorem 2.1) on a sample of sources that determine whether the optimal nonnegative, energy-efficient linear autoencoder modularizes, by showing that weight loss is always minimized by orthogonalizing (modularizing) and then characterizing when activity loss is also minimized. These inequalities are equivalently restated as a geometric condition: a data-derived ellipse (defined by a matrix F built from pairwise correlations and per-source minima) must lie within the convex hull of the datapoints (Theorem 2.2). A clean corollary identifies 'range independence'—the joint support being rectangular at all extremal corners—as a sufficient condition, which is much weaker than statistical independence. The theory extends to linear RNNs, where the ratio of two output frequencies (irrational, rational non-harmonic, or harmonic) determines whether the recurrent representation modularizes, and empirically generalizes to nonlinear feedforward and recurrent networks trained on supervised, autoencoding, and distillation tasks.

## Results

The paper's central theoretical result (Theorem 2.1) provides necessary and sufficient inequalities on source support that determine whether an optimal biologically-constrained linear autoencoder modularises, independent of the energy tradeoff parameter λ. Empirically, these conditions correctly predict modularisation in a binary sense across many generated datasets (Figure 1c), and the degree to which the inequalities are violated serves as a continuous proxy for how mixed the optimal representation is. In nonlinear feedforward and recurrent networks, cutting corners from the source support (breaking range independence) degrades modularity (measured by CInfoM) far more than equivalently increasing normalised source multiinformation (NSI) via central data removal or correlation introduction while preserving rectangular support (Figure 2a). For linear RNNs, the theory predicts and empirically confirms that irrational frequency ratios modularise, harmonic ratios mix, and rational non-harmonic ratios modularise (Figure 3b).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against competing methods in the traditional sense, as it is primarily a theoretical contribution. However, it explicitly contrasts its range-independence conditions against the statistical independence framework of Whittington et al. (2023), which only applies when sources are mutually independent. The key empirical comparison (Figure 1c, right panel) shows that source multiinformation (a measure of statistical interdependence) fails to predict modularisation across datasets, while the paper's range-based inequalities correctly identify which datasets yield modular representations. In the nonlinear experiments (Figure 2a), the paper demonstrates that corner-cutting (breaking range independence) causes substantially more mixing than introducing the same amount of mutual information via correlation or central data removal (preserving range independence), establishing that range properties—not statistical properties—are the operative determinant of modularity under biological constraints.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Whittington et al. (2023), which showed that statistically independent sources modularise in biologically constrained linear autoencoders, by replacing the independence requirement with a much weaker 'range independence' (rectangular support) condition. It also formalises and extends the grid-cell module emergence arguments of Dorrell et al. (2023) within a unified theoretical framework. The conditions bear resemblance to identifiability results in the matrix factorisation literature (Tatli and Erdogan, 2021a;b), though the problem formulation and the precise inequality differ. The key conceptual departure is that modularity is governed by the geometry of the source support in extremal regions rather than by statistical independence, a distinction that the paper shows is critical for explaining both nonlinear network behaviour and conflicting neuroscience findings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first necessary-and-sufficient conditions for modularisation in biologically constrained networks for arbitrary (not just independent) source distributions, and demonstrates that these conditions generalise empirically to nonlinear feedforward and recurrent networks. It resolves a genuine puzzle in neuroscience—why grid cells modularise in one reward paradigm but mix in another—by identifying range independence as the operative variable, and it warns that some reported 'mixed selectivity' may be an artifact of unanalysed correlated variables. Key limitations include the assumption of perfect reconstruction (low-probability support points can be ignored in practice), the main theorem being restricted to 1D sources and L2 activity norms (though extensions are sketched), and the nonlinear generalisation remaining empirical rather than theoretical. Open questions include developing a less binary notion of modularity, handling imperfect reconstruction tradeoffs, and incorporating biological constraints like connection sparsity and noise.
