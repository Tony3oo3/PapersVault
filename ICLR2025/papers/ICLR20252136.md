## Problem

Object-centric (OC) representations are hypothesized to enable systematic compositional generalization and facilitate reasoning by decomposing scenes into discrete objects, yet these claims have not been thoroughly validated on actual reasoning tasks. Meanwhile, large pre-trained foundation models (e.g., DINOv2, MAE, CLIP) have demonstrated strong zero-shot performance across vision tasks but have never been systematically compared with OC models on tasks requiring compositional understanding. This paper fills that gap by conducting an extensive empirical evaluation of both paradigms on Visual Question Answering, a task that demands accurate reasoning about objects and their relationships.

## Input / Output

The framework takes an RGB image (resized to 224×224 for foundation models, native resolution for dataset-specific OC models) and a natural language question as input. The image is encoded by one of 15 upstream models producing either a global single vector, a fixed-region feature map (flattened to a sequence), or a set of object-centric slot vectors; the question is embedded via a frozen T5-base encoder into token-level embeddings. The output is a probability vector over a small closed set of answers (e.g., yes/no, integers 0–14, or categorical property values), predicted by an MLP on the CLS token of a transformer encoder, rather than generated as free-form text.

## Method

The paper is a large-scale empirical study rather than a new architecture: it trains 684 downstream transformer encoders (2, 5, or 15 layers) across 15 upstream representation models spanning global VAEs, fixed-region CNNs and pre-trained foundation models, and object-centric models (Slot Attention, MONet, SPACE, STEVE, DINOSAURv2, ResNet SA, MultiCNN). The downstream pipeline projects image and text representations to a shared dimension, augments them with modality one-hot indicators, applies sinusoidal positional encoding to text tokens, concatenates the sequences with a learnable CLS token, and passes the result through a transformer encoder followed by a 2-layer MLP classifier. The key technical insight is that applying the OC inductive bias (Slot Attention) on top of a pre-trained foundation model backbone (DINOSAURv2) achieves comparable or better VQA accuracy than the foundation model alone while requiring significantly less downstream compute, effectively combining the rich features of large-scale pre-training with the explicit object structure of OC representations. Experiments span three synthetic datasets (Multi-dSprites, CLEVR6, CLEVRTex) and two real-world benchmarks (VQA-v2, GQA), with additional analyses on property prediction correlation, training-set size effects, and per-question-type consistency.

## Results

Across 684 downstream transformer models (T-2, T-5, T-15) and 15 upstream representation types on 5 datasets, large foundation models (DINOv2, MAE, CLIP) achieve VQA accuracy comparable to or slightly above the best OC models (STEVE, ResNet SA, DINOSAURv2) without any fine-tuning. However, OC models reach similar accuracy with substantially fewer downstream GFLOPs, making them more compute-efficient. DINOSAURv2 (Slot Attention on top of DINOv2) outperforms raw DINOv2 on CLEVRTex, Multi-dSprites, and GQA while requiring significantly less downstream compute, though it is slightly worse on CLEVR due to hyperparameter sensitivity. Global VAE representations remain far behind even with 5× more training steps and 8× more data, and end-to-end CNN/MultiCNN baselines consistently score lowest.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Foundation models (DINOv2, MAE, CLIP) match or slightly exceed the best OC models in raw VQA accuracy on all five datasets, but require substantially larger downstream models (T-15 vs. T-2) and more GFLOPs to extract the relevant information, since their fixed-region representations are less explicit than per-object slots. Among OC models, STEVE and ResNet SA are the strongest on synthetic data, while DINOSAURv2 dominates on CLEVRTex, Multi-dSprites, and GQA by combining DINOv2's rich features with Slot Attention's object decomposition. DINOSAURv2 outperforms raw DINOv2 on CLEVRTex and Multi-dSprites with fewer downstream GFLOPs, but is slightly worse on CLEVR due to hyperparameter sensitivity. Smaller pre-trained models (VQ-AE, KL-AE, ResNet50) consistently underperform both large foundation models and OC models. End-to-end CNN and MultiCNN baselines score lowest across all datasets, and the global VAE representation remains near the question-only baseline even when given 5× more training steps and 8× more data, confirming that single-vector encodings are unsuitable for object-level reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two major research threads: object-centric (OC) representation learning (Slot Attention, MONet, SPACE, STEVE, DINOSAUR) and large-scale foundation models (DINOv2, MAE, CLIP). Prior evaluations of OC representations focused on reconstruction, segmentation, and property prediction (Dittadi et al., 2022; Papa et al., 2022; Yoon et al., 2023), while VQA-based reasoning evaluations existed only in narrow settings (Ding et al., 2021a; Wu et al., 2022; Driess et al., 2023). The paper departs from this lineage by conducting the first systematic, large-scale comparison (684 downstream models, 15 upstream representations, 5 datasets) that directly pits OC models against foundation models on a compositional reasoning task, and by introducing the hybrid DINOSAURv2 approach that applies OC inductive bias on top of a pre-trained foundation model backbone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that applying OC bias to a foundation model (DINOSAURv2) achieves comparable or better VQA performance with significantly less downstream compute than either paradigm alone—provides a concrete, actionable recipe for practitioners balancing representation quality against computational budget. The finding that standard upstream metrics (ARI, MSE) are poor predictors of downstream VQA accuracy challenges the common practice of selecting OC models based on segmentation or reconstruction quality. Key limitations include the restriction to static images (no video), the absence of foundation model fine-tuning, and the use of a relatively simple transformer downstream head; open questions include whether these trade-offs persist with fine-tuned foundation models, video data, and more complex reasoning tasks like causal inference.
