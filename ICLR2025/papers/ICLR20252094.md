## Problem

There is no standard, low-cost guideline for allocating a limited pretraining compute budget across heterogeneous data domains. Existing approaches either require training multiple smaller proxy models to search over data mixtures offline, or use dynamic adjustment methods that still depend on proxy models, both of which add substantial computational overhead and pipeline complexity. This paper asks whether a good data curriculum can be identified cheaply and online, concurrent with the main training run, without any external models or additional data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes per-domain training losses (tracked at each step for all K domains) and a prior domain weight vector μ (typically the natural token distribution estimated by sampling 1000 documents per domain). It produces a time-varying sampling distribution π(t) ∈ Δ^K over the K domains, updated every step but with scaling laws refit only every 1000 steps after a 5000-step warmup. The output distribution is clipped so no domain falls below δ_min = 0.01, and the entire overhead is under 0.4% wallclock time (≈20 minutes on a 3.5-day 1.3B run) since fitting is parallelizable and independent of model size.

## Method

ADO fits a per-domain power-law scaling law L̂_k(n) = ε_k + β_k·n^(−α_k) online during training, where the derivative −dL̂_k/dn decomposes into a learning-speed factor α_k and a reducible-loss factor (L̂_k(n) − ε_k), jointly measuring each domain's local information density. A credit-assignment score λ_k(t) ∝ h_k(t)^s is computed from an exponential moving average of the recent sampling policy, ensuring that a domain only receives credit for loss decreases when it has actually been sampled recently. The preference distribution ρ_k(t) ∝ μ_k · λ_k(t) · α_k · (L̂_k(n) − ε_k) is then blended with a temporal moving average via coefficient γ₂ to produce the final sampling policy, stabilizing updates against noise. Unlike DoReMi or DoGE, ADO requires no proxy model, no validation set, and no modification to the model update rule; it is purely a resampling layer driven by the model's own per-domain loss trajectories, making it architecture- and tokenizer-agnostic by construction.

## Results

On zero-shot downstream benchmarks (7 tasks), ADO achieves the highest average accuracy at both scales: 0.470 at 124M parameters and 0.590 at 1.3B parameters, outperforming all baselines. At 124M, ADO wins on 6 of 7 individual tasks; at 1.3B, it wins on 4 of 7. On validation loss, ADO slightly underperforms the Natural (empirical token) baseline on the Pile validation set but outperforms it on SlimPajama and a 1B-token subset of FineWeb, suggesting ADO implicitly selects higher-quality data. The total additional wallclock cost is under 0.4% (~20 minutes on a 3.5-day 1.3B training run).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ADO outperforms DoReMi (Xie et al., 2024) by 1.5% average at 124M (0.470 vs. 0.455) and 1.5% at 1.3B (0.590 vs. 0.575), while requiring no proxy model training (DoReMi needs ~760 GPU-hours per proxy round). Against ODM (Albalak et al., 2023), ADO leads by 0.9% at 124M (0.470 vs. 0.461); ODM is not evaluated at 1.3B. The original Pile weights (Gao et al., 2020) are the weakest baseline at 124M (0.454), trailing ADO by 1.6%. The most notable finding is that the Natural baseline (empirical token distribution) is surprisingly competitive, ranking second at both scales (0.463 at 124M, 0.585 at 1.3B) and beating ADO on Pile validation loss, though ADO still wins on SlimPajama and FineWeb validation loss. The Balanced (uniform) baseline is competitive at 124M (0.457) but falls further behind at 1.3B (0.557), consistent with the observation that optimal data policies differ across compute scales.

## Relation to Prior Work

ADO repurposes neural scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) in a novel way: rather than extrapolating across completed training runs, it fits per-domain power laws online within a single run to forecast each domain's remaining learning potential. This distinguishes it from DoReMi (Xie et al., 2024) and DoGE (Fan et al., 2023), which require expensive proxy models to determine data mixtures offline, and from ODM (Albalak et al., 2023), which adjusts mixtures online but without scaling-law-based learning-potential estimates. The temporal averaging mechanism draws on Defazio et al. (2024), and the overall framing connects to curriculum learning (Bengio et al., 2009), though ADO operates at the domain level rather than individual examples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ADO offers a practical, near-zero-overhead (<0.4% wallclock) method for dynamic data mixture adjustment that integrates seamlessly into standard training pipelines, lowering the barrier to data optimization for practitioners. The finding that the simple 'Natural' baseline (sampling proportional to token counts) is surprisingly competitive is an important reminder for the field to benchmark against trivial policies. Open questions include whether ADO scales to 8B+ models, how to model inter-domain interactions and learning-rate-schedule effects in the scaling laws, and why per-domain power laws emerge from naturally occurring data distributions. A key limitation is that ADO's task-agnostic objective may underweight domains (e.g., code) that are critical for specific downstream capabilities like reasoning.
