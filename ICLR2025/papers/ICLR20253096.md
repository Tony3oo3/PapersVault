## Problem

Developing a generalizable neural tracking controller for dexterous manipulation from human kinematic references is hindered by the intricate contact dynamics of dexterous hands, noisy and morphologically mismatched human references, and the need to handle diverse objects and skills simultaneously. Existing RL approaches require task-specific rewards that limit generalization, while trajectory optimization depends on precise dynamics models and known contact states. Prior tracking methods are restricted to simple grasping or specific skills, leaving complex in-hand re-orientations, thin-object manipulation, and rich contact variations largely unaddressed.

## Input / Output

The controller receives at each timestep the current hand and object state (22-DoF articulated hand plus 6-DoF object pose), the next goal state from the retargeted kinematic reference, a 256-dimensional PointNet latent encoding of the object geometry, and auxiliary features including state differences and world-space finger positions. The output is a residual position command for all 16 finger joints (plus 6 wrist DoFs), which is added to a running baseline trajectory and fed to a PD controller (gain 20, damping 1). Kinematic references are retargeted from human MANO trajectories via L-BFGS optimization of robot hand DoF to minimize keypoint distance, producing 1,269 (GRAB) and 2,316 (TACO) robot manipulation sequences.

## Method

DexTrack employs a data flywheel that iteratively alternates between training a tracking controller (PPO + action-supervision IL loss) on curated robot tracking demonstrations and mining new demonstrations via a homotopy optimization scheme. The homotopy scheme decomposes a hard tracking problem T₀ into a chain of progressively easier sub-problems (T_K, …, T₀), where each sub-problem uses the tracking result of its 'parent task' as the baseline trajectory for residual policy learning—functioning as a chain-of-thought for trajectory tracking. A conditional diffusion model is trained as a homotopy path generator to efficiently propose effective parent-task sequences without brute-force search. The residual action space (learning deltas relative to a baseline rather than absolute targets) is central to sample efficiency, and the three-stage iterative process (100 → 200 → 400 trajectories) progressively improves both demonstration quality and controller generalization, achieving >10% success rate gains over the best baselines on both GRAB and TACO.

## Results

On the GRAB dataset, DexTrack achieves a tracking success rate of 46.70% (strict threshold) and 65.48% (lenient threshold), with a per-frame object translation error of 4.53 cm and rotation error of 0.3303 rad. On the TACO dataset, it reaches 48.77%/74.38% success rate with 2.10 cm translation error. These represent over 10% absolute improvement in success rate over the strongest baseline (PPO with tracking reward) on both datasets, and the method also achieves the lowest translation error across all evaluated metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DexTrack is compared against three baselines: DGrasp (Christen et al., CVPR 2022), adapted for tracking by solving 10-frame subsequences incrementally; PPO with OmniGrasp's reward (Luo et al., 2024), re-implemented for object trajectory tracking; and PPO with the paper's own tracking reward and observation design but without imitation learning supervision. DexTrack outperforms all baselines on both datasets across every metric. On GRAB, it improves success rate by 8.1 points (strict) and 10.7 points (lenient) over the best baseline (PPO w/o sup.), and reduces translation error from 6.11 cm to 4.53 cm. On TACO, the gains are even larger: 13.8 points (strict) and 16.7 points (lenient) in success rate over PPO w/o sup., with translation error dropping from 4.82 cm to 2.10 cm. DGrasp and OmniGrasp-based PPO are consistently the weakest, confirming that task-specific reward designs and subsequence decomposition limit generalization to complex in-hand manipulations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on kinematic reference tracking for dexterous hands (OmniGrasp, DTC, PHC, D-Grasp), which previously focused on grasping and simple trajectory following, to the harder regime of in-hand re-orientations, thin objects, and functional tool-use. Its key methodological departure is a data flywheel that couples RL+IL controller training with a homotopy optimization scheme (analogous to chain-of-thought) to iteratively mine high-quality per-trajectory demonstrations, rather than relying on task-specific rewards or accurate dynamics models. The conditional diffusion model for homotopy path generation is a novel component not seen in prior tracking or manipulation work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a single generalizable tracking controller can handle diverse manipulation skills (stirring, re-orienting, lifting thin objects) from human references, moving the field closer to a universal low-level controller that decouples task planning from execution. However, absolute success rates remain moderate (~47–49% in simulation, ~50–75% in real world), and the method struggles with brand-new object categories (S3 test set drops to ~10% success). The time cost of the iterative demonstration mining (~4 days on 8 A10 GPUs) and the dependence on a pre-trained object encoder limit immediate scalability, leaving open whether faster approximate homotopy or larger-scale data can close the remaining gap.
