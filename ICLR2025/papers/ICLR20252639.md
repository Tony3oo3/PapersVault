## Problem

Existing neural network interpretability methods predominantly analyze individual neurons and their relationship to final predictions, which is hindered by the polysemantic phenomenon where a single neuron encodes multiple unrelated features. These approaches also lack a systematic way to quantify functional interactions between neurons across layers, and prior circuit constructions rely on manual annotation. NeurFlow addresses this by shifting the unit of analysis from individual neurons to groups of neurons, capturing how collective neuron interactions shape concept processing.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pretrained CNN (ResNet50 or GoogLeNet) and a class-specific image set, which is augmented into patches at three crop sizes (100%, 50%, 25% of original dimensions via sliding window with 50% overlap, yielding ~2500 patches). The output is a hierarchical hypertree per target class where each node is a neuron group concept (NGC) defined by the union of visual features its member neurons encode, and each edge carries a weight quantifying the child group's contribution to the parent. A multimodal LLM (GPT-4o) automatically assigns natural-language labels to each NGC and describes how lower-level features combine to form higher-level ones.

## Method

NeurFlow operates in three automated stages. First, for each target neuron, it identifies its concept (top-k most-activating image patches) and selects core concept neurons from the preceding layer using an Integrated Gradients-based importance score computed over those patches, taking the top-τ by absolute score. Second, it decomposes each neuron's concept into semantic groups via agglomerative clustering on representative activation vectors (mean-pooled feature maps from the preceding layer), then clusters neurons into groups by sharing semantic groups. Third, it constructs a hierarchical concept circuit where edge weights between neuron groups are the sum of normalized IG-based attribution scores across all neuron pairs in the child-parent group pair. The key distinction from prior work is that the fundamental explanatory unit is a neuron group (defined by shared visual features) rather than a single neuron, and the entire pipeline—from core neuron identification through group clustering to circuit construction and labeling—requires no human intervention or predefined concept dictionary.

## Results

NeurFlow's core concept neurons achieve high F1 overlap with NeuronMCT (0.72 on ResNet50, 0.79 on GoogLeNet), confirming that the framework identifies neurons that are also critical to the model's final output. When compared to Cammarata et al.'s L2-weight-based method for identifying core concept neurons of a target neuron, NeurFlow achieves a lower average loss (−0.082 on ResNet50, −0.013 on GoogLeNet), indicating superior identification. The IG-based importance scores maintain a correlation exceeding 0.6 with actual knockout loss for τ=1 across all layers in both models, validating the fidelity of the computed interaction weights. Masking core concept neurons at GoogLeNet's layer 5a reduces model accuracy to nearly 0, while masking an equal number of random neurons has minimal effect.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against NeuronMCT (Khakzar et al., 2021), NeurFlow shows the highest F1 overlap (0.72/0.79), indicating that its core concept neurons largely coincide with those identified as critical to the model output, despite NeurFlow not explicitly optimizing for output-level importance. Against NeuCEPT (Vu et al., 2022), the F1 overlap is lower (0.49/0.56), but qualitative image-debugging experiments (Figures 13–15) show that NeurFlow's neuron groups produce more significant prediction probability changes while using fewer neurons, and their top logit-drop images better match the identified concepts. Against Cammarata et al. (2020)'s L2-weight ranking, NeurFlow achieves a consistently lower knockout loss (−0.082 ResNet50, −0.013 GoogLeNet), demonstrating more effective core concept neuron identification, and additionally generalizes to non-consecutive layers. Among attribution methods for edge-weight quantification (Appendix D.1), Integrated Gradients consistently outperforms LRP, Guided Backpropagation, SmoothGrad, Saliency, and the Knockoff method in correlation with actual knockout loss, while also being faster than SmoothGrad and Gradient Shap. Gradient Shap achieves comparable correlation to IG but at higher computational cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

NeurFlow extends the circuit-discovery and critical-neuron-identification literature (Cammarata et al. 2020; Vu et al. 2022 NeuCEPT; Khakzar et al. 2021 NeuronMCT) by shifting the fundamental unit of analysis from individual neurons to groups of neurons, motivated by the polysemantic neuron problem. It repurposes Integrated Gradients (Sundararajan et al. 2017) as an attribution mechanism for quantifying inter-neuron interactions and combines it with agglomerative clustering to form semantically coherent neuron groups, then constructs a hierarchical concept circuit with quantified edge weights. The use of a multimodal LLM (GPT-4o) for automatic concept labeling and layer-by-layer relation explanation builds on recent LLM-assisted interpretability work (Hoang-Xuan et al. 2024; Kalibhat et al. 2023) but applies it to the group level rather than individual neurons.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By operating at the neuron-group level, NeurFlow offers a more robust and interpretable abstraction that partially mitigates the polysemanticity problem that has limited single-neuron explanations. The fully automated pipeline (no manual circuit construction or predefined concept labels) lowers the barrier to applying circuit-level interpretability to new models and classes. Key limitations include the restriction to CNNs, the reliance on top-k activation as the concept definition, and evaluation limited to ImageNet with ResNet-50 and GoogLeNet; extending the framework to transformers and more flexible activation patterns remains an open direction. The practical applications (bias verification, image debugging) demonstrate utility but the causal claims rest on masking experiments that, while convincing, do not fully isolate group-level effects from individual-neuron contributions.
