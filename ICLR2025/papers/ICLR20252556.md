## Problem

While empirical studies have documented that ML models can amplify dataset biases beyond what separate per-group models would produce, the mechanistic conditions under which amplification emerges remain unclear. No prior theory precisely characterizes how model design choices (parameterization, regularization) interact with data distributional properties (feature count, group imbalance, label noise) to produce or prevent bias amplification across different parameterization regimes.

## Input / Output

The theory takes a two-group multivariate Gaussian mixture as input, specified by group-specific covariance matrices Σ₁, Σ₂, ground-truth weight covariances Θ and Δ, label noise variances σ₁², σ₂², and group proportions p₁, p₂. In the random projections setting, the model size is controlled by the projection dimension m, with the key scaling ratios being φ = d/n (features to samples) and ψ = m/n (parameters to samples). The output is a deterministic bias-variance decomposition of per-group test risk, from which the Observed Difficulty Disparity (ODD), Expected Difficulty Disparity (EDD), and their ratio ADD are computed as closed-form functions of these ratios.

## Method

The paper derives exact high-dimensional limits of per-group test error for ridge regression with random projections (a one-hidden-layer linear network) using operator-valued free probability theory (OVFPT), constructing linear pencils for the relevant resolvent traces and solving the resulting fixed-point equations. The key technical contribution is a novel bias-variance decomposition (Theorem 3.1) for a single model trained on a mixture of two Gaussian groups, expressed through four auxiliary functions hⱼ⁽¹⁾–hⱼ⁽⁴⁾ that capture distinct sources of covariance between sample covariances, the resolvent, and the random projection matrix. The random projection dimension m serves as a proxy for model size, enabling analysis of how over- and underparameterization differentially affect bias amplification. The theory reveals phase transitions in ADD at critical values of φ and ψ (e.g., ψ=1 interpolation threshold, φ=1 feature-sample parity), predicts that bias amplification peaks when features and samples are comparable under overparameterization, and identifies an optimal regularization penalty or training time that avoids amplification. The framework non-trivially generalizes Bach (2023) to the mixture-of-Gaussians setting and is validated on synthetic data and Colored MNIST with a CNN.

## Results

The paper's main contribution is a rigorous bias-variance decomposition (Theorem 3.1) for per-group test risk in ridge regression with random projections, derived via operator-valued free probability theory. The theory predicts that bias amplification (ADD > 1) occurs even with balanced groups (p₁ = p₂ = 1/2) and no spurious correlations, peaking at the interpolation threshold ψ = 1 when φ > 1. On Colored MNIST, the theory correctly predicts bias deamplification: as training time t increases, the ODD of a CNN remains relatively low while the EDD is noticeably higher, with the variance term for the separate green-digit model being approximately 5× larger (0.125V vs. 0.025V) due to its higher label noise (0.25 vs. 0.05 flip probability). The theory also reveals that overparameterization amplifies bias when the number of features is close to the number of samples (φ ≈ 1), and that there exists an optimal regularization penalty or training time to avoid bias amplification in the overparameterized regime.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without direct method-vs-method comparisons; instead, it validates its analytical predictions against prior empirical findings. The theory reproduces and extends the bias amplification observations of Bell & Sagun (2023), who showed that a single model trained on mixed group data can amplify bias beyond separate per-group models, by providing exact high-dimensional limits and identifying the specific parameterization regimes (ψ, φ) where amplification occurs. For minority-group bias, the theory extends Sagawa et al. (2020) and Khani & Liang (2021) by showing that the magnitude of overparameterization-induced disparities depends on both the feature-to-sample rate φ and the fraction of core features π, with interpolation thresholds shifting as these parameters change. Compared to Mannelli et al. (2024) and Jain et al. (2024), who use the non-rigorous replica method and study only isotropic covariance, this work uses rigorous OVFPT and accommodates general simultaneously-diagonalizable covariance structures (including diatomic and power-law spectra), enabling analysis of model size effects that prior work does not capture. The Colored MNIST experiment (a CNN classification task) serves as a semi-synthetic validation, confirming that the theory's prediction of bias deamplification under disparate label noise holds for a complex architecture beyond the linear model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the high-dimensional analysis of random features models (Adlam & Pennington, 2020a; Bach, 2023) from single-Gaussian to mixture-of-Gaussians settings, using operator-valued free probability theory (Mingo & Speicher, 2017) to derive exact bias-variance decompositions for test error disparities between groups. It provides a rigorous theoretical account of the empirical bias amplification findings of Bell & Sagun (2023) and the minority-group bias observations of Sagawa et al. (2020), while going beyond prior high-dimensional bias analyses (Mannelli et al., 2024; Jain et al., 2024) by explicitly characterizing how model size and parameterization regime modulate bias amplification. The framework unifies seemingly disparate phenomena—bias amplification, minority-group bias, and double descent in the EDD setting—under a single analytical lens.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theory yields actionable predictions: there may be an optimal regularization penalty or training time that avoids bias amplification, and overparameterization can actively worsen group disparities when the feature-to-sample ratio is near 1—insights not available from prior empirical studies alone. However, the framework requires approximately Gaussian data, known group labels, and a specific proportionate scaling limit (d/n → φ), limiting direct applicability to real-world settings with missing features or non-Gaussian distributions. Key open questions include extending the analysis to nonlinear networks via NTK or lazy-regime Gaussian equivalents, accommodating different scaling limits, and developing robust estimation procedures for the covariance and noise parameters that the theory requires, especially for under-represented groups.
