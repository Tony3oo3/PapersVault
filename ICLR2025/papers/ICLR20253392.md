## Problem

Despite growing interest in LLM-based research agents, no prior work has performed a statistically rigorous, large-scale comparison between LLM-generated and human-expert-generated research ideas under controlled conditions. Existing evaluations rely on small reviewer pools, LLM-as-a-judge surrogates, or constrained problem spaces, leaving it unclear whether current LLMs can produce ideas that are genuinely novel at the expert level.

## Input / Output

The agent takes a predefined research topic description (one of seven NLP prompting topics), retrieves up to 120 relevant papers via Semantic Scholar API function-calling, and prepends the titles and abstracts of the top 10 reranked papers along with six hand-crafted demonstration examples to the generation prompt. It produces 4000 short seed ideas per topic, which are deduplicated (cosine similarity threshold 0.8 via all-MiniLM-L6-v2, retaining ~5%) and expanded into full structured proposals following a seven-section template (title, problem statement, motivation, proposed method, experiment plan, test case examples, fallback plan). All proposals—human and AI—are passed through an LLM style-normalization module to eliminate writing-style confounders before blind review by 79 expert NLP researchers.

## Method

The ideation agent follows a minimalist three-stage pipeline: (1) retrieval-augmented grounding via iterative Semantic Scholar API calls (KeywordQuery, PaperQuery, GetReferences) with LLM-based reranking on relevance, empiricism, and inspiration potential; (2) massive overgeneration of 4000 seed ideas per topic using Claude-3.5-Sonnet, with retrieval augmentation applied to half the generations and previously generated titles appended to discourage repetition; and (3) ranking via a Swiss-system tournament of pairwise comparisons, where Claude-3.5-Sonnet (zero-shot, 71.4% accuracy on ICLR 2024 accept/reject prediction) judges which of two proposals is better over N=5 rounds. The key design insight is that inference-time scaling through overgeneration and reranking can surface high-quality ideas from a large candidate pool, though the paper empirically shows this is bottlenecked by LLMs' limited idea diversity (~200 unique ideas out of 4000) and unreliable self-evaluation (best LLM ranker at 53.3% vs. 56.1% human inter-reviewer agreement). The experimental protocol itself is a major contribution: 49 expert researchers write ideas on self-selected topics, the LLM generates matched-topic ideas, and 79 reviewers perform blind reviews under a standardized form with four anchored metrics (novelty, excitement, feasibility, expected effectiveness) plus an overall score.

## Results

Across 298 blind reviews by 79 expert NLP researchers, AI-generated ideas are rated significantly more novel than human expert ideas (5.64 vs. 4.84, p < 0.01) and more exciting (5.19 vs. 4.55, p < 0.05), while being slightly lower on feasibility (6.34 vs. 6.61, p = 1.00) and comparable on expected effectiveness (5.47 vs. 5.13, p = 0.67). Adding human expert reranking on top of the LLM agent further boosts novelty to 5.81 (p < 0.001) and overall score to 5.34 (p < 0.05). These findings hold robustly across three different statistical tests (per-review, per-idea, and per-reviewer analyses) with Bonferroni correction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between three conditions: human expert ideas (49 ideas from 49 NLP researchers), AI-generated ideas (top-ranked by a Claude-3.5-Sonnet pairwise ranker using a Swiss-system tournament over ~200 deduplicated ideas from 4000 seed generations), and AI ideas + human rerank (top-ranked by the first author). AI ideas win significantly on novelty (+0.80) and excitement (+0.64) but lose slightly on feasibility (−0.27, not significant). The AI + Human Rerank condition outperforms both on novelty and overall score, suggesting human curation adds value. In the secondary LLM-as-judge comparison (Table 6), the paper's own Claude-3.5 pairwise ranker achieves only 53.3% consistency with human expert scores, below the inter-reviewer agreement of 56.1%, and well below the 'AI Scientist' reviewer (43.3%) and GPT-4o direct scoring (50.0%), indicating that current LLMs cannot yet reliably evaluate research ideas.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior research-agent works (Baek et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024) that evaluate LLM ideation using small reviewer pools, constrained idea formats, or LLM-as-a-judge proxies, by instead performing the first large-scale (100+ NLP researchers) blind human comparison with strict controls on topic distribution, writeup format, and style normalization. Methodologically, it repurposes RAG (Lewis et al., 2020) for paper retrieval, inference-time scaling via overgeneration (Brown et al., 2024; Li et al., 2022) for idea generation, and a Swiss-system pairwise tournament for ranking, but the core contribution is the evaluation protocol rather than any single component. The paper explicitly positions itself as complementary to methods-centric works, providing a standardized benchmark and human expert baselines that prior studies lacked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first statistically significant evidence that LLM-generated research ideas can be rated as more novel than human expert ideas (p<0.05), while simultaneously revealing that LLMs produce only ~200 unique ideas out of 4000 generated (a diversity bottleneck) and cannot serve as reliable evaluators (53.3% vs. 56.1% human-human agreement). These findings challenge the optimistic narrative around inference-time scaling for research ideation and the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm that underpins many prior 'AI Scientist' systems. Key open questions remain: whether the novelty advantage persists when human experts submit their best (rather than median) ideas, whether AI ideas can be successfully executed into full projects, and whether the results generalize beyond prompting-based NLP research to other scientific domains.
