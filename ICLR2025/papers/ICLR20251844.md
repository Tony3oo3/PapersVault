## Problem

Fine-tuning LLMs for task-specific capabilities (e.g., mathematical reasoning) in non-English languages is severely limited by the scarcity of in-language task data, and standard cross-lingual transfer from English achieves low performance in lower-resource languages. Existing model merging approaches like model souping and TIES-merging suffer from negative interference when combining language and task expertise, failing to consistently outperform individual experts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251844/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two fine-tuned checkpoints of the same base architecture (LLaMA 3.1 8B, 32 transformer layers): a 'math expert' SFT'd on English math word problems and a 'language expert' SFT'd on generic multi-task instruction data in the target language. It produces a single merged checkpoint by selectively replacing transformer layers from one expert with those from the other, while averaging non-transformer parameters (input/output embeddings, final linear layer). The operation is purely a state-dictionary manipulation requiring no forward or backward passes.

## Method

Parameter-level analysis of SFT deltas reveals that language-specific updates concentrate in the top and bottom transformer layers (attention parameters especially), while math reasoning updates concentrate in the middle layers, particularly the second half of the model. Layer swapping exploits this separability by replacing the bottom 5 and top 2 transformer layers of the math expert with the corresponding layers from the language expert, optionally inserting transition zones (weighted averages of both experts' layers) at the boundaries to smooth the interface. The configuration is flexible—effective ranges span 3–5 bottom layers, 0–2 top layers, and 0–2 transition layers per side—with lower-resource languages generally benefiting from more swapped layers. The entire procedure is post-hoc, training-free, and reduces to iterating over the state dictionary with three operations: keep, replace, or average.

## Results

On MGSM (8-shot), layer swapping achieves average scores of 32.4 (Swahili), 22.7 (Telugu), 37.1 (Bengali), and 38.5 (Japanese) with the default configuration, and up to 37.2, 27.6, 45.2, and 43.2 with the best configuration across 9 expert pairs. These results outperform the base LLaMA 3.1 8B (24.8, 12.0, 29.2, 33.6) and the individual math experts (29.5, 20.1, 38.3, 42.7) by approximately 10% on average across the four target languages. For Swahili, the best layer-swapped model (37.2) also exceeds the joint SFT baseline trained on mixed Swahili+math data (32.8 max) by 4.4 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Layer swapping consistently outperforms model souping (Wortsman et al., 2022) in both average and maximum MGSM scores across all four languages; for example, on Swahili the best layer-swapped model reaches 37.2 vs. 32.0 for model soup, and on Telugu 27.6 vs. 26.4. Against TIES-merging (Yadav et al., 2023), evaluated only for Swahili, layer swapping achieves 32.4/32.8 (avg/best) compared to TIES-merging's 29.5 (avg) and 32.4 (best), showing a clear advantage in both consistency and ceiling. Notably, for Swahili, the best layer-swapped model (37.2) also surpasses the joint SFT baseline that mixes Swahili and math data in a single training run (32.8 max), indicating that post-hoc merging of separately trained experts can exceed what is achievable by extended fine-tuning on combined data. The one exception is Japanese, where the average layer-swap score (38.5) falls slightly below the math expert average (42.7), though the best layer-swapped configuration (40.1) still exceeds model soup (38.7) and the base model (33.6).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model merging literature (model souping, TIES-merging) by shifting the granularity of composition from individual weights to entire transformer layers, motivated by interpretability analyses showing that language-specific representations concentrate in the top and bottom layers while task-specific (math) reasoning updates concentrate in the middle layers. It departs from composable sparse fine-tuning (Ansell et al., 2022), which requires retraining with learned masks, by being fully post hoc and requiring no additional training. The method also builds on the 'LLMs think in English' line of work (Wendler et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2024), which identifies the first and last layers as the multilingual interface, and repurposes this finding as a practical merging strategy rather than a diagnostic tool.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Layer swapping offers a cheap, post hoc recipe for transferring task capabilities (here, math) to lower-resource languages without any in-language task data, outperforming both individual experts and standard merging methods by ~10% on MGSM across four languages. The finding that layer-level composition outperforms weight-level methods (souping, TIES) suggests that preserving the internal coherence of linear transformations matters more than fine-grained magnitude-based selection, which could reshape how practitioners approach model merging broadly. Key open questions include whether the method generalizes beyond LLaMA 3.1 8B, beyond math reasoning, to more divergent experts, and to truly low-resource languages where the base model has minimal capability. The paper also raises the possibility of modular multilingual architectures where language 'interfaces' are swapped independently of task-specific middle layers.
