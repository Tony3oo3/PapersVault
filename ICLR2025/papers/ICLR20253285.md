## Problem

Mechanistic interpretability research has largely focused on Transformers, leaving open whether discovered features and circuits generalize to other architectures such as State Space Models (Mamba). This paper tackles the Architectural Universality question: do Transformer and Mamba models converge to similar internal representations and implement analogous computational circuits, or do they implement fundamentally different algorithms?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253285/cropped_page9_idx10.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes residual-stream activations from Pythia-160M (12 Transformer layers) and Mamba-130M (24 SSM layers), both with hidden dimension D=768 and trained on the Pile. SAEs are trained with expansion factor F=32D=24576 features per layer. For circuit analysis, the method uses 128 randomly generated induction sequences of the form [A][B]...[A] at varying distances (8–128 tokens). Outputs include per-feature MPPC similarity scores (asymmetric, Pythia→Mamba), layer-wise depth-matching statistics, GPT-4o complexity (1–4) and monosemanticity (1–5) scores, and path-patching logit-difference tables identifying critical SSM states and convolution inputs.

## Method

The paper trains vanilla SAEs on the residual stream after each block in both models, then computes the Max Pairwise Pearson Correlation (MPPC) between every Pythia SAE feature and all Mamba SAE features across 1M SlimPajama tokens. To isolate the architectural contribution, two skylines are established: a model-seed variant (different Pythia initialization) and an SAE-seed variant (different SAE training on the same model), with the MPPC difference interpreted as the marginal effect of architecture. GPT-4o is used to auto-score feature complexity and monosemanticity, revealing that high-MPPC pairs are simple token-level features while complex features show lower but still significant cross-architecture correlation. For circuit-level analysis, path patching is applied to Mamba's SSM states and convolution inputs on induction tasks, revealing that Mamba implements the same induction algorithm as Transformers but with an 'Off-by-One' motif: the local convolution layer writes token B's information into the SSM state at position B+1 rather than at B itself, a structural nuance absent in Transformer attention.

## Results

The paper's headline finding is that SAE features exhibit strong cross-architecture similarity between Pythia-160M (Transformer) and Mamba-130M (SSM): the mean Max Pairwise Pearson Correlation (MPPC) is 0.681, with over 55% of SAE features exceeding MPPC > 0.7. This is dramatically higher than neuron-level comparisons, where fewer than 15% of neurons achieve MPPC > 0.4. The cross-architecture MPPC (0.681) is close to the model-seed-variant skyline (0.725) and SAE-seed-variant skyline (0.806), with 90% of features showing an MPPC difference of only −0.23 to +0.07 relative to the seed variant. At the circuit level, path patching identifies layer 17 SSM states as critical for induction in Mamba, with patching c_{B₁+1}^{(17)} producing logit drops of −9.7 to −16.8, confirming a structurally analogous induction circuit to Transformers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper establishes two baselines: a random SAE baseline (matching feature count and sparsity) and a neuron-level baseline. SAE features dramatically outperform both: the cross-arch SAE MPPC mean (0.681) exceeds the 99th percentile of the random baseline, and SAE features show >55% with MPPC > 0.7 versus <15% of neurons reaching even MPPC > 0.4. Against the two skylines (model seed variant at 0.725 and SAE seed variant at 0.806), the cross-architecture result falls slightly short, indicating that architectural change introduces a small but measurable representational shift. More than 60% of features have a near-zero MPPC difference (|Δ| < 0.05) relative to the model seed variant, meaning the vast majority of features are consistently present despite the architecture change. Robustness is confirmed across L1 regularization strengths (1e-4, 2e-4, 4e-4), dictionary sizes (32×, 64×), three datasets (SlimPajama, RedPajama-Github, OpenWebText), SAE variants (vanilla, Top-k), model scales (160M, 2.8B), and a third architecture (RWKV-169M), all showing consistent MPPC patterns.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Universality Hypothesis in mechanistic interpretability from within-architecture comparisons (different seeds, sizes) to a fundamentally cross-architecture setting (Transformer vs. Mamba/SSM). It repurposes Sparse Autoencoders—previously used for monosemantic feature extraction within a single model—as a cross-architecture comparison tool, adapting the neuron-correlation methodology of Gurnee et al. (2024) to SAE features. The circuit analysis builds directly on the induction head framework of Olsson et al. (2022) and the path-patching methodology of Wang et al. (2023), applying them for the first time to Mamba's SSM-based computation. Prior Mamba interpretability work (Ali et al., 2024; Sharma et al., 2024) identified implicit attention-like behavior but did not perform structured circuit discovery or cross-architecture feature matching.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the majority of SAE features are highly similar across Transformer and Mamba architectures provides preliminary evidence that mechanistic interpretability findings in Transformers may generalize to SSM-based models, reducing the interpretability burden for the growing Mamba ecosystem. The off-by-one motif—a specific, previously unidentified architectural nuance where Mamba's local convolution delays information writing by one token position—is a genuinely new observation that may inform future Mamba design and analysis. Key limitations include the qualitative (non-quantitative) nature of the circuit comparison, reliance on SAEs which may miss non-linear or rare features, and primary experiments on small models (160M/130M). Open questions include why the off-by-one preference arises, whether it persists at scale, and whether automated circuit comparison tools can be developed for SSM architectures.
