## Problem

Dense Training is widely assumed to be the de facto approach for maximizing model robustness against image corruption, while Dynamic Sparse Training (DST) is viewed primarily as an efficiency tool. This paper identifies a literature gap: the robustness of DST models under various image corruptions has been systematically overlooked, and it challenges the assumption by proposing the DSCR Hypothesis that DST at low sparsity levels (10%–50%) can consistently outperform Dense Training in corruption robustness without additional resource cost.

## Input / Output

Models are trained on clean RGB images (CIFAR-10/100 at 32×32, TinyImageNet at 64×64, ImageNet at 224×224) or video clips (UCF101) and produce class labels (or segmentation masks for the FCN-VGG16 experiment). Robustness is evaluated by testing on synthetically corrupted counterparts (e.g., CIFAR-100-C, ImageNet-C, ImageNet-3DCC) spanning 10–19 corruption types at 4–5 severity levels, as well as on domain-shift benchmarks (ImageNet-R, ImageNet-v2). The key evaluation protocol is train-clean / test-corrupted, measuring the accuracy drop under distribution shift.

## Method

The paper conducts a comprehensive empirical study comparing four DST algorithms (SET, RigL, MEST, GraNet) against Dense Training across five architecture families (VGG, ResNet, EfficientNet, DeiT, 3D ConvNet/I3D) and nine dataset scenarios. The core mechanism is that the dynamic remove-regrow process in DST acts as an implicit regularization: spatially, it concentrates non-zero weights on specific filters while zeroing out others, reducing connectivity to less important features; spectrally, it causes the model to attend less to high-frequency components, making it inherently less sensitive to noise-type corruptions rich in high-frequency content. The authors validate this through Fourier-domain frequency attenuation experiments (showing DST models degrade less under high-frequency removal) and Grad-CAM visualizations (showing tighter attention regions). The finding holds across random and gradient-based regrowth strategies, fixed/soft/flexible memory budgets, and both CNN and transformer architectures, with DST winning in all 9 evaluated scenarios.

## Results

Across all 9 evaluation scenarios (spanning images and videos, CNNs and transformers, 6 corruption benchmarks), every DST method (RigL, MEST, GraNet) outperforms Dense Training in robustness accuracy — a 9-for-9 sweep. Headline gains include +0.91% on CIFAR100-C (ResNet34: 52.56 vs. 51.65), +1.08% on TinyImageNet-C (EfficientNet-B0: 16.50 vs. 15.42), +1.06% on ImageNet-C (ResNet50: 38.72 vs. 38.38), and +2.72% on UCF101 video (I3D: 56.30 vs. 53.58). At sparsity 0.1–0.4, DST models also save at least 40% of training FLOPs and parameter count while improving robustness, with the largest gains observed on high-frequency noise corruptions (e.g., ~25% relative improvement for MEST_g at severity 5 on impulse/Gaussian noise).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is DST (in four algorithmic variants) versus standard Dense Training as the de facto robustness baseline. All four DST algorithms — SET (Mocanu et al., 2018; random regrow), RigL (Evci et al., 2020; gradient-based regrow), MEST (Yuan et al., 2021; soft memory budget), and GraNet (Liu et al., 2021; flexible memory budget) — consistently outperform Dense Training across every one of the 9 scenarios tested, with no scenario where Dense Training wins. The most recent DST method, CHTs (Zhang et al., 2024), further extends this trend in Appendix A.6, achieving 27.38% on Fashion-MNIST-C at 95% sparsity versus 19.82% for Dense. Gains are largest on high-frequency noise corruptions (impulse, Gaussian, shot noise) and at higher severity levels, where MEST_g shows nearly 25% relative improvement over Dense at severity 5. On video data (UCF101), DST advantages are particularly pronounced: GraNet_g improves I3D robustness by 2.72 points (55.21 vs. 53.58) and 3D ResNet50 by 2.43 points (53.57 vs. 51.14). The paper also shows that DST complements data augmentation (Mixup), with SET+Mixup reaching 54.93% on CIFAR100-C versus 54.75% for Dense+Mixup, and that DST models generalize better to OOD data (ImageNet-R: 40.04% for RigL vs. 37.84% Dense) and segmentation tasks (Pascal VOC 2012-C mIoU: 23.09% for SET at s=0.1 vs. 22.60% Dense).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by repurposing Dynamic Sparse Training (DST) methods—SET, RigL, MEST, GraNet—from their original framing as efficiency/scalability tools into a robustness-enhancing training paradigm. While prior work has examined the robustness effects of post-hoc pruning (Lottery Ticket Hypothesis) and static sparse networks with increased capacity, the specific question of whether the dynamic remove-regrow process during training implicitly regularizes models against image corruption had not been systematically studied. The spectral analysis builds on Yin et al. (2019) and Saikia et al. (2021), who showed that neural networks rely on low-frequency components, but extends this to explain *why* DST models are less sensitive to high-frequency corruptions through the lens of dynamic weight allocation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This finding challenges the default practitioner assumption that dense training is necessary for maximum corruption robustness, offering a free (or even cost-reducing) robustness improvement at low-to-moderate sparsity levels (10–50%). The spectral-domain explanation—that DST implicitly shifts attention away from high-frequency components—provides a mechanistic account that could inform the design of future robustness-aware sparse training methods. Key open questions include: whether the advantage persists at high sparsity (>70%), whether it can be amplified by co-designing DST with existing robustness techniques (e.g., AugMix, PRIME), and whether a theoretical account of the implicit regularization can be formalized. A practical limitation is that the computational savings remain theoretical on current hardware, as sparse-aware inference is not yet widely deployed.
