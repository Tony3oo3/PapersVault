## Problem

Knowledge distillation for large language models is hampered by the substantial capacity gap between teacher and student, which manifests as mode averaging (student oversmooths the teacher's rich output distributions) and mode collapse (student latches onto only dominant modes). Existing KD methods optimize toward a fixed teacher distribution throughout training, failing to adapt to the student's evolving capabilities and exacerbating the curse of capacity gap where larger teachers can paradoxically degrade student performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253323/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

TAID consumes the teacher model's logits and the student model's detached logits at each training step over token sequences, and produces an updated student model that outputs next-token probability distributions over the vocabulary. The interpolation between teacher and student is performed at the logit level (before softmax) to preserve relative confidence between predictions, and the student logits are detached so gradients flow only through the student in the KL denominator. For the vision-language variant, RGB images are additionally processed through a vision encoder before the language modeling objective is applied.

## Method

TAID constructs a time-dependent intermediate distribution p_t by linearly interpolating between the student's detached logits and the teacher's logits, then applying softmax to obtain a target distribution; the KL divergence between p_t and the student's distribution is the training objective. The interpolation parameter t starts near 0 (student-dominated, resembling self-distillation) and increases to 1 (teacher-dominated) over training, with an adaptive update mechanism that modulates the rate of increase based on the relative change in the objective function smoothed by momentum—allowing aggressive early progress when the student is close to the target and gradual refinement as the student approaches teacher complexity. A theoretical analysis using a least-squares regression proxy (borrowed from Mobahi et al.) proves that TAID avoids the mode collapse that inevitably occurs in self-distillation, provided the teacher signal norm is at least Ω(√(Tε)) where T is the number of training steps. This dynamic bridge mechanism is the key distinction from Skew KL, which uses a fixed intermediate distribution and transfers teacher knowledge into it rather than transferring the intermediate distribution into the student.

## Results

On instruction tuning (MT-Bench), TAID achieves 4.05, 4.27, and 3.05 across three teacher-student pairs (Phi-3-mini→TinyLlama, Llama-2→TinyLlama, StableLM→Pythia), outperforming the best baseline (DKD) by up to 1.35 points. On continued pre-training (Open LLM Leaderboard), TAID achieves the highest average score of 40.10 across six tasks, surpassing KL (39.91) and Skew KL (39.84). TAID-LLM-1.5B sets a new state-of-the-art for sub-2B LLMs with a LightEval score of 52.27, while TAID-VLM-2B achieves 56.43 on Open-VLM-Leaderboard, outperforming all VLMs up to 4B parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TAID consistently outperforms all 10 baselines across all three teacher-student pairs in instruction tuning. Against the strongest baselines, TAID beats DKD (best image-classification method) by 1.35, 0.13, and 0.15 points respectively, and DistiLLM (best SGO-based method) by 0.82, 0.30, and 0.08 points. In pre-training, TAID's 40.10 average surpasses KL (39.91), Skew KL (39.84), and GJS (39.81), with the largest gap on ARC (45.48 vs. 44.97). TAID also demonstrates robustness to capacity gap: as teacher size increases from 410M to 6.9B, TAID's LAMBADA score improves monotonically (20.82→22.01) while Skew KL degrades (18.65→18.20). For the deployed models, TAID-LLM-1.5B (52.27 LightEval) surpasses SmolLM-1.7B (51.31) and StableLM-2-1.6B (51.24), and TAID-VLM-2B (56.43 Open-VLM-LB) outperforms InternVL2-2B (53.96) and Phi-3-Vision (53.60). Notably, TAID achieves these gains without student-generated outputs, training ~2× faster than DistiLLM and ~10× faster than GKD.

## Relation to Prior Work

TAID extends the standard KL-divergence knowledge distillation framework of Hinton et al. (2015) by replacing the fixed teacher target with a time-dependent interpolated distribution that blends detached student logits and teacher logits. Conceptually, it sits between self-distillation (which the paper shows theoretically collapses) and full teacher distillation, and differs from Skew KL (Ko et al., 2024) in both the direction of knowledge flow (intermediate→student vs. teacher→intermediate) and the dynamic nature of the interpolation parameter. The theoretical non-collapse analysis borrows the interpolation-regression framework from Mobahi et al. (2020), adapting it to show that the time-varying teacher signal prevents the mode collapse that plagues self-distillation. Unlike SGO-based methods (GKD, DistiLLM), TAID achieves competitive performance without on-policy sampling, and unlike image-classification KD methods (CTKD, DKD), it is specifically designed for the high-entropy, long-tail distributions characteristic of language modeling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TAID addresses the increasingly critical capacity gap problem as frontier LLMs grow larger while deployment constraints demand smaller models, and it does so with a simple, computationally efficient mechanism (no SGOs, ~10× faster than GKD) that is easy to adopt. The development of SOTA compact models (TAID-LLM-1.5B, TAID-VLM-2B) demonstrates practical impact, and the theoretical non-collapse guarantee provides a principled justification absent in prior self-distillation or fixed-interpolation approaches. Open questions include whether TAID can be combined with on-policy distillation for further gains, how it extends to multi-teacher settings, and whether non-linear or task-adaptive interpolation schedules could improve performance. A limitation is that the theoretical analysis relies on a one-dimensional regression proxy rather than the full multi-categorical language modeling objective, and the SOTA model results depend on specific teacher-student pairs that may not generalize to all architectures.
