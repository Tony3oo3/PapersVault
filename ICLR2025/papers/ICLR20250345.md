## Problem

Existing benchmarks for evaluating LLMs on formal language tasks (autoformalization, logical reasoning) rely on static, human-annotated datasets that are susceptible to benchmark contamination and use syntactic metrics (e.g., BLEU) that fail to capture semantic correctness. There is no scalable, autonomous method to generate out-of-distribution evaluation data and rigorously verify whether an LLM preserves truth across natural-language-to-formal-language translations without expensive human labeling.

## Input / Output

The system takes a formal language (FL) expression (propositional logic, first-order logic, 3-CNF, or regular expression) generated from a parameterized context-free grammar, a vocabulary mapping symbols to natural-language terms, and an LLM to evaluate. It produces a truth maintenance score (fraction of FL strings where the round-trip (A∘I)(φ) yields a semantically equivalent φ′), a syntactic compliance rate, an F1 score when the LLM is used as an equivalence verifier, and a predictive power score quantifying how well AutoEval performance ranks LLMs relative to other benchmarks. The evaluation is inherently a round-trip: the LLM informalizes φ to NL, then autoformalizes that NL back to FL, and a formal verifier (Prover9 or Z3) checks semantic equivalence between the original and returned FL strings.

## Method

The key insight is that semantic equivalence of the two FL endpoints (original φ and round-tripped φ′) can be checked by a formal verifier without ever needing to evaluate the quality of the intermediate natural-language description, sidestepping the need for human annotation of NL translations. Datasets are generated dynamically by sampling context-free grammars with controlled branching factor and tree depth, guaranteeing coverage of all representable expressions while producing arbitrarily large, out-of-distribution data that resists memorization. The false-positive probability of the evaluation is bounded as (1−p_I)^n(1−p_A)^n·p_H^n, which decreases with the number of round-trip iterations n, making the metric increasingly reliable as LLMs improve. The system is plug-and-play: any new LLM can be evaluated by feeding it the generated FL strings and vocabulary, with Prover9/Z3 handling equivalence checking and NLTK/Reg2DFA handling parsing of LLM outputs. Five pre-packaged datasets (~85k unique examples, ~170k with prompts) cover propositional logic, first-order logic (synthetic and English vocabularies from VerbNet/Faker), 3-CNF, and regular expressions, parameterized by operator count or CFG tree depth.

## Results

Across 17 SOTA LLMs evaluated on AutoEval, no model exceeds 50% truth-maintenance accuracy on logic expressions with more than 20 operators, and even SOTA Large Reasoning Models (OpenAI o1, DeepSeek R1) fail to maintain truth effectively. On the calibration dataset 3-CNF(12), GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini achieve near-perfect syntactic compliance and accuracy, confirming prompt sufficiency. The calibrated AutoEval score exhibits a strong positive Pearson correlation (ρ ≥ 0.7) with FOLIO, HumanEval, and LogiEval (propositional logic), and a moderate correlation (0.5 ≤ ρ < 0.7) with LogiEval (FOL), attributed to dataset imbalance (80% positive class). Only 0.66% of FOL verification results experienced a prover timeout.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AutoEval is positioned as a scalable surrogate for five existing static benchmarks: FOLIO, LogiEval, HumanEval, and Big Bench Hard. The calibrated AutoEval score achieves strong correlation (ρ ≥ 0.7) with FOLIO, HumanEval, and LogiEval-PL, demonstrating that it can predict LLM rankings on these benchmarks without requiring hand-annotated data. The correlation with LogiEval-FOL is weaker (0.5 ≤ ρ < 0.7), which the authors trace to dataset imbalance (80% positive samples) and difficulty skew rather than a fundamental limitation of AutoEval. Compared to BLEU-based metrics, AutoEval is more robust: a single negation flip (e.g., 'raining' → 'not raining') still yields BLEU = 0.74 but fails truth maintenance, whereas AutoEval correctly flags the error. The paper also shows that AutoEval outperforms random-chance as a predictor of BLEU scores, confirming it captures semantic content rather than surface form. Unlike FOLIO and LogiEval, which rely on fixed human-curated datasets susceptible to contamination, AutoEval generates unlimited out-of-distribution examples via CFGs, and unlike HumanEval, it uses formal verifiers (Prover9, Z3) rather than incomplete test cases for correctness checking.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the autoformalization/informalization task (Wu et al., 2022; Liang et al., 2023) as an evaluation methodology rather than a task to solve, introducing a round-trip composition (A∘I) with formal verifier checking as a truth maintenance metric. It departs from static benchmarks (HumanEval, FOLIO, LogiEval) that are vulnerable to contamination by using context-free grammars to generate unbounded, out-of-distribution datasets, and from prior verifier-based autoformalization work (LeanEuclid, DTV, LINC, Logic-LM) that relied on hand-coded datasets. The key conceptual novelty is treating the LLM's ability to invert its own translations as the evaluation signal, eliminating the need for human-annotated ground truth pairs while retaining soundness through theorem provers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even SOTA reasoning models (o1, R1) fail to maintain truth in formal language translation at moderate complexity (no LLM exceeded 50% for >20 operators) is practically significant for applications in specification synthesis and code generation where semantic fidelity is critical. The demonstrated predictive power (ρ≥0.7) of AutoEval scores over FOLIO, LogiEval, and HumanEval positions it as a scalable surrogate benchmark that can be regenerated for any new LLM without human annotation. Key limitations include the undecidability of FOL equivalence (mitigated by timeout, affecting 0.66% of results) and the restriction to formal syntaxes with decidable equivalence checkers; extending the framework to undecidable or semi-formal domains (e.g., conversational AI, image generation) remains open.
