## Problem

Prior work has established that retraining only the last-layer classifier on OOD data is sufficient for robust generalization, implicitly assuming the penultimate layer captures all relevant information. This paper questions that assumption by asking whether intermediate layers, which have not been examined for this purpose, might actually contain more shift-robust representations for OOD generalization.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images at dataset-specific resolutions (32×32 for CIFAR, 224×224 for ImageNet variants and CelebA) and outputs a single class label. The key operational detail is that the full DNN backbone is frozen; only a linear probe (weight matrix W_l and bias b_l) at a chosen intermediate layer is trained, and at inference the network is truncated to that layer, discarding all subsequent layers. Layer selection requires a small held-out OOD validation set, which is the only OOD data needed in the zero-shot setting.

## Method

Intermediate Layer Classifiers (ILCs) train a linear probe at each intermediate layer l (1 ≤ l ≤ L−2) of a frozen pre-trained ResNet or ViT, using either OOD samples (few-shot) or ID samples only (zero-shot). The best layer is selected by validation accuracy on a small OOD set, and inference simply computes the representation at that layer and applies the corresponding linear map. The paper's central empirical finding is that intermediate layers—particularly the pen-penultimate layer—consistently outperform the penultimate layer for OOD accuracy across nine datasets and four shift types. Analysis via mean pairwise distance ratios and Total Variation Distance shows that intermediate layer features are measurably less sensitive to distribution shifts than penultimate layer features, especially for underrepresented subpopulations, which explains the generalization gap.

## Results

In the zero-shot setting (no OOD data for probe training), ILCs achieve 87.1% worst-group accuracy (WGA) on Waterbirds and 82.0% WGA on CelebA, outperforming last-layer retraining (DFR) by 7.7 and 26.0 percentage points, respectively. In the few-shot setting, ILCs reach 93.0% on Waterbirds, 89.2% on CelebA, and 79.0% on MultiCelebA (using only 20% of OOD data), consistently matching or exceeding DFR. The advantage of intermediate layers is most pronounced when OOD data is scarce: at π=0.03 (3% of OOD samples), ILCs gain +5.7, +3.4, and +27.0 percentage points over last-layer retraining on Waterbirds, CelebA, and MultiCelebA, respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ILCs consistently outperform DFR (Kirichenko et al., 2023) in both few-shot and zero-shot settings; the gap is dramatic in zero-shot (CelebA: 82.0% vs. 56.0%; Waterbirds: 87.1% vs. 79.4%) and moderate in few-shot (0.8–5.1 pp). Against MOO (Kim et al., 2023), ILCs achieve 79.6% vs. 77.9% on MultiCelebA at 100% data, and 79.0% vs. N/A at 20% data. In zero-shot, ILCs narrowly beat JTT (Liu et al., 2021) on both Waterbirds (87.1% vs. 86.7%) and CelebA (82.0% vs. 81.1%). The only baseline ILCs lose to is CnC (Zhang et al., 2022), which achieves 88.5% on Waterbirds and 88.8% on CelebA, but CnC requires training a specialized contrastive model from scratch, whereas ILCs operate on a frozen ERM backbone with only a linear probe. Overall, ILCs match or exceed all baselines that also use a frozen backbone, and remain competitive with methods requiring dedicated training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly challenges the last-layer retraining paradigm established by Kirichenko et al. (2023), Izmailov et al. (2022), and Rosenfeld et al. (2022), which demonstrated that retraining only the penultimate-layer classifier on OOD data suffices for robustness to spurious correlations. It builds on the linear probing literature (Alain & Bengio, 2018) and connects to observations about intrinsic dimensionality in intermediate layers (Ansuini et al., 2019), neural collapse being less severe in intermediate layers (Li et al., 2022), and the tunnel effect (Masarczyk et al., 2023), but is the first to systematically quantify and exploit the reduced sensitivity of intermediate layers to distribution shifts for OOD generalization. The ILC framework subsumes last-layer retraining as a special case, reframing the problem as a layer-selection question rather than a fixed penultimate-layer assumption.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is immediate and low-cost: practitioners can improve OOD generalization by simply probing an earlier layer instead of the penultimate one, with no architectural changes or additional training of the backbone. The zero-shot result—that ID-trained intermediate-layer probes can match or exceed few-shot last-layer retraining on OOD data—broadens applicability to settings where no target-distribution samples are available. Open questions include whether the sensitivity advantage of intermediate layers persists in deeper or larger models, whether non-linear probes at intermediate layers unlock further gains (the paper shows modest improvements), and whether the mechanism (reduced feature sensitivity) fully explains the performance gap or whether information-theoretic factors also contribute. The smaller gains observed for ViTs compared to ResNets suggest the phenomenon may be architecture-dependent, warranting further investigation.
