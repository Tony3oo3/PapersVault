## Problem

Ambient diffusion models trained solely on noisy data significantly underperform clean-data-trained models at realistic sample sizes, yet prior work assumed no access to clean samples at all. This paper asks: how much clean data is actually needed to compensate for noisy data, and what is the theoretical explanation for the differing utility of clean versus noisy samples in learning a distribution?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251009/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The training algorithm consumes a mixture of clean images and noisy images (corrupted by additive Gaussian noise at a known level σ_{t_n}), with the noise level estimable from the variance difference between the two sets. Experiments use CIFAR-10 (60K, 32×32), CelebA-HQ (30K, 256×256), and ImageNet (~1.3M, 64×64) at noise levels σ ∈ {0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4}. The output is a standard pixel-space diffusion model that generates images via the usual SDE sampling procedure, evaluated by FID score.

## Method

The training algorithm (Algorithm 1) applies different losses depending on the diffusion time relative to the noise level of the noisy data: for t < t_n, only clean samples are used with the standard Denoising Score Matching objective; for t ≥ t_n, clean samples use DSM while noisy samples use the Ambient DSM loss (Eq. 4), which leverages a Tweedie-type identity (Lemma 2.1) to relate the high-noise denoiser to the denoiser at the data's noise level. This eliminates the need for the expensive consistency loss used in prior work, since a small set of clean images (even 1%) directly trains the low-noise denoiser. The theoretical contribution derives minimax-optimal sample complexity bounds for learning Gaussian Mixture Models with heterogeneous per-sample variances, showing that noisy samples are downweighted by 1/σ⁴ for the dimensionality-reduction step but by 1/σ^(4k−2) for the fine-grained component estimation step, explaining why clean data is exponentially more valuable for resolving mixture components and why a small clean set dramatically reduces the number of noisy samples needed.

## Results

The headline result (Table 1) shows that training with 90% noisy + 10% clean data achieves FID of 2.81 on CIFAR-10, 2.75 on CelebA-HQ, and 1.68 on ImageNet, closely approaching the 100% clean baselines (1.99, 2.40, 1.41 respectively). In stark contrast, training on 100% noisy data yields FID of 11.93, 12.97, and 5.32, while training on only the 10% clean subset gives 17.30, 11.92, and 10.57. The mixed-data approach thus outperforms both natural baselines by a wide margin (e.g., 2.81 vs. 11.93 vs. 17.30 on CIFAR-10) while requiring only 10% of the data to be clean.

**Table 1: FID scores (lower is better) for models trained on datasets with different percentages of noisy and clean data.**

| Available Data | CIFAR-10 | CelebA-HQ | ImageNet |
|---|---|---|---|

| 100% noisy | 11.93 ± 0.09 | 12.97 ± 0.11 | 5.32 ± 0.08 |
| 10% clean (smaller dataset) | 17.30 ± 0.14 | 11.92 ± 0.05 | 10.57 ± 0.06 |
| **90% noisy + 10% clean** | **2.81 ± 0.02** | **2.75 ± 0.02** | **1.68 ± 0.02** |

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Consistent Diffusion Meets Tweedie baseline (Daras et al., 2024), which trains solely on noisy data with an expensive consistency loss, the proposed mixed-data method wins decisively: on CIFAR-10 at σ=0.2, the noisy-only model achieves 11.93 FID while the 90% noisy + 10% clean model reaches 2.81 FID, a 4.2× improvement. Against the standard DDPM clean-data baseline (Ho et al., 2020), the proposed method is close but slightly worse (2.81 vs. 1.99 on CIFAR-10, 1.68 vs. 1.41 on ImageNet), confirming that a small clean set nearly recovers full clean-data performance. Against the Ambient Diffusion noisy-only baseline without consistency (Daras et al., 2023b), the gap is even larger: 60.73 FID (full sampling) or 12.12 FID (truncated sampling) on CIFAR-10 at σ=0.2, versus 2.81 for the mixed method. The paper further shows that even 1% clean data (99% noisy) on CIFAR-10 at σ=0.2 yields 3.53 FID, still far superior to any noisy-only configuration, and that the data-pricing analysis suggests roughly 1.5–1.75 noisy samples at σ=0.2 are needed to match one clean sample in effective utility.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Ambient Diffusion and Consistent Diffusion Meets Tweedie frameworks, which train diffusion models exclusively from corrupted data, by introducing the previously unexplored setting where a small set of clean samples is available alongside a large set of noisy ones. The theoretical contribution generalizes the classical GMM estimation problem (building on Wu & Yang, 2020 and Doss et al., 2023) to a novel heterogeneous-variance setting where each sample carries its own noise level, yielding minimax-optimal sample complexity bounds. The key conceptual departure from prior work is the recognition that clean and noisy data serve complementary roles—noisy data identifies low-dimensional structure while clean data resolves fine-grained component separation—rather than being interchangeable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical finding that 10% clean data combined with 90% noisy data nearly matches 100% clean performance (e.g., 1.68 vs. 1.41 FID on ImageNet) has direct implications for dataset curation budgets in scientific imaging and enterprise generative AI, where clean data is expensive or inaccessible. The 'data pricing' framework via effective sample size provides a principled, noise-level-dependent valuation of samples that could guide allocation decisions. However, limitations are significant: the analysis covers only additive Gaussian noise, pixel-space diffusion at 64×64 resolution, and the GMM theory may not capture the complexity of real image distributions. Extending to latent diffusion models, more realistic corruption models, and larger scales remains open.
