## Problem

While transformers are known to exploit hidden relationships between words, the training dynamics by which softmax attention learns and builds such correspondence structures through gradient descent remain largely unexplained. Previous theoretical works either studied position-based attention or settings where dictionary tokens lack explicit structural relationships, leaving open how a transformer discovers that a group identifier is associated with a specific classification feature.

## Input / Output

The input is a sequence of L=3 token vectors in R^d drawn from an orthonormal set of K group signals {c_k} and K classification signals {v_k}: one token is a group signal c_k, one is the corresponding signed classification signal y·v_k (whose sign y determines the label), and one is a distractor ε·v_{k'} from a different group. The output is a scalar score from a one-layer two-headed transformer with ReLU neurons; correct classification requires the score to have the same sign as y. The critical difficulty is that the model must discover the hidden index-matching correspondence between c_k and v_k to read off the label.

## Method

The paper analyzes a one-layer symmetric two-headed transformer f(X) = σ(H_+(X)+b) − σ(H_−(X)+b) trained via a three-stage layer-wise algorithm motivated by observed stage-wise learning: (1) train neuron weights under uniform attention, (2) train attention weights on conflicting-sign samples only, (3) retrain neuron weights to drive population loss to zero. The key technical contribution is reducing a 36-dimensional nonlinear ODE system governing all score, key self-score, and query self-score variables to a critical 3×3 subsystem, then proving its growth is lower-bounded by a linear dynamical system to show the attention score c_k^T W_K^T W_Q v_k diverges. The analysis employs a mean-field infinite-width transformer (m→∞) to obtain exact symmetry between groups and heads, which enables showing that attention features evolve from a nearly non-separable state at initialization to a well-separated state after stage 2. The trained attention concentrates softmax probability on the group signal key when queried by classification signals, thereby building the correspondence structure needed for correct classification.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 4.1) proves that under the infinite-width transformer model, the three-stage gradient flow algorithm achieves population loss E[ℓ(yf^(T₃)(X))] ≤ ε with stage durations T₁ = Θ(1), T₂ − T₁ = O(log(1/ω)), and T₃ − T₂ = O(1/ε). The trained attention exhibits concentration on group signal keys (softmax probability difference of Θ(1) between positive and negative heads) and near-symmetry within a single head (deviations bounded by Õ(ω)). Empirically, the two-headed transformer with ReLU neurons successfully learns the two-mixture task, with consistent-sample loss dropping early and conflicting-sample loss dropping only after attention scores exhibit noticeable change, confirming the predicted stage-wise learning dynamics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is a theoretical analysis work and does not compare against other methods or baselines in the traditional sense. The contribution is the first provable characterization of how a two-headed transformer learns hidden correspondence structure via gradient flow on a mixture-of-linear-classification task. The empirical experiments serve to validate the theoretical predictions (stage-wise learning, attention concentration on group signals) rather than to benchmark against competing architectures. The only comparative experiment is in Appendix A.2, where fixing neuron weights at Gaussian initialization and training only the attention shows the model is not trainable, confirming that the neuron-first learning order is necessary. For K ≥ 3 mixtures, the paper notes that the near-symmetry property breaks down and training takes longer, but the transformer still converges, suggesting the K=2 analysis does not directly extend.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the growing body of work on transformer training dynamics (in-context learning, spatial structure, topic models, feature-position correlation) by introducing a setting where dictionary tokens possess an explicit hidden correspondence structure (group identifiers ↔ classification features), which prior works either ignored or handled only through positional encoding. The mixture of linear classification task itself is a classical statistical model, but its use as a probe for how softmax attention learns to build token-to-token associations via gradient flow is new. Methodologically, the paper departs from NTK or mean-field convergence analyses by explicitly tracking a 36-dimensional non-linear dynamical system of score variables and reducing it to a tractable 3×3 subsystem bounded by a linear system, a technique the authors note is of independent interest.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean mechanistic account of how transformers learn hidden correspondence structure: neurons align with signals first, then attention evolves features from a provably non-separable state to a well-separated one, and finally convergence completes. The stage-wise learning phenomenon and the finding that fixing neurons while training only attention renders the model untrainable are practical insights for understanding training order in multi-component architectures. Key open questions include whether the analysis extends to K≥3 mixtures (where symmetry breaks and the near-symmetry properties fail), what data-distribution properties are necessary for the attention-feature margin to increase during training, and whether the infinite-width idealization captures finite-width dynamics at practical scales.
