## Problem

Classical motion planners degrade in performance as environment scale grows, and existing neural approaches to solving the Eikonal PDE (e.g., PINNs) require retraining for every new environment geometry. This paper asks whether a single neural operator can learn the mapping from cost functions to value functions across an entire space of environments, enabling zero-shot generalization to unseen obstacle configurations and goal positions without retraining.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251002/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The PNO takes a binary occupancy grid (representing obstacle layout) and a goal position as input. The occupancy grid is first converted to a sign distance function (SDF) by a separately trained FNO, and both the occupancy map and SDF are fed into the main operator. The output is a continuous value function (Eikonal PDE solution) at any desired resolution — the model is trained at 64×64 but deployed at up to 1024×1024 (16× super-resolution). The value function can be used directly for gradient-descent planning or as an ε-consistent heuristic inside A*, where it is combined with the Euclidean norm via a max operation to preserve admissibility.

## Method

The Planning Neural Operator (PNO) extends the Fourier Neural Operator with three architectural modifications: (1) resolution invariance is inherited from FNO's frequency-space weight matrices; (2) obstacle geometry is hard-encoded into every Fourier layer by multiplying the learnable weight matrix with a smoothed indicator function χ̃_PNO(x) = tanh(β·d_S(x))·(1/c(x) − 0.5) + 0.5, which zeros out unsafe regions and improves generalization to new obstacle layouts; (3) the projection layer uses a Deepnorm structure Q(φ, x, g) = f_θQ(φ(x) − φ(g)) with non-negative activations and positive weights, inductively enforcing the triangle inequality so the predicted value function generalizes across goal positions. The SDF needed for the indicator is itself computed by a second, independently trained FNO (since the SDF solves its own Eikonal PDE), preserving resolution invariance throughout. Training is supervised on FMM-generated value functions with an optional PINN loss term penalizing ‖∇V̂‖ − c(x), and the paper proves both the existence of a neural operator approximating the Eikonal solution operator to arbitrary accuracy and the ε-consistency of the learned heuristic for A*.

## Results

PNO, trained at 64×64 resolution, achieves 0.1194 relative L2 error at 1024×1024 on synthetic maps and 0.2034 on out-of-distribution MovingAI city maps, compared to FNO (0.6405 / 0.7692) and DAFNO (0.4120 / 0.4315). With an additional PINN loss, PNO further reduces error to 0.0872 / 0.1842. Computation time at 1024² is 8.31 ms for PNO versus 104.64 ms for the Fast Marching Method (~12× speedup). As a heuristic for A*, PNO with obstacle erosion reduces expanded nodes by 33% (41,394 vs. 59,965 at 1024²) while maintaining near-optimal path lengths (ε-suboptimality ≈ 1.006).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PNO substantially outperforms FNO on both in-distribution synthetic maps (0.1194 vs. 0.6405 at 1024²) and out-of-distribution city maps (0.2034 vs. 0.7692), confirming that explicit obstacle encoding in the Fourier layers is critical for generalization. Against DAFNO, PNO achieves a 50% reduction in L2 error on unseen city maps (0.2034 vs. 0.4315 at 1024²), attributable to the Deepnorm projection layer enforcing the triangle inequality for goal generalization. Compared to the Fast Marching Method, PNO delivers a ~12× speedup at 1024² (8.31 ms vs. 104.64 ms) while maintaining competitive accuracy, and its inference time remains nearly constant across resolutions (5.3–8.3 ms) whereas FMM scales super-linearly. On the Grid-World benchmark, PNO achieves 99.3% success rate at 28×28, outperforming VIN (96.7%) and IEF2D (97.0%), with 12–13× faster inference than IEF2D. In 3D iGibson environments, PNO matches FMM's value function magnitude more closely than P-NTFields and generalizes to the unseen Samuel environment without retraining, whereas NTFields and P-NTFields each require ~3 hours of per-environment retraining. As an A* heuristic, PNO with erosion achieves near-optimal paths (ε ≈ 1.006 at 1024²) while expanding 33% fewer nodes than the Euclidean norm heuristic, and substantially outperforms RRT (ε ≈ 1.33) and RRT* (ε ≈ 1.29) in path quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) and its domain-agnostic variant (DAFNO) by introducing two motion-planning-specific modifications: hard-encoding obstacle geometry via a smoothed signed-distance indicator in the Fourier layers, and a Deepnorm projection layer that enforces the triangle inequality for goal generalization. It departs from the closest prior work, NTFields and P-NTFields, which also solve the Eikonal PDE for motion planning but require per-environment retraining via physics-informed losses; PNO instead learns a single solution operator across the entire cost-function space, inheriting zero-shot generalization to new obstacle configurations. The theoretical grounding draws on the universal approximation theorem for nonlocal neural operators (Lanthaler et al., 2023) and viscosity solution theory for the Eikonal PDE.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in the resolution-invariance demonstration: training at 64×64 and deploying at 1024×1024 with ~12% relative L2 error, plus a 10× speedup over the Fast Marching Method at scale, makes learned value functions viable for large-scale urban navigation without per-map retraining. The ε-consistency proof (Lemma 1) and the erosion trick (Lemma 2) provide a principled bridge between learned value functions and classical A* search, yielding a 33% reduction in expanded nodes. Open questions include scaling to higher-DOF manipulators beyond 4, handling non-uniform cost functions, and extending to dynamic environments where the operator would need to be updated online; the paper also notes that ε-consistency in continuous space is unbounded, so the guarantee only materializes under discretization.
