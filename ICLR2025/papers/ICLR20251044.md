## Problem

Existing score-based black-box adversarial attacks are designed for top-1 classification and suffer from low success rates and query inefficiency under small perturbation budgets, while the vulnerability of multi-label classifiers to top-K adversarial attacks in the black-box setting remains entirely unexplored. In the aggressive top-K setting, the adversarial region becomes extremely narrow (especially for targeted attacks), making it akin to finding a needle in an ocean to locate an initial boundary point that satisfies the top-K target-label constraints, and further refining the perturbation across the highly irregular adversarial region.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251044/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a benign RGB image (3×224×224 for ImageNet experiments, 3×300×300 for PASCAL VOC 2012) and full query access to the target classifier's prediction probability vector over all C classes. It produces a top-K adversarial example x_adv = x_s + r(Θ̂) that changes the top-K predicted labels, subject to an ℓ2-norm perturbation threshold r_th and a total query budget Q. The attack supports both single-label multi-class and top-K multi-label learning classifiers, and handles both untargeted (push true labels out of top-K) and targeted (replace top-K with a specific target set) objectives.

## Method

GSBA^K is a three-step geometric score-based attack that exploits the geometry of the decision boundary. First, it estimates the gradient in the non-adversarial region using low-frequency DCT noise samples, with novel per-class weighting (Eq. 9 for targeted, Eq. 10 for untargeted) that counts and measures how many target classes gain confidence, iteratively stepping toward a good initial boundary point via binary search. Second, at each boundary point, it estimates the gradient using prediction probabilities with an indicator function ζ that filters out anomalous queries (those that are adversarial but do not positively impact any target class) and assigns adaptive weights proportional to both the number of target classes affected and the magnitude of confidence change (Eq. 12 for targeted, Eq. 13 for untargeted). Third, guided by the boundary gradient, it searches for the next boundary point along a semicircular trajectory in the 2D plane spanned by the gradient direction and the source-to-boundary direction, iteratively reducing the perturbation norm. This geometric exploration, combined with the top-K-aware gradient estimators, enables GSBA^K to achieve substantially higher ASR than Square Attack (adapted as SA^K) across all K values, perturbation thresholds, and query budgets.

## Results

On ImageNet against ResNet-50, GSBA^K achieves 94.6% ASR for top-1 untargeted attacks at r_th=2 with 10,000 queries, compared to 85.6% for SA^K. For the more challenging top-1 targeted attack at r_th=4 with 10,000 queries, GSBA^1 reaches 91.6% ASR versus only 35.5% for SA^1. The advantage grows with K: for top-4 targeted attacks at r_th=4 with 10,000 queries, GSBA^4 achieves 15.0% ASR while SA^4 manages only 3.1%. On PASCAL VOC 2012 with multi-label learning (Inception-V3), GSBA^2 outperforms SA^2 by 12–20 percentage points for random and worst target sets at r_th=6.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GSBA^K is compared against three score-based baselines: SA^K (adapted Square Attack), SimBA-DCT, and PPBA. Against SA^K, the primary top-K baseline, GSBA^K wins decisively on targeted attacks across all K values — e.g., 91.6% vs. 35.5% ASR for top-1 targeted at r_th=4, Q=10000, and 15.0% vs. 3.1% for top-4 targeted under the same conditions. For untargeted attacks, GSBA^K also leads, with the gap widening as r_th decreases and K increases (e.g., 94.6% vs. 85.6% for top-1 untargeted at r_th=2, Q=10000). SimBA-DCT and PPBA are only evaluated in the top-1 setting (Figure 4); GSBA^1 outperforms both, particularly at small perturbation thresholds where SimBA-DCT and PPBA suffer from large default perturbations. On the multi-label PASCAL VOC 2012 benchmark, GSBA^K consistently outperforms SA^K, with the largest margins on random and worst target sets where SA^2 fails to converge within the query budget for worst targets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate lines of adversarial attack research: geometric decision-based attacks (notably CGBA, which explores the decision boundary along a semicircular trajectory) and score-based black-box attacks (SimBA, PPBA, Square Attack), which have traditionally relied on iterative perturbation in estimated gradient or random directions without exploiting boundary geometry. The key conceptual departure is transferring the geometric boundary-exploration paradigm from the decision-based setting (where only the top-1 label is available) to the score-based setting (where full prediction probabilities are accessible), and extending both from top-1 to the more challenging top-K formulation. The novel gradient estimators (Eqs. 9, 12) are specifically designed to handle the multi-class constraint of top-K targeted attacks by assigning per-class adaptive weights, a problem that does not arise in the top-1 case where simpler estimators suffice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first comprehensive score-based black-box attack framework for the top-K setting, covering both single-label multi-class and multi-label learning, which is practically relevant for real-world APIs (Google Cloud Vision, Azure, etc.) that return top-K predictions. The substantial performance gap over SA^K in targeted top-K attacks (e.g., 97.3% vs. 70.4% ASR at r_th=4, Q=20000 for top-2) and the failure of SA^2 to converge on worst-case target labels highlight a genuine vulnerability in multi-label classifiers that was previously unexplored in the black-box regime. Limitations include the requirement for query access to the target classifier, performance degradation as K increases, and the reliance on DCT-subspace noise sampling that may not capture all relevant gradient directions. Open questions include whether the geometric approach can be extended to sparse or structured perturbation constraints, and whether the per-class weighting strategy generalizes to settings with more than 1000 classes.
