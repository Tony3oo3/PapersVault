## Problem

Existing scoring functions for pretraining data detection (Perplexity, Min-k%) suffer from significant score overlap between members and non-members, yielding suboptimal AUC scores often near random guessing. No prior method exploits the readily available pool of unseen data (e.g., content published after an LLM's release date) to sharpen the distinction between seen and unseen examples.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252193/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Given a text sample x and a pre-trained LLM f_θ, the method additionally requires a small auxiliary set of non-member texts from the same domain (e.g., Wikipedia events or arXiv papers published after the model's release date) to fine-tune a copy of the model. The output is a scalar FSD deviation score computed as the difference between the pre-trained and fine-tuned model's scoring function values on x, which is thresholded to yield a binary member/non-member label. The method is scoring-function-agnostic and works with any existing likelihood-based metric.

## Method

FSD exploits the empirical observation that self-supervised fine-tuning an LLM on a small set of previously unseen domain data causes a disproportionately large decrease in likelihood-based scores for other unknown non-members, while member scores remain nearly unchanged. Concretely, the method fine-tunes a copy of the pre-trained model (using LoRA, AdaLoRA, or IA3 with next-token prediction loss) on ~100–300 non-member examples from the same domain, then computes the FSD score as S(x; f_θ) − S(x; f_θ′), where S is any existing scoring function (Perplexity, Min-k%, Zlib, Lowercase). A large positive deviation indicates the sample is a non-member, since the fine-tuned model has already 'learned' the domain patterns that make non-members more predictable. This simple deviation mechanism enlarges the score gap between members and non-members without modifying the underlying scoring function, and is highly data-efficient—achieving dramatic AUC improvements (e.g., 0.62→0.91 on WikiMIA) with only ~100 auxiliary examples.

## Results

On WikiMIA, FSD improves the AUC of the Perplexity baseline from 0.64 to 0.92 under LLaMA-7B, and from 0.62 to 0.91 for Min-k% under OPT-6.7B. On ArXivTection, FSD raises the TPR@5%FPR of the Perplexity method from 0.10 to 0.81 under LLaMA-7B. For copyrighted book detection on BookMIA, FSD boosts Zlib accuracy from 57.4% to 98.6% under LLaMA-7B. Gains are consistent across all five evaluated models (GPT-J-6B, OPT-6.7B, Pythia-6.9B, LLaMA-7B, NeoX-20B) and all four scoring functions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FSD is applied as a wrapper around four existing scoring functions and consistently outperforms each baseline across all models and datasets. Against the Perplexity baseline (Li, 2023), FSD yields the largest absolute AUC gains, e.g. +0.28 on WikiMIA/LLaMA-7B (0.64→0.92) and +0.24 on ArXivTection/LLaMA-7B (0.68→0.92). Against the Zlib and Lowercase baselines (Carlini et al., 2021), FSD improves Zlib substantially (e.g. 0.62→0.90 on WikiMIA/LLaMA-7B) but Lowercase shows more modest gains (0.58→0.69), suggesting Lowercase's case-sensitivity signal is less amenable to the fine-tuning deviation. Against the Min-k% baseline (Shi et al., 2024), FSD still improves AUC (e.g. 0.62→0.91 on WikiMIA/OPT-6.7B) but the relative gain is smaller than for Perplexity or Zlib, since Min-k% already focuses on outlier tokens that are less affected by domain-level fine-tuning. Overall, FSD wins on every comparison cell in Tables 1–4, with the most dramatic improvements on Perplexity and Zlib.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the likelihood-based scoring function lineage for pretraining data detection—Perplexity (Li, 2023), Min-k% (Shi et al., 2024), and the Zlib/Lowercase ratios (Carlini et al., 2021)—which all compute a single-model score and threshold it. The key departure is introducing a *deviation* signal: by fine-tuning the LLM with a small set of domain-specific non-members via LoRA, the authors show that non-member scores drop substantially while member scores remain nearly unchanged, so the difference between pre-trained and fine-tuned scores becomes a far more discriminative metric. Conceptually, this repurposes parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA, AdaLoRA, IA3) not as a detection method per se, but as a calibration step that widens the score gap between seen and unseen data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: FSD lifts AUC from ~0.62 to ~0.91 on WikiMIA and improves TPR@5%FPR by several-fold across five open-source LLMs and five benchmark datasets, with only ~100 unseen examples needed for fine-tuning. However, the method requires white-box access (output probabilities and fine-tuning capability), limiting applicability to closed-source models, and depends on collecting domain-matched unseen data, which becomes harder for diverse or rapidly evolving corpora. Open questions include whether the deviation signal persists under stronger obfuscation of temporal cues, whether it generalizes to multimodal or non-text pretraining data, and whether the fine-tuning step could inadvertently alter the model's behavior in ways that confound downstream evaluation.
