## Problem

Existing causal MBRL methods passively rely on pre-learned causal structures for policy learning or generalization, without actively leveraging them to guide exploration. This paper asks whether an agent can use causal structure to drive empowerment-based exploration, and whether the resulting controllable data can in turn refine the causal model, creating a mutually reinforcing loop between causal understanding and controllability.

## Input / Output

The method takes factored state vectors (e.g., 10-dim for chemical, 25-dim for manipulation) or pixel observations processed through IFactor's identifiable latent encoders (for DMC, Cartpole, Robodesk) as input, and produces a binary causal mask (adjacency matrix over state and action dimensions), a causal dynamics model, a reward model conditioned on causally-filtered state abstractions, and a downstream task policy. The causal mask M^{s→s} and M^{a→s} encode which state and action dimensions causally influence each next-state dimension, and the reward model operates on the state abstraction φ_c(s|M) that masks out irrelevant dimensions.

## Method

ECL is a three-stage framework: (1) Model Learning trains a dense dynamics model, then embeds causal masks via constraint-based conditional independence testing or score-based L1 sparse regularization to learn the causal structure, and trains a reward model on causally-filtered state abstractions; (2) Model Optimization alternates between learning an empowerment policy π_e that maximizes the *difference* in empowerment gain between the causal dynamics model and the dense dynamics model (Eq. 7–10, reducing to a KL divergence term in practice), and updating the causal mask and reward model with trajectories collected under π_e, while keeping the dynamics model fixed; (3) Policy Learning uses CEM planning with a shaped reward combining task reward and a curiosity bonus (Eq. 11) that measures the KL gap between environment dynamics and the causal model versus the dense model, preventing overfitting to the learned causal structure. The framework is method-agnostic, supporting both constraint-based and score-based causal discovery, and extends to pixel-based tasks by operating on IFactor's identifiable latent states.

## Results

On causal graph learning in the chemical environments, ECL-Con achieves perfect 1.00 scores on all five metrics (Accuracy, Recall, Precision, F1, ROC AUC) in both Chain and Collider settings, and 0.97 F1 / 0.98 ROC AUC in the more complex Full setting, outperforming CDL (0.94 F1) and REG (0.85 F1). In the Physical environment, ECL-Con and ECL-Sco both attain 100% on all metrics. For downstream task learning, ECL-Con achieves the highest episodic reward across all three state-based environments and demonstrates superior sample efficiency (reaching steady state after ~400 episodes). On pixel-based DMC tasks, ECL outperforms both IFactor (with video background distractors) and DreamerV3 (noiseless setting) in average return.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CDL (constraint-based, Wang et al. 2022), ECL-Con matches its 100% scores in Chain and Collider but clearly wins in the Full chemical environment (0.97 F1 vs. 0.94; 0.98 ROC AUC vs. 0.96) and achieves higher episodic reward and faster convergence in all three state-based task environments. Against REG (score-based, Wang et al. 2021), ECL-Sco substantially outperforms in the Full setting (0.93 F1 vs. 0.85; 0.95 ROC AUC vs. 0.95) and in Recall (0.90 vs. 0.79), while also surpassing REG in episodic reward across all environments. Against GRADER (Ding et al. 2022), ECL-Con achieves higher F1 in Chain (1.00 vs. 0.95) and Collider (1.00 vs. 0.94), though GRADER edges ahead in the Full environment (0.98 vs. 0.93 F1 for ECL-Sco). Against the dense GNN baseline (Ke et al. 2021), ECL matches prediction accuracy in-distribution but significantly outperforms it in the manipulation environment and on OOD generalization. For pixel-based tasks, ECL surpasses IFactor (Liu et al. 2024) in average return on Robodesk and Modified Cartpole, and outperforms DreamerV3 (Hafner et al. 2023) on all three DMC tasks under noiseless settings, while incurring less than 10% additional computational cost over CDL/REG.

## Relation to Prior Work

ECL sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: causal model-based RL (CDL, REG, GRADER) and empowerment-based intrinsic motivation (Klyubin, Salge, Leibfried). Prior causal MBRL methods passively use a learned causal structure for state abstraction or generalization, while empowerment methods maximize action-state mutual information without explicit causal structure. ECL's key departure is creating an active feedback loop: the causal mask shapes an empowerment-gain signal (causal minus dense model empowerment) that drives exploration, and the resulting controllable trajectories in turn refine the causal mask and reward model. The framework is deliberately method-agnostic, plugging in either constraint-based (CIT) or score-based (L1-regularized) causal discovery, and relies on IFactor's identifiable latent encoders for pixel-based settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The empowerment-through-causal-learning feedback loop offers a principled mechanism for agents to actively improve their own causal understanding rather than passively inheriting a fixed structure, which is relevant for any setting where the environment contains spurious correlations or irrelevant state dimensions. The curiosity reward (KL divergence between environment and causal-model predictions) is a practical safeguard against overfitting to the learned causal structure during downstream task learning. Open questions include whether the approach handles non-stationary or changing dynamics, whether it scales to high-dimensional real-world robotics without the well-defined factored state spaces used here, and whether explicit behavioral disentanglement (beyond implicit empowerment) would further improve controllability. The method-agnostic design and modest computational overhead (<10% over baselines) make it a practical addition to the causal MBRL toolkit.
