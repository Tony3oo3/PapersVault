## Problem

Prior work demonstrated that LLMs can recognize their own writing, but it remained unclear whether this reflected genuine knowledge of one's own writing style or merely detection of surface-level differences (e.g., length, perplexity). The paper also left open how this ability is implemented in the model's internal representations and whether it can be causally controlled.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253094/cropped_page3_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes text passages (summaries, continuations, or QA answers of 1K–2K characters) presented either individually or in pairs, along with the source article and generation instructions as context. The self-recognition vector is extracted from 290 contrastive pairs of self- and human-written texts on which the model gave confident correct judgments, capturing residual stream activations at the final 10 tokens across all layers. The vector is then applied either to the model's output token (steering, at layers 14–16 with multipliers 3–6) or to every token of the input text being evaluated (coloring, at all layers), producing a binary authorship judgment or a switched paired-choice decision.

## Method

The paper applies the contrastive pairs method to isolate a direction in the residual stream differentially activated for self- versus human-written texts, then removes nuisance components (surface-level output confounds like 'A'/'B' or 'I'/'He' tokens) via projection. The resulting vector is most strongly activated at layers 4–6 by text characteristics (cheery, conversational, RLHF-tuned style) in a bottom-up, prompt-independent manner, and accumulates into a decision-relevant signal by layer 16, where it correlates with the model's authorship probability. Steering the vector at layer 16 achieves ~100% control over the model's authorship claims on new and out-of-distribution texts, while zeroing it out reduces self-authorship claims by 50–60%. Critically, the vector also affects perception: adding it to input tokens ('coloring') makes the model believe arbitrary texts are its own, and removing it from input tokens reduces the model's confidence in its authorship judgments, demonstrating that the vector is causally involved in both the model's perception of self-written style and its assertion of authorship.

## Results

In the Individual presentation paradigm, Llama3-8b-Instruct achieves above-chance self-recognition accuracy on three of four datasets (CNN: 57.2%, XSUM: 61.0%, SAD: 65.8%), while Llama3-8b-base performs at chance (48.5–50.3%) across all datasets. Steering with the isolated self-recognition vector at layers 14–16 with multipliers of 3–6 achieves 100% effectiveness at forcing the model to claim or deny authorship regardless of true authorship. Projecting the vector out of the residual stream (zeroing) reduces the model's self-authorship claims by 50–60%, confirming its causal role in the recognition behavior.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between Llama3-8b-Instruct (chat) and Llama3-8b (base), demonstrating that post-training (RLHF) is necessary for self-recognition: the chat model significantly exceeds chance on CNN, XSUM, and SAD, while the base model is at or below chance on all four datasets. Length normalization (trimming all texts to equal character length) destroys or severely degrades the chat model's ability to distinguish its own outputs from other AI models, but it retains the ability to distinguish its own outputs from human-written text, supporting a 'true' self-recognition interpretation. Perplexity analysis rules out a simple familiarity-based mechanism: correlations between text perplexity and the model's authorship judgments are modest (≤0.16) and often in the wrong direction. The self-recognition vector's steering effect is specific to authorship-related tasks: on a control (named-entity recognition) task, positive steering has no effect at layer 16, and negative steering produces only modest effects, confirming the vector encodes a 'self vs. not-self' concept rather than generic affirmation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the self-text-recognition line of work initiated by Panickssery et al. (2024) and Laine et al. (2024), which established that RLHF'd LLMs can distinguish their own outputs from human or other-model text, by asking *how* this is implemented internally. It repurposes the contrastive-pairs and activation-steering methodology from representation engineering (Zou et al., 2023; Turner et al., 2024) and the Tuned Lens interpretability tool (Belrose et al., 2023) to isolate a specific residual-stream vector underlying the recognition behavior. The key departure from prior self-recognition studies is the shift from behavioral measurement to mechanistic identification and causal control of the underlying representation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of a single, causally active vector that governs both the model's self-authorship judgments and its perceptual 'coloring' of input text provides a concrete handle for AI safety applications, including resistance to many-shot jailbreaking and detection of self-to-self communication. However, the findings are demonstrated only on Llama3-8B-Instruct, and it remains open whether the same vector structure generalizes to larger or architecturally different models, or whether multiple distributed mechanisms contribute in more capable systems. The moderate effect sizes of the coloring manipulation (55–58% of trials showing decreased confidence) also suggest the vector captures only part of the perceptual signal for writing-style recognition.
