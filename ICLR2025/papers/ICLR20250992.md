## Problem

Neural activity encodes low-dimensional latent variables that are closely related to, but not identical with, measured behaviour (e.g., an animal 'thinking' of a remote location while physically elsewhere). Existing latent variable models for neural data are typically expensive, hard to tune, scale poorly, or fail to exploit the strong behavioural signal, making them impractical for large-scale spiking datasets. SIMPL addresses this by providing a fast, scalable, EM-style algorithm that refines behaviour-based estimates of latents and tuning curves while remaining interpretable and GPU-free.

## Input / Output

SIMPL takes a spike count matrix (N neurons × T time bins, Poisson-distributed) and a behavioural trajectory of matching dimensionality D as the initial latent estimate x^(0). It outputs an optimised latent trajectory x^(E+1) and non-parametric, kernel-smoothed tuning curves (rate maps) for each neuron. The behavioural trajectory is consumed only as an initialisation — after the first iteration the latent is decoded purely from spikes — and a linear realignment step at each epoch keeps the optimised latent in the same coordinate frame as behaviour for direct interpretability.

## Method

SIMPL iterates two steps: an M-step that fits each neuron's tuning curve via Gaussian kernel smoothing over the current latent trajectory (spikes-at-x / visits-to-x), and an E-step that decodes a new latent by first computing per-timestep MLEs of x from the Poisson emission model, then applying Kalman smoothing under a linear-Gaussian approximation to p(x̂|x). The key theoretical insight is that this linear-Gaussian approximation is placed on the MLE distribution p(x̂|x) — which is provably asymptotically Gaussian in the many-neurons limit via the Bernstein-von Mises theorem — rather than on the non-Gaussian spiking emissions p(s|x), preserving the full non-linearity of the tuning curves. Behavioural initialisation (x^(0) ← measured position) accelerates convergence and mitigates identifiability issues by biasing the solution toward un-warped latent spaces. Computational efficiency is achieved by binning tuning curves onto a fixed spatial grid once per iteration, reducing the MLE and variance estimation to O(T) operations, and the entire algorithm requires only two hyperparameters (velocity prior v and kernel bandwidth σ) and runs in ~40 CPU-seconds on hour-long datasets without a GPU.

## Results

On the synthetic 2D grid cell dataset (225 cells, 36,000 timesteps), SIMPL recovers the true latent trajectory to 4.2 cm final error in 40 CPU-seconds (10 epochs), with log-likelihood approaching the ceiling and negligible overfitting. On the Tanni et al. (2022) hippocampal dataset (226 neurons, 2 hours), optimisation yields +19% more place fields per cell (1.14→1.41, p=0.0035), +45% higher median max firing rate (4.2→6.1 Hz, p=9.8×10⁻⁷), and −25% smaller median field area (0.59→0.44 m²), while the optimised latent remains highly correlated with behaviour (R²=0.86). On the Chowdhury et al. (2020) somatosensory cortex dataset (65 neurons, 37 min), a 4D latent initialised to hand position and velocity achieves correlations of 0.74 (position dims) and 0.57 (velocity dims) with the measured kinematics, outperforming 2D models (corr=0.59). SIMPL is >15× faster than the next-best baseline (pi-VAE, 10.4 min) and achieves half its latent error (4.2 cm vs 8.4 cm).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SIMPL outperforms all four baselines on the synthetic grid cell benchmark. Against pi-VAE (Zhou & Wei, 2020), SIMPL achieves half the final latent error (4.2 cm vs 8.4 cm) and is ~15× faster (40 s vs 10.4 min); the authors attribute pi-VAE's inferior accuracy to its lack of an explicit dynamical systems component. Against CEBRA (Schneider et al., 2023), SIMPL again achieves lower error and faster convergence; CEBRA treats each data point independently rather than modelling whole trajectories, preventing it from exploiting temporal smoothness. Against GPLVM (Lawrence, 2003), SIMPL is faster and more accurate; GPLVM uses Gaussian (not Poisson) emissions and lacks smoothness assumptions on the latent. Against GPDM (Wang et al., 2005), SIMPL is faster and does not require a GPU; GPDM's cubic scaling forced the authors to subsample to 5 minutes of data (vs 60 min for all other methods), limiting its accuracy. Only SIMPL recovered sharp, well-defined grid fields matching ground truth, while all baselines produced blurred or distorted tuning curves.

## Relation to Prior Work

SIMPL sits at the intersection of classical EM-based state-space inference (Smith & Brown 2003; Macke et al. 2011) and modern latent variable methods for neural data (pi-VAE, CEBRA, GPLVM, GPDM), but deliberately departs from both. Where prior state-space models impose restrictive (exponential-)linear tuning curves and where neural-network-based methods (pi-VAE, CEBRA) treat time points as i.i.d. or require expensive training, SIMPL couples a non-parametric kernel-based tuning curve estimator with Kalman smoothing of MLEs in an EM-like loop, using measured behaviour as an inductive-bias initialisation. The theoretical grounding draws on the asymptotic normality of the MLE (Bradley & Gart 1962) and the Bernstein-von Mises theorem to justify replacing the intractable Poisson emission with a linear-Gaussian surrogate, enabling exact O(T) inference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for the neuroscience community: SIMPL is 15× faster than the next-best baseline, requires no GPU, has only two hyperparameters, and natively handles spiking data—making latent variable modelling accessible to experimentalists who previously relied on behaviour-based tuning curves. Scientifically, the finding that optimised hippocampal place fields are 25% smaller, 19% more numerous, and that the well-known size-vs.-distance-to-wall correlation is substantially an artefact of latent-behaviour discrepancy offers a concrete reinterpretation of prior experimental results. Open questions include whether the kernel-based estimator scales to high-dimensional latent spaces (where neural networks may be preferable), how SIMPL handles biased or asymmetric latent-behaviour discrepancies (e.g., theta sequences, replay), and whether the identifiability benefits of behavioural initialisation extend to settings where behaviour is a poor proxy.
