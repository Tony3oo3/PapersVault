## Problem

Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) frequently hallucinate objects in open-ended captioning, yet prior work has identified only high-level symptoms (over-reliance on text, co-occurrence bias) without pinpointing which specific internal components are causally responsible. This paper asks: which modules and attention heads drive hallucination, why do they behave this way, and can targeted intervention on those components reduce hallucination without degrading generation quality?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251852/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard LVLM inputs (an RGB image plus a text instruction such as 'Please describe the image in detail') and produces a free-form text caption. The key operational detail is that the intervention acts on internal attention weights: AD-HH requires explicit computation of per-head attention matrices at each decoding step (making it incompatible with FlashAttention), while TF-HH modifies only the Query and Key projection matrices of identified hallucination heads, leaving Value matrices and all other parameters frozen. The method is applied to 7B-scale models (LLaVA-7B, MiniGPT-4) with 32 layers × 32 heads, and the top 20 (or 10) heads are selected for intervention based on a contrastive influence score computed over ~500 COCO training images.

## Method

The paper first uses causal mediation analysis via zero-ablation with a contrastive influence score (difference in intervention effect on hallucinated vs. non-hallucinated tokens) to identify 'hallucination heads' concentrated in middle and deeper layers, showing MHA modules contribute more to hallucination than MLPs. Analysis reveals these heads attend to text tokens 4.75× more than image tokens, inherit their attention patterns from the base LLM (Vicuna-7B), and change slowly during instruction tuning. Two interventions target these heads: (1) AD-HH, a training-free decoding strategy that adaptively zeros text attention weights for hallucination heads whenever their accumulated text attention exceeds a threshold τ (0.4 for LLaVA-7B), requiring only a single forward pass; and (2) TF-HH, which fine-tunes only the Q and K matrices of hallucination heads for 200 steps using a combined loss of standard next-token prediction plus a text-reliance penalty λ·log(A_h^text), achieving up to 1.7× reduction in CHAIR hallucination rate while preserving or improving generation quality.

## Results

On the COCO captioning task with LLaVA-7B, the training-free AD-HH method reduces CHAIR_S from 51.8 (greedy) to 29.6 and CHAIR_I from 13.3 to 8.0, achieving a 1.7× reduction in hallucination rate. The fine-tuning method TF-HH achieves CHAIR_S=35.0 and CHAIR_I=8.7, also substantially outperforming all baselines. On the out-of-domain Nocaps dataset, AD-HH reduces CHAIR_S from 43.2 to 35.6 (LLaVA-7B) and from 57.4 to 46.8 (MiniGPT-4), confirming transferability. On MM-Vet, AD-HH improves LLaVA-7B's total score from 31.4 to 34.3, and TF-HH improves MiniGPT-4's total from 22.2 to 27.3, showing benefits extend beyond captioning to complex multimodal tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Both proposed methods substantially outperform all six baselines on the primary COCO captioning task. AD-HH achieves CHAIR_S=29.6 and CHAIR_I=8.0 on LLaVA-7B, beating the next-best baseline HALC (CHAIR_S=50.2, CHAIR_I=12.4) by 20.6 and 4.4 points respectively. Notably, DoLA and VCD actually perform worse than greedy decoding on COCO (CHAIR_S=53.8 and 55.4 vs. 51.8), suggesting that layer-contrastive and visual-contrastive decoding strategies can inadvertently amplify language biases in LVLMs. OPERA and LURE show only marginal improvements over greedy (CHAIR_S 50.2 and 51.2 vs. 51.8), while TF-HH achieves the best CHAIR_S on MiniGPT-4 (32.0 vs. OPERA's 35.2). On the out-of-domain Nocaps dataset, AD-HH maintains its lead (CHAIR_S=35.6 vs. HALC's 40.2 for LLaVA-7B), and on MM-Vet, AD-HH achieves the highest total score (34.3 for LLaVA-7B, 23.8 for MiniGPT-4) while TF-HH achieves the best MiniGPT-4 total (27.3), confirming that targeted hallucination-head intervention generalizes across tasks and datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate research threads: mechanistic interpretability of Transformers (causal mediation analysis, zero-ablation, attention head specialization as in Voita et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2023) and hallucination mitigation in LVLMs (decoding-time strategies like VCD, OPERA, HALC, and training-time approaches like LRV, REVERIE). Prior hallucination work treats the model largely as a black box—adjusting decoding distributions, adding contrastive penalties, or using external grounding modules—without identifying which internal components drive the failure. This paper departs by applying interpretability tools (contrastive influence scoring via zero-ablation) to localize hallucination to specific attention heads, then designing interventions (adaptive deactivation, targeted fine-tuning) that act only on those heads rather than the full model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that hallucination is concentrated in fewer than 3% of attention heads, and that these heads inherit their text-biased attention from the base LLM while remaining largely unchanged during 665k-sample instruction tuning, reframes hallucination as a property of the language prior rather than a visual-alignment failure. This has practical implications: targeted fine-tuning of a handful of heads (200 steps, <3% of instruction-tuning compute) achieves a 1.7× hallucination reduction, offering a cheap post-hoc fix for deployed models. Open questions include whether the 'lazy head' phenomenon generalizes to non-object hallucinations (e.g., attribute or relational errors), whether the approach transfers to early-fusion architectures like Chameleon, and whether the AD-HH method's incompatibility with FlashAttention limits its deployment at scale.
