## Problem

Language models can evaluate response quality better than they can generate correct responses, yet a foundational theory explaining how self-improvement extracts this 'hidden knowledge' without external feedback has been missing. This paper asks: when and how does self-training achieve sharpening toward high-quality responses, and what are the fundamental sample-complexity limits of such algorithms?

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained autoregressive base model π_base and accesses it exclusively through sample-and-evaluate queries: sampling responses y ~ π_base(·|x) for prompts x ~ μ and evaluating the sequence-level log-probability π_base(y|x) as a self-reward. The output is a sharpened model π̂ that places at least 1−δ mass on the sequence-level arg-max response for all but an ε-fraction of prompts, effectively amortizing the expensive inference-time best-of-N computation into a single forward pass. The framework is agnostic to the model class Π and response space structure, though the primary motivation is autoregressive text generation over exponentially large sequence spaces.

## Method

The paper formalizes self-improvement as 'sharpening' — tilting a base policy toward responses with high self-reward, specifically the sequence-level log-probability r_self(y|x) = log π_base(y|x). Two algorithm families are analyzed: SFT-Sharpening, which filters best-of-N responses by self-reward and fine-tunes via supervised learning, and RLHF-Sharpening, which solves a KL-regularized RL objective (implemented via DPO or the online XPO algorithm). Within a novel sample-and-evaluate statistical framework, the paper proves a lower bound showing that any sharpening algorithm must incur sample complexity polynomial in the coverage coefficient C_cov = E[1/π_base(y*(x)|x)]. SFT-Sharpening is shown to be minimax optimal when the base model has sufficient coverage, while RLHF-Sharpening with online exploration (XPO) bypasses the coverage requirement entirely, depending instead on the sequential extrapolation coefficient SEC(Π) of the model class. Empirically, best-of-N sharpening improves accuracy over greedy decoding across 7 models and 6 reasoning tasks, and SFT-Sharpening via LoRA fine-tuning successfully amortizes these gains into the model weights.

## Results

Inference-time Best-of-N (BoN) sharpening with N=50 improves accuracy over greedy decoding across 6 tasks and 7 models (Figure 1a), with the largest gains on MATH (e.g., ~19% for Phi3.5-Mini). SFT-Sharpening amortizes this inference-time cost into a fine-tuning step, achieving a 19.24±2.41% accuracy lift over greedy on MATH (Phi3.5-Mini) and 12.46±1.08% on ProntoQA, while also increasing sequence-level log-likelihood by 48.33 and 5.64 points respectively (Table 1). Theoretically, SFT-Sharpening is shown to be minimax optimal in the sample-and-evaluate framework (Theorem 4.1), and RLHF-Sharpening with online exploration (XPO) provably bypasses the coverage lower bound by depending on the sequential extrapolation coefficient instead (Theorem 4.3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary empirical baseline is greedy decoding, against which both inference-time BoN sharpening and SFT-Sharpening are compared. BoN sharpening consistently outperforms greedy decoding for at least one dataset per model and at least one model per dataset, with the most reliable gains on MATH and ProntoQA. SFT-Sharpening (the amortized training-time version) also beats greedy decoding on all four model-dataset pairs tested, though the fine-tuned model does not fully reach the performance of inference-time BoN sharpening. The paper does not directly benchmark against competing self-improvement methods (e.g., STaR, self-rewarding LMs, or constitutional AI); instead, it positions its contribution as a theoretical framework with empirical validation of the sharpening mechanism. The theoretical comparison is between SFT-Sharpening and RLHF-Sharpening: SFT-Sharpening is minimax optimal but requires sufficient coverage of the base model, while RLHF-Sharpening with online exploration (XPO) removes the coverage dependence at the cost of requiring a margin assumption and a bounded sequential extrapolation coefficient.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the empirical self-improvement literature (Huang et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022b; Pang et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2024) by providing the first theoretical framework for understanding why and when self-training works. It repurposes standard alignment tools—DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) for RLHF-Sharpening, BOND-style best-of-N distillation (Sessa et al., 2024) for SFT-Sharpening, and XPO (Xie et al., 2024) for online exploration—but reframes them under a unified 'sharpening' objective where the model's own log-probability serves as the self-reward. The key conceptual departure is connecting self-improvement to the optimization-vs-sampling tradeoff in theoretical computer science, and introducing a sample-and-evaluate statistical framework with coverage-based lower bounds that had no prior analogue in the self-training literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first minimax-optimal analysis of self-improvement algorithms, showing that SFT-Sharpening is optimal under coverage while RLHF-Sharpening can bypass coverage via online exploration—a clean separation that should guide algorithm design. However, the empirical validation is modest (single-digit to ~20% accuracy lifts on small model-dataset pairs), the self-reward is deliberately stylized (raw log-probability), and key assumptions (realizability, margin, bounded concentrability) may not hold for large-scale LLMs. Open questions include whether richer self-rewards (LLM-as-judge, self-consistency) admit similar guarantees, whether the 1/δ factor in SFT-Sharpening is removable, and how the framework extends to truly sequential tasks where amortization is provably impossible.
