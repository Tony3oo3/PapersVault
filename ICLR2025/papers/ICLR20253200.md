## Problem

Existing knowledge distillation methods for image super-resolution are tailored to specific teacher-student architectures, supporting either depth or width compression but deteriorating the student when transferred to another setting. No prior KD framework is universally applicable across diverse backbones and compounded compression scenarios (simultaneous depth and width reduction), which is the more realistic deployment requirement.

## Input / Output

The method takes a bicubically downsampled low-resolution RGB image and produces a high-resolution reconstruction at ×2, ×3, or ×4 scale. It is evaluated on standard SR benchmarks (Set5, Set14, BSD100, Urban100) using EDSR, RCAN, and SwinIR backbones, with training on DIV2K. During training, the method additionally consumes intermediate feature maps from both teacher and student networks at multiple distillation positions, and the Block Prior Mixer stochastically routes the enhanced feature through either the student's or teacher's remaining network blocks.

## Method

MiPKD introduces a two-granularity dark knowledge mixing mechanism inspired by masked autoencoders. The Feature Prior Mixer encodes teacher and student feature maps at each distillation position into a unified latent space via separate CNN encoders, fuses them using a random 3D binary mask (element-wise product with complementary masks), and decodes the mixed latent back to the original feature space; the resulting enhanced feature is supervised by the teacher's feature via L1 loss. The Block Prior Mixer then stochastically (with probability 0.5) routes the enhanced feature through either the student's or teacher's remaining network blocks, forming a dynamic ensemble sub-network whose final SR output is supervised by the teacher's output. An auxiliary auto-encoder loss stabilizes the encoder-decoder in early training. This multi-granularity design—combining fine-grained feature-level mixing with coarse-grained block-level propagation—mitigates the capacity disparity between teacher and student and is applicable to both CNN and Transformer SR architectures without architectural modification.

## Results

On the EDSR ×2 width-compression setting (c256b32 → c64b32), MiPKD achieves 32.56 dB PSNR on Urban100, outperforming the scratch-trained student (31.96 dB) by 0.6 dB and the best prior SR-specific KD method CSD (32.26 dB) by 0.3 dB. On the RCAN ×4 depth-compression setting (c64b20 → c64b6), MiPKD reaches 26.66 dB on Urban100, a 0.30 dB gain over scratch training. MiPKD also extends to the SwinIR Transformer backbone, improving Urban100 PSNR by 0.22 dB over scratch at ×4 (26.37 vs. 26.15 dB), while conventional feature-based KD methods (FitNet, FAKD) actually degrade Transformer SR performance below scratch.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MiPKD consistently outperforms all seven baselines across EDSR, RCAN, and SwinIR backbones at ×2, ×3, and ×4 scales. On the hardest Urban100 benchmark, MiPKD beats Logits-KD (Hinton et al.) by 0.58 dB (EDSR ×2: 32.56 vs. 31.98) and RKD (Park et al.) by 0.60 dB, while AT (Zagoruyko & Komodakis) and FitNet (Romero et al.) actually underperform the scratch-trained student by 0.25 dB and 1.50 dB respectively, confirming that generic feature-alignment losses are harmful for SR. Among SR-specific KD methods, MiPKD surpasses FAKD (He et al.) by 0.52 dB and CSD (Wang et al.) by 0.30 dB on EDSR ×2 Urban100, and outperforms CrossKD (Fang et al.) by 0.35 dB on RCAN ×4 Urban100 (26.66 vs. 26.53 dB). Notably, CSD is only applicable to width compression and CrossKD only to depth compression, whereas MiPKD handles both settings and compounded compression (Table 12), where it achieves 25.89 dB vs. 25.79 dB for the best two-stage alternative (FAKD+CSD). On the SwinIR Transformer, MiPKD gains 0.41 dB over scratch at ×4 Urban100, while FitNet and FAKD degrade performance below scratch (25.80–25.81 vs. 26.15 dB), highlighting MiPKD's architecture-agnostic advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the masked autoencoder (MAE) paradigm—originally designed for self-supervised pretraining—into a knowledge distillation framework for super-resolution. It departs from prior SR-specific KD methods (FAKD, CSD, CrossKD) that are tightly coupled to particular compression axes (depth-only or width-only), and from generic feature-based KD (FitNet, AT, RKD) that the authors show often *deteriorate* SR students. The key conceptual move is introducing a two-granularity 'mixture of priors' mechanism: a feature-level encoder-decoder that fuses teacher and student representations in a shared latent space, and a block-level stochastic router that reassembles sub-networks from both models, thereby making the distillation signal architecture-agnostic.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value lies in providing a single KD recipe that works across depth, width, and compounded compression for both CNN (EDSR, RCAN) and Transformer (SwinIR) backbones, filling a gap where prior methods required re-engineering per setting. However, the gains are modest (0.2–0.6 dB over scratch training) and the added training cost (~0.38 s/step over Logits-KD) may limit adoption in very large-scale training. Open questions include whether the stochastic block routing can be made more principled (e.g., learned rather than Bernoulli-sampled), whether the approach extends to video SR or other low-level restoration tasks, and whether the random masking strategy's superiority over similarity-based masks (Cosine, CKA) reflects a genuine insight or is an artifact of the small model sizes tested.
