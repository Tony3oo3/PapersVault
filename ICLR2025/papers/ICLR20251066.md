## Problem

Existing memorization evaluation, detection, and mitigation methods for text-to-image diffusion models operate from a global perspective, comparing entire generated images to training images. This global approach fails for local memorization—where only specific image regions are replicated—because variations in non-memorized regions can easily trick similarity metrics and inflate detection variance, producing many false negatives. Moreover, no prior method can localize which specific regions are memorized, preventing targeted investigation and mitigation.

## Input / Output

The method requires only a text prompt and a pre-trained Stable Diffusion v1-4 model, performing a single inference pass (~2 s on an RTX 4090) without accessing the training dataset or conducting nearest-neighbor search. The BE mask is extracted from cross-attention maps at the first two 64-pixel downsampling layers of the U-Net during the final denoising step, using raw continuous attention scores as weights rather than a binary threshold. Outputs include a spatial local memorization mask, a masked local magnitude detection score (LD), an optimized prompt embedding for mitigation, and a localized similarity score (LS) combining SSCD with a masked L2 distance.

## Method

The core contribution is the 'bright ending' (BE) phenomenon: during the final denoising step, the end text token's cross-attention map is normally dark (attention shifts from semantics to fine details), but in memorized generations it exhibits abnormally high scores on coarser image-patch structures corresponding to the memorized regions. This BE attention map directly functions as an automatic local memorization mask, requiring no training data, no thresholding, and no nearest-neighbor search. The mask is then integrated into existing frameworks via element-wise multiplication: detection computes a masked local magnitude (LD) that reweights the global magnitude by BE attention scores and normalizes by their mean; mitigation uses LD as both the triggering signal and the loss function for gradient-based prompt optimization; and evaluation replaces the global L2 distance with a BE-masked L2 distance combined with SSCD. This localized perspective amplifies the detection signal for local memorization while leaving global and non-memorized cases largely unchanged, narrowing the performance gap and setting new state-of-the-art across all three tasks.

## Results

For local memorization detection, the proposed BE-masked local magnitude (LD) consistently outperforms the global magnitude baseline (D) across all inference steps: at T=1, LD achieves AUC 0.943 vs. 0.918 and T@1%F 0.731 vs. 0.629; at T=50, the gap widens dramatically with T@1%F of 0.926 vs. 0.560. For global memorization, both methods perform near-perfectly (AUC ≥ 0.979), confirming BE does not degrade existing performance. On the evaluation metric front, the proposed localized similarity (LS) achieves F1 of 0.995 for local memorization, surpassing SSCD alone (0.940) and the global L2+SSCD combination S (0.991), while maintaining perfect F1 (1.000) for global memorization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Wen et al. (2024), the primary baseline for both detection and mitigation, the proposed BE-masked approach wins decisively on local memorization: at T=50, T@1%F jumps from 0.560 to 0.926, and at T=1, AUC improves from 0.918 to 0.943. For global memorization, the two methods are essentially tied (AUC 0.979–0.981 at T=1, both reaching 1.000 at T=10), confirming BE introduces no regression. In mitigation, the paper reports that for local memorization under high-utility settings, the BE-based method reduces similarity scores by roughly twice as much as the Wen et al. baseline while incurring less than half the CLIP score degradation, yielding a substantially better privacy-utility trade-off. Against SSCD (Pizzi et al., 2022) as a standalone evaluation metric, the proposed LS metric improves local memorization F1 from 0.940 to 0.995, while SSCD already achieves perfect 1.000 F1 on global memorization—showing SSCD's well-known weakness on partial/local copies is the specific gap BE addresses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the memorization detection and mitigation framework of Wen et al. (2024), which uses global latent-space magnitude as a detection signal and prompt optimization as mitigation, by introducing a localization dimension via the 'bright ending' (BE) cross-attention phenomenon. It also builds on the broader memorization-in-diffusion-models literature (Carlini et al. 2023, Somepalli et al. 2023a/b, Chen et al. 2024) and the observation that prompts act as 'keys' to model memory, but departs from prior work by shifting from a global image-level perspective to a local patch-level one. Unlike Ren et al. (2024), which uses cross-attention entropy across all tokens and multiple steps for detection, BE exploits the raw final-step attention of a single end token to produce a spatial memorization mask, serving a fundamentally different objective (localization vs. detection).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The local-vs-global distinction is practically critical for copyright litigation, where even a small memorized region poses legal risk while unmemorized regions do not; the BE mask provides a simple, single-inference-pass diagnostic that requires no access to training data, making it deployable by model users who lack the original dataset. However, the evaluation is confined to Stable Diffusion v1-4 with a relatively small prompt set (300 memorized, 500 non-memorized), leaving open whether the BE phenomenon generalizes to newer architectures (e.g., DiT-based models like SD3 or FLUX) or to class-conditional and video diffusion settings. A key open question is whether the end-token attention anomaly is an artifact of the specific tokenizer and U-Net cross-attention design or a more general signature of overfitting in iterative denoising.
