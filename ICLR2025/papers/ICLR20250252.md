## Problem

Existing long-context benchmarks (NIAH, Ruler, Needlebench) evaluate a model's ability to retrieve and understand information from long input sequences but do not assess its capacity to generate coherent, instruction-compliant long-form text outputs. This gap is critical because real-world applications—design proposals, technical documentation, creative writing—require models to produce 16K+ token outputs that adhere to specific constraints at designated positions, a capability that no prior benchmark systematically measures.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance consists of a main task prompt (e.g., 'design a 100-floor skyscraper') augmented with randomly assigned specific task instructions of three types: Single (inject a detail at one position), Range (apply a constraint across a contiguous span), and Periodic (repeat a constraint at fixed intervals). The model must produce a continuous long-form output of 16K or 32K tokens, segmented into subtasks (e.g., one entry per floor or per week). Evaluation yields three scores: Completion Rate (fraction of subtasks produced), STIC-1 (constraint accuracy over generated subtasks only), and STIC-2 (constraint accuracy over the full expected output, penalizing missing subtasks).

## Method

LongGenBench constructs 800 synthetic examples per scenario across four task families—two temporal (weekly/daily diary, weekly/daily menu) and two spatial (100/361-floor skyscraper, 10×10/19×19 urban grid)—each with short (16K) and long (32K) variants. Over 20 templates per instruction type (SI, RI, PI) are used with randomly assigned positions to ensure controlled coverage across all textual locations, and a corresponding check set is generated alongside each prompt for automated verification. The evaluation pipeline splits the model's output into subtasks, matches each against the check set, and uses Llama 3.3-70B as a binary yes/no classifier per constraint (validated at 100% agreement on 300 manually labeled points). The key design insight is that instruction-following over extended generation is a distinct skill from long-input retrieval: the Pearson correlation between RULER scores and LongGenBench scores is only 0.51 at 16K and 0.66 at 32K, confirming that strong retrieval does not guarantee strong generation.

## Results

Across ten long-context LLMs evaluated on LongGenBench, even the best-performing model (GPT-4o-mini) achieves only 32.4% weighted average (wAvg = CR × STIC-2) at 16K tokens, dropping to 16.6% for Llama3.1-70B at 32K tokens. Completion rates (CR) range from 5.6% (Mamba-2.8B at 32K) to 97.0% (GPT-4o-mini at 16K), while STIC-2 scores remain below 34% for all models. Performance degrades sharply as output length increases from 16K to 32K tokens, with most models losing 50–80% of their wAvg score. Notably, the smaller Llama3.1-8B (16.0% wAvg at 32K) outperforms several larger models, highlighting that parameter count alone does not guarantee long-form generation quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is against RULER, a synthetic long-context retrieval benchmark. The paper demonstrates that strong performance on RULER (long-input retrieval) does not translate to strong performance on LongGenBench (long-output generation): the Pearson correlation between RULER scores and LongGenBench wAvg is only 0.51 at 16K tokens and 0.66 at 32K tokens, indicating substantial divergence. For example, models that achieve near-perfect scores on RULER's Needle-in-a-Haystack tasks still score below 35% wAvg on LongGenBench, revealing that input comprehension and output generation are distinct capabilities. The paper further shows that periodic instructions (e.g., 'every 10th floor has a garden') are the hardest task type, with performance hierarchy single > range > periodic, while RULER does not test any generation-side constraint adherence. This gap underscores that existing long-context benchmarks systematically overestimate a model's readiness for real-world long-form generation tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper inverts the Needle-in-a-Haystack paradigm: where NIAH, RULER, and NeedleBench test a model's ability to retrieve information buried in long inputs, LongGenBench tests whether a model can maintain instruction adherence across long outputs (the authors term this 'Reversed NIAH'). It extends the long-form generation literature (ELI5, LongWriter), which has been limited to outputs of ≤2K tokens, by scaling to 16K–32K tokens and introducing structured constraint types (Single, Range, Periodic) that enable automated, interpretable evaluation. The evaluation pipeline repurposes LLM-as-a-judge (Llama 3.3-70B) as a binary classifier for per-segment instruction compliance, sidestepping the interpretability problems of holistic LLM scoring while avoiding the cost of manual evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that models scoring well on RULER (long-input retrieval) still achieve only 15–33% weighted-average on LongGenBench (long-output generation), with a Pearson correlation of merely 0.51–0.66—challenges the assumption that long-context capability is a single unified skill and suggests that output-side coherence is an under-addressed bottleneck. Open questions include whether the gap stems from instruction-tuning data length (most SFT data is <200 tokens), from self-attention's difficulty maintaining constraints over autoregressive generation, or from both. The benchmark's limitations (synthetic scenarios, instruction-following only, no creativity or rationality assessment) mean it captures a necessary but not sufficient slice of real-world long-form generation quality.
