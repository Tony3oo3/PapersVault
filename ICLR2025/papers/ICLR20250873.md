## Problem

Existing multimodal sleep staging methods assume the same modalities are available at both training and testing time, making them impractical for ubiquitous home monitoring where only a single modality (e.g., EEG) is accessible. Prior multimodal coordination approaches lack targeted category-level supervision during knowledge transfer and do not filter unreliable auxiliary modality information, limiting how effectively the primary modality benefits from multimodal training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250873/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 30-second PSG epochs (downsampled to 100 Hz) containing synchronized multi-channel EEG (6 ch), EOG (2 ch), and chin EMG (1 ch) signals. During training all three modalities are jointly processed, but during testing only a single primary modality is fed to the model, with the other two treated as absent. The output is a 5-class AASM sleep stage label (Wake, N1, N2, N3, REM) per epoch, evaluated under a cross-subject protocol where training and testing subjects are disjoint (5-fold subject-level split).

## Method

SleepSMC uses a FeatureNet (dual-scale CNN) backbone for modality-specific feature extraction and classification, augmented by two coordination mechanisms. First, supervised modality-level instance contrastive coordination treats all U×I modality-level instances in a batch as anchors, pulling same-category instances (across and within modalities) together and pushing different-category instances apart via a supervised contrastive loss. Second, uncertainty-based feature weighting estimates each auxiliary modality's reliability by applying T=9 rounds of random masking (15% zero-replacement) with frozen gradients, computing the variance of correct-class confidence scores, and converting this into an exponential-softmax weight that downweights noisy auxiliary features in the contrastive loss. The primary modality is excluded from this weighting. The total loss sums modality-specific cross-entropy classification losses with the supervised contrastive loss, and at test time only the primary modality's feature extractor and classifier are deployed.

## Results

On the multimodal testing scenario, SleepSMC achieves state-of-the-art cross-subject performance across all four datasets: 79.30% Accuracy / 78.15% Macro F1 / 73.44% Kappa on ISRUC-S3, 86.86% / 81.93% / 80.58% on MASS-SS3, 81.58% / 75.58% / 74.50% on Sleep-EDF-78, and 77.10% / 74.62% / 70.18% on ISRUC-S1. In the unimodal testing scenario (only one modality at test time), SleepSMC improves over the second-best method by over 1% Accuracy on ISRUC-S3 for each primary modality (e.g., 76.46% for EEG vs. DrFuse's 75.32%), and by 2.1% for the weakest modality EMG (43.84% vs. 41.77%). The method also demonstrates robustness where several baselines (e.g., BSTT, SleepPrintNet, MMASleepNet) collapse to near-zero F1 on individual categories when tested with a single modality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SleepSMC outperforms all 11 baselines on all four datasets in the multimodal testing scenario, with the largest margins on ISRUC-S3 (+1.74% Accuracy over BSTT) and ISRUC-S1 (+2.66% over XSleepNet). In the unimodal testing scenario, the advantage is even more pronounced: traditional multimodal methods like SleepPrintNet and MMASleepNet collapse to near-zero F1 on multiple categories (e.g., SleepPrintNet achieves 0.0000 F1 on N3 and REM with EOG on ISRUC-S3), while SleepSMC maintains consistent performance across all categories. Among the three multimodal coordination baselines (SimCLR, DrFuse, MERL), SleepSMC leads by 1.14% Accuracy on EEG (76.46% vs. DrFuse 75.32%) and by 1.98% on EOG (74.44% vs. SimCLR 72.46%) on ISRUC-S3. On the weakest modality EMG, SleepSMC achieves 43.84% Accuracy versus 41.77% (SimCLR) and 39.81% (MERL), while BSTT degrades to 30.46% with only one category predicted. On MASS-SS3 unimodal EEG, SleepSMC reaches 85.17% Accuracy, surpassing the best baseline (DrFuse at 83.75%) by 1.42%, and on Sleep-EDF-78 EEG it achieves 79.59% vs. SimCLR's 78.24%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multimodal coordination paradigm (previously applied to ECG+text in MERL and EEG+skin response in EmotionKD) to sleep staging, introducing two key departures: (1) replacing unsupervised contrastive alignment with *supervised* modality-level instance contrastive coordination that uses category labels to define positive/negative pairs across all modality-level instances, and (2) adding an uncertainty-based adaptive weighting mechanism (via random-mask variance) that modulates how much each auxiliary modality contributes to the contrastive loss. The backbone is FeatureNet (a dual-scale CNN), and the contrastive loss formulation directly adapts the supervised contrastive learning framework of Khosla et al. (NeurIPS 2020), but applied at the modality-instance level rather than the sample level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap between high-accuracy but uncomfortable PSG and comfortable but less accurate unimodal devices, showing that multimodal training can substantially boost unimodal testing performance (e.g., +2.1% accuracy for EMG on ISRUC-S3). However, the method only simulates modality degradation via random masking during training and does not handle genuinely missing modalities at test time, nor has it been validated on truly ubiquitous sensors (ear-EEG, smartwatches, BCG). Open questions include whether the uncertainty-weighting mechanism generalizes to more than three modalities or to non-PSG signal types, and whether the theoretical bounds (Theorem 1) hold under distribution shift beyond the cross-subject setting tested here.
