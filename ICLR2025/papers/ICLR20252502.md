## Problem

Aligning LLMs to be both safe and helpful is challenging because these objectives are inherently in tension: a perfectly safe model refuses all requests, while a maximally helpful one may generate harmful content. Existing multi-objective RLHF methods resolve this by training separate reward models and running iterative PPO-based optimization, which requires expensive red teaming, large model-specific datasets, and multiple training iterations. Supervised alternatives like DPO only re-parameterize a single-reward objective and cannot directly handle the multi-reward safety-helpfulness tradeoff.

## Input / Output

The method consumes paired responses (y^hw, y^hl) ranked by helpfulness along with binary safety labels I_safe for each response, drawn from public datasets (UltraFeedback for helpfulness pairs, PKU-SafeRLHF for safety labels). A pre-trained language model serves as the reference policy π_ref, and only selected layers (second MLP in each transformer block) are unfrozen via PEFT. The output is a fine-tuned language model that simultaneously improves harmlessness (measured by discriminative and generative safety benchmarks) while preserving helpfulness (AlpacaEval 2.0). The algorithm samples equal-sized batches from safety and helpfulness datasets at each step to balance the two objectives.

## Method

BFPO re-parameterizes the multi-objective RLHF objective (which combines a helpfulness reward and a safety reward via a bilinear global reward g(y|x) = (p_safe*(y|x)+E_s)(p_help*(y≻π|x)+1/2)) into a single supervised loss. The key mechanism is a labeling function g_I(y^hw, y^hl|x) = (3/2)I_safe(y^hw|x) − (1/2)I_safe(y^hl|x) − α that assigns different target values to four classes of response pairs based on their safety labels, encoding the global preference ordering: safe+helpful > safe+unhelpful > unsafe+unhelpful > unsafe+helpful. The training loss (Eq. 15) minimizes the squared difference between the policy's implicit preference h_π(y^hw, y^hl) and the label g_I/τ, making it a direct supervised objective requiring no reward model or RL loop. The paper proves (Theorems 3.1–3.2) that this supervised problem is exactly equivalent to the multi-objective RLHF problem, and introduces a shifting parameter α to separate unsafe-unsafe pairs from zero, plus balanced batch sampling (buffer training) to mitigate the helpfulness-safety tension during optimization.

## Results

On Mistral-7B fine-tuning (Table 1), BFPO achieves the highest average harmlessness score of 77.16% (Savg.) and the best generative harmlessness of 95.24%, while maintaining a competitive helpfulness score of 13.33 on AlpacaEval 2.0. In the red-teaming fine-tuning of Zephyr-7b-beta (Table 2), BFPO further improves Savg. from 60.99% to 73.90% and Gen. from 62.94% to 88.79% using only 1.5K red-teaming prompts. Compared to PPO-based methods (Table 3), BFPO uses 30K data points with no red teaming and a single training iteration, yet outperforms Beaver (300K data, 3 iterations, Savg. 71.80) and Llama2 (1M data, 6 iterations, Savg. 73.80) on both Savg. and AlpacaEval.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BFPO outperforms all five baselines on the primary safety metric (Savg.), achieving 77.16% versus 60.99% (DPO-H/Zephyr), 76.66% (DPO-S), 49.03% (DPO naive mix), 74.09% (IPO), and 61.71% (MORL). Against DPO-H (Zephyr), BFPO improves generative harmlessness by 32.3 points (95.24 vs. 62.94) while also improving helpfulness (13.33 vs. 10.99). Against DPO-S, which achieves the highest Gen. score (96.91), BFPO trades only 1.67 points on Gen. for a massive 9-point gain on AlpacaEval (13.33 vs. 4.34), demonstrating that safety-only training severely degrades helpfulness. Against the naive-mix DPO baseline, BFPO dramatically improves Gen. (95.24 vs. 39.71) and Savg. (77.16 vs. 49.03), confirming that simply combining datasets without the bi-factorial labeling function leads to contradictory training signals. Against IPO, BFPO wins on Gen. (95.24 vs. 89.76) and Savg. (77.16 vs. 74.09) with a marginal AlpacaEval edge (13.33 vs. 13.15). Against MORL (multi-objective RL with ArmoRM), BFPO achieves substantially higher Gen. (95.24 vs. 64.88) and Savg. (77.16 vs. 61.71), which the authors attribute to the public reward model's insufficient score distinguishability without model-specific red teaming.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DPO/IPO line of supervised preference optimization (Rafailov et al., 2023; Azar et al., 2024) from single-reward to multi-reward settings, specifically targeting the safety-helpfulness tension identified in Llama 2 (Touvron et al., 2023) and Safe RLHF (Dai et al., 2024). The key conceptual departure is introducing a labeling function g_I that encodes a global preference ranking over both safety and helpfulness, enabling the multi-objective RLHF objective to be re-parameterized into a single supervised loss without requiring separate reward models or iterative RL. This positions BFPO as a bridge between the theoretically-grounded multi-objective RLHF formulation and the practical efficiency of DPO-style training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BFPO offers a practical path to safety alignment without the costly red-teaming, reward model training, and multi-iteration RL loops that methods like Llama 2 and Beaver require, potentially lowering the barrier for open-source model developers. However, the method is currently limited to two objectives with specific label formats (binary safety + pairwise helpfulness), and all experiments are on 7B-scale models, leaving open whether the approach generalizes to larger models, more than two conflicting objectives, or different safety label granularities. The theoretical equivalence (Theorem 3.2) is clean but relies on a bilinear reward structure that may not capture all realistic safety-helpfulness interactions.
