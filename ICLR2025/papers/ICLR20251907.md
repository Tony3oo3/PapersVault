## Problem

Learning hierarchical policies from offline data is fundamentally difficult because high-level actions are typically unobservable in the dataset, and the data may have been generated by a completely different (non-hierarchical) policy structure. Even when data comes from a hierarchical controller, any modification to the low-level policy parameters renders standard offline RL formulations ill-posed. This gap has prevented offline RL from being practically useful for hierarchical control in safety-critical, real-world systems where online exploration is infeasible.

## Input / Output

The method consumes offline state-only trajectories (or state-action trajectories) along with an approximate one-step dynamics model and the structural specification of the low-level policy (which may be explicit, e.g., LQR/PID, or implicit, e.g., MPC/LP-based). It produces a reconstructed high-level action dataset where each state transition is paired with an inferred high-level action (goal state or command), which is then fed to any off-the-shelf offline RL algorithm. A key design choice is that the inverse problem is solved per-timestep rather than jointly across the trajectory, trading optimality for computational tractability, and the framework requires no gradient propagation through the low-level policy during high-level training.

## Method

OHIO formulates policy inversion as a regularized maximum likelihood estimation problem: given observed state transitions and the known low-level policy structure, it recovers the most likely high-level action u_t that would have induced each transition under the hierarchical policy. For explicit policies (e.g., LQR), the inverse admits a closed-form solution via the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of the policy gain; for implicit optimization-based policies (e.g., LP-based network controllers), the inverse is itself cast as a linear program using absolute sub-optimality formulations from inverse optimization theory. Numerical alternatives include gradient-based optimization through the differentiable policy or gradient-free methods like CEM. The reconstructed dataset is then trained with standard offline RL algorithms (IQL, CQL, BC) to yield the high-level policy, which at inference time feeds goal states or commands into the fixed low-level controller. This decoupling means the high-level policy can be retrained or fine-tuned without re-deriving the inverse, and the low-level optimization-based policy inherently enforces domain constraints, providing safety guarantees that end-to-end architectures lack.

## Results

On the dynamic vehicle routing task (NYC and Shenzhen), OHIO with BC or IQL achieves 85–98% normalized score across all eight datasets, consistently outperforming end-to-end (E2E) baselines which range from 32–90% and frequently collapse below zero (e.g., E2E BC scores −35.2 on NYC). On supply chain inventory management, OHIO IQL achieves 89.9% on the transfer test (1W10S-MPC-CAP) versus 45.8% for E2E IQL, demonstrating robustness to a 33% reduction in storage capacity. On robotic manipulation (RoboSuite), OHIO maintains 92.7–98.9% under modified controller stiffness/damping, while standard IQL drops to 2.9–86.8%, highlighting OHIO's robustness to low-level configuration changes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OHIO is compared against four baselines: (i) End-to-End (E2E) RL with the same offline algorithms, (ii) an 'Observed State' baseline that naively uses the next observed state as the high-level action, (iii) Hierarchical RL (HRL) where both levels are learned jointly, and (iv) standard offline RL (IQL) without dataset reconstruction. The 'Observed State' baseline is catastrophically ineffective, scoring 0.05–0.4% on robotics tasks, confirming that policy inversion is essential. OHIO substantially outperforms E2E on high-dimensional network problems: on NYC-DISP, OHIO CQL reaches 94.1% while E2E CQL scores only 32.5%, and on the supply chain transfer test (1W10S-MPC-CAP), OHIO IQL achieves 89.9% versus 45.8% for E2E IQL. On robotic manipulation, OHIO is dramatically more robust to controller modifications (98.9% vs. 86.8% for modified stiffness on Lift; 92.7% vs. 18.6% on Door), whereas standard IQL degrades severely. Against HRL, OHIO matches or exceeds performance (89.6 vs. 77.4 on Lift; 94.1 vs. 84.3 on Door) while being independent of dataset coverage, as HRL drops from 84.3% to 72.7% when the Door dataset is reduced.

## Relation to Prior Work

OHIO sits at the intersection of hierarchical RL/IL (Le et al., 2018; Gupta et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2021; Ajay et al., 2021), offline RL (IQL, CQL), and inverse optimization (Chan et al., 2022; Chan & Kaw, 2020). Prior hierarchical offline RL methods either require data collected under the same hierarchical structure, learn high-level actions in raw observation space, or rely on complex multi-step training with intermediate objectives (e.g., trajectory autoencoders with hindsight relabeling). OHIO departs from this lineage by formulating the recovery of unobservable high-level actions as a principled inverse optimization problem, leveraging known low-level policy structure and approximate dynamics to construct a dataset directly amenable to off-the-shelf offline RL algorithms, regardless of the behavior policy that generated the data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This framework addresses a practical bottleneck in deploying hierarchical policies for safety-critical, real-world systems: the inability to train from existing operational data collected under arbitrary or unknown controller configurations. The demonstrated robustness to low-level parameter changes (e.g., stiffness/damping modifications) and the inherent constraint satisfaction via optimization-based low-level policies have direct implications for safe deployment in robotics and network operations. Key limitations include sensitivity to dynamics model accuracy, computational cost of the inverse problem, and the simplified per-timestep decomposition that neglects temporal correlations. Open questions include robustness to model misspecification, more sophisticated temporal estimation (filtering/smoothing), and extension to deeper hierarchies or partially observable settings.
