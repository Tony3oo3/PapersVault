## Problem

Existing knowledge editing methods (ROME, MEMIT, PMET) suffer from a severe and previously under-characterized failure mode called Specificity Failure: when the edited subject appears in the inference context, the model incorrectly predicts the edited object for unrelated queries, with accuracy dropping by more than half. The paper identifies the root cause as an Attention Drift phenomenon, where certain attention heads assign excessive scores to the edited entity's last token, causing the model to over-focus on the edited snippet rather than attending to contextual semantics.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained autoregressive LLM (GPT-J-6B, Llama3-8B, GPT-NeoX-20B, Llama2-13B, TinyLlama-1.1B), a single factual association to edit (s, r, o_edit), and the vanilla model's attention weight distributions as a reference. During optimization of the target vector v*, SADR dynamically computes attention weights for both the vanilla and edited models at each step, selecting heads whose attention on the subject's last token exceeds the vanilla model's per-layer maximum. The output is a rank-one update to the second MLP layer weight matrix, yielding an edited model that preserves specificity when the edited subject appears in context.

## Method

SADR (Selective Attention Drift Restriction) augments the standard locate-then-edit optimization objective (which maximizes P(o_edit|s,r) while controlling essence drift via KL divergence) with an additional regularization term that penalizes attention distribution shifts. Specifically, for each layer l, a head h is selected if its attention weight on the subject's last token in the edited model exceeds the maximum attention weight on that token across all heads in the vanilla model; for these selected heads, a KL divergence between the vanilla and edited attention distributions at the last token position is added to the loss. The final objective is L(z) + γ·L_SADR(z), where γ controls the strength of the attention constraint. The selectivity is critical: ablations show that constraining only over-focused heads outperforms constraining all heads, since not all attention drift is harmful. The method is plug-and-play, requiring only the attention weight comparison and KL computation during the existing v* optimization loop, and is validated across three editing paradigms (locate-then-edit, parameter-preserving, meta-learning) and five model sizes from 1.1B to 20B parameters.

## Results

On GPT-J-6b with ROME, SADR improves the Relation Score (RS) from 11.94 to 27.75 and the Distract Neighborhood Score (DNS) from 30.42 to 49.35, while maintaining Edit Success (ES) at 99.76% (vs. 99.88% baseline). Across five models (1.1B–20B) and five editing methods, SADR achieves relative improvements of up to 130.9% on RS and 295.8% on DNS (e.g., GPT-NeoX-20b ROME: RS 15.11→34.89, DNS 8.84→34.95), with less than a 3% decrease in edit success. The average score (harmonic mean of ES, PS, NS, RS, DNS) increases substantially, e.g., from 33.56 to 57.74 for ROME on GPT-J and from 23.45 to 54.81 for ROME on GPT-NeoX.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SADR consistently outperforms all five baselines on the two specificity metrics (RS and DNS) that expose the Attention Drift problem, while incurring negligible loss in edit success. Against ROME, SADR more than doubles RS on GPT-J (11.94→27.75) and GPT-NeoX (15.11→34.89), and nearly quadruples DNS on GPT-NeoX (8.84→34.95). Against MEMIT, SADR improves RS by 34–103% relative (e.g., 17.44→35.38 on Llama3) and DNS by 25–50% (e.g., 30.55→45.57 on Llama3). Against PMET, gains are similar in magnitude (RS: 19.84→34.68 on Llama3; DNS: 32.12→51.22 on Llama3). The parameter-preserving method WISE and meta-learning method MEND also show specificity failure (RS ~25, DNS ~5–6), and SADR improves both (WISE: RS 25.24→36.44, DNS 5.87→12.73; MEND: RS 24.46→39.73, DNS 5.40→7.38), confirming the phenomenon is not unique to locate-then-edit methods. In all cases, ES remains above 95% and NS is preserved or slightly improved, indicating SADR does not sacrifice the core editing capability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the locate-then-edit knowledge editing paradigm initiated by ROME (Meng et al., 2022) and extended by MEMIT (Meng et al., 2023) and PMET (Li et al., 2024), adding a KL-divergence regularization term (SADR) to their optimization objectives. It draws on causal tracing (Meng et al., 2022) and attention mechanism analysis (Geva et al., 2023) to diagnose the root cause of a failure mode previously noted but not mechanistically explained by Hoelscher-Obermaier et al. (2023). The method is positioned as a plug-and-play constraint applicable across all three editing paradigms (locate-then-edit, parameter-preserving, meta-learning), rather than a new editing algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of Attention Drift as the mechanistic cause of Specificity Failure provides a clear diagnostic for why edited models break when the edited entity reappears in context, and the SADR fix is simple enough to be adopted by any existing editing pipeline. However, the paper is limited to single-fact edits on transformer architectures, and the Relation task still shows only partial recovery, suggesting that attention drift is not the sole source of specificity degradation. Open questions include whether SADR generalizes to batch/sequential editing, non-transformer models (e.g., Mamba), and whether the selective head-restriction criterion remains effective as edit count grows.
