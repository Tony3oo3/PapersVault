## Problem

US copyright law provides no protection for artistic styles, and existing legal standards (the substantial similarity test) rely on subjective, case-by-case qualitative judgments that are ill-suited to the unprecedented scale of AI-powered style mimicry. No prior work offers a quantitative, interpretable, and legally-grounded framework to determine whether a generative model has copied a specific artist's unique style, as opposed to merely producing images that are pixel-similar to individual training examples.

## Input / Output

The tool (ArtSavant) takes as input a portfolio of works by a target artist plus a set of generated images (or can auto-generate them by prompting text-to-image models with '{title} by {artist}'), and compares them against a reference corpus of ~91k artworks from 372 prolific artists scraped from WikiArt. It produces a structured report containing: a recognizability score (fraction of held-out works classified to the artist), a match confidence for the generated set, the specific interpretable tag signatures shared between the two sets, and side-by-side attribution image pairs from both sets exhibiting those signatures. All computation operates on frozen CLIP ViT-B/16 embeddings, so the full pipeline runs in 1–2 minutes on a single GPU.

## Method

The core reformulation treats style infringement detection as classification over image sets: if an artist's works are consistently mapped back to them over 371 others, a unique style exists, and infringement is argued by showing the same classification holds for generated images. Two complementary methods implement this. DeepMatch (holistic) is a 2-layer MLP trained on frozen CLIP embeddings with weighted sampling, aggregated via majority voting over the image set, achieving 89.3% artist recognition on held-out real art. TagMatch (analytic) first assigns atomic tags via per-aspect zero-shot CLIP classification (260 concepts across 16 aspects, z-score threshold 1.75), then runs an iterative powerset-based composition algorithm to surface multi-tag signatures unique to each artist (requiring ≥3 co-occurring works). Matching ranks reference artists by the uniqueness of shared tag compositions, scoring each as (number of artists sharing the tag) + |frequency gap|, averaged over the top-10 most unique matches. This yields inherently interpretable, attributable evidence of which specific stylistic elements are copied, directly mirroring the analytic/holistic dichotomy of the legal substantial similarity test.

## Results

On the WikiArt dataset of 372 artists (~91k artworks), DeepMatch achieves 72.8% per-image classification accuracy and, after majority-vote aggregation, recognizes the correct artist for 89.3% of artists on held-out real art. TagMatch achieves 61.6% top-1, 82.5% top-5, and 88.4% top-10 artist identification accuracy on the same held-out split. In the empirical style-copying study across three text-to-image models (Stable Diffusion v1.4, v2.0, and OpenJourney), the average match rate is 20.2%, meaning roughly one in five prolific artists has their style consistently recognized in generated images under simple prompting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Concept Bottleneck Model (CBM) baseline of Koh et al. (2020), TagMatch achieves 61.5% top-1 accuracy on real art, slightly below CBM's 62.8%, but provides inherently interpretable tag signatures and faithful attribution (retrievable example images) that CBM lacks, making it more suitable for legal argumentation. On the image-retrieval task (Figure 14), DeepMatch substantially outperforms both CSD (Somepalli et al., 2024; mean AUROC 0.891) and zero-shot CLIP (Radford et al., 2021; mean AUROC 0.861), achieving a mean AUROC of 0.979, because DeepMatch is explicitly trained to distinguish individual artists rather than general art styles. The paper notes that per-artist accuracy rankings are highly correlated across different backbones (CLIP, DINOv2, CSD), suggesting the relative recognizability signal is classifier-agnostic. Overall, the paper positions its methods as complementary to image-similarity baselines: DeepMatch provides a stronger holistic signal for detecting style copying, while TagMatch supplies the analytic, interpretable evidence needed in a legal context.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant technical approach to AI copy detection (image-to-image similarity via embeddings like DINO or CSD, as in Somepalli et al. 2022/2023/2024 and Carlini et al. 2023) by arguing that style infringement is fundamentally a classification-over-image-sets problem rather than a pairwise similarity problem. It operationalizes legal arguments from Sobel (2024) and O'Connor (2022) — that style is a constellation of individually unprotectable elements that become protectable in combination — into a quantitative framework with two complementary methods (holistic DeepMatch and analytic TagMatch). The artist identification literature (Karayev et al. 2014, Tan et al. 2017) provides the classification backbone, but the paper's key conceptual move is reframing this for legal interpretability and attribution, which prior technical works on style detection or protection (Glaze, watermarks, unlearning) do not address.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first quantitative, legally-grounded framework for arguing artistic style infringement, providing concrete evidence (20% of 372 artists at risk under simple prompting) for an otherwise purely qualitative legal debate that is actively unfolding in courts. The interpretability requirement (TagMatch's articulation of specific tag signatures with attribution examples) is essential for the tool to be admissible or persuasive in legal settings, distinguishing it from black-box similarity scores. Key limitations include the 372-artist reference set (which may overestimate uniqueness for artists in underrepresented styles), reliance on simple prompting (more sophisticated prompts or fine-tuning could increase copying), and CLIP-based tagging that has known issues with complex concepts. Open questions include how findings generalize to fine-tuned models, whether courts would accept quantitative style-matching evidence, and how the framework adapts as generative models improve.
