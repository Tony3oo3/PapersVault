## Problem

Despite the widespread empirical success of adversarial training, it remains theoretically unclear why standard training of neural networks produces models vulnerable to adversarial perturbations and how adversarial training specifically improves robustness. Prior theoretical analyses either rely on unrealistic data assumptions (e.g., Gaussian mixtures where standard training already achieves robustness) or do not account for the optimization dynamics of feature learning. This paper bridges the gap by providing a feature-learning-theoretic explanation grounded in a realistic structured data model.

## Input / Output

The theoretical model takes patch-structured inputs X = (x_1, ..., x_P) ∈ (R^d)^P with a class label y ∈ [k], where each patch is either a robust-feature patch (α_p·u_y + noise) or a non-robust-feature patch (β_p·v_y + noise). The two-layer smoothed ReLU CNN outputs k logits for classification. In adversarial training, the method additionally produces adversarial examples X̃ = X + Δ with ‖Δ‖_∞ ≤ ε via one-step gradient ascent on the margin loss. For real-image experiments, standard RGB images (28×28 MNIST, 32×32 CIFAR-10/SVHN) are fed into ResNet18 or WideResNet-34-10, and feature learning accuracy is measured on reconstructed feature-representative datasets derived from the network's own representation layer.

## Method

The paper introduces a patch-structured data model where each class has a robust feature (strong magnitude α_p but sparse across patches) and a non-robust feature (weak magnitude β_p but dense across patches), and analyzes a two-layer smoothed ReLU convolutional network trained via gradient descent on cross-entropy loss. The key theoretical result is that standard training implicitly learns non-robust features first because their density (more patches) causes their gradient contributions to dominate early in training, leading to a non-robust global minimum where perturbations aligned with non-robust feature directions flip predictions. Adversarial training introduces a critical saturation mechanism: once non-robust feature learning reaches a threshold proportional to the perturbation radius ε, the adversarial loss gradient for non-robust features vanishes (since ε/β_p ≳ 1), while robust feature learning continues unimpeded (since ε/α_p ≪ 1), provably steering the network toward a robust global minimum. The analysis leverages the Tensor Power Method to track the relative growth rates of robust and non-robust weight-feature correlations throughout training.

## Results

On MNIST, standard training achieves 99.56% clean accuracy but only 0.04% robust accuracy, while adversarial training improves robust accuracy to 94.43% with minimal clean accuracy loss (99.41%). On CIFAR-10, adversarial training raises robust accuracy from 0.00% to 45.13% (at the cost of clean accuracy dropping from 95.74% to 86.28%), and on SVHN from 0.16% to 58.55%. Feature learning accuracy confirms the theory: standard training predominantly learns non-robust features (88.11% on MNIST, 91.79% on CIFAR-10), whereas adversarial training suppresses non-robust feature learning (39.49% on MNIST, 10.88% on CIFAR-10) and strengthens robust feature learning (69.19% on MNIST, 67.88% on CIFAR-10).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that does not benchmark against other adversarial training methods; instead, it compares standard training (gradient descent on clean data) versus adversarial training (PGD-based) as the two paradigms. Across all three datasets, adversarial training dramatically outperforms standard training on robust accuracy (e.g., 94.43% vs. 0.04% on MNIST, 45.13% vs. 0.00% on CIFAR-10, 58.55% vs. 0.16% on SVHN) while incurring only modest clean accuracy degradation. The targeted attack experiments on CIFAR-10 further validate the theory: non-robust-feature-based PGD (NRF-PGD) achieves 71–80% attack success against standard-trained models, whereas robust-feature-based PGD (RF-PGD) achieves only 1–11%, confirming that adversarial examples predominantly exploit non-robust features. After adversarial training, both attack types drop to below 26% success rate, with NRF-PGD falling to 0.34–15.16%, demonstrating that adversarial training effectively neutralizes the non-robust feature vulnerability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the feature purification framework of Allen-Zhu & Li (FOCS 2022) from binary to multi-class classification, and formalizes the robust/non-robust feature decomposition empirically observed by Ilyas et al. (NeurIPS 2019) into a rigorous patch-structured data model. It departs from prior theoretical analyses of adversarial training (Li et al. 2020; Javanmard & Soltanolkotabi 2022; Chen et al. 2023) that study linear classifiers under Gaussian-mixture data, where standard training also converges to robust solutions — a discrepancy with practice. By adopting a non-linear two-layer CNN on patch-structured data, the paper creates a setting where both robust and non-robust global minima coexist, enabling a clean separation of why standard training fails and how adversarial training succeeds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first provable, feature-learning-based explanation for why standard training produces adversarially vulnerable models (non-robust feature dominance due to density) and how adversarial training corrects this (two-phase dynamics where non-robust learning saturates while robust learning continues). The targeted attack experiments (NRF-PGD vs. RF-PGD) offer a novel empirical validation that adversarial examples predominantly exploit non-robust features. Key limitations include the restriction to two-layer networks, a highly structured data model with orthogonal features, and one-step gradient ascent rather than multi-step PGD; extending the analysis to deep networks and realistic architectures remains an open and important direction.
