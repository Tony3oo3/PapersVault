## Problem

Existing LLMs exhibit significant performance gaps between dominant languages (English, Chinese) and non-dominant languages due to imbalanced training data. Prior approaches either require expensive multilingual data or align non-dominant languages to dominant ones, which is bottlenecked by the dominant language's ceiling. This paper reframes the imbalance itself as a usable reward signal, enabling self-improving multilingual optimization without human-authored preference data.

## Input / Output

The method takes parallel multilingual instruction prompts (e.g., Alpagasus instructions translated into en, es, ru, de, fr) or arithmetic reasoning problems (GSM8K translated into multiple languages) as input. The model generates a response in each language, then self-translates responses between the dominant and non-dominant languages to construct preference pairs of the form (instruction, chosen response, rejected response) in every language. The output is an updated instruction-following model that produces improved multilingual text responses and arithmetic reasoning answers, with the preference signal derived entirely from the model's own language-conditional quality gap.

## Method

Language Imbalance Driven Rewarding leverages the inherent quality gap between dominant and non-dominant languages as a preference signal for iterative DPO training. In each iteration, the current model M_t generates responses for multilingual prompts and self-translates them between dominant (e.g., English) and non-dominant languages; the dominant-language response is designated as preferred and the non-dominant as rejected (and vice versa after translation), forming preference pairs in all languages. Training uses a DPO objective augmented with a length-normalized NLL loss on the chosen response, which is critical for maintaining generation quality. Crucially, the dominant language also improves from the negative samples, raising the quality ceiling and enabling the next iteration to produce stronger reward signals. This self-reinforcing loop is repeated until the cross-language performance gap saturates, requiring no external data or human annotations.

## Results

On the X-AlpacaEval leaderboard, two iterations of Language Imbalance Driven Rewarding on Llama-3-8B-Instruct yield an average win rate of 22.18% over GPT-4 Turbo across five languages (en, es, ru, de, fr), a 7.46% absolute improvement over the base model (14.72%). On the MGSM arithmetic reasoning benchmark, the method achieves a 13.9% average accuracy improvement over the base model. On Multilingual MT-Bench, the average score increases from 6.80 (M0) to 7.51 (M2), and no alignment tax is observed on multilingual MMLU, HellaSwag, ARC Challenge, or TruthfulQA benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On X-AlpacaEval, the 8B model after two iterations (M2, 22.18% avg) outperforms all 7B–14B open-source models including Qwen2-7B (16.01%), Mistral-7B (14.75%), Qwen1.5-14B (17.88%), Aya-23-8B (16.62%), and InternLM2.5-20B (16.93%), and approaches the 70B-level Meta-Llama-3-70B-Instruct (23.23%). It also surpasses GPT-4-0613 (16.37%) and GPT-3.5-turbo (13.05%), though it remains well below GPT-4o-mini (45.19%) and Qwen2-72B (27.63%). Against the multilingual alignment SFT baseline, M2 achieves 22.18% vs. 18.32% average win rate, and critically improves English (+9.19%) while SFT degrades it (−3.02%), enabling further iteration. On MGSM arithmetic reasoning, M2 outperforms MAPO† (same sampling budget) across all ten languages and also beats MAPO‡ (matched data size) in most languages, with an 11.6% average training-language improvement vs. 7.8% for multilingual alignment SFT. The method also generalizes to unseen languages, achieving a 16.2% average improvement on five unseen languages (ja, sw, th, zh, bn) in arithmetic reasoning, far exceeding the 4.9% from multilingual alignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the iterative DPO framework from Self-Rewarding Language Models (Yuan et al., 2024) and the DPO+NLL objective from Pang et al. (2024), but repurposes the reward signal in a fundamentally different way: rather than using LLM-as-a-Judge scoring or cross-language consistency (as in MAPO, She et al., 2024), it exploits the *inherent* quality gap between dominant and non-dominant languages within a single model as a free, self-generated preference signal. This departs from prior multilingual work that treats language imbalance as a deficiency to be corrected via additional data or cross-lingual alignment; here, the imbalance itself is the training signal, enabling a closed-loop self-improvement cycle without any human-authored preference data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is a data-free, iterative method for improving multilingual LLM performance that simultaneously benefits both dominant and non-dominant languages, removing the bottleneck of scarce multilingual preference data. However, the reward signal is inherently coarse-grained (binary language-level quality rather than response-level), the approach is validated for only two iterations before expected saturation, and gains on truly low-resource languages (bn, sw) remain modest. Open questions include how many iterations are possible before the language gap closes entirely, whether more refined reward signals (e.g., per-response quality within a language) could extend the self-improvement horizon, and whether the approach transfers to non-English-dominant model families.
