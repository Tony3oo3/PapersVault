## Problem

Selecting high-quality pretraining data for LLMs typically requires expensive proxy model training runs or hand-crafted heuristics, and no data-driven method has consistently matched a simple fastText classifier. The paper asks whether the growing collection of publicly available, heterogeneous LLMs can be leveraged directly for data selection without training any new models, by exploiting the observable relationship between model losses on web text and downstream benchmark performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250630/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a matrix of byte-normalized log-likelihoods (bits-per-byte) computed by 90 heterogeneous Open LLM Leaderboard models over ~9,841 web domains from RedPajama V2, paired with each model's benchmark scores, plus per-domain token availability and a total pretraining token budget. It produces a domain ranking via a rank-correlation coefficient, a valid sampling distribution over domains (respecting per-domain token caps to avoid data duplication), and a fastText bigram classifier trained on selected vs. unselected domains that enables page-level filtering at scale. Notably, the input models vary widely in size (160M to 70B+), architecture, tokenizer, and pretraining data, and none of their training data is known.

## Method

The core insight is that LLM losses on web domains are correlated with downstream benchmark performance, and this correlation can be estimated observationally from public models without knowing their training data. The paper formulates the loss-to-performance relationship as a single-index model y_i = f(⟨θ*, x_i⟩ + ε_i) and estimates θ* using a rank-based U-statistic that is robust to outliers in log-likelihoods, extending the moment-matching estimators of Chen & Banerjee (2017) and Plan et al. (2016) with a theoretical consistency proof under Gaussian assumptions. The estimated weights are then projected onto a valid sampling distribution via a linear program that enforces non-negativity, unit sum, and per-domain token caps (to prevent harmful data repetition), yielding a closed-form greedy solution that selects domains in descending order of correlation. Finally, a fastText classifier trained on selected vs. unselected domains enables page-level data filtering, which the authors find slightly improves downstream performance over domain-level selection alone.

## Results

At the 160M-parameter, 3.2B-token scale across 8 benchmarks (LAMBADA, ARC Easy, PIQA, SciQ, and 4 multilingual LAMBADA variants), Perplexity Correlations achieves an average rank of 1.750, significantly outperforming DSIR (4.500) and the default fastText+EN filter from Li et al. (3.750). It slightly trails the hand-engineered fastText classifier augmented with a manual language filter (1.375), which benefits from task-specific human curation. In preregistered experiments at up to 1.4B parameters on DCLM Core (22-benchmark aggregate), the method's advantage over the best Li et al. filter grows with scale when filtering from the raw data pool, though gains diminish on the extensively pre-filtered pool where correlation coefficients are already narrow (range 0.23–0.33).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DSIR (Xie et al., 2023b), a training-free n-gram overlap method, Perplexity Correlations wins decisively: average rank 1.750 vs. 4.500 across all 8 benchmarks, with the heatmap in Figure 2 showing DSIR's performance is nearly uniform across target tasks while Perplexity Correlations clearly optimizes for each target. Against the state-of-the-art fastText classifier from Li et al. (2024) (trained to distinguish Common Crawl from OH-2.5 + ELI5), Perplexity Correlations beats the default English-filtered version (1.750 vs. 3.750) and the no-filter version (1.750 vs. 3.250), but loses slightly to the version augmented with a manual, task-specific language filter (1.750 vs. 1.375). The paper notes this gap is attributable to the manual filter encoding task-specific language knowledge that the correlation-based method does not explicitly model. In preregistered experiments at 1.4B scale on DCLM Core, Perplexity Correlations outperforms the best Li et al. filter on the raw pool, with the margin increasing with model size, while both methods perform similarly on the pre-filtered pool.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the high-dimensional single-index model estimation framework of Plan et al. (2016) and Chen & Banerjee (2017) to the problem of pretraining data selection, replacing unobserved data distributions with observable log-likelihoods from public LLMs as a surrogate. It departs from both lightweight data selection methods (DSIR's n-gram overlap, GIO's embedding similarity) and heavy proxy-LLM approaches (DoReMi, DSDM, RegMix) by requiring no new model training whatsoever, instead leveraging 90 existing models from the Open LLM Leaderboard. The work also builds on observational scaling law studies (Owen 2024; Ruan et al. 2024) that identified systematic loss-performance relationships across diverse LLMs, but goes further by deriving a concrete, theoretically grounded data selection algorithm from these relationships.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: data selection no longer requires expensive proxy LLM training runs, instead repurposing the collective investment already made in public models. The approach is parameter-free, simple to implement, and matches the best hand-crafted DataComp-LM filter without human curation, with preregistered experiments showing increasing gains at scale (up to 1.4B parameters). Limitations include reduced prediction accuracy for models trained on atypical data (e.g., Phi's synthetic textbooks), and the validation scale (160M–1.4B) remains far below frontier models, leaving open whether the approach transfers to the 70B+ regime where data selection decisions are most consequential. The robustness to the specific correlation coefficient used (Spearman, the proposed U-statistic, L2 vs. linear projection) suggests the underlying signal is broad, but the method's behavior under highly non-Gaussian loss distributions or very small model pools remains untested.
