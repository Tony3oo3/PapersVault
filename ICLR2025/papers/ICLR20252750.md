## Problem

Prior work on generalization in RL focuses on perceptual shifts, simulator parameter randomization, or reward/value equivalence, but none address generalization over the temporal horizon — the ability to learn from easy, nearby goals and transfer to hard, distant goals. This paper formalizes 'horizon generalization' as a distinct property of goal-conditioned RL and identifies the mathematical mechanism (planning invariance via quasimetric structure) that provably enables it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252750/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy takes a start state s and a goal state g (or an intermediate waypoint w) as state-vector inputs and outputs an action a selected greedily to minimize a learned quasimetric distance d(s,a,g). The distance function must satisfy the triangle inequality d(s,g) ≤ d(s,w) + d(w,g), which is the key structural constraint distinguishing this from standard value-function or symmetric-metric approaches. The quasimetric is asymmetric, reflecting that navigation from s to g may differ fundamentally from g to s.

## Method

The paper proves two theorems: (1) quasimetric policies — those acting greedily with respect to a distance satisfying the triangle inequality — are invariant to planning, meaning conditioning on a goal versus an en-route waypoint yields the same action; and (2) this planning invariance, combined with a base-case assumption of success on nearby goals, implies horizon generalization via an inductive argument that repeatedly doubles the reachable radius. The specific quasimetric is the successor distance d_SD, defined as the log-ratio of discounted state occupancy measures, which connects directly to optimal goal-reaching. Empirically, architectures enforcing the triangle inequality by construction (e.g., Metric Residual Networks in the CMD-1 method) achieve stronger horizon generalization (η ≈ 0.8, above the critical threshold of 0.5 for unbounded reach) compared to standard MLP or L2-distance architectures, validated across tabular navigation, Ant, AntMaze, and Humanoid environments.

## Results

The paper introduces the horizon generalization parameter η, measuring the ratio of success rates when the goal distance is doubled (e.g., 15m vs 5m). In the AntMaze environment, CMD-1 (quasimetric architecture) achieves η = 0.93 at 15m and 0.25 at 25m, while CRL (standard architecture) drops to η = 0.10 at 15m and 0.00 at 25m. In the Humanoid environment, CMD-1 achieves η = 0.69 at 15m and 0.24 at 25m, compared to CRL's 0.60 and 0.16. In the high-dimensional Ant task (27D observation, 8DoF control), the L2-distance architecture reaches η ≈ 0.5 (the critical threshold for unbounded reach), while CMD-1 reaches η ≈ 0.8, well above this threshold. No method achieves perfect generalization (η = 1), indicating room for improvement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three existing RL methods: CMD-1 (quasimetric/metric residual network architecture), CRL (contrastive RL with standard L2 or MLP distance), and SAC (Soft Actor-Critic with Q-function). CMD-1 decisively outperforms CRL on horizon generalization in AntMaze (η = 0.93 vs 0.10 at 15m; 0.25 vs 0.00 at 25m), confirming the theoretical prediction that quasimetric architectures enforce planning invariance and thus horizon generalization. In the Humanoid environment, the gap narrows (η = 0.69 vs 0.60 at 15m), suggesting the advantage of quasimetric structure is most pronounced in environments with strong metric structure. In the Ant environment, both CRL and SAC show some horizon generalization, but the value function architecture (L2 vs MLP) significantly affects the degree, with L2 reaching the critical η ≈ 0.5 threshold. The paper also shows that the architecture with stronger horizon generalization (d_l2) simultaneously achieves lower Bellman error throughout training, linking horizon generalization to faster convergence even for TD methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of RL generalization work (perceptual shifts, domain randomization, state-action equivalence) by introducing a fundamentally different axis: generalization over temporal horizon. It builds directly on quasimetric neural network architectures (Liu et al. 2023; Wang & Isola 2022) and the successor distance formulation (Myers et al. 2024a) to prove that the triangle inequality — already an architectural constraint in these methods — is the inductive bias that yields planning invariance and, consequently, horizon generalization. The contrastive RL framework (Eysenbach et al. 2022) serves as the empirical backbone, while the theoretical analysis draws an explicit analogy to how invariance to rotation yields generalization to orientation in vision (Cohen & Welling 2016; LeCun et al. 2004).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal definition and existence proof for horizon generalization, along with a concrete measurable quantity (the η parameter) and a critical threshold (η ≥ 0.5 for unbounded reach) that give practitioners a diagnostic tool for evaluating goal-conditioned policies. The key open question is whether the base-case assumption (perfect success on all nearby goals) can be relaxed, and whether the framework extends to non-goal-conditioned task specifications (rewards, language, preferences). The authors note that no method achieves perfect horizon generalization in high-dimensional settings, leaving room for future work on architectures and training objectives that more tightly enforce the triangle inequality at scale.
