## Problem

Existing data attribution methods for diffusion models (e.g., D-TRAK) use the diffusion loss as the output function, which measures KL-divergence between predicted and ground-truth distributions. This leads to an indirect comparison between predicted distributions that involves the data distribution q, introducing a generally nonzero cross-entropy term that fails to capture true differences in model behavior. The paper argues that TRAK's derivation fundamentally cannot be extended to diffusion models because their outputs are high-dimensional noise predictions rather than scalar class probabilities.

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained diffusion model (35.7M-param DDPM or 25.5M-param LoRA-fine-tuned Stable Diffusion), the full training dataset, and a target generated sample, producing a scalar attribution score for each training sample. Gradients of the noise predictor are computed at a subset of timesteps (10 or 100 out of T=1000) with one Gaussian noise sample per timestep, then projected to k=32768 dimensions via Johnson-Lindenstrauss random projection. The final score is the squared L2 norm of a kernel-weighted gradient interaction, with gradients and residuals normalized across timesteps before averaging.

## Method

DAS defines the output function as the noise predictor output ε_θ(x_t, t) rather than the diffusion loss, enabling a direct KL-divergence comparison between predicted distributions p_θ and p_θ\i without involving the data distribution q. The attribution score is derived by linearizing the noise predictor around optimal parameters, expressing the output change as a function of parameter differences, and estimating those differences via a single Newton step where the Sherman-Morrison formula yields a closed-form leave-one-out inverse. To scale to large models, DAS applies several acceleration techniques: normalizing gradients and residuals across timesteps before averaging, Johnson-Lindenstrauss random projection to reduce gradient dimensionality, restricting gradients to U-Net up-blocks or LoRA trainable parameters, sampling fewer timesteps, and pre-screening candidate training samples via CLIP similarity. The key theoretical insight is that the diffusion loss's involvement of the ground-truth distribution q creates a cross-entropy term that is generally nonzero, making loss-based attribution an indirect and potentially misleading measure of distributional shift between models.

## Results

The primary evaluation metric is the Linear Datamodeling Score (LDS, %), measuring Spearman correlation between predicted and actual model outputs after data intervention. On CIFAR-2 (100 timesteps), DAS achieves 43.08% on validation and 30.68% on generation, outperforming D-TRAK (33.74% / 25.67%) by +9.33% and +5.01% respectively. On the larger CIFAR-10 dataset, DAS reaches 33.23% validation and 23.69% generation, surpassing D-TRAK (20.56% / 16.11%) by +12.67% and +7.58%. On ArtBench-5 (256×256, LoRA-finetuned Stable Diffusion), DAS achieves 37.67% validation and 32.77% generation, beating D-TRAK by +10.21% and +8.92%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DAS consistently outperforms all 8 baselines across every dataset and evaluation setting. Against the closest competitor D-TRAK (Zheng et al., ICLR 2024), DAS gains +9.33% on CIFAR-2 validation, +12.67% on CIFAR-10 validation, +10.21% on ArtBench-5 validation, and +9.21% on CelebA generation. TRAK (Park et al., ICML 2023) and its variants (Relative IF, Renorm. IF) cluster around 13–24% LDS, roughly 10–20 points below DAS. Journey TRAK (Georgiev et al., 2023) is only evaluated on generation sets and scores 7–12%, well below DAS. The simpler baselines—CLIP similarity (Radford et al., ICML 2021), raw pixel similarity, Gradient (Charpiat et al., NeurIPS 2019), TracInCP (Pruthi et al., NeurIPS 2020), and GAS (Hammoudeh & Lowd, CCS 2022)—all score below 10% LDS, confirming that kernel-based methods with proper diffusion-aware output functions are essential. Notably, DAS with only 10 timesteps still outperforms D-TRAK with 100 timesteps in most settings, highlighting the efficiency of the proposed formulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on TRAK (Park et al., 2023) and its diffusion-model adaptation D-TRAK (Zheng et al., 2024), but departs from both on a fundamental conceptual point: it argues that using the diffusion loss (Simple Loss) as the output function, as D-TRAK does, performs an indirect comparison between predicted distributions via the data distribution q, rather than a direct comparison. DAS instead defines the output function as the noise predictor ε_θ(x_t, t) itself, measuring KL-divergence directly between p_θ and p_θ\i. The computational machinery (Newton's method, Sherman-Morrison formula, Johnson-Lindenstrauss projection) is inherited from the influence-function and TRAK literature, but the paper's key contribution is the theoretically correct output function choice and the acceleration techniques (normalization, LoRA-based compression, timestep subsampling) that make it practical for large-scale models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Data attribution in diffusion models is increasingly urgent as copyright and privacy concerns around tools like Stable Diffusion grow, and this paper provides the first theoretically grounded attribution score specifically designed for the generative (distributional) nature of diffusion models rather than borrowing discriminative-model formulations. The consistent ~10% LDS improvement over D-TRAK across five datasets and three model scales, plus the counterfactual visualization showing DAS identifies the most impactful training samples, makes it a practical tool for auditing training data. Open questions include whether the approach extends to conditional/text-to-image generation with complex prompt spaces, video diffusion, and whether the validation-vs-generation performance gap (DAS performs better on validation) reflects a fundamental limitation in attributing influence on out-of-distribution generated samples.
