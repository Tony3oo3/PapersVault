## Problem

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) suffer from severe optimization difficulties because the PDE residual loss is highly ill-conditioned, with a condition number that grows as the fourth power of the maximum frequency in the ansatz. This makes standard optimizers (SGD, Adam, L-BFGS) require thousands of iterations or fail to converge entirely. Moreover, classical PINNs solve a single PDE instance at a time, requiring full retraining for every new parameter configuration, which is impractical for parametric PDE families.

## Input / Output

At inference, the solver takes the PDE parameters (coefficients γ), forcing term f, boundary/initial conditions g, and the current PDE residual gradient ∇_Θ L_PDE as input, and produces updated B-Spline coefficients Θ_L after L iterations (typically 2–5). The solution is reconstructed as a linear combination of degree-3 B-Spline basis functions (32 terms in 1D, 40 per dimension in 2D, 15×15×10 in 2D+time). Training additionally requires ground-truth solutions u(x) on a grid from a numerical solver, but these are never used at test time—only the PDE residual drives inference.

## Method

The core contribution is a learned gradient-transforming solver F_ρ (implemented with Fourier Neural Operator layers) that maps the ill-conditioned PDE residual gradient to a well-conditioned descent direction, parameterized by the PDE coefficients, forcing term, and boundary conditions. The update rule is Θ_{l+1} = Θ_l − η F_ρ(∇_Θ L_PDE(Θ_l), γ, f, g), where the ansatz u_Θ is a linear combination of B-Spline basis functions rather than a neural network, avoiding the additional ill-conditioning from compositional architectures. Training uses a two-level scheme: an inner loop runs L solver steps on a PDE instance to produce Θ_L, and an outer loop updates F_ρ's parameters via gradient descent on a data loss matching the reconstructed solution to ground truth. Theoretically, if F behaves like its linearization P, the effective condition number drops from κ(A) to κ(PA), and when F minimizes the data loss, κ(PA)=1, yielding convergence in O(log(1/ε)) steps instead of O(κ(A)·log(1/ε)). This reduces the number of iterations from thousands (or non-convergence) to just 2–5 steps at test time.

## Results

On the main comparison (Table 2, Relative MSE on test set), the neural solver achieves 2.41e-2 on Helmholtz, 5.56e-5 on Poisson, 2.91e-4 on NLRD, 1.87e-2 on Darcy Flow, and 2.31e-3 on Heat, ranking first or second on all five PDE families. At test time (Figure 2), the solver converges to a good solution in just 5 iterations, while all classical optimizers (SGD, Adam, L-BFGS), from-scratch PINNs, and fine-tuned PINO fail to converge even after 10,000 steps. The method improves over the best unsupervised baseline by at least one order of magnitude on every dataset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The neural solver outperforms all nine baselines on every dataset. Against the most directly comparable unsupervised methods (PINNs+L-BFGS, PPINNs, P2INNs, PO-DeepONet), the improvement is 1–2 orders of magnitude (e.g., 5.56e-5 vs 4.30e-2 for PPINNs on Poisson; 2.91e-4 vs 3.94e-1 for PPINNs on NLRD). Among hybrid methods, PINO is the strongest competitor, achieving 2.80e-3 on Poisson and 4.21e-4 on NLRD, but the neural solver still wins by a factor of ~50× on Poisson and ~1.4× on NLRD. The supervised MLP+basis baseline is competitive on Helmholtz (4.66e-2 vs 2.41e-2) and NLRD (2.85e-4 vs 2.91e-4, a near-tie) but degrades sharply on Poisson (1.50e-1) and Heat (6.00e-1). At test time, the solver's 5-step convergence versus 10,000+ steps for all other methods is the most striking result, demonstrating that the learned preconditioner fundamentally changes the optimization landscape rather than merely providing a small constant-factor speedup.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: physics-informed neural networks (PINNs, Raissi et al. 2019) and learning-to-optimize (Andrychowicz et al. 2016). It departs from standard PINNs in two key ways: (1) it handles parametric PDEs with varying coefficients, forcing terms, and boundary conditions without retraining, and (2) it replaces hand-designed optimizers (SGD, Adam, L-BFGS) with a learned gradient transformer F_ρ that conditions the descent direction. Unlike prior learned-preconditioner work (Hsieh et al. 2019, Li et al. 2023a, Rudikov et al. 2024) that operates on discretized linear systems, this method acts directly on the non-linear PDE residual loss. The closest prior work is Bihlo (2024), which learns optimizers for single PDE instances, whereas this paper explicitly targets the parametric setting where the solver must generalize across a distribution of PDE parameters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ill-conditioning of PDE residual losses is a well-documented bottleneck that limits the practical utility of PINNs; demonstrating that a learned solver can converge in 2–5 iterations versus thousands for standard optimizers is a significant practical advance for physics-informed methods. However, the approach requires ground-truth training data (unlike pure PINNs), relies on a linear B-Spline ansatz rather than a full neural network, and the theoretical convergence guarantee (κ(PA)=1) holds only in the linear case under strong assumptions. Open questions include whether the method scales to higher-dimensional bases without prohibitive memory costs from backpropagation through iterations, whether more expressive (nonlinear) ansatz spaces can be handled without reintroducing ill-conditioning, and whether the learned solver generalizes to PDE families outside the training distribution.
