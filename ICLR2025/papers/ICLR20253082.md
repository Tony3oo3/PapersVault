## Problem

When a pretrained world model and planning policy are transferred to a new autonomous driving task, distribution shift causes significant performance degradation. Existing finetuning strategies (model-only, policy-only, or alternating updates) fail to identify which component—dynamics model or policy—is the dominant source of degradation, leading to suboptimal adaptation. This paper asks: when and how should the pretrained dynamics model and planning policy be selectively finetuned to most effectively mitigate performance drops?

## Input / Output

The agent receives a 128×128 bird's-eye-view (BEV) semantic segmentation image constructed from CARLA's privileged information (location, map topology) along with planned waypoints. At each step it outputs a discrete control action: acceleration from {-2, 0, 2} and steering angle from {-0.6, -0.2, 0, 0.2, 0.6}. The environment returns a reward that is a weighted sum of six factors: time-to-collision (safety), jerk/acceleration (comfort), time-to-destination, velocity penalty, orientation penalty, and waypoint-following encouragement.

## Method

AdaWM operates in two phases at each finetuning step. First, Mismatch Identification computes the Total Variation distance between state-action visitation distributions (dynamics model mismatch) and between policy distributions (policy mismatch), then applies a theoretically derived threshold criterion (from Theorem 1's upper bound on the performance gap) to determine which mismatch dominates. Second, Alignment-driven Finetuning selectively updates only the dominant component: for the dynamics model, it uses a NoLa-style low-rank decomposition where only the weight matrix B of the base vector Z is updated; for the policy, it decomposes the network into a convex combination of sub-units and updates only the mixing weight vector. This selective, low-rank approach is grounded in a theoretical analysis that decomposes the performance gap into model-mismatch and policy-mismatch terms, yielding explicit conditions (involving constants C₁ and C₂ derived from the discount factor, reward bounds, and Lipschitz constants) for when each component should be prioritized.

## Results

On the four finetuning tasks in CARLA, AdaWM achieves substantially higher Time-to-Collision (TTC) and Success Rate (SR) than all baselines. For example, on the most challenging task LTD03 (left turn, dense traffic), AdaWM reaches TTC 1.92 and SR 0.70, compared to DreamerV3's TTC 0.15 and SR 0.35, VAD's TTC 0.09 and SR 0.10, and UniAD's TTC 0.03 and SR 0.04. On ROM03 (roundabout, moderate traffic), AdaWM achieves TTC 2.05 and SR 0.82, more than doubling the best baseline (DreamerV3: TTC 0.95, SR 0.40). On the pretraining task Pre-RTM03, AdaWM matches DreamerV3 exactly (TTC 1.16, SR 0.68), confirming that finetuning does not degrade in-distribution performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AdaWM is compared against three baselines: UniAD (Hu et al., CVPR 2023) and VAD (Jiang et al., ICCV 2023), both supervised-learning end-to-end driving systems evaluated with offline-trained checkpoints, and DreamerV3/Think2Drive (Li et al., 2024), a world-model-based RL method. AdaWM decisively outperforms all three on every finetuning task across both TTC and SR. Against the strongest baseline DreamerV3, AdaWM improves SR by 0.42 (ROM03: 0.82 vs 0.40), 0.34 (RTD12: 0.66 vs 0.32), 0.44 (LTM03: 0.72 vs 0.28), and 0.35 (LTD03: 0.70 vs 0.35), with TTC gains of 1.10, 0.83, 1.07, and 1.77 respectively. The supervised baselines UniAD and VAD perform worst on the harder tasks (LTM03, LTD03), with SR as low as 0.04 and TTC below 0.1, highlighting their inability to adapt to distribution shifts without finetuning. On the pretraining task Pre-RTM03, AdaWM ties DreamerV3 exactly, confirming no in-distribution regression from the finetuning procedure.

## Relation to Prior Work

AdaWM builds directly on the DreamerV3 world model architecture (RSSM-based latent dynamics) and the Think2Drive paradigm of offline RL for autonomous driving, but departs from prior work by addressing the finetuning stage that these methods largely ignore. For efficient parameter updates, it repurposes LoRA/NoLa low-rank decomposition for the dynamics model and a convex ensemble of sub-units for the policy, drawing on techniques originally developed for LLM adaptation. The theoretical analysis follows the model-based policy optimization framework of Janner et al. (2019), extending it to derive a principled criterion for selecting which component to update. Unlike prior finetuning work in robotics (Feng et al. 2023 for model-only, Baker et al. 2022 for policy-only), AdaWM introduces an adaptive, alignment-driven strategy that dynamically chooses between model and policy updates based on measured distribution shift.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck in deploying world-model-based driving systems: pretrained models degrade significantly on new tasks, and the choice of finetuning strategy (model vs. policy vs. alternating) has a large impact that prior work has not systematically addressed. The theoretical criterion (Theorem 1) provides a principled, interpretable rule for when to update which component, and the low-rank updates keep finetuning computationally cheap (1 hour vs. 12 hours for pretraining). Key limitations include evaluation solely in the CARLA simulator with a single-agent setting, the need to tune the threshold C per task, and reliance on privileged BEV information from the simulator. Open questions include whether the adaptive strategy generalizes to multi-agent interactions, real-world sensor noise, and whether the TV-distance-based mismatch estimation remains reliable under more severe distribution shifts.
