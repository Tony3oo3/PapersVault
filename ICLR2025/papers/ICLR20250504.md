## Problem

Existing bilevel coreset selection methods (Greedy Coreset, PBCS, BCSR) rely on implicit gradient scores that disproportionately highlight ambiguous or noisy samples—those with high loss even under full-dataset training—leading to suboptimal coresets that degrade model performance. This is particularly harmful in continual learning, where limited rehearsal memory demands high-quality sample selection, and the computational cost of inverse Hessian computation or extensive sampling further limits practicality.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a labeled image dataset (or streaming batches in the CL setting) along with a predefined coreset size, and produces a compact subset for rehearsal memory. In the continual learning variant, the holdout model is trained on the current task data union the existing memory buffer, and the selection operates per-task with equal memory allocation across tasks. For streaming, the method processes data batch-by-batch without requiring the full dataset to be available, modifying reservoir sampling update probabilities via ReL scores. The output coreset, when used for training from scratch, should achieve test accuracy comparable to training on the full dataset.

## Method

CSReL replaces the implicit gradient score in the greedy incremental bilevel selection framework with Reducible Loss (ReL), defined as the difference between the loss on a sample under a current model (trained on the selected subset S) and a holdout model (trained on the full dataset D). The key insight is that ReL naturally separates representative-informative samples (low holdout loss, high current loss → high ReL) from ambiguous or noisy samples (high loss on both models → low ReL), avoiding the selection of unlearnable points that gradient-based scores erroneously prioritize. The selection proceeds in multiple steps: at each step, the current model is retrained on the growing subset, ReL is computed via a single forward pass for all remaining candidates, and the top-n samples are added. For continual learning, three extensions are introduced: (1) the holdout model is trained on current task data plus memory to account for task interference, (2) ReL serves as a probability scaling factor in modified reservoir sampling for streaming data, and (3) a combined ReL (ReL-cmb) linearly blends the label-based ReL with an MSE-based ReL on model logits to incorporate knowledge distillation objectives.

## Results

On the main continual learning benchmark (Table 1), CSReL-CL-Prv achieves the best final average accuracy on Split CIFAR-10 (39.82%), Split CIFAR-100 (18.47%), and Perm MNIST (80.02%), while matching Greedy Coreset on Split MNIST (95.55% vs. 95.73%). When applied as a drop-in replacement for reservoir sampling in existing CL methods (Table 2), CSReL-LODE-DER++ reaches 41.96% on Split CIFAR-100 (memory 500) and 22.83% on Split Tiny ImageNet (memory 500), outperforming the unmodified LODE-DER++ by 2.82 and 1.68 points respectively. The method also demonstrates robustness under label noise, with only slight performance degradation as noise ratio increases, while baselines drop significantly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CSReL-CL-Prv outperforms all 10 coreset-selection baselines on Split CIFAR-10 (39.82% vs. best baseline PBCS at 38.37%) and Split CIFAR-100 (18.47% vs. PBCS at 16.20%), with margins of 1.45 and 2.27 points respectively. On Perm MNIST, CSReL-CL-Prv (80.02%) surpasses Greedy Coreset (79.23%) by 0.79 points, while on Split MNIST the two methods are statistically tied (95.55% vs. 95.73%). Heuristic baselines such as uniform sampling, k-center clustering, iCaRL's herding, and hardest-sample selection are consistently outperformed by 2–10 points across all datasets. When integrated into existing CL frameworks (Table 2), CSReL-RS improves DER++ by 1.52 points on Split CIFAR-100 (memory 200) and 1.68 points on Split Tiny ImageNet (memory 500), and boosts LODE-DER++ by 0.55 and 1.68 points on the same settings. Under the BCSR evaluation protocol (20 tasks, task-ID provided), CSReL achieves 62.10% average accuracy, outperforming BCSR (61.60%) and all other baselines including Greedy Coreset (59.39%) and PBCS (55.64%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of bilevel-optimization-based coreset selection (Greedy Coreset, PBCS, BCSR) and the reducible loss (ReL) framework introduced by Mindermann et al. for training data scheduling. The key conceptual departure is replacing the implicit-gradient scoring mechanism—which requires expensive Hessian-inverse computation and inadvertently selects ambiguous or noisy samples—with a simple forward-pass loss difference that provably approximates the negative implicit gradient (Eq. 16) while naturally penalizing non-representative samples. The reservoir-sampling extension (CSReL-RS) repurposes ReL as a probability-scaling factor within Vitter's streaming framework, and the knowledge-distillation variant (ReL-cmb) linearly combines label-prediction and logit-matching ReL terms, neither of which has a direct precedent in prior coreset work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in the efficiency-robustness trade-off: ReL eliminates the 4000+ second NTK computation of Greedy Coreset while simultaneously reducing selection of noisy samples, making coreset-based memory construction viable for larger-scale CL deployments. The plug-and-play compatibility with existing rehearsal methods (ER, DER++, LODE-DER++) lowers adoption barriers. Limitations include the need for a well-trained holdout model (whose quality directly affects selection, as shown in Table 3), the approximation θ_{D∪S} ≈ θ_D which may degrade for very small datasets, and evaluation confined to image classification. The authors note future work on fine-tuning large pretrained models, suggesting the method's applicability to LLM/VLM continual adaptation remains unexplored.
