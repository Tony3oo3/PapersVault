## Problem

Reconstruction-based tabular anomaly detection methods rely on reconstruction error in the observation space to flag anomalies, but real-world tabular data often exhibits entanglement between normal and anomalous samples, causing their latent representations to overlap and undermining discriminative power. This paper addresses the gap by asking whether anomaly detection can be made more effective by performing reconstruction in a tailor-designed constrained latent space rather than in the raw observation space.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250887/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a tabular data sample as a heterogeneous feature vector x ∈ R^D (mixing numerical and categorical features of varying ranges) and produces a scalar anomaly score. During training, only normal samples are available (one-class setting); the score at inference is the L2 distance between the sample's latent representation h and its decomposition ŝ = φ(x)B into K fixed orthogonal basis vectors, computed entirely in the latent space rather than the observation space. The basis vectors are randomly generated via Gram-Schmidt before training and remain frozen throughout.

## Method

DRL maps each sample through a feature extractor f to a latent representation h, then enforces that h can be decomposed as a weighted linear combination of K randomly generated orthogonal basis vectors (K < E), with sample-specific weights w produced by a separate weight learner φ that outputs a probability simplex. A separation constraint (Eq. 4) maximizes pairwise cosine distances among normal sample weights within each mini-batch, which—supported by Propositions 1 and 2—amplifies the expected discrepancy between normal and anomalous weight vectors and thus between their reconstructed latent representations. An alignment loss (Eq. 5) additionally preserves intrinsic feature correlations by reconstructing x from h via a linear decoder, preventing the model from discarding task-relevant information. At inference, the anomaly score is the decomposition error d(h, φ(x)B) in the constrained latent space, which penalizes samples whose representations fall outside the subspace spanned by the shared orthogonal basis.

## Results

Across 40 tabular anomaly detection datasets, DRL achieves an average AUC-PR of 0.7344 and AUC-ROC of 0.8574, outperforming all 16 baselines. The headline gain is an absolute average AUC-PR improvement of at least 9.8% over the strongest competitor (NPT-AD), and a 3.9-point AUC-ROC margin (0.8574 vs. 0.8185). Wilcoxon signed-rank tests confirm that DRL's improvement over every baseline is statistically significant at the 95% confidence level across all 40 datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DRL outperforms all 16 baselines on both AUC-PR and AUC-ROC across all 40 datasets, with statistically significant gains (Wilcoxon p < 0.05) in every comparison. The closest competitor is NPT-AD (AUC-ROC 0.8185), which DRL surpasses by 3.9 points; MCM (0.7756) and DTE (0.7747) trail by roughly 8 points. Among classical methods, IForest (0.7556) and OCSVM (0.7181) are the strongest, yet DRL still leads by 10+ points in AUC-ROC. Notably, even on datasets where observation-space entanglement is minimal (e.g., Lympho, WDBC, Wine where DRL reaches AUC-PR = 1.0), DRL maintains its advantage, confirming that the latent-space decomposition and separation constraint provide gains beyond what reconstruction-based methods (MCM, NPT-AD, DTE) can achieve. The ablation (Table 2) further shows that removing the separation constraint drops AUC-PR from 0.7344 to 0.7081, and replacing the learned weight learner with least-squares reduces it to 0.6964, underscoring that both the decomposition architecture and the separation constraint are essential for the observed margin over baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant reconstruction-based paradigm for tabular anomaly detection (MCM, NPT-AD, DTE, RAPP), which learns to reconstruct normal samples in observation space and flags anomalies by reconstruction error. Instead, DRL repurposes concepts from dictionary learning and topic modeling—decomposing representations into a linear combination of shared basis vectors with sample-specific weights—but applies them in a constrained latent space rather than the data space. The key conceptual departure is identifying data entanglement between normal and anomalous samples in observation space as a fundamental limitation of reconstruction-based methods, and addressing it by enforcing decomposition into fixed, randomly-generated orthogonal basis vectors (data-free and training-free) in latent space, augmented by a theoretically grounded separation constraint.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By identifying data entanglement as a core obstacle and showing that a simple decomposition into fixed orthogonal basis vectors in latent space can outperform complex reconstruction strategies by a substantial margin (≥9.8% AUC-PR), this work shifts the design space for tabular anomaly detection away from observation-space reconstruction toward latent-space representation constraints. The data-free, training-free nature of the basis vectors makes the approach lightweight and easy to deploy, while the theoretical propositions provide a principled justification for the separation constraint. Open questions include whether the fixed-basis decomposition generalizes to non-MLP backbones (e.g., transformers for tabular data), how the method behaves under extreme class imbalance or very high-dimensional settings, and whether the separation constraint can be extended beyond mini-batch-level enforcement to capture global weight-space geometry.
