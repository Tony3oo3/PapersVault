## Problem

While prior work has documented that LLMs exhibit cultural bias in open-ended generation—favoring high-frequency cultures and producing templated outputs for under-represented ones—the mechanism linking specific pretraining data patterns to individual biased generations remains untraced. This paper asks whether a given culture-symbol generation can be attributed to memorization of that association in pretraining documents, and how pretraining frequency imbalances drive the different types of biased associations that emerge.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes 300 culture-conditioned generations per culture (110 cultures × 3 gender settings × 100 samples) from OLMo-7B, extracts topic-specific symbols (food/clothing entities) via LLaMA-3-70B-Instruct, and cross-references each symbol-culture pair against the full Dolma pretraining corpus using the model's own tokenizer for n-gram distance computation. The output is a four-way association classification (memorized, diffuse, cross-culture generalization, weak association generalization) per symbol-culture pair, supported by a Contribution Score, a z-score over the distribution of scores across all cultures generating that symbol, and the set of contributory pretraining documents.

## Method

MEMOED (MEMOrization from pretraining document) determines whether a culture-symbol generation arises from memorization through a two-stage document-level analysis. First, a pretraining document is classified as contributory if the culture and symbol n-grams appear within a token distance below the model's max_seq_len (2048) or within 2 sentences, AND the document's Signal-to-Noise Ratio (log₂ ratio of target-culture n-gram frequency to all-other-culture frequency) exceeds a threshold—either ≥ 0 for global relevance or in [−1, 0) for local relevance. Second, the Contribution Score (fraction of contributory documents among all documents containing the symbol) is computed for every culture that generated the symbol, and a z-score ≥ 2.6 over this distribution flags the association as memorized. For the remaining non-memorized symbols, the paper uses InfiniGram frequency counts and overshadowing ratios to characterize diffuse associations, LDA topic modeling on co-occurring culture documents to explain cross-culture generalization, and LLM-generated symbol definitions mapped via F1 score to trace weak association generalization back to memorized or diffuse source symbols.

## Results

Using MEMOED on OLMo-7B's culture-conditioned generations for 110 cultures, 46.12% of food symbols (1098/2370) and 25.78% of clothing symbols (258/1002) are classified as memorized associations from pretraining data. Memorization correlates strongly with culture frequency in pretraining (Spearman ρ=0.670 for food, 0.540 for clothing), with 97/110 cultures having at least one memorized food symbol but only 45/110 for clothing. Diffuse association symbols (generated for >50% of cultures) are frequency-driven, with Spearman ρ=0.551 (clothing) and 0.519 (food) correlation between overshadowing ratio and number of cultures generated for. Cross-culture generalization shows that high-frequency cultures' memorized symbols are generated for other cultures (Spearman ρ=0.763 clothing, 0.716 food), while low-frequency cultures predominantly receive other cultures' symbols.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical analysis paper without traditional method baselines. The closest comparison is MEMOED's memorization classification against human annotation by 257 native-culture annotators across 8 cultures: MEMOED achieves a weighted F1 of 0.845 on clothing and 0.670 on food, showing strong but imperfect agreement (not all memorized symbols are culturally emblematic). An ablation on OLMo-7B-0424 (trained on Dolma 1.7) confirms the main correlations hold: the diffuse-association frequency correlation remains moderate-to-strong (Spearman ρ=0.507 clothing, 0.416 food vs. 0.551/0.519 for OLMo-7B), and the memorization-frequency correlation is actually stronger (Spearman ρ=0.591 clothing, 0.829 food vs. 0.540/0.670). A z-score threshold ablation (z=2 vs. z=2.6) shows the memorization-frequency correlation is robust (Spearman ρ=0.569/0.688 vs. 0.540/0.670), with only a marginal drop in human agreement (F1 0.840 vs. 0.845 for clothing).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) memorization attribution in LLMs, where prior work (Antoniades et al., 2024; Elazar et al., 2024) traces model outputs to specific pretraining documents, and (2) cultural bias detection in culture-conditioned generation (Li et al., 2024b; Naous et al., 2023; Palta & Rudinger, 2023), which identifies biased outputs without tracing their origin. MEMOED repurposes the document-level memorization-attribution idea—previously applied to factual knowledge and n-gram recall—into the domain of culture-symbol associations, introducing a z-score-based classification over contributory documents to distinguish memorized from non-memorized cultural associations. The paper also draws on findings from Zhang et al. (2024) and Chang et al. (2024) on how pretraining frequency imbalances cause overgeneralization to high-frequency knowledge, extending those insights to the cultural domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The MEMOED framework provides a concrete, reproducible diagnostic tool for tracing specific cultural generation patterns back to pretraining data, which is essential for designing targeted mitigations (data augmentation, unlearning, reweighting). The finding that diffuse associations dominate 79–91% of actual responses despite comprising only 4–11% of unique symbols reveals that models overwhelmingly default to generic, non-culture-specific content, a problem more severe than prior bias-detection work suggested. Key limitations include analysis restricted to OLMo-7B and two topics (food, clothing), document-level granularity that cannot replicate exact training batch contexts, and the absence of any mitigation strategy. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes to other model families, whether the frequency-memorization correlation holds at different scales, and how to operationalize the attribution findings into concrete pretraining or fine-tuning interventions.
