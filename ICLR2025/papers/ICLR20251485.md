## Problem

Existing calibration measures (ECE, Platt scaling, histogram binning) assume classifiers output scalar confidence scores, making them inapplicable to humans or LLMs that express uncertainty through natural language certainty phrases like 'Maybe' or 'Likely.' Prior verbalized-confidence work mapped each phrase to a single fixed probability, ignoring both the range of confidence a phrase conveys and population-level variation in interpretation. This paper asks: how can we measure and calibrate uncertainty when it is communicated as distributions over the probability simplex rather than point estimates?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251485/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes certainty phrases (e.g., 'Likely pneumonia' from a radiologist's X-ray report, or 'Probably' from an LLM's answer) paired with ground-truth labels (CT report findings or correct QA answers), along with a predefined set of K beta distributions that encode the semantic meaning of each phrase. It produces a generalized ECE estimate, a smooth (non-binned) reliability diagram, and a discrete probabilistic calibration map T ∈ R^{K×L}_+ — a transport plan where T_kl specifies the fraction of phrase-k usages that should be replaced by phrase-l. Unlike scalar calibration maps, the output is directly interpretable as natural-language editing instructions (e.g., 'replace 30% of "Likely" with "Maybe"').

## Method

The core representation treats each certainty phrase as a beta distribution over [0,1] rather than a point probability, so a classifier g outputs g(x) = u_{γ(x)} where γ selects a phrase index and u maps it to a distribution. The ECE estimator is generalized so each sample contributes to every bin with weight P(S ∈ I_m | X = x_n), yielding a Nadaraya-Watson-style estimator with input-dependent, asymmetric kernels that is robust to binning choices. For post-hoc calibration, the paper formulates a discrete optimal transport problem: the cost matrix C_kl measures the per-unit net change in ECE when substituting distribution v_l for u_k, and the calibration map is obtained by solving an entropy-regularized unbalanced OT problem (via log-stabilized Sinkhorn) that minimizes total calibration-error change while penalizing marginal deviations from the original phrase frequencies. This produces a stochastic, interpretable phrase-to-phrase mapping rather than an abstract score transformation, enabling actionable suggestions for both radiologists and LLMs.

## Results

On the TruthfulQA dataset (where LLMs are less calibrated), the optimal transport calibration method reduces ECE substantially: gpt-4o from 0.22 to 0.10, claude-3.5-sonnet from 0.25 to 0.04, and gemini-1.5-pro from 0.28 to 0.07 (all using verbalized certainty phrases). On SciQ, where models are already well-calibrated (ECE ~0.06–0.17 uncalibrated), improvements are marginal. For radiologists, the method provides interpretable phrase-level recommendations (e.g., replacing 'Present' with 'May') that reduce ECE* and Brier Score on the test set, though no single headline number is reported in the main text.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its optimal transport calibration against two classical baselines: Platt scaling and histogram binning, both of which require reducing certainty phrases to scalar confidence scores (the mean of the corresponding distribution). On TruthfulQA, histogram binning achieves the lowest ECE for gpt-4o (0.05 vs. 0.10 for the proposed method), while the proposed method ties binning for claude-3.5-sonnet (both 0.04) and ties scaling for gemini-1.5-pro (both 0.07). On SciQ, all three methods perform comparably since models are already well-calibrated. The authors explicitly note that their method's key advantage is not outperforming baselines on ECE, but rather directly producing calibrated certainty phrases (e.g., 'replace Likely with Maybe') rather than abstract probability adjustments, making the output actionable for human users like radiologists. Accuracy is preserved across all methods, confirming that calibration does not compromise discriminative performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the expected calibration error (ECE) framework from scalar confidence scores to distributional representations of linguistic certainty phrases, building on the verbalized confidence line of work (Lin et al., 2022) that mapped phrases to fixed probabilities. The key conceptual departure is modeling each phrase as a beta distribution over the simplex rather than a point estimate, which generalizes the ECE estimator into a Nadaraya-Watson kernel density estimator with input-dependent, asymmetric kernels. For post-hoc calibration, the paper departs from classical methods (Platt scaling, histogram binning) that require access to scalar scores, instead formulating calibration as an unbalanced optimal transport problem over a discrete set of phrases, yielding interpretable phrase-to-phrase transport plans.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides the first principled way to measure and improve calibration for both humans (radiologists) and LLMs that communicate uncertainty in natural language, with the optimal transport calibration map offering actionable, interpretable suggestions (e.g., 'use May instead of Present') rather than abstract probability adjustments. However, the radiology study relies on a single hospital's 2,662 paired reports and LLM-based pathology extraction, and no clinical trial validates whether radiologists can actually adopt the suggested phrase changes. Open questions include whether the distributional formulation generalizes beyond binary classification, how calibration maps transfer across clinical settings or model families, and whether the modest ECE improvements over Platt scaling justify the added complexity in practice.
