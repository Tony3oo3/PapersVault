## Problem

Digital twins of the mouse visual cortex have achieved strong single-neuron prediction accuracy, but whether they capture the geometry of population-level neural responses—specifically the differentiability of V1 representations and the hierarchy of object discriminability across visual areas—remains unexplored. This paper shows that state-of-the-art models consistently produce non-differentiable population representations and fail to reproduce the experimentally observed discriminability hierarchy, regardless of dataset, architecture, or loss function.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250619/cropped_page1_idx7.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes 36×64 px video frames at 30 Hz with running speed and pupil size appended as extra channels (forming a 5D tensor of shape Batch×Channels×Frames×Height×Width), plus a separate 3D tensor of pupil center coordinates per frame. Outputs are frame-by-frame predicted firing rates for each neuron (via ELU+1 activation), which are then aggregated to compute population-level quantities: the eigenspectrum of the inter-neuronal covariance matrix (yielding a power-law decay exponent α) and mutual-information-based object discriminability scores across V1, LM, RL, and AL.

## Method

The benchmark model is a 3D CNN core (4 factorized spatial-temporal convolutional layers with 16→128 channels) followed by a per-neuron readout (learnable spatial position + linear feature weights) and a shifter MLP that maps pupil coordinates to a global gaze offset, trained end-to-end with Poisson loss on the MICrONS dataset. The paper's central finding is that this standard training yields a power-law exponent α below the critical threshold 1+2/d, indicating non-differentiable representations, and a flat discriminability hierarchy across areas. Systematic ablations over dataset (MICrONS vs. SENSORIUM), architecture (3D CNN vs. ViV1T transformer), and loss function (Poisson vs. correlation MSE) all fail to fix the geometry. The key intervention is training regularization: dropout (rate ≥ 0.4) applied at each core layer, or random affine augmentation of input videos, which introduces stochasticity into the input–response mapping and pushes α above 1, restoring differentiability and partially recovering the experimental LM > V1 > RL discriminability hierarchy.

## Results

The benchmark digital twin achieves a median CCnorm of 0.47–0.58 on MICrONS (correlation-to-average 0.294), comparable to the best prior model (Wang et al., 2023: CCnorm 0.48–0.65). However, when simulating the Stringer et al. (2019a) experiment, the model's eigenspectrum power-law exponent for natural images is α = 0.82, well below the experimental value of α = 1.04 and below the differentiability threshold of 1 + 2/d, indicating non-differentiable population representations. Applying dropout ≥ 0.4 during training raises α to 1.06, closely matching the experimental 1.04, and shifts the discriminability hierarchy from a flat/wrong ordering to one consistent with experiments (LM > V1 > RL), though AL is still misplaced at the bottom.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On single-neuron prediction, the paper's benchmark model is broadly competitive: on MICrONS it matches Wang et al. (2023) within ~0.01 CCnorm (0.47–0.58 vs. 0.48–0.65), and on SENSORIUM it slightly outperforms the original competition baseline (Turishcheva et al., 2023: Corr 0.229, Corr-to-avg 0.416) but falls short of the transformer-based ViV1T (Corr 0.261, Corr-to-avg 0.478). The critical finding, however, is that all three baselines—including the higher-performing ViV1T—fail identically to the paper's model in capturing population response geometry: none produces a differentiable eigenspectrum (all α < 1 + 2/d) or the correct discriminability hierarchy across visual areas. The paper's contribution is not a single-neuron accuracy win but the demonstration that this failure is universal across architectures (CNN, transformer), datasets (MICrONS, SENSORIUM), and loss functions (Poisson, correlation-based), and that only training-time regularization (dropout ≥ 0.4 or stimulus augmentation) restores differentiable representations and partially recovers the experimental hierarchy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the digital twin framework for mouse visual cortex (Lurz et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2023; Turishcheva et al. 2023) by shifting the evaluation criterion from single-neuron prediction accuracy to population-level response geometry, specifically the eigenspectrum power-law decay (Stringer et al. 2019a) and the hierarchy of object discriminability across visual areas (Froudarakis et al. 2020). It departs from prior work that has focused on single-neuron metrics (Poisson loss, correlation) by demonstrating that these metrics are insufficient to capture the differentiability and hierarchical structure of neural population responses. The regularization strategies explored (dropout, data augmentation) draw on standard deep learning techniques but are applied here with the explicit goal of matching biological population geometry rather than improving single-neuron fit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a fundamental gap: state-of-the-art digital twins that achieve strong single-neuron correlations consistently produce non-differentiable population representations, undermining their utility for in silico experiments on population-level computations. The finding that simple regularization (dropout ≥ 0.4) restores differentiability suggests that biological noise (spiking variability, stochastic synapses) may be essential for the smooth, high-dimensional geometry observed in cortex, reframing noise from a nuisance to a functional ingredient. However, significant limitations remain: the hierarchy of discriminability is only partially recovered (AL is misplaced), discriminability values are overestimated, and a consistent trade-off between single-neuron accuracy and population geometry persists, leaving open how to build models that capture both simultaneously.
