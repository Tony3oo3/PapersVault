## Problem

Existing LLM benchmarks target full-precision models on high-end GPU clusters, while the few mobile-device studies examine only a narrow set of models and platforms, overlooking resource efficiency (memory, power, thermal behavior) and the harmful outputs (hallucinations, toxic content) that quantization introduces. There is no unified, automated framework that systematically evaluates compressed LLMs across diverse mobile hardware, quantization schemes, and inference engines under realistic deployment constraints.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes text prompts drawn from SQuAD, Natural Questions, MTBench, HaluEval, and TruthfulQA, feeding them into quantized LLMs (2-bit through 8-bit, via GPTQ, AWQ, K-quant, and group-wise methods) running on eight mobile/edge platforms (iPhone 12/15 Pro, Pixel 4/5a/7, S22 Ultra, Orange Pi 5, Jetson Orin Nano) through either MLC-LLM or llama.cpp. Outputs are structured as per-run JSON traces capturing clock-aligned time series of CPU/GPU utilization, memory, battery drain, and GPU bus read/write, alongside scalar metrics: prefill/decode throughput (tok/s), peak and average temperature (°C), power consumed (mWh), Exact Match and F1 scores against non-quantized references, Perspective API toxicity scores, and hallucination percentages judged by GPT-4o and Claude-3.5 Sonnet via an LLM-as-a-judge protocol.

## Method

PalmBench is a lightweight, all-in-one automated benchmarking framework comprising three modules: an Evaluator that extracts prompts from datasets and scores outputs, a Scheduler that dispatches inference jobs and monitors resource usage, and a Profiling/Monitor layer that collects platform-specific telemetry via USB without additional hardware. For Android and Linux edge devices, prompts are transferred over USB serial ports and profiling uses Android GPU Inspector (via ADB), btop, or jetson-stats; for iOS, a custom GPU plugin built on IOKit complements Xcode Instruments. The framework standardizes decoding hyperparameters (temperature 0.2, Top-P 0.9, 4096-token context) across both inference engines and repeats each experiment 10 times on factory-reset devices. A key differentiator from prior mobile benchmarks is the joint evaluation of resource efficiency (memory footprint, GPU duty cycles, layer-wise GPU profiling across transformer blocks, power and thermal behavior via FLIR C5 and Klein Tools USB power meters) alongside accuracy degradation and harmful-output frequency, revealing that 4-bit quantization (GPTQ, AWQ, group-wise) best preserves quality while 3-bit and 2-bit variants exhibit substantially higher hallucination and toxicity rates.

## Results

Across Llama3-8B quantization levels, 4-bit GPTQ achieves 9.1% hallucination rate and 92.1% TruthfulQA score, while 2-bit and 3-bit quantization degrade severely to 32.7% and 37.5% hallucination respectively. For power consumption, iPhone 15 Pro is the most efficient platform (10.13 mWh for 3-bit, 13.05 mWh for 4-bit Llama-3.2-3B), while Orange Pi 5 consumes the most (25.4 mWh / 27.8 mWh) and reaches peak temperatures of 71.5–75.4°C. The 4-bit Llama-3.2-3B model consumes 25.2% more power than its 3-bit counterpart, confirming that higher-bit quantization increases both GPU workload and energy draw. On throughput, iPhones (especially iPhone 12 Pro and 15 Pro) deliver significantly higher tok/s than Android devices and Jetson Orin Nano for Llama-2-7B and Llama-3-8B models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among quantization methods, 4-bit GPTQ (9.1% hallucination, 92.1% TruthfulQA) and 4-bit AWQ (8.9% hallucination, 92.3% TruthfulQA) perform nearly identically and both substantially outperform 2-bit (32.7% hallucination) and 3-bit (37.5% hallucination) quantization. Counterintuitively, 3-bit quantization performs worse than 2-bit on both hallucination and toxicity metrics, which the authors attribute to implementation-specific issues in the tested framework versions. Between inference frameworks, llama.cpp generally consumes less memory than MLC-LLM across all platforms due to its lightweight C++ implementation, while MLC shows a lower memory footprint on iPhone compared to Android. On the platform dimension, iOS devices (iPhone 12 Pro, 15 Pro) outperform all Android phones and edge devices in throughput, power efficiency, and peak temperature, with the three-year-old iPhone 12 Pro still beating newer Android devices and Jetson Orin Nano on tok/s for 7B–8B models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of mobile LLM evaluation work (MELT, MobileAIBench, Çöplü et al.) by broadening scope across more models, platforms, and quantization levels, and by adding dimensions those prior benchmarks omitted: power/temperature profiling and harmful-output (toxicity, hallucination) assessment. It repurposes existing quantization methods (GPTQ, AWQ, K-quant, group-wise) and inference frameworks (MLC-LLM, llama.cpp) as evaluation subjects rather than proposing new compression or inference techniques. Conceptually, it departs from cloud-centric LLM benchmarks (MTBench, MMLU) by treating resource efficiency—memory, energy, thermal behavior—as a first-class evaluation axis alongside accuracy, reflecting the constraints that actually govern mobile deployment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides practitioners with actionable, cross-platform guidance for choosing model size, quantization level, and inference framework under real mobile hardware constraints, an area where prior work was fragmented. The finding that 3-bit quantization can produce more hallucinations than 2-bit, and that framework implementation bugs (not just quantization loss) can cause incoherent outputs, challenges the monotonic 'more bits = better quality' assumption and highlights a previously under-studied failure mode. Limitations include a fixed set of 8 devices, 10 repetitions per configuration, and reliance on LLM-as-a-judge for toxicity/hallucination scoring, which may not generalize to newer hardware generations or larger model families. Open questions include whether the observed iOS power-efficiency advantage persists with next-generation SoCs, and whether the framework-implementation hallucination issue is specific to the tested MLC/llama.cpp versions.
