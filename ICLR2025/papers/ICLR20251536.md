## Problem

The Warmup-Stable-Decay (WSD) learning rate schedule produces a counterintuitive loss curve where loss remains elevated during the constant-rate stable phase yet drops sharply during the brief decay phase, ultimately outperforming cosine schedules. The paper asks why this non-traditional behavior occurs and what loss landscape structure explains it, while also addressing the practical limitation that cosine schedules require pre-committing to a total compute budget, making them inflexible for continual pretraining across multiple budget targets.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251536/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a text training corpus (The Pile, OpenWebText, or DCLM) and a LLaMA-architecture transformer (0.1B–1.2B parameters) along with a set of target compute budgets (e.g., 50B, 100B, 200B tokens). It produces a sequence of pretrained checkpoints at each budget target from a single continuous training run. Unlike WSD, which branches off from the main constant-rate branch to decay and then rolls back, WSD-S simply continues training from the most recent decayed checkpoint, eliminating the need to store or restore intermediate main-branch states.

## Method

The paper introduces a 'river valley landscape' formalism (Definition 3.1) in which the loss has a 1D flat manifold (the river) along the minimal Hessian eigenvector direction, surrounded by steep hillsides in all orthogonal directions. Theoretical analysis (Theorems 3.2–3.5) shows that a high constant learning rate drives fast progress along the river while inducing large oscillations in the hill directions (adding a (d−1)ησ²/2 term to the loss), and that a rapid decay schedule linearly reduces this hill component, revealing the accumulated river progress. Motivated by this, WSD-S simplifies WSD by resuming the next stable phase directly from the end of the previous decay phase rather than rolling back to the pre-decay checkpoint, thereby reusing the river-direction progress made during decay. A bigram toy model (Section 4) demonstrates that heterogeneity in next-token uncertainty—deterministic tokens forming the flat river, stochastic tokens forming the sharp hillsides—naturally produces this landscape structure, and empirical probing of GPT-2 checkpoints confirms valley-shaped interpolation in the stable phase and smooth monotone decay in the decay phase.

## Results

On LLaMA models (0.1B–1.2B) trained on The Pile with target compute budgets of 50B, 100B, and 200B tokens, WSD-S achieves validation loss within 6e-3 of independently tuned Cosine-Oracle runs in a single training run. WSD-S outperforms Cyclic-Cosine by 4e-2 in validation loss on 0.6B models at 100B tokens. WSD-S consistently outperforms WSD across all model sizes at ~200B tokens, with the only exception being the smallest-scale 0.1B model at 25k steps. On the DCLM dataset (0.1B, 25k steps), WSD-S achieves a best validation loss of 2.49565 at LR=2e-3, marginally beating Cyclic-Cosine's 2.49672.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WSD-S is compared against three baselines: Cosine-Oracle (independent cosine runs optimally tuned per budget), Cyclic-Cosine (consecutive cosine cycles in one run), and WSD (the original branching schedule from Hu et al., 2024). Against Cosine-Oracle, WSD-S matches performance within 6e-3 validation loss across all model sizes and budgets, confirming it is a practical single-run alternative. Against Cyclic-Cosine, WSD-S wins by a substantial 4e-2 gap on 0.6B models at 100B tokens; the paper attributes this to Cyclic-Cosine's prolonged low-learning-rate phase implicitly damaging the model for future continual training. Against WSD, WSD-S achieves consistent improvements at 200B tokens across 0.3B, 0.6B, and 1.2B models by reusing decay-phase progress rather than rolling back to the main branch, with the sole exception being the 0.1B model at the smallest 25k-step budget where WSD slightly edges out WSD-S.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Warmup-Stable-Decay (WSD) schedule introduced by Hu et al. (2024), providing the first theoretical explanation for WSD's non-traditional loss curve through a formal 'river valley' loss landscape (Definition 3.1). Conceptually, it extends the SGD-in-a-valley picture from Xing et al. (2018) and the ravine structure identified by Davis et al. (2024) by adding a 1D 'river' manifold along which the gradient is nonzero and progress is made, and by connecting this geometry to token-level uncertainty in language modeling. The practical contribution, WSD-S, departs from WSD's two-branch design by eliminating the rollback step, motivated by the theoretical finding that the decay phase also makes progress along the river direction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The river valley framework gives practitioners an intuitive and quantifiable explanation for why maintaining a high learning rate is beneficial despite elevated loss, and why a final decay is essential—resolving a practical confusion that has limited adoption of WSD-style schedules. WSD-S offers a simpler, single-branch alternative that empirically matches or beats WSD and Cyclic-Cosine across 0.1B–1.2B models, making it easier to deploy in open-source continual pretraining pipelines. Key limitations include the strong geometric assumptions (eigengap, slow river spinning) that are hard to verify in real LLMs, experiments capped at 1.2B parameters, and the fact that the bigram toy model, while illustrative, is far from the complexity of actual pretraining. Open questions include whether the river valley structure persists at frontier scale and whether the optimal decay fraction and schedule shape can be derived more generally.
