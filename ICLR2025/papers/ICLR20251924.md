## Problem

Improving trained models with targeted synthetic data is currently a labor-intensive, human-driven loop: a developer inspects evaluation errors, designs data to address weaknesses, trains, and iterates. No simulator or testbed exists for developing and evaluating autonomous data generation agents (teachers) that could automate this iterative, feedback-driven process, making it impossible to systematically compare agent policies, engines, or feedback mechanisms.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251924/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent receives a state encoding the student's current performance—ranging from a raw list of per-example errors (OPEN-ENDED) to skill-partitioned predictions (SKILL-LIST) to a hierarchical skill forest with per-subskill data budgets and accuracy (SKILL-TREE)—and produces a data generation plan that the engine executes into instruction-finetuning examples in Alpaca format. For VQA tasks, the engine additionally generates 1024×1024 images via SDXL-Turbo from LLM-written scene descriptions. The final output is an updated student model whose validation accuracy serves as the teacher's reward signal for the next iteration.

## Method

DATAENVGYM frames data generation as a sequential decision-making task with a modular agent (policy + engine) operating inside an environment that trains and evaluates a student model each iteration. Three environments provide increasing structure: OPEN-ENDED gives an unstructured error list and lets the policy directly specify datapoints; SKILL-LIST adds an LLM-based skill discovery module (two-stage: per-instance skill assignment, then aggregation) that partitions the state by skill; SKILL-TREE further organizes skills into a hierarchical skill forest where the policy's action space is restricted to binary explore (grow the tree with new subskills) and exploit (rebalance data allocation among existing subskills) actions. The skill discovery module follows Didolkar et al. (2024), and the skill organization module maintains per-subskill metadata (data budget, training accuracy) that evolves across iterations. The modular design allows swapping any component—skill discovery, policy, engine, trainer—while measuring downstream student improvement as the evaluation metric, enabling controlled ablations of each module's contribution.

## Results

Across three primary domains, DATAENVGYM's baseline agents improve student models by an average of +4.60% (OPEN-ENDED), +3.15% (SKILL-LIST), and +3.32% (SKILL-TREE) in absolute accuracy when conditioning on student feedback (With State). The largest single-task gain is +7.66% on MATH (Gemma-2-2B) in the OPEN-ENDED environment, while SKILL-TREE achieves the best GQA improvement at +5.58% (PaliGemma-3B). On the additional benchmarks, improvements are even larger: +16.41% on NaturalBench and +21.64% on MnMs (tool-use). Conditioning on student feedback (With State vs. No State) consistently helps, with the gap ranging from +1.08% (SKILL-TREE) to +3.5% (OPEN-ENDED) in average improvement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external competing data-generation methods; instead, it ablates internal design choices. The most important comparison is With State vs. No State: conditioning the data generation policy on student feedback (errors, skill-level performance) consistently outperforms ignoring feedback, with gaps of +3.5% (OPEN-ENDED), +2.05% (SKILL-LIST), and +1.08% (SKILL-TREE) in average improvement. On LiveCodeBench, No State policies fail to improve the student at all in OPEN-ENDED and SKILL-LIST, whereas With State policies achieve +2.41% and +1.75% respectively. Among environments, OPEN-ENDED yields the highest MATH gain (+7.66%) while SKILL-TREE is best for GQA (+5.58%), suggesting domain-dependent trade-offs between unconstrained and skill-structured teaching. A second key comparison is oracle vs. inferred skills in the SKILL-LIST environment: oracle skills improve GQA by +8.84% versus +4.00% for inferred skills, and MATH by +3.74% versus +2.47%, demonstrating that skill discovery quality is a meaningful bottleneck. Finally, an ablation (Table 8) confirms that gains come from new data rather than extra training epochs: training on 20% of the data for 5× more epochs yields substantially smaller improvements than training on 100% of the data for standard epochs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the skill discovery approach of Didolkar et al. (2024) for its two-stage skill induction pipeline (per-instance skill assignment followed by LLM-based aggregation), and draws conceptual inspiration from unsupervised environment design (Dennis et al., 2020; Zala et al., 2024) and LLM-based environment generation (Cobbe et al., 2020; Todd et al., 2023). However, it departs sharply from prior environment generation work, which focused on game or embodied settings with predefined actions, by treating the student model as part of the environment and centering the teacher (data generation agent) as the subject of study. It also extends the synthetic data generation literature (Self-Instruct, Orca, symbolic distillation) by making the process iterative and feedback-driven rather than one-shot, framing it as a sequential decision-making problem with explicit state, action, and reward structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DATAENVGYM fills a clear gap: prior to this work, there was no standardized testbed for developing and comparing data generation agents, forcing researchers to build ad-hoc pipelines. The modular design (swappable skill discovery, data generation engine, and policy) enables systematic ablation of components, and the three environment instantiations (open-ended, skill-list, skill-tree) provide a useful spectrum of structure for studying the trade-off between flexibility and interpretability. Key open questions include: whether the skill discovery module can be improved to close the oracle gap (8.84% vs. 4.00% on GQA), whether learned (rather than LLM-prompted) policies can outperform the current baselines, and whether the framework scales to domains where the student is truly weak rather than already instruction-tuned. A limitation is that all experiments use GPT-4o as the teacher, making the testbed expensive and raising questions about whether the findings transfer to weaker or open-weight teachers.
