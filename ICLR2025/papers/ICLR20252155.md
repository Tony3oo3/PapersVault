## Problem

Current speech language models align with brain responses to natural language but do so primarily through low-level speech features (e.g., tri-phones, articulation) rather than brain-relevant semantics, limiting their utility as model organisms of semantic processing in the brain. This paper asks whether directly incorporating fMRI brain signals into speech model training can induce brain-relevant semantics and, for the first time, whether such improved brain alignment translates into downstream semantic gains.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252155/cropped_page2_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes pretrained ~90M-parameter transformer speech models (Wav2vec2.0, HuBERT, Whisper encoder) and pairs them with fMRI recordings from 8 participants listening to 27 Moth Radio Hour stories (6.4 hours each, TR = 2.0045 s). Audio is processed in 16-second sliding windows at 0.1 s stride, downsampled via a 3-lobed Lanczos filter to match the fMRI acquisition rate, and convolved with a 10-second hemodynamic response filter; voxels are retained only if their estimated noise ceiling exceeds 0.4. The output is a brain-tuned speech model whose internal representations better capture semantic structure, evaluated on held-out fMRI data (1 story) and a suite of downstream semantic tasks.

## Method

Brain-tuning fine-tunes a pretrained speech model to reconstruct fMRI voxel responses by appending an average-pooling layer and a linear projection head on top of the transformer's output tokens, trained with L2 reconstruction loss against the fMRI targets. The CNN feature extractor is frozen while the transformer layers and projection head are updated with separate learning rates (5×10⁻⁵ and 10⁻⁴) under a linear decay schedule with 10% warmup, using 24 of 27 stories for training, 2 for validation, and 1 held out for testing; the process is repeated independently per participant. The key insight is that fMRI signals from late language regions (angular gyrus, inferior frontal gyrus, temporal lobes) and auditory cortex serve as a supervisory signal that biases representations toward brain-relevant semantics, reducing reliance on low-level features while improving downstream semantic task performance. Evaluation combines normalized voxel-wise brain alignment (ridge-regression encoding models with Pearson correlation), low-level feature impact (measuring alignment drop after computationally removing power spectrum, di/tri-phones, and articulation features), and downstream tasks spanning ASR, phoneme/word prediction, sequence understanding, and emotion recognition.

## Results

Brain-tuning significantly improves normalized brain alignment with late language regions by approximately 30% for the self-supervised models (Wav2vec2.0 and HuBERT) on held-out fMRI data, while showing no significant change in primary auditory regions. Across all three model families (including Whisper, which showed no total alignment gain), brain-tuning significantly reduces the impact of low-level speech features (Power Spectrum, Di/Tri-Phones, Articulation) on alignment with late language regions. On downstream tasks, brain-tuning yields consistent improvements on semantic tasks (phonetic sentence type prediction, phonemes prediction, sequence understanding, word identity prediction) while maintaining performance on low-level tasks (MFCC/FBANK prediction) and showing mixed results on emotion recognition. Notably, a brain-tuned HuBERT base (90M params) matches the brain alignment of a pretrained HuBERT large (320M params) while having lower low-level feature impact, using less than 0.7% of the original training data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Brain-tuned models outperform their pretrained counterparts on brain alignment in late language regions (Wav2vec2.0, HuBERT) and on low-level feature reduction (all three families), with the largest downstream gains on phonetic sentence type prediction and phonemes prediction. The Random Brain-tuned baseline (block-permuted fMRI targets) performs worse than pretrained models on both brain alignment and downstream tasks, confirming that the correct fMRI signal is essential. The BigSLM-tuned baseline (Whisper Medium 800M representations) performs on par or slightly better than pretrained models but is consistently outperformed by brain-tuned models on semantic downstream tasks (except emotion prediction). The Stimulus-tuned baseline (additional self-supervised training on the same audio) performs similarly to pretrained models, ruling out the explanation that gains come from extra training data. The LM-tuned baselines (GPT2-Medium, Llama2-7B) improve over pretrained models specifically on phonemes prediction and phonetic sentence type prediction—the same two tasks where brain-tuning yields the largest gains—providing converging evidence that the improvements are semantic in nature.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the brain-tuning approach of Schwartz et al. (2019), who fine-tuned a text-based BERT model using fMRI/MEG recordings, to the speech domain across three transformer-based model families (Wav2vec2.0, HuBERT, Whisper). It directly addresses a key limitation identified by Oota et al. (2024a): that pretrained speech models align with semantic brain regions primarily through low-level acoustic features rather than genuine semantics. Unlike prior alignment studies that only measure post-hoc correspondence between models and brain activity, this work uses brain recordings as a training signal to actively reshape model representations, and is the first to demonstrate that this reshaping transfers to downstream semantic task performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that less than 0.7% of the original training data (in the form of fMRI recordings) can improve downstream semantic performance across multiple tasks is conceptually significant, suggesting brain signals carry complementary information not captured by standard speech pretraining. However, the evidence is constrained by a small dataset (8 participants, 27 stories) and three model families of comparable size (~90M parameters), leaving open whether the approach scales to larger models or benefits from more participants. The paper also raises the question of whether the gains come specifically from late-language-region voxels versus auditory cortex voxels, a hypothesis the authors flag for future work. For the neuro-AI community, this provides a concrete recipe for building better model organisms of auditory language processing, but broader adoption will depend on whether the effect persists with larger, more diverse brain datasets.
