## Problem

Traditional centrality measures such as PageRank and degree centrality produce unbalanced rankings on graphs with underlying community structures: when cores within different communities have different densities, top-ranked nodes systematically over-represent the denser core and can completely omit vertices from sparser communities. While fairness-constrained ranking has been studied in the supervised setting, the balancedness of unsupervised graph ranking—particularly in the presence of multi-core-periphery structures—remains largely unexplored.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a directed adjacency matrix (typically a 20-NN graph embedding of a high-dimensional vector dataset, e.g., single-cell RNA-seq data with 1,400–54,000 nodes) and produces a per-vertex relative centrality score in [0,1], from which a balanced ranking is obtained by sorting in descending order. The algorithm is parameterized by three hyperparameters: t (number of power-method steps for the initial centrality), y (hop radius of the local normalization neighborhood), and z (number of recursive re-normalization passes), with runtime O((t·|E| + (z−1)·k^y·|V|)) for k-regular directed graphs.

## Method

The core mechanism is a local normalization step that converts a global centrality score into a relative one: after computing an initial score F(v) via a t-step power iteration (s ← A^T s), each vertex's final score is F(v) divided by the average F-score of its higher-scoring out-neighbors plus itself, yielding a value in (0,1]. This normalization is graph-dependent and local, so a core vertex in a sparse core and one in a dense core both receive scores near 1, while periphery vertices (whose neighbors include higher-scoring core nodes) receive strictly lower scores. The generalization M-Rank(t,y,z) extends this by using y-hop neighborhoods for the reference set and recursively feeding the normalized scores back as the initial centrality for z iterations. The theoretical analysis is grounded in a newly proposed MCPC (multi-core-periphery with communities) structure, where they prove that degree centrality is linearly proportional to core concentration (Theorem 3.2, causing imbalance) while 1-step N-Rank assigns a Θ(1/k) fraction of vertices from each core a perfect score of 1 regardless of concentration (Theorem 3.3).

## Results

On 7 single-cell RNA-seq datasets (ranging from 1,886 to 54,865 cells), RN-Rank achieves the highest preservation ratio (balancedness) across all datasets, e.g., 0.89 on Tcell5759 vs. 0.73 for Degree centrality and 0.69 for PageRank(0.5), while maintaining comparable Louvain clustering purity (0.79 vs. 0.81 for Degree). N2-Rank also outperforms all baselines on preservation ratio in 6/7 datasets. At the top-20% cutoff, baseline centrality measures completely miss vertices from some ground-truth communities, whereas RN-Rank retains representation from all communities. In simulation on MCPC-block graphs with γ=0.05 (unequal core concentrations), RN-Rank maintains high balancedness while degree centrality and PageRank become severely unbalanced.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against four families of centrality baselines: PageRank (with damping factors 0.5, 0.85, 0.99), Katz centrality, Degree centrality, and Onion decomposition. RN-Rank wins decisively on preservation ratio (balancedness) across all 7 real-world datasets, with the largest gaps on Tcell5759 (0.89 vs. 0.73 for Degree) and TM54865 (0.87 vs. 0.78 for Degree). On clustering purity, the proposed methods are comparable to or marginally below the best baselines (e.g., RN-Rank 0.99 vs. Degree 1.00 on BH1886; RN-Rank 0.79 vs. Degree 0.81 on Tcell5759), confirming the paper's claim of a balancedness–separability tradeoff where balancedness is gained at negligible clustering cost. Onion decomposition is the weakest baseline on both metrics, with the lowest preservation ratio (0.22–0.51) in most datasets. In the MCPC-block model simulation (γ=0.05), all traditional centrality measures show sharply declining balancedness as core concentration asymmetry increases, while RN-Rank maintains high balancedness with comparable core-prioritization AUROC.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) centrality measures for graph ranking (PageRank, degree, Katz, onion decomposition), (2) core-periphery structure analysis (Rombach et al., Yanchenko & Sengupta), and (3) fairness/balancedness in ranking (Celis et al., Singh & Joachims). It departs from prior work in two key ways: it extends the core-periphery literature from single-core to multi-core settings coexisting with community structure (formalized as the MCPC structure), and it moves the balancedness constraint from the supervised fairness-in-ranking setting to the unsupervised graph-ranking context. The N-Rank algorithm's initialization reuses the power-iteration mechanism underlying PageRank, while the stochastic block model framework (Abbe, 2018) is extended into the 4-block MCPC-block model for theoretical analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete failure mode of widely-used centrality measures (unbalanced representation of communities in top-ranked subsets) and provides both a structural explanation (MCPC) and a simple fix (local neighborhood normalization). The practical impact is demonstrated on 11 single-cell RNA-seq datasets where top-ranked subsets from standard centrality measures completely miss some cell types, while relative centrality preserves all communities with comparable clustering accuracy. Limitations include the theoretical analysis being restricted to 2-community balanced block models, the method being evaluated primarily on k-NN graphs (nearly regular), and the clustering accuracy gains being comparable rather than superior to baselines—the main advantage is balancedness, not separability. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to non-regular graphs, more than two communities in theory, and whether the MCPC structure holds in other domains beyond single-cell data.
