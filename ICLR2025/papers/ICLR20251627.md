## Problem

Existing wireless propagation simulators are either model-based ray tracers (accurate but non-differentiable, non-learnable, and suffering from sim-to-real gaps) or fully-learnt neural surrogates that rely on lossy 2D image representations and CNN architectures ill-suited to the inherently 3D geometric nature of electromagnetic wave propagation. No prior fully-learnt surrogate operates directly on full 3D scene geometry with the strong inductive biases needed for sample-efficient, symmetry-robust generalization across diverse indoor environments.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a 3D triangular mesh with per-face discrete material classes, plus 3D position and orientation vectors for one or more transmitters and receivers, all tokenized into 16-dimensional projective geometric algebra (G_{3,0,1}) multivectors (one token per mesh face, one per antenna, one per Tx-Rx link). Outputs are real-valued scalar channel statistics (non-coherent received power in dB, band-limited delay spread), and through the diffusion formulation, the model can also produce receiver positions or reconstructed mesh geometries as probabilistic samples. The architecture handles variable-length token sequences, scaling to scenes with up to 200 receivers and 15 transmitters per layout.

## Method

Wi-GATr combines a novel wireless geometric algebra tokenizer with an E(3)-equivariant Geometric Algebra Transformer backbone. The tokenizer maps each mesh face to a token encoding its center point, vertices, oriented plane, and one-hot material embedding in G_{3,0,1}; antennas are encoded as position (trivector), orientation (bivector), and type scalars; and channel links carry Tx/Rx positions, the Tx-Rx direction vector, and normalized power. The transformer (32 blocks, 16 multivector + 32 scalar channels, 8 heads with multi-query attention, ~16M parameters) processes these tokens via scaled dot-product attention, inheriting E(3) equivariance (translations, rotations, reflections) and permutation invariance from the GA representation. Training uses supervised MSE on received power with reciprocity data augmentation (random Tx/Rx swapping), and the backbone can be wrapped in a DDPM diffusion model (1000 denoising steps, conditional masking) to unify forward prediction, receiver localization, and geometry reconstruction as conditional sampling from a single joint density p(F, x^tx, x^rx, h).

## Results

On the WiPTR dataset, Wi-GATr achieves a mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.39 dB on unseen Rx positions and 0.41 dB on unseen floor plans, substantially outperforming the vanilla Transformer (0.62/0.69 dB) and PLViT (1.27/1.28 dB). Under symmetry transformations (rotation, translation), Wi-GATr maintains its 0.41 dB MAE while the Transformer degrades catastrophically to 38.51 dB (rotation) and 4.96 dB (translation). On the real-world DICHASUS dataset, Wi-GATr achieves >35% lower error than hybrid ray-tracing approaches and >70% lower error than a calibrated wireless ray tracer. Notably, Wi-GATr trained on only 10% of WiPTR data (MAE 0.64 dB) surpasses both PLViT (1.28 dB) and the full-data Transformer (0.69 dB).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Wi-GATr consistently outperforms all four baselines across both simulated datasets. Against the vanilla Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017), Wi-GATr wins on every in-distribution and symmetry-transformation setting; the gap is most dramatic under rotation, where the Transformer's MAE explodes to 78.68 dB (Wi3R) and 38.51 dB (WiPTR) versus Wi-GATr's stable 0.74/0.41 dB. Against the E(3)-equivariant SEGNN (Brandstetter et al., 2022), Wi-GATr achieves lower MAE on Wi3R (0.63 vs. 0.92 dB for unseen Rx; 0.74 vs. 1.02 dB for unseen floor plans) and scales to WiPTR where SEGNN is infeasible (>80 GB memory). Against the image-based PLViT (Hehn et al., 2023), Wi-GATr is 3× more accurate on WiPTR (0.41 vs. 1.28 dB) and 6× more accurate on Wi3R (0.74 vs. 4.81 dB), while also being robust to symmetry transformations that PLViT handles only through fixed centering. On the real-world DICHASUS dataset, Wi-GATr surpasses the hybrid Learned Materials and Neural Materials approaches of Hoydis et al. (2023) by >35% in error, and exceeds a calibrated wireless ray tracer by >70%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Geometric Algebra Transformer (GATr) architecture of Brehmer et al. (2023), adapting it to wireless simulation through a novel tokenizer that encodes mesh faces, antenna positions/orientations, and channel statistics into projective geometric algebra (G₃,₀,₁) tokens. It departs from prior fully-learnt wireless surrogates (PLViT, RadioNet) that rely on lossy 2D top-down image representations with CNNs, arguing instead that propagation is inherently a 3D geometric problem requiring oriented surfaces, points, and directions. It also contrasts with hybrid approaches (WiNeRT, Sionna RT, Hoydis et al. 2023) that retain explicit ray-tracing bookkeeping, positioning Wi-GATr as a single-forward-pass neural surrogate that implicitly learns the physics. The diffusion model formulation for probabilistic inference extends the equivariant generative modelling literature (Köhler et al. 2020, Hoogeboom et al. 2022) to the wireless domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Wi-GATr is the first fully-learnt wireless surrogate operating on full 3D geometry, and its E(3)-equivariance yields dramatic robustness to scene symmetries (e.g., 0.41 dB vs. 38.51 dB MAE under rotation on WiPTR), making it a practical tool for scenarios where coordinate frames vary. The real-world validation on DICHASUS (>70% lower error than a calibrated ray tracer) is a strong signal that data-driven surrogates can outperform physics-based simulators even with limited measurements. Key limitations remain: the model requires training data (unlike model-based tracers), is less configurable (antenna patterns are learned implicitly), and inference time scales quadratically with mesh face count. Open questions include scaling to outdoor/urban scenes, handling diverse antenna radiation patterns explicitly, and whether the diffusion-based geometry reconstruction can be made more reliable with multiple measurements.
