## Problem

The projection head in contrastive learning is widely adopted and empirically improves downstream performance, yet its underlying mechanism remains unexplained. Specifically, it is unclear why discarding the projector after pretraining yields better encoder features than the projector features used during training, and what design principles should guide the projector architecture.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252775/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 224×224 for ImageNet-100) and produces encoder features (the representation before the projector) for downstream linear-probing classification. During pretraining, two augmented views of each image are processed through a ResNet-18 backbone followed by a modified projector, and the contrastive loss is computed on the projector features while the encoder features are retained for evaluation.

## Method

The paper establishes information-theoretic lower and upper bounds on the downstream utility I(Y;Z₁) of encoder features, revealing that performance improves as the mutual information I(Z₁;Z₂) between encoder and projector features decreases while I(Z₁;R) (information about the contrastive objective) is preserved. This identifies the projector as an information bottleneck that should filter irrelevant information. Based on this principle, the authors propose two modification strategies: (1) training regularization, which adds a matrix-based mutual information estimator between encoder and projector features as a penalty term to the contrastive loss, and (2) structural regularization, which reduces I(Z₁;Z₂) architecturally via a discretized projector (Finite Scalar Quantization mapping each dimension to L discrete points) or a sparse top-k autoencoder projector that activates only k of d hidden units. Both approaches are validated across SimCLR and Barlow Twins on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet-100, with the sparse projector achieving up to +3.99% linear probing accuracy over baseline.

## Results

Across three datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet-100) and two contrastive frameworks (SimCLR, Barlow Twins), the proposed sparse projector achieves the largest downstream linear-probing gains: +3.87% on CIFAR-100 (SimCLR: 58.12→61.99) and +3.99% on CIFAR-100 (Barlow Twins: 64.16→68.15). The discretized projector yields +2.04% on CIFAR-100 (SimCLR) and +1.72% on ImageNet-100 (Barlow Twins), while the bottleneck training regularizer provides more modest but consistent gains of +0.26% to +2.15% across all settings. All methods use ResNet-18 as the backbone and are evaluated via linear probing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three proposed projector modifications (bottleneck regularizer, discretized projector, sparse projector) against the standard SimCLR (Chen et al., 2020) and Barlow Twins (Zbontar et al., 2021) baselines. All three methods consistently outperform their respective baselines across every dataset-framework combination, with no losses observed. The sparse projector delivers the largest improvements, reaching 61.99% on CIFAR-100 under SimCLR (vs. 58.12% baseline, +3.87%) and 68.15% under Barlow Twins (vs. 64.16%, +3.99%). The discretized projector is second-best on CIFAR-100 (+2.04% SimCLR, +1.02% Barlow Twins) and ImageNet-100 (+1.02% SimCLR, +1.72% Barlow Twins). The bottleneck training regularizer provides the smallest gains (+0.26% to +2.15%), which the authors attribute to its failure to protect I(Z₁;R) while reducing I(Z₁;Z₂), as confirmed by their ablation in Table 5 where a stronger regularizer (λ=0.1) actually degrades accuracy to 50.72%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the information-theoretic analysis of contrastive learning initiated by Tian et al. (2020) and Tan et al. (2023), which established that maximizing I(Z₂;R) improves downstream performance of projector features. The key departure is shifting the analysis to encoder features Z₁, which are what practitioners actually use downstream after discarding the projector—a gap the authors explicitly identify. For practical methods, the paper repurposes Finite Scalar Quantization (Mentzer et al., 2023) and top-k sparse autoencoders (Gao et al., 2024) as projector architectures, and uses the matrix mutual information estimator from Tan et al. (2023) as a training regularizer, all within the SimCLR and Barlow Twins frameworks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The information bottleneck characterization of the projection head provides a principled design criterion that was previously only empirically observed, potentially guiding future projector architectures beyond ad-hoc MLP choices. However, the practical improvements are modest (1–4% linear probing accuracy), experiments are limited to ResNet-18 and three datasets (no full ImageNet, no ViT backbones), and the theoretical bounds rely on the strong assumption of a strict information chain Y→X→Z₁→Z₂→R that may not hold in practice. Open questions include whether the bottleneck principle generalizes to transformer backbones, larger-scale pretraining, and non-contrastive self-supervised paradigms where the projector plays a different role.
