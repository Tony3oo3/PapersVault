## Problem

Standard LLM alignment procedures (RLHF, DPO) require correct answers or high-quality reference outputs to construct preference pairs, but for emerging challenging tasks like theorem proving and structured reasoning, ground-truth answers are often unavailable, prohibitively expensive to annotate, or beyond even SOTA LLMs' capability. This paper asks whether LLMs can reliably distinguish between varying shades of wrong (i.e., rank incorrect answers by degree of wrongness) and whether alignment with such wrong-over-wrong preferences can still improve model capabilities.

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes a question q and m LLM-sampled answers, filters out any correct answers to retain only wrong ones, then constructs pairwise wrong-over-wrong preference pairs (a_c, a_r) where a_c is judged less wrong than a_r. The preference elicitation uses either LLM-as-a-judge prompts (pairwise comparison or 5-point scoring with margin filtering) or non-LLM signals (answer length, self-consistency frequency, token NLL). The final output is a QLoRA-fine-tuned LLaMA3-8B model that, when evaluated on held-out test sets, produces answers that are measurably less wrong (higher proxy score), more correct (higher task accuracy), and better calibrated (lower ECE) than the unaligned base model.

## Method

The approach is a two-phase pipeline. Phase 1 elicits wrong-over-wrong preferences using four methods: (a) pairwise comparison with order-flipping consistency checks to mitigate positional bias, (b) score-based LLM-as-a-judge where the LLM grades each answer on a 0–5 scale and only pairs exceeding an m-percentile score margin M_m are retained, (c) consistency-based scoring via self-consistency repetition frequency, and (d) logits-based scoring via negative log-likelihood. Phase 2 fine-tunes LLaMA3-8B with DPO (via QLoRA) on the synthesized wrong-over-wrong dataset D_WoW, where the DPO objective treats the less-wrong answer as the chosen response and the more-wrong answer as the rejected response. The key empirical finding is that the score-based method with GPT-4o as evaluator and M_10 margin filtering achieves the highest preference accuracy (70.9%), and that alignment on wrong answers alone yields up to 9.0% reduction in wrongness, 7.0% increase in correct answers, and 9.4% improvement in calibration—demonstrating that the model's output distribution shifts toward the correct solution even though no correct answer ever appears in training data.

## Results

On wrong-over-wrong preference elicitation (RQ1), the best method (GPT-4o score-based with M₁₀ margin) achieves 70.9% Acc_WoW, up to 20.9% above random guess, with an average of 55.3% across all method-LLM combinations. On wrong-over-wrong alignment (RQ2), DPO fine-tuning of LLaMA3-8B with elicited preferences yields average improvements of +0.074 in p_wrong (less wrong), +0.045 in accuracy (more correct), and −0.044 in ECE (better calibration) across four datasets, with peak improvements of +0.163, +0.161, and −0.175 respectively on open-ended tasks (Bio Generation, NLGraph). The score-based elicitation method outperforms all alternatives by at least 9.4% in preference accuracy, and open-ended questions benefit substantially more than multiple-choice ones.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary alignment method is DPO, which is compared against four alternative preference optimization methods (IPO, ORPO, SimPO, SPPO) in Figure 4 on NLGraph. DPO is the only method that consistently improves across all six experimental configurations (3 elicitation methods × 2 generator settings); SimPO collapses on all 6 setups, IPO collapses on 4 of 6, and both SPPO and ORPO show negative improvement relative to the unaligned LLaMA3-8B baseline. Among elicitation methods, the score-based LLM-as-a-judge approach (GPT-4o, M₁₀) dominates, achieving 70.9% Acc_WoW versus 62.4% for pairwise comparison with consistency checks, 58.2% for logits-based, and 56.6% for consistency-based. The oracle (ground-truth proxy) preference serves as an upper bound, and the best DPO+score-based setup approaches but does not exceed it, confirming that elicited preferences capture most of the available signal. Mixing generators (using all 3 LLMs' answers) is especially beneficial for right-over-wrong alignment (+55.7% in ΔAcc) but provides no clear advantage for wrong-over-wrong alignment, suggesting self-generated mistakes are the most informative training signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the preference-optimization alignment paradigm (DPO, PPO, RLHF) by removing the assumption that correct answers are available during training. It repurposes LLM-as-a-judge techniques (pairwise comparison, score-based grading) and confidence signals (self-consistency, token NLL) not to identify correct answers but to rank among incorrect ones, creating 'wrong-over-wrong' preference pairs. The work is explicitly positioned as orthogonal to the choice of preference optimization algorithm (DPO, IPO, ORPO, SimPO, SPPO), focusing instead on the data construction problem. It also connects to the easy-to-hard generalization literature by proposing a complementary path for improving LLMs on hard tasks where supervision is unavailable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine bottleneck in LLM development: many emerging tasks (theorem proving, structured reasoning, novel benchmarks) lack ground-truth annotations, making standard right-over-wrong alignment infeasible. The finding that wrong-only alignment can produce more correct answers (up to 7%) and improve calibration (up to 9.4%) suggests a scalable path for capability expansion without expert annotation. However, the approach is limited to tasks with well-defined wrongness gradients (the paper shows it fails on SciBench and MedMCQA), and the reliance on proxy functions for evaluation introduces circularity concerns. Open questions include extending the framework to domains without clear wrongness distinctions, handling subjective or socially sensitive wrongness, and understanding why alignment improvement is only weakly correlated with preference accuracy.
