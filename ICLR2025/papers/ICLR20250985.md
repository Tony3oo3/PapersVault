## Problem

Although KANs have shown empirical success in scientific computing, their theoretical expressiveness relative to MLPs and their training dynamics—particularly spectral bias—remained uncharacterized. Specifically, no prior work established how KAN parameter counts compare to MLP parameter counts for representing the same functions, nor did any theoretical analysis explain why KANs appear to learn high-frequency components more readily than MLPs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250985/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The theoretical results operate on multivariate continuous functions over bounded domains (e.g., [0,1]^d or [-1,1]^d), comparing the number of parameters needed by a KAN (width W, depth L, grid size G, spline degree k) versus an MLP (width W, depth L, ReLU^k activation) to represent the same function. Empirically, inputs include 1D wave signals sampled at 200 points, d-dimensional Gaussian random field samples (N=5000 points in [-1,1]^d), and PDE domains with Dirichlet boundary conditions; outputs are Fourier-magnitude spectra, L2/H1 regression losses, and pointwise PDE solutions.

## Method

The paper establishes two reparameterization theorems: any ReLU^k MLP of width W and depth L can be exactly represented by a KAN of the same width, depth at most 2L, and grid size G=2 (Theorem 3.2), while any KAN (without SiLU) of grid size G requires an MLP of width O(GW^2), inflating parameters by a factor of O(G). For spectral bias, the key insight is that a single-layer KAN is a linear model in its B-spline coefficients, so the least-squares Hessian is a block matrix whose condition number is bounded by Cd (independent of grid size G), in contrast to ReLU MLPs whose Hessian condition number scales as n^4 or n^{2+2k}. This well-conditioning implies gradient descent converges at roughly the same rate across all frequency components. Empirically, the authors validate these predictions on 1D multi-frequency waves, Gaussian random fields at varying smoothness scales, and 1D/2D Poisson equations with high-frequency solutions, using LBFGS and the grid-extension technique for KANs.

## Results

Theoretically, any ReLU^k MLP can be exactly represented by a KAN of comparable size (same width W, depth ≤ 2L, grid size G=2), establishing that KANs are at least as expressive as MLPs. Conversely, representing a KAN with grid size G requires an MLP whose parameter count inflates by a factor of O(G), suggesting KANs are more parameter-efficient for functions with many breakpoints. On Sobolev spaces, very deep KANs achieve an approximation rate of O(P^{-2s/d}) in terms of parameter count P, matching the superconvergence rate of very deep ReLU networks. For spectral bias, the Hessian condition number of a single-layer KAN is bounded by Cd (independent of grid size G), whereas a two-layer ReLU MLP's condition number scales as n⁴, explaining why KANs learn all frequencies more uniformly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to ReLU MLPs following the Rahaman et al. (2019) 1D wave setup, KANs with sufficient grid size and depth learn all frequency components nearly simultaneously, while even very deep and wide MLPs (6 layers, 256 neurons) struggle with higher frequencies even after 10× more training iterations (80,000 vs. 8,000 steps). On the 1D Poisson equation (following Xu et al. 2019a), KANs (2 layers, 10 neurons, grid sizes 20→40) maintain low L² and H¹ losses as frequency increases to k=32, whereas MLPs (6 layers, 256 neurons) become extremely difficult to optimize at k=16 and k=32. In the 2D Poisson experiment, MLPs fail to learn the correct solution shape even at k=4 after 100 LBFGS iterations, while KANs achieve near-ground-truth solutions in just 5 iterations (k=4) and 10 iterations (k=16). On the Gaussian random field task, KANs outperform MLPs on rough functions (small σ) where spectral bias is most detrimental, while MLPs retain an advantage on very smooth functions (large σ). Overall, KANs consistently win on high-frequency and multi-scale tasks but are more susceptible to overfitting on noisy or undersampled data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the original KAN architecture and its KAT approximation theory (Liu et al. 2024e), extending the analysis to a direct theoretical comparison with MLPs via mutual representability results. For spectral bias, it extends the well-developed MLP literature (Rahaman et al. 2019; Hong et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2023) to the KAN setting, providing the first finite-width gradient descent analysis for KANs where prior work (Wang et al. 2024) had only performed empirical NTK analysis in the infinite-width regime. The approximation rates on Sobolev spaces are inherited from deep ReLU network results (Siegel 2023; Yarotsky 2018) via the representability theorem, positioning KANs within the existing superconvergence framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical explanation for why KANs outperform MLPs on high-frequency scientific computing tasks: the single-layer KAN is a linear model whose Hessian is well-conditioned away from a small number of degenerate directions, unlike the ill-conditioned Hessian of ReLU MLPs. A key practical insight is that the reduced spectral bias, while beneficial for PDE solving, makes KANs more susceptible to overfitting on noisy or undersampled data—a trade-off that grid coarsening can mitigate. Open questions include extending the spectral bias theory to deep KANs, determining whether the MLP-to-KAN parameter overhead is tight, and understanding how grid extension interacts with optimization in the deep regime.
