## Problem

Existing imitation learning methods that handle imperfect demonstrations, such as 2IWIL, only leverage positive (optimal) information when reweighting demonstrations, making the discriminator's reward signal biased by the frequency of state-action pairs in the data. When imperfect demonstrations exhibit a preference for a low-confidence action (i.e., it appears more often), 2IWIL effectively inflates its confidence, causing the learned policy to mimic suboptimal behavior. The paper asks how to incorporate the negative (non-optimal) risk of imperfect demonstrations into the GAIL framework so the discriminator can distinguish truly optimal actions from frequently-occurring but suboptimal ones.

## Input / Output

The method consumes imperfect demonstrations as state-action pairs split into a labeled subset D_c (with confidence scores, typically 20% of data) and an unlabeled subset D_u. A semi-supervised classifier first predicts confidence scores for D_u, after which both subsets are jointly used to train the discriminator. The output is a policy π_θ learned via TRPO using the discriminator's reward signal −log D_w(x), evaluated as normalized cumulative return (1.0 = optimal, 0.0 = random) across six MuJoCo/Pendulum control tasks.

## Method

PN-GAIL modifies the GAIL discriminator objective by decomposing the risk over imperfect demonstrations into a positive term (optimal demonstrations, weighted by η) and a negative term (non-optimal demonstrations, weighted by 1−η), yielding the objective max_w E_{p_θ}[log D_w(x)] + E_p[r(x)log(1−D_w(x))] + E_p[(1−r(x))log D_w(x)]. The optimal discriminator takes the closed form D*(x) = ((1−r)p + p_θ)/(p + p_θ), which theoretically ensures the agent policy minimizes 2·JSD(p_θ‖p) − KL(p_θ‖p_1) − (1−η)KL(p_non‖p_1), explicitly driving p_θ away from non-optimal demonstrations. To improve confidence estimation, the paper introduces a balanced semi-conf (BSC) classifier that adds a balancing term α·E_p[ℓ(g(x))] − E_q[α·ℓ(g(x))] to the original SC loss, preventing the classifier from over-predicting confidence for labeled data and under-predicting for unlabeled data. The full pipeline trains the BSC classifier, then alternates discriminator updates (maximizing the positive-negative objective) and policy updates via TRPO.

## Results

PN-GAIL is evaluated on six control tasks (Pendulum-v1, Ant-v2, Walker2d-v2, Hopper-v2, Swimmer-v2, HalfCheetah-v2) using normalized cumulative returns (1.0 = optimal policy, 0.0 = random policy). The main comparison is presented as training curves (Fig. 2) rather than a summary table; PN-GAIL achieves the highest normalized return across all six environments. In Pendulum-v1, where imperfect demonstrations exhibit a strong preference for low-confidence state-action pairs, PN-GAIL reaches a near-optimal policy while 2IWIL and IC-GAIL fail to converge to good performance. The BSC classifier (Table 1) reduces MAE by 40–90% over the original SC classifier (e.g., 0.056 vs. 0.213 on Ant-v2; 0.014 vs. 0.132 on Walker2d-v2), confirming more accurate confidence estimation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PN-GAIL outperforms all seven baselines across the six MuJoCo/Pendulum environments. Against GAIL (Ho & Ermon, 2016), PN-GAIL consistently achieves higher normalized returns because GAIL assigns equal weight to all demonstrations and cannot distinguish optimal from non-optimal ones. Against 2IWIL and IC-GAIL (Wu et al., 2019), the gap is most pronounced in Pendulum-v1, where 2IWIL's reweighting is distorted by the preference for frequently occurring low-confidence state-action pairs, causing it to learn a poor policy while PN-GAIL reaches near-optimal performance. Against WGAIL (Wang et al., 2021), PN-GAIL wins in all environments; notably, WGAIL actually underperforms GAIL in Walker2d-v2 because its assumption of a high proportion of optimal demonstrations is violated. In the appendix (Fig. 5), PN-GAIL also surpasses the ranking-based methods T-REX (Brown et al., 2019), D-REX (Brown et al., 2020), CAIL (Zhang et al., 2021), and f-IRL (Ni et al., 2021) on Pendulum-v1 and Ant-v2. The ablation (Fig. 3) confirms that both the positive-negative discriminator (removing it degrades to 2IWIL-level performance) and the BSC classifier (removing it degrades to SC-level performance) contribute meaningfully to the final result.

## Relation to Prior Work

PN-GAIL extends the GAIL framework (Ho & Ermon, 2016) by building directly on 2IWIL (Wu et al., 2019), the first method to incorporate confidence-weighted reweighting into adversarial imitation learning. The key conceptual departure is that 2IWIL's discriminator only accounts for positive risk (amplifying optimal demonstrations), while PN-GAIL incorporates both positive and negative risk, drawing on the PU-learning formulation of Xu & Denil (2021) to structure the discriminator's objective. The paper also refines 2IWIL's semi-conf (SC) classifier into a balanced semi-conf (BSC) classifier to correct a systematic bias in confidence estimation. Within the broader landscape of imperfect-demonstration methods, PN-GAIL belongs to the weighting-based family, which the authors argue is more computationally efficient and flexible than ranking-based alternatives like T-REX or D-REX.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation is strong: real-world demonstrations from human experts are rarely optimal due to fatigue, distraction, or variability, and existing GAIL-based methods degrade significantly under such conditions. PN-GAIL's requirement of only a small labeled subset (20% in experiments) for confidence scores makes it more deployable than methods requiring full trajectory rankings or access to true reward functions. However, the evaluation is limited to six low-dimensional MuJoCo/Pendulum control tasks, and the method still presupposes that confidence scores (even if partially labeled) are available, which may not hold in all practical settings. Open questions include whether the approach extends to high-dimensional or visual domains, whether it can operate without any confidence labels, and how it interacts with offline imitation learning constraints.
