## Problem

The renormalisation theory for Bayesian neural networks was established only for linear architectures in the linear-width regime, leaving open whether it extends to nonlinear (e.g., ReLU) networks and how it should be adapted to sublinear-width regimes where empirical discrepancies have been observed. Additionally, no unified framework connected the kernel-theoretic (NNGP) perspective with the statistical-mechanics (random matrix) perspective on BNN generalisation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a training set (X, y) of P examples, a fully-connected network architecture with L layers of width N and input dimension N₀, and a test point x*. It produces the mean and variance of the Bayesian predictor at x* as integral expressions over the limiting spectral measure of the modified NNGP kernel. In the linear-width regime the spectral measure is explicitly the L-fold Marchenko-Pastur composition ρ_MP^α ⊠^L ρ_NNGP^α₀; in the sublinear-width regime only the strictly positive support of the spectral distribution is used, since the full distribution collapses to a Dirac mass at zero.

## Method

The paper establishes that training a (nonlinear) BNN in the linear-width or sublinear-width limit yields a predictor with identical mean and variance to GP regression under a 'modified NNGP kernel' whose Mercer spectrum is characterised by a nonrandom limiting spectral measure. By invoking the correspondence between Mercer eigenvalues and the limiting spectral distribution of the empirical kernel matrix (via Baker's theorem), the authors show that eigenvalues can be sampled independently of eigenfunctions, enabling integral formulas (Theorem 3.4) for the predictor's first two moments that depend only on the spectral measure and the spectral universality assumption (SUA) for eigenfunctions. A key result (Theorem 3.5) proves that the renormalisation theory extends to nonlinear BNNs if and only if the SUA holds, providing a necessary-and-sufficient criterion linking kernel-theoretic and statistical-mechanics frameworks. In the sublinear-width regime where the kernel matrix becomes degenerate and renormalisation breaks, the authors retain the integral estimators by restricting to the strictly positive support of the spectral distribution, yielding a practical mechanism for estimating predictor statistics validated against variational-inference BNNs (Pyro) on synthetic and MNIST data.

## Results

In the linear-width regime (Figure 1), the paper's integral estimators for the predictor mean and variance align closely with the renormalisation theory predictions (fixed-point equation) for both a linear network (N₀=500, P=200) and a nonlinear ReLU network on a MNIST subset (P=105, N₀=784, N=10000 for NNGP kernel estimation). In the sublinear-width regime (Figure 2, P=200, N₀=40), where the renormalisation theory breaks down, the integral estimates (using the strictly positive support of the empirical spectral distribution) provide reasonable matches to the actual mean and variance of BNN predictions obtained via variational inference (Pyro), outperforming the renormalisation theory which becomes inaccurate at high α and α₀.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the renormalisation theory of Li & Sompolinsky (2021), which predicts that the BNN predictor variance equals that of Bayesian linear regression (or NNGP regression) up to a renormalisation factor computed via a fixed-point equation. In the linear-width regime, the paper's integral estimators reproduce the renormalisation theory predictions, confirming the theory's validity for nonlinear ReLU networks under the spectral universality assumption. In the sublinear-width regime (P ∝ N·N₀), the renormalisation theory becomes inaccurate because the kernel matrix becomes degenerate (limiting spectral distribution collapses to a Dirac at 0), and the paper's integral estimators—using only the strictly positive support of the spectral distribution—provide substantially better estimates of the predictor mean and variance compared to what the renormalisation theory would predict. The comparison with variational inference (Pyro) in the sublinear regime shows the integral estimates track the actual BNN predictions reasonably well, though discrepancies remain due to finite-size effects and potential inaccuracy of the SUA.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate theoretical frameworks for analyzing BNNs: the kernel/GP perspective (NNGP limit, Lee et al. 2018) and the statistical mechanics perspective (linear-width limit, Li & Sompolinsky 2021). It builds directly on El Harzli et al. (2024) for the limiting spectral measures of NNGP kernel matrices and on the Marchenko-Pastur machinery from random matrix theory to compose these measures across layers. The key conceptual move is identifying Mercer's eigenvalues of the random kernel with the limiting spectral distribution of the empirical kernel matrix, which allows eigenvalues to be sampled independently and yields integral formulas for predictor statistics. This framework then provides necessary and sufficient conditions (via the spectral universality assumption) for extending the linear-BNN renormalisation theory to nonlinear networks, and a new estimation procedure for the sublinear-width regime where that theory breaks down.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous characterization of when renormalisation theory applies to nonlinear BNNs (iff the SUA holds), resolving an open question raised by Li & Sompolinsky (2021) and Ariosto et al. (2023). The integral estimators offer a practical tool for the sublinear-width regime where existing theory is known to be inaccurate, though the empirical validation is limited to a synthetic Gaussian dataset and a small MNIST subset. Open questions include: characterizing the limiting spectral measure in the sublinear-width regime, whether the kernel-theoretic approach can capture (strong) feature learning, and the precise conditions under which the SUA holds for specific architectures and data distributions. The framework also suggests that progress on SUA accuracy from the kernel regression literature (Liu et al. 2021, Lu & Yau 2023) can be directly transferred to BNN analysis.
