## Problem

Conventional neural networks perform pixel-wise classification without explicitly leveraging contextual semantic information (object density, spatial location, morphological form) during inference, and they lack a mechanism to conditionally activate or deactivate synaptic connections analogous to the brain's connectome. This limits their ability to adapt structure to the specific contextual distribution of an input image, causing performance degradation across domains with varying object densities and spatial arrangements.

## Input / Output

The SMN takes a single RGB image (512×512 in experiments) and produces a pixel-wise softmax segmentation map with C category probabilities per pixel. As auxiliary outputs, it generates per-category density scalars (proportion of image area occupied by each class) and spatial entropy-maps. During the TTA inference phase, only a small C×N matrix M is optimized (typically ≤5 steps), keeping the full encoder-decoder frozen, which yields 32.8 FPS with 47.5M parameters.

## Method

The SMN combines a standard encoder-decoder segmentation pipeline with a density-regression head that produces Class Activation Maps (CAMs) per category. A novel control neuron replaces each pixel in the decoder's feature maps: it receives inter-neuronal inputs, a control signal (a trainable linear combination of predicted densities via matrix M), and a self-activation gate, and outputs the input signal only when the control signal exceeds a trainable threshold OR the neuron is among the top-k% most informative elements (both conditions approximated via sigmoid functions of Heaviside steps). An entropy-map reconstruction algorithm converts stacked CAMs into a pseudo-entropy-map via per-pixel softmax normalization, which is then matched against the segmentation pipeline's own entropy-map using an SSIM loss (L2). During training, three losses (cross-entropy, SSIM entropy-map matching, and density regression) are jointly optimized; during inference, only matrix M is fine-tuned via L2 in a self-supervised TTA step, dynamically reconfiguring which control neurons fire and thus transforming the network's effective architecture to match the input's contextual semantic distribution.

## Results

On six semantic segmentation benchmarks, the SMN achieves the best IoU across all datasets: 72.72% on Inria, 54.28% on LoveDA, 55.76% on ADE20K, 88.66% on Youtube-VOS, 48.83% on BDD100K, and 76.58% on GTA5. The SMN provides an average IoU improvement of 6.36% over the best competing method, with a maximum improvement of 13.15% (on BDD100K). The self-activation path in the control neuron contributes up to 7.51% additional IoU, confirming its critical role in mitigating feature sparsity. The SMN operates at 32.8 FPS with 47.5M parameters and 549.8G FLOPs, comparable to or faster than several heavier baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The SMN outperforms all eight baselines on every dataset. The closest competitor is AOST (a VOS model), which leads the field on Inria (69.30%), LoveDA (50.57%), ADE20K (52.06%), Youtube-VOS (87.09%), BDD100K (43.09%), and GTA5 (71.06%); the SMN surpasses AOST by 3.42%, 3.71%, 3.70%, 1.57%, 5.74%, and 5.52% IoU respectively. Among segmentation-specific SotA models, InternImage is the strongest on BDD100K (47.25%) and Inria (68.60%), while BEiT-3 leads on Youtube-VOS (86.67%); the SMN exceeds both by 1.58–5.74% IoU. The multi-path models (LADDERNet, MPDNet) serve as ensemble-like baselines but are consistently outperformed, with LADDERNet's best result (86.04% on Youtube-VOS) still trailing the SMN by 2.62%. Baseline architectures U-Net and SegFormer lag the SMN by 5–12% IoU across datasets, confirming that the structural adaptation mechanism provides gains beyond simply scaling model capacity. The ablation variant Ours-SA (without self-activation) drops by up to 7.51% IoU, demonstrating that the self-activation gating is essential for the SMN's advantage over all baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Class Activation Maps (Zhou et al., 2016) by repurposing them as a self-supervised signal for test-time adaptation, and on the stochastic adaptive activation function (Lee et al., 2022) to derive its self-activation gating mechanism. Conceptually, it departs from standard test-time adaptation (TTA) methods that only fine-tune network parameters by proposing a 'structural' adaptation where a small control matrix (C×N) dynamically gates neuron outputs, framing this as connectome emulation. It also positions itself against ensemble models as a single-network alternative that stores multiple domain-specific 'shapes' within one architecture, and against pixel-wise segmentation approaches by explicitly incorporating density-based contextual semantic information as a pretext task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core idea of using predicted class densities as a control signal to dynamically reconfigure network connectivity during inference is an interesting middle ground between static architectures and full ensemble models, potentially offering domain robustness without the memory overhead of multiple networks. However, the 'structural transformation' is in practice the optimization of a small C×N gating matrix, which is closer to learned dynamic routing than true architectural mutation, and the connectome-emulation framing may overstate the biological fidelity. Open questions include whether this approach scales to very large category counts (e.g., 150+ in ADE20K), whether the TTA convergence is reliable across diverse test distributions, and whether the density-based pretext task generalizes to domains where object proportions are less informative (e.g., medical imaging with rare pathologies).
