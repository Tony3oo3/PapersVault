## Problem

While multi-modal Transformer models have demonstrated impressive brain alignment when participants view unimodal stimuli (images, silent videos), it remains unclear how accurately these models predict fMRI activity during true multi-modal stimulus engagement (movies with audio). Specifically, it is unknown whether cross-modal or jointly pretrained multi-modal architectures better capture the brain's multi-modal information processing, and how much of the observed alignment is attributable to each individual modality versus genuinely integrated multi-modal information.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes 1.49-second video and audio segments (matching the fMRI TR rate) from the Movie10 dataset, feeding them simultaneously into multi-modal models (ImageBind, TVLT) or separately into unimodal encoders (ViT-B, VideoMAE, ViViT for video; Wav2Vec2.0, AST for audio). Speech features are extracted with a 16-second context window and downsampled via a 3-lobed Lanczos filter to align with the 1.49s acquisition rate. The encoding model outputs per-voxel fMRI predictions, evaluated as normalized brain alignment (Pearson correlation divided by cross-subject prediction accuracy ceiling), and the residual analysis additionally produces residual representations after linearly regressing out unimodal features from multi-modal embeddings.

## Method

The paper trains voxel-wise bootstrap ridge regression encoding models with a 5-tap finite impulse response (FIR) hemodynamic response function to predict fMRI activity from stimulus representations extracted at the last layer of pretrained Transformer encoders. Two multi-modal model types are compared: ImageBind (cross-modal, using separate ViT and AST encoders trained with contrastive binding across six modalities) and TVLT (jointly pretrained, fusing video and spectrogram tokens within a single 12-layer masked autoencoding Transformer). The key analytical contribution is a residual approach: a linear ridge regression model maps unimodal features (e.g., VideoMAE embeddings) onto the multi-modal representation space, and the residual (multi-modal minus projected unimodal) is then used as input to a new encoding model to quantify how much brain alignment is attributable to that specific modality. This is applied separately for video and audio features across both multi-modal model types, revealing that cross-modal alignment is partially explained by video features alone, while jointly pretrained alignment is partially explained by both video and audio features. Statistical significance is assessed via block permutation tests (10 contiguous TRs, 5000 permutations) and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction.

## Results

On the Movie10 fMRI dataset (6 participants watching movies with audio), both cross-modal (ImageBind/IB Concat) and jointly pretrained (TVLT Joint) multi-modal models achieve approximately 50% normalized brain alignment in high-level visual regions (PPA, MT) and significantly outperform unimodal video models in language regions (AG, PCC, PTL, IFG). Compared to unimodal speech models, all multi-modal embeddings show significantly better alignment except in the LOC (object visual processing) region. Residual analysis reveals that removing unimodal video features causes a 40–50% drop in brain alignment for IB Concat in visual regions, while for TVLT Joint the drop appears in language regions (PTL, MFG), indicating that cross-modal models rely more heavily on video information whereas jointly pretrained models integrate both modalities more evenly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the three unimodal video models (ViT-B, VideoMAE, ViViT), both multi-modal models (IB Concat and TVLT Joint) achieve significantly higher normalized brain alignment in language regions (AG, PCC, PTL, IFG) but show no significant advantage in visual regions when averaged, with the exception that TVLT video embeddings improve alignment in EVC. Against the two unimodal speech models (Wav2Vec2.0, AST), all multi-modal embeddings significantly outperform in every region except LOC, where unimodal video models retain an advantage. Between the two multi-modal architectures, IB Concat (cross-modal) significantly outperforms TVLT Joint in semantic regions AG and PCC and the multi-modal processing region MT, while TVLT Joint significantly outperforms IB Concat in dmPFC; at the whole-brain and averaged language/visual level, no significant difference is detected. Residual analysis further differentiates the two: removing video features from IB Concat causes a large alignment drop (40–50%) in visual regions, whereas removing either video or audio features from TVLT Joint causes only a small, balanced drop, indicating that the jointly pretrained model captures more modality-agnostic semantic information.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the brain encoding literature, which has predominantly used unimodal stimuli (images, silent videos, text) paired with unimodal pretrained models, to the setting of true multi-modal naturalistic stimuli (movies with audio). It builds directly on the residual analysis framework of Toneva et al. (2022) and Oota et al. (2023a,b) for probing modality-specific contributions, and on the closest prior work by Dong & Toneva (2023b) who explored video+audio brain alignment with a single jointly pretrained model. The key departure is the systematic comparison of two architecturally distinct multi-modal model families—cross-modal (ImageBind) versus jointly pretrained (TVLT)—under identical multi-modal stimulus conditions, which no prior study had performed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first evidence that the type of multi-modal pretraining (cross-modal vs. joint) differentially shapes which brain regions are predicted and how modality-specific information contributes to alignment, with cross-modal models relying predominantly on video features and jointly pretrained models drawing on both modalities. The finding that multi-modal models improve alignment in language and semantic regions but not in early visual regions (relative to unimodal video) suggests that multi-modal integration is relevant primarily to higher-level processing. However, the low absolute alignment scores (~40–50%), the small subject pool (n=6), and the limited model cohort (one cross-modal, one joint) leave open whether these findings generalize to larger, more diverse multi-modal model families, and whether fine-tuning or MLLM architectures would substantially improve brain alignment.
