## Problem

Computing large kernel sums s_m = Σ_n w_n K(x_n, y_m) naively costs O(MN), and existing fast approximations face a trade-off: random Fourier features achieve O(1/√D) error but only for positive definite kernels, while slicing extends to non-positive-definite kernels (e.g., negative distance, thin plate spline) but still suffers from the same O(1/√P) Monte Carlo rate when directions are drawn uniformly. The paper asks whether structured quasi-Monte Carlo directions on the sphere can break this rate barrier for slicing.

## Input / Output

The method consumes N source points x_n, M query points y_m in R^d, scalar weights w_n, and kernel hyperparameters (σ, α, β) set by the median rule. It produces the vector s = (s_1, …, s_M) of approximated kernel sums. A key practical detail is that the QMC direction set ξ^P ∈ (S^{d-1})^P is precomputed once (independent of the dataset and kernel) and then reused; the per-evaluation cost is O(P(M+N+N_ft log N_ft)) where N_ft is the number of relevant Fourier coefficients for the one-dimensional NFFT-based summation.

## Method

The approach represents the radial kernel basis F(||x||) as an expectation over one-dimensional projections f(|⟨ξ,x⟩|) via the generalized Riemann-Liouville fractional integral transform, then approximates this expectation by a quadrature sum over P directions on S^{d-1}. The key innovation is replacing iid uniform directions with QMC sequences—specifically distance QMC designs that minimize a symmetric energy functional E_sym(ξ^P) = -Σ_{p,q}(||ξ_p - ξ_q|| + ||ξ_p + ξ_q||)—to exploit the symmetry g_x(ξ) = g_x(-ξ) of the integrand. The paper proves that for Gauss and Matérn kernels, the spherical function g_x(ξ) = f(|⟨ξ,x⟩|) belongs to H^s(S^{d-1}) for s below a kernel-dependent threshold, yielding worst-case QMC error bounds of O(P^{-s/(d-1)}) that improve upon the O(1/√P) Monte Carlo rate. Each one-dimensional sum is then evaluated in O(M+N) or O((M+N)log(M+N)) time via non-equispaced fast Fourier transforms (NFFT) or sorting, giving an overall complexity of O(P(M+N+N_ft log N_ft)) independent of the ambient dimension d.

## Results

The Distance QMC slicing method achieves convergence rates of 2.10 (d=3), 1.38 (d=10), and 0.78 (d=50) for the Gauss kernel, compared to 0.50 for standard RFF and non-QMC slicing. On real datasets (Letters d=16, MNIST d=20, FashionMNIST d=30), QMC Fourier-slicing produces significantly smaller relative L¹ kernel summation error than all competing methods at comparable computation times, with the smallest standard deviation. The advantage is most pronounced in low-to-moderate dimensions (d≤100) but persists even at d=50 where the rate still exceeds 0.70 versus 0.50 for random methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Distance QMC slicing dominates all four baselines across every kernel and dimension tested. Against standard RFF (rate 0.50) and non-QMC slicing (rate 0.50), the improvement is dramatic: 2.10× faster convergence at d=3 and still 1.56× faster at d=50. Against QMC-RFF (Sobol RFF, rate 0.98 at d=3), Distance QMC slicing more than doubles the convergence rate (2.10 vs. 0.98), and this gap widens in higher dimensions (1.38 vs. 0.86 at d=10). Orthogonal Random Features (ORF) perform marginally better than RFF at d=50 (0.67 vs. 0.50) but remain far below Distance QMC (0.78). The only method that slightly outperforms Distance QMC is the spherical t-design (rate 2.24 vs. 2.10 for Matérn ν=1.5 at d=3), but spherical designs are only computable for d≤4 and become intractable in higher dimensions, making Distance QMC the best practical choice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the slicing approach for fast kernel summation (Hertrich, 2024) by replacing uniformly random projection directions with quasi-Monte Carlo sequences on the sphere, drawing on the QMC design theory of Brauchart et al. (2014). It is positioned as a natural counterpart to QMC random Fourier features (Avron et al., 2016), but unlike QMC-RFF it does not require the Bochner measure to decouple across dimensions, making it applicable to non-positive-definite kernels like the negative distance kernel and thin plate spline. The one-dimensional kernel sums are computed via non-equispaced fast Fourier transforms, connecting the method to the broader fast Fourier summation literature (Potts et al., 2004; Kunis et al., 2006).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The QMC slicing approach improves the error rate from O(1/√P) to O(P^{-s/(d-1)}) for smooth kernels, which is a meaningful gain for applications requiring high accuracy (e.g., MMD gradient flows, kernel density estimation). However, the practical advantage diminishes in high dimensions (d > 100), and the computation of distance QMC directions can be expensive (up to an hour for d=200, P≈5000). A key open question is closing the gap between the proven worst-case rates and the significantly faster empirical convergence observed numerically, which the authors suggest may be addressed by studying worst-case errors for symmetric functions on the sphere. The method also remains untested in large-scale end-to-end learning pipelines beyond the MMD gradient flow experiments in the appendix.
