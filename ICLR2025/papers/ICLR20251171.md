## Problem

Existing distribution-shift methods (DRO, invariant learning, Shapley-based decomposition) are designed for static data and cannot capture the unique 'shifts in non-stationary properties' that arise in time series, where trends, seasonality, and temporal dynamics themselves evolve between populations. The paper asks: how can we attribute a deployed model's performance degradation between two populations to specific temporal data properties in a principled, interpretable, and theoretically grounded manner?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251171/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes raw multi-variate time series (e.g., 53 hourly-sampled ICU health indexes) and static demographic features from two populations (training P and target Q), along with the fixed deployed model's per-sample prediction errors. It first extracts 17 temporal property metrics (averages, standardized slopes, Savitzky-Golay smoothed trends, FFT dominant frequencies, SNR, short-term variability, high-frequency energy, jitter index, RSI, KPSS p-values, Bollinger-band breakout counts, PELT change points, trend variability, and covariance variability) to form an augmented feature vector X̃. The output is a per-property attribution score quantifying each feature's contribution to the P→Q performance gap, plus interpretable decision-tree rules delineating safe and risk deployment regions.

## Method

TSSA operates in two stages. First, it characterizes temporal properties via 17 metrics spanning global statistics, local dynamics, structural changes, and inter-series relationships, and evaluates their quality through a sufficiency measure (minimum loss of predicting the deployed model's error from X̃). Second, it attributes the performance gap E_Q[ℓ]−E_P[ℓ] to each property S by defining conditional risks R_P(X̃_{−S}) and R_Q(X̃_{−S}) that hold all other features fixed, then estimating the difference E[R_Q−R_P] with an Augmented IPW (AIPW) doubly robust estimator that remains consistent if either the conditional-risk predictors or the propensity score is correctly specified. To handle scarce target-population data, the three nuisance functions (μ_P, μ_Q, π) are jointly learned in a shared neural-network representation space with separate prediction heads, and the paper proves unconfoundedness, double robustness, and √n-consistency under o_P(n^{−1/4})-rate nuisance estimation.

## Results

In Case Study 2 (age shift on MIMIC-III), the TSSA-guided intervention on the top attributed feature (Age) improves test accuracy from 73.8% to 83.9% and Macro-F1 from 63.7% to 68.4%, substantially outperforming simple finetuning (77.0% accuracy, 65.7% Macro-F1). In Case Study 1, the temporal-property-based region analysis identifies a safe region with 3.7% error rate (vs. 8.8% for the static baseline) and a risk region with 52.0% error rate (vs. 36.7% for static), while covering 296 risk-region samples versus only 30 for the static method. In Case Study 4 (ventilator prediction), excluding temporal shifts reduces the performance drop from 12.4pp to just 0.6pp in accuracy, confirming that temporal properties—not demographics—drive the degradation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares TSSA against two baselines. First, against the static region analysis method of Liu et al. (2023a) (Table 3): TSSA's temporal-property-based region analysis achieves a 3.7% safe-region error rate versus 8.8% for the static method, and a 52.0% risk-region error rate versus 36.7%, while identifying 296 risk-region samples compared to only 30 for the static approach—indicating both better discrimination and greater coverage. Second, against simple finetuning (Table 4): the TSSA-guided intervention of reweighting training data by the inverse density ratio of the top attributed feature (Age) yields 83.9% accuracy and 68.4% Macro-F1, outperforming finetuning (77.0% / 65.7%) by 6.9pp in accuracy and 2.7pp in Macro-F1. The ICU Stay Time intervention achieves the best overall result (84.3% / 69.3%), further validating that the attribution correctly identifies actionable features. The paper does not compare against other attribution methods (e.g., Shapley-based approaches) empirically, noting instead that its framework is compatible with Shapley values as a future extension.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the performance-drop attribution framework of Cai et al. (2023) and Feng et al. (2024) from static features to time-series data by introducing temporal property metrics (trends, non-stationarity, structural changes, inter-series relationships) as the units of attribution. The estimation machinery is borrowed from causal inference: the doubly robust AIPW estimator (Robins et al., 1994) is repurposed to attribute risk differences between populations, and the shared-representation neural architecture follows Shi et al. (2019) for sample-efficient estimation when target data is scarce. The paper explicitly departs from DRO and invariant learning approaches that impose structural assumptions without empirical validation, instead advocating an inductive, data-centric diagnosis before intervention.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TSSA provides a principled, interpretable diagnostic layer between model deployment and intervention, enabling clinicians and ML engineers to identify *which* temporal properties drive failure and act accordingly (data balancing, targeted feature collection, safe/risk region gating). The sufficiency measure and its theoretical link to attribution error (Proposition 2) offer a data-driven criterion for metric selection that is absent in prior time-series shift work. Open questions include scalability to very long or high-frequency series, extension to forecasting (rather than classification) tasks, formal treatment of missing data, and how to optimally select or learn the metric set beyond the hand-crafted desiderata.
