## Problem

Existing reasoning benchmarks (MMLU, GSM8K, FOLIO, etc.) conflate genuine rule-following reasoning with recall of memorized domain-specific patterns from pre-training data, making it impossible to isolate a model's true ability to understand and apply novel rules. The paper asks: can we design evaluation tasks where correctness depends on understanding explicitly stated rules rather than retrieving pre-trained knowledge, thereby measuring out-of-distribution reasoning in a controlled way?

## Input / Output

Each problem instance pairs a newly defined rule description (ranging from ~51 to ~4,572 characters depending on category) with a rule-driven question. The model must produce an answer in one of five prescribed formats: Numerical Response, Mathematical Expression, Textual Response, Multiple Choice, or Structured Data. Chat models receive zero-shot prompts (rule + question only), while base models receive three-shot prompts with generic Q&A examples. Evaluation extracts answers via regex on bracketed output, uses SymPy for algebraic equivalence checking, and applies custom parsing for multi-answer, inequality, and unordered-list cases. The benchmark comprises 1,250 questions (5 categories × 25 rules × 10 questions each).

## Method

KOR-Bench operationalizes 'Knowledge Orthogonality' through a formal condition set: the rule R must be logically independent of background knowledge K (R ⊥ K), K's influence is bounded by a Knowledge Impact Factor β ∈ (−1, ε], and correctness must be dominated by R over K. The benchmark is organized into five categories, each introducing a distinct 'new element'—new symbols (Operation), new logical concepts (Logic), new execution rules (Cipher), new problem-solving frameworks (Puzzle), and new story contexts (Counterfactual)—with 25 manually crafted rules per category deliberately modified (e.g., altered substitution tables, non-standard operator definitions, fictional world settings) to avoid overlap with pre-training data. Data construction follows three phases: Rule Design (extraction and redefinition), Rule-Driven Q&A Design (manual crafting or programmatic generation with format specification), and Quality Validation (human review plus LLM-based difficulty filtering). Beyond the core benchmark, the paper contributes a stepwise prompting decomposition of cipher sub-steps (identifying spatial operations like Rotation and Conditional Filling as ~100% error bottlenecks), a self-correction protocol showing ~10.36% average accuracy gain with diminishing returns after two rounds, and a complex task processing suite (Multi-Q, Multi-R, Multi-RQ) that tests rule-switching and multi-problem robustness.

## Results

On KOR-Bench, O1-Preview achieves the highest overall accuracy at 72.88%, followed by O1-Mini at 70.16%, while Claude-3.5-Sonnet and GPT-4o score 58.96% and 58.00% respectively. Performance varies dramatically across task categories: Counterfactual reasoning is the easiest (80–93% for top models), while Puzzle (16.80% for GPT-4o, 36.80% for O1-Preview) and Cipher (42.80% for GPT-4o, 82.80% for O1-Preview) are the most challenging. Among base models, Meta-Llama-3.1-405B leads at 39.68% overall, with a notable drop from its chat counterpart (55.36%) primarily in Operation and Cipher tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

KOR-Bench evaluates 40+ models, revealing clear performance stratification. The O1 series (O1-Preview, O1-Mini) dominates Cipher and Puzzle tasks by a wide margin (82.80%/79.60% vs. 42.80% for GPT-4o on Cipher; 36.80%/35.60% vs. 16.80% for GPT-4o on Puzzle), suggesting their extended reasoning chains are critical for multi-step rule application. Claude-3.5-Sonnet leads in Logic reasoning (67.20%) and is competitive in Operation (88.40%), while GPT-4o outperforms it in Cipher (42.80% vs. 33.20%) and Puzzle (16.80% vs. 14.80%). Among open-weight models, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct (54.72%) outperforms the larger Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct (52.16%), indicating that model size alone does not guarantee better rule-following. All models perform best on Counterfactual reasoning (80–93%), suggesting that literal comprehension within fictional contexts is easier than applying novel mathematical or cryptographic rules. The gap between chat and base models is most pronounced in Operation and Cipher, while Logic shows the smallest degradation, likely due to its shallower inference depth.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing a formal 'knowledge orthogonality' constraint (R ⊥ K) that explicitly decouples task rules from pre-training knowledge, a criterion absent from prior reasoning benchmarks. It positions itself against knowledge-dependent evaluations (MMLU, GSM8K, GPQA) that conflate recall with reasoning, and extends rule-following benchmarks (RuleBench, LOGICGAME, PuzzleBench) by ensuring all 125 rules are manually designed to be absent from common pre-training corpora. The five task categories draw on existing puzzle and cipher traditions (Braingle, dCode, logic textbooks) but systematically re-parameterize them with novel symbols, operators, and story contexts to enforce the orthogonality property.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

KOR-Bench addresses a fundamental confound in reasoning evaluation: whether models are genuinely reasoning or retrieving memorized patterns. The finding that spatial sub-steps (Rotation, Conditional Filling, Conditional Reading) yield near-100% error rates in Cipher tasks, and that even frontier non-O1 models cap at ~43% on Cipher and ~17% on Puzzle, reveals concrete bottlenecks that prior benchmarks masked. Open questions include whether the 1,250-question scale is sufficient for stable estimates, whether the orthogonality constraint generalizes to multimodal settings, and whether the O1 series' advantage reflects genuine reasoning gains or simply longer chain-of-thought computation. The inverse-scaling observation (Qwen2.5-32B > 72B) also raises questions about whether larger models overfit to in-domain patterns that hurt OOD rule application.
