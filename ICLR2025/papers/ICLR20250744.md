## Problem

Existing action-constrained reinforcement learning (ACRL) methods achieve zero constraint violation but at the cost of either solving quadratic programs (computationally expensive and scaling poorly to high-dimensional action spaces) or training sophisticated generative models like Normalizing Flows (adding architectural complexity and pre-training overhead). The paper asks whether a generic, computationally lightweight framework can adapt any standard unconstrained RL algorithm to ACRL without QPs or generative models while maintaining fast training and high valid action rates.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250744/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a state vector from the environment and produces a feasible action guaranteed to lie within the constraint set C(s), which can have arbitrary (non-convex) structure. The policy network outputs an unconstrained action distribution (e.g., Gaussian mean and std), and the acceptance-rejection mechanism filters samples to yield only feasible actions. In the augmented MDP formulation, each transition carries a 2-dimensional reward vector [r(s,a), c(s,a)] where the second component is a penalty for infeasible actions, and infeasible transitions trigger self-loops rather than environment dynamics.

## Method

ARAM augments a standard off-policy RL algorithm (SAC) with two modifications: (1) an acceptance-rejection method that uses the unconstrained policy as a proposal distribution and accepts only actions in the feasible set C(s), eliminating the need for QP solvers or generative models; and (2) an Augmented Unconstrained Two-Objective MDP (AUTO-MDP) that adds self-loop transitions and a penalty signal for rejected actions, incentivizing the policy to concentrate mass near the feasible set and thereby improving the ARM acceptance rate over training. The paper proves that AUTO-MDP and the original constrained MDP share the same set of optimal policies. To avoid hyperparameter tuning of the penalty weight, ARAM employs multi-objective SAC (MOSAC/Q-Pensieve) to learn policies for all preference vectors simultaneously, with a dual-buffer design separating feasible and augmented infeasible transitions for balanced updates.

## Results

Across 8 environments (5 MuJoCo locomotion tasks and 3 resource allocation tasks), ARAM achieves valid action rates of 0.99–1.00 on Hopper, Reacher, and Ant, and 0.94 on NSFnet, matching or exceeding all baselines. ARAM requires 2–5 orders of magnitude fewer QP operations than projection-based or Frank-Wolfe-based methods. On per-action inference time (1M actions), ARAM is the fastest: 44.72 s on Reacher vs. 71.47 s (DPre+), 303.01 s (SPre+), 67.43 s (NFWPO), and 71.47 s (FlowPG). ARAM also achieves the highest evaluation returns in most environments, e.g., 3.07×10³ on Hopper vs. 2.27×10³ for FOCOPS, and 8.38×10³ on HalfCheetah vs. 6.08×10³ for FOCOPS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DPre+ and SPre+ (QP-based projection methods), ARAM achieves substantially higher valid action rates in most environments (e.g., 0.98 vs. 0.25 on Reacher, 1.00 vs. 0.41 on Ant, 0.94 vs. 0.00 on NSFnet) and lower inference time (44.72 s vs. 71.47 s on Reacher), while also avoiding the zero-gradient issue that plagues differentiable projection layers. Against NFWPO (Frank-Wolfe search), ARAM matches or slightly exceeds valid action rates (0.99 vs. 0.99 on Hopper, 0.98 vs. 0.97 on Reacher) but requires 2–5 orders of magnitude fewer QP operations and achieves faster wall-clock training. Against FlowPG (Normalizing Flow-based), ARAM wins on valid action rate in most environments (1.00 vs. 0.83 on Ant, 0.98 vs. 0.95 on Reacher) and avoids the need for a pre-trained generative model, yielding lower inference time (44.72 s vs. 71.47 s on Reacher). Against FOCOPS (CMDP-based with projection), ARAM dominates on both evaluation return (e.g., 3.07×10³ vs. 2.27×10³ on Hopper, 8.38×10³ vs. 6.08×10³ on HalfCheetah) and valid action rate (e.g., 0.99 vs. 0.22 on Hopper, 1.00 vs. 0.26 on Ant), confirming that per-step action constraints require fundamentally different solutions than long-term cost constraints.

## Relation to Prior Work

ARAM builds on SAC as its backbone RL algorithm and on Q-Pensieve/MOSAC for the multi-objective implementation, but departs fundamentally from the dominant ACRL paradigm of enforcing constraints via action projection (QP solvers), Frank-Wolfe search, or learned generative models (Normalizing Flows). Instead, it repurposes the classic acceptance-rejection sampling method as the constraint-enforcement mechanism and introduces an augmented two-objective MDP with self-loop transitions and penalty signals to guide the policy toward the feasible set. The paper also explicitly distinguishes ACRL from the CMDP literature (CPO, RCPO, FOCOPS), arguing that per-step action feasibility requires a different solution than long-term average cost constraints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: ARAM reduces QP operations by 2–5 orders of magnitude and cuts per-action inference time substantially, making ACRL viable in latency-sensitive deployment settings where QP solvers or generative models are prohibitive. The equivalence guarantee (Proposition 1) that the augmented MDP shares optimal policies with the original constrained MDP provides a clean theoretical foundation, and the multi-objective RL formulation eliminates the need for manual penalty-weight tuning. Open questions include how the acceptance rate behaves in very high-dimensional or highly non-convex feasible sets, whether the self-loop penalty mechanism generalizes to state-dependent constraint structures beyond the tested benchmarks, and whether the approach extends naturally to partially observable or multi-agent settings.
