## Problem

State-of-the-art diffusion models each excel in different aspects of compositional text-to-image generation—some in attribute binding, others in spatial or non-spatial relationships—but no single method achieves strong performance across all compositional dimensions simultaneously. The paper asks whether a framework can systematically aggregate the complementary strengths of multiple models to produce a single diffusion model with comprehensive compositional capability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251149/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, IterComp takes a free-form text prompt and produces a single RGB image via the standard SDXL denoising pipeline (40 steps, 5.63 s/image), requiring no additional layout, mask, or LLM-planning inputs. During training, the method consumes a curated composition-aware model preference dataset of 3,500 prompts and 52,500 image-rank pairs (each pair being two images from the same prompt with a human-assigned quality ordering), plus 10,000 prompts sampled from DiffusionDB for the iterative feedback loop. The reward models operate on BLIP-extracted image and text features fused via cross-attention, outputting a scalar preference score per image.

## Method

IterComp first curates a gallery of six open-source diffusion models and collects human-ranked image pairs across three compositional metrics (attribute binding, spatial, non-spatial relationships), then trains three dedicated reward models (BLIP backbone + cross-attention + MLP) on these preference pairs. The base SDXL model is finetuned via multi-reward feedback learning, where the loss is the ReLU of the summed reward scores from all three reward models evaluated on a predicted image decoded from a randomly chosen early denoising timestep. The key innovation is an iterative closed-loop: at each iteration the updated base model generates new images for every prompt, the preference dataset is expanded with these samples (preserving original relative rankings), and both the reward models and the base model are jointly retrained, progressively sharpening compositional guidance. A theoretical analysis (Lemma 1, Theorem 1) shows the resulting bilevel objective reduces to a single-level DPO-like loss plus an additional gradient term that explicitly increases the probability of winning samples and decreases that of losing ones, going beyond standard preference optimization.

## Results

On T2I-CompBench, IterComp achieves the best score across all six compositional metrics: Color 0.7982, Shape 0.6217, Texture 0.7683, Spatial 0.3196, Non-Spatial 0.3371, and Complex 0.4873, outperforming the strongest baselines (GenTron at 0.7674 Color, InstanceDiffusion at 0.2791 Spatial, Dimba-G at 0.4312 Complex). On image realism, IterComp scores 0.337 CLIP, 5.936 Aesthetic, and 1.437 ImageReward, surpassing SDXL (0.322 / 5.531 / 0.780) and all layout-based methods. Inference time is 5.63 s/image, matching SDXL and roughly 4× faster than FLUX (23.02 s) or RPG (15.57 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against text-controlled baselines, IterComp outperforms GenTron (0.7674) by +0.031 on Color, Dimba-G (0.5707) by +0.051 on Shape, and PixArt-α (0.7044) by +0.064 on Texture, while also leading on Spatial (0.3196 vs. 0.2105 for Dimba-G) and Complex (0.4873 vs. 0.4312). Against layout-controlled methods, IterComp surpasses InstanceDiffusion (0.2791) by +0.041 on Spatial and GLIGEN (0.2632) by +0.056, while simultaneously achieving far higher attribute-binding scores (e.g., 0.7982 Color vs. 0.5433 for InstanceDiffusion). Against the LLM-controlled RPG method, IterComp wins on attribute binding and colors (86.73 vs. 84.53 on DPG-Bench Attribute; 0.86 vs. 0.79 on GenEval Colors), while RPG retains a slight edge on relations (87.92 vs. 84.77) and positioning; combining IterComp as RPG's backbone yields the best overall score (84.72 average on DPG-Bench). Against diffusion alignment methods, IterComp achieves 0.5554 average T2I-CB vs. 0.4639 for ImageReward and 0.4417 for Diffusion-DPO, with the largest gap on Aesthetic Score (5.936 vs. 5.613 and 5.572). In inference speed, IterComp matches SDXL at 5.63 s/image, making it ~4× faster than FLUX (23.02 s), ~3× faster than RPG (15.57 s), and ~2× faster than InstanceDiffusion (9.88 s).

## Relation to Prior Work

IterComp extends the reward feedback learning (ReFL) paradigm from ImageReward and the DPO-style alignment formulation from Diffusion-DPO, but departs from both in two key ways: it replaces human-annotated preferences with composition-aware model preferences collected from a curated gallery of six heterogeneous diffusion models, and it introduces an iterative closed-loop where both the reward models and the base diffusion model are jointly refined across multiple rounds. The theoretical analysis (Lemma 1, Theorem 1) builds directly on the bilevel DPO optimization from Wallace et al. but identifies an additional gradient term T1 beyond standard DPO that explicitly guides the model to increase the likelihood of winning samples and decrease that of losing ones. The compositional generation task itself draws on prior work in layout-controlled methods (InstanceDiffusion, GLIGEN) and LLM-planning methods (RPG), which IterComp positions as complementary rather than competing paradigms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The model-gallery approach offers a practical recipe for combining the complementary strengths of multiple diffusion models without requiring architectural changes or additional inference-time computation, which is valuable as the field fragments across many specialized models. The iterative self-refinement loop addresses a real optimization difficulty in compositional generation, and the ablations confirm that both the gallery size and iteration count matter. However, the approach is anchored to SDXL as the base model, relies on human-annotated initial rankings for the gallery, and the model gallery is manually curated—scaling this to new model families or fully automating the preference collection remains open. Whether the iterative loop converges to a stable fixed point beyond three iterations, and whether the method transfers to video or 3D generation, are natural follow-up questions.
