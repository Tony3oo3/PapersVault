## Problem

While LLMs have made significant strides in instruction-following, retrieval systems still rely on traditional lexical and semantic matching that cannot capture user intent beyond content relevance. Existing instruction-aware retrieval models focus almost exclusively on what content to include or exclude, neglecting broader document-level attributes such as format, language, length, audience, and source. No prior benchmark or evaluation metric systematically measures whether a retrieval model truly complies with such multi-dimensional, instance-level instructions.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance pairs a core query with a natural-language instruction that constrains one of six document-level dimensions (Audience, Keyword, Format, Language, Length, Source), and the model must rank a document pool of 6,392 passages. The same query is evaluated in three modes—original (no constraint), instructed (satisfy the constraint), and reversely instructed (violate the constraint via semantic negation)—yielding 600 core queries, 1,598 instructed, and 1,598 reversely instructed instances. The output is a ranked document list scored by two proposed metrics: SICR (a binary ratio of queries where both rank and relevance score move correctly in both instructed and reversed modes) and WISE (a continuous weighted score with top-K-sensitive reward and penalty components).

## Method

The paper introduces InfoSearch, a benchmark constructed through a 7-step pipeline: condition determination per dimension, data collection from existing Q-A datasets (MS MARCO, BioASQ, StackOverflow, etc.), GPT-4-generated natural-language instructions using a two-sentence template, GPT-4 document rewriting to satisfy format/length/audience constraints, systematic instruction reversal for negation testing, hard-negative generation to prevent superficial feature reliance, and multi-round manual review. Two novel metrics are proposed: SICR assigns a binary 1 only when the gold document's rank and score both improve under the instructed instruction and both degrade under the reversed instruction relative to the original baseline; WISE relaxes this to a continuous score with a reward function that weights top-20 ranking improvements via a 1/√R_ins term and a relative-improvement factor, plus a penalty function that handles all six possible rank permutations across the three modes. Fifteen models spanning sparse retrieval (BM25), dense retrieval (8 models including GritLM, E5-Mistral, NV-Embed-v2), point-wise reranking (Llama-3.1, FollowIR, Mistral-7B), and list-wise reranking (GPT-4o, RankZephyr, RankVicuna, Zephyr, Mistral-7B) are evaluated, revealing a consistent hierarchy: list-wise > point-wise > dense > sparse, with GPT-4o achieving the highest WISE (33.5) and SICR (32.1) scores.

## Results

On the InfoSearch benchmark (600 core queries, 6,392 documents across six dimensions), GPT-4o achieves the highest instruction-following scores with WISE=33.5 and SICR=32.1, followed by Llama-3.1 (WISE=14.4, SICR=19.3) among point-wise rerankers and GritLM (WISE=−11.1, SICR=6.9) among dense retrievers. BM25, the sparse baseline, scores WISE=−12.0 and SICR=0.0, confirming that lexical matching alone cannot interpret instructions. Notably, p-MRR produces misleading rankings (e.g., GPT-4o scores only 15.0, below Instructor-XL's 30.4), whereas WISE and SICR correctly identify GPT-4o as the strongest instruction follower, aligning with gold-document rank shifts (R_ori=2.6, R_ins=1.7, R_rev=4.3 for GPT-4o).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o dominates all 15 baselines on both WISE (33.5) and SICR (32.1), outperforming the next-best model Llama-3.1 (WISE=14.4, SICR=19.3) by roughly 2.3× on WISE and 1.7× on SICR. Among dense retrievers, GritLM (WISE=−11.1, SICR=6.9) leads, benefiting from joint encoding-generative training, while task-specific instruction models like Instructor-XL (WISE=−29.8) and BGE-Large-v1.5 (WISE=−29.5) degrade severely under instructions. The clear hierarchy is list-wise reranking > point-wise reranking > dense retrieval > sparse retrieval, with RankZephyr-v1 (WISE=14.5) matching Llama-3.1's point-wise score despite being a list-wise model. A critical finding is that p-MRR misranks models: GPT-4o scores only 15.0 on p-MRR (below Instructor-XL's 30.4 and NV-Embed-v2's 17.7), yet WISE correctly identifies GPT-4o as the top performer because it accounts for both absolute and relative rank changes. Even the best model (GPT-4o) achieves only 32.1% strict instruction compliance (SICR), indicating substantial room for improvement across all architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the instruction-following retrieval line initiated by TART (task-level instructions), InstructIR and ExcluIR (content-level inclusion/exclusion), and FollowIR (combined inclusion/exclusion), by shifting the evaluation focus from content relevance to broader document-level attributes (audience, format, language, length, source, keyword). It departs from prior work in two ways: (1) the benchmark dimensions go beyond what content to retrieve to include how the document is presented, and (2) the proposed metrics (SICR, WISE) explicitly address five identified limitations of Robustness@k and p-MRR, which prior instruction-following IR papers rely on. The paper also positions itself against MAIR, a large-scale instruction retrieval benchmark that covers many tasks but lacks explicit instruction-compliance evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even the strongest model (GPT-4o) achieves only 32.1% SICR, meaning no current retrieval system reliably follows user instructions on document-level attributes, highlighting a substantial gap between generative LLM instruction-following and retrieval. The finding that format and audience dimensions are consistently the hardest across all models suggests that structural and contextual cues remain underrepresented in training data for retrieval models. Open questions include whether joint training on encoding and generative tasks (as in GritLM) can close the gap for dense retrievers, and whether the new metrics will be adopted as standard evaluation tools for instruction-following IR. The paper's scope is limited to English and a relatively small document pool (6,392 documents), and the six dimensions, while well-motivated, may not capture all real-world instruction types.
