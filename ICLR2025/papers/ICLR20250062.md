## Problem

Most density-regression crowd counting methods rely on an 'intersection hypothesis' where a single pixel's value is influenced by multiple ground-truth points via overlapping Gaussian kernels, which is physically inconsistent since one pixel cannot contain two objects. This hypothesis causes predicted density maps to blend together, making it difficult to define clear instance boundaries for localization. The paper asks whether density regression can be formulated without this hypothesis while retaining its counting advantages.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250062/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard RGB crowd image and produces a density map at half the input resolution (upsampled via pixel shuffle from a quarter-resolution feature map). The density map is summed to yield the crowd count, and can be converted to a discrete point map via optimal transport minimization (OTM) for localization. Unlike conventional Gaussian-blurred density maps, the predicted density has sharp, non-overlapping boundaries between instances, enabling direct per-person localization.

## Method

PML follows a divide-and-conquer strategy: (1) a nearest-neighbor assignment partitions the predicted density map into non-overlapping patches, each associated with exactly one GT point; (2) within each patch, the loss is computed by minimizing a transport cost plus a Bregman divergence regularizer over a target distribution p constrained to sum to 1, yielding a dynamic learning target p* that lies between the current prediction and the GT delta function. The final loss uses an L1-norm count term (equivalent to a dynamic τ that adapts to prediction error) and a (c − c̄) background term that assigns negative weights to foreground pixels and positive weights to background, unlike Bayesian loss which penalizes all pixels uniformly. Theoretically, PML subsumes Gaussian-blurred L2 loss, Bayesian loss, P2PNet's Hungarian matching, and DM-Count as special cases obtained by fixing τ, the divergence, or the feasible set ξ. The model backbone (VGG-16bn, VGG-19, or HRNet) is standard; PML replaces only the training loss, and the nearest-neighbor assignment is computed in O(mn) without requiring Sinkhorn iterations.

## Results

With HRNet backbone, PML achieves the best counting performance on ShanghaiTech B (MAE 5.4, MSE 8.2), UCF-QNRF (MAE 73.2, MSE 127.5), JHU Crowd++ (MAE 52.6, MSE 230.8), and NWPU-Crowd (MAE 63.8, MSE 306.9). With VGG-16bn, PML attains MAE 50.6 on ShanghaiTech A, comparable to PET (49.3). For localization on NWPU-Crowd, PML (HRNet) + OTM achieves the best F1-score of 0.802 (Precision 0.809, Recall 0.795), surpassing STEERER+LM (F1 0.770), PET (F1 0.742), and P2PNet (F1 0.729).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PML (HRNet) outperforms STEERER (Han et al., 2023) on UCF-QNRF (MAE 73.2 vs. 74.3), JHU++ (52.6 vs. 54.3), and ShTech B (5.4 vs. 5.8), while matching it on NWPU (63.8 vs. 63.7). Against PET (Liu et al., 2023), PML (VGG-16bn) is slightly behind on ShTech A (50.6 vs. 49.3) but competitive on ShTech B (6.1 vs. 6.2) and UCF-QNRF (79.5 vs. 79.5). PML (VGG-19) substantially improves over BL (Ma et al., 2019) by 12.1 MAE on UCF-QNRF (76.6 vs. 88.7) and over DMC (Wang et al., 2020a) by 9.0 MAE (76.6 vs. 85.6). PML (VGG-16bn) beats P2PNet (Song et al., 2021) on ShTech A (50.6 vs. 52.7), UCF-QNRF (79.5 vs. 85.3), and NWPU (75.7 vs. 77.4). For localization, PML (HRNet)+OTM achieves F1 0.802, exceeding STEERER+LM (0.770), PET (0.742), P2PNet (0.729), GL+OTM (0.683), and RAZNet (0.599). PML also outperforms the frequency-domain method ChfL/GCFL (Shu et al., 2022) on UCF-QNRF (76.6 vs. 80.3 MAE with VGG-19) and NWPU (73.6 vs. 76.8 MAE). The paper further demonstrates that PML, when applied to MCNN and CSRNet backbones, reduces MAE by 4–11 points over GL and BL, confirming its generality across architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard density regression paradigm (MCNN, CSRNet, BL, DMC, GL) that relies on an 'intersection hypothesis'—where a single pixel's loss is influenced by multiple ground-truth points—by introducing nearest-neighbor assignment to partition the density map into non-overlapping point-neighbor regions. It builds on the proximal mapping framework from convex optimization (Drusvyatskiy, 2017) to dynamically construct per-region learning targets, and theoretically unifies Gaussian-blurred L2 loss, Bayesian loss, P2PNet's Hungarian matching, and DMC's balanced OT as special cases of a single formulation. Conceptually, it bridges the gap between density regression (which has dominated counting) and point-detection/segmentation methods (which excel at localization but underperform on counting), arguing that the intersection hypothesis is unnecessary for competitive counting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified theoretical lens is practically useful: it explains why L1-norm count loss outperforms L2-norm (dynamic τ adapts to prediction error, mitigating annotation noise) and why PML's nearest-neighbor assignment approximates UOT's soft transport at a fraction of the computational cost (~3.5× faster than GL). The elimination of the intersection hypothesis yields sharper instance boundaries in predicted density maps, directly improving localization without sacrificing counting accuracy. Open questions include whether the divide-and-conquer strategy generalizes to other dense object counting domains (e.g., cells, vehicles) and whether the nearest-neighbor partition is truly optimal versus learned soft assignments in very dense scenes where Voronoi cells become tiny.
