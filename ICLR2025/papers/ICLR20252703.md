## Problem

Continual reinforcement learning (CRL) agents frequently suffer from negative transfer, where knowledge from a previously learned task actively hampers learning of a subsequent task. The paper demonstrates through extensive experiments across Meta World, DeepMind Control Suite, and Atari that this phenomenon is pervasive and, critically, cannot be resolved by existing methods targeting plasticity loss (e.g., InFeR, CReLU, ReDo, Wasserstein regularization) or by methods promoting positive transfer (e.g., Progress & Compress).

## Input / Output

The method takes environment state observations (low-dimensional state vectors for Meta World and DeepMind Control Suite, or RGB frames for Atari) and a task identifier as input. It produces two outputs: an online actor-critic pair that is reset after each task, and a persistent offline actor network that serves as the final multi-task policy. The offline actor is trained entirely through supervised learning (KL-divergence-based distillation and behavioral cloning) rather than RL bootstrapping, which is central to why it avoids the negative transfer that plagues the online learner.

## Method

Reset & Distill (R&D) employs a dual-network architecture with an online actor-critic (SAC or PPO) and a persistent offline actor. For each task, the online networks are randomly re-initialized to eliminate all prior knowledge, then trained via standard RL to learn the current task. After training, the expert online actor generates a replay buffer D_τ, and the offline actor is updated via a two-term loss: (a) KL divergence distilling the current task's expert policy into the offline actor, and (b) KL divergence matching the offline actor to previously stored expert policies from an expert buffer M to prevent forgetting. The key insight is that by decoupling learning (online, reset per task) from retention (offline, supervised distillation), R&D simultaneously eliminates negative transfer via reset and catastrophic forgetting via distillation, without relying on bootstrapping in the retention phase.

## Results

On the 8-task Meta World sequences, R&D achieves near-zero negative transfer across all settings: transfer values of −0.002/0.025 (Easy), −0.041/0.025 (Hard), and −0.014/0.013 (Random) for SAC/PPO, compared to −0.500/−0.624 for fine-tuning on the Hard sequence. R&D also maintains very low forgetting (0.008/0.029 on Hard), matching ClonEx's forgetting performance while dramatically outperforming it on transfer. On the Easy sequence where negative transfer is minimal, R&D performs comparably to ClonEx and its variants, confirming that its gains come specifically from addressing negative transfer rather than from a general performance boost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

R&D decisively outperforms all baselines on negative transfer, particularly on the Hard sequence where it achieves −0.041 (SAC) versus −0.500 for fine-tuning, −0.513 for ClonEx, and −0.558 for ClonEx+CReLU. On forgetting, R&D (0.008 Hard) matches ClonEx (0.005) and ClonEx+InFeR (0.001), while fine-tuning, EWC, and P&C suffer severe catastrophic forgetting (0.47–0.51 on Hard). The plasticity-loss mitigations (CReLU, InFeR) fail to resolve negative transfer when plugged into ClonEx, with ClonEx+CReLU actually worsening transfer to −0.558 on Hard. P&C, which promotes positive transfer via adaptors, also fails to address negative transfer (−0.507 on Hard), confirming the paper's central claim that positive transfer mechanisms cannot compensate for severe negative transfer. On the Easy sequence where negative transfer is minimal, R&D is comparable to ClonEx and its variants, indicating its advantage is specifically in the high-negative-transfer regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against two concurrent research threads in CRL: (1) the stability-plasticity tradeoff literature (EWC, P&C, ClonEx) that regularizes or isolates parameters to prevent forgetting, and (2) the recent plasticity-loss literature (InFeR, CReLU, ReDo, Wasserstein regularization) that diagnoses why RL agents lose learning capacity. The authors argue that neither thread addresses what they identify as the dominant bottleneck—negative transfer from dissimilar preceding tasks—and that the plasticity-loss remedies fail to resolve it. Methodologically, R&D repurposes the knowledge distillation framework of Hinton et al. (2015) in an offline actor-critic setting, combined with the counterintuitive (in CRL) strategy of full network re-initialization, drawing a deliberate contrast with P&C's reset mode by removing the adaptor that promotes positive transfer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that negative transfer is pervasive across environments (Meta World, DM Control, Atari) and algorithms (SAC, PPO, Rainbow), and that a trivially simple reset-and-distill baseline outperforms all sophisticated CRL and plasticity-loss methods on long task sequences. This challenges the field's implicit assumption that the primary CRL challenge is the stability-plasticity tradeoff, suggesting that task dissimilarity and its induced negative transfer may be the more fundamental obstacle. Key limitations include the absence of any positive-transfer mechanism (the offline actor never informs the online learner), the restriction of long-sequence experiments to Meta World, and the reliance on expert rollouts for distillation. An open question is how to selectively leverage positive transfer when tasks are similar while still discarding harmful knowledge when they are not.
