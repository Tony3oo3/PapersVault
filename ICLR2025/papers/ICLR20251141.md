## Problem

The six benchmarks on the Open LLM Leaderboard (ARC, GSM8K, HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA, WinoGrande; 28,632 items total) exhibit high inter- and intra-benchmark score correlations, indicating substantial redundancy in the abilities they measure. This redundancy means the benchmarks can be compressed to a small subset of items without losing evaluative power, but no principled, data-driven method existed to identify which items carry the most information about underlying LLM abilities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251141/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes only binary correct/incorrect responses from an LLM to 858 selected items (less than 3% of the original 28,632), and produces reconstructed normalized scores (0–100 scale) for each of the six individual benchmarks (mean RMSE 1.24%), a reconstructed grand-average score (RMSE 0.58%), per-benchmark one-dimensional latent ability estimates, and a single meta-ability score via factor analysis. Two disjoint versions of the 858-item set are provided to support repeated evaluation, and answer-label permutation is available as an additional anti-memorization option.

## Method

The pipeline first preprocesses item-wise accuracies from over 5,000 LLMs (removing too-easy, too-hard, and uninformative items), then cross-validates random subsamples of 350 items per benchmark to establish a baseline. It fits one-dimensional IRT models (2PL/3PL/4PL) via marginal maximum likelihood to estimate item difficulty and loading parameters, then applies Fisher-information-based filtering: the ability space is partitioned into quantiles, and the single most informative item per quantile is selected (above a threshold), yielding 58–249 items per benchmark. Latent abilities are estimated via MAP, and generalized additive models with adaptive splines map these abilities plus subscores back to original benchmark scores. A joint GAM across all six benchmarks exploits the synergy effect—each benchmark's latent ability fills in gaps in the others—reducing mean reconstruction RMSE to 1.24% and grand-mean RMSE to 0.58%. Factor analysis on the six latent abilities reveals a single common factor explaining 79.3% of variance with Spearman r = 0.94 against the total score.

## Results

MetaBench distills six benchmarks (28,632 items total) into just 858 items (<3% of original size) while reconstructing individual benchmark scores with a mean RMSE of 1.24% and the grand average score with 0.58% RMSE on the test split. All Spearman rank correlations between predicted and true scores exceed 0.98, preserving LLM rankings. Factor analysis reveals a single meta-ability capturing 79.3% of total variability across latent abilities, with a Pearson correlation of r=0.938 with the original grand average score.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is against tinyBenchmarks (Polo et al., 2024), which also uses IRT-based item selection but with clustering and high-dimensional (up to 15-dim) latent traits on only 395 LLMs. MetaBench halves the score reconstruction error: achieving 0.969% MAE on MMLU and 0.437% MAE on the Open LLM Leaderboard total, compared to ~2% MAE reported by tinyBenchmarks. Against 10,000 random subsamples of matching size, MetaBench's information filtering surpasses the best random subset on 5 of 6 individual benchmarks (GSM8K approximately ties) and substantially outperforms random on the total score (0.582% vs. 0.803% RMSE). A direct ablation comparing information filtering vs. clustering-based selection (Table 6) shows information filtering wins on every benchmark, with the largest gap on HellaSwag (0.999 vs. 1.697 RMSE) and WinoGrande (1.195 vs. 2.494 RMSE).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies psychometric Item Response Theory (IRT) to LLM benchmark compression, building on the marginal maximum likelihood estimation framework of Bock & Aitkin (1981) for fitting 2PL/3PL/4PL models and on Reckase's (2009) Fisher information criterion for principled item selection. It departs from the closest prior work, tinyBenchmarks (Polo et al., 2024), in three key ways: using a much larger model population (5,000+ vs. 395 LLMs) with 1-dimensional IRT models selected via cross-validation rather than high-dimensional models, replacing clustering-based item selection with information filtering that explicitly covers the ability landscape, and going beyond point-score reconstruction to estimate and analyze latent abilities. The score reconstruction step leverages Generalized Additive Models (Wood, 2017) to capture the monotonic but non-linear mapping between latent abilities and observed scores, a choice motivated by the failure of simpler logistic-regression-based reconstructions (Appendix B.3).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MetaBench offers a 97% reduction in evaluation items (858 vs. 28,632) while reconstructing individual benchmark scores at ~1.24% RMSE and the grand total at 0.58% RMSE, making it a practical tool for resource-constrained researchers and for rapid prototyping loops. The factor analysis finding that a single common factor explains 79.3% of variance across all six latent abilities (Spearman r=0.94 with the total score) is a notable conceptual result: it suggests that math, commonsense, knowledge, truthfulness, and pronoun resolution in LLMs are largely governed by one general ability, which has implications for how the field thinks about model capabilities and where to focus improvement efforts. Key limitations include the risk of memorization (mitigated but not eliminated by two disjoint versions and answer permutation), the conditional independence assumption being violated by architecturally related models, and the inability to verify whether the training data was itself contaminated. Open questions include whether the single-factor structure holds for next-generation models, whether adaptive testing can further reduce item counts, and whether the methodology transfers to non-text modalities.
