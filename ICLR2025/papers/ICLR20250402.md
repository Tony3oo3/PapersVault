## Problem

Traditional influence functions assume permutation-invariance of the training algorithm, assigning identical influence scores to duplicate data points regardless of their position in the training sequence. However, modern foundation model training—characterized by non-convex losses, single-epoch passes over massive datasets, and multi-stage curricula—is highly sensitive to data ordering, making this assumption fundamentally violated. The paper asks: how can we efficiently quantify a data point's influence conditioned on the specific iteration at which it is encountered during training?

## Input / Output

During training, the method stores per-sample gradient decompositions (activations and output derivatives) for each data point in each batch, optionally compressed via random projections to dimension p̃ (e.g., 1024 for Pythia-410M), requiring O(TBp̃) disk storage. At query time, the influence of any training point on a test point is computed as a single dot product between the test point's gradient at the final checkpoint and its precomputed data value embedding, enabling real-time attribution without model retraining or prior knowledge of the validation set. The data value embeddings themselves are computed via a backward pass from the final checkpoint using the GGN-approximated Hessian, with per-layer decomposition reducing memory to O(Bp̃²/L²).

## Method

The paper formalizes trajectory-specific LOO (TSLOO), which measures the validation loss change from removing a data point at its specific training iteration, and approximates it via a first-order Taylor expansion that unrolls the parameter perturbation through all subsequent SGD steps, yielding a product of (I − η_k H_k) matrices applied to the data point's gradient. The test-data-independent portion of this expression is defined as the Data Value Embedding (DVEmb), which encapsulates the cumulative interaction between a training point and all subsequent training. Under the Generalized Gauss-Newton Hessian approximation, DVEmb admits a recursive backward computation: starting from the final iteration, each data point's embedding equals its scaled gradient minus a weighted sum of later embeddings, where weights are gradient inner products—capturing how similar future data dilutes a point's influence. Practical efficiency is achieved through gradient decomposition (storing O(√p) components per sample instead of O(p)), random projections for billion-parameter models, per-layer independent computation, and an influence checkpointing technique that parallelizes the backward pass across K evenly spaced checkpoints, reducing wall-clock time by a factor of K while also enabling tracking of how individual data influences evolve across training.

## Results

On MNIST with a small MLP, data value embedding achieves high Spearman correlation with ground-truth LOO for both single-epoch and all-epoch removal settings, while influence function exhibits only very weak correlation. For computational efficiency on Pythia-410M trained on 1% of Pile, data value embedding achieves 667.52 data points/sec throughput for storing embeddings versus 41.6 for LoGRA (over 15× faster), with peak GPU memory of 0.84 GB in the backpropagation step versus 63.6 GB for LoGRA. Disk storage is comparable (~170 GB for both methods). In data selection experiments, performing online selection only in the first 2000 and last 20000 iterations (less than half of training) matches full-iteration selection performance while reducing computational cost by more than 5×.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the classical influence function (Koh & Liang, 2017), data value embedding substantially outperforms in fidelity: it achieves high Spearman correlation with ground-truth LOO on MNIST, while influence function shows only very weak correlation and cannot differentiate between single-epoch and all-epoch removal (assigning identical scores to duplicate points regardless of training position). Against LoGRA (Choe et al., 2024), the most efficient influence-function implementation to date, data value embedding wins decisively on computational efficiency: it is over 15× faster in the embedding storage step (667.52 vs. 41.6 data points/sec) and requires only 0.84 GB peak GPU memory in the backpropagation step versus 63.6 GB for LoGRA, since LoGRA must recompute all training gradients on the final model (equivalent to one full training epoch). Disk storage is essentially tied (~170 GB for both), and the final dot-product influence computation is identical in cost for both methods. The paper does not report a head-to-head fidelity comparison against LoGRA specifically, but the fidelity advantage over the underlying influence function framework is clear from the MNIST experiments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the trajectory-specific LOO (SGD-influence) formulation from Hara et al. (2019) into a practical, scalable data attribution framework for foundation models. It departs from the standard influence function paradigm (Koh & Liang, 2017), which assumes permutation-invariance and only examines the final model state, by explicitly modeling how a data point's influence propagates through the entire optimization trajectory via a product of Hessian-adjusted updates. For efficient computation, it leverages the Generalized Gauss-Newton Hessian approximation and the gradient decomposition technique from Choe et al. (2024) and Wang et al. (2025), but restructures the computation as a backward recursive algorithm that avoids the costly full-epoch gradient recomputation required by LoGRA.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method enables real-time, on-demand data attribution for foundation models without retraining, and reveals a three-regime structure of data influence (high-impact warmup, low-impact basin, gradual ascent) that has direct practical implications: performing online data selection only in the early and late high-influence phases (less than half of training) matches continuous selection performance at 5× lower cost. Key limitations include the method being specific to SGD (not directly extendable to Adam due to normalization terms) and the GGN Hessian approximation whose quality gap relative to the true Hessian remains an open question. The three-regime finding, while compelling, is demonstrated on a single model (Pythia-410M) and dataset (1% of Pile), leaving open whether it generalizes across architectures, scales, and training curricula.
