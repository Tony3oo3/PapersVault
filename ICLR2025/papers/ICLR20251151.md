## Problem

Existing category-agnostic pose estimation (CAPE) methods require annotated support images to localize keypoints in query images, but visual correspondence between support and query is inherently unreliable since no two images contain visually identical parts even within the same category. This paper asks whether text descriptions of keypoints, combined with their structural graph connectivity, can replace support images entirely while improving generalization to unseen categories.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251151/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB query image and a pose-graph whose nodes carry free-form text descriptions (e.g., 'top right side of the left eye') and whose edges encode skeletal connectivity; no support image is needed at inference. The number of keypoints varies per category (8–68), with unused slots zero-padded to a fixed maximum of 100. The graph can be manually annotated or automatically generated by an LLM from the text descriptions alone, and the model is robust to paraphrasing, synonym substitution, translation, and typos in the text.

## Method

CapeX replaces the visual support encoder in GraphCape with a frozen gte-base-v1.5 text encoder that embeds each keypoint's description, while a SwinV2-T backbone extracts patch-level features from the query image. A three-block transformer encoder fuses the two modalities via self-attention to close the representation gap, then a proposal generator computes per-keypoint similarity heatmaps using a trainable inner-product mechanism. A graph transformer decoder with interleaved attention and GCN layers refines the proposals, explicitly modeling inter-keypoint relationships to break symmetry and handle occlusions. The text backbone is frozen during training to prevent overfitting to specific phrasings, and the model is trained end-to-end with a combined heatmap and offset loss under a 1-shot protocol on MP-100, where test categories are entirely unseen during training.

## Results

On MP-100 under the 1-shot setting, CapeX (Text+Graph) achieves a PCK₀.₂ average of 88.81% and PCK₀.₁ average of 74.38%, establishing a new state-of-the-art for category-agnostic pose estimation. This surpasses the previous best, SCAPE (87.55% PCK₀.₂), by 1.26%, and the image+graph baseline GraphCape (87.23%) by 1.58%. On the animal-restricted MP-78 dataset (Setting B), CapeX achieves 92.17% PCK₀.₂, outperforming KDSM (85.33%) by a large margin of 6.84%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CapeX outperforms all 15 compared baselines on MP-100 PCK₀.₂. The most notable margins are: +1.26% over SCAPE (previous SOTA, 87.55%), +1.58% over GraphCape (87.23%), +1.27% over PPM+CPT (87.54%), and +6.07% over ESCAPE (82.74%). Even the graph-less text-only variant (CapeX-no-graph, 88.50%) surpasses all image-based methods, confirming that text descriptions alone provide a stronger support signal than visual features. On MP-78 (Setting B), CapeX (92.17%) beats KDSM (85.33%) by 6.84%, demonstrating superiority over the only other text-based open-vocabulary keypoint method. CLAMP, adapted to the CAPE setting, performs poorly (72.37% on Split 1 only) due to its contrastive-learning design being ill-suited for novel text descriptions. The PCK₀.₁ gap between CapeX and GraphCape widens to 2.55%, suggesting text-graph support is particularly beneficial for precise localization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on GraphCape (Hirschorn & Avidan, 2023), which introduced graph-convolutional structure to category-agnostic pose estimation (CAPE), by replacing the visual support image with text descriptions attached to graph nodes. It departs from the broader image-based CAPE lineage (POMNet, CapeFormer, ESCAPE, MetaPoint, SCAPE, SDPNet) that relies on latent visual correspondence between support and query keypoints, arguing that text's abstraction power better captures semantic identity across visually dissimilar instances. Among text-based pose estimation methods (CLAMP, KDSM, X-Pose, PPM), CapeX is the first to combine textual descriptions with explicit graph connectivity and to evaluate on the full MP-100 benchmark across all 100 categories rather than animal-only subsets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CapeX establishes that text-graph support can outperform image-based support for CAPE (1.58% over GraphCape on average), suggesting that semantic abstraction via language is a viable and sometimes superior alternative to pixel-level visual matching for cross-category generalization. The enhanced MP-100 dataset with standardized text annotations and alternative test annotations (typos, synonyms, translations) provides a useful benchmark for future text-based CAPE research. However, the cross-super-category ablation (Table 7) reveals a significant limitation: when no visually similar training examples exist, text-based methods struggle with spatial terms like 'left' and 'front,' performing substantially worse than image-based methods (e.g., 24.66 vs. 44–52 PCK on vehicles). This highlights an open question about how to ground spatial/relational language in visual context without support images, and whether scaling to larger vision-language models could close this gap.
