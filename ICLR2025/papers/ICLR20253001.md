## Problem

Existing camera-aware ReID methods address camera bias only on training (seen) domains, leaving the bias on unseen domains unexplored. This paper reveals that camera bias becomes more pronounced under distribution shifts and that unsupervised models remain heavily biased even on their own training data, indicating a fundamental gap in how ReID models generalize across camera views.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253001/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At test time, the method takes a set of RGB person images (resized to 384×128) with their associated camera labels and a pretrained ReID encoder (ResNet-50 or ViT backbone). It computes per-camera mean and standard deviation vectors over the extracted embedding features, then outputs camera-normalized embedding vectors used for cosine-distance-based ReID matching. For the unsupervised training strategies, the input is the full training set with camera labels, and the output is a debiased model trained on debiased pseudo labels with single-camera clusters removed.

## Method

The core debiasing mechanism is camera-specific feature normalization: for each camera, the per-dimension mean and standard deviation of its embedding features are computed, and each feature is centered and scaled by its camera's statistics (Eq. 2). The paper provides an empirical explanation for why this works: camera variations induce consistent translational shifts in a small subset of high-variance embedding dimensions, so centering along those dimensions removes the dominant bias component. The normalization is further extended to compound bias factors (e.g., (property-group, camera)-specific or (angle, camera)-specific normalization), yielding gains beyond camera-only debiasing. For unsupervised learning, two simple training modifications are proposed: (1) generating pseudo labels by clustering on the camera-normalized features rather than raw features, and (2) discarding single-camera clusters from the training set to prevent the model from learning camera-specific shortcuts. These modifications, applied to existing USL algorithms like CC and PPLR, yield up to 19.3% mAP improvement on MSMT17.

## Results

On unseen domains, camera-specific feature normalization consistently improves all 12 evaluated ReID models. For example, on Market-1501, CC improves from 22.5 to 30.0 mAP (+7.5), TransReID-SSL from 53.6 to 62.3 mAP (+8.7), and SOLIDER from 72.4 to 79.2 mAP (+6.8). On the challenging CUHK03-NP benchmark, TransReID-SSL gains 8.5 mAP (27.2→35.7) and CC gains 5.1 mAP (8.4→13.5). For the USL training strategies, CC on MSMT17 achieves a 19.3 mAP improvement (29.8→49.1) and PPLR gains 13.5 mAP (27.2→40.7), while PPLR-CAM shows a smaller 3.7 mAP gain (40.7→44.4) due to its already camera-aware loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The normalization method is applied as a test-time postprocessing step to 12 existing ReID models spanning supervised, unsupervised, camera-aware, and domain-generalizable paradigms. On unseen domains, every model benefits: CC gains +7.5 mAP on Market-1501, TransReID-SSL gains +8.7 mAP, and even the strong SOLIDER improves by +6.8 mAP. On seen domains (MSMT17), camera-agnostic unsupervised models like CC show modest gains (+2.4 mAP), while camera-aware models (PPLR-CAM, CAP) and supervised models (SOLIDER, TransReID) show negligible or zero improvement, confirming their training already mitigates camera bias. For the USL training strategies, CC with both debiased pseudo labeling and discarding biased clusters achieves 49.1 mAP on MSMT17, outperforming the original CC (33.0 mAP) by 16.1 mAP and surpassing PPLR (31.4 mAP) and PPLR-CAM (42.2 mAP). The normalization is also complementary to existing postprocessing: when combined with DBA, AQE, or k-reciprocal reranking on Market-1501, it adds ~9 mAP on top of each method, whereas those methods alone sometimes degrade R5/R10. The CD loss (Lee et al., 2023) achieves 40.7 mAP on MSMT17 with CC, which is outperformed by the simpler discarding strategy (45.4 mAP) that avoids the side effect of randomizing effective batch size.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the camera-bias literature in ReID, which has primarily focused on training-domain analysis and camera-aware training objectives (inter-camera contrastive losses, camera-conditional batch normalization). It departs from that line by (1) shifting the analysis to unseen domains where bias is shown to be more severe, and (2) revisiting the simple camera-specific feature normalization previously used as an ad-hoc postprocessing step in domain adaptation (Gu et al., 2020; Luo et al., 2021a) and camera-based BN (Zhuang et al., 2020), providing the first systematic empirical explanation for why it works. On the training side, it builds on standard USL pipelines (CC, PPLR, TransReID-SSL) and adds two minimal modifications—debiased pseudo labeling via normalized features and discarding single-camera clusters—rather than proposing a new architecture or loss.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a few lines of test-time normalization (compute per-camera mean/std, subtract and divide) can yield 7–19 mAP gains across diverse backbones and training regimes on unseen domains, making it an attractive drop-in for deployment where retraining is infeasible. The USL finding that camera-biased pseudo labels are more harmful than a drop in clustering accuracy (toy experiment: 91.9% accuracy 'Random' beats 93.8% accuracy 'Camera') reframes how practitioners should think about pseudo-label quality. Open questions include: the method requires known camera labels at test time (limiting applicability to open-world or single-camera queries), the computational cost of computing statistics scales with gallery size, and whether the residual bias after normalization reflects a fundamental representational limitation or is simply masked in the metric used.
