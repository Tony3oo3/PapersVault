## Problem

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) can both provide valuable external knowledge (benefit) and mislead LLMs with noisy or incorrect retrieved texts (detriment), yet existing methods to balance these effects are data-driven, require additional utility evaluators or fine-tuning, and lack a theoretical explanation for how benefit and detriment manifest in next-token prediction. The paper addresses this gap by providing the first theoretical framework that formalizes and makes comparable the benefit and detriment at the token level without any training or external evaluators.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prefix (query) and a list of retrieved passages as input, and produces a generated text where each token is selected from either the pure LLM or the RAG output based on a token-level benefit-versus-detriment comparison. Unusually, the comparison is computed entirely from internal model representations—hidden states, attention scores, and word embeddings at a dynamically identified layer—rather than from external evaluators or log-probability heuristics. The two generators (pure LLM and RAG) run in parallel with batch size 2, and the selected token at each step becomes the shared prefix for the next step.

## Method

The paper models RAG's next-token prediction as a distribution fusion between the LLM's knowledge distribution and the retrieved texts' distribution using a latent variable (HMM-inspired) framework, then formalizes benefit as a KL divergence (distribution completion: how much out-of-distribution knowledge the retrieved texts add) and detriment as another KL divergence (distribution contradiction: how much the retrieved texts conflict with the LLM's pre-trained knowledge). The key theoretical result (Theorem 3) proves that the sign of benefit minus detriment is determined by comparing two representation similarities: the cosine similarity between the RAG output embedding and the retrieved-texts embedding versus the cosine similarity between the RAG output embedding and the pure-LLM embedding. Practically, Tok-RAG generates tokens in parallel from pure LLM and RAG, dynamically identifies the layer where distribution fusion begins (via attention-score turning point and JSD-based distribution-change detection), approximates the retrieved-texts distribution from attention and word-embedding matching at that layer, and selects the RAG token only when its similarity to the retrieved distribution exceeds its similarity to the pure-LLM distribution—requiring no training, no additional modules, and no access to retrieval utility scores.

## Results

On short-form Q&A with 100% hard-negative passages, Tok-RAG achieves 53.5% accuracy on TriviaQA, 32.9% on WebQ, and 12.8% on SQuAD, outperforming the best baselines (NLI+RAG at 50.8%, 30.7%, 9.9%) by 2.7–2.9 points. With 0% noise, Tok-RAG reaches 85.7% on TriviaQA, 57.3% on WebQ, and 68.1% on SQuAD. On long-form tasks, Tok-RAG leads across all five benchmarks (e.g., 9.68 F1 on Wizard of Wikipedia, 32.59 CodeBLEU on Java, 68.70 accuracy on T-REx). In the benefit-detriment comparison experiment, Tok-RAG achieves the highest AUC across all LLMs and datasets (e.g., 81.89 AUC on WikiText with LLaMA-2-7B vs. 74.96 for the best baseline).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Tok-RAG outperforms all six RAG baselines (NLI+RAG, CRAG, RetRobust, Self-RAG, INFO-RAG, Standard RAG) across every noise level and dataset in Table 1, with the largest margins under high noise (e.g., +9.7 points over Standard RAG on TriviaQA at 100% hard negatives). Crucially, Tok-RAG requires neither LLM fine-tuning nor an external utility evaluator, whereas NLI+RAG and CRAG need a separate evaluator module and RetRobust, Self-RAG, and INFO-RAG require training. On long-form tasks (Table 2), Tok-RAG again leads all baselines, e.g., 9.68 F1 on Wizard of Wikipedia vs. 9.09 for INFO-RAG and 9.03 for RetRobust. In the benefit-detriment prediction experiment (Table 3), Tok-RAG surpasses all four hallucination-detection baselines (Logprobs, Uncertainty, Consistency-Lexical, Consistency-Semantic) in AUC and F1 across OPT-6.7B, Mistral-7B, and LLaMA-2-7B on four datasets, with the largest gap on LLaMA-2-7B/WikiText (81.89 vs. 74.96 AUC). The computational overhead is modest: Tok-RAG uses 23.98 GB GPU memory and 2.24 s per query versus 17.50 GB and 1.95 s for Standard RAG, while delivering the highest accuracy (56.12% average). No baseline matches or exceeds Tok-RAG on any reported metric.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical analysis of in-context learning as latent variable inference (Xie et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2023) to the RAG setting, modeling next-token prediction as a fusion between the LLM's parametric distribution and the retrieved texts' distribution. It departs from the dominant data-driven approaches to RAG robustness (Self-RAG, CRAG, NLI+RAG, RetRobust, INFO-RAG) that rely on utility evaluators, fine-tuning, or prompt engineering, by instead deriving a closed-form theoretical characterization of the benefit-detriment trade-off via KL divergences under HMM-style assumptions. The practical method (Tok-RAG) repurposes standard attention scores and language-head outputs at specific layers to approximate the retrieved-text distribution, drawing on mechanistic interpretability findings about layer-wise specialization (Tenney et al., 2019; Chuang et al., 2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical framework that makes the benefit and detriment of RAG explainable and comparable at token level without requiring training or external utility evaluators, which could guide the design of more principled RAG systems. However, the theory rests on several strong assumptions (HMM structure, delimiter hidden states, bounded transition probabilities) and the key correlation in Theorem 2 is only approximate with error bounds that depend on the detriment magnitude. Open questions include whether the token-level comparison generalizes to multi-turn or multi-document RAG settings, how the layer-identification heuristic (Equation 14) behaves across architectures and scales, and whether the cosine-similarity proxy in Equation 19 remains reliable for larger vocabulary spaces or multilingual settings.
