## Problem

While memorization in pre-training and fine-tuning of large language models has been extensively studied, it remains unclear how memorization is affected by or introduced during the RLHF alignment process, which involves three distinct stages (fine-tuning, reward model training, and RL fine-tuning). This gap is critical because RLHF relies on human-labelled preference data that is often privacy-sensitive, and if such data is memorized and later regurgitated, it raises serious privacy and commercial concerns. Additionally, the memorization behavior of direct preference learning methods (e.g., IPO) relative to RLHF has not been systematically compared.

## Input / Output

The code completion task uses the Fill-in-the-Middle (FIM) PSM format: the model receives [PRE] prefix [SUF] suffix [MID] as input and generates the middle code segment as output. For memorization evaluation, the model is prompted with the prefix of a training example and greedy-decoded for 64 tokens, which are then compared against the first 64 tokens of the target using a sliding-window normalized edit distance. The synthetic evaluation dataset (SD) contains 6,554 Python cryptographic examples split into SD.Base (3,562 examples for full-example memorization) and SD.Links (2,992 examples containing PII-like file paths for sensitive-data memorization), with examples filtered to exclude short targets (<10 tokens) and high prompt-target similarity (edit distance <0.5).

## Method

The paper introduces a systematic empirical framework for measuring memorization at each stage of RLHF using a counterfactual memorization definition (k-approximate, k=0.1) that compares completions from a model trained on data X against a fixed control model trained without X, thereby eliminating false positives from general code completion ability. They train Gemini Nano-1 (1.8B) through the full RLHF pipeline (supervised fine-tuning → T5-Base reward model → REINFORCE-based RL fine-tuning with KL regularization) and separately train Gemma 2B/7B via Identity Preference Optimization (IPO) on the same preference data. Key findings: RLHF reduces memorization of reward model training data to 0.9% (vs. 17.6% for direct fine-tuning), but memorization established during fine-tuning largely persists through RLFT (43–47% retention); IPO, in contrast, increases memorization to 18.2–19.5% on SD.Base, suggesting RLHF is safer than direct preference learning for sensitive data. The KL regularization coefficient α modulates both the persistence of fine-tuning memorization and the rate of RL prompt memorization (<0.5% even at α=0.005 after 70 epochs). Results are validated across multiple datasets (CodeXGLUE, LIMA, Anthropic HH), model scales (up to Gemini Pro), and task domains.

## Results

The paper's central finding is that RLHF dramatically reduces memorization of reward model training data: only 0.9% of SD.Base examples are memorized via RLFT (vs. 17.6% under direct fine-tuning), and 0% of SD.Links PII-like file paths are regurgitated (vs. 50% under direct fine-tuning). In contrast, direct preference learning via IPO exhibits substantially higher memorization: Gemma 2B memorizes 18.2% of SD.Base and Gemma 7B memorizes 19.5%, roughly 20× the RLHF rate. Examples already memorized during fine-tuning largely persist after RLFT (43–47% of memorized SD.Base examples remain memorized), though the KL penalty α modulates this: reducing α from 0.5 to 0.005 drops SD.Links PII memorization from 54.8% to 12.6%. RL prompt memorization remains below 0.5% even after 70 epochs of RLFT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The most critical comparison is between RLHF and IPO (a special case of ΨPO, Azar et al. 2024) on memorization of preference data: IPO on Gemma 2B memorizes 18.2% of SD.Base and 0.6% of SD.Links, while RLHF memorizes only 0.9% and 0% respectively — a roughly 20× difference on SD.Base. Gemma 7B via IPO shows slightly higher memorization (19.5% on SD.Base), consistent with the known capacity-memorization relationship. On code completion benchmarks (Table 2), the paper's RLFT models are competitive with but generally below CodeGemma 2B (0.784 single-line infilling), DeepSeek Coder 2B (0.800), and StarCoder2 3B (0.774); the best RLFT model (RLFT.2, α=5e-4) reaches 0.711 single-line and 0.195 HumanEval, trailing the baselines by 7–9 points on infilling. The paper's key conclusion is that RLHF is a safer alignment method than direct preference learning when the preference data may contain sensitive information, as the intermediary reward model acts as a natural barrier against memorization propagation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the memorization measurement framework of Carlini et al. (2022) and the counterfactual memorization definition of Zhang et al. (2023) to the RLHF alignment pipeline, a context where prior memorization studies have been absent. It applies the FIM task formulation of Bavarian et al. (2022) to code completion and systematically analyzes memorization across all three RLHF stages (fine-tuning, reward model training, RL fine-tuning). The key conceptual departure is from the direct preference learning literature (DPO, ΨPO/IPO), which is often presented as an equivalent alternative to RLHF; this paper shows the two are not equivalent from a memorization standpoint.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that RLHF reduces memorization of reward model training data to near-zero (0.9%) while IPO memorizes ~18-20% of the same data provides a concrete, actionable guideline: organizations can safely use sensitive or proprietary data in reward model training under RLHF but should exercise caution with direct preference learning. The persistence of fine-tuning-stage memorization through RLHF (43-47% retained) means that RLHF cannot be relied upon to 'wash out' previously memorized data. Limitations include reliance on synthetic data, small model scales (primarily 1.8B), and a single task domain (code completion), leaving open whether these findings hold for larger production models and more diverse data distributions.
