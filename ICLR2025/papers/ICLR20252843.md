## Problem

Existing mathematical benchmarks either lack comprehensive coverage of undergraduate-level problems (GSM8K, MATH focus on elementary/competition math) or are static and vulnerable to test-set contamination. No prior benchmark simultaneously offers broad subject coverage (16 subjects, 111 topics), diverse answer types (10 types including compound lists), and dynamic randomized versions to probe true reasoning rather than memorization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252843/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each of the 5,062 problems is presented in 3 randomized versions (generated via WebWork's program-based variable disturbance with different random seeds), formatted as LaTeX text with placeholders [ANS]. Evaluation is zero-shot with greedy decoding (temperature 0, max 2,048 tokens) using vLLM, with prompts tailored to each of the 10 answer types (8 atomic: numerical value, expression, equation, interval, true/false, MC single, MC multiple, open-ended; 2 compound: ordered and unordered lists). The primary output is EAcc — the fraction of problems solved correctly across all 3 versions — alongside per-version accuracy, reasoning gap Δ = AAcc − EAcc, and Robustness Efficiency RE = Δ/EAcc.

## Method

UGMathBench is constructed from an institutional WebWork online homework grading system, where problems are stored as Program Generation scripts that parameterize variables while preserving solution structure. The pipeline converts HTML to LaTeX (via bs4 and re), manually verifies conversions, deduplicates within each subject using text-embedding-ada-002 cosine similarity (filtering 9,382 of 14,444 problems), and annotates answer types. The key evaluation insight is that a model performing genuine reasoning should solve all randomized versions of a problem identically; EAcc captures this by requiring correctness on every version, while Δ quantifies the robustness gap between average per-version accuracy and this stricter criterion. The paper evaluates 23 LLMs (4 closed-source, 15 open-source general, 6 specialized math) and finds the best EAcc is only 56.3% (OpenAI-o1-mini) with RE ranging from 20.78% to 196.6%, revealing widespread inconsistency under variable disturbance.

## Results

On UGMathBench (5,062 undergraduate-level problems × 3 randomized versions), OpenAI-o1-mini achieves the highest Effective Accuracy (EAcc) of 56.30% and Average Accuracy (AAcc) of 68.00%, with a reasoning gap Δ of 11.70%. All 23 evaluated LLMs exhibit substantial reasoning gaps: for models with AAcc > 20%, Δ consistently exceeds 10%, and Robustness Efficiency (RE = Δ/EAcc) ranges from 20.78% (o1-mini) to 196.6% (DeepSeek-MOE-16B-Chat). Among open-source models, Qwen2-Math-72B-Instruct (45.85% EAcc) and Mistral-Large-Instruct-2407 (45.04% EAcc) are the strongest, yet both trail o1-mini by over 10 percentage points in EAcc. More than half of open-source LLMs (10/19) score below 20% EAcc, underscoring the benchmark's difficulty.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OpenAI-o1-mini dominates all metrics, achieving 56.30% EAcc and the lowest RE (20.78%), outperforming GPT-4o (49.94% EAcc, RE 20.89%) by 6.36 points in EAcc. Among open-source models, Qwen2-Math-72B-Instruct (45.85% EAcc) and Mistral-Large-Instruct-2407 (45.04% EAcc) are the closest to GPT-4o, with only a ~4-point gap, while the remaining 17 open-source models fall well behind GPT-4o-mini (41.01% EAcc). Specialized math models consistently outperform their general-purpose counterparts at the same scale: Qwen2-Math-7B (32.46% EAcc) beats Qwen2-7B-Instruct (25.15%) by 7.3 points, and Qwen2-Math-72B (45.85%) beats Qwen2-72B-Instruct (35.78%) by 10.1 points. The gap between the best closed-source (o1-mini, 56.30%) and best open-source (Qwen2-Math-72B, 45.85%) is approximately 10.5 percentage points in EAcc. Notably, all models show Δ > 10% when AAcc exceeds 20%, indicating that no current LLM achieves robust reasoning (Δ = 0) under variable disturbance, with RE values spanning 20.78% to 196.6%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the dynamic benchmark paradigm introduced by Srivastava et al. (2024) and Qian et al. (2024) — who functionalized GSM8K and MATH problems via variable perturbation — to the substantially harder and broader domain of undergraduate-level mathematics. It departs from prior dynamic benchmarks (GSM1K, VarBench-Math, FN-EVAL, LiveBench-Math), which are limited to elementary or competition-level problems, by sourcing 5,062 problems from a real WebWork-based homework grading system spanning 16 subjects and 10 answer types. The EAcc and reasoning gap (Δ) metrics are directly adapted from Srivastava et al., but the addition of Robustness Efficiency (RE) and the multi-answer-type evaluation framework are novel contributions to the benchmarking methodology.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

UGMathBench fills a critical gap: no prior benchmark simultaneously covers undergraduate-level breadth (16 subjects, 111 topics), supports diverse answer types (10 types including compound lists), and provides dynamic randomized versions to combat contamination. The universal finding that all 23 evaluated LLMs exhibit large reasoning gaps (RE from 20.78% to 196.6%) challenges the assumption that high per-version accuracy reflects genuine mathematical reasoning, pointing toward a need for 'large reasoning models' with Δ=0. Open questions include whether the gap narrows with continued scaling, whether the text-only constraint (excluding image-dependent problems) limits generalizability, and whether the ~2.7% 'bad problem' rate from the WebWork source can be further reduced. The benchmark is likely to become a standard evaluation tool given its scale, public labels, and dynamic design.
