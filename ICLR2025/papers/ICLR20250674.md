## Problem

Despite the empirical success of diffusion transformers (DiT) in sequential data generation such as video, there is no rigorous theory explaining how they capture the spatial-temporal dependencies that define sequential data. Existing diffusion model analyses focus on static data and cannot address the fundamental questions of whether transformers efficiently model temporal correlations and how the decay pattern of those correlations affects sample complexity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250674/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes N patches of d-dimensional vectors sampled from a Gaussian process on a uniform time grid, where the joint covariance has Kronecker structure Γ⊗Σ (Γ a symmetric Toeplitz matrix encoding temporal dependencies, Σ a d×d matrix encoding spatial dependencies), along with the diffusion timestep t. The transformer input is each patch lifted to a higher-dimensional vector concatenating the raw data, a time embedding e_i, and diffusion-timestep parameters. The output is a score function estimate used to drive the backward diffusion process, ultimately producing new samples that replicate the spatial-temporal correlation structure of the training data.

## Method

The core insight is to represent the score function as the last iterate of gradient descent on a strongly convex quadratic objective, then construct a transformer architecture that unrolls this algorithm: each gradient descent step is realized by a multiplication module followed by two transformer blocks. The attention layer in the first block computes inner products of time embeddings to approximate the correlation coefficient γ(h_i, h_j), thereby capturing spatial-temporal dependencies; the number of attention heads M scales as O((ℓ log(Nd/ε))^{1/ν}), directly reflecting the decay rate of the covariance function. A correlation truncation at length J = O((ℓ log(N/ε))^{1/ν}) limits the modeled dependency window, making the architecture size logarithmic in sequence length N. The paper establishes the first sample complexity bound for diffusion transformers on sequential data, showing generalization error scales as Õ(√(ℓ^{1/ν} κ² Nd³/n)), where the condition number κ and bandwidth ℓ quantify the difficulty of capturing long-horizon dependencies.

## Results

The paper establishes the first sample complexity bound for diffusion transformers on sequential data: the total variation distance between the learned and ground-truth distribution scales as Õ(√(ℓ^(1/ν) κ²_{t₀} N d³/n) · log^((5ν+1)/(2ν))(κ_{t₀} n d N t₀⁻¹)), where ℓ is the covariance bandwidth, ν controls decay speed, and κ_{t₀} is the condition number. For fast-decay covariance functions (κ_{t₀} = O(1)), this simplifies to Õ(√(ℓ N d³/n)), showing only a mild √N dependence on sequence length. Numerically, with d=8 and N=128, the relative error of the estimated spatial-temporal covariance decreases monotonically with sample size n ∈ {1000, 3200, 10000, 32000, 100000}, and larger ν (faster decay) or smaller ℓ (tighter bandwidth) consistently yields lower error, confirming the theoretical prediction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DiT against a UNet-based diffusion model (with 4 down/up sampling stages, matched in parameter count) on Gaussian process data with d=8, N=128, ν=2, ℓ=64, and n=10000 training sequences. DiT clearly outperforms the UNet baseline on both temporal and spatial dependency recovery: DiT's estimated temporal correlation closely matches the smooth ground-truth exponential decay, while the UNet produces a visibly 'piecewise' pattern attributed to convolution filter size limitations. On spatial dependencies, DiT accurately captures even weak long-horizon correlations (e.g., between tokens 30 and 50), whereas the UNet shows inconsistent and deviating spatial covariance patterns. Additionally, the paper's internal ablation shows that increasing transformer blocks from 1 to 16 improves performance with diminishing returns, and 8 blocks suffice for efficient learning, consistent with the theoretical O(log N) block requirement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the DiT architecture (Peebles and Xie, 2023) and the score-based diffusion framework (Song and Ermon, 2019), but departs from prior diffusion model theory—which has focused almost exclusively on static data—by providing the first theoretical analysis of how transformers capture spatial-temporal dependencies in sequential data. The key methodological departure is the algorithm unrolling perspective (Monga et al., 2021), where the score function is represented as the last iterate of a gradient descent algorithm that the transformer unrolls, rather than being approximated via universal approximation theorems. This connects to Mei and Wu (2023) and Mei (2024), who showed U-Nets perform algorithm unrolling in diffusion models, but extends the idea to transformers and sequential data with explicit characterization of how attention heads encode temporal correlations through time embeddings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical framework explaining how diffusion transformers model sequential data, providing sample complexity bounds that explicitly depend on the decay rate of temporal correlations (via the bandwidth ℓ and exponent ν). The practical implication is clear: faster-decaying dependencies yield weaker dependence on sequence length N, explaining why DiT scales well to long videos. However, the analysis is restricted to Gaussian process data with specific covariance function forms (exponential decay with ν ∈ [1,2]), and the condition number κ_{t₀} for slowly decaying correlations remains unbounded for finite N. Extending the theory to non-Gaussian sequential data, periodic or multi-scale dependencies, and understanding the role of softmax attention (deferred to appendix) are important open questions.
