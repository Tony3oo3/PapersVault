## Problem

Longitudinal neurodegenerative disease data is scarce due to expensive imaging, subject mortality, and irregular visit intervals (6 months to 6 years), making it difficult to model realistic disease progression. Existing conditional generative models for sequential medical data ignore the ordinal, monotonically deteriorating nature of disease labels and fail to handle temporal discontinuities between sparse observations, producing unrealistic trajectories that violate the irreversible progression constraint.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequence of ordinal conditions—age values and discrete disease labels (CN, SMC, EMCI, LMCI, AD) that monotonically deteriorate over time—plus an optional time-invariant domain identifier, and produces a full longitudinal sequence of 148-dimensional brain regional measurements (cortical thickness or PET SUVR values from the Destrieux atlas). Unlike cross-sectional generators, the output is an ordered sequence where each time point's measurements must be consistent with the interpolated ordinal conditions between the given endpoints, and the generation is autoregressive: each follow-up sample is conditioned on the previously generated one.

## Method

ConDOR decomposes generation into two diffusion models: a Regional Diffusion Model (RDM) that produces the baseline sample from Gaussian noise via standard DDPM denoising conditioned on age and label, and a Temporal Diffusion Model (TDM) that autoregressively generates follow-up samples by learning step-wise differences Δx_t^d between D interpolated pseudo-samples spanning each pair of consecutive time points. The key mechanism is a dual-sampling strategy where each pseudo-sample x_t^d is a convex combination (weight λ) of a cohort-level sample drawn from f_{X|A,Y}—estimated via Bayes' theorem combining an ordinal regression (cumulative logit with learned thresholds) for p_{Y|X,A} and a per-ROI kernel density estimate for f_{X|A}—and a subject-level sample obtained by linear interpolation between the two bracketing observations. The TDM's diffusion steps use interpolated ages and discretized ordinal labels at each sub-step, enforcing smooth monotonic transitions. A multi-domain extension introduces a domain condition into both models and replaces the cohort-level distribution with a mixture over domain-specific PDFs, enabling joint learning across datasets with different label distributions.

## Results

On single-domain ADNI experiments across three biomarkers (Cortical Thickness, Amyloid, FDG), ConDOR achieves the best performance on all metrics: WD of 4.25/5.27/2.49, RMSE of 0.21/0.31/0.09, and JSD of 0.007/0.008/0.001 respectively. On the multi-domain Tau experiment (ADNI+OASIS), ConDOR achieves WD 5.63, RMSE 0.293, and JSD 0.012, outperforming 8 of 9 baselines on RMSE and JSD while TVAE edges it on WD by ~0.55. ConDOR's sampling time is ~66 seconds for 36 sequences, slower than flow/GAN-based methods but yielding substantially higher sample quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ConDOR outperforms all nine baselines on every metric for Cortical Thickness, Amyloid, and FDG in single-domain learning. The most notable margins are against TabDDPM (WD 14.23 vs. 4.25 on CT; 31.28 vs. 5.27 on Amyloid) and CRow (RMSE 0.78 vs. 0.21 on CT; 0.85 vs. 0.31 on Amyloid), indicating that ConDOR captures individual-level variation far better than prior longitudinal flow models. On FDG, ConDOR ties with DDPM on JSD (0.001) but leads on WD (2.49 vs. 2.90) and RMSE (0.09 vs. 0.10). In the multi-domain Tau experiment, ConDOR beats GOGGLE on WD (5.63 vs. 8.38) and RMSE (0.293 vs. 0.343) while tying on JSD (0.012), and beats TVAE on RMSE (0.293 vs. 0.300) and JSD (0.012 vs. 0.018) though TVAE is slightly better on WD (5.10 vs. 5.63). Overall, ConDOR's advantage is most pronounced on RMSE and JSD, suggesting it preserves fine-grained sample-level details and distributional shape better than baselines that primarily match global structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

ConDOR builds directly on the DDPM diffusion framework (Ho et al., 2020) for the denoising backbone and extends the conditional longitudinal generation paradigm established by CRow (Hwang et al., 2019), which used a conditional recurrent normalizing flow for neuroimaging sequences. The key departure is the integration of ordinal regression (Winship & Mare, 1984) into the diffusion conditioning, explicitly encoding the monotonic, ordered nature of disease progression rather than treating labels as independent categories. Additionally, the dual-sampling strategy (cohort-level KDE sample + subject-level interpolation) draws on the group-vs-individual decomposition common in face recognition and graph learning, repurposed here to bridge irregular time gaps between clinical visits.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ConDOR addresses a practical bottleneck in neurodegenerative disease research: the scarcity and irregularity of longitudinal biomarker data due to costly imaging, subject dropout, and variable visit intervals. The multi-domain extension with a domain condition offers a principled way to pool data across sites, which is critical for small cohorts like OASIS (32 subjects, heavily imbalanced labels). However, the method assumes strictly monotonic label progression (no reversals), is limited to four AD-specific biomarkers from US-centric cohorts, and the autoregressive generation is ~47× slower than CRow, raising questions about scalability to longer sequences or more time points. Open questions include whether the cohort/subject balance (λ) can be made adaptive, whether the framework generalizes to non-AD neurodegenerative diseases with different biological mechanisms, and whether additional time-dependent conditions (e.g., blood plasma biomarkers) can be incorporated.
