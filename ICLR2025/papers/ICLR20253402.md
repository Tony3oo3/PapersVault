## Problem

While LLM-as-judge methods have automated the scoring of model responses, very few works automate the construction of the evaluation dataset itself (i.e., generating the questions). Existing human-constructed benchmarks like MMLU tend to reveal highly correlated model rankings (94% rank correlation with prior benchmarks) and may miss fine-grained knowledge gaps and safety vulnerabilities. AutoBencher addresses this by casting benchmark creation as a declarative optimization problem over explicit desiderata such as difficulty, novelty, separability, and salience.

## Input / Output

The method takes a broad domain (e.g., history, math, safety), a set of user-specified desiderata, a salience filter (e.g., Wikipedia page views > 500K), and a set of candidate LMs to evaluate. It produces a selected dataset description (topic), a generated set of (question, answer) pairs, and quantitative scores for novelty, difficulty, and separability (capability) or attack success rate (safety). A key structural constraint is information asymmetry: the evaluator LM receives privileged information (retrieved Wikipedia articles, Python math libraries, or a translation system) that is withheld from candidate LMs, enabling the generation of questions harder than the evaluator's base parametric knowledge.

## Method

AutoBencher formalizes each desideratum as a quantitative metric—difficulty as 1 minus the best model's accuracy, separability as mean absolute deviation of model accuracies, and novelty as 1 minus the rank correlation between a new dataset's accuracy vector and its regression prediction from prior datasets—then casts dataset selection as a constrained optimization over natural-language dataset descriptions. To solve this, an evaluator LM (GPT-4-0125) performs adaptive search: it iteratively proposes K dataset descriptions conditioned on a trajectory of previously proposed (description, accuracy) pairs, filters for salience, generates small QA datasets using privileged information (Wikipedia retrieval for knowledge, Python execution for math, translation for multilingual), and scores each by candidate LM accuracy. After N iterations, all proposed descriptions are re-ranked by the full objective J(c) combining novelty, difficulty, and separability, and the top description is used to generate the final large-scale dataset. The privileged information mechanism is central: by grounding answers in retrieved documents or executed code, the evaluator can produce questions whose correct answers exceed its own parametric recall, breaking the difficulty ceiling of pure parametric generation.

## Results

AutoBencher constructs datasets that are significantly more novel and difficult than human-constructed benchmarks (HUMANBENCH), reducing model ranking rank correlation by 27% (higher novelty) and decreasing best-model accuracy by 22% (higher difficulty) on average across domains. For example, in History, AutoBencher achieves NOVEL=0.39±0.10 and DIFF=0.440 versus HUMANBENCH's NOVEL=0.05 and DIFF=0.103. In the safety setting, AutoBencher's dataset induces a 20% higher attack success rate (ASR) than XSTest and HarmBench. Ablating adaptive search (AutoBench-AS) degrades all metrics, particularly difficulty, confirming the value of iterative topic refinement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AutoBencher outperforms all human-constructed baselines on novelty and difficulty across every capability domain. Against MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021), AutoBencher achieves substantially higher novelty (e.g., 0.39 vs. 0.05 in History; 0.43 vs. 0.13 in Economy) and difficulty (0.440 vs. 0.103 in History; 0.321 vs. 0.056 in Economy), while also improving separability. Against XOR QA (Asai et al., 2021) for multilinguality, AutoBencher more than doubles novelty (0.57 vs. 0.24) though difficulty is lower (0.113 vs. 0.606), suggesting XOR QA is already quite hard. Against the Mathematics Dataset (Saxton et al., 2019), AutoBencher achieves much higher novelty (0.84 vs. 0.24) and difficulty (0.514 vs. 0.386). In the safety setting, AutoBencher improves ASR by 20% over both XSTest (Röttger et al., 2024) and HarmBench (Mazeika et al., 2024), though it does not outperform GCG (Zou et al., 2023) because GCG optimizes per-instance adversarial suffixes while AutoBencher searches for systematic failure categories.

## Relation to Prior Work

AutoBencher extends the LLM-as-Judge paradigm (AlpacaEval, MT-Bench) from automatic response evaluation to automatic dataset construction, and departs from LM-Examiner by grounding question generation in privileged information (Wikipedia retrieval, Python execution) rather than parametric memory, yielding substantially harder questions. It draws on adaptive dataset construction (Nie et al., 2020; Jia & Liang, 2017) but replaces human annotators with an iterative LM-based search over dataset descriptions. The key conceptual departure is casting benchmark creation as a declarative optimization problem with explicit, quantifiable desiderata (salience, difficulty, separability, novelty), which prior automatic question-generation work did not formalize.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a scalable, principled way to discover model vulnerabilities invisible to existing benchmarks, with concrete findings (e.g., Gemini-Pro's knowledge gaps on Permian Extinction, GPT-4o's failure to decline crypto-scam prompts) that are immediately actionable for model developers. Limitations include reliance on a single evaluator LM family (GPT-4), the need for human-in-the-loop quality checks, and poor coverage of low-resource languages due to translation-system dependence. Open questions include whether combining multiple evaluator LMs can eliminate family bias, whether new desiderata (diversity, coverage) yield complementary datasets, and whether the approach generalizes to non-multiple-choice evaluation formats.
