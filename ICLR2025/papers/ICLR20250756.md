## Problem

As neural networks are continually trained on sequences of changing tasks, their ability to adapt to new tasks diminishes over time (plasticity loss). Existing reset methods use fixed reset schedules and relative utility thresholds that yield suboptimal detection of inactive neurons, while regularization-based approaches can fail to learn even in the simplest non-trivial setting (a single ReLU). No prior method is simultaneously robust to hyperparameter choices and consistently performant across architectures and benchmark problems.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a stream of training examples (images at 28×28 for MNIST, 32×32 for CIFAR/ImageNet-32, or text tokens with context window 128 for Shakespeare) presented in sequential tasks, along with the current network weights. At each step it tracks per-neuron inter-firing times and produces updated weights in which neurons whose inactivity exceeds a self-normalized percentile threshold are re-initialized (input weights and bias reset randomly, output weights zeroed). The sole hyperparameter is the rejection percentile threshold η, which controls how aggressively inactive neurons are detected.

## Method

SNR is derived from an optimal hypothesis test that distinguishes a neuron firing at positive rate (null) from one that has stopped firing entirely (alternative), with a penalty for delayed detection. The optimal test rejects the null as soon as the elapsed time without a firing exceeds a fixed percentile of the inter-firing time distribution under the null; in practice, SNR approximates this distribution with the empirical histogram of each neuron's past inter-firing times. This makes the reset threshold self-normalizing: frequently firing neurons are reset after shorter inactivity periods, while rarely firing neurons are given more time, unlike fixed-threshold competitors (CBP, ReDO) that apply a uniform inactivity window. The paper proves that competing fixed-threshold schemes incur error rates that grow exponentially relative to SNR as the delay penalty shrinks, and shows theoretically that gradient descent with L2 or L2-Init regularization fails to learn a single ReLU from adversarial initializations while oracle resets achieve O(1/T) regret.

## Results

On the four benchmark continual learning problems (Permuted MNIST, Random Label MNIST, Random Label CIFAR, Continual ImageNet), SNR achieves the highest average accuracy on the last 10% of tasks across both SGD and Adam optimizers: 85.1%, 97.5%, 98.7%, and 88.8% with SGD, and 88.9%, 98.2%, 97.6%, and 84.7% with Adam. On the Permuted Shakespeare transformer experiment, SNR+L2 regularization achieves the lowest average loss (0.2551) over the final 50 tasks, compared to 0.3101 for L2 alone and 3.0147 for no intervention. SNR's single hyperparameter (rejection percentile threshold η) shows at most a 2 percentage point accuracy drop across its full sweep range, while competitors like L2 Init, S&P, and CBP lose over 72 percentage points when their hyperparameter is increased by one order of magnitude.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SNR outperforms all five baselines on every problem and optimizer combination. Against CBP (Dohare et al., 2023), SNR leads by 0.7–2.4 points with SGD and by 1.3–65 points with Adam (the large gap on RC-Adam where CBP collapses to 33.1%). Against ReDO (Sokar et al., 2023), SNR leads by 2–25 points with SGD and by 1.2–31 points with Adam. Against L2 Init (Kumar et al., 2023b), SNR leads by 2.2–6.2 points with SGD and by 0.6–1.5 points with Adam, with L2 Init being the closest competitor (and the consistent second-best under Adam). Against S&P (Ash & Adams, 2020), SNR leads by 2.1–5.5 points with SGD and by 0.3–1.4 points with Adam. Layer Normalization (Ba et al., 2016) performs worst among all interventions, often below the no-intervention baseline on PM and RM, confirming that architectural changes alone are insufficient. Notably, no single competitor is consistently second-best under SGD, whereas L2 Init holds that position under Adam.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground within the plasticity loss literature by reframing the neuron-reset decision as an optimal hypothesis testing problem over a point process, rather than relying on ad-hoc utility heuristics. It departs from existing reset methods (CBP, ReDO) which use fixed reset rates and relative-to-layer thresholds, and from regularization methods (L2 Init, Shrink and Perturb) which implicitly re-initialize weights, by proposing a self-normalized, per-neuron percentile threshold derived from a Bellman-equation solution. The theoretical analysis of learning a single ReLU extends the optimization-landscape perspective initiated by Vardi et al. (2021) to adversarial initializations, showing a clean separation between what regularization and resets can achieve.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SNR's consistent outperformance across MLPs, CNNs, and transformers—combined with its robustness to a single hyperparameter while competitors are brittle—makes it a practically attractive default for continual learning pipelines. The scaling experiments (32.9% dead neurons at 16× scale with L2 alone vs. near-zero with SNR+L2) suggest resets become increasingly critical for large language models, though the largest model tested (5M parameters) remains small by practical standards. Key open questions include whether the hypothesis-testing framing generalizes to attention mechanisms (where resets are inapplicable), whether the single-ReLU theoretical results extend to deeper networks, and how to accelerate the inherently serial evaluation of long task sequences needed to validate these findings at scale.
