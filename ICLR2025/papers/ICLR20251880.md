## Problem

State-of-the-art brain graph transformers treat all nodes equally and encode pairwise node correlations without accounting for the small-world architecture of brain graphs—specifically the presence of hubs with disproportionate roles in information propagation and functional modules with distinct segregation/integration patterns. This omission causes learned representations to lack biological plausibility, limiting their effectiveness in brain disorder detection tasks.

## Input / Output

The model takes a brain graph G=(V,E,X) constructed from resting-state fMRI data, where nodes are ROIs (200 for ABIDE/ADHD-200 via Craddock atlas, 90 for ADNI via AAL atlas), edges are weighted by Pearson correlation coefficients between ROI time series (thresholded to retain high-PCC edges), and X∈R^{n×d} is the ROI feature matrix. The output is a graph-level class label: binary (patient vs. control) for ABIDE and ADHD-200, or ternary (NC/MCI/AD) for ADNI, obtained via mean readout over node representations followed by a classifier.

## Method

BioBGT introduces two biologically motivated components into a standard Transformer backbone. First, a network entanglement-based node importance encoding computes each node's importance as the change in density matrix-based spectral entropy (S(G_i)−S(G)) when perturbing that node's local connections, then adds a learnable embedding indexed by this NE value to the node's input representation—capturing hub structure without requiring explicit degree or centrality features. Second, a functional module-aware self-attention operates on representations refined by a community contrastive extractor: the Louvain algorithm identifies functional modules, an edge-dropping augmentation (prioritizing removal of inter-module edges) generates two graph views, and an InfoNCE loss with same-module positives and cross-module negatives produces module-aware node embeddings h_i. The self-attention then applies a trainable exponential kernel over these h_i representations, and a theoretical bound (Theorem 2) guarantees that intra-module node distances remain small while inter-module distances stay large after attention, preserving functional segregation and integration in the learned representations.

## Results

BioBGT achieves the best test accuracy across all three brain graph datasets: 71.06% on ADHD-200, 74.00% on ABIDE (a 5.76% improvement over the second-best baseline BRAINNETTF at 68.24%), and 52.08% on ADNI. On the F1 score for ADHD-200, BioBGT reaches 74.63%, approximately 4.21% higher than the next-best method. However, on AUC, BRAINNETTF still leads on ADHD-200 (79.36% vs. 71.64%) and ABIDE (78.38% vs. 73.33%), while BioBGT edges out MCST-GCN on ADNI AUC (62.33% vs. 62.25%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BioBGT achieves the highest accuracy on all three datasets, outperforming the strongest graph transformer baseline BRAINNETTF by 0.26% on ADHD-200 (71.06% vs. 70.80%), 5.76% on ABIDE (74.00% vs. 68.24%), and 4.69% on ADNI (52.08% vs. 47.39%). Among graph neural network baselines, MCST-GCN is the closest competitor on ADNI (48.44% ACC, 62.25% AUC), which BioBGT surpasses on both metrics. Notably, BRAINNETTF still outperforms BioBGT on AUC for ADHD-200 (79.36% vs. 71.64%) and ABIDE (78.38% vs. 73.33%), suggesting BioBGT's advantage is most pronounced in classification accuracy rather than ranking quality. Among other graph transformers, Gradformer (68.94% ACC on ADHD-200) and SAT+PE (64.44% ACC) trail BioBGT by 2–7 percentage points in accuracy. The ablation in Table 2 further shows that replacing the network entanglement encoding with standard positional encoding, degree centrality, betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, or eigenvector centrality consistently degrades performance, with the +PE variant dropping to 60.60% ACC on ABIDE versus BioBGT's 74.00%.

## Relation to Prior Work

BioBGT extends the brain graph transformer lineage (BRAINNETTF, Graphormer, SAT) by replacing generic structural/positional encodings with two biologically motivated components: a network entanglement-based node importance encoding (drawing on Huang et al., 2024 and De Domenico & Biamonte, 2016) and a community contrastive strategy-based functional module extractor (adapting the edge-dropping augmentation from Chen et al., 2023). The key conceptual departure is from prior brain graph transformers that treat all nodes symmetrically and encode node similarity purely at the pairwise level; BioBGT explicitly encodes the small-world architecture—hubs via spectral entropy perturbation and functional modules via contrastive learning—into the representation pipeline. Theoretically, the paper provides a quantification theorem for the entanglement measure and a Lipschitz-style controllability bound for the module-aware attention, grounding the design choices in formal analysis rather than purely empirical motivation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine gap: prior brain graph transformers lack explicit encoding of the small-world properties (hubs, functional segregation/integration) that are well-established in neuroscience, and the biological plausibility validation (attention heatmaps aligning with known disease-related connectivity disruptions in ADHD, ASD, and AD) provides a useful interpretability angle beyond accuracy metrics. However, the computational cost of the NE calculation (requiring per-node graph perturbation and spectral decomposition) and the quadratic self-attention limit scalability to larger brain graphs or real-time applications. Open questions include whether the quantum-entanglement framing offers genuine algorithmic advantages over simpler global centrality measures at scale, and how to balance biological plausibility with computational efficiency as brain graph sizes grow.
