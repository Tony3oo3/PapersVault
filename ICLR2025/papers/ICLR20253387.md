## Problem

Foundation models for time series have shown promise for zero-shot forecasting on real-world data, but their ability to generalize to chaotic dynamical systems—where trajectories diverge exponentially and long-term behavior is governed by invariant attractor geometry—has never been systematically tested. Chaotic systems are ideal for this evaluation because they are absent from all public time-series training corpora (ensuring no data contamination) and possess well-defined invariant properties (Lyapunov exponents, fractal dimensions, natural measures) that allow quantifying whether a model captures the system's long-term 'climate' even after point forecasts inevitably fail.

## Input / Output

The model receives a univariate context of 512 timepoints (downsampled to 30 points per Lyapunov time) from a single coordinate of a 3–6 dimensional chaotic system, and autoregressively generates a 300-point forecast (~10 Lyapunov times). Because Chronos is inherently univariate, each attractor coordinate is forecast independently with the model state reset between channels, making the task harder than full-state prediction. Evaluation spans two complementary axes: short-term accuracy (sMAPE, Valid Prediction Time) and long-term attractor fidelity (correlation dimension error, KL divergence between true and forecasted point clouds), with context length varied from 5 to 512 and shuffled to isolate in-context learning effects.

## Method

The paper conducts the first large-scale zero-shot benchmark of the Chronos foundation model (a T5-based time-series LLM with 8M–710M parameters, pre-trained on ~10^11 observations from 42 domains) against five fully-trained baselines (NBEATS, TiDE, NVAR, LSTM, small Transformer) across 135 chaotic ODE systems × 20 initial conditions × 300 forecast points (~10^8 total timepoints). The key empirical finding is that zero-shot Chronos matches or exceeds trained baselines in the data-limited regime, with performance scaling monotonically with both model size and context length well beyond the Lyapunov decorrelation timescale. The authors identify 'context parroting' as the primary mechanism: Chronos identifies the most similar subsequence in its context to the recent history and repeats it, effectively performing pattern matching in delay-embedding space. Shuffling the context (breaking temporal order while preserving the marginal distribution) still improves forecasts, revealing that the model also learns the attractor's natural measure and conditional k-gram statistics in-context, analogous to how LLMs learn language distributions from prompts.

## Results

Across 135 chaotic dynamical systems (2,700 trajectories, ~10⁸ timepoints), the three largest Chronos zero-shot models (200M, 710M parameters) achieve a median Valid Prediction Time (VPT) of approximately 1 Lyapunov time, statistically indistinguishable from each other (Friedman test, p<10⁻³, N=135), and outperform all custom-trained baselines except NBEATS. For long-term attractor reconstruction, measured by correlation dimension accuracy, the fully-trained small Transformer matches the zero-shot Chronos models, while LSTM and NVAR show notably weaker attractor fidelity. Chronos's VPT increases monotonically with context length up to 512 points (~17 Lyapunov times), far exceeding the ~1 Lyapunov time decorrelation scale, and zero-shot inference on a single A100 GPU is computationally cheaper than training NBEATS at long context lengths.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Chronos (zero-shot) outperforms TiDE, NVAR, LSTM, and the small Transformer on short-term VPT, with the three largest Chronos variants (46M, 200M, 710M) all beating these four baselines (Friedman p<10⁻³, N=135). However, NBEATS remains the strongest baseline, outperforming all Chronos variants on both VPT and attractor reconstruction, likely because its very high pointwise accuracy inherently yields better global attractor quality. On long-term attractor reconstruction (correlation dimension), the fully-trained small Transformer surprisingly matches the zero-shot Chronos models, while LSTM and NVAR—both sequential architectures that downweight earlier context—show the weakest attractor fidelity. The paper also notes that when baselines are given full multivariate state information (rather than channel-independent univariate forecasting), all models improve, but the zero-shot Chronos gap narrows, suggesting partial observability is the key challenge. In terms of computational cost, Chronos zero-shot inference becomes cheaper than NBEATS training (including hyperparameter tuning) at long context lengths, and requires no hyperparameter tuning whatsoever.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the time-series foundation model paradigm (exemplified by Chronos, a T5-based model trained on ~10^11 observations from 42 domains) to evaluate zero-shot forecasting on chaotic dynamical systems, a domain explicitly absent from training data. It builds on the dysts benchmark (Gilpin 2021, 2023) for standardized chaotic system evaluation and on prior work showing that trained models (NBEATS, NVAR, reservoir computing) can forecast chaos by learning the underlying vector field. The key departure is testing whether in-context learning—borrowed from the LLM literature (Brown 2020; Xie et al. 2022)—can substitute for explicit training on the system's dynamics, connecting to Takens embedding and Koopman operator theory as interpretive frameworks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a model never trained on chaotic dynamics can forecast them competitively and preserve attractor geometry (fractal dimension, KL divergence) even after pointwise divergence challenges the assumption that forecasting chaos requires learning the specific flow map. The identification of context parroting as the primary mechanism, and the observation that shuffled context still aids forecasting (implying distributional in-context learning), opens questions about what foundation models actually internalize. Limitations include evaluation on a single model family (Chronos), univariate channel-independent forecasting, and low-dimensional (3–6 DOF) systems; whether these results extend to high-dimensional or non-ergodic chaos, and whether fine-tuning or low-rank adaptation can close the remaining gap to NBEATS, remain open.
