## Problem

Existing geometric benchmarks evaluate LLMs solely on final-answer accuracy, which masks whether models truly understand geometric structures—LLMs can produce correct answers by coincidence despite erroneous relationship identification. The paper isolates the fundamental sub-step of geometric relationship identification (GRI) to create a minimal, lossless evaluation of whether LLMs can accurately recognize relationships between geometric elements.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252031/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each question presents a purely textual geometric description (no diagrams) followed by a multiple-choice query asking for the relationship between two specified geometric elements (points, lines, angles, or shapes). The answer space is a finite pool of 26 specific relationships across three domains (Line-based, Angle-based, Shape-based), plus a 'Cannot be inferred' option to test recognition of underdetermined structures. The dataset is split into a basic subset (233 items) and an advanced subset (2,296 items) generated via condition concatenation, with diversity-enhancing operations including point relabeling and injection of irrelevant geometric information.

## Method

The GeomRel benchmark is constructed by first defining a finite geometric relationship pool from four fundamental elements (point, line, angle, shape), then generating basic data via a 'from definition to properties' approach, and building advanced data through rule-based condition concatenation (chains of 2–3 geometric conditions merged via Algorithm 1) plus indeterminate cases. The GeoCoT method is a two-stage prompting pipeline: Stage 1 (Geometry Breakdown) instructs the model to decompose the textual description into all explicit and implicit point/line relationships (e.g., deriving perpendicularity and equality from a rectangle definition), and Stage 2 (Relationship Observation) extracts relevant conditions from the breakdown and applies reverse reasoning—assuming a candidate relationship and working backward to check for consistency or contradiction—thereby overcoming the forward-reasoning obstacles where models skip implicit inferences. This yields average improvements of 9.15% (basic) and 14.79% (advanced) over standard Few-Shot prompting.

## Results

On the GeomRel benchmark, GPT-4o achieves the highest average accuracy at 77.86% (Basic) and 47.93% (Advanced), while the Random baseline sits at 28.95% and 27.59% respectively. The proposed GeoCoT method (Few-Shot setting) improves GPT-3.5-Turbo's average accuracy by 9.15% on Basic (from ~63.3% to 72.55%) and 14.79% on Advanced (from ~37.1% to 51.98%), surpassing all standard prompting techniques. Angle-based relationships remain the hardest domain, with even GPT-4o dropping to 29.00% on Advanced Angle-based tasks, near Random level (20.00%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's GeoCoT method is compared against three standard prompting baselines on GPT-3.5-Turbo: Zero-Shot-CoT (Kojima et al.), Few-Shot (Brown et al.), and Few-Shot-CoT (Wei et al.). GeoCoT (Few-Shot) achieves 72.55% Basic and 51.98% Advanced average accuracy, outperforming Few-Shot-CoT (70.67% / 49.94% on Line-based; 59.20% / 30.94% on Angle-based; 71.53% / 46.68% on Shape-based) and plain Few-Shot (64.87% / 45.75% Line; 53.29% / 24.30% Angle; 72.05% / 41.52% Shape). Notably, Zero-Shot-CoT actually degrades performance below Zero-Shot in several domains (e.g., Line Basic drops from 65.57% to 60.71%), demonstrating that generic 'think step by step' prompting is counterproductive for geometric reasoning. The GeoCoT advantage is most pronounced on Advanced Angle-based tasks, where it reaches 30.94% (Few-Shot-CoT) versus 21.75% (Zero-Shot) and 24.30% (Few-Shot), and the two-stage pipeline with reverse reasoning is essential—removing either Stage 1 (geometry breakdown) or the reverse-thinking component causes 3–9% accuracy drops.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from existing geometric reasoning benchmarks (Geometry3K, PGPS9K, GeoQA, GeoEval) that evaluate LLMs solely on final-answer accuracy, by isolating the geometric relationship identification (GRI) sub-step as a standalone evaluation task. Methodologically, it builds on Chain-of-Thought prompting (Wei et al., 2022) but introduces a domain-specific two-stage GeoCoT pipeline that combines explicit geometry decomposition with reverse reasoning, going beyond the generic 'think step by step' paradigm. The paper also draws on geometry education literature (Clements & Battista, 1992; Lindquist & Shulte, 1987) to motivate why GRI is the foundational step in human geometric problem solving, positioning the benchmark as a minimal but lossless probe of geometric structure comprehension.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that GRI accuracy is substantially lower than final-answer accuracy on existing datasets (e.g., 12% vs. 36% on Geometry3K) challenges the validity of current evaluation paradigms for geometric reasoning and suggests LLMs may be 'lucky' rather than genuinely understanding spatial structures. The GeomRel benchmark provides a focused, scalable tool for tracking progress on this specific capability, while GeoCoT offers a practical prompting strategy that yields meaningful gains (~9–15%) without fine-tuning. Key limitations include the restriction to text-based planar geometry (no diagrams, no 3D), and the observation that fine-tuning on GRI data does not reliably improve performance, leaving open whether the bottleneck is architectural or data-driven. Future work could extend the benchmark to multimodal inputs, solid geometry, and investigate whether GeoCoT's reverse-reasoning component transfers to other spatial reasoning domains.
