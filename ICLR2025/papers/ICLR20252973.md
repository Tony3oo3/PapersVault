## Problem

Random walk neural networks (RWNNs) have shown empirical success on graph tasks but lack a principled theoretical foundation: it is unclear how to systematically enforce isomorphism invariance, what their expressive power upper bound is, and whether the well-known over-smoothing and over-squashing pathologies of message passing neural networks also afflict them. This paper provides the first unified analysis addressing all three questions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252973/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a graph (undirected or directed, optionally with vertex attributes such as paper titles and abstracts) and an optional query vertex, then produces a plain-text record of a random walk where vertices are anonymized by discovery order and previously-seen neighbors are explicitly named. This text record is fed to a reader neural network (a small transformer, DeBERTa, or Llama 3) that outputs a graph-level or vertex-level prediction. For transductive classification, labeled vertices encountered during the walk are recorded inline with their labels, effectively turning the task into an in-context learning problem for the language model.

## Method

The paper formalizes RWNNs as a three-component pipeline (random walk → recording function → reader NN) and shows that probabilistic isomorphism invariance can be guaranteed by making the walk and recording invariant while leaving the reader NN unconstrained. The recording uses anonymization (naming vertices 1, 2, 3… by first discovery) plus a novel named-neighbors mechanism that records edges to already-discovered vertices, ensuring the full induced subgraph is captured once all vertices are visited. The walk uses a minimum-degree local rule (MDLR) conductance with non-backtracking, achieving O(n²) vertex cover time and enabling universal approximation in probability; restarts are added for vertex-level tasks on large or infinite graphs to guarantee finite local cover times. Theoretically, the paper proves that over-smoothing is inherently absent (the output converges to xᵀπ rather than a constant) while over-squashing manifests as probabilistic under-reaching, mitigated simply by longer or faster-mixing walks. Empirically, a frozen DeBERTa language model reading anonymized walk text achieves 100% accuracy on strongly regular graph separation where 3-WL fails, and a frozen Llama 3 model achieves 74.85% on ogbn-arxiv transductive classification without any training, outperforming standard MPNNs.

## Results

On graph isomorphism learning, RWNN-DeBERTa achieves 100% accuracy on all three challenging datasets (CSL, SR16, SR25) where 1-WL, 2-WL, and 3-WL tests respectively fail, making it the first RWNN to solve SR25. On transductive classification over the 170k-vertex ogbn-arxiv citation network, RWNN-Llama3-70b (with in-context labels) reaches 74.85% test accuracy, outperforming GAT (73.91%), RevGAT (74.26%), and fine-tuned DeBERTa (74.13%). On the synthetic Clique (over-smoothing) and Barbell (over-squashing) benchmarks, RWNN-transformer with l=1000 achieves 0.023 and 0.007 test MSE respectively, the best among all compared methods including BuNN (0.03/0.01) and NSD (0.08/1.09).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On isomorphism learning, RWNN-DeBERTa (100% on all three datasets) decisively outperforms MPNNs aligned with WL tests: GIN (1-WL) scores only 10%/50%/6.7% on CSL/SR16/SR25, and PPGN (2-WL) reaches 100% on CSL but drops to 50%/6.7% on SR16/SR25. Among prior RWNNs, CRaWl (100%/100%/46.6%) and AgentNet (100%/100%/6.7%) both fail on SR25 due to limited receptive field and policy-learning difficulty respectively, while RWNN-DeBERTa solves it by leveraging the full sequence capacity of a pre-trained language model. The only prior method matching 100% on SR25 is GIN+ELENE-L(ED), which requires specialized edge-level ego-network structural features. On ogbn-arxiv, RWNN-Llama3-70b (74.85%) beats GAT (73.91%), RevGAT (74.26%), fine-tuned DeBERTa (74.13%), and GPT-3.5 zero-shot (73.50%), while also substantially outperforming one-shot Llama3-8b (52.82%) and Llama3-70b (67.65%) baselines that are given 40 random labeled examples. On the synthetic over-smoothing/over-squashing benchmarks, RWNN-transformer (0.023/0.007 MSE) outperforms BuNN (0.03/0.01), NSD (0.08/1.09), and GCN (29.65/1.05), confirming that RWNNs avoid the over-smoothing/over-squashing trade-off inherent to MPNNs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first principled theoretical framework for random walk neural networks (RWNNs), a class of methods that has been explored heuristically since DeepWalk and node2vec but lacked systematic analysis of invariance, expressiveness, and information propagation. It builds on probabilistic invariance theory (Bloem-Reddy & Teh, 2020; Puny et al., 2022) to show that invariance can be imposed on the random walk and recording function rather than the reader network, repurposing anonymization (originally a privacy mechanism from Micali & Zhu, 2016) as an invariance guarantee. The walk design draws on MDLR (David & Feige, 2018) for optimal cover times and non-backtracking (Alon et al., 2007) for faster mixing, while the reader NN is deliberately left unconstrained—enabling the use of pre-trained language models (DeBERTa, Llama 3) as a novel architectural choice not explored in prior RWNN work like CRaWl or AgentNet.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a long-standing gap in understanding RWNNs by proving that over-smoothing is inherently avoided by construction (unlike MPNNs) while over-squashing manifests as probabilistic under-reaching, eliminating the typical trade-off and giving practitioners clear design guidance (longer or faster-mixing walks). The practical implication of using frozen LLMs as reader networks—turning transductive graph classification into in-context learning—is a broadly applicable paradigm that could reduce the need for task-specific training on large graphs. Open questions include whether the cover-time-based walk length requirements scale gracefully to very large graphs, whether the in-context learning framing generalizes beyond citation networks, and how to optimally balance walk length against the context window limits of language models.
