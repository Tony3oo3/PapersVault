## Problem

Existing multi-task optimization methods (gradient manipulation, loss weighting) focus on balancing the influence of tasks on shared parameters but neglect how task-specific parameters are learned. Since shared and task-specific parameter learning interact during optimization, this oversight limits their ability to mitigate negative transfer. The paper asks: can we improve multi-task performance by selectively grouping tasks and updating them sequentially rather than jointly?

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB or RGB-D images (e.g., NYUD-V2 indoor scenes, PASCAL-Context outdoor scenes, Taskonomy multi-view captures) and produces dense per-pixel predictions across multiple tasks: semantic segmentation masks, depth maps, surface normal maps, edge maps, saliency maps, and keypoint maps. A key operational detail is that each input image is forwarded through the shared ViT encoder M times per batch (where M is the current number of task groups, typically 1.8–4 on Taskonomy's 11 tasks), with each pass backpropagating only the losses of one task group before the next group is updated.

## Method

The core mechanism is a dynamic task-grouping algorithm driven by a new metric called proximal inter-task affinity, which measures how much one task group's gradient update (to both shared and task-specific parameters) changes another task's loss. During each batch, tasks are partitioned into M mutually exclusive groups based on tracked affinity (with exponential decay for stability), and each group is updated sequentially via its own forward/backward pass. Tasks with positive affinity are merged into the same group; tasks with negative affinity are separated. The theoretical analysis (Theorems 1–5) shows that grouping tasks with high affinity aligns their shared-parameter gradients, and that sequential group updates specifically enhance task-specific parameter learning in a way that joint backpropagation cannot replicate, yielding lower multi-task loss with comparable convergence stability.

## Results

On NYUD-v2, the proposed method achieves a multi-task performance of Δm% = −2.58%, outperforming the best gradient-based baseline Nash-MTL (−3.14%) and the hybrid IMTL (−3.31%). On PASCAL-Context, it reaches Δm% = −6.24%, surpassing IMTL (−7.07%) and Aligned-MTL (−7.20%). On the 11-task Taskonomy benchmark with ViT-L, the method achieves Δm% = −1.42%, dramatically outperforming the best baseline Aligned-MTL (−6.22%), while MGDA and Nash-MTL fail to converge entirely.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all 11 baselines on the headline Δm% metric across all three benchmarks. On NYUD-v2, it beats the closest competitors Nash-MTL (−3.14%) and IMTL (−3.31%) by 0.56 and 0.73 percentage points respectively, while also achieving the best per-task scores in semantic segmentation (40.02 mIoU vs. single-task 39.35) and depth (0.618 RMSE vs. 0.661). On PASCAL-Context, the gap to the best baseline IMTL (−7.07%) is 0.83 points, with the method also leading in semantic segmentation (68.14 vs. 63.91 mIoU) and scene parsing (57.15 vs. 55.23 mIoU). On the 11-task Taskonomy benchmark, the advantage is most dramatic: −1.42% vs. Aligned-MTL's −6.22%, a 4.8-point margin, while MGDA and Nash-MTL fail to converge at all. Loss-based methods (UW, DTP, DWA, FAMO) cluster tightly around −6 to −9.5% on both NYUD-v2 and PASCAL-Context, showing limited gains over plain gradient descent (GD). Gradient-based methods that manipulate per-task gradients (PCGrad, CAGrad, GradDrop) improve over GD but remain 2–4 points behind the proposed approach, and Aligned-MTL, while the strongest gradient-based method on Taskonomy, still trails by nearly 5 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the inter-task affinity concept from Fifty et al. (2021), which was originally used to partition tasks into separate networks, into a dynamic, within-network optimization strategy via proximal inter-task affinity. It departs fundamentally from the dominant multi-task optimization paradigm—gradient manipulation (PCGrad, CAGrad, MGDA, Nash-MTL, Aligned-MTL) and loss reweighting (UW, DWA, FAMO)—which all operate within a single joint update step and focus on balancing shared-parameter gradients. The key conceptual departure is the argument that these methods neglect the learning of task-specific parameters, and that sequentially updating dynamically-formed task groups within a single batch addresses this gap in a way conventional convergence analysis cannot explain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a practical and computationally efficient alternative to gradient-manipulation methods, requiring only M forward/backward passes (M < K) rather than K, while achieving the best multi-task performance across three benchmarks. The theoretical contribution—showing that sequential group updates enhance task-specific parameter learning in a manner invisible to standard convergence analysis—opens a new analytical angle for multi-task optimization. Open questions include whether the assumption of correlation between shared and task-specific parameter learning holds in non-convex regimes, how the method behaves with very large task counts (beyond 11), and whether the grouping dynamics interact with other architectural choices like mixture-of-experts or branched networks.
