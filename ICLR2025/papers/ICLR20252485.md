## Problem

Existing data influence estimators (influence functions, TracIn, Data Shapley) assume a single centralized model and compute influence statically post-training, making them inapplicable to fully decentralized learning where data influence dynamically cascades through gossip communication to multi-hop neighbors. No prior work provides a principled way to quantify how a local data instance's contribution propagates and attenuates across an arbitrary peer-to-peer network topology during training.

## Input / Output

The method consumes, at each iteration t, the local training data instance z_j^t, the current model parameters θ_k^t at every node, the row-stochastic communication matrix W^t encoding the gossip topology, and a held-out evaluation instance z'. It produces a scalar DICE-E influence score per data instance (additive over mini-batches), a peer-level proximal influence score computable locally by each participant using only information available under the gossip protocol, and a reciprocity factor measuring the balance of mutual influence between collaborators. The multi-hop DICE-E additionally requires Hessian evaluations at intermediate nodes, making it computationally heavier than the one-hop variant used in practice.

## Method

DICE first defines a ground-truth influence cascade (DICE-GT) via leave-one-out counterfactuals extended to decentralized settings: the one-hop version (Definition 2) captures both the direct loss change at the source node and the indirect loss change at immediate neighbors after gossip averaging, while the multi-hop version (Definition 3) recursively extends this to r-hop neighbors. The key theoretical contribution (Theorem 2) derives a tractable first-order approximation (DICE-E) showing that r-hop influence factorizes into a product of path communication weights, the test gradient at the destination, Hessian-based curvature terms (I − ηH) at each intermediate node, and the local optimization step Δ_j at the source. This reveals that influence decays exponentially with hop count and is jointly shaped by data quality, topological centrality, and loss-landscape curvature. For practical deployment, the paper introduces a proximal influence (Definition 4) that each participant can compute locally from gradient inner products, enabling collaborator selection, reciprocity-based fair collaboration (Definition 5), and anomaly detection of malicious neighbors without requiring global model access.

## Results

The paper validates DICE-E (the gradient-based estimator) against DICE-GT (the leave-one-out ground truth) on a 32-node ring graph using ResNet-18 and MLP models across CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and Tiny ImageNet (512 samples per node, 5 epochs, batch size 128, lr=0.1). DICE-E closely tracks DICE-GT across all three datasets and both architectures, with alignment becoming stronger on the simpler CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 datasets. For anomaly detection on a 32-node exponential graph (CIFAR-10, 5 neighbors per node), malicious neighbors introduced via random label flipping or Gaussian noise are readily identifiable by their elevated proximal influence scores. The influence cascade visualization on a 16-node topology confirms the theoretical prediction that nodes with higher outgoing communication weights in W exhibit significantly larger DICE-E influence scores, demonstrating topological power asymmetries.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark DICE against competing data-influence estimation methods in a head-to-head comparison; instead, it validates its estimator against the leave-one-out ground truth (DICE-GT) as the reference standard. The one-hop DICE-E approximation is shown to align closely with DICE-GT across datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, Tiny ImageNet) and architectures (CNN, MLP), with the gap narrowing on simpler datasets. The paper positions itself against TracIn (Pruthi et al., 2020) and influence functions (Koh & Liang, 2017) as the centralized baselines that DICE generalizes: the first term of DICE-GT reduces to TracInIdeal in the centralized case, while the additional multi-hop terms capture the cascade unique to decentralized gossip. The closest prior work, Terashita & Hara (2022), is noted as providing only a static decentralized hyper-gradient estimate that cannot capture the dynamic influence cascade. No quantitative head-to-head numbers against these baselines are reported; the contribution is primarily theoretical (Theorem 2) with empirical validation of the approximation quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

DICE extends the centralized data influence estimation lineage—rooted in influence functions (Koh & Liang, 2017) and dynamic gradient-tracing methods like TracIn (Pruthi et al., 2020)—to fully decentralized learning, where influence propagates recursively through gossip communication rather than being computed on a single static model. The closest prior work, Terashita & Hara (2022), applies hyper-gradients in a decentralized setting but remains static and cannot capture the multi-hop cascade. DICE's key conceptual departure is formalizing influence as a dynamic, topology-mediated cascade, yielding a tractable r-hop approximation (Theorem 2) that factorizes into data, communication weights, and intermediate-node curvature—none of which appear in centralized estimators.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DICE provides the first principled framework for attributing contributions in decentralized learning, a prerequisite for fair incentive mechanisms, collaborator selection, and free-rider detection in peer-to-peer training ecosystems. The three-factor decomposition (data × topology × curvature) offers actionable insight: topologically central nodes exert disproportionate influence, suggesting that incentive schemes must account for network position, not just data quality. However, empirical validation is limited to 32-node graphs on CIFAR-10/100 and Tiny ImageNet with small models (ResNet-18, MLP), and the Hessian-based multi-hop terms are computationally expensive at scale. Open questions include whether the cascade approximation remains tight under dynamic topologies, asynchronous updates, or Byzantine participants, and whether the framework scales to the thousands of nodes envisioned for practical decentralized training.
