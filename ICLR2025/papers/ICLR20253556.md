## Problem

Previous protein structure generative models were limited to small equivariant networks trained on at most ~500k structures, offered no semantic control during synthesis, and could not scale to long chains. The paper asks whether protein backbone generation can be scaled analogously to image and language generative models—using large transformer architectures, multi-million-structure datasets, and hierarchical semantic conditioning—while maintaining designability and diversity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253556/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a 3L-dimensional Gaussian noise vector (Cα coordinates only, no rotation frames) and optionally hierarchical CATH fold class labels at three granularity levels (C: secondary-structure content, A: architecture, T: topology/fold) as conditioning. For motif scaffolding, it additionally receives the fixed motif coordinates (zeroed at non-motif positions) and a binary motif mask. The output is a full 3D Cα backbone of up to 800 residues, sampled by integrating the learned vector field ODE or an equivalent SDE with tunable noise scale γ.

## Method

Proteina uses rectified flow matching (linear interpolant x_t = t·x₁ + (1−t)·x₀) to learn a vector field mapping Gaussian noise to protein Cα coordinates, implemented in a non-equivariant transformer with up to 400M parameters. The architecture borrows from vision DiT/SiT designs—QK normalization, register tokens, adaptive layer norms for t and fold-class conditioning—and optionally includes triangle multiplicative layers for pair-representation updates, but achieves top performance without them, enabling scaling to 800-residue chains. Training uses hierarchical label dropout (50% unconditional, 10% C-only, 15% C+A, 25% full CAT), self-conditioning, a novel Beta-distributed t-sampling schedule, and an auxiliary distogram loss. The paper introduces classifier-free guidance and autoguidance (using an early checkpoint as the 'bad' model) for protein backbones, LoRA fine-tuning on natural PDB proteins, and three new distributional metrics (FPSD, fJSD, fS) based on a fold-class predictor's feature space to score models at the distribution level rather than per-sample.

## Results

Proteina achieves state-of-the-art unconditional protein backbone generation: M_21M reaches 99.0% designability (vs. 97.2% for the best baseline FoldFlow-OT), while M_FS (γ=0.45) achieves 96.4% designability with 0.63 cluster diversity (305 designable clusters), outperforming all baselines on both metrics. The 21M-data model (M_21M, γ=0.3) also attains the best novelty vs. PDB (0.81) and competitive FPSD (280.7). The LoRA-fine-tuned variant (M_LoRA) further reduces FPSD vs. PDB to 274.1 and fJSD vs. PDB to 0.79, while doubling β-sheet content to 13.0%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Proteina dominates on designability: M_21M reaches 99.0%, surpassing FoldFlow-OT (97.2%), Genie2 (95.2%), RFDiffusion (94.4%), and Proteus (94.2%), while M_FS (96.4%) also beats all equivariant baselines. On diversity (designable clusters), M_FS (305 clusters) and M_no-tri (292) outperform every baseline, including Genie2 (281) and FrameFlow (236), with the triangle-free M_no-tri matching the full model's diversity despite removing all pair-track updates. On novelty vs. PDB, M_21M (0.81) leads all methods, while M_FS is competitive (0.69) but trails Genie2 (0.63) and FrameFlow (0.69) on AFDB novelty. The new distributional metrics (FPSD, fJSD) show mixed results: FrameFlow (129.9) and Chroma (189.0) achieve lower FPSD vs. PDB than Proteina's main variants, but M_LoRA narrows the gap to 274.1 and achieves the best fJSD vs. PDB (0.79), validating that LoRA fine-tuning on natural PDB proteins shifts the generated distribution closer to real structures. In fold-class-conditional generation (Table 2), Proteina (M_FS^cond) achieves 91.4% designability vs. Chroma's 57.0%, with substantially better FPSD (121.1 vs. 157.8) and fJSD (0.58 vs. 0.84) vs. PDB, while also increasing β-sheet content from 11.1% to 13.7%. In motif-scaffolding (Table 6), Proteina achieves 2094 unique successes vs. Genie2 (1445), FrameFlow (1099), and RFDiffusion (889), winning 8 of 24 individual tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

Proteina extends the flow-matching framework (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023) to protein backbone generation by replacing the equivariant networks that have dominated the field (FrameDiff, FoldFlow, FrameFlow, RFDiffusion) with a non-equivariant transformer architecture inspired by vision diffusion transformers (Peebles & Xie, 2023) and AlphaFold3. It introduces hierarchical CATH fold class conditioning as a semantic control mechanism, drawing an analogy to class/text conditioning in image generation, and adapts classifier-free guidance (Ho & Salimans, 2021) and autoguidance (Karras et al., 2024) to this setting for the first time. The paper also brings LoRA fine-tuning (Hu et al., 2022) to protein structure flow models and introduces distributional evaluation metrics (FPSD, fJSD, fS) analogous to FID/Inception Score in image generation, filling a gap where prior work relied solely on sample-level designability, diversity, and novelty scores.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Proteina demonstrates that the scaling paradigm from vision and language (large non-equivariant transformers, massive synthetic datasets, semantic conditioning) transfers to protein structure generation, pushing the field from ~600k training structures to 21M and from ~60M to 400M+ parameters. The ability to generate designable backbones up to 800 residues and to control secondary structure content via fold class guidance opens new design tasks previously infeasible. Open questions include whether the non-equivariant approach generalizes to sequence-structure co-design or all-atom generation, whether the new distributional metrics become standard in the community, and whether the 21M synthetic dataset's strong filtering (pLDDT ≥ 85, radius of gyration ≤ 3nm) inadvertently biases the learned distribution away from biologically relevant but structurally atypical proteins.
