## Problem

Existing scaling laws for language models are studied in the compute-optimal regime and predict next-token prediction loss, but in practice models are over-trained (trained with far more tokens than compute-optimal) to reduce inference costs, and practitioners compare models on downstream benchmark accuracy rather than perplexity. It remains unclear whether scaling laws extrapolate reliably when both model size and the degree of over-training increase beyond the fitting range, and whether loss-based scaling can be connected to downstream task performance.

## Input / Output

The two-stage prediction pipeline takes training compute C (in FLOPs, approximated as 6ND) and a token multiplier M = D/N as inputs to the first scaling law, which outputs a predicted validation loss L. This loss is then fed into a second scaling law that outputs the predicted average top-1 error across 17 downstream tasks. The token multiplier M is the key new parameter: M ≈ 20 corresponds to compute-optimal training, while larger values (up to 640) encode progressively more over-training. The downstream error law is parameterized in terms of perplexity PP = exp(L), making it a power law Err(PP) = ε − k·PP^{−γ} bounded above by ε.

## Method

The paper reparameterizes the standard scaling law L(N,D) = E + AN^{−α} + BD^{−β} (assuming α = β as found by Hoffmann et al.) into L(C,M) = E + (aM^{η} + bM^{−η})C^{−η}, where η = α/2. This form reveals that the scaling exponent η is invariant to the token multiplier M (producing parallel lines in log-log plots of reducible loss vs. compute), while the prefactor aM^{η} + bM^{−η} shifts the intercept, enabling extrapolation in both N and M simultaneously. For downstream prediction, the authors propose Err(L) = ε − k·exp(−γL), an exponential decay of average top-1 error in validation loss, which becomes a power law in perplexity. The two laws are chained: fit (C,M)→L from small-scale runs, then L→Err, to predict downstream performance without training the target model. The approach is validated on a testbed of 104 transformer models (0.011B–6.9B parameters) trained on C4, RedPajama, and RefinedWeb across 8 token multipliers, achieving 0.7% relative error in loss prediction and 0.05% in average downstream error prediction for a 6.9B model using 20× less compute.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that a scaling law (Eq. 4) fit on small models (0.011B–0.411B parameters) predicts C4 eval validation loss for a 1.4B-parameter, 900B-token over-trained model (32× over Chinchilla-optimal) with only 0.7% relative error, using 300× less compute than the target run. For the 6.9B-parameter, 138B-token compute-optimal model, the same fit also achieves 0.7% relative error. Chaining this loss prediction with a loss-to-error power law (Eq. 5) predicts the average top-1 error over 17 downstream tasks for the 6.9B model to within 0.05% relative error (20× less compute) and the 1.4B over-trained model to within 3.6% relative error.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing scaling-law formulations; instead, it validates its proposed functional forms (Eq. 4 for over-training loss, Eq. 5 for loss-to-error mapping) against ground-truth measurements from 104 trained models. The key empirical finding is that the scaling exponent η remains constant across token multipliers M (5–640), while the scalar λ shifts, confirming that parallel power-law lines in log-log space are the correct structure for over-trained regimes. A critical ablation shows that removing the 1.4B model from the Eq. 5 fit causes the 6.9B RedPajama prediction error to jump from 0.05% to 10.64%, demonstrating that downstream error prediction requires substantially more compute (~1,000 A100-hours) than loss prediction (~100 A100-hours). Individual downstream task predictions remain unreliable (errors up to 82% on HellaSwag for RefinedWeb), but the 17-task average is consistently within 3% relative error across all three training distributions, making aggregate performance the practical target for scaling-law-based derisking.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reparameterizes the Chinchilla scaling law (Hoffmann et al., 2022) and the Rosenfeld et al. (2020) risk decomposition in terms of a token multiplier M = D/N and compute C, yielding a closed-form expression (Eq. 4) that explicitly separates the effect of over-training from the effect of scale. While Hoffmann et al. established compute-optimality and Sardana & Frankle (2023) argued for inference-aware allocations, neither validated scaling behavior in the over-trained regime or connected loss to downstream benchmark performance. The downstream error law (Eq. 5) generalizes Schaeffer et al.'s (2023) observation that non-linear metrics complicate prediction by fitting a bounded exponential decay between perplexity and average top-1 error across 17 tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Over-training smaller models is now the dominant practice for production LLMs (LLaMA-2 7B uses M≈290 vs. Chinchilla's M≈20), yet no prior work had validated that scaling laws remain reliable in this regime or provided a method to predict downstream benchmark performance from small-scale experiments. The 104-model testbed across three datasets and the demonstrated 300× compute extrapolation with <1% relative error give practitioners a concrete, low-cost procedure for derisking training runs. Key open questions include whether these trends hold at much larger scales (100B+), whether post-training (fine-tuning, RLHF) preserves the loss-to-error relationship, and whether individual task predictions can be made as reliable as aggregate predictions.
