## Problem

Gaussian Belief Propagation (GaBP) handles complex graphical model structures but scales as O(D³) in node dimension, making it infeasible for high-dimensional spatiotemporal and physical systems. The Ensemble Kalman Filter (EnKF) handles high dimensions efficiently via empirical ensemble statistics but is restricted to state-space filtering and cannot propagate messages over general factor graphs. No existing method combines EnKF's dimension-agnostic efficiency with GaBP's general graphical model inference capability.

## Input / Output

GEnBP consumes N prior ensemble samples (typically N≈64, far smaller than the state dimension D which can reach 10⁶), noisy evidence observations, a black-box generative simulator (no Jacobian required), and a factor graph structure. It produces posterior ensemble samples conforming to the updated beliefs, with all Gaussian parameters stored in Diagonal-plus-Low-rank (DLR) form—i.e., a diagonal nugget plus an N-rank perturbation—rather than full D×D covariance matrices. The ensemble conformation step (Proposition 4) recovers sample-level posteriors via an affine transformation of the prior ensemble, enabling downstream use of individual samples rather than just moments.

## Method

GEnBP replaces GaBP's δ-method (first-order Taylor linearization at the mode) with EnKF-style empirical moment estimation: N simulator evaluations at ensemble members yield joint covariances directly, bypassing both the Jensen gap and Taylor error of linearization. All Gaussian beliefs are maintained in DLR form (K = V + sLLᵀ with L ∈ ℝ^{D×N}), so density multiplication, conditioning, and marginalization reduce to O(N³ + N²D) operations via the Woodbury identity instead of O(D³) full-matrix inversions. The algorithm iterates four steps: (1) ancestral sampling from the generative model, (2) conversion of empirical ensemble statistics to canonical DLR parameters, (3) loopy belief propagation of DLR messages over the factor graph, and (4) ensemble conformation—an affine transformation T found by minimizing the Frobenius-norm gap between the transformed ensemble covariance and the target DLR belief. This yields O(D) scaling in dimension (vs. O(D³) for GaBP) at the cost of O(K³) scaling in node degree, which can be mitigated via Forney factorization.

## Results

On the 1D transport system identification problem, GEnBP achieves MSE = 0.167 and posterior log-likelihood −49.6, compared to GaBP's MSE = 1.93 and log-likelihood −228, representing an ~11× reduction in MSE. On the 2D Navier-Stokes fluid dynamics problem, GEnBP scales to D_Q well beyond 1024 (where GaBP exhausts memory) and attains superior MSE and posterior likelihood versus GaBP, while Langevin Monte Carlo achieves the best RMSE at roughly 10³× the computational cost of GEnBP. GEnBP strictly dominates GaBP in wall-clock speed across all tested viscosities and dimensions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GaBP (Ortiz et al., 2021), GEnBP wins decisively on the 1D transport problem (MSE 0.167 vs. 1.93; log-likelihood −49.6 vs. −228) and on the 2D Navier-Stokes problem in both MSE and posterior log-likelihood in the turbulent (low-ν) regime, while GaBP performs slightly better in the laminar (high-ν) regime. GEnBP also wins on scalability: GaBP runs out of memory for D_Q > 1024, whereas GEnBP continues to operate. Against the global Laplace approximation (Mackay, 1992), GEnBP achieves lower MSE and more stable posterior log-likelihood, particularly as dimension increases beyond 100, though Laplace is faster in wall-clock time. Against Langevin Monte Carlo (Roberts & Tweedie, 1996), LMC achieves the best posterior RMSE on the 2D fluid problem but at approximately 1000× the computational cost of GEnBP and is not competitive on posterior log-likelihood. In the few-shot domain adaptation experiment (Appendix B.3), GEnBP-adapted FNO emulator closely tracks the target state distribution over subsequent timesteps while the unadapted emulator diverges, demonstrating practical utility beyond the system identification benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

GEnBP unifies two well-established but previously separate lineages: the Ensemble Kalman Filter (Evensen, 2003), which performs high-dimensional inference via empirical ensemble statistics and the Matheron (pathwise) conditioning update, and Gaussian Belief Propagation (Yedidia et al., 2005; Kschischang et al., 2001), which performs density-based message passing on factor graphs using the δ-method for nonlinear factors. The paper's key conceptual move is to replace GaBP's first-order Taylor linearization with EnKF-style empirical moment estimation at each factor, while retaining the factor-graph message-passing structure to handle arbitrary (non-chain) dependency graphs. The technical enabler is the Diagonal-plus-Low-rank (DLR) matrix representation, which allows all Gaussian operations (conditioning, marginalization, multiplication, inversion) to be performed in O(N²D) rather than O(D³), where N ≪ D is the ensemble size.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GEnBP addresses a genuine practical bottleneck: GaBP's O(D³) covariance operations make it infeasible beyond ~1000 dimensions, while standard EnKF cannot handle general graphical model structures (only state-space chains). By scaling to millions of dimensions and handling black-box simulators without Jacobians, GEnBP opens the door to joint state-parameter inference in complex physical systems (CFD, geophysics, weather) that were previously out of reach for message-passing methods. Key limitations remain: the Gaussian approximation restricts it to unimodal posteriors, convergence of the loopy BP iterations is not analyzed, and the method introduces additional hyperparameters (nugget terms, ensemble size, relinearization frequency). The full characterization of when GEnBP outperforms GaBP (e.g., the viscosity-dependent crossover in the Navier-Stokes experiments) is left open, as is the integration of practical EnKF stabilizers like covariance localization and inflation.
