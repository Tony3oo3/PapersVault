## Problem

Whether LLMs genuinely generalize to unseen tasks or predominantly memorize pretraining data remains debated, but prior analyses rely on verbatim recall of long text segments or counterfactual memorization that requires expensive model retraining. No scalable framework exists to quantify how different LLM capabilities (translation, factual QA, reasoning) depend on memorization versus generalization across the full pretraining corpus.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251795/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes supervised task input-output pairs (x, y) to mine semantically related n-gram pairs, then searches the full pretraining corpus (Pile ~207B tokens or Dolma ~3T tokens) via WIMBD for co-occurrence counts of those pairs within shared documents. The primary output is a distributional memorization score (Spearman correlation between task-gram LM log-probabilities and LLM log-probabilities over test examples), supplemented by gradient-based influence estimates computed over evenly spaced pretraining checkpoints. A secondary output is an optimized task prompt whose n-gram frequency in the corpus is tuned to encourage either memorization or generalization.

## Method

The core contribution is the task-gram language model, which captures long-range, task-relevant dependencies by counting co-occurrences of semantically paired n-grams (s^x from input, s^y from output) within the same pretraining document, rather than modeling local prefix-based probabilities as in classical or ∞-gram LMs. N-gram pairs are mined from supervised task data using embedding cosine similarity (LASER for translation, E5 for other tasks) with task-specific thresholds, then their joint and marginal counts in the pretraining corpus define the conditional probability P(s^y|s^x) = C((s^x,s^y), D) / C(s^x, D). Distributional memorization is then measured as the Spearman correlation between log task-gram LM probabilities and log LLM probabilities over all n-gram pairs found in test examples, with distributional generalization defined as its complement. The approach is complemented by a gradient-based influence function (following Pruthi et al., 2020) that accumulates dot products of training and test loss gradients across pretraining checkpoints to provide causal evidence of data influence, and by a GPT-4o-driven prompt optimizer that iteratively rewrites task instructions to maximize or minimize n-gram frequency in the pretraining corpus.

## Results

On TriviaQA, distributional memorization (Spearman correlation between task-gram LM and LLM probabilities) reaches Mem_{n=3} > 0.35 and Mem_{n=5} < 0.25, with memorization increasing as model size grows. WMT shows no statistically significant memorization (p > 0.05) across all model sizes, while GSM8K also shows no significant memorization but increasing distributional generalization (Kendall tau ranking distance) with model size. In the prompt optimization experiment (Table 1), memorization-encouraged prompts boost TriviaQA accuracy from 9% to 28.7% (Pythia 12B), while generalization-encouraged prompts improve GSM8K accuracy from 2.6% to 7.9% (OLMo-instruct 7B), confirming that knowledge-intensive tasks benefit from memorization and reasoning tasks from generalization.

**Table 1: Zero-shot accuracy on TriviaQA and GSM8K test set with memorization-encouraged (maximize n-gram counts) and generalization-encouraged (minimize n-gram counts) prompts.**

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pythia (6.9B) | 17.0 | 9.0 | 2.6 | 2.8 |
| Pythia-Instruct (6.9B) | 23.5 | 23.2 | 6.3 | 7.3 |
| Pythia (12B) | **28.7** | 23.2 | 2.7 | 2.8 |
| OLMo (7B) | **36.4** | **29.8** | 2.5 | 3.1 |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is the ∞-gram language model (Liu et al., 2024), which estimates pretraining data distribution using unbounded n-gram backoff. The proposed task-gram LM consistently achieves higher distributional memorization correlation (Mem_n ≥ Mem_∞) on both TriviaQA and MMLU, indicating it better captures the task-relevant data distribution that LLMs actually memorize. Larger LLMs show a decreasing ∞-gram memorization effect while the task-gram LM memorization remains stable or increases (for TriviaQA), further demonstrating the ∞-gram LM's inability to model long-range, task-specific dependencies. The task-gram LM's advantage stems from counting co-occurrences of semantically related n-gram pairs across documents, capturing dependencies that span significant distances, whereas the ∞-gram LM only models local lexical context within a single n-gram window. On GSM8K, the task-gram LM also yields higher distributional generalization (lower Kendall tau distance) than the ∞-gram LM, confirming its superior ability to characterize task-relevant pretraining data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the memorization-vs-generalization literature (Feldman 2020; Zhang et al. 2023) by replacing the expensive counterfactual (retraining-based) definition with a scalable 'distributional memorization' metric based on Spearman correlation between LLM output probabilities and n-gram frequency in the pretraining corpus. Methodologically, it builds on the ∞-gram LM framework (Liu et al. 2024) and WIMBD retrieval system (Elazar et al. 2024) but departs from both by introducing the task-gram language model, which captures long-range, task-relevant dependencies through semantically matched n-gram pair co-occurrences rather than local prefix-based n-gram probabilities. For causal validation, it adapts the gradient-based influence function of Pruthi et al. (2020) to the pretraining setting, providing complementary evidence beyond correlation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first scalable, full-corpus analysis of how different LLM capabilities (translation, factual QA, world knowledge, math reasoning) trace back to pretraining data, revealing that knowledge-intensive tasks benefit from memorization while reasoning-intensive tasks benefit from generalization as model size grows. The practical prompt-optimization application (Section 7) demonstrates that this analysis can directly improve task performance by steering prompts toward or away from pretraining distribution. Key limitations include reliance on the somewhat outdated Pythia/Pile combination, sensitivity of task-gram quality to the embedding-similarity threshold, and the fact that WIMBD struggles with larger corpora like Dolma (3T tokens), leaving open whether findings generalize to frontier-scale models and more diverse task families.
