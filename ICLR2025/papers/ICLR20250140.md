## Problem

Existing molecule-text cross-modal models rely on global alignment that fails to capture fine-grained correspondences such as functional groups matching specific phrases or stereoisomeric distinctions (e.g., D- vs. L-glutamate). No annotated dataset exists for explicit fragment-level correspondence between SMILES substructures and text phrases, making supervised fine-grained alignment infeasible. Additionally, most prior approaches optimize only for prediction or retrieval tasks without jointly training aligned representations for conditional generation.

## Input / Output

The model accepts a SMILES string and a natural-language text description as a paired input, both fed through a single unified T5 encoder (initialized from MolT5) that treats SMILES as a specialized token sequence. At inference, the aligned representations support four output modes: bidirectional retrieval rankings over a candidate set, a generated SMILES string conditioned on text (or vice versa for captioning), and a scalar property-prediction score. The hierarchical alignment produces intermediate token-level, fragment-level, and molecule-level representation sets that are not directly exposed but shape the final embeddings used by the conditional decoder.

## Method

ATOMAS introduces a Hierarchical Adaptive Alignment (HAA) module that automatically discovers fine-grained cross-modal correspondences without explicit annotations. The Adaptive Polymerization Module (APM) applies DPC-KNN density-peak clustering to group token features into semantic units (atoms→functional groups→molecule; words→phrases→paragraphs), while the Weighted Alignment Module (WAM) computes set-wise weighted maximum similarity scores between the two modalities at each level. These two modules are stacked iteratively across three semantic levels, and the resulting alignment losses are jointly optimized with a global contrastive loss (using a momentum encoder and memory queues à la MoCo/BLIP) and a conditional language-modeling loss for generation. The key architectural choice is a single unified encoder for both SMILES and text, which produces isomorphic token representations and, as ablations show, degrades more gracefully under data scarcity than separate encoders.

## Results

On the ChEBI-20 text-based de novo molecule generation task, Atomas-large (825M params) achieves BLEU 0.874, Exact Match 0.387, Levenshtein distance 12.70, and RDK FTS 0.841, outperforming all 11 baselines including MolT5-large (BLEU 0.854, Exact 0.318) and MolReGPT with GPT-4 (BLEU 0.857, Exact 0.280). On the PCdes retrieval task, Atomas-large reaches R@1 of 49.08 (text→molecule) and 46.22 (molecule→text), surpassing the best baseline MolCA (R@1 35.09 / 37.95) by over 14 points. On MoleculeNet property prediction, Atomas achieves an average ROC-AUC of 77.01%, exceeding MolFM (74.62%) and MoleculeSTM (73.33%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Atomas outperforms all 11 baselines across every evaluated task. On molecule-text retrieval (PCdes), Atomas-large beats MolCA (the strongest retrieval baseline) by +14.0 R@1 points (49.08 vs. 35.09 text→molecule) and +8.3 R@1 (46.22 vs. 37.95 molecule→text), while MoleculeSTM and MolFM trail further behind. On text-based de novo generation (ChEBI-20), Atomas-large surpasses MolT5-large by +0.020 BLEU and +0.069 Exact Match, and MolReGPT (GPT-4, >175B params) by +0.017 BLEU and +0.107 Exact Match, despite using only 825M parameters. On molecule captioning, Atomas-base (271M) exceeds Text+Chem T5-augm (220M) by +0.007 BLEU-2 and MolT5-large (783M) by +0.038 BLEU-2, using only 35% of MolT5-large's parameters. On molecular property prediction (MoleculeNet), Atomas achieves 77.01% average ROC-AUC, outperforming MolFM (74.62%), MoleculeSTM (73.33%), and MoMu (71.63%). Notably, Atomas-base without any initial pre-training data already outperforms MolFM-base (which uses 15K training pairs), and the unified encoder design shows significantly less performance degradation under data scarcity (25% training data) compared to separate encoders.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the molecule-text cross-modal representation learning line (MolT5, MoleculeSTM, MolCA, MolFM) by introducing hierarchical fine-grained alignment where prior methods only performed global contrastive alignment. It repurposes the DPC-KNN density-peaks clustering algorithm (originally from granular computing) as an adaptive token polymerization mechanism, and borrows the weighted set-wise alignment from text-video retrieval (Wang et al., 2022) to align clustered token sets across modalities. The momentum-encoder + queue architecture follows MoCo (He et al., 2020) and the soft-label contrastive loss follows BLIP (Li et al., 2022), but these are applied jointly with a conditional decoder in an end-to-end framework, departing from the two-stage training strategies used by MolCA and MolFM. Unlike intra-molecular fine-grained alignment methods (UniMAP, ReLMole, MoleBlend) that align SMILES with graph or 3D representations, Atomas is the first to perform automatic fine-grained alignment between SMILES and natural language text without requiring explicit fragment annotations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that hierarchical fine-grained alignment between molecule and text is both feasible without expert annotations and beneficial for downstream tasks, addressing a key gap in molecular representation learning where global alignment fails to capture stereoisomeric nuances and local structural details. The finding that a unified encoder outperforms separate encoders under data scarcity is practically important for the molecular domain where labeled data is inherently limited. Limitations include reliance on 1D SMILES only (no 2D graph or 3D structure), the clustering-based polymerization may struggle with very long or highly repetitive molecules, and the evaluation is confined to relatively small datasets (ChEBI-20, PCdes). Open questions include whether the hierarchical alignment generalizes to additional modalities (images, 3D conformations) and whether the DPC-KNN clustering scales gracefully to molecules with hundreds of atoms.
