## Problem

In offline preference-based RL, the frequency of pairwise comparisons across trajectories is highly imbalanced—diverse or rare trajectories are compared fewer times, introducing significant epistemic uncertainty. Standard Bradley-Terry MLE-based reward learning is insensitive to this uncertainty, producing risk-neutral policies that may behave unreliably in less-examined decision-making scenarios. The paper asks how to explicitly quantify and propagate this preference-level uncertainty into both reward estimation and policy optimization.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an offline dataset of trajectory segments with pairwise human preference labels, where each trajectory pair carries a comparison frequency ω that varies across the dataset. It produces two outputs: (1) a distributional reward model implemented as a causal transformer that outputs per-step Gaussian parameters (μ_t, σ_t) over the reward range [R_min, R_max], and (2) a risk-sensitive policy parameterized by a quantile-based distributional action-value function (32 quantiles) optimized under a CVaR_0.1 criterion. The Beta prior parameters (α_τ, β_τ) are learned per-trajectory by a separate transformer network, encoding the number of 'wins' and 'losses' as a continuous confidence signal.

## Method

The core mechanism replaces the standard Bradley-Terry MLE with a MAP objective that incorporates an informative Beta prior, where α and β are interpreted as positive and negative 'votes' from the preference dataset. A variational inference procedure (ELBO with Dirichlet-VAE-style KL divergence) trains a neural network to predict per-trajectory Beta parameters, and a provably convergent iterative update rule (Theorem 4.1) refines sampled rewards toward the MAP optimum. A causal transformer serves as the generative reward model, sampling step-wise rewards that are iteratively corrected before backpropagation via the reparameterization trick. For policy learning, the paper combines the Conservative Distribution Evaluation (CDE) objective for offline quantile-based value estimation with a static CVaR policy improvement step (Lim & Malik, 2022) that tracks a moving threshold q to ensure convergence to the optimal risk-averse policy.

## Results

On Risky Ant, UA-PbRL achieves the best CVaR₀.₁ reward of -2215.3±98.6 (vs. -2592.1 for ERS-PbRL and -3092.0 for PbRL) and the fewest violations (mean 21.8±3.9, CVaR₀.₁ 124.5±22.9). On Risky Swimmer, UA-PbRL attains CVaR₀.₁ reward of -3498.2±230.9 and mean violations of 113.6±11.5, substantially outperforming all baselines. In PointMaze Setting 2, UA-PbRL reduces mean violations to 5.2±7.3 compared to 98.0±16.1 for ERS-PbRL. On LLM alignment (PKU-SafeRLHF 10K), UA-PbRL improves safety over both standard RLHF and Distributional Preference Learning (DPL) while maintaining competitive quality scores.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UA-PbRL consistently outperforms all four baselines on worst-case (CVaR₀.₁) rewards and violation counts across both robot control environments. Against standard PbRL (Christiano et al., 2017), which uses MLE-based Bradley-Terry reward learning, UA-PbRL improves CVaR₀.₁ reward by ~877 points on Ant (-2215 vs. -3092) and ~1015 points on Swimmer (-3498 vs. -4513), while reducing mean violations by 63% (Ant) and 66% (Swimmer). Compared to EN-PbRL (Liang et al., 2022), which estimates reward uncertainty via ensembles but still uses a risk-neutral policy, UA-PbRL achieves substantially lower violations (21.8 vs. 51.2 mean on Ant) due to its explicit CVaR-based policy improvement. Against CPL (Hejna et al., 2024), which bypasses reward modeling entirely, UA-PbRL shows clear advantages in worst-case performance (CVaR₀.₁ violations 124.5 vs. 196.6 on Ant), indicating that the distributional reward model captures uncertainty that direct preference optimization misses. In LLM alignment, UA-PbRL outperforms DPL (Siththaranjan et al., 2024) on safety metrics, as DPL targets hidden-context uncertainty rather than the data-scarcity uncertainty that dominates in safety-critical preference datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Bradley-Terry maximum-likelihood framework for offline preference-based RL by replacing it with a MAP objective that incorporates an informative Beta prior, interpreting preference comparisons as a voting process where sparsely-compared trajectories receive wider posterior distributions. For policy optimization, it builds on Conservative Offline Distributional RL (CODAC, Ma et al. 2021) for distributional value estimation and the static CVaR policy improvement scheme of Lim & Malik (2022), combining these with the learned reward distribution. The KL divergence computation for the Beta posterior follows the Dirichlet VAE formulation (Joo et al. 2020). Unlike prior uncertainty-aware PbRL methods (Liang et al. 2022; Shin et al. 2023) that use ensembles or Bayesian dropout but still optimize a risk-neutral MLE objective, UA-PbRL couples the uncertainty signal directly to a risk-averse (CVaR) policy, closing the loop between epistemic uncertainty in preferences and worst-case policy behavior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: offline preference datasets inevitably contain imbalanced comparison frequencies, and ignoring this leads to overconfident reward estimates for rarely-observed trajectories, which is particularly dangerous in safety-critical settings (robot navigation, LLM safety alignment). The LLM alignment experiments on PKU-SafeRLHF demonstrate that capturing uncertainty in rare harmful responses can reduce harmful generation without a large quality penalty, which is timely for the RLHF community. Limitations include the reliance on a single risk level α, the assumption that uncertainty is primarily driven by comparison frequency (rather than annotator disagreement or hidden context), and validation on relatively low-dimensional control tasks plus two LLMs. Open questions include whether the Beta prior is the right functional form for all preference structures, how the method behaves with truly diverse (conflicting) human preferences, and whether the iterative MAP update (Theorem 4.1) scales to very large trajectory sets.
