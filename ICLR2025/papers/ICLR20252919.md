## Problem

Existing preference elicitation methods force users to either fully articulate their preferences in a single written prompt or label a fixed set of examples, placing the entire burden of anticipating edge cases and tradeoffs on the human. No prior approach combines the flexibility of free-form natural language specification with interactive querying that adaptively resolves uncertainty about the user's intent.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252919/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a multi-turn elicitation transcript (up to 5 minutes of LM-user Q&A) concatenated with a single test case (an article headline+description, a moral scenario, or an email string) as a single prompt to GPT-4. The output is a real-valued probability in [0, 1] emitted in token space (not a binary label or calibrated softmax), representing the model's estimate of how likely the user would answer 'yes' to the test case. This probability-based output was chosen over binary classification because pilot experiments showed GPT-4 produced degenerate all-yes or all-no predictions when asked for binary decisions.

## Method

GATE (Generative Active Task Elicitation) uses a pre-trained LM (GPT-4) to serve simultaneously as the elicitation policy and the predictor, with no fine-tuning. The elicitation policy is implemented by prompting the LM to generate the most informative next query given the conversation history, with three variants: generative active learning (LM produces concrete edge-case examples for the user to label), generative yes-or-no questions (binary probes of abstract preferences), and generative open-ended questions (free-form probes allowing the broadest knowledge elicitation). The predictor is a separate prompt that conditions on the full transcript plus a test case and asks the LM to output a preference probability. The key design insight is occupying the previously empty quadrant of the interactivity × flexibility space: unlike active learning, GATE can ask arbitrary free-form questions rather than selecting from a fixed pool; unlike prompting, it iteratively refines its understanding based on user responses.

## Results

The primary metric is the Area Under the Δp(correct)-vs.-interaction-time curve, measuring how quickly and well each elicitation method aligns a GPT-4 predictor with a user's held-out judgments. GATE (especially generative open-ended questions) significantly outperforms all baselines in content recommendation and email validation (p < 0.05), while matching user-written prompting in moral reasoning. All GATE variants improve over no interaction across all three domains. Users also report that interactive GATE elicitation is equally or less mentally demanding than writing prompts, and surfaces novel considerations they had not anticipated.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares GATE against three baseline elicitation strategies: supervised learning (random example labeling from the MIND pool), pool-based active learning (diversity sampling via SentenceBERT clustering, following Margatina et al. 2023b), and user-written prompts. In content recommendation, GATE open-ended questions significantly outperform both the supervised learning and pool-based active learning baselines (Margatina et al.'s diversity sampling), as well as user-written prompts. In email validation, GATE methods (particularly generative active learning) significantly outperform user-written prompts, which actually degrade performance below the no-interaction baseline due to users articulating preferences inconsistent with their own judgments. In moral reasoning, GATE open-ended questions match prompting but do not significantly outperform it. The pool-based active learning baseline (Margatina et al.) is only evaluated in content recommendation, where GATE's generative approach of creating novel edge cases from scratch outperforms selecting from a fixed pool.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the active learning paradigm for pretrained language models (Tamkin et al., 2022b) from selecting informative examples from a fixed pool to generating free-form questions and edge cases interactively. It positions itself in a 2D taxonomy of elicitation methods (interactive vs. passive × example-based vs. free-form), explicitly identifying the interactive + free-form quadrant as unoccupied by prior work. The framework also builds on in-context learning and prompting (Brown et al., 2020) by adding an interactive loop, and on preference learning (Ouyang et al., 2022; Rafailov et al., 2024) by addressing the upstream problem of how to collect the preference data in the first place rather than how to train from it.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GATE offers a practical, low-effort path to personalizing LMs to individual users without requiring them to write detailed prompts or label large example sets, which could lower the barrier to personalized AI systems. Key limitations include the prompt-only implementation (no explicit optimization of the elicitation objective), small held-out test sets, and a homogeneous English-speaking US participant pool. Open questions include whether more principled elicitation policies (e.g., with explicit uncertainty tracking) or larger models would improve results, and whether the approach generalizes to higher-stakes domains like legal or medical decision-making where edge cases are far richer.
