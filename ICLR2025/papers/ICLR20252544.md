## Problem

Existing AI-generated image detection benchmarks rely on randomly sampled outputs from generative models that are far from photorealistic, and detectors are typically trained on a single generator family (e.g., only GANs), making it unclear whether the task is genuinely solved. This paper performs a sanity check by introducing the Chameleon dataset—images that pass a human perception 'Turing Test'—and shows that all 9 off-the-shelf detectors collapse to near-random accuracy on this realistic test set.

## Input / Output

The method accepts a single RGB image (evaluated at resolutions from 256×256 up to 4K) and outputs a binary real/fake score. Internally, the image is divided into 32×32 patches for frequency-domain scoring, and the selected extreme-frequency patches are upscaled to 256×256 before SRM noise-pattern extraction, while the full image is encoded by a frozen OpenCLIP backbone. Training uses random JPEG compression (QF 30–100) and Gaussian blur (σ 0.1–3.0) at 10% probability as augmentation.

## Method

AIDE is a mixture-of-experts detector with two parallel branches fused at the feature level. The Patchwise Feature Extraction (PFE) branch applies DCT to each 32×32 patch, scores patches using K=6 band-pass filters with exponentially weighted frequency bands, selects the top-2 highest- and top-2 lowest-frequency patches, processes them through SRM filters and two separate ResNet-50 encoders, then mean-pools to obtain high- and low-frequency embeddings. The Semantic Feature Embedding (SFE) branch uses a frozen ConvNeXt-based OpenCLIP model to produce a global visual embedding via a linear projection and spatial mean pooling. The two branches are channel-wise concatenated and passed through a small MLP (linear + GELU + classifier) for the final binary prediction. Training is supervised with AdamW (lr 1e-4 or 5e-4 depending on benchmark), batch size 32, and only 5 epochs on 8 A100 GPUs (~2 hours), making it both fast and effective.

## Results

On AIGCDetectBenchmark, AIDE achieves 92.77% mean accuracy across 16 generators, surpassing the previous SOTA PatchCraft (89.31%) by +3.5%. On GenImage, AIDE reaches 86.88% mean accuracy, improving over PatchCraft (82.30%) by +4.6%. On the proposed Chameleon benchmark (which requires images to pass a human 'Turing Test'), AIDE trained on All GenImage achieves 65.77% accuracy—the best among 10 detectors—but all methods remain far from reliable, with most hovering near the 50% random-guessing level, confirming that AI-generated image detection is far from solved.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AIDE outperforms all 10 baselines on AIGCDetectBenchmark mean accuracy (92.77% vs. PatchCraft's 89.31%, NPR's 82.91%, LNP's 83.84%). The gap is most pronounced on diffusion-model generators: AIDE scores 93.43% on ADM and 95.09% on Glide, versus PatchCraft's 82.17% and 83.79%, respectively. On GenImage, AIDE (86.88%) beats PatchCraft (82.30%) by +4.6%, with particularly large margins on ADM (+1.2%), Glide (+13.4%), and Midjourney (+0.4%), while PatchCraft retains a slight edge on BigGAN (72.40% vs. 66.89%). On the Chameleon benchmark, AIDE trained on All GenImage achieves 65.77%—the best result—yet still misclassifies ~73% of AI-generated images as real (26.80% fake-detection accuracy), and all other methods fall between 53.7% and 60.9%, confirming that no existing detector generalizes to deceptively realistic AI images. Notably, UnivFD (CLIP-based) and DIRE-D (diffusion-reconstruction-based) show extreme specialization: UnivFD scores 85.52% on fake detection but only 41.56% on real images when trained on SD v1.4, while DIRE-D achieves 98.25% on ADM but collapses to 52.75% on ProGAN, highlighting the fragility of single-perspective approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

AIDE synthesizes ideas from three established lines of AI-generated image detection: frequency-domain analysis (FreDect, CNNSpot), noise-pattern extraction via SRM filters (LNP), and CLIP-based semantic embedding (UnivFD), combining them in a simple mixture-of-experts architecture with DCT-scored patch selection. The paper's more fundamental departure is in its evaluation protocol: it introduces the Chameleon test set, where every AI-generated image has passed a human 'Turing Test,' and reformulates the training setting to allow multi-generator training (GAN + DM) rather than the single-generator constraint used by nearly all prior work. Rather than building on a single predecessor, the paper positions itself as a diagnostic study that repurposes existing components to probe whether the field's progress on simpler benchmarks translates to genuinely challenging, high-resolution, human-fooling images.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that all nine state-of-the-art detectors collapse to near-random accuracy (~50%) on Chameleon—serves as a strong reality check, suggesting that the artifacts exploited by current methods (frequency anomalies, reconstruction errors, texture statistics) are not the same cues that make high-fidelity AI images indistinguishable to humans. The Chameleon dataset (26k images, 720P–4K, multi-category) is likely to become a standard stress-test benchmark for the community. However, the paper itself concedes the problem is 'far from being solved,' and AIDE's own Chameleon accuracy (65.77% best case) leaves substantial room for improvement. Open questions include what discriminative signals remain at the highest fidelity level, whether the gap between benchmark performance and real-world performance will narrow as generators improve, and whether the multi-generator training formulation is sufficient or whether truly open-world detection requires fundamentally different approaches.
