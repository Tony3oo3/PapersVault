## Problem

Existing automated LLM benchmarks suffer from task composition and skill coverage gaps: MT-Bench has only 80 hand-crafted examples, AlpacaEval contains predominantly simple information-seeking tasks with low diversity, and ArenaHard over-concentrates on coding (57%+). None of these mirror the natural distribution of challenging, multi-constraint tasks that real users actually pose, limiting their ability to holistically evaluate LLM capabilities in practical settings.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance consists of a user query (average 978.5 characters) with up to 4 turns of conversation history (total prompt length averaging 3,402 characters), the candidate model's response, and a task-specific checklist of 5–10 interpretable verification questions. WB-Reward outputs a five-level pairwise verdict (much better / slightly better / tie / slightly worse / much worse) against each of three baseline models, averaged into a single score; WB-Score outputs a 1–10 rating rescaled via (S−5)×2 to a −10 to +10 range. Both metrics additionally produce structured step-by-step explanations (analysis, strengths/weaknesses, or per-criterion reasoning) that enable human verification of the judgment.

## Method

WildBench curates 1,024 challenging tasks from 1M WildChat conversations via length/turn filtering, SentenceBERT-based deduplication (cosine similarity > 0.9), multi-LLM difficulty annotation (GPT-4-Turbo, Claude-3-Sonnet, Claude-3-Opus rating on a 5-point scale, excluding tasks rated easy by all), and human review, while preserving the natural task-category distribution. For evaluation, task-specific checklists are generated by combining GPT-4-Turbo and Claude-3-Opus outputs and manually verified, then injected into a CoT-style prompt that guides GPT-4-Turbo as judge through step-by-step analysis before a final verdict. WB-Reward uses three baseline models at varying performance levels (GPT-4-Turbo-0429, Claude-3-Haiku, Llama-2-70B-chat) to produce a more robust pairwise signal than single-baseline approaches, and applies a length penalty that converts 'slightly better/worse' to 'tie' when the winner's response exceeds the loser's by more than K characters (default K=500). WB-Score individually grades each response on a 1–10 scale using the same checklist-guided CoT prompt, offering a faster and cheaper alternative (10 min, ~$5 vs. 3–4× cost for WB-Reward).

## Results

On the WildBench benchmark (1,024 real-user tasks), WB-Reward (Mix) achieves a Pearson correlation of 0.984 with Chatbot Arena Elo ratings on top-ranking models, and WB-Score achieves 0.955. These correlations surpass ArenaHard (0.909), AlpacaEval2-LCWR (0.892), and AlpacaEval2 WR (0.865) on the same top-model correlation metric. Among the 40 evaluated models, GPT-4o-0513 ranks first with a WB-Reward Mix of 35.7 and WB-Score of 65.3, while Gemma-2B-it ranks last with -74.1 and 6.2 respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WildBench outperforms all three competing automatic evaluation benchmarks on correlation with human-voted Chatbot Arena Elo ratings. WB-Reward (Mix) achieves a top-model Pearson correlation of 0.984, beating ArenaHard (0.909) by 7.5 points and AlpacaEval2-LCWR (0.892) by 9.2 points. WB-Score, despite being a cheaper individual-scoring metric, still reaches 0.955, surpassing ArenaHard (0.909) and AlpacaEval2-LCWR (0.892). On the full-model Pearson correlation, WB-Reward (Mix, K=500) leads at 0.976 versus AlpacaEval2's 0.952 and ArenaHard's 0.925. The paper also notes that on the leaderboard, Llama-3-8B-Inst-SimPO appears to outperform Llama-3-70B-Inst on AlpacaEval2 (LCWR 44.7% vs 34.4%), but WildBench shows the opposite ordering, suggesting AlpacaEval's task selection bias underestimates larger models on harder tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

WildBench extends the LLM-as-a-judge evaluation paradigm established by AlpacaEval, MT-Bench, and ArenaHard, but departs from them in three key ways: sourcing tasks from real WildChat conversations (rather than expert-curated or crowdsourced prompts), introducing task-specific checklists to ground the judge's reasoning, and using three baseline models at varying performance levels for pairwise comparison instead of a single reference. The checklist design draws on InfoBench and Just-Eval, while the length-penalty mechanism directly addresses the bias identified by Dubois et al. (2024). The CoT-style structured prompting for the judge follows Kojima et al. (2022), applied here to open-ended response evaluation rather than reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

WildBench addresses a critical gap: existing automated benchmarks either lack task diversity (AlpacaEval's simple factual queries), over-concentrate on coding (ArenaHard's 57% coding share), or are too small (MT-Bench's 80 examples) to reflect the natural distribution of real user interactions. The 0.98 Pearson correlation with Chatbot Arena Elo on top models, combined with the dynamic update mechanism and data-leakage prevention, positions it as a potentially long-lived evaluation standard. Open questions include whether the benchmark remains discriminative as frontier models converge in capability, how well the checklist-based evaluation generalizes to novel task types not yet represented in WildChat, and whether the GPT-4-Turbo judge introduces systematic biases that could mask true model differences.
