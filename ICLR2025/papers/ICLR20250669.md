## Problem

Existing causal representation learning methods for multimodal data either impose restrictive parametric assumptions on latent distributions (e.g., exponential family, independence) or achieve only coarse subspace-level identification, losing the individual causal components that are essential for interpreting biomedical mechanisms. This paper seeks component-wise identifiability of latent causal variables under a fully nonparametric latent distribution, allowing flexible causal relationships both within and across modalities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250669/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes M multimodal observations x = [x^(1), ..., x^(M)] (tabular, time series, or image data) as input, with the number of latent variables per modality specified a priori. It produces per-modality latent causal variables z_i^(m) (identified up to invertible transformations), a learnable binary adjacency matrix A encoding the causal DAG among all latent components across modalities, and reconstructed observations x̂^(m). The adjacency matrix is subsequently used with standard causal discovery algorithms (e.g., PC) to infer the full graphical structure.

## Method

The identification theory proceeds in two stages: (1) subspace identification (Theorem 4.2) exploits the fact that modality-specific noise η^(m) has no influence on other modalities while z^(m) does, requiring only local invertibility and linear independence of cross-modality Jacobian columns; (2) component-wise identification (Theorem 4.4) leverages structural sparsity of cross-modality causal edges, showing that any mixing of components within a modality would strictly increase the L0 norm of the off-diagonal Jacobian blocks, violating a sparsity constraint. The estimation architecture uses per-modality encoders (producing ẑ^(m) and η̂^(m)) and decoders (reconstructing x̂^(m)) with normalizing flows to model the exogenous variables ε and a learnable adjacency matrix for causal generation. Training minimizes a combined objective of MSE reconstruction loss, KL divergence to a Gaussian prior enforcing conditional independence, and L1 sparsity regularization on the adjacency matrix.

## Results

On the Variant MNIST benchmark, the proposed method achieves R²=0.90±9e-5 and MCC=0.85±3e-5, substantially outperforming all baselines. In numerical experiments across three cases (2–4 modalities), the method recovers the true inter-modal causal graph with SHD=0 and achieves high MCC, while the sparsity ablation confirms that identifiability improves as the cross-modality causal graph becomes sparser. On the real-world Human Phenotype dataset, the discovered causal links (e.g., Sleep→Oxygen saturation, fundus latents→Age, right-eye fundus→left-hand grip strength) align with established biomedical findings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all three baselines on both R² and MCC in the Variant MNIST experiment. Against MCL (multimodal contrastive learning, Daunhawer et al. 2023), which can only block-identify shared latent subspaces, the proposed method gains +0.11 in R² (0.90 vs 0.79) and +0.22 in MCC (0.85 vs 0.63), reflecting its ability to disentangle individual components rather than just shared subspaces. Against BetaVAE (Higgins et al. 2017), which ignores causal structure entirely, the improvement is +0.22 in R² and +0.32 in MCC. Against CausalVAE (Yang et al. 2020), which models causal relations but only within a single modality and requires additional supervision, the proposed method achieves +0.40 in R² (0.90 vs 0.50) and +0.11 in MCC (0.85 vs 0.74). In the numerical experiments, MCL is not applicable to the 4-modality case due to its two-modality constraint, and CausalVAE requires extra supervision signals, further highlighting the advantage of the proposed framework's self-supervised, multi-modality design.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two lines of multimodal causal representation learning: (1) nonparametric subspace identification (Daunhawer et al. 2023; Yao et al. 2023; Von Kügelgen et al. 2021), which recovers latent subspaces but requires direct information sharing across modalities and cannot disentangle individual components; and (2) parametric component-wise identification (Morioka & Hyvarinen 2023, 2024; Gresele et al. 2020), which achieves per-component recovery but imposes exponential-family or independence assumptions on latent distributions. The key departure is leveraging structural sparsity of cross-modality causal edges—a property well-established in gene regulatory networks and metabolic systems—to bridge these two gaps, yielding nonparametric component-wise identifiability without requiring direct sharing or parametric latent distributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides the first theoretical guarantee for component-wise identifiability in a fully nonparametric, multi-modality (>2) setting with flexible causal dependencies, which is directly relevant to biomedical applications where latent factors (gene types, nutrient levels) must be individually interpretable. The real-world validation on the 10K human phenotype dataset, where discovered causal links (e.g., sleep → oxygen saturation, fundus imaging → grip strength) align with established clinical literature, demonstrates practical relevance. Key limitations include the need to pre-specify the number of latent variables per modality and the absence of quantitative benchmarks against state-of-the-art biomedical prediction models, leaving open whether the identified causal structures translate into improved downstream clinical decision-making.
