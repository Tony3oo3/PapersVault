## Problem

On-policy RL agents suffer from catastrophic forgetting in non-stationary context-driven environments, where a time-varying exogenous context process shifts the dynamics over time. Existing mitigations either require explicit task labels (often unavailable), rely on brittle regularization heuristics that assume discrete task boundaries, or use off-policy replay that introduces instability and hyperparameter sensitivity in online settings. The paper asks whether an on-policy method can anchor knowledge from past contexts without task labels or off-policy training.

## Input / Output

The policy network takes the current state s_t and the observed exogenous context z_t (e.g., a wind vector or workload feature) as joint input and outputs an action a_t. A bounded reservoir-sampled buffer B_a of past (state, context, action, reward) tuples is maintained alongside the recent batch B_r; an OOD detector W(B_a, B_r) flags which buffered samples come from contexts sufficiently different from the current batch, and these flagged samples are used only to constrain—not optimize—the policy update. The context process is assumed observed and exogenous (not latent), and no task labels or change-point boundaries are required.

## Method

LCPO formulates each policy update as a constrained optimization: minimize the standard policy-gradient loss (with automatic entropy regularization) on the recent batch B_r, subject to a KL-divergence constraint that bounds how much the policy can change on OOD samples drawn from the past buffer B_a. The OOD samples are identified by a pluggable difference detector (e.g., L2 or Mahalanobis distance threshold on the context vector) that flags experiences whose context lies outside the distribution of the current batch. The constrained problem is solved with a second-order approximation—conjugate gradient on the KL Hessian followed by a line search that also enforces a TRPO-style trust-region bound on the current batch—making the update structurally similar to TRPO but with the constraint applied to old rather than current data. The buffer is maintained via reservoir sampling to a fixed size, ensuring uniform coverage of all past contexts without unbounded memory. The key insight is that anchoring only requires knowing whether two contexts are different (an OOD test), not which task they belong to, eliminating the need for task labels or change-point detection.

## Results

LCPO outperforms all 11 online baselines across six environments (five Gymnasium + straggler mitigation) with synthetic and real context traces, while remaining closest to the prescient (offline-trained) upper bound. In the straggler mitigation environment (real Microsoft production workloads), LCPO achieves 1070±10 ms tail latency on Workload 1 vs. 1701±112 for MBCD, 2531±197 for MBPO, and 3154±464 for TRPO, compared to 984 ms for the best prescient policy. On Pendulum-v1 across four context traces, LCPO achieves lifelong returns of -190, -355, -240, and -378, consistently outperforming A2C (the best online baseline) by 11–67 points and approaching prescient returns of -187, -376, -203, and -357. LCPO is approximately 1.5× as computationally demanding as A2C in wallclock time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LCPO wins against all 11 baselines across all environments and context traces. A2C is the strongest online baseline (e.g., 1716±710 vs. LCPO's 1070±10 on Workload 1; -201 vs. -190 on Context Trace 1), but still trails LCPO by a meaningful margin. Off-policy methods (SAC, DDQN, CLEAR, PT-DQN) suffer from hyperparameter sensitivity and training instability in the online setting; CLEAR and PT-DQN fail catastrophically outside the environment they were tuned on. Regularization-based methods (Online EWC, Sliding OGD, BFDQN) rely on brittle heuristics about task boundaries and do not effectively prevent forgetting. MBCD (change-point detection) struggles to identify meaningful task boundaries in smooth context processes, launching 3–7 policies depending on the random seed. MBPO performs poorly despite having highly accurate learned environment models, due to its sampling strategy over-emphasizing early contexts. LCPO's advantage is most pronounced in environments with gradual context shifts where task-label-based and CPD-based methods break down.

## Relation to Prior Work

LCPO repurposes TRPO's second-order constrained optimization machinery (KL approximation, conjugate gradient, line search) but redirects the constraint from bounding policy change on current data to bounding it on out-of-distribution past-context samples, effectively approximating the locality property that tabular RL enjoys for free. The base on-policy gradient follows A2C, and the OOD detection component draws from standard ML outlier-detection tools (L2, Mahalanobis distance) rather than from RL-specific change-point detection. The key conceptual departure from prior CF-mitigation work is replacing task labels, CPD, or off-policy replay with a single OOD threshold on the context, which the paper argues is strictly weaker in assumptions than piecewise-stationarity or discrete-task requirements.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LCPO offers a practical, on-policy recipe for CF mitigation that only requires an observed exogenous context and a simple distance threshold—no task labels, no CPD, no off-policy instability—making it applicable to real production systems (demonstrated on Microsoft's straggler-mitigation workload). Open questions include combining LCPO with latent-context inference (where the context is unobserved), designing exploration strategies tailored to non-stationary settings, and adaptive buffer management that selectively retains diverse context samples. The bounded network capacity limitation means LCPO cannot represent an unbounded number of distinct context regimes, and the method's reliance on a well-tuned OOD threshold, while robust in experiments, remains a practical hyperparameter concern.
