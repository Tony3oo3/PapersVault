## Problem

Sparse pretraining of LLMs with N:M structured sparsity reduces model accuracy, and prior remedies either require dense fine-tuning (negating inference-time savings) or dynamic transposable mask search (adding significant runtime overhead, sometimes causing slowdowns). The paper asks whether one can keep both forward and backward passes sparse throughout pretraining while recovering accuracy, without expensive mask search or a dense fine-tuning phase.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250748/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard text corpora (OpenWebText for GPT-2, WikiCorpus for BERT) in the usual self-supervised pretraining setup and produces a model whose linear layers consist of a doubly-pruned N:M sparse weight matrix plus a small low-rank adapter pair (L ∈ R^{d_out×r}, R ∈ R^{r×d_in}). The adapters are only instantiated and trained during the final 1% of pretraining iterations, so the stored model at inference is sparse weights plus a few extra low-rank factors rather than a dense matrix. The first linear layer after the input embedding and all classification heads remain dense.

## Method

SLOPE introduces a double-pruned backward pass: the N:M row-wise sparse weight is transposed first, and a second N:M sparsity is imposed on the transposed matrix, enabling hardware-accelerated sparse GEMM in both the forward pass (Eq. 4) and the input-gradient backward pass (Eq. 6) without requiring transposable masks or their associated search overhead. A convergence theorem (Theorem 2.2) shows that the dynamically changing column-wise mask in the backward pass does not harm optimizer convergence because its expectation over random masks recovers the dense gradient scaled by M/N. To recover the accuracy lost by double pruning, SLOPE decomposes the dense weight as W_dense = W_sparse + LR and trains the low-rank factors only during the final 1% of pretraining iterations (lazy low-rank adapters), exploiting their faster convergence due to far fewer parameters. Custom CUDA kernels further optimize the pipeline by tiling upsample matrices into square blocks for better cuSPARSELt utilization and fusing the sparse SpMM with the low-rank multiplication into a single kernel call, yielding up to 1.25× training and 1.54× inference speedup with 0.63× and 0.61× memory reduction on OPT-33B/66B.

## Results

SLOPE achieves up to 1.25× end-to-end training speedup and 1.54× inference speedup on OPT-33B/OPT-66B, while reducing training and inference memory by up to 0.63× and 0.61× respectively. On GPT2-Small zero-shot evaluation, SLOPE (r=0) attains MMLU 23.0, ARC Challenge 19.3, and OpenBookQA 16.0, closely matching the dense baseline (22.9, 20.7, 16.2) and substantially outperforming Extended SR-STE (24.1, 18.3, 12.6) on most tasks. On BERT-Large-Uncased, SLOPE with 6.25% adapters recovers SQuAD F1 to 89.5 and GLUE to 78.2, approaching the dense model's 90.44 and 80.22.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against FST (ICML 2024), SLOPE achieves 1.25× vs 1.06× training speedup and 1.46× vs 1.00× inference speedup on OPT-66B, with 0.63× vs 1.00× inference memory; FST's inference speedup is 1.00× because it requires dense fine-tuning in the final ~17% of pretraining, negating all sparse inference benefits. Against Extended SR-STE, SLOPE wins on 6 of 8 zero-shot tasks (ARC, OpenBookQA, WinoGrande, HellaSwag, PIQA, RACE) with margins of 1.1–3.6 points, while SR-STE leads on MMLU (24.1 vs 23.0) and MathQA (21.2 vs 20.8); SLOPE is consistently closer to the dense baseline on most tasks. Against Wanda (one-shot pruning), SLOPE achieves higher SQuAD F1 (90.17 vs 89.93) and GLUE (79.08 vs 78.84) under uniform 2:4 sparsity, and the gap widens dramatically under mixed sparsity (e.g., 2:8 on first 12 blocks: SLOPE 89.67 vs Wanda 86.57 on SQuAD, 75.92 vs 69.08 on GLUE).

## Relation to Prior Work

SLOPE builds on the N:M structured sparsity paradigm (particularly 2:4 sparsity supported by NVIDIA sparse tensor cores) and the LoRA low-rank adapter framework, but departs from prior sparse pretraining methods in two key ways. Unlike FST (Hu et al., 2024) which requires dense fine-tuning in the final 17% of pretraining and only prunes MLP layers, or SR-STE (Zhou et al., 2021) which uses dynamic masks that waste computation on weights that will be pruned, SLOPE employs a static N:M mask with a novel double-pruned backward pass that transposes the already-sparsified weight before imposing a second round of N:M sparsity, avoiding the costly transposable-mask search used by Bi-Mask and similar methods. The lazy low-rank adapter component repurposes LoRA-style adapters but restricts them to only the final 1% of pretraining iterations, a usage pattern not explored in prior sparse-plus-low-rank work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SLOPE provides a practical, hardware-aligned recipe for accelerating LLM pretraining and inference with modest accuracy loss, achieving up to 1.25× training and 1.54× inference speedups with ~37–39% memory reduction on models up to 66B parameters. The double-pruned backward pass formulation with convergence guarantees (Theorem 2.2) offers a theoretically grounded alternative to heuristic transposable-mask search, and the lazy adapter strategy demonstrates that a tiny fraction of training iterations suffices to recover most of the accuracy gap. However, accuracy evaluation is limited to models up to 774M parameters, the speedups are modest relative to the 2× theoretical ceiling of 2:4 sparsity, and the method is tightly coupled to NVIDIA hardware with sparse tensor core support. Open questions include whether the static-mask assumption and the 1% adapter window generalize to frontier-scale models (100B+), and whether the accuracy gap versus dense pretraining can be further closed without increasing adapter rank.
