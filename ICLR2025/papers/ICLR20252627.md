## Problem

Existing influence-based data selection for LLM pretraining suffers from two critical limitations: computing accurate influence scores for all candidate instances is prohibitively expensive due to Hessian matrix inversion, and selecting top-k instances by influence alone yields a non-diverse training set that hinders generalization to downstream tasks. No prior method simultaneously addresses both the computational cost of influence estimation and the quality-diversity trade-off in data selection.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a large-scale candidate text corpus (627B-token SlimPajama or 15000B-token FineWeb), a reference/validation dataset (LAMBADA, Openwebmath, or FLAN), and the current LLM checkpoint. The candidate data is first embedded via BAAI/bge-large-en-v1.5 and partitioned into ~10,000 k-means clusters; the method then iteratively samples small batches from clusters, computes influence scores, and outputs a selected subset (e.g., 30B or 100B tokens) for pretraining. A threshold τ filters out low-influence instances, and a sampling ratio γ controls how much of each selected cluster is added to the training set per iteration.

## Method

Quad organizes the candidate pool into clusters and treats each cluster as an arm in a Multi-Armed Bandit framework, using an Upper Confidence Bound score (average influence + exploration bonus weighted by α) to iteratively balance exploitation of high-quality clusters and exploration of under-sampled ones. For influence computation, Quad extends the standard influence function to attention layers by treating the QKV projections as a unified layer (rather than computing Q, K, V Hessians independently), capturing the complex interdependencies among attention components. To make this tractable at LLM scale, Quad applies K-FAC to decompose the Hessian into a Kronecker product of smaller matrices (Δ ⊗ X), enabling efficient inverse-Hessian-vector-product computation, and further reduces memory via Johnson-Lindenstrauss random projection of the gradient vectors. Each iteration samples a mini-batch from the top-k scored clusters, updates cluster statistics, and adds instances exceeding the influence threshold τ to the training set, avoiding the need to score the entire candidate pool.

## Results

On the SlimPajama dataset with a 1.3B model trained on 30B tokens, Quad achieves 42.94% overall zero-shot accuracy across 9 downstream tasks, outperforming the best baseline Qurating (42.01%) by 0.93% and Random (41.55%) by 1.39%. Quad also leads on every task category: General Knowledge (52.08%), Commonsense Reasoning (37.03%), and Reading Comprehension (41.07%). In terms of efficiency, Quad requires only 9.15 FLOPs (×10¹⁹), substantially less than Qurating (13.66) and PPL (9.51), while matching or exceeding their accuracy. On the 7B model with 100B tokens, Quad reaches 53.19% overall accuracy, surpassing Qurating (52.18%) and Random-400B (53.11%) with only 25% of the training data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Quad outperforms all five baselines on overall accuracy. The strongest baseline, Qurating (LLM-based data selection), achieves 42.01% but requires 13.66 FLOPs due to GPT-4 queries; Quad surpasses it by 0.93% while using 33% fewer FLOPs. MATES (surrogate-model influence estimation) scores 41.93%, limited by the small proxy model's inability to capture accurate influence scores. Semdedup (semantic deduplication) reaches 41.81%, showing that rule-based heuristics without model awareness provide only marginal gains over random. PPL (perplexity-based filtering) is essentially tied with Random (41.57 vs. 41.55), confirming that low-perplexity data tends to be simple and redundant. Notably, DSIR (n-gram importance resampling) actually underperforms Random (40.53 vs. 41.55), as string-level similarity fails to capture semantic relevance and introduces duplicated instances. On the 7B model, Quad's advantage over Qurating widens to 1.01% (53.19% vs. 52.18%), and Quad-100B matches Clean-7B-400B (53.19% vs. 53.11%) while using only 25% of the training tokens.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the influence function framework (Koh & Liang, 2017) from MLP-only computation to multi-head attention layers, using K-FAC (Martens & Grosse, 2015) Kronecker-factored approximations to keep the Hessian inversion tractable. It departs from prior influence-based selectors (LESS, MATES) that simply pick top-k instances by score, by framing data selection as an exploration-exploitation problem solved via a Multi-Armed Bandit (Vermorel & Mohri, 2005) over clustered data, thereby explicitly balancing quality and diversity. The approach also contrasts with rule-based filters (DSIR, Semdedup), perplexity-based selection, and LLM-as-judge methods (Qurating) that lack a model-aware, gradient-based notion of data utility.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Data selection is a bottleneck in LLM pretraining where compute budgets are fixed, and the demonstrated 75% FLOPs reduction (Quad-7B-100B matching Clean-7B-400B) is practically significant for resource-constrained settings. The quality-diversity tradeoff identified here—where top-influence clusters are semantically concentrated and hurt generalization—is a concrete failure mode that prior influence-based methods overlooked. Key open questions include scalability beyond 7B parameters, sensitivity to the reference set choice, and whether the MAB framework remains effective when the candidate pool grows to trillions of tokens, as the authors themselves note the method's generalizability to larger models needs further exploration.
