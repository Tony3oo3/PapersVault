## Problem

Elo-based open-ended evaluation systems (e.g., LMSYS Chatbot Arena) are vulnerable to redundancy in the prompt and model distributions: adding redundant prompts or models can arbitrarily shift rankings, and rating-maximizing model development can specialize on over-represented skills at the expense of others. Existing game-theoretic evaluation methods guarantee clone-invariance only in 2-player zero-sum settings, while LLM evaluation inherently requires at least 3 players (model-vs-model-vs-prompt) in a general-sum game, where equilibrium selection is both computationally harder and non-unique.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a tabulated 3-player payoff tensor of size |A_p| × |A_m|², where each entry is a pairwise preference rating (from {-1, -1/2, 0, +1/2, +1}) between two model responses on a given prompt, obtained by querying an LLM judge (gemini-1.5-pro) with 8 samples per model pair to average out position bias. Beyond scalar model and prompt ratings, the method outputs full equilibrium strategy distributions over prompts and models, which can be decomposed into per-action marginal contributions from each co-player, enabling interpretability analyses such as identifying which model families drive a given model's rating.

## Method

The paper formulates LLM evaluation as a 3-player general-sum 'king-of-the-hill' game (prompt player, king model player, rebel model player) where the prompt player is rewarded for separating models and the rebel player receives the negated king payoff. The key technical contribution is affinity entropy H_a^p, a generalized Tsallis entropy parameterized by a similarity kernel K (an RBF kernel over expected squared utility differences between actions) that satisfies clone-invariance: its maximizers distribute mass uniformly across clone groups regardless of group size. This entropy is used to select unique equilibria—tracing the QRE continuum to the Log-Linear Equilibrium for NE, or maximizing relative entropy to the affinity-entropy target for CCE—ensuring that redundant actions do not distort the selected equilibrium. Ratings are then defined as the regret of each action at the selected equilibrium, yielding rankings that remain invariant under the addition of redundant prompts or models while preserving intuitive ordering consistent with risk-dominance.

## Results

On the arena-hard-v0.1 dataset (500 prompts, 17 LLMs), the proposed NE and CCE equilibrium ratings are 'fairly consistent' with Elo rankings in the absence of redundant prompts, with only a few models shifting one or two positions. When up to 500 redundant adversarial prompts targeting gemini-1.5-pro-api-0514 are introduced, Elo rankings degrade sharply (the top model falls through the ranks by 250 prompts), while NE and CCE rankings remain fully invariant. The selected NE is empirically confirmed to be the risk-dominant equilibrium among 128 mixed-strategy NEs of the game. Using Shannon entropy instead of the proposed affinity entropy for equilibrium selection breaks clone-invariance, confirming the necessity of the novel entropy definition.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the Elo rating system used by LMSYS Chatbot Arena. The paper demonstrates that Elo is fundamentally vulnerable to redundancy: introducing 250+ redundant adversarial prompts causes the top-ranked model (gemini-1.5-pro-api-0514) to drop significantly in Elo ranking, while the proposed NE and CCE equilibrium ratings keep it at #1 regardless of the number of redundant copies added. A secondary comparison is against equilibrium selection using standard Shannon entropy (max-entropy NE or max-entropy CCE), which also fails to achieve clone-invariance in the N-player general-sum setting, confirming that the affinity entropy is essential. The CCE variant produces a slightly different competitive landscape (top-3 models tied for first) compared to NE, which fully differentiates all models, reflecting the effect of allowing correlation between model and prompt players. Overall, the equilibrium methods win on robustness (invariance to redundancy) while matching Elo on intuitive ranking quality in the clean setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends game-theoretic evaluation from the 2-player zero-sum setting (Balduzzi et al., 2018; Marris et al., 2022b) to N-player general-sum games, specifically a 3-player 'king-of-the-hill' formulation with prompt, king-model, and rebel-model players. The key technical departure is the introduction of affinity entropy, a kernelized generalization of Tsallis entropy, which achieves clone-invariance in equilibrium selection where standard Shannon entropy provably fails in the general-sum setting. The NE solving builds on the QRE continuum tracing framework of McKelvey & Palfrey (1995) and the loss-based optimization of Gemp et al. (2022), while the CCE solving leverages convex optimization in dual space. The paper also positions itself against Social Choice Theory, arguing that SCT's one-sided clone-invariance (invariant to redundant candidates but not redundant votes/prompts) is insufficient for open-ended LLM evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As open-ended evaluation systems like LMSYS Chatbot Arena become the de facto reference for LLM progress, the demonstrated vulnerability of Elo ratings to redundant adversarial prompts raises a practical concern: model developers optimizing for leaderboard position may inadvertently specialize in over-represented skill dimensions. The equilibrium-based framework offers a principled, interpretable alternative with provable invariance properties, and the marginal contribution analysis reveals structural insights (e.g., models benefiting most from intra-family comparisons) that static benchmarks cannot surface. Key limitations include reliance on a single LLM judge (gemini-1.5-pro) with acknowledged self-preference risk, scaling challenges for the payoff tensor, and the fact that clone-invariance is guaranteed only for exact duplicates rather than near-redundant prompts. Open questions include whether the specific gamification (king-of-the-hill) is the right choice, how to scale to thousands of models, and whether equilibrium-based ratings would be adopted by the broader LLM evaluation community.
