## Problem

Diffusion models have proven effective for single-robot motion planning, but extending them to multi-robot settings is hindered by the curse of dimensionality (exponentially higher sample complexity for joint multi-robot trajectory distributions) and the practical difficulty of collecting multi-robot interaction data. Prior diffusion-based planners are therefore confined to single-robot or very small-scale environments, leaving a gap in data-driven, collision-free trajectory generation for teams of dozens of robots.

## Input / Output

The method takes per-robot start states, goal (termination) conditions, and pre-trained single-robot diffusion models (one per local map region, shared across all robots of the same type), along with the environment obstacle map. It outputs a set of collision-free, dynamically feasible trajectories (discretized into H uniform time steps) that conform to the underlying single-robot data distribution. A key design choice is that no multi-robot data is ever required; all coordination is achieved at inference time through soft sphere constraints injected into the denoising process. For long-horizon planning, local models are sequenced via inpainting-style cross-conditioning so that the terminal state of one local trajectory equals the initial state of the next.

## Method

MMD combines single-robot motion-planning diffusion models with constraint-based MAPF search algorithms (CBS, ECBS, PP) to coordinate multiple robots without learning any joint multi-robot distribution. Collision avoidance is enforced by imposing sphere soft-constraints—centered at conflict points with a radius and time window—as additional cost terms in the diffusion guidance function during Langevin-dynamics denoising, so that trajectories are repelled from occupied regions. Five algorithmic variants (MMD-PP, MMD-CBS, MMD-ECBS, MMD-xCBS, MMD-xECBS) differ in how and when constraints are placed: PP uses a fixed priority ordering, CBS splits a constraint tree on detected conflicts, ECBS adds both weak (penalize all other robots' trajectories) and strong (resolve specific conflicts) constraints, and the x-variants accelerate replanning by reusing previously generated trajectories with only 3 denoising steps instead of 25. For large environments, MMD sequences an ensemble of local diffusion models (each trained on a small map segment) with cross-conditioning constraints that enforce state continuity at segment boundaries, enabling long-horizon planning without retraining.

## Results

MMD-xECBS achieves 100% success rate on the Empty map and 96–100% on the Highways map across 3–9 robots, with data adherence scores of 0.96–0.999, while the composite multi-robot diffusion baseline (MPD-Composite) drops to 62% success at 6 robots (Empty) and 0% at 6+ robots (Highways). MMD further scales to 40 agents with no collisions. On the Conveyor and Drop-Region maps, MMD-xECBS maintains 100% success up to 12 robots with data adherence of 0.87–0.94, whereas A*Data-ECBS achieves only 0.03–0.27 data adherence on those maps. Average acceleration per robot remains low and stable (0.04–0.05) for MMD across all scales, indicating consistent smoothness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MPD-Composite (a multi-robot diffusion model trained on joint trajectories), MMD-xECBS wins decisively on scalability: it maintains 100% success and high data adherence (≥0.96) up to 9 robots and beyond (40 in additional tests), while MPD-Composite collapses to 0% success at 6 robots on Highways and 9 robots on Empty. Against A*Data-ECBS (ECBS with learned statistical cost maps), MMD-xECBS achieves substantially higher data adherence—e.g., 0.93 vs. 0.87 on Highways (3 robots) and 0.87 vs. 0.03 on Conveyor (3 robots)—though A*Data-ECBS is faster in wall-clock planning time (e.g., 3.4 s vs. 3.6 s on Highways with 3 robots). Against A*-ECBS (uniform-cost ECBS), MMD-xECBS dominates on data adherence across all maps (e.g., 0.93 vs. 0.57 on Highways, 0.87 vs. 0.00 on Conveyor with 3 robots) while matching or exceeding its success rate. Among MMD variants, MMD-xECBS is the strongest overall: it outperforms MMD-CBS in scalability (100% vs. 0% at 12+ robots on most maps) and MMD-PP in cluttered scenarios (100% vs. 0% at 15+ robots on Conveyor/Drop-Region), while matching MMD-ECBS in data adherence with lower planning time.

## Relation to Prior Work

MMD sits at the intersection of two research threads: diffusion-based motion planning (Carvalho et al. 2023; Janner et al. 2022), which has been limited to single-robot settings, and constraint-based MAPF algorithms (CBS, ECBS, PP), which rely on discrete graph search. The key conceptual move is repurposing single-robot diffusion models as continuous-space 'low-level planners' within a constraint-tree search, with sphere constraints implemented as soft guidance terms in the Langevin denoising process. For long-horizon planning, the paper adapts the generative skill chaining idea (Mishra et al. 2023) and inpainting (Lugmayr et al. 2022) to sequence local diffusion models, and incorporates experience reuse from recent CBS acceleration work (Shaoul et al. 2024a). This departs from prior multi-robot diffusion work (e.g., MotionDiffuser) that learns a joint multi-agent distribution, which the paper shows is intractable at scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By requiring only single-robot data, MMD breaks the curse of dimensionality that prevents joint multi-robot diffusion models from scaling beyond a handful of agents, enabling data-driven trajectory generation for dozens of robots in warehouse-scale environments. The sequencing mechanism for composing local models into long-horizon plans is a practical recipe for large, heterogeneous maps without retraining. Key limitations include the centralized full-horizon assumption (no online adaptation or collaboration), the reliance on sphere approximations for robot geometry, and computational cost that could be substantially reduced through parallelization of the constraint-tree search. Open questions include adapting MMD to decentralized/windowed settings (e.g., via GBP-style factor graphs) and extending from coordination to collaborative task execution.
