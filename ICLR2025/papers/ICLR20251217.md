## Problem

Learning universal temporal value functions (task progress estimators) that generalize across diverse robot embodiments, tasks, and scenes remains a key challenge for embodied agents. Existing vision-only value models lack the semantic and temporal grounding needed for broad generalization, while naively prompting vision-language models with chronological video frames produces degenerate monotonic values that ignore actual trajectory quality. The paper asks whether VLMs' world knowledge can be harnessed for per-frame value estimation without any task-specific training.

## Input / Output

GVL takes a sequence of video frames from a robot trajectory, a natural-language task description, and the first frame (kept unshuffled as a temporal anchor), with the remaining frames randomly permuted. Optionally, in-context example trajectories (frame-value pairs) from the same task, different tasks, or even human videos are prepended to the prompt. The VLM outputs a task completion percentage (0–100%) for each shuffled frame in a single autoregressive pass; these per-frame values can then be converted into a Value-Order Correlation (VOC) score, a binary success/failure label via a VOC threshold, or per-transition advantage weights for offline policy optimization.

## Method

GVL reframes value estimation as an autoregressive next-token prediction problem over deliberately shuffled video frames, exploiting three mechanisms in a frozen VLM (Gemini-1.5-Pro). First, autoregressive prediction lets the model attend to all prior value outputs when generating the next one, enforcing global consistency without Bellman-equation training. Second, random shuffling of input frames breaks the strong temporal correlation bias that causes VLMs to output trivially monotonic values; the first frame is retained unshuffled as an anchor to preserve the arrow of time. Third, multi-modal in-context learning prepends shuffled example trajectories with ground-truth progress values, enabling zero-shot, few-shot, cross-task, and cross-embodiment (e.g., human video) improvements without any fine-tuning. The key insight is that the seemingly harder task of 'unshuffling' frames forces the VLM to ground each prediction in actual visual content and task semantics rather than exploiting chronological order as a shortcut.

## Results

On the Open X-Embodiment suite (50 datasets, 1000 trajectories), GVL zero-shot produces VOC scores heavily skewed positive, with RT-1 reaching 0.74 average VOC versus −0.08 for a single-frame VLM ablation. On the challenging 250-task ALOHA bimanual dataset, GVL zero-shot achieves a median VOC of 0.12 (60% positive), which improves to a median of 0.37 (90% positive) with one in-context example. For success detection on six simulated ALOHA tasks, GVL-SD (one-shot) reaches 75% accuracy and 85% precision, substantially outperforming SuccessVQA (62% accuracy, 33% precision). In real-world ALOHA policy learning, GVL-weighted diffusion policy outperforms unweighted diffusion policy on 5 of 7 tasks, with gains correlating with VOC quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LIV (Ma et al., 2023a), a contrastive vision-language value model fine-tuned on human videos, GVL consistently outperforms on both OXE and ALOHA datasets. On OXE language-goal datasets, LIV's VOC scores are essentially random (near-uniform distribution), while GVL produces strongly positive correlations; on image-goal datasets GVL still edges out LIV with slightly higher VOCs. On the 250-task ALOHA bimanual benchmark, both methods struggle zero-shot, but GVL benefits more from in-context examples. Against SuccessVQA (Du et al., 2023), which frames success detection as VQA over the full video, GVL-SD wins decisively on precision (0.71 vs. 0.33 zero-shot; 0.85 vs. 0.44 one-shot) and accuracy (0.75 vs. 0.62 one-shot), while SuccessVQA has a slight recall edge (0.73 vs. 0.70). The precision gap is critical downstream: SuccessVQA's low precision causes filtered imitation learning to actually hurt policy performance, whereas GVL-SD consistently improves ACT success rates across all tested VOC thresholds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes frozen VLMs (Gemini-1.5-Pro, GPT-4o) as dense per-frame value estimators, departing from prior VLM-based robotics work that used them only as sparse reward models or binary success detectors (Rocamonde et al., 2023; Du et al., 2023). It also contrasts with trained universal value function approximators (Schaul et al., 2015; Ma et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2023a) that require task-specific or domain-specific training on limited vision data. The key conceptual departure is framing value estimation as an autoregressive next-token prediction over shuffled frames, exploiting the VLM's in-context learning and long-context capabilities rather than its discriminative embedding space. The in-context learning component extends prior robotics ICL work (Finn et al., 2017; Duan et al., 2017) by showing that no robot-specific training is needed—examples from even different embodiments (human videos) suffice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GVL provides a training-free, broadly generalizable value estimation tool that immediately enables practical downstream applications (dataset filtering, success detection, advantage-weighted regression) across 300+ real-world tasks without any fine-tuning, lowering the barrier to value-based robot learning. The VOC metric offers a lightweight, scalable evaluation alternative to expensive policy-level evaluation for universal value functions. Key limitations include the offline-only setting (requiring full trajectories), sensitivity to aperiodic tasks (wiping, stirring), and computational cost of re-querying at each step in online settings. Open questions include whether fine-tuning VLMs specifically for value prediction could further improve performance, whether multi-view observations help, and whether the shuffling trick generalizes to other temporal reasoning tasks beyond robotics.
