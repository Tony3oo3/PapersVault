## Problem

Graph diffusion models suffer from a critical reverse-starting bias: due to conservative training strategies constrained by small data scales and limited network capacity, the forward maximum perturbation distribution deviates significantly from the standard Gaussian, yet reverse sampling always initiates from a standard Gaussian. This mismatch, compounded by the inherent exposure bias of diffusion models, degrades generation quality. Existing solutions for image diffusion (e.g., DPM-Fixes, DPM-Leak) are inapplicable to graphs because they rely on large-scale data, pixel independence, and Gaussian assumptions that do not hold for sparse, interdependent graph structures.

## Input / Output

The method is a plug-and-play post-hoc correction that requires no modification to the pretrained score network architecture or parameters. It takes a standard Gaussian noise sample as the initial reverse-starting point and a pretrained graph diffusion score network s_θ,t(·), and additionally trains a lightweight pseudo score network s_ψ,t(·) on a batch of samples generated by the pretrained network. The output is a generated graph (node feature matrix X ∈ R^{V×F} and weighted adjacency matrix A ∈ R^{V×V}) or a molecular graph, in the same format as the base model but with substantially improved fidelity. The approach also shortens the effective sampling chain, reducing inference time by up to ~14× on QM9.

## Method

S++ addresses both biases through two coupled mechanisms. First, a reverse-starting alignment stage applies Langevin sampling (M iterations) using the pretrained score s_θ,T(·) to push the initial sample from a standard Gaussian toward the forward maximum perturbation distribution q(X_T|X_0), which resides in a low-noise regime where the model is more tolerant to score prediction errors. Second, a score correction mechanism computes the score difference s_θ,t(X̂_t) − s_ψ,t(X̂_t) between the pretrained network and a pseudo network trained on generated data; this difference provably contains information about the true data X_0 (since γ_t > γ_t′), and adding λ times this difference to the predicted score, then scaling by 1/ω, steers the score toward the true score without any architectural change. The correction at time T is decoupled from later timesteps, and in truncated models the single correction at T often suffices, eliminating the need for further corrections during the remaining sampling steps.

## Results

On generic graph generation (Table 1), S++ applied to GDSS-OC reduces the average MMD on Community-small from 0.064 to 0.029, on Enzymes from 0.309 to 0.058, and on Grid from 0.070 to 0.066, matching or exceeding the performance of the corrector-based variants (e.g., GDSS-WC-S++ achieves 0.028/0.028/0.057). On molecular generation (Table 2), GDSS-OC-S++ on QM9 achieves FCD 1.661 and NSPDK MMD 0.001 with a sampling time of 5.10 s, compared to GDSS-OC's FCD 4.584, NSPDK MMD 0.016, and 73 s. For drug-like molecule discovery (Tables 3–4), MOOD-S++ improves the novel hit ratio over MOOD on all five protein targets (e.g., 8.29% vs. 7.02% on parp1) and achieves lower (better) top-5% docking scores (e.g., −10.96 vs. −10.87 kcal/mol on parp1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

S++ consistently outperforms all four graph diffusion baselines (GDSS, HGDM, GSDM, MOOD) across every dataset and metric. On Community-small, GDSS-OC-S++ halves the average MMD (0.029 vs. 0.064) and GSDM-OC-S++ reduces it by 93% (0.009 vs. 0.138); on Enzymes, the improvements are even more dramatic (e.g., GSDM-OC-S++ 0.037 vs. GSDM-OC 0.655). For molecular generation on QM9, GDSS-OC-S++ achieves FCD 1.661 versus 4.584 for GDSS-OC and 3.164 for HGDM-OC, while also reducing sampling time from 73 s to 5.1 s. Against non-diffusion baselines in Table 12, S++ variants dominate: on Community-small, GDSS-WC-S++ (Avg. 0.028) beats GraphRNN (0.080), EDP-GNN (0.074), and GraphVAE (0.623); on Enzymes, HGDM-WC-S++ (0.029) outperforms GraphRNN (0.042) and EDP-GNN (0.124). On QM9 (Table 13), GDSS-WC-S++ achieves FCD 1.661 and NSPDK MMD 0.001, surpassing GraphAF (FCD 5.268), EDP-GNN (FCD 2.680), and GraphEBM (FCD 6.143). For drug-like molecule discovery, MOOD-S++ improves novel hit ratio over MOOD on all five targets (e.g., 20.35% vs. 18.67% on 5ht1b) and achieves better docking scores, confirming that bias mitigation benefits downstream property-optimization tasks as well.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the SDE-based diffusion framework (Song et al., 2021) and Langevin sampling (Song & Ermon, 2019) to address a bias issue specific to graph diffusion models: the mismatch between the truncated forward maximum perturbation distribution and the standard Gaussian reverse-starting point. The score correction mechanism draws on the γ_t/δ_t decomposition from Zhang et al. (2023) and the extrapolation principle from classifier-free guidance (Ho & Salimans, 2021), but repurposes them for a novel 'score difference' signal between a pretrained network and a pseudo network trained on its own outputs. The paper explicitly departs from image-domain solutions (DPM-Fixes, DPM-Leak, ADM-ES, TS-DPM), arguing that graph data's sparsity, small scale, and interdependent node-edge structure make those approaches inapplicable, and unlike prior exposure bias remedies (ADM-IP, EB-DDPM, MDSS) that require retraining, S++ is entirely plug-and-play.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and resolves a previously unrecognized systematic bias in graph diffusion models, with the key insight that the conservative truncation strategy (adopted to avoid high-noise instability) actually places the model in a low-noise regime where it is robust to score errors—turning a limitation into an exploitable property. The practical impact is significant: no network modification is needed, sampling time can be reduced by an order of magnitude (e.g., 73s → 5.1s on QM9), and the method is validated across four distinct graph diffusion architectures. Open questions include whether the pseudo-network training cost scales acceptably for very large graph datasets, whether the approach transfers to other structured-data diffusion models (e.g., point clouds, 3D molecules), and whether the score-difference correction can be further formalized with tighter theoretical guarantees on convergence to the true score.
