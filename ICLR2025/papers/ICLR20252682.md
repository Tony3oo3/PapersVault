## Problem

Despite the widespread use of system messages to steer LLM behavior, no comprehensive benchmark exists to evaluate how well models follow these messages in realistic multi-turn settings. Existing instruction-following benchmarks are limited to single-turn conversations, small-scale simplified datasets, or isolated aspects (e.g., priority rules only), failing to capture the interplay between complex multi-constraint system messages, conflicting user instructions, and conversational drift over multiple turns.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance consists of a system message containing 2–3 verifiable atomic constraints (drawn from six categories: action, content, background, role, format, style) paired with 5 rounds of user conversations that are labeled as aligned or misaligned relative to the system message, and further categorized as multi-turn parallel or dependent. The model-based verifier (GPT-4o at temperature 0) receives the full context—system message, all historical turns, current user instruction, and the model's response—along with a manually annotated per-instruction checklist, and outputs a structured JSON verdict (Yes/No per constraint) that feeds into the three aggregate metrics.

## Method

SysBench constructs its dataset through a three-stage pipeline: (1) collecting and filtering thousands of real-world system messages from online logs and internal agent data, (2) pre-generating 10-round dialogues per message using two GPT-4o instances in user/assistant roles, and (3) manual rewriting by 21 trained annotators into 5 high-quality turns with per-instruction evaluation checklists, validated by 5 quality inspectors over 18 days (300+ person-days). Evaluation employs an LLM-as-judge protocol where GPT-4o scores each response against the annotated checklist, with verified 97.3% constraint-level and 94% instruction-level agreement with human annotators. The core contribution is a three-level granularity metric suite—Constraint Satisfaction Rate (CSR) for individual constraint adherence, Instruction Satisfaction Rate (ISR) requiring all constraints in a turn to be met, and Session Stability Rate (SSR) measuring consecutive fully-compliant turns from the start of a session—enabling diagnosis of where exactly a model's system-message following breaks down. Investigative experiments further probe attention score allocation to system message tokens, the effect of replacing historical responses with ground truth, and whether marker tokens (<system> vs. <user>) produce measurably different attention patterns.

## Results

Across 16 LLMs evaluated on SysBench (500 sessions × 5 turns), GPT-4o achieves the highest Constraint Satisfaction Rate (CSR) at 87.1% and Session Stability Rate (SSR) at 54.4%, while GPT-4-Turbo leads in Instruction Satisfaction Rate (ISR) at 76.6%. Performance drops sharply for smaller models: Qwen2-7B scores only 47.0% CSR and 15.0% SSR, and ERNIE-4 achieves 50.7% CSR despite strong performance on other instruction-following benchmarks. The best SSR of 54.4% indicates that even top models fail to maintain full constraint compliance across all five turns in nearly half of sessions, and misaligned instructions (where user queries conflict with system messages) reduce ISR by 5–20 percentage points for most models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o and GPT-4-Turbo form a clear first tier, with GPT-4o leading in CSR (87.1% vs. 86.5%) and SSR (54.4% vs. 53.2%), while GPT-4-Turbo edges ahead in ISR (76.6% vs. 76.4%). Claude-3-Opus trails by 2–3 points on all three metrics. Among open-weight models, Qwen2.5-72B (80.4% CSR) and GLM-4 (78.9% CSR) are the strongest, outperforming Llama3.1-70B (76.6% CSR) and DeepSeek-V2 (76.1% CSR) by 2–4 points. A notable finding is that ERNIE-4, which performs well on existing instruction-following benchmarks, scores only 50.7% CSR on SysBench—comparable to the much smaller Qwen2-7B (47.0%)—suggesting that system-message following is a distinct and harder capability than general instruction following. On misaligned instructions (where user queries conflict with system messages), most models degrade by 5–20 ISR points, with GLM-4-9B showing the largest gap (48.3% aligned vs. 28.4% misaligned), while GPT-3.5 is an outlier that actually scores higher on misaligned (47.7%) than aligned (41.9%) instructions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of instruction-following evaluation and system message research. Existing instruction-following benchmarks (IFEval, FollowBench, IFEval, CFbench) are limited to single-turn conversations and do not capture the multi-turn, priority-based nature of system messages. Prior system message studies (Mu et al. 2023, Li et al. 2024a, Wallace et al. 2024, Zou et al. 2024) examine isolated aspects—priority rules, attention decay, jailbreak robustness—on small-scale or simplified datasets. SysBench departs from both lines by introducing a multi-turn evaluation framework with explicit alignment/misalignment categorization and three-level granularity metrics (CSR, ISR, SSR), while adopting the LLM-as-judge paradigm from Jiang et al. 2023 and Zheng et al. 2023 for verification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even the strongest model (GPT-4o) achieves only 54.4% Session Stability Rate, indicating that reliable system message following across multi-turn conversations remains far from solved—a critical gap for production deployments. The attention analysis showing that system and user messages receive nearly identical attention scores (differing only by marker tokens) challenges the assumption of architectural special treatment and points to training data construction as the true source of system message priority. Open questions include how to improve multi-turn stability without degrading single-turn performance, whether the alignment/misalignment gap can be closed through targeted training, and whether the six constraint categories generalize to more complex or compositional system messages.
