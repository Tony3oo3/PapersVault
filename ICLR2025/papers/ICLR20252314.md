## Problem

While subset learning—training only a fraction of a network's parameters—is widely used for pruning, transfer learning, and continual learning, and is motivated by neuroscience evidence that only a small subset of synapses changes during learning, no theoretical framework existed to characterize how different allocation strategies affect a network's expressive capacity. The paper asks: given a fixed budget of r learnable weights, how should they be distributed across the network to maximize the probability of matching a teacher's outputs?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252314/cropped_page3_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a neural network architecture (linear RNN, linear feedforward, or shallow ReLU), a specific allocation of r learnable weights where r = dm (the minimal number needed to match d outputs on m samples), and m input samples, then computes the match probability (MP)—the probability that the student can exactly replicate the teacher's outputs. The student controls only r < p weights while the remaining p−r weights are fixed at random zero-mean Gaussian values. The output is a classification of allocations as maximal (MP=1) or minimal (MP=0) under explicit structural conditions on row/column weight counts, with heuristic estimates for intermediate cases where the system reduces to polynomial equations.

## Method

The paper introduces a student-teacher framework with match probability as the expressivity benchmark, then analyzes three network families. For linear RNNs, the analysis decomposes into decoder (reduces to a linear estimator), encoder (Kronecker-product linear system), and recurrent connections (polynomial system of degree T reduced to linear and quadratic equations by introducing auxiliary variables F for intermediate hidden states). The key theorems establish sharp dichotomies: recurrent allocations are maximal when learnable weights are uniformly spread across exactly r/(Tb) rows or r/(Td) columns, and minimal when any single row exceeds Tb or any column exceeds Td learnable weights. For multi-layer feedforward networks, analogous Kronecker-product arguments yield similar row/column conditions, while for shallow ReLU networks the nonlinearity introduces sign-pattern constraints (diagonal matrices P_i) that further reduce the fraction of feasible configurations for concentrated allocations, adding an extra incentive to distribute weights. Empirical validation uses second-order solvers (scipy fsolve, AdaHessian) across network sizes, confirming the predicted sharp transition from zero to full expressivity at the theoretical threshold.

## Results

The paper establishes that for linear RNNs, allocations of learnable weights in the recurrent connections exhibit a sharp transition: MP = 0 (minimal) when fewer than r/(Tb) rows are used, and MP = 1 (maximal) when each active row contains exactly Tb learnable weights (Theorems 3.4–3.5). In a small illustrative model (b=1, d=1, n=2, T=2, m=2), suboptimal non-linear allocations yield intermediate match probabilities of 0.83 (off-diagonal) and 0.74 (diagonal). On MNIST with a shallow ReLU network (n=1000, m=1000, q=784, d=10), MP reaches 1 whenever the allocation spans more than 40 rows, confirming that distributed allocations achieve full expressivity even on structured real-world data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical framework paper that does not benchmark against competing methods; instead, it compares different allocation strategies within the same architecture. The central finding is a dichotomy: for the decoder and encoder in LRNNs, and for single intermediate layers in LFFNs, allocations are either maximal (MP=1) or minimal (MP=0) depending on whether row/column weight counts stay below thresholds (m, d, Tm, or Td). For recurrent connections and multi-layer allocations, the problem becomes non-linear, and the paper shows that distributing the same r learnable weights across more rows (fewer per row) consistently increases MP, with a sharp threshold at the minimum number of rows required by Theorem 3.4. The ReLU analysis adds that nonlinearity further penalizes concentrated allocations by reducing the number of feasible activation-pattern configurations (Lemmas D.3–D.4), reinforcing the distributed-allocation principle across all three model families studied.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the student-teacher framework of Gardner and Derrida (1989) and Cover's (1965) sample-based expressivity measurement, but applies them to a fundamentally different question: how the *allocation* of a fixed budget of learnable weights affects expressivity, rather than the expressivity of a fully-trained model. It departs from the expressivity literature (Cybenko, Hornik, Raghu, Collins et al.) which assumes all parameters are trainable, and from the practical subset-learning literature (pruning, LoRA, PEFT) which optimizes for efficiency without characterizing expressivity. The connection to Subag (2024) on random polynomial systems provides the mathematical foundation for the conjecture that more distributed allocations (yielding more quadratic equations) increase match probability. The paper explicitly positions itself as distinct from the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis, which discovers useful sparsity post hoc, whereas here the allocation is designed a priori under a strict resource constraint.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical characterization of how weight allocation affects expressivity, yielding clean maximal/minimal conditions for linear RNNs and feedforward networks, and a general principle that distributing learnable weights across more neurons increases expressivity. This directly informs parameter-efficient fine-tuning strategies (LoRA, adapter design, continual learning) by suggesting that spreading a fixed parameter budget broadly is theoretically preferable to concentrating it. Key limitations include the reliance on linear models, i.i.d. input assumptions, and the gap between expressivity and generalization/optimization performance. Open questions include extending results to deeper non-linear architectures, structured (non-i.i.d.) data, and connecting the theoretical allocation principles to practical training dynamics in large-scale models.
