## Problem

Irregularly sampled multivariate time series (IMTS) forecasting lacks a standardized benchmark, with all prior evaluations relying on just four datasets (USHCN, PhysioNet-2012, MIMIC-III, MIMIC-IV). On these datasets, a trivial constant-prediction baseline matches or outperforms sophisticated neural ODE models, making it impossible to meaningfully differentiate forecasting architectures. This dataset bias hampers progress on ODE-based models, which are the natural model family for continuous-time dynamics.

## Input / Output

Each IMTS instance is a sequence of (time, value-vector) pairs where the value vector has up to C channels, most entries being NaN due to 80% random observation dropout. The model receives the first 50% of the sequence as context and must produce point forecasts for queried (future timepoint, channel) pairs in the remaining 50%. Each of the 50 Physiome-ODE datasets contains 2000 instances with 200 observation steps, Gaussian observation noise (σ=0.05), and channels normalized to zero mean and unit variance.

## Method

The paper introduces a pipeline to generate challenging IMTS datasets from 208 biological ODE models in the Physiome Model Repository. A new complexity metric, Joint Gradient Deviation (JGD), combines within-function gradient variance (MGD) and between-function gradient variance (MPGD) to score how hard a generator is to forecast. For each ODE, spread parameters controlling initial conditions, constants, and duration are optimized via rejection sampling to maximize JGD, yielding 50 selected datasets. The forecasting task is then evaluated with five recent models (GRU-ODE-Bayes, LinODEnet, CRU, Neural Flows, GraFITi) plus a constant-prediction baseline (GraFITi-C) under 5-fold cross-validation, revealing that ODE-based models like LinODEnet and CRU significantly outperform the constant baseline on high-JGD datasets, while no single model dominates across all 50 datasets.

## Results

On the 50-dataset Physiome-ODE benchmark, LinODEnet achieves the best overall performance with an average rank of 2.10, winning the most individual datasets. GraFITi follows closely with an average rank of 2.20, while the constant-prediction baseline GraFITi-C surprisingly achieves an average rank of 2.86, outperforming both Neural Flows and GRU-ODE-Bayes. On the hardest datasets (e.g., DUP01, JGD=2.697), no model significantly outperforms the constant baseline (GraFITi-C MSE 0.951 vs. best ODE model CRU at 0.958), whereas on the easiest datasets (e.g., MIT01, JGD=0.015), all models converge to the Bayes error of ~0.003 MSE. The JGD score correlates with best achievable MSE at a Spearman coefficient of 0.77, validating it as a difficulty measure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On Physiome-ODE, LinODEnet (Scholz et al., 2022) emerges as the overall best model, winning the most individual datasets and achieving the lowest average rank (2.10), particularly excelling on mid-to-easy difficulty datasets (e.g., LI01: 0.084 vs. next-best CRU at 0.175; PUR01: 0.106 vs. GraFITi at 0.153). GraFITi (Yalavarthi et al., 2024) is competitive with LinODEnet (average rank 2.20 vs. 2.10) and wins several datasets, but its constant variant GraFITi-C surprisingly outperforms both Neural Flows and GRU-ODE-Bayes overall (rank 2.86), and on the four hardest datasets (DUP01, JEL01, DOK01, INA01) GraFITi-C actually achieves the lowest MSE, indicating these tasks are too difficult for any learned model to beat a constant prediction. CRU (Schirmer et al., 2022) is consistently competitive, often placing 2nd or 3rd, and is the best ODE-based model on the hardest datasets. GRU-ODE-Bayes (De Brouwer et al., 2019) and Neural Flows (Biloš et al., 2021) are generally the weakest, with GRU-ODE-Bayes frequently achieving the worst MSE on a given dataset. On the traditional four datasets (Table 1), GraFITi dominates all ODE-based models, but GraFITi-C outperforms all ODE-based models on PhysioNet-2012 and MIMIC-IV, motivating the need for the new benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by repurposing the Physiome Model Repository—a pre-existing collection of 208 biological ODE models with auto-generated Python code—into a forecasting benchmark, rather than building on a specific prior method. It departs from the current IMTS evaluation paradigm (which relies on just four datasets: USHCN, PhysioNet-2012, MIMIC-III, MIMIC-IV) by demonstrating that those datasets are fundamentally inadequate, as a constant-prediction baseline matches or beats state-of-the-art ODE-based models on them. The JGD metric for selecting challenging ODE instances is novel, and the paper positions itself against related benchmarks (Monash, Gilpin's chaotic ODEs, PDEBench) by emphasizing irregular sampling, multivariate structure, and biological grounding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Physiome-ODE is the first dedicated IMTS forecasting benchmark and is an order of magnitude larger than the current four-dataset evaluation, providing the field with a standardized, reproducible testbed that can actually differentiate between competing architectures. The finding that no single model dominates across all 50 datasets—while ODE-based models like LinODEnet emerge as the overall best—suggests the field's prior pessimism about ODE-based forecasting was an artifact of poor evaluation rather than a fundamental limitation. Open questions include whether the semi-synthetic (ODE-generated) nature limits transfer to truly observed data, whether 2000 instances per dataset suffices for larger models, and whether the JGD-based selection captures all relevant dimensions of forecasting difficulty.
