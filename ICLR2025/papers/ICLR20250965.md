## Problem

Existing visual analogy benchmarks (ARC, ConceptARC, Raven's Progressive Matrices) rely on abstract geometric shapes and grids that lack real-world relevance, and they measure only final prediction accuracy without revealing where in the reasoning process models fail. No prior benchmark tests the basic visual analogical reasoning that developmental psychology shows is present in 3-year-old children, nor does it use children as a performance baseline. This paper asks whether state-of-the-art LMMs can match even young children on fundamental visual analogical reasoning with physically grounded objects.

## Input / Output

Each trial presents a before/after image pair of a real-world object (rendered from 3D models of household items and children's toys) undergoing one of five transformations, followed by a new test object with three candidate transformations. The model answers three sequential multiple-choice questions: identifying the transformation domain (4 options + 'no change'), specifying the exact rule within that domain (3 options), and selecting which of three visual options correctly extrapolates the rule to the new object. The visual extrapolation is scored independently of the verbal stages, allowing the evaluation to separate verbal reasoning from visual reasoning capabilities.

## Method

The paper introduces KiVA (4,300 transformations) and its harder variant KiVA-adults (2,900 transformations), structured around a three-stage experimental paradigm inspired by Sternberg's component processes model of analogical reasoning and developmental psychology studies of children. Objects are rendered from 3D models in the Google Scanned Objects and children's toy datasets, with transformations applied programmatically across five domains: color (to red/green/blue), size (scaling by factor 2), number (addition/subtraction of 1–2 objects), rotation (90°/180°), and reflection (x/y-axis). The evaluation compares four LMMs (GPT-o1, GPT-4V, LLaVA-1.5, MANTIS) against 250 adults and 42 children (ages 3–5) on identical trials, with additional experiments on no-change detection, trial-to-trial consistency, the effect of verbal questions on visual extrapolation, and four prompt engineering strategies (code generation, self-reflection, instruction-based reasoning, in-context learning). A large vision model trained entirely without linguistic data is also tested to isolate the role of language in visual analogical reasoning.

## Results

On KiVA, GPT-o1, GPT-4V, and MANTIS outperform 3–5-year-old children on verbal classification of rotation and color changes, but all four LMMs underperform children on visual extrapolation in the number and reflection domains. In the harder KiVA-adults benchmark, GPT-o1 approaches adult-level accuracy only in the color domain; performance drops sharply for rotation, reflection, and number. LLaVA-1.5 and MANTIS hallucinate a change in 100% of no-change trials during verbal classification, while GPT-o1 correctly identifies 'no change' across all domains. Model performance degrades monotonically from verbal classification → verbal specification → visual extrapolation, whereas adults show near-perfect accuracy at all three stages.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-o1 (o1-2024-12-17) is the strongest LMM tested, approaching adult performance only in the color domain of KiVA-adults and outperforming 3–5-year-old children on classifying rotation and color changes in KiVA, yet it still falls below child-level accuracy on number and reflection extrapolation. GPT-4V (gpt-4-vision-preview) follows a similar pattern to GPT-o1 but with lower overall accuracy; it correctly identifies 'no change' only in the size domain during verbal classification. LLaVA-1.5 and MANTIS are the weakest models, performing near or at chance on visual extrapolation and hallucinating a transformation in 100% of no-change trials during verbal classification. The Large Vision Model (Bai et al., 2024), tested without any linguistic input, can solve some KiVA analogies and shows the same domain-dependent pattern (stronger on color/size than number/space), suggesting the limitation is not purely linguistic. Across all models, prompt engineering strategies (chain-of-thought, in-context learning, code generation, self-reflection, visual prompting) failed to improve performance, underscoring that the gap is architectural rather than a prompting issue.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by repurposing developmental psychology paradigms (Goddu et al., 2020; Sternberg's component processes model) into a benchmark for LMMs, deliberately departing from prior visual analogy benchmarks (ARC, ConceptARC, Raven's Progressive Matrices) that rely on abstract shapes and grids. Rather than building on a specific prior method, it introduces a child-appropriate difficulty level with real-world 3D-scanned objects and a three-stage decomposition (classify → specify → extrapolate) that no prior benchmark provides. The stimuli are sourced from established 3D object datasets (Downs et al., 2022; Stojanov et al., 2021) to better match LMM training distributions, and the evaluation structure is inspired by how developmental psychologists probe analogical reasoning in toddlers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

KiVA provides the first developmentally-grounded, real-world visual analogy benchmark with both child and adult human baselines, filling a clear gap between abstract puzzle benchmarks and everyday visual cognition. The three-stage decomposition is diagnostically valuable: it reveals that LMMs can often identify *what* changed but fail to *apply* the rule to a novel object, pinpointing a specific reasoning bottleneck rather than a monolithic failure. Open questions include whether symbolic visual representations or 3D-aware architectures could close the gap in spatial/numerical domains, whether language is truly necessary for visual analogical reasoning (the large vision model results hint it may not be), and how compositional multi-transformation reasoning (KiVA-compositionality) will scale. A limitation is the small child sample (n=42) and the restriction to five transformation domains, leaving depth, texture, and physical affordances untested.
