## Problem

Existing molecular graph generators face a fundamental tension: VAE-based methods use fixed-size latent spaces that cannot accommodate variable molecular sizes, while autoregressive decoders impose an artificial generation order that prevents learning permutation-invariant distributions. All-at-once discrete diffusion methods avoid these issues but struggle to accurately capture the joint distribution of nodes and edges, lagging behind autoregressive baselines by large margins on distribution learning benchmarks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250609/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 2D molecular graphs (atom types, formal charges, and bond types as one-hot encodings) as training input and produces 2D molecular graphs as output. The number of atoms N must be specified a priori before sampling, drawn from the empirical size distribution of the training set. For conditional generation, a target property value (e.g., LogP or molecular weight) is additionally provided; for similarity-constrained optimization, an initial molecular graph and a Tanimoto similarity threshold are given. The model is E(3)-equivariant and S_N-invariant, so no generation order or data augmentation is needed.

## Method

The SYCO framework introduces an autoencoder that maps 2D molecular graphs to 3D Euclidean point clouds: the encoder first computes synthetic 3D coordinates via the ETKDG conformer generation algorithm (injecting chemical domain knowledge as an inductive bias), then applies an EGNN to produce a continuous latent embedding; the decoder uses MLP classifiers to predict atom types, formal charges, and bond types from the latent point cloud. An E(3)-Equivariant Diffusion Model (EDM) is then trained in a second stage on the latent point cloud space, reducing graph generation to point cloud generation followed by node/edge classification. The paper further proposes a novel similarity-constrained optimization procedure that combines RePaint-style inpainting (to add new atoms beyond the initial molecule's size) with regressor-based diffusion guidance (to improve target properties), enabling atom-level optimization without retraining. This yields EDM-SYCO, an atom-based, all-at-once, permutation-invariant generator that outperforms all non-autoregressive baselines by over 26% on FCD for ZINC250K and improves conditional generation by up to 3.9×.

## Results

On ZINC250K, EDM-SYCO achieves a FCD of 0.85±0.01 and KL of 0.96±0.00, setting a new state-of-the-art that outperforms all all-at-once diffusion baselines by more than 26% on FCD and all autoregressive baselines as well. On GuacaMol, EDM-SYCO reaches FCD 0.79±0.002 and KL 0.93±0.006, surpassing DiGress (FCD 0.68) by 16%. For conditional generation, EDM-SYCO achieves MW MAE of 3.86±0.08 (vs. 60.30 for DiGress and 15.18 for FreeGress) and LogP MAE of 0.14±0.00, with 88% validity on MW. In similarity-constrained optimization, EDM-SYCO outperforms all optimization baselines by an average factor of 3.5 across LogP, QED, and DRD2 tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EDM-SYCO outperforms all all-at-once diffusion baselines on FCD by a wide margin: 0.85 vs. 0.67 (SwingNN), 0.65 (DiGress), 0.64 (GRUM), 0.17 (GeoLDM), and 0.10 (GDSS) on ZINC250K. It also surpasses the best autoregressive methods (MoLeR at 0.83, MAGNET at 0.76, JT-VAE at 0.75) on FCD, though autoregressive methods maintain 100% validity due to per-step valency checks. On GuacaMol, EDM-SYCO (FCD 0.79) beats DiGress (0.68) by 16%. For conditional generation, EDM-SYCO dramatically outperforms DiGress on MW (MAE 3.86 vs. 60.30, a 15.6× improvement) and LogP (0.14 vs. 0.49, a 3.5× improvement), and slightly edges out FreeGress on LogP (0.14 vs. 0.15) while matching it on MW validity. In similarity-constrained optimization, EDM-SYCO achieves 3.11±1.27 LogP improvement (SIM≥0.4) and 46.4% QED success rate, outperforming all optimization baselines (JT-VAE, CG-VAE, GCPN) by an average factor of 3.5 while remaining competitive with translation approaches (Seq2Seq, JTNN, AtomG2G, HierG2G). The GeoLDM comparison (Table 2) shows that simply applying a 3D model to 2D data without the EGNN-based bond predictor yields only 12% validity versus 88% for EDM-SYCO, highlighting the critical role of the learned decoder.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces the SYCO framework, which bridges 2D molecular graph generation and 3D point cloud generation by mapping graphs to synthetic 3D coordinates (via ETKDG) and learning the inverse with an EGNN-based autoencoder. The diffusion component is directly adapted from EDM (Hoogeboom et al., 2022), while the conditional generation and scaffold-constrained generation procedures repurpose classifier guidance (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021) and RePaint inpainting (Lugmayr et al., 2022) respectively. Conceptually, SYCO is a cross-modality latent generative model: unlike GeoLDM, which embeds 3D point clouds into lower-dimensional point clouds, it maps discrete 2D graphs into continuous 3D point clouds, enabling 3D generative models to be trained on graph-only datasets. The paper departs from prior all-at-once discrete diffusion methods (DiGress, GDSS) by operating in a continuous latent space rather than directly on discrete node/edge attributes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework demonstrates that a continuous Euclidean latent space (synthetic 3D coordinates) is substantially more effective than discrete graph space for diffusion-based molecular generation, closing the long-standing gap between all-at-once and autoregressive methods on FCD. The similarity-constrained optimization procedure, which can add atoms during reverse diffusion via inpainting, addresses a well-known limitation of 3D diffusion models and outperforms prior optimization baselines by ~3.5×. Key limitations include the fixed number of atoms (requiring a priori size specification), inability to remove atoms during optimization, and high inference cost from 1000 diffusion steps. Open questions include whether variable-sized generation can be achieved without the categorical size-sampling workaround, and whether more efficient samplers (flow matching, few-step distillation) can reduce the computational burden.
