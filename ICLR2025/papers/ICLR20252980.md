## Problem

Modern ML systems can produce highly undesirable outputs on very rare inputs, a risk amplified by distribution shift and adversarial inputs such as LLM jailbreaks. While adversarial training is the standard remedy, it requires finding positive examples and has weak transfer between search methods. This paper tackles the more fundamental intermediate problem of simply estimating the probability of a rare output under a formally-specified input distribution, when that probability (10⁻⁹ to 10⁻⁵) is far too small for random sampling to yield a useful estimate under a constrained compute budget.

## Input / Output

The methods take a small transformer language model (1, 2, or 4 layers, d=512, |V|=48262, temperature 0), a formally-specified input distribution over independent token sequences of fixed length (8 distributions ranging from hex strings to in-context learning prompts), and a target token, and produce a single probability estimate for that token being the argmax output. Each method is constrained to 2¹⁶ model calls, while ground truth is obtained from 2³² samples. Estimates are post-processed with a per-(method, distribution) affine fit in log-space to handle zero outputs, and evaluated via Itakura-Saito loss, which is sensitive to multiplicative errors at small probabilities.

## Method

The paper introduces four estimation methods in two families. Importance sampling methods (ITGIS and MHIS) define a Boltzmann posterior q(x) ∝ p(x)·exp(M_t(x)/T) that up-weights inputs likely to produce the target token, then re-weight samples for an unbiased estimate; ITGIS treats tokens independently using gradient-based per-position score functions, while MHIS uses a Metropolis-Hastings random walk with a gradient-guided proposal adapted from Greedy Coordinate Gradient to handle non-independent token interactions. Activation extrapolation methods (QLD and GLD) instead fit a distribution to the model's internal representations: QLD whitens pre-unembed activations, decomposes them into a 1-D direction (the shortest accepting vector) and its orthogonal complement, then constructs n² synthetic samples by recombining components to estimate the acceptance-region fraction; GLD fits a Gaussian to the target-vs-max logit difference. The key finding is that importance sampling outperforms activation extrapolation, which in turn outperforms naive sampling, with MHIS favoring larger models and ITGIS favoring smaller ones.

## Results

Using Itakura-Saito loss (lower is better) as the evaluation metric, importance sampling methods consistently outperform activation extrapolation across all three model sizes. On the 1-layer model, ITGIS achieves the best average loss of 1.37, followed by MHIS (1.55), QLD (1.70), GLD (2.36), and the constant baseline (2.49). On the 4-layer model, MHIS achieves the best average loss of 1.48, while ITGIS (1.92), QLD (2.16), GLD (2.48), and Constant (2.62) follow. GLD is barely better than the constant baseline across all model sizes, indicating that fitting a Gaussian to logit differences provides almost no signal over naive sampling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four proposed methods against a constant baseline (representing naive sampling performance). Both importance sampling methods (ITGIS and MHIS) outperform both activation extrapolation methods (QLD and GLD) on average across all model sizes, with ITGIS winning on the 1-layer (1.37 vs. 1.55) and 2-layer (1.70 vs. 1.65) models, while MHIS wins on the 4-layer model (1.48 vs. 1.92). The authors attribute this shift to larger models having more complex inter-token interactions that MHIS's non-independent token sampling can capture. QLD provides a meaningful improvement over the constant baseline (e.g., 1.70 vs. 2.49 on 1-layer), but is consistently outperformed by both importance sampling methods. GLD, the simplest baseline, is nearly indistinguishable from the constant (e.g., 2.36 vs. 2.49 on 1-layer), confirming that a Gaussian fit to logit differences is insufficient for rare-event estimation. Performance varies significantly across the 8 input distributions: hex and icl favor MHIS, while spanish and camel favor ITGIS.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the low probability estimation problem from computer vision (Webb et al., 2019) to transformer language models, introducing a formal setting with argmax sampling and single-token target behaviors. Methodologically, it builds on two distinct lineages: the importance sampling methods adapt the Greedy Coordinate Gradient jailbreaking algorithm (Zou et al., 2023) into a Metropolis-Hastings proposal distribution, while the activation extrapolation methods (particularly Quadratic Logit Decomposition) operationalize the presumption of independence framework from Christiano et al. (2022). The paper's key conceptual contribution is explicitly distinguishing these two families, showing that importance sampling for rare events is essentially adversarial training, whereas activation extrapolation offers a potentially orthogonal path that does not require finding positive examples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete gap in AI safety: current adversarial training methods have weak transfer between search strategies, and the paper argues that new low probability estimation methods (especially activation extrapolation) could enable reducing rare-event probabilities without the search bottleneck. However, the empirical results are limited to small (1–4 layer) models, single-token behaviors at temperature 0, and independent-token input distributions, leaving open whether the findings generalize to the multi-token, interactive behaviors that are actually concerning in deployment. The connection to deceptive alignment and distribution shift gives the work broader relevance, but the narrow experimental scope means the practical impact remains speculative until methods are validated on larger, more realistic settings.
