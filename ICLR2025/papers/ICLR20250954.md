## Problem

Existing reward model benchmarks (e.g., RewardBench) evaluate RMs using pairwise accuracy on a limited set of scenarios, which does not directly reflect the RM's actual role in alignment—rewarding high-quality responses rather than merely distinguishing binary preferences. This leads to a disconnect between benchmark scores and downstream alignment performance, and fails to expose generalization defects across diverse real-world scenarios. The paper asks whether a more comprehensive, scenario-diverse benchmark with an alignment-aligned evaluation paradigm can better predict RM effectiveness in practice.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250954/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes 3,197 real-world prompts (from WildChat) paired with responses generated by 14 LLMs, organized into two evaluation formats: (chosen, rejected) preference pairs for pairwise testing, and (query, winner, list of losers) triplets for Best-of-N testing where the RM must rank the winner above all losers simultaneously. For discriminative RMs, a scalar score is assigned to each response; for generative RMs (LLM-as-a-Judge), a direct preference comparison is elicited. Outputs include per-scenario and aggregate pairwise/BoN accuracy scores, overall model rankings, and Spearman rank correlations with downstream alignment benchmarks (MixEval, Arena-Hard, AdvBench).

## Method

RMB introduces two key innovations over prior RM benchmarks. First, it covers 49 fine-grained real-world scenarios (37 under helpfulness, 12 under harmlessness) with 18,000+ preference pairs constructed via GPT-4 point-wise scoring using scenario-specific 'key features' to ensure context-sensitive quality judgments. Second, it proposes Best-of-N (BoN) evaluation as a new benchmarking paradigm: for each query, the RM must correctly identify a single winner among multiple candidates, mirroring the actual alignment use case of selecting the best response from N samples. The paper verifies that BoN scores correlate more strongly with downstream alignment performance (measured via BoN sampling on external benchmarks) than pairwise scores, and that RMB overall shows positive Spearman correlation with alignment rankings while RewardBench does not. Additional analyses reveal a helpfulness–harmlessness trade-off (Spearman ρ = −0.57 among top RMs), the superior BoN performance of generative critic models, and the ineffectiveness of majority-voting confidence weighting for RM evaluation.

## Results

On the RMB leaderboard, GPT-4o-2024-05-13 achieves the highest overall score of 0.738, followed by Qwen2-72B-Instruct (0.723) and Starling-RM-34B (0.712). BoN evaluation is substantially harder than pairwise, with an average 17.5% score decline; generative RMs suffer larger drops (0.191–0.222) than discriminative RMs (0.158–0.167). RMB demonstrates positive Spearman rank correlation with downstream alignment performance on MixEval, Arena-Hard, and AdvBench, whereas RewardBench exhibits poor correlation, confirming RMB's stronger connection to actual alignment effectiveness. The correlation between helpfulness and harmlessness rankings among top-10 RMs is −0.57, revealing a significant trade-off between the two alignment objectives.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The most critical comparison is RMB versus RewardBench (Lambert et al., 2024) in terms of correlation with downstream alignment: RMB shows consistent positive Spearman rank correlation across MixEval, Arena-Hard, and AdvBench, while RewardBench exhibits poor correlation, indicating its evaluation paradigm does not effectively predict alignment performance. Among the 19 evaluated reward models, GPT-4o (generative) leads overall (0.738) over the best discriminative RM Starling-RM-34B (0.712), with Claude-3.5-Sonnet achieving the highest helpfulness pairwise score (0.838) but dropping sharply on harmlessness BoN (0.518), illustrating the helpfulness–harmlessness trade-off. Qwen2-72B-Instruct (0.723) is the strongest open-weight generative RM, outperforming Llama3.1-70B-Instruct (0.689) by 0.034 overall. Llama2-70b-chat (0.438) and Tulu-v2.5-13b (0.453) are the weakest, confirming that older or smaller models struggle significantly with preference judgment. BoN evaluation amplifies these gaps: the standard deviation of model scores rises from 0.076 (helpfulness pairwise) to 0.121 (helpfulness BoN), making BoN a more discriminative metric than pairwise accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the reward model evaluation task established by RewardBench (Lambert et al., 2024) in two key ways: broadening scenario coverage from 4 aggregated tasks to 49 fine-grained real-world scenarios, and introducing Best-of-N (BoN) evaluation as a new paradigm that more directly mirrors how RMs are used in alignment (selecting the best from multiple candidates). It builds on the LLM-as-a-Judge framework (Zheng et al., 2024) for evaluating generative RMs and draws on the RLHF alignment literature (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022a) for its helpfulness/harmlessness taxonomy. The critical departure from prior work is the explicit verification that benchmark scores correlate with downstream alignment performance via BoN sampling on external benchmarks, a connection that prior RM benchmarks had not established.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that RewardBench exhibits poor correlation with actual downstream alignment performance while RMB's BoN evaluation shows stronger correlation challenges the field's current standard for RM evaluation and suggests a more meaningful evaluation paradigm. The quantified trade-off between helpfulness and harmlessness (Spearman correlation of −0.57 among top RMs) provides concrete evidence that a single RM struggles to serve both objectives simultaneously, pointing toward the need for multi-objective or scenario-adaptive reward modeling. Open questions remain: whether majority voting can effectively reduce label noise in RM evaluation (the paper finds it does not, but the analysis is limited), whether full RL-based verification (e.g., PPO) would yield different correlations than BoN sampling, and how to handle the bias accumulation in generative RM evaluation that the authors acknowledge but do not resolve.
