## Problem

No publicly available benchmark exists for evaluating LLMs on nutrition estimation from natural language meal descriptions. Existing nutrition datasets are either tabular (requiring exact food-name matches and multiple sequential lookups) or image-based (requiring real-time photos with privacy concerns), neither supporting the flexible, free-form meal descriptions people naturally use. This paper asks whether LLMs can accurately estimate carbohydrates from such descriptions, how they compare to professional nutritionists, and what the real-world health impact would be for diabetes patients.

## Input / Output

The model receives a free-form English meal description specifying food items and serving sizes, where portions are expressed either in natural language terms (e.g., "a tablespoon," "half a cup") or precise metric measurements (e.g., "80g"). The model outputs a single carbohydrate estimate in grams, with accuracy measured as Acc@7.5 (prediction within ±7.5g of ground truth, derived from the 1:15 insulin-to-carbohydrate ratio). The full NUTRIBENCH dataset also annotates each of its 11,857 meal descriptions with protein, fat, and calorie labels, though the primary evaluation task focuses on carbohydrates.

## Method

NUTRIBENCH is constructed by sourcing real-world dietary intake data from the What We Eat in America survey (US) and FAO/WHO GIFT (10 countries), then using GPT-4o-mini to generate diverse natural language meal descriptions from structured food-item records, followed by human verification to eliminate hallucinated or missing food names and servings. For evaluation, twelve LLMs are tested under four prompting paradigms—Base, Chain-of-Thought, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (using a 450K-entry FDC-based retrieval database with semantic similarity search), and RAG+CoT. The paper also fine-tunes Gemma2-27B with qLoRA (rank 8) on 600K FDC-derived meal descriptions to assess whether domain-specific training narrows the gap with GPT-4o. A real-world risk assessment simulates the effect of LLM carbohydrate estimates on blood glucose levels of 20 virtual Type 1 diabetes patients using the Tidepool Data Science Simulator across 44,800 scenarios.

## Results

On NUTRIBENCH (11,857 meal descriptions), GPT-4o with Chain-of-Thought prompting achieves the highest carbohydrate estimation accuracy of 66.82% (Acc@7.5) with a 99.16% answer rate and MAE of 8.61g. Among open-source models, Llama 3.1-405B-FP8 with RAG+CoT performs best at 59.89% accuracy and 96.05% answer rate. Fine-tuning Gemma2-27B with 600K FDC-derived meal descriptions (qLoRA, rank 8) improves MAE from 13.33g to 9.57g and accuracy from 45.61% to 61.71%, though it still trails GPT-4o CoT. In the blood glucose risk simulation (44,800 runs), GPT-4o CoT estimates yield the highest %Time in Range (69.88%), lowest %Time Below Range (4.18%), and lowest Blood Glucose Risk Index (17.83) compared to three professional nutritionists.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o with CoT prompting (66.82% Acc@7.5) outperforms all other evaluated models, including the strongest open-source model Llama 3.1-405B-FP8 with RAG+CoT (59.89%) by nearly 7 percentage points. GPT-4o-mini with CoT surpasses GPT-4o with Base prompting, demonstrating that effective prompting can make smaller models competitive with larger ones. The fine-tuned Gemma2-27B-FT (61.71% Acc@7.5, 100% answer rate) narrows the gap to GPT-4o CoT but does not close it, suggesting GPT-family fine-tuning remains a promising direction. In the nutritionist comparison on 72 U.S. meal descriptions, GPT-4o CoT achieves higher accuracy than all three nutritionists (who averaged 43 minutes for 72 queries vs. ~2 minutes for GPT-4o-mini), though nutritionists with lookup access reach comparable performance (59.72% vs. 60.56% Acc@7.5). In the Type 1 diabetes blood glucose simulation, GPT-4o CoT produces the lowest overall blood glucose risk (BGRI 17.83) compared to Nutritionist 1 (24.01), Nutritionist 2 (22.48), and Nutritionist 3 (20.81), primarily driven by the lowest hypoglycemia rate (%TBR 4.18% vs. 6.76–8.79%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the first publicly available benchmark (NUTRIBENCH) for evaluating LLMs on nutrition estimation from natural language meal descriptions. It departs from two prior lines of work: (1) tabular and image-based nutrition estimation systems (FDC, NutritionVerse, UMDFood90k) that require exact database matches or real-time image capture, and (2) early CNN-based approaches (Korpusik et al. 2017; Taylor et al. 2021) that matched food items from descriptions to external databases but were never publicly released. The paper repurposes established LLM prompting strategies (CoT, RAG) for this nutrition-specific task and adds a novel real-world risk assessment via blood glucose simulation for Type 1 diabetes patients, which has no direct precedent in the nutrition estimation literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NUTRIBENCH provides the field with a much-needed public benchmark for a practically important task—accurate carbohydrate estimation directly impacts insulin dosing for millions of diabetes patients. The finding that GPT-4o with CoT outperforms professional nutritionists in both accuracy and speed (2 minutes vs. 43 minutes) suggests LLMs could serve as accessible, scalable nutrition tools, though the nutritionist comparison is limited to 3 participants and 72 queries. Key open questions include: whether fine-tuning GPT-family models can close the remaining gap, how to address the significant cultural performance disparities (MAE ranging from 2.2g in Nigeria to 15.1g in Sri Lanka), and whether RAG can be made effective by aligning retrieval databases with natural serving descriptions. The benchmark currently covers only carbohydrate estimation, leaving protein, fat, and calorie estimation as natural extensions.
