## Problem

Neural Theorem Proving (NTP) is bottlenecked by severe data scarcity: formal corpora like Mathlib contain only ~110k theorems, orders of magnitude fewer than general text. Existing synthetic-data approaches are either autoformalization-based (labor-intensive, constrained by human-collected problems) or symbolic-rule-based (restricted to narrow domains like inequalities or 2D geometry). No general, scalable framework exists for synthesizing theorems directly in symbolic space on an advanced proof assistant like Lean.

## Input / Output

The synthesis pipeline takes each candidate theorem from Mathlib as input, along with a pool of potentially invocable theorems and predefined instruction templates for the rw and apply tactics. It produces verified Lean theorems (6.3M total, a ~57× expansion) and extracts ~30k deduplicated state-tactic pairs per tactic for training. The trained LLM then takes a Lean proof state (local context + goal) as input and outputs the next tactic to apply, following the proofstep-prediction formulation of Polu & Sutskever (2020).

## Method

ALCHEMY treats theorem synthesis as an up-to-down symbolic mutation process: for each candidate theorem, it identifies all invocable theorems by executing rw and apply instruction templates in an interactive Lean environment (Algorithm 1), then mutates the statement by replacing terms with their equivalent forms (rw) or logical antecedents (apply) using AST-based parsing. Proofs are constructed by inserting a `have` tactic that restores the original component via the inverse mutation, then concatenating with the original proof block; all synthesized theorems are verified by Lean's type checker. For model training, the authors perform continual pretraining (next-token prediction) on the augmented Mathlib corpus, followed by supervised fine-tuning on state-tactic pairs using the proofstep-prediction objective, with best-first-search (1×32×100 budget) at inference time. This increases Mathlib from 110k to 6.3M theorems and yields +4.70% absolute improvement on the Leandojo novel-premises split and +2.47% on the out-of-distribution miniF2F benchmark.

## Results

On the Leandojo benchmark (in-distribution), the best configuration (Llama-3-8B + rw + apply synthetic data) achieves 59.82% on the random split and 43.22% on the novel-premises split, yielding a +4.70% absolute improvement over the Mathlib-train-only baseline (38.52%). On the out-of-distribution miniF2F benchmark, the same configuration reaches 36.48% (89/244 theorems proved), a +2.47% absolute gain over the baseline. The deepseek-coder-7b-base-v1.5 variant with both tactics achieves 60.39% random and 43.46% novel-premises on Leandojo, and the data synthesis pipeline expands Mathlib from ~110k to ~6.3M theorems (×25 for rw, ×44 for apply).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Leandojo/Reprover baselines (Yang et al., 2023), the proposed method substantially outperforms both the base Reprover (47.6/23.2) and its retrieval-augmented variant (51.2/26.3) on both splits, with the largest gap on novel-premises (+16.92 pp over Reprover, +16.92 pp over w/ retrieval). Compared to LLMSTEP (Welleck & Saha, 2023; 47.6% random), the method gains +12.2 pp on the random split. Against the Llama-3-8B and deepseek-coder-7b baselines fine-tuned solely on Mathlib-train, the synthetic data yields consistent improvements of +1.4 to +2.7 pp (random) and +2.8 to +4.7 pp (novel-premises), with the combined rw+apply configuration always outperforming either tactic alone. On the out-of-distribution miniF2F benchmark, the method (36.48%) surpasses llemma-7b (32.38%) and mistral-7b (32.38%) by ~4 pp, though the gain over the in-distribution setting is smaller, which the authors attribute to a mismatch between the synthesized rw/apply tactics and the advanced tactics (norm_num, linarith, omega) preferred for competition-level problems.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the proofstep-prediction training paradigm and best-first-search framework established by Polu & Sutskever (2020), and leverages the Leandojo infrastructure (Yang et al., 2023) for Lean interaction and state-tactic extraction. It departs from the dominant autoformalization-based data synthesis route (Wu et al., 2022; Xin et al., 2024; Ying et al., 2024) by operating entirely in symbolic space, and extends earlier domain-specific symbolic synthesis efforts (Wang & Deng, 2020 on Metamath; Wu et al., 2021 on inequalities; AlphaGeometry on 2D geometry) to the general Lean/Mathlib setting. The key conceptual move is repurposing Lean's own basic tactics (rw, apply) as mutation operators on existing theorems, treating theorem synthesis analogously to function construction in programming.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses the fundamental data scarcity bottleneck in neural theorem proving by demonstrating that a simple, general symbolic mutation pipeline can expand Mathlib by an order of magnitude (110k → 6M theorems) with measurable downstream gains. However, several limitations remain open: the method uses only two basic tactics, is computationally expensive (weeks of CPU time), operates at the theorem level rather than the term level, and is restricted to single-round synthesis due to repository size. The finding that performance saturates quickly with more data points of a single tactic suggests that tactic diversity, not raw volume, is the key scaling axis — a direction the authors identify but do not fully explore. Integration with autoformalization pipelines and RAG-based retrieval are noted as promising but unexplored combinations.
