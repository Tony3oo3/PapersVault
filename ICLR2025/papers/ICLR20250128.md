## Problem

Discovering group structures from data is fundamentally blocked by the non-differentiable nature of group axioms (closure, associativity, identity, inverse), which prevents their direct integration into gradient-based learning. Existing symmetry-discovery methods require external oracle information about orbits or known group structures, leaving the general problem of automatically recovering a group operation and its representations from partial observations unsolved.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250128/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a partially observed Cayley table (a subset of entries from an n×n binary operation over a finite set of n abstract symbols) as input, with the group size n known in advance. It produces the fully completed Cayley table as a 0/1 tensor and, as a byproduct, learns unitary matrix representations (one n×n matrix per group element) that form a regular representation of the discovered group. The problem is cast as tensor completion over an order-3 tensor D ∈ {0,1}^{n×n×n}, where D_abc = 1 iff a∘b = c.

## Method

The core architecture, HyperCube, factorizes the model tensor as T_abc = (1/n)Tr[A_a B_b C_c] using three order-3 tensor factors (cubes) rather than the lower-order matrix factors of standard Tucker or CP decompositions. Each factor slice A_a, B_b, C_c is an n×n matrix embedding of a group element, and the interaction between elements is modeled by matrix multiplication A_a B_b, which inherently encodes associativity through the associativity of matrix multiplication. A novel HyperCube regularizer penalizes the Frobenius norm of the Jacobian of T with respect to the parameters, which provably promotes unitarity of the learned matrix embeddings (C-unitarity and S-unitarity) rather than the low-rank solutions favored by standard L2 regularization. By the Unitarity Theorem, every finite-dimensional representation of a finite group is equivalent to a unitary one, so this unitarity bias restricts learning to the relevant subspace without loss of generality. The interplay between the architecture's associativity inductive bias and the regularizer's unitarity bias implicitly enforces valid group structure, enabling the model to recover both the group operation and its regular representation from as little as 5–18% of the Cayley table.

## Results

On group operations (e.g., modular addition, S₅ multiplication), HyperCube achieves 100% test accuracy with only ~18% of the Cayley table as training data, while HyperCube-SE (shared-embedding variant) reaches 100% with just ~5%. In terms of learning speed, HyperCube converges to perfect test accuracy 100× faster than the Transformer baseline on the S₅ task, and HyperCube-SE achieves a further 10× speedup (1000× total). On the small-scale S₃ experiment with 60% training data, the HyperCube regularizer (𝓗) drives the model to 100% test accuracy and exact recovery of the data tensor, whereas L₂ regularization converges to a low-rank solution that fails to reduce test loss to zero.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Transformer baseline from Power et al. (2022), HyperCube achieves comparable or slightly better test accuracy across most BOC tasks, but with a dramatic 100× reduction in training steps to reach 100% test accuracy (HyperCube) and 1000× (HyperCube-SE). The Transformer exhibits a bias toward commutative operations (e.g., a+b, a²+ab+b²) due to its shared vector embeddings, while HyperCube handles non-commutative groups (e.g., S₅ multiplication, a−b) equally well. Against Tucker and CP tensor decompositions (Appendix D), HyperCube generalizes to non-Abelian groups like S₃ and S₅, whereas CP decomposition (equivalent to HyperCube with diagonal embeddings) can only capture Abelian groups with 1×1 irreps, and Tucker decomposition severely overfits training data and fails to generalize. The paper also shows that L₂ regularization, while achieving perfect test accuracy on S₃, produces a low-rank solution that does not exactly recover the data tensor, unlike the 𝓗 regularizer which enforces full-rank unitary solutions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Binary Operation Completion (BOC) problem from Power et al. (2022) and reframes it through the lens of tensor completion, drawing on the implicit-regularization literature for matrix factorization (Srebro et al., Gunasekar et al., Arora et al.). Its key architectural departure from standard tensor factorizations (Tucker, CP) is the use of order-3 'cube' factors whose interaction is matrix multiplication, directly encoding associativity as an inductive bias. The HyperCube regularizer is positioned as a dual to L2 regularization, and the paper explicitly contrasts its approach with symmetry-discovery methods (Anselmi et al., Forestano et al., Yang et al., Zhou et al.) that require external orbit or group-structure information, whereas HyperCube derives a universal inductive bias toward group algebra from the architecture and regularizer alone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first differentiable framework that recovers both the group operation table and its unitary representations from partial observations, with a ×1000 speedup over the Transformer baseline and an implicit complexity metric that quantitatively explains generalization. The revealed connection between the learned factors and group convolution / Fourier transform on groups suggests a path toward automatically discovering equivariant network architectures, though this application remains speculative. Key limitations include the requirement to know the group size n a priori, restriction to finite groups, and O(n³) parameter scaling (mitigated but not eliminated by the band-diagonal variant). Open questions include proving Conjecture 6.1 (uniqueness of the unitary representation as the regularizer minimizer), deriving rigorous generalization bounds, and extending the framework to Lie groups and multi-symbol operations.
