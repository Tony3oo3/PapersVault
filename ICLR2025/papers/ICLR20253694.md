## Problem

Existing LM decoding procedures (best-of-k, routing, self-critique) apply a uniform computation budget to every query, yet individual problems vary enormously in difficulty. Prior theoretical work (Snell et al., 2024) showed that adaptive allocation could yield significant gains, but relied on a posteriori difficulty estimates that required more computation than the decoding itself. This paper asks whether difficulty can be predicted a priori from the input alone, enabling efficient pre-response compute allocation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text query x_i and a global computation budget B (average samples per query or fraction of queries routed to a strong decoder). A lightweight probe consumes the last hidden state of the base LM after encoding the query (no generation needed) and outputs a vector of predicted marginal rewards Δ̂(x_i) = [Δ̂_1, ..., Δ̂_Bmax], one value per budget increment. The allocation algorithm then maps these predictions to a concrete per-query budget b_i (e.g., number of samples for best-of-k, or a binary route to weak/strong decoder), which is fed to the decoding procedure to produce the final response y_i.

## Method

The approach has two components. First, a marginal reward predictor Δ̂(x;θ) is trained via supervised learning (MSE loss, or cross-entropy for binary-reward domains) to estimate the expected quality gain from each incremental unit of computation; it is parameterized either as a two-layer MLP on the base LM's hidden states or as a LoRA adapter, both of which add negligible inference overhead since the hidden states are already computed during encoding. Second, an allocation algorithm solves the resulting integer linear program (Eq. 5) in O(B_max · n) time via a greedy procedure that sequentially assigns budget units to the query with the highest predicted marginal reward, subject to a total budget constraint. Two deployment modes are provided: an online variant that solves the joint optimization over a known batch, and an offline variant that pre-computes a fixed binning policy mapping predicted difficulty to budget, enabling per-query processing without batch access. For binary-reward domains (code, math), the method exploits the closed-form relationship between single-sample success probability λ and marginal rewards, reducing the prediction target to a single scalar.

## Results

Across Code, Math, and Chat domains, adaptive best-of-k achieves the same success rate as uniform best-of-k while using 25–50% less compute (Math, B≥8) or up to 50% less (Code). In the Chat domain, the full variant yields 0–10% compute savings, while the tranches variant (extreme user distribution) achieves 25–40% savings. For routing, the method matches the strong decoder's performance while invoking it only 50–75% of the time (model-size routing) or reduces expensive decoding calls by 25–40% (value-augmented sampling). On GSM8K, adaptive routing improves absolute success rates by up to 5% at the same compute budget.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is uniform best-of-k (same number of samples for every query). On Math, both online and offline adaptive variants consistently outperform best-of-k across all budgets, achieving equivalent success rates with 25–50% fewer samples at B≥8. On Code, the offline variant always beats best-of-k, but the online variant underperforms in the high-budget regime because small prediction errors (e.g., predicting 1% success for a 0% problem) cause the allocator to over-allocate to impossible queries; 50% of Code problems have zero success probability. On Chat (full), gains are modest (0–10% compute savings), but on the tranches subset (extreme variance queries), adaptive allocation saves 25–40% compute. For routing, the method outperforms random routing and, strikingly, for some budgets actually exceeds the strong decoder alone because the weak decoder occasionally outperforms the strong one on easy queries. The oracle upper bound indicates remaining headroom: the gap between learned and oracle performance suggests further improvements in marginal reward prediction are possible.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalizes the theoretical framework of Snell et al. (2024), who showed that adaptive test-time compute allocation can outperform uniform allocation but relied on a-posteriori difficulty estimates requiring more computation than the decoding itself. The key methodological departure is learning lightweight probes (MLP or LoRA) on frozen LM hidden states to predict marginal rewards, making allocation feasible at inference time without expensive sampling. The approach also connects to adaptive computation time in recurrent networks (Graves, 2016; Dehghani et al., 2019) but is architecture-agnostic, targeting the choice of decoding procedure rather than internal network depth.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: up to 50% compute reduction at matched quality (Math, Code) or 25–40% reduction in extreme-distribution chat settings, with a technique (frozen-representation probes) that adds negligible inference overhead. The finding that pre-trained LMs already encode usable difficulty signals in their hidden states, and that probes generalize across datasets (Numina→MATH) and decoding methods (BoK→routing), suggests this is a broadly applicable principle rather than a domain-specific trick. Open questions include closing the gap to oracle performance, handling distributional shift in deployment, and whether the >3K-query training requirement can be reduced; the paper also notes that verifiers are assumed available, limiting applicability to open-ended generation without reward models.
