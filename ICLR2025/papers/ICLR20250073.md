## Problem

Existing graph backdoor defenses exploit attack-specific properties (e.g., low feature similarity for Prune, outlier status for OD) and fail against in-distribution triggers such as those generated by DPGBA. No prior work provides a unified defense that effectively identifies and neutralizes backdoor triggers regardless of their distributional properties or structural form (single edge vs. multi-edge subgraph triggers).

## Input / Output

The method takes a backdoored attributed graph (adjacency matrix and node feature matrix) in the inductive setting, where the defender has no knowledge of which nodes are poisoned or what the target class is. It first trains a backdoored GNN on the poisoned graph, then produces two outputs: (1) a candidate poisoned node set identified via prediction variance under random edge dropping, and (2) a backdoor-robust GNN classifier that outputs node class labels on unseen graphs. The graph convolution used for identification deliberately excludes self-connections (unlike standard GCN), computing node representations solely from neighbors to ensure theoretical stability for clean nodes.

## Method

RIGBD operates in two stages. First, it identifies poisoned nodes by performing K iterations of random edge dropping (each edge removed with probability β) on the adjacency matrix and measuring prediction variance via KL divergence between the original and perturbed logits; a specially designed neighbor-only graph convolution (Eq. 3, without self-loops) is used so that Theorem 1 guarantees clean-node embeddings are expectation-invariant under edge dropping, while Theorem 2 bounds the deviation probability via Hoeffding's inequality. The target class is inferred as the label of the highest-variance node, and a threshold τ (Eq. 5) selects candidate poisoned nodes. Second, it trains a robust GNN classifier using a novel loss (Eq. 6) that minimizes the model's prediction confidence on the target class for identified poisoned nodes while applying standard cross-entropy to all other nodes, thereby counteracting trigger influence even when some poisoned nodes remain undetected. The approach scales linearly in K (typically 5–20) versus the exponential d^L cost of per-edge dropping, and achieves near-zero ASR across GTA, UGBA, and DPGBA attacks while maintaining clean accuracy comparable to a model trained on clean data.

## Results

RIGBD achieves an attack success rate (ASR) below 0.5% across all six datasets (Cora, Citeseer, PubMed, Physics, Flickr, OGB-arxiv) and all three attack methods (GTA, UGBA, DPGBA), while maintaining clean accuracy (ACC) comparable to or slightly above the vanilla GCN baseline (e.g., 85.19% vs. 83.34% on Cora under DPGBA). Against the hardest attack DPGBA, which generates in-distribution triggers, RIGBD reduces ASR from 95–100% (vanilla GCN) to 0.00–0.33%, whereas the previous best defense OD still suffers 90–98% ASR. On OGB-arxiv (169K nodes), RIGBD achieves 0.00–0.01% ASR with 65.2–66.5% ACC, outperforming all baselines by a wide margin.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RIGBD decisively outperforms all seven baselines on ASR across every dataset and attack. Against DPGBA (the most challenging attack with in-distribution triggers), RIGBD achieves 0.00–0.33% ASR while OD (the previous best defense) still suffers 90–98% ASR and Prune achieves 88–95% ASR, confirming that trigger-similarity-based defenses are ineffective against distribution-preserving triggers. Against GTA and UGBA, both OD and RIGBD achieve near-zero ASR, but RIGBD additionally maintains higher clean accuracy (e.g., 84.81% vs. 83.65% on Cora under UGBA). Robust GNN baselines (GNNGuard, RobustGCN) and randomized smoothing (RS) provide inconsistent or moderate defense (ASR 20–99%), while ABL (an image-domain backdoor defense) shows high variance across attacks (11–87% ASR). RIGBD's clean accuracy is consistently at or above the vanilla GCN level (e.g., 85.19% vs. 83.34% on Cora/DPGBA), indicating that the random edge dropping identification and robust training loss do not degrade legitimate classification performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior graph backdoor defenses (Prune, OD) that exploit specific trigger properties—low feature similarity or outlier status—to remove triggers, by instead identifying poisoned nodes through a property-agnostic signal: prediction variance under random edge dropping. It repurposes the randomized smoothing concept from adversarial robustness (Wang et al., 2021) not for certified robustness but as a detection mechanism, and introduces a novel robust training loss that minimizes prediction confidence on the target class for identified poisoned nodes. The theoretical analysis (Theorems 1–3) builds on Hoeffding's inequality and graph convolutional network theory to guarantee that clean nodes exhibit stable embeddings under random edge dropping while poisoned nodes do not, providing a principled foundation absent in prior empirical defenses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RIGBD is the first graph backdoor defense that uniformly handles outlier triggers (GTA), unnoticeable triggers (UGBA), and in-distribution triggers (DPGBA), closing a gap where property-based defenses (Prune, OD) completely fail against in-distribution triggers with ASR remaining above 90%. The finding that even a model trained on a clean graph remains vulnerable to triggers mimicking target-class neighbor distributions (Table 1) underscores that trigger removal alone is insufficient, motivating the robust training component. Open questions include scalability to graphs with millions of nodes, defense against adaptive attacks aware of the random edge dropping mechanism, and extension to clean-label backdoor settings where the target class is not directly observable.
