## Problem

Despite the empirical success of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting in large language models, the theoretical understanding of how a Transformer can be trained via gradient-based methods to acquire CoT generalization ability remains largely unexplored. Existing works either construct Transformers with CoT expressive power or analyze in-context learning (ICL) training dynamics, but none provide a theoretical analysis of the nonconvex optimization landscape for nonlinear (softmax) attention models to achieve multi-step reasoning generalization on unseen tasks with distribution shift.

## Input / Output

The model takes a prompt composed of l context examples (each containing K intermediate reasoning steps as input-output pairs) followed by a query input vector, and sequentially generates K reasoning step outputs via greedy decoding, where each step's output feeds into the next step's prompt. Context examples can be noisy (additive perturbations on orthonormal pattern vectors with ‖δ‖ ≤ √2/2) and partially inaccurate (some reasoning steps follow incorrect transitions governed by step-wise transition matrices), making the setting more realistic than clean examples. For the ICL comparison, the prompt contains only input-final-label pairs without intermediate steps, and the model outputs a single final prediction rather than a sequence.

## Method

The paper provides the first theoretical analysis of SGD training dynamics for a one-layer single-head softmax-attention Transformer to acquire CoT ability, revealing a two-stage mechanism: attention first concentrates on same-step tokens (eliminating cross-step interference), then further concentrates on tokens sharing the same orthonormal pattern as the query at each reasoning step. The key generalization result (Theorem 2) shows that with l_ts ≥ Ω((α'τ^f ρ^f)^{-2} log M) context examples, the trained model achieves zero CoT error on new tasks with distribution-shifted patterns, even when context examples contain noise and reasoning errors, where α' is the fraction of pattern-matching examples and τ^f, ρ^f quantify reasoning accuracy. The analysis further proves (Theorem 3) that CoT outperforms ICL because ICL requires an additional condition (Condition 1: dominant correct input-label pairs in the prompt) that CoT does not, providing a formal explanation for the empirical superiority of CoT in settings where intermediate reasoning errors accumulate.

## Results

The paper provides the first theoretical analysis of training dynamics for nonlinear Transformers to acquire CoT ability. Theorem 1 shows that a one-layer single-head Transformer achieves O(ε) training loss with l_tr ≥ Ω(α⁻¹) context examples per sample and T ≥ Ω(η⁻¹α⁻²K³ log(K/ε) + η⁻¹MK(α⁻¹ + ε⁻¹)) iterations. Theorem 2 guarantees zero CoT generalization error on unseen tasks with l_ts^f ≥ Ω((α'τ^fρ^f)⁻² log M) noisy testing examples. In GPT-4 experiments on arithmetic tasks, CoT maintains 100% accuracy with up to 2 erroneous context examples, while ICL drops to 60% under the same conditions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between CoT and ICL (one-step reasoning without intermediate steps) using the same trained one-layer Transformer. Theorem 3 formally establishes that ICL requires an additional Condition 1 (the correct input-label pair must be the most probable output in the K-step transition matrix B^f), while CoT does not. When Condition 1 fails, ICL error is Ω(1) regardless of the number of context examples, whereas CoT still achieves zero error. In the GPT-4 arithmetic experiment (Appendix A), with 2 out of 3 context examples containing implementation errors, CoT retains 100% accuracy while ICL drops to 60%, directly validating the theoretical prediction. The synthetic experiments (Figures 3–5) further confirm that both CoT and ICL require more context examples as α', τ^f, or ρ^f decrease, but CoT is strictly more robust when the input-label mapping is ambiguous.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate theoretical lines: (1) expressive-power results showing that constructed Transformers *can* perform CoT (Li et al. 2023c, Feng et al. 2023, Li et al. 2024d, Yang et al. 2024, Wen et al. 2024), and (2) training-dynamics analyses for in-context learning on one-layer Transformers (Zhang et al. 2023a, Huang et al. 2023, Wu et al. 2023, Li et al. 2024a). It departs from both by providing the first analysis of SGD training dynamics for a *nonlinear* (softmax-attention) Transformer on *multi-step* reasoning, and by extending the ICL generalization framework to handle noisy and partially erroneous context examples via step-wise transition matrices. The positional-encoding design follows Huang et al. (2024) and Nichani et al. (2024), while the data formulation with orthogonal patterns is consistent with Tian et al. (2023a) and Chen et al. (2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical account of *why* gradient-based training endows a Transformer with CoT generalization, providing quantitative sample-complexity and iteration bounds that were previously unavailable. The formal identification of Condition 1 as necessary for ICL but not for CoT gives a principled explanation for the empirical observation that CoT is more robust to erroneous context examples. Key limitations include the restriction to one-layer single-head attention, orthogonal pattern data, and simplified positional encoding; extending the analysis to multi-layer/multi-head architectures and more realistic data distributions remains open. The practical impact is indirect but foundational: the two-stage attention mechanism (step-selective then pattern-selective) and the role of context-example similarity (α′) offer testable predictions for understanding and improving CoT in large models.
