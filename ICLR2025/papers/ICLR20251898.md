## Problem

Audio foundation models like Moshi and GPT-4o can engage in real-time duplex conversation, yet no prior work has systematically evaluated whether they understand, predict, or perform turn-taking events (speaking up, backchanneling, interrupting) at appropriate times. Existing corpus-level statistics capture only global event distributions, failing to assess the precise timing of individual turn-taking decisions, leaving the community without a fine-grained understanding of these systems' conversational strengths and limitations.

## Input / Output

The judge model consumes single-channel mixed speech (both speakers' audio combined, not separate channels) in 40ms chunks with a 30-second causal context window, outputting a 5-class turn-taking label (silence, backchannel, interruption, turn change, continuation) for the next chunk. For the audio FM benchmark, input clips are up to 10 seconds (understanding task) or 30 seconds (prediction task), queried with binary yes/no prompts. The evaluation protocol produces per-capability consistency scores with 95% confidence intervals across five conversation abilities, plus confusion matrices for single-label grading.

## Method

The core contribution is a causal turn-taking prediction model that passes a 30-second context window of single-channel mixed speech through the Whisper medium encoder, applies a weighted sum of hidden states, and uses a linear layer with softmax to predict the next 40ms chunk's turn-taking event among five classes. Trained supervised on Switchboard human-human conversations (2000:300:138 split), this model serves as a judge whose predictions generate pseudo ground-truth labels against which AI dialogue systems' actual turn-taking decisions are compared. Five metrics are designed for core conversation capabilities (when to speak up, backchannel, interrupt, convey floor-holding, handle interruptions), each computing agreement between the AI's decisions and the judge's labels at contextually relevant moments (e.g., when the user pauses, when the system pauses). The key architectural choice is operating on single-channel mixed speech rather than separate speaker channels, enabling simultaneous modeling of both speakers' behavior including overlapping speech. The protocol is validated by demonstrating >60% agreement between judge labels and human decisions on in-domain and out-of-domain corpora, then applied to benchmark Moshi, a cascaded system, GPT-4o, and open-source FMs (SALMONN, Qwen-Audio-Chat, Qwen2-Audio-Instruct, Whisper+GPT-4o) on understanding and predicting turn-taking events.

## Results

The supervised judge turn-taking model achieves 92.0% ROC-AUC on Switchboard (in-domain) and generalizes well to OOD datasets (91.5% Columbia Games, 91.0% Fisher). For understanding turn-taking events, Whisper+GPT-4o achieves the best turn change accuracy (66.3%), while all models perform near random (≈50%) on backchannel understanding. For predicting future turn-taking events, the supervised topline reaches 78.6% on turn change, Whisper+GPT-4o reaches 62.2%, and all open-source FMs (SALMONN, Qwen2-Audio-Instruct, Qwen-Audio-Chat) perform at or below the 50% random baseline on backchannel, interruption, and floor-taking prediction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates four audio FMs (SALMONN, Qwen2-Audio-Instruct, Qwen-Audio-Chat, Whisper+GPT-4o) against a supervised topline and random baseline on turn-taking understanding and prediction. Whisper+GPT-4o is the strongest FM overall, achieving 62.2% on turn change prediction (vs. 49.3–49.9% for open-source models) and 66.3% on turn change understanding, but it still trails the supervised topline by 16.4 points on prediction. Qwen-Audio-Chat is the best open-source model on understanding (56.7% turn change, 69.5% interruption) yet remains near random on all prediction tasks. All open-source FMs perform at or below the 50% random baseline on backchannel and floor-taking interruption prediction, indicating they lack meaningful turn-taking awareness. The supervised topline dominates every prediction metric (74.9–78.6%), confirming that current audio FMs have substantial room for improvement in understanding and predicting conversational turn dynamics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the turn-taking prediction literature (Gravano & Hirschberg, Hara et al., Ekstedt & Skantze's VAP models, Wang et al. 2024) by repurposing a supervised turn-taking predictor as a 'judge' to evaluate audio FMs on their ability to *perform* turn-taking events in live human-AI conversations, rather than merely predicting upcoming events in human-human data. It departs from prior corpus-level statistics (Nguyen et al., 2023) that only capture global distributions of gaps, pauses, and overlaps, by introducing timing-centric metrics that assess whether individual turn-taking decisions occur at contextually appropriate moments. The evaluation protocol also builds on the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm (Zheng et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024) but adapts it to the audio domain using a Whisper-based causal model trained on Switchboard.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic evaluation of audio FMs on conversational turn-taking, revealing specific and actionable failure modes (aggressive mistimed interruptions, near-absent backchanneling, failure to speak up when the user yields) that are invisible to standard corpus-level statistics. The open-sourced evaluation platform via ESPnet provides a reusable benchmark for the growing ecosystem of real-time speech models. Key limitations include a small user study (11 participants, mostly authors), judge-model agreement below 80% for some metrics (especially handling interruptions), and backchannel detection relying on lexical heuristics. Open questions include whether the findings generalize to larger, more diverse participant pools and whether the judge-model approach can be replaced by direct human annotation at scale.
