## Problem

Existing group-robust training methods either use group labels for model selection or directly train on them, both of which underutilize the expensive group annotations or restrict the most critical training to a small labeled subset. The paper asks how to better leverage a limited set of group-labeled data to improve worst-group performance on the much larger pool of group-unlabeled training data, without requiring full-network retraining or expensive bilevel optimization.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard classification inputs (RGB images at 224×224 for Waterbirds/CelebA, tokenized text sequences up to 300 tokens for MultiNLI/CivilComments) and produces class labels. Unusually, the group-labeled data is never used to train model parameters directly; instead, a small target set (half the validation split) guides iterative reweighting of a held-out 10% subset of the group-unlabeled training set, while the remaining 90% is used only for ERM representation learning. The final output is a frozen feature extractor plus a retrained last-layer linear classifier trained on the reweighted held-out samples.

## Method

GSR is a two-stage bilevel optimization that reweights individual training samples to minimize worst-group loss. Stage 1 performs standard ERM representation learning on 90% of the group-unlabeled training data. Stage 2 freezes the feature extractor and iteratively retrains only the last linear layer (LLR) on the held-out 10% with learned sample weights, using L-BFGS for the inner loop. The key technical insight is that LLR with cross-entropy loss and ℓ₂-regularization yields a strongly convex inner objective, enabling exact gradient computation for the sample weights via implicit differentiation (equivalent to the influence function) rather than the imprecise one-step truncated backpropagation used by MAPLE. The outer loop performs projected gradient descent on the weights, with an adaptive aggregation of per-group influence scores (weighted multiplicatively by group error rates) to optimize the minimax worst-group objective efficiently.

## Results

GSR achieves an average worst-group accuracy of 82.5% across four benchmarks (Waterbirds, CelebA, MultiNLI, CivilComments), outperforming DFR (81.5%), the state-of-the-art method using the same amount of group labels, by 1.0% on average. GSR attains SoTA worst-group accuracy on MultiNLI (78.5%) and CivilComments (71.7%), and matches DFR on Waterbirds (92.9%). Notably, GSR also outperforms Group DRO (82.0% average), which requires group labels for both training and validation, demonstrating that fine-grained sample reweighting with fewer labels can match or exceed methods with more annotation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DFR (the closest competitor using the same group-label budget), GSR wins on MultiNLI (78.5% vs 74.7%, +3.8%) and CivilComments (71.7% vs 70.1%, +1.6%), ties on Waterbirds (92.9% vs 92.9%), and trails slightly on CelebA (87.0% vs 88.3%, −1.3%). Against MAPLE, the most methodologically similar baseline (bilevel sample reweighting with one-step truncated backpropagation), GSR achieves substantially larger gains on CivilComments (71.7% vs 64.1%, +7.6%) and MultiNLI (78.5% vs 72.7%, +5.8%), confirming the importance of the Hessian-based influence function over last-step gradient approximation. GSR also surpasses Group DRO (which uses group labels for both training and validation) on Waterbirds (92.9% vs 91.4%) and CivilComments (71.7% vs 70.0%), while losing on CelebA (87.0% vs 88.9%). The Hessian-free ablation GSR-HF is consistently 2–4% worse than GSR across all datasets, validating that the inverse Hessian term in the influence function is critical for accurate weight updates. On the additional MetaShift dataset, GSR achieves 78.2% worst-group accuracy versus 71.4% for DFR and 69.2% for ERM.

## Relation to Prior Work

GSR sits at the intersection of three research threads: bilevel optimization for sample reweighting (Ren et al., 2018; MAPLE/Zhou et al., 2022), influence functions for data valuation (Koh & Liang, 2017), and last-layer retraining for group robustness (DFR/Kirichenko et al., 2022). The key conceptual departure is showing that implicit differentiation in the bilevel formulation exactly recovers the influence function, and that last-layer retraining makes the required Hessian computation tractable—something MAPLE's one-step truncated backpropagation avoids but at the cost of accuracy. Rather than using group labels to directly train or select models (as in Group DRO, RWG, JTT), GSR repurposes them as a target set to iteratively reweight group-unlabeled training data, positioning itself as a label-efficient middle ground between full group-annotated training and no group labels at all.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a lightweight two-stage procedure (ERM pretraining + iterative last-layer retraining with influence-guided reweighting) can match or exceed methods that require more group annotations, while also being robust to up to 40% class-label noise in the training data. The main limitation is the persistent trade-off between worst-group and mean accuracy, and the reliance on ERM learning sufficiently good representations for all groups. An open question the authors highlight is whether sample reweighting can be extended to improve the representation learning itself (retraining more than the last layer), which would require solving the computationally prohibitive full-network Hessian problem—currently infeasible for models like ResNet-50 where even one iteration of exact Hessian computation exceeds 18 hours and 2 PB of memory.
