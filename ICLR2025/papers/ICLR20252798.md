## Problem

Traditional fine-tuning for knowledge editing minimizes cross-entropy against one-hot target distributions, which causes overfitting, catastrophic forgetting, and model collapse when only a handful of query-response pairs are available. Existing mitigations—gradient constraints, parameter-efficient adapters, and targeted weight edits—still anchor optimization to a one-hot target, failing to fully prevent degradation. The paper asks whether a model can instead learn toward a softer, self-generated distribution that preserves linguistic quality while still incorporating new facts.

## Input / Output

For each edit, the method consumes a text query q, a target response x*, and a context prompt c (five paraphrased statements generated by GPT-4 that restate the target fact). At every optimization step, five continuation sequences are sampled from the model conditioned on [c, q, x*] and concatenated with x* to form the training target. The output is an updated set of model weights (five consecutive feed-forward layers, following MEMIT's layer selection), with per-parameter gradient clipping to ±5e-4 to bound drift from the original model.

## Method

ICE replaces the one-hot fine-tuning objective with a combined loss L = L_FT + λ·L_ICE, where L_ICE is the KL divergence between the model's contextual distribution p_θ(x|[c,q]) and its unconditioned distribution p_θ(x|q). The key mechanism is a dynamic, iterative target: at step s the contextual distribution p_θ_s(x|[c,q]) is frozen as the target while θ is updated toward it, so both distributions co-evolve toward the desired fact rather than converging to a degenerate one-hot. The paper proves that when λ=1, this is exactly equivalent to sampling continuations from p_θ(x|[c,q,x*]) and maximizing their likelihood under p_θ(x|q), making the procedure a single forward-pass sampling plus one backward pass per step. A consistency condition p_θ(x|[c,q]) = p_θ(x|q) is enforced, meaning the model's predictions should be identical with or without the context once knowledge is internalized. The in-context loss simultaneously preserves locality (unrelated queries see minimal distributional shift) and promotes portability (related queries generalize the new fact), while the fine-tuning loss guarantees edit accuracy.

## Results

On Llama2-7b-chat, ICE achieves 100% Edit Success on WikiData recent, ZsRE, and WikiData counterfact, and 99.88% on WikiBio, matching the strongest baseline FT-M. ICE consistently outperforms all baselines on Locality (e.g., 46.39% vs. 41.54% for FT-M on WikiData recent; 45.88% vs. 40.76% on WikiData counterfact) and Portability (e.g., 61.02% vs. 59.28% on WikiData recent; 73.49% vs. 72.39% on WikiData counterfact). ICE also achieves the lowest normalized perplexity ratio (PPLr) across all four datasets (e.g., 15.50 on ZsRE vs. 27.33 for FT-M; 18.95 on WikiData counterfact vs. 54.71 for FT-M), indicating superior linguistic quality after editing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ICE matches FT-M [55] on Edit Success (both ~100%) but surpasses it on Locality by ~5 points (46.39% vs. 41.54% on WikiData recent; 45.88% vs. 40.76% on WikiData counterfact) and Portability by ~1–2 points, while achieving substantially lower PPLr (34.08 vs. 70.64 on WikiData recent; 18.95 vs. 54.71 on WikiData counterfact), indicating far less linguistic degradation. Against ROME [28] and MEMIT [29], ICE shows approximately 30% higher Portability on WikiData counterfact (73.49% vs. 42.45%/44.16%) and over 15% higher Locality (45.88% vs. 21.13%/19.48%), while also achieving near-perfect Edit Success where ROME and MEMIT fall slightly short (98.68%/98.13%). FT-L [28] is decisively outperformed by ICE on Edit Success (100% vs. 45.63% on WikiData recent; 100% vs. 36.13% on WikiData counterfact), confirming that ICE's contextual distribution target avoids the overfitting that cripples naive fine-tuning. In continual editing, ICE maintains stable accuracy and low PPLr across sequential updates, whereas ROME, MEMIT, and FT-L all deteriorate significantly, sometimes falling below the unedited baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

ICE positions itself within the weight-modified knowledge editing lineage (ROME, MEMIT, FT-M) but departs from the standard one-hot fine-tuning objective by introducing a contextual distribution as the learning target. Conceptually, it repurposes in-context learning — previously used only as a prompt-time mechanism that leaves parameters frozen [57, 6, 24] — as a training-time signal that guides parameter updates. The key theoretical contribution (Observation 1) shows that naive self-sampling without external context is provably equivalent to vanilla fine-tuning, motivating the need for the context-induced distribution. Unlike distillation-based approaches [42, 18] that require two model copies or gist-token methods [32] that add extra parameters, ICE operates within a single model and uses only gradient clipping for locality constraints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The continual editing results are the most practically significant finding: while ROME, MEMIT, and FT-L all deteriorate substantially over sequential edits (sometimes performing worse than the unedited model), ICE maintains accuracy and linguistic quality across the full dataset, suggesting a viable path toward lifelong model updating. However, the method's reliance on GPT-4 for context generation introduces a dependency and potential hallucination risk, and its effectiveness is clearly tied to the base model's in-context learning strength (gains are much smaller on GPT2-xl vs. Llama2-7b-chat). Open questions include how ICE scales to very large knowledge bases where context prompts become unwieldy, whether the dynamic target mechanism can be replaced with cheaper approximations, and whether the approach generalizes beyond factual knowledge to procedural or relational knowledge.
