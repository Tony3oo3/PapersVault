## Problem

Personalized federated learning (PFL) was believed to offer immunity against backdoor attacks because manually designed triggers struggle to survive in personalized models. This paper identifies three specific mechanisms—insufficient regularization transfer, non-shared parameter obstruction, and catastrophic forgetting—that explain why existing federated backdoor attacks fail in PFL, and asks whether a practical attack can overcome all three simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The method consumes clean RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100 and SVHN) along with the current global model parameters θ_g. It produces a sample-specific, invisible trigger δ+ξ (with ||δ||∞ ≤ ε and ||ξ||∞ ≤ σ, both set to 4/255 by default) that, when added to an image, causes the personalized model to predict an attacker-chosen target label. The trigger is generated per-sample by a small encoder-decoder generator network, making it dynamic and imperceptible to human observers.

## Method

Bad-PFL replaces manually crafted triggers with natural features of the target label, exploiting the fact that models trained on natural data inherently associate certain visual patterns with specific classes. The trigger has two components: (1) a target feature δ = ε·G_w(x) produced by a generator network G_w that is optimized to make any input appear most similar to the target class, and (2) a disruptive noise ξ = σ·sign(∇_x L(F(x;θ_g), y)) that corrupts ground-truth label features so the model relies on δ for its prediction. The generator undergoes mutual reinforcement training with the global model—whenever a compromised client is selected, it first optimizes G_w to align with the current global model's learned features, then trains the local model on trigger-added data. Because the trigger is derived from natural data features rather than artificial patterns, it persists in personalized models as long as they are trained on natural data, evading fine-tuning-based defenses and robust aggregation methods.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, Bad-PFL achieves ASR of at least 80% across all seven PFL methods, with standout results of 97.95% on FedRep and 99.10% on FedPAC, while maintaining clean accuracy within 1–2% of unattacked baselines. Against robust aggregation defenses, Bad-PFL retains 82.66% ASR (ClipAvg/FedBN) and 97.28% ASR (ClipAvg/FedRep), far exceeding all baselines. In terms of backdoor persistence, Bad-PFL sustains 97.01% ASR on FedRep even after 45 steps of fine-tuning, whereas the strongest baseline (Neurotoxin) drops to 16.24%. On SVHN, Bad-PFL reaches 99.52% ASR on FedBN, and on CIFAR-100 it achieves 93.96–99.16% ASR across PFL methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Bad-PFL decisively outperforms all six attack baselines on partial model-sharing PFL methods, which are the most challenging setting. On FedRep, Bad-PFL achieves 97.95% ASR versus Neurotoxin (28.41%), LF-Attack (12.82%), DBA (15.54%), FCBA (16.91%), PGD-Bkd (20.25%), and ModRep (23.38%)—a margin of 69–85 percentage points. On FedBN, Bad-PFL (82.22%) still leads the strongest baseline Neurotoxin (59.48%) by 22.7 points, while on FedPAC it reaches 99.10% versus the next-best Neurotoxin at 82.10%. Against robust aggregation defenses, Bad-PFL maintains 50.52–97.28% ASR under Median/ClipAvg on FedBN/FedRep, while all baselines drop below 55%. In backdoor persistence tests (fine-tuning with clean data), Bad-PFL retains 80.12–97.76% ASR after 45 steps on FedBN/FedRep, whereas Neurotoxin and LF-Attack collapse to 11.82–32.07%, confirming that natural-feature triggers are inherently resistant to standard removal techniques.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the federated backdoor attack literature (Neurotoxin, PGD-Bkd, DBA, LF-Attack) into the personalized federated learning (PFL) setting, where prior work (Qin et al., 2023) had suggested PFL offers near-immunity to such attacks. It departs from existing PFL-specific attacks (PFedBA, BadFL) by identifying three concrete mechanisms—regularization insufficiency, non-shared parameter dormancy, and catastrophic forgetting—that cause manually designed triggers to fail, and by repurposing the adversarial-perturbation idea (cf. IBA, Perdoor) to exploit natural data features as inherently persistent triggers. The generator-based, sample-specific trigger design is conceptually related to IBA and Perdoor but is specifically motivated by the need for triggers that survive personalization and fine-tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the widely cited claim that PFL is inherently robust to backdoor attacks, showing that a well-designed trigger based on natural features achieves >80% ASR across all tested PFL methods and survives robust aggregation, client-side defenses, and up to 45 steps of fine-tuning. This has direct practical implications for any deployment of PFL in security-sensitive settings, as the attack is sample-specific, invisible to human inspection, and resists standard detection (Neural Cleanse, STRIP). A key open question is how to defend against attacks that exploit the model's own learned natural features, since the backdoor is not an artifact that can be 'unlearned' without degrading the model's core competence. The paper does not propose a defense, leaving the security community to develop countermeasures.
