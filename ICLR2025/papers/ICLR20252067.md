## Problem

Non-asymptotic linear system identification theory has been well-developed for first-order (p=1) autoregressive models, but existing techniques based on excitation arguments or OLS factorization do not extend to the long-context (p>1) setting. This paper fills that gap by deriving sample complexity bounds for p-th order vector autoregressions, revealing a 'learning-without-mixing' phenomenon where the context length does not degrade the effective sample size, and further extending the analysis to low-rank and misspecified-context scenarios.

## Input / Output

The method consumes N independent trajectories of length T > p, where each state x_t ∈ R^d is generated by a p-th order linear autoregression with sub-Gaussian isotropic noise. The estimator is a constrained least-squares minimizer restricted to matrices whose associated block-Toeplitz operator M_A has operator norm at most D (a known constant), rather than the unconstrained OLS. The output is the full set of design matrices A_k ∈ R^{d×d} for k ∈ [p], with high-probability Frobenius norm error bounds of the form Õ(D²pd²/(N(T−p))) in the full-rank case, Õ(D²pdr/(N(T−p))) under rank-r structure, and Õ(D²(D′+1)²p′dr/(N(T−p))) under misspecified context p′ < p.

## Method

The core technical contribution replaces the excitation-based and OLS-factorization arguments (which fail for p>1 due to non-invertible noise covariance in the companion-matrix formulation and suboptimal p-dependencies) with a uniform concentration argument over prediction differences using empirical process theory. The proof constructs martingale difference sequences from the noise-prediction cross terms, applies Freedman's inequality to control their concentration relative to the quadratic predictable variation, and uses Hanson-Wright inequalities plus covering-number bounds over the operator-norm-constrained parameter set to obtain uniform lower bounds. The key structural insight is that the rate depends on the system only through the operator norm bound D and the logarithm of the condition number κ of the data-generating operator L_*, neither of which scales with the mixing time, yielding the 'learning-without-mixing' property. For the low-rank extension, the constraint set is restricted to rank-r matrices (with nuclear norm regularization discussed as a computable relaxation), reducing the effective dimensionality from d² to rd per matrix. For misspecification, an additional boundedness assumption on the tail contribution ‖(M_{A*} − M_{A*_{1:p′}})L_*‖_op ≤ D′ ensures that the first p′ matrices are recovered at a rate depending only on p′, not the true p.

## Results

The paper establishes that the constrained least-squares estimator achieves ‖Â − A*‖²_F ≤ Õ(D²pd²/(N(T−p))), matching the i.i.d. parametric rate O(pd²/NT) up to logarithmic factors and without any dependence on the mixing time τ_mix of the autoregressive process. Under a low-rank assumption (rank(A_k*) ≤ r), the rate improves to Õ(D²prd/(N(T−p))), replacing the d² dependence with rd. For misspecified context lengths (p' < p), the first p' matrices are recovered at rate Õ(D²(D'+1)²p'dr/(N(T−p))), depending only on the misspecified length p' rather than the true p.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against the naive 'learning-with-mixing' strategy, which subsamples every τ_mix steps and incurs a rate of Õ(τ_mix · pd²/NT) where τ_mix = max(1/(1−ρ), p). The proposed constrained least-squares estimator avoids this mixing-time penalty entirely, achieving the i.i.d.-matching rate Õ(D²pd²/(N(T−p))) — a 'learning-without-mixing' phenomenon that extends Simchowitz et al. (2018) from p=1 to general p. Against brute-force adaptation of p=1 techniques via companion matrices, the paper shows those approaches fail because the block noise covariance is non-invertible (p−1 zero blocks), and excitation-based arguments incur a suboptimal additional p-dependence. Compared to Sarkar & Rakhlin (2019) and Simchowitz et al. (2018), which provide sharp rates only for first-order systems, this paper fills the gap for p > 1 using a uniform concentration argument over prediction differences rather than OLS factorization. For the low-rank setting, the rate improves from pd² to prd, matching the improvement seen in Basu et al. (2019) and Alquier et al. (2020) for p=1, but now valid for arbitrary context length.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends non-asymptotic linear system identification from first-order (p=1) vector autoregressive models to the long-context (p>1) setting, where prior techniques (Simchowitz et al., 2018; Sarkar & Rakhlin, 2019) provably fail due to the non-invertible noise covariance of the companion matrix and suboptimal p-dependencies in excitation arguments. Rather than adapting p=1 methods, the authors develop a new proof strategy based on empirical process theory—specifically Freedman's inequality and Hanson-Wright concentration—applied to a uniform lower bound on prediction differences over an operator-norm-constrained search space. The low-rank and misspecification extensions build on nuclear norm regularization ideas from Basu et al. (2019) and Alquier et al. (2020), but the long-context setting introduces shared-kernel structure and a novel statistical advantage from under-specifying context length.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The results fill a notable gap in the theory of non-asymptotic system identification for multi-step dependencies, providing the first sharp rates for long-context linear autoregressive models that match i.i.d. parametric rates up to logarithmic factors. The 'learning-without-mixing' phenomenon extending to p>1 is practically relevant for understanding when sequential data in autoregressive architectures (e.g., transformers) does not degrade statistical efficiency. Open questions include whether unconstrained OLS can be analyzed in the NT = Ω(pdr) regime without the diameter constraint, whether efficient algorithms exist that exploit the low-rank structure, and whether misspecification benefits extend to marginally stable systems. The misspecification result—that shorter contexts yield lower sample complexity—is a counterintuitive finding with potential implications for practical model design.
