## Problem

Plug-and-play diffusion prior (PnPDP) methods have shown strong results on natural image restoration tasks like inpainting and deblurring, but their performance on scientific inverse problems—where forward models are highly nonlinear, PDE-based, or involve non-additive noise—remains largely unexplored. It is unclear how much insight from image restoration benchmarks carries over to domains like optical tomography, black hole imaging, seismology, and fluid dynamics, which present fundamentally different structural challenges.

## Input / Output

Each of the five benchmark problems provides noisy measurements from a domain-specific forward model (e.g., VLBI closure quantities for black hole imaging, subsampled multi-coil k-space for CS-MRI, scattered light fields for inverse scattering, seismic waveforms for FWI, or partial vorticity observations for Navier-Stokes) along with a pre-trained diffusion prior trained on 10,000–50,000 domain-specific images. The forward models range from linear operators with available SVD to nonlinear PDE solvers requiring CFL stability conditions, and noise profiles span Gaussian, non-additive, and noise-free regimes. The output is a reconstructed source image (permittivity map, MRI slice, black hole image, velocity map, or initial vorticity field) at resolutions from 64×64 to 320×320.

## Method

INVERSEBENCH is a modular benchmarking framework that evaluates 14 PnPDP algorithms (spanning guidance-based, variable-splitting, variational Bayes, and sequential Monte Carlo categories) against domain-specific baselines across five scientific inverse problems. For each problem, a single diffusion prior is trained using the EDM pipeline and shared across all PnPDP methods to ensure fair comparison. The framework defines a unified evaluation protocol combining accuracy metrics (PSNR, SSIM, data misfit, χ² for black hole imaging, relative ℓ₂ error for PDE problems) with efficiency metrics (total and sequential forward/diffusion model evaluations, runtime). Key empirical findings include: PnPDP methods outperform baselines given suitable priors but degrade sharply on PDE-based forward models due to CFL condition violations from Langevin noise; methods with more forward model queries (DAPS, PnP-DM) are more accurate on closed-form problems but numerically unstable on PDE solvers; and PnPDP methods cannot reliably recover sources outside the prior distribution.

## Results

Across five scientific inverse problems, PnPDP methods generally outperform conventional baselines when a suitable diffusion prior is available: in linear inverse scattering, DDNM achieves 36.4 dB PSNR vs. FISTA-TV's 32.1 dB (360 receivers); in CS-MRI at ×4, PnP-DM reaches 31.8 dB PSNR vs. Wavelet+ℓ₁'s 29.5 dB; in black hole imaging at 3% observation, PnPDM scores 24.3 dB PSNR vs. EHT-imaging's 21.7 dB. However, for PDE-based forward models (FWI, Navier-Stokes), methods with inner Langevin loops (DAPS, PnP-DM) suffer numerical instability and underperform simpler approaches; in Navier-Stokes, EnKG achieves the best relative ℓ₂ error of 0.120 (×2, noise-free) vs. EKI's 0.577, while in FWI, well-initialized LBFGS (0.070 rel. ℓ₂) still beats the best PnPDP method (DAPS, 0.201). PnPDP methods also show greater robustness to forward-model changes (0.48 dB PSNR variation) compared to end-to-end methods (9.58 dB) in CS-MRI.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the 14 PnPDP methods, performance varies significantly by problem structure. In linear inverse scattering, DDNM and RED-diff lead (36.4 and 36.6 dB PSNR at 360 receivers), clearly outperforming FISTA-TV (32.1 dB) and DDRM (32.6 dB); IIGDM and LGD are notably weaker (~28 dB). In CS-MRI, PnP-DM (31.8 dB) and DAPS (31.5 dB) beat Wavelet+ℓ₁ (29.5 dB) and TV (27.0 dB), but E2E-VarNet (33.4 dB) still edges out all PnPDP methods on PSNR. In black hole imaging, PnPDM achieves the best PSNR (24.3 dB at 3%) and best χ²_logca (1.67), outperforming EHT-imaging (21.7 dB, χ²_logca 1.70) and SMILI (18.5 dB); DPS and LGD produce high-quality images (30.8 dB Blur PSNR) but severely overfit the data (χ²_cp > 8). In FWI, PnPDP methods (best: DAPS 0.201 rel. ℓ₂) beat randomly-initialized Adam (0.333) but lose to Gaussian-blurred-initialized LBFGS (0.070) and Adam (0.089), and PnP-DM/DAPS show numerical instability. In Navier-Stokes, EnKG dominates (0.120 rel. ℓ₂ at ×2, σ=0) over EKI (0.577) and DPG (0.325), while DPS variants (fGSG/cGSG) perform worst (1.2–2.2 rel. ℓ₂) due to instability with the PDE solver.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the plug-and-play (PnP) reconstruction framework of Venkatakrishnan et al. (2013) and the score-based SDE formulation of Song et al. (2020) by systematically evaluating 14 PnPDP algorithms across five scientific inverse problems, moving well beyond the natural image restoration tasks (inpainting, super-resolution, deblurring) that dominate prior PnPDP evaluations. It departs from prior benchmarking efforts by incorporating domain-specific baselines (e.g., EHT-imaging for black hole imaging, adjoint-state FWI for seismology) and by exposing structural challenges absent from image restoration—PDE-based forward models with CFL stability constraints, non-additive noise, and complex-domain measurements. The paper also builds on the four methodological categories (guidance-based, variable-splitting, variational Bayes, sequential Monte Carlo) already established in the PnPDP literature, providing a unified comparison across them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

INVERSEBENCH fills a critical gap by providing the first systematic, extensible benchmark for PnPDP methods in scientific inverse problems, with open-sourced code, datasets, and pre-trained priors. A key open question it surfaces is how to incorporate forward-model stability constraints (e.g., CFL conditions) into PnPDP algorithms, since current methods that use Langevin Monte Carlo inner loops (DAPS, PnP-DM) fail catastrophically on PDE-based problems. The finding that PnPDP methods cannot reliably recover out-of-distribution sources highlights a fundamental tension between the strength of diffusion priors (capturing complex structure) and their limitation (biasing toward the prior support), motivating future work on adaptive or mixture priors. Inference speed remains a significant practical barrier, as nearly all PnPDP methods are less computationally efficient than conventional baselines.
