## Problem

Existing LLM alignment frameworks sit at two extremes: RLHF relies on costly human annotation, while RLAIF depends on expensive top-tier LLMs (e.g., GPT-4) for preference labeling. The middle ground of using significantly smaller, task-specific weak LLMs to generate alignment feedback remains largely unexplored, with no systematic evaluation of whether such weak supervisors can rival or exceed human feedback quality.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250725/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a small labeled preference dataset D_l (human-annotated triplets of prompt, chosen, rejected) and a large unlabeled dataset D_u (response pairs with unknown preference), along with a weak LLM (e.g., OPT-125M) and a target policy model. The weak LLM produces binary preference labels for every unlabeled triplet via its DPO implicit reward, yielding a fully labeled dataset D_weak. The final output is an aligned LLM policy π_θ* trained via DPO on D_weak, which the paper shows can match or surpass a policy trained on the same data with original human labels.

## Method

The framework is a three-stage semi-supervised alignment pipeline: (1) SFT the weak LLM on the labeled chosen responses to establish a reference model π_w^SFT; (2) DPO-train the weak LLM on the full labeled set to obtain π_w*; (3) compute the DPO implicit reward r_w(x,y) = β log(π_w(y|x)/π_w^SFT(y|x)) for each response in unlabeled triplets, assign preference labels by comparing rewards, and DPO-train the target policy on the resulting weakly-labeled data. The key insight is that the weak LLM's implicit reward—derived from a small labeled set—generalizes effectively to label large volumes of unlabeled data, connecting semi-supervised learning to alignment. Empirically, supervisor size from 125M to GPT-4 has minimal impact on alignment quality, and the weak LLM's errors are concentrated in cases where response quality differences are subtle, meaning its 'noise' is structurally different from random label corruption.

## Results

On HH-RLHF, aligning OPT-1.3B with feedback from OPT-125M (a 125M-parameter weak LLM) achieves a gold reward of 4.84, surpassing the human-feedback baseline of 4.63. The advantage generalizes across model families: on Mistral-7B, weak LLM feedback yields 7.90 vs. 7.19 for human feedback. Notably, even the subset of samples where the weak LLM's preference contradicts human annotation (39.4% of data) still improves alignment by +1.79 over the pretrained model, and 44.3% of these mismatched samples actually have higher gold reward for the weak LLM's chosen response. GPT-4 win-rate evaluation confirms the weak-LLM-aligned policy competitively matches the human-feedback-aligned policy at ~50% across five student model variants.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the DPO-with-human-feedback baseline (Rafailov et al., 2023), the weak LLM (OPT-125M) feedback consistently outperforms: 4.84 vs. 4.63 gold reward on OPT-1.3B and 7.90 vs. 7.19 on Mistral-7B, with the advantage holding across Llama-2-7B, Gemma-7B, and the Reddit TL;DR summarization task. Against the RLAIF-with-GPT-4 baseline (Lee et al., 2023), the paper demonstrates that a task-specific weak LLM (OPT-125M) can match or even exceed GPT-4 as a preference supervisor on OPT-1.3B, challenging the assumption that larger supervisor models yield better alignment feedback. The key insight is that the weak LLM's 'errors' are systematic (concentrated in near-tie cases where even GPT-4 shows 0.66 preference consistency vs. 0.84 for clear cases), not random, making them less harmful to alignment than random label noise would be.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself in the 'middle ground' between RLHF (human-annotated preferences) and RLAIF (strong LLM-annotated preferences), drawing on DPO as the shared optimization backbone for both the weak teacher and the student policy. It extends the weak-to-strong generalization framework of Burns et al. (2024) from discriminative tasks (reward modeling, binary classification) to the generative alignment setting, and introduces a semi-supervised learning perspective by combining a small labeled set with a large unlabeled set annotated by the weak LLM. The Bradley-Terry implicit reward in DPO serves as the mechanism by which the weak LLM produces preference labels, unifying the teacher and student training under a single objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: alignment pipelines no longer require either expensive human annotation or access to frontier models like GPT-4, since a 125M-parameter model trained on a small labeled subset can generate feedback of comparable or superior quality. The finding that nearly half of 'mismatched' samples (where the weak LLM disagrees with human labels) actually have higher gold reward for the weak LLM's choice raises important questions about the reliability of crowd-annotated preference data and suggests that human feedback noise is a first-order concern in alignment. Key limitations include the absence of theoretical guarantees, the reliance on a specific gold reward model for evaluation, and the question of whether these findings generalize to more complex or safety-critical alignment tasks beyond helpfulness/harmlessness.
