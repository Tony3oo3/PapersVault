## Problem

While LLMs demonstrate strong reasoning and knowledge capabilities, it remains unclear how best to leverage them for sequential decision-making: should they generate actions directly as policies, or should their knowledge be extracted indirectly through reward models for downstream RL? This paper systematically investigates this question across diverse interactive domains with varying action granularities, observation modalities, and horizon lengths.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250769/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes environment observations (HTML/text for MiniWob, Wordle, NetHack; RGB pixel frames for MetaWorld) paired with natural-language task descriptions and episode history. For the AI feedback approach, the LLM receives pairs of observations (or observation sequences for exploration) and outputs a preference label (1, 2, or None) indicating which shows more progress toward the goal. These preference tuples are aggregated into a dataset to train a Bradley-Terry reward model, which then produces dense scalar rewards for an RL agent. For direct policy modeling, the LLM receives the same context plus in-context examples and recursive self-critique, outputting action tokens projected onto the environment's atomic action space.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a comprehensive empirical comparison showing that indirect policy modeling via AI feedback (preference-based reward modeling) consistently outperforms direct LLM-as-policy across four diverse domains. The AI feedback pipeline prompts an LLM to express preferences between randomly sampled observation pairs emphasizing task progress, trains a Bradley-Terry model on these preferences, and feeds the resulting dense reward to a standard RL algorithm without any environment-provided reward. The paper demonstrates that this approach inherently solves credit assignment by distributing reward to key intermediate timesteps (e.g., picking up a key, opening a door) and can be steered toward exploration by modifying the preference prompt to favor novel observation sequences. For domains where the LLM lacks sufficient prior knowledge (e.g., MetaWorld's sweep-into task), fine-tuning the LLM on ~100 synthetic image-caption pairs generated by GPT-4o significantly improves reward modeling while preserving general-purpose knowledge on benchmarks like POPE, GQA, AI2D, and MMMU—unlike fine-tuning for direct action selection, which causes catastrophic forgetting.

## Results

AI Feedback (preference-based reward modeling via Bradley-Terry) is the only reward modeling method that successfully crafts useful rewards across all four environments and both text and pixel modalities. On average, AI Feedback achieves a score of 89.93 versus 86.3 for human-designed rewards across domains. In terms of compute efficiency, AI Feedback reaches 72% success rate at 1M environment samples using 3.1×10¹⁵ FLOPS, while direct policy modeling with SFT reaches 87% at the same sample budget but requires 1.09×10¹⁸ FLOPS—approximately two orders of magnitude more compute. Direct LLM Policy (GPT-4o with full prompting stack) only performs competitively on MiniWob-Hard and fails on NetHack and MetaWorld.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AI Feedback outperforms Direct Scalar (Kwon et al., 2023) on all domains except MiniWob-Hard, where the two are nearly equivalent; the gap widens substantially on harder, open-ended tasks like NetHack. Embedding-based methods (Rocamonde et al., 2023; Du et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024b) consistently yield the lowest performance across all four environments. Reward as Code (Xie et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2023) is highly contingent on symbolic features and expert demonstrations: on MetaWorld, it requires GPT-4o plus in-context expert demos to reach 79% success, versus 72% for AI Feedback using the smaller open-source PaliGemma without such assumptions. Direct LLM Policy (GPT-4o with chain-of-thought, in-context learning, and recursive criticism) matches AI Feedback only on MiniWob-Hard but fails on NetHack and MetaWorld, which the authors attribute to the LLM's poor zero-shot understanding of environment dynamics (Figure 2b shows near-random accuracy on next-state and next-action prediction). Finally, AI Feedback achieves an average score of 89.93 versus 86.3 for human-designed rewards, demonstrating competitive performance without manual reward engineering.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RLAIF framework (Lee et al., 2023) and the Bradley-Terry preference model to the sequential decision-making setting, building directly on MOTIF (Klissarov et al., 2024) for AI-feedback-based intrinsic motivation in NetHack. It departs from prior LLM-for-RL work (e.g., ReAct, Reflexion, RCI) that primarily treats LLMs as direct policy generators by systematically comparing this paradigm against indirect policy modeling through reward functions. The fine-tuning analysis draws on RT-2's VQ-VAE token replacement for direct policy fine-tuning, while the credit-assignment analysis connects to heuristic-guided RL (Cheng et al., 2021) and reward redistribution (Arjona-Medina et al., 2018).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clear, well-evidenced practical recipe: use LLMs as zero-shot reward modelers (via AI feedback) rather than as direct policies, since the former consistently outperforms across diverse domains and modalities while also being more sample- and compute-efficient. The finding that fine-tuning for reward modeling mitigates catastrophic forgetting far better than fine-tuning for direct action selection is important for practitioners who need to preserve LLM general capabilities. Open questions include whether online preference elicitation can be made more stable, how to handle non-Markovian reward requirements at scale, and whether the approach transfers to truly open-ended real-world embodied settings where LLM prior knowledge is most limited.
