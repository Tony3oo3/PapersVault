## Problem

Recent calibration literature frequently reports only ECE and test accuracy, which can be trivially gamed: a constant-confidence recalibration (mean replacement recalibration, MRR) achieves near-zero ECE while preserving accuracy, yet catastrophically worsens NLL and MSE. There is no theoretically motivated pairing of calibration and generalization metrics, and no single visualization that simultaneously reveals calibration quality, generalization error, and where predicted confidences concentrate.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pretrained model probability vectors in Δ^{k-1} (from ViT, ResNet-50, EfficientNet, ConvNeXt on ImageNet-1K; ResNet-32 on CIFAR-10/100) paired with ground-truth class labels, split into a 20% calibration set and 80% test set. It produces calibration-sharpness diagrams with three overlaid components: a kernel-regression calibration curve, a shaded band whose width encodes the pointwise sharpness gap ρ(p) scaled by the confidence density p̂_{h(X)}(p), and a kernel density estimate of predicted confidences. Unlike standard reliability diagrams that use uniform binning, all conditional expectations are estimated via Nadaraya-Watson kernel regression with a Gaussian kernel (bandwidth σ=0.05), and the total Bregman divergence (d_φ^TOT) and confidence calibration error (d_φ^CAL) are annotated on each plot.

## Method

The core theoretical contribution is a Bregman divergence decomposition (Lemma 4.1) that splits E[d_φ(π_{Y|X}, g(X))] into a sharpness gap E[φ(Z)] − E[φ(E[Z|X])] plus a calibration error E[d_φ(E[Z|X], X)], establishing that optimizing a Bregman divergence implicitly defines a matching calibration metric. Building on this, Lemma 4.6 proves that the 1-D confidence calibration error d_φ is a lower bound on the full multi-class calibration error d_φ whenever d_φ(x,y) ≥ d_φ(x_i,y_i) coordinate-wise (satisfied by Brier score and KL divergence), justifying the use of confidence calibration as a computable proxy. The calibration-sharpness diagram visualizes the pointwise sharpness gap ρ(p) as a band around the calibration curve, estimated efficiently via 1-D kernel regression; Theorem 4.9 proves consistency of this estimator under standard bandwidth conditions, extending prior results from L^p ECE to general Bregman-based calibration errors. Empirically, the MRR experiment on ViT/ImageNet demonstrates that trivial recalibration dominates all pure calibration metrics (ECE 0.18 vs. 2.59 for temperature scaling) while inflating NLL from 65 to 145, and the diagrams reveal that isotonic regression's calibration gains come at the cost of larger sharpness gaps compared to temperature scaling.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K-Val with a pretrained ViT, the proposed Mean Replacement Recalibration (MRR) achieves near-zero calibration error (ECE = 0.18, SmoothECE = 0.18) while preserving test accuracy at 85.14%, but at the cost of NLL jumping from 65.35 to 144.66 and MSE from 22.73 to 27.51. Among legitimate recalibration methods, Temperature Scaling (TS) is the strongest, reducing ECE from 9.42 to 2.59 while maintaining 85.14% accuracy and improving NLL to 56.83. Histogram Binning and Isotonic Regression both produce infinite NLL (due to zero-probability predictions) and reduce test accuracy to 82.03% and 83.82% respectively. The paper's calibration-sharpness diagrams reveal that TS and IR skew predicted confidences higher, increasing sharpness gaps in exchange for calibration gains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Temperature Scaling (Guo et al., 2017) is the strongest legitimate recalibration baseline: it reduces ECE from 9.42 to 2.59 and NLL from 65.35 to 56.83 while preserving 85.14% test accuracy, making it the only method that improves both calibration and generalization simultaneously. Histogram Binning (Zadrozny & Elkan, 2001) reduces ECE to 5.06 but drops accuracy to 82.03% and produces infinite NLL due to zero-probability class predictions; the calibration-sharpness diagrams further reveal that HB concentrates all confidence at a single point, incurring a large sharpness gap. Isotonic Regression (Zadrozny & Elkan, 2002) achieves ECE of 4.53 with 83.82% accuracy but also has infinite NLL; the paper's diagrams show IR has better generalization (smaller sharpness gap) than HB despite HB appearing to have better calibration in the 0.2 confidence region (where no density exists). The SmoothECE visualization (Błasiok & Nakkiran, 2023) detects MRR's triviality but makes it difficult to distinguish HB from IR, whereas the proposed calibration-sharpness diagrams clearly show IR's smaller sharpness gap, providing a more granular comparison. MRR dominates all pure calibration metrics (ECE, ACE, SmoothECE all at 0.18) but is catastrophically worse on NLL (144.66 vs. 56.83 for TS), demonstrating the paper's central claim that calibration metrics alone are insufficient for model comparison.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the proper scoring rule decomposition literature (Murphy 1973, Gneiting et al. 2007, Kull & Flach 2015, Pohle 2020) that splits divergences into calibration and sharpness/resolution components, and on the Bregman divergence framework for multi-class classification. It most directly departs from Perez-Lebel et al. (2023), which also argues that reporting calibration error alongside accuracy is insufficient, but uses a grouping-loss decomposition applied to the 1-D confidence calibration problem; this paper instead uses a sharpness-gap decomposition in the full multi-class setting and proves (Lemma 4.6) that confidence calibration error provides a valid lower bound on full calibration error for Bregman divergences. The estimation approach extends kernel regression consistency results from Blasiok & Nakkiran (2023) and Chidambaram et al. (2024) from L^p ECE to Bregman-divergence-based calibration errors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The MRR result—that a trivial constant-confidence strategy achieves near-zero ECE while preserving test accuracy, outperforming temperature scaling, histogram binning, and isotonic regression on all pure calibration metrics—exposes a systematic vulnerability in how the calibration literature reports comparative results, with implications for how recalibration methods are evaluated going forward. The calibration-sharpness diagram provides a practical, theoretically grounded visualization that makes trade-offs between calibration and generalization immediately visible, addressing a gap that reliability diagrams and SmoothECE plots leave open. Open questions include whether the Bregman-divergence framework extends naturally to non-Bregman proper scoring rules, how to handle the bandwidth sensitivity of kernel regression estimates in practice, and whether the sharpness-gap perspective generalizes to regression or sequence-level calibration settings.
