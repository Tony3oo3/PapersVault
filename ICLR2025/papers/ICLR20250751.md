## Problem

Language reward models (RMs) are critical for aligning LLMs with human preferences but remain opaque black boxes whose predictions lack explanation. While prior work has characterised specific RM failure modes (length bias, over-optimisation), no explainable-AI technique—particularly contrastive explanations—had been applied to RMs, leaving their local decision boundaries and attribute-level sensitivities unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250751/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single binary comparison (a user prompt x, a chosen response y+, and a rejected response y−) together with black-box access to an RM's scalar reward function r(x, y). It produces 15 attribute-conditioned perturbed responses for each of y+ and y−, each labelled as a counterfactual (CF, preference flips) or semifactual (SF, preference unchanged), which can then be aggregated across many comparisons into per-attribute preference-flip-rate (PFR) rankings and matched to individual comparisons to surface representative examples. The entire pipeline is post-hoc and model-agnostic, requiring no gradients or internal access to the RM.

## Method

The paper formalises CF/SF contrastive explanations for binary RM comparisons and proposes a two-step LLM prompting strategy to generate the required perturbations. In Step 1, GPT-4o identifies words in the target response relevant to a given high-level evaluation attribute (one of 15: helpfulness, harmlessness, clarity, correctness, etc.); in Step 2, the LLM is prompted to modify the response in the direction opposing the RM's original evaluation along that attribute, with changes centred on the Step-1 words to enforce locality. Perturbed responses are categorised as CFs or SFs by checking whether the RM's preference over the new comparison flips relative to the original. For global analysis, CF/SF labels are aggregated across 500 comparisons to compute each attribute's preference-flip rate per RM, and representative examples are found by matching each comparison's local reward-change ranking (Kendall's τ) against the global sensitivity ranking, enabling both single-model interpretation and side-by-side comparison of two RMs' reasoning bases.

## Results

On the harmless and helpful datasets, the proposed attribute-conditioned method (OURS) achieves CF coverage of 69% and 80% respectively for both responses, compared to at most 26% for Polyjuice and 22% for Random Perturbation. Semantic distance to the original response is substantially lower for OURS (0.36 on harmless, 0.28 on helpful) versus baselines (0.69–0.70 for PJ, 0.34–0.70 for RP), confirming explanations remain local. On the longer hs2 dataset, OURS still leads on CF coverage for both responses (33% vs. 4% for RP) and achieves the lowest semantic distance (0.07 vs. 0.18 for RP). SF coverage is comparable across methods, with OURS achieving 90–97% on harmless and 74–99% on helpful.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: Polyjuice (PJ), a GPT-2-based counterfactual generation method adapted for 15 perturbations per response, and Random Perturbation (RP), which prompts GPT-4o to generate 15 random perturbations without attribute-conditioning. The proposed method's dominant advantage is CF coverage, particularly for rejected responses: on harmless, OURS achieves 85% rejected CF coverage versus 40% (PJ) and 38% (RP); on helpful, 98% versus 14% (PJ) and 23% (RP); on hs2, 39% versus 5% (RP). This gap arises because the baselines are not explicitly designed to perturb rejected responses in the direction that would make them more preferred. On semantic distance, OURS is substantially closer to the original (0.36 on harmless vs. 0.69 for PJ; 0.28 on helpful vs. 0.40 for PJ; 0.07 on hs2 vs. 0.18 for RP), confirming locality. The one metric where OURS trails is semantic diversity (e.g., 0.31 vs. 0.49 on harmless), which the authors attribute to the necessary trade-off of keeping perturbations close to the original while grounding them in 15 distinct evaluation attributes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the contrastive explanation paradigm—specifically counterfactual (Wachter et al., 2017) and semifactual (Kenny & Keane, 2021) explanations—from classification and tabular settings to the binary comparison task of language reward models. It builds on recent work using LLMs to generate text perturbations for CF generation (Polyjuice, Gat et al., 2024) and on multi-attribute reward decomposition (Wang et al., 2023; Go et al., 2024), but is the first to use attribute-conditioned LLM prompting to produce diverse, locally-relevant perturbations specifically for explaining RM preferences. The method is model-agnostic and post hoc, requiring only black-box access to the RM, distinguishing it from approaches that modify the RM architecture to output aspect-level rewards.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Reward models are central to LLM alignment yet remain opaque black boxes; this work provides a practical, interpretable framework for understanding both local preferences and global attribute sensitivities of RMs. The discovery that even top-performing 8B RMs (LlamaOB, LlamaSW) exhibit undesirable behaviors—such as preferring responses containing disrespectful language—suggests that current alignment benchmarks may not capture all failure modes, motivating more targeted evaluation. Open questions include whether the attribute list generalizes beyond conversational tasks, whether augmented datasets built from these perturbations improve RM training, and how to formalize the distinction between different types of semifactual responses for finer-grained analysis.
