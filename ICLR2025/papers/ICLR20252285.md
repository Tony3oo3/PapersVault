## Problem

Existing interventional causal discovery methods (augmented DAGs, mutilated DAGs) implicitly assume interventions are applied to the general population, yet real experiments always enroll pre-selected subjects (e.g., only disease patients in drug trials, only existing users in A/B tests). The paper demonstrates that simply adding selection variables to augmented DAGs fails to model the resulting data, because the timing and context of interventions—applied after selection rather than before—produce fundamentally different statistical patterns that no existing graphical paradigm captures.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pooled tabular data from K+1 settings: one pure observational distribution p^(0) and K interventional distributions p^(k), each generated under a soft intervention with an unknown target. The algorithm does not require knowledge of which variables were intervened on or the intervention strength. It outputs a partially ancestral graph (PAG) over the D observed variables, where directed edges (→) indicate identified causal relations, undirected edges (—) indicate edges whose orientation is undetermined within the Markov equivalence class, and circle marks encode uncertainty from latent selection variables. The PAG simultaneously encodes both causal structure and selection mechanisms up to equivalence.

## Method

The core contribution is the interventional twin graph, a DAG that explicitly models two worlds: an observed reality world (X, ζ) where interventions are applied, and an unobserved counterfactual basal world (X*_aff, S*) where selection occurs before any intervention. Only variables in the descendant set of the intervention target are split into paired reality/counterfactual vertices, linked by shared exogenous noise terms (ε) that capture individual-specific traits. Selection variables S* are conditioned on in the counterfactual world, encoding that all observed individuals were pre-selected. The paper characterizes the Markov properties (Theorem 1) showing that d-separations in the twin graph imply both within-distribution CIs and cross-distribution conditional invariances, and derives MAG construction rules (Lemmas 3–6) that yield a graphical criterion for Markov equivalence (Theorem 2). The CDIS algorithm first runs FCI on observational data for a sparse skeleton, then iteratively refines orientations using interventional data with twin-graph-specific rules that prevent false edge propagation—a key departure from naively applying Meek rules on the observational skeleton.

## Results

In simulations across varying numbers of variables (up to 15), CDIS achieves an average precision exceeding 0.7 for directed edges in the estimated PAG across most configurations, while all baselines generally remain below 0.65 precision (with UT-IGSP as a partial exception). CDIS also outperforms baselines on F1 score and edgemark accuracy. On the real-world sciPlex2 gene perturbation data, CDIS recovers biologically validated regulatory edges (e.g., RELA→RUNX1, JUNB→MAFF), and on the college achievement RCT it identifies gender-moderated treatment effects (SSP benefits women, SFP benefits men) rather than spurious selection-driven associations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CDIS is compared against five baselines: GIES (Hauser & Bühlmann, 2012), IGSP (Wang et al., 2017), UT-IGSP (Squires et al., 2020), JCI-GSP (Squires et al., 2020), and CD-NOD (Huang et al., 2020). CDIS outperforms all baselines on precision across most variable-count settings, with its average precision exceeding 0.7 while baselines stay below 0.65. UT-IGSP is the closest competitor, occasionally approaching CDIS's precision in some configurations, but still trails overall. The gap is most pronounced on precision, suggesting that existing methods falsely infer spurious causal edges due to unmodeled selection bias (as illustrated in the paper's Examples 1 and 2). On F1 score and edgemark accuracy, CDIS also leads, though the margins are smaller than on precision. The baselines' lower precision is attributed to their inability to account for the temporal ordering of selection-before-intervention, which the interventional twin graph explicitly models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the augmented DAG paradigm for soft interventions with unknown targets (Yang et al., 2018) and the FCI/MAG framework for latent variables and selection bias (Zhang, 2008; Richardson & Spirtes, 2002) to a setting where both are simultaneously present. The key departure is showing that the augmented DAG representation fundamentally fails under selection because it models interventions as applied 'from scratch' rather than after selection, necessitating a new 'interventional twin graph' that explicitly separates the pre-intervention counterfactual world (where selection occurs) from the post-intervention observed world. The algorithm CDIS builds on FCI's orientation rules but introduces a novel iterative refinement step using interventional twin graph-specific MAG construction criteria (Lemmas 4–6) that have no analogue in prior constraint-based methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Selection bias in interventional studies is ubiquitous (drug trials enroll only patients, A/B tests target existing users, gene perturbation studies focus on specific cell types), yet no prior method handles it in the interventional causal discovery setting. The counterintuitive finding that interventions can *add* dependencies under selection (not just remove them) challenges a core assumption in the field and explains why naively combining existing tools produces false causal conclusions. Open questions include: proving completeness of CDIS (conjectured but unproven), developing a graphical representation for the equivalence class, and extending the model to post-intervention selection (e.g., lost-to-follow-up). The practical impact is significant for any field conducting targeted experiments on pre-selected populations.
