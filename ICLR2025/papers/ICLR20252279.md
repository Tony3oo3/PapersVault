## Problem

Existing benchmarks for LLM-based judges (MTBench, LLMEval, FairEval) primarily measure alignment with crowdsourced human preferences, which conflates stylistic quality with factual and logical correctness. As LLM responses grow more sophisticated, human annotators become unreliable evaluators for complex reasoning, math, and coding tasks, leaving no objective benchmark to test whether judges can actually distinguish correct from incorrect responses on genuinely difficult problems.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252279/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance consists of a question and two responses (A, B) generated by the same model (GPT-4o), where exactly one response is objectively correct per a ground-truth verification algorithm. The judge is run twice per pair with the response order swapped to mitigate positional bias; the aggregate verdict is A>B only if both trials yield A>B or one yields A>B and the other A=B, while inconsistent decisions or double ties are scored as incorrect. The final metric is the judge's accuracy across 350 pairs partitioned into Knowledge (154), Reasoning (98), Math (56), and Coding (42).

## Method

The core pipeline transforms any existing dataset with ground-truth labels and a verification algorithm into a judge-evaluation benchmark by sampling k responses per question from a single strong model (GPT-4o), grading each for correctness, and retaining only questions where at least one response is correct and at least one is incorrect. This single-model generation strategy ensures stylistic consistency across pairs (mitigating length bias and self-enhancement bias) while the incorrect responses contain subtle factual or logical errors rather than obvious formatting failures, making them genuinely hard to distinguish. An additional LLM-based correctness check (GPT-4o-mini) filters out responses where the automated verifier and the LLM disagree, eliminating pairs where the 'incorrect' response is merely a formatting artifact. The resulting 350-pair benchmark is then used to evaluate prompted judges (Vanilla, Arena-Hard, VertexAI), fine-tuned judges (PandaLM, Prometheus2, JudgeLM, AutoJ, Skywork), multi-agent judges (ChatEval), and reward models, with the key finding that even GPT-4o achieves only ~56% accuracy and most fine-tuned judges fall below the 50% random baseline.

## Results

On JudgeBench (350 response pairs across Knowledge, Reasoning, Math, and Coding), most LLM-based judges perform at or below the 50% random-guessing baseline. The best prompted judge (Arena-Hard with GPT-4o) achieves only 56.57% overall accuracy, while the best fine-tuned judge (Skywork-LLaMA-3.1-70B) reaches 57.43%. Among underlying models evaluated with the Arena-Hard prompt, o3-mini at high reasoning effort achieves the highest score of 80.86%, and Claude-3.5-Sonnet leads general-purpose models at 64.29%. The best reward model (Skywork-Reward-Gemma-2-27B) scores 64.29%, matching Claude-3.5-Sonnet despite being a 27B model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

JudgeBench is substantially harder than all prior judge benchmarks: the strongest model (Claude-3.5-Sonnet) achieves only 64% on JudgeBench versus much higher scores on MTBench, LLMEval, FairEval, and LLMBar, and a 31% gap between best and worst models confirms strong separability. Compared to RewardBench's reasoning subcategories (PRM Math, HumanEvalPack), where top models reach 97% accuracy, JudgeBench's top reward models achieve only 64%, indicating far less saturation. Among fine-tuned judges, Skywork-LLaMA-3.1-70B (57.43%) outperforms its base Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct (52.29%) by 5.14 points, while PandaLM (13.14%) and most Prometheus2 variants fall well below random. Reward models (59–64% range) consistently outperform prompted LLM judges of comparable or larger scale, with Skywork-Reward-Gemma-2-27B (64.29%) matching Claude-3.5-Sonnet. The multi-agent ChatEval judge (34.00%) underperforms even single prompted judges, and the Vanilla GPT-4o judge (50.86%) is essentially at chance level, confirming that JudgeBench exposes the reasoning limitations of current judges.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of evaluating LLM-based judges via alignment with crowdsourced human preferences (MTBench, LLMEval, FairEval) by arguing that human preference is an unreliable ground truth for complex tasks requiring factual and logical correctness. It also extends LLMBar's focus on instruction following to the harder axis of objective correctness, and repurposes existing challenging datasets (MMLU-Pro, LiveBench, LiveCodeBench) through a novel pipeline that converts them into response pairs with verifiable ground truth. The hierarchical evaluation framework (instruction following → factual/logical correctness → stylistic preference) provides a principled reorganization of what judges should prioritize, positioning JudgeBench as the first benchmark to systematically test the middle tier at scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLMs produce increasingly sophisticated responses, the reliability of automated judges becomes a critical bottleneck for model improvement (RLHF, best-of-N selection, iterative training). JudgeBench demonstrates that even frontier models like GPT-4o perform near-random on objectively verifiable judge tasks, and that fine-tuned judges can perform below random, raising serious concerns about the current evaluation infrastructure. The finding that compact reward models (27B) match much larger general-purpose LLMs (Claude-3.5-Sonnet) suggests specialized verifiers may be a more efficient path forward. Open questions include whether the single-model generation pipeline introduces irreducible bias, whether the benchmark will saturate as reasoning-enhanced models (o3-mini at 81%) improve, and how to close the gap between solving and verifying, particularly in coding.
