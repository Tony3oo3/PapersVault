## Problem

Despite possessing rich domain knowledge, general-purpose LLMs struggle with structured tabular prediction because of numerical insensitivity and a fundamental modality gap between LLM reasoning and statistical tabular learning. Existing linguistic prompting techniques (zero-shot, few-shot, CoT, TabLLM, LIFT) that work well for unstructured text fail to unlock LLM expertise on heterogeneous numerical features, leaving a significant performance gap compared to fully supervised small tabular models.

## Input / Output

The method requires only unlabeled tabular data (no gold labels) and access to a blackbox LLM via API. The LLM is queried with a zero-shot prompt template that lists feature names and values, returning a per-sample confidence score between 0 and 1 for the target class. The final prediction is produced by the trained small tabular model (FT-Transformer), not the LLM directly, making the pipeline compatible with any LLM that exposes a fine-tuning API. All evaluated tasks are binary classification on medical and non-medical tabular datasets with 7–24 features and 303–48,842 samples.

## Method

SERSAL is an unsupervised self-prompting loop that synergistically co-teaches a small tabular model and a blackbox LLM without gold labels. In each iteration: (1) the LLM is queried with zero-shot prompting to produce per-sample soft confidence scores as noisy pseudo-labels; (2) a small FT-Transformer is trained from scratch using an adapted DivideMix procedure that dynamically fits a GMM on per-sample losses to separate clean from noisy LLM labels, with high-confidence samples (threshold τ=0.9) used for early stopping; (3) the trained small model's soft predictions (temperature-sharpened at 0.1) are used to fine-tune the LLM via its API with conservative settings (max 3 epochs, early stopping) to avoid over-memorization. The loop repeats, with each iteration refining both the small model and the LLM's tabular reasoning, and the final test prediction comes from the small model. This mechanism is orthogonal to linguistic prompting and can be combined with methods like TabLLM for further gains.

## Results

On 10 medical tabular datasets, SERSAL with GPT-3.5 (single loop, no gold labels) achieves AUC scores ranging from 63.97% (Framingham Heart) to 98.37% (Anemia), consistently outperforming all linguistic prompting baselines by 5–15 AUC points. With GPT-4, SERSAL further improves to 94.18% AUC on Heart Failure, surpassing the fully supervised FT-Transformer (88.19%), and reaches 98.82% on Anemia. The method also generalizes to non-medical domains (Churn, Credit, Adult), where SERSAL(GPT-3.5) improves over 0-shot by 5–14 AUC points, and to clinical trial datasets where GPT-4-based SERSAL approaches or matches supervised performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SERSAL(GPT-3.5) decisively outperforms all five linguistic prompting baselines (0-shot, 8-shot, CoT, TabLLM, LIFT) across nearly all 10 medical datasets, with typical gains of 8–15 AUC points over the best prompting method (e.g., HF: 91.39 vs. LIFT's 78.23; HE: 85.14 vs. TabLLM's 74.39; CO: 96.85 vs. LIFT's 87.93). Against the fully supervised FT-Transformer (FSSM), SERSAL(GPT-3.5) wins on HF (91.39 vs. 88.19) and LI (79.39 vs. 78.94) but trails on most other datasets, while SERSAL(GPT-4) extends its lead to HF (94.18 vs. 88.19) and LI (82.51 vs. 78.94) and narrows gaps elsewhere. The paper also demonstrates orthogonality: combining TabLLM prompting with SERSAL yields further gains (e.g., HF 93.82, HE 89.27), confirming SERSAL operates through a distinct mechanism. On non-medical datasets (Churn, Credit, Adult), SERSAL(GPT-3.5) improves over 0-shot by 5.5–13.7 AUC points but still falls short of the supervised FT-Transformer. On the adversarial 'Fake' dataset with random labels, SERSAL degrades below random guessing (38.72 vs. 53.85), confirming the method requires the LLM to possess relevant domain knowledge.

## Relation to Prior Work

SERSAL adapts the semi-supervised learning-with-noisy-labels (LNL) framework, specifically DivideMix, to the novel setting where the 'noisy labels' come from a blackbox LLM's zero-shot confidence outputs rather than human annotations. It departs from standard LNL (which requires a gold-label subset) and from teacher-student distillation (where student ≈ teacher) by using only LLM soft pseudo-labels to train a *better* small tabular model, then feeding that model's predictions back to fine-tune the LLM in an iterative co-teaching loop. The method is explicitly orthogonal to existing linguistic prompting techniques (zero-shot, CoT, few-shot, TabLLM, LIFT), which the paper shows provide minimal gains on numerical tabular data, and can be stacked on top of them for additional improvement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and quantifies a concrete gap: standard prompting strategies that work well for unstructured medical text (e.g., ICD coding) fail to transfer to structured numerical tabular prediction, leaving LLMs far below supervised small models. SERSAL offers a practical, label-free pathway to close this gap, which is particularly valuable in clinical settings where expert annotation is costly and data is limited. Key limitations include the requirement that the LLM possess at least some domain knowledge (SERSAL fails on a synthetic 'Fake' dataset) and dependence on fine-tuning APIs for blackbox LLMs. An open question is how the method scales as LLMs become more numerically capable, and whether the iterative loop converges or can diverge on more complex multi-class or regression tabular tasks.
