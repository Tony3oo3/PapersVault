## Problem

Despite the empirical success of chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting, theoretical understanding of *why* and *when* CoT improves in-context learnability has remained sparse. Prior analyses either restrict to fixed tasks with predetermined decompositions or show generic Transformer expressiveness without specifying actionable decomposition principles. This paper asks: how do specific task decomposition schemes affect the in-context learnability of complex reasoning tasks, and can CoT provably transform unlearnable tasks into learnable ones?

## Input / Output

The method consumes N in-context demonstrations in CoT format, where each demonstration is a tuple (x, z_1, ..., z_{k-1}, y) with k reasoning steps mapping through intermediate spaces Z_0 → Z_1 → ... → Z_k, plus a single query x. The output is a composed predictor h = h_k ∘ ... ∘ h_1 applied to the query, where each h_i is a linear model on fixed non-linear features learned per-step. The key structural constraint is that all k steps share the same sample size N, and the overall sample complexity is determined by the hardest (least sample-efficient) step in the chain.

## Method

The paper introduces a step-by-step learning algorithm A_CoT that independently learns each reasoning step via kernel gradient descent (minimizing squared loss over a linear hypothesis class on non-linear features φ_i), then composes the resulting predictors. A central result (Lemma 1) proves that a Transformer with linear depth kt and constant embedding size 2d can express this algorithm end-to-end by sequentially minimizing each step's loss in successive self-attention layers, leveraging the known correspondence between attention and kernel regression. An error upper bound (Lemma 2) shows the overall prediction error is bounded by c_k · Σ Δ(P_i, A_i), meaning the hardest subtask governs learnability (Theorem 3). For impossibility, the paper proves (Theorem 4, Corollary 5) that sparse parity functions are unlearnable without CoT due to the limited approximation power of the hypothesis class, yet become learnable with a two-step decomposition that first selects relevant bits and then computes a fixed XOR. Empirically, the designed CoT schemes improve GPT-4o and GPT-3.5-turbo accuracy on (n,k)-parities and 3-term DNFs from near-random to near-perfect, and on compositional arithmetic tasks, performance is governed by the hardest step's complexity rather than overall task length.

## Results

On (10,4)-parity learning, GPT-4o improves from 54% success rate without CoT (near random guessing) to 94% with the proposed CoT decomposition, while GPT-3.5-turbo improves from 47% to 74%. On 3-term DNF (width 3), GPT-4o goes from 85% to 96% and GPT-3.5-turbo from 74% to 90%. Theoretically, the paper proves (Theorem 3) that CoT reduces the sample complexity of learning a complex task to that of its hardest subtask, and (Corollary 5) that parity functions are provably unlearnable without CoT but become learnable with a specific two-step decomposition.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods; instead, the key comparison is internal: the same LLM (GPT-4o or GPT-3.5-turbo) evaluated with and without the proposed CoT decomposition. On (10,4)-parities, CoT yields a +40 percentage-point improvement for GPT-4o (54%→94%) and +27 points for GPT-3.5-turbo (47%→74%), lifting performance from near-random to strong. On 3-term DNF (width 3), GPT-4o gains +11 points (85%→96%) and GPT-3.5-turbo gains +16 points (74%→90%). The gains are most dramatic at moderate complexity (k=3–5 for parities, width 3–5 for DNF), where without CoT models perform at or below chance level, while with CoT they approach near-perfect accuracy. At very high complexity (k≥8 for parities, width≥8 for DNF), both with- and without-CoT performance degrades, consistent with the theoretical compounding-error caveat noted in the paper.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work connecting Transformers to optimization algorithms for in-context learning (Von Oswald et al., 2023; Cheng et al., 2024) by showing that a linear-depth Transformer can implement step-by-step kernel gradient descent across multiple CoT reasoning steps. It departs from prior CoT theory (Feng et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023b), which either analyzes fixed tasks with predetermined decompositions or studies expressiveness without quantifying the effect of specific intermediate steps, by introducing a general task-decomposition framework (Definition 2) that explicitly links the choice of CoT steps to PAC learnability and sample complexity. The impossibility results for parity functions build on classical hardness results (Kearns, 1998; Malach & Shalev-Shwartz, 2022) but are the first to show a concrete unlearnable-to-learnable transition via a specific CoT construction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous framework showing that CoT reduces the sample complexity of learning a complex task to that of its hardest substep, offering actionable design principles for constructing effective intermediate reasoning steps. The parity example demonstrates a provable unlearnable-to-learnable transition, which is a strong theoretical guarantee that no prior CoT work had established. However, the authors acknowledge a significant gap: the theory quantifies what a Transformer *can* express, but it remains unclear whether real-world LLMs trained via standard procedures actually achieve these guarantees, and the compounding error issue (exponential in k) limits practical applicability of deep CoT chains. Open questions include whether the decomposition principles transfer to open-ended reasoning tasks beyond the synthetic arithmetic and Boolean settings tested here.
