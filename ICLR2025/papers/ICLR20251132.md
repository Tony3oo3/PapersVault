## Problem

LLMs frequently fail to comply with prompt-specified rules, yet the current literature lacks a theoretical understanding of when and how jailbreak attacks succeed. This paper formalizes rule-following as inference in propositional Horn logic and identifies the precise mechanisms by which adversarial prompts subvert a model's reasoning, bridging the gap between abstract logic and transformer-based attention.

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of Horn logic rules Γ (pairs of binary vectors (α,β) ∈ {0,1}^{2n} representing antecedent-consequent) and initial facts Φ ∈ {0,1}^n, encoded as a sequence of d=2n dimensional tokens. For inference, it produces an autoregressive sequence of binary proof states; for attacks, it produces an adversarial suffix Δ ∈ R^{p×d} (typically p=2 rows) that induces violations of monotonicity, maximality, or soundness. The paper also extracts attention weight matrices to characterize how suppressed rules lose attention in the attacked case versus the clean case.

## Method

The core contribution is a logic-based framework that characterizes correct rule-following via three properties—monotonicity (facts never shrink), maximality (all applicable rules fire), and soundness (no spurious facts appear)—which uniquely characterize forward chaining in Horn logic. The paper constructs a one-layer, one-head transformer with embedding dimension d=2n that implements one step of rule application by using softmax attention to approximate binary disjunctions via weighted summation over applicable rules, then binarizing with a feedforward layer. Three theory-based attacks exploit this summation approximation: fact amnesia injects large negative values to cancel known facts, rule suppression inserts a negated rule (α,−β) to cancel a target rule's contribution, and state coercion uses ±κ-valued tokens to force an arbitrary target state. Empirically, the paper validates that these attacks transfer to GPT-2 reasoners trained from data, and that GCG-generated jailbreaks on Llama-2/3 produce attention suppression patterns and salient-token overlaps consistent with the theoretical predictions.

## Results

Theory-based fact amnesia and rule suppression attacks achieve ASR = 1.00 against learned GPT-2 reasoners (n ≥ 32), while state coercion reaches ASR = 0.57–0.77. On fine-tuned GPT-2 Minecraft models, GCG-based state coercion achieves ASR = 1.0 across all derivation depths (T = 1, 3, 5), while rule suppression reaches ASR of 0.11–0.29 (SSR 0.21–0.46) and fact amnesia reaches ASR of 0.14–0.21 (SSR 0.37–0.45). GCG-generated suffixes contain salient tokens from the adversarial target with 66–67% overlap (fact amnesia) and 53–57% overlap (state coercion), and substituting these tokens with 'and' drops ASR from ~0.25 to 0.02–0.10. Attention weights on suppressed rules drop by 50–70% in attacked versus non-attacked generations across multiple layers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is primarily a theoretical framework with empirical validation rather than a method comparison, so it does not benchmark against competing attack methods. The key internal comparisons are: (1) theory-derived attacks versus learned (gradient-optimized) attacks on small reasoners, where both achieve ASR ≈ 1.0 for fact amnesia and rule suppression, confirming the theory's predictive power; (2) GCG suffixes with versus without salient tokens, where removing salient tokens (substituting with 'and') drops ASR from 0.22–0.25 to 0.02–0.10, demonstrating that the targeted propositions' tokens are causally important for attack success; (3) attention weights in attacked versus non-attacked generations, where suppressed rules receive 50–70% less attention (e.g., 0.24 vs. 0.69 at layer 1 for T=3), providing mechanistic evidence that rule suppression operates through attention reduction. The paper also notes that the state coercion attack (soundness violation) is the hardest to achieve: it fails against learned reasoners even with repetitions, and GCG requires longer suffixes to succeed on LLMs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the GCG adversarial attack framework (Zou et al., 2023) but repurposes it within a formal logic-based setting, using propositional Horn logic to characterize rule-following via three properties (monotonicity, maximality, soundness). It departs from the transformer expressivity literature (Hahn 2020; Merrill & Sabharwal 2023; Strobl et al. 2023) by providing a parameter-efficient, task-specific construction (d=2n) rather than big-O complexity bounds, and from the jailbreak literature (Chao et al. 2023; Wei et al. 2024) by offering a theoretical mechanism—attention suppression via summation-based disjunction approximation—rather than purely empirical observations. The paper positions itself as the first to provide a formal, logic-grounded account of why and how LLMs can be subverted from following prompt-specified rules.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The MMS framework gives practitioners a concrete, checkable taxonomy for diagnosing rule-following failures (fact amnesia, rule suppression, state coercion), which could inform both attack design and defense strategies. The empirical alignment between GCG's gradient-optimized suffixes and the simple theoretical predictions (attention suppression, salient token overlap) is suggestive but explicitly correlational, and the paper acknowledges it cannot make definitive causal claims about LLM behavior. Key limitations include the restriction to propositional Horn logic (no quantifiers), consideration of only prompt-specified rules (excluding safety-training-learned rules), and the use of relatively small models (GPT-2, Llama-2-7B, Llama-3-8B). Open questions include whether the framework extends to more expressive logical systems, whether the attention suppression mechanism holds at larger scales, and whether defenses can be designed by targeting the specific MMS property being violated.
