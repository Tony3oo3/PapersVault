## Problem

Existing black-box optimization methods directly minimize the training loss, which can lead to overfitting and poor generalization because the loss landscape contains many local minima with varying generalization quality. Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) addresses this by seeking flat minima, but it requires access to true or stochastic gradients, making it inapplicable to black-box settings where only function values are available. This paper bridges the gap by adapting the sharpness-aware strategy to the gradient-free black-box optimization regime.

## Input / Output

The method takes a black-box objective function F(x) (only scalar function values are accessible, no gradients) and a training dataset D, and produces optimized parameters θ = {μ, Σ} representing a Gaussian search distribution. In the prompt fine-tuning application, the optimized vector v ∈ R^d is projected via a fixed random matrix A ∈ R^{d×D} into the token embedding space of a frozen RoBERTa-LARGE model, and the output is evaluated as test-set classification accuracy or F1 score. The algorithm requires 2N function queries per iteration (one batch for computing the perturbation, one for computing the update direction), with per-iteration cost O(Nd).

## Method

SABO reparameterizes the objective as J(θ) = E_{p_θ(x)}[F(x)] over a Gaussian distribution p_θ = N(μ, Σ), then formulates a min-max problem where the perturbation δ is constrained to a KL-divergence neighborhood C(θ) = {δ : KL(p_{θ+δ}‖p_θ) ≤ ρ²} in the statistical manifold of distributions, rather than the Euclidean ball used by standard SAM. The maximization subproblem is solved in closed form via Lagrangian duality, yielding δ_μ = (1/λ)Σ∇_μ J(θ) and δ_Σ = 2Σ∇_Σ J(θ)/(λΣ^{-1} − 2∇_Σ J(θ)), with λ determined by the KL constraint. The minimization subproblem then updates μ_{t+1} = μ_t − β_t Σ_t g_t and Σ_{t+1}^{-1} = Σ_t^{-1} + 2β_t G_t, where g_t and G_t are unbiased Monte Carlo estimators of the gradients computed from function queries only. The key distinction from SAM is that the neighborhood geometry follows the Fisher information metric (KL divergence) of the distribution space, and all gradient estimates are obtained via stochastic gradient approximation requiring only function evaluations.

## Results

On the black-box prompt fine-tuning task with d=500, SABO achieves 87.31% accuracy on SST-2, 82.65% on AG's News, 79.62% F1 on MRPC, 53.55% on RTE, 42.29% on SNLI, and 91.83% on Yelp Polarity, consistently outperforming all four baselines across every dataset. The advantage is most pronounced on AG's News (+6.72% over BES) and SNLI (+3.65% over BES), while the gap narrows on SST-2 (+1.19% over CMA-ES) and Yelp Polarity (+0.64% over CMA-ES). At the highest dimension d=1000, SABO still leads on SST-2 (87.96%), AG's News (82.77%), MRPC (79.68%), and Yelp P (91.96%), confirming robustness in high-dimensional settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SABO wins against all four baselines on all six datasets at every tested dimension (d=200, 500, 1000). Against CMA-ES, the closest competitor, SABO gains +1.19% on SST-2, +0.15% on AG's News, +2.52% F1 on MRPC, +0.84% on RTE, +0.95% on SNLI, and +0.64% on Yelp P (d=500). Against INGO (the strongest stochastic-gradient-approximation baseline), SABO improves by +3.02% on SST-2, +6.11% on AG's News, +0.53% on MRPC, +0.36% on RTE, +3.38% on SNLI, and +1.93% on Yelp P. The largest margins appear against BES and INGO on AG's News and SNLI, where the sharpness-aware perturbation most effectively avoids sharp minima. On the synthetic convergence experiments (d=500), SABO matches INGO's O(log T / T) convergence rate but is slightly slower in practice due to the per-iteration perturbation step, while CMA-ES and MMES fail to converge on the l_{1/2}-ellipsoid and Levy functions and BES fails on all four test problems.

## Relation to Prior Work

SABO sits at the intersection of two well-developed lines of work: stochastic gradient approximation methods for black-box optimization (Wierstra et al. 2014; Lyu & Tsang 2021) and sharpness-aware minimization (Foret et al. 2021). The key conceptual departure is moving SAM's perturbation from the Euclidean parameter space to the statistical manifold of Gaussian distributions, replacing the L2-ball neighborhood with a KL-divergence-constrained neighborhood over distribution parameters (μ, Σ). This distinguishes SABO from FSAM (Kim et al. 2022), which also uses information geometry but for a predictive distribution conditional on parameters, and from bSAM (Mollenhoff & Khan 2022), which connects SAM to the Bayes objective via Fenchel biconjugate but still perturbs individual points. Prior black-box methods (CMA-ES, INGO, MMES, BES) directly minimize the training loss; SABO is the first to minimize the worst-case loss within a distributional neighborhood, explicitly targeting flat minima in the search-distribution space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SABO provides the first principled way to improve generalization in black-box optimization settings where gradient access is unavailable, which is increasingly relevant as LLMs are deployed via inference APIs (LMaaS). The theoretical contributions—convergence rates of O(log T/T) (full-batch) and O(1/√T) (mini-batch) plus a generalization bound—give practitioners confidence in the method's behavior. However, the convergence analysis requires convexity of F(x) and a diagonal covariance assumption, which may limit applicability to highly non-convex real-world objectives. Open questions include whether the KL-neighborhood formulation generalizes to non-Gaussian search distributions, how the neighborhood size ρ should be adapted during optimization, and whether the gains persist at much larger scales (e.g., 10K+ dimensional prompt vectors or multi-task settings).
