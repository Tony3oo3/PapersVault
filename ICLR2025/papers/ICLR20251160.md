## Problem

LLM-based conversational agents tend to overhedge or implicitly guess user intent when facing ambiguity, rather than asking clarification questions, and existing tuning approaches (SFT, RLHF, DPO) do not directly optimize for pragmatic conversational actions. High-quality multi-turn dialogue data is often scarce due to annotation costs and privacy constraints, making it difficult to learn optimal dialogue action policies. This paper addresses the gap of data-efficiently teaching LLMs to implicitly select between clarifying and answering in multi-turn conversation.

## Input / Output

The model receives a potentially underspecified user query, task-specific grounding context (a SQL database schema, tabular financial data, or retrieved passages), and prior conversation history, and produces either a clarifying question or a direct answer (SQL query, arithmetic expression, or text span). The action space is restricted to {CLARIFY, ANSWER}, and evaluation operates at two granularities: turn-level (immediate response quality) and trajectory-level (the full simulated multi-turn interaction until the user's information goal is resolved). A post-clarification metric additionally measures answer quality specifically on turns that follow agent clarification questions.

## Method

ACT is a quasi-online extension of DPO that treats dialogue action planning as an implicit subtask of response generation. It first constructs contrastive preference pairs by pairing a ground-truth response (carrying its implicit action) against a rejected response generated by a high-capacity LLM conditioned on the opposite action. During training, the current policy samples an on-policy response; an action classifier checks whether its implicit action matches ground truth, and if it does, a user simulator extends the response into a full multi-turn trajectory whose outcome is scored against task-specific heuristics (e.g., DROP F1, execution match). The contrastive pair is dynamically updated based on whether the simulated trajectory succeeds or fails, and the policy is then updated via the standard DPO objective. This design enables learning when to clarify versus answer without explicit action labels, as pseudo-labels from a zero-shot LLM annotator achieve 98.5% agreement with ground truth.

## Results

On PACIFIC (tabular conversational QA), ACT with only 50 tuning conversations achieves 82.2 Macro F1 for implicit action recognition and 61.9 trajectory-level DROP F1, outperforming SFT (69.0 / 61.3) and IRPO (67.7 / 56.7) by up to 19.1% relative on action-level metrics. At 250 conversations, ACT reaches 89.6 Macro F1 and 65.7 trajectory F1, surpassing SFT (86.9 / 63.3) and IRPO (85.4 / 58.4). On AmbigSQL (conversational text-to-SQL), ACT achieves 52.3% execution match and 53.0% post-clarification execution match at 250 conversations, compared to SFT (51.0 / 50.7) and IRPO (49.7 / 45.6). On Abg-CoQA, ACT attains the best trajectory-level embedding similarity (75.1 at 250 conversations) versus SFT (66.2), demonstrating superior multi-turn goal completion.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ACT consistently outperforms SFT across all three tasks and all data budgets. On PACIFIC with 50 conversations, ACT improves Macro F1 by 13.2 points over SFT (82.2 vs. 69.0) and post-clarification F1 by 13.7 points (57.2 vs. 43.5); at 250 conversations the gap narrows to 2.7 Macro F1 points but ACT still leads on trajectory F1 (65.7 vs. 63.3) and post-clarification F1 (62.0 vs. 56.7). Against IRPO, ACT wins on every metric in every setting, with the largest gap on post-clarification F1 (57.2 vs. 34.4 at 50 conversations on PACIFIC; 53.0 vs. 45.6 at 250 on AmbigSQL). Compared to frontier LLMs with 10 in-context examples (Gemini Pro ICL: 81.4 Macro F1, 58.7 Traj. F1), ACT-tuned Zephyr 7B matches or exceeds them on action-level and trajectory metrics while requiring zero in-context examples at inference. On Abg-CoQA, ACT slightly trails SFT on Macro F1 at 100 and 250 conversations (51.1 vs. 52.6; 53.3 vs. 53.5) but dominates on turn-level and trajectory-level similarity (72.5 vs. 64.0 at 250), indicating better multi-turn reasoning. The off-policy DPO-Dist baseline (distilling from two frontier LLMs) underperforms ACT on PACIFIC across all settings (e.g., 75.5 vs. 82.2 Macro F1 at 50 conversations), confirming that on-policy sampling with action-based contrast is more effective than static preference distillation for conversational tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

ACT extends Direct Preference Optimization (Rafailov et al., 2024) into a quasi-online, multi-turn conversational setting by introducing action-based contrastive pairs (clarify vs. answer) and on-policy trajectory simulation with a user simulator. It departs from the traditional modular architecture of mixed-initiative dialogue systems (separate planning and generation modules) by treating action selection as an implicit subtask of response generation, and differs from other on-policy DPO variants like IRPO (Pang et al., 2024) by optimizing over full multi-turn trajectories rather than single-turn responses. The approach also connects to the broader LLM alignment literature (RLHF, SLiC, IPO) while specifically targeting pragmatic conversational skills that standard SFT and offline preference optimization do not directly address.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck: high-quality multi-turn conversational data is expensive to collect, and standard tuning approaches (SFT, offline DPO) struggle in data-limited regimes. ACT's ability to match or outperform frontier LLMs with in-context learning using only 50–250 conversations on a 7B model is practically significant for deployment scenarios where data is scarce. However, the method relies on task-specific heuristics for trajectory evaluation, commercial LLMs for user simulation and action classification, and is validated only on a binary action space {CLARIFY, ANSWER}, leaving open questions about generalization to richer action spaces, larger-scale data, and multi-task settings. The finding that zero-shot LLM pseudo-labeling achieves 98.5% agreement with human action labels suggests the approach could extend to unlabeled 'in-the-wild' conversational data, but this remains untested at scale.
