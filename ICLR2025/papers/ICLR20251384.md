## Problem

Hybrid large-neighborhood local search (LNLS) algorithms, which partition problems into sub-blocks solved by analog dynamical accelerators (DXs), lack any non-asymptotic convergence guarantees or principled hyperparameter selection schemes. Unlike fully digital methods, the interplay between algorithm parameters (step duration, block selection strategy) and device non-idealities (analog variation) is uncharacterized, forcing practitioners into trial-and-error calibration across different hardware.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a continuously differentiable potential f(x) over R^d satisfying a log-Sobolev inequality, a partition of the d-dimensional space into b blocks, per-block step durations λ_i, an inverse temperature β, and (for the finite-variation case) a fixed perturbation δ bounding the gradient error via constants M and B. The outputs are closed-form bounds: exponential KL-divergence decay rates for ideal devices, a W₂ Wasserstein bias term proportional to step duration and variation magnitude for non-ideal devices, and a combined expected function gap bound of O(d/β log βd + (M+B)(ε + log ε⁻¹)) for optimization tasks.

## Method

The paper reduces hybrid LNLS to Block Langevin Diffusion (BLD), where each block evolves under a Langevin SDE restricted to its subspace via a projection matrix U_i, with blocks swapped at discrete steps. For randomized block selection, they derive a 'de Bruijn inequality' by writing the ensemble-averaged Fokker-Planck equation in a meta-Eulerian form with a weighted Laplacian U_φ, yielding exponential KL convergence at rate 2γβ⁻¹φ_min λ_min. For cyclic selection, they prove a novel cyclic KL contraction lemma (Lemma 1) using the chain rule for KL-divergence, showing identical convergence rates to the randomized case under uniform parameters. For finite device variation, they bound the W₂ distance between ideal and perturbed BLD processes via a Girsanov change-of-measure argument, then combine with the Otto-Villani (Talagrand) transportation inequality to obtain a total Wasserstein error of O(log(1/ε) + ε), where the bias term is governed solely by the analog variation constants M and B rather than discretization error.

## Results

The paper proves exponential KL-divergence convergence for both randomized (Theorem 1) and cyclic (Theorem 2) block Langevin diffusion with rate e^{-2γβ^{-1}λ_min k}, showing that RBLD and CBLD achieve identical contraction bounds under uniform block selection. Numerical experiments on d=50 Gaussian sampling confirm an O(b) real-time slowdown relative to full-gradient Langevin diffusion (b=1), with convergence curves for b∈{1,2,5,10} blocks collapsing onto a single curve when the x-axis is rescaled by b. For finite device variation, Theorem 3 provides a W₂ bias bound of O(log(1/ε)+ε) that is proportional to step duration λ and perturbation magnitude (M,B); experiments show that multiplicative Gaussian perturbations with Δ≤0.5 remain stable (though biased) while Δ=0.6 causes divergence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper, so comparisons are between algorithmic variants rather than competing methods. RBLD and CBLD are shown to produce identical KL-divergence convergence rates under uniform block selection (φ_i=1/b, λ_i=λ), which is confirmed numerically in Fig. 2b where the curves overlap within sampling error after rescaling. Both block methods are compared against full-gradient Langevin diffusion (b=1, Theorem 0), revealing an O(b) slowdown in real time analogous to coordinate descent in optimization. The ideal-device results (Theorems 1–2) are compared against finite-variation devices (Theorem 3), showing that analog non-idealities introduce a non-vanishing W₂ bias that scales with step duration and perturbation magnitude, unlike the discretization error in digital LMC. The paper also positions its results relative to Ding et al. (2021) RCLMC, noting that the new bounds use the weaker LSI assumption (vs. strong log-concavity) and provide explicit constants for the continuous-time case that were previously missing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reduces hybrid Large-Neighborhood Local Search (LNLS) for dynamical accelerators to Block Langevin Diffusion (BLD), then applies the de Bruijn identity / LSI framework of Vempala & Wibisono (2019) to prove convergence, in contrast to the Wasserstein coupling approach of Ding et al. (2021) for randomized coordinate Langevin Monte Carlo. The cyclic block analysis draws on the block-descent structure of Beck & Tetruashvili (2013) via a novel conditional KL-contraction lemma, while the finite-variation (device non-ideality) bounds combine the Talagrand transportation inequality with the non-asymptotic SGLD analysis of Raginsky et al. (2017). The key departure from prior work is working under a log-Sobolev inequality (weaker than the strong log-concavity assumed by Ding et al.) and providing continuous-time bounds with explicit constants, which the authors note was necessary because prior discrete-time analyses were insufficient for the continuous dynamics of physical accelerators.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first non-asymptotic convergence guarantee for hybrid LNLS algorithms, providing a closed-form expression linking device variation magnitude, step duration, and sampling/optimization accuracy—directly addressing the practical problem of cross-device hyperparameter transfer that currently reduces to trial-and-error. The finding that ideal-device RBLD and CBLD have identical convergence rates (under uniform parameters) simplifies hyperparameter selection from O(b) parameters to one, and the O(b) real-time slowdown quantifies the cost of partitioning. Key limitations include the LSI assumption (excluding highly non-convex or discrete problems), the support-over-R^d assumption (real DXs operate on bounded domains like the unit hypercube), and the absence of quantization analysis for low-precision DACs/ADCs. Open questions include extending to the training regime, bounded-support domains via projected dynamics, and whether additional digital steps (Metropolis filters, replica exchange) can improve the non-asymptotic rate.
