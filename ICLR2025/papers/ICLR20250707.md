## Problem

Despite the widespread practical success of adaptive gradient methods like AdaGrad in training large models with large batch sizes, the only prior theoretical result demonstrating their advantage over uniform-step-size methods (SGD) was obtained in the convex nonsmooth setting, which is insufficient for smooth large-batch training. Standard smoothness and noise assumptions are coordinate-wise uniform and cannot capture the anisotropic structure of the problem that makes adaptive methods beneficial, leaving a gap between theory and practice.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes stochastic gradient estimates computed over mini-batches of size M, an initial parameter vector w_0, a step size schedule (constant or decaying), and a small epsilon for numerical stability. The output is the time-averaged iterate (1/T)Σw_t along with convergence guarantees expressed in terms of anisotropic quantities: the AdaGrad bound depends on ||L||_1 and ||σ||_1 (coordinate-wise sums) rather than the isotropic ||L||_∞ and ||σ||_2 used by SGD, and the nonconvex convergence objective is measured in ||∇f(w)||_1 rather than the standard ||∇f(w)||_2, yielding at most √d times tighter guarantees when gradients are dense.

## Method

The paper introduces an anisotropic L-smoothness assumption (||∇f(w)−∇f(w')||_{L⁻¹} ≤ ||w−w'||_L for a positive vector L) and a per-coordinate noise bound (E[n_j²] ≤ σ_j²), then proves convergence theorems for AdaGrad under these conditions in both convex and nonconvex settings. A key generalization is the anisotropic (L_0, L_1)-smoothness, where the local smoothness constant at each coordinate j is L_{0,j} + L_{1,j}|∂_j f(w)|, extending both the anisotropic smoothness and the standard (L_0, L_1)-smoothness to capture unbounded local curvature observed in neural networks. The core insight is that when the Hessian spectrum and gradient noise are highly imbalanced across coordinates (sparse L and σ), AdaGrad's coordinate-wise adaptive step sizes exploit this structure to achieve better dimensional dependence than SGD or AdaGrad-Norm, with the bias-term gap reaching Θ(1/d) in extreme cases. The analysis is validated empirically on logistic regression (libsvm datasets) and GPT-2 instruction-following fine-tuning on Alpaca, where AdaGrad consistently outperforms SGD, especially at large batch sizes.

## Results

On logistic regression (convex), Adagrad achieves training losses of 0.14–0.51 ×10⁻³ on small datasets (a4a, a6a, a9a) and 0.10–0.20 ×10⁻¹ on large sparse datasets (real-sim, rcv1.binary), consistently outperforming SGD by 5–25× across all batch sizes (M=1 to 1024). On the nonconvex GPT-2 instruction-following fine-tuning task (Alpaca), Adagrad reaches a final training loss of 2.11–2.20 versus SGD's 2.20–2.57, with the gap widening at larger batch sizes (e.g., at M=1024: Adagrad 2.20 vs. SGD 2.57). Theoretically, Adagrad's convergence bound depends on ‖L‖₁ and ‖σ‖₁ (coordinate-wise quantities) rather than ‖L‖∞ and ‖σ‖₂, yielding up to Θ(1/d) improvement in the bias term over SGD/AdaGrad-Norm when the Hessian and noise are sparse.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SGD, Adagrad wins decisively on every dataset and batch size tested. On the large sparse dataset rcv1.binary, Adagrad achieves 0.10×10⁻¹ at M=1 versus SGD's 0.48×10⁻¹ (4.8× better), and the gap widens to 25× at M=1024 (0.20 vs. 5.02 ×10⁻¹). On GPT-2 fine-tuning (Alpaca), Adagrad reaches 2.11 loss at M=128 versus SGD's 2.29, and 2.20 vs. 2.57 at M=1024, confirming that Adagrad's convergence is far less sensitive to increasing batch size. Against AdaGrad-Norm (the scalar-step-size variant), the comparison is primarily theoretical: Adagrad's bound uses ‖L‖₁ and ‖σ‖₁ while AdaGrad-Norm uses ‖L‖∞ and ‖σ‖₂, so when the Hessian spectrum and noise are sparse (as empirically verified in Table 2 with C_var ≈ 0.02–0.32), Adagrad's bias term can be Θ(1/d) smaller and its variance term Θ(1/√d) smaller. The paper also notes that under generalized (L₀,L₁)-smoothness, existing clipped-SGD results are incomparable with Adagrad's guarantees, further highlighting the advantage of coordinate-wise adaptivity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the original Adagrad analysis of Duchi et al. (2011), which demonstrated better dimensional dependence only in nonsmooth convex settings, by introducing anisotropic smoothness and noise assumptions that capture coordinate-wise imbalance in the Hessian and gradient noise. It generalizes the (L0,L1)-smoothness framework of Zhang et al. (2019) to a coordinate-wise anisotropic version (Assumption 5.1), drawing conceptual parallels to the anisotropic assumptions used in SignSGD analyses (Bernstein et al., 2018a). The key departure from prior smooth convergence analyses of adaptive methods (Défossez et al., 2020; Ward et al., 2020; Faw et al., 2023) is that those works rely on standard L-smoothness with respect to the 2-norm, which is coordinate-wise uniform and cannot reveal Adagrad's dimensional advantage over SGD.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first theoretical explanation for why adaptive gradient methods like Adagrad outperform SGD in smooth, large-batch training—a gap that has persisted since the original Adagrad paper. The finding that the bias-term gap can be O(1/d) (vs. O(1/√d) for variance) offers a concrete mechanism for the observed empirical advantage of adaptive methods at large batch sizes. Open questions include whether the anisotropic (L0,L1) smoothness assumption better characterizes large-scale LLM pretraining than the standard (L0,L1) smoothness, whether results extend to Adam and other adaptive variants, and whether the ℓ1-norm convergence measure used here is the most natural for practical optimization.
