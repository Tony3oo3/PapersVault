## Problem

Prior theoretical work on in-context learning for transformers has focused almost exclusively on linear regression tasks, leaving the classification setting unexplored. This paper asks: how many pre-training tasks and in-context examples are needed for a trained linear transformer to generalize well on random linear classification tasks, and can such transformers exhibit benign overfitting in-context—memorizing noisy in-context labels while still achieving near-optimal test accuracy?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251165/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The transformer receives a sequence of M labeled in-context examples (x_i, y_i) with x_i ∈ R^d and y_i ∈ {±1}, plus an unlabeled test vector x_{M+1} ∈ R^d, tokenized into a (d+1)×(M+1) embedding matrix E where the last column is (x_{M+1}, 0)^T. The output is a binary prediction sign(ŷ(E;W)) for the test label. Unusually, the test-time in-context examples may carry label-flipping noise (each label flipped with probability p) and a signal-to-noise ratio R̃ that can be substantially smaller than the pre-training SNR R, and the sequence length M at test time can differ from the training sequence length N.

## Method

The paper analyzes a convex single-head linear attention architecture whose prediction is ŷ(E;W) = (1/M Σ y_i x_i)^T W x_{M+1}, where W is a learned d×d preconditioner matrix. Pre-training uses gradient descent on logistic or exponential loss over B random class-conditional Gaussian mixture model tasks (cluster means uniform on a sphere of radius R, Gaussian noise with covariance Λ). Since the prediction is homogeneous in W, gradient descent converges in direction to the ℓ₂ max-margin solution (Soudry et al., 2018), reducing the analysis to characterizing this solution via KKT conditions. The key technical contribution is deriving matching upper and lower bounds on tr(W) and ‖W‖_F from the KKT multipliers, then applying a novel Hanson-Wright-type concentration inequality for vectors uniform on the sphere (where components are dependent) to show the test error is near the label-noise rate p. For benign overfitting, the paper shows that when d ≫ M(R̃² ∨ R̃), the max-margin solution behaves like a scaled identity, causing the transformer to memorize all in-context examples (including mislabeled ones) while still generalizing near-optimally to clean test points.

## Results

The paper's main quantitative result (Theorem 4.1) shows that the max-margin solution of a pre-trained linear transformer achieves in-context test error bounded by p + 2exp(−cM) + 2exp(−cρ√d) + 4exp(−cρR̃/‖Λ^{1/2}‖₂) + 2exp(−cρM^{1/2}R̃²/(‖Λ‖₂√d)), where p is the label-flipping noise rate and ρ = (c_B ∧ 1)/log²(2B²/δ). Theorem 4.2 establishes benign overfitting: when R̃ = d^β for β ∈ (1/4, 1/2) and B = d, the transformer memorizes all M in-context examples with probability 1 − o_d(1) while achieving test error at most p + 0.0001 + o_d(1). Notably, the test-time signal R̃ can be as small as Õ(1) even though pre-training requires R = Ω(√d), and the transformer generalizes under label noise despite being trained on clean data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or head-to-head comparisons against other methods. The contribution is the first theoretical characterization of in-context learning for linear classification with transformers, extending prior results that were limited to the linear regression setting (Wu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024; Ahn et al., 2023). The paper builds on the convex linear transformer architecture from Wu et al. (2024) and the implicit regularization framework of Soudry et al. (2018), but applies them to the classification setting with logistic/exponential loss. The benign overfitting result is novel in the transformer context, adapting proof techniques from Frei et al. (2023a,c) on max-margin solutions in neural networks. The small-scale experiments (Figures 2–3, GD with η=0.01 for 300 steps) serve only as illustrative validation of the theoretical predictions rather than benchmark comparisons.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical in-context learning literature from linear regression (Zhang et al. 2024, Wu et al. 2024, Ahn et al. 2023) to the linear classification setting, using the same convex single-layer linear transformer architecture. The analysis is grounded in the max-margin implicit bias of gradient descent (Soudry et al. 2018), which characterizes the limiting behavior of the trained transformer, and adapts benign overfitting proof techniques from Frei et al. (2023a,c) that showed max-margin solutions over neural networks behave like near-uniform averages of training data. The key departure from prior work is twofold: (1) the first theoretical guarantees for in-context learning in a classification task, and (2) the introduction of 'benign overfitting in-context' as a phenomenon where the transformer's forward pass memorizes noisy examples yet still generalizes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical characterization of when and how many pre-training tasks and in-context examples are needed for a transformer to generalize in a classification setting, filling a gap left by prior regression-focused analyses. The benign overfitting in-context result is conceptually significant: it shows that a transformer's forward pass can simultaneously interpolate all noisy in-context labels and achieve near-optimal test accuracy, a phenomenon previously only observed in the training dynamics of overparameterized networks. Key limitations include the restriction to a convex linear transformer (not general softmax attention), the requirement of high pre-training SNR, and the absence of label noise during pre-training. Open questions include whether these results extend to non-convex homogeneous architectures, softmax-based attention, and pre-training on noisy data where the max-margin solution may not exist.
