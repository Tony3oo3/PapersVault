## Problem

While universality results for equivariant neural networks show they can approximate any equivariant function, they do not reveal which input pairs are fundamentally indistinguishable by a given architecture. Prior expressivity analyses for equivariant models relied on combinatorial tools (WL test, homomorphism counting) limited to graph domains, or provided only upper/lower bounds requiring impractically wide hidden layers. This paper asks for a complete, architecture-level characterization of the identification relation for a broad class of equivariant networks and how hyperparameters (activation, depth, width, representation type) govern it.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a finite group G acting by permutations on finite sets, a sequence of permutation representation spaces V_0,…,V_d, layer subspaces M_i ⊆ Aff_G(V_{i-1}, V_i) (e.g., circulant matrices for circular CNNs or the 15-dimensional equivariant affine space for 2-IGNs), and a continuous point-wise activation σ. It produces the identification relation ρ(N) as an explicit recursive formula over depth, expressed as nested intersections and unions of zero loci of scalar-valued difference layers. The formula is constructive in principle but superpolynomial in execution; its primary value is enabling structural comparisons (inclusions, equalities) between neural spaces with different hyperparameters.

## Method

The core mechanism is the twin network trick (Proposition 2): the identification relation ρ(N) equals the zero locus of a 'twin' neural space that maps (α,β) ↦ η(α)−η(β), converting a separation problem into a zero-locus problem. Theorem 1 then gives a recursive formula over depth d: the zero locus at depth d is an intersection over output coordinates of unions over partitions of intersections of zero loci at depth d−1, where the partitions are determined by the linear part of the final layer and the bias partition of the penultimate layer. From this single formula, the paper derives four structural theorems: (i) any non-polynomial continuous activation achieves maximal separation power and all such activations are equivalent (Theorem 2); (ii) repeating an equivariant layer improves separation only up to a finite stabilization threshold R (Theorem 3); (iii) the identification relation for a representation V′⊕V″ is the intersection of those for V′ and V″, implying hidden feature width and invariant features have no effect (Theorem 4, Remark 1); and (iv) minimal representations R^{G/H} form a separation-power hierarchy mirroring the subgroup lattice of G, with the regular representation R^G achieving maximum and the trivial representation R achieving minimum (Theorem 5).

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical experiments or quantitative benchmarks. The key results are: (1) Theorem 2 proves all non-polynomial continuous activations (ReLU, sigmoid, tanh, etc.) achieve identical and maximal separation power for a fixed architecture; (2) Theorem 3 shows depth increases separation power only up to a finite repetition threshold R, after which further layers have no effect (e.g., Proposition 3 shows R=1 for regular representations); (3) Remark 1 proves hidden feature width (invariant features) has zero impact on separation power, strengthening prior results that required large widths; (4) Theorem 5 establishes a separation power hierarchy among minimal representations R^{G/H} ordered by subgroup inclusion K<H<G, with the regular representation R^G achieving maximum and the trivial representation R achieving minimum separation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper contains no empirical baselines or experimental comparisons, as it is a theoretical characterization study. However, it provides direct theoretical comparisons with prior expressivity results: Corollary 1 strengthens Proposition 5 (from Maron et al. 2019a and Geerts 2020) by removing the requirement for large hidden feature dimensions F, showing that k-IGNs match k-WL separation power for any positive feature width. Proposition 6 characterizes the separation power hierarchy of circular CNNs by filter size, showing ρ(n-CNN) ⊊ ρ(1-CNN) for n>2, which is a new result not available from prior WL-based or homomorphism-counting approaches. The paper also positions itself against the limitations of WL isomorphism tests and homomorphism counting (Lovász 2012), noting those methods are restricted to combinatorial/relational structures and cannot handle geometric graphs or continuous domains, whereas the proposed framework applies to all permutation-representation equivariant networks including Circular CNNs and Icosahedral CNNs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the separation power analysis of equivariant networks from graph-specific combinatorial settings (WL isomorphism test, homomorphism counting) to a broader continuous-domain class of regular G-convolutional networks, building directly on the equivariant affine map decomposition framework of Pacini et al. (2024) and the architectural formalism of Cohen & Welling (2016a). Where prior work on IGNs (Maron et al., 2019a; Geerts, 2020) provided only upper or lower bounds on expressivity, this paper delivers a complete recursive characterization of the identification relation via the twin network trick, and strengthens existing results by removing the large hidden-width requirement in Corollary 1. The approach departs from the WL-based paradigm by operating in the continuous domain where combinatorial isomorphism tests are inapplicable, as highlighted by Joshi et al. (2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The results provide practitioners with a clear taxonomy of which architectural choices actually govern expressivity: all non-polynomial activations are equivalent, depth stabilizes after a finite threshold, and hidden feature width is irrelevant to separation power—meaning computational resources can be allocated to depth and representation type rather than width. The block decomposition hierarchy (Theorem 5) gives a principled method for comparing models across representation types. Key limitations include restriction to permutation representations (excluding Clebsch-Gordan and polynomial equivariant models), assumption of bias in intermediate layers, and the open problem of characterizing which polynomial activations achieve maximal separation. Extending the framework to non-permutation representations and bias-free architectures would significantly broaden its applicability.
