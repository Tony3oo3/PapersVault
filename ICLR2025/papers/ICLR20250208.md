## Problem

Recent CLIP-based DG methods have dramatically improved benchmark results, but it remains unclear whether these gains stem from genuinely better finetuning or simply from stronger pre-training that already contains target-domain-like data. Prior work showed perceptual similarity to pre-training images predicts zero-shot accuracy (the Image Similarity Hypothesis), but whether this relationship holds after source-domain finetuning in the DG setting was unexplored. The paper asks whether DG methods are learning new generalizable features or merely exploiting pre-existing image-text alignment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250208/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The DG pipeline takes RGB images with class labels from multiple source domains for training and evaluates on held-out target domains with disjoint distributions. The novel evaluation protocol assigns each target sample an AlignmentScore (cosine similarity between its CLIP image embedding and its class text embedding, computed before finetuning) and splits the test set into In-Pretraining (IP, score ≥ 0.21) and Out-of-Pretraining (OOP, 0.15 ≤ score < 0.21) subsets. Samples below 0.15 are discarded due to high mislabeling rates, and samples above 0.4 are excluded to avoid OCR confounds where text in the image trivially matches the label.

## Method

The paper introduces the Alignment Hypothesis, which states that DG performance after source finetuning is determined by the pre-training alignment between image and class text embeddings (cosine similarity in CLIP's embedding space), rather than by perceptual similarity to pre-training images. Using OpenCLIP-ViT/B-16, they compute AlignmentScores for all samples across five DomainBed datasets, clean the data, and split evaluation sets at a threshold of 0.21 into DomainBed-IP and DomainBed-OOP. They then benchmark 11 DG methods (CLIPood, MIRO, SWAD, MPA, VL2V-SD, CORAL, ERM, and combinations) on both splits, finding that all methods achieve near-oracle performance on IP data but drop substantially on OOP data, with SOTA methods not consistently outperforming older combinations (e.g., MIRO+MPA matches CLIPood on OOP). This demonstrates that current DG methods primarily exploit pre-trained alignment rather than learning new generalizable features from source data.

## Results

On the novel DomainBed-OOP split (low pre-training alignment), the best DG method MIRO+MPA achieves 57.2% average accuracy, only marginally above CLIPood (57.1%), while ERM reaches 47.0% and the oracle (target-domain finetuning) reaches 75.8%. On DomainBed-IP (high alignment), CLIPood achieves 84.7% average, with MIRO+MPA at 84.1% and MIRO at 81.7%, and some methods even surpass the 91.0% oracle on OfficeHome and PACS. On the standard unsplit DomainBed-All, CLIPood leads at 78.1% average, followed by MIRO+MPA (77.7%) and MIRO (75.0%), versus an oracle of 86.8%. The critical finding is that the ~27-point gap between IP and OOP performance persists across all methods, revealing that current DG approaches primarily exploit pre-training alignment rather than learning new generalizable features.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On DomainBed-OOP, the SOTA method CLIPood (57.1%) is essentially tied with the older combination MIRO+MPA (57.2%), indicating that CLIPood's advantage on standard benchmarks comes from high-alignment IP data rather than superior OOP generalization. MIRO+MPA also outperforms MIRO alone (52.4%) by 4.8 points on OOP, while MIRO is 2% worse than MPA on IP, showing MPA's complementary value for low-alignment data. SWAD (51.1%) underperforms MPA (53.0%) on OOP despite conceptual similarity, attributed to source-domain overfitting in its averaging interval selection. The zero-shot OpenCLIP baseline (48.1% on OOP) is competitive with ERM (47.0%), confirming that DG finetuning provides only ~10% gain over no training at all on misaligned data. On DomainBed-IP, CLIPood (84.7%) leads all methods, with MIRO+MPA (84.1%) and MIRO (81.7%) close behind, and the zero-shot model (79.0%) sometimes outperforming finetuned methods on PACS and VLCS, revealing catastrophic forgetting during DG finetuning. CORAL (45.8%) and SAGM (46.9%) remain the weakest DG methods on OOP, barely above ERM, while VL2V-SD (53.7%) and Attn Tune (51.8%) occupy the middle tier.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the Image Similarity Hypothesis (Mayilvahanan et al., 2024), which showed that perceptual similarity to pre-training data predicts zero-shot CLIP performance, by demonstrating that this relationship is limited in the domain generalization setting. Instead, it introduces the Alignment Hypothesis—measuring cosine similarity between image and text-label embeddings before fine-tuning—as a stronger predictor of post-finetuning DG accuracy. The paper positions itself as an analytical re-examination of the CLIP-based DG method family (MIRO, CLIPood, VL2V-SD, SWAD, MPA, etc.) within the established DomainBed benchmark framework, rather than proposing a new DG algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all evaluated DG methods—including state-of-the-art CLIPood—perform poorly on low-alignment (OOP) data while excelling on high-alignment (IP) data challenges the field's assumption that DG methods are genuinely learning new generalizable features from source data. The released DomainBed-OOP split provides a concrete diagnostic tool that will likely reshape how DG progress is measured, exposing that apparent SOTA gains may be artifacts of stronger pre-training rather than better finetuning. Open questions include whether the Alignment Hypothesis extends to non-CLIP backbones (e.g., DINOv2), whether the 0.21 threshold generalizes across architectures, and how to develop finetuning strategies that improve performance on low-alignment samples without sacrificing IP performance.
