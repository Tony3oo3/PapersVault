## Problem

Current vision-language models answer confidently even when questions are unanswerable, because standard training recipes incentivize predictions regardless of confidence and existing benchmarks focus exclusively on scenarios with definitive answers. This paper identifies a notable gap: no systematic benchmark or taxonomy exists for evaluating whether VLMs can distinguish between epistemic uncertainty (missing information) and aleatoric uncertainty (inherent unpredictability) in multimodal settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250456/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes an RGB image paired with a free-form text question and expects a text answer that is either a definitive response or an 'I don't know' variant (e.g., 'I don't see any X', 'unanswerable', a follow-up question). Confidence is extracted at inference time by prompting the model to self-verify its prediction and normalizing the 'yes' token probability over {'yes','no'}. The dataset is structured as contrastive pairs: the same image paired with one answerable and one unanswerable question, or the same question paired with an original and an inpainted (object-removed) image, totaling 178K VQA instances across 95.8K images.

## Method

The paper introduces a five-category taxonomy of multimodal uncertainty (knowledge, complexity, extraneous under epistemic; temporal, ambiguity under aleatoric) and synthesizes CERTAINLYUNCERTAIN via two pipelines: (1) identifying salient objects with GPT-4, masking them with Grounded-SAM, and inpainting with LaMa to render previously answerable questions unanswerable; and (2) prompting GPT-4 with DOCCI captions to generate contrastive answerable/unanswerable question pairs. For evaluation, the authors extend LAVE with a dual-stage IDK-normalization mechanism and propose confidence-weighted accuracy, which multiplies LAVE_idk correctness by the model's self-reported prediction probability, yielding a metric positively correlated with accuracy and negatively correlated with ECE. Training experiments use three strategies—LoRA-SFT, R-tuning (re-annotating wrong predictions as IDK), and DPO on contrastive pairs—demonstrating that fine-tuning with CERTAINLYUNCERTAIN improves refusal accuracy, reduces calibration error, and lowers hallucination rates without degrading standard VQA performance.

## Results

On the CERTAINLYUNCERTAIN benchmark (178K VQA samples across 5 uncertainty categories), state-of-the-art VLMs perform poorly: GPT-4V achieves only 71.70% LAVE_idk accuracy and 75.76% F1_idk, while open-source models like LLaVA-1.5-7B score just 47.46% accuracy and 0.47 confidence-weighted accuracy. After supervised fine-tuning with CERTAINLYUNCERTAIN, Qwen-VL-Chat improves from 63.87% to 78.59% LAVE_idk accuracy and from 13.45 to 49.35 confidence-weighted accuracy, while reducing ECE from 0.79 to 0.31. On refusal benchmarks, the fine-tuned model improves UNK-VQA accuracy from 41.32% to 59.70% and TDIUC from 95.10% to 99.20%, while maintaining VQAv2 performance (77.32 vs. 72.96 baseline).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among existing VLMs, GPT-4V leads with 71.70% total accuracy but still falls well short of human performance (94%), and all models show a large gap between LAVE_idk accuracy and confidence-weighted accuracy, indicating poor calibration. For training strategies, SFT with CERTAINLYUNCERTAIN (49.35 conf-w acc, ECE 0.31) substantially outperforms SFT with LRV data (4.16 conf-w acc, ECE 0.73) and SFT with LLaVA data (14.44 conf-w acc, ECE 0.68), demonstrating the dataset's effectiveness. R-tuning with CERTAINLYUNCERTAIN (45.58 conf-w acc, ECE 0.29) achieves the lowest ECE but slightly lower accuracy than SFT. DPO with CERTAINLYUNCERTAIN (19.44 conf-w acc) underperforms both SFT and R-tuning, suggesting preference optimization is less effective for uncertainty awareness. On generalization benchmarks, the SFT-trained model improves UNK-VQA (59.70 vs. 41.32 baseline) and TDIUC (99.20 vs. 95.10), reduces POPE hallucination rate (0.39 vs. 0.41), and improves VizWiz (67.44 vs. 66.85), while AMBER scores slightly degrade (85.90 vs. 87.70), likely due to the absence of attribute/relation IDK questions in the dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on abstention and selective prediction in VQA (Whitehead et al., 2022; Kamath et al., 2020) and unanswerable-question datasets (UNK-VQA, TDIUC) by introducing a systematic taxonomy of multimodal uncertainty—splitting it into epistemic (knowledge, complexity, extraneous) and aleatoric (temporal, ambiguity) sub-categories—and constructing a 178K-sample benchmark that covers all five types via image inpainting and LLM-generated contrastive pairs. It builds on the LAVE evaluation metric (Mañas et al., 2024) to define a dual-stage IDK-aware scoring mechanism, and on R-tuning (Zhang et al., 2023) and DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) as training strategies, but departs from prior refusal datasets by ensuring contextually aligned question-image pairs rather than pairing unrelated questions with images. The confidence-weighted accuracy metric is novel, filling the gap between accuracy (which ignores confidence) and ECE (which ignores correctness).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a fundamental blind spot in current VLM training: even GPT-4V and the latest open-source models (Qwen2-VL, InternVL2, LLaVA-OV) show no improvement in uncertainty awareness despite large gains on standard VQA, suggesting the dominant instruction-tuning recipe structurally discourages calibrated abstention. The finding that fine-tuning with CERTAINLYUNCERTAIN reduces hallucination ratios and improves refusal accuracy without degrading standard VQA performance makes it a practical tool for building more trustworthy VLMs. Open questions include whether the AMBER degradation (attribute/relation hallucination) can be addressed by extending the dataset, whether confidence-weighted accuracy can serve as a reward signal in RLHF, and whether the taxonomy generalizes beyond VQA to other multimodal tasks like captioning or grounding.
