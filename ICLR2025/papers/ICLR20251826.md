## Problem

While mirror descent generalizes gradient descent by replacing Euclidean distance with a Bregman divergence defined by a convex potential, its implicit bias for overparameterized neural networks has not been characterized in terms of the returned solution functions—prior work only described parameter-space properties. Additionally, existing function-space characterizations of gradient flow bias for univariate ReLU networks required either an intractable linear adjustment of training data or the addition of skip connections, limiting their practical applicability.

## Input / Output

The method takes a finite set of scalar training pairs (x_i, y_i), a two-layer network with n hidden units initialized with unit-norm input weights and sub-Gaussian biases/output weights, and a separable convex potential function Φ. The output is a trained network function whose difference from initialization is characterized as the solution to a variational problem: for unscaled potentials, a p_B^{-1}-weighted L² norm of the second derivative plus two boundary-condition functionals; for scaled potentials, a Bregman-divergence functional of the second derivative that is generally non-homogeneous and cannot be expressed as any RKHS norm.

## Method

The paper proves that mirror flow with unscaled separable potentials exhibits lazy training (parameters remain O(n^{-1/2}) from initialization), causing the potential's effect to reduce to a quadratic form and making the implicit bias identical to gradient flow in the infinite-width limit. The proof proceeds in three steps: (1) linearization showing the full-parameter network is approximated by training only output weights; (2) characterizing the parameter-space bias as a Bregman-divergence minimization (for unscaled potentials this reduces to an L² norm, while for scaled potentials it retains the full Bregman structure); and (3) solving the minimal representation cost problem to pull the cost back to function space, using an even-odd decomposition of ReLU networks into absolute-value and affine components. For scaled potentials, the kernel matrix H(t) does not remain close to H(0), placing training outside the kernel regime, and the resulting bias depends on the potential's shape—determining how different curvature magnitudes are penalized—while the initialization distribution determines where in the input domain curvature is penalized most strongly.

## Results

The paper establishes two main theoretical results. Theorem 2 shows that mirror flow with unscaled potentials (e.g., φ(x)=x², cosh(x), hypentropy) exhibits lazy training with parameters staying O(n^{-1/2}) from initialization, and in the infinite width limit has the *same* implicit bias as ordinary gradient flow, characterized by a variational problem minimizing a weighted L² curvature penalty ∫(h''(x))²/p_B(x) dx plus two boundary terms. Theorem 9 shows that mirror flow with scaled potentials (Φ(θ)=n^{-2}Σφ(n(θ_k-θ̂_k))) also exhibits lazy training (parameters O(n^{-1}) from initialization) but is *not* in the kernel regime; the implicit bias is a Bregman-divergence functional ∫D_φ(h''(x)/(2p_B(x)), 0) p_B(x) dx that depends on the potential and cannot be expressed as an RKHS norm for non-homogeneous φ. Numerical experiments (Figures 1–2) verify that the variational problem solutions match trained networks with decreasing L∞ error as width increases, and that different unscaled potentials produce nearly identical parameter trajectories while different scaled potentials produce visibly different curvature profiles.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without standard empirical baselines, but it makes several important comparisons. Against ordinary gradient flow, the paper proves equivalence of implicit bias for unscaled potentials in the infinite width limit (Theorem 2), which is surprising since finite-dimensional mirror descent is known to differ from gradient descent. Against the prior result of Jin & Montúfar (2023), the paper removes a key limitation: their characterization required either skip connections or an intractable linear adjustment of training data, whereas the present result holds for standard networks without such modifications. Against Williams et al. (2019) and Sahs et al. (2022), who suggested shallow ReLU networks in the kernel regime are well-approximated by natural cubic splines, the paper shows (Figure 1, right panel) that this only holds for very specific training data; a constant vertical shift of labels produces solutions with different slopes and U-shapes governed by the G₂ and G₃ functionals. For scaled potentials, the paper demonstrates (Figure 2) that the choice of potential φ controls the magnitude distribution of the learned curvature: φ₁=x² allows more extreme curvature values, while φ₃=x⁴+x² concentrates curvature on moderate values, a distinction absent in the unscaled/kernel regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the implicit bias analysis of mirror descent from linear models (Gunasekar et al., 2018a) to wide shallow neural networks, building on the lazy-training/NTK framework of Du et al. (2019) and Lee et al. (2019) for gradient flow. It directly generalizes the function-space variational characterization of Jin & Montúfar (2023) for gradient flow, removing their requirement for skip connections or intractable data adjustments. The introduction of scaled potentials departs from prior mirror descent work by showing that the kernel regime and lazy regime can be decoupled, a separation that does not exist for ordinary gradient flow.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first function-space characterization of mirror flow's implicit bias for neural networks, revealing that the parameter initialization controls where curvature is penalized while the potential controls how curvature magnitudes are penalized—a clean conceptual decomposition. The finding that non-homogeneous scaled potentials induce regularizations not expressible as any RKHS norm opens a path to richer implicit regularizations beyond the kernel framework. Key limitations include restriction to univariate, shallow networks with unit-norm input weights and separable potentials; extending to deep or multivariate networks with general initialization remains open.
