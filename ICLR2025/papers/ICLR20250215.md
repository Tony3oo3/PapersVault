## Problem

Prior work on in-context least squares with Transformers evaluates solutions against statistical criteria (e.g., Bayes-optimal estimators) but does not address whether learned solutions meet the numerical analysis standards of machine precision and numerical generality. This paper shows that standard softmax Transformers fail both criteria—precision saturates ~10^6× worse than machine epsilon and generalization is brittle to out-of-distribution inputs—due to a fundamental expressivity gap: softmax attention cannot exactly represent element-wise multiplications, which are essential to gradient descent.

## Input / Output

The model receives a 2D sequence of shape (N+1)×(D+1) where each of the first N rows is a pair (a_i, b_i) from the least squares problem and the final row contains the initial iterate x_0 padded with a zero; the model reads its prediction from the last position. For the explicit gradient task, the output is the gradient vector ∇L(x_0) ∈ R^D; for the k-th iterate task, it is x_k ∈ R^D. In the ODE setting, inputs are 25 in-context examples of forcing functions and initial conditions plus a query, and the output is the solution value at a query time t_query. All experiments use float32, targeting MSE near 2^{-46} ≈ 1.42×10^{-14}.

## Method

The paper identifies three linear algebra primitives (READ, LINEAR, MULTIPLY) that compose to form GD and Newton's method, proves that single-layer softmax attention cannot exactly express MULTIPLY, and shows that polynomial architectures—specifically BASECONV (gated convolutions) and non-causal linear attention—can express a single GD iterate in O(1) layers with O(D) state size. To overcome the optimization bottleneck where minibatch gradient variance dominates the population gradient signal at high precision, the authors propose a training recipe combining (1) an adaptive LR scheduler that exponentially increases or decays the learning rate based on the cosine similarity of successive minibatch gradients, and (2) an EMA over Adam's update vectors to smooth stochastic noise. Trained on the explicit gradient task (predicting ∇L(x_0) given A, b, x_0) rather than end-to-end least squares, 3-layer BASECONV and linear attention models reach 10^{-13} MSE; applied iteratively, they achieve 10^{-12} MSE and 10,000× smaller out-of-distribution generalization gaps than end-to-end Transformers.

## Results

On the explicit gradient task (1-step GD), 3-layer BASECONV and linear attention models trained with the proposed high-precision recipe achieve 5.0×10⁻¹³ MSE, approaching float32 machine precision (~1.4×10⁻¹⁴). Applied iteratively, these models reach ~10⁻¹² MSE on least squares, which is 100,000× lower than the best end-to-end Transformers (64 layers, ~10⁻⁸ MSE). The BASECONV model also incurs a 10,000× smaller generalization gap on out-of-distribution regression targets compared to Transformers. On in-context ODE solving, iterative BASECONV converges to ~10⁻¹⁰ MSE versus ~10⁻⁴ for 12-layer Transformers (1,000,000× improvement).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to the standard in-context least squares setup from Garg et al. (2022), the paper shows that end-to-end Transformers saturate at ~10⁻⁸ MSE regardless of depth (32 vs. 64 layers show negligible difference), while the proposed BASECONV + high-precision recipe reaches ~10⁻¹² MSE iteratively—a 100,000× improvement. Against the Transformer scaling results of Ahn et al. (2024), the paper confirms that Transformer precision gains diminish rapidly with depth and identifies the root cause as an expressivity gap in softmax attention (Theorem 3.1: single-layer softmax attention cannot exactly represent element-wise multiplication). On out-of-distribution generalization, BASECONV matches the robustness of true gradient descent (fixed η converges to the same precision regardless of input scale), while Transformers degrade by 10⁸× when inputs are scaled by 10×. Notably, linear attention—despite matching BASECONV's in-distribution precision—exhibits poor numerical generality with diverging iterations beyond σ=4, suggesting that expressivity alone does not guarantee algorithmic behavior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the in-context least squares literature (Garg et al. 2022; Von Oswald et al. 2023; Ahn et al. 2024), which evaluates Transformers against Bayes-optimal estimators in statistical (underdetermined/noisy) settings, by reframing the problem through the lens of numerical analysis: machine precision and numerical generality. It builds on the expressivity results showing non-causal linear attention can implement GD and Newton's method (Von Oswald et al. 2023; Giannou et al. 2024) and on the BASECONV architecture's equivalence to arithmetic circuits (Arora et al. 2023, 2024), unifying these under a polynomial-architecture framework. The adaptive LR scheduler draws on the cosine-similarity gradient diagnostic from Liu et al. (2023b), repurposing it as a precision-saturation signal rather than a generalization probe.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration of training sequence models to near machine precision (~10⁻¹³ MSE) for a numerical algorithm, and the first to identify that softmax attention has a fundamental expressivity gap for multiplication that prevents precise implementation of GD. The finding that standard components (LayerNorms, MLPs) degrade precision by up to 10⁶× challenges default architectural choices for scientific ML. Key open questions include: whether the high-precision training recipe scales to deeper networks and more complex algorithms (conjugate gradients, Newton's method), whether the precision bottleneck from stochastic gradients persists on harder end-to-end tasks, and whether these techniques transfer to realistic PDE/climate modeling where current ML approaches are known to be brittle out-of-distribution.
