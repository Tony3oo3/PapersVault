## Problem

Traditional economics research on ESG disclosure mandates relies on empirical analysis that cannot test counterfactual policies or simplified theoretical models limited to two agents or single-period games. No existing multi-agent reinforcement learning benchmark was designed to model the specific policy question of how ESG disclosure interacts with investor preferences to influence corporate climate mitigation over extended time horizons. InvestESG fills this gap by providing a scalable, open-source MARL environment grounded in finance and climate science literature.

## Input / Output

The environment is a partially observable Markov game running 100 annual periods (2021–2120) with M company agents and N investor agents (default 5 and 3). Each company outputs a continuous 3D vector in [0,1]³ specifying capital shares for mitigation, greenwashing, and resilience; each investor outputs a binary vector of length M selecting which companies to invest in, with capital distributed equally among chosen firms. The system produces evolving climate risk (three Bernoulli event probabilities), per-company capitals and ESG scores, investor portfolio values, and aggregate social outcome metrics (final climate risk P₁₀₀ and total market wealth W₁₀₀).

## Method

InvestESG models an intertemporal social dilemma where companies face a trade-off between short-term profit losses from mitigation spending and long-term collective benefits from reduced climate risk, formalized through a climate risk function P_t^e = μ_e·t/(1+λ_e·U_{t,m}) + P_0^e that decelerates with cumulative mitigation. ESG scores are computed as Q = u_m + β·u_g, where β>1 makes greenwashing cheaper than genuine mitigation, introducing an information asymmetry. The social dilemma structure is validated via Schelling diagrams showing that defection dominates when investors are profit-driven, but cooperation becomes optimal when sufficiently ESG-conscious investors (α>0) are present. Agents are trained with Independent PPO (MLP policies with 256/128 hidden layers, tanh activation, learning rate 3×10⁻⁵) built on Stable Baselines 3, with each agent maintaining independent parameters to model selfish optimization. The environment is implemented in both PyTorch and JAX for hardware-accelerated parallel simulation.

## Results

In the default 5-company, 3-investor setting, mandatory ESG disclosure alone (α=0) produces final climate risk and market wealth nearly identical to the no-mandate status quo, confirming that disclosure without investor demand does not resolve the social dilemma. As investor ESG-consciousness increases (α=0.5, 1, 10), mitigation spending rises monotonically, final climate risk decreases, and total market wealth increases, with the strongest effects at α=10. Providing climate-risk information to companies (even with no investors) reduces final climate risk to ≈0.7, comparable to the α=1 scenario. When greenwashing is enabled (β=2, 10, 20), companies initially explore greenwashing but quickly abandon it, and final climate risk remains similar to the no-greenwashing case.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark paper, so the 'baselines' are alternative policy configurations rather than competing algorithms. The central comparison is between (i) no mandate, (ii) mandate with profit-driven investors (α=0), and (iii) mandate with ESG-conscious investors at increasing levels (α=0.5, 1, 10). The mandate alone (ii) fails to outperform the status quo (i) on any metric—climate risk, market wealth, or mitigation spending—demonstrating that disclosure without investor demand is insufficient. The α=10 configuration (iii) dominates all other settings on every social outcome metric. A secondary comparison shows that enabling greenwashing (β up to 20) does not degrade final climate risk relative to the no-greenwashing α=10 case, and that adding resilience spending shifts company budgets toward resilience but yields comparable final climate risk. The paper does not compare IPPO against other MARL algorithms (e.g., QMIX, MAPPO), using IPPO as the sole agent algorithm throughout.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sequential social dilemma (SSD) framework of Leibo et al. (2017) and the Schelling diagram analysis of Hughes et al. (2018) from abstract particle-based environments to a concrete, policy-relevant domain: corporate climate investment under ESG disclosure mandates. It builds on the single-period analytical models of Pástor et al. (2021) and Pedersen et al. (2021) by introducing multi-period dynamics, climate evolution, and greenwashing, which are intractable in closed-form equilibrium analysis. Unlike prior MARL benchmarks (Melting Pot, SMAC) that use abstract or game-based settings, InvestESG is the first to encode a specific, actively debated regulatory question (SEC ESG rules) into a multi-agent environment, and it departs from the single-agent L2RPN power-grid benchmark by modeling a multi-party social dilemma between companies and investors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

InvestESG provides a scalable, open-source MARL benchmark that enables efficient testing of alternative ESG policy designs without the cost and infeasibility of real-world policy experiments, offering a new methodological bridge between ML and climate economics. Key open questions include whether more sophisticated MARL algorithms (beyond IPPO) can resolve the social dilemma more effectively, how the findings generalize to richer settings with employees, customers, and cross-industry dynamics, and whether the simplified ESG score can be redesigned to better incentivize genuine mitigation over greenwashing. The paper's limitations—binary investor choices, a single ESG metric, and a 'first-principles' climate model—mean the benchmark captures directional incentives rather than full market complexity, leaving room for future extensions that could sharpen policy recommendations.
