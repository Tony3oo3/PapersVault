## Problem

Existing networked causal inference methods assume the interference graph is known and identical to the social network, and that peer effects are summarized by a fixed function (e.g., sum or average). In real applications, the interference graph often differs from the social network and the true aggregation function is unknown, leading to model misspecification and biased treatment effect estimation. This paper tackles causal effect estimation under both an unknown interference graph and an unknown summary function.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253583/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes node features x ∈ R^d, binary treatment assignments t ∈ {0,1}, and a social network adjacency matrix A as input; critically, the true interference graph E is NOT provided. It outputs individual-level effects (IME, IPE, ITE) and population-level effects (AME, APE, ATE) for arbitrary peer-treatment configurations t_P, along with learned interference representations R_x and R_t that embed L-order neighbor information. The peer-treatment vector t_P is treated as a free variable at inference time, enabling counterfactual queries over different treatment distributions.

## Method

CauGramer learns interference representations via an L-layer GCN with cross-attention, where queries are linear projections of self-features and keys/values are GCN aggregations over the social network, broadening the receptive field beyond first-order neighbors to capture complex sequential interference. It separately learns feature representations R_x and treatment representations R_t (initialized from R_x), with the treatment GCN incorporating treatment values to mask node information. The training objective combines four terms: MSE for outcome regression, Wasserstein IPM for confounder balancing between treated and control groups, a cross-entropy term ensuring peer representations predict self-treatment (adjusting peer confounding), and a minimax moment constraint using an attention-based bridge function that enforces E[y−ŷ|q(r,t)]=0 for any bridge function q, providing a residual correction against model misspecification. The minimax game alternates between updating the main networks and the bridge function, with hyperparameters α, β, γ controlling the trade-offs.

## Results

On BlogCatalog, CauGramer achieves the best ε_AVE across all three population-level effects: AME 0.054, APE 0.034, and ATE 0.039, outperforming the next-best baselines (CFRNet at 0.058 for AME, NetEst at 0.078 for APE, and NetEst at 0.065 for ATE). On Flickr, the gains are even more pronounced: APE ε_AVE of 0.019 (vs. NetEst 0.055) and ATE ε_AVE of 0.032 (vs. NetEst 0.073). At the individual level, CauGramer achieves the lowest √ε_PEHE for ITE on both datasets (0.063 on BC, 0.051 on Flickr), representing a 46–51% reduction over the best baseline on BC and a 51% reduction on Flickr.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CauGramer achieves the best (lowest) error on all six metrics across both BlogCatalog and Flickr, outperforming all nine baselines (CFRNet, DRLearner, NetDeconf, G-HSIC, SPNet, CAL, Graphormer, RRNet, NetEst). The closest competitor for peer and total effects is NetEst (Jiang & Sun, 2022), which CauGramer beats by 56% on APE ε_AVE (0.034 vs. 0.078) and 40% on ATE ε_AVE (0.039 vs. 0.065) on BlogCatalog, and by 65% on APE (0.019 vs. 0.055) and 56% on ATE (0.032 vs. 0.073) on Flickr. For main effects, CFRNet is the strongest baseline (0.058 on BC, 0.066 on Flickr), with CauGramer improving by 7% and 58% respectively. Methods that assume a known interference graph identical to the social network (G-HSIC, SPNet, RRNet, CAL, Graphormer) perform substantially worse on peer and total effects, with APE ε_AVE values ranging from 0.22 to 0.44 on BC, confirming that the structural assumption of known interference introduces significant bias. DRLearner, which uses a doubly robust estimator, performs poorly on individual-level effects (IME √ε_PEHE of 0.545 on BC), while representation-based methods like NetDeconf and G-HSIC struggle with peer effects due to their reliance on fixed summary functions.

## Relation to Prior Work

CauGramer extends the Transformer architecture (Vaswani et al., 2017) to networked causal inference by replacing self-attention with cross-attention between self-features (query) and GCN-aggregated neighbor features (key/value), creating an L-order receptive field for learning interference representations without knowing the interference graph. It builds on the IPM-based representation balancing paradigm from CFRNet (Shalit et al., 2017) for confounding adjustment, but generalizes it to handle both self-treatment and peer-treatment confounding simultaneously. The key departure from prior networked causal inference methods (RRNet, NetEst, SPNet, G-HSIC) is the rejection of two structural assumptions: that the interference graph equals the social network, and that peer treatment effects are captured by a fixed summary function (sum/average). The minimax moment constraint with attention-based bridge functions draws from the conditional moment restriction literature but is novel in its application to networked interference settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap: in real-world networked data (epidemiology, marketing, economics), the interference graph is rarely known and rarely identical to the observed social network, yet most existing methods assume both. By being 'interference-agnostic,' CauGramer removes these structural assumptions, making causal effect estimation more applicable to real scenarios. Key limitations include reliance on a known social network for information aggregation, the quadratic complexity of attention computation, and the need for negative controls (requiring domain expertise) when unconfoundedness is violated. Open questions include scalability to very large networks, handling completely unknown social networks, and whether the L-order aggregation can capture interference beyond a fixed neighborhood depth.
