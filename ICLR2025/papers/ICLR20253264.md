## Problem

Standard multimodal DPO, which simply extends text-based preference optimization to include images, fails to align image and text representations and cannot effectively distinguish hallucinated from non-hallucinated descriptions. The paper identifies this through representation distribution analysis, showing that DPO-trained MLLMs keep image and ground-truth description embeddings far apart while collapsing the distance between hallucinated and correct text.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253264/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a text instruction as input and produces a free-form text response (e.g., image description or VQA answer). During training, each sample consists of a prompt, an image, a chosen (non-hallucinated) response, and a rejected (hallucinated) response from the RLHF-V dataset (5k pairs). Additionally, a rejected image is constructed by applying forward diffusion noise (T=500 steps) to the chosen image, creating a visual preference pair that forces the model to ground its response in visual evidence rather than language priors.

## Method

CHiP augments standard multimodal DPO with two modules. First, a Hierarchical Textual Preference Optimization module decomposes the text preference signal into three granularities: response-level (standard DPO log-likelihood ratio), segment-level (extra reward weight γ on tokens that differ between chosen and rejected responses, normalized by sequence length to prevent length bias), and token-level (sequential KL divergence between reference and policy models at each autoregressive step, with a stop-gradient on the chosen response term). Second, a Visual Preference Optimization module constructs image preference pairs (chosen image vs. diffusion-noised rejected image) and maximizes the log-likelihood ratio of the chosen response conditioned on the chosen image over the rejected image, directly penalizing over-reliance on language priors. The final loss is a weighted sum: L_CHiP = L_DPOv + L_DPOr + λ·L_DPOs + γ·L_POk, where λ and γ (<1) control the contribution of segment- and token-level terms respectively.

## Results

On Object HalBench, LLaVA-1.6+CHiP achieves a 4.9% response-level and 3.2% mention-level hallucination rate, reducing hallucinations by 55.5% relative points over DPO (11.0%/6.6%) and 65.3% over the base LLaVA (14.1%/7.4%). On AMBER, LLaVA+CHiP reduces the hallucination rate (Hal) to 24.5% and cognitive hallucination (Cog) to 1.6%, compared to 38.9% and 3.0% for DPO. Notably, LLaVA+CHiP outperforms GPT-4V on ObjHal (4.9% vs 13.6% R.) and AMBER Hal (24.5% vs 30.7%), while also improving general capabilities (e.g., +1.0 on MMMU-val, +1.3 on LLaVA-Wild) without degrading output length.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CHiP consistently outperforms standard DPO across all four hallucination benchmarks on both LLaVA-1.6 and Muffin backbones: on ObjHal, response-level hallucination drops from 11.0% to 4.9% (LLaVA) and 13.1% to 6.2% (Muffin); on AMBER, the Hal metric drops from 38.9% to 24.5% (LLaVA) and 26.5% to 17.6% (Muffin). Against GPT-4V, LLaVA+CHiP achieves lower hallucination rates on ObjHal (4.9% vs 13.6% R.) and AMBER (24.5% vs 30.7% Hal, 1.6% vs 2.6% Cog), though GPT-4V retains an edge on MMHal (31.3% vs 39.6% R.) and HallusionBench (65.3% vs 58.5% aA). Compared to prior preference-learning methods, CHiP surpasses RLHF-V (12.2% ObjHal R.) and HALVA (49.1% HallusionBench aA) on hallucination-specific metrics. The one metric where CHiP underperforms DPO is HallusionBench fA (Figure Accuracy) on LLaVA (26.0 vs 28.3), which the authors attribute to the multi-objective optimization diluting focus on logical consistency. On general capability benchmarks (MMMU, MMBench, ScienceQA, LLaVA-Wild), CHiP maintains or slightly improves over the base LLaVA, confirming that hallucination reduction does not come at the cost of general understanding.

## Relation to Prior Work

CHiP extends DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024) from text-only to multimodal settings, but departs from prior multimodal DPO work (Pi et al., 2024; Sarkar et al., 2024a) by arguing that simply swapping in multimodal preference pairs is insufficient. It incorporates the segment-level reward formulation from RLHF-V (Yu et al., 2024a) and the sequential KL divergence from token-level DPO (Zeng et al., 2024), but the key conceptual departure is the introduction of a visual preference optimization term that constructs image-level preference pairs (via diffusion noise) to explicitly align cross-modal representations. The paper positions itself as addressing a diagnosed failure mode of multimodal DPO—cross-modal semantic misalignment—rather than merely adding more preference data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The representation-distribution analysis providing a mechanistic explanation for why naive multimodal DPO fails (inability to align image and text embeddings) is a useful diagnostic that could inform future alignment work beyond hallucination. The finding that training the visual encoder during CHiP actually degrades performance (Table 3) is a practical caveat for practitioners combining preference optimization with full-model fine-tuning. Open questions include whether the 5k-sample RLHF-V dataset is sufficient for robust generalization, whether the diffusion-based rejection image construction is the optimal strategy at scale, and whether the hierarchical decomposition transfers to video or audio modalities.
