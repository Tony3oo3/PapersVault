## Problem

While large-scale benchmarking of image models against the human brain has revealed how architecture, training data, and task shape representational alignment, no comparable systematic study existed for video models. This paper asks what can be learned from comparing 99 deep video and image models to 10 human brains watching videos, specifically disentangling the effects of temporal modeling, classification task, architecture, and training dataset on alignment to fine-grained brain regions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251578/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes 3-second video clips (268×268 px, 15–30 fps) from the Moments in Time and Multi-Moments in Time datasets, processed through 99 publicly available models (47 video action-recognition, 41 image object-recognition, 11 image action-recognition) to extract per-layer feature vectors reduced to 100 PCA components. Brain data come from the Bold Moments Dataset: whole-brain 3T fMRI (2.5 mm isotropic, 1 s TR) from 10 subjects viewing 102 test videos over 10 repetitions, with 17 ROIs grouped into Early Visual Cortex, Ventral-Occipital, Dorsal, and Lateral streams. The output is a matrix of Spearman RSA scores (model layer × brain ROI, averaged across subjects), plus noise ceilings and significance tests for each comparison.

## Method

The core measurement is Representational Similarity Analysis: for each brain ROI and model layer, a Representational Dissimilarity Matrix is computed via pairwise Pearson correlation across the 102 test videos, and alignment is the Spearman correlation between the two RDMs, averaged over subjects. RSA is chosen over voxel-wise encoding (veRSA) because it preserves the model's native representational geometry without linear re-weighting. The benchmarking design varies one factor at a time while holding others constant: temporal modeling is isolated by comparing video models to image models trained on the same action-recognition task (Kinetics 400), architecture by contrasting CNNs and Transformers within the same task and dataset, and training dataset by comparing identical architectures trained on Kinetics 400 vs. Kinetics 710 vs. Something-Something-v2. Statistical significance is assessed via 1000-permutation null distributions and two-tailed sign tests with Bonferroni correction across group comparisons. A key finding is that temporal modeling drives alignment in early visual areas while action-space optimization drives alignment in late areas, and that model FLOPs negatively correlate with alignment in high-level lateral and dorsal stream regions.

## Results

Across 99 models and 10 human brains (102 test videos, 17 brain ROIs), video models significantly outperform image models in Early Visual Cortex (V1–V3), confirming temporal modeling as the critical factor for early-stage alignment. In late brain regions (e.g., LOC, OFA, FFA), action-recognition-trained models outperform object-recognition models, with top performers being MViT, CSN, and SlowFast in V2, and CSN, TimeSformer, and VideoSwin in LOC. A significant negative correlation between model FLOPs and RSA alignment is observed in six late ROIs of the Lateral and Dorsal Streams, indicating that computationally efficient networks produce more brain-like high-level representations. MViT_v2_S approaches the upper noise ceiling in V2 for a subset of subjects, capturing nearly all explainable variance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Video models (CSN, SlowFast, MViT, TimeSformer, VideoSwin) consistently outperform both image object-recognition and image action-recognition models in Early Visual Cortex, with the gap driven by temporal modeling rather than the classification objective. In late regions (LOC, OFA, FFA), image action-recognition models close much of the gap with video models, and in some cases (OFA classification layer) match or exceed video models, indicating that action-space optimization—not temporal modeling—dominates late-stage alignment. CNNs and Transformers are roughly equivalent in peak RSA score across ROIs, but differ sharply in layer-wise patterns: Transformers reach high early-cortex correlation at ~10% network depth while CNNs peak at ~60%, and CNNs show a clearer monotonic hierarchy toward late regions. Among training datasets, Kinetics 710 models perform nearly identically to Kinetics 400 models, but Something-Something-v2 models (which contain no faces) show significantly lower alignment in the face-selective FFA and OFA regions, demonstrating that dataset biases transfer directly to brain alignment in functionally selective areas. The negative FLOPs–alignment correlation is strongest in Lateral Stream (MT, STS, EBA) and Dorsal Stream (IPS) regions, with no consistent correlation found for model parameter count or top-1 accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the large-scale model-brain alignment benchmarking paradigm established by Conwell et al. (2022; 2024) on static images (NSD) to the video domain, using the Bold Moments fMRI dataset (Lahner et al., 2024). It builds on the RSA methodology (Kriegeskorte et al., 2008) and the Algonauts/BrainScore benchmarking infrastructure, but introduces a critical new axis of variation specific to video: disentangling temporal modeling from action-space optimization via image action-recognition controls. Prior video-specific comparisons were limited (Mineault et al., 2021 tested 5 models; Garcia et al., 2024 tested 8 video vs. 200 image models), making this the first systematic 99-model video benchmark with controlled factor manipulation across architecture, dataset, and task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that computationally expensive models are *less* aligned to high-level brain areas challenges the default assumption that scaling compute improves human-like representations, suggesting efficiency-oriented model design may be a path toward better brain alignment. The clean decomposition—temporal modeling drives early-area alignment while action-space optimization drives late-area alignment—provides actionable guidance for both neuroscience (identifying which mechanisms to model) and ML (which components to prioritize). Key limitations include reliance on a single fMRI dataset, volume-space analysis, BOLD's indirect and low-temporal-resolution nature, and coverage limited to library-available models, leaving open whether the FLOPs effect generalizes across architectures and whether hybrid models can achieve the best of both CNN and Transformer alignment patterns.
