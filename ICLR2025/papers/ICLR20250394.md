## Problem

Existing clean-label backdoor attacks poison training samples randomly, requiring high poisoning rates to achieve strong attack success rates, while prior selective-poisoning methods (Xia et al. 2022, Gao et al. 2023) demand access to the full multi-class training set and expensive surrogate model training. This paper studies the most constrained data-poisoning threat model, where the attacker controls only a single target class and has no knowledge of the victim model or other classes, and shows that intelligently selecting 'hard' samples to poison dramatically boosts clean-label attack effectiveness under this severe information limitation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes only the target class's training images (e.g., 5,000 images for CIFAR-10 class 0, 1,500 for GTSRB class 1) plus either a pre-trained feature extractor (VICReg, SimCLRv2, DINO, ResNet50, ViT, ConvNext) or an OOD dataset (TinyImageNet, 200 classes) as auxiliary signal, with no access to other classes, the victim architecture, or the training pipeline. It outputs a subset of m samples (5%, 10%, or 20% of the target class) flagged for trigger injection; these poisoned samples are then supplied to the victim alongside clean data from other sources, where the victim trains a standard model (ResNet18, VGG19, ViT, DeiT) on the combined dataset. Trigger patterns (BadNets checkerboard, Blended Hello Kitty at α=0.2, SIG sinusoidal with Δ=20, f=6) are applied to selected samples without altering their labels.

## Method

The core insight is that samples dissimilar to their class-mates are harder to learn, so injecting triggers into them forces the model to rely on the trigger as a shortcut rather than on existing visual features, yielding higher attack success rates at the same poisoning budget. The Pretrained strategy extracts features via a pre-trained model, computes a k-NN score (mean cosine distance to k=50 nearest neighbors within the target class), and selects the m highest-scoring samples—requiring no training and no extra data. The OOD strategy merges the target class with an OOD dataset, trains a surrogate model on this combined set, and ranks target-class samples by loss to identify hard ones, with two variants: single-class OOD (collapsing OOD into one class for a binary classifier) and multiple-class OOD (preserving OOD labels for an n+1-class classifier). Both strategies are trigger-agnostic and model-agnostic, and the pretrained strategy additionally avoids any training step, making it the most practical option under the constrained threat model.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with ResNet18 and 10% poisoning of the target class, the Pretrained strategy (self-supervised VICReg features) boosts SIG attack success rate from 60.54% (random) to 80.59%, and BadNets from 45.01% to 91.68%. On the more challenging GTSRB dataset (43 classes, imbalanced, high distribution shift from ImageNet), the same strategy improves SIG ASR from 48.07% to 57.12% and BadNets from 5.72% to 10.91%. The OOD strategy (training a surrogate on TinyImageNet + target class) achieves comparable gains, e.g., 80.76% ASR for SIG on CIFAR-10. Clean accuracy is preserved (94.5–94.8% on CIFAR-10, 98.7–99.3% on GTSRB), confirming the attacks remain stealthy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Gao et al. (2023), which requires access to the full multi-class training set and trains a surrogate model from scratch, the paper's Pretrained strategy (needing only the target class + a pre-trained feature extractor) achieves higher ASR on BadNets (91.68% vs. 87.62%) and Blended (66.45% vs. 58.20%) on CIFAR-10/ResNet18 at 10% poisoning, while matching on SIG (80.59% vs. 80.76%). This is a strict win in both effectiveness and practicality, since Gao's method is infeasible under the paper's single-class threat model. Against Narcissus (Zeng et al., 2023), the paper's hard-sample selection is orthogonal and complementary: combining Narcissus's optimized triggers with the proposed Pretrained selection boosts ASR from 56.16% (original Narcissus with random selection) to 89.65% on CIFAR-10/ResNet18, a ~35-point improvement. Conversely, poisoning easy samples with Narcissus triggers collapses ASR to 13.06%, underscoring that sample selection is the critical factor. Overall, the proposed strategies consistently outperform random selection by 20–40+ percentage points across all attacks, models, and datasets tested.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the selective data-poisoning line of work (Xia et al. 2022; Gao et al. 2023) by operating under a far more constrained threat model: the attacker has access only to a single target class and no knowledge of the victim model or other classes. Rather than training a surrogate on the full dataset (as prior selective-poisoning methods require), the paper repurposes pre-trained self-supervised features (VICReg, DINO, SimCLR) or OOD data to identify 'hard' samples via k-NN distance in feature space. The approach is orthogonal to trigger-optimization attacks like Narcissus (Zeng et al. 2023), which the paper demonstrates can be combined with its sample-selection strategy for further gains.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The results expose a practical and serious vulnerability in distributed data-collection pipelines (federated learning, multi-source training) where a single malicious data supplier can inject a backdoor with extremely limited information and a tiny poisoning budget. The finding that the attack remains stealthy against a broad suite of defenses (STRIP, Fine-pruning, Neural Cleanse, SPECTRE, ABL, CLP) underscores that current countermeasures are insufficient for this threat model. Open questions include developing targeted defenses for single-class poisoning, extending the framework to non-classification tasks (segmentation, LLM in-context learning), and understanding the mechanistic reasons why hard samples are preferentially susceptible to trigger-based shortcuts.
