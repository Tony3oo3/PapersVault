## Problem

Single-layer transformers can theoretically model first-order Markov chains, yet empirically they often get trapped in local minima corresponding to the unigram (marginal) distribution, while deeper models reliably converge to the correct bigram (Markov kernel). This stark depth-dependent failure mode lacks a systematic theoretical explanation, leaving open the question of precisely when and why single-layer transformers fail to learn even this simple sequential structure.

## Input / Output

The method takes a binary sequence {x_n} of length N drawn from a first-order Markov chain with transition kernel P(p,q) = [[1-p, p], [q, 1-q]] and initial distribution π(p,q), and produces a next-token prediction probability f_θ(x_1^n) = P_θ(x_{n+1}=1 | x_1^n) via a single-layer, single-head softmax attention transformer with ReLU feed-forward. The analytical output is a full characterization of the loss landscape: explicit constructions of global minima (achieving the entropy rate H(x_{n+1}|x_n)) and bad local minima (achieving the marginal entropy H(π)), along with the precise switching-factor threshold (p+q > 1) and weight-tying condition that govern their existence.

## Method

The paper introduces a framework for principled analysis of transformers via Markov chains, then characterizes the loss landscape of single-layer transformers through explicit parameter constructions and Hessian analysis. For the global minimum, they construct parameters where the attention and feed-forward layers are zeroed out, forcing the model to rely solely on the current symbol x_n via the skip connection, yielding the Markov kernel. For the bad local minimum (Theorem 2), they construct θ_π with e = a = 0 and bias b = log(p/q), making the model output the constant stationary probability π_1 regardless of input; the Hessian at this point has block-diagonal structure where the (b,a) block is positive-definite if and only if p+q > 1, establishing local minimality. Crucially, removing weight tying (Theorem 3) decouples the embedding e from the linear weight a, which introduces a negative-curvature direction in the Hessian and converts the bad local minimum into a saddle point. The key insight is that the interplay between the Markov switching factor p+q and the architectural choice of weight tying determines whether the unigram solution is a true local minimum or merely a saddle point, explaining the empirical observation that weight-tied single-layer models get stuck while non-tied or deeper models escape to the bigram.

## Results

The paper proves that for single-layer transformers trained on first-order binary Markov chains, the loss landscape contains global minima (bigram, loss = H(x_{n+1}|x_n)) and, when weight tying is used and the switching factor p+q > 1, bad local minima corresponding to the unigram (loss = H(π)). Specifically, for p=0.5, q=0.8 (p+q=1.3>1), the weight-tied model converges to the unigram with the linear weight |a|≈0.01 and bias b≈−0.4, making the prediction input-independent. When p+q<1 (e.g., p=0.2, q=0.3), the model always converges to the global minimum regardless of weight tying. The attention contribution is empirically negligible (ratio ≈0.01), confirming the model relies solely on the skip connection to capture the Markov kernel.

**Summary of loss landscape regimes for single-layer transformers on first-order Markov chains P(p,q).**

| Condition | Weight-Tied | Non-Weight-Tied | Depth ≥ 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| p+q > 1 (e.g., p=0.5, q=0.8) | Bad local min (unigram, loss = H(π)) | Saddle point → global min (bigram) | Global min (bigram) |
| p+q < 1 (e.g., p=0.2, q=0.3) | Global min (bigram) | Global min (bigram) | Global min (bigram) |
| p+q = 1 | Global min (unigram = bigram) | Global min | Global min |

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baselines; instead, it contrasts architectural and data regimes. The key comparison is between weight-tied and non-weight-tied single-layer transformers: when p+q>1, weight tying introduces a bad local minimum at the unigram (Thm. 2), while removing the tie converts it to a saddle point (Thm. 3), allowing gradient-based methods to escape to the global minimum. A second comparison is between single-layer and multi-layer (depth ≥ 2) transformers: deeper models reliably converge to the bigram global minimum regardless of weight tying or switching factor, first plateauing at H(π) before dropping to the entropy rate. For multi-state Markov chains (S=5), the switching threshold for getting stuck exceeds the binary case's 0.5, with the model still converging to the global minimum at p=0.6 with weight tying. These findings align with prior empirical observations (Chung et al., 2021) that weight tying can be detrimental, and with the induction-head literature (Olsson et al., 2022) showing that multi-layer transformers develop a different mechanism entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the growing line of work studying transformers on Markovian inputs (Nichani et al., 2024; Bietti et al., 2023; Edelman et al., 2024), which has primarily focused on multi-layer models and the emergence of induction heads for in-context learning. The key departure is shifting attention to single-layer transformers, which cannot form induction heads, and characterizing their loss landscape rather than their convergence dynamics. The framework also connects to optimization-theoretic analyses of transformers (Tarzanagh et al., 2023a,b) but focuses on the geometry of local optima rather than implicit biases of gradient descent. The finding that weight tying introduces bad local minima resonates with Chung et al. (2021), who observed drawbacks of embedding coupling in encoder-only models, but here the mechanism is made precise via Hessian analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled explanation for why single-layer transformers, despite being theoretically capable of modeling first-order Markov chains, empirically fail to do so — they get trapped at unigram local minima whose existence is governed by the switching factor and weight tying. This has practical implications: it suggests that weight tying, a standard practice in LLMs, can be actively harmful in certain data regimes, and that adding depth (even just one extra layer) resolves the issue. Open questions include extending the analysis to higher-order Markov chains, deriving explicit switching thresholds for multi-state settings, and understanding how depth affects loss curvature at bad local minima in deeper transformers.
