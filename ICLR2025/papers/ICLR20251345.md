## Problem

Empirical evidence suggests that neural network representations align as model scale and performance increase (the Platonic representation hypothesis), yet the various alignment metrics in use—kernel alignment, CKA, SVCCA, HSIC, mutual information—remain fragmented without unified theoretical connections. Crucially, no learning-theoretic guarantee links the degree of representation alignment to downstream task performance, leaving the practical significance of alignment unquantified.

## Input / Output

The framework takes two representation maps f_1, f_2 (from potentially different modalities or training objectives), their associated reproducing kernels K_1, K_2, a task loss, and a stitching map s_{1,2} (typically a linear layer to avoid introducing learned transformations). It produces population and empirical kernel alignment values via spectral decomposition of integral operators in RKHS, and—most importantly—closed-form upper bounds on stitching error and excess stitching risk expressed in terms of the misalignment quantity Ã_2 and the Lipschitz constant of the downstream head. The bounds are distributional (expectation over the joint data measure) rather than sample-specific.

## Method

The paper first unifies alignment notions by showing that CKA equals normalized HSIC, that distance alignment is equivalent to kernel alignment under unit-norm feature maps, and that MMD, mutual information, and Wasserstein distance all reduce to spectral quantities of the cross-covariance operator Σ_{1,2} in the RKHS. The central technical result (Theorem 1) bounds the stitching error R_{1,2}^{stitch} by R_2(h_2) + κ_2² Ã_2 + 2κ_2(Ã_2 R_2(h_2))^{1/2}, where κ_2 is the Lipschitz constant of the downstream head g_2 and Ã_2 = ‖I‖²_{η_2} − ‖C_{1,2}‖²_{η_2} captures the spectral misalignment between the two representations. For linear heads (Lemma 2), the stitching error collapses to the risk difference, making alignment the sole source of excess error. A corollary (Theorem 2) then bounds the risk gap between two models by the minimum over layers of the stitching bound, yielding the informal principle that alignment at matched depth bounds differences in generalization risk.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical contribution is Theorem 1, which bounds the stitching error between two models by the kernel alignment of their representations: R_{1,2}^{stitch}(S_{1,2}) ≤ R₂(h₂) + κ₂² Ã₂ + 2κ₂(Ã₂ R₂(h₂))^{1/2}, where Ã₂ = ‖I‖_{η₂} − ‖C_{1,2}‖_{η₂}² measures misalignment in the spectral basis. Lemma 2 shows that when the head functions g_q are linear (e.g., output layers of neural networks or RKHS predictors), the stitching error reduces exactly to the risk of the source model, i.e., R_{1,2}^{stitch}(S_{1,2}) = R₁(h₁). Theorem 2 further establishes that the risk difference between two models is bounded by the excess stitching risk, yielding the informal principle that 'alignment at similar depth bounds differences in risk.'

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical experiments, baselines, or comparison tables. Instead of benchmarking against competing methods, the paper unifies several existing alignment notions—Kernel Alignment (KA), Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA), Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion (HSIC), Distance Alignment (DA), Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD), Mutual Information (MI), and Wasserstein distance—and demonstrates their mathematical equivalences and relationships through spectral decompositions. Specifically, it shows that CKA equals normalized HSIC, that DA is equivalent to KA under the assumption d_q² = 2(1−K_q), and that MI and W₂ for Gaussian measures are expressible in terms of the singular values ρ_i of the cross-covariance operator. The paper positions kernel alignment as the central unifying object, with stitching providing a task-aware extension that connects representation similarity to generalization error bounds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies several previously fragmented notions of representation alignment—kernel alignment (Cristianini et al., 2001), CKA (Kornblith et al., 2019), HSIC (Gretton et al., 2005), distance alignment, and mutual information—under a common RKHS and spectral framework, showing their equivalence or tight relationships. It then bridges to the stitching literature (Lenc & Vedaldi, 2015; Bansal et al., 2021), which had been used empirically to compare representations but lacked theoretical grounding, by proving that stitching error is bounded by kernel alignment of the underlying representations. The work positions itself as a first step toward the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al., 2024) by providing the missing learning-theoretic guarantees that alignment correlates with generalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous connection between a representation-level metric (kernel alignment) and a task-level metric (stitching error), giving practitioners a theoretical justification for why aligned representations should transfer well across tasks and modalities. However, the results are limited to two modalities, linear stitching maps, and Lipschitz heads, and the paper contains no empirical validation—bounds are stated but not tightness-tested on real networks. Open questions include extending to nonlinear stitching, handling the full depth of deep networks beyond the 'stitching forward' assumption, and connecting the population-level bounds to finite-sample generalization in practice.
