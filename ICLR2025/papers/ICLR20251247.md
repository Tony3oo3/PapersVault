## Problem

Diffusion models generalize beyond their training set, yet the optimum of the denoising score matching (DSM) objective is the score of the training distribution itself, and the networks used are expressive enough to learn it accurately—so one would naively expect only memorization. No existing theory characterizes when and to what extent diffusion models generalize, or how the associated inductive biases depend on training details, model architecture, and data structure.

## Input / Output

The theoretical framework takes a discrete training distribution (M point masses in R^D), a forward/reverse diffusion process (e.g., VP-SDE or EDM), and a parameterized score estimator, and produces an analytic expression for the 'typical' learned distribution averaged over all training sample realizations. The key intermediate object is the V-kernel, a state- and time-dependent covariance structure that quantifies how ensemble variance in score estimates translates into stochastic deviations from deterministic PF-ODE dynamics, ultimately determining which gaps in the training distribution get filled.

## Method

The paper introduces a Martin-Siggia-Rose path integral representation of PF-ODE dynamics in which the learned score appears linearly in the exponent, making the ensemble average over training samples tractable and yielding an effective SDE whose noise term (the V-kernel) is governed by the covariance structure of the proxy score—the noisy DSM target that equals the true score only in expectation. The V-kernel is computed explicitly for three tractable architectures: a naive score estimator with interleaved training-sampling, expressive linear models, and lazy infinite-width neural networks in the NTK regime, revealing that generalization is driven by proxy score variance concentrated in boundary regions between training examples and modulated by feature-related inductive biases such as spectral bias. A semiclassical approximation in the small noise limit connects the V-kernel to the learned distribution as a curvature factor, showing that the typical learned distribution equals the noise-corrupted data distribution times a path-likelihood term that preferentially fills gaps between nearby training examples.

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no standard benchmark metrics. The central result (Proposition 3.1) shows that the typical learned distribution of a diffusion model is equivalent to sampling from an effective SDE whose noise term (the V-kernel) is determined by the ensemble covariance of score estimates. Propositions 4.1, 5.1, and 5.2 compute this V-kernel for three model classes (naive score estimator, expressive linear model, and lazy infinite-width NTK network), showing that generalization is driven by the proxy score covariance C(x,t), which is largest in boundary regions between training examples. Qualitative illustrations (Figs. 2–3) confirm a gap-filling inductive bias: the average learned distribution fills in gaps between training points, with the degree of filling controlled by the over/underparameterization ratio F/P and the time cutoff ε.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baselines; instead it compares three internal model classes to isolate the role of different inductive biases. The naive score estimator (Prop. 4.1) produces a V-kernel proportional to D_t C(x,t) D_t δ(t−t′), showing that even a model that perfectly memorizes training data still generalizes due to proxy score variance. The expressive linear model (Prop. 5.1) adds a feature-kernel modulation, making the effective SDE noisier where features take atypical values. The lazy NTK network (Prop. 5.2) interpolates between the two extremes as training time τ varies: at infinite τ it recovers the naive memorization result, while at finite τ the spectral bias of the NTK modulates the V-kernel. The paper also contrasts its 'generalization through variance' mechanism with kernel density estimation, arguing that boundary regions across different noise scales are smeared out in a way linked by PF-ODE dynamics, potentially yielding better generalization than convolving with a single kernel.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of diffusion model theory, which asks whether the true score can be recovered in the infinite-data limit (Bortoli 2022; Chen et al. 2023; Han et al. 2024), and instead asks the qualitatively different question of what distribution a model trained on finite data actually learns. It repurposes the Martin-Siggia-Rose path integral—a tool from statistical physics previously applied to random neural networks (Crisanti & Sompolinsky 2018) and learning dynamics (Bordelon & Pehlevan 2022, 2023)—to derive a path-integral representation of PF-ODE sampling that makes ensemble averaging tractable. The analysis of feature-related inductive biases builds directly on the NTK and spectral bias literature (Jacot et al. 2018; Canatar et al. 2021), while the central object of study, the proxy score covariance, connects to the DSM objective introduced by Vincent (2011) and Song & Ermon (2019). Concurrent work (Kamb & Ganguli 2024; Niedoba et al. 2025) addresses the same phenomenological questions but without the mechanistic path-integral framework developed here.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical framework that explains *why* diffusion models generalize beyond their training set, identifying the noisy proxy score's covariance structure as the root cause of a 'gap-filling' inductive bias. The six-factor decomposition (noisy objective, forward process, nonlinear score-dependence, model capacity, model features, training set structure) provides a structured vocabulary for understanding how training, sampling, architecture, and data interact. Key limitations include restriction to unconditional models, simplified architectures (linear and NTK-regime networks), and neglect of learning dynamics; extending the theory to conditional models, realistic U-Net architectures, and rich (non-lazy) learning regimes remains open. The finding that generalization can occur even in the complete absence of model-related inductive biases (Sec. 4) suggests the DSM objective itself is a sufficient source of useful regularization, which could inform future training objective design.
