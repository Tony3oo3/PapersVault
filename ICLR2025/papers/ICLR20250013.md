## Problem

Current spoken dialogue systems rely on ASR to transcribe speech into text before generating responses, discarding critical acoustic cues such as volume, speech rate, emphasis, non-verbal expressions, and background sounds. No existing benchmark comprehensively evaluates whether these systems can perceive and respond to such non-textual acoustic information within multi-turn dialogue scenarios, leaving a gap between what spoken dialogue systems should understand and what is actually measured.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250013/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark provides 4.5K multi-turn spoken dialogues (~42.56 hours, averaging 6.8 turns with a maximum of 10, most turns ~4 seconds long) where each dialogue is synthesized to control exactly one of 12 acoustic attributes across three categories (speaker info, paralinguistic info, environmental info). The evaluated system receives the full audio sequence of the dialogue and must produce a text response for the final turn that appropriately reflects the controlled attribute—e.g., using gendered salutations, requesting the speaker to slow down, or identifying background music—rather than merely answering the semantic content.

## Method

The VoxDialogue construction pipeline comprises five stages: (1) GPT-4o generates multi-turn dialogue scripts conditioned on specific acoustic scenarios; (2) attribute-specific TTS synthesis is applied—CosyVoice-300M-Instruct for gender/emotion/speed via style instructions, CosyVoice-300M-SFT with special tokens for stress and non-verbal expressions, CosyVoice-300M zero-shot with age-matched reference timbres for age, and edge-TTS for 7 accent variants; (3) automatic quality filtering removes samples with WER > 5% (Whisper) or timbre inconsistencies (speaker-diarization-3.1); (4) post-processing simulates volume (8× amplification or 50% reduction), fidelity (downsample to 4 kHz then resample to 16 kHz), and splices audio events or music as foreground/background; (5) human annotators verify naturalness and logical consistency. Evaluation combines four quantitative text metrics (BLEU, ROUGE-L, METEOR, BERTScore) with a GPT-4-based 1–5 qualitative scale that jointly scores contextual relevance and explicit attribute awareness in the response.

## Results

On the VoxDialogue benchmark (4.5K multi-turn dialogues, 12 acoustic attributes), the GPT-based metric (1–5 scale) reveals a clear split: FunAudioLLM (ASR-based) scores 4.32–4.61 on Speaker Information (Age, Gender, Language) and 4.09 on Emotion, but drops sharply to 1.79–1.92 on Volume, Speed, and Fidelity. Qwen2-Audio (direct) achieves the best scores on acoustic-dependent attributes—Volume (3.06), Speed (3.29), Fidelity (2.98), Audio (3.81), and Music (3.98)—while remaining competitive on Speaker Info (Gen 4.18, Lan 4.43). On quantitative metrics, Qwen2-Audio reaches 4.56 BLEU / 23.13 ROUGE-L / 22.11 METEOR / 88.00 BERTScore on Volume, versus FunAudioLLM's 4.20 / 8.28 / 10.19 / 84.87, confirming the advantage of direct audio processing for paralinguistic cues.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FunAudioLLM (ASR-based) dominates on context-sensitive attributes: it leads on Gender (4.39 vs. Qwen2-Audio's 4.18), Language (4.61 vs. 4.43), and Emotion (4.09 vs. 3.73), confirming that LLM-based text reasoning still outperforms direct audio models for semantic and speaker-identity tasks. However, FunAudioLLM collapses on purely acoustic attributes—scoring only 1.79 on Fidelity, 1.82 on Volume, and 1.92 on Speed—where Qwen2-Audio achieves 2.98, 3.06, and 3.29 respectively, a gap of over 1.0 point on the 5-point scale. Among direct systems, Qwen2-Audio is the clear leader, outperforming SALMONN (e.g., 3.98 vs. 2.23 on Music, 3.81 vs. 2.15 on Audio) and Qwen-Audio (e.g., 3.06 vs. 1.48 on Volume) by large margins. Audio-Flamingo and Qwen-Audio perform near the floor (mostly 1.0–1.5) across most attributes, indicating they are not yet suitable for spoken dialogue. SALMONN achieves a deceptively high BLEU on Stress (87.53 BERTScore) by repeating query text, yet its GPT-based score (3.86) is still below Qwen2-Audio (3.93), exposing a limitation of surface-level metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by identifying a gap between existing spoken language benchmarks (SUPERB, SLUE, LeBenchmark) that focus on coarse-grained semantic tasks, audio comprehension benchmarks (AirBench, Audio-Flamingo) that evaluate dialogue only in text modality, and task-oriented spoken dialogue datasets (SpokenWOZ) that lack fine-grained acoustic labels. It repurposes the TTS synthesis pipeline (CosyVoice) and LLM-based script generation (GPT-4o) to construct the first benchmark that systematically evaluates 12 acoustic dimensions within multi-turn spoken dialogue. The evaluation methodology draws on SD-EVAL's attribute-focused approach and AirBench's GPT-based scoring, but extends both to a true dialogue setting with 4.5K multi-turn samples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clear performance split—ASR-based systems dominating context-sensitive attributes while direct audio-language models (especially Qwen2-Audio) excelling on purely acoustic cues like volume and fidelity—reveals that no current system handles both dimensions simultaneously, pointing to a fundamental architectural gap. The finding that most direct audio-language models (Audio-Flamingo, Qwen-Audio) score near 1.0 on the GPT-based dialogue metric suggests they are trained for QA-style interactions and are poorly suited for conversational settings. Key limitations include reliance on synthetic TTS data rather than real conversations, evaluation on only five models, and the GPT-based metric's susceptibility to surface-level matching (as shown by SALMONN's inflated BLEU from query repetition). Open questions include whether a unified architecture can bridge the context-acoustics divide and how findings transfer to real-world, noisy, multi-speaker dialogue.
