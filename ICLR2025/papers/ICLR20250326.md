## Problem

Aligning LLMs with personalized preferences—shaped by cultural, educational, and political differences—requires high-quality datasets and substantial computational costs for policy optimization under existing RLHF/DPO frameworks. Moreover, most prior methods cannot generalize to preferences unseen during training, and many require training multiple policy models or reward models per preference dimension. The paper asks: can we align LLM outputs to diverse, potentially unseen, personalized preferences at inference time without any additional policy training?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250326/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, PAD takes a text instruction (up to 2,048 tokens) and a natural-language personalized preference description (e.g., 'expert and comprehensive', 'concise and factual') as input, and produces a single personalized text response (up to 128 tokens). The preference description is encoded by a value head into a weight vector w_p ∈ R^4096, which is then used to score the base model's top-k (k=10) token candidates at every decoding step. The base model (e.g., Llama-3-8B-SFT) remains frozen; only the PersRM and the decoding procedure are active, adding ~17 GB of memory and 2–3× the generation time of standard greedy decoding.

## Method

PAD decouples personalized preferences from MDP dynamics by expressing the token-level reward as R(p,s,a) = w_p^T φ(s,a), where φ(s,a) are successor features of the state-action pair and w_p is a preference-specific weight vector independent of the generation process. A Personalized Reward Model (PersRM) is trained in two stages on preference-annotated data (HelpSteer2, RiC, SafeRLHF): first, the backbone learns general features with w_p fixed to a unit vector; second, the backbone is frozen and a value head learns preference-specific w_p vectors. At decoding time, the PersRM computes an implicit Q* score for each of the base model's top-k candidates via w_p^T β log(π_θ*(a|s_t)/π_ref(a|s_t)), and the next token is selected by maximizing the sum of this reward and the base model's log-probability (Eq. 13). A theoretical bound (Theorem 1) guarantees that alignment quality for an unseen preference w_{n+1} degrades gracefully with its distance to the nearest trained preference, enabling generalization without retraining.

## Results

On the P-Soups dataset (3-dim alignment), PAD achieves an overall reward model score of 0.85 and a GPT-4 win rate of 78%, substantially outperforming the base model (0.32 RM score) and the best baseline MOD (0.74 RM, 74% GPT-4). On HelpSteer2 (3-dim), PAD reaches 0.70 overall RM and 69% GPT-4 win rate versus the base's 0.17 and the best baseline MOD's 0.56/59%. In the single-dimension setting, PAD achieves 84% GPT-4 win rate on P-Soups and 80% on HelpSteer2, surpassing all baselines in 7 out of 10 metrics on both datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PAD outperforms all nine baselines on the overall reward model score in the 3-dim setting on both datasets: 0.85 vs. the next-best MOD (0.74) on P-Soups, and 0.70 vs. MOD (0.56) on HelpSteer2. On GPT-4 win rate (3-dim, P-Soups), PAD achieves 78% versus MOD's 74%, MetaAligner's 71%, and MORLHF's 33%. In the single-dimension setting, PAD's 84% GPT-4 win rate on P-Soups exceeds Personalized Soups (81%) and Preference Prompting (78%), while its humor RM score of 1.50 dramatically surpasses all baselines (next best: MOD at 0.38). Training-based methods like MORLHF and MODPO show the weakest overall performance, with MORLHF scoring only 33% GPT-4 win rate on P-Soups 3-dim. PAD also generalizes to unseen preferences (expert, informative, creative), consistently outperforming both Preference Prompting and MetaAligner across all eight preference combinations in Figure 3.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes two distinct research threads: the DPO-style implicit Q-function formulation (Rafailov et al., 2024b), which shows that a language model's log-likelihood ratio encodes a reward signal, and the successor features framework from reinforcement learning (Barreto et al., 2017; Dayan, 1993), which decouples reward from MDP dynamics. By expressing the personalized Q-function as w_p^T · ψ(s,a) where ψ is a successor feature, the authors achieve a clean separation: the feature ψ is learned once under a fixed policy, while the preference vector w_p can be swapped at inference time without any policy retraining. This departs from prior multi-objective alignment work (MORLHF, MODPO, Personalized/Rewarded Soups) that requires training or merging multiple policy models, and from prior decoding-time alignment (ARGS, MOD, Controlled Decoding) that does not explicitly model personalized preference vectors or provide theoretical guarantees for generalization to unseen preferences.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PAD offers a practical path to personalized LLM alignment without the computational burden of policy retraining for each preference combination, and its model-agnostic property (a single PersRM transfers across Llama-3, Gemma, Mistral, and Llama-2) is a significant practical advantage. However, the 2–3× inference time increase and ~17 GB additional memory overhead limit its applicability in latency-sensitive or resource-constrained settings, and the authors acknowledge that reliable evaluation benchmarks for personalized alignment remain lacking. Open questions include finding compute-optimal decoding strategies, handling implicit preferences embedded in dialogue history rather than explicit prompts, and resolving inconsistencies between reward model scores and GPT-4 judgments that suggest the PersRM may not always capture true preference alignment.
