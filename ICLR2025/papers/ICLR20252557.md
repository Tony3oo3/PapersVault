## Problem

Continuous-time reinforcement learning (CT-RL) methods face a fundamental trade-off: state-of-the-art deep fitted value iteration (FVI) methods require millions of simulation episodes even for simple tasks like the inverted pendulum, while adaptive dynamic programming (ADP) methods, despite fifteen years of theoretical development, have failed to produce meaningful controllers beyond toy problems with known closed-form solutions. No existing CT-RL method has successfully addressed complex, mission-critical systems such as hypersonic vehicles with unstable and nonminimum-phase dynamics while simultaneously providing guarantees of learning convergence, solution optimality, and closed-loop stability.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a partially-known affine nonlinear dynamic model (f, g), a single state-action trajectory of roughly 10–30 samples collected under a user-designed reference command input r(t) for exploration, and quadratic cost matrices Q and R. It produces a nonlinear optimal policy μ*(x) computed in closed form from the value function gradient, along with the quadratic value function V*(x) = xᵀP*x. Unlike FVIs that train deep networks over millions of episodes, IPA requires only one trajectory from the actual physical process and iterates a low-dimensional linear regression (dimension n(n+1)/2) to converge in 5–10 algorithm iterations.

## Method

IPA constructs a quadratic critic V̂(x) = xᵀP_i x parameterized by quadratic bases, and approximates the integral reinforcement signal by replacing the nonlinear policy μ_i(x) with its first-order linearization −K_i x, yielding a tractable linear regression Θ_i c_i = Ξ_i for critic weight updates. The policy is then updated in closed form via μ_{i+1}(x) = −½ R⁻¹ gᵀ(x) ∂V̂/∂x, mirroring the Pontryagin minimum principle. The key theoretical result (Theorem 3.1) proves that IPA produces sequences {P_i}, {K_i} identical to Kleinman's algorithm to first order, thereby inheriting guaranteed monotone convergence (P* ≤ P_{i+1} ≤ P_i), optimality to second order, and H∞ stability robustness margins (‖S_u‖ ≤ 0 dB, ‖T_u‖ ≤ 2 dB). Exploration is achieved through a reference command input r(t) injected at the feedback loop rather than the plant-input probing noise standard in ADP, which the paper shows is heavily attenuated by closed-loop dynamics (by at least 25 dB for the HSV flightpath angle channel).

## Results

IPA achieves 6 orders of magnitude greater data efficiency (30 trajectory samples vs. 173 million for cFVI on HSV) and 3 orders of magnitude greater time efficiency (2.75 s vs. 4,600 s training time). On the HSV environment at 25% modeling error, IPA achieves an average cost of 32.45, far surpassing cFVI (63.80) and rFVI (74.15), while maintaining a 100% evaluation success rate across all seven HSV tasks at every uncertainty level. On the nominal HSV model, IPA also delivers the lowest cost (24.69 vs. 29.79 for cFVI and 33.37 for rFVI) with the fastest convergence (10 iterations vs. 50). The only environment where a baseline outperforms IPA on nominal cost is the pendulum, where cFVI achieves 8.99 vs. IPA's 12.12, though IPA provides faster swing-up response with no overshoot.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against cFVI (Lutter et al., 2021), IPA wins decisively on the HSV environment: at 25% modeling error, IPA's cost of 32.45 is roughly half that of cFVI (63.80), and IPA achieves 100% success on all seven HSV tasks while cFVI drops to 48% on strict velocity regulation (Task 3) and 4.2% on combined strict regulation (Task 6). On the pendulum, cFVI achieves lower nominal cost (8.99 vs. 12.12), but IPA generalizes better under uncertainty (5.77 vs. 7.01 at 25% error) and delivers faster, overshoot-free swing-up responses. Against rFVI (Lutter et al., 2023b), IPA dominates across all environments and uncertainty levels: on HSV at 25% error, IPA's cost (32.45) is more than twice as good as rFVI's (74.15), and rFVI's success rate on strict HSV tasks collapses to 5.7% (Task 4) and 1.95% (Task 6) while IPA maintains 100%. On the SOS benchmark, all three methods perform comparably (~0.50 cost, 100% success), confirming that the differentiation emerges on higher-order, unstable, nonminimum-phase systems like the HSV.

## Relation to Prior Work

IPA sits at the intersection of classical optimal control (Kleinman's 1968 iterative Riccati algorithm, Beard & McLain's generalized HJB framework) and data-driven reinforcement learning (temporal difference learning, integral reinforcement learning). It departs from the ADP lineage (Vrabie & Lewis, Vamvoudakis & Lewis, Jiang & Jiang) by replacing general basis-function critics with a fixed quadratic value structure that exactly matches the Q-R cost, and from the SOTA deep FVI methods (Lutter et al. 2021, 2023b) by being model-based rather than model-free, leveraging a known affine nonlinear dynamics model. The key conceptual move is showing that the IPA learning update, when the model is linearized, produces sequences identical to Kleinman's algorithm to first order, thereby inheriting its convergence and stability guarantees while operating on real trajectory data with unmodeled dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a 30-sample, 5-iteration quadratic method can match or exceed deep networks trained on 173M samples for continuous-time optimal control, with the gap widening dramatically under model uncertainty (31% vs. 114–122% cost degradation on HSVs). This is the first CT-RL method to successfully control a hypersonic vehicle with unstable, nonminimum-phase dynamics, a problem where FVIs require weeks of hyperparameter tuning and still fail on strict regulation tasks. The main limitation is the requirement for an affine nonlinear model structure, which excludes fully general nonlinear systems; whether the quadratic value assumption remains valid for higher-order or strongly nonlinear regimes remains open. The reference-command-input exploration strategy (replacing problematic plant-input probing noise) is a practical contribution applicable to other CT-RL algorithms.
