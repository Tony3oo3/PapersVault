## Problem

While the mechanisms of single-layer and two-layer transformers (e.g., induction heads, modular addition circuits) have been partially understood, the internal workings of deeper multi-layer transformers remain an open problem. This paper introduces the retrieval problem—a task that provably requires a minimum number of layers growing logarithmically with input size—as a controlled testbed to study how stacked attention heads form and emerge in multi-layer transformers.

## Input / Output

In the minimal formulation, input consists of N=4 interleaved retrieval chains (each with D+1 unique K=4-dimensional symbols drawn i.i.d. from a standard normal distribution) concatenated with rotary positional encodings, yielding N(2D+1) vectors of dimension 2K; output is N target vectors, one per query token. The implicit curriculum (IC) variant uses DN-dimensional targets containing all intermediate chain tokens, while the non-IC variant uses only the final K-dimensional target x_D. For LLM benchmarking, five natural-language formulations (equations, lives-with, kingdoms, functions, relatives) are used with 500 randomly generated questions each, requiring the model to output a single answer token.

## Method

The paper combines a theoretical lower-bound proof with extensive empirical reverse-engineering. Theoretically, under simplified attention assumptions (a position can attend only if it shares information with the target, and attending retrieves all information), it proves that at least log_3(2D) layers are needed because the reachable information set grows by at most a factor of 3 per layer. Empirically, the author trains small transformers (1–24 layers, 1–8 heads) on the minimal retrieval problem and discovers that the implicit curriculum is essential: without it, learning fails entirely even at D=3. By manually reverse-engineering attention maps and validating circuits through targeted ablations, the author uncovers that transformers solve the task via stacked 'retrieval heads'—an initial induction head followed by successive heads that reuse earlier ones to extend the retrieval chain. Crucially, these heads emerge sequentially during training (always induction head first, then one additional head per retrieval step), a process that the implicit curriculum enables by providing a natural sequence of increasing difficulty.

## Results

Large language models solve the equations retrieval formulation almost perfectly for D ≤ 5 retrieval steps, and achieve strong performance on the lives-with, kingdoms, functions, and relatives formulations at D = 5. Theoretically, at least log₃(2D) transformer layers are required to solve a retrieval problem with D steps (Theorem 1). Empirically, in the non-IC formulation, 100% of trained transformers fail to learn (final validation loss > 0.7), while the IC formulation enables successful learning with partial losses decreasing in strict order (x₁ before x₂, etc.) in 100% of runs. Reverse-engineered circuits in 12-layer transformers achieve validation MSE < 0.01, and a 24-layer Transformer D reaches MSE 0.031 (rising to 0.045 after ablation of the identified circuit).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, its central empirical comparison is between the implicit curriculum (IC) and non-IC formulations of the retrieval problem. The IC formulation is essential: in the non-IC setting, all 64 trained transformers (1–8 layers, D = 3) fail to learn, with 100% of runs ending above validation loss 0.7, whereas the IC formulation enables successful learning with clear layer-dependent difficulty scaling. A second internal comparison is between different circuit topologies discovered in three 12-layer transformers (A, B, C): all three use 4 attention heads despite only 3 being theoretically sufficient for D = 3, and their circuits differ substantially after the first (induction) head. The theoretical lower bound of log₃(2D) layers is confirmed empirically—later positions in the retrieval chain require more layers to learn, consistent with the logarithmic scaling predicted by Theorem 1.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the induction problem of Olsson et al. (2022) from a single retrieval step (D=1) to D arbitrary steps, creating the 'retrieval problem' that provably requires multi-layer transformers. It sits at the intersection of mechanistic interpretability for shallow transformers (single-layer: Nanda et al. 2023; two-layer: Olsson et al. 2022) and circuit analysis in large language models (Wang et al. 2022; Conmy et al. 2023), bridging the gap where multi-layer mechanisms remained unexplained. The implicit curriculum finding connects to Reddy (2023) on induction head emergence and Chan et al. (2022) on data distributional properties driving emergent in-context learning, while the emergent abilities discussion positions the work as a counterargument to Schaeffer et al. (2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical lower bound (log₃(2D) layers) and a concrete mechanistic explanation for why larger transformers can solve tasks smaller ones cannot, offering a principled account of emergent abilities that is not merely a metric artifact. The finding that an implicit curriculum is strictly necessary for learning (100% failure without it) and that heads emerge in a fixed sequential order has implications for how natural language data's difficulty gradient enables reasoning. Key open questions include whether the minimal-formulation circuits generalize to LLMs, the full training dynamics beyond the observed sequential emergence, and whether the conditional retrieval variant (DAG-structured) reveals fundamentally different mechanisms.
