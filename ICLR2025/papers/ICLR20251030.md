## Problem

Modeling rare failure events from observational data is difficult because failure datasets are extremely small (often just a handful of examples) while nominal data is abundant. Existing prior-regularization approaches require specifying a regularization strength β a priori, which is intractable when nominal and target distributions shift substantially, and standard bootstrapping ensembles provably fail to reduce data sensitivity in highly data-constrained regimes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251030/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a large nominal dataset D_0 (e.g., 2235 UAV trajectories or 9 days of flight data) and a much smaller target/failure dataset D_t (e.g., 58 points or 4 days), where each sample pairs noisy observations x with context variables y. It outputs a calibrated posterior q_φ(z; c*) over latent variables z, which can also serve as an anomaly score via the ELBO for classifying previously unseen failure datapoints. The key constraint is N_t ≪ N_0, with target sets as small as 4 samples in the air traffic benchmark.

## Method

CALNF trains a single conditional normalizing flow (neural spline flow) to map a low-dimensional label c to a posterior distribution, using one-hot labels for K random target subsamples and a zero label for the nominal data. A self-regularization term penalizes KL divergence between the K candidate target posteriors (justified by their i.i.d. construction), while a calibration step freezes the flow weights and optimizes a continuous label c* to select the best target posterior from the learned family. Theorem 1 provides an implicit W2 bound between the nominal and calibrated target posteriors proportional to L‖c*‖, giving data-adaptive regularization without a user-specified penalty. This differs from standard ensembles (which combine candidates via a fixed mixture) by performing a secondary optimization in label space, and from prior regularization by making the effective regularization strength emerge from the data rather than being set a priori.

## Results

CALNF achieves the best held-out ELBO (nats/dim) across all four benchmarks: 2D (-0.90±0.10), SWI (46.4×10⁻³±0.26), UAV (7.55±0.60), and ATC (-2.11×10⁻³±0.13). The largest margin is on the UAV problem, where CALNF scores 7.55 nats/dim versus 6.65 for the ensemble baseline and 3.26 for the best KL-regularized variant. On the SWI problem, CALNF is the only method that correctly recovers the target density profile (Fig. 3). For anomaly detection (Table 2), CALNF achieves AUROC 0.79±0.02 on SWI and 0.70±0.03 on UAV, outperforming all NF-AD variants.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CALNF outperforms all baselines on every benchmark. Against the KL-regularized prior (Asim et al., 2020), CALNF wins by a wide margin on UAV (7.55 vs. 3.26 best β) and 2D (-0.90 vs. -1.04 best β), while the KL baseline is highly sensitive to β, overfitting at low values and underfitting at high values. The W₂-regularized baseline (Finlay et al., 2020; Onken et al., 2021) performs substantially worse, with negative ELBO on UAV and ATC across all β values, indicating severe overfitting. The ensemble baseline (Choi et al., 2019) is the closest competitor, achieving -0.84 on 2D (slightly better than CALNF's -0.90) and 46.1 on SWI, but CALNF clearly wins on UAV (7.55 vs. 6.65) and ATC (-2.11 vs. -2.23). For anomaly detection (Table 2), CALNF beats the NF-AD variants (Gudovskiy et al., 2022; Kang et al., 2022; Rudolph et al., 2021) on both AUROC and AUCPR for SWI and UAV, with notably lower variance. On few-shot image generation (Table 3), CALNF with Glow (Kingma & Dhariwal, 2018) achieves -5.99 bits/dim on MNIST (slightly worse than vanilla Glow's -5.77) but dramatically outperforms it on CIFAR-10 (23.30 vs. 148.6 bits/dim), showing that the self-regularization is most beneficial in the more challenging, higher-dimensional setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

CALNF sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: prior-regularized variational inference for inverse problems (Asim et al., 2020) and bootstrapped ensemble methods (Efron, 1992). The paper explicitly positions itself as building on both, but departs from prior regularization by replacing the fixed KL/W2 penalty (which requires manual tuning of β) with a self-regularization mechanism: training a single conditional normalizing flow on random target subsamples and then optimizing a low-dimensional calibration label. It departs from standard bootstrapping by showing (Lemma 2) that ensembles are provably insufficient in the data-constrained regime, and instead learns a shared representation whose Lipschitz structure provides implicit W2 regularization (Theorem 1). The underlying flow architecture (neural spline flows) and ELBO objective are standard; the novelty is in the training procedure and the theoretical guarantee on the calibration label.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: learning a posterior from a handful of real-world failure observations when thousands of nominal examples exist, a setting common in safety-critical autonomous systems. The first-of-its-kind Southwest Airlines case study demonstrates that the learned posteriors can reveal hidden causal mechanisms (aircraft deficits cascading through the network) that were not apparent from public reports. Key limitations include the K+1× training cost, the assumption that failure and nominal distributions share structure (otherwise the implicit regularization is unhelpful), and the absence of a failure probability estimate. Open questions include whether the calibration approach extends to settings where the nominal and target distributions are radically different, and whether theoretical bounds on failure risk can be derived via large deviation theory.
