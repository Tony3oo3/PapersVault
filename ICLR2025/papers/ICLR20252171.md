## Problem

Existing analyses of GNN phenomena such as heterophily and oversmoothing rely heavily on graph topology and spectral properties, treating the graph as the primary source of structure. This paper asks whether node features alone—an intrinsic, graph-independent property—can provide a unified geometric explanation for why GNNs succeed or fail on different datasets, and whether the shape of the feature space fundamentally limits what any aggregation-based model can achieve.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252171/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The theoretical framework consumes node feature vectors in [0,1]^m and class labels, computing per-class geometric centroids and their convex hull (the feature centroid simplex) without requiring the graph structure. The practical tricks (random same-class edge addition, very early stopping, L2 feature normalization) are model-agnostic post-processing steps applied to any GNN's input graph or features, producing standard node class probability distributions. The analysis is graph-independent: the same feature centroid simplex is computed regardless of how the graph is constructed, making it a dataset-level diagnostic rather than a model-level one.

## Method

The paper introduces the feature centroid simplex Δ_e (the convex hull of per-class probabilistic centroids) and analyzes its shape using coarse geometry, specifically ε-quasi-isometry to model shapes: a regular simplex (easy dataset) versus a degenerate simplex (hard dataset). Theorem 1 shows that aggregated features concentrate near a mixed centroid determined by the neighborhood class distribution, with error bounds depending on the simplex's regularity; Theorem 2 proves a linear layer can map aggregated features to the neighborhood label distribution with error controlled by the simplex's condition number ρ(r₁,r₂,ε). Oversmoothing is reinterpreted as the simplex Δ_e^(t) shrinking to a single point under repeated multiplication by a stochastic transition matrix M derived from the graph's class-mixing coefficients. From these results, the paper derives three simple graph-independent tricks—randomly adding edges between same-class training nodes, stopping training after very few epochs, and normalizing feature vectors to unit norm—that reshape Δ_g toward a more regular form and improve classification on heterophilic datasets, with the normalization trick yielding up to 38.9% relative improvement on the Actor dataset.

## Results

The paper proposes two graph-independent tricks (random same-class edge addition and very early stopping) applied to six existing GNN base models. Across 48 comparisons on six heterophilic datasets, the tricks significantly improve performance in 34 instances. The best overall result is ACM-GCN-AE achieving 91.18% on Wisconsin, 90.81% on Texas, and 88.91% on Cornell. On the hardest datasets (Chameleon, Squirrel, Actor), improvements are modest or insignificant, which the authors attribute to near-degenerate feature centroid simplexes. An additional feature normalization trick further boosts Actor accuracy from 42.60% to 47.62% (a 38.9% relative gain over the base ACM-GCN).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new GNN architecture but rather model-agnostic tricks applied to six existing baselines. ACM-GCN (Luan et al., NeurIPS 2022) is the strongest base model, and the tricks push it to the best results on all six datasets (e.g., Texas 88.92→90.81%, Wisconsin 88.24→91.18%, Actor 34.39→42.60%). GCN (Kipf & Welling, ICLR 2017) and GAT (Veličković et al., ICLR 2018) show the largest absolute gains from the tricks on Texas, Cornell, and Wisconsin (e.g., GCN-I on Texas: 68.92→79.45%, a +10.5 pp improvement), confirming that simpler models benefit most from variance reduction. GraphCON (Rusch et al., ICML 2022) and CDE (Zhao et al., IJCAI 2023) see modest gains (1–3 pp), while GloGNN (Li et al., ICML 2022) shows minimal improvement, suggesting its global homophily mechanism already partially addresses the issues the tricks target. On Chameleon, Squirrel, and Actor, all models—including the trick-enhanced versions—show only marginal or insignificant improvements, which the authors explain via the near-degenerate shape of the feature centroid simplex (Corollary 1), indicating an intrinsic feature-level limitation that no aggregation-based method can overcome.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant graph-topology-centric paradigm in GNN research (graph spectral analysis for heterophily, Markovian analysis for oversmoothing) by introducing a feature-centric geometric framework. It builds on the GCN convolution as the analytical substrate but reframes the problem through the lens of coarse geometry: the convex hull of class centroids forms a 'feature centroid simplex' whose shape (regular vs. degenerate) is analyzed via quasi-isometry. The 'eventual GCN type' abstraction unifies attention models, rewiring methods, and neural diffusion models under a single geometric analysis, positioning the graph as merely determining the aggregation mechanism rather than as the primary object of study.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a unified explanation for heterophily, oversmoothing, and feature re-shuffling from a single geometric perspective, and identifies an intrinsic, graph-independent measure of dataset 'hardness' (simplex regularity) that no architecture change can overcome. This reframes the question from 'how do we design better GNNs?' to 'has the current approach reached the Bayesian error rate for this dataset?' The practical tricks (random same-class edge addition, very early stopping, feature normalization) are simple and model-agnostic, but their gains are modest (a few percentage points) and limited to datasets with more regular simplexes. Open questions include extending the analysis beyond 1-layer GCN to deeper architectures with non-linearities, and whether the simplex shape can be used as a diagnostic to select appropriate model families per dataset.
