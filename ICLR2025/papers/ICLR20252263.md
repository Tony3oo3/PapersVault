## Problem

Every CPWL function can be written as a difference of two convex CPWL functions, but no finite procedure is known to guarantee finding a decomposition with the minimum number of linear pieces. Existing constructions (hyperplane extension, local maxima decomposition) provably do not yield minimal decompositions, and a recent 2D construction by Tran & Wang (2024) is shown not to generalize to higher dimensions. The paper establishes a polyhedral-geometric foundation for understanding the structure of all decompositions and identifying minimal ones.

## Input / Output

The method takes a CPWL function f and a fixed regular polyhedral complex P that constrains where breakpoints of g and h may occur. The primary output is the decomposition polyhedron D_P(f), a polyhedron in the finite-dimensional space of balanced weight functions on P's codimension-1 faces, from which minimal decompositions are extracted by vertex enumeration. The framework additionally produces neural network representations with user-tunable depth-width tradeoffs and decompositions of set functions into differences of submodular functions via the Lovász extension.

## Method

The core insight is to parameterize CPWL functions via balanced weight functions on the codimension-1 faces of a fixed polyhedral complex (an isomorphism inherited from tropical geometry), reducing the decomposition problem to polyhedral geometry. The set of all decompositions f = g − h compatible with P forms a polyhedron D_P(f) that is the intersection of two shifted copies of the cone of nonnegative weight functions. The paper proves that irreducible decompositions correspond exactly to bounded faces of D_P(f), minimal decompositions must be vertices, and a unique vertex yields a unique minimal decomposition. Reduced decompositions are obtainable via linear programming with an objective in the interior of the dual cone. These structural results are applied to show that hyperplane functions (1-hidden-layer ReLU networks) and k-th order statistics admit unique minimal decompositions, and to construct neural networks with free depth-width tradeoffs for both convex and nonconvex CPWL functions.

## Results

The paper establishes that the set of decompositions of a CPWL function f into a difference of two convex CPWL functions compatible with a fixed polyhedral complex P forms a polyhedron D_P(f) that is the intersection of two shifted copies of the cone of convex functions (Theorem 3.5). Key structural results include: reduced (irreducible) decompositions correspond exactly to bounded faces of D_P(f) (Theorem 3.8), and minimal decompositions must be vertices (Theorem 3.13), yielding a finite enumeration procedure. For important function classes (hyperplane functions, k-th order statistics), a unique minimal decomposition exists with at most as many pieces as f itself (Proposition 3.15). For neural network constructions, Theorem 6.3 achieves a free depth-size tradeoff for convex CPWL functions: depth ⌈log₂(n+1)⌉ + ⌈log₂ r⌉ + 1 and size O(rs^{n+1}) for any r,s with rs ≥ k, interpolating between the low-depth/high-size bound of Hertrich et al. (2021) and the low-size/high-depth bound of Chen et al. (2022).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not present experimental benchmarks but provides theoretical comparisons against existing decomposition constructions. Kripfganz & Schulze's (1987) two main constructions — hyperplane extension (Construction B.1) and local maxima decomposition (Construction B.2) — are shown to fail to produce minimal decompositions for k-th order statistics (Proposition 4.1, Examples B.4–B.5): the unique minimal vertex (g*, h*) from Example A.3 is a non-trivial coarsening of both constructions' outputs, meaning neither yields a vertex of D_Q(f) for any regular complex Q. The paper further refutes Tran & Wang's (2024) proposed generalization of their 2D minimal decomposition construction to higher dimensions by providing an explicit counterexample (Example C.3) where the required 'gluing' of local 2D balancings into a global polytope is impossible in R³. The paper's framework subsumes both prior approaches by showing their suboptimality stems from the choice of underlying polyhedral complex rather than the construction method itself (Proposition B.3).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the tropical geometry framework (Maclagan & Sturmfels) for parameterizing CPWL functions via balanced weight functions on polyhedral complexes, and on the DC programming tradition of decomposing nonconvex functions into differences of convex ones (Melzer, Kripfganz & Schulze, Zalgaller, Wang). It departs from prior decomposition constructions (hyperplane extension, local maxima decomposition) by reframing the problem in polyhedral geometry: the set of all compatible decompositions is shown to be a polyhedron arising as the intersection of two shifted copies of the convex CPWL cone. The paper also directly engages with and refutes the 2D-to-higher-dimensional generalization proposed by Tran & Wang (2024), and extends the neural network construction results of Hertrich et al. (2021) and Chen et al. (2022) to a unified depth-width tradeoff.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic structural characterization of the space of CPWL decompositions, linking geometric properties of the decomposition polyhedron (vertices, bounded faces) to combinatorial properties of decompositions (minimality, irreducibility), and showing that linear programming over this polyhedron can find minimal decompositions. The refutation of Tran & Wang's proposed generalization is a significant negative result, leaving open whether any finite algorithm exists for minimal decompositions without fixing an underlying complex. The central open problem—whether polynomial-size decompositions always exist—connects to practical questions in DC programming, submodular optimization, and neural network construction, but the paper explicitly provides no algorithms or implementations, limiting near-term practical impact.
