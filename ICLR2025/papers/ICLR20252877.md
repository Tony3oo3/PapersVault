## Problem

Existing computational neuroscience models of color vision assume the color dimensionality is known a priori (e.g., hard-coded 3D RGB or LMS), sidestepping the fundamental question of how the cortex actually infers color dimensionality from raw optic nerve signals. This paper asks whether a self-supervised learning mechanism operating solely on optic nerve signal fluctuations during fixational eye drift can naturally discover the correct color dimensionality without any access to the visual scene or teacher signal.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252877/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The eye simulation engine takes 512×482×31-channel hyperspectral images and produces spatially distorted, noisy optic nerve signal streams (256×256 real-valued images) that entangle scene content with retinal invariants (cone positions, spectral types, lateral inhibition weights). The cortical model receives only this optic nerve signal stream as input and produces N-dimensional color vectors per pixel as internal percepts, from which color dimensionality is formally measured via CMF-SIM (Maxwellian color matching) and visually inspected via Neural Scope (a learnable N×3 projection to RGB).

## Method

The framework comprises a biophysical eye simulation engine (fixational drift, Stockman-Sharpe cone spectral sampling, foveation, DoG lateral inhibition, LIF spiking) and a cortical prediction model with three factorized functions: decoder Φ = Φ_P ∘ Φ_C ∘ Φ_W, translation Ω, and re-encoder Ψ = Ψ_W ∘ Ψ_C ∘ Ψ_P, trained to minimize prediction error of optic nerve signals at t+dt from signals at t. Learnable 'neural buckets' store cone positions P (via normalizing flow), spectral identities C, lateral inhibition weights W (Fourier-domain convolution), a U-Net demosaicing network D for spatial color interpolation, and motion estimation M. The key insight is that predicting signal fluctuations during eye motion forces the cortex to disentangle invariant retinal properties from scene content, and the intrinsic color dimensionality emerges as a geometric property of the learned representation—correctly yielding 1D, 2D, 3D, or 4D vision matching the number of cone types, validated by formal colorimetry.

## Results

The cortical model learns the correct color dimensionality K when the retina contains K cone types: 1D monochromacy (1 cone), 2D dichromacy (2 cones), 3D trichromacy (3 cones), and 4D tetrachromacy (4 cones), formally verified by CMF-SIM. Learning proceeds incrementally: monochromacy emerges at ~700 steps, dichromacy at ~1,200 steps, and full trichromacy converges after ~10⁶ steps. Gene therapy simulations successfully boost 2D→3D (squirrel monkey protanopia) and 3D→4D (human trichromacy) color dimensionality after cortical re-learning. CMF-SIM outputs for the trichromat model closely align with human psychophysical color-matching data from Stiles & Burch (1955).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external ML methods but instead validates its framework against three internal baselines and established vision science data. (1) A no-learning baseline (identity decoder, raw ONS as percept) fails entirely: the base perceptual error between the same color at two retinal locations exceeds the energy of the percept itself, making color matching procedurally impossible. (2) A simplified baseline using raw cone activations (omitting lateral inhibition and foveation) yields only 1D color per CMF-SIM, demonstrating that cortical processing is necessary even on spectrally-encoded signals. (3) An ablation removing the demosaicking U-Net (neural bucket D) also collapses to 1D, confirming spatial color interpolation is essential. Against human psychophysical data (Stiles & Burch, 1955), the simulated CMF curves for the trichromat model show close alignment. Against known color vision deficiency literature (Brettel et al., 1997; Judd, 1948; Alpern et al., 1983), the NS visualizations of the three dichromat variants reproduce the correct hue-shift patterns (blue-yellow for protanopia/deuteranopia, teal-pink for tritanopia).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of computational neuroscience and self-supervised learning. It departs from the dominant paradigm in computational vision science that hard-codes color dimensionality to 3 (RGB, LMS, or cone-opponent space) and instead treats color dimensionality as an emergent property to be inferred purely from optic nerve signal fluctuations. The retinal simulation builds on the textbook midget private-line pathway model (Rodieck, 1998) with established parameters (Stockman-Sharpe spectral sensitivities, Curcio cell densities, DoG lateral inhibition), while the cortical learning mechanism draws on predictive coding, sensorimotor contingency theory, and slow feature analysis. The closest prior work (Brainard et al., 2008; Benson et al., 2014) demonstrated that retinal properties can be statistically inferred from sensory signals, but this paper provides the first complete computational realization with a specific self-supervised learning objective that achieves full disentanglement and correct color dimensionality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This framework provides the first existence proof that a simple self-supervised prediction task (predict next optic nerve signal during fixational eye drift) is sufficient to produce color vision of the correct dimensionality, challenging the assumption that color perception requires hardwired neural circuits. The simulation's consistency with multiple vision science phenomena (specific dichromatic hue spaces, gene therapy boosting in squirrel monkeys, potential 3D→4D enhancement) suggests a generalizable principle: perception may emerge from disentangling sensory signals from sensor invariants via predictive learning. Key limitations include reliance on backpropagation (not bio-plausible), restriction to the foveal midget pathway, and absence of higher-level color processing (chromatic adaptation, perceptual nonuniformity). Open questions include whether this learning actually occurs in biological cortex, whether it generalizes to other sensory modalities, and whether more bio-plausible learning rules (e.g., forward-forward algorithm) can achieve the same results.
