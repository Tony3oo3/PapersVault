## Problem

Random subspace methods for nonconvex optimization have improved iteration complexity and reduced computational cost by restricting search to low-dimensional subspaces, but existing algorithms either achieve only O(ε^{-2}) global convergence, fail to guarantee convergence to second-order stationary points, or lack local convergence rate guarantees. The paper asks: to what extent can theoretical guarantees be improved by performing gradient and Hessian operations in a low-dimensional random subspace, and can a trust-region-based subspace method achieve the best-in-class O(ε^{-3/2}) rate with both global and local convergence guarantees?

## Input / Output

The algorithm requires access to the objective function f only through gradient evaluations and Hessian-vector products (never the full Hessian matrix), along with a subspace dimension s (typically s = O(log n) ≪ n) and two scalar parameters δ and Δ set from the target accuracy ε. It outputs an ε-approximate first-order stationary point satisfying ‖∇f(x̂)‖ ≤ O(ε) in O(ε^{-3/2}) iterations, or under a rank-deficiency assumption on the Hessian at the output, an ε-approximate second-order stationary point with λ_min(∇²f(x̂)) ≥ Ω(-√ε). A notable practical advantage is that the restricted Hessian P∇²f(x)P^T ∈ R^{s×s} never needs to be explicitly formed—only Hessian-vector products are required, keeping space complexity at O(sn) rather than O(n²).

## Method

RSHTR is a random subspace variant of the Homogeneous Second-Order Descent Method (HSODM). At each iteration, a random Gaussian matrix P_k ∈ R^{s×n} projects the problem onto an s-dimensional subspace, and the descent direction is obtained by solving a homogenized trust region subproblem—minimizing [ṽ; t]^T [H̃_k, g̃_k; g̃_k^T, -δ] [ṽ; t] over ‖[ṽ; t]‖ ≤ 1—via minimum eigenvalue computation (e.g., Lanczos tridiagonalization). The key insight is that this homogenized subproblem reduces to finding the leftmost eigenvector of an (s+1)×(s+1) matrix, making it naturally amenable to dimension reduction and solvable without ever forming the full restricted Hessian. The step size is fixed at η_k = Δ/‖d_k‖, and the algorithm switches from a global mode (with δ > 0) to a local mode (δ = 0) once ‖d_k‖ ≤ Δ, yielding local linear convergence in general and local quadratic convergence when f has low effective dimensionality (rank(Π) ≤ s) and is strongly convex within its effective subspace.

## Results

RSHTR achieves the best theoretical guarantees among random subspace methods for nonconvex optimization: global convergence to an ε-approximate first-order stationary point in O(ε^{-3/2}) iterations, convergence to an ε-approximate second-order stationary point under rank-deficient Hessian conditions in O(ε^{-3/2}) iterations, and local quadratic convergence under low effective dimensionality. The total computational complexity is O(ε^{-3/2}s^{5/4}n^{7/4}) with space complexity O(sn), both strictly better than the full-space HSODM's O(ε^{-3/2}n^2) time and O(n^2) space. Numerical experiments on 8 problems (dimensions up to 416,682) confirm that RSHTR consistently outperforms all compared baselines in function value vs. computation time, with a notable advantage in rapidly escaping flat regions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RSHTR dominates HSODM (Zhang et al., 2022) on both theory and practice: it reduces total computational complexity from O(ε^{-3/2}n^2) to O(ε^{-3/2}s^{5/4}n^{7/4}) and space complexity from O(n^2) to O(sn), and on the Low Effective Rosenbrock problem (n=10,000, r=50), HSODM fails to complete even a single step while RSHTR converges rapidly. Against RSRN (Fuji et al., 2022), RSHTR improves the global convergence rate from O(ε^{-2}) to O(ε^{-3/2}) and achieves local quadratic convergence (under low effective dimensionality) versus RSRN's linear rate, and consistently outperforms RSRN in wall-clock time across all 8 experimental problems. Compared to RSGD (Kozak et al., 2021), RSGD initially shows faster function value decrease due to not requiring Hessian-vector products, but RSHTR quickly surpasses it on all datasets (MovieLens, News20, RCV1, SCOTUS, MNIST, CIFAR-10), with the gap widening as optimization progresses. The key practical advantage of RSHTR is its rapid escape from flat regions, attributed to the combination of second-order information, homogenized subproblem structure, and random subspace projection.

## Relation to Prior Work

RSHTR is a direct random subspace variant of the Homogeneous Second-Order Descent Method (HSODM, Zhang et al. 2022), which reformulates the trust region subproblem as a minimum eigenvalue problem. This eigenvalue formulation is the key enabler: unlike classical trust region methods whose intricate update rules resist dimension reduction, HSODM's structure allows the subproblem to be solved in a randomly projected s-dimensional space using Lanczos iteration. Among prior random subspace methods for nonconvex optimization (Shao 2022, Zhao et al. 2024, Fuji et al. 2022), RSHTR achieves the best combination of global rate O(ε^{-3/2}), second-order stationary point guarantees under rank deficiency, and local quadratic convergence under low effective dimensionality, while requiring only s=Ω(log n) versus s=Ω(n) for Zhao et al.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The rank-deficient Hessian assumption (Assumption 2) and low effective dimensionality assumption (Assumption 4) are well-motivated by the observed low-rank structure of neural network Hessians, making the theoretical guarantees practically relevant for modern ML optimization. The method's space complexity of O(sn) versus O(n²) for full-space HSODM, combined with the absence of a need to form the restricted Hessian explicitly, makes it attractive for very high-dimensional problems. Open questions include whether the fixed parameter δ can be made adaptive, whether stochastic or distributed variants are possible, and whether the approach extends to the growing family of HSODM variants (stochastic, constrained, generalized trust region). The practical limitation is that Hessian-vector products must be available, restricting applicability to problems where second-order information is computable.
