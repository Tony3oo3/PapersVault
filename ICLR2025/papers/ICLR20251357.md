## Problem

All-atom geometric GNNs have revolutionized molecular force field prediction but remain largely unexplored for protein modeling due to computational constraints, and their scaling behavior is poorly characterized. This paper asks whether pre-trained Geom-GNNs can serve as effective zero-shot transfer learners for protein systems and whether they follow the power-law scaling principles observed in language and vision domains.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251357/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes atomic coordinates and atomic numbers as graph inputs, but for protein tasks the full structure is decomposed into local sliding windows of nearest-neighbor residues (e.g., 3/2 residues on each side) to produce per-residue graph embeddings, avoiding over-smoothing from treating the entire protein as a single graph. These residue-level tokens are then mixed via MLP-Mixer or self-attention before feeding into task-specific heads. Outputs range from VAMP-2 scores and Koopman singular vectors for kinetic modeling, to fold class labels, to scalar molecular property values (dipole moment, polarizability, HOMO/LUMO energies, force vectors).

## Method

The core approach uses two equivariant message-passing architectures—Equivariant Transformer (ET) and ViSNet (vector-scalar interactive message passing)—pre-trained with a denoising objective that adds scaled Gaussian noise to atomic coordinates and trains the network to predict the perturbation. After pre-training on equilibrium (PCQM4Mv2) or non-equilibrium (OrbNet-Denali) small-molecule datasets, the denoising head is discarded and the last message-passing layer activations are extracted as frozen graph-level embeddings for zero-shot transfer. For kinetic modeling, these embeddings feed a VAMPNet head optimized with the VAMP-2 score; for fold classification, they are concatenated into a protein-specific GNN (ProNet). The paper systematically varies width, depth, aspect ratio, and radius cutoff to characterize scaling, finding that Geom-GNNs deviate from power-law scaling with early saturation, and that deeper-narrower aspect ratios and a ~5 Å cutoff radius are optimal.

## Results

On the VAMP-2 kinetic modeling task, pre-trained ViSNet graph embeddings (64-dim, self-attention token mixing) achieve VAMP-2 scores of 4.71 (Ala2), 7.00 (Pentapeptide), and 8.49 (λ6-85), outperforming the pairwise-distance VAMPNet baseline (Mardt et al.) by 20.4%, 50.0%, and 15.1% respectively. On Scaffold-QM9, pre-trained ViSNet (384-dim) achieves the best MAE of 23.2 mD for dipole moment and 77.0 meV for HOMO-LUMO gap, compared to 34.3 mD and 96.2 meV for the from-scratch counterpart. Scaling analysis reveals no power-law behavior: pre-training loss saturates beyond ~10⁷ parameters, and supervised/unsupervised downstream tasks show no predictable scaling with model size or dataset size.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the VAMPNet model from Mardt et al. (2018), which uses heavy-atom pairwise distances as input features. The pre-trained ViSNet graph embeddings with MLP-Mixer token mixing outperform this baseline by 20.4% on Ala2 (4.71 vs. 3.92), 50.0% on Pentapeptide (7.00 vs. 5.14), and 15.1% on λ6-85 (8.49 vs. 7.40) in VAMP-2 score. The improvement is most pronounced for the mid-sized Pentapeptide system, where token mixing alleviates early-stage information compression; for the larger λ6-85 protein, gains are more modest, suggesting that treating the entire protein as a single graph introduces over-smoothing that limits local feature quality. On supervised tasks (Scaffold-QM9, xxMD-Temporal), pre-trained models consistently outperform from-scratch counterparts of identical architecture, with the gap widening under out-of-distribution splits. However, the paper finds no predictable scaling law: increasing model width beyond 256–384 dimensions yields diminishing or inconsistent returns, and pre-training benefits can even reverse at larger downstream dataset sizes (parameter ossification beyond ~10³ samples).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the denoising pre-training paradigm for equivariant GNNs (Zaidi et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023) by repurposing the resulting embeddings as frozen, zero-shot geometric descriptors for protein systems, rather than fine-tuning them for specific force-field or property tasks. It positions itself against the emerging literature on neural scaling laws in molecular and graph domains (Frey et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024), which has established power-law relationships for supervised molecular property prediction, and asks whether these laws generalize to self-supervised pre-training and unsupervised kinetic modeling. The VAMPNet objective (Mardt et al., 2018) and protein-specific GNNs like ProNet (Wang et al., 2022) serve as downstream evaluation frameworks, but the key departure is treating pre-trained all-atom graph embeddings as plug-and-play features that replace hand-crafted descriptors (pairwise distances, rotamer angles) without any task-specific retraining of the backbone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that Geom-GNNs exhibit early saturation and no predictable power-law scaling across self-supervised, supervised, and unsupervised settings—challenges the assumption that molecular representation learning can be guided by the same scaling principles as language or vision models, with direct implications for compute allocation and architecture design in molecular ML. Practically, the demonstration that pre-trained ViSNet embeddings achieve up to 50% VAMP-2 score improvements over pairwise-distance baselines for kinetic modeling offers a scalable pathway to protein dynamics analysis without training graph representations on-the-fly. Key limitations include the treatment of entire proteins as single graphs (causing over-smoothing), the restriction to denoising as the sole pre-training objective, and the modest 15% gain on the largest system (λ6-85), suggesting that global relationship modeling from local tokens remains an open challenge. Future work should explore topology-aware pre-training, better residue-level localization of embeddings, and co-pre-training for protein design and PPI tasks.
