## Problem

Current LLM safety evaluations measure whether attacks force explicitly impermissible outputs (jailbreak success rate), but miss a more fundamental compositional threat: an adversary can decompose a malicious question into individually benign subquestions, collect the safe responses, and infer the harmful answer without ever triggering input or output filters. The paper argues that robustness to adversarial prompts is fundamentally insufficient for safety because knowledge is dual-use and compositional, and introduces an information-theoretic framework to formalize this 'inferential adversary' threat model and the defenses it demands.

## Input / Output

The attack consumes a malicious multiple-choice query (curated from WMDP-Bio/Chem/Cyber where the question itself would trigger safety filters), interaction parameters (k=2 rounds × m=3 subquestions, capped at 6 total interactions), and three few-shot behavioral templates guiding decomposition, extraction, and aggregation. The output is the adversary's updated belief distribution over answer choices, scored as Impermissible Information Leakage (IIL) — a posterior-weighted pointwise mutual information that calibrates the information gain by the adversary's final confidence, distinguishing it from raw PMI by penalizing low-confidence gains less heavily.

## Method

The Decomposition Attack runs a three-phase loop: an adversarial LLM generates m self-contained benign subquestions per round that collectively answer the malicious query, each subquestion is submitted to the victim LLM through its input/output filters and relevant information is extracted into a growing knowledge pile, and finally all subquestion-answer pairs are aggregated to produce the final answer. The paper formally distinguishes security adversaries (who force specific impermissible outputs, evaluated dichotomously) from inferential adversaries (who gain information, evaluated by IIL), and empirically shows DAs outperform the PAIR jailbreak baseline while triggering fewer safety filters across four victim models including unlearning-based defenses. To defend against inferential adversaries, the paper introduces Information Censorship — a bound on expected impermissible information leakage over k interactions — instantiated via a randomized response mechanism that returns safe strings with probability (1−t_ε) and victim responses with probability t_ε, where t_ε is derived from the leakage budget. The paper proves a non-adaptive composition theorem bounding multi-interaction leakage by kε plus a dependency term, and establishes safety-utility tradeoffs showing that any effective information censorship mechanism necessarily reduces utility for benign inferential users.

## Results

Across all curated WMDP questions (126 Bio, 283 Chem, 335 Cyber), Decomposition Attacks (DAs) consistently outperform the PAIR jailbreaking baseline in extracting impermissible information, measured by Impermissible Information Leakage (IIL), while triggering input/output safety filters less frequently. DAs maintain their advantage even against victim models with stronger defenses such as DeRTa (decoupled refusal training) and Llama RMU (unlearning), where PAIR's performance degrades more sharply. In execution time, DAs also outperform PAIR, often completing information extraction in fewer than the maximum 6 allocated interactions. The Llama RMU unlearning model reduces WMDP accuracy from 0.660 to 0.375 (base vs. unlearned) while retaining 0.760 MMLU (vs. 0.824 base), yet DAs still extract meaningful information from it.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline compared is PAIR (Chao et al., 2023), a black-box jailbreaking method that iteratively refines adversarial prompts to force the victim LLM into producing an explicitly harmful output. DAs outperform PAIR on the primary metric of average IIL across all four victim models (Meta-Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct, DeRTa, and Llama RMU) and all three adversary models (Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, Meta-Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Mistral-Nemo-Instruct-2407). DAs also achieve higher attack success rates (fraction of times the adversary changes from a wrong to a correct answer) and trigger safety filters (PromptGuard, Llama-Guard, ShieldGemma) less frequently than PAIR, making them harder to detect under user monitoring. Critically, DAs operate in distinct context windows and do not rely on priming the victim model, so they are not vulnerable to output-censorship defenses that would block multi-turn jailbreaks. PAIR can still succeed in some cases by recontextualizing the problem (e.g., reframing a virulence-increase question as virulence-decrease), but its success is more binary and filter-dependent compared to DAs' gradual information accumulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes question decomposition (Radhakrishnan et al., 2023) as an attack primitive and adapts randomized response mechanisms from differential privacy (Mangat, 1994) as a theoretical defense, while drawing on privacy-utility tradeoff theory (Zamani et al., 2023) to formalize safety-utility constraints. Conceptually, it departs sharply from the dominant jailbreak/robustness framing (PAIR, Crescendo, many-shot jailbreaking) by arguing that preventing explicit harmful outputs is insufficient when knowledge is compositional and dual-use. The paper explicitly positions inferential adversaries as a distinct threat model from security adversaries, drawing a direct parallel to how differential privacy was needed to address compositional privacy attacks that k-anonymity could not handle.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper makes a compelling case that current LLM safety evaluations (attack success rate on single outputs) systematically understate deployment risk by ignoring information accumulated across multiple individually-safe interactions. The information-theoretic framework (IIL, Exp-IIL, ε-ICM) provides a principled language for discussing safety guarantees, though the proposed randomized response defense is clearly impractical for deployed LLMs. Key open questions include: how to implement deployable information censorship for autoregressive models, whether the multiple-choice WMDP evaluation generalizes to open-ended harmful knowledge extraction, and how to handle adaptive adversaries who can condition subquestions on prior responses. The safety-utility tradeoff theorem is an honest and important acknowledgment that effective defenses will necessarily reduce model usefulness for benign users.
