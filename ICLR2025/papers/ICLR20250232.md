## Problem

RLHF fine-tuning of LLMs relies on centralized preference data, but real-world users are reluctant to share sensitive prompts and preferences due to privacy regulations (GDPR, CCPA). No prior work has enabled RLHF in a federated learning setting, and naive federated approaches face two critical challenges: excessive computation from training regression-based reward models on edge devices, and preference heterogeneity combined with reward hacking that degrades LLM performance over training iterations.

## Input / Output

Each FL client holds a local preference dataset of (instruction, preferred completion, dispreferred completion) triples built from daily LLM interactions, which never leaves the device. The server holds only unlabeled prompts and the aggregated binary selector; it generates n completions per prompt, forms all C(n,2) pairs, and uses the selector to assign binary preference labels, constructing a synthetic DPO training set. The binary selector itself takes an instruction plus a pair of completions and outputs a single binary label (A or B), replacing the traditional scalar reward model and eliminating the need to retain two computation graphs during optimization.

## Method

FedBis replaces the expensive regression reward model with a lightweight binary selector trained via FedAvg: each client locally optimizes the selector with cross-entropy loss on its preference pairs, and the server aggregates via weighted averaging. The aggregated selector then labels synthetic preference data generated by the base LLM, which is used for DPO fine-tuning on the server. FedBiscuit extends this by training U (odd) binary selectors in two phases: a warm-up phase where each selector is trained sequentially on random client subsets, followed by a clustered FL phase where clients are periodically regrouped into balanced disjoint clusters based on validation loss (greedy clustering with capacity capping), and each cluster trains one selector. A new aggregation rule (Equation 6) accounts for fluctuating cluster sizes across rounds, and majority voting across all U selectors determines the final preference label, making it harder for the LLM to exploit a single selector's weaknesses (reward hacking) while clustering reduces preference heterogeneity.

## Results

On the summarization task (win rate vs. human-written TL;DR summaries via Auto-J), FedBis with a Qwen-2-0.5B selector achieves 86.69% for Gemma-2B and 89.63% for LLaMA-2-7B, surpassing the raw models (67.27% and 76.79%) by 19.4 and 12.8 percentage points respectively. On the QA task (length-controlled win rate via AlpacaEval 2.0), FedBiscuit (U=3) with SafeRLHF prompts reaches 5.63% for LLaMA-2-7B and FedBis reaches 4.58% for Gemma-2B, outperforming the raw models (4.80% and 2.40%). Across both tasks, all proposed methods improve over the raw model by at least 6% on summarization and 1.2–2.2% on QA, while both baselines (FedAvg, FedDPO) degrade or only marginally improve.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017) performs dramatically worse than the raw model on both tasks: on summarization it drops from 67.27% to 28.66% (Gemma-2B) and from 76.79% to 28.23% (LLaMA-2-7B), and on QA it falls below the raw model (1.88% vs. 2.40% for Gemma; 3.93% vs. 4.80% for LLaMA-2). FedDPO (Ye et al., 2024, OpenFedLLM) shows mixed results: it slightly exceeds the raw model on LLaMA-2 summarization (77.02% vs. 76.79%) and QA (4.98% vs. 4.80%), but substantially underperforms on Gemma-2B summarization (49.03% vs. 67.27%). FedBis and FedBiscuit consistently outperform both baselines by large margins—e.g., FedBis with Qwen-2 achieves 86.69% vs. FedDPO's 49.03% and FedAvg's 28.66% on Gemma-2B summarization. The paper attributes FedDPO's weakness to a distribution mismatch: client preference data contains human-written completions that differ stylistically from model outputs, confusing the DPO objective, whereas FedBis/FedBiscuit generate synthetic preference pairs from the model's own outputs, making the alignment signal more actionable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RLHF pipeline (reward modeling + DPO fine-tuning) into a federated learning setting, building on FedAvg as the aggregation backbone and DPO as the alignment objective. It departs from prior federated LLM fine-tuning work (e.g., OpenFedLLM's FedDPO) by replacing the expensive regression-based reward model with a lightweight binary selector trained via cross-entropy, and by introducing clustered FL with majority voting across multiple selectors to handle preference heterogeneity and reward hacking. The clustered FL mechanism draws on Sattler et al.'s partitioned training paradigm, adapted here with a balanced reassignment step to prevent cluster imbalance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first federated RLHF benchmark, providing a concrete evaluation framework for privacy-preserving LLM alignment that the field previously lacked. The binary selector approach is practically important for edge-device deployment where full reward model training is infeasible, and the finding that naive federated baselines (FedAvg, FedDPO) can degrade below the raw model underscores the non-triviality of federated preference aggregation. Open questions include scalability to thousands of clients, generalization beyond summarization/QA to open-ended generation, and whether the approach holds under stronger privacy guarantees (e.g., differential privacy) rather than the assumed honest-but-curious server.
