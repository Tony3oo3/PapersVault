## Problem

Existing LLM reasoning benchmarks suffer from data contamination (test problems likely seen during training) and fail to capture how reasoning complexity can grow arbitrarily through composition of subproofs. This makes it impossible to determine whether high accuracy reflects genuine generalization or memorization. The paper asks whether LLMs can generalize from simple proof structures to arbitrarily complex ones in a controlled, contamination-free setting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253614/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a proof tree specification (target depth, width, linear/nonlinear shape, and sentence ordering) and produces a natural-language math word problem paired with a ground-truth chain-of-thought solution. Problems are generated from five logical form predicates (container, comparison, transfer, part-whole, comp-eq) and five inference rules, with quantities sampled uniformly from 2–20 and agent/entity names drawn from predefined vocabularies. Evaluation produces answer accuracy under four prompting conditions: zero-shot, primitive examples, ranged OOD examples, and in-distribution examples, with 400 problems per test set.

## Method

MathGAP represents math word problems as proof trees over logical forms (a subset of first-order logic from Opedal et al. 2023), where leaf nodes are axioms and internal nodes are inference-rule applications. The generation procedure is top-down: sample a root logical form, select a matching inference rule to create child premise nodes, and recurse until a user-specified depth or width is reached. A key design choice is the introduction of the comp-eq predicate, which enables nonlinear proof trees of arbitrary depth by allowing a comparison to be defined in terms of another comparison's difference, thereby avoiding the expansion bottleneck that limits comp-only trees. Traversing the completed tree in post-order automatically yields both the problem text (from leaf nodes) and the CoT solution (from all proof steps). The evaluation framework then generates in-context examples at lower complexity levels and tests on higher-complexity problems, enabling systematic easy-to-hard OOD generalization studies without data contamination.

## Results

Across all evaluated models (Mixtral-8x7B, Llama3-8B/70B, GPT-3.5-Turbo, GPT-4o), answer accuracy decreases steadily as proof depth and width increase, with the decline being sharpest for nonlinear proof structures. On the hardest nonlinear problems (depth 7, randomly ordered sentences, 25k output tokens), even state-of-the-art reasoning models o1-preview and DeepSeek-R1 achieve only 5.0% and 11.0% accuracy, respectively. For o1 specifically, increasing the output token limit from 4,096 to 10,000 on depth-7 problems raises accuracy from 0.25% to 76.5%, highlighting the importance of reasoning budget. Llama3-70B achieves near-perfect accuracy on linear depth problems but drops sharply on nonlinear and wide problems, while GPT-4o is the most robust overall but still fails on the most complex nonlinear cases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four prompting strategies (zero-shot, primitive in-context examples, ranged OOD examples, and in-distribution examples) across five models on systematically varied proof complexity. Ranged OOD examples (varying complexity, all simpler than test) generally outperform primitive examples and are often comparable to in-distribution examples, suggesting diverse demonstrations are most beneficial. However, in-context examples do not consistently beat zero-shot: for Llama3-8B and Llama3-70B, adding OOD examples often *decreases* performance relative to zero-shot, and GPT-3.5-Turbo is the only model for which in-context examples consistently help. On nonlinear problems, o1-preview and DeepSeek-R1 substantially outperform all other models (e.g., GPT-4o, Llama3-70B), but the gap narrows at the highest complexities where all models approach zero accuracy. Nonlinear problems are consistently harder than linear ones even when controlling for the number of axioms (width), with considerably lower accuracies across all models and prompting strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the world-model formalism from Opedal et al. (2023; 2024), which represents math word problems as sequences of logical forms under a subset of first-order logic, and extends it by introducing a new comp-eq predicate specifically to enable nonlinear proof trees of arbitrary depth. It positions itself against the data contamination problem afflicting standard benchmarks like GSM8k (Zhang et al., 2024) and the broader OOD generalization literature (Saparov & He, 2023; Dziri et al., 2023), which has studied length generalization but not systematic control over proof-tree structure. The generation methodology is analogous in spirit to Dynabench (Kiela et al., 2021) but eliminates the need for human annotators by deriving both problems and CoT annotations from proof trees, and connects to curriculum learning (Bengio et al., 2009) and easy-to-hard generalization (Burns et al., 2023; Hase et al., 2024) through its in-context example design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MathGAP provides the first principled, contamination-free framework for studying how LLMs generalize across proof complexity dimensions (depth, width, shape, ordering), filling a gap that fixed benchmarks like GSM8k cannot address. The finding that nonlinear proof structures are substantially harder than linear ones even at matched width, and that in-context examples do not consistently improve over zero-shot, challenges common assumptions about how LLMs leverage demonstrations. Open questions include whether findings generalize to higher linguistic diversity, whether the comp-eq predicate is truly absent from training corpora, and whether the framework extends to non-arithmetic reasoning domains. The framework's extensibility (new predicates, rules, templates, languages) makes it a reusable tool for the community.
