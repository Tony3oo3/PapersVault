## Problem

Traditional image captioning and VQA benchmarks evaluate literal accuracy and fluency but cannot assess whether multi-modal models provide historically accurate, culturally sensitive, and objective contextualisation of sensitive images. No existing dataset or evaluation protocol specifically targets the cross-modal socio-cultural reasoning required to describe images of contested historical events, figures, and locations across all world regions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253467/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation takes a single historical image (resized to 300px wide from Wikimedia Commons) paired with a text instruction—either an explicit prompt requesting historical context or a minimal 'What is in this image?'—and produces a free-text description. The Century dataset itself comprises 1,500 images with metadata (Wikipedia page links, geographic sub-region, image type, content type, concept theme) and quality labels (sensitivity, controversiality, commemorativeness) from both six automated LLM labellers and 151 human annotators. Model responses are scored on a 5-point Likert scale across sub-dimensions of accuracy (correct identification, factuality), thoroughness (beginner-friendliness, appropriate summary), and objectivity (due weight, no loaded language, opinions not stated as facts).

## Method

The dataset is constructed by mining 37.8 million records in the Wikipedia-based Image Text Dataset (WIT) using two complementary automated search-term generation methods: (1) querying a knowledge graph (Freebase/Wikipedia) with manually expanded concepts under four themes—conflict, oppression, discrimination, reform—targeting events, organizations, people, and locations from the 20th–21st centuries, yielding 7,385 search terms; and (2) prompting GPT-4 Omni and Claude Opus to generate candidate Wikipedia page titles likely containing sensitive historical images, yielding 1,297 additional titles. Quality and diversity criteria are synthesized from over 20 principles drawn from museum, archive, and digital collection guidelines, operationalized into three evaluation dimensions (accuracy, thoroughness, objectivity) with specific sub-elements. The evaluation protocol uses an LLM-as-judge ensemble of six models (GPT-4 Turbo, GPT-4 Omni, Gemini Pro, Gemini Flash, Claude Opus, Claude Haiku) to score responses, complemented by human evaluation from 151 crowd-workers with relevant research experience, enabling both automated and human-grounded measurement of historical contextualisation quality.

## Results

On the Century benchmark (n=1,500 images), GPT-4 Omni achieves the highest scores across all dimensions, reaching 53.3% (automated) and 45.5% (human) on correct identification, 63.7% (automated) and 54.5% (human) on beginner-friendliness, and 76.5% (automated) and 36.5% (human) on absence of loaded language. Even the best-performing model struggles substantially: human-evaluated scores for 'appropriate summary' range from only 9.5% (Claude Opus) to 20.6% (GPT-4 Omni), and 'correct identification' peaks at just 45.5% under human evaluation. Gemini Pro consistently outperforms Gemini Flash across most dimensions (e.g., 44.0% vs. 37.2% automated on correct identification), while Claude Opus trails all other models on nearly every metric.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against prior evaluation methods or baselines; instead, it benchmarks four foundation models against each other on the new Century dataset. GPT-4 Omni leads on nearly every dimension under both automated and human evaluation, with the largest gaps over Claude Opus appearing in correct identification (45.5% vs. 12.2% human) and beginner-friendliness (54.5% vs. 20.1% human). Gemini Pro consistently outperforms Gemini Flash (e.g., 44.0% vs. 37.2% automated on correct identification; 53.4% vs. 39.2% on beginner-friendliness), with the notable exception of 'appropriate summary' under human evaluation where Flash edges ahead (20.2% vs. 15.3%). Claude Opus is the weakest performer overall, though it is the only model to beat GPT-4 Omni on 'no loaded language' under automated evaluation (56.3% vs. 76.5%—actually GPT-4 Omni still leads here; Claude Opus leads Gemini Flash on this metric at 56.3% vs. 44.3%). The authors note no systematic self-enhancement bias but observe that labeller model choice can shift results by up to 30 percentage points, underscoring the sensitivity of automated evaluation to the judge model used.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends image captioning and visual question answering benchmarks (Flickr30k, MSCOCO, OKVQA, A-OKVQA) by introducing an evaluation task that requires open-ended, context-rich descriptions of historically sensitive images rather than literal or multiple-choice responses. Dataset construction draws on knowledge graph mining (Freebase/Wikipedia) and LLM-based synthetic search term generation (building on Radharapu et al., 2023), while evaluation criteria are synthesized from museum and archive curatorial guidelines rather than from prior NLP metrics. The LLM-as-judge evaluation protocol adapts the MT-Bench paradigm (Zheng et al., 2023b) to a cross-modal, socio-cultural reasoning setting, and the WIT dataset (Srinivasan et al., 2021) serves as the image source.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Century fills a genuine gap: no prior benchmark systematically evaluates how multimodal models handle the socio-cultural and normative dimensions of describing historically sensitive images, a failure mode with real representational-harm and misinformation consequences. Key open questions include how to incorporate affected-community perspectives into evaluation criteria (the paper acknowledges museum guidelines may contradict community wishes), how to mitigate geographic over-representation (North America and Western Europe dominate), and whether LLM labellers can reliably assess objectivity without introducing their own majoritarian biases. The paper's finding that labeller models sometimes refuse to engage with sensitive content—suggesting implicit historical erasure—raises important questions about the limits of automated evaluation for culturally charged material.
