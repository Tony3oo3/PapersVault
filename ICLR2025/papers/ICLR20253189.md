## Problem

It has been widely assumed that interpretability and classifiability are in conflict for vision representations: imposing interpretability constraints degrades classification accuracy, and classifiability-oriented pre-trained representations are thought to contain uninterpretable semantics that cause information loss during interpretation. This paper challenges that assumption by asking whether classifiability-oriented pre-trained representations can simultaneously achieve high interpretability and high classifiability, and whether improving one can promote the other.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253189/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes frozen pre-trained model representations (ResNet, ViT, Swin, ConvNeXt at 224×224 or 512×512) and a task-relevant concept library of M concepts (visual patches/segments aggregated via prototype, k-means clustering, or end-to-end learning; or textual phrases extracted by GPT-3 and encoded via CLIP soft labels). It produces the Inherent Interpretability Score (IIS), defined as the area under the accuracy-retention-rate curve across sparsity ratios s∈[0,1], along with interpretable class predictions made solely from the top-(1−s)·M concept contribution scores after a sparsification step that zeros out the least-contributing dimensions.

## Method

The core contribution is the IIS metric and the empirical discovery that interpretability and classifiability are positively correlated for classifiability-oriented representations. IIS is computed by projecting representations into a concept space via learned or averaged concept vectors, sparsifying the projection to retain only the most salient concepts, training a linear classifier on the sparse interpretations, and integrating the accuracy retention rate (interpretation accuracy / representation accuracy) over a range of sparsity ratios. The key finding is that representations with higher downstream accuracy retain more task-relevant semantics under sparsification, yielding higher ARR and thus higher IIS. Two practical applications follow: (1) fine-tuning pre-trained models with a simplified IIS maximization objective (a learnable projection matrix plus fixed sparsity) as an auxiliary loss improves classification accuracy by 0.3–1.7% across ResNet-50, Swin-T, ConvNeXt-T, and ViT-B; and (2) using the interpretations of high-accuracy representations for prediction achieves 86.85% on ImageNet (ViT-L), surpassing interpretability-oriented methods like LaBo (83.97%) while requiring only a linear head to be trained.

## Results

On ImageNet, interpretable predictions based on ViT-L representations achieve 86.85% accuracy (ARR = 98.70%), surpassing the best interpretability-oriented baseline LaBo (83.97%) by 2.88%. On CUB-200, the method with 247 concepts reaches 83.50% accuracy, outperforming ECBM* (83.21%) while only training the classification head. IIS maximization fine-tuning improves classifiability across all tested architectures, e.g., ResNet-50 from 75.66% to 77.36% and ViT-B from 80.58% to 81.07% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On ImageNet, the method with ViT-L representations achieves 86.85% interpretable prediction accuracy, outperforming LaBo (83.97%) by 2.88%, Ante-Hoc (65.09%) by 21.76%, and SENN (58.55%) by 28.30%. On CUB-200, with 247 concepts the method reaches 83.50%, beating ECBM* (83.21%) and all other interpretability-oriented baselines (CBM 75.90%, CEM 79.60%, ProtoPNet 80.80%), while only training the linear classification head rather than the full model. Against LF-CBM, the method improves accuracy by 0.56% (ViT-B: 78.50 vs. 77.94) and 1.33% (ViT-L: 86.85 vs. 85.52) on ImageNet. Compared to DEAL on ImageNet, the method achieves 70.8% vs. 70.0% (CLIP-RN50) and 73.0% vs. 70.8% (CLIP-ViT-B/32). The paper also demonstrates that higher-accuracy representations yield higher ARR (ViT-L: 98.70% vs. ViT-B: 97.42%), confirming the positive interpretability–classifiability correlation across model scales.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the sparsification mechanism from Shen et al. (2024) and the concept library construction framework from Oikarinen et al. (2023, LF-CBM) to serve a fundamentally different purpose: rather than building interpretable prediction architectures, it uses concept-based interpretation as a diagnostic lens to quantify how much task-relevant semantic information survives the projection from representation space to concept space. It departs sharply from the dominant interpretability-oriented literature (CBMs, ECBM, LaBo) that imposes interpretability constraints during training and observes accuracy degradation, instead studying classifiability-oriented pre-trained representations and asking whether their interpretability is inherently limited by their accuracy. The IIS metric is positioned as distinct from faithfulness metrics (which compare interpretation methods) and from CBM performance metrics (which evaluate specific architectures), targeting the representation itself as the unit of measurement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The positive correlation between interpretability and classifiability challenges a long-standing assumption in the field that these two properties are in inherent tension, suggesting practitioners need not sacrifice accuracy to obtain interpretable predictions from strong pre-trained models. However, the paper provides no theoretical explanation for why this correlation holds, and the practical gains from IIS maximization are modest (e.g., +1.7% Acc@1 for ResNet-50 on ImageNet), limiting immediate adoption. Open questions include whether the correlation persists under distribution shift or adversarial conditions, whether it can be formalized theoretically, and how IIS might be integrated into pre-training objectives for next-generation vision-language foundation models.
