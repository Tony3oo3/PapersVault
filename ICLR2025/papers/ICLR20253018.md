## Problem

Standard neural network architectures (ReLU, polynomial, rational) can only represent specific subsets of semialgebraic functions, yet many important numerical algorithms—solving linear systems, extracting eigenvalues, PDE solution operators—produce exactly semialgebraic outputs. No prior architecture was capable of exactly representing the entire class of bounded semialgebraic functions, including discontinuous ones, on high-dimensional inputs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253018/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The SANN takes an input vector x ∈ R^m and produces y ∈ R^n, the exact value of a bounded semialgebraic function F(x), which may be discontinuous. Computation proceeds by numerically integrating an ODE from s=0 to s=1; the output is the first n components of the ODE state at s=1. The numerical ODE solver's step count N controls approximation error independently of network parameters, so increasing N improves accuracy without adding model capacity.

## Method

The SANN encodes the graph of a target semialgebraic function F as the kernel of a piecewise polynomial G, then constructs a homotopy H(x,y,s) that deforms from a trivially solvable function G_0 at s=0 to G at s=1. The architecture's ISD network N (a Matrix-Recurrent Neural Network) outputs a matrix M and vector b at each ODE step, and the state update is z_dot = clamp-sol(M,b) = M^{-1}b clamped to [-c_max, c_max], which recapitulates the curve-tracing ODE of a homotopy continuation method. For continuous functions, a single homotopy with a fast-growing barrier function g guarantees the traced curve reaches F(x); for discontinuous functions, the domain is decomposed into finitely many semialgebraic connected components, each handled by a separate homotopy, and the pieces are glued using s-glueing and t-glueing lemmas that exploit the discontinuity of M^{-1}b across singular boundaries. Training uses a combined accuracy loss and a direction loss that penalizes deviation of intermediate ODE states from the target, optimized with standard backpropagation or adjoint sensitivity.

## Results

In the hand-crafted example, a SANN configured to replicate Jacobi iteration solves 50×50 linear systems to machine precision (‖Xy−g‖₂ → 0), a task impossible for standard piecewise-linear feed-forward networks. In the trained experiment on 10×10 linear systems, the SANN achieves a validation error of ‖Xy−g‖₂ = 0.101 with 291,720 parameters, compared to 0.166 for a two-layer feed-forward network with 306,140 parameters. The authors note these results demonstrate feasibility of training SANNs with standard techniques (Adam optimizer, direction + accuracy loss) but explicitly refrain from claiming significant numerical advantages at this stage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the SANN against a single baseline: a two-layer feed-forward neural network with a comparable number of parameters, trained on the same task of solving 10×10 linear systems. The SANN achieves a 39% relative reduction in validation error (0.101 vs. 0.166 for ‖Xy−g‖₂) while using approximately 5% fewer parameters (291,720 vs. 306,140). The authors are cautious in their interpretation, noting they 'refrain from claiming significant numerical advantages for SANNs at this stage' and that the primary purpose of the experiment is to demonstrate that SANNs can be trained with standard deep-learning techniques (Adam optimizer, backpropagation through explicit time-stepping). The input matrices were sampled from strictly diagonally dominant distributions, ensuring a SANN theoretically exists that can solve to machine precision, though the trained network does not yet reach that level. No other baselines (e.g., iterative solvers, rational networks, or other implicit architectures) are included in the empirical comparison.

## Relation to Prior Work

SANNs sit at the intersection of three research threads: Neural ODEs (Chen et al., 2019), which provide the ODE-based computation paradigm; Matrix-Recurrent Neural Networks (de Hoop et al., 2022), which supply the ISD piecewise-polynomial building blocks; and classical homotopy continuation methods (Chen & Li, 2015), whose curve-tracing ODE system is directly embedded as the network's computational core. The key departure from Neural ODEs is that SANNs parameterize a family of ODEs indexed by the input x with a fixed initial condition, rather than a fixed ODE with input-dependent initialization, and the departure from Deep Equilibrium Models is the elimination of an explicit fixed-point iteration in favor of a continuous homotopy path. Prior semialgebraic ML work (polynomial networks, rational networks, Christoffel-Darboux approximations) handles only specific subsets of semialgebraic functions; no prior architecture claims exact representation of the full class of bounded semialgebraic functions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first neural network architecture with a proven exact representation theorem for all bounded semialgebraic functions, including discontinuous ones, which opens a direct bridge between real algebraic geometry and deep learning. The practical impact is currently limited: training SANNs remains an open challenge, the empirical validation is confined to small 10×10 linear systems with only marginal improvement over feed-forward baselines, and the expressivity proofs are existence results rather than constructive algorithms. Open questions include whether specialized SANN variants (e.g., with LU factorization) can be made both expressive and efficient, how to design training strategies that exploit the ODE structure, and whether the Pierce-Birkhoff conjecture (which would extend ISD to all piecewise polynomials) affects the practical scope of the architecture.
