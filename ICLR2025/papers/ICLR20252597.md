## Problem

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) incur prohibitive computational costs due to processing all tokens through every layer, yet existing efficiency methods like Mixture-of-Experts only reduce activated parameters while leaving token-level redundancy unaddressed. Although Mixture-of-Depths (MoD) can skip redundant tokens per layer, directly converting all dense MLLM layers to MoD causes severe performance degradation (e.g., −33.3% on TextVQA) because there is no principled way to identify which layers are actually redundant, and the original MoD design was built for text-only LLMs rather than multimodal settings.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard MLLM inputs: an RGB image (potentially high-resolution, producing hundreds to thousands of visual tokens via a vision encoder and projector) paired with a free-form text question. The output is an autoregressive text response generated token-by-token. The key operational detail is that at each MoD layer, a router assigns a binary skip/keep decision to every token in the concatenated [image tokens; question tokens; response tokens] sequence, so the effective sequence length shrinks dynamically at each layer while the final output format remains unchanged.

## Method

γ-MoD is a plug-and-play adaptation that converts most dense transformer layers in an existing MLLM into Mixture-of-Depths layers during the instruction-tuning stage, without requiring pre-training from scratch. It introduces ARank (average rank of attention maps across heads) as a data-driven metric to identify which layers are redundant: layers with lower ARank have more token-level redundancy and are prioritized for MoD conversion, with the fourth-largest ARank serving as the threshold. Two design innovations maximize sparsity while preserving performance: (1) a shared vision-language router that applies a binary softmax over the full multimodal token sequence (image + question + response) and shares its parameters across all MoD layers to stabilize early training, and (2) masked routing learning that forces question tokens to always be kept during training so the model does not learn to skip critical conditioning information. With these components, over 90% of dense layers can be replaced by MoD layers, achieving up to 51.6% FLOPs reduction and 53.2% faster inference on LLaVA-HR with only ~1% average accuracy drop.

## Results

On LLaVA-HR (7B), γ-MoD-0.3 achieves 58.3% average accuracy across GQA, SQA, MMMU, and TextVQA with a 37.9% token skip rate, while γ-MoD-0.5 reaches 57.6% average with 57.7% skip. In terms of efficiency, γ-MoD-0.5 reduces training time by 31.0% (20.7h → 14.3h), increases inference throughput by 53.2% (4.7 → 7.2 samples/s), and cuts TFlops by 51.6% (19.2 → 9.3), all with only a −0.9% average accuracy drop. On the 9-benchmark comparison (Table 6), γ-MoD-LLaVA-HR (13B) matches or exceeds the dense LLaVA-HR (7B) on most benchmarks (e.g., +3.0 on MM-Vet, +1.7 on MMMU) while running at comparable speed (4.8 vs 4.7 samples/s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the default MoD (Raposo et al., 2024) applied naively to LLaVA-HR, γ-MoD recovers the average accuracy from 37.1% to 57.6% while simultaneously increasing the skip rate from 34.7% to 57.7%, confirming that ARank-guided layer selection is critical. Compared to MoE-LLaVA (5B), γ-MoD-LLaVA-HR (7B) outperforms on 5 of 8 shared benchmarks (e.g., +93 on MME, +13.5 on TextVQA) while also achieving +28% higher inference speed (7.2 vs 5.6 samples/s). Against inference-time acceleration methods, γ-MoD-0.3 beats AWQ-4bit on MMMU (35.4 vs 34.8) and MM-Vet (29.1 vs 26.5), and outperforms FastV on both MMMU (35.4 vs 33.9) and MM-Vet (29.1 vs 28.8), with a +31% next-token speedup over FastV on MM-Vet. The 13B γ-MoD-LLaVA-HR matches the dense LLaVA-HR (7B) in speed (4.8 vs 4.7 samples/s) while surpassing it on MM-Vet (+2.8) and MMMU (+0.6), demonstrating that sparsity enables a larger model at the same latency budget. Across architectures (LLaVA, LLaVA-HR, Mini-Gemini-HD) and scales (7B, 13B), γ-MoD-0.3 consistently maintains within 1–2% of the dense baseline while skipping 31–38% of tokens, confirming broad generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the Mixture-of-Depths (MoD) mechanism from Raposo et al. (2024), originally designed for LLMs and requiring pre-training from scratch, to the multimodal setting as a plug-and-play instruction-tuning procedure. It departs from the MoE line of work (which reduces activated parameters) by targeting activated tokens instead, and introduces ARank—a rank-based metric on attention maps—as a principled, data-driven replacement for the hand-craft layer selection used in the original MoD paper. Two MLLM-specific designs (shared vision-language router and masked routing learning) address the fact that visual tokens exhibit far greater redundancy than textual ones and that instruction tokens must not be skipped during training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: γ-MoD achieves 53.2% inference speedup and 31% training time reduction on LLaVA-HR with only a 0.9% average accuracy drop, making high-resolution MLLMs substantially more deployable. The observation that high-resolution MLLMs carry proportionally more token redundancy than low-resolution ones provides a useful design principle for future efficient MLLM architectures. Open questions include whether the approach extends to video or audio modalities, whether the 90%+ layer conversion ceiling can be pushed further, and whether the method remains effective at much larger scales (e.g., 70B+) where attention patterns may differ. The reliance on instruction tuning (rather than true zero-shot adaptation) also limits its applicability to frozen or proprietary models.
