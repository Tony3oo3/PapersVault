## Problem

Traditional Data Shapley requires retraining models on exponentially many data subsets to compute Shapley values, making it computationally infeasible for foundation models. Moreover, retraining-based Data Shapley measures average contribution to a general learning algorithm rather than to the specific model produced by a particular training run, which is what practitioners actually need for debugging, data curation, and copyright attribution.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250447/cropped_page16_idx5.webp)

## Input / Output

At each gradient update iteration, the method consumes the current mini-batch of training points, a validation data point, and the model parameters at that checkpoint. It outputs a per-data-point Shapley score for that iteration (a scalar per training point in the batch), which is then accumulated across all T iterations to yield a final cumulative attribution score per training point. Crucially, no per-sample gradient vectors or Hessian matrices are ever instantiated in memory; all required quantities are extracted from the forward/backward pass activations.

## Method

The key insight is a divide-and-conquer decomposition: the global utility is split into per-iteration local utilities measuring the loss change from a single gradient update, and the Shapley value of the global utility equals the sum of per-iteration Shapley values by linearity. Each local utility is approximated via Taylor expansion—first-order yields a closed-form Shapley value as a gradient dot product between the training point and validation point, while second-order adds a gradient-Hessian-gradient interaction term capturing how other batch members affect a point's contribution. To compute these without instantiating per-sample gradients, the paper introduces a 'ghost dot-product' technique (inspired by differential privacy's ghost clipping) that decomposes each per-sample gradient into input and output-gradient factors already available during backpropagation, reducing the computation to scalar inner products within a single backward pass. A 'ghost gradient-Hessian-gradient product' technique extends this to the second-order term with one additional backpropagation. In its most efficient (first-order) form, the method adds negligible runtime overhead over standard training, making data attribution feasible at foundation-model scale.

## Results

On GPT2-Small (single A100), first-order In-Run Data Shapley with the ghost dot-product technique achieves 70.5 tokens/s throughput vs. 76.2 for regular training (only ~8% overhead), while the direct per-sample gradient implementation drops to 4.2 tokens/s (>30× slower). In the data curation experiment on ~10B tokens of Pile, removing the ~16% of corpora with negative second-order In-Run Data Shapley values reduces the number of training iterations needed to reach test loss 3.75 by approximately 25%. In the paraphrase-robustness experiment (Table 2), In-Run Data Shapley ranks the original training corpus at position 145.6 out of ~320k for a complete topical rewrite, compared to 292 for Influence Function and 20,917 for BM25.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Influence Function (Koh & Liang, 2017), In-Run Data Shapley consistently achieves lower (better) value ranks in the paraphrase-robustness experiment: 32.3 vs. 39.3 for significant paraphrase and 145.6 vs. 292 for a complete topical rewrite, indicating more reliable identification of the contributing training point. In the data curation experiment (Section 5.3.1), filtering by In-Run Data Shapley yields faster convergence than filtering by Influence Function, which the authors attribute to IF's reliance on the final model only, making its leave-one-out scores noisier. Against BM25 (Robertson et al., 2009), In-Run Data Shapley is dramatically superior for semantic-level similarity (similar-topic rewrite: rank 145.6 vs. 20,917.3), confirming that data contribution extends well beyond lexical overlap. However, BM25 outperforms In-Run Data Shapley on the 'significant paraphrase' category (rank 1.6 vs. 32.3), where high lexical overlap makes a simple TF-IDF signal sufficient. Overall, In-Run Data Shapley wins on the most practically important regime—identifying contribution when the output is a substantial rewrite—while BM25 remains a useful lexical-similarity oracle for near-verbatim cases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Data Shapley framework (Ghorbani & Zou, 2019) by reformulating the utility function from a global, retraining-based definition to a per-iteration 'local' utility, enabling closed-form Shapley values via Taylor expansion. The first-order In-Run Data Shapley is shown to be mathematically equivalent to TracIN-Ideal (Pruthi et al., 2020), which was previously deemed computationally infeasible; the paper's key technical contribution is the 'ghost dot-product' technique (inspired by differential privacy's ghost clipping) that computes per-sample gradient dot products in a single backpropagation without instantiating individual gradient vectors. Conceptually, the paper departs from prior work by shifting from algorithm-level attribution (averaging over all possible training runs) to model-specific attribution that incorporates the exact random seed, batch ordering, and training trajectory of a single run.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work makes principled data attribution computationally feasible at foundation-model scale for the first time, with first-order In-Run Data Shapley adding negligible overhead to standard training. The finding that ~16% of the Pile dataset (despite multiple curation layers) receives negative Shapley values, and that removing them accelerates convergence by ~25%, has direct practical implications for pretraining data curation. The copyright analysis—showing significant data contribution even when validation text is a complete topical rewrite with no lexical overlap—challenges the prevailing 'near-verbatim replication' standard in AI copyright discourse. Key limitations include the requirement for validation data before training, restriction to SGD (Adam extension is open), and experiments limited to GPT-2/Pythia-410M scale.
