## Problem

Existing visual reward learning methods for robotic manipulation struggle with long-horizon tasks involving multiple subtasks: they either rely on human-engineered reward functions requiring extensive task-specific knowledge, use only a few consecutive frames or a final goal image without awareness of subsequent subtasks, or (as in DrS) assume the environment explicitly provides the ongoing subtask during online interaction. The paper addresses how to learn a dense, subtask-aware reward function from action-free video demonstrations with minimal supervision, such that the reward model can autonomously infer the current subtask from video alone during online RL.

## Input / Output

The model takes a stack of K consecutive RGB images (64×64×3 for Meta-World; 224×224×3 from front and wrist cameras for FurnitureBench) and a set of subtask text instructions as input. During training, subtask segmentations (mapping each timestep to its ongoing subtask index) are provided as supervision; during online inference, only the video segment is used, and the subtask is inferred via cosine similarity between the video representation and pre-computed subtask text embeddings. The output is a scalar dense reward signal conditioned on the inferred subtask, normalized by the maximum reward observed in expert demonstrations, along with the inferred subtask index.

## Method

REDS encodes each frame with a frozen CLIP ViT-B/16 visual encoder, processes the sequence through a 3-layer, 8-head causal GPT transformer to capture temporal dependencies, and concatenates the resulting video representation with a subtask text embedding (CLIP text encoder + 2-layer MLP) before a final MLP produces the reward. Training jointly optimizes three objectives: (1) an EPIC loss that minimizes the Equivalent-Policy Invariant Comparison distance between the learned reward and the subtask segmentation function treated as ground-truth reward, guaranteeing a theoretical upper bound on regret; (2) a progressive reward regularization that enforces monotonically increasing reward within each subtask on expert demonstrations; and (3) a contrastive loss aligning video representations with their corresponding subtask embeddings so the model can infer the ongoing subtask from video alone at inference time. An iterative fine-tuning loop collects suboptimal rollouts, automatically infers their subtask labels using the current reward model with per-subtask similarity thresholds, and fine-tunes the reward model on the combined expert and suboptimal data to mitigate reward misspecification.

## Results

On Meta-World (8 tasks), REDS consistently outperforms all baselines in sample efficiency, maintaining non-zero success rates across all tasks while baselines deteriorate on complex ones (e.g., Peg Insert Side). In some tasks (Drawer Open, Push, Coffee Pull), REDS even surpasses human-engineered reward functions without task-specific reward engineering. On the real-world FurnitureBench One Leg task, REDS achieves 2.45 completed subtasks after online fine-tuning (from 1.10 offline), substantially outperforming VIPER (1.25), DrS (1.10), and the sparse-reward baseline (1.8). EPIC distance measurements confirm alignment: REDS achieves 0.0713 on One Leg versus 0.7035 (VIPER) and 0.6001 (R2R).

## Baselines & Comparisons

REDS outperforms all eight baselines across both simulation and real-world settings. Against VIPER (video prediction likelihood as reward), REDS achieves substantially higher success rates on all 8 Meta-World tasks and a 2.45 vs. 1.25 completed-subtask gap on FurnitureBench One Leg, as VIPER fails to provide context-aware signals for later subtasks. Against DrS (discriminator-based, assumes environment-provided subtask labels), REDS wins decisively in the real-world (2.45 vs. 1.10) because DrS produces sparse rewards within each subtask and requires manual subtask identification during online interaction. Against ORIL and Rank2Reward (adversarial imitation learning methods), REDS achieves lower EPIC distances (e.g., 0.4674 vs. 0.6989 on Peg Insert Side) and higher success rates, as these methods lack subtask awareness. Against the human-engineered reward functions in Meta-World, REDS matches or exceeds performance on 3 of 8 tasks (Drawer Open, Push, Coffee Pull) while remaining competitive on the rest, all without task-specific reward engineering. Against CLIP and LIV (distance-to-text-instruction baselines in Figure 12a), REDS significantly outperforms both, confirming that explicit subtask decomposition is crucial for long-horizon tasks. Against the sparse-reward FurnitureBench baseline (500 demos), REDS achieves 2.45 vs. 1.8 completed subtasks using only 300 demos, demonstrating superior sample efficiency.

## Relation to Prior Work

REDS builds on the line of video-based reward learning (temporal contrastive methods, goal-proximity rewards, video-prediction-as-reward via VIPER) and adversarial imitation learning (ORIL, DrS), but departs from these in two key ways. First, it uses EPIC distance (Gleave et al., 2021) as a training objective rather than merely an evaluation metric, ensuring the learned reward induces the same optimal policies as the ground-truth subtask segmentation. Second, unlike DrS which assumes the environment provides explicit subtask identification during online interaction, REDS infers the ongoing subtask purely from visual observations via a contrastive alignment between video representations and text-encoded subtask embeddings, making it applicable in settings where automatic subtask detection is infeasible.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

REDS addresses the reward-engineering bottleneck for long-horizon robotic manipulation by showing that minimal supervision (video + subtask labels) suffices to produce context-aware, progressive dense rewards that outperform both learned and human-engineered baselines. The demonstrated generalization to unseen tasks, embodiments, and visual distractions suggests a path toward scalable deployment without per-task reward design. Key limitations remain: the method assumes object-centric subtask structure is known, relies on pre-trained CLIP/R3M representations that struggle with subtle changes (e.g., screwing), and the number of fine-tuning iterations is set empirically. Automating subtask definition via multimodal LLMs and improving representation quality for fine-grained manipulation are the most pressing open directions.
