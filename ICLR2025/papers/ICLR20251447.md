## Problem

Existing GNNs produce suboptimal representations for subgraph-level tasks because they cannot capture arbitrary high-order interactions both within and between subgraph structures, forcing reliance on hand-crafted channels, node labeling, or structural approximations. No prior work has applied graph kernels to subgraph-level tasks by measuring structural similarity between subgraphs and their surrounding neighborhoods in the global graph.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251447/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of subgraphs (each defined by its node and edge subsets) within a global graph, plus optional continuous node features. It produces an M×M positive semi-definite kernel matrix where each entry is the inner product of WL color histograms for a pair of subgraphs, which is then fed to an SVM for classification. Unusually, for k=0 only the subgraph's internal structure is used (no neighborhood sampling), and for k=D the WL algorithm runs once on the entire global graph and the resulting coloring is restricted to each subgraph's nodes, eliminating per-subgraph neighborhood extraction.

## Method

WLKS generalizes the WL graph kernel to subgraphs by running the 1-WL color refinement on the induced k-hop neighborhood of each subgraph, then extracting the color histogram only for the original subgraph's nodes. A key theoretical result (Proposition 3.2) shows that WLS^{k+1} is not strictly more expressive than WLS^k in distinguishing isomorphic structures, motivating a linear combination of kernel matrices across multiple k values (WLKS-𝕂). To balance expressiveness and efficiency, the authors restrict k to {0, D} where D is the global graph diameter, avoiding exponential neighborhood growth while capturing both local internal structure and the full global context. For continuous features, WLKS can be linearly combined with feature-based kernels or used as the adjacency matrix for GNNs (S2N+0, S2N+A with GCNII), enabling integration of structural and feature information without pre-computation or GPU training.

## Results

WLKS-{0,D} achieves the highest micro F1-score on 5 out of 8 benchmarks: PPI-BP (64.8), EM-User (91.8), Density (96.0), Coreness (91.3), and Component (100.0). On the three remaining datasets (HPO-Neuro, HPO-Metab, Cut-Ratio), it trails the best baseline by 3.2, 5.3, and 33.5 points respectively. In terms of efficiency, WLKS-{0,D} reduces training time to 0.01×–0.25× and inference time to 0.12×–0.43× of the fastest GNN baselines, and requires no GPU, pre-computation, or pre-trained embeddings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WLKS-{0,D} outperforms all five baselines on PPI-BP (64.8 vs. best 63.7 for S2N+AGCNII), EM-User (91.8 vs. 89.0), Density (96.0 vs. 93.2), and Coreness (91.3 vs. 85.7), and ties for best on Component (100.0, shared with GLASS and both S2N variants). On HPO-Neuro, GLASS leads (68.5) with WLKS at 65.3, a 3.2-point gap; on HPO-Metab, S2N+AGCNII leads (63.2) with WLKS at 57.9. The largest gap is on Cut-Ratio, where GLASS achieves 93.5 versus WLKS's 60.0, though the authors show that adding a Random Walk Structural Encoding kernel closes this to 96.0. In efficiency, WLKS trains in 3.5–25.2 s across real-world datasets versus 14.9–4597 s for GNN baselines, and does not require GPU acceleration, pre-computation, or pre-trained node embeddings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the classical WL graph kernel (Shervashidze & Borgwardt, 2009) from graph-level to subgraph-level tasks by applying the WL algorithm on induced k-hop neighborhoods around each subgraph. It departs from the dominant GNN-based paradigm for subgraph representation learning (SubGNN, GLASS, S2N, SSNP), which relies on hand-crafted channels, node labeling, or structural approximations to capture interactions within and between subgraphs. The authors position WLKS as the first kernel-based approach specifically designed for subgraph-level prediction, and they introduce a theoretical argument (Proposition 3.2) that motivates combining kernels across multiple k-hop levels rather than relying on a single large k.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

WLKS offers a dramatically simpler and faster alternative to deep GNNs for subgraph classification—requiring no GPU, no pre-training, no neighborhood sampling, and no hand-crafted features—while matching or exceeding state-of-the-art on 5 of 8 benchmarks. However, the WL histogram's limited expressiveness means it underperforms on structure-sensitive tasks (e.g., Cut-Ratio), and the paper acknowledges that seamless integration with GNNs for feature-rich settings remains open. The practical takeaway is that for many subgraph tasks, a well-tuned kernel method with SVM can be a competitive, low-compute baseline that practitioners should consider before reaching for complex architectures.
