## Problem

Running parallel budget-controlled A/B tests in segmented submarkets of advertising auction markets introduces interference bias: items targeted by buyers across multiple submarkets shift the equilibrium pacing multipliers away from the ideal separated-market values. While the parallel design dramatically increases experimentation throughput compared to a single full-market budget split, no formal framework existed to quantify or correct the resulting first-order bias in pacing multipliers and revenue estimates.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a finite FPPE dataset of t auction items, each with per-buyer valuations v_i(θ) and first-price allocations x_i(θ), along with buyer budgets b_i, the observed equilibrium pacing multipliers β_α^γ, the known contamination fraction α (the proportion of 'bad' items targeted by buyers in multiple submarkets), and a desired confidence level. It outputs an n-dimensional debiased pacing multiplier vector with an ellipsoidal confidence region (via a plug-in covariance estimate), a debiased revenue scalar with its own CI, and a treatment effect estimate τ̂_k = REV̂_k(1) − REV̂_k(0) for each submarket. The Hessian can be estimated either by finite differences on the sample EG objective or, under a bid-gap regularity condition, by the closed-form diagonal Diag(b_i/(β_α,i^γ)²).

## Method

The paper models cross-submarket interference as supply contamination in the FPPE framework: the observed item distribution is s_α = αs′ + (1−α)s where s′ is supported on 'bad' items (those with positive values for buyers in ≥2 submarkets). The key insight is that the pacing multiplier β*_α as a function of contamination level α admits a directional derivative dβ*(α) = −(P_α H_α P_α)† δ_α with a closed-form expression, where H_α is the EG Hessian, P_α is a diagonal indicator of paced buyers, and δ_α = ∫∇f(θ,β*_α)(s−s′)dθ captures the supply mismatch. The debiased surrogate β̃* = β*_α + α·dβ*(α) eliminates first-order bias (‖β̃*−β*‖ = o(α) vs. Θ(α) for the raw contaminated equilibrium). A plug-in estimator replaces each component with sample analogues (finite-difference Hessian, Radon-Nikodym-weighted subgradients for δ_α, thresholded indicators for P_α), and asymptotic normality is established via influence functions, yielding a consistent covariance estimate for ellipsoidal confidence regions. Under an additional bid-gap condition the Hessian simplifies to a diagonal form, enabling a simpler estimator with a modified influence function d_2 that accounts for the second-order effect of Hessian estimation error.

## Results

In production experiments at Meta, the parallel budget-controlled A/B test design agrees with the gold-standard budget-split design in 75% of 99 paired experiments at 90% confidence (optimal agreement is 81.5%), improving to 79% after applying a guardrail metric. In semi-synthetic experiments with 40 buyers and up to 15,000 items, the debiased surrogate reduces normalized bias from Θ(α) to o(α) for both pacing multipliers and revenue. Coverage of the debiased pacing multiplier estimator ranges from 82.6% to 87.7% (nominal 95%), while the analytic revenue CI achieves 100% coverage and the bootstrap revenue CI achieves 81–95% coverage across contamination levels α = 1000/11000 to 5000/15000.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary empirical comparison is between the proposed parallel submarket A/B test design and the traditional full-market budget-split design (Basse et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2021), which is treated as the gold standard. Across 99 paired production experiments at Meta, the parallel design matches the budget-split design's sign (negative/neutral/positive) in 75% of cases at 90% confidence, versus an optimal agreement rate of 81.5%; this improves to 79% after removing experiments that fail a guardrail metric. The remaining gap (2–6 percentage points below optimal) is attributed to residual interference bias from cross-submarket items, which the paper's debiased estimator is designed to correct. On the theoretical side, the debiased surrogate (Theorem 1) provably reduces the first-order bias of the contaminated FPPE pacing multipliers from Θ(α) to o(α), a strict improvement over the naive estimator that simply uses the contaminated market equilibrium. The semi-synthetic experiments confirm this: the normalized bias of the debiased surrogate is substantially smaller than that of the contaminated limit quantities across all tested contamination levels, though the finite-sample coverage of the variance estimator remains below the nominal 95% level (82.6–87.7% for β̂), indicating room for improvement in the variance estimation procedure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the FPPE statistical inference framework of Liao & Kroer (2023), which established asymptotic normality for pacing multipliers in a single-market budget-split A/B test, to the new setting of parallel submarket experiments where interference arises from 'bad' items shared across submarkets. The key conceptual move is recasting cross-submarket interference as a supply contamination problem in the Eisenberg-Gale program, then using directional derivatives of the equilibrium with respect to the contamination level to construct a first-order debiased surrogate. This departs from the broader network-interference A/B testing literature (Ugander et al., Aronow & Samii, Wager & Xu), which models interference through exposure mappings or regression adjustments but does not capture the competitive-equilibrium feedback that pacing multipliers create in auction markets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for large ad platforms (demonstrated on Meta production data) where increasing A/B test throughput via parallel submarket experiments is essential, and the 75–79% sign-agreement rate with the gold-standard budget-split design validates the approach. However, the paper is candid about limitations: finite-sample coverage of the debiased estimator falls below the nominal 95% level (around 83–88% for pacing multipliers), and the authors identify constructing better finite-sample variance estimators as an open problem. Extending the framework to autobidding models with non-uniform bid scaling, and to settings where submarket structure must be learned rather than assumed known, are natural next steps.
