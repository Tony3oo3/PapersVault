## Problem

Plug-and-Play (PnP) methods leverage pretrained denoisers for image restoration but struggle on generative tasks like inpainting, while Flow Matching (FM) models excel at generative sampling yet lack an efficient, principled mechanism for solving inverse problems. Existing FM-based restoration methods (OT-ODE, D-Flow, Flow-Priors) require expensive backpropagation through ODEs, trace computations, or rely on restrictive assumptions (Gaussian latent, independent coupling), limiting their efficiency and generality.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes a degraded observation y ∈ R^m (noisy, blurred, downsampled, or masked image), a known linear degradation operator H: R^d → R^m, and a pre-trained FM velocity field v^θ (a U-Net trained via OT Flow Matching or Rectified Flow). It outputs a single restored image x ∈ R^d after N time steps (typically 100), with the denoising step optionally averaged over 5 noise realizations for stability. The method is agnostic to the latent distribution P_0 (Gaussian, Dirichlet, etc.) and requires no backpropagation through the network—only forward passes of v^θ at each step.

## Method

The core contribution is a time-dependent denoiser D_t = Id + (1−t)v_t^θ derived from the FM velocity field, which in the ideal case computes E[X_1 | X_t = x], the best L² approximation of the clean image given the noisy path point. This denoiser is plugged into an adapted Forward-Backward Splitting algorithm that, at each time t_n, performs three steps: (1) a gradient step on the data-fidelity term with a time-decaying learning rate γ_n = (1−t_n)^α, (2) a linear interpolation z̃ = (1−t_n)ε + t_n z with ε ~ P_0 that reprojection the iterate onto the flow trajectory (critical because D_t is only well-defined on the path support), and (3) application of D_t to push the result toward the data distribution. Proposition 1 proves that the denoising loss vanishes if and only if the flow is straight-line, motivating the use of OT Flow Matching or Rectified Flows. The algorithm requires no backpropagation through ODEs, no Jacobian trace computations, and uses only ~0.1 GB peak GPU memory, making it substantially more efficient than competing FM-based methods.

## Results

On CelebA (128×128), PnP-Flow achieves the best PSNR in 4 out of 5 tasks: deblurring (34.51 dB), super-resolution (31.49 dB), random inpainting (33.54 dB), and box inpainting (30.59 dB), while tying PnP-GS on denoising (32.45 dB). On AFHQ-Cat (256×256), PnP-Flow leads in super-resolution (26.75 dB), random inpainting (32.98 dB), and box inpainting (26.87 dB). The method also uses only 0.10 GB peak GPU memory versus 0.65–5.91 GB for competing Flow Matching methods, with a per-image deblurring time of 3.40 s.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PnP-Flow outperforms all Flow Matching-based baselines (OT-ODE, D-Flow, Flow-Priors) across every task on both CelebA and AFHQ-Cat, with the largest margins in deblurring (+1.86 dB over PnP-GS on CelebA) and box inpainting (+0.89 dB over D-Flow). Against the PnP baselines, PnP-Flow ties PnP-GS on CelebA denoising (32.45 dB) but surpasses it by 0.86 dB in deblurring and 5.09 dB in random inpainting; PnP-Diff is competitive on denoising and super-resolution but is not applicable to box inpainting. Among diffusion-based methods (DDRM, DPS), PnP-Flow leads in PSNR on all four CelebA tasks (e.g., 34.51 vs. 31.44 dB in deblurring) and on AFHQ-Cat random inpainting (32.98 vs. 30.24 dB). Notably, PnP-Flow is the only method that ranks first or second in both PSNR and SSIM across all five tasks simultaneously, whereas D-Flow and Flow-Priors excel in inpainting but degrade sharply in denoising (26.42 and 29.26 dB respectively on CelebA). In terms of efficiency, PnP-Flow requires only 0.10 GB peak GPU memory and 3.40 s per image, compared to 5.91 GB and 32.19 s for D-Flow and 2.96 GB and 16.01 s for Flow-Priors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: Plug-and-Play (PnP) image restoration, which replaces proximal operators in Forward-Backward Splitting with off-the-shelf denoisers (Venkatakrishnan et al., 2013; Hurault et al., 2022), and Flow Matching, which learns a velocity field connecting a latent distribution to the data distribution (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023). The key departure from prior FM-based restoration methods (OT-ODE, D-Flow, Flow-Priors) is the construction of a simple time-dependent denoiser D_t = Id + (1-t)v_t^θ that can be plugged into an adapted FBS loop without backpropagating through ODEs or computing Jacobian traces. The interpolation/reprojection step onto flow trajectories is the critical algorithmic addition that makes the denoiser effective at intermediate times, drawing on the straight-line flow structure guaranteed by OT couplings (Pooladian et al., 2023; Tong et al., 2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method offers a compelling practical advantage: it achieves competitive or superior PSNR/SSIM across denoising, deblurring, super-resolution, and inpainting while using an order of magnitude less GPU memory (0.10 GB vs. 5.91 GB for D-Flow) and running ~10× faster, making it accessible for larger-scale restoration problems. Its generality—supporting non-Gaussian latents (Dirichlet), arbitrary straight-line flow models (Rectified Flows), and initialization-independence—positions it as a flexible framework rather than a single-task solution. Limitations include a tendency toward over-smoothed reconstructions (inherent to the MSE-optimal denoiser), evaluation only on 128×128 and 256×256 images, and the open question of whether the PnP-Flow loop can be extended to full posterior sampling or blind inverse problems.
