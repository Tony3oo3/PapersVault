## Problem

Prior neural game simulators (World Models, GameGAN) were limited in game complexity, simulation speed, long-horizon stability, or visual quality. This paper asks whether a neural model can run a complex game like DOOM in real time (20 FPS) on a single accelerator while maintaining visual fidelity comparable to the original game over multi-minute play sessions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252654/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a context of 64 past game frames (320×240, padded to 320×256) and 64 past key-press actions. Frames are encoded into 4-channel latents via a VAE autoencoder and concatenated along the channel dimension with the noised target latent; actions are mapped to single embedding tokens that replace the text cross-attention. The output is the next frame at 320×240, generated with 4 DDIM steps plus a VAE decode, yielding 20 FPS on a single TPU-v5.

## Method

GameNGen is a two-stage pipeline: first, a PPO agent learns to play DOOM (ViZDoom) and its full training trajectories (70M frames) are recorded as the dataset; second, a Stable Diffusion v1.4 U-Net is fine-tuned to predict the next frame conditioned on past frames (channel-concatenated latents) and past actions (token embeddings replacing text cross-attention), trained with velocity-parameterized diffusion loss. The critical architectural contribution is noise augmentation: during training, Gaussian noise at a random level (up to 0.7, discretized into 10 buckets with learned embeddings) is added to context latents, which prevents the error accumulation and quality collapse that plagues autoregressive sampling. A separate fine-tuning of the VAE decoder with pixel-level MSE loss improves small-detail fidelity (e.g., HUD text) without affecting the autoregressive latent conditioning. Inference uses 4 DDIM steps with classifier-free guidance (weight 1.5) applied only to the frame condition, and the model is trained for 700k steps on 128 TPU-v5e with Adafactor at lr 2e-5.

## Results

GameNGen achieves a PSNR of 29.43 and LPIPS of 0.249 for single-frame prediction (teacher forcing), comparable to lossy JPEG at quality 20–30. Under auto-regressive generation, FVD is 114.02 over 16 frames and 186.23 over 32 frames. Human raters identify the real game only 58% (1.6 s clips) and 60% (3.2 s clips) of the time, and drop to 50% (chance) after 5–10 minutes of auto-regressive play. The model runs at 20 FPS on a single TPU-v5 using just 4 DDIM sampling steps, with no measurable quality loss versus 20+ steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not include a direct quantitative comparison table against prior methods, but provides a qualitative side-by-side (Figure 2) showing GameNGen substantially outperforms World Models (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018) and GameGAN (Kim et al., 2020) in visual fidelity, temporal stability, and detail preservation over multi-minute trajectories. In the data-collection ablation (Table 3), training on agent-generated trajectories outperforms random-policy data by 0.64 PSNR on single-frame generation (25.06 vs. 24.42) and by 2.18 PSNR after 3 s of auto-regressive generation (19.02 vs. 16.84), with the largest gap on medium-difficulty scenes (20.21 vs. 16.50 PSNR). The noise-augmentation ablation (Figure 7) shows that without it, LPIPS degrades rapidly after 10–20 auto-regressive steps, confirming its critical role in long-horizon stability. The 4-step DDIM result (Table 1) demonstrates that the strong conditioning from 64 past frames makes additional denoising steps unnecessary, a property not shared by standard text-to-image diffusion models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Stable Diffusion v1.4 (Rombach et al., 2022) as a next-frame prediction engine, replacing text cross-attention with action embeddings and concatenating past frame latents as conditioning. It departs from prior game-simulation work (World Models, GameGAN, Genie) by using a pre-trained latent diffusion model rather than training from scratch, and introduces noise augmentation (following Ho et al., 2021) as the key mechanism for stable auto-regressive generation over long trajectories. The two-phase pipeline—first training an RL agent to collect diverse gameplay data, then fine-tuning the diffusion model on those trajectories—distinguishes it from approaches that train on random rollouts or human recordings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GameNGen demonstrates for the first time that a single neural model can run a complex, interactive game (DOOM) at 20 FPS on one TPU with quality indistinguishable from the original to human raters, opening a path toward 'games as neural weights' rather than hand-written code. Key limitations include a ~3-second context window that constrains long-horizon game state, residual gaps between agent and human behavior, and the inability to author new games. The finding that only 4 DDIM steps suffice (vs. 20+) for this task, and that noise augmentation is critical for auto-regressive stability, are broadly applicable insights for real-time and long-horizon diffusion-based generation.
