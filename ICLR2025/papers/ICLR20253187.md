## Problem

Current LLM evaluations and fine-tuning procedures (e.g., RLHF) are designed around single prompt-output interactions, yet LLMs are increasingly deployed in multi-turn and multi-agent settings where they consume and produce LLM-generated content iteratively. The paper asks whether small, negligible biases at the single-output level accumulate across iterated transmissions to produce qualitatively different distributions of text properties—potentially converging toward attractor states—that are invisible in single-turn evaluations.

## Input / Output

Each transmission chain takes a human-generated initial text (drawn from scientific abstracts, news articles, or social media comments to span the range of toxicity, positivity, difficulty, and length) and a fixed task instruction (rephrase, take inspiration, or continue), then iterates 50 generations across 5 independent seeds per initial text, yielding 100 chains per model-task combination. At every generation, four text properties are extracted: toxicity (Detoxify classifier, 0–1), positivity (VADER sentiment score, −1 to 1), difficulty (Gunning-Fog index, years of education), and length (character count). The primary outputs are per-generation property distributions, per-condition attractor position and strength estimates, and per-generation KS-test p-values comparing each generation's distribution against the first-generation distribution.

## Method

The paper adapts the transmission chain (telephone game) design from human cultural evolution to LLMs: a linear chain of agents each receives the previous agent's output plus a fixed task prompt and generates a new text, repeated for 50 generations. To quantify attractor dynamics, the authors fit a linear regression of the final-generation property value on the initial property value across all 100 chains; if the fitted slope s satisfies |s| < 1, the recurrent sequence converges to a fixed point l = I/(1−s) (attractor position) with convergence rate 1−s (attractor strength), providing a continuous [0,1] measure of attraction. Multi-turn effects are tested via Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests comparing each generation's property distribution against the first-generation distribution, and Bayesian hierarchical models (fitted with PyMC) disentangle the contributions of task, model, and property to attractor strength and position using 95% credible intervals. Robustness is verified by replicating with 100 initial texts, five prompt paraphrases, varied temperature settings, and Base-vs-Instruct model comparisons to isolate the effect of fine-tuning on attractor geometry.

## Results

Across all 72 experimental conditions (6 models × 3 tasks × 4 properties), the fitted linear recurrence converges, confirming the existence of a theoretical attractor in every case. Less constrained tasks produce significantly stronger attractors: Continue yields stronger attraction than Take Inspiration (95% CI [0.0840; 0.2824]) and Rephrase (95% CI [0.2195; 0.4187]). Toxicity exhibits significantly stronger attractors than length (95% CI [0.2422; 0.4747]), positivity (CI [0.1345; 0.3626]), and difficulty (CI [0.1274; 0.3584]). KS-test p-values decrease steadily across generations for most model-task-property combinations, confirming that single-turn evaluation is insufficient to predict multi-turn output properties.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baselines in the traditional ML sense; instead, it systematically compares the effects of task constraint level, model identity, and text property on attractor dynamics. On the task axis, Continue (least constrained) consistently produces the strongest attractors, followed by Take Inspiration, then Rephrase (most constrained), with all pairwise differences statistically significant. On the property axis, toxicity is the most 'attractor-prone' property, converging rapidly to near-zero values, while length shows the weakest attraction effects. On the model axis, Llama3-70B and GPT-4o-mini exhibit significantly weaker attraction than GPT-3.5, Llama3-8B, and Mixtral-8x7B, suggesting that model size and architecture influence how strongly outputs are pulled toward attractor states. Fine-tuning (Base → Instruct) shifts attractor positions for length (lower) and difficulty (higher) but has minimal effect on toxicity and positivity positions, while increasing attraction strength for toxicity and decreasing it for difficulty. A practical consequence highlighted is that single-turn evaluation can mislead model selection: e.g., Mistral-7B appears less toxic at generation 1, but Llama3-8B becomes substantially less toxic by generation 9 under the Take Inspiration task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the transmission chain design from human cultural evolution (Bartlett, 1932; Mesoudi, 2021) and the concept of cultural attractors from Cultural Attraction Theory (Sperber, 1985; Morin, 2016) to the domain of LLM evaluation. It extends the closest prior work by Acerbi and Stubbersfield (2023), who ran similar LLM transmission chains but focused on specific content biases (gender, positivity) rather than introducing a general formal framework. The paper deliberately departs from Bayesian Iterated Learning (Kirby et al., 2014) by not assuming LLMs possess human-like priors, and from model collapse literature (Shumailov et al., 2024) by studying frozen LLMs in chains rather than re-training on generated outputs. Its key methodological contribution is formalizing attractor position and strength as quantitative evaluation metrics for multi-turn LLM dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the widespread assumption that single-turn evaluation is sufficient for assessing LLMs, showing empirically that multi-turn interactions produce significantly different property distributions and that model rankings can reverse across generations (e.g., Mistral-7B vs. Llama3-8B for toxicity). The attractor position/strength framework offers a concrete, computable tool for multi-turn LLM evaluation that could complement existing single-turn benchmarks. Key limitations include the restriction to linear homogeneous chains (no network topologies), only six models, and four text properties; open questions include how network structure, heterogeneous model populations, and hybrid human-LLM interactions shape attractor dynamics, and whether the linear regression approximation holds for longer or more complex chains.
