## Problem

Prior work demonstrated that LLMs encode some truthfulness signals in their internal representations, but left critical questions unanswered: where exactly in the generated text these signals are concentrated, whether truthfulness encoding is universal across tasks or skill-specific, what fine-grained error information is encoded beyond binary correctness, and whether the model's internal knowledge aligns with its external behavior. This paper systematically addresses all four gaps through a model-centric analysis of intermediate activations.

## Input / Output

The method consumes intermediate hidden states (from the final MLP layer) at specific token positions within long-form LLM generations, with the key innovation being the identification of 'exact answer tokens'—the minimal token span whose modification would change the answer's correctness—extracted via heuristics or an instruct-LLM. Outputs include a binary correctness score (AUC), a five-way error type classification (refusal, consistently correct, consistently incorrect, two competing, many answers), and a selected answer from K=30 resampled candidates scored by the probe. The white-box setting requires access to all intermediate layer activations, restricting applicability to open-source models.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a linear probing classifier (logistic regression with L2 penalty) trained on intermediate LLM representations at the exact answer tokens, rather than the conventional last-generated-token or end-of-prompt positions. The paper first establishes that truthfulness signals peak at these answer-specific tokens across layers and datasets, yielding AUC improvements of 5–15 points over prior token choices. It then demonstrates that probes trained on one task do not meaningfully outperform logit-based baselines when transferred to tasks requiring different skills, refuting the hypothesis of a universal truthfulness direction. For error type prediction, a one-to-many probing setup classifies five behavioral error categories from greedy-decoding representations. Finally, the probe is used as a diagnostic tool to select the most likely correct answer from 30 resampled candidates, revealing that the model internally encodes the correct answer even when it consistently generates an incorrect one externally.

## Results

Probing at exact answer tokens achieves the highest AUC across all model-dataset combinations, reaching 0.92 on Winobias and Math (Mistral-7b-Instruct) and 0.95 on Math (Llama3-8b-Instruct). This consistently outperforms the strongest logit-based baseline (Logits-min-exact) by 0.10–0.24 AUC points and the p(True) prompting baseline by 0.12–0.33 AUC points. The advantage is most pronounced on Winobias (0.92 vs. 0.59 for Logits-min) and Math (0.92 vs. 0.71 for Logits-min-exact), while the gap narrows on TriviaQA (0.85 vs. 0.75). Across all 10 datasets and 4 models tested, probing at exact answer tokens is the best-performing method in nearly every setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's probing-at-exact-answer-tokens method dominates all four baseline families across every model-dataset pair in Table 1. Against Kadavath et al.'s p(True) prompting, the probe wins by 0.11–0.33 AUC (e.g., 0.92 vs. 0.45 on Winobias/Mistral), and the gap widens further when p(True) is restricted to exact tokens (0.40 on Winobias). Against Guerreiro et al.'s and Varshney et al.'s logit-aggregation baselines (Logits-mean, Logits-min), the probe leads by 0.10–0.33 AUC; the strongest logit variant (Logits-min-exact) narrows the gap on TriviaQA (0.75 vs. 0.85) and Math/Llama3 (0.89 vs. 0.95) but still trails by 0.10–0.39 on other settings. Against Slobodkin et al.'s probe-at-end-of-question approach, the exact-answer probe improves by 0.05–0.23 AUC, confirming that the truthfulness signal is localized in the answer tokens rather than the prompt. Notably, the probe at the last generated token (a common prior-work choice) already outperforms all non-probing baselines, but the exact-answer location adds a further 0.09–0.14 AUC gain, validating the paper's central token-selection contribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work showing that LLMs encode truthfulness signals in their internal representations (Kadavath et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024) by identifying where those signals are concentrated (exact answer tokens) and what they encode beyond binary correctness. It departs from the 'universal truthfulness direction' hypothesis (Marks & Tegmark, 2023; Slobodkin et al., 2023) by demonstrating that truthfulness encoding is skill-specific rather than task-agnostic, and extends probing classifiers (Belinkov, 2021) from error detection to fine-grained error type classification and answer selection from resampled candidates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that truthfulness encoding is multifaceted and skill-specific has direct practical implications: deploying a single trained error detector across diverse tasks is unreliable, and task-specific probes are needed for critical applications. The discrepancy between internal encoding and external generation (the model 'knows' the correct answer but produces an incorrect one) opens a promising direction for error mitigation by leveraging existing internal knowledge. Key limitations include the requirement for white-box model access, restriction to QA tasks with clear gold labels, and the open question of how these findings extend to open-ended generation tasks like summarization.
