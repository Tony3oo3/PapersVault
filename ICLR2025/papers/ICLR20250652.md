## Problem

Diffusion models for solving inverse problems have traditionally required training on clean, fully observed data, which is infeasible in many imaging applications such as MRI where fully sampled k-space is unavailable. While Ambient Diffusion demonstrated how to learn generative models from linearly corrupted measurements, it remained unexplored whether such models could be used to perform posterior sampling on measurements from a *different* forward operator than the one encountered during training. This paper addresses that gap, showing that corrupted-data-trained models can actually outperform clean-data-trained models in high-corruption regimes.

## Input / Output

For MRI, the method consumes multi-coil Fourier subsampled k-space measurements at retrospective acceleration factors R∈{2,4,6,8} (with 20 central autocalibration lines always retained) and outputs reconstructed images via 500-step posterior sampling. For natural images (CelebA, FFHQ, AFHQ), training uses randomly inpainted data with erasure probabilities p∈{0.2,0.4,0.6,0.8}, while inference evaluates on different corruption types (Gaussian compressed sensing, super-resolution, deblurring) to test cross-corruption generalization. The critical design choice is that the training forward operator A_train and inference operator A_inf are drawn from different distributions, making the method applicable whenever clean reference data is unavailable.

## Method

The core algorithm, A-DPS, modifies the Diffusion Posterior Sampling update rule by replacing the standard score term E[x₀|x_t] with the ambient score E[x₀|ỹ_train, Ã_train], which is estimated by a neural network trained under the Ambient Diffusion objective. For the MRI extension, the authors introduce additional corruption in the Fourier domain (randomly removing extra k-space lines beyond the training subsampling pattern) and prove via Theorem 2.1 that the training objective's minimizer recovers the conditional expectation, provided E[A_train|P̃] is full-rank. The multi-coil measurements are aggregated through the adjoint operator (zero-filling reconstruction) to form a single image-domain signal, with the further-corrupted sampling mask P̃ concatenated as an additional input channel. A surprising empirical result is that models trained at high acceleration (R=6,8) produce better priors for high-acceleration reconstruction than models trained on fully sampled data, suggesting that exposure to corruption during training yields more robust priors for severely ill-posed problems. The framework also supports a one-step (A-OS) variant for in-distribution reconstruction and can be paired with Annealed Langevin Dynamics for multi-step sampling.

## Results

On accelerated MRI reconstruction (FastMRI, 100 test samples), A-DPS trained on R=8 subsampled data achieves NRMSE 0.126, SSIM 89.50, and PSNR 32.10 dB at R=8 inference, outperforming FS-DPS (NRMSE 0.179, SSIM 85.21, PSNR 29.46) by a wide margin. In the conditional FID evaluation at R=8, A-DPS (trained at R=2) achieves 0.71 FID versus 4.16 for FS-DPS. At lower accelerations (R=2,4), FS-DPS still leads (e.g., NRMSE 0.034 vs. 0.058 at R=2), but the crossover occurs around R=6 where A-DPS begins to match or exceed clean-data-trained models. On natural images (AFHQ), ambient models trained with inpainting (p=0.6–0.8) outperform clean-data models in compressed sensing below ~1000 measurements and in super-resolution at downscaling factors ≥4×.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against FS-DPS (Chung et al., 2023), A-DPS loses at R=2 (NRMSE 0.058 vs. 0.034) and R=4 (0.074 vs. 0.065) but decisively wins at R=8 (NRMSE 0.126 vs. 0.179; PSNR 32.10 vs. 29.46 dB), with the conditional FID gap even larger (0.71 vs. 4.16 for A-DPS trained at R=2). Against FS-ALD (Jalal et al., 2021), A-DPS (R=8) achieves NRMSE 0.126 vs. 0.157 and PSNR 32.10 vs. 31.22 at R=8. Against L1-CS (Lustig et al., 2007), A-DPS dominates at all accelerations ≥4 (e.g., R=8: NRMSE 0.126 vs. 0.259). Against supervised MoDL (Aggarwal et al., 2019), MoDL(R=8) retains a slight edge at R=8 (NRMSE 0.113 vs. 0.126), but A-DPS is competitive and does not require any clean training data. Against self-supervised SSDU (Yaman et al., 2020) and 1D-Partitioned SSDU (Millard & Chiew, 2023), A-DPS wins at R=8 on all three metrics (e.g., vs. SSDU: NRMSE 0.126 vs. 0.177; vs. 1D-Part. SSDU: NRMSE 0.126 vs. 0.148). For unconditional generation (Table 1), Ambient Diffusion at R=6 and R=8 achieves FID 31.50 and 48.15 respectively, outperforming L1-EDM (51.43 and 102.98) but remaining behind EDM trained on clean data (FID 10.41).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: Ambient Diffusion (Daras et al., 2023b), which learns a generative model for the clean distribution from linearly corrupted measurements, and Diffusion Posterior Sampling (DPS, Chung et al., 2023), which uses a diffusion model as a prior to sample from the posterior of an inverse problem. The key conceptual departure is that prior work either trained on clean data and solved inverse problems (DPS, ALD) or trained on corrupted data but only for in-distribution restoration (Ambient Diffusion, SSDU, MoDL). This paper is the first to show that a model trained on one type of corruption (e.g., random inpainting) can serve as a prior for an entirely different forward operator (e.g., Gaussian compressed sensing or Fourier subsampling), and it extends the Ambient Diffusion training objective to the structured, multi-coil Fourier-domain setting of MRI.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for domains like MRI, astronomy, and dynamic imaging where fully sampled training data is infeasible or impossible to collect; this work shows that useful generative priors can still be learned and deployed for reconstruction. The surprising empirical finding that corrupted-trained models outperform clean-trained models in the high-corruption regime challenges the default assumption that more data quality is always better, yet the paper acknowledges no theoretical explanation for this cross-over. Key open questions include whether the result generalizes beyond the DPS solver to other recent algorithms (DDRM, DPS variants), whether the effect persists at larger scales, and how to handle the shared auto-calibration region that may bias training.
