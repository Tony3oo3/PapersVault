## Problem

Current LLM-based human simulations are limited to group-level studies, produce inconsistent performance across tasks, and lack the depth to capture complex individual personality traits. No comprehensive benchmark exists that grounds personification in established psychological theory and provides rigorous evaluation from both self and third-party perspectives. The paper asks: how far are LLMs from replacing human subjects in psychological and sociological experiments?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250400/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a virtual character's detailed life story (up to ~12k tokens per character from the 129k-text Human Simulacra dataset), structured character attributes (including an 8-dimensional Jungian personality ranking with 20 trait descriptions), and a stimulus (self-report questionnaire item, observer-report scenario, or Asch conformity trial prompt) as input. It produces a personified text response scored either by exact match (self-report) or by human-judge cross-evaluation on description matching and response similarity (observer report). The conformity experiment additionally records binary correct/incorrect line-matching judgments across 18 trials with 12 critical trials under group pressure.

## Method

The paper introduces three interlocking contributions. First, a human-in-the-loop dataset construction pipeline that iteratively expands character biographies over 50+ rounds using GPT-3.5-Turbo, with psychology and CS graduate reviewers checking each iteration for bias and coherence, yielding 11 virtual characters with 640 possible personality descriptions grounded in Jung's eight-dimensional theory. Second, a Multi-Agent Cognitive Mechanism (MACM) comprising four LLM agents—Top Agent (coordination), Thinking Agent (logical analysis and content memory), Emotion Agent (emotional analysis and affective memory), and Memory Agent (retrieval from structured long-term memory)—that first transforms the narrative life story into structured long-term memories (participants, scenes, thoughts, feelings) and then, upon receiving a stimulus, retrieves relevant memories into working memory, performs parallel logical and emotional analysis, and synthesizes a response. Third, a two-phase psychology-guided evaluation combining character-specific self-report questionnaires (cloze, single-choice, multiple-choice) with observer reports where four human judges independently describe the simulacrum's personality from scenario responses and compare them against the target character's life story, followed by a replication of the Asch conformity experiment to test group-pressure behavior.

## Results

On self-report evaluations, GPT-4-Turbo with MACM achieves the highest score of 88.00 (out of 100), approaching the human baseline of 100.00, while the same model without any auxiliary method scores only 12.00. On observer reports (third-party human judges), MACM achieves a final score of 77.50, outperforming Prompt (69.00) and RAG (65.50) on GPT-4-Turbo. In the Asch conformity replication, MACM-based simulacra exhibit human-like bandwagon effects with personality-dependent conformity, whereas Character.ai shows 0% correct rate across all critical trials (complete submission to group pressure).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four simulation methods (None, Prompt, RAG, MACM) across 14 LLMs. MACM consistently outperforms all other methods for strong models: GPT-4-Turbo gains +2.67 over RAG and +9.33 over Prompt on self-report, and +8.50 over Prompt and +12.00 over RAG on observer report. For weaker models (e.g., Llama-2-7b, Vicuna-7b), RAG actually underperforms Prompt, suggesting that retrieved context overwhelms limited processing capacity. In the Asch conformity experiment, MACM on GPT-4-Turbo is the only method that stably reproduces the bandwagon effect (rated 'Yes'), while Prompt is 'unstable' and RAG/None fail entirely. Character.ai, a commercial simulation platform, shows complete submission to group pressure (0% correct rate), whereas MACM captures personality-dependent conformity (resolute characters resist, compliant characters yield) with a more rigid but directionally correct pattern compared to human variability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from existing LLM role-playing work (Character-LLM, Role-LLM) that simulates real or fictional characters from fragmented data, instead constructing virtual characters from scratch with complete life stories grounded in Jung's eight-dimensional personality theory. It also extends the emerging line of LLMs-as-human-participants in social science (Demszky et al., Dillion et al., Grossmann et al.) by introducing a psychology-guided two-phase evaluation (self-report + observer-report) and a Multi-Agent Cognitive Mechanism (MACM) inspired by cognitive psychology memory models (Atkinson & Shiffrin, Baddeley & Hitch). The authors explicitly reject MBTI-based personality modeling as insufficiently nuanced, opting for a 640-description trait system derived from Jung's complementary tendencies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first benchmark specifically targeting deep personification of LLMs with psychology-expert supervision, providing a structured testbed for the question of whether LLMs can replace human participants in psychological experiments. The notable gap between self-report performance (88% on GPT-4-Turbo) and observer-report performance (77.75% even with MACM) reveals that factual knowledge of a character is insufficient for consistent behavioral embodiment, and the 'robotic' conformity behavior (binary resolute/submissive rather than gradual yielding) highlights a fundamental limitation in current LLM simulations. Open questions include whether fine-tuning LLM parameters to align with target character values can close the observer-report gap, whether the approach scales beyond 11 characters, and whether the high cost of dataset construction (5+ days per character, ~$15 per character) is sustainable for broader experimental use.
