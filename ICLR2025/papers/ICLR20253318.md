## Problem

Current T2I models treat text prompts as bags of words, generating nearly identical images for semantically different word-order permutations (e.g., 'a cat chasing a mouse' vs. 'a mouse chasing a cat'). Existing evaluations rely on indirect text-image alignment scores that mask this failure by rewarding frequent word combinations while obscuring poor handling of complex or uncommon linguistic patterns. No direct metric exists to measure whether a T2I model causally responds to word-order-induced semantic changes in its outputs.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation sample is a triplet of sentences (T_a, T_pv, T_pi) sharing identical words but differing in order—T_pv changes meaning (permutation-variance) while T_pi preserves it (permutation-invariance). The T2I model generates one image per sentence, and four MLLM evaluators (GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Pro, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, GPT-4 Turbo) score each text-image pair on object accuracy (0–50) and relation/triple accuracy (0–50), normalized to [0,1]. The final output is the SemVarEffect score κ (theoretically bounded 0–1), decomposed into γ_w (visual variation under semantic change; higher is better) and γ_wo (visual variation under semantic maintenance; lower is better).

## Method

The paper frames semantic variation evaluation as a causal inference problem, defining the average causal effect (ACE) of input semantic variation on output visual variation. Two interventions are applied: do(T≠T_a) substitutes a permutation-variance sentence (meaning changes) and do(T=T_a) substitutes a permutation-invariance sentence (meaning preserved); the SemVarEffect score κ equals the difference between the resulting visual semantic variations, isolating the causal contribution of meaning change from mere word-order noise. The SemVarBench benchmark (11,454 samples; 648 test) is constructed from 171 Winoground seed pairs via template-guided LLM generation for T_a, rule-based word swapping for T_pv, and paraphrasing for T_pi, followed by 14-criteria LLM-plus-human annotation. A key empirical finding is that cross-modal alignment in the image generator (UNet or Transformer), not the text encoder, is the primary bottleneck: models with weaker text encoders (e.g., FLUX.1 with CLIP-T5) can outperform those with stronger ones (e.g., PixArt with T5-XXL) when the cross-attention mechanism fails to transfer token-level semantic distinctions to visual regions.

## Results

On SemVarBench (648 test samples), the best-performing T2I models—CogView-3-Plus and Ideogram 2—achieve a SemVarEffect (κ) score of only 0.20/1.0 under GPT-4o evaluation, far below the ideal of 1.0. All 13 evaluated models score below 0.20 on κ, indicating that no current model reliably captures semantic variations caused by word order changes. Despite high text-image alignment scores (S̄ ranging 0.55–0.83), visual semantic variation under semantic change (γ_w) remains below 0.52 for all models, while γ_wo (which should approach 0) is unexpectedly high, revealing that models overreact to meaning-preserving permutations. Semantic variations in object relations are particularly poorly handled (κ ≈ 0.07) compared to attribute values (κ ≈ 0.19).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 13 T2I models (9 open-source, 4 API-based) on the proposed SemVarBench using SemVarEffect (κ) as the primary metric. CogView-3-Plus and Ideogram 2 lead with κ = 0.20 (GPT-4o), followed by DALL-E 3 (0.13) and FLUX.1 (0.10); all open-source diffusion models score below 0.10. Notably, higher alignment scores do not guarantee better semantic variation handling: DALL-E 3 achieves the highest S̄ (0.82) but a lower κ (0.13) than CogView-3-Plus (S̄ = 0.81, κ = 0.20), suggesting that cross-modal alignment in the image generator (UNet/Transformer) matters more than text encoder quality alone. The paper further shows that fine-tuning (SFT or DPO) on SemVarBench training data improves sensitivity (γ_w) but degrades robustness (γ_wo), netting only marginal κ gains in data-rich categories (Color: 0.08→0.18) and actually hurting performance in data-sparse categories (Direction: 0.05→0.01). Across semantic variation types, all models handle Color variations best (κ > 0.4 in many cases) but struggle severely with Relation (κ ≈ 0.07) and Attribute Comparison (κ ≈ 0.13) categories.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Winoground paradigm of permutation-based semantic variation testing—previously applied to VLM retrieval and matching—into the T2I generation setting, where the challenge shifts from discriminating between two images to measuring whether a single model's output responds causally to input semantic changes. It introduces a causal inference framework (average causal effect via do-calculus) to formalize what prior T2I evaluations captured only indirectly through alignment scores, and it explicitly departs from the field's focus on text encoder quality by showing that cross-modal alignment in the image generator (UNet or Transformer) is the critical bottleneck. The benchmark construction draws on Winoground's 171 compositional seed pairs but extends them with template-guided LLM generation, rule-based permutation, and a 14-criteria annotation protocol tailored to T2I visualizability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even SOTA models (CogView-3-Plus, Ideogram 2) achieve only 0.2/1 on SemVarEffect, with Relation categories scoring just 0.07, reveals a fundamental gap in T2I models' compositional understanding that alignment-based metrics have masked. The observation that FLUX.1 with weaker text encoders outperforms PixArt/Kolors with stronger ones on semantic variation shifts the research focus from text encoder design to cross-modal alignment mechanisms—a direction the paper argues has been overlooked. Open questions include whether architectural changes to cross-attention (e.g., capturing inter-token dependencies) can close the gap, and whether the causal evaluation framework generalizes beyond word-order permutations to other types of semantic variation (negation, quantification, counterfactuals).
