## Problem

Direct Alignment Algorithms (DAAs) like DPO rely on off-policy preference data collected before training, which causes two issues: the policy model overfits to spurious correlations (e.g., response length) present in the dataset rather than learning the intended alignment signal, and the model receives no feedback on its own on-policy generations during training. Existing remedies—RLAIF with GPT-4 scoring or self-rewarding LLMs—are either prohibitively expensive or require large models with strong instruction-following capability, limiting applicability to smaller LLMs.

## Input / Output

SeRA takes a standard offline preference dataset of (prompt, chosen, rejected) triples, an SFT-initialized policy model, and a DAA loss function (DPO, IPO, SLiC-HF, or SimPO) as input, and outputs an aligned policy model after T=3 iterative rounds. At each iteration t≥2, the training set D_t is constructed by selecting the top-k off-policy samples by IRM and augmenting them with k̃ bootstrapped preference pairs generated from the current policy's own sampled responses, with the reference model updated to the latest policy after each round. The method requires no external reward model, no LLM-as-a-judge API calls, and no additional human annotation beyond the original offline dataset.

## Method

SeRA introduces the Implicit Reward Margin (IRM), defined as the difference in log-ratios between chosen and rejected responses under the current policy versus the reference model (Eq. 5), and uses it for two purposes. First, IRM-based sample selection retains only the top-k off-policy samples with the largest margins, filtering out ambiguous pairs that drive over-optimization to spurious features like response length—supported by a theoretical risk bound (Theorem 1) showing that training on confident samples reduces the gap between estimated and true preferences. Second, IRM-based preference bootstrapping samples R candidate responses from the current policy for each prompt, computes implicit rewards for each, and selects the pair maximizing the IRM as a new on-policy preference pair, thereby closing the train-inference distribution gap without external supervision. To mitigate reward hacking from self-generated signals, SeRA ensembles reward margins across the last three policy iterations (Eq. 7) with a coefficient γ, and the entire framework is DAA-agnostic since all supported losses (BCE for DPO, hinge for SLiC-HF, squared for IPO) operate on the same IRM term.

## Results

On AlpacaEval (pairwise win rate vs. SFT), SeRA-DPO achieves 74.2% on TinyLlama-1.1B, 70.7% on Pythia-2.8B, and 86.9% on Mistral-7B, consistently outperforming DPO (55.1%, 53.0%, 71.4%) and Iterative DPO (64.5%, 63.2%, 89.0%). The best-SeRA-DPO variant (with 2N bootstrapped samples) reaches 92.7% on Mistral-7B AlpacaEval-SFT and 90.6% on UltraFeedback-SFT. On noisy preference data (40% label swap), SeRA-DPO maintains a clear advantage over DPO, Iterative DPO, cDPO, and rDPO across all four benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SeRA-DPO outperforms DPO by 19.1 pp (TinyLlama) to 15.5 pp (Mistral) on AlpacaEval-SFT, and beats Iterative DPO by 9.7 pp on TinyLlama and 7.5 pp on Pythia, though Iterative DPO edges out SeRA-DPO by 2.1 pp on Mistral-7B (89.0 vs. 86.9). Against Curri-DPO, SeRA-DPO leads by 11.9 pp (TinyLlama) and 0.9 pp (Mistral) on AlpacaEval-SFT. On Mistral-7B, SeRA-DPO surpasses SPIN by 21.8 pp and ORPO by 2.8 pp on AlpacaEval-SFT. Extending to other DAAs (Table 2), SeRA-IPO improves over Iterative IPO by 3.3 pp (Pythia AlpacaEval-SFT) and SeRA-SLiC improves over Iterative SLiC by 2.0 pp (Pythia). On HH-RLHF and TL;DR (Table 3), SeRA with bootstrapping (k̃=0.3N) beats OAIF (which requires a separate Mistral-7B reward model) by 1.5 pp and 3.2 pp respectively, at substantially lower cost. On 40% noisy UltraFeedback (Figure 3), SeRA-DPO maintains a clear lead over cDPO and rDPO, confirming that IRM-based selection effectively filters swapped-preference samples.

## Relation to Prior Work

SeRA builds directly on the implicit reward formulation of DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), extending it to IPO (Azar et al., 2024) and SLiC-HF (Zhao et al., 2023) by recognizing that all DAAs share the IRM term in their loss functions. It departs from RLAIF-based approaches (DNO, OAIF) that rely on expensive external LLM API calls for preference annotation, and from self-rewarding LMs (Yuan et al., 2024) that require LLM-as-a-Judge prompting only viable for instruction-following-capable models. Instead, SeRA uses the policy model's own implicit reward margins—computed at no additional inference cost—for both off-policy sample filtering and on-policy preference pair construction, positioning itself as a drop-in augmentation to any existing DAA.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SeRA offers a practical, cost-efficient path to improve DAA-based alignment without external reward models or proprietary API access, which is significant for resource-constrained settings and smaller LLMs where LLM-as-a-Judge approaches fail. The theoretical grounding (Theorem 1) connecting IRM-based selection to reduced risk upper bounds provides a principled justification beyond empirical observation. Limitations include the reliance on the policy model's potentially miscalibrated implicit rewards (mitigated but not eliminated by the ensemble mechanism), diminishing returns beyond 2N bootstrapped samples suggesting diversity issues in self-generated data, and modest gains when combined with IPO which already addresses over-optimization. Open questions include whether the approach scales to much larger models, whether more sophisticated decoding strategies can push the bootstrapping ceiling higher, and whether the ensemble coefficient γ can be adapted dynamically rather than fixed.
