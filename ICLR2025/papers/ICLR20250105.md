## Problem

Existing conditional independence (CI) tests assume direct observation of the variables being tested, but in many real-world settings (finance, mental health, entertainment) continuous variables are only available in discretized form. The paper proves that discretization inevitably distorts CI relationships (Theorem 2.1): even when X₁ ⊥ X₃ | X₂ holds for the latent continuous variables, X₁ ⊥̸ X₃ | X̃₂ does not hold for the observed discretized variable, causing standard tests to reach incorrect conclusions.

## Input / Output

The method accepts a mixed-observation matrix where some columns are continuous and others are discretized (with arbitrary cardinality ≥ 2), along with the tested pair indices, conditioning set, and significance level α. It internally binarizes all discretized variables at their sample-mean boundary to construct bridge equations, then outputs a p-value and reject/fail-to-reject decision for the CI hypothesis, plus the full estimated covariance matrix Σ̂ and nodewise regression coefficients β̂ that serve as surrogates for precision matrix entries. The binarization step means the method requires more samples than a tailored per-cardinality approach would, as noted by the authors.

## Method

DCT estimates the latent covariance σ_ij from discretized observations via bridge equations of the form τ̂_ij = T(σ̂_ij; {ĥ_i, ĥ_j}), where τ̂_ij is the empirical probability that both binarized variables exceed their sample means and T is the bivariate normal tail probability Φ̄(h_i, h_j; σ_ij); the boundary ĥ_j is recovered from the univariate tail probability via the inverse normal CDF. A unified binarization trick merges the discretized-discretized and continuous-discretized cases into a single equation form. To obtain the CI test statistic, the paper applies nodewise regression, showing that the regression coefficient β_jk = −ω_jk/ω_jj is a valid surrogate for the precision entry ω_jk, and then expresses β̂_jk − β_jk as a linear combination of the already-derived asymptotically normal covariance estimation errors ξ_ij^l. The asymptotic null distribution of √n(β̂_jk − β_jk) is derived via the Z-estimator framework (Taylor expansion + CLT + Slutsky's lemma), yielding a Gaussian with a sandwich variance that accounts for the dependence among the covariance estimation errors across the conditioning set.

## Results

On Type I error control, DCT maintains a false positive rate close to the nominal 0.05 across all three data types (continuous, mixed, discrete) and across varying sample sizes (n = 100–2000) and conditioning set cardinalities (D = 1–5), whereas Fisher-z and Chi-square baselines show significantly inflated Type I error rates that grow with sample size and conditioning set size. On Type II error (1 − power), DCT requires approximately n = 1000 samples to reach satisfactory power, particularly in mixed and continuous cases, while baselines achieve higher power at smaller sample sizes. In causal discovery (PC algorithm), DCT achieves F1, precision, and SHD nearly on par with the theoretical upper bound (Fisher-z applied to the original continuous data) when p is small and n is large, while significantly outperforming Fisher-z on discretized data and Chi-square on all skeleton and DAG discovery metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DCT against two baselines: the Fisher-z test applied to discretized observations and the Chi-square test (discrete data only), with Fisher-z on the original continuous data serving as a theoretical upper bound. On Type I error, DCT is the only method that maintains the nominal 0.05 significance level; both Fisher-z and Chi-square produce substantially higher false positive rates that worsen as sample size and conditioning set cardinality increase. On Type II error (power), the baselines actually outperform DCT at small sample sizes (n < 1000), likely because DCT's binarization step discards information; however, DCT's power stabilizes and remains robust as the conditioning set grows, while baselines degrade. In the causal discovery experiment (PC algorithm on Bipartite Pairing DAGs), DCT achieves F1, precision, and SHD scores nearly matching the Fisher-z-on-continuous upper bound, while Fisher-z on discretized data and Chi-square both perform substantially worse. DCT's recall is consistently lower than the baselines, which is expected because the baselines tend to infer spurious conditional dependencies from discretized observations and retain extra edges (a fully connected graph would score recall = 1).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the nonparanormal model framework (Liu et al., 2009) for handling discretized observations by introducing bridge equations that recover the latent continuous covariance from discrete data, and combines it with nodewise regression (Callot et al., 2019) to derive a full conditional independence test. It departs from the large body of CI testing literature (Fisher-z, kernel-based, chi-square) by explicitly addressing the setting where only discretized proxies of continuous variables are observed, showing via Theorem 2.1 that existing tests are fundamentally inconsistent in this regime. The Z-estimator asymptotic normality framework (van der Vaart, 2000) provides the theoretical backbone for deriving the null distribution of the test statistic.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Discretization is ubiquitous in real-world data (financial ratings, clinical scales, viewer scores), and the paper demonstrates that naively applying standard CI tests to such data leads to systematically incorrect causal structure recovery. The practical impact is significant for constraint-based causal discovery (e.g., PC algorithm), where DCT achieves performance near the theoretical upper bound of testing on the true continuous data. Key limitations include the multivariate Gaussian assumption on latent variables, the binarization step that discards ordinal information (requiring larger sample sizes for adequate power), and the restriction to monotone discretization. Open questions include developing tailored bridge equations for multi-level discrete variables to improve sample efficiency, extending to non-Gaussian latent distributions, and handling unknown or non-monotone discretization functions.
