## Problem

Standard manifold learning methods such as Diffusion Maps are inherently unsupervised and fail to exploit label information available in many practical settings. Existing supervised and semi-supervised variants typically modify distance metrics or add label-penalty terms to the objective, but they treat labels as a distortion of the data geometry rather than as a separate geometric structure. This paper asks how to properly fuse the intrinsic geometry of the data with the intrinsic geometry of the labels to produce embeddings that are more informative for downstream tasks.

## Input / Output

The method takes n labeled training samples (x_i, y_i) ∈ R^d × Y and m unlabeled test samples x̄_j ∈ R^d, and produces low-dimensional embeddings Ψ(x_i), Ψ(x̄_j) ∈ R^ℓ (ℓ < d) whose Euclidean distances approximate label-driven diffusion distances. For classification, labels are discrete and a class-based distance metric is used; for regression, labels are continuous with standard Euclidean distance. Unlabeled test samples are handled by inserting isolated nodes into the label kernel, so the method naturally accommodates partial label availability without requiring label imputation.

## Method

The core idea is to treat labels as a second data view and build two separate affinity kernels: a data kernel D (Gaussian kernel on pairwise data distances) and a label kernel P (Gaussian kernel on pairwise label distances, with zero off-diagonal entries for unlabeled samples). The key mechanism is a multiplicative interpolation Γ(t) = P^(1−t) D^t for t ∈ [0,1], which defines a two-step Markov chain that first diffuses on the label graph and then on the data graph — termed 'label-driven diffusion.' The embedding is extracted from the right eigenvectors of Γ(t), weighted by their eigenvalues, following the standard Diffusion Maps spectral construction. The hyperparameter t controls the balance between label and data structure, with small t emphasizing label geometry and large t recovering the unsupervised data geometry. Theoretical analysis via ε-pseudo-spectra shows that the available kernel PD closely approximates the inaccessible fully-labeled kernel LD, with error bounded by O(1/√N) where N is the neighborhood size. SSDM (semi-supervised) constructs a single kernel over all n+m samples for O(n³) runtime, while SDM (supervised) processes each test sample individually at O(n⁴) cost for slightly better accuracy.

## Results

On the supervised setting (7 datasets), SDM achieves the best error on all datasets, with particularly large gains on Yacht (NMSE 0.12 vs. 0.208 for LE) and Ionosphere (misclassification 0.073 vs. 0.108 for DM). On the semi-supervised setting (5 datasets), SSDM achieves the best error on 4 out of 5, with notable improvements on Concrete (NMSE 0.036 vs. 0.051 for LLE) and Silhouettes (misclassification 0.248 vs. 0.297 for LLE). The only dataset where SSDM does not win is Mice, where LLE achieves 0.002 misclassification rate versus SSDM's 0.016. On the toy problem, SSDM achieves an angular MAE of 4.1° (R²=0.99) versus 79° for unsupervised DM and 85.35° for AD interpolation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SDM/SSDM consistently outperforms unsupervised Diffusion Maps (DM) on all 12 datasets, with the largest gap on Mice (0.016 vs. 0.162 misclassification) and Raisin (0.176 vs. 0.26). Against the supervised/semi-supervised baselines S/SUMAP and SStSNE, SDM/SSDM wins on every dataset where both methods are applicable, e.g., Ionosphere (0.073 vs. 0.155 for SUMAP) and Silhouettes (0.248 vs. 0.315 for SStSNE). Among unsupervised methods, SDM/SSDOM also beats UMAP, Isomap, t-SNE, LE, and LLE on nearly all datasets; the sole exception is Mice in the semi-supervised setting, where LLE achieves 0.002 misclassification versus SSDM's 0.016. On the toy problem, the proposed interpolation scheme (MAE 4.1°, R²=0.99) dramatically outperforms the simpler AD-style product kernel (MAE 85.35°, R²=−0.01), confirming that the tunable interpolation parameter t is critical. SSDM also runs significantly faster than SDM (e.g., 0.1 s vs. 5.5 s on Iris; 3 s vs. 934 s on Mice) while maintaining comparable accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Diffusion Maps (Coifman & Lafon, 2006) into supervised and semi-supervised settings by borrowing the multiplicative kernel product framework from Alternating Diffusion (Lederman & Talmon, 2018), which was originally designed for two aligned data views. The key conceptual departure from prior supervised manifold learning (which typically modifies distance metrics, adds inter/intra-class penalty terms, or splits objectives) is treating labels as a second data view and extracting the common geometry between data and label spaces via a tunable interpolation Γ(t) = P^(1-t) D^t. This extends AD in two ways: accommodating partially aligned kernels (unlabeled test samples appear as isolated nodes in the label kernel) and introducing a hyperparameter t that balances the influence of the two views in the frequency domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, theoretically grounded approach to label-aware spectral embedding with explicit approximation guarantees (pseudo-spectrum bounds) for the partially aligned setting, filling a gap where prior supervised manifold methods lacked such analysis. However, the practical impact is limited by computational cost (SDM is O(n⁴), SSDM is O(n³)), the narrow scope to spectral/kernel methods, and the acknowledged failure mode when data and label geometries do not share structure (demonstrated on the Customers dataset). Open questions include whether the label-driven diffusion framework can be adapted to geometric deep learning architectures and whether the optimized O(k²·n) variant maintains quality at very large scale.
