## Problem

Prior model-free OPE methods for POMDPs with large observation spaces achieve polynomial sample complexity only for memoryless target policies, and extending to general history-dependent policies was an open problem. This paper asks: under what combinations of revealing assumptions (single-step vs. multi-step) and behavior-policy structure (memoryless vs. history-dependent) can polynomial sample complexity be achieved for OPE of history-dependent target policies, and is there a fundamental separation between model-free and model-based approaches?

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes an offline dataset of n full trajectories (o_1, a_1, ..., o_H, a_H) collected by a known behavior policy π_b, a target policy π_e that may depend on the entire observation-action history, a realizable model class M of POMDPs with the same state/action/observation spaces, and a known reward function R. It outputs a point estimate Ĵ(π_e) of the expected cumulative return. Crucially, the algorithm queries π_e on synthetic trajectories generated by the learned model M̂ (not just on observed data), which is what distinguishes it from model-free methods and enables circumventing the information-theoretic hardness. The sample complexity targets polynomial dependence on H, log(|M|/δ), ε, and coverage parameters, while avoiding any explicit dependence on the observation space size O.

## Method

The paper's positive result is a simple three-step MLE-based model-based algorithm: (1) pre-filter the model class M to exclude models violating the relevant revealing assumption, (2) fit a POMDP model M̂ by maximizing the log-likelihood of observed trajectories under π_b, and (3) estimate J(π_e) by computing the expected return of π_e in M̂ via rollout. The analysis leverages observable operator models (OOMs) to decompose the evaluation error into single-step estimation errors, introducing a new policy-specific coverage parameter C_eff that measures the ratio of model estimation error under π_e versus π_b distributions (using L1 norm of belief-state-weighted operator differences). The paper proves polynomial sample complexity under single-step revealing (Theorem 4) or under multi-step revealing with a memoryless π_b (Theorem 5), while simultaneously proving an information-theoretic lower bound showing no model-free algorithm can achieve polynomial complexity for history-dependent π_e even under the strongest single-step revealing assumption (Theorem 3). This establishes a formal separation: the hardness arises because model-free algorithms can only query π_e on observed histories, and two history-dependent policies can have identical behavior on all but an exponentially rare history while differing in return by a constant.

## Results

The paper establishes a formal separation between model-free and model-based OPE in POMDPs. Theorem 3 proves that no model-free algorithm can achieve polynomial sample complexity for history-dependent target policies, even under the strongest single-step revealing assumption (C_O = 1) with a memoryless behavior policy. In contrast, Theorem 4 shows that a simple MLE-based model-based algorithm achieves |J(π_e) − J_M̂(π_e)| ≤ O(H² C_O² C_eff,1 √(log(|M|/δ)/n)) under single-step revealing, and Theorem 5 extends this to multi-step revealing (replacing C_O with C_F) when π_b is memoryless. The coverage parameter C_eff,1 (or C_eff,m) is upper-bounded by C_A · C_H under uniform belief coverage, yielding a full poly(H, log(|M|/δ), ε, C_A, C_O/C_F, C_H) sample complexity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between model-free OPE (Zhang & Jiang 2024; Uehara et al. 2023a) and the proposed model-based MLE algorithm. For memoryless target policies, both approaches achieve polynomial sample complexity under belief and outcome coverage (Theorem 1 restates Zhang & Jiang's Theorem 7). However, for history-dependent target policies, the paper proves an information-theoretic lower bound (Theorem 3) showing that no model-free algorithm can succeed, even when the POMDP is fully known (|M|=1), the observation is identity (C_O=C_F=1), and π_b is memoryless. The model-based algorithm circumvents this hardness by querying π_e on synthetic trajectories from the learned model, achieving polynomial guarantees (Theorems 4–5). The remaining open problem is whether model-based OPE can handle both history-dependent π_b and multi-step revealing simultaneously (the '?' in Table 1). Additionally, Section 5 shows that replacing exact model realizability with the weaker observable-equivalent realizability breaks the guarantee (Theorem 7), but Theorem 8 recovers it under the assumption that all models in M satisfy multi-step revealing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model-free OPE framework for POMDPs developed by Zhang & Jiang (2024) and Uehara et al. (2023a), which achieved polynomial sample complexity only for memoryless target policies under belief and outcome coverage assumptions. The key departure is showing that this model-free approach is information-theoretically impossible for history-dependent policies (Theorem 3), and then providing the first polynomial guarantees via a simple MLE-based model-based algorithm that leverages the observable operator model (OOM) framework from Liu et al. (2022a). The paper also addresses state-space misspecification (Section 5), a concern largely ignored in prior POMDP OPE work, revealing that observable-equivalent realizability is insufficient without the learned model itself satisfying revealing conditions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This establishes a formal, provable separation between model-free and model-based OPE in POMDPs, resolving an open question about when polynomial sample complexity is achievable for history-dependent target policies. The counterintuitive finding that a memoryless behavior policy can accurately evaluate a history-dependent target policy (under appropriate coverage) has practical implications for exploration strategy design. The major open problem is whether model-based OPE can handle both history-dependent π_b and multi-step revealing simultaneously (the '?' in Table 1), which would complete the landscape. The state-space mismatch results (Theorems 7–8) are important for practical applicability since the true latent state space is typically unknown.
