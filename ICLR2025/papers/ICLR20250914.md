## Problem

Existing visual brain decoding methods are limited to individual subjects or small groups with subject-specific heads and tokenizers, making them unable to generalize to unseen individuals. Whether brain decoding can be generalized across subjects at all remains an open question, partly because publicly available datasets contain fewer than 10 participants, which is insufficient to study generalization as a function of training population size.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250914/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes whole-brain fMRI voxel volumes (113×136×113 after upsampling normalization, originally from 7T HCP scans at 1.6mm MNI resolution) as input and produces a retrieved image from a gallery of 100 stimulus frames. Each fMRI response is computed by averaging voxels over 4 seconds following the stimulus frame to account for hemodynamic delay, and images are encoded via CLIP ViT-L/14 into a 257×1024 token-level feature space rather than the single CLS token, preserving fine-grained spatial detail for retrieval.

## Method

The core paradigm projects both stimulus images and fMRI voxels into a shared CLIP feature space using contrastive learning (CLIP loss with cosine similarity), where the image encoder is a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 and the brain decoder is a trainable network (MLP, 1D/3D CNN, or Transformer). The key design choice is a uniform pipeline: all subjects' fMRI data are upsampled to a common 113×136×113 volume and fed through the same network without subject-specific heads or tokenizers, enabling scaling to 177 subjects without increasing model complexity. The authors consolidate 177×3,127 image-fMRI pairs from the HCP movie-viewing task (4 films, 7T scanner, TR=1s) and use whole-brain data rather than manually labeled visual regions, arguing that whole-brain data are more consistent across subjects and scalable. Experiments show generalization to unseen subjects emerges as training subjects increase (2%→45% top-1 accuracy from 1 to 167 subjects), holds across architectures, and is modulated by inter-subject fMRI similarity measured via a rank-based scoring algorithm.

## Results

On the consolidated HCP dataset (177 subjects, 3,127 image-fMRI pairs each), the model's TOP1 retrieval accuracy on 10 unseen subjects rises from 2% with 1 training subject to 45% with 167 training subjects (TOP3: 61%), while accuracy on seen subjects remains stable at ~82%. Across backbones with 167 subjects, MLP achieves the best generalization (45% TOP1), followed by 1D CNN (42%), 3D CNN (40%), and Transformer (34%). On the NSD dataset, the proposed pipeline outperforms MindEye1 on whole-brain data (46% vs. 35% TOP1 for Subj 1) but both methods show only 1% TOP1 on unseen NSD subjects due to the limited 8-subject pool.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The only direct baseline comparison is against MindEye1 (Scotti et al., 2024a) on the NSD dataset. On NSDGeneral data (manually labeled visual regions), the proposed method matches or slightly exceeds MindEye1: 86% vs. 85% TOP1 for Subj 1 and 70% vs. 70% for Subj 7. On whole-brain data, the proposed method's upsampling normalization yields a substantial improvement over MindEye1: 46% vs. 35% TOP1 for Subj 1 and 29% vs. 23% for Subj 7. When trained on 6 NSD subjects and tested on seen subjects, the multi-subject model further improves to 49% (Subj 1) and 35% (Subj 7), surpassing the single-subject models. However, on unseen NSD subjects (Subj 2 and 5), both methods collapse to 1% TOP1, confirming that 8 subjects is far too few for generalization. The paper does not compare against MindEye2, UMBRAE, or CLIP-MUSED on the HCP dataset, as those methods require subject-specific heads and cannot scale to 177 subjects.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the MindEye (Scotti et al., 2024a) paradigm of mapping fMRI voxels into CLIP feature space via contrastive learning, but departs from it in two key ways: it replaces subject-specific heads or tokenizers (as in MindEye2, UMBRAE, CLIP-MUSED) with a single uniform network applied to all subjects, and it consolidates a 177-subject dataset from HCP movie-viewing data rather than relying on the 8-subject NSD or 4-subject BOLD5000. It also explicitly contrasts with subject-alignment approaches (e.g., Ferrante et al., 2024) by demonstrating that generalization to unseen subjects can emerge from training on a sufficiently diverse subject pool without any explicit alignment step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic study showing that visual brain decoding can generalize to unseen subjects, challenging the long-held assumption that individual brain differences preclude cross-subject transfer. The finding that subject similarity (e.g., gender, fMRI response correlation) modulates generalization performance opens a new axis of analysis for brain decoding. However, the absolute performance (45% top-1 on 100 images) remains far from practical deployment, the study is limited to a single dataset and retrieval task, and the path to a true brain-decoding foundation model requires substantially larger and more diverse data. Open questions include whether reconstruction tasks (e.g., via diffusion models) benefit similarly from multi-subject training, and whether the observed scaling trend continues beyond 177 subjects.
