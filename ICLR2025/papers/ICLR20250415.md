## Problem

The alignment research community has converged on LLM-judge preference benchmarks (Arena-Hard-Auto, MT-Bench, Alpaca Eval) as the primary measure of post-training progress, implicitly assuming that Bradley-Terry convergence over pairwise preferences will naturally optimize for safety, factuality, and instruction following. No prior work had systematically tested whether LLM-judge preferences actually correlate with concrete, ground-truth-based alignment metrics, or identified the specific implicit biases that cause them to diverge.

## Input / Output

The evaluation pipeline consumes text prompts paired with model responses and produces two distinct output streams: (1) SOS-BENCH alignment scores as normalized accuracies with 95% confidence intervals across 19 ground-truth benchmarks (152,380 total data points) aggregated into world knowledge, instruction following, and safety factors; and (2) LLM-judge preference scores as win-rates over a baseline model on a 5-point Likert scale. The modified judge template used in the bias experiments additionally requires the judge to emit independent per-criterion verdicts (correctness, completeness, safety, conciseness, style) before the overall preference, enabling factor-level correlation analysis that the standard Arena-Hard-Auto template does not support.

## Method

The paper introduces SOS-BENCH, a standardized meta-benchmark combining 19 existing world-knowledge, instruction-following, and safety benchmarks under a unified HHH (Helpful, Honest, Harmless) mapping, and uses it to conduct the largest controlled meta-analysis of publicly available post-training methods to date. To expose implicit judge biases, the authors modify the Arena-Hard-Auto judge template to elicit explicit per-criterion scores, then show that style correlates near-perfectly (Pearson R = 0.999) with the overall judgment while safety and conciseness are nearly ignored, and that systematic interventions (sarcasm, conciseness) cause far larger score drops than factual errors. The SFT-stage analysis demonstrates that dataset size and prompt diversity, not curation method, are the dominant predictors of alignment gains, while the PO-stage analysis reveals a consistent trade-off: preference optimization degrades world knowledge (−1.0 to −1.8 points) while yielding only marginal improvements in safety and instruction following, with the largest observed gains appearing exclusively on LLM-judged benchmarks.

## Results

On SOS-BENCH (152,380 data points across 19 benchmarks), the paper finds that LLM-judge style scores correlate 0.999 (Pearson) with overall Arena-Hard scores, while safety correlates only 0.727 and conciseness 0.114 (Table 2). Introducing a sarcastic tone into responses causes a 96% loss in LLM-judge score, versus only 13% for a factual error and 8% for bland/undiverse text (Table 3). In the two-stage post-training comparison (Table 5), DPO degrades world knowledge by 1.0–1.8 points while improving instruction following by 0.7–1.9 and safety by 0.4–5.0, yet Arena-Hard scores jump by 8.1–22.8 points — indicating LLM-judge benchmarks reward stylistic shifts rather than substantive alignment gains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SOS-BENCH is compared against LLM-judge benchmarks (Arena-Hard-Auto, ChatBot Arena, Alpaca Eval, MT-Bench) and standard benchmarks (LiveBench, HELM). LLM-judge benchmarks correlate strongly with each other (Arena-Hard vs. ChatBot Arena: r=0.81) but weakly with standard benchmarks (LiveBench vs. Arena-Hard: r=0.51; HELM vs. Arena-Hard: r=0.40), while LiveBench and HELM correlate at r=0.94. In the SFT comparison (Table 4), generalist methods (Llama 3 Instruct at 3000K, Bagel at 4000K) outperform all specialist datasets — FLAN (1875K), MetaMath (400K), Code Llama 3 (800K), Numina-CoT (860K), and Replete Coder (2830K) — even on the specialists' own domain benchmarks (e.g., Llama 3 Instruct scores 18.3 on Coding-Avg vs. Code Llama 3's 9.3). For preference optimization (Table 9), no PO method is Pareto-dominant over the SFT-only baseline: ORPO improves IF (+1.5) but degrades Safety (−4.8); DPO and IPO degrade WK by ~1.8–1.9 points; KTO is closest to neutral on WK (−1.1) but still loses on IF. The paper's key finding is that the largest gains from PO appear exclusively on the LLM-judged Arena metric (up to +15.7 for IPO), while concrete alignment metrics show small or negative changes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the growing line of critiques on LLM-as-judge evaluation (Chen et al., 2024; Bavaresco et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2024) by introducing a structured analytical framework for identifying implicit biases in judge pipelines, going beyond previously documented confounds like length bias (Dubois et al., 2024; Park et al., 2024) and self-preference (Panickssery et al., 2024). It operationalizes the HHH alignment principles from Askell et al. (2021) into a concrete, ground-truth-based meta-benchmark (SOS-BENCH), and uses Arena-Hard-Auto (Li et al., 2024c) as its primary case study for exposing judge biases. The data-scaling findings in SFT directly challenge the 'less is more' narrative of LIMA (Zhou et al., 2023a), aligning instead with broader scaling-law literature (Hoffmann et al., 2022; Miller et al., 2021) applied for the first time to post-training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Because LLM-judge benchmarks (Arena-Hard, Alpaca Eval, MT-Bench) are the de facto standard for reporting alignment progress, the finding that style alone predicts overall judge scores with R=0.999 while safety and conciseness are nearly uncorrelated raises serious questions about whether the field is optimizing the right objective. The introduction of SOS-BENCH (152K data points, 19 sub-benchmarks) provides a concrete, reproducible alternative that the authors argue should accompany LLM-judge scores in future papers. Key open questions include whether the implicit style bias generalizes beyond GPT-4-family judges and the Arena-Hard pipeline, whether the HHH-to-benchmark mapping is the correct operationalization of alignment, and whether the world-knowledge degradation from PO persists at larger model scales. The paper's scope is limited to one LLM-judge benchmark and English-only evaluation, and systematic violations were generated by GPT-4o-mini rather than human annotators.
