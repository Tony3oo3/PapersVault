## Problem

Classical message-passing GNNs are at most as powerful as the 1-Weisfeiler-Lehman test and can map structurally distinct MILP instances (e.g., one feasible, one infeasible) to identical representations, making them fundamentally unable to solve certain MILP representation tasks. Existing quantum GNNs fail to simultaneously handle multi-dimensional node/edge features, preserve permutation equivariance, and operate as fully quantum circuits on practical graph tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252465/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a MILP instance as a weighted bipartite graph where variable nodes carry 4-dimensional features (objective coefficient, lower/upper bounds, integer indicator) and constraint nodes carry 2-dimensional features (RHS, operator type), with edge weights from the constraint matrix A. After Pauli-Z measurement on the first qubit of each node, the circuit outputs q+p scalar values; these are summed for feasibility/objective-value prediction (graph-level) or indexed per variable node for optimal-solution prediction (node-level), with task-specific scaling factors applied.

## Method

GQGLA is a fully quantum circuit composed of a repeated block of three layers: (1) a node feature layer that angle-encodes features onto qubits with learnable parameters shared across nodes of the same feature dimension (ensuring permutation equivariance), (2) a graph message interaction layer that applies controlled-K two-qubit gates between qubits of edge-connected nodes to entangle them and capture edge connectivity, plus controlled-G gates for intra-node interaction, and (3) an optional auxiliary layer of symmetric RZZ gates connecting auxiliary qubits to all nodes. The key mechanism is that different edge sets produce different unitaries in the interaction layer via the term exp(-i Σ(A_ij+β)(I-σ_z)_i P^K_{q+j}), allowing the circuit to distinguish graphs that are 1-WL-indistinguishable. Theoretical proofs establish that the full circuit is permutation equivariant and that sum-aggregated outputs are permutation invariant. A scalable variant (S-GQGLA) uses GraphSAINT subgraph sampling to handle large graphs within limited qubit budgets.

## Results

On the GNN-intractable MILP dataset, GQGLA achieves 98.7%–100% feasibility prediction accuracy (4–8 blocks), while all classical GNN baselines (GAT, GATv2, Graph Transformer, MILP-GNN) are stuck at 50% (random chance). On the GNN-tractable MILP dataset, GQGLA reaches 94.6% feasibility accuracy with 8 blocks, matching or exceeding GATv2 (94.3%) and GAT (94.0%). On the BREC Regular benchmark, GQGLA achieves 96.4% graph distinguishability, substantially outperforming the best classical method KP-GNN (75.7%). On PROTEINS, the scalable variant S-GQGLA attains 91.64% accuracy versus 78.53% for the best classical baseline U2GNN.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MILP-GNN (Chen et al., 2023), GQGLA achieves a decisive win on GNN-intractable MILPs (100% vs. 50% accuracy) using only 48 parameters versus 30,565 for MILP-GNN with random features at embedding size 32; on GNN-tractable MILPs, GQGLA is competitive (94.6% vs. 90.2% at 8 blocks). Against advanced GNNs (GAT, GATv2, Graph Transformer), GQGLA matches or slightly exceeds their GNN-tractable accuracy (94.6% vs. 94.3% for GATv2) while completely outperforming them on GNN-intractable instances where all three remain at random (50%). In the quantum comparison (Table 4), GQGLA achieves 11.27% test error versus 34.75% for QGCN and 46.65% for the problem-agnostic HEA, confirming that graph-aware quantum design is essential. On BREC, GQGLA ties the best classical methods (KP-GNN, δ-k-LGNN, PPGN, GSN) at 100% on Basic and Extension but dominates on the challenging Regular set (96.4% vs. 75.7% for KP-GNN). On PROTEINS, S-GQGLA (91.64%) outperforms all classical baselines including U2GNN (78.53%) and SEG-BERT (77.20%), though it is marginally below U2GNN on PTC (68.57% vs. 69.63%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes three research threads—quantum machine learning for structured data (QGNN, QGCN, egoQGNN, EQGC), equivariant quantum neural networks (Schatzki et al., Larocca et al.), and the expressiveness limitations of message-passing GNNs characterized by the WL test (Xu et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2023)—into a unified fully-quantum architecture. Unlike prior quantum GNNs that either lack permutation equivariance, ignore edge features, or rely on hybrid classical-quantum layers, GQGLA is a fully quantum circuit that encodes both node and edge features via angle encoding while provably maintaining node permutation equivariance through parameter sharing. The paper departs from the classical GNN paradigm by replacing message passing with quantum entanglement between qubits representing connected nodes, motivated specifically by the 'GNN-intractable MILPs' identified by Chen et al. (2023) that are fundamentally indistinguishable by any message-passing architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean 100%-vs-50% accuracy separation on GNN-intractable MILPs provides a concrete, theoretically grounded demonstration that quantum entanglement can capture graph structural patterns (edge connectivity differences) that are provably invisible to all classical message-passing GNNs, opening a path for quantum advantage in graph representation learning. However, practical impact is currently constrained by the NISQ-era qubit limit (≤26 qubits in simulation), and the S-GQGLA sampling-based scalability approach introduces approximation that has not been rigorously characterized. Open questions include whether the expressiveness advantage persists for larger, non-bipartite graphs, whether barren plateaus degrade training at scale, and whether the approach generalizes beyond the specific MILP bipartite structure to arbitrary weighted graphs.
