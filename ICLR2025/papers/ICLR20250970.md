## Problem

Standard autoregressive LLM decoding generates one token per step from its marginal distribution, making inference slow and ignoring inter-token dependencies that could improve output quality. Existing speculative decoding methods accelerate inference by drafting and verifying multiple tokens, but each token is still accepted based on its single-token probability, so output quality remains unchanged. This paper addresses the gap by simultaneously improving both decoding speed and output effectiveness through joint multi-token distribution sampling.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text context (prompt), a small auxiliary draft model (e.g., Llama-3-1B), and a large target model (e.g., Llama-3-8B) as inputs, and produces a generated token sequence. Unlike standard decoding that emits one token per model call, MTAD emits multiple tokens per iteration: a variable-length accepted draft prefix (0 to γ tokens) plus one correction token sampled from the large model's conditional distribution. The draft tokens are generated via beam search over the small model's joint distribution, and the number of accepted tokens per iteration adapts dynamically based on a verification threshold τ.

## Method

MTAD approximates multi-token joint decoding (MTJD) by using a small auxiliary model to draft γ tokens via beam sampling from the joint distribution q(x_{t+1:t+γ}|x_{1:t}), then verifying all prefix sub-sequences in parallel against the large model's joint likelihood p(x_{t+1:t+j}|x_{1:t}). A prefix x_{t+1:t+j} passes verification if min(1, p/q) > τ, and MTAD accepts the longest passing prefix rather than stopping at the first rejection—this is the key mechanism that yields more accepted tokens per iteration than vanilla speculative decoding. The theoretical analysis (Theorem 3.5) bounds the perplexity ratio between approximate MTAD and exact MTJD by a function of τ, the model discrepancy U, and beam-search error ε, showing convergence to exact MTJD as q→p. Empirically, MTAD achieves 25% higher downstream scores than single-token sampling while running 1.42× faster and consuming 1.54× less energy than vanilla speculative decoding.

## Results

MTAD achieves the best downstream performance across all three main benchmarks (HumanEval, Spider, MT-Bench) while also being the fastest and most energy-efficient method. On Llama-3-Instruct, MTAD reaches 38.4% pass@1 on HumanEval (vs. 32.9% for the best lossless baseline Spectr), 44.0% accuracy on Spider (vs. 35.5% for Spectr), and 4.40 MT-Bench score (vs. 4.02 for MCSS). In terms of efficiency, MTAD averages 29.8 tokens/s on MT-Bench and 7.6 J/token on HumanEval, outpacing the fastest lossless baseline MCSS by 17.2% in speed while consuming 6.1% less energy. Overall, MTAD improves downstream performance by 25% over standard single-token sampling and achieves a 1.42× speed-up with 1.54× less energy compared to vanilla speculative decoding.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MTAD outperforms all six baselines on both efficiency (speed and energy) and downstream quality simultaneously. Against the lossless baselines (SpD, Spectr, SpecInfer, MCSS), MTAD achieves 17.2% higher speed and 6.1% lower energy than the best (MCSS) while improving task scores by 25% on average — e.g., 38.4% vs. 32.9% pass@1 on HumanEval and 44.0% vs. 35.5% accuracy on Spider. Against the lossy baselines, MTAD is 27.2% faster and 17.9% more energy-efficient than BiLD, and 15.1% faster and 14.1% more energy-efficient than Typical decoding, while also achieving higher task scores (38.4% vs. 37.8% for BiLD on HumanEval; 44.0% vs. 42.0% for Typical on Spider). A key structural advantage is that MTAD continues verification after a token rejection to find the longest acceptable prefix sub-sequence, whereas baselines like SpD, Spectr, and SpecInfer terminate immediately upon the first rejection, yielding shorter acceptance lengths per iteration (MTAD averages 3.17–4.30 tokens/iteration vs. 2.02–3.45 for baselines). Notably, MTAD uses only a single draft sequence yet outperforms multi-draft methods (Spectr, SpecInfer, MCSS), suggesting the joint-likelihood verification criterion is more effective than generating more candidates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the speculative decoding framework (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023) by replacing single-token verification with joint-distribution verification over multi-token sequences. While prior speculative decoding methods (Spectr, SpecInfer, MCSS, BiLD) all sample from the target model's single-token conditional distribution p(x_t | x_{<t}), MTAD instead verifies draft prefixes against their joint likelihood p(x_{t+1:t+j} | x_{1:t}), which the authors prove theoretically reduces output perplexity. The practical algorithm uses beam search with a small auxiliary model to draft tokens and a threshold-based acceptance criterion on joint likelihood ratios, distinguishing it from the per-token acceptance in vanilla speculative decoding. The paper also introduces the first quantified energy-consumption analysis of speculative decoding, decomposing GPU energy into FLOP and memory-access components.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MTAD achieves a rare triple improvement—25% higher downstream accuracy, 1.42× speed-up, and 1.54× less energy—over vanilla speculative decoding, breaking the typical efficiency-vs-quality trade-off that constrains most decoding methods. The energy analysis reveals that memory access, not FLOPs, dominates GPU energy during LLM inference, a finding with broader implications for hardware-aware inference optimization. Limitations include the requirement for a well-aligned small auxiliary model, the lossy nature of the approximation (bounded but non-zero error), and energy measurements conducted on a single GPU type (Nvidia L40). Open questions include whether tree-based multi-sequence drafting can further improve acceptance length without the memory overhead the authors note, and whether the energy savings generalize across different hardware architectures and model scales.
