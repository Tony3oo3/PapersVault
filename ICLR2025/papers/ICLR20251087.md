## Problem

Existing LLM pruning methods (dense-matrix, attention-head, filter, or hidden-dimension pruning) introduce structural irregularities that complicate storage and deployment, while concurrent layer-pruning approaches either remove layers without any recovery mechanism or rely on expensive full-model fine-tuning (e.g., LoRA/QLoRA) that requires storing the entire model in GPU memory. Additionally, the standard accuracy metric used to evaluate compressed models can overestimate performance because pruned models may guess correct answers on samples where the original model was uncertain.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained LLM (e.g., Llama2-7B/13B, OPT, Baichuan, Mixtral) and a small corpus of 30,000 text samples drawn from SlimPajama following the domain distribution of Sheared-LLaMa. It produces a compressed LLM in which a contiguous block of n Transformer layers is replaced by a single lightweight network (FFN, SwiGLU, or one Transformer layer) with far fewer parameters. Notably, the training phase only requires storing the lightweight network's weights and activations in GPU (≈25–28 GB on an A800), not the full model, making it feasible under tight hardware constraints.

## Method

LLM-Streamline operates in two stages. First, layer pruning: it computes the average cosine similarity between input and output hidden states of every layer over 500 sampled sequences, then identifies the window of n consecutive layers (determined by a target sparsity) whose cumulative transformation is smallest (highest cosine similarity) and removes them. Second, layer replacement: it records the hidden states at the boundaries of the pruned block and trains a lightweight network (FFN, SwiGLU, or a single Transformer layer initialized from the first pruned layer) to map the input hidden state to the output hidden state via MSE loss, effectively distilling the pruned layers' residual transformation into a compact module. This avoids the need to fine-tune the full pruned model, reducing both GPU memory and training data requirements (30k vs. 300k samples for LoRA). The paper also introduces a 'stability' evaluation metric that weights each sample's contribution by the original model's prediction confidence (PPL standard deviation across answer choices), penalizing cases where the pruned model merely guesses correctly on uncertain items.

## Results

On Llama2-7B at ~25% pruning, LLM-Streamline (FFN) retains 93.1% of the original model's average accuracy (45.8 vs. 49.2) across 12 classification benchmarks, and 91.9% with a Transformer layer replacement. On Llama2-13B, the method retains 93.3% (FFN) and 93.2% (Layer) of original accuracy. For generation tasks, LLM-Streamline (Layer) retains 77.2% of Llama2-7B's capability (24.7 vs. 32.0 average), substantially outperforming LLM-Pruner (63.8%) and SliceGPT (64.1%). The proposed stability metric shows LLM-Streamline (FFN) achieves 83.7% average stability on Llama2-7B, versus 72.0% for LLM-Pruner and 68.2% for SliceGPT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LLM-Pruner, LLM-Streamline (FFN) achieves 45.8% average accuracy vs. 38.4% on Llama2-7B (a 7.4-point gain) and 53.2% vs. 38.6% on Llama2-13B (14.6-point gain), with stability of 83.7% vs. 72.0% on 7B. Against SliceGPT, the gap is similar: 45.8% vs. 38.4% on 7B and 53.2% vs. 34.9% on 13B, where SliceGPT suffers the largest degradation. Against the concurrent layer-pruning method LaCo, LLM-Streamline leads by 5.5 points on Llama2-7B (45.8% vs. 40.3%) and 1.9 points on Llama2-13B (53.2% vs. 51.3%), while also requiring only 30k training samples versus LaCo's 1B. In the direct comparison with LoRA fine-tuning (Table 6), layer replacement with FFN achieves 46.3% accuracy and 85.2% stability versus LoRA's 44.5% and 82.1%, while using 27.0 GB GPU memory compared to LoRA's 56.4 GB and 10× less training data (30k vs. 300k samples). Against perplexity-based pruning (SLEB), cosine-similarity-based layer selection yields 40.2% average accuracy versus 37.6% for perplexity-based selection on Llama2-7B, and the pruned layers are consistent across different pre-training datasets, unlike perplexity which selects different layers depending on the data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself within the structured pruning literature for LLMs, departing from methods that prune attention heads, FFN filters, or hidden dimensions (LLM-Pruner, SliceGPT) in favor of layer-level pruning, which avoids structural irregularities and simplifies deployment. Among concurrent layer-pruning works (LaCo, ShortGPT, UIDL, SLEB, Shortened Llama), LLM-Streamline shares the cosine-similarity-based layer importance criterion with ShortGPT and UIDL but introduces a distinct recovery mechanism: training a lightweight network (FFN, SwiGLU, or Transformer layer) to approximate the cumulative transformation of pruned layers, rather than directly removing them or fine-tuning the remaining model with LoRA/QLoRA. The stability metric also carves new ground by reframing compression evaluation around prediction consistency weighted by model confidence, rather than raw accuracy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Layer pruning is the most deployment-friendly form of LLM compression since it only reduces model depth without introducing irregular sparsity patterns, and this paper demonstrates that a 25% depth reduction can retain ~93% of classification performance with only 30k training samples and ~5 hours of GPU time. The stability metric exposes a real evaluation gap—accuracy can increase after pruning due to the model guessing correctly on uncertain samples—providing the field a more honest compression benchmark. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes well beyond ~25–30% pruning ratios (50% results show larger degradation), whether the lightweight network can be further compressed or distilled, and whether the method extends naturally to MoE architectures where layer redundancy may differ.
