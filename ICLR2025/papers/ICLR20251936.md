## Problem

Causal representation learning (CRL) has produced many identifiability results across different settings (multiview, interventional, temporal, multi-task), each framed within Pearl's causal hierarchy, yet these categories do not always map cleanly onto the hierarchy and their assumptions are often too rigid for real-world applications. The paper identifies that these disparate methods actually share a common methodological principle—aligning representations with known data symmetries—but this principle was implicit and never made explicit, preventing practitioners from mixing assumptions or applying CRL tools to problems where the causal framing is imperfect.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a set of K observables (e.g., multiple views, environments, time steps, or tasks) together with a finite set of known invariance properties, each specifying an equivalence relation on a known-size subset of latent indices. It produces block-identified latent causal variables (up to a diffeomorphism within each invariant block) and, under additional parametric assumptions, the latent causal graph up to isomorphism. In the ecology case study, the input is per-frame video of ant triplets with treatment annotations, and the output is a per-frame representation used for supervised grooming classification and Average Treatment Effect estimation via the TERB metric.

## Method

The core contribution is a unified identifiability theorem (Thm. 3.1) showing that if encoders and selectors jointly satisfy two constraints—(i) an invariance constraint requiring selected representations to be equivalent under the known relation across observables in the same equivalence class, and (ii) a sufficiency constraint requiring the selected representation to preserve all mutual information of the invariant latent partition—then all invariant latent blocks are simultaneously block-identified. The key insight is that the invariance principle need not be causal: distributional invariances (marginal, score, transition, risk) arising from non-causal transformations (formalized as 'ninterventions') are sufficient for identification, as demonstrated by a synthetic ablation where a non-sensical distributional change still yields block-identifiability of non-intervened nodes. The framework subsumes 30 existing identification results as special cases and enables new results, such as element-wise identifiability from one imperfect intervention per node in the nonparametric setting. In practice, the invariance constraint is enforced via task-specific regularizers (L2 alignment, MMD, risk variance as in V-REx) and sufficiency via reconstruction loss, mutual information, or entropy maximization, making the framework compatible with autoencoder, normalizing flow, and contrastive architectures.

## Results

On the ISTAnt ecology benchmark, enforcing invariance via V-REx with λ_INV=100 reduces the Treatment Effect Relative Bias (TERB) from 100% (ERM baseline) to 20% under experiment subsampling, while balanced accuracy initially improves before degrading at higher invariance weights due to insufficient sufficiency. In the synthetic ablation with non-causal 'ninterventions,' the invariant latents z₁ and z₃ are block-identified with R² scores of 0.863±0.031 and 0.872±0.035, respectively, while the nintervened latent z₂ remains unidentified (R²=0.065±0.017), confirming that identification is driven by distributional invariance rather than causal intervention per se.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not perform a head-to-head comparison against other CRL methods; its primary contribution is theoretical unification. Empirically, the main comparison is between ERM (λ_INV=0) and V-REx with varying invariance strength on ISTAnt: ERM yields 100% TERB, while the best invariance-weighted model (λ_INV=100) achieves 20% TERB under experiment subsampling. The synthetic ablation further demonstrates that existing interventional CRL methods (e.g., Zhang et al. 2024a, Ahuja et al. 2024) rely only on marginal distributional invariance — a property preserved even under non-causal 'ninterventions' — rather than on the causal semantics of the intervention itself. This finding implies that the invariance constraint, not the causal mechanism, is the operative ingredient for latent variable identification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies a broad family of causal representation learning (CRL) methods—multiview, interventional, temporal, multi-task CRL, and domain generalization—under a single invariance-principle framework, showing that 30 existing identifiability results are special cases of one general theorem (Thm. 3.1). It builds directly on the block-identifiability notion from von Kügelgen et al. (2021), the selection operator from Yao et al. (2023), and the invariance principle lineage from Peters et al. (2014), while drawing on V-REx (Krueger et al., 2021) for practical enforcement. The key conceptual departure from prior CRL work is the explicit argument that latent variable identification requires only distributional invariance (which need not arise from valid causal interventions), whereas causal graph identification does require causal assumptions such as interventions or parametric constraints—a distinction the paper formalizes through the 'nintervention' construct.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper clarifies a persistent confusion in CRL about which assumptions are actually needed for which sub-goal: variables can be identified under non-causal invariance, but the causal graph cannot. This reframing broadens practical applicability, as demonstrated by improved treatment-effect estimation (TERB reduced from 100% to 20%) on the ISTAnt ecology benchmark using a domain-generalization objective rather than a dedicated interventional CRL method. Open questions include extending the framework to discrete latent variables, providing finite-sample guarantees, and characterizing when multiple invariance types can be jointly exploited to tighten identifiability beyond block-level. The distinction between variable and graph identifiability assumptions may also influence how practitioners design experiments when full interventional access is unavailable.
