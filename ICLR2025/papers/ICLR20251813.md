## Problem

Schrödinger Bridge (SB) methods provide a principled framework for distribution matching in Euclidean spaces, but they cannot be directly applied to topological domains such as graphs and simplicial complexes where signals (node values, edge flows) are defined. Existing SB models ignore the underlying topological structure, leading to suboptimal transport paths that fail to respect the network geometry. This paper asks: how can one formulate and solve the SB problem for topological signals so that the reference dynamics and learned bridge inherently encode the domain's topology?

## Input / Output

The method takes two sets of topological signal samples (e.g., node signals on a graph with n nodes, or edge flows on a simplicial 2-complex with n edges) along with the topological domain structure, encoded as a Laplacian-type operator L ∈ R^{n×n} (graph Laplacian or Hodge Laplacian). The output is a learned stochastic bridge process parameterized by forward-backward policies, which can generate new topological signals from a simple prior or transport signals between two arbitrary distributions on the same topology. The drift term in the reference SDE involves a matrix-vector product H_t(L)Y_t, exploiting the sparsity of L for efficient computation on large networks.

## Method

The paper defines the Topological Schrödinger Bridge Problem (TSBP) where the reference process follows a topology-aware SDE with a linear topological convolution drift H_t(L)Y_t + α_t, where H_t(L) = Σ h_k(t)L^k is a matrix polynomial of the Laplacian. For Gaussian boundary distributions, the authors derive a closed-form optimal Gaussian topological SB as a stochastic interpolant (Theorem 6) and its equivalent SDE representation (Theorem 7), generalizing prior results that were limited to scalar drift coefficients. In the general case, the optimal process satisfies a pair of forward-backward TSDEs governed by unknown policy functions Z_t and Ẑ_t. These policies are parameterized by topological neural networks (GCNs for node signals, simplicial neural networks for edge flows) and trained by maximizing a likelihood objective derived from the SB optimality condition, extending the framework of Chen et al. (2022a) to topological domains. The key design choice is using topological heat diffusion (e.g., dY_t = -cLY_t dt + g_t dW_t) as the reference, which naturally encodes network connectivity and topology into the bridge dynamics.

## Results

The paper evaluates TSB-based models against Euclidean SB baselines using 1-Wasserstein distance (lower is better) across five real-world topological signal tasks. TSB models achieve consistent and substantial improvements: on seismic magnitudes, TSB-VE reaches 7.69 vs. the best SB baseline (SB-VE) at 11.49; on traffic flows, TSB-VP reaches 9.92 vs. SB-VP at 18.22; on brain signals, TSB-BM reaches 7.51 vs. SB-BM at 12.08; on single-cell data, TSB-BM reaches 0.14 vs. all SB baselines at 0.33; and on ocean currents, TSB-VP reaches 0.53 vs. SB-VP at 0.83 (both using Gaussian SB priors). Qualitatively, SB-VE fails to produce low-energy aligned brain states, SB-BM yields noisy intermediate single-cell predictions, and SB-BM fails to reach the target curl-free ocean current state, while their TSB counterparts succeed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares TSB-based models (TSB-BM, TSB-VE, TSB-VP) against Euclidean SB-based models (SB-BM, SB-VE, SB-VP) from Chen et al. [2022a], which use standard Brownian motion, variance-exploding, and variance-preserving references without topological structure. TSB models win on every dataset and every reference-dynamics variant: the best TSB method outperforms the best SB method by approximately 33% on seismic magnitudes (7.69 vs. 11.49), 46% on traffic flows (9.92 vs. 18.22), 38% on brain signals (7.51 vs. 12.08), 58% on single-cell data (0.14 vs. 0.33), and 36% on ocean currents (0.53 vs. 0.83). The gap is most dramatic for traffic flows (edge signals on a simplicial complex) and single-cell data, where the Euclidean baselines completely ignore the underlying topology. Qualitative analyses confirm the quantitative gap: SB-VE fails to produce the low-energy aligned brain state that TSB-VE achieves, SB-BM produces very noisy intermediate single-cell predictions while TSB-BM tracks the ground-truth Phate embedding closely, and SB-BM fails to reach the curl-free ocean current target while TSB-BM becomes more divergent and closer to the target. The authors attribute the consistent advantage to the topology-aware reference process (topological heat diffusion via graph/Hodge Laplacian) that encodes structural priors the Euclidean baselines lack.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Schrödinger Bridge (SB) framework from Euclidean spaces to topological domains (graphs, simplicial complexes) by replacing the standard Wiener process reference with topology-aware SDEs driven by Laplacian-type operators (graph Laplacian, Hodge Laplacian). Theoretically, it generalizes the closed-form Gaussian SB of Bunne et al. (2023) from scalar drift coefficients to matrix-polynomial drifts (topological convolutions), and adapts the forward-backward SDE optimality and likelihood training framework of Chen et al. (2022a) and De Bortoli et al. (2021) to the topological setting. The reference dynamics subsume stochastic heat diffusion on graphs and simplicial complexes, connecting to the broader topological signal processing literature on graph/simplicial convolutions and Gaussian processes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic treatment of distribution matching on topological domains within the SB framework, providing both a closed-form Gaussian solution and a practical likelihood-trained model that consistently outperforms Euclidean SB baselines across five diverse applications (brain signals, single-cell data, ocean currents, seismic events, traffic flows). The paper identifies several open directions: extending to time-varying topology, matrix-valued diffusion coefficients, learnable higher-order convolutions, and discrete distributions on graphs. A key open question is whether the Wasserstein gradient flow interpretation of the TSB optimality can be leveraged for more flexible, application-specific topological dynamics beyond the fixed Laplacian-based priors considered here.
