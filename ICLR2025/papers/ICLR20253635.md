## Problem

While mechanistic interpretability has catalogued diverse attention head types (induction heads, previous-token heads, multigram heads) at the end of training, no principled quantitative tool exists to track how this functional diversity emerges during the learning process. The Local Learning Coefficient (LLC) from singular learning theory measures model complexity but is defined globally over the full parameter space and training distribution, making it unable to resolve which specific components are being shaped by which data structures.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253635/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a transformer checkpoint at a given training step, a subset of weights V (e.g., a single attention head's QKV projections), and a data distribution q′ that may differ from the training distribution (e.g., a code-only sub-distribution from GitHub). It produces a scalar rLLC value per head per checkpoint, estimated by SGLD sampling from a Gibbs posterior restricted to V and evaluated on q′, yielding per-head time-series trajectories that can be clustered by Euclidean K-means to recover functional head types and compared across data distributions to identify specialization.

## Method

The paper introduces two refinements to the LLC: the weight-refined LLC (wrLLC) restricts the parameter space to a model component (e.g., one attention head) while keeping the training distribution fixed, and the data-refined LLC (drLLC) keeps the full parameter space but evaluates on a sub-distribution. Both are estimated by projecting SGLD updates onto the restricted parameter space V and computing the expected loss deviation under a Gibbs posterior p(v; v*, q′, β, γ) ∝ exp{−nβℓ′_n(u*, v) − γ/2‖v − v*‖₂²}. The key finding is that wrLLC trajectories over training naturally cluster by head functional type, with higher wrLLC corresponding to heads memorizing more multigrams, while drLLC with a code distribution differentially elevates induction heads specialized to syntactic patterns common in code. Combining both refinements with a one-layer transformer as the data-generating process reveals a previously unidentified multigram circuit where layer 0 multigram heads transition from memorizing simple multigrams to supporting layer 1 heads in predicting complex nested Dyck patterns.

## Results

The paper's primary findings are structural rather than benchmark-based. Weight-refined LLCs (wrLLCs) reveal that attention heads of the same functional type (induction, previous-token, multigram/Dyck) cluster together in their wrLLC trajectories over training, with K-means clustering of wrLLC curves recovering the same partition as manual behavioral classification. Data-refined LLCs (drLLCs) evaluated on GitHub code show that induction head 1:6 has a significantly higher drLLC than 1:7, indicating 1:6 is further specialized to code-like induction patterns (syntactic/structural). The paper identifies a novel multigram circuit: layer 0 multigram heads (e.g., 0:7) transition around stage LM3 from memorizing simple multigrams to a supporting role, while layer 1 multigram heads (e.g., 1:5) specialize to complex nested Dyck patterns, confirmed by K-composition scores and path patching.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares rLLCs against three families of alternative metrics in Appendices D and E. Against the Hessian trace (LeCun et al., 1989), the LLC identifies similar developmental stage transitions and, when weight-refined, differentiates attention head clusters, but the LLC provides cleaner signals and stronger theoretical grounding as it captures higher-order geometric information about the loss landscape rather than only second-order curvature. Against ablation-based metrics (zero, mean, and resample ablations; Zhou et al., 2018), the rLLC offers a continuous, quantitative trajectory over training that reveals *when* specialization occurs, whereas ablations only provide a snapshot of which patterns are affected at a given checkpoint; the two methods are complementary, with ablations validating rLLC predictions. Against Hessian rank estimation (Ubaru & Saad, 2016), the LLC is more reliable in practice: Hessian rank proves challenging to estimate accurately and does not clearly reflect the structural development observed through rLLCs or ablations. Overall, the paper concludes that rLLCs consistently outperform or complement these baselines for tracking developmental differentiation and specialization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Local Learning Coefficient (LLC) from singular learning theory (Lau et al., 2023; Watanabe, 2009) by introducing weight-refined and data-refined variants that measure the complexity of individual model components (e.g., a single attention head) with respect to a chosen sub-distribution. It builds directly on Hoogland et al. (2024), who used the unrefined LLC to identify macroscopic developmental stages in two-layer attention-only transformers, and on the transformer circuits framework of Elhage et al. (2021) for head classification and K-composition scores. Conceptually, the paper positions itself at the intersection of singular learning theory and mechanistic interpretability, proposing 'developmental interpretability' as a new lens that tracks how data distributional structure imprints geometric structure in the loss landscape, which in turn shapes computational structure over training — a synthesis drawing on Rogers & McClelland's (2004) waves of differentiation and Saxe et al. (2019).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The rLLC framework offers a principled, quantitative alternative to purely qualitative head-by-head mechanistic analysis, potentially automating the identification of head types and their developmental trajectories. The discovery of the multigram circuit — where layer 0 heads transition from memorizing simple multigrams to supporting layer 1 heads in nested Dyck pattern prediction — is the first identification of such a circuit in a transformer trained on natural language. Key limitations include the restriction to small two-layer attention-only models, the acknowledged inaccuracy of SGLD-based LLC estimation (ground truth is unknown), and the lack of validation at scale or across architectures. Open questions include whether the data-structure-to-geometric-structure correspondence generalizes to deeper or wider models, whether rLLC can be computed efficiently at scale, and whether the developmental interpretability framework yields new predictions beyond what ablation or path-patching methods already provide.
