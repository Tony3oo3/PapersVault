## Problem

Direct preference optimization (DPO) and its variants are designed to increase the probability of preferred responses relative to dispreferred ones, yet prior work has observed that the likelihood of preferred responses often *decreases* during training. The causes and implications of this counterintuitive phenomenon—termed likelihood displacement—remained poorly understood, with prior attributions to model capacity, dataset size, or the SFT phase. This paper shows that likelihood displacement can be catastrophic even in the simplest setting (a single prompt with single-token responses), shifting probability mass to responses with the *opposite* meaning and causing unintentional unalignment in safety training.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a preference dataset of (prompt, preferred response, dispreferred response) triples and a frozen language model. A single forward pass over the dataset yields the contextual hidden embeddings needed to compute the CHES score per sample, which is a scalar similarity measure between the preferred and dispreferred responses. The output is a per-sample CHES score (or its length-normalized variant) that can be used to rank, filter, or diagnose the dataset; in the safety alignment experiments, filtering to the 5% lowest-scoring samples produces a cleaned preference dataset for DPO training.

## Method

The paper derives the instantaneous gradient of ln π_θ(y⁺|x) under gradient flow using the unconstrained features model, which treats hidden embeddings as directly trainable parameters. For single-token responses, the analysis shows displacement is governed by the inner product of preferred and dispreferred token unembeddings and the alignment of other token unembeddings with W_{y⁺} − W_{y⁻}; for multi-token responses, additional terms involving inner products of contextual hidden embeddings appear. Setting the empirically positive coefficients to one yields the CHES score (Definition 2), a closed-form similarity measure computable from a single forward pass. Empirically, the CHES score perfectly ranks samples by their contribution to likelihood displacement on UltraFeedback and AlpacaFarm, outperforming edit distance and last-hidden-embedding inner product baselines. In safety alignment on SORRY-Bench, filtering out the 5% highest length-normalized CHES samples prevents the refusal rate from dropping (e.g., Llama-3-8B-Instruct stays near 74% instead of falling to 33%), outperforming the addition of an SFT regularization term to the DPO loss.

## Results

Training Llama-3-8B-Instruct via DPO to refuse unsafe prompts (SORRY-Bench) causes the refusal rate to drop from 74.4% to 33.4%, and Gemma-2B-IT from 80.5% to 54.8%, due to likelihood displacement shifting probability mass from preferred refusals to harmful responses. Filtering out the 95% of samples with the highest length-normalized CHES scores (keeping only the 5% lowest) successfully mitigates this unalignment, restoring refusal rates above the pre-training baseline. In simple single-token settings (Table 1), preferred token probabilities drop by 0.21–0.96 absolute value across OLMo-1B, Gemma-2B, and Llama-3-8B, with probability mass often shifting to semantically opposite tokens (e.g., training to prefer No over Never sharply increases Yes).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four strategies for mitigating likelihood displacement in safety alignment: (i) original DPO, (ii) DPO + SFT regularization term (as in Pal et al. 2024, Xu et al. 2024a, Pang et al. 2024, Liu et al. 2024), (iii) DPO on 'gold' preference data from SORRY-Bench, and (iv) DPO on CHES-filtered data (5% lowest length-normalized CHES scores). CHES filtering outperforms the SFT regularization term in boosting refusal rates, while both prevent the catastrophic drop seen with unfiltered DPO. Gold data also avoids likelihood displacement entirely (preferred responses increase in log probability), confirming that sufficiently distinct preferences are the key factor. For identifying which samples cause displacement, the CHES score perfectly ranks samples by their contribution to likelihood displacement across all three models, whereas normalized edit distance (Pal et al. 2024) and the inner product of last hidden embeddings both fail to predict displacement, with most percentiles showing no decrease in preferred response log probability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the DPO framework (Rafailov et al., 2023) and its variants (IPO, SLiC, REBEL, GPO), and on prior empirical observations that preferred-response likelihoods decrease during preference training (Pal et al., 2024; Tajwar et al., 2024; Rafailov et al., 2024b). However, it departs sharply from those works by showing that likelihood displacement is not an artifact of model capacity, multiple training samples, or the SFT phase, but is instead governed by the geometry of token unembeddings and hidden embeddings. The theoretical analysis leverages the unconstrained features model (Mixon et al., 2022) and the linear representation hypothesis for unembeddings (Mikolov et al., 2013; Park et al., 2024) to derive the CHES score, a model-aware diagnostic that prior work (e.g., edit-distance heuristics in Pal et al., 2024) had not identified.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that DPO can *unalign* a safety-aligned model (Llama-3-8B-Instruct refusal rate dropping from 74.4% to 33.4% when trained specifically to refuse) is a practical alarm for the alignment community, and the CHES score offers a concrete, single-forward-pass diagnostic for identifying problematic training samples. The result that filtering by CHES outperforms adding an SFT regularization term to the loss suggests that data curation, not loss modification, is the more effective mitigation. Open questions include whether the CHES score generalizes to general instruction-following settings, whether it can guide more complex curation pipelines (e.g., selecting from diverse model-generated candidates), and whether the instantaneous gradient-flow analysis extends to full training trajectories with architecture-specific dynamics.
