## Problem

Existing home-scale rearrangement benchmarks like Habitat 2.0 rely on 'magical grasp' (teleporting objects to the gripper) rather than realistic low-level manipulation, and their CPU-based simulation limits parallelization to ~1400 SPS. There is no unified benchmark that combines GPU-accelerated simulation with realistic grasping, home-scale long-horizon tasks, extensive baselines, and scalable controlled data generation in a single platform.

## Input / Output

The policy consumes 128×128 depth images from head and arm cameras (stacked 3 frames for partial observability), target object pose, goal position, TCP pose relative to base, a binary grasp indicator, and robot proprioception; the observation space is kept uniform across subtasks via masking. Actions are PD joint delta position commands for the 7-DOF arm, torso, and head pan/tilt, plus linear and angular velocity for the mobile base, all normalized to [-1,1]. Beyond policy outputs, the benchmark produces chronologically ordered event lists (Contact, Grasped, Dropped, Excessive Collisions, Success) per trajectory and filtered demonstration datasets categorized by mutually exclusive success/failure modes.

## Method

MS-HAB implements a GPU-accelerated version of the Home Assistant Benchmark using ManiSkill3, replacing magical grasp with realistic low-level control and achieving 4300+ SPS at 15 GB VRAM (3× faster than Habitat 2.0). For RL baselines, per-object Pick and Place policies are trained with SAC (1M replay buffer, D4PG CNN encoder) to overfit to specific YCB geometries, while Open/Close policies use PPO with NatureCNN; all policies are chained by a perfect task planner with teleport navigation. A key insight is that per-object policies significantly outperform all-object policies when grasping diverse geometries or manipulating in tight constraints (e.g., +14.7% on PrepareGroceries Pick). The automated trajectory categorization system uses privileged simulator signals to label each trajectory with a chronological event list, then assigns it to one of several mutually exclusive success or failure modes (e.g., 'straightforward success' requires exactly Contact→Grasp→Success with no Dropped or Excessive Collisions events). This labeling enables rule-based dataset filtering for controlled IL training, where Behavior Cloning policies trained on filtered demonstrations exhibit corresponding behavioral biases (e.g., place-in-goal vs. drop-to-goal ratios shift with dataset composition).

## Results

MS-HAB achieves 4299.18 ± 26.36 SPS at 15.35 GB VRAM with 4096 parallel environments, 3.08× faster than Habitat 2.0 (1397.65 ± 11.02 SPS at 22.60 GB) while rendering 2×128×128 RGB-D images and interacting with 5 dynamic objects. On subtask success once rate, the best per-object RL policies reach 81.75% (TidyHouse Pick, train) and 88.49% (SetTable Place, val), while IL (BC) baselines lag by 10–20 points on Pick and Place. Per-object RL policies outperform all-object counterparts by up to +14.69% on PrepareGroceries Pick (train) and +10.22% on PrepareGroceries Place (train), with the gap largest when object geometry diversity or tight placement tolerances are involved. Long-horizon progressive completion rates remain low (well below the optimistic upper bound of independently multiplied subtask success rates), indicating that whole-body low-level control in cluttered scenes is the primary bottleneck.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Habitat 2.0 (Szot et al., 2021), MS-HAB achieves 3.08× higher throughput (4299 vs. 1398 SPS) with 32% less GPU memory (15.35 vs. 22.60 GB) on the Interact benchmark, while also supporting realistic low-level control that Habitat 2.0's magical-grasp formulation does not. Against Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., 2023), the simpler BC baseline consistently outperforms DP on Pick subtasks (e.g., TidyHouse Pick: 61.11% vs. 28.37% train; PrepareGroceries Pick: 44.64% vs. 19.35% train), though the gap narrows on Place and Open/Close subtasks; notably, DP is the only method achieving non-zero Close Fridge success on validation (0.10% vs. 0.00% for BC). Against Behavior-1K (Li et al., 2022) on ray-traced rendering, MS-HAB is 3.51× faster (69.90 vs. 19.92 SPS) while using 17.85% less GPU memory (6.26 vs. 7.62 GB) at comparable visual quality. The paper also shows that per-object RL policies beat all-object RL policies by 7–15 points on Pick and up to 10 points on Place in geometry-sensitive settings, and that trajectory filtering (e.g., 'place in goal' vs. 'drop to goal') measurably biases IL policy behavior (3.17:1 vs. 1:2.22 place-to-drop ratio), though additional methods are needed for full behavioral control.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate research threads: GPU-parallelized simulation (ManiSkill3), home-scale rearrangement tasks (HAB/Habitat 2.0), and multi-skill mobile manipulation with skill chaining (Gu et al., 2023a). The key departure from Habitat 2.0 is replacing magical grasp with realistic low-level control, which the original CPU-based simulator cannot support; the key departure from ManiSkill3 is scaling to apartment-scale scenes with long-horizon rearrangement rather than simpler tabletop tasks. The automated trajectory filtering system for controlled dataset generation is a novel contribution not present in prior simulators like RoboCasa or ManiSkill3.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By achieving 3x the simulation speed of Habitat 2.0 while supporting realistic grasping, MS-HAB removes a practical bottleneck for training and data generation in home-scale manipulation, enabling researchers to scale RL training and IL dataset creation in feasible wall-clock time. The trajectory categorization system provides a reusable framework for diagnosing failure modes and generating behaviorally-controlled demonstrations without manual annotation. However, the baselines reveal that low-level whole-body control in cluttered environments remains far from solved (long-horizon success rates are low, and the Close Fridge policy completely fails on unseen validation scenes), and the authors explicitly do not claim transfer to real robots, leaving significant open questions about generalization and real-world deployment.
