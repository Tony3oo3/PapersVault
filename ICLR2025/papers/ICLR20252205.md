## Problem

While structured sensitive information (e.g., PII, phone numbers) in LLM outputs has been studied and mitigated via regex matching and content moderation, semantic sensitive information (SemSI)—viewpoints, statements, or incorrect claims expressed in natural language that pose privacy, reputational, or public-safety risks—lacks a formal definition, a labeled dataset, and a systematic benchmark. This paper addresses the gap by showing that even simple natural questions (no jailbreak or adversarial prompt) can induce SemSI in state-of-the-art LLMs, making it a serious but under-appreciated safety concern.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes simple natural-language questions in a concise 'Why somebody do something?' subject-verb-object form (10,830 prompts from PolitiFact, Snopes, and FactCheck.org, compressed to a 1,000-sample coreset) and feeds them to target LLMs. For each generated answer, GPT-4o produces a 9-field structured label: a binary existence indicator, a ternary toxicity score (1–3), and the specific SemSI text segment for each of three categories (sensitive identity attributes, reputation-harmful contents, incorrect hazardous information). These labels are aggregated into 12 metrics—occurrence rate, toxicity score, and coverage—each computed per-category and overall, enabling cross-model comparison.

## Method

The paper introduces a three-category taxonomy of SemSI (sensitive identity attributes, reputation-harmful contents, incorrect hazardous information) and constructs SemSI-Set by crawling hot news from three fact-checking websites and reformulating them into simple 'Why…?' prompts spanning 2016–2024, with a four-dimensional taxonomy (subject, topic, attitude, truthfulness). Labeling is performed by GPT-4o using a carefully engineered prompt template that enforces JSON output and requires marked segments to contain at least a subject and predicate expressing a harmful viewpoint; this is validated through a mixed human-verification protocol combining a priori independent labeling (5 annotators) and a posteriori review (3 annotators), yielding 95% accuracy on existence and 97% good-case rate. To make benchmarking affordable, the authors apply a coreset-selection strategy that proportionally samples across all 8 intersection subsets of the three SemSI categories, reducing 10,830 prompts to 1,000 (SemSI-cSet) while preserving metric values within ~1% and cutting labeling cost from $200+ to ~$20 per model. The resulting SemSI-Bench evaluates 25 SOTA LLMs (both closed-source and open-source) across 12 metrics, revealing that SemSI is widespread, that chat fine-tuning reduces it by up to 20%, and that model capability does not guarantee lower SemSI risk.

## Results

SemSI-Bench evaluates 25 LLMs using 12 metrics across three SemSI categories (sensitive identity attributes, reputation-harmful contents, incorrect hazardous information). The overall occurrence rate (o-OR) ranges from 26.8% (Gemma-7B-Instruct) to 83.9% (Llama2-7B), with even top commercial models like GPT-4o (42.1%) and Claude3 Opus (43.1%) exhibiting substantial SemSI. Toxicity scores (o-TS) are relatively low (0.6–4.1), but coverage (o-CR) varies dramatically from 5.0% (GPT-J-6B) to 42.0% (Llama3-8B), indicating that some models embed SemSI densely within their outputs. Chat/instruction-tuned models reduce SemSI by up to 20% compared to their base completion counterparts (e.g., Llama3-8B: 72.4% → Llama3-8B-Instruct: 52.0%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a benchmark paper, SemSI-Bench does not propose a new method to compare against baselines; instead, it systematically compares 25 LLMs against each other on SemSI generation. The key comparative findings are: (1) Completion models consistently outperform their chat/instruction-tuned counterparts in SemSI generation — Llama2-7B (83.9% o-OR) vs. Llama2-7B-Chat (59.1%), Llama3-8B (72.4%) vs. Llama3-8B-Instruct (52.0%), and GPT-3.5-Turbo-Instruct (62.8%) vs. GPT-3.5-Turbo (45.3%), showing up to 20% reduction from chat fine-tuning. (2) Model capability does not correlate with safety: within the Claude3 family, Opus (43.1% o-OR) generates more SemSI than Sonnet (30.5%) and Haiku (25.1%), despite being the most capable. (3) Open-source models generally exhibit higher SemSI rates than commercial closed-source models, with Llama2-7B (83.9%) and Llama3-8B (72.4%) at the top, while Gemma-7B-Instruct (26.8%) and GPT-J-6B (35.1%) are the safest open-source options. (4) S-SemSI and R-SemSI are positively correlated across models, but I-SemSI is independent, with Gemini 1.0 Pro (24.7% I-OR) and GPT-J-6B (30.1% I-OR) being notable outliers in incorrect hazardous information generation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by formalizing 'semantic sensitive information' (SemSI) as a distinct safety concern from the well-studied structured sensitive information (PII, phone numbers) that prior work addresses via regex matching and content moderation. It departs from jailbreak-based safety evaluation by showing that simple subject-verb-object questions suffice to trigger SemSI, and from existing LLM safety benchmarks (ToxicChat, HELM, ModelBench) by targeting a previously undefined and unlabeled category of harm. Methodologically, it repurposes the LLM-as-judge paradigm (GPT-4o labeling) combined with human verification and coreset selection, but applies them to a novel labeling task with no prior formalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that roughly 50% of outputs from safety-aligned SOTA LLMs contain SemSI when asked simple natural questions reveals a significant blind spot in current safety alignment, which primarily targets explicit harmful content rather than subtle semantic sensitivity. The three-category taxonomy (identity attributes, reputation harm, incorrect hazardous info) provides a structured framework for future mitigation research, but the paper offers no solution—only measurement. Open questions include whether the three categories are comprehensive, how to mitigate SemSI without the structural anchors that enable regex-based detection, and whether the GPT-4o-based labeling generalizes to non-English or culturally different contexts where sensitivity boundaries shift.
