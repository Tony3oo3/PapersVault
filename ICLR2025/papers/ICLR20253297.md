## Problem

Existing LLM benchmarks suffer from test set contamination (questions leaking into training data) and rely on LLM or human judges that introduce systematic biases such as self-preference, verbosity bias, and formatting sensitivity. No prior benchmark simultaneously resists contamination through frequent question updates, uses only objective ground-truth scoring without LLM judges, and covers a diverse range of challenging tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20253297/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each question prompt includes zero-shot chain-of-thought instructions, a best-guess fallback, and a structured output format (e.g., XML tags or **double asterisks**) to enable automated parsing. The benchmark comprises 1000 questions across 18 tasks in 6 categories, with each question scored 0 or 1 against an objective ground truth; scores are averaged hierarchically from question → task → category → overall LiveBench score. Questions are refreshed monthly by replacing ~1/6 of the set, with the newest batch kept private for one month to maintain a contamination buffer.

## Method

LiveBench constructs questions from two sources: (1) recent information sources (math competitions from the past 11 months, arXiv abstracts with injected typos, Kaggle/Socrata datasets, Guardian news articles, IMDb movie synopses) and (2) harder, modified versions of existing benchmark tasks (AMPS_Hard with larger primitive distributions, Web of Lies v2 with added red herrings, IFEval adapted to Guardian articles with 16 verifiable instructions). All tasks require objectively verifiable answers—multiple choice, exact numeric values, pass@1 code execution, edit distance for proof reordering, or format-compliance checks—eliminating the need for LLM or human judges. The benchmark is maintained through monthly updates that replace the oldest and easiest tasks, with a sustainability plan capping the leaderboard at 40–50 models to keep computational costs tractable. Evaluation uses single-turn inference at temperature 0 with each model's native chat template, and the overall score is the unweighted mean of the six category scores.

## Results

On LiveBench (1000 questions, 18 tasks, 6 categories), o1-preview-2024-09-12 achieves the highest overall score of 64.7%, outperforming all other models by at least 6 percentage points. No model exceeds 70% accuracy, confirming the benchmark's difficulty. The best open-source models (llama-3.1-405b-instruct at 51.1% and qwen2.5-72b-instruct) virtually tie and outperform gpt-4-turbo (49.6%), while the best small open-source model phi-3.5-moe-instruct (6.6B active params) outperforms gpt-3.5. Across monthly updates, model rank correlations remain >0.997, while average scores drop by ~1.2%, indicating the benchmark becomes harder over time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LiveBench is compared to two prominent LLM benchmarks: ChatBot Arena (human preference-based) and Arena-Hard (LLM-judge-based). LiveBench achieves a Pearson correlation of 0.91 with ChatBot Arena and 0.88 with Arena-Hard, indicating broadly consistent model rankings. However, notable divergences exist: GPT-4 models (gpt-4-0125-preview and gpt-4-turbo-2024-04-09) score substantially higher on Arena-Hard than on LiveBench, which the authors attribute to the known self-preference bias of GPT-4 as an LLM judge. Conversely, Gemini-1.5 models appear stronger on ChatBot Arena relative to LiveBench, likely because their output style is preferred by human raters. These discrepancies highlight the advantage of LiveBench's ground-truth scoring, which is immune to style and self-bias. Additionally, an ablation study shows that LLM judges (GPT-4-Turbo) make errors on 10–46% of hard math and reasoning questions (AMC, AIME, SMC, Zebra Puzzles), further motivating objective ground-truth evaluation over LLM-as-a-judge for challenging tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

LiveBench sits at the intersection of two lines of work on LLM evaluation: contamination-resistant benchmarks (LiveCodeBench, which regularly refreshes coding questions) and ground-truth-scored benchmarks (IFEval, AMPS, Big-Bench Hard). It departs from the dominant LLM-as-a-judge paradigm (Arena-Hard, AlpacaEval, MT-Bench) and human-preference paradigms (ChatBot Arena) by insisting on objective, automatically verifiable answers drawn from fresh information sources. Conceptually, it generalizes the 'harder version of an existing task' strategy (AMPS_Hard, Web of Lies v2) across six categories simultaneously, while adding a monthly refresh cycle that no prior multi-category benchmark has attempted.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Test set contamination has rendered many static LLM benchmarks unreliable, and LLM judges introduce documented biases (self-preference, verbosity) that distort rankings—LiveBench addresses both by combining fresh, sourced questions with ground-truth scoring. The preliminary finding that GPT-4-Turbo as a judge errs 38–46% of the time on hard math and Zebra puzzles, even when judging its own outputs, is a concrete warning against the widespread practice of LLM-judging difficult problems. Open questions include whether the monthly refresh cadence is sufficient as models improve rapidly, whether ground-truth scoring can ever cover open-ended generation tasks, and whether prompt-format biases across model families will persist despite the authors' plans to rotate prompts.
