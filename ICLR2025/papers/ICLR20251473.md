## Problem

Common practice in machine learning assumes that more accurate imputation of missing values leads to better downstream predictions, yet theoretical results suggest simple constant imputation can be asymptotically optimal. This paper addresses the critical empirical gap: how much do gains in imputation accuracy actually translate into gains in prediction performance, and under what conditions (model expressiveness, missingness mechanism, use of indicators) do advanced imputers provide meaningful benefits?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251473/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark operates on 19 tabular regression datasets (3–79 features, 3K–50K samples) with MCAR missingness injected at 20% or 50% rates, plus semi-synthetic versions where the outcome is a linear function of the features. Inputs are the imputed feature matrix optionally concatenated with a binary missingness indicator; outputs are test-set R² scores for each (imputer, predictor, indicator) combination. The key analytical outputs are per-dataset regression slopes (prediction R² regressed on imputation R² across 4 imputers × 10 splits) and Pearson correlations, which together quantify both the magnitude and reliability of the imputation-to-prediction transfer.

## Method

The paper constructs a controlled factorial experiment crossing four imputation methods (mean, iterative Bayesian-ridge, missForest, conditional-expectation/Buck's method) with three prediction models (MLP, SAINT transformer, XGBoost), each with and without a binary missingness indicator, at two missing rates, under both MCAR and MNAR (self-censoring) mechanisms. For each dataset and model configuration, 40 (imputation R², prediction R²) pairs are generated (4 imputers × 10 random splits), and a linear regression with the split ID as a covariate is fit to estimate the slope linking imputation accuracy to prediction accuracy. The central finding is that slopes are typically ≤0.1 (often near zero for XGBoost), meaning a 0.3 R² improvement in imputation yields only ~0.0075 R² in prediction. The effect is systematically smaller for more expressive models, when the missingness indicator is included, and for non-linear (real-data) outcomes versus linear (semi-synthetic) outcomes, establishing MCAR as a best-case upper bound on imputation's predictive value.

## Results

Across 19 regression datasets with MCAR missingness (20% and 50%), the slope relating imputation R² gains to prediction R² gains is typically ≤0.1, meaning a 0.1 improvement in imputation R² yields at most a 0.01 improvement in prediction R². For XGBoost, the average slope is ~0.025 or less (near zero without the indicator at 20% missingness), so the full 0.3 R² gap between the best and worst imputers translates to only ~0.0075 in prediction R². The best pipeline (missforest + XGBoost + indicator) outperforms all other methods in only 4 out of 19 datasets. Correlations between imputation and prediction quality are systematically 0.1–0.3 lower for real (non-linear) outcomes than for semi-synthetic linear outcomes, and under MNAR (self-censoring) the effects are consistently smaller or even negative for XGBoost and SAINT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the four imputation methods, missforest achieves the highest imputation R² at 20% missingness, followed by condexp and iterativeBR (nearly tied), while mean imputation is significantly worse; at 50% missingness, condexp is the most robust and is two orders of magnitude faster than missforest. However, these imputation advantages translate into minimal prediction gains: missforest-based predictors outperform mean-imputation predictors by only ~0.0075 R² with XGBoost (slope ≈ 0.025), and the effect is statistically significant only for the least expressive model (MLP), not for SAINT or XGBoost. Adding the missingness indicator as input reduces the effect size of imputation quality on prediction (e.g., MLP slope drops from 0.23 to −0.01 on Bike_Sharing_Demand at 50% missingness), and the indicator itself improves prediction even under MCAR where missingness is uninformative. Under MNAR self-censoring, mean imputation performs comparably to or better than advanced methods for XGBoost and SAINT without the indicator, further diminishing the value of sophisticated imputation. Overall, the paper's central finding is that investing in better imputation (e.g., missforest over mean) yields diminishing and often negligible returns for downstream prediction, especially with expressive models like XGBoost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper empirically tests and quantifies predictions from a line of theoretical work (Le Morvan et al. 2021, Josse et al. 2019, Ayme et al. 2023) showing that simple imputations can be asymptotically optimal for prediction, by measuring the actual slope between imputation R² and prediction R² across controlled settings. It departs from prior imputation-for-prediction benchmarks (Paterakis et al. 2024, Perez-Lebel et al. 2022, Shadbahr et al. 2023) by focusing on MCAR as a best-case upper bound, systematically varying model expressiveness and missingness-indicator usage, and including semi-synthetic linear outcomes to isolate the effect of response non-linearity. Rather than identifying the best pipeline, the paper's methodological contribution is the regression-based quantification of how much prediction gains per unit of imputation gain, across 19 datasets, 4 imputers, 3 predictors, and 2 missing rates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that investing in advanced imputation yields diminishing returns for prediction, especially with expressive models and missingness indicators—provides clear guidance for practitioners who currently default to complex imputation pipelines. The finding that the missingness indicator improves prediction even under MCAR (where it carries no information about the missing values) is a genuinely new result with a plausible mechanistic explanation (simplifying discontinuous prediction functions on imputed points). Open questions remain about whether stochastic/multiple imputations outperform deterministic ones, whether better missingness encodings (learned embeddings, missingness-aware layers) can further reduce the need for accurate imputation, and whether these conclusions hold for classification tasks or high-dimensional settings.
