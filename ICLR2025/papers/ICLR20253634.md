## Problem

Density-based clustering methods like DBSCAN do not optimize a closed-form objective, making it difficult to incorporate group-level fairness constraints that ensure sensitive groups are proportionally represented within each cluster. Existing fair clustering algorithms (FairSC, Fairlets, Scalable Fair Clustering) are either centroid/spectral-based, cannot handle categorical features, support only a single binary sensitive attribute, or destroy the density-connectivity structure when locally merging points. No method existed that produces fair, density-connected clusters of arbitrary shape while handling mixed-type data and multiple sensitive attributes simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The method takes a dataset with at least some numerical features (required for dc-distance computation) plus optional categorical features, group-membership labels for one or more sensitive attributes, a target number of clusters k, and a minimum cluster size μ (default 2d−1). It outputs a hard cluster assignment for each point into one of k clusters, with points belonging to subtrees of the dc-distance hierarchy smaller than μ labeled as noise (index −1). Categorical attributes are incorporated via a Goodall similarity measure weighted proportionally to the number of categorical vs. numerical features, and multiple sensitive attributes are combined into intersectional groups (e.g., 'Female-Asian') that are balanced jointly rather than independently.

## Method

FairDen transforms the discrete density-connectivity problem into a continuous spectral one by computing the density-connectivity distance (dc-distance) between all pairs of points, which yields an ultrametric tree hierarchy analogous to single-linkage clustering. An affinity matrix is constructed from normalized dc-distances (or a weighted blend with Goodall similarity for categorical features), and its graph Laplacian is formed. Fairness is enforced by constructing a fairness matrix F whose columns encode deviations of combined sensitive-group memberships from their global proportions, then imposing the linear constraint F^T H = 0 on the cluster indicator matrix H. The constrained normalized-cut objective is solved by projecting onto the orthonormal basis of the nullspace of F^T, symmetrizing the resulting quadratic form via a square-root transform, and extracting the k smallest eigenvectors of the transformed Laplacian. K-means is applied to the resulting spectral embedding to obtain the final clustering, and subtrees of the dc-distance hierarchy with fewer than μ points are automatically labeled as noise.

## Results

On four real-world benchmark datasets (Adult, Bank, Communities and Crime, Diabetes), FairDen achieves balance values of 0.83–0.98, substantially outperforming DBSCAN (0.01–0.79) and FairSC (0.23–0.89) on the group-level fairness metric. For density-based cluster quality measured by DCSI, FairDen attains 0.04–0.24, the highest among all fair clustering methods (FairSC: 0.00–0.19, Fairlet: 0.00–0.05, Scalable: 0.01–0.08), though well below unconstrained DBSCAN (0.65–0.99) which does not enforce fairness. On the Diabetes dataset with k=4, FairDen reaches balance 0.95 and DCSI 0.24, while FairSC drops to balance 0.23 and DCSI 0.01.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DBSCAN, FairDen dramatically improves balance (e.g., 0.86 vs. 0.01 on Adult-gender, 0.92 vs. 0.01 on Communities) at the cost of DCSI (0.04–0.24 vs. 0.65–0.99), which is expected since DBSCAN does not enforce any fairness constraint. Against FairSC and normalized FairSC, FairDen consistently wins on balance (e.g., 0.98 vs. 0.42/0.88 on Bank; 0.83 vs. 0.34/0.32 on Adult-race) and on DCSI (0.04–0.24 vs. 0.00–0.19), confirming that the dc-distance-based Laplacian preserves density structure better than a kNN-graph Laplacian. Against Fairlet (MCF) and Scalable Fair Clustering, the balance comparison is mixed: Fairlet/Scalable occasionally achieve slightly higher balance (0.94–0.99 vs. 0.86–0.92 on Adult-gender and Communities), but FairDen substantially outperforms them on DCSI (0.04–0.24 vs. 0.00–0.08), indicating that fairlet-based merging destroys density-connectivity. Additionally, FairDen is the only method that handles non-binary sensitive attributes (e.g., race with 5 groups, marital status with 7 groups), multiple sensitive attributes simultaneously, and categorical features, whereas Fairlet and Scalable are restricted to binary sensitive attributes and all four baselines cannot process categorical data.

## Relation to Prior Work

FairDen bridges two previously separate lines of work: the density-connectivity distance (dc-distance) from Beer et al. (2023), which provides a continuous, ultrametric representation of DBSCAN's discrete density-connectivity property, and the fairness-constrained spectral clustering framework of Kleindessner et al. (2019b), which enforces group balance via nullspace projection on a Laplacian. The key conceptual departure is that all prior fair clustering methods (FairSC, Fairlets, Scalable Fair Clustering) operate on centroid-based or partitioning objectives that are incompatible with density-connectivity, categorical attributes, and noise detection. FairDen repurposes the dc-distance hierarchy as the affinity structure for spectral clustering, thereby making the fairness constraint applicable to density-based clusters for the first time, while extending the fairness formulation to handle multiple sensitive attributes simultaneously (intersectional fairness) and mixed-type data via Goodall similarity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work fills a concrete gap: no prior method could produce fair, density-connected clusters that handle categorical sensitive attributes (the most common real-world case) and noise simultaneously. The practical impact is significant for domains like school district planning, medical resource allocation, and criminal justice where density-connected geographic clusters and categorical protected attributes coexist. Key limitations include O(n³) complexity limiting scalability, the requirement for at least k members per sensitive group, and the fact that balance is optimized over combined groups rather than individual attributes, which may not always align with application-specific fairness goals. Open questions include whether Nyström approximation or GPU parallelism can make the method practical for very large datasets, and how the approach extends to other fairness notions beyond group balance.
