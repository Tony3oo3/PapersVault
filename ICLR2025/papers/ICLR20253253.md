## Problem

While it is commonly assumed that more sensing always improves a robot's task performance, there is no systematic empirical framework to quantify when and how much sensory information actually matters at specific moments during task execution. This paper addresses the fundamental question of what value sensory information holds to a decision-making agent at various points in time, revealing that sensing is surprisingly rarely task-critical in many standard robotic benchmarks.

## Input / Output

The method takes a frozen look-ahead policy (TD-MPC2 or Diffusion Policy) and a simulator environment as input, and produces VoSI profiles that map each state s and open-loop horizon h to the expected reward loss from withholding sensing for h steps. For each state, the evaluation requires generating approximately 500 Monte Carlo trajectories (100 h-values × 5 rollouts), making it computationally expensive; for Diffusion Policy, states are pre-filtered using a plan-disagreement metric to focus on object-interaction regions. The framework also outputs task regret profiles (normalized regret vs. sensing rate) and, optionally, a greedy sensing schedule that allocates a user-specified regret budget across the episode.

## Method

The core contribution is the Value of Sensory Information (VoSI) framework, which exploits the mixed-loop execution mode of look-ahead policies: the policy generates an n-step action chunk, but the agent can commit to executing only h ≤ n steps open-loop before re-sensing. VoSI(s, h) is defined as the difference in expected cumulative reward between closed-loop execution and mixed-loop execution (sensing withheld for h steps) starting from state s, yielding a state-wise regret profile. The paper identifies three prototypical VoSI profile shapes—flat, gradual, and stepped—that correspond to distinct task phases (post-completion, sustained contact, and dynamic transitions, respectively). A key theoretical insight is that under deterministic dynamics and perfect sensing, an optimal policy requires no sensing after t=0, and that finite policy expressivity (model error, limited training data) plays an analogous role to stochastic dynamics in generating sensing needs. The framework is validated across 7 robotic tasks with two policy architectures, extended to noisy sensing and a quadruped climbing task, and leveraged to construct a greedy execution strategy that outperforms fixed-rate sensing at most sensing budgets.

## Results

Across 7 robotic tasks, most policies achieve near-zero normalized regret even when sensing is reduced to once every 5 steps, and the three Robosuite manipulation tasks (square-nut, coffee-pod, threading) show no substantial performance degradation even under fully open-loop execution (sensing only at t=0). VoSI profiles across tasks fall into three prototypical shapes: flat (sensing rarely matters), gradual (value accrues steadily with delay, common in contact-rich tasks like Push-T and finger-spin), and stepped (sharp increases at phase transitions, as in cup-catch and swingup). The proposed greedy VoSI-based execution strategy outperforms fixed-rate mixed-loop execution on the four-rooms task at most sensing rates, and the reward-free VoSI-S metric achieves rank correlations of 0.78–0.98 with the reward-based VoSI-R across tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares TD-MPC2 (model-based RL) and Diffusion Policy (imitation learning) on the shared tasks swingup and Push-T: TD-MPC2 exhibits slightly lower regret under reduced sensing on Push-T, while DP degrades more sharply during the dynamic swingup phase but less during the balance phase. On the four-rooms grid world, the proposed greedy VoSI-based strategy outperforms the fixed-rate mixed-loop baseline at most sensing rates, achieving lower normalized regret for the same sensing budget. Against an event-triggered control baseline (which has access to a coarse sub-grid sensor at every step), the greedy strategy is outperformed in the deterministic setting but matches it under stochastic dynamics without the extra sensor. Across training stages, expert TD-MPC2 checkpoints (200K steps on finger-spin, 2M on Push-T) retain substantially more performance under reduced sensing than beginner checkpoints (14K/200K steps), confirming that sensing efficiency correlates with task proficiency. Larger model capacities (512-dim latent) also show greater robustness to sensor loss than smaller ones (32-dim latent), with the effect more pronounced on Push-T (73.7 vs. 33.8 return at full sensing) than finger-spin (137.9 vs. 124.3).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the classical Value of Information (VoI) framework from decision theory (Howard, 1966) into a practical empirical tool for robotics, introducing 'Value of Sensory Information' (VoSI) measured via mixed-loop execution of frozen look-ahead policies. It departs from event-triggered control (Aström, 2008) and information-constrained optimization (Tishby & Polani, 2010), which design controllers under sensing budgets, by instead characterizing the sensing needs of an already-trained policy. It also extends prior VoI applications in control (Flaspohler et al., 2020; Majumdar et al., 2023) from simple analytic or POMDP settings to complex, learned policies (TD-MPC2, Diffusion Policy) on realistic robotic benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The VoSI framework provides a practical, policy-agnostic diagnostic for identifying when a robot truly needs to sense, enabling more resource-efficient deployment of learned policies. The finding that well-trained policies are paradoxically less sensor-dependent, and that many manipulation tasks tolerate near-open-loop execution, challenges the default assumption in robotics that continuous sensing is necessary. Key limitations include restriction to frozen policies, predominantly deterministic dynamics, and simulation-only validation; open questions include whether VoSI can inform policy training itself, how it behaves under partial observability and sensor noise in deployment, and whether the greedy execution strategy generalizes beyond the toy grid-world demonstration.
