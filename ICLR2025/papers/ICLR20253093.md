## Problem

While interactive proofs from complexity theory offer a principled way for a weak verifier to check a strong prover's work, prior neural implementations (adp, debate, mac) suffer from theoretical limitations—most notably that the adp protocol's equilibrium-to-validity correspondence breaks down under realistic assumptions—and have only been tested on small models and toy problems. This paper addresses the gap between the theoretical power of interactive proofs and their practical deployment with neural networks, asking how to design and train prover-verifier protocols whose equilibria provably correspond to valid (and optionally zero-knowledge) proof systems.

## Input / Output

The verifier receives a problem instance x sampled from a distribution P (a natural-language specification paired with a Python program for code validation, or a pair of ~10-vertex Erdős-Rényi graphs for isomorphism testing) along with a labeled training dataset D. Through multi-round messaging over protocol-defined channels, the prover and verifier exchange messages before the verifier emits a binary accept/reject decision m_T ∈ {0,1}; the full message sequence constitutes the 'proof.' In the code validation experiment, GPT-4o serves as the prover and GPT-4o-mini as the verifier, with bugs of median Levenshtein distance 7 inserted into correct solutions to create negative instances.

## Method

The paper introduces a unifying framework of generalized prover-verifier games (PVGs) built on messaging games, which subsumes adp, debate, and mac while enabling new protocols: nip (single prover/verifier with worst-case losses), mnip (two non-communicating provers), and zk-nip/zk-mnip (adding a simulator and malicious verifier to enforce zero-knowledge). The key theoretical result (Theorem 8) establishes that a strategy profile is a valid interactive proof system if and only if it is an approximate verifier-leading Stackelberg equilibrium of the nip game, with the prover's loss defined as the difference between worst-case losses on positive and negative instances. Training exploits timescale separation (LOLA-style updates) so the prover adapts faster than the verifier, approximating the Stackelberg hierarchy. Empirically, the code validation task uses 8 rounds of expert iteration to fine-tune GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini, while the graph isomorphism task trains a GNN verifier and transformer prover via independent PPO, with a 'stabilised' expert iteration variant introduced to prevent mode collapse in the verifier's acceptance rate.

## Results

On the code validation task (APPS dataset with GPT-4o provers and GPT-4o-mini verifiers), the proposed nip and mnip protocols achieve approximately 11% improvement in mean test accuracy over a solo verifier baseline, with mnip performing best on worst-case robustness (percentage of data points where the verifier always fails). On the graph isomorphism task, nip verifiers learn to near-perfectly classify valid instances when interacting with the prover, while adp, debate, and mac protocols show no meaningful improvement. The zk-nip protocol demonstrates a roughly negative correlation between the prover's ZK loss coefficient and information leakage, confirming convergence toward lower-knowledge equilibria.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the adp protocol (Anil et al., 2021), the proposed nip protocol clearly outperforms in both experimental domains: on graph isomorphism, adp verifiers show no performance increase (the protocol's single-message structure prevents the verifier from querying the prover), while nip verifiers reach near-perfect accuracy; on code validation, nip achieves ~11% accuracy improvement over the solo baseline whereas adp shows only modest gains. Against the debate protocol (Irving et al., 2018), nip and mnip again outperform: debate verifiers suffer from a high precision-to-recall ratio (mode collapse) exacerbated by expert iteration, and debate shows no improvement on graph isomorphism due to the difficulty of learning cross-examination with multiple provers. Against the mac protocol (Waldchen et al., 2024), nip and mnip achieve superior worst-case robustness on code validation, with mnip performing best on the percentage of always-failing data points. The paper also shows that the adp protocol's theoretical guarantees are fundamentally limited (Proposition 7): its Stackelberg equilibria are neither necessary nor sufficient for validity, a gap that nip closes by using worst-case loss and approximate equilibria (Theorem 8).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalises the prover-verifier game framework of Anil et al. (2021) into a unified messaging-game formalism that subsumes the adp, debate (Irving et al., 2018), and Merlin-Arthur Classifier (Waldchen et al., 2024) protocols, while introducing new protocols (nip, mnip, zk-nip, zk-mnip) with explicit theoretical guarantees. A key departure is the demonstration (Proposition 7) that the adp protocol's Stackelberg equilibria do not correspond to valid interactive proofs once standard assumptions (deterministic verifier, zero soundness error) are relaxed, motivating the new nip protocol whose equilibria do correspond (Theorem 8). The empirical evaluation with GPT-4o/GPT-4o-mini on the APPS code-validation benchmark positions this work alongside concurrent efforts (Kenton et al., 2024; Kirchner et al., 2024; Arnesen et al., 2024) but distinguishes itself by providing both a unifying theoretical framework and a systematic cross-protocol comparison.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a much-needed theoretical foundation for neural interactive proofs, clarifying which protocol structures actually yield valid proof systems and under what conditions worst-case (rather than expected) loss minimisation is achievable. However, the empirical gains are modest (~11% accuracy improvement over a solo verifier on code validation), and the authors acknowledge significant limitations: only two evaluation domains, no use of the advanced bi-level optimisation or adversarial training methods described in the theory, and no evaluation of all protocol variants. The most important open question is closing the gap between complexity-theoretic guarantees (which assume unbounded provers and PPT verifiers) and learning-theoretic results that would characterise how finite-capability neural agents and finite datasets affect verification quality as model scale grows.
