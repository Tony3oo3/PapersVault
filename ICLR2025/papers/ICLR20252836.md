## Problem

Goal-conditioned RL agents typically require dense reward functions, expert demonstrations, or manually-designed curricula of subgoals to explore effectively toward a target state. This paper tackles the harder setting where only a single observation of the goal state is provided—no rewards, no demonstrations, no distance metrics, and no set of easier training goals—yet the agent must still learn to reach that goal from scratch.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252836/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single goal-state observation (e.g., a screenshot of the block in the target bin) and the current state observation during rollout; no reward signal of any kind is ever provided. It produces a goal-conditioned policy π(a|s,g) that, when conditioned on the single target goal, outputs continuous actions. Notably, the single goal is used for both data collection and evaluation, while the actor loss is trained on goals sampled from future states in the replay buffer rather than exclusively on the target goal.

## Method

The approach is a minimal modification of Contrastive RL (CRL): during data collection, the policy is always commanded to reach the single hard goal s*, rather than sampling from a distribution of goals of varying difficulty. The critic uses an inner-product architecture φ(s,a)ᵀψ(s_f) trained with an infoNCE contrastive objective plus LogSumExp regularization, where positive examples are future states along the trajectory and negatives are shuffled. The actor maximizes the log-Q value φ(s,a)ᵀψ(s_f) plus an adaptive entropy bonus, with goals drawn from the replay buffer. The key empirical finding is that despite the value function predicting unreliable values for the (initially unreachable) target goal, the contrastive representations still encode environment dynamics that drive directed exploration and emergent skill acquisition—ablations confirm that replacing the inner-product critic with a monolithic Q(s,a,s_f) network eliminates the single-goal advantage.

## Results

Single-goal CRL achieves its first success within thousands of trials: 3,197 trials (Sawyer Box), 9,329 (Sawyer Bin), 15,895 (Sawyer Peg), and 11,724 (Point Spiral), compared to zero successes for a random policy over 75M environment steps (Sawyer tasks) or 40M steps (Point Spiral). All five random seeds solve all three manipulation tasks, and the method significantly outperforms the multi-goal CRL oracle (which has access to a human-designed curriculum of easy-to-hard goals) across all four environments. The method also outperforms SAC with dense rewards, SAC with sparse rewards + HER, and RIS (imagined subgoals), with prior methods rarely reaching the goal at all except RIS on the simplest task (Point Spiral).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Single-goal CRL outperforms multi-goal CRL (the oracle variant with human-designed goals of varying difficulty) on all four environments, despite the oracle having access to a richer set of training goals. Against SAC with dense rewards (which uses the Metaworld reward function or distance-to-goal for spiral), single-goal CRL achieves substantially higher success rates, showing that the contrastive RL algorithm itself is critical for single-goal exploration to work. SAC with sparse rewards and SAC+HER both fail to reach the goal in the manipulation tasks, confirming that standard actor-critic methods cannot handle this level of sparsity. RIS (Reinforcement Learning with Imagined Subgoals) is the strongest baseline, reaching the goal on Point Spiral but failing on the three Sawyer manipulation tasks, while single-goal CRL solves all four. The ablation in Figure 10 further confirms that the gains are not due to overfitting: training the actor on only the single goal degrades performance, and the contrastive inner-product critic architecture (vs. a monolithic Q-network) is essential for single-goal exploration to be effective.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is a direct, minimal modification of Contrastive RL (CRL; Eysenbach et al., 2022): rather than sampling a distribution of training goals for data collection, the agent always commands the single hard target goal. It departs from the broader GCRL literature that typically assumes access to dense rewards, distance metrics, expert demonstrations, or manually-designed curricula of subgoals, and from automatic subgoal-proposal methods like RIS (Chane-Sane et al., 2021). The contrastive inner-product critic architecture and infoNCE + LogSumExp training objective are inherited unchanged from CRL; the only algorithmic change is the data-collection policy. The paper positions itself as lifting the 'easier for humans, harder for agents' assumption that prior work relies on.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result challenges a core working assumption in exploration research: that directed exploration requires either shaped rewards, curricula, or uncertainty-based mechanisms. A trivially simple rule (always aim for the one hard goal) outperforms all of these, yet the authors explicitly acknowledge they lack a theoretical explanation for why. Open questions include: (1) what mechanism in the contrastive representations drives the emergent curriculum of skills, (2) whether the approach extends to settings without even a single goal observation, and (3) whether the manipulation-task focus generalizes to other domains. The practical implication is significant — a method with no additional hyperparameters that automatically trades off exploration and exploitation could simplify the RL practitioner's toolkit.
