## Problem

Adversarial imitation learning (IRL) is traditionally framed as a GAN-like two-player zero-sum game, inheriting well-known issues of unstable training dynamics and mode collapse. While diffusion models have emerged as a stable, non-adversarial alternative for generative modeling via regression-based score matching, it remains unclear how to lift these insights to the sequential imitation learning setting where the learner must interact with the environment over a horizon.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252573/cropped_page3_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes state-only expert demonstrations (no action labels required) and reward-free environment rollouts, operating in the harder observation-only setting. It produces a stochastic policy mapping continuous states to actions. The cost signal used for RL is computed by sampling noise levels uniformly, adding Gaussian noise to states, and evaluating the difference between pre-trained expert and current learner score-matching losses in a noise-prediction form analogous to DDPM training.

## Method

SMILING replaces the adversarial discriminator with a Diffusion Score Divergence (DS Divergence), a novel sequential generalization of Fisher divergence that measures discrepancy between expert and learner state distributions by comparing their diffusion score functions along the forward OU process. The algorithm pre-trains an expert score function via least-squares regression, then iteratively (i) trains a learner score function on the DAgger-aggregated mixture of all prior policies, and (ii) updates the policy via RL (SAC or DreamerV3) under a cost equal to the difference between expert and learner score-matching losses. A critical design choice is the variance-correction term: naively substituting an unbiased score estimator into the squared objective introduces unbounded error, so the algorithm subtracts a second regression loss whose minimizer is the Bayes-optimal learner score, yielding a bounded and faithful approximation. Theoretically, this yields first- and second-order instance-dependent regret bounds with linear (not compounding) scaling in the horizon, and the second-order bound scales with the minimum of expert and learner return variances.

## Results

SMILING is evaluated on six continuous control tasks (Ball-in-Cup-Catch, Cheetah-Run, Humanoid-Walk, Humanoid-Sit, Humanoid-Crawl, Humanoid-Pole) from DeepMind Control Suite and HumanoidBench, using state-only expert demonstrations of 5K steps. The method matches expert performance (normalized return = 1) on five of six tasks and outperforms all baselines on five of six, with large margins on four. On humanoid-sit, no method reaches expert performance but SMILING converges fastest; on cheetah-run, SMILING surpasses DAC at 3M steps after DAC becomes unstable. Results are reported as learning curves with normalized returns (expert = 1, random = 0) across five random seeds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DAC (the primary GAN-style baseline using JS-divergence), SMILING wins on 5 of 6 tasks: it outperforms DAC by a large margin on Ball-in-Cup-Catch, Humanoid-Walk, Humanoid-Crawl, and Humanoid-Pole, where DAC collapses entirely on the latter two. On Cheetah-Run, DAC initially converges faster but oscillates significantly after 2M steps, while SMILING remains stable and surpasses it by 3M steps. Against IQ-Learn, SMILING outperforms it on all tasks. DiffAIL (diffusion-based adversarial IL) collapses on all four HumanoidBench tasks due to numerical instability (network weights diverging to infinity), so no results could be reported for those tasks. Against Behavioral Cloning (which uses state-action data and thus serves as an upper bound for observation-only BC), both SMILING and DAC consistently outperform BC, confirming the advantage of interactive RL over pure offline cloning. In a linear-function ablation on Cheetah-Run, DAC's performance degrades substantially (slower convergence, higher variance) while SMILING remains effective, supporting the paper's claim that score functions are more expressive than discriminators for the same function class.

## Relation to Prior Work

SMILING departs from the dominant adversarial IRL paradigm (GAIL, DAC, AIRL, f-GAIL) that frames imitation as a two-player zero-sum game approximating f-divergences or IPMs via discriminator training. Instead, it lifts the score-matching objective from diffusion models (Ho et al., Song & Ermon) into the sequential setting, defining a novel Diffusion Score Divergence that is minimized via pure regression. This distinguishes it from prior diffusion-for-IL work (DiffAIL, Huang et al.) that still embeds diffusion within the f-divergence discriminator framework. Theoretically, it connects to the line of second-order regret bounds in RL (Wang et al. 2024c) by showing that score matching, unlike MLE, is a computationally tractable path to these bounds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, non-adversarial alternative to GAN-style IRL with provable avoidance of compounding errors and instance-dependent (second-order) regret bounds that were previously only achievable via intractable MLE. Empirically, it is the first IRL method to solve multiple HumanoidBench tasks from state-only demonstrations, suggesting practical value for robot learning where action labels are unavailable. Open questions include whether the theoretical assumptions (Lipschitz score functions, bounded second moments) hold in high-dimensional real-world settings, how the method scales to longer horizons or more complex state spaces, and whether the DS Divergence can be extended to other diffusion processes beyond the OU process.
