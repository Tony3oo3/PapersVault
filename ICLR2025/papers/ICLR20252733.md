## Problem

Non-equilibrium relaxation dynamics reach different degenerate ground states at different rates, but how this bias arises in algorithms that combine reversible (detailed-balance-preserving) and non-reversible (chaotic) search dynamics remains unexplored. Existing MCMC methods like simulated annealing struggle with rugged landscapes and broken ergodicity, while purely chaotic methods like CAC do not sample from the correct Boltzmann distribution. This paper asks: what happens to ground-state sampling when you hybridize continuous-time chaotic dynamics with Metropolis-Hastings corrections, and how does the resulting non-equilibrium dynamics differentially access 'easy' versus 'hard' degenerate solutions?

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes an N×N symmetric Ising coupling matrix (zero diagonal), an inverse temperature β, and a set of hyperparameters (α, ξ, a, γ, β_eff, f_MH, n) that control the deterministic dynamics and MH frequency. Internally, discrete spins are relaxed to continuous 'soft spins' x ∈ [-1,1]^N, and the algorithm iterates Euler steps of a second-order ODE before probabilistically sampling a discrete configuration σ ∈ {-1,1}^N. The output is either a single ground-state configuration, the ground-state energy, or a full sample from the Boltzmann distribution; on the constructed degenerate Wishart planted instances, the output distribution over D degenerate ground states is explicitly non-uniform at finite runtime, with 'easy' states (larger basins of attraction) sampled more frequently.

## Method

MHCACm (Metropolis-Hastings Chaotic Amplitude Control with momentum) operates in two alternating phases: (1) a deterministic continuous-time trajectory of n Euler steps governed by a second-order ODE with momentum (γ term) and auxiliary amplitude variables e that dynamically pre-condition the Hessian of the energy function, and (2) a probabilistic jump to a discrete spin configuration followed by a Metropolis-Hastings acceptance test that restores detailed balance. The MH acceptance ratio is computed from the log-probabilities of the deterministic trajectory endpoints, making it computationally cheap since all required terms are already available from the ODE integration. The algorithm generalizes simulated annealing, Hopfield networks, AIM, CAC, and CACm as limiting cases (Table 1), with the MH frequency f_MH controlling the transition between purely chaotic search (f_MH=1) and purely MCMC (f_MH=1/T). The key empirical finding is that the MH step introduces a systematic bias toward 'easy' degenerate ground states—those surrounded by denser low-energy basins—reducing TTS_easy while increasing TTS_hard, a behavior absent in the purely chaotic CACm variant.

## Results

On biased degenerate Wishart planted instances (b=12, N=100), MHCACm achieves a time-to-solution (TTS) of 1.40×10⁻³ steps, outperforming CACm (9.41×10⁻³), CAC (238.81×10⁻³), and simulated annealing (27.56×10⁻³) by 6.7×, 170×, and 20× respectively. For the easy ground state (TTSeasy), MHCACm reaches 1.47×10⁻³ steps versus 18.54×10⁻³ for CACm, while CACm remains superior for the hard ground state (TTShard: 25.08×10⁻³ vs. 318.16×10⁻³). On GSET Max-Cut instances (N=800), MHCACm finds best-known solutions up to 23× faster than dSBM (e.g., TTS 14,592 vs. 339,332 steps on instance 1). On GPU (NVIDIA V100), MHCACm achieves a 100× real-time speedup over CPU-based simulated annealing for sampling easy optimal solutions (5.18×10⁻⁵ s vs. 0.0567 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MHCACm decisively outperforms simulated annealing (SA), parallel tempering (PT), and Gibbs-with-gradient (GWG) on TTS for both N=100 and N=140, with SA and GWG completely unable to sample the hard degenerate ground state (TTS_hard = N/A). Against the closest analog baseline CACm, MHCACm achieves a ~6.7× speedup on TTS and ~12.6× on TTS_easy (N=100), but CACm is ~12.7× faster on TTS_hard, revealing that the MH step introduces a bias toward easily accessible ground states at the cost of harder ones. On GSET Max-Cut instances (N=800), MHCACm beats dSBM (Goto et al., 2021) by 1.5×–23× in TTS across all eight tested instances. In the appendix comparison with iSCO (Sun et al., 2023), MHCACm wins on unbiased instances (p₀ = 0.0008 vs. 0.0003) but loses on biased instances (p₀ = 0.15 vs. 0.51) after T=500 steps, suggesting iSCO's non-reversible dynamics are more effective at navigating biased landscapes in the short-time regime. Overall, MHCACm is the fastest algorithm for finding any ground state and the easiest ground state, while purely analog methods (CACm, AIM) retain an advantage for the hardest degenerate solutions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of work on Ising/Max-Cut optimization: non-reversible chaotic search dynamics (Chaotic Amplitude Control, Leleu et al. 2019/2021; Analog Iterative Machines, Kalinin et al. 2023) and reversible MCMC methods (Simulated Annealing, Parallel Tempering). The key conceptual move is wrapping the deterministic CAC trajectory with a Metropolis-Hastings acceptance criterion, yielding MHCACm — a valid MCMC that provably converges to the Boltzmann distribution while retaining the global-exploration properties of chaotic dynamics. The authors show in Table 1 that SA, Hopfield networks, AIM, CAC, and CACm are all limiting cases of MHCACm, positioning it as a strict generalization. The benchmark contribution extends the Wishart Planted Ensemble (Hamze et al. 2020) with a tunable bias parameter that creates degenerate ground states of unequal accessibility, enabling controlled study of non-equilibrium sampling bias.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that MH-driven dynamics systematically reach 'easy' degenerate ground states faster than 'hard' ones — while purely chaotic dynamics (CACm) show no such bias — provides a concrete mechanism for understanding how non-equilibrium optimization dynamics select among degenerate solutions, with direct implications for why certain minima are preferentially found during training of overparameterized neural networks. The 100× GPU speedup over CPU simulated annealing makes the approach practically relevant for large-scale combinatorial optimization. However, the theoretical understanding of why the MH step induces this bias remains out of scope, the advantage disappears when no bias exists among ground states, and the connection to neural network generalization is only mentioned as future work rather than demonstrated.
