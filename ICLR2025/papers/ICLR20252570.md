## Problem

Current construction-based neural solvers for vehicle routing problems predominantly rely on Transformer architectures, which suffer from quadratic self-attention complexity that limits scalability to large instances and produces solutions lacking diversity. This paper addresses both limitations by proposing a GFlowNet-based constructive solver that avoids Transformers entirely while leveraging adversarial training to balance exploration and exploitation during solution generation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252570/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a sparse graph representation of a VRP instance where each node retains only k = |V|/4 shortest outgoing edges, with node features derived from 2D coordinates and edge features from travel distances. For CVRP, customer demands and a fixed vehicle capacity (C=50) are additional inputs. At inference, the model produces N=100 candidate routes via hybrid decoding (5% sampling, 95% greedy), from which the best route and its total travel distance are selected.

## Method

AGFN couples a GFlowNet generator (using a lightweight GNN with residual node/edge updates and mean-pooling aggregation) with a discriminator trained to distinguish raw generated paths from locally-searched 'true' paths. The generator is trained via trajectory balance loss where the discriminator's quality score is embedded into the reward function, providing nuanced feedback that prevents over-exploration while preserving solution diversity. The two networks are trained alternately at a 4:1 generator-to-discriminator ratio. At inference, a hybrid decoding strategy selects each next node by sampling from the edge probability distribution with probability P=0.05 and by greedy argmax with probability 0.95, exploiting GFlowNet's balanced edge evaluation to avoid premature convergence to local optima.

## Results

On synthetic CVRP instances, AGFN-500 (trained on 500 nodes) achieves a gap of −2.11% relative to LKH-3(100) on 1,000-node instances, while solving each instance in 0.72 s—roughly 2.3× faster than POMO (1.62 s) and 6.8× faster than GANCO (4.88 s). On 500-node instances, AGFN-500 attains a 3.70% gap with 0.46 s runtime, outperforming POMO(*8) by 8.90% in objective value. On the CVRPLib and TSPLib real-world benchmarks, AGFN-100 achieves gaps of 17.95% and 27.36% respectively, beating POMO(*8) by 12.61% and 15.10% while reducing computation time by 73.06% and 82.61%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LKH-3, AGFN-500 and AGFN-1000 (trained at matching scale) surpass LKH-3(100) on 1,000-node CVRP instances (gaps of −2.11% and −1.65%), but LKH-3 with 1,000 or 10,000 iterations still achieves lower objectives at all scales. Against POMO and POMO(*8), AGFN wins on 500- and 1,000-node instances in both objective (8.90% and 30.51% better than POMO(*8) at 500 and 1,000 nodes) and runtime (0.46 s vs 0.88 s; 0.72 s vs 3.20 s), though it is slightly worse on 200-node objectives. Against GANCO, AGFN achieves a 0.29% objective improvement on 500 nodes and 7.86% on 1,000 nodes while being 2.9× and 6.8× faster, respectively. Against NeuOpt, AGFN is dramatically superior—18.76% better on 200 nodes and 62.17% better on 500 nodes—with runtimes 99% lower. Against ACO and GFACS (GFlowNet+ACO), AGFN outperforms by 57–66% in objective and 95–97% in runtime across all three scales, confirming that the adversarial GFlowNet framework eliminates the need for external heuristic search at inference time.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the GFlowNet generative framework (Bengio et al., 2021) and its Trajectory Balance training objective (Malkin et al., 2022), repurposing them for constructive VRP solving with a GNN-based encoder instead of the dominant Transformer architecture used by POMO, GANCO, and related solvers. The closest prior work is GFACS (Kim et al., 2024b), which pairs GFlowNet with ant colony optimization at inference; AGFN departs by removing the ACO dependency entirely, relying solely on the GFlowNet generator plus a proposed hybrid decoding scheme. The adversarial training component draws conceptual inspiration from GANCO (Xin et al., 2022) but inverts its role: rather than generating adversarial training instances, a discriminator scores generated solutions to provide fine-grained feedback into the GFlowNet's reward function.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in demonstrating that a GNN-based GFlowNet solver can match or exceed Transformer-based construction solvers at 500–10,000 nodes while running an order of magnitude faster, with the 10,000-node result (14.6 s) being the first neural constructive solver to handle that scale without heuristic post-processing. However, the gap to LKH-3 remains substantial at smaller scales (8.4% at 200 nodes), and the method's reliance on graph sparsification (k = |V|/4 edges) introduces an approximation that may affect solution quality on dense or irregular instances. Open questions include whether the adversarial discriminator is truly necessary beyond accelerating convergence, whether the approach extends to VRP variants with time windows or heterogeneous fleets, and whether more expressive GNN architectures could close the remaining gap to state-of-the-art heuristics.
