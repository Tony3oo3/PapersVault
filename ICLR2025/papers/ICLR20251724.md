## Problem

Existing LLM graph benchmarks rely on synthetic graphs (e.g., Erdős-Rényi), focus on basic structural tasks like connectivity checks, and evaluate via string matching that allows models to succeed through guesswork. This leaves unexplored whether LLMs can perform genuine multi-step algorithmic reasoning and meta-algorithmic planning on realistic, diverse graph structures, and provides no fine-grained diagnosis of failure modes such as hallucination versus suboptimality.

## Input / Output

Each problem is a text-encoded graph (edge-list format, up to ~6,000 tokens) sampled from real-world sources (DBLP, DBpedia, Social Network, OpenFlights, PubChemQC) with a task instruction and one worked example. The LLM must output the full solution path or component (e.g., the sequence of nodes forming a shortest path, the set of nodes in a maximum clique, or the node mapping for GED), not merely a scalar answer. The evaluation pipeline classifies each response as correct, suboptimal (feasible but not optimal), hallucinatory (properly formatted but infeasible), or missing, yielding accuracy, feasibility, hallucination rate, and ranking-based metrics (MRR, Top-1, Top-3).

## Method

GraphArena samples subgraphs from five real-world graph collections using random walk with restart to preserve local topology, then generates 10,000 problems across 10 tasks: four polynomial-time tasks (Common Neighbor, Shortest Distance, Connected Component, Graph Diameter) testing direct algorithmic execution, and six NP-complete tasks (Maximum Clique, Maximum Independent Set, Minimum Vertex Cover, Maximum Common Subgraph, Graph Edit Distance, TSP) testing meta-algorithmic planning. The evaluation uses a three-step path-based protocol: regex-based path extraction from the LLM response, script-based feasibility verification against graph constraints, and optimality comparison against exact-algorithm ground truth. Beyond the benchmark itself, the paper empirically evaluates four enhancement strategies—chain-of-thought prompting, instruction tuning on 10,000 graph problems, code-writing-and-execution, and parallel/sequential test-time compute scaling—each showing distinct trade-offs in reducing hallucination versus improving accuracy on large or NP-hard instances.

## Results

Claude-3.5-Sonnet achieves the highest accuracy across all settings: 82.2% on polynomial-time small graphs, 58.7% on polynomial-time large graphs, 47.8% on NP-complete small graphs, and only 7.2% on NP-complete large graphs. All ten LLMs drop below 10% accuracy on NP-complete large-graph tasks, with hallucination rates exceeding 40% for most models in that regime. Smaller models suffer dramatically more: Llama3-8b and Gemma-7b exhibit 78–84% hallucination on polynomial-time large graphs, compared to 13–37% for frontier models. The gap between 70B and 8B parameter models (e.g., Llama3-70b vs. Llama3-8b: 61.2% vs. 28.5% accuracy on P-Small) is far wider than on standard benchmarks like GSM8K (93.0% vs. 79.6%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GNNs (GIN, GAT, GraphSAGE), Claude-3.5-Sonnet substantially outperforms on polynomial-time tasks (82.2% vs. 59.9%/42.3%/41.0% on P-Small; 58.7% vs. 39.6%/22.5%/22.6% on P-Large) and NP-small (47.8% vs. 33.8%/32.3%/33.7%), but GNNs recover on NP-large (GIN 25.8% vs. 7.2%), suggesting GNNs may rely on pattern regression rather than genuine reasoning. GraphWiz (a Llama2-7b graph-reasoning model) performs poorly on GraphArena (0.2–1.2% accuracy), attributed to task and format mismatch with its training corpus. GraphToken (GIN-as-tokenizer hybrid) lands between its Gemma-7b backbone and GIN encoder, with mutual benefits only on the large-scale Neighbor task. Against classical algorithms, GPT-4o consistently beats Random solutions (e.g., 95.2% win rate on TSP-Large) and matches Greedy on small graphs, but is dominated by problem-specific Approximation algorithms on large graphs (e.g., only 10.6% win rate vs. Christofides on TSP-Large), indicating LLMs can serve as weak heuristics but not yet replace dedicated approximation methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

GraphArena extends the line of LLM graph-reasoning benchmarks initiated by NLGraph, GraphQA, and CLRS-Text, which relied on small synthetic (Erdős-Rényi) graphs and basic structural or algorithm-execution tasks evaluated via string matching. It departs from this lineage in three conceptual ways: sampling subgraphs from real-world networks (DBLP, DBpedia, OpenFlights, PubChemQC) to capture topological diversity, introducing NP-complete tasks that require meta-algorithmic planning rather than step-by-step execution, and replacing answer-string matching with a path-based evaluation protocol that distinguishes correct, suboptimal, hallucinatory, and missing responses. The paper also positions itself against Graph-LLM hybrids (GraphWiz, GraphToken) and GNN baselines, showing that current hybrid architectures have not yet closed the gap to strong LLMs on these tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even frontier LLMs (GPT-4o, Claude-3.5-Sonnet) drop below 10% accuracy on NP-complete tasks with larger graphs, and that hallucination rates scale nearly monotonically with graph size—findings that are harder to surface with string-matching evaluation. The much larger performance gap between 70B and 8B models on graph tasks (e.g., 61% vs. 29% accuracy) compared to standard benchmarks like GSM8K or GPQA suggests graph computation is a more sensitive probe of reasoning depth. Open questions remain: whether code-writing or test-time compute scaling can eventually close the gap on complex NP tasks, whether instruction tuning on graph solution paths can develop genuine algorithmic planning, and how to integrate external graph encoders with LLMs without losing the natural-language interface advantage.
