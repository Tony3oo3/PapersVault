## Problem

While existing scaling laws relate training compute to model size and loss, there is no principled, quantitative characterization of how much factual knowledge (measured in bits) a sufficiently trained language model can store as a function of its parameter count. This paper asks: what is the exact knowledge-per-parameter constant, and how do architecture, quantization, sparsity, training duration, and data quality each modulate it?

## Input / Output

The method consumes synthetic knowledge datasets where each piece is a (name, attribute, value) tuple rendered as English text (e.g., bioS with 6 attributes per person, bioR with LLaMA2-generated 40-paragraph biographies, and bioD with controlled diversity parameters). Evaluation does not use average perplexity but instead computes cross-entropy losses exclusively on knowledge-bearing tokens (names and values), feeding them into Theorem 3.1's bit-complexity lower bound to yield a capacity ratio in bits-per-parameter. The output is a single scalar R(F) per model-dataset pair, with R^max(F) providing the theoretical ceiling for that dataset.

## Method

The paper defines knowledge as (name, attribute, value) tuples and derives an information-theoretic bit-complexity lower bound (Theorem 3.1) stating that any model storing N such tuples at a given accuracy must have at least N·log(N₀/e^{loss_name}) + NK·log(D^C/e^{loss_value}) + KD·log(T^L/(De^{loss_value1})) bits of parameters. Empirically, GPT2, LLaMA, and Mistral models of varying sizes are trained from scratch via standard autoregressive pretraining (AdamW, fp16) on controlled synthetic datasets with 100 or 1000 exposures per knowledge piece, and their capacity ratios are computed from the three loss terms. The key finding is a universal 2 bits/parameter ratio for sufficiently trained transformers, which persists under int8 quantization but drops to 0.7 under int4, is reduced 1.3× by MoE sparsity (32 experts, 8.8% active), and is degraded 20× by a 1:7 useful-to-junk data ratio (mitigable to 2× via a special prepended token). Architecture ablations isolate gated MLPs as the cause of LLaMA/Mistral's 1.3× underperformance relative to GPT2 in the undertrained (100-exposure) regime.

## Results

The central finding is a universal 2 bits/parameter knowledge capacity ratio for sufficiently trained transformers (1000 exposures per knowledge piece), holding across GPT2, LLaMA, and Mistral architectures, various model sizes, and int8 quantization. With only 100 exposures, capacity drops to 1 bit/parameter for GPT2, and int4 quantization further reduces capacity to 0.7 bits/parameter. MoE models with 32 experts (using only 8.8% of total parameters at inference) suffer only a 1.3× capacity reduction, while junk data at a 1:7 useful-to-junk token ratio degrades capacity by 20× (mitigable to 2× with a special prepended token). A sufficiently trained 7B model is predicted to store ~14B bits of knowledge, exceeding the estimated knowledge content of English Wikipedia and textbooks combined.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three transformer architectures—GPT2 (Radford et al., 2019), LLaMA (Touvron et al., 2023), and Mistral (Jiang et al., 2023)—on identical synthetic knowledge datasets. At 1000 exposures, all three architectures achieve the same 2 bits/parameter capacity ratio, indicating a universal scaling law independent of architecture. However, at 100 exposures, LLaMA and Mistral underperform GPT2 by a factor of 1.3 (≈0.77 vs. 1.0 bits/param), and controlled experiments attribute this gap specifically to the gated MLP design used in LLaMA/Mistral. GPT2 thus emerges as the more robust architecture under data-limited (low-exposure) training, while all architectures converge to the same ceiling given sufficient training. The paper also contrasts dense vs. MoE models, showing that MoE's 1.3× capacity penalty is far smaller than its 11.4× parameter reduction at inference, suggesting the performance gap between MoE and dense models is not primarily a knowledge-storage issue.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is the third installment in the 'Physics of Language Models' series, building directly on Part 3.1 (Allen-Zhu & Li, ICML 2024), which introduced the bioS synthetic biography dataset and the (name, attribute, value) knowledge-tuple framework. It departs from the dominant scaling-law literature (Kaplan et al. 2020; Hoffmann et al. 2022) that characterizes model capability via cross-entropy loss or benchmark scores, instead formulating a new information-theoretic objective: the minimum bits required to store factual knowledge, lower-bounded by Theorem 3.1. The overparameterization theory of Allen-Zhu et al. (2019a,b) provides the conceptual backdrop that larger models can memorize more, but this paper supplies the precise constant (2 bits/parameter) that prior asymptotic analyses left undetermined.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The universal 2-bit/parameter constant, if it generalizes beyond synthetic data, gives practitioners a simple rule-of-thumb for estimating how much factual knowledge a model of a given size can hold, and for deciding whether a smaller model trained longer can match a larger one's knowledge base. The controlled synthetic setting isolates variables (architecture, quantization, sparsity, data noise) that are entangled in real-world pretraining, making it a cleaner comparison tool than loss-based benchmarks. Key open questions include whether the constant holds on natural-language data with overlapping and redundant facts, how it behaves at the 100B+ parameter scale, and whether the 2-bit ceiling implies a fundamental limit on knowledge density that constrains the path to AGI.
