## Problem

Existing dynamic 3D scene reconstruction methods depend on per-scene optimization (hours to days), dense spatio-temporal observations, and explicit motion supervision such as optical flow or dynamic masks, which limits generalization, introduces noise from pseudo-labels, and prevents leveraging data-driven scaling laws. No prior method achieves fast, feed-forward, self-supervised reconstruction of large-scale outdoor dynamic scenes from sparse multi-timestep inputs.

## Input / Output

STORM takes 4 context frames (spaced 0.5 s apart) from 3 frontal cameras at 160×240 resolution along with camera intrinsics and extrinsics, which are encoded as Plücker ray maps and frequency-encoded time embeddings. It outputs pixel-aligned 3D Gaussians (center, quaternion rotation, scale, opacity, color) each carrying a 6-D velocity vector (backward and forward), which collectively support novel view rendering at arbitrary timesteps, 3D scene flow extraction, and unsupervised motion-group segmentation via argmax over per-pixel motion-token assignment weights.

## Method

STORM uses a 12-layer ViT-B (patch size 8, ~100M params) to predict per-frame 3D Gaussian splats and their velocities in a single forward pass. The key mechanism is bottom-up amodal aggregation: Gaussians from all context frames are translated to a target timestep using predicted constant velocities and unioned into a unified scene representation, so reconstruction errors from mis-estimated dynamics directly penalize the motion prediction. M=16 learnable motion tokens are prepended to the input sequence and decoded into shared velocity bases and motion queries; SAM-style query-key similarity over a 32-D embedding space yields per-pixel weights that combine the bases, regularizing motion to low-dimensional shared primitives and enabling unsupervised motion-group segmentation. Training uses only reconstruction losses (RGB L2, normalized depth L1, LPIPS) plus a sky opacity loss and a velocity L2 regularization term—no motion, mask, or correspondence supervision is required. Practical extensions include a sky token with a modulated MLP for unbounded sky rendering, per-camera affine tokens for exposure correction, and an optional Latent-STORM variant that replaces pixel-aligned color Gaussians with patch-aligned latent Gaussians decoded by a convolutional upsampler for fine-grained human motion and large-baseline extrapolation.

## Results

On the Waymo Open Dataset, STORM achieves 22.10 PSNR and 0.624 SSIM in dynamic regions, surpassing the best per-scene optimization method (DeformableGS, 17.10 PSNR) by 5.0 dB and the best feed-forward method (GS-LRM, 20.02 PSNR) by 2.1 dB. For full-image reconstruction, STORM reaches 26.38 PSNR and 5.48 D-RMSE, with an inference time of 0.18 s per 2-second clip and real-time rendering capability. In scene flow estimation, STORM achieves 0.276 m EPE3D and 81.12% Acc₅ using only camera images, outperforming LiDAR-based NSFP++ (0.711 m, 53.10%) by 0.422 m and 28.02 percentage points respectively, while being ~6,680× faster (0.025 s vs 167 s per frame).

## Baselines & Comparisons

STORM decisively outperforms all four per-scene optimization baselines (EmerNeRF, 3DGS, PVG, DeformableGS) in dynamic-region PSNR by 4.3–6.6 dB, while reducing inference time from 14–29 minutes to 0.18 s. Against the two feed-forward baselines, STORM gains +2.1 dB PSNR over GS-LRM and +2.5 dB over LGM* in dynamic regions, with substantially better depth accuracy (5.48 vs 7.94 D-RMSE). In scene flow estimation (Table 3), STORM surpasses both NSFP and NSFP++ on every metric—EPE3D (0.276 m vs 0.698/0.711 m), Acc₅ (81.12% vs 42.17/53.10%), and inference speed (0.025 s vs 27/167 s per frame)—despite using only camera images while the baselines require LiDAR at test time. On the additional NuScenes and Argoverse2 datasets, STORM achieves the best full-image PSNR (24.90 and 24.80) and D-RMSE (5.43 and 13.51) over both LGM and GS-LRM. No baseline matches STORM on any reported metric; the closest competitor is GS-LRM on full-image PSNR (25.18 vs 26.38), but STORM still leads by 1.2 dB and by a wide margin in dynamic regions.

## Relation to Prior Work

STORM builds on the 3D Gaussian Splatting representation (Kerbl et al., 2023) and the feed-forward Transformer architecture popularized by GS-LRM (Zhang et al., 2024) for static scene reconstruction, extending both to the dynamic setting. It borrows the query-key similarity mechanism from SAM (Kirillov et al., 2023) to implement motion tokens that decompose per-Gaussian velocities into shared motion bases, and uses DiT-style adaptive LayerNorm (Peebles & Xie, 2023) for sky modeling. The key departure from prior work is the amodal aggregation framework: rather than optimizing a canonical deformation field or chaining per-point flows (as in NeRF-based and 3DGS-based per-scene methods), STORM predicts per-frame Gaussians and their velocities in a single forward pass, then transforms and unions them across timesteps, with reconstruction loss alone providing the self-supervision signal for motion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating per-scene optimization and explicit motion supervision, STORM reduces dynamic scene reconstruction from hours to 200ms while simultaneously improving quality in dynamic regions, making large-scale 4D scene understanding feasible for autonomous driving simulation, robotics, and mixed reality. The emergent motion group segmentation from reconstruction losses alone opens a path to controllable scene editing without expensive 3D bounding box annotations. Key limitations include the requirement for calibrated camera parameters, the ~7k token sequence length constraint limiting resolution and temporal window, and the 8× downsampling; open questions include whether scaling laws apply, whether joint camera estimation is feasible, and whether the approach generalizes beyond driving scenes to indoor or unstructured environments.
