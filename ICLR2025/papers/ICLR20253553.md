## Problem

Denoising diffusion MRI (dMRI) is uniquely challenging because no clean ground-truth data exists (unlike CT, where normal-dose scans serve as references), and the noise distribution is complex and dataset-dependent. Existing self-supervised methods either require many additional diffusion volumes (Patch2Self needs ≥10), rely on multi-stage pipelines with separate noise model training (DDM2 uses three stages), or suffer from drift and over-denoising that degrades downstream clinical tasks like tractography and microstructure fitting.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a 4D dMRI volume X ∈ R^{w×h×d×l} and processes it slice-by-slice: for a target slice x at (slice index i, volume index j), it uses the adjacent volume slice x' at (i, j−1) as the sole independent noisy measurement, requiring only one extra volume rather than the ten-plus needed by Patch2Self. A user-defined CSNR threshold controls adaptive termination during sampling, allowing per-slice control over the denoising strength. The output is a denoised 2D slice per iteration, and processing all slices and volumes yields the full denoised 4D volume.

## Method

Di-Fusion is a single-stage, fully self-supervised denoising framework built on a U-Net data predictor trained with the Noise2Self J-invariance loss, requiring no clean data or separate noise model. Three key modifications to standard DDPM enable stable denoising: (1) a Fusion process replaces the standard forward trajectory with a dynamic linear interpolation x*_t = λ₁ᵗ·x + λ₂ᵗ·x' that progressively injects the target slice x into the diffusion path, preventing the drift that accumulates when the reverse process diverges from the forward process; (2) a 'Di-' process substitutes the standard Gaussian noise with a spatially shuffled, zero-mean version of (x − x'), preserving the variance statistics of real-world noise without explicit noise modeling; (3) training is restricted to only the last T_c diffusion steps (e.g., T_c=300 out of T=1000), converting the generative task into a conditional one that reduces hallucination and increases per-step training stability. At inference, a Run-Walk accelerated sampling strategy uses non-uniform step sizes (accelerating in early slow steps, decelerating in later fast steps), and an adaptive termination criterion monitors the brain-tissue-corrected distance d_x = ‖x − x_out‖²·b_x against the CSNR threshold to stop sampling as soon as sufficient denoising is achieved.

## Results

On in-vivo dMRI data, Di-Fusion achieves the best R² for microstructure model fitting across all four settings: CSD-CC (0.967), CSD-CSO (0.939), DTI-CC (0.976), and DTI-CSO (0.876), outperforming the next-best method Nr2N (0.959, 0.908, 0.961, 0.872). On simulated fastMRI data, Di-Fusion achieves the highest PSNR at high noise intensities (e.g., 19.91 dB vs. 17.92 dB for DDM2 and 16.78 dB for Nr2N at Simulation 1) with the smallest standard deviation, indicating stable performance. On tractography, Di-Fusion generates the minimal number of streamlines while maintaining high Fiber Bundle Coherency (FBC), whereas DDM2 misses high-FBC regions due to over-denoising.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Di-Fusion outperforms all nine baselines on R² for microstructure model fitting across all four in-vivo settings. The closest competitor is Nr2N, which Di-Fusion surpasses by 0.8–3.1 R² points (e.g., 0.939 vs. 0.908 on CSD-CSO). DDM2, the prior state-of-the-art self-supervised diffusion method, is substantially outperformed (e.g., 0.939 vs. 0.810 on CSD-CSO) and exhibits over-denoising that shifts the data distribution and causes it to miss high-FBC streamlines in tractography. Patch2Self (P2S) shows the weakest performance among deep-learning baselines on DTI-CSO (0.675 vs. 0.876 for Di-Fusion), partly because it requires ≥10 volumes for optimal results while Di-Fusion needs only one additional volume. On simulated data, Di-Fusion achieves the best PSNR at high noise levels (Simulation 1: 19.91 dB) with notably smaller variance than Nr2N and DDM2, while P2S (modified for 2-volume input) lags behind at 11.20 dB. DIP performs poorly on crossing-fiber structures (CSD-CSO: 0.477, DTI-CSO: 0.381), confirming its limitation for dMRI denoising.

## Relation to Prior Work

Di-Fusion sits at the intersection of self-supervised denoising (Noise2Self's J-invariance, Noise2Noise) and diffusion probabilistic models (DDPM, DDIM), specifically targeting the dMRI setting where clean ground truth is unavailable. Its most direct predecessor is DDM2 (Xiang et al., 2023), a three-stage framework that integrates statistical denoising into diffusion models; Di-Fusion collapses this into a single-stage pipeline, introduces a Fusion process to correct trajectory drift between the forward and reverse processes, and replaces the Gaussian noise assumption with a data-driven 'Di-' process derived from the difference of two noisy volumes. The Run-Walk accelerated sampling extends DDIM's deterministic schedule with a non-uniform step strategy that accelerates early (slow) steps while preserving detail in later (fast) steps, and the decision to train only the latter diffusion steps repurposes the generative capacity of DDPM as a regularizer for a conditional denoising task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Self-supervised dMRI denoising is clinically critical because acquisition time and resolution trade-offs directly limit patient throughput and diagnostic quality, yet no clean reference data exists for supervised training. Di-Fusion's single-stage design and adaptive termination make the method more practical than DDM2's multi-stage pipeline, and the controllable CSNR threshold offers clinicians a dial between denoising strength and structural preservation. Key limitations remain: the additive Gaussian noise assumption excludes physiological artifacts (cardiac pulsation, motion), inference time is still substantial for a diffusion model, and the method has only been validated on three in-vivo datasets and one simulated benchmark. Open questions include whether the Fusion process and 'Di-' noise characterization generalize to non-additive or spatially-correlated noise, and whether the latter-steps-only training strategy transfers to other inverse problems in medical imaging.
