## Problem

Prior moral evaluation benchmarks (ETHICS, Delphi, Value Kaleidoscope) focus on clear-cut scenarios or narrow domains, leaving it unclear how LLMs resolve value conflicts in the ambiguous, multi-stakeholder dilemmas people actually face daily. This paper asks: what value preferences do LLMs reveal when forced to choose between competing values in realistic everyday dilemmas, and do those preferences align with the providers' published alignment principles?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20251941/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each dilemma is a three-sentence vignette (background, conflict point, binary question) paired with two 80-word negative-consequence stories and extracted party-value-reason triples for both actions. The model receives the dilemma text and must output a binary action choice; the chosen action's associated values are scored as 'selected' while the alternative's are 'neglected.' In the steerability experiment, a system prompt instructing the model to prioritize a specific value group is prepended. Outputs are aggregated into normalized value-preference differences per theoretical dimension and weighted score differences per alignment principle.

## Method

The dataset is generated via a three-step GPT-4 pipeline: (1) formulate a non-clear-cut dilemma from Social Chemistry seed actions with <50% community agreement, (2) generate 80-word negative-consequence stories for each action (grounded in loss aversion to surface affected parties), and (3) use multi-step chain-of-thought to extract all direct/indirect parties and their associated fundamental human values with justifying reasons. The 301 resulting values are mapped onto five theoretical frameworks (World Values Survey, Moral Foundations Theory, Maslow's Hierarchy, Aristotle's Virtues, Plutchik's Wheel of Emotions), and model preferences are computed as normalized (selected − neglected) value counts per dimension across 1,360 stratified dilemmas. For principles analysis, GPT-4-turbo classifies each OpenAI ModelSpec or Anthropic Constitutional AI principle into supporting and opposing value groups with empirical weights, then the model's dilemma responses yield a weighted score difference E[n_sup] − E[n_opp] per principle. Finally, a system-prompt modulation experiment tests whether end-users can override GPT-4-turbo's value preferences by instructing it to favor either the supporting or opposing value group.

## Results

Across six LLMs evaluated on 1,360 everyday moral dilemmas, all models uniformly favor Self-expression over Survival (World Values Survey) and Care over Loyalty (Moral Foundations Theory). The largest inter-model divergence appears on Truthfulness (Aristotle's Virtues): Mixtral-8x7B neglects it by 9.7% while GPT-4-turbo selects it by 9.4%, and on Fairness: Claude-3-haiku neglects it by 1.4% while Llama-3-70B selects it by 7.5%. On principle adherence, Claude-3-haiku aligns with Anthropic CAI-45 (existential risk; weighted score +17.9) but misaligns with CAI-56 (flexibility; score −2.7), while GPT-4-turbo aligns with ModelSpec-13 (don't change minds; score +0.9) but misaligns with ModelSpec-5 (protect privacy; score −1.5, favoring transparency over privacy). System-prompt steering of GPT-4-turbo's value preferences proves ineffective regardless of the steering direction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it compares six LLMs (GPT-4-turbo, GPT-3.5-turbo, Llama-2-70B, Llama-3-70B, Mixtral-8x7B, Claude-3-haiku) on value-preference dimensions. GPT-4-turbo and Llama-3-70B cluster together, showing stronger preferences for Self-esteem, Love and belonging, and Truthfulness, while Claude-3-haiku and Mixtral-8x7B cluster on the opposite end, neglecting Truthfulness (−7.9% average) and Fairness (−1.89% average). Mixtral-8x7B is a notable outlier, showing neutral preference on Purity and a unique tendency to neglect Loyalty, partly due to its refusal to answer 25% of dilemmas. On principle adherence, each model shows a mixed record: Claude-3-haiku aligns with the safety principle (CAI-45, +17.9) but contradicts the flexibility principle (CAI-56, −2.7), and GPT-4-turbo aligns with the non-manipulation principle (ModelSpec-13, +0.9) but contradicts the privacy principle (ModelSpec-5, −1.5). The steerability experiment further shows that neither supportive nor opposing system prompts can reliably shift GPT-4-turbo's value preferences, indicating that closed-source API models resist end-user value steering.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of LLM moral evaluation work (ETHICS, Delphi, Value Kaleidoscope, MoralExceptionQA) by shifting from clear-cut or narrowly-scoped scenarios to complex, multi-stakeholder everyday dilemmas with no definitive right answer. It repurposes five established social-science frameworks (World Values Survey, Moral Foundations Theory, Maslow's Hierarchy, Aristotle's Virtues, Plutchik's Wheel) as a unified analytical lens for interpreting model choices, rather than treating them as separate evaluation axes. The synthetic generation pipeline (seeded from Social Chemistry, expanded via GPT-4 chain-of-thought) departs from prior human-annotated datasets to mitigate privacy risks while validating against r/AITA posts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset and multi-framework analysis provide a reusable resource for probing value conflicts in LLMs, and the finding that models' actual value preferences diverge from their providers' stated principles (e.g., GPT-4-turbo favoring transparency over privacy despite ModelSpec's privacy principle) raises concrete alignment concerns for practitioners. The negative steerability result—that system prompts fail to shift GPT-4-turbo's value priorities—highlights a fundamental limitation for end-users of closed-source APIs. Open questions include whether these findings generalize beyond the six tested models, whether the Western cultural bias in GPT-4-generated dilemmas distorts the value landscape, and whether more targeted fine-tuning (rather than prompting) could resolve the principle-behavior gaps.
