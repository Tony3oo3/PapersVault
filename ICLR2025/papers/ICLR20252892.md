## Problem

Off-the-shelf vision-language models default to literal, object-level interpretations of images and fail to reason about visual abstractions—concepts defined by lifted rules over symbols rather than concrete visual features (e.g., recognizing that candy canes form maze walls or that hula hoops constitute a tic-tac-toe grid). The paper asks: what structural knowledge allows humans to flexibly interpret such abstractions across infinitely many real-world instantiations, and can we give VLMs access to that knowledge?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252892/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a real-world RGB image and a natural language question that explicitly names the underlying abstract concept (e.g., 'Imagine that the image represents a maze. Are there clear entrances or exits?'). It produces a question answer (multiple-choice or free-form) along with a resolved schema: a mapping from each abstract subcomponent to its concrete visual realization in the image (e.g., {layout: rectangular, walls: coffee beans, entry-exit: coffee cups}). The benchmark comprises 180 diverse internet-sourced images across 12 concepts in 4 categories, each paired with 3 questions of varying type (binary, counting, open-ended), totaling 540 examples.

## Method

Deep Schema Grounding (DSG) is a three-stage, inference-only framework that bridges LLM commonsense knowledge and VLM visual reasoning via explicit structured schemas. First, an LLM extracts a schema for the target concept as a directed acyclic graph program, decomposing it into 3–4 subcomponent symbols with dependency edges (e.g., maze → layout, walls, entry-exit, where entry-exit depends on layout). Second, a VLM hierarchically grounds the schema onto the image: it first resolves leaf nodes (concrete symbols with no dependencies), then conditions the grounding of higher-level abstract symbols on those resolved components. Third, the fully resolved schema—including the component-to-visual-entity mapping and the complete grounding conversation history—is injected as augmented textual context into a final VLM query for question answering. The key insight is that the composition patterns of lower-level concepts remain invariant across visual instantiations, so explicitly decomposing and grounding them provides the holistic structural context that raw image+concept prompts lack. The framework is model-agnostic and requires no training; ablations confirm that hierarchical (vs. sequential) grounding and inclusion of the full grounding trace each contribute to performance gains.

## Results

On the Visual Abstractions Benchmark (540 VQA examples across 12 abstract concepts), DSG with GPT-4o as the base VLM achieves 73.0% overall accuracy, outperforming GPT-4o alone (66.4%) by 6.6 percentage points (9.9% relative improvement). The largest gain is on counting questions, where DSG reaches 70.4% versus GPT-4o's 60.4% (10 pp / 16.6% relative improvement). DSG also improves open-ended questions from 69.3% to 77.6%, while binary-choice questions see a smaller gain (65.5% → 69.0%). Human annotators achieve 84.6% overall, indicating remaining headroom.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DSG (GPT-4o base) outperforms all five baselines across every question type. Against the strongest baseline, GPT-4o (66.4% overall), DSG gains 6.6 pp overall and 10 pp on counting questions. The gap widens dramatically against program-aided baselines: ViperGPT (26.0%) and VisProg (30.4%) trail DSG by 47 and 42.6 pp respectively, suggesting that Python-execution-based visual reasoning is poorly suited to abstract-concept VQA. Open-source VLMs LLaVA (41.5%) and InstructBLIP (43.7%) are also substantially outperformed (by 31.5 and 29.3 pp). Notably, DSG is model-agnostic: when applied to LLaVA it still yields a 7.0% relative improvement (41.5% → 44.4%), and DSG with the weaker GPT-4v base (70.7%) surpasses GPT-4o alone (66.4%) by 4.3 pp, demonstrating that the schema-grounding framework can compensate for weaker visual backbones.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws on cognitive science's theory-theory of concepts (Schank & Abelson's scripts, Carey's conceptual change) to motivate schema-based representations, but departs from prior symbolic approaches by grounding these schemas in natural images via VLMs rather than restricting to object-level concrete concepts. It repurposes program-aided reasoning ideas from PAL and Parsel (hierarchical program representations for text-only reasoning) and chain-of-thought prompting in VLMs, but redirects them toward scene-level abstract concept interpretation rather than object/relational reasoning. Unlike prior VQA and commonsense reasoning benchmarks that target concrete scene content or 'what-if' predictions, DSG introduces a three-stage pipeline (LLM schema extraction → hierarchical VLM grounding → schema-augmented QA) specifically designed for concepts defined by lifted rules with infinite visual instantiations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Visual Abstractions Benchmark (540 examples, 12 concepts, 4 categories) fills a gap in evaluating whether VLMs can interpret images through the lens of abstract concepts rather than defaulting to literal object recognition. The finding that DSG with GPT-4v outperforms GPT-4o alone by 4.3 points suggests that structured decomposition can compensate for weaker base models, which is practically relevant for resource-constrained settings. However, the framework is limited by VLMs' spatial reasoning weaknesses (e.g., identifying unobstructed paths in mazes), the benchmark is relatively small, and the schema complexity ablation reveals a tradeoff where more detailed schemas actually hurt performance with current VLMs—suggesting the approach's ceiling is tied to underlying model capabilities that have not yet matured.
