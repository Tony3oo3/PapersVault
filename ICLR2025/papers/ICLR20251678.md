## Problem

The Bradley-Terry model is the standard choice for converting pairwise preference annotations into reward scores in LLM alignment, yet its theoretical justification in the sparse-comparison regime typical of reward modeling (where each pair is compared only once) remains unexplored. It is also unclear whether the BT model's specific functional form and anti-symmetric architecture are necessary, or whether simpler objectives suffice for the downstream goal of ranking responses at inference time.

## Input / Output

The method consumes fixed-dimensional embeddings of prompt-response pairs (generated by Gemma2b, scaled to [0,1]^d) along with binary preference labels indicating which of two responses is preferred. The BT variant requires a Siamese MLP that maps each embedding to a scalar and computes a softmax over the two scores, while the classification variant treats each pair independently, producing a single logit per pair that serves as the reward. The resulting scalar scores are used for Best-of-N (N=500) sampling at inference, where the highest-scoring response is selected.

## Method

The paper establishes the first asymptotic convergence rate for MLP-based BT regression in reward modeling, showing that under Hölder smoothness and small-value-boundedness assumptions on the true reward, the truncated KL risk of the learned preference probability decays at rate n^{-(1+α)β/((1+α)β+d)} up to logarithmic factors. It then argues that the essential requirement for a reward model is order consistency—preserving the ranking of the true reward up to any monotonic transformation—rather than accurate probability estimation. This motivates a classification-based alternative that drops the BT anti-symmetry constraint: a single classifier (MLP or LightGBM) is trained independently on each prompt-response pair to predict whether it is preferred, and its logit serves as the reward score. A union-bound argument shows that high per-pair classification accuracy implies order consistency, and a variance decomposition reveals the classification target has lower variance than the BT pairwise target, making it easier to learn from noisy annotations. Across 12,000+ experimental runs (6 base LLMs, 2 datasets, 6 noise levels, 4 annotation quantities, 5 seeds), the classification-based models outperform BT under moderate-to-high annotation noise and cross-prompt comparisons consistently improve reward model quality, especially when same-prompt responses lack diversity.

## Results

Across 12,000+ experimental runs (6 base LLMs × 2 datasets × 3 reward model implementations × 6 noise levels × 4 annotation quantities × 5 seeds), the classification-based reward models (CLF-MLP, CLF-LGB) consistently outperform the Bradley-Terry model (BT-MLP) in golden reward value improvements via Best-of-N (N=500) sampling. CLF-LGB (LightGBM classifier) is the strongest method overall, while BT-MLP only edges ahead when annotation error rates are below ~10% (β ≥ 5). Cross-prompt preference annotations further improve reward model performance over same-prompt annotations, with the gap being largest when same-prompt responses lack diversity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the Bradley-Terry model implemented with an MLP (BT-MLP), which is the standard reward modeling approach in LLM alignment (Ouyang et al., 2022; Touvron et al., 2023). The proposed classification-based reward models (CLF-MLP and CLF-LGB) outperform BT-MLP across most settings on both Anthropic-Harmless and Anthropic-Helpful datasets, measured by golden reward value improvement via BoN sampling. Specifically, CLF-LGB achieves the highest improvements across all 6 base models (Gemma2b, Gemma7b, LLaMA3-8b, and their SFTed variants), while CLF-MLP is competitive but slightly below CLF-LGB. BT-MLP only surpasses classification models under high-quality annotations (annotation error < 10%, corresponding to β = 5 or 10), where the BT model's richer pairwise probability modeling provides an advantage. Under noisy annotations (error rates of 14–38%, β = 0.5–3), classification models show substantially greater robustness with smaller performance degradation. The paper also shows that cross-prompt annotations outperform same-prompt annotations for all three methods, with the advantage being most pronounced when same-prompt response pairs are similar in quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the nonparametric logistic regression theory of Schmidt-Hieber (2020) and Bos & Schmidt-Hieber (2022) to provide the first asymptotic convergence rate for neural-network-based Bradley-Terry reward models in LLM alignment, extending the BT-as-logistic-regression formulation of Bockenholt (1988) to the sparse, prediction-oriented setting of reward modeling. Conceptually, it departs from the standard RLHF pipeline (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022) by reframing the reward modeling objective from estimating BT preference probabilities to the weaker requirement of order consistency, which subsumes both BT and classification-based approaches. The cross-prompt annotation strategy connects to RPO (Yin et al., 2024) and KTO (Ethayarajh et al., 2024) but is motivated by the theoretical observation that neither BT nor classification requires same-prompt pairing, and is evaluated in the explicit reward-modeling setting rather than direct alignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical and empirical argument that the widely-defaulted-to BT objective is not necessary for reward modeling—only order consistency is required for downstream optimization—which opens the door to simpler, more flexible classifiers (e.g., LightGBM) that empirically outperform BT-MLP, especially under noisy annotations. The cross-prompt annotation finding is a practical, low-cost improvement that practitioners can adopt immediately. Limitations include evaluation on only two Anthropic datasets with relatively small base models (2B–8B), reliance on golden reward models as annotators rather than true human labels, and the separation of reward modeling from representation learning, which may understate gains in end-to-end systems. Open questions include whether order-consistency objectives combine well with generative reward models or learned embeddings, and whether the classification-over-BT advantage persists at larger scales or with PPO-based optimization.
