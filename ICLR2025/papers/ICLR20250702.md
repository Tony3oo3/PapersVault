## Problem

The prevailing practice for training LLM reasoners is to generate synthetic reasoning data from strong, expensive models, but it remains unclear whether this is compute-optimal under a fixed inference budget. This paper asks whether sampling from a weaker but cheaper (WC) model—generating proportionally more samples to match the FLOPs cost—can produce training data that is at least as useful, or even superior, to data from a stronger but more expensive (SE) model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20250702/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of reasoning problems with ground-truth final answers (e.g., MATH, GSM-8K), a WC model, an SE model, and a fixed sampling compute budget. It produces a filtered synthetic dataset of (question, reasoning chain, final answer) tuples where the final answer matches the gold label, which is then used for supervised fine-tuning. The compute-matched constraint dictates that the WC model generates P_SE/P_WC times as many candidate solutions per problem as the SE model (e.g., 30 vs. 10 for Gemma2-9B vs. Gemma2-27B), and for API-based models the ratio is derived from per-token pricing rather than parameter count.

## Method

The paper introduces a compute-matched sampling framework where, at a fixed FLOPs budget, the WC model generates proportionally more samples per problem than the SE model (S_WC = (P_SE/P_WC)·S_SE), and then evaluates the resulting synthetic data across three quality axes: coverage (fraction of problems solved), diversity (unique correct solutions per problem), and false positive rate (correct answer but wrong reasoning). The core empirical contribution is a systematic comparison across three fine-tuning paradigms—knowledge distillation (a separate student LM), self-improvement (WC model trained on its own data), and a newly introduced weak-to-strong improvement (W2S-I, where the SE model is fine-tuned on WC-generated data)—using Gemma2-9B/27B, Gemini-1.5-Flash/Pro, and Llama-3.2-3B/3.1-8B model pairs. Across all paradigms and model families, WC-generated data consistently yields higher final-answer accuracy than SE-generated data, with relative gains up to 31.6%, despite the WC data having a higher FPR. Ablations confirm that both the higher coverage and higher diversity of WC data contribute to the performance advantage, and that the result holds even when the WC data costs only ~15% of the SE data's sampling cost.

## Results

At a fixed compute budget, finetuning on synthetic data from a weaker but cheaper (WC) model consistently outperforms finetuning on data from a stronger but more expensive (SE) model across all three paradigms (knowledge distillation, self-improvement, weak-to-strong improvement). On MATH with Gemma2 models (compute-matched), WC data yields relative gains of 5.8–6% (Student-LM), 2–3.8% (WC-LM), and 4.3–5.8% (SE-LM) over SE data. Scaling to Gemini-1.5 (price-matched) on MATH, the gains are much larger: 31.6% (Gemma-7B), 14.4% (Gemma2-9B), and 10.9% (Gemma2-27B) relative improvement for Flash (WC) over Pro (SE) data, and even at 0.15× the cost the WC data still wins by 5.7–19.1%. The WC data achieves 11% higher coverage and 86% higher diversity than SE data on MATH, at the cost of a 7% higher false positive rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between synthetic data generated by a weaker-but-cheaper (WC) model versus a stronger-but-more-expensive (SE) model under a fixed compute (or price) budget, across three finetuning paradigms. WC data wins in every compute-matched comparison: on MATH, Gemma2-9B data outperforms Gemma2-27B data by 5.8–6% (distillation), 2–3.8% (self-improvement), and 4.3–5.8% (weak-to-strong) in relative pass@1 accuracy; on GSM-8K the gains are smaller (1.2–4.2%) partly due to task saturation. At the Gemini-1.5 scale (price-matched), Flash data beats Pro data by up to 31.6% relative on MATH, and even at 0.15× the monetary cost of Pro data, Flash still wins by 5.7–19.1%. The paper also shows that in a number-matched setup (equal number of samples from both models), SE data does outperform WC data, explaining why prior work favored SE models. Human-written chain-of-thought solutions serve as a ground-truth baseline; both WC and SE synthetic data generally approach or exceed this baseline, with WC data closing the gap more effectively. On Llama models (3B vs 8B), the same pattern holds: WC data yields +1.6 (Student), +1.6 (WC-LM), and +1.8 (SE-LM) absolute pass@1 gains over SE data on MATH500.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the STaR framework (Zelikman et al., 2022) for sample-filter-finetune synthetic data generation, and on the broader literature of knowledge distillation and self-improvement for LLM reasoning. It departs from prior work in two key ways: (1) it introduces compute-matched sampling as the correct comparison framework, arguing that prior number-matched comparisons (equal samples from both models) unfairly disadvantage weaker models, and (2) it formalizes a 'weak-to-strong improvement' (W2S-I) paradigm where a weaker model's synthetic data improves a stronger model, extending the weak-to-strong generalization literature (Burns et al., 2023; Bowman et al., 2022) from capability recovery to capability enhancement. The repeated-sampling-for-inference literature (Brown et al., 2024; Snell et al., 2024; Hassid et al., 2024) provides the conceptual motivation but focuses on inference-time compute rather than training-data generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This result challenges the default practice in the field of sampling synthetic training data from the strongest available model, suggesting that under realistic compute constraints, weaker models can produce more useful training data due to higher coverage and diversity. The practical impact is significant: labs can reallocate compute from expensive inference to broader problem coverage, and the W2S-I paradigm offers a new route to improve frontier models without requiring even stronger generators. Key limitations include the dependence on ground-truth labels for filtering (results are mixed without them for Gemma models), the focus on math reasoning as the primary domain, and the open question of whether the advantage persists as the small-large performance gap continues to narrow. The paper also leaves open how these findings extend to iterative self-training, pretraining, and verifier training.
