## Problem

Tokenization is a critical step in the NLP pipeline, yet its theoretical underpinnings remain largely unexplored: the impact of tokenization on language model estimation has been studied primarily through empirical means, and there is no formal characterization of when a tokenizer preserves the statistical consistency of estimators. This paper addresses that gap by asking: under what necessary and sufficient conditions does a tokenizer model guarantee that a consistent estimator at the token level yields a consistent estimator at the character level?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252594/cropped_page5_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework operates on two string sets: character strings over an alphabet Σ and token sequences over a vocabulary Δ. A tokenizer is a pair of stochastic maps (encoder τ: Σ* → Δ*, decoder κ: Δ* → Σ*), and the central object of study is their composition κτ. The input is a reference probability distribution p* over Σ*, and the output is a tokenized language model p = κq over Σ* derived from a token-level distribution q over Δ*. The key constraint is that κτp* = p* must hold for the estimation to be consistent.

## Method

The paper defines a tokenizer as an arbitrary pair of composable stochastic maps and proves the Fundamental Principle of Tokenization (Theorem 3.1): a consistent estimator {q_n} of q* = τp* yields a consistent estimator {κq_n} of p* if and only if κτp* = p*. They introduce the notion of an exact tokenizer (κτ = id_{Σ*}) and show it is equivalent to consistency with respect to every distribution, with the consequence that the encoder must be injective and the decoder deterministic on the encoder's image. Known practical issues are then characterized through formal properties: inconsistency arises from non-injective encoders (e.g., normalization, unk tokens), ambiguity from non-injective decoders (spurious, stochastic, or linguistic), finiteness is guaranteed by multiplicativity of the decoder with a trivial kernel (bounding preimage size), and sequentiality follows from bounded variation, which multiplicativity implies, enabling realization by finite-state transducers.

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical experiments or quantitative benchmarks. The central result is Theorem 3.1 (Fundamental Principle of Tokenization), which establishes that a consistent estimator {q_n} of the token-level reference distribution q* = τp* yields a consistent estimator {κq_n} of the character-level distribution p* if and only if κτp* = p*. The paper further proves that exact tokenizers (κτ = id) are equivalent to consistency with respect to every distribution (Proposition 3.1), that exact tokenizers force the decoder to be deterministic on the encoder's support (Proposition 3.2), and that multiplicative decoders with trivial kernels guarantee finite preimage sets (Proposition 5.1, Corollary 5.0.1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not conduct empirical comparisons or benchmark against baseline methods. Instead, it provides a formal lens through which existing tokenizers are analyzed: BPE, WordPiece, and Unigram are identified as multiplicative tokenizers whose decoders have trivial kernels, ensuring finite preimage sets. The framework shows that deterministic encoders (as in BPE and WordPiece) risk inconsistency when combined with non-injective preprocessing (e.g., lowercasing, accent stripping), while stochastic encoders (as in Unigram with regularization) introduce deliberate ambiguity that is not spurious. The paper positions its contribution relative to prior formal work by Zouhar et al. (2023a,b) and Berglund & van der Merwe (2023), arguing that the stochastic-map framework is more general and yields the necessary-and-sufficient consistency condition that those works do not establish.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new theoretical ground by proposing a unified formal framework for tokenizers based on the category of stochastic maps (Baez & Fritz, 2014), extending that framework to countably infinite sets. Prior theoretical work on tokenization (Guo, 1997; Kudo, 2018; Zouhar et al., 2023a,b; Berglund & van der Merwe, 2023; Rajaraman et al., 2024) addressed individual aspects in isolation, while the bulk of the literature has been empirical. The paper also draws on classical formal language theory—monoids, finite-state transducers, subsequential functions (Berstel, 1979; Choffrut, 1979)—to characterize computational properties like finiteness and sequentiality, repurposing these tools for the modern subword tokenization setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes the first necessary and sufficient condition (κτp* = p*) for a tokenizer to preserve estimator consistency, providing a clean criterion that was previously only informally assumed. It also formally characterizes well-known practical issues—spurious ambiguity, non-injectivity from preprocessing, unbounded preimage sets—within a single compositional framework, which could guide the design of provably sound tokenizers. However, the work is entirely theoretical with no empirical validation, and the broader question of whether tokenization introduces a genuinely helpful inductive bias for language modeling remains open. The framework is most relevant in settings where formal guarantees, verification, or interpretability matter as much as raw performance.
