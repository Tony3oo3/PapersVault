## Problem

When neural implicit functions (occupancy networks, SDFs) are pre-trained on dense synthetic data and fine-tuned on sparse, corrupted real data, the epistemic uncertainty introduced by fine-tuning the decoder remains unmodeled. This is particularly problematic in safety-critical applications like medical imaging, where the model fills in missing anatomy from a prior and clinicians need to know which regions are inferred rather than observed. To the best of the authors' knowledge, no prior work quantifies this decoder-level uncertainty during fine-tuning of implicit shape representations.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sparse set of 3D observations (e.g., 17–21 sagittal MRI slices out of a full volume, or eroded occupancy grids at 128³ resolution) paired with a latent code produced by a frozen convolutional encoder (ReconNet or a small autoencoder). It outputs a dense 3D reconstruction (binary occupancy or continuous SDF at 256³ or 128³ resolution) together with per-voxel epistemic uncertainty, computed as the variance across M=4 thinned network instances. The sparse input is bicubically upsampled to match the encoder's expected resolution, and the latent representation is bilinearly upsampled to provide a spatially varying conditioning signal at each query coordinate.

## Method

Dropsembles generates an ensemble of correlated 'thinned' networks from a single dropout-trained model: after pre-training one network with dropout on the dense dataset A, M binary masks are sampled to produce M thinned instances that share most weights. Each thinned instance is then independently fine-tuned on the sparse dataset B, and at inference the ensemble is treated as a uniformly weighted mixture model whose variance yields epistemic uncertainty. To prevent fine-tuning from erasing the dense prior, an EWC-inspired regularization term (using the Fisher information diagonal computed during pre-training) is added to each instance's fine-tuning objective, anchoring weights near the dataset-A solution. The key efficiency gain is that only one expensive pre-training run on dataset A is needed regardless of ensemble size M, whereas vanilla deep ensembles require M independent pre-training runs, reducing total training time by roughly a factor of M for the pre-training phase.

## Results

On the lumbar spine reconstruction task (the paper's primary real-world application), Dropsembles+EWC achieves 86.9% DSC and 10.9 Hausdorff distance, matching or exceeding deep ensembles (86.5% DSC, 10.5 HD) while reducing pre-training cost from 192 GPU-hours to 48 GPU-hours. On MNIST digit reconstruction, Dropsembles+EWC reaches 70.3% DSC with 6.1% ECE, outperforming MC dropout (55.7% DSC, 8.4% ECE) and matching ensembles (69.3% DSC, 5.2% ECE). On ShapeNet SDF reconstruction, Dropsembles+EWC achieves 84.3% mDSC and 17.67% ECE, the best DSC among all methods. Across all tasks, EWC regularization consistently improves both accuracy and calibration over the unregularized variants.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MC Dropout, Dropsembles consistently wins on reconstruction quality: on the lumbar spine task, Dropsembles achieves 86.8% DSC vs. MC dropout's 84.8%, and 11.4 HD vs. 18.15 HD. More critically, MC dropout produces noisy, incoherent reconstructions when used for uncertainty estimation (individual dropout samples do not yield plausible shapes), a failure mode absent in Dropsembles. Against Deep Ensembles, Dropsembles matches or slightly exceeds performance on most metrics (86.9% DSC vs. 86.5% on lumbar spine; 84.3% mDSC vs. 83.4% on ShapeNet) while reducing pre-training cost by a factor of 4 (48h vs. 192h on the lumbar spine task), since Dropsembles trains a single model on dataset A and only fine-tunes M thinned instances on dataset B. On calibration (ECE), the two methods are comparable, with ensembles sometimes slightly better (e.g., 3.9% vs. 4.5% ECE on lumbar spine). The key practical advantage is that Dropsembles' pre-training time remains constant regardless of ensemble size, whereas ensembles scale linearly.

## Relation to Prior Work

Dropsembles synthesizes two established uncertainty-estimation paradigms—dropout (Srivastava et al., 2014) and deep ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017)—into a single pipeline: one model is trained with dropout on a dense synthetic prior, then multiple thinned sub-networks are sampled and independently fine-tuned on sparse real data, with predictions aggregated as a uniform mixture. The fine-tuning stage is further regularized with Elastic Weight Consolidation (Kirkpatrick et al., 2016), borrowed from continual learning, to prevent the thinned networks from diverging from the dense prior. The paper positions this as the first treatment of epistemic uncertainty specifically in the implicit *decoder* during fine-tuning from synthetic data, complementing prior work that modeled uncertainty in the encoder or in the output distribution of NeRFs (Shen et al., 2021; Liao et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is strongest in safety-critical medical imaging, where sparse and corrupted MRI inputs force models to fill in anatomy from a synthetic atlas prior, and clinicians need to know *where* the model is guessing. A notable negative result is that MC dropout, while producing reasonable average predictions, yields individual samples that are noisy and topologically incoherent—undermining the fundamental purpose of 3D shape modeling—whereas Dropsembles preserves shape continuity across ensemble members. The main limitation is that Dropsembles still require M independent fine-tuning passes, matching ensemble cost at the fine-tuning stage; the savings come only from the (typically much longer) pre-training phase. An open direction the authors flag is combining decoder-level Dropsembles with encoder-level uncertainty to cover the full pipeline.
