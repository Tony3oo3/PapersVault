## Problem

Contrastive learning for audio representations relies on real-world recordings and post-hoc data augmentations (spectral masking, time shifts, etc.) that statistically alter observed data without manipulating the underlying data-generating process. This results in high correlation between augmented samples, limited controllability, and a dependence on large curated datasets. The paper asks whether entirely synthetic audio, generated by perturbing synthesizer parameters to create causally meaningful variations, can serve as an effective training signal for general-purpose audio representation learning.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 1-second audio clips synthesized on-the-fly from a modular synthesizer (no stored data), resampled to 16 kHz and converted to 64-band mel spectrograms with 96 time steps. A ResNet18 encoder (with a 1-channel convolution replacing the initial layer) maps each spectrogram to a 512-dimensional embedding. Downstream evaluation uses linear probing on these frozen embeddings across 8 classification tasks, with the embedding serving as the sole output of the pretraining stage.

## Method

The core mechanism generates positive pairs by sampling a random parameter vector θ uniformly from [0,1]^m_S (where m_S is the number of synthesizer control parameters, 78 for the Voice architecture), then independently perturbing it twice with isotropic Gaussian noise scaled by a single hyperparameter δ: θ_i = clip(θ + δ·z_i, 0, 1). The two perturbed parameter vectors are fed through the SYNTHAX modular synthesizer (oscillators, LFOs, ADSR envelopes, noise generator, mixer) to produce two distinct but similar 1-second sounds that form a contrastive positive pair. Training optimizes the alignment-uniformity objective (Wang & Isola, 2020) with SGD, using a batch size of 768 per GPU over 200 epochs with 100,000 sounds generated per epoch. The key distinction from standard augmentation-based contrastive learning is that the perturbation acts on the causal data-generating process (synthesis parameters controlling pitch, timbre, and temporal envelope) rather than on the observed signal, yielding variations that are interpretable, continuously tunable via δ, and not achievable through post-hoc signal transformations.

## Results

The best synthetic-trained model (Voice, δ=0.25) achieves 58.90 on ESC-50, 66.71 on UrbanSound8K, 39.45 on VIVAE, 44.40 on NSynth Pitch, 48.43 on CREMA-D, 24.12 on FSD50k, 9.15 on Vocal Imitation, and 58.60 on LibriCount. These scores uniformly outperform the VGGSound SSL (augmentations) baseline across all 8 tasks and beat the strongest internal baseline (VGGSound SSL with temporal jitter) on 6/8 tasks. The approach is notable for achieving these results without any real audio during pretraining, using only on-the-fly synthetic generation with a single hyperparameter δ.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the supervised VGGSound ResNet18 baseline, the best synthetic model wins on 3/8 tasks (VIVAE 39.45 vs 39.38, NSynth Pitch 44.40 vs 43.80, LibriCount 58.60 vs 56.10) but trails substantially on the remaining five. Against MS-CLAP Linear, the synthetic model wins on 2/5 comparable tasks (CREMA-D 48.43 vs 23.15, LibriCount 58.60 vs 54.51) but loses on ESC-50, UrbanSound8K, and FSD50k. Against GURA (the strongest overall HEAR model), the synthetic model wins only on NSynth Pitch (44.40 vs 38.20) and loses on all other comparable tasks. The most compelling comparison is against the internal self-supervised baselines: the synthetic approach uniformly beats VGGSound SSL with augmentations on all 8 tasks and beats VGGSound SSL with temporal jitter (the strongest internal baseline) on 6/8 tasks, with particularly large margins on NSynth Pitch (44.40 vs 14.20) and Vocal Imitation (9.15 vs 3.43). The paper notes that adding further augmentations to the doppelgänger training provides no significant benefit, suggesting the δ-based parameter perturbations already capture sufficient variation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the visual representation learning line of work on learning from synthetic/procedural data (Baradad Jurjo et al., 2021; Kataoka et al., 2020) to the audio domain, using the SYNTHAX modular synthesizer as the data generation engine. It departs fundamentally from the dominant audio contrastive learning paradigm that relies on augmenting real recordings (SpecAugment, temporal jitter, spectral masking) by instead generating positive pairs through causal perturbation of synthesizer parameters, intervening in the data-generating process rather than the observed signal. The training procedure (alignment/uniformity objectives with SGD) is directly adapted from the visual synthetic-data literature, while the evaluation protocol draws on the HEAR and ARCH audio benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The approach offers a storage-free, single-hyperparameter alternative to large real audio datasets, which is practically attractive for practitioners facing data scarcity or licensing constraints. However, absolute performance remains well below strong baselines (e.g., 58.90 vs. 96.65 on ESC-50), limiting immediate deployment impact. Open questions include whether the method scales to larger encoders (AST, transformers), how hybrid synthetic+real training strategies perform, and whether more sophisticated (anisotropic, learned) perturbation schedules could close the gap. The authors also flag the need to audit synthetic data-generating procedures for task-specific biases analogous to those in real datasets.
