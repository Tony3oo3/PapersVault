## Problem

While adversarial attacks on image classifiers are well-characterized, there is no systematic understanding of how attack susceptibility scales with model size, activation dimension, and the ratio of input-to-output space dimensions. Language models offer a uniquely controllable test-bed because the output space dimension (number of target tokens t) can be swept independently, accessing input-output dimension regimes far beyond what is possible in standard vision tasks. The paper also highlights a practical gap: multi-modal and retrieval-augmented LLMs expose activations directly to external components, creating an attack surface that has not been quantified.

## Input / Output

The attacker specifies a context of s tokens, an attack length a ≤ s (number of leading tokens whose d-dimensional float16 activations are controlled), and a target sequence T of t tokens. The perturbation P ∈ ℝ^{a×d} is added to the embedding-layer activations of the first a tokens, and the model is run autoregressively to check whether argmax sampling reproduces T. The key derived quantities are the attack multiplier κ (output tokens controllable per input token's activation, measured at 50% success threshold) and the attack resistance χ (input bits needed per output bit, computed as χ = dp/(κ·log₂V)), both of which are shown to be remarkably stable across model families and sizes.

## Method

The language model is split into an embedding function f_before and a transformer function f_after, exposing the residual-stream activations for direct manipulation. An adversarial perturbation P is optimized via Adam (lr=0.1, 300 steps) to minimize the cross-entropy loss over the t target tokens, with gradients flowing only through P. By sweeping target length t and fitting a sigmoid to the success-rate curve, the paper extracts t_max (the 50%-success threshold) and verifies the linear scaling law t_max = κ·a·f/n, where f is the fraction of activation dimensions used and n is the multi-attack multiplicity. A geometric dimensionality-mismatch theory predicts κ ∝ d and κ_token/κ = log₂V/(dp), both confirmed empirically across models from 33M to 2.8B parameters. Token-substitution attacks (greedy exhaustive search over V tokens per position) serve as a lower-dimensional comparison, yielding κ_token ≈ 0.12 versus κ ≈ 24 for activations on the 70M model.

## Results

The paper establishes a linear scaling law t_max = κa for adversarial attacks on language model activations, where κ (the attack multiplier) is model-specific. Across models from 33M to 2.8B parameters (Pythia, Phi, TinyStories), κ ranges from 24.2 to 128.4, but κ/d (dimensions per controlled output token) stays remarkably constant between 14.6 and 28.2. The attack resistance χ — the number of input bits needed to control one output bit — is stable between ≈15 and ≈29 across all models and families. For the 1.4B Pythia model, a single token's activation perturbation (2048 dimensions) can precisely dictate ~119 subsequent tokens, while token substitution attacks require ~8 input tokens to control just 1 output token (κ_token ≈ 0.12).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between activation attacks and token substitution attacks on pythia-70m. Activation attacks achieve κ = 24.2 (one token's activation controls ~24 output tokens), while token substitution achieves only κ_token ≈ 0.12 (requiring ~8 input tokens to control 1 output token) — a factor of ~200 difference. The geometric theory predicts κ_token/κ = log₂(V)/(dp) ≈ 2×10⁻³, and the empirically measured ratio is ≈5×10⁻³, matching within a factor of 2.5, which the authors note is surprisingly close given the theory's simplicity. The attack resistance χ for token attacks is 8.3 (vs. 21.7 for activations), reflecting that the model was trained on token inputs and thus has lower resistance to token-level perturbations. Across model families, Pythia models show the most consistent κ/d (14.6–24.4), while Phi-1 is an outlier with d/κ = 47.9, suggesting architectural differences affect attack susceptibility. The paper also validates that using only a fraction f of activation dimensions scales the effective attack length as predicted (t_max = κfa/n), confirming the dimensional theory.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adversarial attack literature from image classifiers to language model activations, building directly on the gradient-based optimization framework of Szegedy et al. (2013) and the multi-attack concept from Fort (2023) where a single perturbation simultaneously targets multiple input-output pairs. It positions LLMs as a controllable test-bed for the dimensionality-mismatch hypothesis of adversarial vulnerability (Goodfellow et al., 2015; Ilyas et al., 2019; Fort & Lakshminarayanan, 2024), exploiting the fact that target sequence length t provides an exponential dial on output space dimensionality that is inaccessible in standard vision tasks. The paper departs from prior token-level jailbreaking and adversarial suffix work (Zou et al., 2023b; Wang et al., 2023) by operating on the continuous residual stream rather than discrete tokens, and introduces a scaling-law formalism (t_max = κa) with a derived attack resistance metric χ that unifies activation and token attacks under a single geometric framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical relevance is sharpened by the growing attack surface in retrieval-augmented and multi-modal LLMs, where non-text modalities are injected directly as activations, bypassing the token interface and giving an attacker the exact access this paper exploits. The remarkable stability of attack resistance χ (≈16–25) across two orders of magnitude in model size and across three model families provides strong empirical support for the dimensionality theory of adversarial vulnerability in regimes previously inaccessible in computer vision. Open questions include whether adversarial training or architectural changes can shift χ toward the ideal χ=1, whether the linear scaling law holds for much larger models (70B+), and whether the activation-level threat model translates to practical defenses for deployed multi-modal systems.
