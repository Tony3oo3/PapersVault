## Problem

Post-hoc explanation methods (SHAP, SAGE, Expected Gradients, Feature Effects) universally rely on i.i.d. sampling from the dataset to form background/reference data, but the approximation error this introduces has never been quantified or systematically improved upon. The paper asks whether a principled, distribution-aware sampling strategy can reduce this error without adding meaningful computational cost.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252478/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a feature matrix X (n×d, where n ranges from ~1k to 50k and d from 5 to 1024) and a trained prediction model f, and produces a compressed coreset of size √n selected from the original data points (not synthetic). This coreset replaces the i.i.d. background sample in any existing explanation pipeline, yielding feature attributions, importance rankings, PDP/ALE curves, or expected gradients. The compression step itself takes less than 1 second for 20k→128 samples on CPU, making it a negligible overhead relative to the explanation computation it precedes.

## Method

Compress Then Explain (CTE) replaces i.i.d. sampling with kernel thinning (KT) via the COMPRESS++ algorithm, which selects a coreset of size √n that minimizes the kernel maximum mean discrepancy (MMD_k) between the compressed and full empirical distributions using a Gaussian kernel with σ=√(2d). The key theoretical insight is that the explanation approximation error is upper-bounded by C_f · MMD_k (Proposition 1 for local feature marginalization, Proposition 2 for global aggregated explanations), so minimizing MMD_k directly restricts explanation error. Practically, CTE is a 3-line plug-in: call compresspp_kt on X to get indices, subset X to the compressed sample, and pass it as background data to any existing explainer (SHAP, SAGE, Captum's IntegratedGradients, or partial dependence). Across 50+ datasets and 6 explanation estimators, CTE achieves 20–45% lower MAE and ~50% lower variance than i.i.d. sampling, often reaching on-par error with 2–3× fewer model inferences.

## Results

CTE reduces the mean absolute error (MAE) of explanation estimation by 20–45% over standard i.i.d. sampling across four explanation methods (Kernel-SHAP, Permutation-SHAP, Kernel-SAGE, Permutation-SAGE) on five OpenXAI datasets, while also decreasing the standard deviation of estimates by approximately 50%. For gradient-based Expected Gradients on image datasets, CTE achieves 35% error reduction on MNIST, 40% on Fashion-MNIST, and 21% on CIFAR-10 (all p < 1e-10). CTE achieves on-par approximation error using 2–3× fewer samples, i.e. requiring 2–3× fewer model inferences, with negligible compression overhead (<1 s to compress 20k→128 samples on CPU).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CTE (kernel thinning via COMPRESS++ with a Gaussian kernel) against two baselines: standard i.i.d. random sampling (the dominant practice in explanation estimation) and k-medoids clustering (a deterministic sanity-check baseline). CTE consistently outperforms i.i.d. sampling on all 50+ dataset-model pairs across four explanation methods, achieving 20–45% lower MAE and ~50% lower standard deviation. In terms of efficiency, CTE reaches the same approximation error as i.i.d. sampling using 2–3× fewer background/foreground samples, directly translating to 2–3× fewer model inferences. Against the k-medoids clustering baseline, CTE is superior in both explanation accuracy and distribution fidelity: clustering shows no consistent improvement over i.i.d. in MMD, total variation, Wasserstein distance, or KL divergence, and leads to higher explanation estimation error. The compression overhead is negligible—compressing 20k samples to 128 takes under 1 second on CPU, compared to 30 minutes for a single Kernel-SHAP estimation on 9k samples.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges distribution compression from statistical learning theory (kernel thinning, Dwivedi & Mackey 2021; COMPRESS++, Shetty et al. 2022) with post-hoc explanation estimation, proposing 'compress then explain' as a replacement for the ubiquitous 'sample then explain' paradigm. It is orthogonal to prior work on conditional distribution sampling (Aas et al., Olsen et al.), perturbation sampling (Petsiuk et al., Slack et al.), and coalition sampling (Chen et al., Covert & Lee), instead targeting the marginal distribution used as background/reference data. The theoretical contribution (Propositions 1–2) establishes that explanation approximation error is bounded by the MMD between data samples, providing the first formal link between distribution compression quality and explanation fidelity. The paper is also the first to empirically evaluate kernel thinning on supervised learning datasets, extending KT's prior applications in kernel testing and robotics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is direct: CTE is a 3-line plug-in that reduces model inferences by 2–3× while improving accuracy by 20–45% and stability by ~50%, applicable to any explanation method relying on i.i.d. background sampling (SHAP, SAGE, Expected Gradients, Feature Effects, G-LIME). Open questions include tuning COMPRESS++ hyperparameters (kernel choice, bandwidth), handling categorical features, developing stratified KT for supervised settings, and exploring oversampling beyond the √n coreset size. The broader implication is that the field's default assumption that i.i.d. sampling is a negligible approximation is wrong—its error can flip feature importance rankings—making CTE a potential safeguard against both random-seed instability and adversarial manipulation of explanations.
