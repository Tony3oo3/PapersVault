## Problem

Existing quantitative measures of stereotypes in text-to-image models conflate stereotypes with general bias by relying on statistical parity, which fails to capture the directionality inherent in the sociological definition of stereotypes (e.g., generating predominantly female doctors is a bias but not a stereotype, since the known stereotype is that doctors are male). Additionally, prior work provides no mechanism to understand where stereotypical attributes originate within the T2I model's internal representations or generation process.

## Input / Output

OASIS consumes a dataset of 2000 generated images per concept, the latent-space trajectory at every diffusion/flow time step, and an open-set stereotype candidate set (≥15 attributes with positive/negative text descriptions) produced by an LLM (ChatGPT o1-preview or 4o). Outputs are per-attribute rather than a single aggregate: a Stereotype Score (Ψ) and WALS value for each candidate attribute, top-K optimized prompts from StOP, and a per-time-step SPI curve tracking attribute emergence. The CLIP ViT-G-14 model (trained on LAION-2B) serves as the zero-shot attribute classifier, and the latent-space access requires model-specific hooks into the velocity/noise predictor at each step.

## Method

The core contribution is a new mathematical definition of stereotype (Def. 1) that measures only directional over-representation relative to the true real-world distribution P*(A|C), rather than any deviation from uniformity. Building on this, OASIS provides four complementary components: (M1) Stereotype Score computes Ψ = max(0, P(A|D,C) − P*(A|C)) using CLIP cosine-similarity zero-shot classification over the generated dataset; (M2) WALS measures spectral variance along a stereotypical attribute by projecting SVD singular vectors of CLIP image features onto the attribute direction δA (estimated as the difference of positive/negative text embeddings); (U1) StOP discovers hidden internal associations by spectral-clustering CLIP features, then solving a ZeroCap-style beam-search optimization to find prompts that maximize mean CLIP score with a stereotypical cluster; (U2) SPI quantifies stereotype emergence during generation by computing the cosine similarity between the latent velocity v_Θ(x_t, t, ε_t) and the attribute direction δA (derived from A-aware noise predictions) at each time step, revealing that stereotypical attributes are injected predominantly in the first few steps.

## Results

Across three nationalities (Iranian, Indian, Mexican) and 15 stereotypical attributes, the Stereotype Score (Ψ) reveals that newer T2I models generally reduce but do not eliminate stereotypes. FLUX.1 achieves the lowest gender stereotype scores (e.g., Ψ=33.6 for Iranian 'Man' vs. 48 for SDv2 and 49.8 for SDv3), yet it exhibits the highest mustache stereotype for Mexican (Ψ=59.7) and hat stereotype (Ψ=44.4). SDv3 shows the largest improvement on Mexican attributes (Ψ=0 for hat, sombrero, and embroidered clothing) but worsens the Iranian turban stereotype to Ψ=68.8 (vs. 27.1 for SDv2). WALS analysis further shows that models with lower stereotype scores (e.g., SDv3) often sacrifice spectral variety along those attributes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three T2I models—SDv2 (2022), SDv3 (2024), and FLUX.1[dev] (2024)—as baselines for stereotype measurement. FLUX.1 achieves the lowest gender-related stereotype scores across all nationalities (e.g., Ψ=33.6 for Iranian 'Man' vs. 48/49.8 for SDv2/SDv3; Ψ=2.1 for Mexican 'Man' vs. 47.1/37), indicating the most progress on demographic parity. However, FLUX.1 performs worst on several cultural attributes: Mexican mustache (Ψ=59.7 vs. 52.8 for SDv2 and 9.1 for SDv3), Mexican hat (Ψ=44.4 vs. 22.3/0), and Indian Tilak/Bindi (Ψ=36.7 vs. 11.7/9.3). SDv3 shows the strongest improvement on Mexican cultural attributes (Ψ=0 for hat, sombrero, and embroidered clothing) but paradoxically worsens the Iranian turban stereotype to Ψ=68.8 (vs. 27.1 for SDv2). SDv2, the oldest model, consistently shows the highest beard and gender stereotypes (e.g., Ψ=62.6 for Iranian beard, Ψ=53.2 for 'Old') but is not uniformly worst. No single model dominates across all attributes, and the WALS metric reveals that SDv3's lower stereotype scores on Mexican attributes come at the cost of reduced spectral variety, underscoring that distributional and spectral metrics must be considered jointly.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior stereotype-detection work (OpenBias, SeeGULL, ViSAGe) by rejecting statistical parity as a valid stereotype metric and instead proposing a directional, distribution-violation formulation (Def. 1) that aligns with the sociological definition of stereotypes. For candidate-set generation it follows D'Incà et al. (2024) using an LLM, and for the StOP prompt-optimization component it adapts the beam-search sequence optimization from ZeroCap (Tewel et al., 2022). The key conceptual departure is the introduction of complementary spectral (WALS) and latent-space (SPI) analyses that go beyond distributional counting to probe *where* and *when* stereotypes originate inside the model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that stereotypical attributes are injected in the first few denoising steps (SPI) reframes mitigation: rather than correcting a gradual drift, one must intervene at the initial velocity, which has direct implications for flow-matching and rectified-flow architectures. The observation that data-balancing for a single concept (e.g., gender in 'doctor') actually *worsens* intersectional stereotypes (e.g., 'Iranian doctor') highlights a fundamental limitation of current debiasing pipelines. Open questions include whether the early-step predisposition can be removed via targeted latent-space editing, and whether the Internet-footprint correlation will persist as training corpora diversify.
