## Problem

Existing approaches to stylized LLM responses—few-shot prompting and supervised fine-tuning—are either insufficient for complex style adaptation (e.g., Shakespearean English, classical Chinese) or prohibitively expensive when multiple characters are needed (e.g., game NPCs). Conventional representation editing methods (ITI, RepE, TrFr) apply a single steering vector without disentangling style from semantics, causing semantic degradation and fluency loss. The paper asks: how to solve steering vectors that shift LLM outputs into a target style while minimally affecting the underlying semantics?

## Input / Output

At inference, DRESS takes a natural-language user query and produces a single stylized text response in the target style (e.g., Shakespearean early modern English or Dream of the Red Chamber classical Chinese), with no change to the LLM's parameters. During the offline setup phase, the method consumes paired QA datasets where each question has both an ordinary-style and a target-style response (constructed via GPT-4 rewriting), plus a general-purpose QA set for query-style diversification. The editing is applied at the attention-head activation level inside the transformer forward pass, specifically after the Attn operator and before the output projection W^o, so the model's weights remain frozen.

## Method

DRESS (Disentangling Representation Editing in Style Subspace) is a train-free, three-stage representation editing pipeline. First, linear probing classifiers are trained on last-token activations to identify the top-H attention heads most discriminative of style, restricting editing to those heads. Second, within each selected head, the activation differences between target-style and ordinary-style responses are collected and decomposed via SVD; the top-K right singular vectors form an orthogonal style subspace that captures style variation while filtering out semantic noise. Third, an adaptive editing strength α_i = λ(1+γ_i)β_i is computed per basis vector and per generated token, where β_i is the global mean projection onto the basis and γ_i is a context-dependent cosine similarity between the current token's activation gap and the target-style mean, enabling dynamic, token-level modulation of steering intensity. This higher-rank, subspace-constrained, adaptively-scaled editing is the key distinction from prior single-vector methods like ITI or RepE.

## Results

On the Shakespeare-style benchmark, DRESS achieves an overall assessment (OA) of 28.9% and a GPT-4 rating of 8.85/10, outperforming the best baseline (TrFr) by 7.84% on OA and 1.37% on GPT-4 rating. On the more challenging Dream of the Red Chamber-style benchmark, DRESS reaches 29.1% OA and 7.96 GPT-4 rating, with improvements of up to 23.8% on OA and 4.19% on GPT-4 rating over the strongest baseline. DRESS also achieves the highest semantic preservation (73.3% on Shakespeare, 70.8% on Dream of the Red Chamber) and fluency score (39.6% and 42.4% respectively) among all methods, confirming that style subspace editing preserves meaning while enhancing style.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DRESS outperforms all five baselines on both benchmarks across every metric. Against the closest competitor TrFr (multi-vector steering with orthogonality regularization), DRESS gains 5.7% OA on Dream of the Red Chamber (29.1% vs. 23.4%) and 2.1% OA on Shakespeare (28.9% vs. 26.8%), with a notable 2.5-point GPT-4 rating advantage on the Chinese benchmark (7.96 vs. 6.96). ITI (attention-head-level steering) is the second-strongest baseline, but DRESS still leads by 7.3% OA on Dream of the Red Chamber and 3.4% on Shakespeare, primarily through superior semantic preservation and fluency. Mean-Centring and RepE, which use single-vector steering without head selection, perform substantially worse (OA of 15.5% and 16.7% on Dream of the Red Chamber), confirming that disentangling the style subspace is critical. SFT with LoRA achieves moderate style intensity but lags DRESS on OA by 5.6% (Dream) and 4.4% (Shakespeare), while also requiring per-character fine-tuning that DRESS avoids entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

DRESS extends the representation editing lineage (Mean-Centring, RepE, CCS, ITI, TrFr) that uses steering vectors added to LLM activations to control output attributes. While ITI already identifies style-relevant attention heads via probing, DRESS departs by adding two further disentanglement steps: SVD-based subspace filtering within those heads to isolate style from semantic components, and a per-token adaptive editing strength that modulates each subspace basis dynamically. The approach is grounded in the linear representation hypothesis and the approximate orthogonality of activations in over-parameterized networks, but applies these principles specifically to the multi-dimensional, context-dependent nature of language style rather than the more binary attributes (truthfulness, knowledge) targeted by prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DRESS offers a practical, train-free alternative to per-character fine-tuning for building stylized conversational agents (NPCs, role-playing characters), which is a growing application area. The two constructed benchmarks (Shakespeare-style English, Dream of the Red Chamber-style Chinese) provide reproducible evaluation for stylized QA, though they cover only two literary styles. Key open questions include scalability to 100B+ models, handling multi-turn dialogue with evolving style, and whether the subspace disentanglement generalizes to more granular style attributes (e.g., individual character personalities within a single work). The paper acknowledges that full humanoid agents still require dialogue memory and retrieval-augmented generation beyond what representation editing alone provides.
