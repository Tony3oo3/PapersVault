## Problem

Existing one-shot pruning methods face a trade-off: global second-order methods (e.g., WoodFisher, CHITA) optimize an accurate proxy for the network loss but are computationally intractable for large vision networks, while local layer-wise methods (e.g., OBC, SparseGPT) are efficient but minimize only a linear least-squares reconstruction at a single layer, ignoring how pruning propagates through nonlinear activations in deeper layers. SNOWS bridges this gap by optimizing a more global reconstruction objective that accounts for subsequent layers' representations while retaining the layer-wise computational structure.

## Input / Output

SNOWS takes a pre-trained network (CNN or ViT), a small calibration set (1,000–20,000 samples depending on model size), and a binary sparsity mask (N:M semi-structured or unstructured) obtained from any prior mask-selection method such as magnitude pruning, OBC, or SparseGPT. It outputs the readjusted weights on the active (non-zero) entries of the mask, producing a pruned network with the same sparsity pattern but improved deep-feature fidelity. The method processes layers sequentially in a cascading fashion, where each pruned layer's output becomes the next layer's input, and the K-step horizon parameter controls how many subsequent operations are included in the reconstruction target.

## Method

SNOWS replaces the standard layer-wise linear reconstruction with a K-step nonlinear objective that minimizes the sum of squared errors between the dense and pruned network outputs across K subsequent operations (including ReLU, batch norm, residual connections, attention, GeLU, etc.). Because this objective is no longer a linear least-squares problem, the Hessian is no longer simply 2X^T X and cannot be formed explicitly. Instead, SNOWS uses Hessian-free optimization: the Hessian-vector product is computed via a finite-difference gradient evaluation, and a customized Conjugate Gradient solver exploits the sparsity structure of the masked Hessian (sparsity s² when the mask has sparsity s) to compute exact Newton descent steps on a reduced linear system of dimension equal to the number of active weights. Levenberg-Marquardt regularization ensures positive definiteness, and a sub-sampled stochastic Newton scheme with Armijo line search handles memory constraints. The framework is mask-agnostic, so it can be layered on top of any existing mask-selection technique to readjust weights while preserving deep feature representations.

## Results

On ResNet50 pruned to 1:4 N:M sparsity, SNOWS (with MP mask) achieves 75.87% top-1 accuracy on CIFAR-100 (1.5% drop from the dense 77.40%) and 62.75% on ImageNet-1k (12.96% drop from 75.71%), using only 3,000 calibration samples. For unstructured sparsity at 70%, SNOWS prunes MobileNetV1 to 65.28% on ImageNet-1k (5.6% drop) and ResNet20 to 90.87% on CIFAR-10 (1.7% drop). On ViT/B-16 pruned to 2:4 sparsity across QKV, output projection, and MLP layers, SNOWS achieves 76.57% top-1 accuracy versus 70.89% for magnitude pruning alone (dense: 80.42%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SNOWS consistently outperforms all five baselines across every sparsity level and both architectures tested. At 70% unstructured sparsity on ResNet20/CIFAR-10, SNOWS achieves 90.87% (−1.69%), beating the strongest competitor FALCON (84.42%, −6.94%) by 6.45 points and CHITA (84.12%, −7.24%) by 6.75 points. On MobileNetV1/ImageNet-1k at 70% sparsity, the gap widens dramatically: SNOWS retains 65.28% (−5.61%) while CHITA drops to 59.40% (−12.55%) and FALCON to 58.40% (−13.55%), a 5.9–6.9 point advantage. At 80% sparsity the advantage is even more pronounced, with SNOWS at 87.82% on ResNet20 versus 65.17% for FALCON (the best competitor), a 22.65 point margin. For N:M semi-structured sparsity, SNOWS applied on top of an MP mask recovers ResNet50 to 75.87% on CIFAR-100 at 1:4 (vs. 69.79% for OBC alone, a 7.6% drop), while on ImageNet-1k the OBC-only baseline suffers a 29.42% drop. SNOWS also outperforms magnitude pruning alone on ViT/B-16 by 5.68 points (76.57% vs. 70.89%) when pruning all QKV, output projection, and MLP layers to 2:4 sparsity.

## Relation to Prior Work

SNOWS bridges two established lines of one-shot pruning: global second-order methods (OBS, WoodFisher, CHITA) that optimize the full network loss but require expensive full-network Hessian or Fisher computations, and local layer-wise methods (OBC, SparseGPT, Net-Trim) that use tractable linear least-squares reconstruction but ignore nonlinear activations in subsequent layers. The K-step reconstruction objective (Eqn 3) generalizes Net-Trim's ReLU-specific formulation to arbitrary twice-differentiable compositions, while the Hessian-free Newton solver (Martens, 2010) departs from prior global methods that rely on Fisher or Kronecker-product approximations by computing exact Newton steps via Hessian-vector products. The decoupling of the discrete mask problem from the continuous weight-update problem, combined with sparsity-aware Conjugate Gradient, is a novel algorithmic contribution not present in either the global or local pruning literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SNOWS provides a practical, drop-in weight-readjustment step that can be applied on top of any existing mask selection method, dramatically closing the accuracy gap at high sparsity (e.g., 1:4) where prior one-shot methods degrade severely. The ability to prune a 304M-parameter ViT-L/16 on a single A100 GPU without retraining addresses a real deployment bottleneck for practitioners who lack training data or compute. Key limitations include the absence of mask selection (the method inherits mask quality from upstream), the need to tune the K-step horizon per architecture, and the restriction to post-training settings. Open questions raised by the authors include whether discrete optimization directly on the SNOWS loss could improve mask selection, and whether the framework extends to LLMs and TTS with appropriate K-step formulations.
