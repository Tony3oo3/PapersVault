## Problem

Computing full probability distributions of equilibrium flow states (e.g., RMS, two-point correlations) in complex 3D turbulent flows requires prohibitively long numerical simulations, and existing deep learning surrogates predict only mean flows or single trajectories rather than the full state distribution. Prior graph-based diffusion models fail on large meshes because sequential message passing cannot propagate denoising signals across thousands of nodes, and no existing method can recover complete distributions from the short, incomplete training trajectories that are practically obtainable.

## Input / Output

The model consumes an unstructured mesh (nodes with positions, surface normals, and type indicators; edges with relative positions and velocity projections) plus physical parameters such as Reynolds number, and produces multiple independent samples from the equilibrium distribution of pressure and/or velocity fields on that mesh. For the WING task, the surface mesh has ~7,000 nodes versus over 10^5 cells for an equivalent volumetric grid, and the LDGN variant operates on a 16×-compressed latent graph, reducing inference cost while the VGAE decoder maps samples back to physical space. Dirichlet boundary conditions (e.g., inlet velocity, wall values) are enforced deterministically rather than learned.

## Method

The paper introduces Diffusion Graph Networks (DGNs) that apply the DDPM denoising framework to mesh nodes, with the denoiser implemented as a multi-scale GNN using Guillard's coarsening algorithm to build a U-Net-like hierarchy of progressively coarser graphs, enabling global denoising that single-scale message passing cannot achieve on large systems. A second variant, the Latent DGN (LDGN), first compresses states into a low-dimensional latent space via a Variational Graph Auto-Encoder (VGAE) with a dedicated condition encoder (necessary because the state is unavailable at inference), then performs diffusion on the coarse latent graph, decoupling high-frequency detail (handled by the VGAE) from mid-to-large-scale patterns (handled by the LDGN). Training uses the Nichol & Dhariwal loss with learned variance and importance sampling. A key empirical finding is that both DGNs and LDGNs accurately recover full distributions even when each training trajectory covers only a fraction of the oscillation period or equilibrium variance, by recognizing shared physical patterns across the ensemble of training systems rather than overfitting to individual trajectories.

## Results

On the ELLIPSE task, LDGN achieves the highest sample accuracy (R² = 0.995 ± 0.007 on ELLIPSE-INDIST), closely followed by DGN (R² = 0.984 ± 0.017). On ELLIPSEFLOW, DGN leads with R² = 0.990 ± 0.010, with LDGN close behind at 0.987 ± 0.013. For the WING task, LDGN generates 3,000 samples in 2.43 minutes on a single GPU versus 2,989 minutes for the ground-truth DES simulator on 8 CPU threads — a ~5-order-of-magnitude speedup for a single converged state. LDGN also reduces high-frequency noise by ~3× compared to DGN, thanks to the VGAE decoder filtering residual noise in physical space.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DGN and LDGN against five GNN-based probabilistic baselines: Vanilla GNN, Bayesian GNN, Gaussian Regression GNN (GR-GNN), Gaussian Mixture GNN (GM-GNN), and VGAE. On sample accuracy (R²), LDGN and DGN consistently outperform all baselines across in- and out-of-distribution settings; for example, on ELLIPSE-INDIST, LDGN (0.995) beats GM-GNN (0.981), VGAE (0.994), and Vanilla GNN (0.962). On distributional accuracy (Wasserstein-2 distance), the advantage is more pronounced: LDGN and DGN are the only methods that faithfully reproduce the ground-truth PDF, while GM-GNN produces spatially discontinuous samples (high W₂^graph) and the Bayesian GNN incorrectly assumes Gaussian output distributions. The VGAE baseline suffers from mode collapse on the more complex ELLIPSEFLOW task despite careful tuning of the KL penalty and latent dimension. A key finding is that DGN/LDGN maintain distributional accuracy even when trained on trajectories too short to capture full statistics (e.g., 10 time-steps vs. 21–38 needed for a full vortex-shedding cycle), whereas GM-GNN and other baselines degrade sharply under the same conditions. The Bayesian GNN, while the strongest non-diffusion baseline, is ~8× slower to train and still substantially inferior in both sample and distributional accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper combines three established threads: the DDPM/latent diffusion framework (Ho et al., 2020; Rombach et al., 2022), multi-scale graph neural networks for mesh-based fluid simulation (Lino et al., 2022; Pfaff et al., 2021), and graph-based diffusion models (Hoogeboom et al., 2022; Wen et al., 2023). The key departure from prior graph-diffusion work is the use of a multi-scale U-Net-like GNN processor (with Guillard's mesh coarsening) to overcome the receptive-field bottleneck of single-scale message passing on large meshes, and the shift to a VGAE latent space to decouple high-frequency detail from large-scale pattern learning. Unlike prior fluid-dynamics diffusion models (Gao et al., 2024; Lienen et al., 2024) that relied on CNNs restricted to Cartesian grids, this work operates directly on unstructured meshes, enabling complex 3D geometries with adaptive resolution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: generating a single converged turbulent wing state is 4–5 orders of magnitude faster than running the DES simulation, and the new WING datasets (1,000 training / 16 test 3D turbulent simulations on ~7k-node meshes) fill a gap in publicly available large-scale unsteady turbulence data. The finding that full equilibrium distributions can be learned from trajectories covering only ~10% of the time needed for statistical convergence is practically important for expensive 3D simulations where data generation is the bottleneck. Open questions include temporal correlation of samples (currently i.i.d.), incorporation of physical constraints via guidance, and whether the incomplete-data learning capability generalizes beyond the cross-system pattern-sharing regime demonstrated here.
