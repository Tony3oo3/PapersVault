## Problem

Existing experimental design methods for PDE-based inverse problems rely on expensive, non-differentiable numerical PDE solvers and typically require sequential, adaptive adjustments of sensor placements between trials. No prior framework exploits the meshless, differentiable, and amortizable properties of physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) to perform continuous, one-shot optimization of design parameters for inverse problems.

## Input / Output

The method takes a known PDE operator with IC/BCs, a prior distribution over PDE parameters (which may be finite-dimensional scalars or function-valued, parameterized by a small NN), a feasible set of observation inputs (free placement, regular time intervals, or grid-constrained, parameterized by a continuous design vector γ), and an observation budget M. It outputs the optimal observation input X_γ for one-shot sensor deployment, after which a single inverse PINN trained on the collected noisy data yields the estimated PDE parameter β̂. Unlike discrete grid-search baselines, the design parameter is optimized continuously via gradient descent, and the same forward PINNs are reused across all optimization iterations.

## Method

PIED constructs a fully differentiable ED loop with N parallel threads, each containing a PINN forward simulator (trained on the PDE loss for a reference β_i), an observation selector that queries the simulator at candidate inputs X_γ with added Gaussian noise, and a PINN inverse solver that jointly learns θ and β̂_i from the mock observations. A shared meta-learned initialization θ_SI is obtained via REPTILE across all reference β values, enabling fast fine-tuning of both forward and inverse PINNs. Two novel differentiable criteria approximate the inverse solver's converged performance without full training: FIST partially trains inverse PINNs for r steps (initialized from perturbed forward PINN parameters) and backpropagates the parameter error through the training steps, while MoTE uses the empirical Neural Tangent Kernel of the PINN to perform closed-form kernel regression that predicts the converged β̂. Because the entire pipeline is differentiable w.r.t. γ, the design parameter is optimized via standard gradient-based methods rather than Bayesian optimization or combinatorial search.

## Results

PIED's two criteria (FIST and MoTE) consistently achieve the lowest expected loss across all five experimental scenarios. The most dramatic gains appear on the 2D Navier-Stokes equation, where MoTE achieves an expected loss of 1.18 (×10⁻²) versus 6.08 for MI and 4.33 for VBOED — roughly a 5× improvement over the best baseline. On the function-valued Eikonal equation, FIST reduces the loss to 0.74 (×10¹) compared to 2.02 for MI, while also exhibiting the smallest semi-interquartile range (0.02), indicating highly consistent performance across IP instances. On real-world datasets (groundwater flow and cell growth), FIST again leads with losses of 1.93 and 2.62 respectively, outperforming all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PIED is compared against two learned ED baselines — MI (Mutual Information Neural Estimation, Belghazi et al. 2018) and VBOED (Variational Bayesian Optimal Experimental Design, Foster et al. 2019) — as well as simple Random and Grid placement strategies. FIST and MoTE outperform both MI and VBOED on all five datasets. The margin is largest on the 2D Navier-Stokes scenario, where MoTE (1.18) beats VBOED (4.33) by ~3.7× and MI (6.08) by ~5.2×, reflecting the higher difficulty of optimizing sensor placement in a 2D spatial domain. On the Eikonal equation with function-valued PDE parameters, FIST (0.74) nearly halves the loss of MI (2.02) and VBOED (1.82), while also showing far tighter variance (SIQR 0.02 vs. 0.50 for VBOED). On the two real-world datasets, FIST leads by a moderate but consistent margin (e.g., 1.93 vs. 2.10 for MI on groundwater; 2.62 vs. 2.82 for VBOED on cell growth), confirming that the advantage persists even when noise is non-i.i.d. and data fidelity is limited.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the PINN framework (Raissi et al., 2019) by repurposing it as both a forward simulator and an inverse solver within a single differentiable pipeline, rather than using it solely for solving a given inverse problem. It departs from prior experimental design methods (Bayesian ED, VBOED, MI-based criteria) that rely on expensive numerical PDE solvers and sequential/adaptive sensor adjustments, by making the entire ED loop end-to-end differentiable and targeting one-shot deployment. The meta-learned shared initialization via REPTILE (Nichol et al., 2018) and the NTK-based convergence estimate (Jacot et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2018) are borrowed from meta-learning and NTK theory respectively, but their combination for ED criterion approximation is novel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PIED addresses a practical bottleneck in PDE-governed inverse problems: the high cost of forward simulations and the infeasibility of sequential sensor repositioning in field experiments. By enabling continuous, gradient-based optimization of sensor placement in a single deployment, it opens the door to more efficient experimental protocols in geophysics, fluid dynamics, and biomedical imaging. Limitations include reliance on the NTK linearized-regime assumption for MoTE, testing on relatively low-dimensional PDEs (1D–2D), and the fact that PINN training quality still governs the ceiling of ED performance. The authors note future work on extending to operator learning and other differentiable physics-informed architectures.
