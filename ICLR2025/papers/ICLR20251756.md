## Problem

Existing retrieval benchmarks (BEIR, MTEB, NQ, MS MARCO) consist of information-seeking queries where keyword or semantic matching is typically sufficient to identify relevant documents. However, many real-world queries—such as finding documentation for a coding bug or locating a theorem that solves a math problem—require in-depth reasoning to connect the query to the relevant document, a capability that current benchmarks do not measure. BRIGHT is introduced as the first text retrieval benchmark that explicitly requires intensive reasoning to determine query-document relevance.

## Input / Output

The input is a long, technical natural-language query (averaging 93–819 tokens depending on domain) paired with a large retrieval corpus of 7,894–413,932 documents per dataset, where documents are web passages, code problem-solution pairs, or formal theorem statements. Relevance is defined not by surface-form overlap but by deeper reasoning connections: a document is positive if it is cited in an accepted StackExchange answer, shares the same algorithm or data structure as a coding problem, documents the required syntax for a rare language, or references the same theorem used in a math solution. The primary output is a ranked document list scored by nDCG@10; a secondary evaluation feeds retrieved documents into a generator (Claude-3.5-sonnet) for end-to-end question answering.

## Method

BRIGHT comprises 12 sub-datasets across three construction paradigms: (1) seven StackExchange domains where annotators select posts with accepted/high-voted answers containing URL links, verify that linked web passages are relevant through unanimous multi-annotator agreement, and collect hard negatives from topically similar but unhelpful Google results; (2) two coding datasets (LeetCode and Pony) where relevance is grounded in shared algorithmic design or required syntax documentation; and (3) three theorem-based datasets (AoPS, TheoremQA-Q, TheoremQA-T) where GPT-4 rephrases theorem-driven questions into applied scenarios and relevance is defined by shared theorems or problem-solving techniques. The paper evaluates 13 retrieval models (BM25, small/large open-source dense encoders and decoders, and four proprietary APIs) and explores three improvement strategies: augmenting queries with LLM-generated chain-of-thought reasoning steps (yielding up to +12.2 nDCG@10), reranking with cross-encoders or LLMs, and continual training on in-domain data to test robustness against pre-training leakage. The best model (Qwen) achieves only 22.5 average nDCG@10, and even with GPT-4 reasoning steps the top score remains below 30, demonstrating that reasoning-intensive retrieval is far from solved.

## Results

On BRIGHT, the best-performing model (gte-Qwen1.5-7B-instruct) achieves only 22.5 nDCG@10 on average across 12 datasets, while the leading MTEB model SFR-Embedding-Mistral scores just 18.3 (vs. 59.0 on MTEB/BEIR). BM25, the sparse baseline, achieves 14.5 nDCG@10, and small dense models (<1B) range from 13.7 (BGE) to 14.9 (SBERT). Incorporating LLM-generated chain-of-thought reasoning steps as queries improves retrieval by up to 12.2 points, yet even the best augmented score remains below 30 nDCG@10.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All 13 evaluated retrievers perform poorly on BRIGHT, with the best model (gte-Qwen1.5-7B-instruct) achieving only 22.5 nDCG@10 on average. Qwen leads on 5 of 12 datasets (Biology, Earth Science, Stack Overflow, AoPS, TheoremQA-Theorems) but is outperformed by GritLM on Pony (22.0 vs. 9.9) and by Voyage on LeetCode (30.6 vs. 25.5). SFR-Embedding-Mistral, the top MTEB/BEIR model (59.0 nDCG@10), drops to 18.3 on BRIGHT, confirming that semantic-similarity-trained retrievers fail on reasoning-intensive queries. BM25 (14.5) is competitive with small dense models (BGE 13.7, Inst-L 14.2, SBERT 14.9), suggesting keyword matching is nearly as effective as learned embeddings for this task. Proprietary models (Cohere 16.6, OpenAI 17.9, Voyage 17.9, Google Gecko 20.0) cluster in the middle, with Gecko (1.2B) outperforming several 7B open-source models. The paper further shows that augmenting queries with GPT-4 or Llama-3-70B reasoning steps improves BM25 the most (up to +12.2 points), while LLM reranking (GPT-4) adds ~3.1 points over BM25 retrieval, yet no combination exceeds 30 nDCG@10.

## Relation to Prior Work

BRIGHT extends the retrieval benchmarking lineage (BEIR, MTEB) by introducing what the authors term 'level 3' retrieval, where query-document relevance requires multi-step reasoning (deductive, analogical, causal, or analytical) rather than surface-form matching. It draws on TheoremQA for theorem-grounded math questions, LeetCode and CodeSearchNet for algorithmic coding problems, and StackExchange for realistic web-based queries, but departs from prior reasoning-retrieval efforts like RAR-b (which uses short multiple-choice options as documents) and BIRCO (limited to reranking over ~100 candidates) by aligning natural user queries with full-length documents from large corpora. The paper also builds on the RAG literature by demonstrating that retrieval quality directly impacts downstream QA, and on chain-of-thought prompting by showing LLM-generated reasoning traces can serve as improved retrieval queries.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dramatic performance collapse (59.0 nDCG@10 on MTEB/BEIR → 18.3 on BRIGHT for the same model) exposes a fundamental gap in current retrieval systems: they cannot perform the reasoning required to connect complex queries to relevant documents. The finding that fine-tuning on all in-domain data yields no improvement suggests the bottleneck is reasoning capability, not knowledge coverage, pointing toward architectures that integrate reasoning into the retrieval process itself. Open questions include how to train retrievers that natively perform multi-step reasoning, whether the 12.2-point gain from LLM reasoning steps can be internalized into the retriever, and how to close the 4.1-point oracle-to-best-retriever gap in downstream QA. Limitations include the subjectivity of relevance annotation (mitigated by unanimous multi-annotator agreement) and the text-only scope.
