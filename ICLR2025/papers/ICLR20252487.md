## Problem

While preference models like Bradley-Terry and Plackett-Luce are central to value alignment (RLHF, DPO), prior work has focused on fitting fixed target distributions without examining how sensitive these models are to small changes in the probabilities they model. This paper asks: how do minor changes in the probability of some preferences propagate to predictions for other preferences, and under what conditions does this sensitivity become large enough to compromise the robustness of value alignment?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20252487/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Theoretically, the analysis takes preference probabilities (or equivalently score differences) as input and produces M-sensitive regions—subsets of the probability simplex where the rate of change of one preference probability w.r.t. another exceeds a threshold M—along with closed-form area formulas for those regions. Empirically, the method consumes synthetic pairwise preference datasets over three options (dog, cat, bird) with controllable dominance (e.g., p₁₂ᴰ = 0.99 fixed, p₂₃ᴰ varied from 0 to 1 in steps of 0.05), trains LLMs via DPO, and outputs learned preference probabilities estimated by querying the model 200 times per pair under different random states.

## Method

The paper derives a general decomposition (Lemma 1) showing that in any pairwise model satisfying symmetry, monotonicity, and smoothness assumptions, p_ij can be written as g(g⁻¹(p_ik) + g⁻¹(p_kj)), enabling sensitivity analysis via the chain rule. Theorem 1 proves that for any M > 0, p_ij is M-sensitive to p_ik whenever p_ik approaches dominance (near 0 or 1), with the sensitivity arising because g′(x) → 0 at infinity while 1/g′(x) → ∞. For the Bradley-Terry model specifically, the authors derive exact M-sensitive region boundaries (Eq. 5) and compute their area in closed form (Proposition 1), showing the regions are non-negligible even for large M. The analysis extends to K-tuple Plackett-Luce models, where Theorem 2 proves that the M-sensitive area strictly decreases as K increases, making longer-tuple models more robust. Empirical validation trains Llama-3-8B-Instruct and zephyr-7b-alpha via DPO on synthetic datasets with dominant preferences, confirming that a 1% deviation in one learned probability can shift an unseen preference probability from ~0.96 to ~0.50.

## Results

The paper proves that for any M > 0, the Bradley-Terry model exhibits M-sensitivity: a 1% deviation in p_ik (from 0.99 to 0.9999 or 0.9801) can shift p_ij from ≈0.96 to ≈0.50 (Example 1). The area of the M-sensitive region for Bradley-Terry is (1/2)ln((M−1)/(M+1)) + (1/(2√M))ln((√M+1)/(√M−1)), while for K-tuple Plackett-Luce it is β²/(6αM²), and Theorem 2 shows the latter is strictly smaller for K > 2, meaning longer tuples are more robust. Experiments with Llama-3-8B-Instruct (DPO, 3 seeds) confirm that minor shifts in training data distribution (p_23^D varying by 0.05) produce near-binary flips in learned p_13^L. On the real-world hh-rlhf test set, 21% of samples (1,795/8,552) have p_wl ≥ 0.99 under Llama-3.1-Nemotron-70B-Reward-HF, confirming dominant preferences are prevalent.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical analysis paper without a direct method-vs-baseline comparison. The key internal comparison is between the Bradley-Terry model (K=2) and the K-tuple Plackett-Luce model (K>2): Theorem 2 proves that for all M > 1, the M-sensitive region area of Bradley-Terry strictly exceeds that of the K-tuple Plackett-Luce model, meaning longer preference tuples are inherently more robust. Empirically, the paper contrasts dominant (p_12^D = 0.99) versus non-dominant (p_12^D = 0.5) training settings on Llama-3-8B-Instruct: with dominant preferences, learned p_13^L exhibits large non-proportional jumps, while with p_12^D = 0.5, p_13^L changes proportionally to p_23^L, confirming that sensitivity is specifically triggered by dominance. The paper also compares two real-world reward models (Llama-3.1-Nemotron-70B vs. DeBERTa-v3-large), finding that the former produces far more extreme probabilities (21% in [0.99,1.00) vs. 2%), making it more susceptible to the identified sensitivity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Bradley-Terry model (Bradley & Terry, 1952) and the Plackett-Luce model (Plackett, 1975; Luce, 1959), which are the standard probabilistic frameworks for modeling pairwise and K-tuple preferences in value alignment. Prior work on these models (RLHF, DPO, and subsequent preference optimization methods) has focused on how to better fit a fixed target preference distribution, but the authors note that 'there has been limited exploration of how changes in the probability of a modelled preference could influence others.' The paper departs from this fitting-centric perspective by introducing a sensitivity analysis framework (M-sensitivity, M-sensitive regions) borrowed from robustness analysis in decision theory, and applies it to characterize exactly when and how preference probabilities become unstable under perturbation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a fundamental trade-off in value alignment: modeling dominant preferences (probabilities near 0 or 1) inherently makes preference models sensitive to small perturbations, which can cause drastically different predictions for unseen preferences. The practical demonstration showing a real reward model ranking a 'good' response below a 'bad' one due to this sensitivity is a concrete safety concern for RLHF/DPO pipelines. Key limitations include the assumption of transitive preferences (excluding non-transitive human preferences), finite option sets, and the gap between idealized preference models and actual LLM behavior. Open questions include whether the sensitivity can be mitigated during training (e.g., via regularization that avoids dominant probabilities), how it interacts with non-transitive preference structures, and whether the K-tuple robustness advantage (Theorem 2) holds in practice given the higher data collection costs of longer tuples.
