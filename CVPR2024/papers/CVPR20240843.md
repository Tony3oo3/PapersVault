## Problem

Non-exemplar lifelong person re-identification suffers from catastrophic forgetting when learning from sequential datasets, and existing solutions either store exemplar images (raising privacy concerns) or impose strict output-consistency distillation (limiting plasticity for new data). Prototype-based approaches borrowed from class-incremental learning either reduce each identity to a single feature point (discarding distribution information) or compute a single statistical distribution over all samples (ignoring instance-level diversity), both of which produce inaccurate prototypes that mislead fine-grained ReID matching.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequential stream of five ReID datasets (Market1501, CUHK-SYSU, DukeMTMC-reID, MSMT17-V2, CUHK03) as RGB person images, with 32 identities × 4 images per mini-batch of 128. A dual-branch ResNet50 predicts, for each input image, a d-dimensional feature center vector and a d-dimensional diagonal variance vector. At inference, only the predicted center vector is used for person matching, while the learned per-identity multivariate Gaussian prototypes (mean + diagonal variance) are stored between training steps to guide subsequent learning.

## Method

DKP builds a dual-branch CNN where one branch predicts per-instance distribution centers and the other predicts per-instance variances, then applies three modules: (1) Instance-level Distribution Modeling (IDM) samples m feature candidates from each instance's predicted Gaussian and enforces identity consistency via a distribution-aware cross-entropy loss and a distribution-oriented triplet loss that selects hardest positives/negatives from the sampled set; (2) Distribution-oriented Prototype Generation (DPG) aggregates all instance-level Gaussians within an identity into a single identity-level Gaussian prototype using closed-form expressions that incorporate both instance centers and variances, yielding more accurate distribution boundaries than mean-only statistics; (3) Prototype-based Knowledge Transfer (PKT) samples features from the previous step's prototypes, computes a prototype-aware coordinate matrix via softmax-scaled dot products, and minimizes KL divergence between the prototype-conditioned inter-instance affinity and the raw feature affinity to simultaneously preserve old knowledge and acquire new data. A post-training model fusion step averages the new and old model parameters to further consolidate knowledge.

## Results

On the LReID benchmark with Training Order-1 (Market-1501 → CUHK-SYSU → DukeMTMC → MSMT17 → CUHK03), DKP achieves 51.8% mAP and 64.1% R@1 on seen domains (Seen-Avg), and 59.2% mAP and 51.6% R@1 on unseen domains (Unseen-Avg). This represents an 8.1%/10.4% mAP/R@1 improvement over the strongest LReID baseline PatchKD on seen domains, and a 10.1%/6.2% improvement on unseen domains. The method also outperforms the best CIL baseline PRAKA by 10.5%/9.9% mAP/R@1 on seen domains across both training orders.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DKP outperforms all four LReID baselines (CRL, AKA, PatchKD, MEGE) on average seen and unseen domain performance, with the largest gap over PatchKD (the strongest LReID method): +8.1% mAP and +10.4% R@1 on Seen-Avg, and +10.1% mAP and +6.2% R@1 on Unseen-Avg. Against CIL baselines, DKP surpasses PRAKA (the best CIL method) by 10.5% mAP and 9.9% R@1 on Seen-Avg, and by 11.5% mAP and 9.0% R@1 on Unseen-Avg. However, DKP does not win on every individual dataset: PatchKD achieves higher mAP/R@1 on the first dataset (Market-1501: 68.5/85.7 vs. 60.3/80.6) due to its strict knowledge distillation constraint, and PRAKA outperforms DKP on the last dataset (CUHK03: 54.0/55.6 vs. 43.6/44.2). The paper attributes these per-dataset losses to the trade-off between anti-forgetting (favored by strict distillation) and plasticity for new data (favored by DKP's looser prototype-based transfer). On the second training order (Table 2), DKP maintains its lead with 51.3% mAP / 63.2% R@1 Seen-Avg and 59.0% / 51.6% Unseen-Avg, confirming robustness to dataset ordering.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends prototype-based continual learning from class-incremental learning (CIL) to lifelong person re-identification (LReID), departing from both exemplar-based LReID methods (which raise privacy concerns) and strict knowledge-distillation LReID methods (which limit plasticity). Conceptually, it builds on the statistical-distribution prototype of Zhu et al. (CVPR 2021) and the feature-uncertainty modeling of Yu et al. (ICCV 2019), but introduces a key shift: rather than computing a single class-level distribution over all samples, it models a per-instance Gaussian distribution and then aggregates these into a distribution-aware identity prototype. This instance-level granularity is motivated by the large intra-person variance in ReID (due to temporal/environmental changes) that class-level statistics would average away.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 8.1%/9.1% average mAP/R@1 improvement over the prior best LReID method (PatchKD) is substantial and demonstrates that distribution-aware prototyping is a viable non-exemplar alternative to both rehearsal and strict distillation. However, the paper acknowledges that performance on the first and last datasets still lags behind specialized baselines, and that prototypes from steps older than the immediately preceding one become outdated and are discarded—suggesting a potential avenue for multi-step prototype fusion. The broader implication is that fine-grained tasks like ReID benefit from preserving per-instance diversity in knowledge representations, a principle that could transfer to other fine-grained continual learning settings.
