## Problem

Existing speech-driven 3D facial animation methods learn a deterministic one-to-one mapping from audio to mesh, but the true relationship is one-to-many with both inter-speaker and intra-speaker variation. This is compounded by the lack of sufficiently large multi-speaker datasets and the absence of evaluation metrics that account for the multi-modal nature of the conditional distribution, making it impossible to train or fairly assess probabilistic models.

## Input / Output

The model takes a driving speech signal y ∈ R^{T×D_y} and a reference speaker 3D mesh sequence s ∈ R^{T_s×3V} (providing inter-speaker style control) as input, and produces a sequence of 3D mesh deformations x ∈ R^{T×3V} as output. The reference clip is not a full utterance but a short style exemplar; during inference, multiple samples can be drawn from the conditional distribution and aggregated (via KNN, averaging, or SyncNet rejection) to trade diversity for lip precision. The method is benchmarked on meshes reconstructed from VoxCeleb2 videos using DECA and SPECTRE, spanning over 1M sequences and 6,112 speakers.

## Method

The core architecture is a two-stage probabilistic autoregressive model over residual vector-quantized (RVQ) codes of 3D facial motion. A temporal transformer produces a per-frame audio-visual embedding from the speech signal and previously decoded codes, while a depth transformer (masked self-attention) then generates the D RVQ code indices for each frame autoregressively, with the encoded reference speaker style injected as the first token of the depth sequence rather than the temporal sequence—a design choice shown to be critical for maintaining speech synchronization. The model is trained end-to-end with cross-entropy loss under teacher forcing. At inference, three sampling strategies (KNN-based local Gaussian approximation, code averaging, and SyncNet-based rejection sampling) allow controlled trade-offs between diversity and lip fidelity, and a knowledge distillation step amortizes the multi-sample overhead into a single-pass student network.

## Results

On the proposed VoxCeleb2(Mesh) benchmark, the method achieves a style cosine similarity of 0.707 (DECA) and 0.673 (SPECTRE) with a style rank of 7.3 and 20.5 respectively, substantially outperforming FaceFormer+Style (0.127/0.237, rank 1596/650) and MeshTalk+Style (0.229/0.231, rank 1135/955). In the downstream noisy AVSR task on LRS3, synthetic meshes from the method yield a WER of 7.1%, improving relative WER by 11.3% over training on ground-truth lip meshes (8.0%) and by 47.0% over FaceFormer-generated meshes (13.4%). In perceptual studies, the method is preferred over VOCA for style matching (85.1%), lip realism (70.2%), and upper-face realism (80.9%), and over FaceFormer+Style for style matching (75.0%), lip realism (86.1%), and upper-face realism (94.4%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against VOCA and FaceFormer (deterministic regression baselines), the proposed method wins decisively on realism/diversity (lower averaged FD score), speech synchronization (higher SyncNet score), and style matching (cosine similarity 0.707 vs. 0.127 for FaceFormer+Style on DECA), while losing on raw lip vertex error due to the inherent diversity of probabilistic sampling; however, on the proposed coverage and mean-estimate error metrics, the method outperforms both. Against MeshTalk (which uses a Gumbel-Softmax autoencoder with a regression bottleneck on the lower face), the method achieves higher sync scores and lower FD scores, and its MeshTalk-ND variant (without regression) is more diverse but suffers degraded lip articulation quality and cannot cover ground-truth lip sequences as well. Against CodeTalker, the method is preferred in perceptual evaluation on all three axes (style matching 78.8%, lip realism 87.9%, upper-face realism 90.9%). The key differentiator is that the two-stage AR design with speaker conditioning in the depth model (rather than the temporal model) is critical for maintaining synchronization, as ablation in Table 3 shows a drop from 0.463 to 0.287 sync score when style is moved to the first stage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the residual vector-quantized (RVQ) two-stage autoregressive framework from Lee et al. (CVPR 2022, originally for image generation) to the domain of speech-driven 3D facial animation, replacing the deterministic regression formulations used by VOCA, FaceFormer, CodeTalker, and MeshTalk with a full conditional distribution model p(x|y,s). The key conceptual departure is treating the speech-to-facial-motion mapping as inherently one-to-many rather than one-to-one, which prior methods implicitly assumed by regressing vertices or codes directly from audio. The paper also carves new ground on the evaluation side by introducing large-scale VoxCeleb2-derived mesh benchmarks (6,112 speakers vs. 12–14 in prior work) and probabilistic-appropriate metrics (coverage error, SyncNet-FD, Style-FD) that existing lip-vertex-error-based evaluation cannot capture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The large-scale benchmarks and probabilistic metrics address a genuine gap: prior small datasets and deterministic metrics made it impossible to properly evaluate whether a model captures the full diversity of real facial motion, and this paper provides the infrastructure for that evaluation. The finding that synthetic meshes from the probabilistic model outperform ground-truth meshes for noisy AVSR training (11.3% relative WER improvement) suggests that diversity in synthetic data can be more valuable than pixel-level fidelity for downstream audio-visual tasks. Open questions include how the autoregressive approach compares to the concurrent diffusion-based methods (FaceDiffuser, Diff-PoseTalk), whether the monocular reconstruction noise in the benchmarks limits ceiling performance, and whether the sampling strategies (KNN, code averaging, SyncNet rejection) generalize to other conditioning signals beyond speaker style.
