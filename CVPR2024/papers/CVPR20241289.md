## Problem

Previous infrared physical adversarial attacks focused on 2D pedestrian detection or small model cars with limited viewing angles (e.g., ±30° horizontal, 0° pitch), leaving a critical gap for full-angle attacks on real vehicles. Real cars present unique challenges: uneven temperature distributions (hot engine vs. cooler body), complex 3D geometry, and the need to design adversarial patterns across the entire vehicle surface. This paper addresses the absence of any infrared attack demonstrated on real cars at full 360° horizontal and 0–90° pitch angles.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a 3D car mesh (Mercedes-Benz A200L), real infrared photos captured with a FLIR T560 camera to build a texture map, and a target object detector (Faster R-CNN fine-tuned on FLIR_ADAS_1_3). It produces N=5 two-dimensional adversarial shadow patterns, each rendered as an ultra-thin (0.08 mm) aluminum film sticker placed on flat car surfaces (doors, roof, hood, rear). The aluminum film works by modifying surface emissivity (~0.1) rather than altering temperature, making it a passive, easily attachable physical perturbation costing ~$0.2 per sticker.

## Method

The core contribution is the Mesh Shadow Attack (MSA): instead of directly optimizing a 2D adversarial pattern, the method optimizes N 3D adversarial meshes and projects their rendered shadows (dark regions with uniform grayscale matching the aluminum film) onto the car texture map. To keep the resulting 2D patterns manufacturable, a 3D control-points-based smoothing algorithm parameterizes mesh vertex offsets as weighted averages of a smaller set of anchor control points, and four smoothness losses (normal consistency, edge-length regularization, chamfer distance to a sphere, and Laplacian smoothing) are added to the detection loss. The full pipeline is differentiable end-to-end via PyTorch3D rendering, with EOT-style random perturbations (vertex jitter, pattern noise, grayscale shifts, position offsets) for robustness. Optimization is performed by backpropagating the target detector's object-confidence loss through the rendered adversarial car images, updating control points, pasting positions, and shadowing angles simultaneously.

## Results

On the FLIR_ADAS_1_3 dataset, the proposed 3D mesh shadow attack achieves a 96.31% attack success rate (ASR) against Faster R-CNN in the digital world, compared to only 2.10% for the clean car and 18.26% for random-shape stickers. In physical experiments on two real Mercedes-Benz A200L cars, the adversarial aluminum stickers achieve a 91.49% ASR (engines running) and 88.42% (engines off), versus 6.21% and 0.66% for random and no stickers respectively. Against six unseen detectors in a black-box setting, the method maintains ASRs between 73.35% (Cascade R-CNN) and 95.80% (SSD), demonstrating strong cross-architecture transferability including to transformer-based Deformable DETR (83.83%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two prior infrared physical attack methods: AIP [32] (points-clustering-based patch) and UAP [31] (boundary-limited shape optimization). Both baselines were originally designed for 2D model-car attacks and are extended here to the 3D real-car setting. The proposed method decisively outperforms both: on Faster R-CNN, it achieves 96.31% ASR versus 14.97% (AIP) and 20.06% (UAP) — a gap of over 75 percentage points. This advantage holds across all six unseen detectors; for example, on YOLOv3 the proposed method reaches 86.52% while AIP and UAP achieve only 38.32% and 39.82% respectively. The authors attribute the large margin to the 3D mesh shadow approach exploring a higher-dimensional solution space without the geometric constraints (e.g., non-overlapping boundaries, single-piece patterns) that limit the 2D baselines. Even the simple random-shape baseline (18.26% on Faster R-CNN) is far below the proposed method, confirming that the attack effectiveness comes from the optimized adversarial pattern rather than mere surface modification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of infrared physical adversarial attacks (Zhu et al. 2021/2022; Wei et al. 2023) that previously targeted pedestrians or small model cars with limited viewing angles. The key conceptual departure is the 3D mesh shadow attack (MSA): rather than directly optimizing a 2D adversarial pattern (as in AIP or UAP), the authors optimize a 3D adversarial mesh and project its shadow to obtain the 2D sticker pattern, effectively searching a higher-dimensional space. The physical implementation also departs from prior temperature-modification approaches by using ultra-thin aluminum film to alter surface emissivity instead, making the stickers practical and inexpensive.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As infrared cameras become standard in autonomous driving stacks, demonstrating that a $0.2 aluminum sticker can hide a real car from multiple detectors (including transformer-based ones) at 91.49% ASR raises urgent safety concerns for the field. The 3D mesh shadow technique is potentially generalizable to other 3D physical adversarial tasks beyond vehicles. However, the study is limited to a single car model (Mercedes-Benz A200L), one weather condition, and a hiding-only objective; open questions include whether the approach transfers to different vehicle geometries, dynamic driving scenarios, and whether effective defenses exist beyond the mild ones tested.
