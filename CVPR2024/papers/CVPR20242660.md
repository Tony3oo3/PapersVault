## Problem

Neural field training (e.g., NeRF, image fitting) typically constructs training batches via uniform sampling, which wastes compute on already-learned smooth regions and leads to slow convergence. Existing alternatives—edge-based heuristics or quadtree subdivision—are handcrafted for specific tasks, do not generalize across neural field applications, and yield only marginal gains on real-world data. The paper asks whether a principled, architecture-agnostic sampling strategy can focus training on high-error regions to substantially accelerate convergence.

## Input / Output

For 2D image fitting, the method takes a single high-resolution RGB image (up to 8000×8000) and produces a trained neural field that reconstructs it; for NeRF, it takes 8 multi-view scene images (object-centric synthetic or forward-facing real-world LLFF) and produces a trained radiance field for novel-view rendering. The method does not alter the network architecture, loss function, or rendering pipeline—it only changes which pixels or rays are selected for each mini-batch, with the sole added computation being an LMC update step and a backward pass through the final layer to obtain gradients with respect to input coordinates.

## Method

The paper reformulates neural field training as importance sampling, defining an importance distribution Q(x) proportional to the L1 norm of the prediction error at each coordinate, so that high-error regions are sampled more frequently. The key insight is 'soft mining': rather than pure hard mining (which biases the objective and degrades performance) or pure importance sampling (which is suboptimal), the loss is reweighted by 1/Q(x)^α with a stop-gradient on Q(x), where α ∈ [0,1] interpolates between the two extremes (typically α = 0.6–0.8). To sample from Q(x) without an expensive full forward pass, the authors use Langevin Monte Carlo, whose update rule depends only on the local gradient of log Q(x)—already available from the training backward pass—plus Gaussian noise, adding less than 4% overhead. Practical details include a linear warm-up of α from 0 over the first 1k iterations, re-initialization of stuck or out-of-domain samples using uniform and edge-based distributions, and retaining 10% of each batch as uniformly sampled to avoid ignoring any region entirely.

## Results

On the NeRF Synthetic dataset, the proposed soft mining method achieves 28.57 PSNR / 0.93 SSIM / 0.12 LPIPS at 1k iterations, compared to 26.50 / 0.90 / 0.15 for uniform sampling and 27.24 / 0.90 / 0.17 for Edge [14]. On the LLFF dataset, it reaches 24.04 PSNR at 1k iterations versus 21.00 (uniform) and 21.13 (Edge). In terms of convergence speed, the method more than doubles the rate compared to uniform sampling (e.g., ~2,400 vs ~8,500 iterations to reach 30 dB on NeRF Synthetic) and approximately doubles that of Edge [14]. For 2D image fitting, convergence to 35 dB PSNR is ~4x faster than uniform sampling (~2,400 vs ~10,600 iterations), with only a <4% increase in per-iteration compute time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: uniform sampling (the standard NeRF training strategy) and Edge [14] (a heuristic that allocates more rays to detected image edges). Soft mining consistently outperforms both baselines across all scenes and iteration counts on both the NeRF Synthetic and LLFF datasets. At 1k iterations, the gap is most pronounced: on LLFF, the method achieves 24.04 PSNR versus 21.00 (uniform) and 21.13 (Edge), a ~3 dB improvement; on NeRF Synthetic, it reaches 28.57 versus 26.50 and 27.24, a ~2 dB improvement. The Edge [14] baseline shows stronger gains on the synthetic dataset (object-centric, flat white background) but provides only marginal improvement on the more textured LLFF scenes, whereas soft mining improves convergence regardless of data type. In terms of convergence speed, soft mining requires less than half the iterations of uniform sampling to reach target PSNR thresholds (e.g., ~1,700 vs ~3,800 iterations for 25 dB on LLFF), and approximately doubles the convergence speed of Edge [14]. The authors also note that Edge [14]'s originally reported ~1 dB average improvement is not reproduced under their NerfAcc implementation, where uniform sampling already achieves competitive results on several scenes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the hard/soft mining concept from object detection [33,34] and metric learning [42] to the neural field training setting, reformulating batch construction as importance sampling with a stop-gradient relaxation. It departs from prior NeRF-specific heuristics (edge-based ray allocation [14], quadtree subdivision [51]) by proposing an architecture-agnostic, principled sampling strategy. The implementation leverages Langevin Monte Carlo from the Bayesian optimization literature [5,26] to sample from the importance distribution Q(x) with minimal overhead, avoiding the intractable forward pass over all data points that multinomial sampling would require.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The >2× convergence speedup with <4% compute overhead is practically significant for any neural field workload, and the architecture-agnostic framing means the technique can be dropped into existing pipelines (NeRF, Instant-NGP, 3D Gaussian Splatting) without modification. A key limitation is that the method relies solely on the training loss to define Q(x), which may not align with application-specific quality metrics (e.g., novel-view rendering quality in NeRF); the authors note that alternative distributions (ray uncertainty, active learning) could be plugged in. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to 4D light fields or physics simulation, and whether the LMC approximation gap to ideal multinomial sampling widens in higher-dimensional or more complex domains.
