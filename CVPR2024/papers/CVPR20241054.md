## Problem

Reconstructing 3D shape, material, and illumination from unconstrained in-the-wild image collections is severely underdetermined because camera poses are unknown, backgrounds vary, and COLMAP-based pose estimation fails on more than half of such scenes. Existing methods that jointly optimize cameras and geometry either require near-perfect pose initialization, run for over 12 hours per object, or produce overly smooth reconstructions when starting from coarse poses. The core technical obstacle is that multi-resolution hash grids, which enable fast optimization, backpropagate noisy and discontinuous gradients with respect to input positions, destabilizing the already ill-posed camera pose estimation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a collection of RGB images of the same object captured in diverse environments with varying resolutions, backgrounds, and illuminations, along with only rough quadrant-level camera pose annotations (not full 6-DoF poses) and optionally imperfect foreground masks. It outputs a neural field encoding shape (volume density), Cook-Torrance BRDF parameters (basecolor, metallic, roughness), per-view 128-dimensional latent illumination embeddings, and refined camera extrinsics/intrinsics. From this representation, users can extract novel view renderings, relight the object under arbitrary illumination, edit material properties, and extract a 3D mesh for integration into standard graphics pipelines.

## Method

SHINOBI replaces the MLP-based volume representation with a hybrid encoding that concatenates a multi-resolution hash grid H(x) with a Fourier feature embedding γ(x), both subject to coarse-to-fine resolution annealing, which regularizes low-frequency gradient flow and stabilizes joint camera-shape optimization. A camera multiplex maintains multiple jittered pose proposals per image, and a novel projection-based consistency loss projects rendered points from all proposals into the best-ranked camera to enforce geometric coherence across the multiplex. Per-view importance weighting dynamically down-weights misaligned cameras in both the network and camera losses using a running loss buffer, while multi-scale patch-based Charbonnier losses (16×16 to 32×32) and a silhouette XOR loss constrain local alignment. The BRDF decomposition follows Neural-PIL with a per-image latent illumination embedding, and the full system is optimized with three separate ADAM optimizers (networks, hash grid embeddings, cameras) under a carefully scheduled curriculum that progressively increases render resolution, fades from direct color to BRDF optimization, and reduces the multiplex size.

## Results

On the NAVI in-the-wild dataset with GT poses, SHINOBI achieves 27.69 PSNR and 0.0607 LPIPS on the COLMAP-successful subset (S_C), outperforming SAMURAI (25.34 PSNR, 0.0958 LPIPS) by ~2.3 dB in PSNR and ~36% lower LPIPS, while running 3× faster (4 h vs. 12 h on a single GPU). In the more challenging joint shape-and-pose setting (Table 2), SHINOBI matches SAMURAI on PSNR (25.15 vs. 25.34) but achieves lower pose errors (22.84° vs. 26.16° mean rotation) and better perceptual quality (0.090 vs. 0.096 LPIPS). SHINOBI also substantially outperforms NeRS (18.67 PSNR) and NeROIC (19.77 PSNR, which fails entirely on the COLMAP-failure subset).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SAMURAI (the closest prior work and direct baseline), SHINOBI wins on PSNR (+2.35 dB on S_C), SSIM (+0.02), and LPIPS (−0.035) in the GT-pose setting, while cutting runtime from 12 h to 4 h on a single GPU. In the joint pose-and-shape setting, SHINOBI is on-par with SAMURAI on PSNR (25.15 vs. 25.34) but achieves consistently lower rotation error (22.84° vs. 26.16°) and translation error (0.250 vs. 0.24, with lower std), yielding better LPIPS (0.090 vs. 0.096). Against NeROIC, SHINOBI wins on every metric and, critically, does not depend on COLMAP initialization—NeROIC reports no results on the COLMAP-failure subset (~S_C). Against NeRS, SHINOBI gains ~9.7 dB PSNR and ~0.018 LPIPS improvement, though NeRS is slightly faster (3 h vs. 4 h). GNeRF, included as a reference, is far behind on all metrics (8.30 PSNR, 93° rotation error), confirming it is not competitive for multi-illumination in-the-wild data.

## Relation to Prior Work

SHINOBI is a direct architectural successor to SAMURAI (Boss et al., NeurIPS 2022), retaining its skeleton (Neural-PIL renderer, Cook-Torrance BRDF decomposition, camera multiplex) while replacing the MLP-based NeRF volume with a hybrid multi-resolution hash grid encoding combined with Fourier features. The key departure addresses a known limitation of Instant-NGP-style hash grids: their discontinuous gradients destabilize joint camera pose optimization. Prior attempts to fix this (BAA-NGP, CAMP, Heo et al.) remained sensitive to initialization quality, whereas SHINOBI adds a projection-based multiplex consistency loss, per-view importance weighting, and patch-based alignment losses to make the optimization robust to very coarse quadrant-based pose initialization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 3× speedup over SAMURAI (4h vs 12h on a single GPU) while improving reconstruction quality makes in-the-wild object capture more practical for AR/VR, gaming, and e-commerce pipelines where turnaround time matters. However, the method still fails on symmetric objects, highly specular materials, and thin/transparent structures, and cannot model shadows or inter-reflections—limitations shared by all current single-object inverse rendering approaches. Extending the decomposition to handle complex light transport and more diverse material models remains an open direction.
