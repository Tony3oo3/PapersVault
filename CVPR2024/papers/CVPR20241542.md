## Problem

Existing medical VQA benchmarks are too small (<5K images), cover only 3–5 imaging modalities, and focus on a narrow set of anatomical regions, making it impossible to comprehensively evaluate Large Vision-Language Models on real-world medical images. This gap has left the community without a reliable measure of whether medical-specialized LVLMs truly outperform general-purpose ones across the full breadth of clinical imaging.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241542/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation item pairs a real medical image (from one of 12 modalities including CT, MRI, X-Ray, fundus photography, OCT, histopathology, etc.) with a natural-language question and 2–4 candidate answer options. The model must select the correct option; correctness is scored via two complementary metrics: a Question-answering Score (free-text generation matched to the nearest option by similarity) and a Prefix-based Score (log-likelihood of each option conditioned on the image-text prefix), the latter probing inherent knowledge independent of instruction-following ability.

## Method

OmniMedVQA is constructed by collecting 73 existing medical classification datasets (118,010 images, 127,995 QA items) and converting their classification labels into VQA format through a four-step pipeline: (1) designing question templates per dataset that target five capability axes—modality recognition, anatomy identification, disease diagnosis, lesion grading, and other biological attributes; (2) applying Inverse Proportional Sampling so that templates with more associated images receive a smaller sample ratio, preventing category bias; (3) using GPT-3.5 to paraphrase each question for linguistic diversity and to generate plausible incorrect distractors, yielding multi-choice items; and (4) performing human double-checking for validity. The benchmark is then used for zero-shot evaluation of 12 LVLMs (8 general-domain, 4 medical-specialized) under both scoring metrics, revealing that general-purpose models like BLIP-2 and InstructBLIP outperform all medical-specialized counterparts on average, and that medical models only gain an edge on modalities far from general-image distributions (e.g., CT, MRI).

## Results

On OmniMedVQA (118,010 images, 127,995 QA items across 12 modalities and 20+ anatomical regions), BLIP-2 achieves the best overall Question-answering Score of 50.69% and Prefix-based Score of 33.43%, substantially outperforming all four medical-specialized LVLMs. Among medical models, MedVInT leads with 41.50% (QA) / 25.81% (Prefix), followed by Med-Flamingo at 36.17% / 26.54%, while LLaVA-Med and RadFM trail at 28.78% and 26.82% respectively. All models remain close to the random-guess baseline of 28.28%, indicating that OmniMedVQA is extremely challenging for current LVLMs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The central finding is that the general-domain BLIP-2 (50.69% QA) outperforms every medical-specialized LVLM by a wide margin, beating the best medical model MedVInT (41.50%) by over 9 points and LLaVA-Med (28.78%) by more than 21 points. InstructBLIP (42.49%) also surpasses all four medical models on the QA score, though MedVInT edges it out on the Prefix-based Score (25.81% vs. 28.71% — actually InstructBLIP leads here too at 28.71%). Among medical-specialized models, MedVInT and Med-Flamingo are the strongest, benefiting from extensive medical pre-training (381K image-caption pairs and 4K+ textbooks, respectively), while LLaVA-Med — which fine-tunes LLaVA with only 60K GPT-4-generated instructions — delivers the worst medical-model performance (28.78%), barely above random guess. RadFM, despite being trained on 19M radiologic pairs, achieves the lowest overall QA score (26.82%), though it excels specifically on CT (45.47% Prefix) and X-Ray (55.21% Prefix) modalities. The paper concludes that medical instruction tuning on limited data (as in LLaVA-Med) is insufficient, and that a robust medical-domain image-text alignment model (analogous to BLIP-2's role in the general domain) is the critical missing component.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the medical VQA evaluation landscape by building on the prefix-based scoring methodology from LVLM-EHUB [120] and the BLIP-2 evaluation framework [73], while departing from prior medical VQA datasets (VQA-RAD, SLAKE, Path-VQA, VQA-Med) that were limited to fewer than 5K images, 3–5 modalities, and a narrow set of anatomical regions. Conceptually, it repurposes the general-domain LVLM evaluation paradigm—zero-shot multiple-choice VQA with dual scoring metrics—into the medical domain, and positions itself as the first comprehensive benchmark spanning 12 modalities and 20+ anatomical regions. The construction pipeline (converting classification datasets into VQA via GPT-3.5 templating) is a practical departure from prior medical VQA datasets that relied on manually authored or paper-extracted question-answer pairs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark's scale (118K images, 128K QA items) and modality/anatomy coverage make it the most comprehensive evaluation resource for medical LVLMs, and it is likely to become a standard reference for the field. The finding that medical-specialized models underperform general-domain models (especially BLIP-2) challenges the assumption that domain-specific pre-training automatically transfers to broader medical understanding, pointing to a critical gap in medical image-text alignment. Key open questions include: whether the relatively simple classification-derived questions underestimate model capability, how to obtain the massive high-quality medical captioning data the authors argue is necessary, and whether the gap between medical and general LVLMs narrows with more diverse training data across all 12 modalities.
