## Problem

Existing LVLM benchmarks primarily evaluate object hallucination on limited topics and visual formats, offering no systematic way to distinguish between language hallucination (relying on parametric memory over visual context) and visual illusion (misinterpreting the actual image). There is also no quantitative diagnostic framework to measure response bias, logical consistency, or the specific failure mode behind each incorrect prediction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240879/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation sample consists of a Yes/No question paired with one of three visual conditions: no image (empty), an original internet-sourced image, or a human-edited variant (flipped, masked, color-changed, order-reversed, etc.). The benchmark's defining structural element is the control pair: every question is answered under two conditions (e.g., original vs. edited image, or with vs. without visual input), and the differential response is what enables failure-type diagnosis. Outputs include four accuracy metrics (qAcc, fAcc, Easy aAcc, Hard aAcc), a Yes/No bias score, a consistency score, and a three-way failure classification (language hallucination, visual illusion, mixed).

## Method

HALLUSIONBENCH is built around 455 visual-question control pairs spanning 346 images and 1129 handcrafted Yes/No questions across topics like math, geometry, charts, maps, illusions, and video sequences. Questions are taxonomized into Visual Dependent (unanswerable without the image) and Visual Supplement (answerable from parametric memory, where the image provides correction or dense information). The diagnostic test applies a decision tree over each control pair: if the model answers correctly on the original but incorrectly on the edited image with the same response, the failure is attributed to language hallucination; if it fails on both with visual input, it is visual illusion; VS questions without visual input that yield a confident wrong answer are also language hallucination. Three progressive analytical tests—Yes/No Bias Test (Pct. Diff and FP Ratio), Consistency Test (fAcc over logically correlated question sets), and the Diagnostic Test—are applied in order, with later tests being informative only if earlier ones pass. Evaluation is performed via GPT-4-assisted judging (temperature 0, 3 runs averaged), validated against human annotation.

## Results

On HALLUSIONBENCH (1129 questions across 346 images), GPT-4V achieves the highest Question Pair Accuracy at 31.42% (human eval) / 28.79% (GPT-4-assisted), while all other 14 evaluated LVLMs score below 16% qAcc. Claude 3 ranks second at 21.76% qAcc, followed by BLIP2-T5 at 15.16%. Notably, several open-source models (Qwen-VL at 5.93%, MiniGPT4 at 8.79%, BLIP2 at 5.05%) perform worse than the Random Chance baseline (15.60% qAcc), indicating their visual reasoning is below chance level on this diagnostic benchmark.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4V dominates the benchmark with 28.79% qAcc, nearly 7 points above the second-best Claude 3 (21.76%) and roughly 13 points above BLIP2-T5 (15.16%). Among open-source models, BLIP2-T5 (15.16%) and mPLUG-Owl-v2 (13.85%) lead, while LLaVA-1.5 (10.55%) and InstructBLIP (9.45%) trail despite their 13B and 8.2B parameter counts respectively. Gemini Pro Vision (7.69%), Qwen-VL (5.93%), and BLIP2 (5.05%) all fall below the Random Chance baseline (15.60%), suggesting their visual reasoning on edited/nuanced images is effectively at chance. The diagnostic analysis (Table 3) reveals that LLaVA-1.5 and mPLUG-Owl-v1 exhibit strong Yes-bias (FP Ratio 0.75 and 0.79), while GPT-4V's failures are split more evenly between Language Hallucination (22.19%) and Visual Illusion (45.66%), indicating its primary weakness is misreading edited visual content rather than relying on parametric priors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in LVLM evaluation by introducing a control-pair diagnostic structure that no prior benchmark employs. While existing hallucination benchmarks (POPE, M-HalDetect, GAVIE) focus narrowly on object hallucination, and broader evaluation suites (MME, MMBench, MM-Vet) assess general multimodal competence, HALLUSIONBENCH is the first to systematically disentangle language hallucination from visual illusion through paired original/edited images and with/without-visual-input comparisons. The control-pair design repurposes the concept of ablation from model architecture research into an evaluation protocol, enabling quantitative attribution of failures to specific cognitive modes (parametric memory vs. visual perception) rather than reporting only aggregate accuracy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even GPT-4V achieves only 31.42% question-pair accuracy and that several open-source models perform at or below random chance, exposing a substantial gap between LVLMs' impressive demo-level performance and their actual visual reasoning reliability. The diagnostic framework (Yes/No bias, consistency, and failure-type classification) provides a more actionable signal for model developers than raw accuracy, pointing toward specific failure modes like overreliance on parametric memory and inability to track temporal relations across image sequences. Open questions include whether the control-pair structure generalizes beyond Yes/No questions, how to close the gap between parametric knowledge and visual grounding, and whether the GPT-4-assisted evaluation introduces systematic biases that could mask or inflate certain failure patterns.
