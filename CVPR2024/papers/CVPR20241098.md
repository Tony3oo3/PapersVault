## Problem

Existing self-supervised pre-training methods like MAE only pre-train the vision encoder, forcing a task-specific decoder to be trained from scratch for each downstream task (detection, segmentation, pose, depth). This creates a large pretrain-finetune architectural gap that prevents the decoder from benefiting from large-scale pre-training and demands substantial task-specific data to reach competitive performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241098/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes RGB images (192×192 during pre-training, 640×640 for segmentation fine-tuning) and produces task-specific outputs through a unified query-to-answer interface: bounding boxes with class labels for detection, C-dimensional mask embeddings with class labels for segmentation, per-keypoint heatmap feature vectors for pose, and adaptive bin lengths/embeddings for depth. During pre-training, 75% of patches are randomly masked and the decoder reconstructs their pixel values; during fine-tuning, the same queries (initialized from the pre-trained [CLS] token plus zero-initialized learnable embeddings) produce the task-specific predictions through a swapped linear head.

## Method

GLID reformulates both the MIM pretext task and all downstream tasks as query-to-answer problems within a single encoder-decoder architecture (Swin Transformer + Bi-FPN + 6-layer query-based transformer decoder with 512-dim hidden states). During pre-training, mask tokens with positional embeddings serve as queries alongside a [CLS] token for global context, and the decoder reconstructs masked patch pixel values via MSE loss. For fine-tuning, the entire pre-trained encoder-decoder and query mechanism are retained; only the topmost linear transformation layer is replaced with a task-specific linear head (e.g., bbox+class for detection, mask embedding+class for segmentation, bin length+embedding for depth). This design minimizes the pretrain-finetune architectural gap to a single linear layer, allowing the model to inherit nearly all pre-trained weights and achieve strong performance with less task-specific data compared to methods that discard the pre-trained decoder.

## Results

GLID (Swin-B, pre-trained on ImageNet-1K only) achieves 76.7 APkp and 51.2 APbox on COCO, 52.7 mIoU / 30.9 mAP / 44.7 PQ on ADE20K, and 0.085 REL / 0.293 RMSE on NYUv2. Scaling to Swin-L further improves all metrics to 78.5 APkp, 52.4 APbox, 53.9 mIoU, 32.5 mAP, 45.7 PQ, 0.081 REL, and 0.287 RMSE. In the pre-training ablation (Table 2), GLID outperforms supervised pre-training by an average of 3.8 mIoU on ADE20K segmentation and by 4.6/6.4 points on COCO APkp/APbox, while also surpassing self-supervised baselines (MAE, MixMAE, SimMIM, EsViT) across all six tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against specialist models, GLID (Swin-B) outperforms Mask2Former (Swin-B) on all three ADE20K segmentation metrics (52.7 vs 52.4 mIoU, 30.9 vs 30.7 mAP, 44.7 vs 44.3 PQ), surpasses DETR by 6.3 APbox (51.2 vs 44.9) and Deformable-DETR by 4.3 APbox (51.2 vs 46.9) on COCO, and beats BinsFormer (Swin-L, pre-trained on ImageNet-21K) on NYUv2 depth estimation (0.081 vs 0.094 REL, 0.287 vs 0.330 RMSE). GLID (Swin-L) also edges out ViTPose (ViT-L) on COCO pose estimation (78.5 vs 78.3 APkp). Among generalist baselines, GLID improves over Pix2seq v2 by 10.5 APkp and 5.9 APbox on COCO, and over Painter by 4.6 APkp and 2.8 mIoU, though Painter retains a slight edge on NYUv2 depth (0.080 vs 0.085 REL, 0.288 vs 0.293 RMSE). In the pre-training comparison (Table 2), GLID dominates all self-supervised baselines: it leads MixMAE by 0.9 APkp and 3.7 APbox on COCO, and by 3.0 mIoU / 1.2 mAP / 1.7 PQ on ADE20K, while also achieving the best depth estimation (0.085 REL vs 0.098 for SimMIM). Notably, GLID uses only ImageNet-1K without labels for pre-training, whereas several baselines use ImageNet-21K or multi-dataset supervision.

## Relation to Prior Work

GLID sits at the intersection of three research threads: MAE-style masked image modeling for self-supervised pre-training, DETR's query-to-answer formulation for dense prediction, and Mask2Former's query-based transformer decoder architecture. The key departure from MAE is that GLID retains and pre-trains the full encoder-decoder (rather than discarding the decoder after pre-training), and the key departure from existing generalist models (Mask2Former, OneFormer, UViM) is that the entire decoder is pre-trained rather than randomly initialized at fine-tuning time. By reformulating the MIM reconstruction target as a query-to-answer problem, GLID achieves architectural identity between pre-training and fine-tuning, requiring only a linear head swap to transition between tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The pretrain-finetune architectural gap is a practical bottleneck: task-specific decoders trained from scratch cannot leverage large-scale pre-training, forcing generalist models to demand large amounts of task-specific data. GLID's data-efficiency results (4.2 mIoU advantage over supervised pre-training at 10% fine-tuning data) and the clean ablation showing 1.8 mIoU gain from loading the full pre-trained decoder vs. backbone-only are practically significant for resource-constrained settings. However, the multitask fine-tuning results reveal that task interference still degrades performance relative to single-task training, and the approach is limited to tasks expressible as query-to-answer problems, leaving open whether it extends to generative or language-grounded tasks.
