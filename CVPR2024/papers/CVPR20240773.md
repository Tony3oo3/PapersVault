## Problem

Standard denoising diffusion models (DDPM/DDIM) are uninterpretable for image restoration: the forward process contains no information about the degraded input, and the reverse process unnecessarily starts from pure noise when the degraded image is already known. This paper asks whether the single denoising process can be decomposed into interpretable components that explicitly encode the degradation direction, enabling a unified framework for both generation and restoration.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240773/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For image restoration, the method takes a degraded RGB image (shadow, low-light, blurred, or rainy) as a conditional input I_in and produces a restored image; for generation, I_in is set to zero so the terminal state is pure noise. The terminal state is I_T = I_in + ε, meaning the reverse process starts from a noise-carrying degraded image rather than pure noise. The method uses a single generic UNet trained with only an L1 loss and batch size 1 across all tasks, requiring fewer than 5 sampling steps for restoration.

## Method

RDDM decouples the traditional single denoising diffusion into two independent processes: a residual diffusion (directional shift from target I_0 toward degraded input I_in, controlled by schedule α_t) and a noise diffusion (random perturbation, controlled by schedule β_t), yielding the forward state I_t = I_0 + ᾱ_t·I_res + β̄_t·ε. The reverse process predicts residuals, noise, or both via three sampling strategies (SM-Res, SM-N, SM-Res-N), with an automatic objective selection algorithm for unknown tasks. The authors prove that their sampling process is consistent with DDPM and DDIM through a coefficient transformation (Eq. 17), and further show that the two diffusion processes can be decoupled in the reverse direction—first removing residuals then noise, or vice versa—yielding a partially path-independent generation process where the diffusion speed curves can be readjusted at inference without retraining.

## Results

On shadow removal (ISTD), RDDM achieves the best MAE of 4.67, PSNR of 30.91 dB, and SSIM of 0.962 across the whole image, outperforming all baselines with only 5 sampling steps. On low-light enhancement (LOL), RDDM attains the highest PSNR (25.39 dB) and SSIM (0.937) with 2 sampling steps, though LLFlow achieves a marginally lower LPIPS (0.111 vs. 0.116). On deraining (RainDrop), RDDM reaches PSNR 32.51 dB and SSIM 0.9563, surpassing all competitors including diffusion-based RainDiff128 (32.43/0.9334). For image generation on CelebA 256×256, converting a pre-trained DDIM to RDDM via coefficient transformation preserves quality nearly exactly (FID 23.66 vs. 24.92 at 100 steps).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On shadow removal (ISTD), RDDM outperforms all four baselines—DSC, FusionNet, BMNet, and DMTN—on every metric (MAE, PSNR, SSIM), with the largest margin over FusionNet (PSNR 30.91 vs. 27.19 dB) and a clear lead over the strongest competitor DMTN (PSNR 30.91 vs. 30.42, SSIM 0.962 vs. 0.965 where DMTN edges ahead slightly on SSIM). On low-light enhancement (LOL), RDDM achieves the best PSNR (25.39 dB) and SSIM (0.937), surpassing LLFlow (25.19/0.93) and LLFormer (23.65/0.816), though LLFlow retains a marginal LPIPS advantage (0.111 vs. 0.116). On deraining (RainDrop), RDDM leads all six baselines on both PSNR and SSIM, with the most notable gap over the diffusion-based RainDiff128 (PSNR 32.51 vs. 32.43, SSIM 0.9563 vs. 0.9334) and a substantial margin over GAN-based AttnGAN (PSNR 32.51 vs. 31.59). Notably, RDDM uses a single generic UNet trained with only L1 loss and batch size 1 across all tasks, whereas baselines employ multi-stage architectures, Transformers, GANs, or specialized loss functions. The image generation consistency check (CelebA) confirms that coefficient transformation from DDIM to RDDM introduces negligible quality loss (FID 23.66 vs. 24.92 at 100 steps), validating the theoretical equivalence.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends DDPM and DDIM by decoupling the single denoising diffusion process into two independent processes: a directional residual diffusion (from target image to degraded input) and a random noise diffusion, each governed by its own coefficient schedule. It departs from prior restoration methods (SDEdit, ColdDiffusion, InDI, I2SB) that either start the reverse process from pure noise or estimate only the target image, by explicitly modeling residuals as a first-class diffusion component alongside noise. The three-term mixture (I₀, I_res, ε) aligns with concurrent works like InDI and I2SB, but RDDM treats residuals and noise as equally independent, enabling a unified framework where the same model handles generation (I_in=0) and restoration (I_in=degraded) without architectural changes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The interpretability gain is significant: the forward process now has a clear physical meaning for restoration (progressive degradation toward the known input), addressing a long-standing criticism that denoising diffusion is uninterpretable for image-to-image tasks. The practical finding that a generic UNet with only L1 loss and batch size 1 matches SOTA restoration methods (which use multi-stage Transformers, GANs, and elaborate losses) suggests that the diffusion framework itself, rather than architectural complexity, drives restoration quality. Open questions include whether the partially path-independent property generalizes to larger-scale generation (beyond 64×64 CelebA), whether the dual-schedule design transfers to latent diffusion or video, and whether the residual/noise decoupling can be extended to multi-dimensional degradation models.
