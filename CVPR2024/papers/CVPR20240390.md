## Problem

Existing video reasoning benchmarks primarily evaluate factual or situated reasoning confined to visible content, rarely requiring models to integrate broader open-world knowledge with dynamic, spatio-temporal context. They also lack structured knowledge representations and explicit multi-step rationales, making it difficult to assess whether models truly reason or merely pattern-match. This paper addresses the gap by introducing a benchmark that demands both situated facts from videos and general commonsense knowledge, with aligned knowledge graphs making the reasoning chain explicit.

## Input / Output

Each question is paired with a video clip (sourced from YouCook2 cooking videos or HOMAGE daily-activity clips) and a text question spanning 12 types (e.g., object counterfactual, action purpose, spatiotemporal inference). The model must produce an answer in two formats: a direct free-text response and a selection among four multiple-choice options, with wrong options drawn from the same knowledge category to minimize shortcut learning. Each QA pair is additionally linked to a hypergraph unifying three knowledge graphs, providing a structured rationale from question to answer.

## Method

The pipeline automatically constructs three aligned knowledge graphs per video situation: a Situated Knowledge Graph (SKG) extracting objects, actions, attributes, and spatial relations via LLM text parsing plus MLLMs (BLIP-2 for object detection, MiniGPT-4 for attribute/relation description); a General Knowledge Graph (GKG) generated by prompting LLMs with a triplet of examples, goal, and output format to produce structured, parseable knowledge about each entity; and a Situated Commonsense Knowledge Graph (SCKG) that integrates SKG and GKG using a Few-Shot Self-Prompting technique where previously generated situated commonsense examples guide the LLM toward concrete, situation-specific inferences. QA pairs are then generated bottom-up from manually designed templates that jump across graph edges (e.g., from a situated attribute edge to a commonsense consequence edge), ensuring multi-hop reasoning, or top-down by prompting an LLM with the full integrated graph. The entire process is iterative, using multi-turn LLM/MLLM dialogues with self-prompting and demonstrations to refine quality, followed by human validation (93.08% QA validity rate).

## Results

On SOK-Bench's multiple-choice setting, GPT4V achieves the highest overall accuracy at 53.9%, followed by AskAnything (45.5%) and blind ChatGPT (36.5%). Deployable models (Video-LLaMA, PandaGPT, Video-ChatGPT, Valley) all cluster between 26.4% and 31.2% overall accuracy. In the direct-answer setting, even the best model (AskAnything) reaches only 0.110 BLEU score, while GPT4V achieves 0.085 BLEU, indicating that situated open-world commonsense reasoning in videos remains a significant challenge for current vision-language models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT4V leads all baselines with 53.9% overall multiple-choice accuracy, but notably scores 0.000 on the most complex question type (OCT+ST, requiring combined spatiotemporal and situated commonsense reasoning), revealing a critical weakness in multi-hop situated reasoning. AskAnything (ChatGPT-based) achieves 45.5% overall, outperforming all deployable models by 14–19 percentage points. Interestingly, blind ChatGPT (no video input) achieves 36.5% overall, surpassing Video-LLaMA (26.4%) and approaching PandaGPT/Video-ChatGPT/Valley (~31%), suggesting that current video models add limited value over text-only reasoning for this task. Among deployable models, PandaGPT, Video-ChatGPT, and Valley are nearly tied at 31.1–31.2%, while Video-LLaMA lags at 26.4%. In the direct-answer setting, the gap widens further: AskAnything achieves the best BLEU of 0.110, while GPT4V drops to 0.085, and all deployable models fall below 0.070, confirming that generating free-form situated commonsense answers is substantially harder than selecting from options.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of video question answering and commonsense reasoning by introducing 'situated open-world' reasoning, which requires models to simultaneously leverage what is observable in a dynamic video (situated knowledge) and general world knowledge beyond the visible content. Prior video QA benchmarks (CLEVR, NExT-QA, STAR) focus on within-video factual or physical reasoning, while visual commonsense benchmarks (VCR, OK-VQA, A-OKVQA) rely on static images with crowd-sourced, free-form knowledge descriptions. The key methodological departure is replacing crowd-sourced annotation with an automated LLM/MLLM pipeline that produces structured knowledge graphs (Situated, General, and Situated Commonsense) and multi-hop question-answer pairs, enabling compositional and temporally grounded reasoning that neither prior line of work supports.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark (44K QAs over 10K video clips) provides the first large-scale, structured evaluation of whether models can bridge situated video evidence with open-world commonsense, a capability critical for embodied and interactive AI. The finding that even GPT-4V achieves only ~54% multiple-choice accuracy and very low BLEU in direct-answer settings underscores a substantial gap in current video-language models' reasoning depth. However, the domain is limited to cooking and home-activity videos (YouCook2, HOMAGE), and the suspiciously uniform BERT-F1 scores (~0.95–0.96 across all models) raise questions about the discriminative power of that metric. Open questions include whether the automatic generation pipeline generalizes to more diverse video domains and whether the structured graph annotations can be leveraged for training rather than just evaluation.
