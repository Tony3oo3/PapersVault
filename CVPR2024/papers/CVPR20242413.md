## Problem

Existing data influence estimation methods either retrain models on many training subsets or compute gradients for every training point, both of which scale poorly to large datasets and models. Gradient computation is up to 10.92× slower and 5.36× more memory-intensive than a forward pass, yet all prior approaches apply the expensive backward pass to the entire (large) training set. The paper asks whether the computationally cheaper forward pass can be leveraged more heavily by reformulating the influence problem.

## Input / Output

The method takes a fully trained model θ̂, the complete training dataset D_trn (potentially millions of points), and a small target test set D_tst (often a single point or <1% of training size). It outputs a scalar influence score per training point, computed as the loss difference between two models evaluated via forward passes, yielding a ranking of training points by their influence on the test prediction. The critical asymmetry exploited is |D_tst| << |D_trn|, so the expensive backward pass is applied only to the few test samples while the cheap forward pass handles the large training set.

## Method

The core contribution is the Mirrored Influence Hypothesis, which posits that train-to-test influence (how removing a training point changes test loss) is correlated with test-to-train influence (how adding a test point changes training-point loss). The paper proves this equivalence holds exactly under both Influence Function and TracIn approximators, since the Hessian is symmetric and the gradient dot product is commutative. The Forward-INF algorithm operationalizes this by: (1) performing K steps of gradient ascent on the small test set to obtain a perturbed model θ̂_K, and (2) computing each training point's influence as L(θ̂_K, D_i) − L(θ̂, D_i) via two forward passes. Gradient ascent (rather than descent) is used by default to amplify loss differences for points similar to the test sample, improving discriminative power. Theoretical justification for using continual learning (updating θ̂ on D_tst) rather than retraining from scratch is provided in the appendix, showing the approximation is valid when n is large and the two models are close.

## Results

On data leakage detection, Forward-INF achieves 100% top-1 accuracy on CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18 and 95% top-1 on CIFAR-100 with ResNet-50, while being 30× faster than Influence Functions and 40× faster than TracIn. The Mirrored Influence Hypothesis is validated with Pearson correlations of 0.9673 (logistic regression) and 0.8851 (CNN) between train-to-test and test-to-train influence on CIFAR-10. For language model behavior tracing on FTRACE-TREx, Forward-INF achieves MRR 0.2101 and precision 0.1650 with a 15K candidate set, outperforming TracIn (MRR 0.1658) while inspecting 4.2× more queries per minute (1306 vs. 308).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Forward-INF decisively outperforms Influence Functions (Koh & Liang, ICML 2017) on data leakage detection, achieving 100% top-1 accuracy on CIFAR-10/ResNet-18 versus IF's best of 45% (IF-1000), while being 30× faster (0.3 min vs. 15 min per test point). Against TracIn (Pruthi et al., NeurIPS 2020), Forward-INF achieves 100% top-1 on CIFAR-10 versus TracIn's 2–7% (depending on checkpoint count), and is 40× faster; on CIFAR-100/ResNet-50, Forward-INF reaches 95% top-1 while TracIn peaks at 10%. For language model behavior tracing on FTRACE-TREx, Forward-INF achieves MRR 0.2101 and precision 0.1650 (15K candidate set), surpassing TracIn's MRR 0.1658 and precision 0.1367, while processing 4.2× more queries per minute. Compared to Feldman & Zhang (NeurIPS 2020) for memorization analysis, Forward-INF identifies the same influential training-test pairs without the expensive re-training procedure, matching their qualitative results at a fraction of the computational cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the well-established data influence estimation framework (Influence Functions, TracIn, retraining-based methods) by identifying an unexploited symmetry: the train-to-test influence is correlated with the test-to-train influence. Rather than building on a single prior method, it reframes the problem so that the computationally expensive backward pass is applied only to the small test set, while the large training set requires only forward passes. The theoretical equivalence for Influence Functions and TracIn follows directly from Hessian symmetry and dot-product commutativity, but the broader empirical hypothesis (especially in non-convex, stochastic settings) is a new observation that no prior influence estimation work had leveraged algorithmically.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: by exploiting the typical size asymmetry between training and test sets, Forward-INF achieves 30–40× speedups over gradient-based baselines while matching or exceeding their accuracy in applications like data leakage detection and mislabeled data identification. However, the theoretical guarantee is limited to first-order approximators under convexity assumptions; the hypothesis in general non-convex, stochastic settings remains empirically validated but not formally proven. Open questions include whether the continual-learning step (gradient ascent on the test set) can suffer from catastrophic forgetting in larger models, and whether strategic layer selection or other heuristics can further improve the approximation quality.
