## Problem

Pre-trained vision-language models like CLIP are highly vulnerable to adversarial perturbations, yet existing defenses focus on model weights (adversarial fine-tuning) or image pixels (visual prompting), leaving the text prompt — a natural, parameter-efficient input channel — largely unexplored. This paper shows that both the strength of adversarial attacks and the model's robustness are sensitive to the specific text prompt used, motivating prompt-level adaptation as a new defense axis.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241488/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a learned text prompt context (a sequence of M learnable 512-dimensional vectors in the CLIP word-embedding space, concatenated with the class embedding) as input, and produces a class label via cosine similarity between frozen image and text encoder features. Adversarial perturbations are bounded in the ℓ∞ norm with budgets of ε=1/255 or 4/255, and the prompt context can be either unified across all classes (UC) or class-specific (CSC), with the latter scaling linearly in the number of classes.

## Method

Adversarial Prompt Tuning (APT) freezes both the image and text encoders of a CLIP-like VLM and learns only the text prompt context vectors via adversarial training. The context is parameterized as a sequence of M continuous vectors (soft prompts) in the word-embedding space, either shared across all classes (UC) or assigned per class (CSC). During training, adversarial perturbations are generated on-the-fly using PGD against the latest updated prompts, making the training adversary track the evolving defense. Three prompting strategies (constant, on-the-fly, perturbed) are compared, with on-the-fly achieving the best effectiveness-efficiency trade-off. A single learned prompt word (M=1) already yields substantial gains over hand-engineered prompts, and the approach is both parameter-efficient and data-efficient, working from 1-shot to full training data.

## Results

On 11 datasets averaged, APT-CSC with the full training set achieves 59.4% accuracy and 27.0% robustness at ε=4/255, improving over hand-engineered prompts (HEP) by +26.4% and +16.7% respectively. At ε=1/255, the same setting yields 73.5% accuracy and 57.1% robustness (+28.3% and +25.0% over HEP). Even in the 1-shot regime, APT-UC boosts accuracy and robustness over HEP by +6.1% and +3.0% at ε=1/255, demonstrating strong data efficiency. In zero-shot transfer (Table 3), APT-UC improves average accuracy by 1.1% and robustness by 1.7% over the TeCoA baseline across 10 unseen datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against HEP (hand-engineered prompts from CLIP [51]), APT achieves the largest gains: at ε=4/255 with all shots, APT-CSC improves accuracy by +26.4% (59.4% vs 33.0%) and robustness by +16.7% (27.0% vs 10.3%), and at ε=1/255 the gains are +28.3% and +25.0% respectively. Against AVP (Adversarial Visual Prompting [9]), APT dominates in both accuracy and robustness across all shot settings; at ε=4/255 with all shots, APT-CSC reaches 59.4%/27.0% versus AVP's 34.4%/13.1%, a gap of +25.0% accuracy and +13.9% robustness. Against PAFT (Partial Adversarial Fine-Tuning [10]), APT achieves comparable robustness (27.0% vs 27.5% at ε=4/255 all shots) but considerably higher accuracy (59.4% vs 54.9%), indicating a better accuracy-robustness trade-off; at ε=1/255 all shots, APT-CSC leads on both metrics (73.5%/57.1% vs 71.1%/56.9%). In the zero-shot setting (Table 3), APT-UC outperforms the TeCoA [45] pre-training baseline by +1.1% average accuracy and +1.7% average robustness across 10 unseen datasets, with the largest gain on OxfordPets (+4.3% acc, +7.3% rob). Notably, PAFT underperforms even HEP in the 1-shot setting (19.2% vs 33.0% accuracy at ε=4/255), while APT-UC maintains a clear advantage over HEP even with a single example per class.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the soft prompt tuning framework of Zhou et al. (2022) for vision-language models and couples it with the PGD adversarial training loop of Madry et al. (2018), but applies it to a previously unexplored lever: the text prompt context in CLIP-like architectures. Prior work on adversarial robustness of VLMs has focused on adapting model weights (adversarial fine-tuning, PAFT) or image-space perturbations (adversarial visual prompting, AVP), while the text prompt was treated as a fixed, hand-engineered artifact. The paper's key conceptual departure is showing that the prompt itself is a first-order determinant of adversarial vulnerability, thereby elevating it from a convenience parameter to a trainable defense variable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that a single learned 512-dim vector in the prompt can yield double-digit gains in both accuracy and robustness with minimal data, the paper opens a lightweight, architecture-agnostic pathway to harden frozen foundation models without the cost of adversarial fine-tuning. Two limitations temper the impact: the learned context vectors are semantically opaque (nearest-neighbor word decoding yields irrelevant tokens), and the method's effectiveness depends on the pre-trained weights already having reasonable robustness (e.g., TeCoA). Open questions include whether the approach transfers to non-CLIP VLMs (e.g., LLaVA, Flamingo), whether interpretability of the learned prompts can be recovered, and whether the prompt-robustness sensitivity generalizes to multi-turn or generative VLM settings.
