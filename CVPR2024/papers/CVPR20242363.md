## Problem

Single-view lighting estimation from an LDR image requires predicting a full 360° HDR environment map, but existing neural regressors are trained on small, limited-diversity HDR panorama datasets and fail to generalize to in-the-wild, uncontrolled scenes. The core challenge is producing convincing, high-dynamic-range light estimates without access to large, diverse HDR training data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242363/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single LDR image of arbitrary resolution (upscaled to SDXL's native size while preserving aspect ratio) and an off-the-shelf monocular depth map as auxiliary conditioning; no camera pose or FOV is required. It outputs a 128×256 HDR environment map by inpainting a chrome ball into the image and unwrapping the ball's surface. Internally, it generates three LDR chrome ball renderings at exposure values EV = −5, −2.5, and 0, which are merged by replacing overexposed luminance regions with exposure-corrected values from lower-EV images while retaining chroma from the EV0 image.

## Method

The core idea is to leverage a pre-trained text-to-image diffusion model (Stable Diffusion XL) with depth-conditioned ControlNet to inpaint a chrome ball into the input image, then unwrap the ball to an environment map. To ensure consistent, high-quality chrome balls, the paper introduces an iterative inpainting algorithm: N balls are generated with different random seeds, a pixel-wise median ball is computed and composited back, and SDEdit is applied from this median (at t = 0.8T) to locate a noise neighborhood that reliably produces clean reflective spheres; this is repeated twice. For HDR generation, a rank-4 LoRA is fine-tuned on 1,412 synthetically rendered chrome balls (from Text2Light panoramas) with varying exposures, conditioned on a linear interpolation between the text embeddings of 'a perfect mirrored reflective chrome ball sphere' and the same prompt with 'black dark' appended, enabling continuous EV control at test time. The key empirical insight is that the initial diffusion noise map encodes semantic appearance patterns (e.g., a 'disco ball' noise), and sample averaging over multiple generations identifies a noise region that consistently yields correct chrome ball reflections.

## Results

On the Laval Indoor dataset (three-sphere protocol), the full method (SDXL + iterative inpainting + LoRA) achieves an Angular Error of 2.14° (diffuse), 3.42° (matte), and 5.94° (mirror), outperforming StyleLight's reproduced scores of 4.24°, 4.74°, and 6.78° respectively. On Poly Haven, the method achieves 4.29° Angular Error on mirror spheres versus StyleLight's 7.12°, a 39.7% improvement. On the array-of-spheres protocol (2240 input-output pairs), the method achieves si-RMSE 0.090 and Angular Error 5.25°, ranking second overall behind Weber et al. (0.081, 4.13°) and EMLight (0.099, 3.99°), while outperforming EverLight and StyleLight.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against StyleLight (the primary baseline, trained on Laval Indoor), the proposed method wins on Angular Error and Normalized RMSE across both Laval Indoor and Poly Haven, while being slightly worse on si-RMSE for Laval Indoor (0.14 vs 0.13 diffuse). On Poly Haven, the method outperforms StyleLight on all nine metric-sphere combinations, with the largest gap being 39.7% lower Angular Error on mirror spheres (4.29° vs 7.12°). On the array-of-spheres protocol, the method ranks second overall: it beats EverLight (si-RMSE 0.090 vs 0.091; Angular Error 5.25° vs 6.36°) and StyleLight (0.090 vs 0.123; 5.25° vs 7.09°) on both metrics, but trails Weber et al. (0.090 vs 0.081; 5.25° vs 4.13°) and EMLight (0.090 vs 0.099 on si-RMSE but 5.25° vs 3.99° on Angular Error). Notably, Weber et al. and EMLight were explicitly trained on the Laval Indoor dataset, whereas the proposed method was not, and the paper emphasizes superior generalization to in-the-wild scenes where all baselines fail.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in single-view lighting estimation, where dedicated neural regressors (StyleLight, EverLight, EMLight) are trained on limited HDR panorama datasets to map LDR inputs to full environment maps. Instead, it repurposes a pre-trained text-to-image diffusion model (SDXL) as a scene-aware renderer by inpainting a chrome ball, leveraging the massive image prior from billions of training images rather than a small curated HDR set. The technical pipeline combines depth-conditioned ControlNet for reliable ball placement, SDEdit-based iterative inpainting to locate a favorable noise neighborhood, and a continuous LoRA fine-tuning on synthetically rendered chrome balls to enable exposure bracketing for HDR reconstruction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The main practical contribution is that this is the first lighting estimation method to produce convincing results on truly in-the-wild images (e.g., inside a safari jeep, a car interior, a snowboard scene) where dedicated regressors trained on indoor/outdoor panoramas fail. However, the method is currently slow due to iterative inpainting with N=30 samples per median computation plus multi-exposure generation, and it assumes orthographic projection when unwrapping the ball, which may not match the diffusion model's implicit projection. Open questions include whether sampling-efficient diffusion models (LCM, InstaFlow) can close the speed gap, whether the noise-map-to-appearance relationship generalizes beyond chrome balls to other light probe geometries, and how to handle unknown focal length more rigorously.
