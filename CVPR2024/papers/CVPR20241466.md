## Problem

Backdoor attacks on visible light object detection are well-studied, but thermal infrared object detection (TIOD)—which is essential in dark, smoky, and temperature-sensitive scenarios—has no prior investigation of backdoor vulnerabilities. The core challenge is that TIOD images are single-channel grayscale with minimal texture, so conventional color-based triggers from VLOD are ineffective, and trigger design must instead exploit temperature differences.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241466/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes single-channel thermal infrared images (640×512 for Flir_v2_T, 640×480 for FIR_Det, 1420×1080 for physical experiments) and produces detection bounding boxes with class labels (person, car, bike, truck). The trigger is not a visual pattern but a temperature-defined pixel block whose value p maps to physical temperature T via p = 1.4221×10⁻⁴·T⁴ − 15.4760, meaning the same trigger appears as a uniform-temperature region in the thermal domain rather than a textured pattern.

## Method

The paper proposes two data-poisoning backdoor attacks on YOLOv5/YOLOv3/Faster R-CNN: Object-Affecting Attack (OAA), which inserts a temperature-defined pixel block inside each target object's BBox and modifies only that object's label, and Range-Affecting Attack (RAA), which places a single trigger near objects and modifies labels of all objects within a configurable radius, exploiting grid-overlap in one-stage detectors. The key novelty is temperature-modulated triggering: by implanting triggers at different pixel-value (temperature) ranges during poisoning, the attacker can remotely activate or deactivate the backdoor via a button-controlled electric heating device (OAA) or scale the attack radius between 80–160 pixels (RAA). Adversarial triggers at out-of-range temperatures are also implanted to suppress false activations, and the physical trigger costs under 5 USD to produce.

## Results

In the digital world, OAA achieves an ASR of up to 98.21% (p=64, Flir_v2_T, YOLO v5) and RAA achieves up to 98.19% (ar=300), while maintaining BAF within ±5% for most configurations. In the physical world, OAA with temperature-modulated triggering (≤26.6°C) reaches an ASR of 98.38% for object misclassification and 98.38% for object disappearance, while RAA achieves 97.0% ASR. Across three detector architectures (YOLO v5, YOLO v3, Faster RCNN) and two datasets, ASR consistently exceeds 81%, with YOLO-based detectors being most vulnerable (96–98% ASR) compared to Faster RCNN (81–92% ASR).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three defense countermeasures against the proposed attacks. Pruning [12] and Fine-Pruning [33] can reduce ASR to 63.33%, but at a severe cost to benign detection accuracy: person mAP drops from 78.3% to 26.2% and car mAP from 81.7% to 42.7%, making these defenses impractical for deployment. Neural Cleanse [47], a popular trigger-reverse-engineering defense, completely fails to detect either attack: the anomaly index for OAA is 1.218572 and for RAA is 1.767544, both well below the detection threshold of 2.0. Thus, none of the evaluated defenses can simultaneously neutralize the backdoor while preserving normal detection performance, highlighting the practical severity of the proposed temperature-based backdoor attacks on TIOD systems.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the backdoor attack literature from visible light object detection (VLOD) to thermal infrared object detection (TIOD), a modality where the trigger design space is fundamentally constrained to temperature differences rather than color or texture patterns. It builds on prior TIOD adversarial attack work (Zhu et al. on aerogel shirts, Wei et al. on infrared patches) but shifts the threat model from adversarial examples to data-poisoning-based backdoors, which had been entirely unexplored for thermal infrared. The temperature-modulated triggering mechanism—using an electric heating device whose pixel value can be remotely adjusted—is a novel repurposing of the physical trigger concept, departing from the static pixel-pattern triggers used in VLOD backdoor attacks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TIOD is increasingly deployed in safety-critical settings (autonomous driving in darkness, security surveillance, pandemic screening), and this work demonstrates that a sub-$5 electric heating device can compromise detectors with >96% ASR in real-world scenes while remaining invisible to human observers. The temperature modulation adds a remote on/off control dimension that has no analogue in VLOD backdoor attacks, raising new defense challenges. Notably, all three evaluated countermeasures (Pruning, Fine-Pruning, Neural Cleanse) either fail to detect the attack or degrade benign accuracy severely, leaving a significant gap in TIOD security. Open questions include whether defenses can exploit the thermal-specific structure of triggers, how attacks transfer across different thermal camera models and wavelength bands, and whether the temperature modulation mechanism generalizes to other physical sensing modalities.
