## Problem

Most face reenactment methods rely on CNN-based optical flow estimation between source and driving frames, making them sensitive to initialization (especially in relative motion transfer) and prone to leaking driving-frame shape into the output during cross-reenactment. This paper asks whether a transformer-based scene representation can factorize appearance, head pose, and facial expression into independent channels, enabling more robust and temporally consistent motion transfer without explicit correspondence modeling.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241815/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes one or more 256×256 source face images (encoded with 10 normalized keypoints and a learned 256-dim latent expression vector) and a driving video frame (providing driving keypoints and expression vector) as input, and produces a 256×256 reenacted face frame per query. Notably, the set-latent source representation is computed once and each output pixel is decoded independently via cross-attention, requiring no camera intrinsics/extrinsics and operating entirely in pixel space. The architecture naturally scales to an arbitrary number of source images at inference without retraining.

## Method

FSRT reformulates the Scene Representation Transformer (SRT) for face reenactment: a shared Patch CNN extracts patch embeddings from source images (conditioned on keypoints and expression), which a 5-block self-attention encoder compresses into a set-latent representation; a cross-attention decoder then queries this set for each pixel, conditioned on driving keypoints and a latent expression vector extracted by a network that reuses keypoint-detector feature maps. The key design insight is that appearance lives in the set-latent vectors, head pose in the 10 keypoints, and expression in a separate latent vector, enabling clean cross-reenactment. Training proceeds in three phases (MSE → perceptual loss → adversarial + feature-matching loss) with a VICReg-inspired covariance/variance regularization and color-jitter invariance losses that prevent the expression vector from absorbing pose or appearance information. The decoder's per-pixel independence allows linear GPU scaling, and a reduced decoder variant achieves 23 fps on a single RTX 4090.

## Results

On the VoxCeleb test set for self-reenactment, FSRT with two source images achieves SSIM 0.7891, PSNR 25.00, L1 0.0360, and AKD 2.04, outperforming all baselines. With a single source image, FSRT reaches SSIM 0.7576, PSNR 23.67, L1 0.0421, and AKD 2.13, matching or exceeding DaGAN and FOMM. In the cross-reenactment user study, FSRT is preferred over all SOTA methods in 95–99% of pairwise comparisons for absolute motion and 87–98% for relative motion, with the largest margins against OSFS (87% relative) and TSMM (97% relative).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FSRT outperforms all five baselines (DPE, FOMM, DaGAN, OSFS, TSMM) in the two-source self-reenactment setting, achieving the best SSIM (0.7891 vs. 0.7660 for TSMM), PSNR (25.00 vs. 23.76), L1 (0.0360 vs. 0.0433), and competitive AKD (2.04 vs. 2.00). With a single source image, FSRT slightly trails TSMM on SSIM (0.7576 vs. 0.7660) and PSNR (23.67 vs. 23.76), but the authors note TSMM only inpaints disoccluded background regions, inflating its static-region metrics. In the cross-reenactment user study, FSRT is strongly preferred: 97–98% for relative motion against FOMM, DaGAN, and TSMM, and 94–99% for absolute motion against all baselines including DPE (which has no relative mode). The largest relative-motion advantage is over OSFS (87% preference), indicating OSFS struggles most with pose/expression mismatch. FSRT's key advantage is robustness to poorly matching driving keypoints, producing fewer shape deformations and better identity preservation in cross-ID scenarios.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reformulates the Scene Representation Transformer (SRT) architecture for face reenactment, adapting its set-latent scene encoding from static novel-view synthesis to dynamic facial animation. It departs from the dominant CNN-based warp-and-refine paradigm (FOMM, DaGAN, TSMM) by eliminating explicit optical flow estimation in favor of attention-based querying of a factorized latent representation. The factorization strategy—appearance in set-latents, head pose in keypoints, expression in a learned vector—draws on the disentanglement ideas of DPE and the relative motion transfer concept from FOMM, but achieves it through a fundamentally different mechanism. VICReg-inspired statistical regularization is adapted to enforce expression invariance, a training technique not previously applied to face reenactment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first transformer-based architecture for face reenactment, this work opens a path toward more flexible, multi-source, and temporally consistent facial animation without the brittleness of keypoint-driven warping. The demonstrated real-time capability (23 fps with a small decoder on a single GPU) and linear GPU scalability make it practically relevant for applications like video conferencing and VR avatars. However, the method still struggles with out-of-distribution expressions (tongue, looking back) and loses fine background/hair detail compared to CNN baselines, suggesting the set-latent representation may need higher capacity or volume-rendering extensions. An open question is whether the factorization quality can be further improved to handle extreme cross-identity shape differences without the current mild shape leakage.
