## Problem

Existing multi-object tracking methods are designed for high-quality inputs and perform poorly under low-light conditions, where sensor noise severely degrades both detection and appearance-based association. No dedicated dataset or method specifically targets multi-object tracking in dark scenes, leaving a gap for real-world applications like nighttime autonomous driving and surveillance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240714/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 12-bit RAW video frames (pre-ISP, preserving full sensor information) at 20 fps with 10 ms exposure, paired with spatially and temporally aligned well-lit frames that serve as additional supervision during training. At inference, only the low-light RAW frames are required. The output consists of per-frame bounding boxes with persistent object IDs across the video sequence, class labels from six categories (car, person, bicycle, motorcycle, bus, truck), and visibility status for occluded objects.

## Method

LTrack builds on a YOLOX detector paired with ByteTrack or OC-SORT, introducing two components to learn noise-invariant features. The Adaptive Low-pass Downsampling (ALD) module applies a softmax-constrained spatial low-pass convolution (5×5, Gaussian-initialized) to extract low-frequency components from noised feature maps, then adaptively fuses them back via channel descriptors computed from global average pooling and a fully connected layer. The Degradation Suppression Learning (DSL) strategy enforces L2 consistency between feature maps of paired well-lit and low-light images at multiple depths, and adds a Total Variation loss on low-light features to spatially smooth shallow representations. The total loss combines detection losses on both well-lit and low-light images with the DSL and TV terms (weights α=1, β=1, γ=0.01). The key insight is that sensor noise in low-light images behaves analogously to adversarial perturbations, so the network is trained to extract invariant semantic content rather than relying on a separate enhancement preprocessing step.

## Results

On the LMOT test set, LTrack achieves 29.4 HOTA with ByteTrack and 29.8 HOTA with OC-SORT, outperforming the strongest baseline (Base-all) by 1.3 HOTA points while using nearly identical parameters (100.43M vs. 99.00M) and FLOPS (800.92G vs. 793.29G). On the LMOT-real dataset (real-world night scenes), LTrack reaches 35.1 HOTA with OC-SORT, surpassing the best enhancement-based method (LLFlow at 33.6) by 1.5 points. The detector component achieves 23.4 mAP and 40.6 AP50, compared to 19.0 mAP for Base-all.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LTrack outperforms all five baselines on both HOTA and detection metrics across both trackers. Against the low-light enhancement pipelines, LTrack wins by 0.6–1.8 HOTA points (e.g., 29.4 vs. 28.8 for LLFlow, 29.4 vs. 29.1 for SDSD with ByteTrack) while using roughly 1/10th the FLOPS of LLFlow (801G vs. 8756G) and SMOID (801G vs. 7286G). The RAW detection baseline RAOD [52] is the closest in computational cost (897G FLOPS, 99.07M params) but trails LTrack by 3.3 HOTA points (26.1 vs. 29.4), indicating that its HDR-oriented preprocessing does not transfer well to low-light tracking. On the LMOT-real dataset, the gap widens: LTrack (35.1 HOTA) beats the best enhancement method LLFlow (33.6) by 1.5 points and RAOD (32.2) by 2.9 points, while Base-all (34.6) nearly matches LTrack, suggesting that the ALD and DSL modules provide the critical robustness gain in unseen real-world conditions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the tracking-by-detection paradigm using YOLOX as the detector and ByteTrack/OC-SORT as association backbones, but departs from the standard 'enhance-then-track' pipeline by directly learning tracking features from degraded low-light inputs. Conceptually, it draws an analogy between sensor noise and adversarial perturbations, motivating the adaptive low-pass downsample module and degradation suppression learning strategy as noise-invariant feature extractors rather than image restorers. The data collection leverages the dual-camera system design from Jiang et al. [23] but extends it to capture RAW-format video pairs specifically for multi-object tracking annotation, filling a gap left by prior low-light datasets that only support static or single-image tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The LMOT dataset is the first dedicated benchmark for multi-object tracking under low-light conditions, providing a much-needed evaluation ground truth for a scenario prevalent in autonomous driving and nighttime surveillance. The finding that cascading low-light enhancement modules (LLFlow, SDSD, DNF, SMOID) does not improve—and sometimes degrades—downstream tracking performance challenges the common assumption that visual quality restoration is a prerequisite for high-level tasks. However, the method's gains over strong baselines are modest (~1–2 HOTA points), the dataset covers only urban pedestrian/vehicle scenes, and the authors explicitly acknowledge that other degradations (rain, fog) remain unaddressed, leaving the broader question of robust MOT under diverse real-world conditions open.
