## Problem

Existing memory-efficient PETL methods like Ladder Side Tuning (LST) suffer from three structural limitations: scalability (side network complexity grows linearly with the backbone), adaptability (static gated connections cannot handle semantic misalignments or adapt aggregation to different inputs), and generalizability (designs are tightly coupled to Transformer architectures and fail on CNNs or Encoder-Decoder structures). This paper asks whether a single lightweight parallel tuning framework can work across diverse backbone architectures while dynamically adapting its fusion strategy per input.

## Input / Output

UniPT consumes the intermediate hidden states from all N layers of a frozen pretrained backbone (Transformer, CNN, or Encoder-Decoder) at a reduced dimension d = D/r, where r is a reduction factor (typically 4 or 8). The backbone itself receives standard task inputs (image+text pairs at 336×336 or 224×224, video clips of 16–32 frames, or raw text sequences), but UniPT never backpropagates through it. The output is a task-specific representation of the same dimension as the original backbone's final layer, which is then fed to a standard task head producing retrieval scores, answer tokens, bounding boxes, or classification logits depending on the downstream task.

## Method

UniPT builds a lightweight parallel network with two modules that operate entirely on detached intermediate features from a frozen backbone. The Parallel Interaction Layer maps all N layer features to a unified reduced dimension, then performs a top-down attention where the last layer's output serves as a shared query against each earlier layer's features as keys; ReLU activation, L1 normalization, and an identity-matrix bias produce attention weights that re-weight each layer's tokens to highlight discriminative representations under the guidance of the strongest (last-layer) features. The Confidence Aggregation Layer computes per-layer holistic embeddings via mean pooling, derives input-dependent confidence weights via element-wise multiplication with the last-layer embedding followed by FC + softmax, and produces the final output as a weighted sum of blended features upsampled through an MLP and added residually to the original last-layer output. For CNNs, the interaction is decomposed into spatially-localized pre-interaction (within non-overlapping chunks aligned to the output resolution) and post-interaction to avoid prohibitive cost on large shallow feature maps; for Encoder-Decoder models, the decoder's interaction is similarly split with the encoder-parallel output serving as pre-interaction guidance. The entire parallel network is trained with no gradient flow through the backbone, reducing training memory by roughly 2× compared to LST while matching or exceeding its accuracy across 18 datasets.

## Results

On Flickr30K with BERT backbone, UniPT achieves 510.5 Rsum with only 5.9M trainable parameters and 3.1G training memory, outperforming LST (501.9 Rsum, 7.5M params, 4.6G memory) while remaining competitive with non-memory-efficient methods like SSF (512.8) and AdaLoRA (510.3) that require 8.4–8.8G memory. On the GLUE benchmark with T5-base, UniPT reduces training memory from 17.6G (Fully-FT) to 2.9G while achieving 84.3 average score, and with T5-large it reaches 87.2 average at 9.1G memory, surpassing LST (87.1 at 12.2G). Across VL tasks in Table 3, UniPT consistently outperforms LST by 1.5–3.3% R@1 on retrieval and 1.5–4.4% on grounding, while using roughly half the training memory.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UniPT outperforms LST (the strongest memory-efficient competitor) across nearly all settings: on Flickr30K with BERT, UniPT gains +8.6 Rsum (510.5 vs. 501.9) with 33% fewer parameters and 33% less memory; with ResNeXt-101, it gains +5.4 Rsum (532.1 vs. 526.7) at equal memory. On GLUE with T5-large, UniPT edges out LST by +0.1 average (87.2 vs. 87.1) while using 25% less training memory (9.1G vs. 12.2G). Against non-memory-efficient PETL methods, UniPT is within 0.1–2.3 Rsum of SSF, FacT, and AdaLoRA on Flickr30K-BERT, but at roughly 2.7× lower training memory (3.1G vs. 8.4–8.8G). UniPT slightly trails Fully-FT by 3.0 Rsum on Flickr30K-BERT (510.5 vs. 513.5) and 6.9 Rsum on ResNeXt (532.1 vs. 539.0), but reduces memory from 9.9G to 3.1G and parameters from 109.5M to 5.9M. On the VQA/GQA and visual grounding tasks (Table 3), UniPT consistently surpasses LST by 1.5–4.4% absolute accuracy while halving training memory (e.g., 2.9G vs. 6.4G on VQAv2, 3.4G vs. 6.3G on RefCOCO+).

## Relation to Prior Work

UniPT extends the memory-efficient PETL line initiated by SideTuning, Y-tuning, and most directly LST (Ladder Side Tuning), which introduced a reduced-dimension side network with static gated ladder connections to avoid backpropagating through the full backbone. The key departure is architectural independence: rather than mirroring the backbone's structure at reduced width (as LST does), UniPT employs a parallel interaction module that processes each layer's features detachedly, guided top-down by the last-layer output, and a confidence aggregation module that learns input-dependent fusion weights. This design decouples the method from any specific backbone topology, enabling direct application to CNNs (where spatial/channel dimensions vary across layers) and Encoder-Decoder architectures, whereas LST's one-to-one gated addition is inherently Transformer-centric.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in nearly halving training memory relative to LST (e.g., 2.9G vs. 5.5G on T5-base GLUE) while matching or exceeding its accuracy, making PETL viable on consumer-grade GPUs for large multimodal models. The cross-architecture generality (Transformer, CNN, Encoder-Decoder) addresses a real gap, since most prior memory-efficient PETL methods are Transformer-only. However, a performance gap versus full fine-tuning persists, and the pre/post-interaction decomposition for CNNs increases trainable parameters somewhat, leaving open whether the approach scales cleanly to very large backbones (e.g., T5-3B) or to architectures with highly heterogeneous intermediate representations.
