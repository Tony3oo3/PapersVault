## Problem

Existing automatic colorization methods suffer from grayish, desaturated outputs due to color ambiguity, and their results are essentially fixed—difficult to modify or iteratively refine. The paper addresses both limitations by proposing a framework that first 'imagines' multiple plausible color references (mimicking how human experts mentally generate diverse color options) before colorizing, thereby enabling diverse, controllable, and editable results.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241770/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a single-channel grayscale image (L channel in CIE Lab space) at 512×512 resolution and outputs the corresponding ab chrominance channels. Beyond the final colorized image, the pipeline also produces N diverse reference candidates and a composed refined reference, which serve as the editable interface: users can click on any semantic segment in the result and swap it with an alternative from the reference pool to trigger localized re-colorization without re-running the full pipeline.

## Method

The framework is a three-stage pipeline. First, the Imagination Module uses ControlNet to condition a pre-trained Stable Diffusion model on Canny edges and HED boundaries of the grayscale input (with BLIP2-generated captions), synthesizing N diverse, structurally aligned color reference candidates. Second, the Reference Refinement Module segments the input with Semantic-SAM and, for each segment, selects the best-matching reference candidate by nearest-neighbor search in DINOv2 feature space, composing a single refined reference via per-segment union. Third, the Colorization Module (adapted from UniColor) computes semantic correspondence via VGG19 features to warp reference colors onto the input, applies a coarse-to-fine DBSCAN clustering step to resolve intra-segment color ambiguity, and propagates the refined hint colors through a HybridTransformer decoder. The key architectural insight is decoupling color imagination (diverse reference generation) from color assignment (reference-guided propagation), which is what makes per-segment user editing tractable.

## Results

On COCO-Stuff, the proposed method achieves the highest Colorfulness (CF) score of 47.2, surpassing the next-best baselines CT2 and DISCO (both 46.2) and DDColor (44.7). On ImageNet, it reaches CF 48.8 and the best PSNR of 23.8 dB, while on the In-the-wild set it attains CF 48.9, outperforming all baselines by a clear margin. In a user study with 30 participants, the method is preferred 43.3% of the time versus 26.7% for DDColor, 10.0% for UniColor and CT2, 6.3% for BigColor, and 3.3% for DISCO.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method achieves the best CF (Colorfulness) on COCO-Stuff (47.2 vs. 46.2 for CT2/DISCO and 44.7 for DDColor) and In-the-wild (48.9 vs. 44.5 for DISCO and 41.9 for DDColor), confirming its advantage in producing vivid, photorealistic colors. On ImageNet, Coltran reports a higher CF (55.9) but at the cost of very poor FID (15.5) and PSNR (8.65), indicating its colorfulness comes from unrealistic color distributions; among methods with competitive realism, Ours leads with CF 48.8. DDColor achieves the lowest FID on both COCO-Stuff (6.19 vs. 7.21) and ImageNet (3.16 vs. 3.62), suggesting slightly better distributional fidelity to ground truth, but trails Ours on PSNR (23.1 vs. 23.3 on COCO-Stuff; 23.4 vs. 23.8 on ImageNet) and CF across all three benchmarks. UniColor (Automatic) and BigColor are consistently outperformed on CF by 4–11 points, while GCP, despite strong FID, produces the most muted results (CF 37.9 on COCO-Stuff). In the user study, Ours is preferred at 43.3% versus 26.7% for DDColor, a 16.6-point gap that underscores the perceptual advantage of the imagination-based reference synthesis over dual-decoder approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of two established lines of work: automatic colorization (which produces a single deterministic output) and exemplar-based colorization (which requires a manually selected reference). It builds directly on ControlNet to condition Stable Diffusion on structural cues (Canny edges, HED boundaries) for synthesizing reference candidates, and on UniColor's HybridTransformer pipeline for the final colorization step. The key conceptual departure is the 'imagination module' framing: rather than treating the generative model as the colorizer itself (as in DDColor or GCP), it is repurposed as a reference synthesizer, with a segment-level composition strategy (using DINOv2 features and Semantic-SAM) to assemble an optimal reference from multiple candidates. This explicitly models the coloring samples, enabling iterative and localized edits that neither pure automatic nor pure user-guided methods previously supported.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practical gap in automatic colorization: the inability to iteratively refine or locally modify results without re-running the entire pipeline. By decomposing the problem into reference synthesis + segment-level composition + colorization, it offers a controllable workflow that mimics how human experts actually colorize. Limitations include struggles with crowded scenes containing many identical instances (a known weakness of ControlNet) and an inference cost of ~8 seconds per 512×512 image. The authors note that the 'imagination' concept—harnessing generative priors to synthesize task-specific references—could generalize beyond colorization to other vision tasks, though this remains speculative.
