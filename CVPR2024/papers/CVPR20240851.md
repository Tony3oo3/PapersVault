## Problem

Existing Transformer-based deraining methods operate at a single image scale, ignoring potentially useful multi-scale rain information, while prior multi-scale approaches (coarse-to-fine, multi-patch) propagate estimation errors from coarser to finer scales and fail on complex spatially-varying rain streaks. Additionally, these methods rely on explicit feature representations that are sensitive to input variation rather than capturing the common degradation patterns shared across diverse rainy images.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240851/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single rainy RGB image of arbitrary resolution, internally downsamples it via bilinear interpolation to 1/2 and 1/4 scales, and processes all three scales simultaneously through unequal-depth Transformer branches. The INR sub-branch additionally consumes normalized 2D pixel coordinates (encoded with periodic positional encoding at L=4 frequencies) as MLP inputs to predict per-pixel RGB values. Training uses 256×256 patches with batch size 1, and the final output is a full-resolution derained RGB image.

## Method

NeRD-Rain constructs three unequal Transformer branches (1, 2, and 3 UNets at 1/4, 1/2, and full scale respectively) for scale-specific feature extraction, with each UNet built from Restormer-style Transformer blocks. Between adjacent scale branches, an intra-scale INR branch with two coordinate-based MLPs (coarse and fine feature grids) learns a shared mapping from pixel coordinates to RGB, using a shared encoder that interacts with the Transformer branch to form a closed-loop architecture for capturing common rain degradation representations. An inter-scale bidirectional feedback propagation unit (BFPU) performs element-wise gated fusion of bottleneck features from two UNets at the current fine scale and feeds the result back to the previous coarse scale, enabling both coarse-to-fine and fine-to-coarse information flow. The entire network is trained end-to-end with a hybrid loss combining Charbonnier, frequency, edge, and L1 (for INR) terms, with the INR's natural low-pass filtering property providing an implicit rain-removal mechanism without additional operations.

## Results

NeRD-Rain achieves the best PSNR and SSIM across all five benchmark datasets. On the synthetic benchmarks, it reaches 41.71 dB / 0.9903 (Rain200L), 32.40 dB / 0.9373 (Rain200H), 35.53 dB / 0.9659 (DID-Data), and 34.45 dB / 0.9596 (DDN-Data). On the real-world SPA-Data benchmark, it attains 49.58 dB / 0.9940, surpassing the previous best (DRSformer) by 1.04 dB in PSNR. The lighter variant NeRD-Rain-S (10.53M params, 79.2G FLOPs) also outperforms all prior methods on SPA-Data (48.90 dB) while using fewer parameters than DRSformer (33.65M).

## Baselines & Comparisons

NeRD-Rain outperforms all 15 compared baselines on every dataset. Against the strongest Transformer-based competitor DRSformer [8], it gains +0.48 dB on Rain200L, +0.23 dB on Rain200H, +0.18 dB on DID-Data, +0.10 dB on DDN-Data, and a notable +1.04 dB on the real-world SPA-Data. Compared to other Transformer methods (Restormer, IDT, Uformer), the gains are larger: e.g., +0.72 dB over Restormer and +0.97 dB over IDT on Rain200L. Against the best CNN-based methods (DualGCN, SPDNet, MPRNet), NeRD-Rain leads by 0.98–2.24 dB on Rain200L and 5.40–6.38 dB on SPA-Data. The prior-based methods (DSC, GMM) are far behind, with PSNR gaps exceeding 13 dB on Rain200L. On the real-world RE-RAIN dataset (no ground truth), qualitative evaluation confirms that NeRD-Rain removes spatially-long and random rain streaks more effectively than all compared deep models.

## Relation to Prior Work

NeRD-Rain sits at the intersection of three research threads: multi-scale deraining (coarse-to-fine pipelines like MSPFN and MPRNet), Transformer-based image restoration (Restormer, Uformer, DRSformer), and implicit neural representations (INR) previously applied to compression, super-resolution, and low-light enhancement. The paper departs from prior multi-scale deraining by replacing unidirectional coarse-to-fine propagation with a bidirectional feedback mechanism, and from prior single-scale Transformer derainers by introducing unequal-depth Transformer branches across scales. The most distinctive conceptual move is repurposing INR—typically a per-image fitting tool—as a shared, end-to-end trainable component that learns common rain degradation representations across diverse inputs, with a shared encoder forming a closed loop with the Transformer backbone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 1.04 dB gain on the real-world SPA-Data benchmark over the prior best Transformer method (DRSformer) is practically meaningful for deployment in real rainy conditions, and the framework's extensibility to CNN backbones (Table 6) suggests the bidirectional multi-scale + INR design is not tied to a specific architecture. However, the authors acknowledge that INR-based training is significantly slower than standard multi-scale pipelines, and the method fails on veiling/mist effects, leaving a gap for complex mixed-weather scenarios. An open question is whether the INR's low-pass bias that 'free-rides' deraining can be made more explicit or controllable without sacrificing the generalization benefit.
