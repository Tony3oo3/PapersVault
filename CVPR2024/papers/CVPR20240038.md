## Problem

Ray-tracing-based point cloud rendering achieves high fidelity but is too slow for practical use due to expensive 3D point searching (K-d trees, octrees, uniform grids) and redundant sampling across multiple surfaces along each ray. The paper observes that the primary (first-encountered) surface dominates the rendered output, making subsequent surface sampling largely unnecessary, yet existing methods either sample all surfaces uniformly or rely on fixed K-nearest-point selection that misses primary surface features in sparse regions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a point cloud with per-point 3D positions, RGB colors, and high-dimensional appearance features, along with calibrated camera intrinsics (focal length, principal point). A key design detail is that the number of sample points per ray is not fixed but adapts from 0 to n based on the local point cloud density and the pseudo-UDF distribution, unlike baselines that use a constant K or uniform N samples. The output is a rendered novel-view image at the target camera pose, with the method being plug-and-play with existing ray-tracing renderers (Point-NeRF, Point-SLAM, NPLF, Pointersect).

## Method

HashPoint combines two techniques. First, Hashed-Point Searching replaces 3D spatial partitioning by rasterizing all points onto the 2D image plane, ordering them per pixel in Z-order (Morton code), and storing start-index and count in a hash table keyed by pixel index, yielding O(1) per-pixel lookup; an adaptive search radius proportional to the sample point's distance from the camera origin controls the cone of influence. Second, Adaptive Primary Surface Sampling projects nearby points onto the ray as candidates, computes a pseudo-UDF (mean distance to K neighbors) for each candidate, converts it to a Gaussian confidence α_j, and applies a volume-rendering-style occlusion-aware product weight w_j = α_j ∏(1−α_k). Only candidates with non-negligible weight near the primary surface are retained, so each ray adaptively uses 0 to n sample points rather than a fixed count, dramatically reducing MLP evaluations while preserving or improving accuracy.

## Results

On the Synthetic-NeRF dataset, integrating HashPoint into Point-NeRF achieves PSNR 33.22, SSIM 0.961, and LPIPS 0.055 at 9.60 FPS — an 80× speedup over Point-NeRF (0.12 FPS) while maintaining near-equivalent quality. On Replica, Point-SLAM+Ours reaches PSNR 35.43 and SSIM 0.983, running 1.8× faster than depth-guided sampling and 11.5× faster than uniform multi-surface sampling. On Waymo, NPLF+Ours improves PSNR from 29.96 to 30.57 with a 6× speedup, and on ShapeNet, Pointersect+Ours boosts PSNR from 28.0 to 29.5 with an 8× speedup.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the closest ray-tracing baseline Point-NeRF [62], HashPoint trades a marginal quality drop (PSNR 33.22 vs. 33.31, SSIM 0.961 vs. 0.978) for an 80× rendering speedup (9.60 vs. 0.12 FPS). Compared to rasterization-based methods (NPBG, NPBG++, Huang et al., FreqPCR), Point-NeRF+Ours achieves substantially higher PSNR (33.22 vs. 24.56–31.24) and SSIM, though at lower FPS (9.60 vs. 33–40). On Replica, Point-SLAM+Ours outperforms both NICE-SLAM [69] (PSNR 35.43 vs. 24.42) and the original Point-SLAM [48] (PSNR 35.43 vs. 35.17, SSIM 0.983 vs. 0.975) while being 1.8× faster than depth-guided and 11.5× faster than uniform multi-surface sampling. On Waymo, NPLF+Ours surpasses NPLF [41] on all quality metrics (PSNR 30.57 vs. 29.96, SSIM 0.912 vs. 0.868, LPIPS 0.105 vs. 0.119) and is 6× faster, while also outperforming radiance-based NeRF [36] and DS-NeRF [14]. On ShapeNet, Pointersect+Ours improves over Pointersect [10] in PSNR (29.5 vs. 28.0) and LPIPS (0.02 vs. 0.04) with an 8× speedup, and substantially outperforms the rasterization baseline NPBG++ [46] (PSNR 29.5 vs. 19.3).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two established paradigms in point-cloud neural rendering: rasterization (NPBG, FreqPCR) and ray tracing (Point-NeRF, Point-SLAM, NPLF, Pointersect). It repurposes rasterization not for final image synthesis but as an acceleration structure—projecting all points onto the image plane and organizing them via Morton-code Z-order into a hash table for O(1) lookup—while retaining ray marching for high-fidelity rendering. The key conceptual departure from prior ray-tracing methods is the 'primary surface sampling' principle: rather than uniformly sampling across all surfaces a ray intersects (Point-NeRF) or collecting K nearest points regardless of surface membership (NPLF, Pointersect), HashPoint adaptively samples only on the first surface encountered, guided by a pseudo-UDF confidence and volume-rendering-style occlusion weighting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 80× speedup over Point-NeRF (0.12 → 9.60 FPS) with comparable PSNR, and the 0.5 ms search time for 1M points on a single GPU, make ray-tracing-based point rendering practically viable for near-real-time applications. The primary-surface insight simplifies a problem that prior methods treated as inherently multi-surface, reducing both compute and memory (35 MB vs. 200 MB for 3D Gaussian Splatting on the same scene). Limitations include a dependency on brief geometry refinement (point pruning/growing) for best accuracy, and the finding that in heavily noisy point clouds, K-nearest-point selection can still outperform primary-surface sampling—suggesting the approach may need adaptation for very low-quality inputs. Open questions include scalability to unbounded outdoor scenes and whether the primary-surface assumption holds for highly transparent or multi-layered materials.
