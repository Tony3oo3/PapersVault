## Problem

Existing test-time adaptation methods for object detection either update all network parameters (inefficient and vulnerable to catastrophic forgetting under continual domain shifts) or rely on batch-normalization statistics (inapplicable to Transformer backbones and less effective for detection). Continual test-time adaptation for object detection—where the test distribution evolves over time—remains largely unexplored, with no principled answer to which parameters to update, how to compute the adaptation signal, and when adaptation is actually needed.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequential stream of unlabeled RGB test images (batch size 4) whose distribution shifts over time, together with pre-computed first- and second-order feature statistics (mean and covariance) extracted from a fixed subset of 2,000 training samples at deployment time. It produces standard Faster-RCNN detections—bounding boxes and class labels—while optionally skipping backward passes when the two adaptation criteria indicate the model is already well-adapted, thereby reducing the number of gradient updates to as little as ~12% of total test samples.

## Method

The approach freezes the pre-trained backbone and inserts a shallow low-rank adaptor (down-projection to d/32, ReLU, up-projection back to d) in parallel with every N block, contributing only 0.54–0.89% of total parameters and supporting both CNN (1×1 conv) and Transformer (MLP) blocks. Adaptation is driven by a composite loss that minimizes KL divergence between Gaussian feature distributions at two granularities: image-level (all backbone features) and object-level (RoI-pooled features grouped by predicted class with a log-frequency weighting to counter class imbalance), where test-domain means are tracked via an exponential moving average initialized to the training mean. Two lightweight, parameter-free criteria gate each backward pass: the ratio of the current image-level loss to a pre-computed in-domain KL gap (τ₁ = 1.1) and the ratio of the current loss to its own EMA (τ₂ = 1.05); if neither exceeds its threshold, the model skips adaptation and performs inference only, roughly doubling throughput.

## Results

On COCO → COCO-C (15 corruption types + original), Ours achieves 22.6 avg mAP with Swin-T and 22.2 with ResNet50, improving over Direct-Test (17.7 / 17.3) by up to 4.9%p. On SHIFT-Discrete, Ours reaches 40.4 avg mAP (Swin-T) and 38.7 (ResNet50) vs. Direct-Test's 38.5 and 35.9, with the largest gains in fog (+9.1%p) and dawn (+3.5%p) conditions. On SHIFT-Continuous, Ours improves mAP by up to 7%p over Direct-Test. Ours-Skip reduces backward passes by up to 90.5% (from 80K to 7.6K for ResNet50) while maintaining mAP within 0.3%p, boosting FPS from ~10 to 21.2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Ours outperforms all baselines on every dataset and backbone. Against ActMAD (which aligns BN/LN feature maps), Ours gains +8.7%p avg mAP on COCO-C (Swin-T) and +11.7%p (ResNet50), while ActMAD suffers severe catastrophic forgetting (Org. mAP drops from 43.0 to 36.3 for Swin-T). Against Mean-Teacher (reproducing TeST's teacher-student approach), Ours improves by +5.4%p (Swin-T) and +7.7%p (ResNet50) on COCO-C, and also avoids the 2× forward-pass overhead. NORM and DUA (BN-statistics-only methods, ResNet-only) provide negligible gains (+0.2%p and −0.2%p over Direct-Test on COCO-C), confirming that BN adjustment alone is insufficient for object detection. On SHIFT-Discrete, Ours achieves 40.4 avg mAP (Swin-T) vs. ActMAD's 35.5 and Mean-Teacher's 38.0, with the largest margin in fog conditions (+10.8%p over ActMAD). Ours-Skip matches Ours within 0.3–1.0%p mAP while reducing backward passes by 88–90%, yielding 2× higher FPS than any full-adaptation baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the LoRA/AdaptFormer parameter-efficient adaptor architecture (originally for NLP and vision transformers) into object detection backbones, replacing the full-parameter updates used by prior TTAOD methods (ActMAD, TeST, STFAR) and the BN-layer-only updates used by classification TTA (NORM, DUA, TTT). The adaptation loss extends CAFA's class-aware feature alignment and Su et al.'s anchored clustering to the object-detection setting by adding region-level, class-wise KL-divergence alignment with frequency weighting, while the 'when to update' criterion is novel and draws on EMA-based monitoring rather than any prior CTA mechanism. Conceptually, the paper bridges three previously separate threads—parameter-efficient fine-tuning, distribution-alignment TTA, and continual adaptation—into a single framework for detection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method offers a practical recipe for deploying object detectors in environments with continuously shifting conditions (autonomous driving, surveillance), where full fine-tuning is infeasible and BN-based TTA is architecturally limited. The adaptor's architecture-agnosticism (CNN and Transformer) and the skip criterion's ~2× speedup make it directly applicable to real-time systems. Limitations include evaluation on only Faster-RCNN with two backbones, reliance on pseudo-labels from the model's own RoI classifier for object-level alignment (which can be noisy under severe shift), and the fact that the 'when to update' thresholds, while consistent across backbones, were tuned on a small set of scenarios. Open questions include whether the approach extends to one-stage detectors, multi-modal inputs, or longer-horizon continual settings with more than 15 domain shifts.
