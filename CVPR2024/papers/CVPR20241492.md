## Problem

CLIPScore, the dominant method for pruning noisy web-crawled image-text datasets, suffers from false positives (misaligned pairs receiving high scores due to spurious correlations in CLIP's pretraining) and false negatives (genuinely aligned but 'hard' pairs receiving low scores). These errors degrade the quality of the pruned dataset and, consequently, the zero-shot performance of CLIP models trained on it.

## Input / Output

The method takes a web-crawled image and its associated alt-text label as input. For each image, it generates r synthetic captions (r=8 in the main experiments) via nucleus sampling from a BLIP captioning model, then computes the maximum cosine similarity between the sentence-transformer embeddings of the generated captions and the alt-text (after masking medium phrases like 'an image of'). The output is a scalar alignment score per image-text pair, which is optionally fused with CLIPScore via min-max normalization and a weighted average (α=0.5) to produce a final ranking used to select the top-k% of samples for CLIP pretraining.

## Method

Sieve replaces CLIP's joint image-text embedding comparison with a two-stage text-only similarity check: (1) a captioning model (BLIP, pretrained on only 14M curated image-text pairs) generates multiple captions per image via nucleus sampling, and (2) a lightweight sentence transformer (all-MiniLM-L6v2, further finetuned with self-supervised contrastive learning on unlabeled text) encodes both the generated captions and the alt-text, with the maximum cosine similarity across captions serving as the alignment score. Medium phrases (e.g., 'a photo of') are masked from both sides to prevent spurious similarity inflation. The key insight is that a captioning model trained on small, clean data avoids the spurious correlations that plague CLIPScore, while the sentence transformer's rich semantic embedding space bridges the diversity gap between the limited vocabulary of generated captions and the highly diverse alt-text. Fusing Sieve with CLIPScore via a 50/50 weighted average after independent min-max normalization yields complementary gains, improving average zero-shot accuracy by 2.6% (medium) and 1.7% (large) over CLIPScore alone on the DataComp benchmark.

## Results

On the DataComp benchmark, Sieve+CLIPScore achieves 35.4% average zero-shot accuracy across 38 downstream tasks on the medium scale (128M pool) and 54.6% on the large scale (1.28B pool), surpassing CLIPScore (32.8% and 52.9%) by 2.6% and 1.7% respectively. On retrieval tasks specifically, Sieve alone achieves 28.9% (medium) and 52.0% (large), while Sieve+CLIPScore yields 27.8% and 51.1%, improving over CLIPScore (25.1% and 46.6%) by 2.7% and 4.5%. Sieve also peaks at 20% data retention (34.6% avg) whereas CLIPScore peaks at 30%, indicating Sieve achieves better performance with less data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CLIPScore (the primary baseline), Sieve+CLIPScore wins on average accuracy at both scales (+2.6% medium, +1.7% large) and on retrieval tasks (+2.7% medium, +4.5% large), while Sieve alone achieves the best retrieval scores (28.9% medium, 52.0% large) but slightly trails CLIPScore on average at large scale (52.3% vs 52.9%). Against LAION Filtering, Sieve+CLIPScore leads by a wide margin: +6.2% on medium average (35.4 vs 29.2) and +4.5% on large average (54.6 vs 50.1). Against Basic Filtering (heuristic size/length filters), Sieve+CLIPScore improves by +6.9% on medium (35.4 vs 28.5) and +8.8% on large (54.6 vs 45.8). Notably, Sieve underperforms CLIPScore on OCR-dependent tasks (MNIST, SVHN, Rendered SST-2) and context-based tasks (Country211), but fusing with CLIPScore recovers these losses while retaining Sieve's advantages on retrieval and medical diagnosis tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CLIPScore-based dataset pruning paradigm by introducing a complementary alignment signal derived from image-captioning models (BLIP, GIT) pretrained on small curated datasets, combined with a lightweight sentence transformer for semantic similarity scoring. It departs from prior work in two key ways: (1) rather than relying on a pretrained CLIP model's embedding space—which inherits spurious correlations from noisy web-scale pretraining—it uses a captioning model trained on only 14M curated pairs to generate synthetic descriptions, and (2) rather than replacing alt-text with synthetic captions (as in Nguyen et al., 2023), it uses the captions purely as a scoring signal for pruning while preserving original labels. The approach is positioned as orthogonal to concurrent work like T-MARS (text masking) and DID (multi-filter ensembles), and explicitly avoids dataset-as-prior sampling strategies that risk overfitting to evaluation distributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical finding that a captioning model pretrained on 14M curated pairs produces a better pruning signal than one pretrained on 129M noisy pairs challenges the common assumption that more pretraining data always improves downstream utility, and suggests that data quality is paramount for the pruning signal itself. Sieve also achieves better performance with less data (20% vs. 30% for CLIPScore), which has direct compute implications for large-scale VLM pretraining. However, the method underperforms on OCR-dependent and geolocation tasks, revealing a bias toward visual-concept alignment over contextual or textual content. Open questions include whether the fusion weight (α=0.5) generalizes across different data pools and scales, and whether the approach extends to video-text or audio-text pruning.
