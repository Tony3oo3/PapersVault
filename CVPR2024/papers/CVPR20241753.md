## Problem

Existing multi-illuminant white balance methods directly predict pixel-level illumination via encoder-decoder networks without modeling the underlying lighting structure, violating the linearity constraint of the Lambertian image model and producing unnatural, inconsistent illumination maps. Moreover, these approaches cannot identify individual light sources, making illumination editing or selective white balance impossible.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241753/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a single raw RGB image (H×W) as input and produces a mixed illumination map (R and B channels per pixel, with G normalized to 1) as the primary WB output. Additionally, it outputs K=7 decomposed illuminant chromaticity vectors (2D each) and K pixel-wise weight maps (H×W each), from which the number of active illuminants can be inferred by thresholding the maximum weight-map value at 0.3. The architecture uses 64 latent channels and 3 GRU calibration iterations.

## Method

AID adapts slot attention to illumination decomposition: a U-Net encoder extracts spatial features, and K learnable slot vectors are iteratively calibrated (T=3 GRU steps) via dot-product attention against the feature map, where each slot converges to represent one illuminant's chromaticity. The attention maps directly serve as per-illuminant weight maps, and slot vectors pass through a chromaticity MLP to yield 2D chromaticity vectors; the final illumination map is their linear combination, enforcing the Lambertian mixing constraint. A novel centroid-matching loss pre-computes K-means centroids over the dataset's illuminant distribution, then uses Hungarian matching to assign each ground-truth illuminant to the nearest centroid slot, supervising only the matched slot's chromaticity and weight map with L1 loss. This prevents all slots from being indiscriminately activated and forces each slot to specialize in a specific color gamut.

## Results

On the LSMI cross-camera benchmark, AID achieves a mean angular error (MAE) of 2.04° (median 1.73°), outperforming the previous best LSMI-U (2.31°/1.89°) and TransCC (2.78°/2.15°). With multi-domain learning (AID+MDL), the MAE drops further to 1.93°/1.60°. On the NUS-8 single-illuminant benchmark, AID achieves a geometric mean angular error of 1.21°, surpassing CLCC (1.42°) and FFCC (1.44°). On the MIIW dataset, AID reduces the mixed-illuminant MAE from 4.28° (LSMI-U) to 2.46° (mean).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AID outperforms all nine baselines across every dataset and metric. On LSMI (cross-camera), AID (2.04° MAE) beats TransCC [31] (2.78°) by 0.74°, LSMI-U [27] (2.31°) by 0.27°, and the GAN-based AngularGAN [42] (4.69°) by 2.65°. On the NUS-8 single-illuminant benchmark, AID achieves the best geometric mean (1.21°) versus CLCC [32] (1.42°), FFCC [4] (1.44°), AlexNet-FC4 [24] (1.66°), and CCC [3] (1.73°). On MIIW, AID reduces the mixed-illuminant MAE from 4.28° (LSMI-U [27]) to 2.46°, a 42% relative improvement. The traditional AWB baseline [1] (9.54°) and Patch CNN [8] (4.82°) are substantially outperformed, confirming that the slot-attention decomposition with centroid-matching loss provides a meaningful structural advantage over both classical and prior deep-learning approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes slot attention [Locatello et al., NeurIPS 2020]—originally designed for object-centric representation learning—toward the task of multi-illuminant white balance, where each slot implicitly encodes an individual light source's chromaticity rather than an object's features. It departs from prior multi-illuminant WB methods (encoder-decoder U-Nets, GANs, transformer-based approaches) that directly regress pixel-level illumination maps without enforcing the physical linearity constraint of the Lambertian model. The centroid-matching loss, which uses Hungarian matching to bind slots to pre-computed chromaticity clusters, is a novel training mechanism not present in prior slot-attention or WB literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is illumination decomposability: AID outputs per-illuminant chromaticities and spatial weight maps, enabling post-hoc editing (e.g., warming one light while cooling another) that is impossible with prior end-to-end WB pipelines. This positions the work as a step toward interpretable and controllable image enhancement. The main limitation acknowledged is the fixed number of slots (K=7), requiring a preset that may not match the true number of illuminants; dynamic slot allocation remains an open problem. Broader applicability of slot-based decomposition to other physically-constrained inverse problems (e.g., material decomposition, spectral unmixing) is a natural but unexplored direction.
