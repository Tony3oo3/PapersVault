## Problem

Standard GAN-based image-to-image translation assumes a bijective correspondence between source and target domain classes, but real-world domains often contain unmatchable classes with no equivalent in the other domain. When this assumption is violated, GANs hallucinate spurious instances of unmatchable classes (e.g., toponyms in satellite-to-map translation, tumors in healthy MRI scans) to satisfy the adversarial objective, severely degrading the reliability of translated images. The paper tackles this by repurposing CycleGAN's steganography mechanism—typically viewed as an optimization flaw—as an explicit tool to detect and prevent such hallucinations.

## Input / Output

The model takes a single unpaired image from source domain X (e.g., an aerial photo or T1 MRI slice) and produces a translated image in target domain Y (e.g., a topographic map or FLAIR MRI) that stylistically matches Y while omitting any instances of unmatchable classes. It additionally outputs a per-pixel, per-channel unmatchability mask in feature-space that localizes where target-domain features lack a source-domain counterpart. Training is fully unsupervised, requiring no aligned image pairs or annotations of unmatchable regions, and at inference the model operates identically to a standard image translation generator.

## Method

StegoGAN decomposes CycleGAN's generators into encoder and decoder halves and introduces a small 3-layer CNN (module M) that assigns a per-pixel, per-channel unmatchability score to the intermediate feature map. In the backward cycle, the feature map is split via Hadamard product into matchable and unmatchable components; the generated image is decoded from matchable features only, while reconstruction of the original image combines the unmatchable features with a noise-perturbed re-encoding of the generated image, forcing explicit feature-space steganography rather than pixel-space hiding. In the forward cycle, the generator produces two translations of the source image—one with explicit access to the unmatchable features (removing the incentive to hallucinate them) and one clean—whose difference is penalized only in matchable regions by a consistency loss. An L0.5 norm regularizes the unmatchability masks to be sparse and near-binary, and the entire framework remains unsupervised with no additional data requirements beyond the two unpaired image sets.

## Results

On PlanIGN, StegoGAN achieves the lowest RMSE (22.5) and highest pixel accuracy (66.1% at σ₁=2, 74.8% at σ₂=5), outperforming the next best method SRUNIT by 9.8 RMSE and +17.3/+22.0 accuracy points respectively. On Brats MRI, StegoGAN reduces the per-pixel false positive rate to 1.1% (vs. 15.1% for SRUNIT and 21.9% for CycleGAN) and per-instance FPR to 4.2% (vs. 21.8% and 22.7%), while also achieving the best RMSE (36.3). On GoogleMaps, StegoGAN maintains a 0% false positive rate for highways across all unmatchable ratios (0–65%), whereas all competing methods show increasing false positives as the ratio grows.

## Baselines & Comparisons

StegoGAN outperforms all five baselines on the primary fidelity metrics across both main datasets. On PlanIGN, it beats SRUNIT (the closest competitor) by 9.8 RMSE and +17.3/+22.0 accuracy points, and surpasses CycleGAN by 4.5 RMSE and +51.0 Acc(σ₁). On Brats MRI, the hallucination-prevention advantage is dramatic: StegoGAN's pFPR of 1.1% is roughly 20× lower than CycleGAN (21.9%) and 14× lower than SRUNIT (15.1%), while also achieving the best RMSE (36.3 vs. 42.5 for SRUNIT). On distributional metrics (FID, KID), StegoGAN is competitive but not uniformly best—SRUNIT achieves slightly lower KID on PlanIGN (2.2 vs. 2.4) and GcGAN achieves lower FID on Brats MRI (61.9 vs. 58.5 is actually StegoGAN winning; GcGAN is 61.9). On GoogleMaps, StegoGAN is on par with CycleGAN at 0% unmatchable ratio but remains stable as the ratio increases to 65%, while CUT, DRIT, GcGAN, and SRUNIT all degrade in both fidelity and false-positive rate.

## Relation to Prior Work

StegoGAN builds directly on CycleGAN by decomposing its generators into encoder/decoder components and introducing an explicit feature-space steganography mechanism. It repurposes the steganography phenomenon—originally identified by Chu et al. as an optimization flaw that undermines cycle consistency—as a constructive tool for disentangling matchable from unmatchable information. This contrasts with prior approaches that suppress steganography (via blurring, noise, or compression) or address asymmetric translation through contrastive losses and geometric augmentations, all of which assume a bijective class correspondence. The closest conceptual relative is StegAnomaly, which also exploits steganography for domain analysis but targets anomaly detection rather than translation fidelity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important failure mode—GANs hallucinating spurious tumors in MRI or toponyms in maps—that directly undermines reliability in medical and cartographic applications. The unsupervised unmatchability mask offers an interpretable diagnostic tool for quantifying semantic divergence between arbitrary dataset pairs, a capability with broader utility beyond translation. Key limitations include the subjective nature of 'unmatchability,' the added training complexity (module M plus two hyperparameters), and the GAN-specific formulation; the authors note that adapting the approach to diffusion-based translation remains an open question. The three curated non-bijective benchmarks (PlanIGN, GoogleMaps, Brats MRI) fill a gap in the evaluation landscape for this setting.
