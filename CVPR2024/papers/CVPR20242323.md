## Problem

Deep image transformation methods (enhancement, super-resolution, style transfer) assume pixel-aligned training pairs, but real-world data often exhibits geometric misalignment. Element-wise losses are highly sensitive to even imperceptible shifts, while existing distribution-based losses (e.g., PDL) compute distances in the spatial domain and discard positional information, leading to structural errors in predictions. The paper asks whether a loss can be both misalignment-robust and structurally faithful.

## Input / Output

FDL takes a predicted RGB image and a target RGB image of the same spatial size. A shared pre-trained VGG19 encoder projects both into perceptual feature space at five layers (Relu_1_1 through Relu_5_1); DFT is then applied to each feature map to decompose it into amplitude and phase. The output is a single scalar loss: the sum of Sliced Wasserstein Distances between the amplitude distributions and (weighted by λ) the phase distributions of the two feature sets. The weight λ is task-dependent (e.g., 0.01 for enhancement where texture dominates, 1.0 for super-resolution and style transfer).

## Method

The core idea is to compute distribution distance in the frequency domain of image features rather than the spatial domain, exploiting the fact that frequency representations encode global structural information. Concretely, FDL applies DFT to VGG19 features of the predicted and target images, separates the resulting spectrum into amplitude and phase, and measures the Sliced Wasserstein Distance (SWD) between each pair of component distributions independently. A toy 1-D experiment demonstrates that spatial-domain WD is shift-invariant but structurally blind, whereas frequency-domain WD retains structural fidelity while remaining shift-robust. A mixing experiment further shows that amplitude carries texture/illumination information while phase carries shape/edge information, motivating the separate treatment of both components. The final loss linearly combines the two SWD terms with a tunable weight λ, and the entire pipeline is plug-and-play: it can replace any perceptual loss in existing CNN or Transformer-based restoration architectures without modifying the model itself.

## Results

On the DPED image enhancement benchmark with NAFNet, FDL achieves PSNR 23.05 dB, SSIM 0.811, LPIPS 0.114, DISTS 0.121, and FID 37.5, outperforming PDL (FID 75.1), CTX (FID 74.6), and LPIPS (FID 215.4) across all metrics. On the synthetic strongly-misaligned DIV2K SISR benchmark with NLSN, FDL reaches PSNR 26.70 dB on Urban100, surpassing CTX (25.14 dB) by 1.56 dB and PDL (23.85 dB) by 2.85 dB, while also achieving the best LPIPS (0.137) and FID (7.90). FDL consistently delivers the best FID scores across all tasks and models, indicating superior perceptual quality under geometric misalignment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FDL outperforms all four baselines (CTX, PDL, LPIPS, and Gatys et al.) across every evaluated task and model. Against PDL (the closest spatial-domain distribution-based competitor), FDL gains +0.38 dB PSNR and reduces FID from 75.1 to 37.5 on NAFNet/DPED, and from 22.4 to 7.9 on NLSN/Urban100, confirming the benefit of frequency-domain distribution matching over spatial-domain. Against CTX, FDL improves PSNR by +0.79 dB (NAFNet/DPED) and +1.56 dB (NLSN/Urban100) while also achieving lower LPIPS and DISTS, showing that global frequency information better preserves structural accuracy than patch-matching. LPIPS (Alex) is the weakest baseline, trailing FDL by over 2 dB PSNR and 170+ FID points on NAFNet/DPED, highlighting the fragility of element-wise perceptual losses under misalignment. In style transfer, FDL captures structural style information that both Gatys et al. (color-only) and CTX (local texture-only) fail to transfer, demonstrating the advantage of frequency-domain global information for content-agnostic tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself between two families of misalignment-robust losses: element-wise losses (LPIPS, CTX) that are sensitive to geometric shifts, and spatial-domain distribution losses (PDL, GAN loss) that are shift-invariant but discard structural information. It departs from PDL by relocating the Sliced Wasserstein Distance computation from the spatial feature domain to the frequency domain via DFT, and further decomposes the spectrum into amplitude and phase components treated with separate weights. The frequency-domain global-information argument draws on prior work in focal frequency loss and Fourier domain adaptation, but the specific combination of DFT decomposition + SWD as a training loss for misaligned image transformation is new.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play nature of FDL (swap the loss, keep the architecture) makes it immediately applicable to any image transformation pipeline suffering from imperfect alignment, a common practical bottleneck. The consistent FID improvements (roughly 2× over PDL on DPED) and the clean 1-D toy experiment provide convincing evidence that frequency-domain distribution distance preserves structure while remaining shift-invariant. Open questions include the need for per-task manual tuning of λ, the reliance on a fixed pre-trained VGG19 backbone, and whether the approach extends to tasks where spatial locality is more critical than global structure. The authors themselves suggest future work on attention-weighted frequency regions.
