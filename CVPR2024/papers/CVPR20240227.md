## Problem

Optical flow methods struggle to generalize to real-world scenarios because acquiring large-scale real-world flow datasets requires expensive sensors (LiDAR, GPS, IMU) and manual annotation, while synthetic datasets suffer from domain gaps and self-supervised methods are limited by indirect photometric losses and occlusion artifacts. This paper addresses the data bottleneck by proposing an automated data factory that generates high-quality optical flow supervision from ordinary monocular camera photos alone.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240227/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The data factory takes a sequence of photos captured by a single monocular camera (300 scenes, ~58,800 image pairs total) and produces dense optical flow labels plus corresponding confidence masks. The downstream optical flow network (RAFT or Scale-flow) consumes two RGB images at 384×768 crop resolution and outputs a dense 2D flow field; the Scale-flow variant additionally outputs a normalized scene flow (3D motion + depth change rate). Labels are zeroed out at pixels failing any credibility threshold, so the network only receives supervision on geometrically reliable regions.

## Method

The core pipeline reconstructs each scene with Zip-NeRF (hash-voxel radiance field), renders depth maps at randomly generated camera pose pairs, and projects 3D points between views to compute optical flow labels. A key technical choice is using midpoint depth (the depth at which cumulative transmittance reaches 0.5) instead of the standard expected-value depth, which is more robust to multi-surface or reconstruction-failure artifacts in anti-aliased NeRF. Dynamic foreground is injected via randomly shaped Bezier-curve slices with perspective transforms between frames. Generated labels are filtered by three complementary credibility masks: radiation field confidence (measuring weight-distribution sharpness along each ray), SSIM-based warping accuracy (detecting flow mismatches), and multi-view depth consistency (catching geometric inconsistencies). The filtered labels directly supervise RAFT or Scale-flow trained from scratch, and the resulting models exhibit 'light flow' behavior—faithful to actual pixel motion including rotating wheels and transparent surfaces—rather than the semantic 'object flow' typical of KITTI-supervised methods.

## Results

On KITTI 2015 zero-shot evaluation, Scale-flow trained on ADF58 achieves 3.88 Fl_epe and 13.36 F_all, dramatically outperforming MDFlow trained on Sintel (10.05 Fl_epe, 23.12 F_all). On KITTI 2012, ADF58-trained RAFT reaches 1.59 Fl_epe and 6.43 F_all, competitive with fine-tuned self-supervised methods like UPFlow (1.27 Fl_epe) and UFlow (1.68 Fl_epe). In real-world zero-shot generalization on DAVIS, ADF58-trained Scale-flow achieves 0.21 S_fg_loss and 11.56 P_fg_loss, surpassing the same architecture fine-tuned on KITTI (0.26 and 13.62). For normalized scene flow, ADF58-trained Scale-flow achieves 149.83 Mid_error at 0.2s inference, beating SMMSF (288.99) and approaching traditional stereo methods (115–124) that require 300–3000s.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In zero-shot optical flow evaluation, ADF58-trained methods decisively beat MDFlow (Sintel) by a wide margin: 3.88 vs. 10.05 Fl_epe on K15 and 1.59 vs. 3.49 on K12. Against fine-tuned self-supervised baselines, ADF58 is competitive but not dominant: UPFlow (Km+Kr) still leads on K12 (1.27 vs. 1.59 Fl_epe) and UFlow (Km+Kr) leads on K15 (2.71 vs. 3.88), though ADF58 matches or beats MDFlow (Km) and SMMSF (Km+Kr) on both splits. In the real-world DAVIS zero-shot test (Table 4), ADF58-trained Scale-flow, RAFT, and GMFlow all outperform their KITTI-fine-tuned counterparts by 0.05–0.09 in S_fg_loss and 1.4–4.2 in P_fg_loss, confirming superior real-world generalization. For scene flow (Table 2), ADF58-trained Scale-flow halves the Mid_error of SMMSF (149.83 vs. 288.99) and matches Expansion (Kr) on Fl_epe (3.46 vs. 6.56), while running 1500× faster than PRSM (0.2s vs. 3000s) and 1500× faster than OSF (0.2s vs. 300s). The paper acknowledges that ADF58 underperforms on driving-specific KITTI scenes due to the absence of road footage and Zip-NeRF's inability to reconstruct reflective/transparent car surfaces.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes NeRF-based 3D scene reconstruction (specifically Zip-NeRF) as an automated data generation engine for optical flow training, departing from the two dominant paradigms of synthetic dataset pre-training (Sintel, FlyingThings3D) and self-supervised learning from real video. The key conceptual departure is treating NeRF rendering as a controllable 'data factory' where camera pose pairs yield geometrically exact flow labels, combined with a multi-criteria filtering pipeline (radiation field confidence, SSIM-based matching, depth consistency) that goes beyond the ambient-occlusion checks used in prior NeRF-based generation work. The midpoint depth formulation also corrects a known issue with standard NeRF depth rendering when multiple or erroneous surfaces exist along a ray.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a monocular camera and ~300 casually captured scenes suffice to produce a training set whose zero-shot real-world generalization surpasses supervised methods trained on large-scale synthetic + real data, addressing the core data bottleneck for deploying optical flow in production. However, the evaluation relies on indirect SSIM/photometric metrics on DAVIS rather than ground-truth flow, and the method cannot handle reflective/transparent surfaces or dynamic scenes due to Zip-NeRF's limitations. Open questions include whether dynamic NeRF variants (e.g., 4D Gaussian Splatting) can extend the factory to moving objects, whether the 'light flow vs. object flow' distinction can be reconciled with semantic supervision, and whether the approach scales to the diverse, uncontrolled conditions of autonomous driving.
