## Problem

Existing 3D edge reconstruction methods are limited to straight lines (LIMAP, NEAT) or rely on biased edge density fields that produce inaccurate reconstructions (NEF), leaving a gap for robustly reconstructing both lines and curves in complex real-world scenes. The core challenge is that UDF values are non-monotonic along camera rays, making standard volume rendering biased, and that extracting precise parametric edges from a learned implicit field is non-trivial without a true zero-level set.

## Input / Output

The method takes multi-view posed 2D edge maps (probability values in [0,1] per pixel, generated by pre-trained detectors such as PiDiNet for indoor or DexiNed for outdoor scenes) along with camera intrinsics and extrinsics. It produces a learned 3D UDF field (an 8-layer MLP) and, after a five-stage extraction pipeline, outputs parametric 3D edges represented as line segments (defined by two 3D endpoints) and cubic Bezier curves (defined by four 3D control points), with shared endpoints where edges meet.

## Method

EMAP represents 3D edges via an unsigned distance function (UDF) learned with a vanilla NeRF-style MLP, rendered through a volume-rendering equation that accumulates view-independent density weights along camera rays. The key technical contribution is an unbiased UDF rendering formulation that adapts NeuS's monotonic density function using a differentiable visibility function Ψ(t) to handle the non-monotonicity of UDF values along rays, eliminating the rendering bias present in NEF. Training uses importance sampling (50% rays from edge regions, 50% from non-edge regions) with MSE on rendered edge maps plus an Eikonal loss. Edge extraction proceeds in five stages: voxel-grid point initialization, iterative point shifting along the UDF gradient to converge to the true edge surface, edge direction estimation via SVD on local inverse-normal vectors, direction-guided polyline growth with non-maximum suppression, and final fitting into line segments (RANSAC + PCA) and Bezier curves, followed by dual-criterion merging and 2D-projection-based refinement.

## Results

On the ABC-NEF dataset, EMAP with DexiNed achieves the best completeness (8.9 mm), edge direction consistency (95.4%), recall at τ=5 (56.4%), and F-score at τ=5 (59.1%), while maintaining competitive accuracy (8.8 mm) comparable to LIMAP with SOLD2 (5.9 mm). On the DTU dataset, EMAP attains the highest recall (77.5%) and precision (75.4%) at τ=5 among all baselines. The method also demonstrates strong performance at tighter thresholds, with R10 of 88.9% and F10 of 88.9% on ABC-NEF, substantially outperforming NEF (R10 ≈ 48–62%) and NEAT (which fails entirely on ABC-NEF due to its texture dependency).

## Baselines & Comparisons

EMAP outperforms NEF [70] by a large margin on all metrics: NEF's recall at τ=5 is only 11–12% versus EMAP's 56.4% (DexiNed), and NEF's F5 is 10.8–13.0% versus 59.1%, reflecting NEF's inherent rendering bias and fragile fitting-based post-processing. Against LIMAP [25], EMAP with DexiNed achieves substantially better completeness (8.9 mm vs. 18.7–29.6 mm) and recall (56.4% vs. 36.2–64.2%), while LIMAP retains a slight accuracy edge (5.9 mm with SOLD2 vs. 8.8 mm); this gap stems from LIMAP's inability to reconstruct curves, which limits its completeness. NEAT [64] fails entirely on ABC-NEF due to its dependence on textured objects, but on DTU it achieves R5=66.4% and P5=73.9%, still below EMAP's 77.5% and 75.4%. On DTU, EMAP achieves the best recall and precision simultaneously, whereas LIMAP trades precision for recall (74.9% R5 but only 66.1% P5) due to redundant line segments, and NEF produces noisy, scene-sensitive outputs. Overall, EMAP is the only method that consistently handles both lines and curves across all four datasets without requiring textures or scene-specific tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

EMAP builds directly on the neural implicit representation paradigm (NeRF, NeuS, NeuralUDF) and the most recent 3D edge reconstruction efforts, particularly NEF (which used a dedicated edge density field with volume rendering) and NEAT (which used SDF-based volumetric wireframe parsing). The key conceptual departure is the use of an Unsigned Distance Function (UDF) to represent edges rather than SDF or edge density, combined with an adaptation of NeuS's unbiased rendering to handle the non-monotonicity of UDF values along camera rays. The parametric extraction pipeline—especially the iterative point shifting and SVD-based direction estimation—draws from occupancy network refinement and line detection literature but is assembled into a unified algorithm that handles both line segments and Bézier curves, unlike prior methods that addressed only one type.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By unifying line and curve reconstruction in a single neural implicit framework, EMAP addresses a practical gap: real-world scenes (indoor, outdoor, CAD) contain both edge types, yet prior methods were forced to choose. The learned UDF field also serves as a useful geometric prior for dense surface reconstruction, as demonstrated by the MonoSDF initialization experiment. Limitations include dependence on the quality of the 2D edge detector, the need for posed multi-view input, and the lack of ground-truth edges on large-scale scene datasets (Replica, Tanks & Temples), which restricts quantitative evaluation to qualitative comparisons. Open questions include whether the approach extends to dynamic scenes, whether the UDF representation can be made more efficient for very large environments, and whether the parametric extraction can be made fully differentiable for end-to-end optimization.
