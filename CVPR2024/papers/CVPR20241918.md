## Problem

Traditional pedestrian trajectory predictors treat coordinate sequences as continuous numerical signals regressed by encoder-decoder networks, limiting their ability to capture high-level social reasoning. While language models excel at contextual understanding and social reasoning, they have not been effectively applied to numerical trajectory prediction because standard text tokenizers mishandle decimal coordinates, lack precision for numerical data, and cannot extrapolate time-series sequences. This paper asks whether a language model, properly adapted, can outperform dedicated numerical regressors on trajectory prediction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241918/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 8 frames (3.2 s) of 2D pedestrian coordinates for all agents in a scene plus a top-down RGB scene image, and produces 12 frames (4.8 s) of future coordinates for a target pedestrian. Numerically, coordinates are rounded to two decimal places and serialized as bracketed text strings (e.g., [(6.73, 6.34), (6.94, 6.50), …]); the scene image is converted to a natural-language caption via BLIP-2. Outputs are generated as text and parsed back into coordinates: a single most-likely path via beam search (deterministic) or K=20 diverse paths via temperature-sampled decoding (stochastic).

## Method

LMTraj recasts trajectory prediction as a question-answering task over a T5 encoder-decoder language model. Raw coordinates and image captions are wrapped into QA prompt templates (context = all agents' histories + scene description; question = 'What trajectory does pedestrian n follow?'; answer = future coordinate string). A custom BPE tokenizer is trained on these trajectory prompts so that integer and decimal parts of numbers are cleanly separated, reducing token count and preserving sequential numeric structure. Five auxiliary multi-task QA prompts (destination, direction, similar-pattern, group-membership, collision-risk) are jointly trained to inject social-reasoning knowledge. At inference, beam search (depth d=2) yields the deterministic path, while temperature tuning (τ=0.7) over the token probability distribution generates multimodal futures, mirroring the deterministic/stochastic split of conventional predictors.

## Results

On the ETH/UCY benchmark (deterministic, K=1), LMTraj-SUP achieves an average ADE/FDE of 0.48/0.88 m, outperforming the previous best (NPSN at 0.52/1.08 m) by a clear margin. For stochastic prediction (K=20), it reaches 0.22/0.32 m average ADE/FDE, matching or exceeding all prior methods. On the drone-view SDD and Grand Central Station (GCS) datasets, LMTraj-SUP sets new state-of-the-art with 17.5/34.5 and 16.9/34.8 px (deterministic) and 7.8/10.1 and 7.1/9.6 px (stochastic), respectively. The zero-shot variant LMTraj-ZERO with GPT-4 achieves 0.39/0.79 m average ADE/FDE on ETH/UCY without any training, approaching supervised baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LMTraj-SUP achieves the best average ADE/FDE across all 17 compared baselines on both deterministic (0.48/0.88 m) and stochastic (0.22/0.32 m) ETH/UCY evaluation, with the largest gains on ETH (0.65 vs. 0.88 m ADE for GP-Graph) and HOTEL (0.26 vs. 0.29 m for EigenTrajectory). However, it underperforms on the ZARA1 and ZARA2 subsets in both settings (e.g., 0.51 vs. 0.40 m ADE on ZARA1 deterministic vs. GP-Graph; 0.17 vs. 0.13 m on ZARA2 stochastic vs. SocialVAE), suggesting the language-based approach struggles with the dense, highly interactive Zara scenes. On the drone-view SDD and GCS datasets, LMTraj-SUP clearly dominates all baselines in both deterministic and stochastic settings (e.g., 7.8/10.1 vs. 8.1/11.7 px on SDD stochastic vs. SocialVAE). Among zero-shot methods, LMTraj-ZERO with GPT-4 (0.39/0.79 m avg) outperforms GPT-3.5 (0.58/1.01 m) and all algorithmic baselines (Linear 0.52/1.14, Kalman 0.51/1.11), approaching the supervised Social-STGCNN (0.45/0.75 m stochastic). The computational cost is competitive: 1401 MB GPU memory and 18.3 ms inference per trajectory, comparable to PECNet (1733 MB, 57 ms) and far lighter than AgentFormer (9639 MB, 8.2 ms).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant numerical regression paradigm in pedestrian trajectory prediction (Social-LSTM, Social-GAN, Trajectron++, AgentFormer, etc.) by recasting the task as a sequence-to-sequence question-answering problem solved by a language model. The closest prior work is Kuo et al. [45], which uses linguistic intermediate representations for trajectory prediction, but LMTraj goes further by converting the entire input-output pipeline into text prompts, training a custom numerical BPE tokenizer, and introducing multi-task QA for social reasoning. It also draws on the prompt-engineering line of work for time-series forecasting (PromptCast [131], ForecastQA [38]) and repurposes standard NLP components (T5 seq2seq, beam search, temperature sampling) for a spatially grounded numerical task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a frozen GPT-4 with prompt engineering can match supervised trajectory predictors in zero-shot settings, suggesting that the social-reasoning capacity of large language models transfers to spatial forecasting without task-specific training. For the supervised variant, the custom numerical tokenizer and multi-task QA template yield consistent SOTA across ETH/UCY, SDD, and GCS, but inference is slower (18.3 ms vs. 8.2 ms for AgentFormer) and the image understanding is indirect (via BLIP-2 captions rather than direct visual features). Open questions include whether this paradigm scales to 3D or multi-agent dense scenes, whether the gains come from genuine social reasoning or from the seq2seq architecture itself, and whether the approach remains competitive as dedicated trajectory models continue to improve.
