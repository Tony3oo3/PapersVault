## Problem

Existing image forgery detection and localization (IFDL) methods rely on either macroscopic semantic features (e.g., ImageNet-pretrained encoders) or microscopic noise artifacts, but neither alone generalizes well to unseen tampering types or social-media laundering. The paper argues that effective forensics should target mesoscopic properties—intermediate-scale cues that survive post-processing yet remain semantically meaningful—and that no prior method explicitly enforces this focus through its training procedure.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241862/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image resized to 512×512 and produces three outputs: an image-level binary forgery classification score, a pixel-level tampering mask (authentic vs. manipulated), and multi-scale edge prediction probability maps (one per decoder level, upsampled to full resolution) that delineate tampered-region boundaries. During the self-supervised pre-training stage, no forgery labels are used; the model is trained purely on the denoising objective. During fine-tuning, a fixed diffusion timestep t_f=5 is used for the time embedding rather than sampling over the full [1,1000] range.

## Method

DiffForensics is a two-stage encoder-decoder framework. In Stage 1, a SegFormer encoder pre-trained on ADE20K is frozen to preserve macroscopic semantic features, while a U-Net decoder is trained via a self-supervised denoising diffusion objective using Simplex noise (ν=2⁻⁶, N=6, γ=0.8) instead of standard Gaussian noise; this structured corruption makes the decoder learn to 'repair' edge-like anomalies, steering the combined model toward mesoscopic representations. In Stage 2, both encoder and decoder are fine-tuned with a multi-task loss combining weighted binary cross-entropy + Dice for pixel-level localization, weighted BCE for image-level detection, and a novel Edge Cue Enhancement Module (ECEM) applied at all three decoder scales. The ECEM computes absolute horizontal and vertical differences of feature maps to amplify boundary cues, then a 3×3 convolution and sigmoid produce per-scale edge probability maps supervised with a multi-scale weighted Dice loss that assigns higher weight to finer-grained edges (1/2^(k-1) scaling). This coarse-to-fine edge supervision progressively refines tampered-region boundaries while the frozen-encoder/frozen-decoder weight split prevents catastrophic forgetting of macroscopic features.

## Results

On the localization task, DiffForensics achieves the best average pixel-level F1 of 0.466 and AUC of 0.862 across eight datasets, substantially outperforming the next-best method MVSS-Net (F1 0.303, AUC 0.773). On the detection task, it attains the best average AUC of 0.916 and lowest EER of 0.131, while achieving the second-best ACC of 0.770 (behind PSCC-Net at 0.788). Notably, on the Columbia dataset the method reaches an F1 of 0.912 and AUC of 0.931, and on AutoSplicing (DGM) it achieves F1 0.507 and AUC 0.940, though HiFi-Net edges it on AutoSplicing F1 (0.613).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DiffForensics outperforms all ten baselines on average localization F1 (0.466 vs. 0.303 for MVSS-Net, the strongest competitor) and average AUC (0.862 vs. 0.773), with particularly large margins on IMD2020 (F1 0.511 vs. 0.295 for PSCC-Net) and DSO-1 (F1 0.485 vs. 0.318 for PSCC-Net). On detection, it achieves the best average AUC (0.916) and lowest EER (0.131), surpassing MVSS-Net (AUC 0.867, EER 0.193) and PSCC-Net (AUC 0.870, EER 0.176), though PSCC-Net retains a slight ACC advantage (0.788 vs. 0.770). The one notable loss is on AutoSplicing localization F1, where HiFi-Net (designed specifically for DGM forgeries) scores 0.613 versus DiffForensics' 0.507, though DiffForensics still leads on AutoSplicing AUC (0.940 vs. 0.831). On the Columbia dataset, DiffForensics achieves a dominant F1 of 0.912, exceeding the next-best CAT-Net (0.854) by nearly 6 points. Overall, the method demonstrates consistent superiority across both traditional editing and deep generative model forgeries, with the mesoscopic feature strategy providing robustness gains under JPEG compression and Gaussian noise perturbations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself within the IFDL literature by adopting the mesoscopic framing from MesoNet and operationalizing it through a two-stage architecture: a frozen ADE20K-pretrained SegFormer encoder (macro) paired with a U-Net decoder trained via a DDPM-based denoising pretext (micro). It departs from prior self-supervised IFDL methods (ManTra-Net, SPAN, CAT-Net) that learn micro features through manipulation-specific pretext tasks, instead repurposing the denoising diffusion objective from generative modeling and the Simplex noise formulation from Anoddpm as a general-purpose micro-feature learner. The Edge Cue Enhancement Module with multi-scale weighted supervision is a new architectural component not present in prior IFDL pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work demonstrates that a diffusion-based pre-training stage can serve as a general micro-feature extractor for forensics without requiring manipulation-specific supervision, which is a practical advantage over task-specific pretext designs. However, the 'mesoscopic' narrative remains somewhat informal—the paper does not rigorously characterize what distinguishes mesoscopic from macro or micro features, nor does it test whether the gains persist under more aggressive social-media laundering (e.g., multiple JPEG recompressions, resizing, cropping). Open questions include whether the diffusion pre-training stage is truly necessary versus a strong regularizer, and whether the approach extends to video or multi-modal forgery detection.
