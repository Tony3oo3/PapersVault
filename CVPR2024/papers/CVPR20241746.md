## Problem

Standard NeRF assumes static scenes with constant illumination and negligible attenuation, making it unsuitable for underwater environments where three coupled dynamics coexist: distance-dependent visibility from water absorption, unstable illumination that varies across viewpoints and time, and moving marine objects. Existing underwater NeRF methods like SeaThru-NeRF model only the attenuation effect while ignoring illumination changes and dynamic content, producing blurry and floater artifacts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241746/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes underwater monocular video clips (4 in-the-wild sequences) or sparsely captured image sequences (SeaThru dataset), with camera poses estimated via structure-from-motion and motion masks generated by a pre-trained optical flow model. It produces per-scene novel view renderings at up to 1920×1080 in under 30 seconds, and additionally enables scene editing by removing the water medium parameters (drained view) or substituting the static branch with a pre-trained above-water NeRF to transfer water effects to terrestrial scenes. Training completes in approximately 45 minutes on a single RTX 3090.

## Method

The core idea is to treat water as a semitransparent volumetric object with a wavelength-dependent density triplet σ_w (RGB), integrated into the volume rendering transmittance alongside object densities, so that attenuation is modeled in 3D rather than as a 2D image blending prior. The scene is decomposed into a static branch (MLP-S with hash encoding), a dynamic branch (MLP-D conditioned on time), and a shared illumination field f_I(x,d,t) that outputs a scalar exposure λ. To handle unstable illumination without gradient explosion, all colors are represented in log space so that exposure adjustment becomes addition (ψ(λ + c) = c̃), followed by a learned tone mapper ψ(·) to recover non-linear camera colors. A mixed progressive sampling strategy combines uniform ray samples (for the water medium) with multi-resolution grid samples (for objects), and a two-stage training schedule first optimizes static structure, water, and illumination jointly, then refines dynamic objects only in front of the reconstructed static scene. An entropy loss penalizes ambiguous object density along rays where visibility is low, encouraging the network to concentrate density on actual surfaces rather than spreading it through the water volume.

## Results

On the proposed underwater dataset (4 monocular videos), the full method achieves the best PSNR on 3 of 4 scenes (CORAL 26.17, TURTLE 28.10, COMPOSITE 25.09) and the best SSIM and LPIPS on all 4 scenes. On the SeaThru dataset in the static setting, the simplified 'Proposed-T' variant outperforms SeaThru-NeRF on PSNR and LPIPS across all four scenes (e.g., 30.03 vs 29.27 PSNR on CURAÇAO). In a user study with 122 participants, the proposed method receives the highest average ratings on realism (4.46/5), fidelity (4.77/5), and multi-view consistency (4.29/5), substantially ahead of SeaThru-NeRF (3.56, 2.29, 1.71) and Instant-NGP (3.13, 3.64, 3.92).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SeaThru-NeRF [15], the proposed method wins decisively on the dynamic scenes: on CORAL it gains +2.28 dB PSNR and on COMPOSITE a massive +8.88 dB, while on the SeaThru static scenes the simplified Proposed-T variant edges out SeaThru-NeRF on PSNR and LPIPS in 3 of 4 scenes (narrowly losing PSNR by 0.12 dB on JAPANESE). Against Instant-NGP [25], the proposed method dominates on all scenes except SARDINE, where Instant-NGP leads by a marginal 0.15 dB in PSNR; however, the proposed method still wins SARDINE on SSIM (.7226 vs .6485) and LPIPS (.4541 vs .4674). Against DynamicNeRF [11], the gap is the largest: the proposed method outperforms it by 2.8–8.8 dB PSNR across all four scenes, confirming that a generic dynamic NeRF without water-specific modeling is inadequate for underwater content. In the user study, the proposed method is preferred over both SeaThru-NeRF and Instant-NGP on all three criteria (realism, fidelity, multi-view consistency), with the most dramatic margin over SeaThru-NeRF on fidelity (4.77 vs 2.29) and MVC (4.29 vs 1.71), reflecting the floater artifacts that plague the latter.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the underwater NeRF line initiated by SeaThru-NeRF [15], which modeled wavelength-dependent attenuation but treated illumination as static and ignored moving objects. It also builds on DynamicNeRF [11] for handling dynamic content, but departs from its assumption of constant illumination and negligible medium attenuation. The key conceptual departure is treating water as a volumetric semitransparent object with an RGB density triplet (grounded in radiative transfer) rather than a 2D blending prior, and introducing a log-space illumination field with a learned tone mapper to disentangle exposure changes from geometric motion—something neither prior underwater nor dynamic NeRF methods addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By jointly modeling all three underwater dynamics (distance-dependent attenuation, unstable illumination, and moving objects), the paper enables not only higher-fidelity reconstruction but also practical scene editing such as draining water or transferring water effects to above-water scenes, with applications in filmmaking and VR. The log-space illumination trick (converting multiplicative exposure to additive log-radiance) is a reusable insight for any NeRF variant dealing with variable lighting. Open questions include handling rapid water currents, the instability of SFM pose estimation in turbid media, and whether the two-stage static-then-dynamic scheme generalizes to scenes where static and dynamic content are deeply interleaved.
