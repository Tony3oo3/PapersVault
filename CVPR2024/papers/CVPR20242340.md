## Problem

SNNs are widely praised for energy efficiency, but comparisons with ANNs are typically unfair because ANNs are not subjected to equivalent optimization (quantization, pruning). The paper asks whether a T-step SNN is fundamentally different from a T-bit quantized ANN, and whether the conventional Synaptic Operations (SOPs) metric adequately captures the true computational cost of SNNs when weight precision and spike patterns are considered.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242340/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework accepts both static RGB images (ImageNet 224×224, CIFAR-10/100 32×32) and asynchronous event-based sensor streams (DVS-CIFAR10, DVS128 Gesture, N-Caltech101) and outputs class labels. The key design variable is not the input format but the Bit Budget allocation: how total representational bits are split among time steps (T), spike-pattern bit-width (b_s), and weight bit-width (b_w), with the constraint that their product BB = T·b_w·b_s is held fixed for fair comparison.

## Method

The paper establishes a formal equivalence showing that a T-step SNN's temporal spike integration is structurally identical to a T-bit quantized ANN's bit-wise accumulation, unifying both under a single computational model. It then introduces the Bit Budget (BB = T·b_w·b_s) as a more atomistic replacement for SOPs, validated by a linear correlation with measured synaptic energy on FPGA. Two hardware-independent metrics—S-ACE (for general compute) and NS-ACE (incorporating firing rate for neuromorphic hardware)—are defined to quantify total arithmetic cost. The central empirical finding is that, under a fixed Bit Budget, shifting bits from time steps toward spike-pattern bit-width (and weight quantization) consistently improves accuracy on static images, while neuromorphic data benefits from a moderate balance (e.g., T=4 with multi-bit spikes). FPGA experiments on the xck26 platform confirm that different bit allocations with equal Bit Budget yield nearly identical throughput, validating the theoretical equivalence.

## Results

On ImageNet, the proposed Quantized Spikformer-8-512 with 8-bit weights, 8-bit spike patterns, and 1 time step (bit budget = 32) achieves 79.37% top-1 accuracy with only 210.1G S-ACE, surpassing the unquantized Spikformer-8-768 (74.81%, 1197G S-ACE) by 4.56% while using ~5.7× less computational effort. On CIFAR-100, the 4/4/1 allocation (bw/bs/T) reaches 80.13% accuracy with 12.32G S-ACE, outperforming the 16/1/4 baseline (78.21%, 59.10G S-ACE) by 1.92% at ~4.8× lower cost. On neuromorphic DVS128 Gesture, the 1/2/8 allocation achieves 98.48% accuracy, exceeding the 16/1/16 baseline (98.3%) while reducing the bit budget from 256 to 16.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed quantized SNNs consistently outperform all six baselines on ImageNet while using substantially lower computational budgets. Quantized Spikformer-8-512 (8/8/1, bit budget 32) reaches 79.37% accuracy, beating Spikformer [44] (74.81% at bit budget 64) by 4.56%, Spiking ResNet [13] (72.75%) by 6.62%, TET [6] (68.00%) by 11.37%, and Hybrid training [29] (61.48%) by 17.89%. Quantized SEW ResNet-34 (8/8/1, bit budget 64) achieves 70.13%, surpassing the original SEW ResNet [7] (67.04%) by 3.09% and tdBN [42] (63.72%) by 6.41%, while reducing parameters from 21.79M to 10.89M. Notably, the quantized methods achieve these gains at equal or lower S-ACE/NS-ACE values, demonstrating that the Bit Budget framework enables better accuracy-per-operation trade-offs than the conventional SOP-based approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new conceptual ground by unifying two previously separate research threads—spiking neural networks and quantized ANNs—demonstrating that a T-step SNN is computationally equivalent to a T-bit quantized ANN. It departs from the conventional SNN evaluation paradigm (Synaptic Operations) by introducing the 'Bit Budget' metric, which jointly accounts for time steps, weight bit-width, and spike pattern bit-width. The work repurposes quantization techniques well-established in the ANN literature (weight quantization, activation quantization) and applies them systematically to SNNs, while also drawing on burst neuron models to motivate multi-level spike patterns. It explicitly positions against the common practice of benchmarking SNNs against unoptimized ANNs, arguing this comparison is fundamentally unfair.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This reframing challenges the field's prevailing narrative that SNNs are inherently more energy-efficient than ANNs, showing that the apparent advantage largely dissolves when ANNs are given equivalent quantization budgets. The Bit Budget framework and S-ACE/NS-ACE metrics provide a practical, hardware-agnostic tool for fair comparison and for guiding resource allocation in SNN design. Open questions include whether the framework extends to more complex architectures (e.g., recurrent or attention-based SNNs), how it interacts with emerging neuromorphic hardware that may break the linear bit-budget-to-energy assumption, and whether truly brain-inspired SNN designs (as opposed to ANN-paradigm SNNs) can escape the quantized-ANN equivalence the paper identifies.
