## Problem

Existing activation-based OOD detection methods uniformly rectify anomalous activations across all penultimate-layer channels, implicitly assuming every channel contributes equally to OOD detection. However, empirical evidence reveals substantial variation in per-channel OOD detection performance, and discarding uninformative channels can significantly improve detection. The key challenge is identifying which channels are most useful for OOD detection without access to OOD data at test time.

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR benchmarks, 224×224 for ImageNet-1K) and processes them through a pre-trained CNN backbone to extract penultimate-layer activations of dimension M. It then applies a binary pruning matrix B ∈ ℝ^{M×C} to zero out low-discriminability channels before computing the OOD score (default: Energy score via logsumexp of classifier logits). The final output is a scalar OOD score that, compared against a threshold λ calibrated to retain 95% of ID data, yields a binary ID/OOD decision.

## Method

DDCS consists of two modules. First, the Channel-level Anomalous activations Pre-rectifying (CAP) module truncates per-channel activations into a local Gaussian high-density region [μ−λσ, μ+λσ] and applies a global threshold t to cap extremely high values, preventing anomalous activations from corrupting downstream discriminability estimation. Second, the Channel Selection (CS) module computes class prototypes as the mean of CAP-processed features per training class, then scores each channel k by combining inter-class cosine similarity S_k and inter-class variance V_k into a discriminative score J_k = a·S_k − (1−a)·V_k; the K channels with the lowest J_k (i.e., lowest similarity and highest variance) are retained via a pruning matrix. The key insight is leveraging the positive correlation between ID class discriminability and OOD separability, so that channels most discriminative for multi-class ID classification are also most informative for OOD detection. The selected-channel activations are then fed into any downstream OOD score (Energy, MSP, etc.), making DDCS a plug-and-play preprocessing step.

## Results

On the ImageNet-1K benchmark (ResNet-50 backbone), DDCS achieves an average FPR95 of 19.36% and AUROC of 95.55%, outperforming the strongest prior method LINE (20.69% / 95.03%) and the next-best activation-based method VRA (25.49% / 94.57%). On CIFAR-10 (DenseNet-101), DDCS reaches 13.57% FPR95 and 97.22% AUROC, and on CIFAR-100 it achieves 32.83% FPR95 and 90.31% AUROC. The largest gains over LINE appear on iNaturalist (11.63% vs. 22.52% FPR95) and Textures (18.40% vs. 28.54% FPR95) in the ImageNet-1K setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DDCS outperforms all 11 baselines on average FPR95 and AUROC across CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet-1K. Against the closest competitor LINE [1], DDCS reduces average FPR95 by 1.33 points on ImageNet-1K (19.36 vs. 20.69) and by 1.18 points on CIFAR-10 (13.57 vs. 14.75), with the largest per-dataset gains on iNaturalist (−10.89 FPR95) and Textures (−10.14 FPR95). However, DDCS loses to LINE on the Places OOD dataset in the ImageNet-1K setting (28.78 vs. 12.24 FPR95; 92.89 vs. 97.56 AUROC), indicating that the channel selection strategy is less effective for scene-level OOD data. Compared to activation-only methods (ReAct, BATS, VRA), DDCS consistently achieves 5–12 points lower FPR95, confirming that channel selection provides a substantial boost over uniform activation rectification. Against confidence-based and distance-based baselines (MSP, ODIN, Mahalanobis, Energy), DDCS improves FPR95 by 30–68 points, highlighting the advantage of activation-aware methods. The generalization experiments further show DDCS improves over LHAct by 18.30% FPR95 on MobileNetV2 and works with both MSP and Energy scores, confirming plug-and-play compatibility.

## Relation to Prior Work

DDCS sits at the intersection of activation-based OOD detection (ReAct, BATS, VRA) and pruning-based methods (LINE, DICE), but departs from both in a key way: rather than rectifying or pruning activations uniformly across all channels, it selectively retains only channels with high ID discriminability. The CAP module directly inherits BATS's typical-set truncation (local) and ReAct's threshold-based clipping (global), while the channel selection criterion draws on CIDER's observation that ID discriminability positively correlates with OOD separability. Unlike LINE, which uses class-specific Shapley-value-based pruning, DDCS applies a single global channel mask derived from inter-class similarity and variance, making it simpler and avoiding LINE's inference-time ambiguity for OOD samples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a subset of channels suffices (and that discarding low-discriminability channels improves detection) challenges the default assumption in activation-based methods that every channel must be rectified, offering a practical plug-and-play improvement that generalizes across backbones (DenseNet, ResNet, MobileNet) and OOD scores (Energy, MSP). Limitations include reliance on a static channel mask computed from training data, evaluation restricted to image classification, and the untested assumption that ID discriminability remains a reliable proxy for OOD separability under near-OOD or domain-shift settings. Open questions include whether dynamic or input-dependent channel selection could further improve performance, and whether the approach extends to non-visual modalities or sequential data.
