## Problem

Existing data reconstruction attacks on Split Learning either require the server to behave maliciously (easily detected and halted by monitoring mechanisms) or rely on unrealistic assumptions such as knowing the client's model architecture or having access to a subset of private training data. This paper asks whether a semi-honest server, with only access to public auxiliary data from the same domain, can still effectively reconstruct a client's private training data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241570/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The server consumes two inputs: (1) smashed data (intermediate feature tensors) streamed from the victim client at each training iteration, and (2) a public auxiliary dataset (e.g., CINIC-10 or FFHQ) that shares the same semantic domain but has a different distribution from the private data. The output is a set of reconstructed RGB images approximating the client's private training samples, evaluated via SSIM, PSNR, and LPIPS. Notably, the server does not know the client's architecture, hyperparameters, or any private data, and the auxiliary set may even lack entire semantic categories present in the private set.

## Method

FORA exploits the observation that smashed data encodes a client's unique 'representation preference'—its characteristic feature-extraction behavior. The attack proceeds in three phases: (1) Substitute Model Construction, where the server trains a substitute client F̂_c in parallel with the victim using a domain Discriminator (adversarial loss) and Multi-Kernel Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MK-MMD) to align the substitute's feature space with the target's; (2) Attack Model Training, where an inverse network of transposed convolutions and Tanh activations is trained on the auxiliary data to map F̂_c's outputs back to raw images; (3) Private Data Reconstruction, where the inverse network is applied to a snapshot of the target client's final-iteration smashed data to recover the private samples. The key differentiator is that the substitute client is built purely from feature-level transfer learning on smashed data, requiring no knowledge of the client's structure or any private data.

## Results

On CelebA at layer 2, FORA achieves PSNR 17.11, SSIM 0.476, and LPIPS 0.381, outperforming PCAT (PSNR 12.05, SSIM 0.316, LPIPS 0.653) by 1.42× in PSNR and UnSplit (PSNR 8.70, SSIM 0.170, LPIPS 0.747) by 1.97× in PSNR. On CIFAR-10 at layer 2, FORA reaches PSNR 22.19 and SSIM 0.830, compared to PCAT's 17.29/0.642 and UnSplit's 10.48/0.101. FORA maintains superiority across all split points (layers 1–4) on both datasets in LPIPS, the metric most aligned with human perception, with the sole exception of CIFAR-10 layer 4 where PCAT slightly edges FORA in SSIM and PSNR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FORA dominates both semi-honest baselines across nearly all settings. Against UnSplit, FORA achieves roughly 2× higher PSNR on CelebA (17.11 vs. 8.70 at layer 2) and 2.1× on CIFAR-10 (22.19 vs. 10.48), while UnSplit's SSIM values remain below 0.17 in all configurations, indicating near-complete failure. Against PCAT, FORA gains 5.06 dB PSNR on CelebA layer 2 (17.11 vs. 12.05) and 4.90 dB on CIFAR-10 layer 2 (22.19 vs. 17.29), despite PCAT having access to 5–10% of the actual private training data—a privilege FORA does not enjoy. The sole exception is CIFAR-10 layer 4, where PCAT slightly outperforms FORA in SSIM (0.121 vs. 0.030) and PSNR (11.08 vs. 10.45), though FORA still wins on LPIPS (0.628 vs. 0.676). Against the malicious FSHA attack, FORA achieves comparable reconstruction quality in unprotected settings, but FSHA is promptly detected and halted by the Gradients Scrutinizer defense, rendering its reconstructions incomplete, whereas FORA operates undetected throughout training.

## Relation to Prior Work

FORA departs from prior semi-honest data reconstruction attacks on Split Learning (UnSplit, PCAT) by eliminating their reliance on client architecture knowledge or partial private data access. Conceptually, it repurposes domain adaptation tools (domain discriminator and MK-MMD) from the transfer learning literature to measure and minimize 'representation preference' distance between a substitute client and the target client, rather than directly optimizing input-output matching as in UnSplit or leveraging task-specific pseudo-client construction as in PCAT. The malicious FSHA attack shares the goal of data reconstruction but operates under a fundamentally different threat model that is detectable by gradient monitoring (GS), whereas FORA maintains indistinguishability from normal training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FORA demonstrates that Split Learning's core privacy guarantee is vulnerable even under a minimal-knowledge semi-honest server, challenging the assumption that hiding client architecture and data is sufficient protection. The representation preference insight provides a new vulnerability characterization that is orthogonal to existing defenses, and the paper shows that standard countermeasures (differential privacy, distance correlation minimization, noise obfuscation) offer only partial mitigation at significant utility cost. Open questions include whether the attack generalizes to multi-party SL, non-visual modalities, or settings where no same-domain auxiliary data is available, and whether adaptive defenses can specifically target the representation preference channel without degrading model utility.
