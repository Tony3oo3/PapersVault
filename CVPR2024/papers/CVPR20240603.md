## Problem

NAS methods are predominantly developed and evaluated on a small set of well-known benchmarks (CIFAR-10, ImageNet, NAS-Bench), which risks overfitting search strategies to problems whose solutions are already known. This undermines the core promise of NAS—removing the need for domain experts—since it does not test whether discovered architectures generalize to genuinely novel, unseen problems. The paper addresses this gap by introducing eight purpose-built datasets and a competition framework that evaluates NAS on data withheld from participants during development.

## Input / Output

Inputs span a wide range of formats and channel counts: 3×28×28 (AddNIST, MultNIST), 1×24×24 (Language), 3×64×64 (CIFARTile), 1×27×18 (Gutenberg), 1×64×128 (Isabella spectrograms), 3×60×60 (GeoClassing satellite patches), and 12×8×8 (Chesseract board encodings). Outputs are single class labels with varying cardinality (3–20 classes per dataset). Notably, data shapes and split sizes were deliberately chosen to mimic familiar benchmarks (e.g., 50,000 training images to resemble CIFAR-10), and the 12-channel Chesseract format broke many hardcoded pipelines that assumed 1 or 3 channels.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is the design and release of eight datasets split into Type-1 (solvable by a human with domain knowledge, e.g., AddNIST requires summing three MNIST digits) and Type-2 (nearly impossible for humans without task-specific tools, e.g., Language encodes letter-frequency patterns of ten languages into 24×24 binary grids). Baseline experiments train seven standard CNNs (ResNet-18, AlexNet, VGG16, ConvNext, MNASNet, DenseNet, ResNeXt) under a unified SGD + cosine-annealing protocol, and three NAS methods (PC-DARTS, DrNAS, Bonsai-Net) are run in the DARTS and Bonsai search spaces alongside random-search baselines. A 24-hour competition then tasks participants with building NAS algorithms on a small set of 'development' datasets and submitting them for evaluation on unseen 'evaluation' datasets, with dataset identities and properties deliberately obfuscated. Results show that NAS methods outperform the ResNet-18 baseline on seven of eight datasets, but competition submissions designed for unseen data often match or exceed traditional NAS methods, suggesting current NAS approaches do not generalize as expected.

## Results

Across the eight introduced datasets, NAS methods (PC-DARTS, DrNAS, Bonsai-Net) found the best-performing architecture on 7 of 8 datasets, with the exception of Chesseract where a competition submission (62.98%) surpassed both the best CNN (59.60%, DenseNet) and best NAS method (60.76%, Bonsai-Net). The most dramatic gap between NAS and CNN baselines appears on CIFARTile, where PC-DARTS reaches 92.28% versus the best CNN (DenseNet) at 51.28%. On the Type-2 datasets (Language, Gutenberg, Isabella, GeoClassing), all methods achieve moderate-to-high accuracy (45–96%), while Type-1 datasets (AddNIST, MultNIST) are near-saturated with NAS methods exceeding 97%. Competition participants, given only 24 hours and three development datasets, matched or exceeded traditional NAS methods on three datasets, suggesting that approaches designed for unseen data generalise better.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among CNN baselines, AlexNet leads on AddNIST (94.87%), MultNIST (94.01%), and Gutenberg (45.53%), while DenseNet-161 dominates on Isabella (63.27%), GeoClassing (94.21%), Chesseract (59.60%), and CIFARTile (51.28%). ResNeXt-50 is the clear outlier on Language at 93.97%, nearly 7% above the next-best CNN. VGG16 collapses to near-random on CIFARTile (24.43% vs. 25% random), and ConvNext underperforms across all datasets (38–73%), suggesting its inductive biases do not match these problem structures. Among NAS methods, PC-DARTS achieves the top score on four datasets (AddNIST 96.60%, Language 90.12%, CIFARTile 92.28%, GeoClassing 94.61%), while Bonsai-Net wins on AddNIST (97.91%) and Chesseract (60.76%). DrNAS trails on CIFARTile (81.08%, ~10% below PC-DARTS) but leads on MultNIST (98.10%). Notably, random search in the DARTS space outperforms both PC-DARTS and DrNAS on AddNIST, Chesseract, and Isabella, corroborating prior findings that these search spaces offer limited advantage over random sampling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard NAS evaluation paradigm, which has relied heavily on CIFAR-10, ImageNet, and pre-computed benchmark suites (NAS-Bench-101, NAS-Bench-201, NATS-Bench). Rather than proposing a new search algorithm, it repurposes existing NAS methods (PC-DARTS, DrNAS, Bonsai-Net) and off-the-shelf CNNs as baselines to stress-test generalization on eight bespoke datasets constructed from MNIST, CIFAR-10, BigEarthNet, Project Gutenberg, and the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum. The conceptual lineage draws on the 'no free lunch' theorem and prior observations (Mehta et al., 2022) that strong performance on one NAS benchmark does not transfer to another, but the paper operationalizes this concern through a structured challenge format with deliberately withheld evaluation data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides concrete evidence that current NAS methods do not reliably generalize to unseen data, with random search frequently matching or exceeding dedicated NAS algorithms across multiple datasets. This challenges the implicit assumption that NAS methods have learned transferable architecture-design principles rather than overfitting to a handful of benchmark distributions. A key open question the authors themselves flag is the 'rolling problem': once these eight datasets become widely known, they too will be subject to overfitting, necessitating a continuous pipeline of novel evaluation data. The Type-1/Type-2 taxonomy (human-solvable vs. human-impossible) offers a useful framework for future NAS evaluation design.
