## Problem

Semi-supervised and self-supervised learning methods for medical image classification have been developed in isolation, with no fair head-to-head comparison under realistic resource constraints. Prior benchmarks either omit hyperparameter tuning entirely or tune on validation sets far larger than the training set—settings that do not reflect how practitioners with scarce labeled data actually operate. This paper asks: can hyperparameter tuning be effective with realistically-sized validation sets, and when all methods are tuned fairly, which semi- or self-supervised method delivers the most reliable accuracy gains?

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes 2D medical images at four resolution tiers (28×28 TissueMNIST/PathMNIST, 112×112 grayscale TMED-2 echocardiograms, 384×384 RGB AIROGS fundus images) paired with a small labeled set (30–1000 images per class) and a much larger unlabeled set (thousands to hundreds of thousands of images). The model outputs a C-dimensional probability vector over class labels, evaluated primarily via balanced accuracy to handle natural class imbalance, with AUROC, partial AUROC, and sensitivity-at-95%-specificity additionally tracked on AIROGS for clinical utility.

## Method

The paper proposes a unified experimental protocol (Alg. D.1) that frames all three paradigms—supervised, semi-supervised, and self-supervised—under a single objective (Eq. 1) differing only in loss weights and training phases. For each of 13 SSL methods plus 3 supervised baselines, it performs serial random search over method-specific and shared hyperparameters on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU with a fixed wall-clock budget (25–100 hours depending on dataset size), using validation sets that are never larger than the labeled training set. CNN backbones (ResNet-18, ResNet-50, WideResNet-28-2) are trained with Adam and cosine LR schedules, with early stopping after 20 epochs of validation plateau. The protocol records best-so-far checkpoints every 30–60 minutes across 5 random seeds, enabling accuracy-over-time profiling. The key empirical finding is that MixMatch achieves the highest median balanced-accuracy gain (+4.7 pts) and is the only method never to underperform the best labeled-only baseline by more than 1 point across all four datasets.

## Results

Across 4 medical datasets (TissueMNIST, PathMNIST, TMED-2, AIROGS) with 30–1000 labeled images per class, MixMatch delivers the most reliable gains over the best labeled-set-only baseline, with a median improvement of +4.7 percentage points in balanced accuracy and a worst-case deficit of only −0.2 points. On PathMNIST, the top methods (CoMatch +14.1, DINO +12.6, MixMatch +12.4, BYOL +12.1) substantially outperform the 69.8% reference; on AIROGS, MixMatch achieves +5.7 points over the 71.5% reference. On TMED-2, nearly all SSL methods fail to beat the 94.9% MixUp baseline, with only FixMatch (+0.2) and MixMatch (−0.2) remaining competitive. The study confirms that hyperparameter tuning with realistic-sized validation sets (never larger than the train set) is effective for all 16 methods tested.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MixMatch is the standout method: it is the only algorithm that never falls more than 1 percentage point below the best labeled-set-only baseline across all four datasets, and it achieves the highest median gain (+4.7 pp). On PathMNIST, CoMatch (+14.1) and DINO (+12.6) slightly edge out MixMatch (+12.4), while on TissueMNIST, DINO (+6.5), BYOL (+6.2), and SimCLR (+5.9) outperform MixMatch (+3.7). On AIROGS, MixMatch (+5.7) clearly beats SimCLR (+1.6) and BYOL (+0.9), while FixMatch (−4.5) and FlexMatch (−4.2) underperform the baseline. The paper notes this ranking contrasts sharply with the USB benchmark [76], where FixMatch and FlexMatch were ranked above MixMatch, underscoring the sensitivity of method rankings to evaluation protocol. Weaker semi-supervised methods (Pseudo-Label, Mean Teacher) consistently fail to beat the labeled-only baseline on most datasets, and self-supervised methods like MOCO (−18.8) and SwAV (−10.1) suffer large drops on TMED-2 due to its uncurated unlabeled set.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by being the first to benchmark semi-supervised and self-supervised methods together on an equal footing for medical image classification, whereas prior benchmarks (Oliver et al., Su et al., USB, Ericsson et al.) each focused on only one paradigm. It departs from the prevailing evaluation practice of using validation sets larger than the training set or skipping hyperparameter tuning entirely, instead enforcing a unified protocol with realistic validation sizes, equal compute budgets (one A100 GPU, fixed wall-clock hours), and serial random search for all 13 methods. The unified objective formulation from Chen et al.'s survey provides the mathematical framing that makes the cross-paradigm comparison coherent.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For resource-constrained medical imaging labs, this 20,000+ GPU-hour study provides actionable guidance: MixMatch is the most reliable SSL method across diverse tasks, and hyperparameter tuning with realistic-sized validation sets is both effective and necessary. The finding that most self-supervised methods actually degrade performance on TMED-2 (an uncurated, distributionally shifted unlabeled set) highlights an important practical caveat. Open questions include whether conclusions hold for ViT-based backbones, more extreme few-shot regimes, and whether the protocol sensitivity (e.g., MixMatch ranking differently from USB's FixMatch/FlexMatch) reflects fundamental method differences or evaluation artifacts.
