## Problem

Animating a static sketch traditionally requires extensive manual keyframing, skeletal annotations, or reference motions, making it inaccessible to non-experts. Existing pixel-based image-to-video methods struggle to preserve the abstract, sparse nature of sketches, and skeleton-based approaches are limited to human figures with predefined motions. No prior method can animate a single-subject vector sketch from a free-form text prompt alone, without any human annotations or explicit motion references.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240881/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single-subject sketch represented as a set of cubic Bézier curves (each with 4 control points, totaling N points) and a free-form text prompt describing the desired motion. It outputs a short animation of k frames in the same vector format, where each frame is a set of N control points with learned 2D displacements applied. The vector output is resolution-independent and directly editable (e.g., dragging control points, changing stroke styles), and a differentiable rasterizer converts frames to pixel space only for loss computation during optimization.

## Method

The core contribution is a 'neural displacement field' — a small network that maps initial control points to per-frame 2D displacements, trained via Score-Distillation Sampling (SDS) to extract motion priors from a pretrained text-to-video diffusion model (ModelScope) without any fine-tuning or training data. The key architectural insight is decomposing motion into two parallel branches: a local path (unconstrained MLP predicting per-point offsets for small deformations) and a global path (predicting a per-frame affine transformation matrix parameterized by scaling, shear, rotation, and translation applied uniformly to all points). This separation prevents the SDS loss from distorting the sketch shape to match the video model's semantic prior while still enabling large coordinated movements. User control is provided through per-transformation scaling parameters (λ_t, λ_r, λ_s, λ_sh) that allow attenuating specific motion components. The entire pipeline is optimization-based (~1000 steps, ~30 min on one A100), requiring no dataset and easily adaptable to different text-to-video backbones.

## Results

On a 30-sketch evaluation set (10 each from humans, animals, and objects), the proposed method achieves 0.965±0.003 sketch-to-video consistency (CLIP cosine similarity) and 0.142±0.005 text-to-video alignment (X-CLIP), outperforming all pixel-based baselines on both metrics. The method surpasses VideoCrafter (the strongest baseline) by 0.089 in consistency and 0.018 in alignment. A user study with 31 participants over 30 video pairs confirms that the full method is preferred for both sketch preservation and motion quality over all ablation variants.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed vector-based method wins decisively on sketch-to-video consistency against all three open-source pixel-based baselines: ZeroScope (0.754), ModelScope (0.779), and VideoCrafter (0.876), achieving 0.965. On text-to-video alignment, it also outperforms VideoCrafter (0.142 vs. 0.124), the only baseline jointly conditioned on both image and text. Gen-2 (Runway) is compared qualitatively only; it either fails to animate the sketch or converts it into a photorealistic image, losing the sketch domain entirely. The skeleton-based Animated Drawings method [Smith et al.] better preserves sketch shape but requires per-sketch manual annotations and is restricted to a fixed set of human motions, so it cannot generalize to non-human subjects or novel actions. The paper notes that ModelScope's text-to-video model serves as the diffusion backbone for the proposed method, yet the vector representation still yields a 0.186 consistency gain over using ModelScope directly in pixel space, underscoring the importance of the vector formulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Score-Distillation Sampling (SDS) loss from DreamFusion (originally for 3D shape optimization) to extract motion priors from a pretrained text-to-video diffusion model, applying them to a vector sketch representation inherited from CLIPasso. It departs from pixel-based image-to-video methods (ZeroScope, ModelScope, VideoCrafter, Gen-2) that struggle to preserve sketch characteristics, and from skeleton-based animation approaches (Smith et al.) that require per-sketch annotations and are limited to human figures. The key architectural departure is the neural displacement field that separates motion into an unconstrained local MLP branch and a constrained global affine-transformation branch, a design motivated by the observation that SDS alone produces jitter and shape deformation when both scales of motion are handled uniformly.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work demonstrates that text-to-video models, even when they cannot generate sketches directly in pixel space, still encode semantically meaningful motion priors that can be transferred to abstract vector representations via SDS — a finding that broadens the applicability of video diffusion priors beyond their native domain. The vector output is resolution-independent and editable, making it practically useful for designers who need to iterate on animated sketches. Key limitations remain: the method assumes single-subject sketches, faces a trade-off between motion expressiveness and sketch fidelity (the diffusion prior tends to 'correct' atypical drawings), and inherits biases and blind spots of the underlying text-to-video model. Open questions include extending to multi-object scenes, supporting diverse vector sketch representations (e.g., C1-continuous curves), and finding better mechanisms to decouple motion from shape correction.
