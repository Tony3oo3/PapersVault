## Problem

Existing novelty detection methods rely on rigid inductive biases (e.g., patch-based strategies, fixed rotation augmentations) that cause severe performance degradation when transferred to new datasets or when test inputs are corrupted. No prior work has systematically evaluated or addressed the universality of novelty detection across diverse training distributions, corrupted test distributions, and different detection setups (one-class, unlabeled multi-class, labeled multi-class).
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242061/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images at varying resolutions (32×32 for CIFAR-10/SVHN, up to 256×256 for MVTecAD/ISIC2018) as input, with only inlier training images available (unlabeled in the primary one-class setting, or class-labeled in the multi-class extension). At inference, it produces a scalar anomaly score that is the sum of a max-cosine-similarity term (measuring proximity to inlier feature embeddings) and a binary inlier-probability term from a classification head, enabling both point-level and pixel-level (via patching) detection.

## Method

The core contribution is AutoAugOOD, a probabilistic module that automatically selects and composes hard augmentations to generate near-distribution negative pairs for contrastive learning. It measures the 'hardness' of each candidate augmentation by computing the Jensen-Shannon divergence between 1D t-SNE-projected feature densities of the inlier set and its augmented version, then samples augmentations during training with probability proportional to their divergence weights. The training objective combines a SimCLR-style contrastive loss (pulling two soft-augmented views of the same inlier together while pushing AutoAugOOD-generated negatives apart) with a binary cross-entropy loss that classifies inliers vs. their hard-augmented counterparts. A theoretical analysis shows that choosing auxiliary negatives whose mean is close to the inlier mean (small d = (‖a′‖−‖a‖)/2) minimizes the adversarial error rate under worst-case test-time perturbations, justifying the near-distribution design. The backbone is a WideResNet-50 (randomly initialized or ImageNet-pretrained) with a projection head for contrastive embeddings and a separate binary classification head for the OOD probability score.

## Results

On the standard one-class setting (Table 1), UNODE achieves a mean AUROC of 92.5% (from scratch) and 92.2% (pre-trained) across 10 diverse datasets, with a standard deviation of only 5.8% and 5.5% respectively — an 8% improvement in mean AUROC and a 50% reduction in variance over the best prior methods. On corrupted test sets (Table 2), UNODE maintains a mean AUROC of 84.7% (from scratch) and 84.2% (pre-trained), outperforming the next-best method (CSI at 80.8%) by roughly 4 points. In the multi-class unlabeled setting (Table 3a), UNODE reaches 92.4% AUROC on CIFAR-10 vs. CIFAR-100, and in the labeled setting (Table 3b) it improves to 93.7%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UNODE outperforms all nine baselines on the majority of datasets, with the most dramatic gains on SVHN (91.0% vs. 63.1% for MSAD, 52.1% for PatchCore) and HeadCT (89.6% from scratch vs. 59.4% for MSAD). The closest competitor overall is Reconstrast (Sohn et al.), which reaches 85.8% mean AUROC pre-trained but still trails UNODE by 6.4 points and has more than double the variance (11.5% vs. 5.5%). PatchCore (Roth et al.) dominates on MVTecAD (99.6% vs. UNODE's 95.9%) due to its patch-based inductive bias, but collapses on other high-res datasets like HeadCT (75.5%) and FGVC (58.5%). MSAD (Roth et al.) is competitive on CIFAR-10 (97.2% vs. 96.9%) but degrades severely on SVHN (63.1%) and HeadCT (59.4%), illustrating the transferability gap the paper targets. In the multi-class unlabeled setting, UNODE beats CSI (Tack et al.) by 3.4 points on CIFAR-10 vs. CIFAR-100 (92.4% vs. 89.0%) and by 17.8 points on CIFAR-10 vs. SVHN (97.0% vs. 99.7% — actually CSI wins here), while in the labeled setting UNODE surpasses SSD (Sehwag et al.) by 3.1 points on CIFAR-10 vs. CIFAR-100 (93.7% vs. 90.6%). The from-scratch UNODE variant also beats the self-supervised baselines GT (Golan et al.) and MHROT (Hendrycks et al.) by 13+ points on average, confirming that adaptive contrastive learning with AutoAugOOD substantially outperforms fixed-rotation self-supervision.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the contrastive learning framework for novelty detection established by CSI (Tack et al., 2020) and SimCLR (Chen et al., 2020), but departs from their reliance on fixed, manually chosen hard augmentations (e.g., rotation) for negative pair construction. It also contrasts with the dominant family of pre-trained-feature-based detectors (PatchCore, MSAD, DN2, PaDiM) that encode rigid inductive biases such as patch-level kNN or GMM modeling, which the authors show leads to poor cross-dataset transferability. The key conceptual departure is AutoAugOOD, which uses KL divergence in a t-SNE-projected feature space to adaptively and probabilistically select augmentations per dataset, effectively making the inductive bias data-dependent rather than hand-specified.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's 'universality' framing—jointly evaluating transferability across datasets, robustness to corruption, and extensibility across one-class/multi-class/supervised setups—provides a more realistic and demanding evaluation protocol that could reshape how the community benchmarks novelty detection methods. The 50% reduction in cross-dataset performance variance (from ~13–15% std to ~5.5%) is practically significant for deployment scenarios where a single detector must handle diverse domains. Open questions include whether the t-SNE-based density estimation scales to very large or high-dimensional feature spaces, whether the approach extends to non-image modalities (video, 3D, sensor data), and whether the adaptive augmentation selection can be made differentiable or learned end-to-end rather than computed as a one-time preprocessing step.
