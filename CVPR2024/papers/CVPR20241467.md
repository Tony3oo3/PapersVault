## Problem

Bayesian Neural Networks provide principled uncertainty quantification but are notoriously unstable and computationally expensive to train on large DNNs, while existing scalable alternatives (Deep Ensembles, last-layer Laplace) either incur high training/inference costs or restrict uncertainty signals to high-level features. The paper addresses the gap of converting pre-trained deterministic DNNs into BNNs in a post-hoc manner with minimal architectural changes and only a few fine-tuning epochs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241467/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard RGB images at dataset-specific resolutions (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 224×224 for ImageNet, variable for segmentation datasets) and produces class probabilities, pixel-wise segmentation masks, and per-sample uncertainty estimates. At inference, each ABNN copy is evaluated with L independently sampled Gaussian perturbations (ε ~ N(0,1)) across all normalization layers, and the final prediction is the average over M ABNN copies × L samples, yielding both a point prediction and a spread that quantifies epistemic uncertainty.

## Method

ABNN replaces every normalization layer (Batch, Layer, or Instance) in a pre-trained DNN with a Bayesian Normalization Layer (BNL) that injects a multiplicative Gaussian perturbation into the scale parameter: BNL(h) = (h − μ̂)/σ̂ × γ(1 + ε) + β, where ε ~ N(0,1). Only the γ and β vectors of these BNLs are fine-tuned for a small number of epochs using a MAP loss augmented with a class-weight perturbation term that biases different ABNN copies toward different class subsets, preventing collapse into the same local minimum. Multiple ABNN copies (typically 3–4) are trained sequentially from the same pre-trained checkpoint, each exploring a different mode of the posterior. The key insight is that applying stochasticity to the latent normalization space rather than to weight matrices reduces gradient variance, yielding more stable backpropagation than classical variational BNNs while preserving the predictive accuracy of the original DNN.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-50, ABNN achieves 95.0% accuracy, 0.160 NLL, 1.0% ECE, and 17.5% FPR95 for OOD detection, matching or exceeding all single-model and lightweight ensemble baselines while training in only 2.0 hours (vs. 6.8 h for Deep Ensembles). On the MUAD semantic segmentation benchmark, ABNN achieves 61.96 mIoU and 21.68% FPR95, substantially outperforming Deep Ensembles (58.29 mIoU, 37.60% FPR95). On ImageNet with ViT, ABNN attains 80.6% accuracy and 55.1% FPR95, beating Laplace (70.6% FPR95) and Deep Ensembles (60.3% FPR95) on OOD detection while maintaining competitive calibration (4.32% ECE).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ABNN is most competitive with Deep Ensembles [51], the primary state-of-the-art baseline: on CIFAR-10/ResNet-50 it trails by ~1% FPR95 (17.5 vs 15.5) and ~0.5% AUPR (96.5 vs 97.0) but trains in 2.0 h versus 6.8 h; on MUAD segmentation it actually surpasses Deep Ensembles on both mIoU (61.96 vs 58.29) and FPR95 (21.68 vs 37.60). Against Laplace [12], ABNN wins on FPR95 (17.5 vs 18.8 on CIFAR-10; 55.1 vs 70.6 on ImageNet/ViT) and ECE (1.0 vs 1.3; 4.32 vs 10.8), while Laplace retains a slight edge on accuracy. ABNN clearly outperforms MC Dropout [26] (FPR95 17.5 vs 43.8), MCBN [80] (FPR95 17.5 vs 20.1), BatchEnsemble [87] (FPR95 17.5 vs 20.1, and 3× faster training), MIMO [30] (FPR95 17.5 vs 26.0), and LPBNN [25] (FPR95 17.5 vs 54.9) on CIFAR-10 OOD detection. On semantic segmentation, ABNN also beats TRADI [23] on StreetHazards (AUPR 7.85 vs 6.93, FPR95 32.02 vs 38.26) and BDD-Anomaly (AUPR 5.98 vs 4.54, ECE 14.03 vs 16.61). The overall picture is that ABNN matches Deep Ensembles within 1–2% on most metrics at roughly one-third the training cost, and decisively outperforms all other baselines on OOD detection quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

ABNN sits at the intersection of three research threads: variational BNNs (Blundell et al., 2015), which provide the reparametrization trick here repurposed from weight matrices to normalization-layer parameters; last-layer post-hoc methods (Daxberger et al., 2021), which establish the principle of adapting a pretrained deterministic network into a Bayesian one with minimal retraining; and BatchEnsemble (Wen et al., 2019), which already parameterizes stochasticity in normalization layers but requires full training from scratch. The key departure is that ABNN applies the stochastic perturbation only to the γ and β vectors of existing normalization layers (rather than to convolutional weights or a single final layer), then fine-tunes just those parameters for a handful of epochs starting from a pretrained checkpoint. This positions ABNN as a middle ground: more stochastic parameters than last-layer Laplace, far fewer than full variational BNNs, and with the practical advantage of inheriting the pretrained encoder's representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical appeal is clear: any pretrained ConvNet or ViT with normalization layers can be converted into an uncertainty-aware BNN with a few lines of code and a few fine-tuning epochs, avoiding the training instability and multi-day compute of full BNNs or Deep Ensembles. However, ABNN does not uniformly outperform Deep Ensembles—on several OOD metrics (e.g., FPR95 on CIFAR-10 with WRN-28×10) it trails by a meaningful margin—and the class-weight perturbation in the loss, while empirically helpful, lacks a rigorous non-convex justification. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very large foundation models (e.g., ViT-L/16 or beyond), how sensitive the method is to the choice of normalization type, and whether the M×L forward passes at inference can be further reduced without sacrificing uncertainty quality.
