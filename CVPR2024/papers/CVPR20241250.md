## Problem

Existing methods for generating novel dynamics from 3D scenes rely on intermediary geometric representations (surface meshes, cage meshes, tetrahedralizations) that create a fundamental disconnect between the simulated geometry and the rendered appearance. This multi-stage pipeline introduces resolution mismatches and artifacts, contradicting the natural coupling of physical behavior and visual appearance in real materials. The paper addresses this by asking whether a single representation can simultaneously drive both physics simulation and photorealistic rendering.

## Input / Output

The method takes a static 3D Gaussian Splatting scene reconstructed from ~150 multi-view RGB images (via COLMAP for camera parameters), user-specified material parameters (density, Young's modulus, Poisson ratio, and a choice of plasticity model: elastic, von Mises, Druker-Prager, or Herschel-Bulkley), and a manually selected simulation region normalized to a unit cube of edge length 2. The output is a time-evolving set of 3D Gaussians whose centers, covariance matrices, and spherical harmonic orientations are updated per time step, directly renderable via standard GS splatting at real-time frame rates (25–36 FPS on an RTX 3090 for simple scenes).

## Method

PhysGaussian treats each 3D Gaussian kernel as a Material Point Method particle, so the same set of Gaussians discretizes both the continuum for simulation and the scene for rendering. The key kinematic insight is that the MPM deformation gradient F_p directly yields the deformed Gaussian covariance a_p(t) = F_p A_p F_p^T, guaranteeing the kernel remains Gaussian under local affine deformation without any mesh extraction. Spherical harmonic orientations are rotated via the polar decomposition R_p of F_p, applied as an inverse rotation on view directions to avoid modifying the hard-coded GS rendering pipeline. The MPM solver uses C¹ B-spline kernels for two-way particle-grid transfer with forward Euler integration, and an optional internal filling step populates hollow objects using the reconstructed opacity field to ensure physically realistic volumetric behavior. An anisotropy regularizer added during GS training constrains the major-to-minor axis ratio of kernels to prevent burr artifacts under large deformations.

## Results

On the lattice deformation benchmark (PSNR, higher is better), PhysGaussian outperforms all three baselines across all six test cases (Wolf/Stool/Plant × Bend/Twist), achieving 26.96/26.46 on Wolf, 31.15/26.15 on Stool, and 25.81/23.87 on Plant. The largest margins are on the Stool and Plant scenes, where PhysGaussian leads by 4.4–6.0 dB over the best baseline. The method also achieves real-time rendering at 25–36 FPS for several material types (plane, toast, jam) on a single RTX 3090 GPU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PhysGaussian wins on all six test cases against all three baselines. Against NeRF-Editing [51], which relies on an extracted surface mesh, PhysGaussian gains up to 5.96 dB (Plant Bend: 25.81 vs 19.85) and 4.79 dB (Plant Twist: 23.87 vs 19.08), largely because NeRF-Editing's mesh-based deformation struggles with complex geometries and produces void borders. Against Deforming-NeRF [47], which uses cage-mesh interpolation, PhysGaussian leads by 4.83–7.91 dB (e.g., Stool Bend: 31.15 vs 22.32), as the cage-based smooth interpolation filters out fine local details. Against PAC-NeRF [18], the closest competitor, PhysGaussian achieves a modest +0.05 dB on Wolf Bend but a substantial +4.89 dB on Stool Twist (26.15 vs 21.26) and +5.96 dB on Plant Bend, reflecting PAC-NeRF's lower rendering fidelity for complex textures. The paper attributes its advantage to leveraging both zero-order (deformation map) and first-order (deformation gradient) information from each lattice cell, enabling accurate Gaussian kernel deformation without mesh extraction.

## Relation to Prior Work

PhysGaussian unifies two previously separate pipelines—3D Gaussian Splatting for rendering and Material Point Method for physics simulation—by using the same set of 3D Gaussian kernels as both the discrete particles in MPM and the splatting primitives for rendering. This departs from prior dynamic NeRF/GS methods (NeRF-Editing, Deforming-NeRF, PAC-NeRF) that require extracting meshes, constructing cage meshes, or embedding visual geometry into a separate simulation geometry. The key technical novelty is using the deformation gradient F from continuum mechanics to analytically deform Gaussian covariance matrices (F·A·Fᵀ) and rotate spherical harmonic bases via polar decomposition, rather than learning or approximating these transformations. The simulation backbone builds directly on the MPM implementation from Zong et al. [53], while the rendering pipeline follows Kerbl et al. [16].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the meshing/embedding step, PhysGaussian removes a major source of fidelity loss and workflow complexity in physics-based 3D content generation, enabling direct simulation of scenes reconstructed from real photos with the 'what you see is what you simulate' principle. The demonstrated versatility across elastic, plastic, fracture, granular, and viscoplastic materials in a single framework is practically valuable for animation and VFX pipelines. Key limitations include the absence of shadow evolution, manually specified material parameters, and no support for liquids; the authors identify automatic parameter estimation from video and LLM-driven user controls as important future directions. The real-time performance (25–36 FPS) for simple dynamics is encouraging but the method remains constrained to relatively small simulation domains.
