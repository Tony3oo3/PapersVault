## Problem

Vision Transformers, despite being inspired by CNNs, are not shift-equivariant: small spatial shifts in the input produce different token representations, attention patterns, and merged patches because each module (tokenization, window-based self-attention, patch merging, positional encoding) relies on a fixed spatial grid. This sensitivity undermines the shift-invariance expected in classification and the shift-equivariance expected in segmentation, even though prior ViT architectures explicitly cite translation invariance as a design motivation.

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images resized to each architecture's native resolution (224×224 for Swin-T, CvT-13, MViTv2-T; 256×256 for SwinV2-T) and produces either a class label (classification) or a pixel-wise segmentation mask (segmentation via UperNet decoder). For circular-shift evaluation, all convolutional layers use circular padding to eliminate boundary effects; for standard-shift evaluation, CIFAR uses zero-padding while ImageNet uses a shift-then-center-crop protocol. The adaptive modules internally evaluate L (or W) candidate circular shifts of the input to select a consistent alignment before processing.

## Method

The paper redesigns four ViT modules so that each is provably circularly shift-equivariant, and by composition the full network becomes end-to-end shift-equivariant. (1) Adaptive tokenization (A-token) selects, among L possible circular shifts of the input, the one whose reshaped patches maximize a shift-invariant ℓp-norm score, guaranteeing identical tokens under any circular shift. (2) Adaptive window-based self-attention (A-WSA) picks the window-partition offset that maximizes a shift-invariant function of per-window energies, so the same group of tokens is always grouped together. (3) Adaptive patch merging (A-PMerge) replaces the fixed strided convolution with Adaptive Polyphase Sampling plus a lowpass anti-aliasing filter, adaptively choosing which M/P tokens to retain based on their ℓ2 norm. (4) Adaptive RPE encodes relative distances as (p_i − p_j) mod M rather than linearly, matching the circular-shift assumption. The design is applied to Swin, SwinV2, CvT, and MViTv2, achieving 100% circular shift consistency while improving both classification accuracy and standard-shift consistency.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, the adaptive models achieve near-perfect circular shift consistency (99.99–100%) while simultaneously improving top-1 accuracy over their baselines: A-Swin-T reaches 93.39% (vs. 90.15% for Swin-T), A-CvT-13 reaches 93.87% (vs. 90.06%), and A-MViTv2-T reaches 96.41% (vs. 96.00%). Under standard shifts, consistency also improves substantially (e.g., A-CvT-13: 96.47% S-Cons vs. 84.66% for CvT-13). On ImageNet, all adaptive variants maintain 99.98–100% circular shift consistency with modest accuracy gains (e.g., A-SwinV2-T: 79.91% vs. 78.95% for SwinV2-T).

## Baselines & Comparisons

All four adaptive variants (A-Swin-T, A-SwinV2-T, A-CvT-13, A-MViTv2-T) outperform their respective baselines on both top-1 accuracy and shift consistency across CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet. The most dramatic gains are in circular shift consistency: A-CvT-13 jumps from 75.80% to 100% C-Cons on CIFAR-10, and A-Swin-T from 83.30% to 99.99%. Accuracy improvements range from ~0.4% (A-MViTv2-T on CIFAR-10) to ~4.1% (A-CvT-13 on CIFAR-100). Under standard shifts, the adaptive models also consistently improve S-Cons (e.g., A-CvT-13: 96.47% vs. 84.66% for CvT-13 on CIFAR-10), though they do not reach 100% due to boundary effects. The one exception is A-CvT-13 on ImageNet under standard shifts, where top-1 accuracy drops marginally (81.48% vs. 81.59% for CvT-13), while all other comparisons show accuracy gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CNN shift-equivariance literature (Chaman & Dokmanic's Adaptive Polyphase Sampling, Zhang's anti-aliasing, Rojas-Gomez et al.'s Learnable Polyphase Sampling) to Vision Transformers, where prior methods are inapplicable due to architectural differences. It departs from the recent anchoring approach [10], which achieves circular shift-invariance by pre-aligning inputs via polyphase decomposition before feeding them to a standard ViT, by instead redesigning every internal module that breaks equivariance—tokenization, window-based self-attention, patch merging, and relative position embedding—so that equivariance is preserved end-to-end through composition. The adaptive modules draw on the polyphase/energy-based selection ideas from the CNN literature but apply them at the token and window level rather than the pixel level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first provably circularly shift-equivariant ViT framework, achieving 100% circular shift consistency across four architectures (Swin, SwinV2, CvT, MViTv2) for both classification and segmentation, while also improving standard shift consistency and classification accuracy. A notable practical limitation is the ~10–20% throughput decrease from the adaptive selection steps, and the theory-to-practice gap under standard shifts (where boundary effects prevent perfect consistency) remains an open challenge. Future work could explore extending the adaptive design to other transformations (rotation, scale) or reducing the computational overhead of the argmax-based alignment.
