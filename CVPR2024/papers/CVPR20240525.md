## Problem

Reconstructing accurate 3D geometry from short egocentric omnidirectional video is difficult because the small camera baseline and large scene depth ranges make ray crossings sparse, while each pixel covers a wide angular extent (~5× perspective cameras). Existing neural SDF methods rely on fixed-depth Cartesian grids that either waste memory on empty space or undersample detailed regions, and prior binoctree-based approaches use a static subdivision that cannot adapt to the evolving surface during optimization.

## Input / Output

The method consumes equirectangular RGB-D omnidirectional video (2048×1024) captured along a small circular sweep, along with per-frame camera poses from SfM and an initial depth map from a pre-trained spherical depth estimation network. It outputs a 3D surface mesh extracted from the optimized neural SDF, with the binoctree structure itself serving as an adaptive spatial index that requires roughly 10,000× fewer voxels than an equivalent-resolution Cartesian grid.

## Method

The core contribution is an adaptive spherical binoctree of 'sphoxels' that dynamically subdivides from coarse to fine granularity during iterative optimization, concentrating sampling and memory near the evolving surface. Three sampling levels operate simultaneously: whole-space sphere sampling for global coverage, coarse sphoxel sampling (dilated to parents/grandparents) for geometric guidance, and fine sphoxel sampling where membership is reassessed every 10k iterations by comparing |SDF| to each sphoxel's radius. A novel intersection test approximates each irregular sphoxel as a trapezoidal prism (square-based frustum) for ray-triangle testing, and a breadth-first traversal strategy manages the variable number of child nodes. Two MLPs (8-layer SDF network and 4-layer color network with positional encoding) are trained with L1 photometric loss and eikonal regularization, with the binoctree providing voxel-guided sampling rather than direct depth supervision.

## Results

On three synthetic scenes (Sponza, Lone-monk, San Miguel), OmniSDF achieves the best average depth RMSE of 0.69 and MAE of 0.32, outperforming the best classical baseline COLMAP (RMSE 0.84, MAE 0.28) and all neural baselines. The advantage is most pronounced on the detailed San Miguel scene, where OmniSDF reaches RMSE 0.56 / MAE 0.27 versus COLMAP's 1.24 / 0.41 and Neuralangelo's 0.58 / 0.29. In terms of memory efficiency, the adaptive binoctree requires roughly 10,000× fewer voxels than an equivalent dense Cartesian grid (e.g., 4.3M vs. 33.3B for Sponza).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the classical COLMAP baseline, OmniSDF achieves a lower average RMSE (0.69 vs. 0.84) and is substantially better on the detailed San Miguel scene (RMSE 0.56 vs. 1.24), but COLMAP retains an edge on average MAE (0.28 vs. 0.32) and on the simpler Lone-monk scene (RMSE 0.40 vs. 0.69). Against the prior binoctree-based EgocentricRecon [8], OmniSDF reduces average RMSE by 22% (0.69 vs. 0.88) and MAE by 3% (0.32 vs. 0.33), with the largest gain on Lone-monk (0.69 vs. 1.05). Among neural SDF methods, OmniSDF clearly outperforms NeuS (avg RMSE 0.69 vs. 2.13) and NeuS-facto (0.69 vs. 2.91), which struggle with unbounded scenes. The closest neural competitor, Neuralangelo [15], achieves average RMSE 1.16 / MAE 0.81, still roughly 40% worse in RMSE than OmniSDF, and is particularly weak on Lone-monk (1.91 vs. 0.69). The paper also shows that using the binoctree as a sampling guide (OmniSDF) is superior to direct depth supervision (NeuS-facto-D, avg RMSE 2.67), confirming that the adaptive spatial partitioning provides more reliable geometric constraints than raw depth losses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends NeuS's neural SDF volume-rendering framework by replacing its Cartesian coordinate sampling with an adaptive spherical binoctree of 'sphoxels,' and builds on Jang et al.'s fixed-depth binoctree for omnidirectional reconstruction by making the tree structure dynamically subdivided during optimization rather than set once from initial depth. The key conceptual departure from both is the iterative, optimization-driven refinement of spatial resolution: the binoctree grows finer where the SDF indicates a surface, concentrating both memory and ray samples where they are needed. The novel sphoxel intersection test (trapezoidal-prism approximation) and breadth-first traversal strategy are practical enablers that make this spherical tree structure usable within a differentiable rendering pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~10× voxel reduction over equivalent Cartesian grids makes large-scale unbounded scene reconstruction from a single omnidirectional sweep feasible on a single GPU, which is practically important for VR/AR content creation and robotic mapping where egocentric capture is natural. The finding that binoctree-guided sampling outperforms direct depth supervision (Table 3) suggests that geometric structure as a sampling prior is more robust than pixel-level depth signals, an insight that could transfer to other sparse-view settings. Open questions include robustness to poor initial depth estimates (acknowledged as a limitation), handling of concave geometry that remains sensitive to optimization minima, and whether the adaptive subdivision strategy generalizes beyond the small-baseline circular-sweep setting to more complex camera trajectories.
