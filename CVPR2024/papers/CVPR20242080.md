## Problem

Fine-grained hashing-based retrieval faces two inherent contradictions that prior methods have overlooked: (1) low-dimensional binary hash codes have limited information capacity, yet fine-grained categories require rich detailed descriptions, so simply extracting more features does not translate to better codes; and (2) comprehensive multi-region/multi-layer feature extraction modules add parameters and computation that undermine the efficiency goal of hashing. Existing methods treat the task as feature extraction followed by code mapping, rather than rethinking what information the codes should actually encode.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image (resized to 255×255, then center-cropped to 224×224) and produces a b-bit binary hash code (b ∈ {12, 24, 32, 48}). During inference, only the penultimate stage feature vector of a ResNet backbone is extracted and matched against pre-learned characteristic vectors (C-vectors) of shape y×k×d, where y is the number of categories and k (typically 2) is the number of critical characteristics per category. The matching score matrix of size y×k is then linearly mapped and sign-thresholded to produce the final binary code, avoiding any multi-region or multi-layer processing at test time.

## Method

CMBH replaces the standard 'extract features → map to codes' pipeline with a characteristics-matching strategy: instead of embedding full feature vectors into hash codes, it computes cosine-similarity matching scores between a single-layer instance feature vector and a set of learnable C-vectors, then maps these compact scores to binary codes. Two training-only modules optimize the C-vectors without adding inference cost: a cross-layer semantic information transfer module (combining early-fusion concatenation with a knowledge-distillation-style later-fusion loss) embeds multi-granularity information into the C-vectors, and a parameter-free multi-region feature embedding module (using iterative NMS-based region localization inspired by SCDA) associates each C-vector with distinct local image patches to enforce inter-vector distinctiveness. The overall loss combines pairwise and proxy-based hash code losses with classification and cross-layer transfer losses, all trained end-to-end. At inference, the cross-layer and multi-region modules are discarded entirely, leaving only backbone feature extraction, C-vector matching, and a single linear hash mapping.

## Results

On CUB-200-2011, CMBH achieves 84.07% MAP at 12 bits, 85.79% at 24 bits, 86.21% at 32 bits, and 86.47% at 48 bits, outperforming all baselines by a clear margin. On FGVC-Aircrafts, the method reaches 92.88% MAP at 48 bits, and on NABirds it achieves 82.07% at 48 bits—substantially ahead of the next-best method (CNET at 78.81%). The advantage is most pronounced at 12-bit code length, where CMBH leads by 6–7 points on CUB and NABirds, demonstrating the effectiveness of the characteristic-matching strategy under extreme compression. In terms of efficiency, CMBH uses only 14.33M parameters and 4.35 GFLOPs (ResNet-50), compared to 42.39M/7.09 GFLOPs for SEMICON and 41.13M/9.35 GFLOPs for CNET.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CMBH outperforms all ten compared baselines on every dataset and code length tested. The closest competitor is CNET [27], which is surpassed by 6.97 points on CUB-200-2011 at 12 bits (84.07 vs. 77.10) and by 3.26 points on NABirds at 48 bits (82.07 vs. 78.81); CNET also uses ~2.9× more parameters (41.13M vs. 14.33M) and ~2.1× more FLOPs. FISH [3] and sRLH [24] are competitive on CUB at longer code lengths (e.g., FISH reaches 80.88% at 48 bits) but fall 5–6 points behind CMBH and are less efficient (23.92M params, 4.13 GFLOPs for FISH-ResNet50). AGMH [13] and A²-Net++ [23] trail by 2–4 points on CUB and by larger margins on NABirds, where they report no results at 12 bits. ExchNet [4], A²-Net [21], FCAENet [28], SEMICON [18], and DLTH [12] are consistently the weakest baselines, with gaps exceeding 10 points on most datasets. Notably, CMBH achieves its largest relative gains on NABirds (555 classes), where the fine-grained discrimination challenge is most acute, and at 12-bit code length, validating the paper's claim that characteristic-matching is particularly effective under severe information compression.

## Relation to Prior Work

CMBH departs from the dominant paradigm in fine-grained hashing (FPH, ExchNet, A2-Net, FISH, SEMICON, CNET, AGMH) that focuses on extracting increasingly comprehensive multi-region, multi-layer feature vectors and then mapping them to binary codes. Instead, it introduces a 'characteristics matching' formulation where per-category characteristic vectors (C-vectors) are learned during training, and hash codes are generated from matching scores between a single-layer sample feature and these C-vectors. The multi-region localization algorithm draws on SCDA and NMS, and the cross-layer information transfer borrows from knowledge distillation, but both are used only during training to optimize C-vectors rather than to produce richer inference-time features. This reframing explicitly targets two contradictions the authors argue prior methods ignore: the information bottleneck of short binary codes and the tension between complex feature extraction and retrieval efficiency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for large-scale fine-grained retrieval: at inference, CMBH requires only a single backbone layer's output (no multi-region cropping, no multi-layer concatenation), yet it outperforms methods that perform all of these, with roughly 3× fewer parameters and ~40% fewer FLOPs than SEMICON. The particularly large margin at 12-bit codes (e.g., 84.07 vs. 77.10 on CUB) suggests the matching-based representation is more information-efficient for very short codes, which is the regime most relevant to billion-scale retrieval. Open questions include whether the C-vector concept generalizes beyond fine-grained tasks, whether adaptive α scheduling based on code length (noted as future work) yields consistent gains, and whether the approach extends to asymmetric or unsupervised hashing settings.
