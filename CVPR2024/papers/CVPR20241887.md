## Problem

While NeRF-based methods can produce photo-realistic novel view renderings, they remain passive representations that cannot support real-time interaction, rigid-body physics, or integration into game engines. The paper addresses the largely unexplored problem of automatically converting a single video of a real-world scene into a fully playable, physically interactive, and browser-compatible game environment at interactive frame rates.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single video (or sequence of posed images) of an arbitrary scene as input, requiring no additional modalities such as LiDAR or depth sensors. It produces a set of game-engine-compatible assets: a UV-mapped neural textured mesh (4096×4096 texture), per-object collision geometries (box, sphere, convex polygon, or trimesh), rigid-body physics parameters (mass, friction), and a GLSL shader for view-dependent specular appearance. The final output is a WebGL-based interactive environment running at 100+ FPS in a standard browser, exportable to Blender or Unreal Engine.

## Method

The pipeline has three stages. First, a large-scale NeRF built on Instant-NGP is trained with a contraction function for unbounded scenes, monocular depth/normal/semantic priors for geometry regularization, spatial and sky density penalties, and block-based decomposition (16 blocks for KITTI-360) to handle large-scale scenes. Second, the NeRF is distilled into a mesh via marching cubes on the density field, followed by UV mapping (xatlas), mesh decimation, and training of a neural texture map (base color + specular features) with a lightweight two-layer GLSL shader MLP for view-dependent appearance, optimized via differentiable rendering with anti-aliasing through random camera center perturbation. Third, the scene is decomposed into individual objects using semantic logits from the NeRF, each assigned a collision geometry and physics parameters (mass, friction), then deployed in a WebGL game engine (Sketchbook/Three.js) with Cannon.js for real-time rigid-body dynamics, including precomputed fracture effects for dynamic objects.

## Results

On KITTI-360, Video2Game's NeRF achieves 27.62 PSNR / 0.871 SSIM / 0.131 LPIPS, outperforming Instant-NGP (27.46/0.853/0.165) and Nerfacto (23.20/0.763/0.238). The baked mesh reaches 23.35 PSNR on KITTI-360, surpassing BakedSDF (22.37) and MobileNeRF (19.67), while on Gardenvase it ties MobileNeRF (22.81 vs 22.80 PSNR). For geometry reconstruction, Video2Game's NeRF achieves 13.23% outlier rate and 3.028 m RMSE, substantially better than all baselines (best competitor: Instant-NGP at 22.89% / 4.300 m). Video2Game is the only method supporting all three interactive capabilities: real-time rendering, rigid-body physics, and scene decomposition.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Instant-NGP, Video2Game's NeRF wins on KITTI-360 (27.62 vs 27.46 PSNR) and Gardenvase (26.57 vs 25.90 PSNR), with a much larger geometry advantage (13.23% vs 22.89% outlier rate). Against Nerfacto, Video2Game dominates across all metrics on both scenes (e.g., 27.62 vs 23.20 PSNR on KITTI-360). Against 3D Gaussian Splatting, Video2Game wins decisively on KITTI-360 (27.62 vs 17.85 PSNR) where Gaussians fail under sparse cameras, but loses on Gardenvase (26.57 vs 27.50 PSNR) where Gaussians excel. Against MobileNeRF, Video2Game's mesh achieves 23.35 vs 19.67 PSNR on KITTI-360 and essentially ties on Gardenvase (22.81 vs 22.80). Against BakedSDF, Video2Game wins on KITTI-360 mesh rendering (23.35 vs 22.37 PSNR) and ties on Gardenvase (22.81 vs 22.68), while uniquely supporting scene decomposition for object-level interactions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the NeRF-to-mesh pipeline (building on Instant-NGP, Mip-NeRF 360's contraction, and BlockNeRF's spatial partitioning) by adding monocular depth/normal/semantic cues to improve geometry, then distilling the result into a UV-mapped neural-textured mesh compatible with game engines. It departs from prior NeRF baking work (BakedSDF, MobileNeRF, NeRF2Mesh) by explicitly decomposing the scene into individual rigid-body entities with distinct physical parameters, rather than treating the scene as a single static mesh. The broader positioning bridges three previously separate threads—novel view synthesis, controllable video generation, and data-driven simulation—into a single automated pipeline that outputs a browser-playable game, a combination the authors note 'remains largely unexplored.'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a single video can be converted into an interactive, physics-enabled environment running at 100+ FPS in a standard browser, bypassing the multi-million-dollar manual asset-creation pipeline that dominates game and simulator development. The robot simulation demos (Stretch, Fetch) suggest a path toward low-cost, photorealistic training environments for embodied AI without LiDAR or manual scene construction. Key limitations include reliance on posed input frames, heuristic/LLM-based physics parameter estimation, and scene decomposition quality that depends on semantic accuracy; dynamic or deformable objects remain out of scope. Open questions include whether the pipeline generalizes to long unbounded sequences, whether learned physics parameters can replace manual/LLM estimation, and how to handle non-rigid interactions.
