## Problem

Existing pixel-contribution methods (Grad-CAM, Attention rollout, Shapley values) rank pixels by individual contribution, so the top-ranked set often contains redundant information and fails to identify the minimal pixel group that collectively drives classification confidence. Game-theoretic interactions capture this cooperative effect but require an exponential number of forward passes, making them impractical for image-scale problems.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240117/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained image classifier (ViT-T, DeiT-T, or ResNet-18) and a single RGB image, operating on a 14×14 grid of 16×16-pixel patches (matching ViT token structure). It outputs an ordered sequence of patches from most to least important for both insertion (starting from a fully masked image) and deletion (starting from the full image), which can be rendered as heatmaps or evaluated via insertion/deletion accuracy curves. For Vision Transformers, masking is implemented via attention masking rather than pixel-value replacement to avoid injecting spurious features.

## Method

MoXI justifies a greedy patch-selection algorithm from a game-theoretic perspective: for pixel insertion, selecting the next patch to maximize confidence is equivalent to maximizing the sum of a self-context Shapley value and a self-context interaction (Eqs. 5–7); for pixel deletion, it is equivalent to maximizing a full-context Shapley value plus a weighted full-context interaction (Eqs. 10–13). The self-context and full-context variants are the key theoretical contributions—they restrict the averaging in Shapley values and interactions to a single relevant coalition, reducing the number of required forward passes from O(|N|·2^|N|) to O(|N|²). The interaction term penalizes patches whose information overlaps with already-selected patches, so the algorithm naturally avoids redundancy. In practice, the method is applied to 196 patches per image, and the resulting ordering is evaluated via insertion/deletion curves and corruption-based robustness tests on ImageNet.

## Results

On ImageNet with ViT-T, MoXI achieves 90% classification accuracy with only 4% of image patches unmasked in the insertion task, compared to 2% (Grad-CAM), 4% (Attention rollout), and 25% (Shapley values). In the deletion task, masking just 10% of patches selected by MoXI drops accuracy to 16%, whereas Grad-CAM and Attention rollout only reduce it to ~79%. On the synthetic visual SET-SUM task (MNIST), MoXI outperforms both self-context Shapley values (MoXI(-)) and standard Shapley values at 50% and 75% unmasked rates, confirming the benefit of interactions in avoiding redundant pixel selection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoXI substantially outperforms all four baselines on both insertion and deletion tasks. In insertion, MoXI reaches 90% accuracy at 4% unmasked patches versus 2% for Grad-CAM, 4% for Attention rollout, and 25% for Shapley values — a 3.6× improvement over the strongest baseline (Shapley values). In deletion, MoXI drops accuracy to 16% at 10% masked, while Grad-CAM and Attention rollout only reduce it to ~79%, indicating MoXI identifies far more decision-critical patches. The ablation MoXI(-) (self-context Shapley values without interactions) also underperforms full MoXI on the visual SET-SUM task, confirming that the interaction term is essential for avoiding redundant pixel selection. On the consistent-explainability experiment (Fig. 7), Attention rollout's accuracy degrades as the number of training classes increases from 10 to 1000, while MoXI maintains stable performance, suggesting its patch selection is less sensitive to the classifier's internal feature representation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends game-theoretic model explanation from individual pixel contributions (Shapley values, as used in SHAP and FastSHAP) to collective pixel contributions via interactions (Grabisch & Roubens, 1999). It builds on Covert et al.'s (ICLR 2023) strategy of using attention masking in Vision Transformers for Shapley value computation, but departs from all prior saliency methods (Grad-CAM, Attention rollout, standard Shapley values) by arguing that the set of individually important pixels is not the same as the most informative pixel set as a whole. The key theoretical contribution is showing that a simple greedy selection algorithm is exactly justified by self-context Shapley values and interactions, reducing the computational cost from exponential to quadratic forward passes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The quadratic-cost guarantee makes game-theoretic pixel explanation practically feasible for ViTs, removing the primary barrier (exponential sampling) that has limited adoption of Shapley-based methods. The large accuracy gap over Shapley values (90% vs 25% at 4% unmasked) and the consistent-explainability result (MoXI does not degrade as the number of classes grows, unlike Attention rollout) suggest that interaction-aware selection captures a fundamentally different and more useful signal. Limitations include reliance on ViT attention masking (less natural for CNNs), evaluation on 14×14 patches rather than individual pixels, and testing on only 1000 ImageNet images with three architectures. Open questions include whether the quadratic cost remains tractable for very high-resolution inputs and whether the method extends to multi-label or dense prediction tasks.
