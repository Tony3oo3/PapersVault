## Problem

Existing federated learning defenses are typically specialized to a single attack type (utility or privacy) and lack a unified defender's perspective that accounts for robustness, budget, visibility, and multipurpose efficiency. No prior work simultaneously defends against both evasion data poisoning attacks and model inversion attacks in FL, forcing defenders to deploy separate mechanisms that escalate computational cost and reduce adaptability in production deployments.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240398/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images (sizes ranging from 30×30 for GTSRB to 32×32 for CIFAR-10/100) and a shared secret key K ∈ ℝ^h (where h is the image height, containing h unique random integers in [0, h-1]) as input. The FCD transformation transposes the image and then reorders its rows according to K, producing an encrypted representation that is fed into the model for both training and inference. The server evaluates the global model on FCD-encrypted test data (never decrypting), outputting class labels and global test accuracy; under the MIA threat model, the adversary's reconstructed images remain in encrypted form, yielding a reconstruction MSE of 73.23 versus ≤0.05 for prior defenses.

## Method

FCD transfers the entire FL training and testing pipeline into a homomorphically encrypted data space using a row-wise transposition cipher: the input image is first transposed (rows become columns), then its rows are permuted according to a secret key K shared by the server with all clients. On the client side, a novel distillation loss guided by KL divergence is introduced: a local teacher model (pretrained on normal data) provides soft predictions P_k, while the student model (trained on FCD-encrypted data) produces Q_k, and the total loss is L_k = L_CE_k + α_k · L_KLD_k, where L_KLD_k = Σ Q_k^r log(Q_k^r / P_k^r) over all classes r. On the server side, the global model is tested on FCD-transformed test data E(X̂_test) rather than the original poisoned data, eliminating decryption and reducing computational complexity to O(nh) per encryption call. The ζ-separability property guarantees that the encrypted data space maintains a minimum distance ζ from any μρ-bounded adversarial perturbation, and a convergence theorem establishes that the global model still converges under L-smoothness and τ-strong convexity, with the distillation term accelerating convergence by transferring knowledge from the normal-data teacher to the encrypted-data student.

## Results

Under TM2 (model inversion attack on CIFAR-100 in split FL), FCD achieves a reconstruction MSE of 73.23, exceeding the second-best method (ResSFL, MSE = 0.05) by over 1,400×, indicating the adversary cannot meaningfully reconstruct private data. Under TM1 (M-SimBA evasion data poisoning), FCD consistently yields the lowest utility impact (U) across all attack percentages (30%, 50%, 100%) and client counts on GTSRB, KBTS, CIFAR-10, and EMNIST, with 2–6 units lower U than the next-best defense at 30% attack. In the benign (no-attack) setting, FCD matches or slightly exceeds vanilla FL accuracy on GTSRB (97.72% vs. 96.35% homogeneous) and KBTS, while incurring a modest 4–5% accuracy drop on CIFAR-10 and EMNIST.

## Baselines & Comparisons

For the TM2 privacy defense (MIA on CIFAR-100), FCD dominates all seven baselines by an enormous margin: it achieves MSE 73.23 versus ResSFL (0.05), Bottleneck Layers (0.02), DistCorr (0.019), AdvNoise (0.018), Laplacian (0.011), Dropout (0.009), and TopkPrune (0.005), meaning the reconstructed images are essentially unrecognizable. For the TM1 utility defense (M-SimBA evasion attack), FCD outperforms both FAT [61] and Randomized Smoothing (RS) [8] across all tested attack percentages and client configurations on GTSRB and KBTS, with the gap widening as attack volume increases (e.g., at 100% attack on GTSRB with 25 clients, FCD's U is roughly 2–6 units lower than FAT or RS). In the benign no-attack setting, FCD slightly outperforms FAT and RS on GTSRB and KBTS but trails vanilla FL by ~4–5% on CIFAR-10 and EMNIST, a trade-off the authors attribute to the encrypted data space. Overall, FCD is the only method in the paper's Table 1 comparison that simultaneously defends against both evasion utility attacks and model inversion privacy attacks from a unified defender's perspective.

## Relation to Prior Work

FCD builds on the standard FedAvg aggregation framework (McMahan et al., 2017) and repurposes a classical symmetric transposition cipher (Sokouti et al., 2009) as a lightweight data-space encryption mechanism, combined with a KL-divergence-guided knowledge distillation loss (Zhang et al., 2021) to enable learning on encrypted data. It departs from prior FL defenses that operate in the gradient or model space (Byzantine-robust aggregation, differential privacy, MPC, TEE) by shifting the entire training and testing pipeline into an encrypted data space, and from adversarial training methods (FAT, Randomized Smoothing) that address only utility threats. The key conceptual departure is unifying utility-evasion and privacy-MIA defense under a single cryptographic transformation rather than deploying separate mechanisms for each attack class.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified defense framing addresses a genuine gap: prior FL security work treats utility and privacy threats in isolation, forcing defenders to stack multiple mechanisms with compounding computational cost. FCD's O(nh) encryption complexity (versus O(n_w N² log N) for model-parameter HE) makes it practical for large-scale deployments, and the >1000× MSE gap over ResSFL on MIA defense is striking. However, the approach's reliance on a shared secret key introduces a single point of failure, and the authors acknowledge that adaptive key-guessing and multi-step gradient attacks remain open threats requiring multi-key encryption extensions. The modest accuracy degradation in benign settings (e.g., CIFAR-10: 77.28% vs 82.14% for vanilla FL) also raises questions about the utility-privacy trade-off ceiling for this class of data-space transformations.
