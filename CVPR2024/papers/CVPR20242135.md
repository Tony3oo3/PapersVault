## Problem

Existing audio-visual segmentation (AVS) datasets use short trimmed clips (5–10 seconds) where the sounding object is present throughout, causing methods to degenerate into salient object segmentation. In real-world untrimmed videos, the start/end sounding times, durations, and spatial positions of audible objects vary significantly, yet no benchmark exists to evaluate AVS under these conditions. This paper introduces LU-AVS to expose the limitations of current AVS methods on long-untrimmed videos.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242135/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The task takes an untrimmed video (average 42 seconds, up to ~1 minute) with its synchronized audio track and must produce per-frame segmentation masks or bounding boxes for all audible objects, along with the temporal start and end frames of each sounding segment. Unlike prior AVS datasets that annotate only one frame per second, LU-AVS provides exhaustive pixel-level annotations across all frames and additionally supplies bounding boxes for categories where masks are impractical (e.g., first-person sailing footage). Evaluation uses both temporal metrics (m_tIoU, m_tF) and spatial metrics (m_vIoU, m_vF) to jointly assess when and where objects are sounding.

## Method

The primary contribution is the LU-AVS dataset, constructed from VGGSound's original untrimmed YouTube videos (cut to ~1 minute) spanning 88 categories with 10M mask and 11M bounding-box annotations. Annotation is semi-automatic: SAM generates initial masks from annotator clicks, XMem tracks objects across frames, and human annotators correct tracking failures; bounding boxes are labeled at 1-second intervals with mean-shift and kernelized correlation filter tracking filling intermediate frames. For benchmarking, the authors adapt existing AVS, AVL, AVE, and STVG methods to the untrimmed setting and propose a simple baseline that uses MaskFormer/DETR for visual object detection, VGGish for 1-second audio segment classification, and aggregates predictions by semantic labels to produce both spatial and temporal grounding. The key finding is that all adapted methods perform poorly (below 15% on most spatial metrics), confirming that current approaches cannot handle the temporal uncertainty and high silent-segment proportion of untrimmed videos.

## Results

On the LU-AVS dataset, the proposed mask-based baseline achieves the best spatial segmentation performance among all methods that produce pixel-level or bounding-box outputs, reaching 17.32% m_vIoU and 18.76% m_tIoU, substantially outperforming the strongest AVS method TPAVI (6.03% m_vIoU) and the best STVG method STCAT (7.01% m_vIoU). AVE methods, which focus solely on temporal localization without spatial segmentation, achieve the highest overall scores (up to 40.67% m_tIoU for CMPAE), highlighting the difficulty of jointly performing spatial and temporal grounding. All AVS and AVL baselines remain below 15% on every metric, confirming that existing methods struggle significantly with long-untrimmed videos.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed mask-based baseline outperforms both AVS methods (TPAVI and ECMVAE) by a large margin on m_vIoU (17.32% vs. 6.03% and 5.24%), demonstrating that explicit audio-visual correlation across the full video is critical for untrimmed settings. Against the best AVL method EZLSL, the BBox-based variant gains 8.96% on m_vIoU (15.89% vs. 6.93%) and 3.52% on m_tIoU (16.53% vs. 13.01%), showing resilience to the ~12% silent segments that AVL methods trained on consistently-audible data fail to handle. Compared to STVG methods, the BBox-based baseline surpasses STCAT by 8.88% on m_vIoU (15.89% vs. 7.01%), attributed to the explicit audio-visual correlation rather than the transformer-based multi-modal interaction used in STVG. However, all spatially-grounding methods (AVS, AVL, STVG, and the proposed baseline) score well below AVE methods (33–41%), which perform only temporal localization and avoid the spatial segmentation challenge entirely. This gap underscores that jointly achieving accurate spatial and temporal grounding in long-untrimmed videos remains an open problem.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the audio-visual segmentation (AVS) task introduced by Zhou et al. (AVSBench-Object/Semantic) from short trimmed clips (5–10 s, single sounding object audible throughout) to long untrimmed videos with variable sounding durations, uncertain start/end frames, and multiple concurrent sound sources. It draws on VGGSound as a data source and leverages SAM and XMem for semi-automatic mask annotation, while also borrowing evaluation concepts from spatio-temporal video grounding (STVG) and audio-visual event localization (AVE). Rather than proposing a new architecture, the paper carves new ground by formulating AVS as a joint spatial-temporal grounding problem and benchmarking methods from four distinct task families (AVS, AVL, AVE, STVG) under a unified protocol.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that all existing AVS, AVL, and STVG methods drop below 15% on untrimmed videos, confirming that the field's reliance on trimmed clips has masked a fundamental gap in temporal reasoning. The large performance gap between AVE methods (33–40%, temporal-only) and spatial+temporal methods highlights that spatial segmentation under deformation and occlusion remains the primary bottleneck. With 10M mask and 11M bounding-box annotations across 7.2K videos and 88 categories, LU-AVS is likely to become the standard evaluation platform for next-generation AVS research. Open questions include how to jointly model long-range temporal context with per-frame spatial segmentation, how to handle multiple asynchronous sound sources, and whether the semi-automatic annotation pipeline generalizes to even longer or noisier real-world footage.
