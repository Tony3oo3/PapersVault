## Problem

Existing transferable sparse adversarial attacks (e.g., TSAA) constrain only the magnitude and count of perturbed pixels via pixel-level ℓ₁ sparsity, ignoring where perturbations are placed. This results in scattered, model-specific perturbation patterns that degrade transferability across target models. The paper asks: can structuring perturbation positions to concentrate in classification-relevant regions improve transferability while maintaining sparsity?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240098/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image (299×299 for Inception-V3 surrogate, 224×224 for ResNet-50 surrogate) and its ground-truth label as input to a pre-trained generative network. It outputs a sparse adversarial perturbation δ = r ⊙ m, where r ∈ [0,255]^{W×H×C} is a per-pixel magnitude and m ∈ {0,1}^{W×H} is a binary position mask with exact k-group sparsity (only k out of P×Q non-overlapping S×S groups are active). The perturbation is bounded by an ℓ∞ constraint (ε=10 or 255) and achieves sparsity levels comparable to baselines (e.g., ~14% at ε=10, ~0.5% at ε=255). Inference is a single forward pass through the generator, taking ~0.01s per image.

## Method

The core contribution is an exact group sparsity training method that enforces structural sparsity at two granularities: pixel-level ℓ₁ norm for fine-grained sparsity and group ℓ₂₀ norm (exactly k active groups out of P×Q) for coarse-grained structural sparsity. Group feature importance (GFI) is computed from Grad-CAM on a surrogate model to rank groups by classification relevance, and the top-k groups form a binary group mask c that gates the position decoder output ρ through a masked quantization network Q. This network uses Bernoulli sampling combined with threshold-based quantization (q(ρ·c) > τ) to make discrete perturbation positions differentiable during training, with a masked smoothness loss (hard or soft) regularizing ρ toward the quantized m. A two-stage optimization algorithm first trains without sparsity penalty (λ₁=0) to let the model discover vulnerable positions under mild group-mask guidance, then introduces ℓ₁ sparsity with a much stronger smoothness weight (λ₂⁽²⁾ ≫ λ₂⁽¹⁾) to achieve the target sparsity level without perturbation collapse.

## Results

On ImageNet with ℓ∞=10, EGS-TSSA (soft) using Inception-V3 as surrogate achieves 58.88% average ASR and 47.95% black-box average ASR, outperforming TSAA (53.10% / 41.56%) by ~5.8 and 6.4 percentage points respectively. With ResNet-50 as surrogate, EGS-TSSA (soft) reaches 52.14% average ASR and 39.10% black-box average, again surpassing TSAA (43.19% / 33.29%). Under the relaxed ℓ∞=255 setting, EGS-TSSA (soft) with IncV3 achieves 68.41% average ASR vs. TSAA's 64.29%. Inference speed is comparable to TSAA (0.0096s vs. 0.0057s on IncV3), confirming the generator-based approach remains practical.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EGS-TSSA consistently outperforms TSAA [14], the closest generator-based sparse attack, across both surrogate models: with IncV3, the soft variant gains +5.78 pp average ASR (58.88 vs. 53.10) and +6.39 pp black-box ASR (47.95 vs. 41.56); with Res50, gains are +8.95 pp average and +5.81 pp black-box. Against the non-generative baselines (PGD0, SparseFool, GreedyFool), EGS-TSSA achieves substantially higher transferability — e.g., 58.88% vs. 32.12% (PGD0) and 25.90% (GreedyFool) average ASR with IncV3 — confirming that the structural sparsity constraint and group feature importance guidance are critical for cross-model generalization. The one notable exception is the Res50-surrogate black-box attack on Dense161, where EGS-TSSA (44.86%) falls slightly below TSAA (51.18%), which the authors attribute to Res50's perturbation pattern being more aligned with VGG16 than Dense161. In targeted attacks (Table 3, ℓ∞=255, target class 'bubble'), EGS-TSSA improves average ASR by ~27 pp over TSAA with IncV3 (41.82 vs. 14.63) and ~21 pp with Res50 (28.74 vs. 8.02), a much larger margin than in the untargeted setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends TSAA (He et al., CVPR 2022), which introduced a generative encoder-decoder framework for transferable sparse adversarial attacks, by adding a structural sparsity constraint on perturbation positions guided by Grad-CAM-based group feature importance. It repurposes exact-k sparsity training techniques from network compression (Han et al., ICLR 2016; Raihan et al., NeurIPS 2020) to enforce group-level ℓ₂₀ constraints, and introduces a masked quantization network with Bernoulli sampling to make discrete perturbation positions differentiable during training. The multi-stage optimization algorithm addresses a practical training instability that arises when structural sparsity penalties are applied too aggressively within the generative framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine gap in sparse adversarial attacks: pixel-level sparsity alone does not encode where perturbations should be placed, leading to inconsistent patterns across surrogate models and reduced transferability. The conceptual insight that concentrating sparse perturbations in classification-relevant regions (identified via Grad-CAM) unifies perturbation patterns across architectures is well-motivated and empirically validated. However, the method's advantage over TSAA is modest in non-targeted classification (≈3–6% ASR gain) and actually reverses in semantic segmentation (Table 5), while ViT transferability drops sharply for both methods, suggesting the structural continuity exploited by CNNs is not preserved under patch-based architectures. Open questions include whether the GFI-guided grouping generalizes to architectures without clear spatial feature maps and whether the multi-stage training protocol is necessary or an artifact of the specific loss landscape.
