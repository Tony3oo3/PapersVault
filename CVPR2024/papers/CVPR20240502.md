## Problem

Existing Multimodal LLMs (MLLMs) such as GPT-4V and LLaVA-1.5 fail at basic object-level perception tasks—counting and identifying objects in a scene—despite performing well on complex visual reasoning. This failure stems from three gaps: the absence of a perception-focused vision-language dataset, the reliance on CLIP ViT encoders trained on captions that emphasize salient objects while ignoring background entities, and the lack of metrics that explicitly account for object counts when evaluating hallucination.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240502/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes three visual/textual streams: an RGB image (resized to 336×336, yielding 576 tokens), a segmentation map from OneFormer (encoded by a separate CLIP ViT as SegCoder), and optionally a depth map from DINOv2 DPT for object-order tasks. Each modality is tokenized with a dedicated special token (<img>, <seg>, <depth>) and concatenated with the text query before feeding the LLM. The output is a free-form text response listing object names with their counts in word form (e.g., 'two people, one chair') or a depth-ordered sequence of object names.

## Method

VCoder is a lightweight adapter that encodes perception modalities (segmentation or depth maps) with a pretrained CLIP ViT-L-336px and projects the resulting features into the LLM embedding space via modality-specific two-layer MLPs, inspired by control-image conditioning in diffusion models. The key insight is that a segmentation map explicitly delineates both foreground and background objects, compensating for CLIP's caption-biased focus on salient regions. During training, only the VCoder's MLP parameters are updated on the newly constructed COST dataset (280k COCO images paired with OneFormer segmentation outputs and GPT-4-generated questions), while the entire LLaVA-1.5 backbone (ImCoder, projector MLP, and Vicuna LLM) remains frozen to preserve general reasoning. The paper also introduces three evaluation metrics—Count Score (CS), Hallucination Score (HS), and Depth Score (DS)—that perform one-to-one object-word matching between ground truth and prediction to quantify counting accuracy and hallucination at the object level.

## Results

On the COST validation set, VCoder LLaVA-1.5-7b achieves Count Scores (CS) of 88.6, 71.1, and 86.0 with Hallucination Scores (HS) of 10.4, 26.9, and 12.8 on the semantic, instance, and panoptic object identification tasks, respectively. The 13b variant further improves to CS 89.0/73.3/87.2 and HS 10.0/25.0/11.6. For object order perception, VCoder-DS LLaVA-1.5-7b reduces the Depth Score (DS, lower is better) from 166.1 (LLaVA-1.5-7b) to 65.9, a 60% reduction. These results substantially outperform GPT-4V (panoptic CS 38.4, HS 83.0) and all open-source MLLM baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VCoder LLaVA-1.5-7b outperforms all five off-the-shelf MLLM baselines by a wide margin on every task. Against GPT-4V, VCoder more than doubles the panoptic Count Score (86.0 vs. 38.4) while reducing the Hallucination Score from 83.0 to 12.8. Compared to the strongest open-source baseline CogVLM-17b (panoptic CS 40.6, HS 75.9), VCoder-7b improves CS by 45.4 points and reduces HS by 63.1 points. Among the COST-trained ablation baselines, VCoder also wins: it surpasses COST IT LLaVA-1.5-7b (panoptic CS 71.9) by 14.1 points and ImCoder LLaVA-1.5-7b (panoptic CS 70.8) by 15.2 points, confirming that the segmentation map as a control input is more effective than simply mixing in COST data or using an RGB image as the auxiliary input. The Soft-Prompted variant performs worst among the trained baselines (panoptic CS 26.8), indicating that learnable tokens alone cannot compensate for the lack of explicit perception modality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on LLaVA-1.5 as its base MLLM, adding a VCoder adapter that projects perception modalities (segmentation maps, depth maps) into the LLM's embedding space via a CLIP ViT and modality-specific MLPs. The conceptual inspiration comes from diffusion model control mechanisms (ControlNet, T2I-Adapter), where auxiliary conditioning images guide generation; here, the same idea is repurposed to guide perception in MLLMs. The paper departs from prior MLLM work (MiniGPT-4, InstructBLIP, CogVLM) by arguing that the standard CLIP ViT encoder, trained on image-caption pairs, inherently under-represents background objects, and that explicit perception modalities are needed to compensate. It also introduces the COST dataset and CS/HS/DS metrics, filling a gap the authors identify in the absence of dedicated object-perception benchmarks for MLLMs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper highlights a concrete and underappreciated failure mode of MLLMs: they excel at complex visual reasoning yet fail at basic object counting and identification, a phenomenon the authors frame as Moravec's Paradox in perception. The COST dataset and its metrics provide a useful starting point for evaluating object-level perception, though they are constrained to COCO's 80 categories and require manual synonym mappings, limiting generalizability. A key open question is whether the VCoder approach extends to open-vocabulary or long-tail object perception, and whether the gains persist when the segmentation/depth inputs come from imperfect real-world models rather than the near-oracle OneFormer. The finding that even GPT-4V struggles with simple counting suggests that perception remains a fundamental bottleneck for MLLMs broadly.
