## Problem

Few-Shot Class-Incremental Learning (FSCIL) faces three compounding challenges: catastrophic forgetting of base classes, overfitting to scarce incremental samples, and intransigence in balancing knowledge preservation with adaptation. Existing FSCIL methods typically pretrain with standard cross-entropy, which the authors argue does not necessarily yield optimal generalization in subsequent few-shot incremental sessions, and lack a principled mechanism to reserve feature-space capacity for future classes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242117/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images in two regimes: an abundant base dataset (e.g., 60 classes on mini-ImageNet) during pretraining and base alignment, and few-shot incremental sessions (5-way, 5-shot or 10-way, 5-shot) with only 1–5 labeled samples per new class plus a small replay buffer of saved exemplars from prior sessions. At inference, a sample is classified by nearest-neighbor lookup against the fixed set of assigned pseudo-targets on the unit hypersphere, producing a single class label. Only the lightweight projection head is updated during incremental sessions; the backbone encoder remains frozen after Phase 1.

## Method

OrCo is a three-phase framework built on two pillars: mutual orthogonality of class representations and contrastive learning. Phase 1 pretrains a backbone encoder and MLP projection head with a weighted combination of supervised and self-supervised contrastive losses (SCL + SSCL) on the base data. Before Phase 2, a set of mutually orthogonal pseudo-targets is generated on the representation hypersphere via a Target Generation loss and then fixed; the Hungarian algorithm assigns them to class means. Phases 2 (base alignment) and 3 (few-shot alignment) optimize only the projection head with the OrCo loss, which combines three terms: (i) a Perturbed Supervised Contrastive Loss (PSCL) that augments the positive set with each class's assigned pseudo-target and its uniform perturbations, and additionally uses unassigned pseudo-targets as anchors whose negatives include all real data—thereby pushing every class away from reserved space; (ii) a standard cross-entropy loss pulling features toward assigned targets; and (iii) an orthogonality loss enforcing a geometric constraint on within-batch class-mean features. The perturbations around pseudo-targets act as feature-space augmentations that maximize inter-class margins and explicitly reserve capacity for incoming classes, directly mitigating overfitting and forgetting under extreme data scarcity.

## Results

On mini-ImageNet (5-way, 5-shot, 8 sessions), OrCo achieves an average harmonic mean (aHM) of 58.12%, surpassing the runner-up NC-FSCIL (52.62%) by 5.5 points. On CIFAR-100, OrCo improves over the best prior method by 9.4% in aHM, and on CUB-200 (10-way, 5-shot, 20 sessions) it also sets a new state of the art. The method maintains strong performance across all incremental sessions, with the final-session harmonic mean reaching 53.12% on mini-ImageNet compared to 45.57% for NC-FSCIL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OrCo outperforms all eight baselines on mini-ImageNet by a wide margin in aHM. The closest competitor, NC-FSCIL [40] (52.62% aHM), is surpassed by 5.5 points, with OrCo leading in every single session (e.g., 68.71% vs. 62.34% in session 1, 53.12% vs. 45.57% in session 8). BiDist [45] (40.35%) and LCwoF [22] (37.49%) trail by 17.8 and 20.6 points respectively. The geometry-preservation methods CEC [42] (29.35%) and FACT [46] (24.16%) show the largest gaps, indicating that their virtual-prototype strategies are less effective than OrCo's perturbation-based margin maximization. On CIFAR-100 and CUB-200 (Figure 4), OrCo similarly leads all baselines, with a 9.4% aHM improvement over the runner-up on CIFAR-100. Notably, the Imprinted Weights baseline [30] evaluated under OrCo's own pretraining achieves 45.36% aHM, confirming that the contrastive pretraining strategy itself contributes substantially to downstream few-shot transfer.

## Relation to Prior Work

OrCo sits at the intersection of contrastive representation learning and few-shot class-incremental learning (FSCIL). It builds directly on the pseudo-target / virtual-prototype lineage of FACT [46], NC-FSCIL [40], and C-FSCIL [12], which all reserve geometric space for incoming classes, but departs by making the targets data-agnostic, explicitly orthogonal, and—critically—perturbed within a contrastive objective (PSCL) rather than used only as regression targets. The pretraining phase extends standard supervised contrastive learning [17] by mixing in self-supervised contrastive loss [6, 29], following the 'perfectly balanced' insight [5] that combining both improves transferability. The overall three-phase architecture (contrastive pretrain → base alignment → few-shot alignment with frozen backbone) is a deliberate departure from prior FSCIL methods that rely on cross-entropy pretraining and replay/distillation for forgetting mitigation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By unifying orthogonality constraints, contrastive margin maximization, and perturbation-based space reservation into a single loss, OrCo offers a practical recipe for FSCIL that avoids the data-hungry replay or distillation pipelines common in the field. The 5.5–9.4% aHM gains over prior SOTA across three benchmarks, backed by thorough ablations isolating each loss component, make the pseudo-target perturbation technique a potentially reusable building block. Limitations include evaluation only on three mid-scale datasets with CNN backbones, a fixed number of pre-generated pseudo-targets that must exceed total class count, and a frozen backbone that trades adaptability for stability—open questions include whether the approach transfers to transformer encoders, larger-scale benchmarks (e.g., ImageNet-scale CIL), and whether the perturbation magnitude λ can be learned rather than hand-set.
