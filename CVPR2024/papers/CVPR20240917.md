## Problem

State-of-the-art video anomaly detectors rely on costly object detection pipelines, limiting throughput to 20–30 FPS per GPU and making large-scale surveillance (hundreds of cameras) prohibitively expensive. Frame-level methods are faster but typically sacrifice accuracy, and standard auto-encoders generalize too well to anomalies, undermining reconstruction-error-based detection. This paper seeks a lightweight frame-level model that closes the accuracy gap with object-centric methods while achieving orders-of-magnitude higher speed.

## Input / Output

The model takes individual RGB video frames as input (not objects or spatio-temporal cubes), with patch sizes adapted per dataset: 16×16 on Avenue, 8×8 on ShanghaiTech and UBnormal, and 4×4 on UCSD Ped2. At inference, each frame is passed through both teacher and student decoders, and the pixel-level anomaly map is a weighted sum of the teacher's reconstruction error, the teacher–student output discrepancy, and a binary classification score. Frame-level scores are obtained by taking the maximum value from each pixel map and applying a temporal Gaussian filter.

## Method

The architecture is a lightweight masked auto-encoder built from CvT blocks (3 encoder, 3 teacher-decoder, 1 student-decoder; 3M parameters, 0.8 GFLOPs) that replaces standard ViT blocks and dense layers with pointwise convolutions for speed. Three key mechanisms differentiate it from a vanilla MAE: (1) a motion-gradient weighting scheme that up-weights high-motion tokens in the reconstruction loss, steering the model to foreground objects rather than static backgrounds; (2) a self-distillation pipeline where a shallow student decoder branches from the teacher after one decoder block and is trained (with the shared encoder frozen) to reproduce the teacher's reconstruction, so the teacher–student gap serves as an anomaly signal at near-zero extra inference cost; (3) synthetic anomaly augmentation that superimposes cropped abnormal events from the UBnormal dataset onto normal training frames, forcing the model to reconstruct the original (anomaly-free) frame and predict a pixel-level anomaly map as an extra channel. Training is two-stage: the teacher is optimized with the weighted MSE plus a binary cross-entropy classification head on the [CLS] token, then the student decoder is fine-tuned via a weighted self-distillation loss that mirrors the teacher's motion-weighted objective.

## Results

On Avenue, the proposed self-distilled masked AE achieves 91.3% micro AUC and 90.9% macro AUC, the best scores among all frame-level methods, while running at 1655 FPS. On ShanghaiTech, it reaches 79.1% micro AUC (third-best among frame-level) and 84.7% macro AUC (best among frame-level). On UCSD Ped2, it obtains 95.4% micro AUC and 98.4% macro AUC. The model processes 1655 FPS on a single GTX 3090, making it 8–70× faster than competing methods (which range from 12 to 195 FPS).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On Avenue, the proposed method (91.3% micro AUC) is the best frame-level result, trailing only the top object-centric method [27] (92.3%) by 1.0% and [84] (92.2%) by 0.9%, while being 32–138× faster. On ShanghaiTech, it achieves the best macro AUC (84.7%) among frame-level methods and the third-best micro AUC (79.1%), behind object-centric [10] (83.8%) and [84] (84.3%) but ahead of all other frame-level baselines such as [47] (72.8%) and [60] (72.2%). On UBnormal, the method's micro AUC (58.5%) falls below TimeSformer [9] (68.5%) and object-centric [10] (62.1%), but its macro AUC (81.4%) surpasses TimeSformer (80.3%) and all other frame-level methods. On UCSD Ped2, the micro AUC (95.4%) is 3.9% below the best object-centric [49] (99.3%), yet the macro AUC (98.4%) is competitive with [26] (99.8%) and [36] (97.8%) at 30–138× higher speed. Overall, the method trades a small accuracy gap (1–4% micro AUC) against object-centric approaches for a dramatic 8–70× speedup, achieving 1655 FPS versus 12–195 FPS for the fastest competitors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the masked autoencoder framework (He et al., CVPR 2022) to video anomaly detection, replacing ViT blocks with more efficient CvT blocks and introducing three modifications: motion-gradient-weighted reconstruction loss (building on Ionescu et al.'s gradient estimation), a self-distillation scheme with a shared encoder and two decoders of different depths (adapting the self-distillation paradigm from classification into a reconstruction setting), and synthetic anomaly augmentation using the UBnormal dataset. It deliberately departs from the dominant object-centric paradigm, which achieves higher accuracy but at 8–70× lower throughput, by operating at the frame level with a 3M-parameter model. The reconstruction-based anomaly detection lineage (where AEs fail to reconstruct out-of-distribution inputs) is retained, but the generalization problem is addressed through the synthetic anomaly training strategy rather than memory modules or dummy anomalies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 1655 FPS throughput (0.8 GFLOPs, 3M parameters) makes large-scale video surveillance economically feasible—processing ~66 streams at 25 FPS on a single GPU—addressing a practical bottleneck that object-centric SOTA methods cannot overcome. The self-distillation-with-shared-encoder design is a clean, low-overhead way to exploit teacher-student discrepancy for anomaly scoring, and the synthetic anomaly augmentation provides a practical path toward open-set supervised training without collecting real anomalies. Limitations include a persistent micro-AUC gap versus the best object-centric methods (e.g., 91.3% vs. 93.2% on Avenue), and the reliance on UBnormal's synthetic anomalies for augmentation may not fully capture the diversity of real-world abnormal events. Open questions include whether the motion-gradient weighting generalizes to dynamic-camera settings and whether the approach scales to higher-resolution or longer-sequence inputs.
