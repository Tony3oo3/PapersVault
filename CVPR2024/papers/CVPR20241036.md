## Problem

Learning-based robot manipulation methods trained on limited simulator categories treat low-level action prediction as a black box, sacrificing interpretability and failing to generalize across extensive object categories. While Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) possess strong common-sense reasoning and generalization, it remains unexplored whether they can be adapted to predict 3D end-effector poses for object-centric manipulation while retaining their inherent reasoning abilities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241036/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes an RGB image, a text prompt (e.g., 'Specify the contact point and gripper direction'), and a depth map as input. It outputs a 2D contact pixel coordinate (x, y) projected into 3D via the depth map, plus two 3D unit vectors — gripper up direction and gripper forward direction — that jointly define the end-effector SO(3) rotation. During inference, the model generates these predictions through a chain-of-thought sequence (category identification → affordance reasoning → pose prediction), making the reasoning process interpretable. After initial contact, an active impedance adaptation policy generates subsequent waypoints in a closed-loop manner using force feedback.

## Method

ManipLLM builds on LLaMA-Adapter (CLIP visual encoder + 7B LLaMA decoder) and only fine-tunes injected LoRA adapters and the multi-modal projection module, freezing the backbone to preserve MLLM reasoning. Training uses a four-task paradigm: Object Category Identification (no parameter update, provides category prior), Affordance Prior Reasoning (binary yes/no classification on sampled pixels to learn manipulation-aware localization), Masked Language Modeling (infilling masked coordinate/direction values), and direct pose Fine-tuning (predicting contact point and discretized direction vectors in 100 bins). At inference, chain-of-thought reasoning mirrors the training sequence, and an active impedance adaptation policy applies small perturbations around the predicted forward direction, selecting the direction yielding maximum end-effector displacement as the next waypoint. For sim-to-real transfer, a test-time adaptation strategy updates only the visual adapter using manipulation success/failure as a binary supervision signal on the affordance question, allowing the model to recalibrate position predictions for the target hardware configuration.

## Results

On the PartNet-Mobility benchmark with 20 train and 10 test categories, ManipLLM achieves a 64.0% initial-movement success rate on train categories and 51.0% on unseen test categories, substantially outperforming all baselines. For long-distance movement (δ=0.1), the rates are 63.0% (train) and 50.0% (test), showing the active impedance adaptation policy preserves performance over extended trajectories. On the real-world Opening Drawer task, ManipLLM reaches 69.0% success versus VoxPoser's 14.0%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ManipLLM outperforms all four main baselines on both train and test categories for initial movement. On train categories, it leads the strongest baseline Implicit3D (0.43) by 21 percentage points (0.64), and Where2Act (0.40) by 24 points. On unseen test categories, the gap widens: ManipLLM (0.51) beats Implicit3D (0.41) by 10 points, FlowBot3D (0.32) by 19 points, UMPNet (0.28) by 23 points, and Where2Act (0.21) by 30 points, demonstrating strong cross-category generalization. Notably, ManipLLM does not require a movable-part mask at test time, whereas Where2Act, UMPNet, and FlowBot3D all depend on one. In a separate real-world comparison on the Opening Drawer task, ManipLLM achieves 69.0% success versus VoxPoser's 14.0%, a 55-point margin attributed to VoxPoser's difficulty in finding correct grasping poses and trajectories for challenging configurations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on LLaMA-Adapter as its MLLM backbone but departs fundamentally from prior manipulation approaches (Where2Act, FlowBot3D, UMPNet, Implicit3D) that treat pose prediction as a black-box regression problem. Rather than following the RT-1/RT-2 paradigm of directly regressing action trajectories from MLLMs, ManipLLM reframes manipulation as an object-centric reasoning task structured through a chain-of-thought fine-tuning paradigm (category identification → affordance reasoning → pose prediction), preserving the MLLM's pre-trained commonsense while injecting manipulation-specific capabilities via lightweight adapters. The active impedance adaptation policy for post-contact trajectory planning and the test-time adaptation strategy for sim-to-real transfer are additional contributions not present in prior manipulation or MLLM literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a 7B MLLM with only adapter fine-tuning can generalize across 30 object categories for articulated manipulation, suggesting that pre-trained commonsense reasoning is a viable inductive bias for manipulation rather than a liability. However, the scope remains narrow: only pulling-type actions with a suction gripper, a single robot platform, and limited real-world validation (few objects, one hardware configuration). Open questions include whether this object-centric MLLM framing extends to pushing, grasping, or multi-step manipulation, whether the TTA strategy generalizes beyond position correction, and whether the approach scales to more complex scenes with multiple objects or occlusions.
