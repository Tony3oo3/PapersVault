## Problem

Existing robotic manipulation agents either learn end-to-end policies (sample-inefficient, poor generalization on long-horizon tasks) or pair next-best-pose predictors with fixed motion planners that lack task-context awareness and frequently violate robot kinematic constraints. Inverse kinematics solvers used to convert predicted end-effector trajectories into joint commands introduce a compounding error rate that grows with trajectory length, causing most failures in pose-diffusion baselines.

## Input / Output

The system consumes multi-view calibrated RGB-D camera observations (converted to world-frame point clouds for 3D feature extraction via PointNet++), a natural-language task instruction, and the robot's low-dimensional state vector. It outputs a 64-timestep joint position trajectory (resampled to fixed length for batched training) plus a binary gripper open/close action. The high-level agent separately produces a 6-DoF end-effector pose (3D translation + 4D quaternion) that conditions the low-level trajectory generation.

## Method

HDP factorises the manipulation policy into a PerAct-based high-level agent that predicts next-best end-effector poses from 3D voxel action-value maps, and a low-level RK-Diffuser that generates joint position trajectories via conditional diffusion with trajectory inpainting. The key innovation in RK-Diffuser is learning two coupled diffusion models—an end-effector pose diffuser (accurate but kinematics-unaware) and a joint position diffuser (kinematics-aware but less accurate)—and distilling the former into the latter through differentiable forward kinematics (PyTorch Kinematics), optimising joint positions via gradient descent to match the predicted end-effector trajectory. Training uses keyframe-based trajectory chunking with hindsight-style relabeling of sub-goals, a trajectory-rank conditioning variable (Euclidean/travelled distance ratio) to encourage near-optimal paths, and direct action prediction rather than noise prediction for improved performance.

## Results

On 11 RLBench manipulation tasks, HDP achieves an overall 80.18% success rate, outperforming the strongest baseline PerAct+Diffuser (71.27%) by 8.91 percentage points. The gap widens substantially on articulated-object tasks: HDP reaches 90% on open box, 94% on open door, and 58% on open oven, where PerAct+Planner scores 0%, 64%, and 0% respectively. Flat end-to-end baselines (ACT at 18.36% and Diffusion Policy at 15.18%) struggle across nearly all tasks, particularly those requiring fine-grained trajectory control. On the real robot, HDP achieves 100% success on opening an oven and 94% on sorting objects into a drawer using only 20 demonstrations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HDP outperforms all four baseline families across the 11 RLBench tasks. Against flat end-to-end policies, HDP's 80.18% overall success rate exceeds ACT (18.36%) by 61.8 points and vanilla Diffusion Policy (15.18%) by 65 points, with the largest gaps on articulated-object tasks where both baselines score 0% on open oven and knife on board. Against the hierarchical PerAct+Planner (57.72%) and PerAct+Planner+Bezier (56.73%), HDP gains 22–23 points, primarily because the learned RK-Diffuser trajectories respect task context (e.g., hinge resistance) that sampling-based planners cannot capture. The closest competitor is PerAct+Diffuser (71.27%), which uses a vanilla Diffuser as a goal-conditioned joint-position model; HDP still leads by 8.9 points overall, with the most pronounced advantage on open oven (58% vs. 18%) and open box (90% vs. 82%), where the differentiable-kinematics distillation in RK-Diffuser provides both accuracy and kinematic feasibility that the plain joint-position diffusion lacks.

## Relation to Prior Work

HDP sits at the intersection of next-best-pose (NBP) agents (Q-Attention, PerAct, C2F-ARM) and diffusion-based policies (Diffusion Policy, Diffuser). The closest concurrent work is ChainedDiffuser (Xian et al., 2023), which also chains a high-level pose predictor with a low-level diffusion policy but relies on inverse kinematics solvers to convert end-effector trajectories into joint commands. HDP's key departure is replacing the IK solver with a differentiable kinematics distillation step: RK-Diffuser learns both an end-effector pose diffusion and a joint position diffusion, then refines the joint trajectory by backpropagating through forward kinematics to match the pose trajectory, thereby avoiding the kinematic constraint violations that plague IK-based pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that IK errors account for 24.55% of failures (and 75% of those failures stem from invalid quaternions) in pose-diffusion approaches is practically important: it identifies a previously underappreciated bottleneck in the standard 'generate pose → solve IK' pipeline for over-actuated robots. The differentiable-kinematics distillation offers a clean, training-free-at-inference fix that preserves the accuracy of pose-space inpainting while guaranteeing kinematic feasibility. A key open question the authors flag is error accumulation in hierarchical behaviour cloning over long horizons, where high-level pose errors compound and push the low-level agent out of distribution; designing more unified architectures that minimise this compounding remains an active direction.
