## Problem

Existing vision-language datasets provide captions averaging only 5–10 words, far too sparse to capture the rich visual detail in an image, and current evaluation benchmarks (ARO, VL-Checklist) rely on hard negatives that language-prior methods can solve without looking at images. No dataset offers high-quality, dense, mask-aligned human annotations that could serve as a reliable benchmark for fine-grained vision-language alignment or as a fine-tuning resource for CLIP-style models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242140/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation takes RGB subcrops (crops centered on SAM-extracted hierarchical masks) and text captions constrained to 77 CLIP tokens, and produces a cosine similarity score used to rank which caption belongs to which subcrop within a batch of 8 drawn from the same image. The fine-tuning setup consumes the same subcrop-caption pairs with an optional LLM-generated negative per example, and outputs a contrastive similarity matrix over the batch. A key constraint is that the original DCI captions average 1,282 CLIP tokens per image, necessitating Llama2-based summarization into the 77-token sDCI format for compatibility with current CLIP architectures.

## Method

The paper constructs the DCI dataset by (1) sampling images from SA-1B, (2) extracting hierarchical submasks using SAM with canny-edge-guided point sampling to bias toward visually interesting regions, and (3) crowdsourcing dense annotations via Mephisto where workers describe each submask bottom-up and then provide a global relational description, yielding ~1,111 words per image. To fit CLIP's 77-token limit, Llama2 recursively summarizes each caption and generates hard negatives, producing the sDCI variant. The novel Subcrop-Caption Matching (SCM) benchmark requires a model to correctly assign each of 8 captions to its corresponding subcrop within a single image, testing fine-grained spatial-linguistic alignment beyond standard image-caption retrieval. For fine-tuning, they apply LoRA to a ViT/32B CLIP model trained with standard contrastive loss plus an optional NegCLIP-style text-negative loss on 7,800 sDCI examples (batch size 32, lr 5e-5, up to 10 epochs), demonstrating that a small set of highly aligned dense captions outperforms 10× more automatically generated DAC captions on ARO and VL-Checklist.

## Results

On the new sDCI Subcrop-Caption Matching (SCM) benchmark, no existing model performs well: CLIP achieves 40.06% and NegCLIP 43.35%, while DAC_SAM (the strongest on negatives at 84.17%) drops to 37.90% on SCM. The best sDCI-fine-tuned CLIP model reaches 64.02% on All SCM and 31.60% on All SCM Pick5, setting an upper bound. For fine-tuning, sDCI_P1 (7,800 images) outperforms DAC_LLM at 10,000 and 100,000 images on most ARO metrics (e.g., 76.23% vs. 61.53% on VG-R) and improves VL-Object from 81.17% (CLIP baseline) to 88.37% without explicit negatives.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the sDCI SCM benchmark, NegCLIP (43.35%) is the only model to beat CLIP (40.06%), while DAC_SAM and DAC_LLM perform worst (37.90% and 37.45%), suggesting that automated dense captions do not improve fine-grained spatial matching. On negatives-based tests, DAC_SAM dominates (84.17% All Neg, 73.61% All Hard Negs) far ahead of CLIP (60.79%, 41.34%), but this advantage does not transfer to SCM, revealing a trade-off between negatives-based training and spatial understanding. For fine-tuning, sDCI_P1 (7,800 images) substantially outperforms DAC_LLM at 10,000 images (76.23% vs. 61.53% on ARO VG-R; 88.58% vs. 46.28% on ARO COCO) and at 100,000 images, though it falls short of DAC at the full 3M scale (81.28% VG-R). Without negatives loss, sDCI_P1NL0 degrades on ARO but notably improves VL-Object from 81.17% (CLIP) to 88.37%, surpassing even DAC_SAM_3M (88.50%) and NegCLIP (81.35%). BLIP2 and SVLC, while competitive on VL-Checklist, are not directly comparable as they use different architectures and training objectives.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CLIP contrastive pre-training paradigm and the NegCLIP negative-based training recipe, but its core departure is from DAC (Doveh et al., 2023), which used an automated LLM pipeline to generate dense captions. Here, the authors replace that automation with human annotation via crowdsourcing, arguing that LLM-generated captions suffer from hallucinations and miss important visual elements, making them unreliable for evaluation. The Subcrop-Caption Matching (SCM) task is positioned as a complement to existing benchmarks like ARO and VL-Checklist, which the authors argue are confounded by linguistically unlikely hard negatives that text encoders can exploit without looking at images. SAM is used for mask extraction and Llama2 for summarization to fit within CLIP's 77-token constraint, bridging the gap between the full dense annotations and current model limitations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The DCI dataset is the first human-annotated dense image captioning resource with pixel-level mask alignment, and the SCM benchmark exposes a clear gap: no existing VLM reliably matches captions to their correct subregions within a single image. The finding that 7,805 high-quality human-annotated pairs outperform 10,000–100,000 automatically generated DAC pairs on ARO and VL-Checklist suggests that annotation quality can substitute for scale in fine-tuning, though the 3M-image DAC models still lead overall. A key open question is how to leverage the full 1,000+ word captions as models grow beyond 77 tokens, and whether incorporating the pixel-level masks (rather than just crops) during training would further improve fine-grained alignment. The dataset's small size (7,805 images) and CC-BY-NC license also limit its immediate utility for large-scale pre-training.
