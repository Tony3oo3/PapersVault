## Problem

Recent language-guided depth estimators (VPD, TADP, EVP) report state-of-the-art results, but the actual impact, generalization, and robustness of the language prior remain unexplored. This paper asks: does language guidance genuinely improve low-level depth estimation, or do these methods merely exploit scene-level biases? The study quantifies how language conditioning behaves under distribution shift, adversarial perturbations, and varying levels of linguistic detail.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242545/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluated model (VPD) takes an RGB image and a natural language sentence as input, producing a per-pixel dense depth map (max depth 10 m on NYUv2). The language input is encoded by a frozen CLIP text encoder into 768-dimensional embeddings, while the image is encoded by a frozen VQGAN encoder; cross-modal alignment occurs in a Stable Diffusion U-Net. The paper's key contribution is generating diverse sentence types—scene-level descriptions, object-pair spatial relationships (front/behind/above/below/left/right), image captions, and activity-level descriptions—directly from ground-truth depth and segmentation annotations, enabling controlled ablation of what the language signal actually contributes.

## Method

This is an empirical study rather than a new architecture. The authors build a sentence-generation framework that extracts pairwise 3D spatial relationships between objects using centroid coordinates, radii, and depth statistics from ground-truth annotations, then maps them into templated natural language sentences. They systematically evaluate VPD (and compare against vision-only baselines AdaBins, MIM-Depth, IDisc) in both supervised and zero-shot settings across four sentence types, two embedding-combination strategies (equal-weight stacking vs. template-concatenation), and varying numbers of sentences. Robustness is probed via object-level masking, a novel non-overlapping scene split on NYUv2, and zero-shot transfer to Sun RGB-D. Failure-mode analysis uses CLIPScore to show that CLIP cannot reliably distinguish correct from incorrect spatial sentences, and identifies the cross-attention layers of Stable Diffusion as the bottleneck for spatial faithfulness.

## Results

Under scene distribution shift (20 train / 7 test non-overlapping scenes on NYUv2), VPD suffers the largest relative RMSE increase at 107.48% above its in-domain baseline, compared to 100.54% for AdaBins, 83.62% for MIM-Depth, and 94.56% for IDisc. In zero-shot cross-dataset evaluation on Sun RGB-D, VPD's RMSE degrades by 74.01% relative to its NYUv2 baseline, far exceeding AdaBins (30.76%), MIM-Depth (27.87%), and IDisc (23.64%). Under object masking, VPD's δ₁ drops by 0.062 and RMSE by 0.093, while AdaBins drops only 0.008 and 0.007 respectively, despite VPD receiving explicit language about the masked object's spatial position.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VPD (872M params) is compared against three vision-only depth estimators: AdaBins (78M), MIM-Depth (195M), and IDisc (209M). Under scene distribution shift, VPD achieves the second-best absolute RMSE (0.547) behind MIM-Depth (0.527), but suffers the largest relative degradation (107.48% increase over its in-domain RMSE), indicating it is the least robust to novel scenes. In zero-shot cross-dataset transfer to Sun RGB-D, VPD (RMSE 0.442) is outperformed by MIM-Depth (0.367) and IDisc (0.387), with a 74% relative RMSE increase versus 24–31% for the vision-only baselines. Under object masking, AdaBins is dramatically more resilient than VPD: AdaBins' RMSE increases by only 0.007 while VPD's increases by 0.093, even though VPD is given explicit language describing the masked object's spatial relationship to another object. Overall, the paper demonstrates that language guidance does not confer robustness advantages and in fact makes VPD more vulnerable to both distribution shift and targeted perturbations compared to simpler vision-only architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a systematic empirical audit of language-guided depth estimation, building directly on VPD (Zhao et al., 2023) and its use of a frozen CLIP text encoder, VQGAN image encoder, and Stable Diffusion U-Net for depth prediction. It extends the robustness evaluation literature on monocular depth estimation (Ranftl et al., adversarial patch attacks) by specifically interrogating how the language conditioning channel interacts with distribution shift and adversarial perturbations. Unlike prior work that proposes new architectures or training objectives, this paper departs from the method-development paradigm to ask a diagnostic question: what does the language prior actually contribute, and where does it break down?

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that explicit spatial depth language ('A is in front of B') degrades performance relative to a trivial scene label ('a photo of a bedroom') challenges the core motivation for incorporating language into low-level vision and has direct implications for safety-critical deployments in autonomous driving and robotics. The paper identifies two concrete failure mechanisms—CLIP's inability to discriminate spatial relationships and Stable Diffusion's cross-attention layers lacking spatial faithfulness—that point toward specific architectural fixes for future work. Open questions include whether these limitations are inherent to the CLIP/Stable Diffusion stack or specific to VPD's training protocol, and whether alternative language encoders or alignment strategies could recover the benefit of low-level spatial descriptions.
