## Problem

Consistency training enables one-step image generation from diffusion models but produces lower-quality samples and demands very large batch sizes and model capacities. The paper identifies the root cause: the consistency training loss forms an upper bound on the Wasserstein distance that accumulates errors from all previous timesteps, forcing the model to simultaneously minimize both current and historical losses. This explains why reducing batch size or model size degrades performance so sharply.

## Input / Output

The model takes a Gaussian noise vector z ~ N(0,I) and a data sample x_0, constructs a noisy input x_0 + t·z at a sampled timestep t, and outputs a single-step generated image x_0 in one forward pass of the consistency network f(x_t, t, θ). For zero-shot inpainting, a partial image with a binary mask serves as input and the model fills in the masked region. Experiments use RGB images at 32×32 (CIFAR-10), 64×64 (ImageNet), and 256×256 (LSUN Cat) resolutions, with the DDPM U-Net as the backbone architecture.

## Method

ACT augments consistency training with a time-conditioned discriminator D(x_t, t, θ_d) that directly minimizes the Jensen-Shannon divergence between the generated and target distributions at each timestep, bypassing the accumulated-error problem of the pure consistency loss. The generator is trained with a weighted combination L_f = (1−λ)·L_CT + λ·L_G, where λ follows a schedule λ_N(n) = w·(n/(N−1))^{log(1/2)(w_mid/w)} that increases with timestep to counteract the growing Wasserstein bound. The discriminator uses a zero-centered gradient penalty applied intermittently, and for small datasets an adaptive data augmentation probability is modulated by the EMA of the gradient penalty to stabilize training. Theoretically, the paper proves (Theorem 3.1) that under a Lipschitz condition, minimizing the consistency loss reduces an upper bound on W-distance that includes the accumulated consistency loss, the sampling-distribution gap, and ODE discretization error—all of which grow with t, motivating the adversarial term that operates independently at each timestep.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, ACT achieves FID 6.4 (6.0 with adaptive data augmentation) in one NFE, improving over consistency training's 8.7 while using only 1/6 the batch size (80 vs. 512) and 300K vs. 800K training steps. On ImageNet 64×64, ACT reaches FID 10.6 (vs. CT's 13.0) with batch size 320 vs. 2048. On LSUN Cat 256×256, ACT achieves FID 13.0 (vs. CT's 20.7) with batch size 320 vs. 2048 and 165K vs. 1000K steps, using fewer than 1/2 the model parameters (113M+57M vs. 458M).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ACT outperforms consistency training (CT) on all three datasets: FID 6.4 vs. 8.7 on CIFAR-10, 10.6 vs. 13.0 on ImageNet 64×64, and 13.0 vs. 20.7 on LSUN Cat 256×256, while using less than 1/6 the batch size and fewer than 1/2 the training steps. Among one-step methods on CIFAR-10, ACT (FID 6.4) beats BigGAN (14.7), AutoGAN (12.4), TransGAN (9.26), CT (8.7), and all flow-based methods (Glow 48.9, Residual Flow 46.4, DenseFlow 34.9, 1-Rectified Flow 378), but trails ViTGAN (6.66) and the GAN baselines StyleGAN2-ADA (2.92) and StyleGAN2-XL (1.85). On ImageNet 64×64, ACT (FID 10.6) surpasses DDPM (11.0) and DDIM (13.7 at 50 NFE, 18.3 at 10 NFE) in one step, but remains behind multi-step methods EDM (2.44 at 79 NFE) and ADM (2.07 at 250 NFE), as well as BigGAN-deep (4.06). On LSUN Cat 256×256, ACT (13.0) beats CT (20.7), DDPM (17.1), and Progressive Distillation (18.3), but is outperformed by Consistency Distillation (11.0), EDM (6.69), and ADM (5.57). ACT also achieves higher recall than CT on ImageNet (0.56 vs. 0.47) and LSUN Cat (0.30 vs. 0.23), indicating better diversity despite slightly lower precision.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Consistency Models (Song et al., 2023) by incorporating a GAN-style discriminator into the consistency training loop, motivated by a theoretical analysis showing that the consistency training loss is an upper bound on the Wasserstein distance that accumulates errors across timesteps. The score-based generative model framework (Song et al., 2021) provides the ODE trajectory foundation, while the adversarial loss structure is borrowed from the classical GAN minimax formulation (Goodfellow et al., 2014) to directly minimize JS-divergence at each timestep. The key departure from prior consistency training is that the discriminator decouples the optimization at time t_k from accumulated losses at earlier times, eliminating the need for the large batch sizes that consistency training empirically requires.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: ACT achieves better FID scores with less than 1/6 the batch size, roughly 1/2 the model parameters, and 1/2 the training steps, making one-step diffusion generation accessible on a single GPU rather than requiring 64 A100s. The theoretical connection between consistency training loss and W-distance upper bound provides a clear explanation for the batch-size sensitivity that was previously only observed empirically. Open questions include the interaction dynamics between L_CT and L_G (which can conflict when L_CT is large), whether other divergence measures beyond JS-divergence could further improve performance, and whether the gradient-penalty-based adaptive augmentation generalizes to larger datasets. The method still trails multi-step diffusion models (e.g., EDM FID 2.04 vs. ACT 6.0 on CIFAR10), so the quality-efficiency tradeoff remains an active frontier.
