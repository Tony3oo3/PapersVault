## Problem

State-of-the-art keypoint trackers are trained exclusively on synthetic benchmarks whose scenes lack the visual artifacts (texture, lighting variation, occlusions, motion distortion) prevalent in real-world videos, yet existing real-world benchmarks (DAVIS, Kinetics) provide only tens of human-labeled tracks per scene—far too sparse for fine-tuning. DriveTrack addresses this gap by automatically generating 1 billion point tracks across 24 hours of real-world driving video, matching the annotation scale of synthetic benchmarks while capturing genuine visual imperfections.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241288/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

DriveTrack consumes timestamped LiDAR point clouds, 3D bounding box annotations, and camera extrinsic/intrinsic matrices from autonomous driving datasets (Waymo Open Dataset at 1280×1920, 10 fps), and produces per-object 2D point tracks of shape N×F×2, binary occlusion maps of shape N×F, and dense depth maps. Unlike synthetic benchmarks that render exact correspondences, DriveTrack is confined to the finite set of points sampled by the ego vehicle's LiDAR sensor, which lack 1:1 correspondence across sweeps, necessitating a geometric shifting procedure rather than direct rendering.

## Method

The core mechanism is a geometric transformation pipeline that 'shifts' each LiDAR point cloud X_t across all timesteps τ using the formula X̂_τ^(t) = W_τ⁻¹ B_τ B_τ⁻¹ W_t X_t, which projects points to the global frame, re-expresses them in the target object's bounding-box coordinate system, applies the next timestep's bounding-box transform, and projects back to the ego-vehicle frame before camera projection to image space. For occlusion estimation, DriveTrack completes sparse LiDAR depth into dense depth maps (via Nearest Neighbor interpolation or CompletionFormer) and flags a point as occluded when its 3D distance exceeds the nearest physical surface along the same ray. The paper then fine-tunes TAP-Net, TAPIR, and PIPs++ on these automatically generated annotations, yielding 4–7% AJ improvements on DriveTrack and 1–2% transfer gains on DAVIS, and uses the large-scale annotations to quantify heavy-tailed tracking error sensitivity to visual artifacts, motivating track-aware keypoint selectors.

## Results

Fine-tuning state-of-the-art trackers on DriveTrack improves Average Jaccard (AJ) by 7% for TAP-Net (63.6→70.3), 5% for TAPIR (78.8→84.1), and 4% for PIPs++ (<δx_avg: 81.5→85.3) on DriveTrack's test set. Transfer to the DAVIS real-world benchmark yields 1–2% AJ gains (e.g., TAPIR: 62.8→64.0). The best fine-tuned TAPIR achieves 84.1% AJ on DriveTrack, matching the 84.7% AJ of TAPIR trained on the synthetic Kubric benchmark, demonstrating that real-world fine-tuning closes the synthetic-to-real gap. Positional accuracy (<δx_avg) shows the largest improvements (e.g., TAPIR: 87.1→90.9 on DriveTrack), while occlusion accuracy (OA) improves more modestly (e.g., TAPIR: 94.4→95.1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three state-of-the-art trackers (TAP-Net, TAPIR, PIPs++) in their original synthetic-trained form against versions fine-tuned on DriveTrack. On the DriveTrack test set, all three trackers improve substantially: TAP-Net gains +6.7 AJ points (63.6→70.3), TAPIR gains +5.3 (78.8→84.1), and PIPs++ gains +3.8 <δx_avg points (81.5→85.3). On DAVIS, a real-world benchmark from a different domain, fine-tuning still yields consistent 1–2% AJ improvements (TAP-Net: 38.4→39.2; TAPIR: 62.8→64.0; PIPs++: 70.4→71.2 <δx_avg), demonstrating cross-domain transfer. However, fine-tuning on DriveTrack degrades performance on the synthetic Kubric benchmark (e.g., TAP-Net AJ drops from 65.4 to 37.0; TAPIR from 84.7 to 80.8), confirming a trade-off between synthetic and real-world generalization. The fine-tuned TAPIR (84.1 AJ on DriveTrack) nearly matches the synthetic-trained TAPIR on Kubric (84.7 AJ), indicating DriveTrack closes the synthetic-to-real performance gap for real-world scenes.

## Relation to Prior Work

DriveTrack repurposes the annotation infrastructure of autonomous driving datasets (LiDAR sweeps, 3D bounding boxes, camera poses) to generate dense point tracking labels, adapting the geometric transformation pipeline used by synthetic benchmarks (Kubric, PointOdyssey) to a setting where no simulator exists. It departs from prior real-world TAP benchmarks (DAVIS, Kinetics via TAP-Vid) by replacing sparse human annotations (tens of tracks per scene) with automatic, LiDAR-derived annotations at a scale comparable to synthetic datasets. The occlusion estimation via depth completion (Nearest Neighbor, CompletionFormer) borrows from the depth-completion literature, and the evaluation protocol follows the standard TAP-Vid metrics used by TAPIR, TAP-Net, and PIPs++.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DriveTrack is the first real-world point tracking benchmark at synthetic scale (1B tracks, 84B annotated points), enabling for the first time the fine-tuning of trackers on real-world visual artifacts and revealing that tracking error is heavy-tailed in a way that no amount of additional training data can fully resolve. This motivates the underexplored direction of track-aware keypoint selectors as a complement to trackers. A key limitation is that the workflow requires LiDAR, 3D bounding boxes, and camera poses, confining it to autonomous driving (or similar) datasets; the authors note that smartphones with LiDAR could be a future target. Open questions include whether the heavy-tailed error structure persists across architectures and whether a learned keypoint selector can meaningfully reduce tail error.
