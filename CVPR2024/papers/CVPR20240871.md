## Problem

In-vehicle gaze estimation lacks a comprehensive dataset with dense gaze annotations, as existing vehicle datasets only provide coarse gaze zone labels and traditional gaze collection systems are impractical in confined vehicular environments. Additionally, in-vehicle face images suffer from low resolution due to camera limitations, making it difficult for conventional methods to capture subtle eye-region details needed for accurate gaze estimation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240871/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an infrared face image (1280×800) captured by a DMS camera behind the steering wheel, along with its perspective-transformed (normalized) counterpart and the corresponding camera pose matrix. Gaze is annotated as a unit 3D direction vector from the nose position (via 3D morphable face model fitting) toward a physical gaze target sticker. The primary output is a 3D gaze direction vector; the extended task outputs a discrete gaze zone class label (e.g., windshield, left mirror, center console).

## Method

GazePTR extracts multi-level feature maps from a CNN backbone, unifies their dimensions via 1×1 convolutions and global average pooling, and feeds all levels into a transformer with a learnable aggregation token; per-level gaze regression losses prevent the network from discarding low-level features critical for eye-region detail. GazeDPTR extends this to a dual-stream architecture that processes both the original and perspective-normalized images through separate GazePTR branches, then merges the two feature streams in a transformer where camera pose (identity for original, R for normalized) serves as positional encoding to establish cross-stream correspondence. The data normalization strategy defines the virtual camera z-axis along the camera-to-face-center direction with a fixed x-axis, avoiding the instability of head-axis-based rotation at extreme poses. For gaze zone classification, the estimated gaze vector is projected onto a foundational tri-plane (xy, yz, xz at origin), and positional features from the three intersection points—encoded via positional encoding and a 2-layer transformer—are fused with visual features for final classification. The entire network is trained end-to-end with L1 loss for gaze regression and cross-entropy loss for zone classification.

## Results

On the IVGaze dataset (44,705 images, 125 subjects, 3-fold cross-validation), GazeDPTR achieves the best angular error of 6.71° and the highest average precision at all thresholds (22.1% at <2°, 36% at <4°, 50.3% at <6°, 68.4% at <8°). GazePTR, the single-stream variant, achieves 7.04° angular error, marginally outperforming XGaze (7.06°) in overall error but substantially surpassing it in precision (17.6% vs 11.7% AP at <2°). The dual-stream design of GazeDPTR provides an additional 0.33° improvement over GazePTR, and the multi-level feature pyramid contributes 0.29° over the single-level GazeTR baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GazeDPTR outperforms all five baselines on both angular error and average precision. Against the closest competitor XGaze (7.06°), GazeDPTR improves angular error by 0.35° and more than doubles the AP at <2° (22.1% vs 11.7%), indicating substantially more precise gaze predictions even when mean error is similar. GazePTR (7.04°) edges out XGaze in angular error and significantly surpasses it in precision (17.6% vs 11.7% at <2°), which the authors attribute to XGaze's deeper ResNet50 backbone overlooking the small eye region. Compared to GazeTR (7.33°), which shares the same backbone and transformer architecture, GazePTR gains 0.29° purely from multi-level feature integration. The older in-vehicle method DWG (8.82°) and the outdoor Gaze360 (8.15°) are both outperformed by over 1.4°, highlighting the advantage of purpose-built in-vehicle data and architecture. FullFace (13.67°) and its ResNet18 variant FullFace+ (7.48°) are the weakest and strongest CNN-only baselines respectively, with GazeDPTR still leading FullFace+ by 0.77°.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends transformer-based gaze estimation (GazeTR) and dual-view gaze estimation (DVGaze) to the in-vehicle setting, introducing a feature pyramid to capture multi-level details and a dual-stream architecture that fuses original and perspective-normalized images using camera pose as positional encoding. The tri-plane projection strategy for gaze zone classification is adapted from GazeOnce's self-supervised loss formulation, repurposed here as a positional feature extractor. A key departure from prior in-vehicle work is the shift from coarse zone classification to dense 3D gaze vector estimation, enabled by a novel vision-based collection system that avoids eye-tracking hardware and solves the out-of-field-of-view calibration problem via a transparent chessboard.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

IVGaze fills a critical gap as the first in-vehicle gaze dataset with dense gaze annotation and natural face images, unblocking research that was previously limited to zone-level classification. The reproducible collection protocol (single DMS camera + stickers + depth camera for calibration) lowers the barrier for future in-vehicle gaze research. However, the dataset is collected in a single left-hand-drive vehicle with one IR camera, raising open questions about generalization across vehicle types, camera modalities, and driving conditions. The modest algorithmic gains (~0.3° over GazeTR) suggest the primary value lies in the benchmark and collection methodology rather than a new architectural paradigm.
