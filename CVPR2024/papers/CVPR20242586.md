## Problem

State-of-the-art image processing algorithms (translation, segmentation, super-resolution) significantly outperform their video counterparts in quality, yet applying them per-frame to video introduces severe temporal flickering. Existing video representations like layered neural atlases suffer from limited reconstruction capacity (missing subtle motions) and produce distorted canonical images that degrade downstream image algorithm performance, creating a fundamental barrier to lifting image methods to video.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242586/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a video clip (sequence of RGB frames, ~100 frames typical) and optionally a text prompt for translation tasks. It outputs a 2D canonical image (whose spatial extent can exceed the original frame size to accommodate scene motion) and a 3D temporal deformation field mapping each frame's pixels back to canonical coordinates. Downstream, any image algorithm applied to the canonical image produces results that are warped to every frame via the deformation field, yielding temporally consistent translated frames, segmentation masks, super-resolved video, or keypoint trajectories without per-frame inference.

## Method

CoDeF factorizes a video into a 2D multi-resolution hash-encoded canonical content field (mapping 2D positions to RGB) and a 3D multi-resolution hash-encoded deformation field (mapping (x,y,t) to canonical position x'), each followed by a tiny MLP. Three key design choices ensure the canonical image remains semantically natural while reconstruction stays faithful: (1) annealed hash encoding progressively introduces high-frequency deformation features during training, starting from a smooth grid that captures rigid motion before adding fine details; (2) a flow-guided consistency loss computed from RAFT optical flow enforces that high-confidence correspondences between consecutive frames map to the same canonical point; (3) for multi-object scenes, SAM-track segmentation defines grouped canonical/deformation field pairs with a background regularization loss on unsupervised regions. Training is a per-scene optimization (~5 min on an A6000 GPU for 100 frames) minimizing L2 reconstruction loss plus the flow and background regularizers, after which the canonical image is extracted by zeroing all deformation and processed by off-the-shelf image algorithms.

## Results

On video reconstruction (DAVIS), CoDeF achieves 2.3 dB higher PSNR than the Layered Neural Atlas baseline (e.g., 34.26 vs 28.37 on Kite-surf, 34.13 vs 31.51 on Boat). For text-guided video-to-video translation, CoDeF attains the highest user-rated frame consistency score (4.76/5) and textual alignment score (4.13/5) across 1,120 responses from 56 users, outperforming all baselines. Training takes approximately 5 minutes on a single A6000 GPU for 100 frames, compared to over 10 hours for the layered atlas approach.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CoDeF outperforms all three video translation baselines on user-rated frame consistency (4.76 vs. 4.21 for Text2LIVE, 3.75 for FateZero, 2.12 for Tune-A-Video) and user-rated textual alignment (4.13 vs. 2.32, 3.34, 3.89 respectively). On CLIP-based metrics, Text2LIVE scores higher (99.17 frame consistency, 30.72 textual alignment), but the authors note this is expected since Text2LIVE is explicitly optimized with CLIP loss. For video reconstruction, CoDeF surpasses the Layered Neural Atlas by 2.3 dB PSNR on DAVIS (e.g., 34.26 vs. 28.37 on Kite-surf), while also producing a more natural canonical image that better supports downstream image algorithms. The grouped variant (K=2) provides a small additional reconstruction gain over the single-field version (K=1), and removing the flow consistency loss causes visible flickering in smooth regions.

## Relation to Prior Work

CoDeF builds directly on the dynamic NeRF lineage (NeRFies, HyperNeRF) for its deformation-field concept, but replaces Fourier positional encoding with multi-resolution 3D hash encoding (à la Instant-NGP) to gain both capacity and training speed. It departs from layered neural atlas approaches (Text2Live, Layered Neural Rendering) by using a single canonical 2D image plus a continuous deformation field rather than explicit per-layer atlases, and introduces annealed hash encoding to resolve the tension between reconstruction fidelity and canonical-image naturalness. The 'lifting' framing—applying an off-the-shelf image algorithm once on the canonical image and propagating results via the deformation field—repurposes ControlNet, SAM, and R-ESRGAN without any video-specific training, positioning CoDeF as a bridge between the mature image-processing ecosystem and video tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The lifting paradigm offers a practical shortcut: expensive generative or detection models need to run on a single image rather than every frame, yielding strong temporal consistency and a ~100× training speedup over layered atlases (5 min vs. 10 h). However, the method requires per-scene optimization, struggles with extreme viewpoint changes, large non-rigid deformations, and scenes with sudden object appearance/disappearance, all of which limit its generality. Open questions include whether feed-forward implicit fields can eliminate per-scene fitting, whether 3D priors can handle viewpoint changes, and whether multiple canonical images can cover highly non-rigid content—directions the authors themselves flag as future work.
