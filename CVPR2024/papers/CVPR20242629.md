## Problem

Diffusion models suffer significant quality degradation when the number of function evaluations (NFEs) is reduced for fast sampling, because the Gaussian assumption on the reverse process breaks down with large step sizes. This creates a gap between the training objective (which assumes fine-grained steps) and the actual negative log-likelihood at inference, leading to blurry, inconsistent, or unrealistic samples. The paper asks how to train a denoising network that remains accurate under coarse discretization without altering the inference procedure.

## Input / Output

The method takes random Gaussian noise (and optionally a class label for conditional generation) and produces RGB images at 32×32 (CIFAR-10), 64×64 (CelebA), or 256×256 (LSUN Church). A key practical detail is that the time-dependent discriminator used during training is entirely discarded at inference, so sampling uses only the standard denoising network with any compatible ODE solver (Euler, Heun's, PNDM) at no extra computational or memory cost. The method is compatible with both unconditional and conditional generation and can be applied from scratch or as fine-tuning on pretrained baselines.

## Method

OGDM augments the DDPM Markov chain with an observation process: at each time step, a Bernoulli variable indicates whether the noisy state lies on the real data manifold. This observation is implemented by projecting the current noisy state forward in time (by s steps) using a numerical ODE solver step, then scoring the projected sample with a time-dependent discriminator that distinguishes real data from denoiser predictions. The training objective combines the standard diffusion transition loss with an adversarial emission loss (−γ log D_φ(ẑ_{t−s}^θ, t, s)), trained via alternating optimization with the discriminator. The theoretical analysis shows that the emission loss minimizes the JS-divergence between the model's predicted distribution and the true data distribution, corresponding to a KL-divergence term in the inference NLL bound that vanilla diffusion models neglect. Because the discriminator is only active during training, inference remains identical to the baseline with no additional NFEs or architectural changes.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 (32×32), OGDM achieves FID 2.17 at 25 NFEs and 4.97 at 10 NFEs, substantially outperforming all compared fast-sampling methods (e.g., GENIE: 5.28 at 10 NFEs; Watson et al.: 7.86 at 10 NFEs; DDIM: 13.36 at 10 NFEs). On CelebA (64×64), OGDM reaches FID 2.62 at 25 NFEs and 4.35 at 10 NFEs, beating S-PNDM (7.33 at 10 NFEs) and F-PNDM (no result at 10 NFEs) by large margins. The fine-tuned variant (OGDM ft) on the EDM backbone reduces CIFAR-10 FID from 19.32 (baseline) to 9.28 at 10 NFEs, and from 15.57 to 6.85 for the conditional EDM model, demonstrating that even 5–10% of training iterations suffice for strong gains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OGDM consistently outperforms all compared fast-sampling methods on both CIFAR-10 and CelebA across all reported NFEs. On CIFAR-10 at 10 NFEs, OGDM (FID 4.97) beats GENIE (5.28), Watson et al. (7.86), FastDPM (9.90), DDIM (13.36), and Analytic-DPM (14.00); at 25 NFEs it achieves 2.17 versus GENIE's 3.64 and Watson et al.'s 4.25. On CelebA, the advantage is even more pronounced: at 10 NFEs OGDM reaches 4.35 while S-PNDM scores 7.33, F-PNDM has no result, and DDIM/FastDPM/Analytic-DPM all exceed 15. Against the unmodified ADM and EDM backbones (which serve as the 'Baseline' rows in Tables 1–3), OGDM reduces FID by roughly 30–50% at low NFEs (e.g., CIFAR-10/EDM: 19.32→9.28 at 10 NFEs; CelebA/ADM: 11.92→7.04 at 10 NFEs). The fine-tuned variant (OGDM ft) matches or exceeds from-scratch training with only 5–10% of the training iterations, and the method is compatible with both Euler and Heun's-method samplers as well as PNDM, showing broad applicability across inference strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DDPM Markov chain framework by introducing an observation (emission) process at each time step, where the emission is a Bernoulli variable indicating whether the noisy state lies on the real data manifold. The transition loss recovers the standard denoising objective, while the emission loss is implemented via a time-dependent discriminator trained in a GAN-like fashion—conceptually borrowing from non-saturating GANs but repurposing the discriminator purely as a training-time observation signal rather than an inference-time guide (as in DG) or a non-Gaussian reverse-process corrector (as in DDGAN). The method is orthogonal to fast sampling strategies like PNDM, DDIM, and EDM's Heun solver, and is compatible with both training from scratch and fine-tuning pretrained diffusion backbones (ADM, EDM, LDM).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical advantage is that the discriminator is entirely discarded at inference, so the method incurs zero extra sampling cost while substantially improving few-step generation quality—particularly when fine-tuning pretrained models with only 5–10% of original training iterations. The theoretical decomposition showing that the inference-time KL-divergence splits into a score-matching term (optimized by standard diffusion loss) and a distribution-matching term (optimized by the emission/GAN loss) clarifies why vanilla diffusion degrades under coarse discretization. Open questions include whether the observation formulation can be generalized beyond Bernoulli/discriminator implementations, whether adaptive selection of the lookahead variable s or task-specific emission distributions yield further gains, and whether OGDM-trained score models serve as stronger teachers for consistency distillation.
