## Problem

Prior work on corneal imaging could only recover panoramic environment maps or simple 3D structures (e.g., box meshes) from manually specified correspondences in a single eye image. This paper asks whether a full 3D radiance field of the scene a person is observing can be reconstructed from a sequence of eye reflections captured by a stationary camera, a task made difficult by the entanglement of iris texture with scene reflections and the imprecision of pose estimation from small corneal regions in portraits.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240002/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 5–15 RAW 16-bit portrait frames of a person moving their head within a fixed camera's field of view, along with the camera focal length. Unlike standard NeRF, the camera never moves; multi-view information is extracted from the changing cornea reflections as the subject's head translates and rotates. The output is a NeRF radiance field of the observed scene (enabling novel view synthesis from arbitrary viewpoints), a learned 2D iris texture map, and refined 6DoF cornea poses. The cornea is modeled as a fixed-geometry ellipsoid (eccentricity 0.5, curvature 7.8 mm, base radius 5.5 mm), so only its 6DoF pose is unknown per frame.

## Method

The core contribution is a joint optimization that simultaneously trains (a) a NeRF radiance field for the scene, (b) a shared 2D neural field Φ representing the iris texture, and (c) the 6DoF SE(3) pose of each cornea. Camera rays are intersected with the ellipsoidal cornea in closed form, reflected using the surface normal, and then volume-rendered through the NeRF to produce the scene component; the iris component is obtained by intersecting the same camera ray with a flat iris plane behind the cornea and querying Φ. A radial symmetry regularization loss penalizes iris texture colors that deviate from the local ring mean beyond a 10th–90th percentile band, preventing static scene regions from being absorbed into the texture field. Cornea poses are initialized via a weak-perspective depth estimate from the known iris radius and refined end-to-end during training, following the noisy-pose NeRF paradigm. The data pipeline uses GroundingDINO for eye localization, ELLSeg for ellipse fitting, and SAM for occlusion-aware iris segmentation masks.

## Results

The paper reports no external baseline comparisons; quantitative evaluation is limited to ablation studies on synthetic data. Pose optimization tolerates approximately 1% error in initial eye center estimate, 2.5% error in initial iris radius estimate, or 5 degrees of rotation error before reconstruction quality degrades significantly. For texture decomposition, the method achieves better SSIM and LPIPS scores when the iris texture is explicitly modeled via a 2D neural field compared to training without decomposition (Figure 7). PSNR is not reported due to the drastic intensity difference between cornea reflections and the scene itself.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baselines or prior scene-reconstruction methods; the `compares_to` list is empty. All quantitative comparisons are internal ablations: (1) with vs. without 6DoF cornea pose optimization, showing that pose refinement is the most critical component for coherent reconstruction and that without it, the radiance field exhibits severe artifacts; (2) with vs. without iris texture decomposition (2D neural field Φ), where the decomposed version yields higher SSIM and lower LPIPS; and (3) with vs. without radial regularization, which prevents low-disparity scene regions from being absorbed into the learned iris texture. The authors note that pose optimization is the single most important component, validated by both the synthetic noise-sensitivity study (Section 5.1) and real-world ablations (Figure 10).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the foundational catadioptric imaging work of Nishino and Nayar (2004, 2006), which recovered panoramic images and simple box meshes from single-image eye reflections with manual correspondences, to full radiance field reconstruction from a sequence of frames with a moving head. It integrates this eye-reflection formulation with NeRF-based volume rendering (Mildenhall et al., 2020) and adapts the joint pose-and-scene optimization strategy from BARF (Lin et al., 2021) to the cornea pose problem. A key methodological departure from prior reflection-removal work (e.g., NerfRen) is the use of a radial symmetry prior on the iris texture field rather than fitting a separate NeRF for the reflection component, and the use of a radial symmetry prior instead of the radial autocorrelation prior from earlier eye-reflection work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work demonstrates the feasibility of recovering a full 3D radiance field from the tiny, noisy reflections in a person's eyes, opening a new modality for non-line-of-sight and accidental imaging that requires no special hardware beyond a standard camera. The practical limitations are significant: the current results depend on a controlled laboratory setup with deliberate head movement, high-resolution RAW captures, and post-processing, making unconstrained scenarios (video conferencing, natural motion, compressed video) an open challenge. The finding that 6DoF cornea pose optimization is the single most critical component—more so than texture decomposition—provides a clear design principle for future work in this area and suggests that accurate small-object pose estimation remains a bottleneck for reflection-based reconstruction.
