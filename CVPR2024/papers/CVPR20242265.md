## Problem

Natural Light Uncalibrated Photometric Stereo (NaUPS) aims to reconstruct surface normals from images under arbitrary, unknown environment lighting, but remains an open problem due to intrinsic ill-posedness and high-dimensional light-object ambiguities. Existing unsupervised methods rely on restrictive light and material models (e.g., low-order spherical harmonics, Lambertian reflectance) that fail on general objects, while supervised deep-learning approaches lack interpretability and are prone to data bias toward specific light types or reflection variations.

## Input / Output

The method takes a sequence of 50 RGB images (512×512 synthetic, 540×540 real) of a single object captured while the object and a fixed camera rotate together 360° on a rotatable platform, along with pre-computed occluding-boundary normals. It jointly outputs a per-pixel surface normal map, a 64-basis spherical Gaussian environment light, a spatially varying isotropic reflectance map (diffuse albedo + 12 SG specular lobes), and a neural depth field representing the 3D surface. The key constraint is that the camera-object relative geometry is fixed across all views, so only the apparent light direction changes via 1-DoF rotation.

## Method

Spin-UP exploits a spin-light setup where the object and camera co-rotate on a platform, modeling the environment as a single uniform light rotated by a 1-DoF angle per image, reducing light unknowns from ~1.4M (EqvDir) to 434 parameters and enabling advanced spherical Gaussian light and Disney BRDF reflectance models in an unsupervised setting. A light initialization method derives an initial 64-SG environment light from occluding-boundary pixel values and pre-computed boundary normals, applying a threshold filter (removing top 20% brightest pixels to suppress specular bias), a 3rd-order spherical harmonics low-pass filter, and a grayscale conversion to reduce chromatic bias from spatially varying materials. The inverse rendering framework uses an MLP neural depth field for shape, a separate MLP for diffuse albedo, and 12 weighted SG bases with learnable roughness for specular reflectance, optimized via inverse rendering loss, total variation smoothness, boundary normal cosine loss, and normalized color loss in a three-stage schedule. Two training strategies—interval sampling (extracting non-overlapping sub-image blocks to compute smoothness terms at reduced resolution, cutting GPU memory from 25 GB to 5 GB) and shrinking range computing (gradually reducing the interpolation neighborhood for normal calculation from k=3 to k=1 to avoid local optima on down-sampled images)—enable convergence in ~25 minutes.

## Results

On the synthetic dataset, Spin-UP achieves the lowest mean angle error (MAE) across all four evaluation groups: 8.72° (shape), 4.54° (light), 9.08° (reflectance), and 9.15° (spatially varying material), outperforming the strongest supervised baseline S23 by 3.7°–6.3° per group. For environment light estimation, Spin-UP attains PU-PSNR of 22.06 and PU-SSIM of 0.60, substantially exceeding random (16.86/0.45) and Fibonacci (18.98/0.52) initialization baselines. On real-world data, Spin-UP captures high-frequency details (e.g., clothing wrinkles) while remaining robust to indoor lighting where S23 degrades significantly. Training requires only ~5 GB GPU memory and ~25 minutes per object.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Spin-UP (unsupervised) outperforms all three baselines on every group of the synthetic benchmark. Against the strongest supervised baseline S23 [11], Spin-UP reduces MAE by 3.7° on shape, 6.3° on light, 3.9° on reflectance, and 4.4° on spatially varying material — the largest gap appears in the light group, where Spin-UP's boundary-based light initialization provides a clear advantage. The older supervised method S22 [10] lags further behind (16.63° vs. 8.72° on shape), and the unsupervised optimization-based HY19 [7] is far inferior (54.49°), confirming that its low-order SH light model cannot handle complex natural lighting. On real-world captures, Spin-UP is visually competitive with S23 and more robust in indoor scenes where S23's performance degrades due to data bias and low pixel variance. When a dominant point light is added to the environment (to match S23's training distribution), Spin-UP still achieves a lower average MAE (9.48° vs. 11.46°), demonstrating generalization to unseen light sources.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the NaUPS problem (introduced by Guo et al. [6]) by proposing a rotatable-platform 'spin light' setup that models the environment as a single static light rotated by 1-DoF, drastically reducing the number of light unknowns compared to per-image light modeling in prior unsupervised methods (QL15, HY19, GM21). This reduction in unknowns is what enables the use of advanced spherical-Gaussian light and Disney-BRDF material models in an unsupervised framework, whereas prior unsupervised methods were restricted to low-order SH or equivalent directional lights. The light initialization from occluding boundaries repurposes a boundary-prior idea from multi-view and photometric stereo [7, 16, 27] in a new way: deriving an environment light estimate rather than a shape or directional light position. The overall framework draws on neural inverse rendering (NeRD, PhySG, DANI-Net) but applies it to the photometric stereo setting with a static camera-object relative pose.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Spin-UP is the first unsupervised NaUPS method that handles general (non-Lambertian, spatially varying) objects under complex natural light, and it does so with modest compute (5 GB GPU, ~25 min), making it practical for settings where large training datasets are unavailable. However, significant limitations remain: the method assumes infinitely far (spatially uniform) light, isotropic reflectance, and no inter-reflections, and the real-world evaluation is purely qualitative with no quantitative metrics. Open questions include whether the spin-light setup can be relaxed to free rotations for portable devices, whether spatially varying lighting and anisotropic materials can be incorporated, and whether the boundary-based light initialization generalizes to objects with complex self-occlusion.
