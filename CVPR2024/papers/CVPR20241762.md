## Problem

Interactive motion synthesis for games and VR requires generating diverse, high-quality character animations at real-time rates, but existing approaches face a trilemma: Motion Matching is high-fidelity yet scales poorly, neural networks are fast but produce limited diversity, and diffusion models capture multimodal transitions but are too slow due to expensive iterative denoising. No prior method simultaneously achieves quality, diversity, and interactive-speed generation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a 64-dimensional embedded vector (xz) representing the previous frame's pose—comprising a 52-dim learned latent code and 12-dim engineered features (2D trajectory positions and facing direction at 20/40/60 frames ahead)—along with random noise, and produces the next 338-dim full-body pose vector (joint translations, rotations, and velocities) autoregressively. User commands (desired trajectory and facing direction) are injected via gradient-based guided diffusion at each denoising step rather than through a separate projection network. The full pipeline requires only 5 forward passes per frame (3 GAN + 2 ADM), achieving 173 FPS on an RTX 3080 Ti.

## Method

AAMDM operates in a learned 64-dim embedded space (via an autoencoder trained with value, velocity, and regularization losses) rather than the 338-dim full pose space, reducing training complexity and improving multimodal capture. The core generation pipeline splits each frame's prediction into two sub-steps: a Generation Module using Denoising Diffusion GANs (3 reverse steps with a multimodal conditional GAN parameterization) produces a fast draft, and a Polishing Module using an Auto-regressive Diffusion Model (2 reverse steps that directly predict the target vector rather than noise) refines it for long-horizon stability. This hybrid design exploits the insight that DDGANs excel at fast coarse generation while ADM provides the stable fine-tuning needed to prevent divergence over extended sequences. User control is achieved by perturbing the predicted vector at each diffusion step with the gradient of a tracking objective toward the commanded trajectory, unifying pose transition and command following in a single guided process. All networks are simple MLPs trained with RAdam over 1M iterations on 10-frame windows, with the Generation and Polishing modules trained separately using their respective adversarial and reconstruction losses.

## Results

On LaFAN1, AAMDM achieves a FID of 14.051 and FFR of 0.131 in random motion synthesis, closely matching AMDM200 (FID 12.132, FFR 0.129) while running at 173 FPS versus AMDM200's 4.72 FPS (~40× faster). In the interactive (user-controlled) setting, AAMDM attains TE-UC of 0.034, FID-UC of 15.367, and FFR-UC of 0.143, outperforming all other baselines on quality metrics while maintaining interactive rates. The diversity score (DIV) of 11.574 is closest to the dataset value of 14.533 among all fast methods, indicating strong mode coverage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AAMDM decisively outperforms LMM [25] on all quality metrics (FID 14.051 vs 49.706; FFR 0.131 vs 0.194; FID-UC 15.367 vs 55.674), attributed to LMM's MSE-based training that cannot capture many-to-many transitions, though LMM is faster (812 vs 173 FPS) due to a single feed-forward pass. Against MVAE [31], AAMDM improves FID (14.051 vs 22.981) and FFR (0.131 vs 0.312) substantially, while MVAE retains a speed advantage (703 vs 173 FPS). Compared to AMDM5 [50], AAMDM achieves markedly better FID (14.051 vs 18.741) and FFR (0.131 vs 0.214) at similar speed (173 vs 192 FPS), because DD-GANs handle the multimodal distribution better than Gaussian-assumption diffusion at only 5 steps. The closest competitor is AMDM200 [50], which edges out AAMDM slightly on FID (12.132 vs 14.051) and FFR (0.129 vs 0.131) but is ~40× slower (4.72 vs 173 FPS), making it impractical for interactive use. In the user-controlled setting, AAMDM achieves the best FID-UC (15.367) and FFR-UC (0.143) among all methods running above 100 FPS, while AMDM200's marginally better TE-UC (0.012 vs 0.034) is irrelevant at 4.72 FPS.

## Relation to Prior Work

AAMDM sits at the intersection of three established lines of work: DDPM-style autoregressive diffusion for motion (AMDM, Shi et al.), Denoising Diffusion GANs for single-step generation (Xiao et al.), and guided diffusion for user-controllable animation (Rempe et al.). Its key architectural departure is the two-stage decomposition—using DDGANs as a fast 'Generation Module' (3 steps) followed by a standard ADM as a 'Polishing Module' (2 steps)—operating entirely in a learned 64-dimensional embedded space rather than the 338-dimensional full pose space. This contrasts with AMDM, which simply reduces diffusion steps in full pose space, and with LMM/MVAE, which use single-step feed-forward networks that cannot capture multimodal transitions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is the 40× speedup over AMDM200 (173 vs 4.72 FPS) while maintaining comparable FID, making diffusion-quality motion synthesis viable for real-time interactive applications like games and VR. However, the evaluation is limited to a single small dataset (LaFAN1, 25 clips, 26.67 min) and simple MLP backbones, leaving open whether the approach scales to larger, more diverse motion corpora or benefits from richer architectures. The authors also note that gradient-based guidance for user control is a limitation, and that the trade-off between quality and speed may be further improved with structured latent spaces (e.g., matrix-Fisher distributions) or learned control mechanisms.
