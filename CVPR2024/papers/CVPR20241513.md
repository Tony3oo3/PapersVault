## Problem

Coded-aperture light-field imaging requires acquiring 2–4 images sequentially, making measurement time long and unsuitable for dynamic scenes. Joint aperture-exposure coding reduces this to a single exposure but demands complex, non-commercial hardware. The paper addresses this gap by combining a coded aperture with an off-the-shelf event camera so that all necessary parallax information is captured within a single image-frame exposure.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241513/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single grayscale image frame (346×260 px from a DAVIS 346 sensor) that is the temporal sum of N=4 coded-aperture images, plus N−1=3 event stacks (same spatial resolution) obtained by summing asynchronous events during the transient periods between consecutive coding-pattern changes. The output is a reconstructed light field of 8×8 views (64 views) at full sensor resolution. Unusually, the events are not caused by scene motion but are actively induced by the camera's own aperture-coding sequence, and the image frame is a superposition rather than an individual coded image.

## Method

The core mechanism applies four binary, complementary coding patterns sequentially on an LCoS display during one 22 ms exposure; the resulting image frame is the sum of four coded-aperture images, while the events triggered by each pattern transition encode the parallax between consecutive coded images. The algorithm is an end-to-end trainable pipeline (AcqNet + RecNet) based on deep optics: AcqNet simulates acquisition with a differentiable event simulator (quantized log-intensity differences with Gaussian noise on the contrast threshold τ, which is randomly sampled per batch for hardware robustness), and RecNet is a plain 2-D CNN that fuses the image frame and three event stacks (stacked along the channel dimension) to output 64 views. A key theoretical result establishes quasi-equivalence to baseline coded-aperture imaging: under ideal conditions, the N individual coded images can be analytically recovered from the summed frame and event stacks, giving the method a principled advantage over joint aperture-exposure coding. Training uses MSE reconstruction loss plus an optional event-count penalty to keep throughput within the camera's limits, and the learned coding patterns are transferred to the physical LCoS display for real-world capture.

## Results

On the BasicLFSR test dataset (23 light fields, 5 groups), the proposed method with the flexible-τ model (test τ=0.15) achieves 33.79 PSNR / 0.9149 SSIM averaged over all groups, completing the measurement in a single ~22 ms exposure. The best fixed-τ model (τ=0.075) reaches 34.45 PSNR / 0.9219 SSIM, approaching the upper-bound reference of coded-aperture imaging with N=4 (35.39 / 0.9346) which requires four sequential exposures. The method outperforms all single-exposure baselines: Full-4D (33.22 / 0.9076), JAEC (31.97 / 0.8862), and lens-array (26.22 / 0.7218).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method (single exposure, ~22 ms) is compared against four categories of baselines. Against the multi-exposure coded-aperture reference CA(N=4) (35.39/0.9346), the proposed method's best configuration (fixed-τ-low) achieves 34.45/0.9219, a gap of ~0.9 dB PSNR, while the flexible-τ model (33.79/0.9149) is comparable to CA(N=2) (34.09/0.9210) — all in a single exposure versus 2–4 sequential ones. Against single-exposure competitors, the proposed method clearly wins: it outperforms Full-4D (33.22/0.9076) by ~0.6 dB PSNR, JAEC with RecNet (31.97/0.8862) by ~1.8 dB, JAEC with Mizuno et al.'s original implementation (30.70/0.8689) by ~3.1 dB, and lens-array imaging (26.22/0.7218) by ~7.6 dB PSNR. The flexible-τ model maintains competitive quality across a wide range of test-time contrast thresholds (τ ∈ [0.075, 0.275]), consistently outperforming all single-exposure baselines, which is critical for compatibility with real event cameras where τ cannot be explicitly controlled.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of coded-aperture light-field imaging (Inagaki et al. 2018, Sakai et al. 2020, Liang et al. 2008) and event-camera sensing (Brändli et al. 2014, Gallego et al. 2022), but is the first to combine the two for light-field acquisition. It departs from joint aperture-exposure coding (Mizuno et al. 2022, Tateishi et al. 2021), which also multiplexes coding patterns within a single exposure but requires complex pixel-level hardware control, by instead using an off-the-shelf event camera to capture the temporal differences between successive aperture patterns. The deep-optics framework (Inagaki et al. 2018, Mizuno et al. 2022) provides the end-to-end training paradigm, extended here with a differentiable event simulator and hardware-driven binary/complementary constraints on the LCoS display.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The quasi-equivalence theorem (Eqs. 6–8) provides a clean theoretical bridge between the proposed single-exposure method and the multi-image coded-aperture baseline, clarifying exactly what information the event stacks recover. Practically, the 22 ms single-exposure measurement with a working prototype is a meaningful step toward real-time light-field capture, though the current hardware is limited to monochrome 346×260 resolution. Key open questions include extending to RGB and higher spatial resolution, handling moving scenes (light-field video), and whether light fields can be reconstructed from continuous event streams alone without the image frame. The ~1 dB PSNR gap to the CA(N=4) upper bound suggests the noisy, quantized nature of real events still costs information that a cleaner multi-exposure acquisition would not lose.
