## Problem

High-quality text-to-video diffusion models require large-scale, well-filtered high-quality video datasets that are inaccessible to academia due to copyright and curation costs. Models trained on the most available academic dataset, WebVid-10M (~320p), inherit its poor picture quality and cannot match commercial systems. This paper asks whether a high-quality video model can be trained using only low-quality videos for motion and high-quality images for appearance, without any high-quality video data.

## Input / Output

The model takes a free-form text prompt as input and generates a short video clip at 512×320 resolution in the latent space of a Stable Diffusion 2.1 autoencoder, with an FPS condition controlling temporal dynamics. During training, the first stage consumes WebVid-10M video clips (336×596) sampled at varying frame rates alongside LAION-COCO images for joint training, while the second stage consumes 512×512 Midjourney-synthesized images from JourneyDB. The output is a sequence of latent frames decoded to RGB video, evaluated on the EvalCrafter benchmark (~512 prompts).

## Method

The core insight is that fully training both spatial and temporal modules of a 3D U-Net (initialized from SD 2.1) creates a stronger spatial-temporal coupling than partial training (temporal-only), making the model far more tolerant to subsequent spatial parameter perturbation without motion degradation. The proposed two-stage pipeline first fully trains the video model on low-quality WebVid-10M videos to learn motion, then directly fine-tunes only the spatial modules (not via LoRA) on high-quality Midjourney images from JourneyDB to shift the appearance distribution. Direct fine-tuning of spatial modules alone is identified as optimal: it maximizes picture quality and aesthetic scores while preserving motion stability, whereas fine-tuning temporal modules or using LoRA introduces flicker or insufficient quality gains. Using synthesized images with complex concepts (JDB) rather than real web images (LAION Aesthetics V2) further improves concept composition ability by transferring the compositional knowledge of T2I models like Midjourney into the video generator.

## Results

On the EvalCrafter benchmark, VideoCrafter2 achieves a visual quality score of 63.28, comparable to Pika Labs (63.52) and VideoCrafter1 (61.64), despite being trained exclusively on low-quality WebVid-10M videos and high-quality Midjourney images (JDB). Text-video alignment reaches 64.67, ranking second only to AnimateDiff (74.79), while motion quality (53.95) and temporal consistency (62.02) surpass all open-source baselines (Show-1, AnimateDiff, VideoCrafter1) but remain below commercial models Gen-2 (62.53 motion, 69.71 temporal) and Pika Labs (57.74 motion, 69.35 temporal). In human preference studies, the method is preferred over Show-1 on visual quality (82%) and over AnimateDiff on visual quality (69%) and motion quality (64%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the two commercial models (Gen-2 and Pika Labs), VideoCrafter2 trails on motion quality (53.95 vs. 62.53 and 57.74) and temporal consistency (62.02 vs. 69.71 and 69.35), but matches or exceeds them on visual quality (63.28 vs. 67.35 and 63.52) and text-video alignment (64.67 vs. 52.30 and 54.11). Against VideoCrafter1, the method wins on visual quality (63.28 vs. 61.64) and temporal consistency (62.02 vs. 60.36) but loses on text-video alignment (64.67 vs. 66.76) and motion quality (53.95 vs. 56.06). Against Show-1, VideoCrafter2 wins on all four metrics, with the largest gap in visual quality (63.28 vs. 52.19). Against AnimateDiff, the method wins on visual quality (63.28 vs. 58.89), motion quality (53.95 vs. 51.38), and temporal consistency (62.02 vs. 56.61), but loses substantially on text-video alignment (64.67 vs. 74.79). In human preference, the method is preferred over Show-1 on visual quality (82%) and over AnimateDiff on visual quality (69%) and motion (64%), but is less preferred than Gen-2 on visual quality (34%) and motion (46%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the VideoCrafter1 architecture and the VDM/ModelScopeT2V factorized 3D-UNet paradigm for latent-space video diffusion, but its core contribution is an empirical analysis of how full versus partial training affects spatial-temporal coupling in SD-based video models. It explicitly departs from AnimateDiff's strategy of bolting pretrained temporal modules onto arbitrary LORA SD models, arguing that the weaker coupling in partially trained models explains AnimateDiff's fragility. The proposed pipeline—full training on low-quality video followed by direct spatial-module finetuning on synthetic high-quality images—repurposes the successful T2I ecosystem (Midjourney/SDXL) as a quality source without requiring high-quality video data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a practical, reproducible recipe for academia and smaller labs to close the visual-quality gap with commercial T2V models that rely on proprietary high-quality video corpora. The spatial-temporal coupling finding provides a useful design principle: full training before any appearance-level finetuning preserves motion robustness. However, motion quality still lags behind models trained on large high-quality video datasets, and the analysis is conducted on a single architecture at 512×320 resolution, leaving open whether the coupling behavior generalizes to larger models, higher resolutions, or alternative temporal module designs (e.g., DiT-based). The reliance on synthetic images (JDB) also raises questions about long-term diversity and potential mode collapse in the generated video distribution.
