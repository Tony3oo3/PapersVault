## Problem

Existing scene graph generation and visual relationship detection methods rely on a limited set of predefined relation categories and simple relationship models, struggling to capture the full diversity of visual interactivities (appearance, situation, position, interaction, and relation) that occur dynamically in video. This restriction hinders holistic comprehension of complex subject interplay across temporal sequences, as prior datasets and benchmarks predominantly model only pairwise relations within single connected components.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240032/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a video clip of T frames (H×W×3) and produces, for every pair of detected subjects, a set of interactivity triplets spanning five predicate types (appearance, situation, position, interaction, relation) with associated temporal ranges. Subjects are detected via a DETR + ResNet-50 backbone yielding bounding boxes and masks; edges are pruned using scatter_min/scatter_max aggregation and top-k (k=12) nearest-neighbor selection via cosine similarity. Final predictions are taken from higher hierarchy levels where confidence ≥ 0.9, rather than per-frame outputs, to reduce noise and improve recall.

## Method

HIG represents a video as a sequence of per-frame graphs at the base level, then progressively combines graphs from the previous level into fewer, more comprehensive graphs at each subsequent hierarchy level, culminating in a single graph cell covering the entire video. At every level, a single unified layer (not a stack of layers) performs message passing where each node's feature is updated by summing level-specific weighted transformations of its neighbors' features from the prior level. Training uses a sequential unfreezing strategy—activating the base level first and progressively unfreezing higher levels—with weight sharing across levels acting as regularization, and Focal Loss applied at each hierarchy level and aggregated into a total loss. The key design insight is that higher-level graph cells cover larger temporal spans, so predictions made there inherently integrate multi-frame context, reducing per-frame noise and yielding substantially higher recall than frame-level baselines.

## Results

On the ASPIRe video dataset, HIG achieves R@100 of 42.33 (Position), 41.65 (Interaction), and 41.47 (Relation), substantially outperforming the best prior method GPSNet (12.89 across all three attributes at R@100). On the PSG image dataset, HIG reaches R@100 of 26.3, surpassing VCTree (22.5) by 3.8 points, though it trails VCTree slightly at R@20 (19.4 vs. 20.6). The hierarchical design yields the largest gains at higher K values (R@50, R@100), where temporal aggregation across levels accumulates interactivity evidence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HIG dominates all four SOTA baselines (IMP, MOTIFS, VCTree, GPSNet) on the ASPIRe video dataset across all three double-actor attributes. The most striking gap is at R@100, where HIG reaches 42.33 for Position versus GPSNet's 12.89 (a ~30-point margin), and 41.65 for Interaction versus IMP's 12.79. At R@20 the margins are smaller (e.g., 13.02 vs. 12.89 for Position), but HIG's hierarchical temporal aggregation causes the advantage to grow sharply at R@50 and R@100. On the PSG image dataset (Table 7), HIG achieves R@100 of 26.3, beating VCTree (22.5) by 3.8 points and GPSNet (20.1) by 6.2 points, though it trails VCTree by 1.2 points at R@20 (19.4 vs. 20.6), suggesting the temporal hierarchy contributes less when only a single frame is available. Against the four re-implemented PVSG baselines (Vanilla, Handcrafted, Convolution, Transformer), HIG leads by 2.67% at R@20 on single-actor Appearance and by 1.34% on double-actor Interaction, confirming the benefit of the hierarchical graph over flat architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the video scene graph generation line (PVSG, VidOR, Action Genome, VidSTG) by broadening the predicate space from a single relation type to five interactivity categories (Appearance, Situation, Position, Interaction, Relation) and by replacing flat per-frame graphs with a hierarchical structure that progressively aggregates temporal context across levels. The message-passing and focal-loss training follow standard GNN-for-SGG practice (IMP, MOTIFS, VCTree, GPSNet), and the DETR+ResNet-50 backbone is borrowed directly from the object-detection literature. The 'interlacement' framing—treating interactivities that persist or evolve across frames as first-class graph edges—is the paper's main conceptual departure from prior SGG work, which typically models each frame independently.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ASPIRe dataset (1.5K videos, 833 object categories, 4.5K interactivity types) fills a gap left by prior video SGG benchmarks that cover only 1–2 predicate types, and could serve as a useful multi-attribute evaluation resource. However, the hierarchical aggregation mechanism is a relatively straightforward extension of standard GNN message passing, and the very low mR values (often < 1.0) alongside the modest R@20 gains suggest the model still struggles with fine-grained interactivity discrimination. Open questions include whether the hierarchical depth scales to longer videos without the knowledge-decay issue the authors acknowledge, and whether the five-type decomposition generalizes beyond the curated seven-source collection.
