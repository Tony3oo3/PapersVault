## Problem

Existing domain adaptation methods for Lidar 3D object detection treat detectors as black boxes, relying on target-domain data without understanding how underlying architectural choices and source-domain training strategies affect generalization. No prior work has systematically isolated and compared the impact of specific design decisions—architecture, voxel encoding, data augmentations, and anchor strategies—on robustness across the three main domain gap types (sensor resolution, weather, and geographical location).
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241498/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The detectors consume single-frame Lidar point clouds from sensors with varying vertical FOV and beam counts (64-line KITTI/Waymo/Kirkland, 32-line NuScenes, and downsampled 16-line KITTI), and produce 3D bounding boxes with class labels for car, pedestrian, and cyclist. Evaluation uses 3D Average Precision with 40-point recall, with KITTI metrics for most benchmarks and Waymo metrics for Waymo-to-Kirkland. The Kirkland dataset lacks the cyclist class, and the downsampled KITTI variants (K32, K16) are created by vertically subsampling the 64-line scans by factors of 2 and 4 respectively.

## Method

The paper conducts a large-scale empirical study rather than proposing a new detector. It first establishes a taxonomy of nine state-of-the-art Lidar 3D detectors (PointRCNN, PointPillars, SECOND, VoxelRCNN, VOTR-VoxelRCNN, CenterPoint, PV-RCNN variants, VOTR-TSD) decomposed into four architectural components (3D feature encoder, BEV backbone, dense head, RCNN head), enabling controlled ablations. It then evaluates all models on six domain adaptation benchmarks spanning three gap types, followed by targeted experiments: (1) test-time anchor size tuning via a greedy per-dimension search on the target validation set, (2) varying voxel height (0.0625 m, 0.1 m, 0.125 m) to study empty-voxel effects under sparser sensors, and (3) introducing two new augmentations—line downsampling (vertically subsampling Lidar beams with probability-weighted factors) and mixed GT-sampling (injecting ground-truth objects from a dataset with different V-FOV)—alongside standard GT-sampling and shape augmentation. Key findings include that transformer backbones with local point features outperform 3D CNNs, that test-time anchor resizing yields large gains without retraining, that point-sparsity augmentations are the most effective for high-to-low resolution gaps, and that training on clean weather data generalizes better to bad weather than training on bad weather data itself.

## Results

Across six domain adaptation benchmarks (K64→K32, K64→K16, W→K, W→N, W→Kr, N→K), VOTR-TSD (transformer backbone with point-voxel features) achieves the best mean 3D AP on the hardest location gap (N→K: 19.2 mAP) and remains competitive across all others. Anchor size tuning at test time yields the single largest improvement: SECOND on W→K jumps from 16.4 to 66.8 3D AP on the car class without any retraining. On the weather gap (W→Kr), anchorless detectors (CenterPoint, PVRCNN-Centerhead) lead with ~40 mAP, and surprisingly, models trained only on clean Waymo data outperform those trained on the rainy Kirkland target data, especially on pedestrians (22.2 vs. lower oracle). Point sparsity augmentations (line downsampling, shape augmentation) boost PVRCNN on K64→K16 from 54.1 to 71.6 car AP, approaching the K16 oracle of 72.7.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VOTR-TSD (transformer + point-voxel features) is the most consistently robust architecture, achieving the best mAP on N→K (19.2) and W→N (10.6), and remaining within 2–4 mAP of the best on all other benchmarks. VoxelRCNN (3D CNN, voxel-only) leads on the W→K location gap (36.7 mAP) but is outperformed by VOTR-TSD on N→K (9.5 vs. 19.2), confirming that transformers with local point features generalize better across locations. Anchorless detectors (CenterPoint, PVRCNN-Centerhead) dominate the weather gap (W→Kr: 40.5 and 40.9 mAP vs. 30–36 for anchor-based models), with a 4–5 AP advantage on the pedestrian class. PointRCNN, the only pure point-based model, is the weakest overall, scoring below 12 mAP on W→K and W→N. PVRCNN with line downsampling augmentation nearly closes the K64→K16 resolution gap (71.6 car AP vs. 72.7 oracle), outperforming all other models on that benchmark. SPG (a dedicated weather DA method) is outperformed on the vehicle class by a simple PVRCNN with smaller voxel size (55.8 vs. 53.5 AP on W→Kr), showing that architectural choices can rival purpose-built adaptation methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of two prior research threads: (1) robustness benchmarking for 3D object detection (Dong et al., Yu et al., Zhu et al.), which focused on environmental corruptions and sensor-representation comparisons, and (2) domain adaptation methods for Lidar 3D-OD (ST3D, SPG, Lidar Distillation, SN), which treat detectors as black boxes and rely on target-domain data. The key departure is a 'devil-in-the-details' empirical study—borrowing the investigative spirit of Chatfield et al. for image CNNs—that systematically isolates architectural design choices (backbone type, voxel encoding, augmentations, anchor strategy) and their interplay on generalization, rather than proposing a new adaptation algorithm. It extends prior robustness work by including non-corruption domain gaps (geographical location, sensor beam count) and by studying design choices that precede any adaptation step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides actionable, pre-adaptation guidance for practitioners deploying Lidar 3D detectors across locations, sensors, or weather conditions, showing that simple choices (larger voxel height, test-time anchor tuning, point-sparsity augmentations) can close a substantial fraction of the domain gap without retraining. The finding that clean-weather training outperforms bad-weather training reframes the weather problem as one of discriminability rather than transferability, pointing toward better model architectures or multi-modal fusion rather than more target-domain data. Limitations include the use of only 20% of Waymo training data, restriction to Lidar-only models, and the fact that test-time anchor tuning requires a small labeled target set, which may not always be available. A universally adaptive model that handles all three domain gaps simultaneously remains an open challenge.
