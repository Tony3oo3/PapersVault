## Problem

Existing 3D scene reconstruction methods handle only static scenes, while dynamic scene novel view synthesis assumes continuous observation at all time steps. This paper introduces the novel task of point-level 3D scene interpolation: given only two observed states (start and end) from multiple views, synthesize smooth, plausible intermediate geometries and appearances without any supervision in between, even when the scene undergoes large non-rigid changes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241792/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes multi-view RGB images of a scene at two distinct states (start at t=0 and end at t=T) along with their camera poses, and produces per-point trajectories through space-time plus rendered novel views at any intermediate state. Notably, PAPR in Motion requires only 30,000 randomly initialized points for pre-training, compared to 100,000–500,000 COLMAP-derived points needed by Dynamic Gaussian, reflecting PAPR's ability to learn parsimonious representations from scratch. The output geometry is a point cloud at each intermediate timestep, and appearance is rendered via the attention-based PAPR pipeline with interpolated feature vectors and attention weights.

## Method

PAPR in Motion first trains a Proximity Attention Point Rendering model on the start state, then finetunes only the point coordinates (freezing feature vectors and attention) toward the end state, so that intermediate optimization iterations naturally form Lagrangian interpolation trajectories. Two regularization mechanisms ensure physical plausibility: a local distance preservation loss (L_rigid) that penalizes changes in pairwise distances among k-nearest neighbours to enforce local rigidity, and a local displacement averaging step (LDAS) applied every m iterations that replaces each point's displacement with the mean displacement of its neighbours to promote cohesive part motion. After geometry converges, the method finetunes appearance parameters (view-independent feature vectors and ray-dependent attention weights) on the end state, then interpolates both geometry and appearance for intermediate states using a progress weight α_t computed from total point displacement. PAPR is chosen over splat-based renderers because its attention mechanism normalizes point contributions, preventing vanishing gradients under large geometry changes and preserving shape contiguity under non-rigid deformations.

## Results

PAPR in Motion outperforms Dynamic Gaussian across all three scene interpolation metrics (SI-FID, SI-CD, SI-EMD) on both synthetic and real-world scenes. On synthetic scenes, the method achieves an average SI-FID of 120.30 vs. 194.64, SI-CD of 3.94×10⁻³ vs. 9.68×10⁻³, and SI-EMD of 12.62×10⁻³ vs. 31.49×10⁻³. The gains are most pronounced on real-world scenes, where SI-CD drops from 37.13×10⁻³ to 9.05×10⁻³ and SI-EMD from 95.31×10⁻³ to 53.02×10⁻³, reflecting the method's superior handling of large non-rigid deformations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is Dynamic Gaussian [24], the leading point-based renderer for dynamic scenes. PAPR in Motion wins on every metric and every scene. The most striking gap is in geometry quality on real-world scenes: SI-CD improves from 37.13×10⁻³ to 9.05×10⁻³ (≈4× reduction) and SI-EMD from 95.31×10⁻³ to 53.02×10⁻³ (≈45% reduction), indicating that Dynamic Gaussian fails to move a large fraction of points to their correct end-state positions when scene changes are substantial. On synthetic scenes, the geometry advantage is even larger in relative terms (e.g., SI-EMD on Dolphin drops from 22.29 to 2.67, an 8× improvement). Appearance quality (SI-FID) also improves consistently, with the largest gain on the Butterfly scene (352.43 → 99.06). The paper notes that Dynamic Gaussian requires 100k–500k COLMAP-initialized points per scene, whereas PAPR in Motion uses only 30k randomly initialized points, yet still achieves superior interpolation quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on PAPR (Proximity Attention Point Rendering) as its rendering backbone, repurposing its attention-based point interpolation mechanism to handle large non-rigid deformations between scene states. It departs from the dynamic scene novel view synthesis line (NeRF-based methods, Dynamic Gaussian) by eliminating the assumption of continuous temporal observation, and from 3D object motion analysis/synthesis by avoiding 3D supervision and articulation annotations. The key conceptual move is treating the finetuning trajectory of a point-based renderer as a natural Lagrangian interpolation, augmented with two novel regularizers (local distance preservation loss and local displacement averaging) to enforce physical plausibility of point trajectories.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By formalizing point-level 3D scene interpolation as a distinct task and providing the first baseline method and evaluation metric (Scene Interpolation Metric), this work opens a new research direction at the intersection of 3D reconstruction, motion synthesis, and novel view rendering. The practical implication for animation and filmmaking—generating plausible intermediate states from just two observed snapshots—is compelling. However, significant limitations remain: the method cannot handle new objects entering the scene, lacks semantic understanding of motion, and the evaluation is limited to a single baseline (Dynamic Gaussian) and only eight scenes, leaving open questions about scalability, robustness to appearance changes, and comparison against other point-based or NeRF-based approaches adapted to this setting.
