## Problem

Existing ensemble-based adversarial attack methods (SVRE, AdaEA) explicitly reduce the diversity of perturbations generated by different surrogate models to achieve a unified optimization direction. While this works for homogeneous CNN-to-CNN attacks, it causes a sharp drop in attack success rate under heterogeneous conditions (CNN surrogates attacking ViT victims) and mixed ensembles, because constraining gradient diversity suppresses the individual strengths of each architecture. The paper argues that diversity between surrogates is a resource to be exploited rather than a variance to be eliminated.

## Input / Output

The method takes a benign RGB image x and its ground-truth label y, along with a set of M pre-trained surrogate models (CNNs, ViTs, or a mix) and their corresponding ensemble weights. It produces a single adversarial example x̂ = x + δ constrained to L∞ norm with ε = 16/255, generated over T = 10 outer iterations each containing K = 4M internal loops. The method is plug-and-play: it wraps around any base gradient attack (I-FGSM, MI-FGSM, TIM, TI-DIM) and only modifies how the ensemble gradient is computed and how surrogate weights are assigned.

## Method

SMER replaces the batch-like ensemble gradient fusion with a stochastic mini-batch scheme: in each inner loop, a single surrogate f_{ξ_k} is randomly selected and the perturbation is updated solely from that model's gradient (x̃_{k+1} = x̃_k + β·sign(∇_x J(f_{ξ_k}(x̃_k), y))), preserving each model's individual perturbation direction. To further differentiate surrogate contributions, ensemble reweighing treats the weight vector {ω_1,...,ω_M} as reinforcement-learning agents: the reward R = J(Σω_i f_i(x̃_k), y) is transformed into a minimized surrogate loss J_R = −ln(R), and an SGD optimizer (lr = 0.02) updates the weights at every inner step to maximize total attack loss. The combined objective is x̂ = argmax_x J(ω_ξ f_ξ(x), y) − J_R(Σω_i f_i(x), y), which simultaneously maximizes the current surrogate's attack loss while minimizing the ensemble-level reward loss. This design avoids the convergence bottleneck of forcing all surrogates toward a shared gradient direction, and the random selection pattern (vs. sequential) yields an additional ~5% ASR gain in ablation.

## Results

On ImageNet-compatible datasets, SMER integrated with TI-DIM achieves 96.4% average ASR against adversarial-trained models, 97.7% against transformation-based defenses, and 83.3% against robust ViTs, consistently outperforming all baselines. With MI-FGSM, SMER reaches 62.1% (adversarial training), 81.6% (transformation-based), and 64.6% (robust ViT) average ASR. The largest gains appear in heterogeneous settings (CNN surrogates → ViT victims), where SMER with TI-DIM surpasses SVRE by 11.7% and AdaEA by 21.3% on robust ViT ASR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SMER consistently outperforms all three baselines (Ens, SVRE, AdaEA) across every base attack and every evaluation setting. Against the strongest baseline SVRE, SMER gains +3.4% (TI-DIM, adversarial training), +7.5% (TI-DIM, transformation-based), and +11.7% (TI-DIM, robust ViT) in average ASR. Against AdaEA, the margins are even larger: +14.2%, +11.3%, and +21.3% respectively under TI-DIM. Against the simplest baseline Ens, SMER leads by up to +13.3% (adversarial training), +14.7% (transformation-based), and +25.4% (robust ViT) with TI-DIM. The advantage is most pronounced in heterogeneous attack scenarios (CNN surrogates attacking ViT victims), where diversity-preserving strategies like SMER clearly outperform diversity-reducing methods like SVRE and AdaEA. On targeted attacks (Table 3), SMER with TI-DIM achieves 27.3% average ASR versus 5.1% (SVRE), 2.6% (AdaEA), and 2.3% (Ens), a gap of over 22 percentage points.

## Relation to Prior Work

SMER builds directly on the ensemble-based black-box attack lineage initiated by MI-FGSM (ensemble-on-logits with momentum) and extended by TIM/TI-DIM (translation-invariant kernels), using these as plug-in base attacks. Its key conceptual departure is from SVRE and AdaEA, which treat ensemble optimization as a batch operation and explicitly reduce gradient variance or output discrepancy across surrogates. SMER inverts this philosophy: rather than constraining perturbations to converge toward an ensemble average, it treats each surrogate independently via stochastic mini-batch perturbation and introduces RL-based ensemble reweighing to let models customize their own contributions, arguing that unconstrained diversity is the source of transferability rather than a nuisance to be suppressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a prevailing assumption in ensemble-based adversarial attacks—that reducing inter-model gradient diversity improves transferability—and provides consistent evidence (across four base attacks, three defense families, and three ensemble types) that preserving and exploiting diversity yields substantially higher ASR, particularly in heterogeneous CNN-to-ViT settings where prior methods degrade sharply. The plug-and-play design makes SMER immediately adoptable alongside existing gradient-based attacks. Open questions include whether the simple SGD-on-log-reward reweighing scales to very large ensembles, whether the stochastic mini-batch schedule (random vs. sequential) matters beyond the modest ~5% gap observed, and whether the diversity-preserving principle extends to other transferable-attack domains such as object detection or segmentation.
