## Problem

Neural Radiance Fields trained on sparse inputs (e.g., 3–9 views) produce degenerate scene representations with significant 3D inconsistencies, because insufficient view coverage leaves large regions of the scene unsupervised. Existing regularization methods (sparsity, depth supervision, 2D color/semantic consistency) operate at the ray or image-patch level and only indirectly constrain 3D spatial coherence, failing to explicitly enforce that physically neighboring 3D points share similar radiance properties.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242496/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a small set of posed RGB images (3, 6, or 9 views on DTU; 3 or 8 on the Synthetic dataset) and produces a per-scene MLP radiance field that renders novel views via volume rendering. A distinguishing detail is that CVT is a plug-in module: it is applied only to the fine-level MLP and is compatible with multiple baselines (NeRF, BARF, SPARF, FreeNeRF), adding roughly 2 GB of peak memory and ~1.2× training time without altering the base architecture's input or output interface.

## Method

CVT-xRF introduces a Contrastive In-Voxel Transformer with three coupled components. First, a voxel-based ray sampling strategy partitions the scene into uniform voxels and selects rays by which voxel they intersect, so that all sampled 3D points within a voxel are expected to share similar radiance. Second, a local implicit constraint uses a lightweight encoder–decoder Transformer: the encoder self-attends over sphere-sampled surrounding points in the voxel to produce a pooled region feature, and the decoder cross-attends between that feature and line-sampled ray points to predict their colors and densities, which are then inserted into the volume-rendering sum so that rendering gradients flow back through the Transformer to the MLP. Third, a global explicit constraint applies a voxel-level contrastive loss (NT-Xent style) on the encoder's pooled features, pulling features from the same voxel together and pushing features from different voxels apart. The total loss is the standard photometric MSE plus a weighted contrastive term, and the entire CVT module is applied only at the fine-level MLP during per-scene optimization.

## Results

On the DTU dataset (3-view), CVT-xRF (w/ BARF) achieves 18.06 PSNR / 0.762 SSIM (full-image), outperforming FreeNeRF (18.02 / 0.680) and SPARF (18.30 / 0.780). On the 9-view setting, CVT-xRF (w/ SPARF) reaches 27.04 PSNR / 0.919 SSIM, the best among all compared methods. On the Synthetic dataset (3-view), CVT-xRF (w/ BARF) attains 21.58 PSNR / 0.851 SSIM, surpassing FreeNeRF (20.75 / 0.842) and ConsistentNeRF (19.63 / 0.830); for 8-view, CVT-xRF (w/ FreeNeRF) achieves 24.56 PSNR / 0.883 SSIM, edging out FreeNeRF (24.26 / 0.883). The method also brings 7.45, 0.95, and 1.20 PSNR improvements over NeRF, BARF, and SPARF respectively on DTU 3-view.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CVT-xRF (w/ BARF) outperforms FreeNeRF by 0.04 PSNR on DTU 3-view (18.06 vs 18.02) but with a substantially higher SSIM (0.762 vs 0.680), and by 1.01 PSNR on 9-view (26.56 vs 24.20). Against SPARF, CVT-xRF (w/ SPARF) gains 0.68 PSNR on 3-view (18.98 vs 18.30) and 1.29 PSNR on 9-view (27.04 vs 25.75), while requiring less GPU memory (22G vs 20G peak) and training time (13h vs 11h). On the Synthetic 3-view benchmark, CVT-xRF (w/ BARF) leads all methods including pre-trained ones (21.58 PSNR vs FreeNeRF 20.75, ConsistentNeRF 19.63, ENeRF 18.14). For Synthetic 8-view, CVT-xRF (w/ FreeNeRF) achieves 24.56 PSNR, beating FreeNeRF (24.26) and DietNeRF_200k (23.59). The method clearly dominates weaker baselines: RegNeRF (15.33), mip-NeRF (7.64), and DietNeRF (10.01) on DTU 3-view are all surpassed by 2.7–11.4 PSNR. The one notable exception is PixelNeRF (18.74 PSNR, pre-trained on DTU), which slightly edges out CVT-xRF (w/ BARF) (18.06) on DTU 3-view full-image PSNR, though CVT-xRF wins on SSIM (0.762 vs 0.618).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the NeRF radiance field representation (Mildenhall et al., ECCV 2020) and the Transformer architecture (Vaswani et al., NeurIPS 2017) to propose a 3D spatial field consistency regularization for sparse-view novel view synthesis. It departs from prior sparse-view NeRF regularizers—RegNeRF (2D patch consistency), DietNeRF (CLIP semantic alignment), SPARF (matching network), and FreeNeRF (frequency regularization)—which operate at the 2D image-plane level or rely on off-the-shelf pre-trained models, by explicitly modeling 3D neighborhood consistency through a voxel-based sampling strategy and a contrastive in-voxel Transformer. The key conceptual shift is treating radiance field consistency as a 3D spatial property rather than an indirect consequence of 2D rendering supervision.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The modular CVT structure can be plugged into any NeRF-based baseline without architectural changes, making it a broadly applicable regularization for the sparse-view setting. However, evaluation is limited to the relatively small and controlled DTU and Synthetic datasets, leaving open whether the voxel-based consistency assumption and the contrastive loss scale to larger, more complex scenes (e.g., unbounded outdoor environments or dynamic scenes). The added training overhead (~40% more memory and time) and the sensitivity to voxel size remain practical considerations. An open question is whether the 3D consistency signal can be further strengthened by incorporating geometric or semantic priors beyond the current purely radiance-based formulation.
