## Problem

Customizing robotic behaviors to diverse human preferences in complex embodied AI tasks remains challenging: hand-crafted reward functions are impractical for non-experts, and existing preference-learning methods require retraining the agent for each new preference. This paper addresses the gap by asking whether a single trained policy can be efficiently adapted to arbitrary human preferences at inference time without any policy fine-tuning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241742/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy consumes 3×224×224 RGB images and a K-dimensional reward weight vector on the simplex (K=5 for ObjectNav, K=3 for FleeNav), producing discrete navigation actions from {MoveAhead, RotateRight, RotateLeft, Done, LookUp, LookDown}. The reward weight vector acts as a 'prompt' that modulates behavior at inference. For weight prediction, the system additionally accepts one of three user signals: a single human demonstration trajectory, N binary preference labels on trajectory pairs or groups, or a natural-language instruction processed by ChatGPT.

## Method

The core contribution is a single-policy multi-objective RL framework where the policy is conditioned on a reward weight vector w randomly sampled from a K-dim simplex during training, making it adaptable to any linear combination of objectives at test time without fine-tuning. The architecture uses a frozen CLIP ResNet-50 encoder for visual features, a codebook module (FFN expansion to 30-dim followed by 30 learnable K×12-dim latent codes) to encode the reward weights, and a multi-objective DD-PPO recurrent policy. Three distinct weight prediction methods infer the user's preference vector: (1) maximum-likelihood fitting to human demonstrations, (2) Bradley-Terry optimization over trajectory comparisons—introducing a novel group comparison that is 17.8% more effective than pairwise—and (3) LLM-based translation of language instructions using in-context learning and chain-of-thought reasoning. The key insight is that training on diverse random weight vectors during RL makes the policy 'promptable,' reducing personalization to inferring a low-dimensional weight vector rather than retraining.

## Results

In ProcTHOR ObjectNav, Promptable Behaviors achieves the highest success rate (0.680) when house exploration is prioritized, 11.3% above EmbCLIP (0.611), and the best SPL (0.543) and path efficiency reward (0.907) when path efficiency is prioritized, outperforming EmbCLIP by 19.3% and 56.1% respectively. In ProcTHOR FleeNav, the method maintains success rates above 0.70 across all objective prioritizations. For reward weight prediction, group trajectory comparison (M=2, N=25) achieves 93.5% cosine similarity with expert-designed weights, and in human evaluation it yields a 0.650 win rate, 17.8% higher than pairwise comparison.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: EmbCLIP (single-objective RL) and Prioritized EmbCLIP (multi-policy MORL training K separate policies). Promptable Behaviors consistently outperforms EmbCLIP on the prioritized objective's sub-reward while maintaining competitive success rates: e.g., 0.907 path efficiency reward vs. 0.581 for EmbCLIP (56.1% gain) and 0.995 house exploration vs. 0.703 (41.5% gain). Prioritized EmbCLIP suffers severe degradation when house exploration is prioritized (success drops to 0.200, episode length balloons to 351 steps), whereas Promptable Behaviors maintains 0.680 success under the same setting. In FleeNav, Promptable Behaviors achieves success rates of 0.711–0.737 across all prioritizations, while Prioritized EmbCLIP drops to 0.691 when time efficiency is prioritized. The single-policy approach also requires substantially less training compute than Prioritized EmbCLIP, which must train K separate policies. For reward weight prediction, group trajectory comparison (M=2) achieves 93.5% cosine similarity and a 0.650 human win rate, outperforming pairwise comparison (0.897 sim, 0.552 win rate) and human demonstrations (0.707 sim, 0.556 win rate).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends single-policy multi-objective reinforcement learning (Siddique et al., ICML 2020) and the reward-conditioned policy idea from Ask4Help (Singh et al., NeurIPS 2022) to complex embodied navigation, replacing the need to retrain per preference with inference-time adjustment of a low-dimensional reward weight vector. It builds on DD-PPO as the base algorithm and on Christiano et al.'s (NeurIPS 2017) preference-based learning framework, but departs from prior preference-based RL by decoupling policy training from preference inference: the policy is trained once on random weight vectors, and only the K-dimensional weight is optimized at test time. The introduction of group trajectory comparison as a more feedback-efficient alternative to pairwise comparison, and the use of LLMs (ChatGPT with in-context learning and chain-of-thought) to translate natural language into reward weights, are novel contributions not present in prior preference-learning or MORL work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical path to personalizing robot behaviors without the cost of retraining, which is significant for deployment scenarios where many users with different preferences interact with the same agent. However, the paper assumes static, linear preferences and evaluates only in simulated navigation tasks (ProcTHOR, RoboTHOR), leaving open whether the approach generalizes to manipulation, dynamic preferences, or real-world robots. The finding that a single ChatGPT language instruction achieves the second-best human-evaluation win rate (0.600) among all methods is a promising signal for LLM-mediated preference capture, though the evidence base (5 human evaluators, simulated environments) limits confidence. Future work on non-linear and time-varying preferences, as well as extension beyond navigation, would be needed to assess the framework's broader applicability.
