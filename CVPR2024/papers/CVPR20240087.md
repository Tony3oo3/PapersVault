## Problem

Existing adversarial defense methods assume a static, one-shot threat model, but in real deployment new attacks emerge sequentially. When a defense model adapts to a new attack via adversarial training, it catastrophically forgets robustness to previously defended attacks—a challenge this paper formalizes as 'continual adversarial defense' and validates empirically for the first time.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240087/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives standard images (MNIST 28×28 grayscale, CIFAR-10/100 32×32 RGB) perturbed by a known attack type (e.g., FGSM, PGD, or none), with the attack ID available at both training and test time. It outputs a single class label from a fixed output space shared across all attacks in the sequence, so the classifier head is never expanded. The model is trained sequentially over attack tasks (e.g., None → FGSM → PGD), where each task provides its own labeled adversarial training and test sets.

## Method

AIR is a memory-free self-distillation pseudo-replay framework that combines three components. (1) Isotropic Replay (IR) adds Gaussian perturbation plus random geometric augmentations to current adversarial samples, breaking specific adversarial patterns to create independent pseudo-replay data, then aligns the current model's output with the frozen previous model's output via KL divergence on this data. (2) Anisotropic Replay (AR) performs mixup-style interpolation between shuffled batch samples to generate mixed semantics that bridge the feature manifolds of previous and new attacks, with teacher-student alignment applied directly on the mixed samples. (3) An R-Drop-style regularizer aligns the outputs of new attack data and isotropic replay data through dual forward passes with random dropout, optimizing the plasticity-stability trade-off in a chain-like manner. The total loss is the sum of vanilla PGD adversarial training for the new attack, the two self-distillation losses, and the regularizer, all trained end-to-end without auxiliary networks or stored old data.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, AIR achieves the best robust accuracy among all continual learning baselines across all six attack sequences. For the 'None to FGSM' sequence, AIR reaches 76.73%/51.48% (Task 1/Task 2), outperforming Vanilla AT (70.60%/49.30%), EWC (72.66%/49.17%), and LFL (74.23%/50.17%). On the more challenging 'PGD to FGSM' sequence, AIR achieves 43.00%/52.26% versus Vanilla AT's 17.14%/60.24% and EWC's 24.44%/45.18%, demonstrating substantial mitigation of catastrophic forgetting. On MNIST, AIR approaches or matches the Joint Training upper bound (e.g., 99.37%/98.84% vs. 99.11%/98.52% for 'None to FGSM'), while on CIFAR-100 it consistently leads all baselines (e.g., 50.77%/24.32% for 'None to FGSM' vs. 42.27%/20.67% for Vanilla AT).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AIR outperforms all four baselines (Vanilla AT, EWC, Feature Extraction, and LFL) on the vast majority of attack sequences across all three datasets. Against Vanilla AT, the gains are most dramatic on 'hard-to-easy' sequences: on CIFAR-10 'PGD to FGSM,' AIR improves Task 1 accuracy from 17.14% to 43.00% (+25.9 pp), and on 'PGD to None' from 15.19% to 41.21% (+26.0 pp). Compared to EWC, AIR consistently leads by 3–10 pp on Task 1 (the previously learned attack) across CIFAR-10 sequences, while Feature Extraction and LFL suffer severe collapse on several sequences (e.g., Feat. Extraction drops to 12.72% on MNIST 'None to PGD' Task 1). Notably, AIR sometimes exceeds the Joint Training upper bound: on MNIST 'None to FGSM' it reaches 99.37%/98.84% vs. JT's 99.11%/98.52%, and on CIFAR-10 'FGSM to PGD' it achieves 53.39%/44.12% vs. JT's 49.81%/42.56%. However, AIR still trails Joint Training on CIFAR-100 'FGSM to PGD' (27.59%/23.19% vs. 35.27%/21.45%), indicating that the gap to the upper bound widens with task complexity.

## Relation to Prior Work

AIR sits at the intersection of adversarial training (Madry et al.) and pseudo-replay continual learning, repurposing mixup-style augmentation and R-Drop regularization within a self-distillation framework specifically designed for the adversarial setting. Unlike prior continual learning methods (EWC, LwF, LFL) that are applied off-the-shelf, AIR's isotropic and anisotropic replay modules are motivated by the structure of adversarial perturbations: isotropic augmentation breaks specific attack patterns while anisotropic mixup creates a compromise manifold between old and new attack distributions. The paper departs from one-shot adversarial defense by reframing it as a domain-incremental continual learning problem where the output space is fixed but input distributions shift with each new attack, and from test-time adaptation defenses (TTAD) by explicitly addressing forgetting of previous attacks rather than only adapting to the current one.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first formalization of continual adversarial defense as a distinct task, establishing a problem setting and baseline that the field can build upon. The memory-free pseudo-replay approach is practically important because it avoids storing old attack data (privacy) and avoids retraining on all past attacks (efficiency). However, the experimental scope is narrow—only FGSM and PGD with 2–3 attacks in sequence on three small datasets—leaving open whether the approach generalizes to diverse attack families (CW, AutoAttack, physical attacks), longer sequences, or larger-scale benchmarks. The conceptual observation that the accuracy-robustness trade-off and the plasticity-stability trade-off are structurally analogous (both resolved by aligning outputs across related inputs) is a useful unifying lens that may inform future work on both problems simultaneously.
