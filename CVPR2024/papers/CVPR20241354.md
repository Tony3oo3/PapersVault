## Problem

Rendering dynamic, long-duration radiance fields in real-time on mobile devices remains unsolved: existing 4D NeRF methods either rely on canonical spaces that break under large motion and topological changes, require storage too large for streaming, or have inference complexity incompatible with mobile GPUs. No prior approach simultaneously achieves compact representation, hardware-accelerated video decoding, and shader-based rendering for free-viewpoint dynamic scenes on phones and tablets.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241354/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes approximately 80 multiview RGB images per frame at 1920×1080 and 30 fps from a dynamic scene. It produces a uint8-quantized 2D feature video stream (first channel = density, remaining h channels = appearance features) compressed with H.264, a 512×512 3D-to-2D mapping table stored as an RGB image to conserve bitrate, and a tiny global MLP. At playback, the player decodes the video stream via hardware codec, reconstructs a 3D volume via compute shader, and renders novel views at up to 116 FPS on desktop or 23 FPS on iPhone 14 Pro.

## Method

VideoRF serializes the 4D feature volume into a 2D feature image stream where each image encodes one frame's densities and appearance features, with a 3D-to-2D mapping table enabling O(1) density/feature lookup per sample point. Rendering uses a deferred shading model: features are volumetrically accumulated along each ray, then a single tiny global MLP (shared across all frames) decodes the integrated feature vector plus view direction into pixel color, making the entire pipeline fragment-shader compatible. Training employs adaptive grouping (GOFs) that shares a mapping table across temporally coherent frames based on union occupancy grid capacity, 3D Morton sorting to preserve spatial continuity, and 2D Morton ordering within 8×8 blocks to align with video codec block compression. Spatial (total variance) and temporal (L1 inter-frame) consistency losses are applied directly on the 2D feature images to enforce the redundancy that off-the-shelf H.264 codecs exploit for high compression ratios.

## Results

On the ReRF dataset (Kpop scene, 200 frames), VideoRF achieves the best rendering quality across all metrics: PSNR 32.01, SSIM 0.976, and LPIPS 0.023, while maintaining a compact storage of 0.658 MB—comparable to ReRF (0.645 MB) and far smaller than MeRF (7.529 MB). On the HumanRF Actors-HQ dataset (Actor3, 250 frames), VideoRF achieves PSNR 28.46, SSIM 0.838, and LPIPS 0.278 at only 0.426 MB, slightly trailing HumanRF in quality (PSNR 28.98, LPIPS 0.151) but using 5.4× less storage. In runtime, VideoRF renders at 116 FPS on a desktop (RTX 3090) versus 4 FPS for ReRF, and achieves 23–40 FPS on mobile devices (iPhone 14 Pro, iPad Pro M2), making it the first dynamic radiance field method to support real-time mobile rendering.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ReRF [65], VideoRF wins on all quality metrics on the ReRF dataset (PSNR 32.01 vs 31.84, SSIM 0.976 vs 0.974, LPIPS 0.023 vs 0.042) at nearly identical storage (0.658 vs 0.645 MB), and is dramatically faster at inference (116 FPS vs 4 FPS on desktop). Against MeRF [50], VideoRF matches or exceeds quality (PSNR 32.01 vs 31.12 on ReRF) while using 11.4× less storage (0.658 vs 7.529 MB), making it viable for streaming where MeRF is not. Against HumanRF [18], VideoRF is clearly superior on the ReRF dataset (PSNR 32.01 vs 28.82, LPIPS 0.023 vs 0.069) but slightly trails on the Actors-HQ dataset (PSNR 28.46 vs 28.98, LPIPS 0.278 vs 0.151), though at 5.4× smaller storage (0.426 vs 2.286 MB). Against TiNeuVox [11], VideoRF dominates on quality (PSNR 32.01 vs 22.70 on ReRF) at a modest storage increase (0.658 vs 0.337 MB). Critically, VideoRF is the only method among all four baselines that supports real-time rendering on mobile devices via hardware video codec decoding and shader-based rendering.

## Relation to Prior Work

VideoRF sits at the intersection of two previously separate research threads: dynamic NeRF methods (D-NeRF, HumanRF, TiNeuVox, ReRF) that achieve photorealistic 4D view synthesis but are too computationally heavy for mobile, and mobile NeRF rendering approaches (MeRF, MobileNeRF, ReND) that handle static scenes via mesh/texture baking but cannot accommodate per-frame geometric change. The key conceptual departure is reinterpreting the 4D feature volume as a sequence of 2D feature images amenable to standard video codecs (H.264), combined with Morton-sorted mapping tables and adaptive frame grouping to enforce the spatial-temporal redundancy that codecs exploit. This repurposes the well-studied video compression pipeline—previously applied to RGB video or to per-frame NeRF residuals in ReRF—as the primary storage and transmission mechanism for the radiance field itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By making dynamic radiance field streaming and rendering feasible on commodity mobile hardware (23 FPS on iPhone 14 Pro, 40 FPS on iPad Pro M2), VideoRF opens a practical path toward consumer-facing free-viewpoint video experiences in VR/AR that were previously confined to desktop GPUs. The deferred-shading-plus-tiny-MLP rendering pipeline and the codec-tailored training scheme (spatial/temporal consistency losses, adaptive grouping) are modular ideas likely to transfer to other streaming neural representations. Limitations include the human-centric focus, the requirement for ~80 calibrated multi-view captures per sequence, and a quality gap versus ReRF at very low bitrates; open questions include whether the approach generalizes to non-human dynamic scenes, real-time single-camera capture, and whether higher-level semantic priors could further reduce the feature dimensionality.
