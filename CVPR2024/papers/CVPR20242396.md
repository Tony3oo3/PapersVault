## Problem

Single-image reflection separation is severely ill-posed because the transmission and reflection layers lack distinguishing auxiliary content information. Existing methods rely on statistical priors or learned content distributions that fail when both layers contain complex, recognizable content. This paper introduces natural language descriptions as a new form of layer-specific content guidance, but must address three challenges: cross-modality inconsistency between text and blended image content, the recognizable layer ambiguity (uncertain number of descriptions), and the absence of language annotations in all existing reflection separation datasets.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB mixture image M of arbitrary resolution plus one or two free-form natural language descriptions, where each description specifies the content of one layer. Due to the recognizable layer ambiguity, one description may be null (e.g., only the transmission layer is visible), so the network must handle a variable number of text inputs under a unified interface. The output consists of two full-resolution RGB images: the recovered transmission layer T and reflection layer R, each produced by an independent lightweight decoder.

## Method

The framework uses a ResNet-50 image encoder with multi-scale hypercolumn fusion to produce a shared visual feature, and a CLIP language encoder to extract global text features. The core interaction stage consists of 2N cascaded groups, each containing an Adaptive Global Aggregation Module (AGAM) that pools spatial context via cross-attention, a language gate that switches between cross-attention (description available) and self-attention (description null) to handle the recognizable layer ambiguity, and an Adaptive Global Interaction Module (AGIM) that performs channel-wise cross-attention between global language and visual features to rearrange channels for layer separation. Training employs a contrastive correspondence loss that forces the estimated layer to be more similar to its own description than to the counter layer, a layer correspondence loss matching similarity to ground-truth layers, and standard pixel/perceptual/reconstruction losses. A randomized training strategy drops one description 30% of the time to improve robustness to variable input counts.

## Results

The proposed method achieves the best average PSNR of 25.72 dB and SSIM of 0.914 across 540 real mixture images spanning six datasets (Postcard, Object, Wild, Real20, Nature, and SIR² subsets). It outperforms the strongest baseline DSRNet [23] by 0.21 dB in PSNR and 0.008 in SSIM on average, and leads on every individual dataset. The largest margin is on the Nature dataset (PSNR 23.87 vs. 22.26 for DSRNet), where reflections are more challenging.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method wins on both PSNR and SSIM against all six baselines on every dataset. The closest competitor is DSRNet [23] (ICCV 2023), which trails by 0.21 dB PSNR and 0.008 SSIM on average; the gap widens to 1.61 dB on the Nature dataset where reflections are more complex. Dong et al. [10] (ICCV 2021) and IBCLN [31] (CVPR 2020) occupy the middle tier (24.31 and 24.02 dB average PSNR respectively), while YTMT [22] (NeurIPS 2021) and CoRRN [52] (TPAMI 2019) are slightly behind at 24.01 and 23.70 dB. The earliest baseline, Zhang et al. [67] (CVPR 2018), lags furthest at 22.77 dB average PSNR, confirming that language-guided separation provides a consistent and meaningful improvement over purely image-driven approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by transplanting the language-guided vision paradigm (established in image editing, segmentation, and colorization) into the reflection separation domain, where no prior work has used natural language as an input modality. Methodologically, it repurposes CLIP's pre-trained language encoder and adapts cross-attention mechanisms (from vision-language pre-training) to operate at the feature-channel level for layer disentanglement, building on the feature-channel-rearrangement idea from DSRNet and Wei et al. The contrastive correspondence loss is a direct analogue of CLIP's in-batch contrastive objective, re-targeted to distinguish a layer from its counter-layer within a mixture image rather than matching a caption to a clean image.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By introducing language as a flexible content prior, the paper offers a practical solution to the ill-posedness of single-image reflection separation without requiring specialized capture hardware, making it applicable to arbitrary internet-downloaded images. The identified 'recognizable layer ambiguity' problem and its gated/randomized training solution are practically relevant for real-world deployment where users can only describe what they can see. However, the authors acknowledge that the method struggles when both layers share similar content, and the modest ~0.2 dB average PSNR gain over the strongest baseline (DSRNet) suggests the language signal provides a helpful but not transformative prior; a more flexible interaction mechanism for ambiguous cases remains open.
