## Problem

Existing speech-driven talking head synthesis methods suffer from fundamental synchronization failures across four dimensions: GAN-based approaches produce inconsistent subject identity across frames, while NeRF-based methods exhibit mismatched lip movements, limited facial expression control (often only blinking), and unstable head poses that cause head-torso separation. The paper argues that this multi-faceted synchronization gap, rather than any single component, is the core obstacle to photorealistic talking head generation.

## Input / Output

The method requires a per-subject cropped reference video (512×512 or 450×450, ~8,843 frames at 25 FPS) paired with its original speech for training (~2 hours on an RTX 3090), and at inference takes arbitrary driving audio to produce a 512×512 synchronized talking head video at 50 FPS. The Portrait-Sync Generator post-processes the NeRF output by merging it with the original frame via Gaussian-blurred compositing to restore hair strands and filling head-torso gaps with average neck color, yielding a high-resolution final video.

## Method

SyncTalk is a NeRF-based framework built on tri-plane multiresolution hash encoders (Instant-NGP style) that conditions the implicit radiance function on disentangled lip and expression features. The Face-Sync Controller pre-trains an audio-visual encoder on LRS2 under lip-sync discriminator supervision so that extracted audio features share the same distribution as lip movements (rather than ASR-oriented features from HuBERT/Wav2Vec), and captures expressions via 52 semantically labeled 3D blendshape coefficients; a Facial-Aware Masked-Attention module then spatially separates lip and expression attention to prevent cross-interference. The Head-Sync Stabilizer combines a 3DMM-based motion tracker with an optical-flow keypoint tracker and a two-stage bundle adjustment (inspired by SLAM) to produce smooth, jitter-free rotation and translation parameters. Training uses a coarse-to-fine strategy (100K + 25K iterations) with MSE followed by LPIPS-weighted patch losses, and the entire pipeline renders at 50 FPS with CUDA acceleration.

## Results

On the head reconstruction benchmark, SyncTalk (with Portrait-Sync Generator) achieves a PSNR of 37.40 dB, LPIPS of 0.0113, and FID of 2.71, outperforming the best GAN baseline IP-LAP (PSNR 35.15, LPIPS 0.0443, FID 8.21) and the best NeRF baseline ER-NeRF (PSNR 32.52, LPIPS 0.0334, FID 5.29). For lip synchronization, SyncTalk attains the lowest LSE-D (7.72/8.02) and highest LSE-C (6.67/6.26) across two OOD audio samples, surpassing DINet (LSE-D 8.50/8.20) and ER-NeRF (LSE-D 11.81/10.73). In the user study (35 participants, 24 clips), SyncTalk leads all five MOS categories, scoring 4.30/5 on lip-sync accuracy and 4.02/5 on video realness versus the next-best IP-LAP at 3.16/5 on realness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SyncTalk outperforms all nine baselines on the majority of metrics in Table 1. Among GAN methods, IP-LAP is the closest competitor (PSNR 35.15 vs. 37.40; LPIPS 0.0443 vs. 0.0113), but SyncTalk still leads by 2.25 dB in PSNR and a 4× reduction in LPIPS. Among NeRF methods, ER-NeRF is the strongest baseline (PSNR 32.52, LPIPS 0.0334, FID 5.29); SyncTalk improves LPIPS by roughly 3× and FID by nearly 2× over ER-NeRF. On lip synchronization (Table 2), SyncTalk achieves the best LSE-D and LSE-C on both OOD audio samples, beating DINet by ~0.8 in LSE-D and ER-NeRF by ~4.1 in LSE-D. In the user study (Table 3), SyncTalk leads every category; the largest margin is on Video Realness (4.02 vs. IP-LAP's 3.16, a 20% gap) and Lip-sync Accuracy (4.30 vs. Wav2Lip's 3.84). The ablation confirms that removing the Head-Sync Stabilizer causes the largest single degradation (PSNR drops from 37.31 to 28.98), while replacing the Audio-Visual Encoder with HuBERT increases LMD by 21%.

## Relation to Prior Work

SyncTalk sits at the intersection of NeRF-based talking head synthesis (AD-NeRF, ER-NeRF, GeneFace) and 3D facial animation (EmoTalk's blendshape model), but its conceptual contribution is reframing the problem around multi-dimensional synchronization rather than any single component. It inherits the tri-plane hash encoding and region attention from ER-NeRF, the two-stage bundle adjustment from AD-NeRF, and the optical-flow keypoint tracking from DFA-NeRF, but departs from prior NeRF methods by replacing ASR-trained audio encoders (HuBERT, Wav2Vec 2.0) with a lip-sync-discriminator-supervised audio-visual encoder trained on LRS2, and by introducing semantically controlled 3D blendshape coefficients for expression generation beyond blinking. The Portrait-Sync Generator for hair and background restoration is a practical addition not present in prior NeRF talking head pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a useful diagnostic framework—identifying four synchronization axes (identity, lips, expressions, head pose)—that clarifies why prior methods produce unrealistic outputs, and the ablations confirm that each axis contributes meaningfully (e.g., removing the Head-Sync Stabilizer drops PSNR by ~8 dB). However, the method remains per-subject (requiring a reference video per person), limiting its applicability to one-shot or multi-subject scenarios. Open questions include whether the audio-visual encoder's lip-sync advantage generalizes to unseen speakers without retraining, whether the blendshape-based expression control scales to more diverse or emotional expressions, and whether the 50 FPS rendering speed holds at higher resolutions or with more complex scenes.
