## Problem

Existing multi-sound source localization methods require prior knowledge of the number of sound-making objects to separate and localize them individually. When this prior information is incorrect or unavailable—as is typical in real-world environments—these methods fail to accurately localize sound sources. This paper addresses the gap by proposing a method that autonomously determines the number of sound sources and localizes each one without any prior source count.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241074/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image frame (224×224, extracted from the central frame of 3-second video clips; for multi-source, two frames are concatenated to 448×224) and a corresponding mixed audio spectrogram (log-scale, 16 kHz resampled) as input. It produces per-object sound source localization heatmaps and automatically determines the number of sound sources in the scene. The method is fully self-supervised, requiring no class labels or source-count annotations during training or inference.

## Method

The framework uses a two-stream ResNet-18 to extract visual and audio features, then computes a sound-associated map via cosine similarity between spatial visual features and the globally pooled audio feature, trained with an audio-visual contrastive loss. The core contribution is the Iterative Object Identification (IOI) module, which repeatedly selects the highest-scoring cell in the sound-associated map, identifies its foreground region by comparing inner products against a positive (selected cell) and negative (background) reference vector, and zeros out the identified region before the next iteration—continuing until no foreground remains. An Object Similarity-aware Clustering (OSC) loss then guides the learned features so that cells belonging to the same object are pulled together while different objects and background are pushed apart, reducing the number of iterations needed. Finally, a union-find algorithm clusters the identified cells into distinct objects, and cosine-similarity thresholds eliminate spurious background detections, yielding the final per-object localization maps without any prior source count.

## Results

On the multi-sound source localization task, the proposed method achieves 52.1% CAP, 72.5% PIAP, 38.6% CIoU@0.3, and 30.1% AUC on MUSIC-Duet, and 32.5% CAP, 44.4% PIAP, 46.9% CIoU@0.3, and 29.2% AUC on VGGSound-Duet, outperforming the previous best (AVGN) by up to 15.3% in PIAP and 20.7% in CIoU@0.3. For single-source localization, it reaches 77.4% AP and 59.4% AUC on MUSIC-Solo, and 46.2% AP on VGGSound-Single. The method also generalizes to three-source mixtures (VGGSound-Trio) and mixed-count scenarios (VGGSound-Mixed) where AVGN cannot operate due to its fixed prior source count requirement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against nine baselines spanning single- and multi-source localization. On MUSIC-Duet, the proposed method beats the strongest baseline AVGN [26] by +1.5% CAP, +15.3% PIAP, +6.1% CIoU@0.3, and +5.5% AUC; the gap is even larger on VGGSound-Duet (+10.6% CAP, +16.3% PIAP, +20.7% CIoU@0.3, +5.4% AUC). Mix-and-Localize [17] is the second-strongest multi-source baseline but trails the proposed method by 4.6–18.4 points across metrics. For single-source tasks, the method edges out AVGN on MUSIC-Solo (+0.2% AP, +4.0% IoU@0.5, +10.9% AUC) and achieves the best AP on VGGSound-Single (46.2% vs. 35.3%), though AVGN retains a slight AUC advantage (42.3% vs. 41.2%). Critically, AVGN and other multi-source baselines require a fixed prior on the number of sources, so they cannot be evaluated on VGGSound-Mixed (variable source count), where the proposed method still reports 27.9% CAP and 36.0% PIAP. The method also uses 38.5M parameters versus AVGN's 61.2M, with 28.3% faster inference per image.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multi-sound source localization line of work (DSOL, Mix-and-Localize, AVGN, Coarse-to-Fine) by removing the requirement for prior knowledge of the number of sound sources, which all existing multi-source methods depend on. It builds directly on the two-stream audio-visual architecture and the audio-visual contrastive loss from Chen et al. (CVPR'21), repurposing the sound-associated map as an input to a novel Iterative Object Identification (IOI) module that progressively discovers and separates objects. The key conceptual departure is replacing the fixed number of source slots used by prior methods with an iterative, data-driven process that terminates when no further foreground regions remain, guided by a new Object Similarity-aware Clustering (OSC) loss.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Removing the prior source count requirement makes multi-sound source localization applicable to real-world scenarios where the number of concurrent sound sources is unknown, which is the norm outside controlled benchmarks. The IOI module's iterative discovery mechanism is a reusable pattern that could transfer to other tasks requiring unknown-count object separation. However, the method's sound source counting accuracy drops sharply from 90.8% (single) to 68.9% (trio), and single-source performance gains are modest, suggesting the iterative mechanism introduces overhead that does not always pay off. Open questions include scalability to more than three sources, handling spatially overlapping objects, and whether the approach generalizes to video (temporal) localization rather than single-frame maps.
