## Problem

Existing sketch semantic segmentation methods rely on a single representation (image, graph, or sequence) and thus capture only a subset of the structural, positional, and proximity information needed for accurate stroke labeling. Additionally, prior auto-regressive decoders label one stroke at a time, failing to exploit the group-level context that is naturally available during segmentation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241604/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a sketch as a sequence of strokes, each rendered as a 256×256 binary image augmented with two coordinate channels (CoordConv-style) to preserve positional information. At inference, the auto-regressive decoder outputs a group code at each step, and strokes are assigned to that group via a sigmoid dot-product threshold (p > 0.5), producing a partition of all strokes into semantically labeled parts.

## Method

ContextSeg is a two-stage pipeline. First, a 2D CNN autoencoder learns stroke embeddings by jointly minimizing image reconstruction loss and a novel dense distance-field regression loss, where the distance field is a smooth unsigned distance confined to a narrow band around the stroke (DF^s = 1/(1+k·exp(dis))), forcing the encoder to capture fine structural geometry that reconstruction alone misses. Second, an auto-regressive Transformer (built on the standard encoder-decoder with self- and cross-attention) segments the sketch by labeling an entire group of strokes per decoding step rather than one stroke at a time; inspired by Pointer Networks, the decoder emits a group code that is matched against stroke codes via dot product to select member strokes. The decoder explicitly conditions on both previously labeled and remaining unlabeled strokes, and training uses focal loss (γ=2.0) to mitigate the severe imbalance in stroke counts across semantic parts.

## Results

On the SPG dataset (20 categories), ContextSeg achieves average SAcc of 96.2%, GAcc of 97.2%, and CAcc of 92.6%, setting new state-of-the-art across all three metrics. On the more challenging CreativeSketch dataset (2 categories with 8 and 17 semantic parts), it reaches 60.2% SAcc, 87.5% GAcc, and 55.8% CAcc. The most notable gain is in component accuracy: +18.7% over SPGSeg, +1.7% over SketchGNN, and +1.4% over Sketch-Segformer on SPG, and +22.9%, +13.3%, and +11.9% respectively on CreativeSketch.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ContextSeg outperforms all three baselines on average across both datasets and all three metrics. Against the most recent competitor, Sketch-Segformer [34], it gains +1.0 SAcc, +0.5 GAcc, and +1.4 CAcc on SPG, and +2.2 SAcc, +3.6 GAcc, and +11.9 CAcc on CreativeSketch. Against SketchGNN [31], the margins are larger: +1.6 SAcc, +0.6 GAcc, +1.7 CAcc on SPG and +3.2 SAcc, +4.6 GAcc, +13.3 CAcc on CS. The largest gap is over the earliest baseline SPGSeg [15], where ContextSeg leads by +17.4 SAcc, +9.7 GAcc, and +18.7 CAcc on SPG, and +13.1 SAcc, +8.5 GAcc, and +22.9 CAcc on CS. The only category where ContextSeg does not lead is Face on SPG, where Sketch-Segformer edges it out by 0.1 CAcc (94.9 vs. 94.8), attributed to highly overlapped hair and eye strokes that challenge embedding learning.

## Relation to Prior Work

ContextSeg positions itself at the intersection of three established sketch representation paradigms (image, graph, sequence), arguing that each captures complementary information that no single format fully encodes. At the stroke level, it extends the autoencoder-based embedding approach by adding a novel dense distance field regression branch to the reconstruction objective, addressing the sparsity and geometric variability of sketch strokes that plain reconstruction fails to capture. At the sketch level, it departs from prior stroke-by-stroke decoding (as in SPGSeg, SketchGNN, Sketch-Segformer) by adopting a group-based auto-regressive Transformer decoder inspired by Pointer Networks, where each decoding step assigns an entire semantic group rather than a single stroke, explicitly leveraging labeled and unlabeled context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean, well-ablated recipe for sketch semantic segmentation that outperforms recent Transformer-based baselines, and its distance-field regularizer for stroke embedding is a transferable idea for any sparse-structure reconstruction task. The preliminary cross-category experiment (13.1% gain on ant face) and semantic-aware copy-paste augmentation (+19.8% on rare parts) open practical avenues for handling class imbalance and limited per-category data, though both remain early-stage. Key limitations acknowledged by the authors—failure on highly cluttered strokes (hairs, eyelashes) and inability to encode strokes with rapid large geometric variation—point to open questions about whether richer stroke representations or hierarchical decoding could close the remaining gap.
