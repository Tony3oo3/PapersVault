## Problem

Conventional prediction mimicking in object detection KD forces the student to simultaneously match ground-truth targets (from its own assigner) and the teacher's predictions, which often conflict—especially when teacher and student use different label assigners. Previous work either down-weights conflict regions (sacrificing informative uncertain areas) or abandons prediction mimicking in favor of feature imitation. The paper identifies that heavily uncertain regions, precisely where conflicts arise, actually contain the most task-relevant knowledge for the student.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB images processed by dense one-stage detectors (GFL, RetinaNet, FCOS, ATSS) and produces bounding boxes with class labels. Unusually, the student's intermediate detection-head feature maps (at a chosen layer index i, default i=3) are routed into the corresponding frozen layers of the teacher's detection head to generate cross-head predictions, which then serve as the distillation target. The teacher must be pre-trained and frozen; the student is trained end-to-end with both detection and distillation losses.

## Method

CrossKD feeds the student's i-th intermediate head feature into the (i+1)-th convolutional layer of the teacher's detection head, producing cross-head predictions that share part of the teacher's head architecture. The KD loss is computed between these cross-head predictions and the teacher's original predictions, rather than between the student's and teacher's predictions directly. This architecturally separates the supervision: the student's own head receives only the detection loss (ground truth), while the cross-head path (student features through frozen teacher layers) receives the distillation loss, eliminating the contradictory gradient signals. For the classification branch, Quality Focal Loss (QFL) is used as the distance function; for regression, GIoU is applied for direct box regression and KL divergence for distribution-based regression (e.g., GFL). The method is orthogonal to feature imitation and can be combined with methods like PKD for further gains.

## Results

On MS COCO (minival), CrossKD boosts GFL ResNet-50 (1× schedule) from 40.2 AP to 43.7 AP (+3.5), outperforming all prior KD methods. Combined with the feature imitation method PKD, CrossKD further reaches 43.9 AP (+3.7). The method also generalizes across detectors: RetinaNet, FCOS, and ATSS gain +2.3, +2.8, and +2.4 AP respectively, and CrossKD outperforms PKD by 0.8–0.9 AP on heterogeneous backbone pairs (Swin-T→ResNet-50, ResNet-50→MobileNetv2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CrossKD (43.7 AP) outperforms the strongest feature imitation baseline PKD (43.3 AP) by 0.4 AP and the strongest prediction mimicking baseline LD (43.0 AP) by 0.7 AP on the GFL R101→R50 setup. Against earlier feature imitation methods, CrossKD leads GID by 2.2 AP, FGD by 2.4 AP, and FitNets by 3.0 AP. The method is also orthogonal to feature imitation: combining CrossKD with PKD yields 43.9 AP, the best result in the table. Under severe target conflict (different label assigners), conventional prediction mimicking (KD) drops to 30.3 AP with a RetinaNet teacher, while CrossKD still achieves 41.2 AP (+1.0 over baseline). On heterogeneous backbones (Swin-T→ResNet-50 and ResNet-50→MobileNetv2), CrossKD surpasses PKD by 0.8 and 0.9 AP respectively, confirming its advantage over the prior SOTA feature imitation method in cross-architecture settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

CrossKD extends the prediction mimicking lineage (Hinton et al. 2015; LD by Zheng et al. 2022) by diagnosing a previously underappreciated 'target conflict' problem: the student is simultaneously pulled toward ground-truth labels and teacher predictions that disagree in heavily uncertain regions. Rather than mitigating this conflict through region selection or loss weighting (as prior methods like Defeat and LD do), CrossKD resolves it architecturally by routing the student's intermediate features through the teacher's frozen head layers, so that the distillation target and the cross-head prediction share a common computational pathway. The paper also positions CrossKD as orthogonal to feature imitation methods (e.g., PKD), showing that the two paradigms can be combined for further gains.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a purely prediction-mimicking scheme (no feature imitation) can surpass all prior feature-imitation SOTA methods challenges the long-standing assumption in detection KD that intermediate feature consistency is necessary for strong distillation. The method's simplicity—no auxiliary heads, no region selection, no additional parameters—makes it easy to adopt across detector architectures, and its robustness under severe assigner mismatch (e.g., RetinaNet teacher → GFL student) addresses a practical pain point in real-world model compression. Open questions include whether the cross-head mechanism extends naturally to DETR-family detectors with set-prediction heads, and the authors note future work on 3D object detection.
