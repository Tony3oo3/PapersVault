## Problem

CLIP's visual prompting (e.g., a red circle) can guide attention to a region but cannot localize objects without precise proposals from external detectors like Faster R-CNN, whose concept coverage lags behind CLIP's. Existing zero-shot referring expression comprehension methods therefore depend on pre-extracted bounding boxes, failing on objects outside the detector's predefined categories. This paper asks whether CLIP itself can be made to localize the target region described by a referring expression, without relying on such proposals.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a free-form text prompt (referring expression) as input. Rather than using detector-generated bounding boxes, it generates 486 candidate proposals by placing 6 anchor ellipses of varying sizes at each of 81 uniformly sampled grid points (9×9). The output is a single tuned ellipse parameterized by five coefficients (center x/y, major axis, minor axis, rotation angle) that encapsulates the target region, which can be converted to a bounding box or mask for evaluation.

## Method

The core contribution is differentiable visual prompting: a rotated ellipse is rendered onto the image via a Gaussian of the ellipse equation (exp(−φ²/2σ²)), making the visual prompt differentiable so that gradients from CLIP's similarity score can flow back to the ellipse parameters. Initialization selects the best anchor ellipse from Top-K CLIP scores by highest average Grad-CAM activation inside the ellipse. A small MLP (64-128-128-5 with learnable tokens) then predicts per-step transformations to the five ellipse coefficients over 200 iterations. Three complementary losses drive the tuning: a matching loss (L_sim) that maximizes CLIP similarity between the prompted image and the text prompt against background prompts, an inflation loss (L_inf) that encourages the ellipse to encompass more Grad-CAM activation, and a squeezing loss (L_sqz) that maximizes the average activation density inside the ellipse to prevent over-inflation. The combination prevents the ellipse from collapsing onto background or sub-word regions while still converging to a compact, accurate localization.

## Results

On the P-Ellipses setting (no precise object proposals), Tune-An-Ellipse achieves 26.3% / 32.3% / 19.8% Top-1 accuracy on RefCOCO val / testA / testB, substantially outperforming Red Circle (8.34% / 6.56% / 10.2%) and ReCLIP (7.35% / 6.59% / 7.69%). On RefCOCO+ and RefCOCOg with P-Ellipses, the method reaches 27.8% / 32.7% / 22.1% and 29.9% / 29.0%, respectively. In the F-RCNN proposal setting, Ours* achieves 49.8% / 58.0% / 40.9% on RefCOCO, marginally surpassing the re-implemented Red Circle* (48.6% / 56.2% / 41.7%) on val and testA. The method also consistently outperforms Grad-CAM in pixel-level mask accuracy across all thresholds on RefCOCO testA and testB.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The most critical comparison is in the P-Ellipses setting where no precise object proposals are available: Tune-An-Ellipse achieves 26.3% on RefCOCO val, more than 3× the accuracy of Red Circle (8.34%) and ReCLIP (7.35%), demonstrating that the iterative ellipse tuning is essential when proposals are imprecise. In the F-RCNN setting, Ours* (49.8% / 58.0% / 40.9% on RefCOCO) slightly edges out the re-implemented Red Circle* (48.6% / 56.2% / 41.7%) on val and testA but trails by 0.8 points on testB, while both substantially outperform CPT (32.2% / 36.1% / 30.3%) and ReCLIP (45.8% / 46.1% / 47.1%). Against supervised baselines DTWREG (39.2%) and Pseudo-Q (56.0%), the zero-shot Ours* is competitive on val but still below Pseudo-Q on testA/testB. On RefCOCO+ testA, Ours* (62.8%) surpasses Red Circle* (61.5%) and ReCLIP (50.1%), confirming gains on appearance-only expressions. Grad-CAM, used as a pixel-level mask baseline in Fig. 4, is consistently outperformed by Tune-An-Ellipse across all thresholds, with the gap widening at higher thresholds where Grad-CAM's sensitivity causes significant accuracy drops.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the visual prompting paradigm discovered by Shtedritski et al. (ICCV 2023), who showed that a red circle drawn on an image can steer CLIP's attention to a specific region, by making the visual prompt differentiable and optimizable. Prior zero-shot REC methods (ReCLIP, CPT, Red Circle) all depend on pre-extracted object proposals from external detectors like Faster R-CNN, which limits their concept coverage; this work replaces those proposals with uniformly distributed anchor ellipses and tunes them via gradient descent using a small MLP. The three-loss optimization (similarity, inflation, squeezing) repurposes Grad-CAM not merely as an explanation tool but as a training signal, and the differentiable rendering of the ellipse via Gaussian and arctan transforms is a straightforward but effective technical contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that CLIP can localize objects described in referring expressions without any external detector, which is conceptually appealing for open-world scenarios where pre-trained detectors lack the relevant categories. However, the performance gap versus F-RCNN-based methods (26–32% vs. 45–62% on RefCOCO) is substantial, and the authors themselves identify three fundamental limitations of CLIP's visual prompting (background response, partial inclusion, sub-word attention) that cap the method's ceiling. The systematic taxonomy of these failure modes and the discussion of potential remedies (contrastive training with negative visual prompts) provide a useful roadmap for improving CLIP's visual grounding. An open question the paper raises is whether a perfectly trained visual prompting capability would render the Grad-CAM-based auxiliary losses entirely unnecessary, reducing the method to a single similarity loss.
