## Problem

NeRF-based dense SLAM methods rely on ray-based volume rendering, which is too slow for full-resolution images, forcing them to render only sparse pixel subsets and producing maps that lack fine geometric detail. This creates an inherent efficiency-accuracy trade-off that limits both tracking speed and reconstruction quality. No prior work had applied the fast tile-based 3D Gaussian Splatting rendering pipeline to the SLAM problem, where the dynamic, expanding scene representation poses challenges absent in static novel-view synthesis.

## Input / Output

The system consumes a sequential RGB-D image stream with known 3×3 camera intrinsics and produces per-frame camera poses (rotation and translation), a dense scene map represented as a set of 3D Gaussians (each with 3D position, covariance parameterized as scale vector + quaternion, opacity, and 12 spherical harmonic coefficients), and rendered RGB-D images at full resolution. The scene representation is initialized by back-projecting half the pixels of the first frame and grows adaptively at keyframes; the full pipeline runs at 8.34 FPS on an RTX 4090 with ~198 MB scene memory, and a lightweight variant using zero-order spherical harmonics reduces memory to ~40 MB.

## Method

GS-SLAM represents the scene as an explicit set of 3D Gaussians and renders RGB-D images via differentiable tile-based splatting rasterization (sorting by depth and front-to-back α-blending), replacing the slow ray-marching of NeRF-based SLAM. An adaptive expansion strategy manages the growing scene: at each keyframe, pixels with low cumulative opacity or large depth error are back-projected to add new Gaussians for newly observed geometry, while floating Gaussians whose positions deviate from observed depth surfaces have their opacity degraded to suppress artifacts. For tracking, a coarse-to-fine scheme first optimizes pose on a half-resolution render to obtain a rough estimate, then selects only reliable Gaussians (within a depth threshold ε of observed surfaces) to render a full-resolution image for fine pose refinement, avoiding drift from noisy regions. The paper derives analytical gradient equations for backpropagating rendering loss through the splatting pipeline to the camera pose, and a bundle adjustment step jointly refines keyframe poses and the Gaussian scene parameters.

## Results

On the Replica dataset, GS-SLAM achieves the best tracking ATE of 0.50 cm (vs. 0.54 cm for Point-SLAM and 0.63 cm for ESLAM) at 8.34 FPS, and the best mapping metrics with Depth L1 of 1.16 cm and Precision of 74.0%. For rendering, GS-SLAM achieves PSNR 34.27 dB, SSIM 0.975, and LPIPS 0.082, outperforming the second-best CoSLAM by 1.52 dB in PSNR while running at 386 FPS (≈100× faster than Vox-Fusion's 3.88 FPS). On TUM-RGBD, GS-SLAM achieves 3.7 cm average ATE, competitive with ESLAM (2.0 cm) and CoSLAM (2.4 cm) but trailing them slightly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GS-SLAM outperforms all five NeRF-based SLAM baselines on Replica tracking, achieving 0.50 cm ATE vs. Point-SLAM (0.54 cm), ESLAM (0.63 cm), CoSLAM (1.00 cm), NICE-SLAM (1.06 cm), and Vox-Fusion (3.09 cm). On rendering, GS-SLAM dominates with PSNR 34.27 dB, beating CoSLAM (30.24 dB), ESLAM (29.08 dB), NICE-SLAM (24.42 dB), and Vox-Fusion (24.41 dB) by 1.5–10 dB, while rendering at 386 FPS versus 3.68 FPS for CoSLAM and 0.30 FPS for NICE-SLAM. For mapping, GS-SLAM achieves the best Depth L1 (1.16 cm) and Precision (74.0%), but trails ESLAM on Recall (67.6% vs. 84.2%) and F1 (70.2% vs. 78.3%). On TUM-RGBD, GS-SLAM (3.7 cm ATE) is competitive but loses to ESLAM (2.0 cm) and CoSLAM (2.4 cm), with the gap attributed to more sophisticated tracking schemes in those methods. In terms of speed, GS-SLAM runs at 8.34 FPS, making it 20× faster than Point-SLAM (0.42 FPS) and 3× faster than NICE-SLAM (2.91 FPS), though slower than CoSLAM (16.64 FPS) and ESLAM (13.42 FPS).

## Relation to Prior Work

GS-SLAM is the first system to integrate 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) into a dense RGB-D SLAM pipeline, repurposing the tile-based splatting rasterization from the static novel-view synthesis setting [13] for real-time tracking and mapping. It departs from the dominant NeRF-based SLAM lineage (iMAP, NICE-SLAM, ESLAM, CoSLAM, Point-SLAM) by replacing ray-based volume rendering with explicit Gaussian splatting, eliminating the need for MLP decoders entirely. The adaptive expansion strategy (adding Gaussians for new geometry, deleting floaters) is a key conceptual extension, since 3DGS was originally designed for reconstructing a fixed set of Gaussians from a static object rather than incrementally building a scene map.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 100× rendering speedup (386 FPS vs. ~4 FPS for prior methods) and 20× system FPS improvement over the closest point-based competitor make high-quality dense SLAM practically viable for downstream applications like VR, robot navigation, and autonomous driving. However, the 198 MB memory footprint (4× NICE-SLAM) and dependence on high-quality depth sensors limit immediate deployment in large-scale or outdoor settings. Open questions include whether the explicit Gaussian representation can scale to city-level scenes without prohibitive memory, whether quantization or clustering can close the memory gap, and whether the method generalizes to monocular or stereo inputs without depth.
