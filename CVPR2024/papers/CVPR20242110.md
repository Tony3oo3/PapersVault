## Problem

Content-aware dataset deduplication (e.g., SemDeDup) can halve training costs for vision-language models with negligible performance loss, but its effects on social biases in trained models have never been studied. This paper shows that SemDeDup has mixed fairness effects—reinforcing gender disparities while inconsistently affecting skin-tone and age disparities—leaving a gap in understanding how a widely deployed cost-saving step interacts with bias.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242110/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes precomputed image embeddings (from a WIT-trained CLIP-ViT-B/16), a set of 110 user-defined sensitive concept text prototypes (330 captions covering intersectional combinations of age, gender, skin tone, race, and ethnicity), and a similarity threshold ε that defines duplicate neighborhoods. It outputs a pruned dataset (50% of LAION-400M) where exactly one sample is retained per duplicate neighborhood within each of 50,000 k-means clusters, with the retained sample chosen to boost underrepresented sensitive concepts. The selection step is CPU-parallelizable across clusters and takes ~1 hour on 32 CPUs, dominated by the shared GPU embedding and clustering steps.

## Method

FairDeDup replaces SemDeDup's maximum-distance-from-centroid selection heuristic with a fairness-aware rule: within each duplicate neighborhood, it preserves the sample that maximizes cosine similarity to the least-represented sensitive concept prototype, where representation is tracked as a running average of similarities between already-preserved samples and each prototype. This running average is maintained independently per k-means cluster to avoid synchronous cross-worker updates and to prevent algorithmic 'gaming' (e.g., balancing underselection of feminine-presenting doctors by overselecting feminine-presenting nurses in a separate cluster). The first sample selected in each cluster is chosen by highest average similarity across all prototypes as a base case. The overall pipeline—CLIP embedding, FAISS k-means partitioning into 50,000 clusters, then per-cluster neighborhood selection—scales to web-scale data with O(n²/k) pairwise comparisons, and the only change from SemDeDup is the selection criterion within each neighborhood.

## Results

On standard zero-shot classification and retrieval benchmarks (ImageNet-1K, ImageNetV2, CIFAR-10/100, Flickr30k, COCO), both SemDeDup and FairDeDup preserve performance within ≤0.8% of the full-data baseline (e.g., ImageNet-1K Acc@5: 0.897 vs. 0.899 full). On FACET zero-shot classification disparity, FairDeDup reduces worst-class gender disparity to 0.298 (vs. 0.346 for SemDeDup) and worst-class age (middle/older) disparity to 0.153 (vs. 0.248), while on FairFace retrieval skew, FairDeDup lowers MinSkew by 0.0555 and MaxSkew by 0.0285 over SemDeDup across gender and race subgroups.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is SemDeDup [Abbas et al., 2023], the standard semantic deduplication algorithm. On standard performance benchmarks (Table 1), the two methods are essentially tied, with a maximum gap of 0.6% (e.g., ImageNet-1K: 0.897 vs. 0.897; Flickr30k R@5: 0.871 vs. 0.874). On FACET fairness (Table 2), FairDeDup wins on 10 of 12 disparity metrics, with the largest gains on worst-class gender disparity (0.298 vs. 0.346, −14% relative) and worst-class age middle/older disparity (0.153 vs. 0.248, −39% relative); the single loss is on mean age middle/young disparity (0.075 vs. 0.059). On FairFace retrieval skew (Table 3), FairDeDup outperforms SemDeDup on all gender metrics (MinSkew 0.182 vs. 0.223, MaxSkew 0.125 vs. 0.153, NDKL 0.012 vs. 0.015) and on race MinSkew (0.513 vs. 0.583) and MaxSkew (0.372 vs. 0.401), though it is slightly worse on age MaxSkew (0.296 vs. 0.224). In the smaller-scale FACET deduplication study (Table 4), FairDeDup retains a higher percentage of minority-class samples than SemDeDup across gender (32.29% vs. 31.91%), skin tone (51.06% vs. 50.46%), and age (44.06% vs. 43.62%), with differences significant at >99.9% confidence.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is a direct modification of SemDeDup (Abbas et al., 2023), replacing its maximum-distance sample selection heuristic with a fairness-aware one that tracks running similarity to user-defined sensitive concept prototypes. It sits at the intersection of two established literatures: large-scale dataset deduplication for VLP training (SemDeDup, D2 Pruning, coreset selection) and VLP fairness evaluation (FairFace, FACET, MaxSkew metrics). The authors explicitly position their work as the first to study how deduplication affects fairness outcomes in trained VLP models and to propose a fairness-aware variant of a deduplication algorithm, carving out a new sub-problem within the broader dataset pruning task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Since aggressive deduplication is becoming standard practice for reducing VLP training costs, the finding that it systematically worsens gender disparity while having mixed effects on other attributes means a 'neutral' cost-saving step carries hidden fairness consequences that practitioners should account for. FairDeDup's simplicity (a selection-heuristic swap with natural-language concept specification) makes it easy to adopt, but its effectiveness is bounded by clustering granularity and the biases of the embedding model used for both clustering and concept matching. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to other deduplication algorithms, to sensitive attributes beyond gender/race/age (e.g., disability, national origin), and to settings where the embedding model itself encodes strong stereotypes that the concept prototypes cannot correct.
