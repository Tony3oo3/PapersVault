## Problem

Existing fMRI-to-image reconstruction methods treat neural signals independently and rely on limited paired annotations, making it difficult to achieve high semantic fidelity in reconstructed images. Moreover, prior approaches cannot produce stylized outputs without additional optimization or image-to-image translation steps. This paper addresses both gaps by reframing neural decoding as an optimal transport problem that captures inter-sample semantic relationships and by decoupling semantic content from visual style.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240152/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The sole neural input is an fMRI signal divided into fixed-size voxel vectors fed to ViT-based encoders; no raw image or text is required at inference time. The visual stream produces 512×512 reconstructed images via a latent diffusion model, while the language stream generates a free-form caption. Style control is applied training-free by prepending a text prompt (e.g., 'A Van Gogh style painting showing') to the predicted caption before conditioning the diffusion model, yielding outputs in oil, cartoon, Picasso, ink wash, abstract, or doodling styles without any fine-tuning.

## Method

MindArt operates in two stages. First, a graph matching (GM)-guided representation learning module constructs a dynamic multiplex graph over randomly sampled fMRI and image entities, propagates information via a multiplex attention GNN with alternating self- and cross-domain attention layers, and then solves an entropy-regularized optimal transport problem (via Sinkhorn iterations) to learn a coupling matrix that assigns neural patterns to semantically similar images rather than enforcing strict one-to-one pairs; a dustbin mechanism handles unmatched nodes. Second, a two-stream decoding architecture combines a visual branch (L2-regularized linear regression predicting a latent diffusion embedding, decoded to an intermediate image) with a language branch (frozen GPT-2 decoder bridged to fMRI encoders via 12-head cross-attention, trained to produce image captions). The two streams are fused by concatenating the predicted caption with an optional style prompt as the text condition for Stable Diffusion, enabling training-free semantic editing and style transfer.

## Results

On the DIR dataset, MindArt achieves the best semantic fidelity with CLIP_T@10 of 40.3% and CLIP_T@50 of 26.0%, surpassing the next-best MindGPT (31.6% and 14.0%) by 8.7 and 12 percentage points respectively. On the GOD dataset, MindArt reaches CLIP_T@10 of 43.6% and CLIP_T@50 of 24.0%, outperforming GESS (43.1% and 12.0%) and MinD-Vis (39.7% and 12.0%). MindArt also attains the best CLIP visual score (0.622 on DIR, 0.631 on GOD) and lowest FID (2.67 on DIR, 2.55 on GOD) among all compared methods. The only metric where MindArt does not lead is SSIM, where the pixel-level reconstruction method VQ-fMRI achieves substantially higher values (0.433 on DIR, 0.423 on GOD) due to its inpainting-based approach.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MindArt decisively outperforms all five baselines on semantic fidelity metrics (CLIP_T@10 and CLIP_T@50) across both datasets, with the largest margin over VQ-fMRI (which lags by ~15–19 points on CLIP_T@10) and a clear 12-point gap over GESS on CLIP_T@50 for GOD. On the DIR dataset, MindArt beats MindGPT by 8.7 points on CLIP_T@10 and 12 points on CLIP_T@50, while also achieving a lower FID (2.67 vs. 2.70) and higher CLIP visual score (0.622 vs. 0.575). On GOD, MindArt edges out GESS on CLIP_T@10 (43.6% vs. 43.1%) but doubles its CLIP_T@50 (24.0% vs. 12.0%), and surpasses MinD-Vis by 3.9 points on CLIP_T@10 and 12 points on CLIP_T@50. The sole exception is SSIM, where VQ-fMRI's pixel-level inpainting strategy yields substantially higher values (0.433/0.423) than MindArt (0.223/0.242), reflecting a trade-off between low-level pixel fidelity and high-level semantic accuracy. In language reconstruction (Table 2), MindArt also surpasses MindGPT on all five metrics (B@1, B@4, ROUGE, METEOR, SPICE), confirming its advantage in capturing semantic content from fMRI signals.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the fMRI-to-image reconstruction line (Miyawaki, Naselaris, Takagi, MinD-Vis, GESS) but departs from the prevailing paradigm of treating fMRI samples independently by casting neural decoding as a graph-matching optimal transport problem. The graph neural network backbone is adapted from SuperGlue's MAGNN architecture, and the entropy-regularized OT solver follows Cuturi's Sinkhorn framework, but their combination into a self-supervised cross-domain alignment objective for brain decoding is new. The '1+N' decoupling of amodal semantics from contextual priors (style, layout) extends prior single-stream decoders into a multi-branch architecture that reuses off-the-shelf components (ViT, CLIP, GPT-2, Stable Diffusion) in a novel compositional arrangement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The OT/graph-matching formulation provides a principled, annotation-light self-supervision signal that could generalize to other cross-modal alignment problems beyond fMRI, and the training-free style transfer via prompt editing opens a practical path toward personalized neural decoding without per-style fine-tuning. However, all experiments are conducted on a single subject (subject 3) across two small datasets (1250 images, 8 subjects total), leaving open whether the GM-guided representation learning and the large CLIP_T@50 gap (24–26% vs. 10–14%) hold across subjects and at scale. The authors note that extending the framework to video, 3D, and other modalities is future work, and the reliance on frozen pre-trained models (CLIP, GPT-2, Stable Diffusion) means the method's ceiling is bounded by those components' capabilities.
