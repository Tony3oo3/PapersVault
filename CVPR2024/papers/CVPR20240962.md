## Problem

Current end-to-end driving policies are trained on fixed, curated datasets and retrained from scratch when failures emerge, making them brittle to out-of-distribution scenarios and unable to accumulate knowledge over time. Prior continual learning approaches either assume ground-truth labels, operate on simplified classification benchmarks, or require storing entire datasets in memory, none of which scale to the massive, unlabeled, non-stationary video streams available on the open web. This paper addresses how to build a truly never-ending, self-supervised driving policy that learns incrementally from a one-pass stream of unlabeled driving videos while retaining previously acquired skills.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a one-pass stream of sequential RGB video frames (extracted at 10 Hz from driving videos), along with estimated vehicle speed and a categorical high-level driving command, and produces K=5 sequential ego-relative waypoints in a top-down BEV coordinate frame at 0.5-second intervals. Critically, the input images are never revisited during training—each frame is seen exactly once—and the supervision signals (waypoints, speed, command) are entirely pseudo-labeled by an ensemble of inverse dynamics models rather than obtained from ground-truth annotations, GPS, or HD maps.

## Method

The framework (∞-Driver) couples three modules in a knowledge-distillation loop. First, an ensemble of M=5 inverse dynamics models (teacher) regresses ego-motion between consecutive image pairs using a matrix Fisher distribution for probabilistic rotation estimation, then integrates a sequence of poses into waypoint, speed, and command pseudo-labels; the ensemble disagreement provides epistemic uncertainty. Second, a purification module splits samples into high- and low-uncertainty sets via a two-component GMM on the uncertainty distribution, applies temporal-consistency windowed averaging to refine noisy high-uncertainty waypoints, and removes deprecated images (via matrix Fisher entropy with a three-sigma rule) and deprecated pseudo-labels (via a low-loss GMM filter on student training losses). Third, an uncertainty-guided episodic memory buffer of fixed size B retains the highest-uncertainty samples across all seen data, rehearsed alongside incoming data with a weighted L1 behavior-cloning objective that progressively shifts emphasis toward the buffer as training progresses, thereby balancing adaptation to new experiences against catastrophic forgetting.

## Results

On the cross-city incremental learning benchmark (10 cities from KITTI, nuScenes, and Argoverse2), ∞-Driver achieves the best Average Loss (L) of 0.61 and lowest Forgetting (F) of −0.04 at buffer size 6,000, outperforming DER (L=0.82, F=−0.19), Rainbow (L=0.87, F=−0.31), and PuriDivER (L=0.84, F=−0.11). The advantage is most pronounced at smaller buffer sizes (B=3,000), where ∞-Driver's L of 0.66 beats the next-best DER (0.76) by 13% and Rainbow (1.29) by nearly 2×. On the open-web experiment (10M YouTube images), ∞-Driver achieves the lowest ADE of 1.13 and tied-best Forgetting (−0.02) on a held-out evaluation set mixing all three standard datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

∞-Driver wins on all three metrics (Average Loss, Forgetting, Intransigence) across all buffer sizes against all three baselines. Against DER (reservoir-sampling replay), the gap is largest on Forgetting: at B=3,000, ∞-Driver's F of −0.07 is roughly 2× less negative than DER's −0.14, indicating substantially less catastrophic forgetting. Against Rainbow (uncertainty + augmentation-based memory), the advantage is most dramatic at B=3,000 where Rainbow's L of 1.29 is nearly double ∞-Driver's 0.66, suggesting Rainbow's interval-based sampling fails to maintain buffer diversity. Against PuriDivER (consistency-based relabeling for noisy streams), ∞-Driver leads on Average Loss by 0.13–0.22 and on Forgetting by 0.04–0.09, showing that combining temporal-consistency relabeling with uncertainty-guided buffer selection outperforms relabeling alone. On the open-web experiment, ∞-Driver's ADE of 1.13 beats DER and PuriDivER (both 1.18) and Rainbow (1.24), with the Forgetting metric tied at −0.02 with DER.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes three established threads—conditional imitation learning for driving (Codevilla et al.), deep ensembles for epistemic uncertainty (Lakshminarayanan et al.), and inverse-dynamics pseudo-labeling from unlabeled video (Chang et al.; Zhang et al.)—into a unified continual learning pipeline. The key departure from prior continual learning work (e.g., Mirza et al., Kim et al., Bang et al.) is the removal of the ground-truth label assumption: instead of streaming labeled data, the system processes a single pass over unlabeled video, generating pseudo-labels via an ensemble of inverse dynamics models and then purifying them through temporal consistency smoothing and adaptive filtering. Unlike prior scalable driving approaches (SelfD, VPT) that repeatedly revisit an entire stored dataset, ∞-Driver processes each frame once and maintains a fixed-size uncertainty-guided replay buffer, making it memory-scalable to arbitrarily long streams.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practical bottleneck in autonomous driving: the inability to continually adapt policies from the vast, unlabeled, and unconstrained video data available on the web without prohibitive storage or repeated passes. By demonstrating consistent gains over strong continual learning baselines (DER, Rainbow, PuriDivER) across both cross-city and 10-million-image web-scale settings, the paper validates that uncertainty-guided memory with pseudo-label purification is a viable recipe for never-ending driving policy training. Open questions remain around closed-loop performance at this scale, the quality ceiling imposed by the inverse dynamics teacher, and whether the approach generalizes to more complex multi-agent or long-horizon planning tasks. The authors note the system has not yet plateaued after 10,000+ miles of video, suggesting further gains are possible with larger architectures and more data.
