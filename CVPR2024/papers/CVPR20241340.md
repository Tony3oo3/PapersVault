## Problem

Existing video generation metrics (FVD, FID, IS, CLIPSIM) reduce model quality to a single number and are inconsistent with human judgment, while VQA methods target real videos and ignore generative artifacts. There is no comprehensive, fine-grained, and human-aligned benchmark that decomposes video generation quality into disentangled dimensions to reveal individual model strengths and weaknesses.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241340/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes 2-second generated video clips (sampled at 8 FPS) paired with their conditioning text prompts as input. It produces a 16-dimensional score vector (e.g., subject consistency, motion smoothness, spatial relationship), per-category scores across 8 content types (Animal, Architecture, Food, Human, etc.), and human preference win ratios used to compute Spearman correlation coefficients validating alignment. Each dimension's score is computed by a dedicated pre-trained model (DINO, CLIP, RAFT, MUSIQ, GRiT, ViCLIP, etc.) rather than a single unified metric.

## Method

VBench decomposes 'video generation quality' hierarchically into two top-level axes—Video Quality (temporal consistency, frame-wise aesthetics/imaging) and Video-Condition Consistency (semantics, style, overall text-video alignment)—further subdivided into 16 disentangled dimensions, each with a tailored evaluation pipeline using a specific pre-trained model. For each dimension, ~100 carefully curated prompts probe the targeted ability (e.g., movable subjects for Subject Consistency, Kinetics-400 actions for Human Action), and 800 human-curated prompts are distributed across 8 content categories. Human alignment is validated via large-scale pairwise preference annotations (4 models × 5 groups × 6 pairs per prompt), showing high Spearman correlation between VBench scores and human win ratios across all 16 dimensions. The annotations are further used for instruction tuning of VideoChat to improve VLM-based evaluation alignment.

## Results

Across 16 VBench dimensions, no single model dominates all aspects. LaVie leads in temporal quality (Subject Consistency 91.41%, Background Consistency 97.47%, Temporal Flickering 98.30%, Motion Smoothness 96.38%) and Object Class (91.82%), while CogVideo achieves the highest Subject Consistency (92.19%) but trails in nearly all other dimensions. VideoCrafter stands out with the highest Dynamic Degree (89.72%) and Spatial Relationship (36.74%), yet scores lowest on temporal consistency metrics. A clear trade-off emerges: models strong in temporal consistency (LaVie, CogVideo) tend to have low Dynamic Degree (49.72% and 42.22%), while VideoCrafter's high dynamics come at the cost of temporal coherence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the four T2V models, LaVie [91] achieves the best overall temporal quality (Background Consistency 97.47%, Temporal Flickering 98.30%) and Object Class (91.82%), but its Dynamic Degree (49.72%) is well below VideoCrafter [29] (89.72%), illustrating the temporal-consistency-vs-dynamics trade-off. ModelScope [62,87] leads in Multiple Objects (38.98%) and is competitive across most dimensions, while CogVideo [34] achieves the highest Subject Consistency (92.19%) but underperforms in nearly every other dimension, scoring only 7.70% on Overall Consistency versus 25–26% for the others. In the T2V-vs-T2I comparison (Figure 6a), all four video models significantly underperform SDXL [71] on Multiple Objects and Spatial Relationship, indicating a compositionality gap between current video and image generation. Stable Diffusion 1.4/2.1 [74] also outperforms T2V models on frame-wise dimensions like Aesthetic Quality and Imaging Quality, confirming that T2V models have not yet fully inherited the per-frame generation capability of state-of-the-art T2I models. The Empirical Max baselines (from WebVid-10M [1] real videos) show that even the best T2V models remain far from real-video quality on dimensions like Scene (best 52.69% vs. 82.22% max) and Appearance Style (best 23.56% vs. 28.55% max).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the single-valued evaluation paradigm (FVD, FID, IS, CLIPSIM) that has dominated video generation assessment, arguing that these metrics neither align with human perception nor reveal model-specific strengths and weaknesses. It repurposes a suite of existing off-the-shelf tools (CLIP, DINO, RAFT, MUSIQ, GRiT, ViCLIP, Tag2Text, UMT) into a unified 16-dimensional hierarchical framework, drawing conceptual inspiration from T2I compositional benchmarks (T2I-CompBench, HRS-Bench) but extending the evaluation to the temporal and motion dimensions unique to video. Unlike prior VQA methods designed for real-world video artifacts, VBench explicitly targets generative-model-specific failure modes such as subject identity drift and temporal flickering.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

VBench provides the first comprehensive, human-aligned, multi-dimensional evaluation standard for video generation, giving the community a diagnostic tool to identify specific model weaknesses (e.g., compositionality, dynamic motion) rather than a single opaque score. The revealed trade-off between temporal consistency and dynamic degree, and the significant T2V-versus-T2I gap in spatial composition, point to concrete architectural and data strategies for future models. Open questions include extending the benchmark to image-to-video and video-to-video tasks, incorporating safety and fairness dimensions, and whether the 16-dimension decomposition generalizes as models improve. The finding that data quality (Food category, 11% of WebVid-10M) can outperform data quantity (Human category, 26%) suggests curation strategies may matter more than scale for complex categories.
