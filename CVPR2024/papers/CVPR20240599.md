## Problem

Text-based image editing (TBIE) requires simultaneously preserving the source image's visual fidelity and applying a meaningful semantic change described by a text prompt, but existing single-image fine-tuning methods overfit to the source image during reconstruction, destroying the generative model's ability to comprehend new prompts. The paper formalizes TBIE as a counterfactual inference problem, identifying this editability-fidelity trade-off as the fundamental challenge, and shows that no existing method achieves a good balance across diverse editing types (addition, removal, manipulation, replacement, style transfer, face manipulation).

## Input / Output

The method takes a single source RGB image I and a text prompt decomposed into two subprompts: P describing the current image content and P' describing the desired post-edit content (e.g., P='a castle', P'='a castle covered by snow'). It produces an edited image I' at the same resolution as the input, generated via 30-step DDIM sampling in the latent space of Stable Diffusion V2.1-Base. The entire pipeline is zero-shot in the sense that no ground-truth edited image or spatial mask is required—only the source image and the (P, P') pair drive the edit.

## Method

DAC performs two sequential abductions on a frozen Stable Diffusion model. Abduction-1 parameterizes an exogenous variable U as a UNet LoRA (rank 512, applied to attention, convolution, and FFN layers) and fine-tunes it for 1,000 iterations to reconstruct the source image from prompt P, encoding all image-specific details. Abduction-2 then parameterizes a second exogenous variable Δ as a CLIP text encoder LoRA (rank 4, applied only to self-attention layers) and fine-tunes it to reconstruct I from the *target* prompt P' while U is frozen, with an annealing schedule (γ) that progressively reduces U's influence at larger diffusion time steps to prevent Δ from collapsing to zero. The key insight is that Δ exclusively encodes the semantic transition from P' back to P; by setting Δ' = −Δ (the action step), the prediction step I' = G(P', U, Δ') effectively subtracts the overfitting-induced rigidity and recovers editability, producing the edited image via standard DDIM sampling.

## Results

In a user study with 110 AMT participants evaluating 54 image-prompt pairs across six editing types (5,940 total responses), 75.3% of evaluators preferred DAC's edited images over those from SINE, DDS, and Imagic. On CLIP-score (text alignment), DAC achieves the best scores for object removal, manipulation, addition, face manipulation, and style transfer, while matching DDS on object replacement. In terms of computation, DAC takes 15 minutes per image (6 min for Abduction-1, 9 min for Abduction-2, 4 s for 30-step DDIM sampling), compared to 120 min for SINE, 12 min for Imagic, and 0.33 min for DDS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DAC is compared against three single-image fine-tuning baselines: SINE [43], DDS [10], and Imagic [14]. In the user study, DAC is preferred by 75.3% of evaluators, clearly outperforming all three baselines. On CLIP-score (text alignment), DAC wins on object removal, manipulation, addition, face manipulation, and style transfer; it ties with DDS on object replacement. The paper argues that LPIPS (image alignment) is a misleading fidelity metric here because methods like DDS and SINE that fail to perform the requested edit (e.g., not removing a hat) retain high LPIPS simply by making no changes, while DAC achieves both higher CLIP-score and true visual fidelity. In terms of speed, DAC (15 min) is slower than DDS (0.33 min) and Imagic (12 min) but substantially faster than SINE (120 min), with the overhead coming from two separate 1,000-iteration LoRA optimization phases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Pearl's counterfactual inference framework [22] and applies it to text-based image editing via Stable Diffusion, positioning TBIE as a three-step abduction-action-prediction problem. It departs from prior single-image fine-tuning methods (PTI, NTI, SINE) that model only the source-image exogenous variable U, and from inversion-based methods (P2P, PnP, MasaCtrl) that model only the semantic-change variable Δ, by explicitly separating and parameterizing both: U as a UNet LoRA and Δ as a CLIP text encoder LoRA. The key methodological departure from Imagic and DDS, which learn U and Δ jointly, is the sequential doubly-abductive design where the sign-flip of Δ serves as the 'action' step, decoupling fidelity encoding from editability recovery.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The counterfactual framing provides the first principled explanation for why TBIE is hard: the editability-fidelity trade-off is an inherent consequence of overfitting in single-image reconstruction, not a bug to be patched. This reframing suggests that any effective TBIE method must perform some form of abduction, which the authors argue explains why fast non-fine-tuning methods (Emu2, InfEdit) underperform. However, the 15-minute-per-image cost (6+9 min for two abductions) severely limits practical adoption, and the authors acknowledge the absence of a non-fine-tuning-based abduction as a critical open problem. Future directions include extending to visual example-based editing and integrating fast diffusion or consistency models to reduce inference time.
