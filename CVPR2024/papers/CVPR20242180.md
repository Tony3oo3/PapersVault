## Problem

Existing surveillance video datasets provide only predefined category labels for anomalous events, limiting methods to detecting and classifying fixed event types without semantic understanding of video content. No multimodal surveillance video dataset with sentence-level language descriptions and fine-grained temporal annotations existed, preventing the study of tasks like temporal sentence grounding, video captioning, and dense video captioning in the surveillance domain.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242180/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The UCA dataset contains 1,854 real-world surveillance videos (121.9 hours total, 110.7 hours annotated) with 23,542 sentence-level queries averaging 20.15 words each, annotated at 0.1-second temporal resolution. For TSGV, the input is a full surveillance video plus a natural-language query, and the output is a start-end time pair; for VC and DVC, the input is a video clip or untrimmed video, and the output is a caption or a sequence of timestamped event descriptions; for MAD, the input is a surveillance video processed through both visual and caption feature extractors, producing a per-frame anomaly score aggregated into a micro-averaged AUC.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is the construction of UCA through fine-grained manual annotation of UCF-Crime: 10 annotators with a 3-person review team labeled every describable event with a detailed sentence and 0.1-second start/end timestamps over approximately two months. The paper then benchmarks 17 SOTA methods (spanning sliding-window, anchor-based, reinforcement learning, 2D temporal adjacency, metric learning, diffusion, and Transformer architectures) across four multimodal tasks, revealing that all perform substantially worse on surveillance video than on conventional datasets due to long video durations, low visual quality, and domain-specific abnormal behaviors. For multimodal anomaly detection, the authors propose Enhanced TEVAD, which augments the original TEVAD framework by adding a Surveillance SwinBERT (SwinBERT retrained on UCA) as a plug-and-play caption feature branch alongside the General SwinBERT (pretrained on VATEX), improving AUC from 84.9% to 85.3% and demonstrating that domain-specific captioning captures anomalous semantics missed by general-purpose models.

## Results

On the UCA dataset, all TSGV baselines achieve R@1 below 10% at IoU=0.3, with MMN leading at 8.68% (R@5=21.38) and 2D-TAN close behind at 7.76% (R@5=19.67), underscoring the difficulty of temporal grounding in surveillance video. For video captioning, CoCap achieves the highest CIDEr (21.24) and ROUGE-L (28.14), while SwinBERT attains the best CIDEr (25.29) and ROUGE-L (27.16) among Transformer-based models. For multimodal anomaly detection, the paper's Enhanced TEVAD (using a UCA-trained SwinBERT) reaches 85.3% AUC, outperforming the original TEVAD (84.9%) and visual-only detection (83.1%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

For TSGV, the 2D temporal adjacency network family (2D-TAN, MMN) clearly outperforms sliding-window (CTRL), anchor-based (SCDM), reinforcement-learning (A2C), regression (LGI), and diffusion-based (MomentDiff) approaches, with MMN achieving the best R@1 across all IoU thresholds; A2C is the weakest at 4.25% R@1 (IoU=0.3). For video captioning, the Transformer-based models CoCap and SwinBERT dominate on CIDEr (21.24 and 25.29 respectively), while SGN leads on BLEU-1 (29.17) thanks to its semantic-group design; older RNN-based methods (S2VT, RecNet, MARN) trail by 3–5 points on CIDEr. For dense video captioning, PDVC consistently surpasses TDA-CG in both localization recall (e.g., 49.53 vs 31.72 at IoU=0.3 with I3D) and caption quality (BLEU-1: 8.02 vs 6.35), and with ground-truth proposals PDVC edges out UEDVC on CIDEr (22.65 vs 18.83 with I3D). For multimodal anomaly detection, the paper's Enhanced TEVAD (adding a UCA-trained Surveillance SwinBERT as a plug-and-play branch) improves AUC from 84.9% (TEVAD with general-domain SwinBERT) to 85.3%, demonstrating that domain-specific captioning features provide a measurable but modest gain over general-domain features.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the UCF-Crime surveillance anomaly detection dataset by adding fine-grained sentence-level language annotations and event timing, creating the first multimodal surveillance video dataset (UCA). It builds on TEVAD's multimodal anomaly detection framework by introducing a domain-specific SwinBERT captioning module trained on UCA as a plug-and-play enhancement. Conceptually, the paper bridges two previously separate research threads: surveillance video analysis (which relied solely on predefined category labels) and general-domain video-language understanding (TSGV, VC, DVC), arguing that the unique characteristics of surveillance footage—long durations, low resolution, complex backgrounds, and abnormal behaviors—create a distinct learning challenge not addressed by either line of work alone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The UCA dataset fills a clear gap: no prior multimodal video dataset targeted the surveillance domain, and no prior surveillance dataset contained sentence-level annotations. The empirical finding that SOTA models from conventional video-language benchmarks (e.g., ActivityNet, DiDeMo) degrade substantially on surveillance video (TSGV R@1 below 10% at IoU=0.3) quantifies the domain gap and motivates domain-specific model design. However, the dataset remains relatively small (1,854 videos, 23,542 sentences) compared to general-domain counterparts, and the MAD improvement (83.1%→85.3% AUC) is modest, leaving open whether the domain-specific captioning benefit scales with larger surveillance corpora or more diverse anomaly types.
