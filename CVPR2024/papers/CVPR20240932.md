## Problem

MAC, a state-of-the-art 3D point cloud registration method based on maximal clique search, suffers from exponential complexity in the clique search stage, making it too slow (over 1 second per registration) for real-time applications like SLAM. The correspondence graph used by MAC had not been studied from a signal-processing perspective, and no principled graph-downsampling strategy existed that could reduce graph size while preserving maximal clique registration performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240932/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of 3D correspondences (pairs of matched source-target points) generated by any descriptor (FPFH, FCGF, Predator, SpinNet, CoFiNet, GeoTransformer) and produces a relative 6-DoF pose (rotation + translation). Correspondences are not treated as 3D points but as vertices in a weighted compatibility graph; the method samples a subset of these vertices and feeds them into the standard MAC pipeline. The sampling ratio is a tunable hyperparameter (tested from 1% to 100%), with 20% achieving real-time speed (<35 ms) and 5% yielding ~80× acceleration over vanilla MAC.

## Method

FastMAC introduces graph signal processing to the correspondence graph by defining a generalized degree signal (sum of weighted edges per node) and passing it through a Laplacian high-pass graph filter to extract high-frequency components. Nodes with large filter response are identified as high-frequency nodes, which the paper shows are structurally important for maximal clique search: every clique must contain at least one, cliques tend to contain three or more, and cut-points (always high-frequency) appear in multiple cliques. Rather than using the optimal but SVD-heavy deterministic sampling operator, FastMAC uses the per-node response magnitude as a probability distribution for stochastic sampling, achieving O(M+N) complexity instead of O(MN³+M³N). The sampled correspondences are then fed into the unchanged MAC pipeline (maximal clique search, node-guided clique selection, pose estimation), making the sampling module a drop-in replacement between graph construction and clique search.

## Results

On KITTI with FCGF descriptor, FastMAC@20 (real-time) achieves 97.48% registration recall (RR) at 0.028 s per registration, matching or slightly exceeding MAC's 97.25% RR while running ~20× faster (0.570 s). At 5% sampling, the paper reports an 80× speedup over MAC with minimal performance drop. On 3DMatch (FCGF), FastMAC@20 reaches 92.30% RR at 0.038 s versus MAC's 93.72% at 0.914 s (~24× speedup). On the challenging low-overlap 3DLoMatch benchmark, FastMAC@20 achieves 55.25% RR at 0.051 s compared to MAC's 59.85% at 1.181 s.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FastMAC@20 outperforms all ten baselines on KITTI (FCGF) in registration recall (97.48% vs. best baseline SC2-PCR at 97.12%) while being 30× faster than SC2-PCR (0.028 s vs. 0.850 s) and 1875× faster than RANSAC-4M (52.40 s). Against MAC itself, FastMAC@20 achieves comparable RR (97.48% vs. 97.25%) with a 20× speedup, and FastMAC@50 even slightly exceeds MAC in RR (97.84% vs. 97.25%). On 3DMatch, FastMAC@20 trails MAC by 1.4% RR (92.30% vs. 93.72%) but runs 24× faster; it still beats TEASER++ (85.77%), DGR (88.85%), and RANSAC-4M (91.44%) in RR. On the low-overlap 3DLoMatch benchmark, FastMAC@20 drops to 55.25% RR (vs. MAC's 59.85%), a 4.6% gap, yet remains 23× faster and still surpasses TEASER++ (46.76%), DGR (43.80%), and PointDSC (56.20% is close). The consistent pattern across all three datasets is that FastMAC trades a small RR loss (≤4.6%) for an order-of-magnitude speedup, making it the only method achieving real-time (<50 ms) registration while maintaining >90% RR on KITTI and 3DMatch.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on MAC (Maximal Clique registration) [71], which searches for maximal cliques in a correspondence graph to estimate relative poses, and introduces graph signal processing [10, 11] into the correspondence graph domain for the first time. While prior consistency-graph-based registration methods [22, 40, 33, 68] have explored more efficient clique search algorithms and relaxations, and one earlier work [50] considered sampling correspondences, none framed the down-sampling problem through the lens of spectral graph filtering. The key departure from deterministic graph sampling theory [10] is the replacement of iterative SVD-based greedy selection with a constant-time stochastic strategy whose optimality is inherited from prior stochastic recovery results [11].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By reducing MAC's runtime from over 1 second to under 35 ms at 20% sampling while preserving registration recall, FastMAC makes maximal-clique-based registration viable for real-time applications such as SLAM, which was previously infeasible. The method's robustness across six different descriptors (FPFH through GeoTransformer) and three benchmarks (KITTI, 3DMatch, 3DLoMatch) suggests broad practical applicability. A noted limitation is that graph construction itself becomes the bottleneck at very low sampling ratios, and the authors identify learning a graph prior without explicit graph construction as a natural next step. An open question is whether the spectral-sampling principle generalizes beyond the MAC pipeline to other graph-based registration or matching frameworks.
