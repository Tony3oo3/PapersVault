## Problem

As AI-generated images flood the internet, future computer vision models will inevitably train on data containing large fractions of synthetic images. While generative models are known to produce biased outputs and existing datasets contain demographic imbalances, it remains unclear whether this creates a detrimental feedback loop that amplifies social bias in downstream models, or whether the interaction between pre-existing dataset biases and generation artifacts produces more complex, non-monotonic effects.

## Input / Output

The method takes original image-text pairs from COCO (~120K images) and CC3M (~2.8M valid images), uses the existing text captions as prompts for Stable Diffusion v1.5 to generate replacement images, and constructs contaminated datasets D(α) at six contamination levels (α = 0.0 to 1.0 in steps of 0.2). The same captions serve dual roles: as generation prompts and as the text side of training pairs, ensuring the only variable is the image source. Trained models (OpenCLIP, ClipCap, Transformer) are evaluated on bias metrics spanning four demographic attributes (gender, ethnicity, age, skin tone) and caption quality scores, with evaluation conducted on held-out sets including FairFace, PHASE-annotated CC3M, and COCO validation splits.

## Method

The paper simulates progressive dataset contamination by generating images with Stable Diffusion v1.5 from original captions and replacing α-fraction of real images in COCO and CC3M, then training OpenCLIP (contrastive image-text pretraining) and two captioning models (ClipCap with CLIP prefix, and a standard Transformer decoder) on each contaminated variant. Bias is measured through three complementary lenses for pretraining — text-to-image retrieval R@k gaps, self-similarity in the embedding space, and person preference scores — and through LIC (leakage of protected attributes in captions) and gender misprediction error for captioning. The key finding is that bias does not uniformly amplify: some attributes show consistent mitigation (e.g., gender gap narrowing), others show amplification (e.g., age retrieval gap widening, skin-tone LIC in ClipCap), and many show no clear trend. The authors attribute this to two interacting factors: alignment between Stable Diffusion's biases and the pre-existing imbalances in the original datasets (which can mask amplification), and generation artifacts such as blurred faces that prevent models from learning demographic features.

## Results

On image captioning (Table 1), caption quality degrades significantly only at full contamination (α=1.0): ClipCap's BLEU-4 drops from 31.9 to 23.8 and CIDEr from 105.0 to 84.6, while Transformer's BLEU-4 falls from 28.3 to 21.0. In contrast, CLIPScore remains stable or slightly increases (ClipCap: 76.4→78.3; Transformer: 72.8→72.9), suggesting semantic alignment is preserved. For bias, ClipCap's LIC-Skin nearly triples from 1.1 (α=0) to 3.1 (α=1.0), indicating skin-tone bias amplification, while LIC-Gender and gender misprediction error show no consistent trend across contamination levels. For OpenCLIP image retrieval, R@k scores remain essentially unchanged across all α values on both COCO 2014 and Flickr30K test sets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, it compares the effect of progressively replacing real images with Stable Diffusion-generated images (α = 0.0 to 1.0) on the same model architectures. The original dataset (α=0) serves as the reference baseline. For OpenCLIP, retrieval performance (R@1, R@5, R@10) is virtually identical across all contamination levels, indicating that generated images do not degrade or improve retrieval quality at this scale. For image captioning, lexical metrics (BLEU-4, CIDEr, SPICE) degrade gradually from α=0 to 0.8 but drop sharply at α=1.0, suggesting a small fraction of real images is necessary for captioning quality. The most notable bias finding is that ClipCap's LIC-Skin increases from 1.1 to 3.1 at full contamination, while Transformer's LIC-Skin remains stable around 2.2, indicating that the interaction between the captioning architecture and generated data determines whether skin-tone bias is amplified. Gender-related bias metrics (LIC-Gender, gender misprediction error) show no consistent amplification or mitigation trend across either model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the dataset contamination framework introduced by Hataya et al. [17], who showed that synthetic data can corrupt future training sets, by specifically investigating whether such contamination amplifies social bias in vision-language models. It leverages established bias evaluation methodologies—self-similarity and person preference from Wolfe et al. [58], demographic retrieval gaps from Garcia et al. [15], and the LIC metric from Hirota et al. [20]—and applies them to OpenCLIP and image captioning models trained on progressively contaminated COCO and CC3M datasets. The generative component relies on Stable Diffusion v1.5 [40], and the broader motivation draws on Taori et al. [53]'s data feedback loop analysis, but the paper's specific contribution is the systematic multi-attribute, multi-task bias evaluation under controlled contamination ratios.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that generated images do not uniformly amplify bias—sometimes even mitigating it—challenges the straightforward 'biased generator → biased downstream model' narrative and suggests that interactions between pre-existing dataset imbalances and generation artifacts (e.g., blurred faces) play a critical role. However, the study is limited to ~3 million images (roughly 130× less than CLIP's training set), a single generative model, and four demographic attributes without intersectional analysis, leaving open whether these findings hold at scale or with more capable/biased generators. The practical implication is that simply filtering AI-generated images from training data may be insufficient; understanding the specific bias-alignment and artifact mechanisms is necessary for responsible data curation as synthetic content proliferates online.
