## Problem

Existing post-training backdoor defense methods require certified clean data to repair models before deployment, but in realistic test-time scenarios defenders only have access to partially poisoned data (a mix of clean and backdoor-poisoned samples). The paper asks whether a backdoor can be removed using only a small batch of such unlabeled, partially poisoned test-time images, without any clean data for fine-tuning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241529/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single batch of 100 unlabeled RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 for Tiny-ImageNet) with a known poisoning rate (default 10%) and a pre-trained backdoored CNN (PreAct-ResNet18, VGG19, or DenseNet161). Since the samples are unlabeled, the original model's own predictions serve as pseudo-labels for Shapley estimation. The output is a structurally pruned model (a small fraction of neurons removed) that retains high clean accuracy while reducing ASR to near zero, with no fine-tuning step required.

## Method

TTBD is a two-stage framework. In Stage 1, the proposed DDP detector identifies poisoned samples by computing a Prediction Change Score (PCS): neurons with the highest activation on each candidate sample are temporarily pruned, and samples whose predictions change most across the batch are flagged as poisoned (top-6 selected). In Stage 2, Monte-Carlo Shapley estimation (40 random neuron permutations with early stopping) scores each neuron's marginal contribution to both ASR (computed on detected poisoned samples) and ACC (computed on the full batch). The final pruning criterion selects neurons that simultaneously have top-k ASR Shapley value and bottom-m ACC *absolute* Shapley value, which isolates backdoor-specific neurons while protecting neurons important for clean classification. This dual-criterion design tolerates imperfect poisoned-sample detection, since a few misclassified clean samples in the ASR set are counterbalanced by the ACC constraint.

## Results

On PreAct-ResNet18 with CIFAR-10, TTBD-DDP reduces the average ASR from 88.18% to 2.32% while maintaining 89.67% clean accuracy (a 2.64% drop from the original 92.31%). TTBD-TeCo achieves an even lower average ASR of 1.55% at 89.35% ACC. On VGG19 with CIFAR-10, TTBD-DDP reduces average ASR from 85.40% to 2.64% with 87.57% ACC, while TTBD-TeCo struggles (average ASR 65.59%) due to its architecture sensitivity. On Tiny-ImageNet with PreAct-ResNet18, TTBD-DDP achieves an average ASR of 2.11% with 50.12% ACC, outperforming all baselines on ASR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TTBD-DDP decisively outperforms all three baselines on ASR reduction: it achieves an average ASR of 2.32% versus 66.09% for Fine Pruning (FP), 12.65% for Adversarial Neuron Pruning (ANP), and 15.49% for DBD on PreAct-ResNet18/CIFAR-10. FP essentially fails to remove the backdoor (ASR remains above 50% for most attacks), confirming that neuron activation alone is an unreliable indicator of poisoned neurons. ANP achieves moderate ASR reduction but at the cost of a larger accuracy drop (87.82% vs. 89.67% for TTBD-DDP), and it cannot be applied to VGG19 at all due to the absence of batch normalization layers. DBD, a training-stage method with access to the full poisoned dataset, removes the backdoor reasonably (ASR 15.49%) but suffers a catastrophic accuracy collapse to 75.93%, a 16.4% drop compared to TTBD-DDP's 2.64% drop. TTBD-TeCo achieves the lowest average ASR (1.55%) but is unreliable on VGG19 (average ASR 65.59%), whereas TTBD-DDP maintains consistent performance across architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the post-training backdoor defense paradigm (Fine-pruning, ANP, DBD) by removing the requirement for clean training data, instead operating on a single batch of partially poisoned test-time images. It builds on Neuron Shapley (Ghorbani & Zou, ICLR 2020) for neuron importance estimation and TeCo (Liu et al., CVPR 2023) for inference-stage poisoned sample detection, but introduces a novel DDP detection method that uses prediction-change scores during pruning to overcome TeCo's architecture sensitivity. Conceptually, the work bridges test-time adaptation (TTA) literature with backdoor defense, repurposing the TTA idea of adapting models using only test-time data to the security setting where that data may itself be partially poisoned.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a realistic MLaaS scenario where defenders lack access to clean training data and can only observe test-time inputs that may be partially poisoned by an active attacker. The main limitation is the non-trivial accuracy cost (~2–3% on CIFAR10, larger on Tiny-ImageNet) due to the absence of clean-data fine-tuning, and the method's effectiveness is tightly coupled to the accuracy of poisoned sample detection, which remains imperfect. Open questions include whether the accuracy gap can be closed with even a small amount of clean data, whether DDP detection generalizes to more complex architectures or multi-target attacks, and whether the Shapley-based pruning strategy scales to larger models with millions of parameters.
