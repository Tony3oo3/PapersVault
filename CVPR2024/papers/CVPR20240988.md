## Problem

Discovering which instances in a dataset are prototypical (most representative) without labels remains an open problem. Existing metrics for prototypicality—gradient magnitude, adversarial robustness, model confidence—depend heavily on training setup or attack specifics, and no prior method learns features in which prototypicality is inherently encoded by the geometry of the representation space itself.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240988/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes unlabeled RGB images (MNIST 28×28, CIFAR-10 32×32, USA10kF face photos) and produces 2D hyperbolic feature embeddings in the Poincaré ball, where the radial distance from the origin encodes prototypicality (closer = more typical) and the angular position encodes semantic similarity and diversity. A standard CNN encoder (LeNet, ResNet-20, or ResNet-18) with a fully connected head projects features to 2D Euclidean space, which is then mapped into the Poincaré ball via the exponential map. The method also outputs a one-to-one assignment of each image to a uniformly packed particle, from which the hierarchical level of each sample is directly read off.

## Method

HACK (Hyperbolic space with sphere pACKing) operates in two stages. First, it generates n uniformly packed particles in the Poincaré ball by minimizing a repulsion loss (derived from the hyperbolic area per point) plus a boundary loss, creating a fixed geometric scaffold that is maximally evenly spread. Second, it trains an image encoder by alternately (a) solving a bipartite matching problem with the Hungarian algorithm to assign each image in a batch to the nearest unassigned particle in hyperbolic distance, and (b) updating encoder parameters to minimize the total hyperbolic distance between image features and their assigned particles. The key insight is that in hyperbolic space, an image similar to many neighbors is best placed near the origin (the root of the implicit tree), while atypical images are pushed toward the boundary—so prototypicality emerges automatically from the metric learning objective without any explicit prototypicality loss. This paradigm sits between supervised learning (fixed targets, fixed assignment) and standard unsupervised metric learning (no predefined geometry), using known target locations but learning the image-to-target assignment.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, training with the most atypical 10% of images (selected by HACK's hyperbolic norm) achieves 22.67% higher accuracy than training with the most typical 10%. For adversarial robustness, removing the most atypical 1% of examples improves FGSM adversarial accuracy by 8.7% on MNIST and 7.3% on CIFAR-10, while clean accuracy remains unchanged. On MNIST, HACK outperforms UHML by 10.2% when incorporating 10% atypical samples and by an additional 5.7% when excluding 1% atypical examples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Carlini et al.'s [5] prototypicality metrics, HACK and Model Confidence (MC) both successfully identify typical and atypical images, but Holdout Retraining (HR) fails due to the high variance introduced by different training setups. HACK's advantage over MC is that it defines prototypicality as distance to other samples (more aligned with human intuition) and additionally organizes examples by semantic similarity in the angular direction. Against UHML [46], the prior unsupervised hyperbolic metric learning method, HACK achieves a 10.2% accuracy improvement on MNIST when selecting 10% atypical samples for training, and a further 5.7% gain when excluding 1% atypical examples for robustness. UHML does not explicitly encode prototypicality in the feature norm, making it less suitable for the instance-selection tasks that HACK targets. Overall, HACK wins on all quantitative comparisons reported, though the baselines are limited to two methods and the evaluation is primarily on small-scale datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10).

## Relation to Prior Work

HACK repurposes the Poincaré ball model from hyperbolic representation learning (Nickel & Kiela) and the sphere-packing / uniform-distribution idea from contrastive representation learning (Wang & Isola) into a new unsupervised feature-learning paradigm. It departs from prior prototypicality metrics (Carlini et al., adversarial-robustness criteria) by making prototypicality an emergent geometric property of the learned embedding rather than a post-hoc score, and from UHML by replacing free metric learning with fixed, uniformly packed target particles and a Hungarian-matching assignment. The congealing procedure (Miller et al.) is borrowed solely as a validation tool to ground the notion of 'typical' in pixel space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a clean, label-free mechanism for separating prototypical from atypical examples, with immediate practical payoffs in sample-efficiency and adversarial robustness. However, all experiments are confined to MNIST, CIFAR-10, and 10k faces, leaving open whether the sphere-packing + Hungarian-matching pipeline scales to large-scale datasets (ImageNet, web-scale) or to non-image modalities. The O(n³) per-batch Hungarian step and the need to run HACK per class also raise questions about computational feasibility at scale. A key open question is whether the emergent prototypicality signal remains informative when the dataset contains multiple well-separated clusters rather than a single class.
