## Problem

Existing scene recovery methods face a fundamental trade-off: physical models depend on assumptions about attenuation coefficients and scene depth that break down across diverse weather conditions, while deep learning methods are constrained by the scarcity of large-scale, diverse, aligned paired data and incur high computational costs. This paper asks whether transmission can be estimated without either physical parameter assumptions or learned parameters, by exploiting a measurable image statistic that correlates with transmission across all adverse conditions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241807/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single degraded RGB image at any resolution (tested from 360p to 4K) and produces a restored clear image. It is entirely non-parametric with no learnable weights, requiring only two fixed hyperparameters (k=0.5 and sliding window size Ω=7). The atmospheric light A is estimated on-the-fly via a two-step mean-filtering and regional-maximum procedure, so no external depth map, paired clean image, or pre-trained model is needed.

## Method

The core insight is that the rate of change in luminous flux (ΔF) within a local sliding window is a strong proxy for transmission t, validated via Kullback-Leibler divergence across 90,960 images spanning haze, sandstorm, and underwater conditions. Starting from the Bouguer-Lambert law (F = F̃·e^(−βd)) and a Retinex-inspired division to eliminate incident light, the authors analytically derive t ≈ 1 + k·log(F)·ΔF, which eliminates both scene depth d and attenuation coefficient β. In practice, F is computed as the mean intensity over a sliding window divided by window area, and ΔF is the min/max ratio of F within that window (with truncation to [0.005, 0.95] for noise robustness). The final recovery uses the standard atmospheric scattering model J(x) = (I(x) + A·ω(t(x)−1)) / max(ω·t(x), t₀) with a guided filter for transmission smoothing, yielding O(N log N) total complexity and sub-millisecond GPU inference at 4K.

## Results

On the dehazing benchmarks, LFP achieves the best PSNR and SSIM across all four datasets: 19.76/0.869 on RESIDE-IN, 21.05/0.861 on RESIDE-OUT, 18.29/0.873 on Haze-4K, and 18.24/0.786 on RS-Haze, outperforming the strongest prior-based baseline (DCP) by 3.1–4.3 dB in PSNR and 0.03–0.11 in SSIM. In runtime, LFP processes a 4K image in 0.863 s on CPU and 0.019 s on GPU, which is 7–50× faster than competing methods (e.g., BCCR takes 64.26 s on CPU, FFA takes 30.61 s on GPU at 4K). The method requires no training and only two hyperparameters (k=0.5, window Ω=7).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LFP outperforms all three prior-based dehazing baselines (DCP, BCCR, FVR) on every PSNR and SSIM metric across all four datasets, with the largest margin over FVR (up to +6.4 dB PSNR on RESIDE-OUT). Among deep learning methods, LFP is not directly compared on PSNR/SSIM (the authors justify this by noting LFP is non-parametric), but on runtime it is 7–34× faster than the fastest DL baseline (gUNet-T: 0.197 s vs 0.001 s at 360p on GPU) and up to 1600× faster than the slowest (FFA: 1.675 s at 360p). In qualitative comparisons, LFP surpasses DCP, HRDCP, and CVC on sandstorm images (SIRB) by avoiding color distortion and dim skies; it outperforms UWCNN, Waternet, and ROP on underwater images (UIEBD) by eliminating green-channel bias and preserving contrast; and it beats MSCNN, gUNet, and DCP on haze (RESIDE) by restoring sky detail without halo artifacts. The closest quantitative competitor is BCCR on RS-Haze (18.84 vs 18.24 PSNR), where BCCR actually edges out LFP by 0.6 dB, though LFP still leads on SSIM (0.786 vs 0.747). Overall, LFP wins or ties on all reported metrics and is consistently the fastest method tested.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the classical atmospheric scattering model and the Bouguer-Lambert law for luminous flux, combined with the Retinex division principle to eliminate incident light. It departs from both prior physical-model approaches (DCP, BCCR, FVR, CAP, ROP) that rely on hand-crafted priors over depth or attenuation, and from data-driven deep learning methods that require large paired datasets, by deriving a transmission estimate that eliminates both scene depth d and attenuation coefficient β entirely. The key conceptual move is replacing the intractable estimation of t(x) = e^{-βd(x)} with a computation over the local rate of change of luminous flux ΔF, validated statistically via Kullback-Leibler divergence against ground-truth transmission maps.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in achieving O(N log N) real-time scene recovery with only two hyperparameters (k, Ω) and no training, making it deployable on resource-constrained devices across haze, sandstorm, and underwater conditions without retraining. However, the theoretical derivation (particularly the 'integration' step in Eq. 9 and the informal simplification from Eq. 11 to Eq. 12) lacks rigor, and the quantitative evaluation compares against only three prior-based baselines rather than the full suite of deep learning methods shown qualitatively. Open questions include whether the luminous-flux prior generalizes to more complex degradation models (e.g., mixed weather, dynamic scenes) and whether the heuristic atmospheric-light estimation can be made more principled.
