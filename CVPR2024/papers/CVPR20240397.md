## Problem

Existing imitation learning-based traffic simulators fail to produce stable long-term simulations (beyond ~20 seconds) due to covariate shift in multi-agent settings: behavior cloning diverges from the expert state distribution, while adversarial IRL methods (GAIL, AIRL) suffer from training instability and sample inefficiency in dynamic multi-agent environments. The paper addresses this gap without requiring costly expert supervision or unstable min-max/RL optimization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240397/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a multi-agent traffic state graph where each node encodes a past trajectory (positions over several past time steps at 0.4 s resolution) and static context features (vehicle type, waypoint positions, road width, traffic light status, destination), while directed edges carry relative position information between agents. All states are transformed into per-agent coordinate systems with a Gaussian-perturbed origin to reduce implicit covariate shift. The policy outputs a future position distribution over T time steps modeled as a product of multivariate Gaussians; during simulation, this is post-processed via sampling, nearest on-road projection, and LQR trajectory smoothing to yield concrete vehicle positions and velocities.

## Method

LASIL introduces a context-conditioned VAE that simultaneously models the expert and learner trajectory distributions in a shared latent space, so that reconstructing an expert state yields a 'learner-aware augmented' state that bridges the distribution gap. The policy network (an edge-enhanced graph attention network) is trained purely by supervised NLL loss on these augmented expert states, using the original expert future trajectory as the regression target—no expert policy, GAN discriminator, or RL loop is needed. During training, the policy is periodically rolled out in closed-loop (with on-road projection and LQR smoothing) to generate learner states that are fed back into the VAE, creating an iterative self-improvement cycle. The context-conditioning design exploits the observation that static traffic context exhibits far less covariate shift than trajectories, making the VAE's reconstruction task more tractable and the augmentation more targeted.

## Results

On the pNEUMA dataset, LASIL achieves a position RMSE of 19.21 m and velocity RMSE of 3.02 m/s over 20-second microscopic rollouts, substantially outperforming the next-best baseline MARL+BC (29.78 m / 4.54 m/s). For long-term 800-second macroscopic evaluation, LASIL attains a road density RMSE of 45.13 veh/km and road speed RMSE of 3.17 m/s, while maintaining an off-road rate of only 0.34%. The method also achieves the lowest minADE20 (12.79 m) and off-road rate (0.28%) among all baselines in the short-term setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LASIL outperforms all six baselines on every reported metric in both short-term (20 s) and long-term (800 s) evaluations. Against the strongest learned baseline MARL+BC, LASIL reduces position RMSE by 10.57 m (19.21 vs. 29.78) and off-road rate by 10.86 percentage points (0.28% vs. 11.14%). The IRL-based methods PS-GAIL and RAIL show moderate short-term accuracy but suffer from significantly higher off-road rates (7.65% and 2.57% respectively) and worse long-term macroscopic metrics (road density RMSE 54.06 and 54.45 veh/km vs. LASIL's 45.13). Pure behavior cloning (BC) and MARL (IPPO) are the weakest baselines, with BC exhibiting a 33.43% off-road rate and MARL the worst position RMSE (48.66 m). The rule-based SUMO simulator, while producing zero off-road events, has the second-worst position RMSE (41.25 m) and the worst long-term road density RMSE (52.70 veh/km), confirming that heuristic car-following models cannot capture realistic multi-agent interactions at scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

LASIL carves new ground at the intersection of imitation learning and generative modeling for traffic simulation. It departs from DAgger/DART by eliminating the need for expert policy access at augmented states, and from GAIL/AIRL by avoiding adversarial min-max optimization and online RL entirely. The core idea—using a context-conditioned VAE to project expert and learner states into a shared latent space so that reconstructed expert states become 'learner-aware'—repurposes VAEs not as generative models but as distribution-matching augmentation tools within a purely supervised training loop. The graph-based architecture (EGAT) and context-conditioned state decomposition draw from prior multi-agent trajectory prediction work, but the VAE-augmentation-for-covariate-shift mechanism is novel in this context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first IL-based traffic simulator to produce stable 10+ minute microscopic simulations (40× longer than prior SOTA), which is practically significant for evaluating traffic management strategies and infrastructure changes that unfold over minutes rather than seconds. The method's simplicity—pure supervised learning with no GAN or RL training—makes it far more accessible and stable to train than adversarial alternatives. Key limitations include evaluation on a single dataset (pNEUMA), the assumption of a shared policy across all agents, and the absence of explicit collision modeling. Open questions include whether the VAE augmentation strategy generalizes to heterogeneous multi-policy settings, larger-scale networks, and whether the approach can be extended to real-time simulation.
