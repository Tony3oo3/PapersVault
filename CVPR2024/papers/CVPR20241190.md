## Problem

Existing diffusion-based scene text generation methods rely on a predefined layout stage (e.g., a transformer-predicted text mask or canny edge map) to guide where and how text appears, which constrains font diversity, text positioning, and stylistic variety. Standard latent diffusion models, meanwhile, lack the character-level structural information needed to render legible, contextually appropriate text within images.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241190/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a free-form text prompt as input, where the text to be rendered is enclosed in quotation marks; the quoted substring feeds the character-level encoder while the full caption feeds the CLIP text encoder. The output is a 512×512 RGB scene text image with visually integrated text. During training, ground-truth OCR transcriptions and bounding boxes (obtained from a pre-trained detector) provide supervision; during inference, only the user's prompt is required with no layout, mask, or style reference.

## Method

SceneTextGen builds on a latent diffusion model and introduces three key components. First, a character-level encoder tokenizes the target text and encodes each character into a high-dimensional feature vector capturing typographic properties, which is injected into a second cross-attention layer in the U-Net (the first cross-attention layer handles overall spatial layout via CLIP caption features, establishing a hierarchical integration). Second, a word-level OCR loss is computed by decoding the predicted clean latent through the VAE and running inference with a pre-trained GLASS scene-text spotting model, applying masked cross-entropy over detected word sequences. Third, a character-level segmentation loss is applied directly in latent space using a U-Net-based segmentation head (adapted from TextDiffuser) that predicts a 96-class per-pixel character map at 64×64 resolution, preventing the repetitive-text artifact observed with OCR loss alone. The total training objective combines the standard denoising loss with weighted OCR-word and OCR-char terms (λ_word=0.01, λ_char=0.02), and no predefined layout or spatial conditioning is required at inference.

## Results

On the MARIO-7M-Eval set, SceneTextGen-7M achieves AP=0.5274, AR=0.4420, F1=0.4424, and AC=0.3088, outperforming all same-data baselines (TextDiffuser-7M, LDM, DeepFloyd) and the layout-based ControlNet (AP=0.5102). Cross-dataset generalization is strong: on TMDB-Eval it reaches F1=0.3790 and on OpenLibrary-Eval F1=0.3945, surpassing TextDiffuser-7M (F1=0.2961 and 0.3318 respectively) despite being trained only on MARIO-7M. In CLIP score (Table 2), SceneTextGen-7M attains 0.3455, the highest among all methods, indicating superior overall image-text alignment without any predefined layout input.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SceneTextGen-7M is compared against five baselines: LDM, DeepFloyd, ControlNet, TextDiffuser (both 7M and 10M variants), and GlyphControl-10M. Against same-data baselines (TextDiffuser-7M, LDM, DeepFloyd), SceneTextGen-7M wins on all three evaluation sets across AP, F1, and AC; for example, on MARIO-7M it improves F1 from 0.3682 (TextDiffuser-7M) to 0.4424 and AC from 0.2512 to 0.3088. Against layout-based methods, SceneTextGen-7M beats ControlNet on MARIO-7M (AP 0.5274 vs 0.5102) and TMDB (F1 0.3790 vs 0.3284), and beats GlyphControl-10M on all three sets. However, it trails TextDiffuser-10M on MARIO-7M AP (0.5274 vs 0.6135) and OpenLibrary AP (0.4136 vs 0.5617), though TextDiffuser-10M benefits from 3M additional training images and a predefined layout stage. The critical distinction is that SceneTextGen achieves these competitive scores without any spatial/layout conditioning, and the font-diversity analysis (t-SNE in Fig. 5) shows its generated text occupies a distinct feature space far from the 'rendered text' baseline, whereas ControlNet and TextDiffuser cluster near it—indicating more natural, varied typography.

## Relation to Prior Work

SceneTextGen builds directly on the Latent Diffusion Model architecture and departs from the dominant two-stage paradigm (layout generation followed by conditional diffusion) used by TextDiffuser, ControlNet, and GlyphControl. Rather than feeding spatial layout masks or canny edge maps as conditioning, it introduces a character-level encoder whose features are injected through a hierarchical cross-attention mechanism, allowing the UNet to learn text placement implicitly. The loss design extends TextDiffuser's character segmentation loss with a word-level OCR loss computed via the pre-trained GLASS model, creating a dual-granularity supervision signal that addresses both text accuracy and quantity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the layout bottleneck, SceneTextGen unlocks substantially greater font and style diversity (confirmed via t-SNE analysis of a font-recognition model's feature space) while maintaining competitive OCR accuracy, suggesting that explicit spatial conditioning is not strictly necessary for legible text rendering. However, the paper acknowledges clear limitations: complex scene composition with text (e.g., a sticker on a specific car window) and long-text generation remain unreliable. Open questions include whether the character-level encoder generalizes to non-Latin scripts, whether the approach scales to higher resolutions without the layout crutch, and whether the OCR-guided losses can be made more robust to the blurry intermediate images at high noise levels.
