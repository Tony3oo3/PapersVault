## Problem

Supervised deep denoisers overfit to the specific noise type and level in their training data, resulting in poor generalization to out-of-distribution (OOD) noise. While CLIP has demonstrated exceptional generalization in high-level vision tasks like recognition and segmentation, its potential for enhancing robustness in low-level restoration tasks such as denoising remained entirely unexplored.

## Input / Output

The method takes a noisy RGB image (H×W×3) or a single-channel CT image (converted to three channels via a learnable 1×1 convolution) and produces a denoised image of the same spatial resolution. The noisy input is consumed in two ways: it is passed through the frozen CLIP RN50 encoder to extract four multi-scale feature maps, and it is also directly concatenated as a dense feature at the decoder's final upsampling level to preserve fine spatial details. Training uses a single fixed noise type and level (e.g., i.i.d. Gaussian σ=15), while inference is evaluated on diverse unseen noise types and levels without any adaptation.

## Method

The key insight is that the first four multi-scale dense features from CLIP's frozen RN50 image encoder are simultaneously distortion-invariant (highly similar between clean and noisy versions of the same image) and content-related (clearly distinct across different images), a property not shared by supervised ResNet50 or Restormer features. The architecture is an asymmetrical encoder-decoder: the frozen RN50 extracts features F¹–F⁴, and a 4-level learnable convolutional decoder starts from F⁴, progressively upsamples while concatenating F³, F², F¹ at levels 4→2, then at level 1 concatenates the noisy input image itself before a final 3×3 convolution yields the denoised output. No global residual learning is used, as the goal is to reconstruct the image from robust features rather than predict noise. To counteract feature overfitting caused by natural similarities among training images, a Progressive Feature Augmentation (PFA) strategy applies element-wise Gaussian perturbations to the frozen features during training, with standard deviation scaling linearly with depth (γ×i), injecting more randomness into deeper semantic features while preserving shallow texture information.

## Results

On synthetic OOD noise (Table 1), CLIPDenoising achieves the best or near-best PSNR/SSIM across all four noise types and four test datasets, e.g., 27.43/0.727 PSNR/SSIM on McMaster for Gaussian σ=50 and 28.31/0.775 for Spatial Gaussian σ=55, while requiring only a single noise level (σ=15) for training. On real-world sRGB noise (Table 2), the method achieves 34.79/0.866 on SIDD Val, 37.54/0.960 on PolyU, and 36.30/0.941 on CC, matching or slightly exceeding DIL. On low-dose CT (Table 3), CLIPDenoising reaches 45.88/0.976 PSNR/SSIM, approaching Noise2Sim (45.98/0.978) without requiring adjacent noisy CT images for training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLIPDenoising consistently outperforms DnCNN and Restormer by a large margin (e.g., ~7 dB PSNR on Gaussian σ=50), confirming that standard supervised denoisers severely overfit to training noise. Among the three generalizable denoising baselines, CLIPDenoising surpasses MaskDenoising on all noise types (e.g., 27.43 vs. 20.63 PSNR on McMaster for Gauss σ=50) and HAT on most settings, while matching or slightly exceeding DIL on average (DIL leads by ~0.1 dB on Poisson McMaster, but CLIPDenoising wins on Spatial Gaussian and Salt&Pepper across all datasets). A key practical advantage is that CLIPDenoising trains on a single noise level (σ=15), whereas HAT and DIL require multiple noise levels during training. On real-world sRGB noise, CLIPDenoising (34.79/0.866 on SIDD) is essentially tied with DIL (34.76/0.848) and outperforms HAT (28.58/0.570) and MaskDenoising (33.14/0.913), while also beating the unsupervised CVF-SID and LUD-VAE on PolyU and CC. On low-dose CT, CLIPDenoising (45.88/0.976) is within 0.1 dB of Noise2Sim (45.98/0.978) and clearly outperforms ScoreSDE (45.36/0.972), all without requiring adjacent noisy CT images for training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on CLIP's frozen ResNet-50 image encoder by discovering that its multi-scale dense features are distortion-invariant and content-related, a property absent in supervised or self-supervised ResNets of the same architecture. It departs from prior generalizable denoising methods (MaskDenoising, DIL, HAT) that rely on dropout, meta-learning, or adversarial training by instead leveraging a pre-trained encoder's inherent robustness, requiring only a learnable decoder. The work also extends the CLIP-for-dense-prediction line (MaskCLIP, DenseCLIP, ZegCLIP) from high-level tasks like segmentation to low-level restoration, a domain gap the authors explicitly identify as unexplored.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that CLIP's contrastive image-text training naturally produces noise-robust features offers a simple, interpretable mechanism for generalizable denoising that avoids the complex training procedures of prior OOD-robust methods. The cross-domain transfer to low-dose CT denoising demonstrates practical value in medical imaging where paired data is scarce. However, the property is fragile: it holds only for RN50 (not RN50x4/x16 or ViT), only for the first four scales, and finetuning the encoder destroys it—limiting the approach's extensibility. Open questions include whether other self-supervised objectives (MoCo-v3 shows partial success) can replicate this property and whether Transformer-based decoders can be made to work without overfitting.
