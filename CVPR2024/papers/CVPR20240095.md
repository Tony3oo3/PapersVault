## Problem

Existing volumetric super-resolution methods for 3D medical imaging are typically trained for fixed upsampling factors, requiring separate models for different clinical slice-thickness conversions. Additionally, newly generated inter-slice images frequently exhibit over-smoothing that creates perceptible noise-level discontinuities between slices, a problem that standard metrics like PSNR and SSIM fail to capture.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240095/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a low-resolution 3D medical volume (e.g., CT with 5 mm slice thickness) and a target upsampling factor r (which can be any real number, including decimals, since coordinates are normalized to [-1,1]) as input, and produces a high-resolution 3D volume with enhanced inter-slice resolution along the z-axis only. The x and y in-plane resolution is preserved, so the output dimensions are X×Y×(Z·r). A single trained model handles all upsampling scales without retraining, and inference uses Gaussian window-weighted sliding windows to avoid stitching artifacts.

## Method

CycleINR formulates volumetric super-resolution as continuous function approximation via an implicit neural representation: a coordinate-based voxel function f_θ(x, z) maps 3D spatial coordinates and a per-voxel latent code to intensity values. A residual CNN encoder extracts 128-dimensional latent codes from the LR volume, and an attention-enhanced latent code grid sampling (ALCGS) mechanism aggregates 27 neighboring latent codes using a query-key-value attention scheme that weights by both spatial proximity and latent-code similarity (analogous to bilateral filtering). An 8-layer fully connected decoder with a residual connection predicts voxel intensities. The key training innovation is a cycle-consistent loss: the generated HR image is fed back through the same INR to reconstruct the LR image, and the L1 discrepancy with the original LR volume penalizes over-smoothing and enforces noise-level consistency across slices. Training samples random upsampling scales uniformly from 2.0 to 6.0 (one decimal precision) on the fly, and the total loss combines the forward INR fitting L1 loss with the cycle-consistent loss weighted by λ=1.

## Results

On the chest CT dataset at x5 upsampling, CycleINR achieves PSNR 35.00 dB, SSIM 0.9354, LPIPS_vgg 0.1289, and SNLI 0.6050. While PSNR and SSIM are slightly lower than TVSRN (36.85 dB) and SAINT (36.99 dB), CycleINR substantially outperforms all deep learning baselines on perceptual quality (LPIPS_vgg: 0.1289 vs. 0.1989–0.2044) and the proposed SNLI metric (0.6050 vs. 1.17–1.70). On the downstream liver/tumor segmentation task (MSD Task03_Liver) at x5, CycleINR achieves the best DSC_T of 0.6881 and NSD_T of 0.5640, surpassing the next-best method (TVSRN: 0.6440 / 0.5431).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CycleINR is compared against three deep learning volumetric super-resolution methods (TVSRN, ArSSR, SAINT) and two interpolation baselines (cubic, trilinear) across x2, x3, and x5 scales. On PSNR and SSIM, CycleINR trails SAINT and TVSRN by roughly 1–2 dB at x5 (35.00 vs. 36.99/36.85), reflecting the known bias of these metrics toward over-smoothed outputs. However, on perceptual quality (LPIPS_vgg) CycleINR achieves 0.1289 at x5, nearly halving the error of TVSRN (0.1989) and SAINT (0.2044), and on the proposed SNLI metric it scores 0.6050 versus 1.17–1.70 for the three deep learning baselines, indicating far less inter-slice noise inconsistency. On the downstream liver/tumor segmentation task at x5, CycleINR achieves the best DSC_T (0.6881) and NSD_T (0.5640), outperforming TVSRN (0.6440/0.5431), ArSSR (0.6582/0.5297), and SAINT (0.6006/0.5050), confirming that the reduced over-smoothing translates into clinically meaningful segmentation fidelity. At x2, CycleINR also leads on SNLI (0.3527) and LPIPS_vgg (0.0625) while remaining competitive on PSNR (43.01 vs. SAINT's 44.40).

## Relation to Prior Work

CycleINR extends the INR-based arbitrary-scale 3D medical super-resolution framework introduced by Wu et al. (ArSSR, IEEE JBHI 2022), which first demonstrated that a coordinate-based implicit voxel function can produce super-resolved volumes at any upsampling factor without retraining. The latent-code grid sampling design draws on Chen et al.'s local implicit image function for 2D SR, while the cycle-consistent loss adapts the CycleGAN principle to the INR setting—a combination not previously explored in volumetric SR. The local attention mechanism for grid sampling is a novel departure from the trilinear interpolation or cell decoding used in prior INR-based SR work, borrowing the spirit of bilateral filtering to weight neighbors by both spatial proximity and latent-code similarity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The SNLI metric fills a genuine gap in volumetric SR evaluation by quantifying the slice-wise noise inconsistency that PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS all miss, and it reveals that existing deep learning methods (TVSRN, ArSSR, SAINT) produce worse inter-slice consistency than simple cubic or trilinear interpolation—a finding that challenges the assumption that learned methods always improve volumetric coherence. The cycle-consistent loss is a practical, lightweight fix for over-smoothing that could be adopted by other INR-based SR pipelines. Limitations include evaluation on only two datasets (in-house chest CT and MSD liver), modest PSNR/SSIM scores relative to SAINT at x5, and no analysis of computational cost or inference speed compared to CNN-based baselines. Generalizability to other modalities (MRI sequences, ultrasound) and to video frame interpolation remain open.
