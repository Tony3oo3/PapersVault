## Problem

Privacy-preserving collaborative (federated) learning for unsupervised video anomaly detection has not been studied, despite surveillance videos being inherently privacy-sensitive and distributed across multiple organizations. The US-VAD task is already extremely challenging due to the complete absence of annotations and the rarity of anomalies, and combining it with a distributed participant-based training setting where no raw data leaves each node is an unexplored and practically critical problem.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241076/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each participant provides unlabelled surveillance videos that are partitioned into variable-length non-overlapping segments (no temporal compression to a fixed count), from which a pre-trained feature extractor produces a d-dimensional feature vector per segment. The trained detector outputs a continuous anomaly score in [0,1] per segment, and all frames within a segment inherit that segment's score to yield frame-level predictions. Crucially, participants share only low-dimensional Gaussian parameters (mean and covariance of normal-segment features) and model weight deltas with the server—never raw video or features.

## Method

CLAP operates in three stages. First, Common Knowledge-based Data Segregation (CKDS) generates video-level pseudo-labels by representing each video with a 2D statistic (variance of inter-segment feature-magnitude differences and Von Neumann entropy of the segment-feature covariance matrix) and clustering via GMM, then generates segment-level pseudo-labels through collaborative statistical hypothesis testing: participants share their local Gaussian parameters for normal segments, the server constructs a weighted Gaussian mixture, and anomalous segments are identified by p-value thresholding combined with a sliding-window temporal-consistency rule. Second, Server Knowledge Accumulation (SKA) trains local anomaly detectors via SGD on the pseudo-labelled data and aggregates weight updates across participants using FedAvg over T collaboration rounds. Third, Pseudo-Label Refinement (PLR) uses the local model's confidence scores to re-identify the maximum-confidence anomalous window and intersect it with the initial pseudo-labels, correcting missed or spurious labels before the next training round.

## Results

On the unsupervised setting (the paper's primary focus), CLAP achieves 80.9% AUC on UCF-Crime and 81.71% AUC on XD-Violence under centralized training, and 78.02% / 77.65% AUC under collaborative (federated) training with 5 participants. In the local (non-collaborative) setting, CLAP scores 63.93% / 62.37% AUC, showing a substantial gap that collaborative learning closes. Under weakly-supervised evaluation, CLAP reaches 85.50% AUC on UCF-Crime and 90.04% on XD-Violence (centralized), and 83.23% / 85.67% (collaborative), outperforming all compared baselines in the collaborative setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the unsupervised collaborative setting (the paper's core contribution), CLAP outperforms GCL [39] by 10.9% AUC on UCF-Crime (78.02 vs 67.12) and 9.5% on XD-Violence (77.65 vs 68.19), and beats C2FPL [2] by 2.8% and 3.3% respectively. In the weakly-supervised collaborative setting, CLAP (83.23% UCF-Crime) surpasses PRV [7] (82.90%), the only other federated VAD method, and leads GCL and C2FPL by 6.4% and 5.6% AUC. Against centralized weakly-supervised baselines, CLAP (85.50% UCF-Crime, 90.04% XD-Violence) beats RTFM [31] (84.30/89.34), MSL [15] (85.30/–), CLAWS+ [38] (80.90/–), and Sultani et al. [29] (75.41/–), but is slightly below S3R [33] on UCF-Crime (85.50 vs 85.99) while dramatically exceeding it on XD-Violence (90.04 vs 53.52). PRV [7] achieves a higher centralized UCF-Crime score (86.30 vs 85.50), but PRV requires weak labels and does not support the unsupervised setting. Overall, CLAP is the only method that supports both unsupervised and collaborative training while matching or exceeding centralized performance within ~3% AUC.

## Relation to Prior Work

CLAP extends the unsupervised VAD line initiated by GCL [39] and C2FPL [2], both of which rely on pseudo-label generation from unlabelled video, but transplants this paradigm into a federated learning setting for the first time in the unsupervised regime. The closest prior work, PRV [7], addresses weakly supervised VAD under federated learning but requires video-level labels and does not tackle the harder problem of label-free collaborative training. CLAP departs from both by introducing Von Neumann entropy–based data segregation, collaborative Gaussian mixture hypothesis testing for segment-level pseudo-labels, and a local feedback loop for pseudo-label refinement, all operating without any ground-truth annotations at any participant.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first rigorous baseline for privacy-preserving, fully unsupervised collaborative VAD, and the three proposed evaluation protocols (random, event-class, and scene-based splits) fill a gap in how the community benchmarks distributed anomaly detection under realistic data heterogeneity. A key limitation is the notable AUC drop (78.02 → 67.92) when scaling from 5 to 50 participants, which the authors attribute to per-participant data scarcity rather than a fundamental algorithmic failure. Open questions include whether the approach scales to truly large-scale collaborative deployments, how it performs under non-IID temporal distributions, and whether more sophisticated aggregation than FedAvg (e.g., SCAFFOLD) could close the gap to centralized training.
