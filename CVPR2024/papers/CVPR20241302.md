## Problem

Existing change understanding datasets focus on discrete changes (adding, deleting, or moving objects) between paired images, often relying on synthetic data or simplified tabletop scenes. They overlook continuous, gradual changes—such as vegetation growth, infrastructure aging, or seasonal transitions—occurring over extended time periods in real-world outdoor environments. This paper addresses this gap by introducing a large-scale benchmark for long-term continuous change recognition and description in street-level imagery.

## Input / Output

The dataset provides 640×640 street view photographs captured at identical geographic coordinates over 18 years (2006–2023) across 50 cities, organized into three task formats: image pairs for change captioning, sequences of 3–6 images for trend description, and fixed 5-image sequences for instance segmentation. Outputs are human-annotated English captions (3–8 sentences per pair, covering 10 subject types with attribute-level distinctions, tendencies, superlatives, and similarities) or pixel-level instance masks with consistent object IDs maintained across the sequence for 12 categories (vehicle, building, tree, road, sky, lawn, soil, fence, motorbike, bicycle, human, animal).

## Method

The dataset was constructed by querying the Google Street View API at 300–1,000 randomly sampled coordinates per city (defined via OpenStreetMap boundaries), yielding 71,900 photographs after filtering for projection distortions and coordinates with fewer than two temporal captures. Annotations were crowdsourced with dual-annotator validation, targeting 10 dominant street-view subjects and their attribute-level changes, with open-ended reporting permitted beyond the guidelines. For empirical evaluation, the authors benchmark five change captioning methods (DUDA, MCCFormers-D/S, CLIP4IDC, VARD-Trans) and two multimodal LLMs (OpenFlamingo, BLIP-2+GPT-4) on both captioning tasks, and two video instance segmentation methods (Mask2Former, CTVIS) adapted to track static scene objects across years. Results show that even the best methods (CLIP4IDC at 47.8% human-rated accuracy; CTVIS at 7.08 AP) fall far short of human performance (94.4% accuracy, 4.62 coverage), underscoring the difficulty of recognizing continuous changes in real-world scenes.

## Results

On the continual change captioning (image pair) task, CLIP4IDC achieves the best trained-method scores with BLEU4=28.5, CIDEr=69.5, and GPT4=32.4, while OpenFlamingo attains the highest GPT4 score (37.9) and human accuracy (43.8%) among all methods. For the image sequence task, BLIP2+GPT4 leads on GPT4 (30.3) and OpenFlamingo on human accuracy (34.4%) and coverage (1.10 changes detected). For change-aware sequential instance segmentation, CTVIS with SwinT-L backbone achieves the best AP of 7.08 (AP50=10.42, AP75=7.00), substantially outperforming Mask2Former (AP=6.46). All methods remain far below human performance (94.4% accuracy, 3.58 coverage for pairs; 89.8% accuracy, 4.62 coverage for sequences).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the five trained change-captioning baselines, CLIP4IDC [28] clearly dominates on the image pair task, scoring 28.5 BLEU4 and 69.5 CIDEr versus DUDA [7] (21.7/39.1), MCCFormers-D [8] (22.4/52.7), MCCFormers-S [8] (25.4/51.3), and VARD-Trans [29] (16.4/19.4). However, the untrained multimodal LLMs (OpenFlamingo [42] and BLIP2+GPT4 [43,44]) surpass all trained methods on GPT4-evaluated meaning similarity and human-rated accuracy, with OpenFlamingo reaching 43.8% human accuracy versus CLIP4IDC's 47.8%—a narrow gap that still falls far short of the 94.4% human baseline. On the image sequence task, BLIP2+GPT4 achieves the highest GPT4 score (30.3) while OpenFlamingo leads in human accuracy (34.4%) and coverage (1.10), but both remain well below the 89.8% human accuracy. For the change-aware sequential instance segmentation task, CTVIS [39] with SwinT-L backbone (AP=7.08) outperforms Mask2Former [35] with the same backbone (AP=6.46) by 0.62 AP points, and both methods struggle significantly, with AP scores below 11, indicating that existing video instance segmentation approaches are poorly suited to long-term scene change tracking.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by reframing the well-studied change detection and change captioning tasks around *continuous, gradual* changes over multi-year time spans, rather than the discrete add/delete/move operations that dominate existing datasets (CLEVR-Change, COCO-Inpainted, Meta-rooms, etc.). It repurposes existing captioning methods (DUDA, MCCFormers, CLIP4IDC, VARD-Trans) and video instance segmentation methods (Mask2Former, CTVIS) as baselines, but applies them to a fundamentally different input regime: sequences of 3–6 street-view images spanning up to 18 years from the same geographic location. The use of the Google Street View API to construct a 71,900-image, 50-city temporal corpus is itself a new data-collection strategy not seen in prior change-understanding benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

STVchrono is the first benchmark to jointly support continuous change captioning (pairs and sequences) and change-aware sequential instance segmentation on real-world outdoor scenes, filling a clear gap left by synthetic or discrete-change-only datasets. The experiments reveal a large and consistent performance gap between even the strongest multimodal LLMs (OpenFlamingo, BLIP-2+GPT-4) and human annotators (e.g., 47.8% vs. 94.4% accuracy on image pairs), suggesting that current models lack robust temporal-reasoning capabilities for gradual, multi-year scene evolution. Open questions include whether unified architectures can simultaneously perform change description and adaptive region detection, and whether the low single-digit AP scores in segmentation indicate a fundamental mismatch between short-term video tracking and long-term scene-change tracking. Limitations noted by the authors include uneven city distribution and restricted weather/time-of-day variability.
