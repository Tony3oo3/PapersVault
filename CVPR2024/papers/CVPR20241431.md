## Problem

Cross-domain few-shot learning requires both transferring knowledge across dissimilar domains and fine-tuning with very few target-domain samples. Existing flatness-based methods (e.g., SAM) operate in the parameter space and only achieve short-range flatness, which is insufficient to handle the large representation shifts caused by domain gaps. This paper identifies that sharp minima in the representation-space loss landscape are the root cause of both poor transferability and difficult few-shot fine-tuning.

## Input / Output

The model takes standard RGB images as input and outputs class labels via a prototype-based or fine-tuned classifier. During base-class training on miniImageNet, the mixing ratio δ is randomly sampled from a Beta(a,b) distribution at each forward pass; during novel-class fine-tuning, δ becomes a learnable scalar initialized to the Beta expectation. The FLoR layer is a drop-in replacement for the original normalization layer (BN for CNNs, LN for ViTs), adding only the parameters of one extra normalization branch per layer.

## Method

The core contribution is the FLoR (Flatten Long-Range) normalization layer, which exploits the observation that different normalization methods (BN, IN, LN) produce distinct minima in the representation-space loss landscape for the same input. At each layer, the pre-normalized representation is passed through two normalization branches, and the output is a random convex combination (1−δ)·f_norm1(x) + δ·f_norm2(x) where δ ~ Beta(a,b). For CNNs, the two branches are BN and IN; for ViTs, they are LN and BN applied to the CLS token. Because classification is computed on the interpolated representation, the network is forced to assign low loss to all intermediate points, effectively flattening the high-loss valley between the two minima over a range far larger than gradient-based methods like SAM can cover. The layer introduces only two hyperparameters (a, b) and the parameters of one additional normalization branch, making it lightweight and architecture-agnostic.

## Results

Using ResNet10 as backbone, FLoR achieves the best average 5-way 5-shot accuracy of 66.22% (with both fine-tuning and transductive calibration), outperforming the previous best LDP-net (64.10%) by 2.12 points. In the 5-way 1-shot setting, FLoR reaches 52.67% average accuracy, surpassing LDP-net (50.85%) and RDC (50.81%). The largest per-dataset gain is on ISIC2018, where FLoR improves over LDP-net by 9.10 points (57.54% vs 48.44%) in the 5-shot FT+TR setting. With ViT-S (DINO-pretrained), FLoR achieves 74.75% average 5-shot accuracy with fine-tuning, exceeding StyleAdv (74.06%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FLoR outperforms all 8 compared baselines in average accuracy across every evaluation setting (no FT/no TR, FT only, TR only, and FT+TR). Against the strongest ResNet10 baselines, FLoR beats LDP-net [36] by 2.12 points (66.22% vs 64.10%) and RDC [20] by 3.01 points (66.22% vs 63.21%) in the 5-shot FT+TR setting, with the largest per-dataset margin on ISIC2018 (+9.10 over LDP-net). In the 1-shot setting, FLoR leads LDP-net by 1.82 points (52.67% vs 50.85%) and TPN+ATA [30] by 2.76 points (52.67% vs 49.91%). Older baselines such as GNN+FT [25] (57.72% avg in 5-shot) and MN+AFA [16] (54.96%) are surpassed by 8.5 and 11.3 points respectively. With the ViT-S backbone, FLoR edges out StyleAdv [10] by 0.69 points in 5-shot FT (74.75% vs 74.06%) and by 0.33 points in 1-shot FT (58.90% vs 58.57%), while PMF [23] is only reported on a subset of datasets where FLoR also leads. FLoR does not win on every individual dataset (e.g., it trails LDP-net on Places in 5-shot FT+TR: 74.25% vs 75.47%), but its consistent gains across the majority of domains yield the best average.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the loss-landscape flatness analysis from the parameter space (as in SAM [8]) to the representation space, arguing that the latter more directly captures domain shifts relevant to CDFSL. It repurposes the well-known diversity of normalization methods (BN, IN, LN) as a mechanism for identifying multiple, well-separated minima in the representation-space loss landscape, then interpolates between them to flatten the high-loss region connecting them. The interpolation formulation superficially resembles Mixup, but the paper explicitly distinguishes its purpose: rather than augmenting data, it constrains the loss landscape between two normalization-induced minima. The method is implemented as a drop-in FLoR normalization layer for both CNNs and ViTs, building on the CDFSL training protocol established by Guo et al. [11].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The FLoR layer is a lightweight, drop-in replacement for standard normalization that simultaneously improves transferability and ease of few-shot finetuning, which is practically attractive for CDFSL practitioners. The RSLL analysis provides a cleaner conceptual lens for understanding why IN-based backbones outperform BN-based ones in cross-domain settings, unifying observations from prior work [10] under a single geometric interpretation. However, the ablation revealing that simply replacing BN with IN captures a large fraction of the total gain raises the question of how much of the improvement truly comes from the long-range flattening mechanism versus the intrinsic flatness of IN. Open questions include whether the specific BN/IN (or LN/BN) pairing is optimal, whether the Beta(0.01, 0.01) distribution is robust across architectures and domains, and whether the approach extends to larger pretrained models or non-visual modalities.
