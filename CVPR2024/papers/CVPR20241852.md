## Problem

Neural fields (NeFs) have been proposed as data representations for downstream tasks, but the impact of NeF hyperparameters (initialization, training duration, architecture expressivity) on representation quality is largely unexplored. This gap is compounded by the prohibitive cost of fitting millions of small networks sequentially, which prevents systematic empirical study at scale.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241852/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes discretely sampled signals—RGB image pixels (MNIST 28×28, CIFAR-10 32×32, MicroImageNet 64×64) or 3D shape point clouds (200k random points, 100k near-surface)—and fits a per-sample neural field (SIREN, RFFNet, or MFN) whose full parameter vector θ becomes the representation. This parameter vector, along with its computational graph structure, is then fed to a permutation-invariant downstream classifier (MPNN, DWS, or Relation Transformer) that outputs a class label. The unusual aspect is that the 'feature' is an entire small network's weights rather than a fixed-dimension embedding.

## Method

The paper introduces Fit-a-NeF, a JAX/Flax library that uses vmap-based vectorization and JIT compilation to parallelize NeF fitting across and within accelerators, achieving 100–300× speedup over sequential training (e.g., Neural MNIST in 17 s vs. 3 h). Using this library, the authors conduct a large-scale empirical study (≈30,000 preliminary + 220 in-depth experiments) revealing three key insights: (1) sharing a single random initialization across all NeFs in a dataset groups representations in parameter space and substantially improves downstream accuracy (up to ~120% gain); (2) overtraining degrades representation quality—classification accuracy peaks and then declines as NeFs overfit, with the off-grid/on-grid reconstruction ratio serving as a useful early-stopping heuristic; (3) increasing network width (expressivity) improves reconstruction PSNR/IOU but monotonically worsens downstream accuracy. These findings are packaged into Neural Field Arena, a benchmark of NeF-variant datasets (Neural MNIST, Neural CIFAR10, Neural MicroImageNet, Neural ShapeNet-10) evaluated with state-of-the-art permutation-equivariant classifiers.

## Results

On the Neural Field Arena benchmark, the simple GNN baseline achieves 96.40% on Neural MNIST, 39.83% on Neural CIFAR10, 25.08% on Neural MicroImageNet, and 82.96% on Neural ShapeNet. The more expressive Transformer [20] and DWS [29] baselines outperform the GNN on the three harder datasets (CIFAR10: 44.11%/44.01%, MicroImageNet: 28.50%/28.61%, ShapeNet: 90.31%/91.06%), but the GNN actually surpasses both on Neural MNIST (96.40% vs. 95.08% and 93.51%). The key finding is that proper NeF training choices (shared initialization, early stopping before overfitting) yield a 94.69% relative accuracy gain on Neural MNIST and 119.63% on Neural ShapeNet over random initialization, demonstrating that representation quality is driven more by how NeFs are fitted than by the downstream architecture.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks three downstream architectures (its own GNN baseline, DWS [29], and a Relation Transformer [20]) on four Neural Datasets. On Neural MNIST, the simple GNN baseline (96.40%) outperforms both DWS (93.51%) and the Transformer (95.08%), suggesting that with well-trained NeF representations, architectural complexity is less critical. However, on the three harder datasets, the more expressive models consistently win: on Neural CIFAR10, the Transformer (44.11%) and DWS (44.01%) beat the GNN (39.83%) by ~4 points; on Neural MicroImageNet, DWS (28.61%) and Transformer (28.50%) lead the GNN (25.08%) by ~3.5 points; and on Neural ShapeNet, DWS (91.06%) and Transformer (90.31%) surpass the GNN (82.96%) by ~7-8 points. The paper's central claim is not that the GNN is the best downstream model, but that the NeF training protocol (shared initialization, controlled overtraining) is the dominant factor: the same GNN baseline jumps from 37% to 96.40% on Neural MNIST simply by changing the NeF fitting strategy, a gain far exceeding the 1-4 point differences between downstream architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) implicit neural representations (SIREN, RFFNet, MFN) for signal reconstruction, and (2) the emerging line of work treating NeF parameters as data representations for downstream tasks (Dupont et al., Navon et al., Kofinas et al.). It departs from prior NeF literature, which has focused almost exclusively on reconstruction fidelity, by systematically studying how NeF training hyperparameters (initialization, number of steps, architecture expressivity) affect the quality of the resulting parameters as *representations* for downstream classification. The practical contribution—Fit-a-NeF, a JAX/Flax/vmap-based library for parallelized NeF fitting—addresses a bottleneck that prior works (notably Dupont et al.) identified as prohibitive for large-scale dataset fitting, enabling the empirical study in the first place.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic, large-scale empirical study of NeF training choices for representation quality, yielding actionable guidance (shared initialization, early stopping before overfitting, moderate architecture expressivity) that is directly applicable to anyone building NeF-based pipelines. The Neural Field Arena benchmark fills a critical gap in the field by offering standardized datasets and baselines for comparing downstream models on NeF representations. Key limitations include the restriction to images and shapes (no NeRFs or video), the use of classification accuracy as the sole proxy for representation quality, and the absence of hyperparameter tuning for the downstream classifiers. Open questions include whether the findings generalize to other modalities, other downstream tasks (segmentation, generation), and whether the shared-initialization trick interacts with more sophisticated alignment methods like Re-Basin or optimal transport.
