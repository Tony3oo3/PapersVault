## Problem

Existing neural network interpretability methods can explain at most one or two of the three critical elements of a model decision: 'what' a neuron represents, 'where' in the input the relevant evidence lies, and 'why' the model made a particular prediction. No prior framework unifies all three, leaving users without a complete, human-understandable account of how a network arrives at its output.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242511/cropped_page6_idx5.webp)

## Input / Output

WWW takes a pre-trained target model (CNN or ViT), a probing image dataset, and a concept set (e.g., ~80k WordNet nouns or ImageNet labels) as inputs. Concept discovery is pre-computed offline, while at inference time a single test image is consumed to produce per-neuron concept annotations (major and minor), a weighted concept heatmap, a concept region map, and parallel class-wise and sample-wise explanations. An additional output is an uncertainty estimate derived from the cosine similarity between the class and sample heatmaps, which the paper shows outperforms maximum softmax probability for misprediction detection.

## Method

WWW comprises three modules. The Concept Discovery module identifies each neuron's concept by computing Adaptive Cosine Similarity (ACS) between CLIP visual features of high-activating images and CLIP text features of candidate concepts, where ACS subtracts the base-template similarity to decouple concept-specific signal from template bias; concepts are then selected via an adaptive threshold (α × max score). The Localization module computes per-neuron contributions using a Taylor approximation of Shapley values and generates a concept heatmap as the weighted sum of neuron activation maps (NAMs) for the top-k most important neurons, localizing 'where' each concept appears in the input. The Reasoning module pre-computes class-wise Shapley contributions and, at test time, contrasts them with sample-wise contributions to explain 'why' the prediction was made; the cosine similarity between the resulting class and sample heatmaps serves as a reliability indicator, with lower similarity flagging potentially unreliable predictions.

## Results

On ResNet-50 trained on ImageNet-1k, WWW achieves an F1-score of 0.7719 and Hit Rate of 0.955 when using the ImageNet label set as concepts, outperforming CLIP-Dissect (F1 0.7286, Hit Rate 0.933). With the larger Wordnet (80k) concept set, WWW reaches F1-score 0.4197 and Hit Rate 0.645, compared to CLIP-Dissect's 0.3647 and 0.456. The same pattern holds on ViT-B/16 (F1 0.7718 vs 0.7289 on ImageNet 1k) and ResNet-18 on Places365 (F1 0.2242 vs 0.1096 on Broden), confirming that WWW's advantage grows with concept set size.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CLIP-Dissect (the strongest baseline), WWW wins on F1-score across all three concept-set sizes (e.g., 0.7719 vs 0.7286 on ImageNet-1k; 0.4197 vs 0.3647 on Wordnet-80k) and on Hit Rate for ImageNet-1k (0.955 vs 0.933) and Wordnet-80k (0.645 vs 0.456), while CLIP-Dissect retains a marginal edge on raw CLIP cosine similarity for the small ImageNet-1k set (0.9340 vs 0.9325). Against FALCON, MILAN, and Network Dissection, WWW's advantage is overwhelming: those methods achieve F1-scores near zero (≤0.001) because they either rely on pixel-level Broden annotations or lack a mechanism to match neuron concepts to a large open vocabulary. The gap between WWW and CLIP-Dissect widens as the concept set grows from 1k to 80k entries, indicating that adaptive cosine similarity (ACS) and adaptive thresholding become increasingly effective at distinguishing relevant concepts in a large, noisy vocabulary. Results on ViT-B/16 and ResNet-18/Places365 replicate the same ranking, confirming that the gains are architecture- and dataset-independent.

## Relation to Prior Work

WWW unifies three previously separate lines of interpretability research: neuron-concept association (Network Dissection, CLIP-Dissect, FALCON) for 'what', feature attribution via Shapley values (LINE, Taylor-approximated Shapley) for 'where', and concept-based reasoning (TCAV, CRAFT) for 'why'. The key methodological departure from CLIP-Dissect is the introduction of adaptive cosine similarity (ACS), which subtracts the base-template similarity to reduce uniformity in the CLIP feature space, yielding more distinct concept assignments. The combination of Shapley-weighted neuron activation maps for localized concept heatmaps is a novel integration that prior methods (which produce either global neuron descriptions or input-region attributions) do not provide.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified What-Where-Why framework offers practitioners a single plug-and-play tool that works across CNNs and Vision Transformers, reducing the need to combine multiple interpretability methods. The finding that heatmap similarity outperforms maximum softmax probability for detecting mispredictions is a practical contribution to uncertainty estimation, though the evidence is limited to a single rejection-test experiment. Open questions include scalability to very large concept sets (performance degrades notably from ImageNet labels to WordNet), applicability to non-vision modalities, and whether the CLIP-based concept matching generalizes to domains where CLIP's pretraining is less relevant.
