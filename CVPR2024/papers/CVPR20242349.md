## Problem

Acquiring the bulk optical properties of translucent materials (extinction coefficient, albedo, phase function) from ordinary photographs remains unsolved: existing approaches either require dedicated hardware (integrating spheres, structured light, lasers), handle only optically thin single-scattering media, or depend on differentiable rendering that demands known lighting/camera parameters and is notoriously unstable in convergence. No practical method exists that works with consumer-level cameras under unknown illumination and geometry.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242349/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes at least three 256×256 RGB images of the same homogeneous translucent object against a black background, captured under different white-light illuminations with a commodity camera. From these images, a per-pixel linear regression derives two auxiliary maps: a pseudo-albedo map (normalized regression slopes) and a bias map (mean squared deviation from the fitted line). The network outputs three scalar first-order similarity parameters — σ_t', Λ', and the HG phase-function parameter g — that fully characterize the material for rendering.

## Method

The core insight is an inter-pixel translucency prior: pixels at the same spatial location across multiple images form curves through the origin in RGB space, whose shape encodes the material's optical properties. A per-pixel, per-channel least-squares regression over the image set yields a pseudo-albedo map (proxy for single-scattering albedo) and a bias map (highlighting optically thin translucent edges where the linear model breaks down). IPTNet is a multi-branch CNN: weight-sharing encoders process the raw images, separate encoders process the pseudo-albedo and bias maps, and a translucency-aware fusion module combines them via max-pooling across images followed by element-wise multiplication with bias features to emphasize translucent edges. A cascaded decoder first predicts Λ' from the global feature, then feeds that estimate into a second MLP to predict {σ_t', g} from the edge-weighted feature, preserving both global and local optical cues. The network is trained end-to-end with L1 loss on 16,800 synthetic image groups rendered in Mitsuba across 22 shapes, 5 viewpoints, and varied lighting.

## Results

On the TrView test set, IPTNet achieves MSE of 0.014 (σ_t'), 0.001 (Λ'), and 0.025 (g), substantially outperforming the best ITN variant (ITN(M)) which scores 0.015, 0.045, and 0.079 respectively. In the image domain, IPTNet reaches 38.68 dB PSNR and 0.993 SSIM on the hexahedron shape (TrView), compared to 35.94 dB and 0.975 for ITN(M). Gains are even larger under distribution shift: on TrLight, IPTNet achieves 36.34 dB PSNR versus 29.62 dB for ITN(M), a 6.7 dB improvement. Inference takes roughly 30 ms per scene versus ~20 minutes for the differentiable rendering baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ITN (Che et al., ICCP 2020) in its three variants (single-image with differentiable rendering, multi-image averaged, and single-image without differentiable rendering), IPTNet wins on every metric across all three test sets. The most dramatic advantage is on Λ' prediction: IPTNet's MSE is 0.001–0.010 versus 0.011–0.167 for ITN variants, reflecting the benefit of the pseudo-albedo map. On the phase-function parameter g, IPTNet achieves MSE 0.025–0.033 while ITN(-Dr) reaches 0.053–0.505, a 2–16× gap. In the image domain, IPTNet gains 2.7–8.0 dB PSNR over the best ITN variant, with the largest gap (6.7 dB) on TrLight where lighting conditions differ most from training. Against the Mitsuba 3 + Dr.jit differentiable rendering baseline (Jakob et al., 2022), IPTNet is compared only in the image domain (due to the similarity relation making parameter-space comparison ill-defined) and wins on all rendered shapes; additionally, IPTNet runs in ~30 ms versus ~20 minutes per scene for the optimization-based method, a roughly 4000× speedup.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multi-image CNN architecture from Deschaintre et al. [10] (weight-sharing encoders for SVBRDF capture) to the translucent material domain, where the challenge is fundamentally harder due to volumetric light transport. It departs from the differentiable-rendering-based inverse rendering paradigm (Mitsuba 3 + Dr.jit) that requires known lighting and camera parameters, and from ITN [5] which couples a CNN with a differentiable renderer for loss computation. The key conceptual departure is the inter-pixel translucency prior — the observation that same-position pixels across multiple images trace curves in RGB space whose shape encodes material properties — which is used to derive a pseudo-albedo map and bias map as explicit network inputs, rather than relying on end-to-end differentiable rendering to disentangle lighting from material.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method makes translucent material acquisition practical with only a commodity camera and a few uncalibrated photos, reducing the barrier from dedicated hardware or expensive optimization loops to a ~30 ms inference. The inter-pixel translucency prior is a simple, physically-motivated signal that could inform other inverse problems in participating media. However, the method is limited to homogeneous materials, requires at least three images, fails on near-transparent objects that blend with backgrounds, and is validated primarily on a synthetic dataset with limited real-world examples (waxy cubes, orange juice). Open questions include whether the prior generalizes to inhomogeneous or spatially-varying translucent materials, and whether the pseudo-albedo/bias maps can be derived from fewer images or single-image settings.
