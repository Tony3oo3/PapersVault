## Problem

Dynamic 4D scene reconstruction from video remains a trade-off between quality and speed: NeRF-based methods require hours of training and cannot render in real time, while direct 3DGS extensions either optimize each frame separately (storage-intensive, incompatible with monocular input) or introduce implicit neural motion fields that negate the rendering speed advantage of 3DGS. The paper asks whether a purely explicit, network-free deformation model can achieve both state-of-the-art novel view quality and 3DGS-level training/rendering speed for 4D scenes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes either multi-view video (e.g., 21 cameras at 2704×2028, downsampled to 1352×1014) or monocular video (540×960) along with sparse SfM points for Gaussian initialization. It produces a compact set of deformable 3D Gaussian particles, each parameterized by base attributes (position, rotation, scale, opacity, color) at a reference frame plus per-dimension polynomial and Fourier-series coefficients that encode the time-dependent residual. Rendering is performed via standard tile-based rasterization and α-blending, yielding real-time novel view images at ~125 FPS on a single RTX 4090.

## Method

The core contribution is the Dual-Domain Deformation Model (DDDM), which models each Gaussian point's time-varying attributes (position, rotation, radiance) as a base value plus a residual D(t) = P_N(t) + F_L(t), jointly fitting a polynomial in the time domain and a Fourier series in the frequency domain per attribute dimension. This hybrid avoids the overfitting oscillations of high-order polynomials on violent motion and the blurriness of low-order polynomials on smooth motion, while remaining fully parametric with no neural network forward pass. An adaptive timestamp scaling factor (t_s = λ_s·t + λ_b) per Gaussian prevents coefficient explosion for fast-moving points, and two regularizations—KNN rigid loss (enforcing local spatial consistency in alternating optimization stages) and time smoothness loss (penalizing attribute jumps between adjacent timestamps)—stabilize the per-point optimization. The entire pipeline inherits 3DGS's tile-based rasterization and adaptive density control, achieving 5× faster training than per-frame 3DGS and ~125 FPS rendering with no implicit field evaluation.

## Results

On the HyperNeRF monocular dataset, Gaussian-Flow achieves a mean PSNR of 25.6 dB (30K steps) and 26.3 dB (60K steps) with 125 FPS rendering, compared to 24.3 dB and 0.5 FPS for TiNeuVox and 20.1 dB and 0.013 FPS for vanilla NeRF. Training takes only 7 minutes (30K steps) versus 30 minutes for TiNeuVox and 16 hours for NeRF. On the Plenoptic Video multi-view dataset, the method reaches 30.5 PSNR / 0.97 SSIM in 22.5 minutes, a ~5× speedup over K-Planes (1.8 hours) and DyNeRF (1344 hours), while on D-NeRF synthetic scenes it attains 34.27 PSNR, outperforming 4D-GS (33.30) and TiNeuVox (32.67).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gaussian-Flow outperforms all NeRF-based baselines (NeRF, Nerfies, HyperNeRF, NeRFPlayer, TiNeuVox) on both quality and speed: it achieves 1.3–5.5 dB higher mean PSNR on HyperNeRF while training 4–137× faster and rendering at 125 FPS versus 0.011–0.5 FPS. Against the closest 3DGS-based competitor 4D-GS [35], Gaussian-Flow wins on D-NeRF (34.27 vs 33.30 PSNR) and on Plenoptic Video SSIM (0.97 vs 0.94), though 4D-GS edges it slightly on Plenoptic Video PSNR at 30K steps (31.0 vs 30.5); at 60K steps Gaussian-Flow surpasses 4D-GS (32.0 vs 31.0). On Plenoptic Video, K-Planes [8] achieves a marginally higher PSNR (31.6 vs 30.5 at 30K) but requires 1.8 hours of training versus 22.5 minutes, and Gaussian-Flow matches or exceeds its SSIM (0.97 vs 0.96). DyNeRF [18] is dominated on both axes (29.6 PSNR / 1344 hours vs 30.5 PSNR / 22.5 minutes). The one clear loss is on extremely thin structures (e.g., 3D Printer thread in HyperNeRF), where 4D-GS and TiNeuVox produce sharper results due to their implicit deformation fields.

## Relation to Prior Work

Gaussian-Flow builds directly on 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [14], extending its static point-based representation to the dynamic 4D setting. It departs from the dominant NeRF-based dynamic scene lineage (D-NeRF, HyperNeRF, Nerfies) and from concurrent 3DGS-based dynamic methods (4D-GS, [38]) that rely on implicit neural fields to encode temporal deformation. The key conceptual departure is replacing the neural motion field with a purely parametric Dual-Domain Deformation Model (DDDM) that fits each Gaussian's position, rotation, and radiance residuals via joint polynomial and Fourier series, preserving the discretized, network-free nature of the original 3DGS pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the neural network from the deformation model, Gaussian-Flow achieves 5× faster training than per-frame 3DGS and 125 FPS rendering while matching or exceeding prior SOTA in novel-view quality, making real-time 4D reconstruction practically viable for VR and interactive applications. The discretized point representation also naturally supports segmentation, editing, and composition of dynamic scenes, opening downstream applications. However, the method struggles with extremely thin structures (e.g., 3D printer thread), and the fixed-order polynomial/Fourier basis may limit scalability to very long or highly non-stationary videos. An open question is whether the parametric deformation model can be made adaptive per-point in order, or whether hybrid approaches combining DDDM with learned components could close the remaining quality gap on complex topologies.
