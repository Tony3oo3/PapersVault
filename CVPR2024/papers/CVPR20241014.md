## Problem

Existing deep image watermarking methods protect copyright but cannot localize tampered regions, while passive tamper-localization networks require training on specific tampering types and fail on realistic AIGC-based edits. No prior method simultaneously achieves tamper-agnostic pixel-level localization and robust copyright recovery with high precision and broad generalizability to unseen manipulation types.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241014/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

On the encoding side, EditGuard takes an original RGB image (128×128 or 512×512), a user-chosen 2D localization watermark of identical spatial dimensions (any natural image or even a solid-color image), and a 1D copyright watermark (64-bit binary string), producing a single imperceptible container image. On the decoding side, the received (potentially tampered and degraded) image is processed to yield a binary H×W tamper mask and the recovered 64-bit copyright string. The localization watermark must be pre-shared between encoder and decoder, and the entire pipeline operates zero-shot with respect to tampering—no tampered samples are needed at inference or training time.

## Method

The key insight is that image-into-image (I2I) steganography exhibits fragility (decoded secret degrades when the container is altered) and locality (degradation artifacts correspond pixel-wise to the tampered region), allowing the dual forensics task to be reformulated as a united image-bit steganography network (IBSN) whose training is fully decoupled from tampering types. The IBSN comprises an Image Hiding Module (stacked invertible blocks with DWT, enhanced affine coupling layers, and a lightweight feature interaction module), a Bit Encryption Module (U-style network modulating copyright bits into multi-level image features), a Bit Recovery Module, and an Image Revealing Module, plus a prompt-based posterior estimation module that uses N learnable degradation prompts to predict the lost high-frequency channels of the localization watermark. Training uses a bi-level strategy: first the bit modules are optimized with an L2 loss, then they are frozen while the image modules are jointly trained with L1+L2 losses under only degradation (no tampering). At inference, the binary tamper mask is obtained by thresholding the per-pixel maximum absolute difference between the pre-defined and decoded localization watermarks, while copyright bits are read directly from the BRM output.

## Results

On the constructed AGE-Set-C benchmark covering six AIGC-based editing methods (Stable Diffusion Inpaint, ControlNet, SDXL, RePaint, Lama, FaceSwap), EditGuard achieves F1-scores of 0.896–0.968, AUC of 0.943–0.989, and IoU of 0.876–0.940, while maintaining bit accuracy above 99.86% for copyright recovery. On four classical tamper localization benchmarks (CASIA, MFC, COVERAGE, and one additional), EditGuard outperforms the prior SOTA (TruFor) by F1 margins of 0.065–0.441 without any labeled tampered data. Under degradations (Gaussian noise σ=5, JPEG Q=70, Poisson noise), EditGuard retains F1 > 0.92 and BA > 97.7%, whereas the best passive baseline (finetuned MVSS-Net) drops to F1 0.458–0.652.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EditGuard decisively outperforms all passive tamper localization baselines on AIGC-based editing: the strongest competitor HiFi-Net [18] reaches at most F1 0.781 (FaceSwap), while EditGuard achieves 0.896–0.968 across all six editing methods. Even after finetuning MVSS-Net [8] on the constructed AGE-Set-C (denoted MVSS-Net†), its F1 drops to 0.185–0.694 with catastrophic forgetting across tamper types, whereas EditGuard maintains >0.89 F1 zero-shot. On classical benchmarks, EditGuard surpasses TruFor [17] (prior SOTA) by F1 margins of 0.065–0.441, and also outperforms PSCC-Net [39], OSN [61], and IML-ViT [45] by 0.7+ F1 points. For watermarking quality (Table 2), EditGuard achieves the best NIQE (5.567 at 128×128; 4.257 at 512×512) among all compared methods, slightly trails CIN [44] in PSNR (36.93 vs 41.35 dB) and SSIM (0.944 vs 0.981), but surpasses SepMark [62], PIMoG [10], and MBRS [26] on all visual quality metrics while embedding far more information (a full RGB localization watermark plus 64-bit copyright trace). Notably, none of the localization baselines provide any copyright protection capability, whereas EditGuard simultaneously achieves >99.8% bit accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

EditGuard builds on the invertible neural network steganography paradigm (Lu et al., CVPR 2021; Mou et al., CVPR 2023) for its image-hiding/revealing core, and on deep bit-into-image watermarking (CIN, SepMark, PIMoG) for copyright traceability. It departs from both lines by observing that I2I steganography's fragility and locality—previously treated as limitations—can be repurposed as a tamper-localization signal, thereby converting the dual forensics problem into a single joint image-bit steganography training objective. This fundamentally differs from passive localization networks (MVSS-Net, TruFor, HiFi-Net) that require tampered training data and search for artifacts, and from prior watermarking methods that embed only 1D copyright bits without spatial localization capability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

In the AIGC era, the ability to simultaneously verify ownership and pinpoint exactly where an image was manipulated is a practical legal and trust need that no prior method addresses in a unified, tamper-agnostic way. The zero-shot generalization to unseen AIGC editing methods (Stable Diffusion Inpaint, ControlNet, SDXL, RePaint, Lama, FaceSwap) without any tampered training data is a strong practical advantage over passive forensics. Open questions include robustness to adversarial watermark removal, extension to video/audio/3D modalities, whether the pre-defined solid-color localization watermark is optimal versus learned alternatives, and whether the approach degrades under extreme global transformations (e.g., full-image regeneration) that the paper acknowledges as Case 1 failure.
