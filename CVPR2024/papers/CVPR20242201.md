## Problem

Existing autonomous driving systems rely solely on fixed-format inputs (sensor data, waypoints, action commands), limiting their ability to comprehend natural language and interact with human passengers or navigation software. While LLM-based driving methods have been proposed, all prior work is evaluated in open-loop settings, ignoring critical issues like cumulative error, temporal action consistency, and the need to adapt to environment changes caused by one's own actions. LMDrive is the first framework to leverage LLMs for closed-loop end-to-end autonomous driving with natural language instruction following.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a temporal sequence of up to T_max frames, each containing 4 RGB camera views (left, front, right, rear, plus a center-cropped front focus view), a 64-channel LiDAR point cloud (600K points/sec), a navigation instruction string, and an optional notice instruction string. At each frame, the vision encoder produces ~406 visual tokens (50×50 BEV tokens, 5 waypoint tokens, 1 traffic light token) that are compressed to M=4 tokens per frame by the Q-Former before entering the LLM. The model outputs N=5 future waypoints and a binary instruction-completion flag per frame; at inference, only the latest frame's prediction is executed, and two PID controllers (latitudinal and longitudinal) convert predicted waypoints into brake, throttle, and steering signals.

## Method

LMDrive couples a frozen multi-modal vision encoder with a frozen 7B LLM (LLaVA-v1.5) via a trainable Q-Former and two 2-layer MLP adapters. The vision encoder (ResNet-50 for images, PointPillars for LiDAR, followed by a transformer BEV decoder) is first pre-trained on three perception tasks—object detection (CenterPoint), future waypoint prediction (GRU), and traffic light classification—then frozen to produce scene-rich visual tokens. The Q-Former, following BLIP-2, uses M=4 learnable queries with cross-attention to compress each frame's ~406 visual tokens down to 4, making it feasible to feed hundreds of historic frames into the LLM's context window. During instruction-finetuning, only the Q-Former and adapters are trainable while the LLM and vision encoder remain frozen; the model is trained with l1 waypoint loss and cross-entropy completion loss, and is explicitly trained to reject misleading instructions by labeling them as 'completed' after ~1 second. The resulting framework is evaluated in closed-loop on the CARLA simulator using the newly proposed LangAuto benchmark, which tests instruction following under varied weather, route lengths, adversarial notice instructions, and sequential multi-sentence commands.

## Results

On the LangAuto benchmark (routes >500m), LLaVA-v1.5 as the LLM backbone achieves the best driving score of 36.2±2.3, route completion of 46.5±4.3%, and infraction score of 0.81±0.03. On the shorter LangAuto-Short track (150–500m), it reaches DS 50.6±1.7 and RC 60.0±3.4%, and on LangAuto-Tiny (<150m), DS 66.5±3.6 and RC 77.9±2.3%. A randomly initialized 7B LLM performs far worse (DS 10.7±3.8 on LangAuto), confirming the necessity of pretrained language models. Ablations show that removing the Q-Former drops DS from 36.2 to 31.7, and removing vision encoder pre-training drops DS to 16.9.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare LMDrive against other end-to-end driving methods; instead, it ablates the choice of LLM backbone within the same framework. LLaVA-v1.5 (multi-modal pretrained) outperforms all text-only LLMs, achieving DS 36.2 vs. 34.0 (Vicuna-v1.5) and 32.8 (LLaMA2) on LangAuto, demonstrating that multi-modal pretraining is the single most important factor. Among text-only models, instruction-tuned variants (Vicuna-v1.5, Vicuna) consistently outperform their base counterparts (LLaMA2, LLaMA) by 1–2 DS points, confirming the value of conversation fine-tuning. A randomly initialized 7B LLM achieves only DS 10.7, roughly one-third of the best pretrained model, underscoring that pretrained language knowledge is essential for instruction-following driving. On the LangAuto-Notice track, both LLaVA-v1.5 and Vicuna-v1.5 show improved infraction scores (0.87 and 0.91 vs. 0.81 and 0.85 on LangAuto) with fewer collisions, validating the benefit of real-time notice instructions.

## Relation to Prior Work

LMDrive sits at the intersection of two previously separate research threads: end-to-end autonomous driving (UniAD, TransFuser, ReasonNet, InterFuser) and LLM-based driving reasoning (GPT-Driver, LanguageMPC, DriveGPT4). Prior LLM-driving methods operated exclusively in open-loop settings, generating actions evaluated against expert trajectories without executing them in a dynamic environment, while prior end-to-end driving methods relied solely on fixed-format inputs (waypoints, sensor data) with no language interface. LMDrive departs from both by adopting the Q-Former token compression strategy from BLIP-2 and the multi-modal sensor fusion architecture inspired by InterFuser, then integrating them with a frozen LLM backbone (best: LLaVA-v1.5) to produce control signals in a closed-loop CARLA environment for the first time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework to demonstrate that a frozen LLM can serve as the reasoning core for closed-loop, instruction-following autonomous driving, opening a path toward AVs that interact with passengers and navigation software via natural language. The LangAuto benchmark and 64K-clip dataset provide the community with the first standardized evaluation protocol for language-guided closed-loop driving, including adversarial (misleading) and sequential instruction settings. Key open questions remain: whether the approach generalizes beyond CARLA to real-world driving, how it scales to more complex or ambiguous instructions, and whether the 7B model can meet real-time latency constraints. The modest driving scores (DS ~36 on the full benchmark) indicate substantial room for improvement before practical deployment.
