## Problem

3D Gaussian Splatting suffers from severe aliasing artifacts and rendering speed degradation at lower resolutions or distant camera positions, because small Gaussians in detail-rich regions concentrate into single pixels and the per-Gaussian low-pass filter in EWA splatting paradoxically enlarges them, causing front-most small Gaussians to dominate pixel color. No prior anti-aliasing solution has been proposed specifically for the 3D Gaussian splatting representation, which lacks the positional encodings or feature grids that enable multi-scale strategies in NeRF-based methods.

## Input / Output

The method takes a 3D Gaussian scene representation (initialized from a point cloud and densified via splitting/cloning) along with camera poses, and produces rendered novel view images at arbitrary resolution scales. The scene is maintained at 4 levels of detail corresponding to 1×, 4×, 16×, and 64× downsampled resolutions, and the rendering resolution scale determines which subset of Gaussians is selected via a pixel-coverage criterion (threshold S_T = 2 px, relative bounds 0.5–1.5). Training uses both original and downsampled supervision images, with the model trained for 40,000 iterations on a single RTX 3090.

## Method

The core mechanism introduces multi-scale 3D Gaussians by aggregating small fine-level Gaussians into larger coarse-level Gaussians during training: after a 1,000-iteration warm-up, Gaussians whose pixel coverage falls below a threshold are binned into a voxel grid and merged via average pooling, then enlarged to an appropriate size for their target LOD level. At render time, each Gaussian's 'pixel coverage' (the smaller of its horizontal or vertical splatted axis length at a 1/255 opacity level set) is compared against stored min/max coverage ranges to selectively include or exclude it, ensuring only Gaussians at the appropriate scale for the current resolution contribute to the image. This scene-level filtering replaces the per-Gaussian low-pass filter of EWA splatting, avoiding both the color-dominance artifact from enlarged small Gaussians and the sequential alpha-blending slowdown from rendering all Gaussians at every pixel. The approach adds fewer than 5% extra Gaussians while achieving 13–66% PSNR and 160–2400% speed improvements at 4×–128× downsampled scales.

## Results

On Mip-NeRF360, the proposed multi-scale 3D Gaussian splatting achieves 25.35 PSNR at 64× downsampled resolution versus 15.23 PSNR for the original 3D Gaussian Splatting, while reducing rendering time from 103.3 ms to 4.9 ms (≈21× speedup). At 16× scale, PSNR improves from 17.79 to 24.75 and time drops from 27.9 ms to 4.9 ms. At the native 1× resolution, quality remains comparable (27.39 vs. 27.52 PSNR) with a slight speed advantage (9.1 ms vs. 10.5 ms). Across all three datasets, the method delivers 6–10 dB PSNR gains and 16–21× speed improvements at 64× scale, with the gap widening as resolution decreases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the original 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., SIGGRAPH 2023). The proposed method decisively outperforms this baseline at every downsampled scale (4×, 16×, 64×) on all three datasets in both PSNR and rendering time. On Mip-NeRF360, the PSNR gap grows from +2.32 dB at 4× to +10.12 dB at 64×, while rendering time drops from 9.3 ms to 4.9 ms (vs. 103.3 ms for the baseline). On Tanks and Temples, the 64× PSNR improves from 13.88 to 19.67 dB with time reduced from 82.6 ms to 5.9 ms. On Deep Blending, the 64× PSNR jumps from 17.75 to 25.70 dB and time falls from 59.7 ms to 3.4 ms. At the native 1× resolution, the method is essentially on par: PSNR is within 0.3 dB (slightly lower on Mip-NeRF360 and Tanks, marginally higher on Deep Blending) and rendering time is comparable, confirming that the multi-scale representation does not sacrifice high-resolution fidelity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023) by introducing a multi-scale Gaussian hierarchy inspired by mipmap and level-of-detail algorithms from traditional computer graphics. It departs from the per-Gaussian low-pass filter inherited from EWA Splatting (Zwicker et al., 2001), which the authors show actually worsens aliasing at low resolutions by inflating small Gaussians. Unlike NeRF-based anti-aliasing methods (Mip-NeRF, Zip-NeRF, Tri-MipNeRF) that operate on positional encodings or feature grids, this work addresses aliasing at the scene-representation level by aggregating small Gaussians into coarser ones and performing selective rendering based on pixel coverage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This addresses a critical practical limitation of 3D Gaussian Splatting for large-scale or multi-scale scenes, where rendering from distant cameras or at low resolutions currently produces severe artifacts and paradoxically slows down. The 160%–2400% speed gains at 4×–128× downscaling make real-time rendering at varying resolutions feasible without retraining. A key open question is the acknowledged limitation that all Gaussians must still be splatted before filtering, preventing linear speed scaling with resolution; the authors suggest future work on pre-splatting filtering criteria. The method's reliance on empirical thresholds (S_T, S_rel^max, S_rel^min) and the 4-level hierarchy also leave room for adaptive or learned scale selection.
