## Problem

Large kernel convolutions suffer from O(K²) parameter complexity, causing parameter explosion and severe optimization difficulties that prevent further kernel scaling beyond ~51×51. Current workarounds like stripe convolution (51×5+5×51) sacrifice dense grid convolution's superior spatial perception, and the paper identifies that dense convolution consistently outperforms stripe forms across kernel sizes, especially on downstream tasks. The key question is whether dense grid convolution can be preserved while drastically reducing its parameter cost to enable unprecedented kernel sizes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240388/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images (224×224 or 384×384 for classification, 2048×512 for segmentation, 1280×800 for detection) and produces task-specific outputs via standard heads: class labels for ImageNet, pixel-wise masks for ADE20K segmentation, and bounding boxes with instance masks for COCO detection. The backbone uses extremely large dense kernels (up to 101×101) that, after the 4× stem downsampling, can cover the entire feature map at 384×384 input, effectively performing global convolution in a single layer.

## Method

PeLK introduces peripheral convolution, a spatial parameter-sharing mechanism inspired by human foveal-peripheral vision that reduces dense convolution's parameter complexity from O(K²) to O(log K). The sharing grid keeps fine-grained (unshared) parameters in a small central region (~5×5 or 7×7) while applying exponentially-increasing sharing granularity outward, so peripheral positions share weights over progressively larger areas. A kernel-wise positional embedding (a small learned bias added to input features at each kernel position) compensates for detail blurring caused by wide-range sharing, with negligible parameter overhead. The architecture follows a 4-stage ConvNeXt/SLaK framework with dynamic sparsity and a partial peripheral convolution design where only 3/8 of channels pass through the large kernel while the rest use identity mapping, further reducing FLOPs. This enables dense kernels of 51×51 and 101×101 with over 90% parameter reduction compared to fully dense counterparts, yielding consistent gains on ImageNet, ADE20K, and COCO.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K (224×224), PeLK-B achieves 84.2% top-1 accuracy with 89M params and 18.3G FLOPs, outperforming ConvNeXt-B (83.8%), SLaK-B (84.0%), and Swin-B (83.5%). At 384×384, PeLK-B-101 reaches 85.8% top-1, the best among all compared methods. On ADE20K semantic segmentation, PeLK-B-101 achieves 50.6% mIoU (vs. SLaK-B 50.2%, RepLKNet-B 49.9%), and on COCO detection, PeLK-B-101 attains 53.1 AP^box / 46.1 AP^mask, surpassing all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PeLK consistently outperforms all compared baselines across all three tasks. On ImageNet-1K (224²), PeLK-B (84.2%) beats ConvNeXt-B (83.8%) by 0.4%, SLaK-B (84.0%) by 0.2%, and Swin-B (83.5%) by 0.7%, while using fewer parameters (89M vs. 95M for SLaK-B). At 384², PeLK-B-101 (85.8%) edges out SLaK-B (85.5%) and ConvNeXt-B (85.1%), demonstrating that scaling the kernel to 101×101 yields consistent gains. On ADE20K, PeLK-B-101 (50.6% mIoU) surpasses SLaK-B (50.2%) and RepLKNet-B (49.9%), with the gap widening at smaller model sizes (PeLK-T: 48.1% vs. SLaK-T: 47.6%). On COCO, PeLK-B-101 achieves 53.1 AP^box and 46.1 AP^mask, exceeding ConvNeXt-B (52.7/45.6) and SLaK-B (52.5/45.5). Notably, PeLK also achieves 1.5× higher inference throughput than SLaK-T (1138 vs. 754 images/s on A100) despite using dense rather than stripe convolution.

## Relation to Prior Work

PeLK extends the large-kernel CNN lineage (ConvNeXt 7×7 → RepLKNet 31×31 → SLaK 51×51 stripe) by first empirically establishing that dense grid convolution consistently outperforms stripe convolution, then addressing the resulting O(K²) parameter explosion through a biologically-inspired peripheral convolution with spatial parameter sharing. It adopts SLaK's unified framework and dynamic sparsity, and ConvNeXt's 4-stage architecture, but departs from both by preserving the dense computational form while reducing parameter complexity to O(log K) via an exponentially-increasing sharing grid and kernel-wise positional embedding. The design is explicitly motivated by human peripheral vision's focus-and-blur mechanism, distinguishing it from prior large-kernel works that relied on decomposition or re-parameterization tricks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PeLK demonstrates that dense kernels can be scaled to 101×101 with only ~1% parameter overhead relative to a 5×5 kernel, opening a path to much larger effective receptive fields for high-resolution tasks (e.g., 2048×512 segmentation) where prior stripe-based methods saturate. The finding that a tiny central region (~1% of the kernel) carries most of the representational load while peripheral regions tolerate heavy sharing provides a principled, biologically-grounded design principle for future large-kernel architectures. Open questions include whether even larger kernels benefit at higher training resolutions, whether the approach transfers to 3D/video settings, and whether the dense-over-stripe advantage persists under different training regimes or with attention-based backbones.
