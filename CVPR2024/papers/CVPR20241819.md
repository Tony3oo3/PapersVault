## Problem

Existing roadside perception methods rely on single-infrastructure sensors, which suffer from limited sensing range and blind spots that prevent comprehensive understanding of a traffic area. While cross-infrastructure cooperation could resolve these limitations, no real-world, large-scale dataset exists for roadside cooperative perception, and the unique challenges of roadside cooperation—particularly data heterogeneity from mixed LiDAR types (multiline + MEMS) and infrastructure-specific mounting characteristics—remain unexplored.

## Input / Output

Each frame provides multi-agent sensor data: corridor scenes contain 4 RGB images (1920×1200 at 42 Hz) and 2 pre-merged point clouds (80-beam + 32-beam LiDAR groups), while intersection scenes add 2 raw MEMS LiDAR point clouds, with inter-agent synchronization under 50 ms. The relative posture (LiDAR-to-World calibration) between infrastructure nodes is supplied per frame, and 3D bounding boxes are annotated in each agent's local LiDAR coordinate system. Outputs are 7-DoF 3D bounding boxes (x, y, z, l, w, h, yaw) with 10-class category labels and confidence scores for detection, and persistent trajectory IDs for tracking, evaluated over 400 m × 400 m fixed traffic regions.

## Method

The paper introduces the RCooper dataset (50 k images, 30 k point clouds across 410 scenarios at 3 Hz) with a three-step annotation pipeline: manual 7-DoF labeling per infrastructure, automatic cooperative annotation via Hungarian-algorithm matching in a unified world coordinate system, and final manual refinement. For benchmarking, four fusion strategies—no fusion, late fusion (NMS on per-agent detections), early fusion (concatenated point clouds), and intermediate fusion (AttFuse, F-Cooper, Where2Comm, CoBEVT)—are evaluated with a shared PointPillar backbone trained for 50 epochs. Tracking uses the AB3Dmot tracker (3D Kalman filter + Hungarian association) on top of cooperative detection outputs. A key empirical finding is that in corridor scenes cooperation consistently improves detection, but in intersection scenes the MEMS LiDAR data heterogeneity causes early and intermediate fusion to underperform even the no-fusion baseline, while late fusion remains robust—revealing a critical gap for future roadside cooperative representation learning.

## Results

On the corridor scene, cooperative detection substantially outperforms the no-fusion baseline (AP@0.5: 29.2%), with early fusion reaching 54.7% and CoBEVT achieving the best intermediate-fusion result of 57.2% AP@0.5 and 36.2% AP@0.7. On the intersection scene, the picture is more nuanced: late fusion leads at AP@0.3 (65.1%) and AP@0.5 (47.6%), while early fusion actually degrades below the no-fusion baseline (50.0% vs. 58.1% AP@0.3) due to data heterogeneity from mixed MEMS and multiline LiDARs. For tracking on the corridor scene, early fusion achieves the best AMOTA (23.78) and MOTA (44.30), while on the intersection scene late fusion leads with AMOTA 21.57 and MOTA 50.58.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In corridor scenes, all four intermediate-fusion baselines (AttFuse, F-Cooper, Where2Comm, CoBEVT) clearly surpass the no-fusion baseline by 12–28 points in AP@0.5, confirming the benefit of cross-infrastructure cooperation for extending sensing range. CoBEVT [40] edges out F-Cooper [7] and Where2Comm [19] at AP@0.5 (57.2 vs. 55.8 vs. 55.6) and AP@0.7 (36.2 vs. 36.1 vs. 34.3), while AttFuse [38] trails by roughly 5–6 points. In intersection scenes, the comparison is starkly different: F-Cooper [7] collapses to 32.0% AP@0.5 (below the 44.1% no-fusion baseline), and AttFuse [38] also underperforms no-fusion at AP@0.3 (45.5 vs. 58.1), revealing that vehicle-centric cooperative methods fail under the data heterogeneity introduced by mixed MEMS and multiline LiDARs. CoBEVT [40] is the most robust intermediate-fusion method in intersections, achieving 45.6% AP@0.5 and 32.6% AP@0.7, but still does not surpass the simple late-fusion strategy (47.6% AP@0.5). The ablation in Fig. 6 confirms that removing the two MEMS LiDARs restores the expected cooperative gains, pinpointing sensor heterogeneity as the root cause of the SOTA methods' failure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by shifting cooperative perception from the well-studied vehicle-centric setting (V2V, V2I) to infrastructure-infrastructure cooperation, where fixed mounting positions, heterogeneous sensor types (multiline + MEMS LiDAR), and area-coverage objectives create fundamentally different challenges. It extends the roadside perception line (Rope3D, DAIR-V2X-I, MonoGAE, BEVHeight), which has focused on single-infrastructure sensing, by introducing cross-infrastructure collaboration as the core mechanism. The benchmark repurposes existing vehicle-centric cooperative methods (AttFuse, F-Cooper, Where2Comm, CoBEVT) as baselines, deliberately exposing their limitations when transplanted to the roadside domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first real-world large-scale dataset for roadside cooperative perception, RCooper unblocks a previously intractable research direction for both autonomous driving and traffic management. The empirical finding that data heterogeneity (mixed multiline and MEMS LiDAR) causes SOTA feature-fusion methods to underperform even naive no-fusion baselines in intersection scenes is a practical warning: existing cooperative perception pipelines cannot be naively transferred to infrastructure settings. Key open questions include learning unified representations across heterogeneous sensor modalities, end-to-end cooperative tracking that exploits spatial-temporal context, and robustness to calibration noise in deployed systems.
