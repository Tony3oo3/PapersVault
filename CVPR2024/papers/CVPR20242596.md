## Problem

State-of-the-art inverse rendering methods rely on volumetric (NeRF-style) rendering, which incurs excessive computational cost from per-ray multi-sampling and struggles with ambiguous BRDF and normal estimation under limited light-view angular samples. Point-based rendering offers a more efficient alternative but faces three critical gaps for inverse rendering: discrete points cannot simultaneously capture smooth and detailed surfaces, joint geometry-reflectance recovery from sparse angular samples is ill-posed, and efficient shadow handling compatible with point representations is missing.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242596/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes either multi-view multi-light images (e.g., 15 views × 16 lightings on DiLiGenT-MV) or photometric images (300 views with co-located point lights), along with binary object masks for initialization and shape guidance. It produces an explicit 3D point cloud (positions and radii), a continuous SDF for smooth normals, a spatially-varying BRDF decomposed into per-point diffuse albedo and K weighted specular basis BRDFs, and per-point visibility flags. The forward rendering uses a simplified single-light rendering equation per pixel, valid for both multi-light and photometric setups, and extends to environment-map rendering by accumulating radiance across multiple light sources.

## Method

DPIR introduces a hybrid point-volumetric geometry representation where 3D points (position + radius) serve as the sole rendering primitives for fast splatting, while a coordinate-based SDF MLP provides smooth surface normals via gradient differentiation, decoupling point motion from normal estimation. For reflectance, a regularized basis-BRDF model decomposes the BRDF into a per-position diffuse albedo MLP and a specular component expressed as a weighted sum of K basis BRDFs, with l1-norm regularization (lower bound ε) and positivity constraints on the specular coefficients to exploit spatial coherency and mitigate ill-posedness. Shadow detection uses a point-based shadow map: a virtual orthographic camera at each light source renders a z-buffer via splatting, and visibility is determined by a differentiable step function comparing point depth to the stored depth. Forward rendering projects point radiances onto the camera plane using α-blending with distance-sorted points, and all parameters (point positions, radii, SDF, diffuse albedo, specular basis, and coefficients) are jointly optimized in a single stage via analysis-by-synthesis with L2, SSIM, SDF zero-level, coefficient-regularization, and mask losses—requiring no pretrained network.

## Results

On DiLiGenT-MV (single-light setting), DPIR achieves PSNR of 43.21 (Bear), 37.62 (Buddha), 38.23 (Cow), 39.32 (Pot2), and 35.75 (Reading), outperforming PS-NeRF by 5–8 dB across objects. In terms of efficiency, DPIR trains in 2 hours with 5 MB memory versus PS-NeRF's 22 hours and 40 MB (10× faster, 8× less memory). On the photometric dataset, DPIR reaches PSNR 35.56 and MAE 8.74° versus IRON's 31.91 and 9.38°, while training 6× faster (2 h vs. 12 h) with 5× lower memory (5 MB vs. 28 MB).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DPIR outperforms all four baselines across every reported metric. Against PS-NeRF [46] (the closest competitor in rendering quality), DPIR gains 5–8 dB PSNR on DiLiGenT-MV single-light (e.g., 43.21 vs. 35.19 on Bear) and reduces training time from 22 h to 2 h with 8× less memory (5 MB vs. 40 MB); in the multi-light setting the PSNR gap narrows to 1–5 dB but DPIR still leads on all objects. Against PhySG [51] and TensoIR [11], DPIR achieves substantially higher PSNR (e.g., 43.21 vs. 24.52 for PhySG on Bear) and lower normal MAE (4.35° vs. 11.35° for PhySG, 4.35° vs. 35.93° for TensoIR on Bear), while also training faster than both. On the photometric dataset, DPIR surpasses IRON [50] by 3.65 dB PSNR (35.56 vs. 31.91), 0.64° lower normal MAE (8.74 vs. 9.38), 6× faster training (2 h vs. 12 h), and 5× lower memory (5 MB vs. 28 MB). Notably, DPIR requires no pre-trained network, unlike PS-NeRF which depends on a pre-trained photometric-stereo network.

## Relation to Prior Work

DPIR bridges two previously separate research threads: point-based rendering (developed primarily for novel-view synthesis, e.g., 3D Gaussian Splatting [12], differentiable point-based radiance fields [52]) and analysis-by-synthesis inverse rendering (dominated by volumetric NeRF-based methods like PS-NeRF [46], NeRV [37], and NeRFactor [53]). It inherits the SDF-based normal estimation from NeuS [40] and the basis-BRDF decomposition from Li et al. [18], but departs from the volumetric per-ray integration paradigm by replacing it with point splatting and a z-buffer-based shadow test. The key conceptual move is treating points as the sole rendering primitive while retaining a continuous SDF for geometric stability, a hybrid that had not been applied to inverse rendering before.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 10× training speedup and 8× memory reduction over the closest competitor (PS-NeRF) make high-quality inverse rendering substantially more accessible, and the explicit point representation enables intuitive geometry and reflectance editing that volumetric methods cannot offer. However, DPIR models only direct reflection without global illumination effects (inter-reflections, ambient occlusion) and does not handle transmission, limiting its applicability to complex scenes and translucent materials. Extending the point-based framework to handle indirect lighting and BSDFs beyond BRDFs would be a natural next step to broaden its practical impact.
