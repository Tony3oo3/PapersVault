## Problem

Existing photometric stereo datasets define reflectance using qualitative semantic labels (e.g., 'steel,' 'wood,' 'specular metallic'), which are ambiguous, non-metric, and unordered, making it impossible to systematically evaluate method performance across a continuous reflectance range or predict accuracy for intermediate materials. Additionally, prior real-world datasets focus almost exclusively on opaque surfaces, leaving the impact of varying translucency levels on photometric stereo largely unexplored.

## Input / Output

Each object in DiLiGenRT is captured as 100 RGB images under uniformly distributed directional point lights from a fixed camera, with ground truth surface normals known analytically (hemisphere) or from CAD models (DiLiGenRT-S). The dataset is structured as a 6×9 translucency-by-roughness matrix where each cell corresponds to a specific pair of quantified reflectance properties (absorptive capacity σ_t and surface area roughness Sa), enabling interpolation-based performance prediction for unmeasured materials. Evaluation outputs are mean angular error (MAE) matrices over the full reflectance grid, plus per-method performance profiles under sparse (10) and dense (100) light settings.

## Method

The dataset is manufactured by sandblasting 9 identical aluminum alloy molds with grit sizes ranging from #3000 to #20 (FEPA standard) to create 9 discrete roughness levels, then injecting transparent silicone blended with color paste at 6 concentrations (1/1 to 1/64) to create 6 translucency levels, yielding 54 hemispheres with controlled, quantified reflectance. Roughness is measured via white light interferometry (Zygo Nexview NX2, 0.15 nm vertical resolution) using the Sa metric, while translucency is quantified via the Beer-Lambert law (σ_t = εc) measured on 3 mm thin-disk samples. The benchmark evaluates 11+ photometric stereo methods (non-learning, learning-based, and uncalibrated) to produce roughness-translucency MAE heatmaps that reveal each method's working range. A supplementary synthetic dataset PS-SSS (3,000 scenes with Disney Principled BSDF, subsurface scattering parameter 0.1–1) is rendered in Blender to enable fine-tuning of existing networks, reducing PS-FCN's mean MAE from 16.38° to 9.92° on highly translucent surfaces.

## Results

On DiLiGenRT (54 sphere objects), SDM-UniPS achieves the smallest average MAE, threefold lower than the second-best method MS-PS. On DiLiGenRT-S (3 diverse shapes: PAW, LEAF, FLOWER), SDM-UniPS degrades significantly (e.g., 29.5° on LEAF vs. 17.9° for PS-FCN), likely due to overfitting to sphere-like shapes in its training data. Among non-learning-based methods, TH46 (position thresholding) consistently outperforms LS, TH28, and DeconvPS. Fine-tuning PS-FCN on the synthetic PS-SSS dataset reduces its mean MAE from 16.38° to 9.92°, with the largest gains on highly translucent surfaces (σ_t ≤ 0.57).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among non-learning-based methods, TH46 (position thresholding at 46%) dominates LS, TH28, and DeconvPS across all DiLiGenRT-S objects, e.g., 16.5° vs. 17.9° on PAW. DeconvPS, designed for translucent surfaces, only marginally improves over LS (21.1° vs. 21.4° on LEAF) and remains limited by its Lambertian assumption. Among learning-based methods, MS-PS excels on highly translucent FLOWER (20.7°) and LEAF (18.1°), while PS-FCN wins on LEAF (17.9°) and CNN-PS on FLOWER (23.7°). SDM-UniPS, the best performer on DiLiGenRT spheres, collapses on DiLiGenRT-S (29.5° on LEAF, 23.2° on FLOWER), confirming overfitting to sphere geometry. Under sparse illumination (10 lights), all-pixel methods (Att-PSN, MS-PS) outperform per-pixel methods (CNN-PS, TH46) on 49 of 54 materials, whereas with 100 lights the advantage shifts to per-pixel methods for semi-translucent surfaces. The uncalibrated method SDPS-Net is more stable than PF14 across roughness and translucency levels, and fine-tuning PS-FCN on PS-SSS reduces its mean MAE from 16.38° to 9.92°.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the photometric stereo benchmarking lineage established by DiLiGenT, DiLiGenT10², and DiLiGenT-II, which all characterize reflectance through semantic labels (e.g., 'steel', 'wood', 'specular metallic'). The key conceptual departure is replacing these qualitative labels with physically quantified properties—surface area roughness (Sa) measured via white-light interferometry and absorptive capacity (σt) derived from the Beer-Lambert law—enabling continuous, ordered, and interpolable reflectance characterization. For the synthetic PS-SSS dataset, the paper follows the CyclesPS construction paradigm (Blender + Disney Principled BSDF) but specifically targets subsurface scattering parameters, filling a gap absent from all prior synthetic photometric stereo datasets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DiLiGenRT provides the first systematic, quantified performance profile of photometric stereo methods across a 6×9 roughness-translucency grid, enabling practitioners to select the best-fit method for a given material and to interpolate performance for unmeasured reflectances. A key open question is that highly translucent surfaces (σt ≤ 0.28) remain largely unsolved, with most methods exceeding 20° MAE, and the paper identifies explicit subsurface scattering modeling and differentiable rendering as promising but unexplored directions. The finding that SDM-UniPS overfits to sphere geometry in its synthetic training data, degrading sharply on non-spherical shapes, highlights a broader concern about shape bias in learned photometric stereo. The PS-SSS fine-tuning result (16.38° → 9.92° MAE) suggests that even modest synthetic data augmentation can substantially close the translucency gap, pointing toward a practical path forward.
