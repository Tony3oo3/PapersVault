## Problem

Prior work has linked specific biases that separate model vision from human vision—texture/shape bias, spectral biases, and critical band width—to generalization performance, but these studies suffered from architecture confounders, subset-only findings, and inconsistent benchmarking pipelines. It remains unclear whether any single bias can holistically explain generalization across diverse training methods and evaluation settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242682/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The study takes 48 ResNet-50 model checkpoints trained on ImageNet with varying methods (augmentation, adversarial training, contrastive learning, etc.) and evaluates each at 224×224 resolution on a unified suite of benchmarks spanning in-distribution, robustness, conceptual, and adversarial categories. Bias measurements are computed on the same checkpoints (cue-conflict dataset for shape bias, bandpass-filtered ImageNet for spectral bias, Gaussian-noise-inserted contrast-reduced images for critical band), and the final output is a matrix of Spearman rank correlations with permutation-test p-values linking each bias to each generalization category.

## Method

The core method is a controlled large-scale empirical study that fixes the architecture to unmodified ResNet-50 to eliminate inductive-bias confounders, then varies only the training procedure across 48 models in seven categories (baseline, augmentation, SIN pretraining, adversarial training at multiple ε budgets, contrastive learning, improved recipes, random convolutions). Generalization is assessed via top-1 accuracy averaged over four benchmark families (ImageNet/IN-v2/IN-ReaL; IN-C/IN-C̄/IN-A; IN-Renditions/IN-Sketch/SIN; PGD attack), all run under identical transformation pipelines on a single system to avoid system-noise artifacts. Biases are measured with methodological refinements over prior work: the critical band test is normalized by contrast-reduced accuracy, uses all 1,000 ImageNet classes, and leverages the full validation set rather than a 16-class subset. Correlations are computed via Spearman rank coefficient with two-sided permutation tests (p < 0.05 threshold), and scatterplots are inspected for non-monotonic trends and outliers that the rank statistic misses.

## Results

Across 48 ResNet-50 models, no single bias (shape, spectral, or critical band) holistically explains generalization. Shape bias is strongly *negatively* correlated with in-distribution accuracy (more texture bias → higher ID performance), while high-frequency bias shows a surprising moderate-to-strong positive correlation with most generalization categories except adversarial robustness. Low-frequency bias correlates only with adversarial robustness, and critical band bandwidth correlates with ID/robustness but *inversely* with adversarial robustness. The low-frequency and shape biases correlate with each other (r=0.66, p=2e-4), but ImageNet-C̄ emerges as the single best predictor of overall generalization (r̄=0.83 with other benchmarks).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method but compares 48 ResNet-50 models across seven training categories (baseline, augmentation, SIN pretraining, adversarial training, contrastive learning, TIMM/PyTorch recipes, random convolutions). Adversarially-trained (AT) models consistently show divergent bias–generalization trends from non-AT models: AT models exhibit an inverted-U relationship between shape bias and generalization, peaking at a balanced shape/texture ratio (~0.5), while non-AT models show only moderate correlations. SHAPENET (SIN-pretrained [18]) is a prominent outlier, achieving a high shape bias of 0.81 yet only a low-frequency bias of 0.56, contradicting the expected coupling. The baseline ResNet-50 [19] (shape bias 0.21, low-frequency bias 0.63) and the YFCC-100M self-supervised model [67] share the same 0.21 shape bias despite 100M additional training samples, showing data scale alone does not shift the bias. On ImageNet-A, the most texture-biased model (TIMM A3, shape bias 0.13) still underperforms the best model (TORCHVISION V2, shape bias 0.17) by 10.4%, indicating texture bias is neither necessary nor sufficient for natural adversarial robustness. Augmentation-based models (AugMix [22], DeepAugment [23], PRIME [37], etc.) cluster in the mid-range of shape bias (0.1–0.3) with no consistent advantage over the baseline on any single generalization category.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a systematic sanity check on three lines of prior work: Geirhos et al.'s texture/shape bias [17], Yin et al.'s spectral bias [61], and Subramanian et al.'s critical band analysis [52], each of which claimed correlations between model biases and generalization. It departs from these studies by fixing the architecture to ResNet-50 to eliminate inductive-bias confounders, using a consistent benchmarking pipeline across all 48 models, and evaluating generalization across four distinct categories (ID, robustness, concepts, adversarial) rather than single benchmarks. The paper explicitly identifies methodological limitations in prior work—architecture confounders, subset-only findings, and inconsistent test pipelines—as reasons why previously reported correlations may not hold under a more holistic evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that none of the three biases singularly explains generalization, and that some biases are even negatively correlated with human alignment on in-distribution performance—challenges the prevailing narrative that aligning model perception with human perception is a reliable path to better generalization. The counter-intuitive positive correlation between high-frequency bias and generalization (except adversarial robustness) opens a new question about whether models exploit frequency shortcuts in the data rather than achieving intrinsically better representations. Key limitations include restriction to ResNet-50 and ImageNet, and the open question of whether combinations of biases or additional unmeasured factors can explain generalization more completely. For practitioners, the practical takeaway is that directly optimizing for these biases as training priors is unlikely to yield broad generalization gains without additional safeguards.
