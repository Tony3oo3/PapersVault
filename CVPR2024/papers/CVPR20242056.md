## Problem

Existing CZSL methods overlook the varying specificity levels among attributes, tending to favor generic descriptors (e.g., 'Red') over more informative specific ones (e.g., 'Sliced') for the same object. Additionally, transitioning from Closed-World to Open-World CZSL causes a combinatorial explosion of the output space, and similarity-based composition filtering risks discarding valid but specific attribute-object pairs in favor of more generic compositions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242056/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image as input and predicts an attribute-object composition label (e.g., 'Sliced-Strawberry'). In Closed-World mode the output is restricted to the union of seen and unseen pairs; in Open-World mode the output space expands to all possible attribute-object combinations (up to 278,362 pairs on C-GQA). The text side encodes compositions via structured soft prompts of the form '[a] [photo] [of] [attribute] [object]' processed by a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 text encoder, while the image side uses the corresponding frozen CLIP image encoder augmented with lightweight adapters.

## Method

CDS-CZSL employs a 3-branch architecture on a frozen CLIP backbone: a composition-wise branch with fully learnable soft prompts for contextual pair-level classification, and two primitive-wise branches (attribute and object) each with a dedicated Multi-Head Self-Attention adapter and partially learnable prompts. The key novelty is a context-based and diversity-driven specificity learner: object representations are first attribute-denoised via a uniformity loss, then clustered with batch-level K-Means; an attribute's specificity is estimated by whether objects sharing that attribute co-cluster (low diversity → high specificity). A small FCN predicts context-dependent specificity from attribute and object word embeddings, which is applied as a training-time penalty to amplify specific attributes and, at test time in OW, as a dual-threshold filter to prune both overly generic and overly specific compositions from the search space.

## Results

In Closed-World CZSL, CDS-CZSL achieves AUC of 22.4 (MIT-States), 39.5 (UT-Zappos), and 11.1 (C-GQA), setting new SOTA on all three benchmarks. In Open-World CZSL with specificity-based filtering, it reaches AUC of 8.5, 32.3, and 2.68 respectively, again outperforming all prior methods. The model also attains the highest unseen accuracy (U) across all datasets in both settings (e.g., 52.9% on MIT-States CW, 61.3% on UT-Zappos OW), demonstrating that specificity learning does not compromise generalization to unseen compositions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CDS-CZSL outperforms the strongest CLIP-based baseline DFSP [19] by 1.8%, 3.2%, and 0.6% in AUC on MIT-States, UT-Zappos, and C-GQA respectively in CW, and by 1.7%, 2.0%, and 0.28% in OW. Against HPL [38], gains are larger: 2.2%, 4.5%, and 3.9% AUC in CW. The method also surpasses CSP [27] by 3.0–4.9% AUC across datasets. The only notable loss is on UT-Zappos CW HM, where the non-CLIP CGE [24] scores 60.5 vs. CDS-CZSL's 52.7, though CDS-CZSL still leads in AUC (39.5 vs. 33.5). In OW, CDS-CZSL with specificity filtering beats DFSP on all metrics except C-GQA seen accuracy (37.6 vs. 38.3), and its specificity-based filtering retains fewer candidate pairs (176 vs. 189 for similarity-based) while achieving higher HM and AUC, confirming it discards both overly generic and overly specific compositions more effectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate CZSL strategies—composition-wise classification (adapted from CSP's soft-prompt projection space) and primitive-wise attribute/object prediction (inspired by HPL's hierarchical prompts and Liu et al.'s attribute denoising)—into a single 3-branch CLIP-based framework. Its key conceptual departure is introducing attribute specificity as a first-class learning signal: prior CLIP-based CZSL methods (CSP, HPL, DFSP) optimize for overall accuracy without distinguishing informative specific attributes (e.g., 'Sliced') from generic ones (e.g., 'Red'), and the paper's diversity-driven specificity learner (using K-Means co-clustering of denoised object features) has no direct precedent in the CZSL literature. The specificity signal is then repurposed for composition-space filtering in the open-world setting, going beyond the similarity-based feasibility calibration used by DFSP and Co-CGE.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The specificity framing addresses a genuine gap: CZSL models systematically under-predict specific attributes in favor of generic ones, and the paper shows that explicitly modeling this bias improves both CW and OW performance. The specificity-based filtering for OW-CZSL is practically useful, reducing the search space from 278k to 30k pairs on C-GQA while retaining valid specific compositions that similarity-based methods discard. However, the approach has acknowledged limitations—the binary K-Means co-clustering proxy for diversity is heuristic, the filtering occasionally retains invalid specific pairs (e.g., 'Pressed-Camera'), and the model sometimes hallucinates specific attributes unsupported by image content. Open questions include whether the specificity concept generalizes to richer attribute taxonomies or multi-attribute compositions, and whether the batch-level K-Means approximation introduces systematic biases at scale.
