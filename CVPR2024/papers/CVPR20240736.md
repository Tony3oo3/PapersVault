## Problem

Training-free NAS methods rely on single zero-cost proxies to rank candidate architectures, but these proxies show weak correlations with actual network performance—sometimes performing no better than simple FLOPs or parameter counts. Naively ensembling existing proxies fails because gradient-based proxies are theoretically correlated with each other and several are computationally expensive, preventing a comprehensive and efficient evaluation of architectures from multiple perspectives.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240736/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes candidate network architectures (cells, residual blocks, or attention blocks) and evaluates them using a single batch of 64 Gaussian random inputs, with a FLOPs budget constraint guiding the evolutionary search. All four proxy scores are computed simultaneously within one forward and backward pass per architecture, yielding a final AZ-NAS score that ranks candidates. The output is a selected architecture (e.g., a MobileNetV2-style CNN or a ViT) that is then trained from scratch on the target dataset.

## Method

AZ-NAS assembles four complementary zero-cost proxies: (1) expressivity, measuring feature-space isotropy via the entropy of L1-normalized PCA eigenvalue coefficients at each primary block; (2) progressivity, capturing the minimum increase in block-level expressivity scores along network depth; (3) trainability, approximating the Jacobian of each block via Hutchinson's method with Rademacher random vectors and scoring how close the spectral norm is to 1; and (4) complexity, using FLOPs directly. These proxies are integrated through a non-linear ranking aggregation that sums the log of each proxy's rank ratio, penalizing architectures that rank poorly on any single proxy. An evolutionary search then mutates top-k architectures by AZ-NAS score to explore the search space, requiring no network training during the search phase.

## Results

On NAS-Bench-201, AZ-NAS achieves the best ranking consistency across all three datasets: Kendall's τ of 0.741 (CIFAR-10), 0.723 (CIFAR-100), and 0.710 (ImageNet16-120), substantially outperforming the next-best method ZiCo (0.589/0.590/0.584). The selected networks reach 45.43% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet16-120 (ground truth: 47.05%), with a runtime of only 42.7 ms/architecture. On the MobileNetV2 search space evaluated on ImageNet, AZ-NAS achieves 78.6%, 79.9%, and 81.1% top-1 accuracy at 450M, 600M, and 1000M FLOPs respectively, surpassing all training-free and most training-based baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On NAS-Bench-201, AZ-NAS outperforms all 10 training-free baselines in ranking consistency (Kendall's τ) by large margins: the closest competitor GradSign achieves 0.618/0.594/0.575 on CIFAR-10/100/IN16-120, while AZ-NAS reaches 0.741/0.723/0.710. In terms of selected-network accuracy on ImageNet16-120, AZ-NAS (45.43%) surpasses NASWOT (44.45%), ZiCo (42.04%), and GradSign (41.89%), approaching the ground-truth best (47.05%). On the MobileNetV2 search space (ImageNet), AZ-NAS beats the strongest training-free baselines ZiCo (78.1/79.4/80.5%) and ZenNAS (–/79.1/80.8%) at 450M/600M/1000M FLOPs, and also outperforms training-based methods like OFA (77.7/78.7/–) and DONNA (78.0/78.4/–). On the AutoFormer ViT search space, AZ-NAS achieves 76.4% (Tiny) and 82.2% (Small) top-1 accuracy, exceeding AutoFormer (74.7%/81.7%) and TF-TAS (75.3%/81.9%), though it slightly trails AutoFormer on the Base subset (82.3% vs. 82.4%). AZ-NAS achieves all results with a runtime of 42.7 ms/arch, far faster than TE-NAS (1311.8 ms), GradSign (1823.9 ms), and ZiCo (372.8 ms), while remaining competitive with the fastest methods NASWOT (36.9 ms) and ZenNAS (19.9 ms).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the training-free NAS paradigm, where prior methods (NASWOT, TE-NAS, ZenNAS, GradSign, ZiCo) each rely on a single zero-cost proxy to rank candidate architectures. The key departure is the deliberate assembly of multiple *complementary* proxies—expressivity (PCA-based isotropy), progressivity (depth-wise expressivity growth), trainability (Jacobian spectral norm via Hutchinson's method), and complexity (FLOPs)—designed to capture distinct architectural traits with low mutual correlation, rather than combining similar gradient-based metrics as in prior ensemble attempts. The non-linear ranking aggregation (log-rank summation) further distinguishes AZ-NAS from the linear aggregation used in TE-NAS, penalizing networks that are weak on any single proxy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a well-documented weakness in training-free NAS: that many sophisticated proxies perform no better than trivial #Params or FLOPs baselines in predicting final accuracy. By showing that a carefully designed ensemble of complementary proxies, combined with non-linear aggregation, can substantially close this gap (Kendall's τ from ~0.58 to 0.74 on NAS-Bench-201) while remaining computationally cheap (42.7 ms/arch), AZ-NAS offers a practical, plug-and-play improvement that could be integrated into other NAS pipelines. Open questions include whether the specific proxy set is optimal, whether the approach generalizes to non-visual modalities or generative architectures, and whether the non-linear aggregation principle extends to other ensemble-based scoring problems beyond NAS.
