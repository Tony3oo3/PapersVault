## Problem

Previous automatic trailer generation methods frame the task as per-shot binary classification or pairwise ranking, which suffer from severe class imbalance, fail to condition each selection on previously chosen shots, and cannot model shot composition (non-chronological ordering). No prior approach could generate a trailer whose shot sequence deviates from the original movie's temporal order, a fundamental aspect of how real trailers are constructed.

## Input / Output

The method takes a full-length movie video, segments it into shots via TransNet-V2, and encodes each shot into a CLIP ViT-H/14 visual embedding, producing a sequence of 1024-dim tokens (plus learnable SOS/EOS tokens). At inference, the model autoregressively outputs a sequence of predicted trailer-shot embeddings, which are matched back to the original movie shots via greedy nearest-neighbor retrieval to assemble the final ordered trailer. Optionally, an IMDb plot summary encoded by RoBERTa can be concatenated or contextualized and fed to the decoder as additional conditioning.

## Method

TGT (Trailer Generation Transformer) formulates trailer generation as a sequence-to-sequence task analogous to machine translation, using an encoder-decoder Transformer architecture. The movie encoder consists of a trailerness encoder (one self-attention layer predicting per-shot trailerness scores via sigmoid, trained with MSE against max cosine similarity to trailer shots) followed by a context encoder (four Transformer encoder layers) that contextualizes each shot representation across the full movie. The trailer decoder (five autoregressive Transformer decoder layers) predicts the next shot embedding conditioned on the contextualized movie and all previously generated trailer shots, using a causal mask during training and sequential generation at inference. Training optimizes three losses jointly: trailerness MSE, per-shot reconstruction MSE, and a KL-divergence loss on the softmax-normalized sequence distribution to explicitly enforce correct shot ordering. This autoregressive, regression-based formulation avoids the long-tail and composition limitations of prior classification/ranking approaches.

## Results

On the MAD benchmark, TGT achieves 55.30% Precision, 49.92% Recall, and 52.38% F1-score, with a Levenshtein distance (LD) of 21.18 and sequence length difference (SLD) of 10.78. On MovieNet, TGT reaches 49.74% Precision, 44.32% Recall, and 46.77% F1-score, with LD of 24.66 and SLD of 13.28. These results substantially outperform both CCANet (F1: 31.63/29.58) and CLIP-It (F1: 41.73/39.34) across both datasets, with the LD metric showing TGT requires roughly 4× fewer edits than CCANet and 4.5× fewer than CLIP-It to match the ground truth trailer sequence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TGT outperforms CCANet [38] by a large margin on all metrics: F1-score improves from 31.63% to 52.38% on MAD (+20.75 pts) and from 29.58% to 46.77% on MovieNet (+17.19 pts). The LD metric highlights the sequencing advantage: TGT requires only ~21 edits to match the ground truth trailer on MAD, versus ~81 for CCANet, because CCANet's pairwise co-attention cannot model global shot ordering. Against CLIP-It [23], TGT gains +10.65 F1 on MAD and +7.43 F1 on MovieNet, with a particularly large improvement in SLD (10.78 vs. 47.10 on MAD), indicating TGT predicts trailer length far more accurately. The paper attributes CLIP-It's weaker performance to its shot-level binary classification paradigm, which cannot capture temporal dependencies between shots. TGT's autoregressive decoder, by conditioning each prediction on previously generated shots, produces a more coherent and correctly ordered trailer sequence.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior trailer generation work that framed the problem as either binary shot classification (Smith et al., Smeaton et al.) or pairwise shot ranking (CCANet, Gaikwad et al.), by recasting it as a sequence-to-sequence task inspired by neural machine translation. The encoder-decoder Transformer architecture is borrowed from the NLP literature (Vaswani et al.), with CLIP providing visual token embeddings and TransNet-V2 handling shot segmentation as off-the-shelf components. The key conceptual departure is the autoregressive decoder that models shot composition (ordering), a dimension entirely absent from prior formulations, along with a 'trailerness encoding' mechanism analogous to positional encoding that injects per-shot relevance scores into the sequence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to jointly address both shot selection and shot ordering in automatic trailer generation, and the newly constructed MAD-ATG and MovieNet-ATG benchmarks (602 and 989 movie-trailer pairs) provide a foundation for future research in a previously under-benchmarked area. The top-5/top-10 shot retrieval analysis (69% precision at top-10 on MAD) suggests practical value as a human-in-the-loop editing assistant rather than a fully autonomous system. Key open questions include whether the approach generalizes to non-English films, whether incorporating audio/dialogue signals (explicitly excluded here) would substantially improve results, and whether the seq2seq formulation scales to longer-form content beyond feature films.
