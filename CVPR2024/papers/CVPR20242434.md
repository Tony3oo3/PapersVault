## Problem

Existing video grounding methods are optimized for short videos with only a few text queries and fail to scale to long-form videos (tens of thousands of time steps) with hundreds of queries due to prohibitive computational cost. The paper identifies cross-modal fusion as the overlooked design choice that governs this scalability, showing that the widely adopted early fusion forces redundant video encoding for every query, while late fusion can amortize video processing across all queries.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242434/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a long video partitioned into M overlapping sliding-window snippets (each of length T_w, up to 16384 frames) and N sentence queries, using pre-extracted video features (C3D, I3D, SlowFast, CLIP, or EgoVLP) and text embeddings (GloVe, BERT, or CLIP). For each query, it outputs a single moment as start and end times (s, e), decoded from point-based classification and boundary regression at every time step, followed by Soft-NMS to merge overlapping predictions. The video-centric design means the expensive video encoder output is computed once per snippet and cached/reused across all N queries of that video.

## Method

SnAG is a single-stage Transformer with a late-fusion architecture: an ActionFormer-based multi-scale video encoder and a vanilla Transformer text encoder operate independently, and their outputs are fused via a lightweight multi-head cross-attention that produces affine modulation weights and biases applied to the video features. A key theoretical contribution is a cost analysis proving that late fusion reduces inference cost by a factor of N (number of queries) and training cost by a factor of B_q (queries per video) relative to early fusion, since video encoding is amortized. This insight motivates a novel video-centric mini-batch sampling scheme for training, where a video is drawn first and then B_q queries are sampled from it, enabling reuse of video representations within a batch. Moment decoding uses point-based classification (Focal loss) and boundary regression (DistanceIoU loss) with lightweight 1D convolutional heads at each scale of the multi-scale pyramid.

## Results

On the challenging MAD long-form video grounding benchmark, SnAG achieves 13.75% R@5 at tIoU=0.5, outperforming CONE by 43% in accuracy while being 1.5× faster in inference (45.3 queries/sec on a P100 GPU). On Ego4D-NLQ, SnAG reaches 13.57% R@1 / 32.92% R@5 with EgoVLP features, more than doubling the official baselines. On short-video benchmarks, SnAG sets new state of the art on TACoS (44.86% R@1 / 70.66% R@5 at tIoU=0.5) and Charades-STA (46.26% R@1 / 71.94% R@5 at tIoU=0.7 with I3D features), while remaining highly competitive on ActivityNet-Captions (30.56% R@1 / 63.41% R@5 at tIoU=0.7).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SnAG outperforms the concurrent long-form grounding methods CONE [17] and SOONet [36] on both Ego4D-NLQ and MAD, with the largest gains at stricter tIoU thresholds (e.g., +4.16 points R@5 at tIoU=0.5 over CONE on MAD). On MAD, SnAG improves over the CLIP [39] and VLG-Net [46] baselines by 3.90/7.76 absolute points on mean R@1/R@5 at tIoU=0.5, and over Moment-DETR [21] by a wide margin (13.75% vs. 0.28% R@5 at tIoU=0.5). On TACoS, SnAG surpasses the strongest prior method MATN [66] by +7.5% R@1 and +12.1% R@5 at tIoU=0.5, and outperforms 2D-TAN [67] by +9.09% R@1. On Charades-STA, SnAG exceeds the previous state of the art SSRN [71] by 2.10/4.52 points (C3D) and 3.61/6.46 points (I3D) on R@1/R@5 at tIoU=0.7. On ActivityNet-Captions, SnAG is competitive but slightly behind the top methods (SSRN, G2L [22]), trailing by ~1–4 points on R@1 at tIoU=0.7, while being substantially simpler in design.

## Relation to Prior Work

SnAG builds directly on ActionFormer [61] for its multi-scale video encoder, extending that architecture from temporal action localization to the more challenging task of open-vocabulary video grounding. Conceptually, the paper departs from the dominant early-fusion paradigm (where video and text are jointly encoded from the input) by revisiting late fusion with a lightweight cross-attention module, arguing that the field's abandonment of late fusion was driven by model-design complexity rather than a fundamental accuracy trade-off. It also introduces video-centric mini-batch sampling as a novel training scheme, contrasting with the query-centric sampling used by virtually all prior video grounding methods. The work is positioned against concurrent long-form grounding efforts (CONE, SOONet) that rely on multi-stage coarse-to-fine pipelines and restrictive assumptions about moment duration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central insight—that late fusion amortizes expensive video encoding across many text queries, yielding an N-fold inference speedup—provides a principled, cost-analysis-based justification for a design choice the field had largely abandoned. The video-centric sampling scheme is a practical, drop-in training modification that saves up to 50% training time and 40% GPU memory without accuracy loss, making it broadly applicable to any multi-query video understanding pipeline. Open questions include whether the late-fusion advantage persists when video and text require deeper iterative interaction (e.g., multi-round cross-attention), and whether the scaling analysis generalizes to other long-sequence multimodal tasks beyond grounding. The paper does not explore end-to-end training from raw pixels, relying instead on pre-extracted features, which limits the generality of the efficiency claims.
