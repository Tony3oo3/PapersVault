## Problem

Prior action segmentation methods either refine frame features with temporal convolutions/transformers (limited long-range modeling) or learn action features in a separate second stage without bidirectional communication back to frame features. This ignores the complementary information between frame-level details and action-level dependencies, and two-stage methods incur high inference cost from post-processing (e.g., Viterbi decoding) or frame-level transformers.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240925/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

FACT takes pre-extracted frame features (T×D matrix) and a fixed set of M learned action tokens (initialized to zero, or to transcript embeddings when available) as input. It outputs a fused T×A framewise probability matrix combining predictions from both the frame branch (direct convolutional classification) and the action branch (token labels projected to frames via the final cross-attention map Λ_B^f). The attention maps also implicitly encode segment boundaries, and the number of active tokens (non-null) adapts per video.

## Method

FACT runs a frame branch (dilated convolutions) and an action branch (transformer self-attention over M tokens) in parallel, connected by bidirectional cross-attentions in each of B update blocks: frame-to-action cross-attention lets tokens attend to frames to capture segment locations, while action-to-frame cross-attention lets frames attend to tokens to inherit high-level action dependencies. A novel matching loss extends DETR's Hungarian one-to-one assignment to support one-to-many token-to-segment matching (for repeated actions), supervised via a soft-IoU similarity between token attention maps and ground-truth segment intervals. Training applies frame loss, action token loss, cross-attention alignment loss, and temporal smoothing loss at every block. When video transcripts are available, they initialize action tokens and provide known matchings, eliminating the need for Hungarian assignment and enabling strong performance with as little as 25% of training data.

## Results

FACT achieves state-of-the-art action segmentation on all four datasets. On Breakfast, it reaches F1@50 of 66.2 and Edit of 79.7, exceeding the best prior (DiffAct: F1@50=64.6, Edit=78.4) by 1.6% and 1.3% respectively. On GTEA, FACT attains F1@50 of 84.1 and Edit of 91.4, outperforming UVAST (F1@50=81.0, Edit=92.1) on F1 while matching on Edit. On the more challenging long-video datasets, FACT improves F1@50 by 6.2% on EgoProceL (60.8 vs. DiffAct's 54.6) and by 7.0% on EPIC-Kitchen (18.5 vs. LTContext's 11.5), while also being 3.5× faster than DiffAct at inference.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FACT outperforms all 18 compared baselines on every dataset and metric where they overlap. Among frame-based methods, DiffAct is the strongest prior, but FACT exceeds it by 1.9% F1@50 on Breakfast, 6.2% on EgoProceL, and 7.0% on EPIC-Kitchen, while also being 3.5× faster at inference (5.5 vs. 63.0 GFLOP). Among two-stage methods, UVAST achieves the best GTEA Edit (92.1) but FACT surpasses it on all F1 metrics and GTEA Acc (86.1 vs. 80.2); RTK is competitive on GTEA F1@50 (83.4 vs. 84.1) but trails on Breakfast across all metrics. LTContext, the best frame-based method on EgoProceL among non-diffusion approaches, is outperformed by FACT by 5.5% F1@50 on that dataset. On EPIC-Kitchen, UVAST and DiffAct fail to converge well due to the large number of segments per video, while FACT's one-to-many token matching handles this efficiently, achieving F1@50 of 18.5 versus 11.5 (LTContext) and 7.2 (DiffAct). Methods using different frame features (ED-TCN, TDRN, SSA-GAN, Bridge-Prompt) are not directly comparable but Bridge-Prompt's GTEA results (F1@50=83.0) are still below FACT's 84.1.

## Relation to Prior Work

FACT departs from the dominant two-stage paradigm (UVAST, RTK, MuCon) where action features are built only after frame features are finalized, instead performing frame-level (convolution) and action-level (transformer) temporal modeling in parallel with iterative bidirectional cross-attention between the two branches. It also extends DETR's one-to-one Hungarian matching loss to a one-to-many variant suited to videos with repeated actions, and repurposes video transcripts to initialize action tokens rather than relying on costly Viterbi post-processing. The frame branch deliberately uses only dilated convolutions (as in MSTCN++), while the transformer is confined to a small set of action tokens, avoiding the quadratic cost of full-frame attention used by ASFormer and DiffAct.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 3.5× inference speedup over the prior best transformer-based method (DiffAct) while simultaneously achieving SOTA on four datasets makes FACT a practically attractive option for long-video action segmentation where compute is a bottleneck. The finding that 25% of training data with transcripts outperforms 100% without them opens a path for semi-supervised or annotation-sparse deployment. Open questions include whether the fixed token count and one-to-many matching generalize to open-vocabulary or extremely long (>10 min) videos, and whether the parallel frame-action design transfers to other temporal tasks such as activity recognition or video captioning.
