## Problem

Existing self-supervised audio-visual speech representation methods predominantly use audio as a teacher to guide visual learning, which inherently limits the learned representations to shared (modality-invariant) and at most audio-unique information. They fail to explicitly capture video-unique speech information (e.g., visually distinct but acoustically similar phonemes) and synergistic information that only emerges when both modalities are jointly considered, while the inherent asymmetry between the two modalities (clean high-rate audio vs. occluded, redundant, ambiguous video) makes naive joint optimization biased toward audio.

## Input / Output

The model consumes paired audio-visual inputs: audio as log filter bank features sampled at 100 FPS and lip-region video frames at 25 FPS (a 4:1 temporal ratio), with the audio encoder downsampling by 4× to align frame rates. A simple sum operation fuses modality-specific features, allowing the model to gracefully handle missing modalities by adding a zero vector. The pre-trained output is a d-dimensional contextualized representation γ ∈ R^{d×T_v}; for downstream ASR/VSR/AVSR, a linear projection head is added and the model is fine-tuned end-to-end with the LF-MMI criterion over bi-char (English) or tonal phone-level (Chinese) targets.

## Method

ES³ employs a three-stage progressive ('evolving') learning strategy within a Siamese student-teacher framework with an EMA-updated teacher and online clustering (codebook-based pseudo-labels) as the distillation loss. Stage 1 learns audio-unique and shared information via masked audio self-distillation (a→a′) plus a symmetric InfoNCE cross-modal alignment loss (a↔v) that bootstraps visual representations. Stage 2, with the audio encoder frozen, learns video-unique information via masked video distillation (v→v′) and injects it into the joint representation through an asymmetric AV→V distillation loss with a learnable temperature. Stage 3 maximizes total audio-visual information including synergistic components by co-masking identical positions in both modalities and performing joint AV→AV′ distillation, forcing the model to leverage both streams to recover masked regions. The base architecture pairs a 2-layer CNN audio encoder and a lightweight VGG-(2+1)D video encoder with a modality-agnostic Transformer fusion encoder using rotary positional embeddings, and the entire pre-training is followed by LF-MMI fine-tuning with a compact 1-state HMM topology.

## Results

On LRS2-BBC (high-resource, 223h labeled), ES³'s smallest BASE* model (46M params, 223h unlabeled) achieves 30.3% VSR, 3.6% ASR, and 2.9% AVSR WER, matching or exceeding SoTA methods that use 4–8× more unlabeled data. Scaling to the LARGE model (317M, 433h unlabeled) yields 25.8% VSR, 2.5% ASR, and 2.5% AVSR WER, rivaling methods pre-trained on 1759h of data. On LRS3-TED (high-resource, 433h labeled), ES³ BASE achieves 39.2% VSR, 2.4% ASR, and 2.0% AVSR WER, competitive with AV-data2vec (39.0/2.0/1.8) while using the same 433h pre-training data. On the newly introduced CAS-VSR-S101 Mandarin dataset, ES³ outperforms a strong supervised Conformer baseline in ASR (11.6 vs 13.0 CER) and AVSR (11.0 vs 12.6 CER) with only 50 fine-tuning epochs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ES³'s key advantage is data efficiency: its BASE* model (46M, 223h unlabeled) matches AV-HuBERT (103M, 1759h) on VSR (31.4 vs 31.2) while using only 1/8 the pre-training data, and beats VATLM (107M, 1759h) on AVSR (3.2 vs 2.9 WER). At the LARGE scale, ES³ (317M, 433h) outperforms AV-HuBERT (325M, 1759h) on VSR (26.7 vs 25.5) and VATLM (332M, 1759h) on VSR (26.7 vs 24.3), but trails both on AVSR (3.1 vs 2.5 and 2.3 respectively), suggesting the Transformer fusion benefits more from scale in the visual modality. Against RAVEn (same 433h data), ES³ BASE wins on ASR (3.4 vs 3.9) but loses on VSR (30.7 vs 32.1); at LARGE scale, ES³ substantially outperforms RAVEn on VSR (26.7 vs 23.2) despite using half the parameters (317M vs 671M). On LRS3-TED, ES³ BASE is competitive with AV-data2vec (39.2 vs 39.0 VSR, 2.0 vs 1.8 AVSR) and beats RAVEn on ASR (2.4 vs 2.2). On CAS-VSR-S101, ES³ surpasses the supervised E2E Conformer [39] in ASR (11.6 vs 13.0 CER) and AVSR (11.0 vs 12.6 CER) but slightly lags in VSR (55.6 vs 56.5 CER), indicating the visual encoder remains a bottleneck.

## Relation to Prior Work

ES3 builds directly on the data2vec/DinoSR lineage of Siamese student-teacher frameworks with EMA teachers and online clustering for self-supervised representation learning, but departs from prior audio-visual speech methods (AV-HuBERT, AV-data2vec, RAVEn, AV2vec) that treat audio as a fixed teacher/anchor modality. The key conceptual departure is a Partial Information Decomposition (PID)-based reformulation that explicitly targets shared, modality-unique, and synergistic information in a three-stage progressive schedule, rather than relying on a single joint objective with manually tuned balancing hyperparameters (masking ratios, modality dropout, scheduling). This positions ES3 as a principled alternative to the ad-hoc audio-centric distillation paradigm that has dominated the field.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The progressive three-stage strategy eliminates the extensive manual hyperparameter search (modality schedules, masking ratios, dropout rates) that plagues competing methods, and the 46M model with only 223h unlabeled data rivaling SoTA models trained on 4× more data represents a meaningful efficiency gain for practitioners with limited compute. The newly contributed CAS-VSR-S101 Mandarin dataset addresses a gap in non-English audio-visual speech benchmarks. However, VSR performance still lags slightly behind the best supervised and self-supervised baselines, suggesting the lightweight VGG-(2+1)D visual encoder is a bottleneck, and the synergistic information learning in Stage 3 yields only modest gains over Stages 1–2, leaving open whether the PID decomposition translates into larger improvements with stronger visual backbones or more diverse data.
