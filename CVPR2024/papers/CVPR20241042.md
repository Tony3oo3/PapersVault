## Problem

Training embodied agents for long-horizon navigation and manipulation has relied on either sample-inefficient RL with extensive reward shaping or imitation learning on expensive human-collected trajectories. Prior work demonstrated that IL on shortest-path planners produced suboptimal agents (e.g., 4.4% success on OBJECTNAV), casting doubt on whether heuristic expert imitation could yield capable, generalizable robots. This paper shows that with sufficient scale, environmental diversity, and modern transformer architectures, imitating shortest-path experts in simulation produces agents that navigate, explore, and manipulate effectively in both simulation and the real world with no adaptation.

## Input / Output

At each timestep the agent receives an open-vocabulary text instruction, two RGB frames from perpendicular cameras (one facing the navigation direction, one facing the manipulation arm), and the full history of past visual observations and actions. It outputs one of 20 discrete actions covering base movement (±20 cm), base rotation (±6°, ±30°), arm translation (±2 cm, ±10 cm), grasper rotation (±10°), pickup, dropoff, subtask completion, and episode termination. No depth maps, GPS coordinates, or scene maps are provided at test time.

## Method

SPOC is a three-component transformer: a pretrained text encoder (SIGLIP) maps the instruction to goal tokens; a goal-conditioned visual encoder processes two RGB frames through a SIGLIP image encoder into patch embeddings, concatenates them with goal features, camera-type embeddings, and a learnable [CLS] token, and passes the result through a 3-layer transformer encoder to produce a single visual representation per timestep; and a causal transformer action decoder with cross-attention on the goal predicts the current action from the sequence of past and current visual representations plus past action embeddings. Training is pure behavior cloning (cross-entropy on one-hot expert actions) over millions of frames of shortest-path expert trajectories generated in ~200,000 procedurally generated ProcTHOR houses containing ~40,000 Objaverse 3D assets. The key empirical finding is that four factors unlock effective shortest-path IL: (1) orders-of-magnitude more diverse houses than prior work, (2) powerful visual encoders (SIGLIP nearly doubles CLIP's success rate), (3) long context windows (100 frames vs. 10), and (4) transformer encoders and decoders over RNNs, with the decoder's ability to attend over hundreds of past steps being critical for long-horizon tasks.

## Results

On CHORES-S (15 object categories), multi-task SPOC achieves 49.9% average success across OBJNAV, PICKUP, FETCH, and ROOMVISIT, dramatically outperforming the RL-trained EmbSigLIP baseline (31.2%). Equipping SPOC with ground-truth object detection boosts average success to 65.0% on CHORES-S and 63.1% on the harder CHORES-L (863 categories), where the RGB-only model scores 38.6%. In the real world with no fine-tuning, SPOC achieves 39.5% average success (56.1% with a DETIC detector), with minimal performance drop from simulation for navigation tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole quantitative baseline is EmbSigLIP [38], the EmbCLIP architecture upgraded to use the SIGLIP visual backbone and trained with single-task RL (requiring extensive reward shaping). SPOC's multi-task IL approach outperforms this RL baseline by 18.7 points in average success on CHORES-S (49.9% vs. 31.2%), with particularly large margins on OBJNAV (+18.5 pts), FETCH (+14.0 pts vs. 0.0%), and ROOMVISIT (+24.0 pts). The RL baseline was run for 2× the training hours on the same hardware and still could not achieve non-zero performance on FETCH. The paper also notes that single-task IL (50.0%) matches multi-task IL (49.9%), indicating no task-competition degradation, and that the gap between SPOC and the RL baseline is attributable to the training paradigm (IL vs. RL) rather than architecture, since both use the same SIGLIP backbone.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPOC departs from the dominant RL-based training paradigm for embodied agents (EmbCLIP, DDPPO) and from human-demonstration IL (RT-1, Habitat-Web) by showing that imitation of heuristic shortest-path planners—previously shown to yield only 4.4% success on OBJNAV—can produce highly capable agents when combined with sufficient environmental scale (~200k procedural houses), strong visual encoders (SIGLIP, DINOV2), and transformer architectures with long context windows. Conceptually, it repurposes the Decision Transformer / Trajectory Transformer lineage of casting sequential decision-making as sequence modeling, but applies it to a data source (simulator ground-truth shortest paths) that prior work considered fundamentally suboptimal. The sim-to-real transfer without domain randomization, CycleGAN, or Phone2Proc-style adaptation also contrasts with the standard sim-to-real toolkit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that the 'imitation gap' for shortest-path IL is a scale and architecture problem rather than a fundamental limitation—opens a much cheaper path to training embodied agents, eliminating the need for expensive human trajectories or slow RL. The CHORES and CHORESNAV benchmarks provide a unified multi-task evaluation suite that the community can adopt. Key open questions include: perception remains the primary bottleneck (85% vs 55% success with ground-truth detection), real-world validation is limited to 88 trials across two settings, and it is unclear whether further scaling or the addition of depth/GPS sensors would yield diminishing returns. Whether this paradigm generalizes beyond household environments to outdoor or industrial settings remains unexplored.
