## Problem

Window-based self-attention in SR Transformers confines the receptive field to local rectangular windows, limiting the model's ability to capture long-range dependencies without incurring quadratic computational cost. Moreover, this content-agnostic partitioning strategy groups spatially adjacent but semantically unrelated tokens together, degrading attention quality. The paper addresses both the limited receptive field and the content-independent partitioning by introducing an adaptive token dictionary that enables global information propagation and content-aware token grouping.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single low-resolution RGB image and produces a high-resolution RGB image at ×2, ×3, or ×4 scale. The LR input is first mapped to a feature map via a 3×3 convolution, then processed through six ATD blocks (each with six transformer layers, 210 channels, and 128 dictionary tokens). The final HR estimate is generated by a convolution layer followed by a pixel-shuffle upsampling operation. A lightweight variant (ATD-light) uses 48 channels, 4 blocks, and 64 dictionary tokens for resource-constrained settings.

## Method

The ATD architecture introduces three complementary attention mechanisms that operate in parallel within each transformer layer. First, Token Dictionary Cross-Attention (TDCA) uses a learnable auxiliary dictionary of M≪N tokens to generate Key and Value, while input features generate Query; cosine similarity (rather than dot product) is used to give each dictionary atom equal selection opportunity, yielding linear complexity in image size. Second, Adaptive Dictionary Refinement (ADR) updates the dictionary across layers via a reversed-attention operation: the attention map from the previous layer selects relevant tokens from the updated feature to reconstruct and blend into the next layer's dictionary, progressively incorporating image-specific global information. Third, Adaptive Category-based Multi-head Self-Attention (AC-MSA) uses the TDCA attention map to assign each pixel to the category of its most similar dictionary token, then performs self-attention within each content-defined category (further subdivided into fixed-size sub-categories for parallelism), thereby establishing long-range connections between similar structures across the entire image while avoiding the quadratic cost of full self-attention.

## Results

On the classical SR task (×2), ATD achieves 34.92 dB PSNR on Set14, 34.73 dB on Urban100, and 40.35 dB on Manga109, outperforming the previous SOTA HAT by 0.06, 0.28, and 0.09 dB respectively. For ×4 upscaling, ATD leads HAT on all five benchmarks, with the largest gain of +0.25 dB on Urban100 (28.22 vs 27.97 dB). In the lightweight setting, ATD-light (769K params) surpasses OmniSR by up to 0.46 dB on Manga109 ×4 (31.48 vs 31.02 dB) while using fewer parameters (769K vs 792K).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ATD (20.1M params) outperforms HAT (20.6M) on 4 of 5 benchmarks at ×2 (Set14 +0.06, BSD100 +0.02, Urban100 +0.28, Manga109 +0.09 dB) while trailing by only 0.02 dB on Set5; at ×4, ATD wins on all five benchmarks with gains ranging from +0.02 to +0.25 dB. Against CAT-A and ART, ATD achieves substantially larger margins on Urban100 (+0.47 and +0.43 dB at ×2) and Manga109 (+0.25 and +0.11 dB), confirming the benefit of the token dictionary and category-based attention for structured and textured content. In the lightweight setting, ATD-light (753K/769K params) consistently beats OmniSR (772K/792K) across all benchmarks at both ×2 and ×4, with the largest gap of +0.46 dB on Manga109 ×4. ATD-light also surpasses SwinIR-light, ELAN, and SwinIR-NG by 0.13–0.32 dB on Urban100 ×4, demonstrating that the adaptive token dictionary mechanism is particularly effective under parameter constraints. Overall, ATD achieves the best PSNR on 9 of 10 benchmark-scale combinations in the classical setting and 10 of 10 in the lightweight setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws a direct conceptual bridge between classical dictionary-learning-based SR (Yang et al., 2010) and modern Vision Transformer architectures, arguing that the sparse-representation-and-reconstruction pipeline is mathematically analogous to the attention-weight-and-value-combination process. Architecturally, it builds on SwinIR's shifted-window transformer backbone and borrows the sub-category balancing strategy from NLSA, but departs from the window-centric paradigm by introducing a learnable token dictionary that provides external prior via linear-complexity cross-attention and enables content-aware (rather than spatially fixed) token grouping for self-attention. The adaptive dictionary refinement across layers further distinguishes it from prior SR transformers, which treat attention windows as static spatial partitions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a principled, low-cost mechanism to inject global and external information into SR transformers without the quadratic cost of full self-attention, addressing a well-recognized bottleneck in the field. The category-based attention is a natural step toward content-aware partitioning, and the token dictionary concept could transfer to other restoration tasks (denoising, deblurring, inpainting). However, the gains over the strongest baseline (HAT) are modest (~0.25 dB on Urban100), the method still relies on window-based attention as a complementary branch, and the fixed dictionary size (128 tokens) limits expressiveness. Open questions include whether the dictionary can be made interpretable or task-adaptive, and whether the approach scales to video SR or higher-resolution settings where token counts grow substantially.
