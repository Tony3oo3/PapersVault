## Problem

Existing inherently interpretable classifiers (ProtoPNet, PIP-Net, etc.) derive explanations exclusively from the last feature map, providing only high-level, object-centric semantics while leaving the decision processes at low and mid-level features opaque. The authors argue that such single-level explanations are neither fully faithful nor useful for understanding how a model actually makes its classification decisions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241656/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

MCPNet takes standard RGB images and processes them through off-the-shelf CNN backbones (ResNet50, Inception V3, ConvNeXt-tiny) without adding any extra modules or trainable parameters. Instead of a fully connected classifier, it produces a class label by matching the image's Multi-level Concept Prototype (MCP) distribution against precomputed class-specific centroid MCP distributions via Jensen-Shannon divergence. The MCP distribution itself encodes prototype responses across all selected layers, serving simultaneously as the classification signal and the multi-scale explanation (low-level color/texture concepts to high-level object-part concepts).

## Method

MCPNet partitions each selected layer's feature map along the channel dimension into fixed-size concept segments, then enforces inter-segment dissimilarity via a Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) loss so that each segment captures a distinct semantic concept. Concept prototypes are extracted globally per segment using a weighted PCA (weighted by L2-norm to prioritize salient feature vectors), yielding one prototype direction per segment per layer. For each input image, prototype responses are computed as max-pooled cosine similarities between the prototype and its segment, forming the MCP distribution. A Class-aware Concept Distribution (CCD) loss then minimizes Jensen-Shannon divergence within a class and enforces a margin between classes, replacing the conventional FC classifier entirely. At inference, classification is performed by selecting the class whose centroid MCP distribution (averaged over training instances) is closest in JS divergence to the image's MCP distribution, and the same distribution provides the multi-level explanation.

## Results

With ResNet50 backbone, MCPNet achieves 93.92% accuracy on AWA2, 93.88% on Caltech101, and 80.15% on CUB_200_2011, matching or exceeding the non-interpretability baseline (94.92%, 94.21%, 77.94%) while providing multi-level concept explanations. On the fine-grained CUB dataset, MCPNet outperforms all ProtoPNet-series baselines by 2.2–62.15 percentage points. In the 5-shot classification setting, MCPNet reaches 73.79% accuracy on AWA2, substantially surpassing the baseline (60.55%) and all other interpretable methods (19.71%–33.68%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MCPNet consistently outperforms all four ProtoPNet-series baselines on the fine-grained CUB_200_2011 dataset: 80.15% vs. ST-ProtoPNet (76.34%), Deformable ProtoPNet (73.42%), PIP-Net (70.99%), and ProtoTree (18.00%) with ResNet50. On AWA2, MCPNet (93.92%) beats PIP-Net (85.99%) and Deformable ProtoPNet (85.51%) by 8+ points, and is within 1% of ST-ProtoPNet (93.76%) and the baseline (94.92%). On Caltech101, MCPNet (93.88%) trails ST-ProtoPNet (95.95%) by 2.07 points but substantially outperforms PIP-Net (87.86%) and ProtoTree (72.19%). PIP-Net degrades catastrophically with Inception V3 (43.82% on AWA2), while MCPNet remains robust (94.62%). In the 5-shot setting, MCPNet (73.79%) leads the baseline (60.55%) by 13.24 points and all interpretable baselines by 40+ points, demonstrating superior generalization to unseen categories.

## Relation to Prior Work

MCPNet extends the ProtoPNet lineage of inherently interpretable prototype-based classifiers by generalizing from single-level (last-layer) prototype extraction to multi-level concept prototypes spanning multiple feature map layers. It departs from prior prototype methods in two key ways: (1) it partitions feature map channels into segments and uses a CKA-based loss to enforce inter-segment diversity, rather than treating entire channels as atomic concepts, and (2) it replaces the conventional FC classifier with a distribution-matching mechanism (Jensen-Shannon divergence over MCP distributions) guided by a CCD contrastive loss. This positions MCPNet against both post-hoc explainers (TCAV, CRAFT, LIME, SHAP) and single-level inherently interpretable methods (ProtoPNet, PIP-Net, ProtoTree, ST-ProtoPNet), claiming that explanations restricted to the final layer are neither fully faithful nor comprehensive.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The multi-level explanation capability addresses a genuine gap: prior interpretable classifiers illuminate only high-level semantics, leaving low- and mid-layer decision processes opaque. The distribution-based classification paradigm (no FC layer) is practically interesting because it yields a 13-point accuracy gain in 5-shot settings over the baseline, suggesting that multi-level concept distributions carry more transferable structure than last-layer features alone. Limitations include reliance on CNN backbones, the post-hoc (human-assigned) interpretation of what each prototype 'means,' and the computational overhead of CKA computation across all layer pairs. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very large datasets (e.g., ImageNet), whether multi-level explanations are genuinely more useful to end-users than single-level ones, and how the method behaves with non-CNN architectures like vision transformers.
