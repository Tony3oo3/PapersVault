## Problem

Existing atmospheric turbulence mitigation methods initialize a reference template via heuristics such as temporal or space-time averaging, which produces blurry references that degrade subsequent optical flow estimation and registration quality. This paper addresses the chicken-and-egg problem of needing a good template to estimate deformations but needing good deformations to build a good template, by showing that frames can be registered to a latent template without ever explicitly constructing it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242679/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a sequence of T turbulence-corrupted images (grayscale or RGB, tested at 220×220 to 1024×1024) and requires no training data or paired clean images. It selects one frame as a keyframe (the first frame in experiments) and produces two outputs: a registered template (the aligned mean of all frames) and a final restored image after ADMM blind deconvolution. The pipeline is purely analytical with no learned parameters, and the flow inversion step runs in under 0.02 seconds per 256×256 frame on a laptop CPU.

## Method

The core insight is that by the Central Limit Theorem, the temporal average of optical flows from a keyframe I_k to all other frames converges to the inverse of the deformation from the ground-truth irradiance to I_k, enabling registration to the latent template without explicit access to it. The method estimates per-frame flows w_kt using Horn-Schunck optical flow, averages them, and inverts the average via a novel flow inversion algorithm that maps non-integer flow endpoints to four nearest integer pixels with L1-distance-weighted averaging and inpainting for zero-weight regions. Each frame is then warped to the template by composing the inverted average flow with the per-frame flow, and the registered template is deblurred via ADMM blind deconvolution assuming a Gaussian PSF derived from Zernike wavefront decomposition. The approach is entirely plug-and-play: any optical flow estimator, frame selection strategy, or downstream deconvolution/fusion module can be swapped in without altering the pipeline.

## Results

On the HeatChamber dataset, the proposed method (Ours-lucky) achieves PSNR 20.74 and SSIM 0.789, outperforming the best multi-frame baseline TurbRecon (20.62 / 0.787) and NDIR (20.08 / 0.763). Even the unprocessed registered template (Ours-template: 20.30 / 0.780) surpasses NDIR without any downstream deconvolution or fusion. On CLEAR-sim at 1024×1024 resolution, Ours-lucky reaches PSNR 25.95 and SSIM 0.906, beating NDIR (24.13 / 0.871) and TurbRecon (24.73 / 0.897), while also achieving a ~100× speedup over NDL [51] (20.65→20.84 PSNR, 0.715→0.723 SSIM).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method outperforms all nine baselines on both PSNR and SSIM. Among single-frame deep-learning methods (TRDN, Uformer, Restormer, TurbNet, PiRN), the best is PiRN at 20.59 PSNR / 0.712 SSIM, but all suffer from low SSIM due to their inability to model deformation; Ours-lucky exceeds PiRN by 0.15 dB PSNR and 0.077 SSIM. Among multi-frame methods, Ours-template (20.30 / 0.780) already beats NDIR (20.08 / 0.763) by 0.22 dB and 0.017 SSIM before any deconvolution, and Ours-lucky (20.74 / 0.789) further surpasses TurbRecon (20.62 / 0.787) by 0.12 dB and 0.002 SSIM. On CLEAR-sim, the gap widens: Ours-lucky leads NDIR by 1.82 dB PSNR and TurbRecon by 1.22 dB, while also handling 1024×1024 resolution that NDIR cannot process at full resolution. Against NDL [51], the method improves PSNR from 20.65 to 20.84 and SSIM from 0.715 to 0.723 while reducing runtime by approximately 100× (from over 1 hour to seconds). The method is also robust to keyframe selection, with SSIM varying by only 0.001 between the sharpest and blurriest frames (Table 2).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in atmospheric turbulence mitigation, where a reference template is constructed via heuristics (temporal averaging, space-time non-local averaging as in TurbRecon [30], or learned grids as in NDIR [24]) before estimating per-frame deformations. Instead, it repurposes the Central Limit Theorem to argue that the mean of optical flows from any chosen keyframe to all other frames converges to the inverse deformation from ground truth to that keyframe, eliminating the need for an explicitly constructed (and typically blurry) template. The novel flow inversion algorithm (Algorithm 1) is the key technical enabler, allowing registration to the latent template without ever materializing it. The method is deliberately positioned as a generic, plug-and-play registration module that can replace the template-initialization step in existing pipelines (TurbRecon, NDL, etc.) without altering downstream deconvolution or fusion stages.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The conceptual insight—that one can register frames to a template without having the template—provides a clean, assumption-light baseline that is immune to the domain-shift and inductive-bias problems of learned methods, which is particularly valuable in discovery-oriented applications like astronomical imaging where ground truth is unknowable. The method's robustness to arbitrary frame selection (Table 2) and its ~100× speedup over NDL make it practically attractive as a drop-in replacement. Open questions include handling dynamic scenes (where occlusions violate the diffeomorphic assumption), whether turbulence-specific flow estimators (e.g., RAFT trained on turbulence simulators) can further close the gap, and whether the Gaussian PSF approximation (Eq. 12) holds under extreme anisoplanatic conditions. The runtime bottleneck in optical flow estimation remains a practical limitation for real-time applications.
