## Problem

Existing diffusion-based video editing methods rely on dense correspondences (optical flow, depth, edge maps, attention) to enforce temporal consistency, but these impose strict shape constraints that make them ineffective when the target edit involves a shape change. The paper tackles customized video subject swapping, where the replacement subject has a clearly defined identity in both appearance and shape that must be preserved while following the source subject's motion trajectory.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241612/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 16-frame source video, a text prompt specifying the target subject, and K user-defined semantic points at a single keyframe (propagated to all frames via Co-Tracker). For customized concepts, a set of reference images is used to train an ED-LoRA adapter. The output is a 16-frame video where the source subject is replaced by the target subject with its own shape and appearance, while the background is preserved via latent blending and the motion trajectory follows the source.

## Method

The core insight is that a small number of semantic points suffice to align a subject's motion trajectory and modify its shape, unlike dense correspondences that lock the shape. The pipeline VAE-encodes the source video, applies DDIM inversion, swaps the text prompt to the target subject, and denoises with AnimateDiff motion layers for temporal consistency. Semantic points are extracted by propagating user-defined keypoints via Co-Tracker and averaging DIFT features at each point location across all frames. These point embeddings are projected by learnable MLPs into multi-scale sparse motion features that are added element-wise to the U-Net encoder, trained with a point patch loss (reconstructing only local patches around points to prevent structure leakage) and a semantic-enhanced schedule (T_min = T/2 to emphasize high-level alignment). User-point interactions—removing redundant points for partial correspondence or dragging points with displacement propagation through a Layered Neural Atlas canonical space—handle shape morphing between source and target subjects.

## Results

The paper evaluates via human preference (1000 Amazon Mturk questionnaires) across four criteria: Subject Identity, Motion Alignment, Temporal Consistency, and Overall Preference. VideoSwap achieves the highest preference rate against all compared methods, with the strongest margins against Rerender-A-Video (84% overall preference) and Tune-A-Video (78% overall). Against the AnimateDiff baselines, VideoSwap leads by 4–15 percentage points on each metric, with the largest gap over DDIM+T2I-Adapter (66% vs 34% overall).

## Baselines & Comparisons

VideoSwap wins against all five compared methods on every evaluated criterion. Against Tune-A-Video [43], which implicitly encodes motion via model tuning, VideoSwap leads by 8–60 percentage points, with the largest gap on Motion Alignment (72% vs 28%), confirming that tuning-based motion transfer suffers from structure and appearance leakage. Against FateZero [31], which fuses cross- and self-attention for spatial layout, VideoSwap achieves a 70% overall preference, with the weakest margin on Motion Alignment (67% vs 33%), suggesting attention-based guidance partially captures motion but cannot handle shape changes. Against Rerender-A-Video [46], which aligns optical flow for temporal consistency, VideoSwap leads most strongly (84% overall), as dense flow correspondence imposes rigid shape constraints that fail for subject swapping with shape changes. TokenFlow [12] and StableVideo [5] are compared qualitatively in Fig. 1; both struggle to reveal the correct target shape, while VideoSwap achieves significant shape change while preserving the source motion trajectory. Among the AnimateDiff baselines, DDIM+T2I-Adapter (spatial depth control) performs worst on Motion Alignment (59% vs 41%), confirming that low-level spatial cues restrict both shape and deformable motion.

## Relation to Prior Work

VideoSwap departs from the dominant paradigm in diffusion-based video editing, which relies on dense correspondences (optical flow, depth maps, attention maps, deformation fields) to enforce temporal consistency and motion alignment. By drawing on DIFT's emergent semantic point correspondences and Co-Tracker for point propagation, the paper repurposes image-domain point-based editing ideas (DragDiffusion, DragonDiffusion) into a video setting, replacing dense cues with a sparse set of user-defined semantic points. The pipeline is built on AnimateDiff's motion layers for temporal consistency and ED-LoRA for subject identity encoding, while Layered Neural Atlas provides the canonical-space machinery for propagating user drag displacements across frames. Conceptually, the paper positions itself at the intersection of point correspondence, video editing, and concept customization, arguing that sparse semantic points are sufficient to transfer motion trajectories without the shape rigidity imposed by dense correspondences.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper opens a practical path toward video editing with shape changes—a capability that dense-correspondence methods fundamentally cannot support—by showing that a handful of semantic points can anchor motion while leaving the target subject's geometry free. The user-point interaction design (removing, dragging points) offers an intuitive interface for handling partial or mismatched correspondences between source and target subjects. Limitations include the requirement for manual point definition, reliance on point tracker accuracy, and evaluation limited to 16-frame clips with primarily qualitative and human-preference metrics. Open questions include whether semantic point selection can be automated, how the approach scales to longer videos or multiple simultaneous subjects, and whether the sparse-point formulation generalizes beyond subject swapping to other shape-changing edits.
