## Problem

Existing neural stereo video codecs, notably LSVC, compress left and right views sequentially using explicit cross-view motion compensation, which prevents parallel processing and introduces latency unsuitable for real-time applications like VR and autonomous driving. This paper addresses the gap by replacing sequential disparity compensation with a parallel, learned module that exploits cross-view redundancy without requiring one view to be fully encoded before the other.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242332/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a rectified stereo video frame pair (left and right views) at resolutions up to 1920×704 (CityScapes) or 1216×320 (KITTI), along with previously encoded frames for temporal motion compensation. Both views are processed simultaneously through parallel branches, producing a joint compressed bit stream and reconstructed stereo frames. The codec operates with a Group-of-Picture size equal to the full sequence length (30 or 21 frames), and the transmitter and receiver are separately characterized for complexity analysis.

## Method

LLSS replaces sequential inter-view compensation with a Bidirectional Shift Module (BiShiftMod) that horizontally shifts left and right features bidirectionally (max disparity D, stride S), then computes groupwise cosine correlation and concatenation-based correlation to capture cross-view redundancy. This module is inserted after each convolutional block in both the parallel motion autoencoder and parallel context autoencoder, enabling information flow between the two view branches in a 'disparity-agnostic' fashion. The key insight is that rectified stereo disparity is always horizontal, so complex feature-based motion compensation (as in LSVC) can be simplified to learned horizontal shifts, reducing FLOPs to 35% of LSVC. Training proceeds in three stages: single-view pretraining on Vimeo90K, BiShiftMod-only training on CityScapes with frozen backbone, and full-network finetuning, with a rate-distortion loss combining MSE/MS-SSIM distortion and entropy-coded bit rate.

## Results

LLSS achieves 50.6% BD-rate savings over MV-HEVC on CityScapes, 18.2% on KITTI 2012, and 15.8% on KITTI 2015, substantially outperforming the prior state-of-the-art neural stereo codec LSVC (32.7%, 17.1%, 13.4% respectively). In terms of computational efficiency, LLSS uses only 35% of LSVC's FLOPs, with a 1.7× faster transmitter and 1.9× faster receiver on 512×512 stereo frame pairs. The ablation shows the BiShiftMod contributes 13.0% BD-rate reduction when applied to the full network (7.3% on codec + 6.1% on hypercodec).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LLSS outperforms all four baselines across all three datasets. Against the closest competitor LSVC (the only prior neural stereo video codec), LLSS achieves a dramatic 17.9-point BD-rate improvement on CityScapes (−50.6% vs −32.7%), while the gap narrows to 1.1 points on KITTI 2012 and 2.4 points on KITTI 2015, where distribution shift from training data limits all neural codecs. Against single-view neural codecs FVC and DCVC, LLSS wins by 35+ points on CityScapes and 5–15 points on KITTI, confirming the value of explicit cross-view modeling. Against the standard HEVC applied independently per view, LLSS achieves 84-point savings on CityScapes. Crucially, LLSS also runs 1.7× faster (transmitter) and 1.9× faster (receiver) than LSVC while using only 35% of its FLOPs, making it the first neural stereo codec suitable for real-time low-latency streaming in VR and autonomous driving.

## Relation to Prior Work

LLSS builds directly on LSVC [10], the first end-to-end neural stereo video codec, which used sequential cross-view disparity compensation (encode right view, then predict left). The per-branch autoencoder architecture is inherited from FVC [25] and DCVC [34], while the key architectural departure is the Bidirectional Shift Module (BiShiftMod), which replaces LSVC's complex sequential motion/disparity compensation networks with a parallel, learned horizontal-shift mechanism inspired by stereo matching cost-volume designs [9, 20, 50] and the latent-shift idea from SASIC [63]. The paper also frames its design through rate-distortion theory, arguing that parallel autoencoders with shared information flow reduce the mutual information I(Y^L; Y^R) between view latents, thereby lowering total bitrate overhead.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The parallel processing of both views eliminates the sequential latency bottleneck that limits LSVC and MV-HEVC for real-time applications like VR headsets and autonomous driving, while simultaneously achieving 50.6% BD-rate savings over MV-HEVC on CityScapes and a 35% FLOP reduction over LSVC. However, the gains over LSVC narrow considerably on KITTI (1.1–2.4%), suggesting sensitivity to training data distribution, and the DCVC comparison lacks finetuning, weakening that particular baseline. Open questions include whether the bidirectional-shift paradigm generalizes beyond two views (e.g., multi-camera arrays), whether it can be combined with perceptual or task-aware losses for downstream vision tasks, and whether the approach scales to higher resolutions and longer sequences without the current GoP-length constraint.
