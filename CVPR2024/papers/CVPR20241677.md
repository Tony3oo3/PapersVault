## Problem

Current speech-preserving facial expression manipulation (SPFEM) methods rely on inaccessible paired training samples—two temporally aligned frames of the same person with identical speech content but different emotions—limiting real-world applicability. Existing approaches either use imperfect surrogate reference images or global cyclic consistency constraints that fail to capture fine-grained facial changes, resulting in either inaccurate emotion transfer or distorted mouth shapes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241677/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a source video with speech content and a reference image (or video) specifying the target emotion, and produces an expression-modified video that preserves the original mouth animations. The ASCCL loss is plug-and-play: it is pre-trained on paired data from 36 MEAD speakers and then applied without retraining to supervise any other person's generation, operating on both intermediate 3DMM mesh parameters and final rendered images at multiple spatial scales.

## Method

ASCCL learns a spatial coherent correlation metric by extracting multi-scale features (4 layers) from a pre-trained ArcFace network and computing visual disparities between adjacent local regions via a two-layer MLP mapping of feature differences. A contrastive (InfoNCE-style) loss is trained on paired data to maximize similarity between corresponding regions of input and output images while pushing apart non-corresponding regions, with a temperature of 0.07. A Disparity-Aware Adaptive Strategy (DAAS) then modulates the loss per region using a sigmoid-weighted function of the visual disparity magnitude, assigning higher weights to challenging regions (e.g., mouth) and lower weights to simple regions (e.g., cheeks). During SPFEM training, the learned metric is applied as an auxiliary loss constraining the visual disparities of adjacent regions in the source to align with those in the generated output, improving both emotion transfer and lip-sync preservation simultaneously.

## Results

On the MEAD dataset (Cross-ID setting), integrating ASCCL into NED reduces FAD from 4.448 to 4.264, LSE-D from 9.906 to 9.238, and improves CSIM from 0.773 to 0.791. In the Inter-ID setting, the gains are more pronounced: FAD drops from 2.108 to 1.234 and CSIM rises from 0.831 to 0.900. A web-based user study with 25 participants confirms these gains, with ASCCL achieving 70% preference over NED's 30% for realism, 69% vs 31% for emotion similarity, and 73% vs 27% for mouth shape similarity across seven emotions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ASCCL is evaluated as a plug-and-play loss added to two existing SPFEM baselines: NED (two-stage 3DMM + rendering) and ICface (single-stage GAN). Against NED on MEAD Cross-ID, ASCCL reduces FAD by 0.184 (4.448→4.264), LSE-D by 0.668 (9.906→9.238), and improves CSIM by 0.018 (0.773→0.791); on Inter-ID the FAD improvement is larger (2.108→1.234) and CSIM jumps from 0.831 to 0.900. Against ICface on MEAD Cross-ID, ASCCL lowers LSE-D by 0.863 (11.238→10.375) and raises CSIM by 0.038 (0.688→0.726), while FAD improves modestly (9.540→9.480). The same plug-and-play gains transfer to the RAVDESS dataset without retraining ASCCL, where NED+ASCCL reduces Cross-ID FAD from 5.412 to 4.808 and LSE-D from 8.034 to 7.820. In all settings and both baselines, ASCCL consistently wins on every reported metric (FAD, LSE-D, CSIM), with the largest relative gains in LSE-D (lip-sync preservation) and CSIM (emotion transfer accuracy).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a plug-and-play loss module for existing speech-preserving facial expression manipulation (SPFEM) methods, specifically the two-stage NED (CVPR 2022) and single-stage ICface (WACV 2020). It departs from prior SPFEM work that relies on paired training samples (same speech, different emotions) or global cyclic consistency by instead exploiting a local, region-level correlation: visual disparities between adjacent local regions are preserved across emotional states of the same speaker. The metric learning component draws on contrastive learning frameworks (SimCLR, MoCo, CCPL), while the adaptive weighting strategy is inspired by focal loss, making the contribution a synthesis of established techniques applied to a new supervisory signal for face manipulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play design means practitioners can improve existing SPFEM pipelines without architectural changes, and the empirical validation across two datasets (MEAD, RAVDESS) and two baselines (NED, ICface) supports generalizability. However, the improvements, while consistent, are moderate (e.g., FAD reduction of ~0.18 in Cross-ID on MEAD), and the method is tested only on two datasets with a limited number of speakers. Open questions include whether the spatial coherent correlation assumption holds for highly stylized or non-photorealistic faces, whether the approach transfers to other face manipulation tasks beyond SPFEM, and whether the correlation metric can be learned in a fully unsupervised or few-shot manner to reduce the need for paired data.
