## Problem

Existing point-based interactive editing methods for diffusion models, such as DragDiffusion, rely on 'inner-inversion'—optimizing the noisy latent map to reflect user drags—which causes global latent map alterations, imprecise content preservation, and gradient vanishing when feature differences are small. This paper asks whether diffusion semantics can be edited directly within the U-Net's internal representations rather than through the latent map, avoiding these failure modes while maintaining editing precision.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241600/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 512×512 RGB image (real or diffusion-generated, treated uniformly with classifier-free guidance disabled), user-specified anchor–objective point pairs, and an optional spatial mask. Before editing, a LoRA adapter is fine-tuned on the input image for 200 steps to anchor reconstruction fidelity, and DDIM inversion produces the noisy latent map at t=35. The output is a single edited image where anchor points have been relocated to their objectives while all other semantic content is preserved; multi-point editing is supported simultaneously.

## Method

DragNoise's core insight is that the U-Net bottleneck feature at an early denoising timestep (t=35) already encodes the high-level semantics that remain stable through subsequent steps, making it an ideal single-point-of-edit for the entire denoising trajectory. Rather than optimizing the latent map, the method performs 'diffusion semantic optimization' by iteratively updating only the bottleneck feature using a semantic alignment loss (Eq. 1) computed on the U-Net decoder's third-layer features, which provide a spatially informative intermediate representation for point-to-feature mapping. After optimization converges (≤80 Adam steps, lr=0.01), the resulting 'manipulation noise' is produced, and the optimized bottleneck feature is propagated to all subsequent timesteps via direct feature substitution ('diffusion semantic propagation') until a refinement stage at t′=10, where original features are restored to recover fine details. This short optimization path (single bottleneck feature vs. full latent map) eliminates the long back-propagation chain responsible for gradient vanishing in DragDiffusion, reducing optimization time by over 50%.

## Results

On the DragBench benchmark, DragNoise achieves the best mean distance (MD) and image fidelity (IF) among all four compared methods (DragGAN, FreeDrag, DragDiffusion, and DragNoise), with diffusion-based methods generally outperforming GAN-based ones. In terms of optimization efficiency, DragNoise completes editing in ~25 optimization steps versus ~56 for DragDiffusion, and reduces total wall-clock time by over 50% (≈10 s vs. >22 s for a 512×512 image on a Tesla V100). Each individual optimization iteration is also ~10% faster due to the shorter back-propagation chain through the bottleneck feature rather than the full noisy latent map.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against **DragGAN** [23], DragNoise clearly wins on both MD and IF: DragGAN's 1D latent space causes unintended global shifts (e.g., facial shape changes when moving a local point), incorrect anchor tracking leading to flipping/mirroring artifacts, and blurring around anchor points where new content must be synthesized. Against **FreeDrag** [19], DragNoise similarly outperforms, as FreeDrag inherits the same GAN generation and inversion limitations, producing blurred regions and constrained semantic versatility. Against the closest competitor **DragDiffusion** [29], DragNoise achieves superior MD (more accurate point relocation) and IF (better preservation of unchanged content), while also being over 50% faster in total optimization time. DragDiffusion suffers from gradient vanishing when feature differences near anchor points are small (causing under-dragging, e.g., the horse example in Fig. 6f) and from semantic drift in the noisy latent map (e.g., hairstyle changing after a face rotation). DragNoise avoids both issues by optimizing the semantically rich bottleneck feature in a single timestep and propagating it forward, yielding stable, locally confined edits with higher fidelity.

## Relation to Prior Work

DragNoise builds directly on DragGAN's (Pan et al., SIGGRAPH 2023) point-based editing framework—adopting its semantic alignment loss and iterative anchor-point tracking—but departs fundamentally from the 'inner-inversion' paradigm shared by both DragGAN and the concurrent DragDiffusion. Rather than optimizing a 1D latent code (DragGAN) or a 2D noisy latent map (DragDiffusion), DragNoise identifies the U-Net bottleneck feature as a semantically rich, early-stable representation and optimizes only that feature at a single timestep, then propagates it to subsequent denoising steps. This reframing is grounded in prior observations that intermediate diffusion features encode semantic structure (Baranchuk et al. [4], Voynov et al. [34]), but the paper's systematic DDIM-inversion feature-copying experiments (Figs. 2–3) provide new empirical evidence that bottleneck features at t≈35 already capture the core image semantics needed for editing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the need to retrace the full latent map, DragNoise cuts optimization time by over 50% while improving both dragging accuracy and image fidelity on DragBench, making interactive diffusion editing more practical for real-time applications. The conceptual insight—that predicted noise can be treated as a sequential semantic editor and that a single-timestep bottleneck optimization suffices—opens a path toward faster, more stable diffusion-based editing without sacrificing semantic control. However, the method still relies on per-image LoRA fine-tuning to preserve real-image fidelity, and it struggles with edits requiring a global perspective (e.g., full object rotation), leaving open the question of whether a universal adapter or integrated multi-point understanding can close these gaps.
