## Problem

Existing model stealing defenses either impose heavy inference overheads (e.g., gradient redirection methods requiring surrogate model forward passes per query) or rely on strong assumptions about attack patterns (e.g., detection-based methods that fail against non-adaptive queries). This paper seeks a perturbation-based defense that is both computationally lightweight at inference time and effective against realistic, non-adaptive stealing attacks without substantially degrading utility for benign users.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242472/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the method takes a standard image query to the prediction API and returns a perturbed posterior probability vector of the same dimensionality as the original (C classes). The perturbation is a single matrix-vector multiplication: the predicted posterior is left-multiplied by a pre-optimized C×C stochastic noise transition matrix, adding negligible latency. The matrix is optimized offline via a bi-level procedure and is fixed during deployment, so no additional model forward pass is needed at serving time.

## Method

The core mechanism is applying a noise transition matrix (NTM) to predicted posteriors via linear mapping, which the paper proves (Theorem 1) makes the attacker's cloning task equivalent to learning from noisy-labeled data with instance-wise weights, thereby degrading clone generalization. To find the optimal NTM, the authors propose a bi-level optimization framework: the inner loop performs a one-step-forward gradient update on a surrogate model (simulating the attacker) under an attack loss (knowledge distillation loss on perturbed posteriors), while the outer loop updates the matrix under a defense loss that combines an alignment term (preserving the argmax label for benign users) and a misinformation term (suppressing the surrogate's confidence on the true predicted class). The matrix is projected to a valid stochastic matrix after each gradient step by clipping negatives and row-normalizing. This approach is model-agnostic, requires only a single matrix multiplication at inference, and exploits inter-class correlations learned during optimization to produce more adversarial noise than a uniform matrix at the same noise rate.

## Results

EMMA achieves the lowest attacker accuracy across all six attack scenarios and all three defender-accuracy-drop budgets (ΔAcc = 1, 2, 5). Under knowledge-limited attacks at ΔAcc=1, EMMA reduces attacker accuracy to 61.79% (CIFAR100→CIFAR10), 28.01% (CIFAR10→CIFAR100), and 19.10% (Caltech256→CUB200), compared to 77–84% for the next-best baselines. Under distribution-aware attacks at ΔAcc=1, EMMA achieves 84.10%, 56.73%, and 36.35% attacker accuracy on the three ImageNet-based scenarios, again the lowest in every column. The paper highlights that in the CIFAR100→CIFAR10 scenario, EMMA reduces attacker accuracy by 23% while only decreasing defender accuracy by 1%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EMMA outperforms all six baselines in every attack scenario and budget setting. Against the detection-based AM [Kariyappa, CVPR 2020], EMMA reduces attacker accuracy by 15.31 points on CIFAR100→CIFAR10 (61.79 vs 77.10) and by 9.02 points on Imagenet-CUB200→CUB200 (36.35 vs 45.12) at ΔAcc=1. Against the accuracy-constrained MAD [Orekondy, ICLR 2020], EMMA achieves a 18.78-point gap on CIFAR100→CIFAR10 (61.79 vs 80.57) and a 14.63-point gap on Imagenet-CUB200→CUB200 (36.35 vs 50.98). Against the self-undermining NASTY [Ma, ICLR 2021], EMMA leads by 14.86 points on CIFAR100→CIFAR10 (61.79 vs 76.65) and by 10.21 points on Imagenet-CUB200→CUB200 (36.35 vs 46.56). Against the gradient-redirection methods GARD² and MIN-IP [Mazeika, ICML 2022], EMMA achieves the largest margins: 15.63 points over GARD² and 17.09 points over MIN-IP on CIFAR100→CIFAR10, and 13.92 and 13.74 points respectively on Imagenet-CUB200→CUB200. The advantage of EMMA is most pronounced under knowledge-limited attacks where query distributions are semantically disjoint from the defender's training data, and it also maintains a clear lead under distribution-aware attacks where queries are semantically related.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the noise transition matrix (NTM) from the noisy-label learning literature, where it models the probability of true labels flipping to incorrect ones, and applies it as a perturbation mechanism for model stealing defense. It departs from existing perturbation-based defenses (MAD, GARD², MIN-IP) that require expensive gradient computations on a surrogate model at inference time, replacing them with a single matrix-vector multiplication that adds negligible overhead. The bi-level optimization framework for learning the optimal NTM draws on alternating optimization techniques, while the threat model follows the knockoff-nets / knowledge-distillation attack paradigm established by Orekondy et al. and subsequent work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in the efficiency gap: a single linear mapping achieves defense comparable to or exceeding gradient-redirection methods that impose substantial per-query computational cost, making it far more deployable in real MLaaS settings. Theorem 1 provides a clean theoretical guarantee that the attacker's cloning process degrades to noisy-label learning with instance-wise weights, giving practitioners a principled understanding of why the defense works. Limitations include evaluation restricted to image classification with ResNet backbones, reliance on a surrogate model during optimization (which may not perfectly represent the true attacker), and the defense being tuned to specific attack distributions. Open questions include whether the approach extends to generative models, NLP tasks, or adaptive attackers who can detect and counter the perturbation pattern.
