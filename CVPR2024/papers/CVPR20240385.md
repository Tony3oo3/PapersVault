## Problem

Existing continual learning methods rely on one-hot labels with randomly initialized classifier heads, ignoring the rich semantic information contained in category names. This leads to representation drifting (each class's target is optimized only for its current task, causing feature-space conflicts) and inefficient knowledge transfer (random initialization cannot capture semantic correlations across tasks). The paper asks whether leveraging pretrained language model knowledge in the classifier can mitigate both issues.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240385/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images for the vision encoder and plain-text category names (e.g., 'eagle', 'parrot') for the language model. The category names are processed once via a single forward pass through a PLM (e.g., CLIP-B/32 text transformer) to produce fixed d-dimensional semantic target vectors that form the frozen classifier. The output is a standard class label predicted by matching the image embedding against these frozen targets via inner-product similarity.

## Method

LingoCL replaces the randomly initialized, jointly trained classifier with a frozen classifier whose weights are generated by feeding each class's category name into a pretrained language model. During continual training, only the vision encoder is optimized to align image embeddings with these frozen semantic targets, while the classifier remains fixed to preserve the PLM's semantic knowledge. Because the PLM was pretrained on a vast and diverse corpus, each generated target implicitly encodes semantic relationships to all other classes (past, present, and future), which prevents representation drift and enables backward knowledge transfer. The approach is orthogonal to existing CL strategies (replay, distillation, regularization, architecture expansion) and can be plugged into any baseline with negligible computational overhead—just one extra forward pass through the text model per task.

## Results

On ImageNet-100 under class-incremental learning (B=50, C=10), LingoCL improves average accuracy by 3.2%–6.1% and reduces the forgetting rate by 2.6%–13.1% across eleven baselines. For example, LUCIR's average accuracy rises from 67.0% to 70.2% while its forgetting rate drops from 34.5% to 21.4%. Under task-incremental learning on CIFAR-100, accuracy gains range from 3.9% to 9.7%, and under domain-incremental learning on OfficeHome, accuracy improves by 1.2%–4.0% with forgetting reduced by 3.8%–7.6%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LingoCL consistently improves every baseline it is integrated with, with the largest gains observed on distillation- and rectification-based methods. On ImageNet-100 (B=50, C=10), LUCIR benefits most in forgetting reduction (−13.1%) and IL2M most in average accuracy (+4.2%), while DyTox and AANet (architecture-based) see smaller but still positive gains (+0.8% and +0.4% Avg, respectively). In task-incremental learning, SI shows the single largest accuracy jump (+9.7% Last, from 41.4% to 51.1%), surpassing the rehearsal-based GEM baseline. In domain-incremental learning on OfficeHome, all four regularization/rehearsal baselines (EWC, MAS, GEM, SI) improve in both accuracy and forgetting, with MAS gaining +4.0% Last and SI gaining +3.5% Last. In the logits-rectification comparison (Table 5), LingoCL (67.5% Avg, 22.5% F) outperforms BiC (65.4%), EEIL (65.0%), and Divergence head (63.7%), confirming that semantic correlation in the classifier provides complementary benefits beyond statistical corrections.

## Relation to Prior Work

LingoCL departs from the standard CL paradigm where classifiers are randomly initialized and jointly optimized with the encoder, instead repurposing pretrained language model outputs as frozen semantic targets for the classifier head. Conceptually, it draws on the cross-modality adaptation literature (CLIP, Tex, DUET) that transfers language knowledge into visual models, but applies this idea specifically to the classifier component in continual learning—a role that prior CL work (architecture-based, replay-based, distillation-based, regularization-based, and rectification-based methods) has largely ignored. The method is explicitly orthogonal to existing CL strategies, positioning itself as a plug-in enhancement rather than a competing approach, and it builds on the observation that category names encode inter-class semantic structure that one-hot labels cannot capture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is high: a single forward pass through a PLM replaces the randomly initialized classifier, with negligible computational cost, and the technique integrates seamlessly into any existing CL method. The finding that PLM-generated frozen targets can match or slightly surpass an oracle classifier (trained on all tasks) suggests that the conceptual diversity of PLM pretraining data exceeds that of typical CL benchmarks, raising the question of how much of the remaining gap to oracle is attributable to the encoder rather than the classifier. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to open-vocabulary or streaming settings where category names may be unavailable or ambiguous, and whether the benefits persist at much larger class scales (e.g., full ImageNet-1k) where the semantic granularity of PLM embeddings may become less discriminative.
