## Problem

Existing public anomaly detection datasets contain very few defective images (typically ~100 or fewer, often only in test sets), making supervised anomaly detection impractical and prone to overfitting. Additionally, current supervised AD methods either require pixel-level defect labels or overfit to seen defect types, failing to generalize to unseen anomalies in real production settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240466/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

SegAD takes RGB images (512×512 in VAD, resized to 256×256 for evaluation) and a segmentation map that partitions the image into L mutually exclusive regions (L=7 for VAD: background, outer half-circle, piezo border, solder dots, wires, pins, piezo center; SAM-generated binary or multi-part masks for VisA). It outputs a single scalar anomaly score representing the probability of defect presence. No pixel-level labels are required—only an image-level good/bad flag per training sample.

## Method

SegAD is a three-stage pipeline: (1) one or more one-class anomaly detectors (PatchCore, FastFlow, RD4AD, EfficientAD) produce pixel-level anomaly maps, and optionally a supervised classifier (Wide ResNet50) yields a global classification score; (2) the segmentation map splits each anomaly map into L segments, and per-segment statistics—99.5% quantile, skewness, kurtosis, and mean—are extracted for each detector, forming a feature vector of length K×L×4+1; (3) these features are fed into a Boosted Random Forest (XGBoost) trained on image-level labels to produce the final anomaly score. The key insight is that local per-segment statistics emphasize relatively low anomaly scores in structurally important regions while suppressing high scores in noisy areas, so the exact position and appearance of a defect matters less as long as it falls within a designated segment. This design eliminates the need for pixel-level annotations and allows ensembling multiple one-class detectors in a supervised manner, improving robustness to both seen and unseen defect types.

## Results

On the VAD high-shot supervised benchmark, SegAD (AllAD + WRN + Ours) achieves 96.5% Cl. AUROC and 16.4% FPR@95TPR, outperforming the best supervised anomaly detector DRA (87.4% Cl. AUROC, 60.9% FPR@95TPR) by +9.1 Cl. AUROC and the supervised classifier WRN (95.0%, 32.8%) by +1.5 Cl. AUROC and −16.4 FPR@95TPR. On the VisA supervised benchmark, All AD + Ours reaches 98.4% Cl. AUROC and 7.5% FPR@95TPR, surpassing EfficientAD alone (98.0%, 8.1%) and DRA (88.9%, 42.4%). In the low-shot setting (100 bad images), All AD + Ours still achieves 92.7% Cl. AUROC and 36.8% FPR@95TPR, well above DevNet (73.1%) and DRA (78.9%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SegAD consistently outperforms all one-class anomaly detectors when used as a wrapper: PatchCore improves from 88.3% to 91.7% Cl. AUROC (+3.4), RD4AD from 84.5% to 90.2% (+5.7), FastFlow from 79.1% to 86.9% (+7.8), and EfficientAD from 89.1% to 91.4% (+2.3) on VAD. Against supervised anomaly detection baselines, SegAD (AllAD+WRN+Ours) beats DevNet by +9.6 Cl. AUROC and DRA by +9.1 Cl. AUROC on VAD, and by +9.5 and +9.1 respectively on VisA. The supervised classifier WRN alone achieves 95.0% Cl. AUROC on VAD but cannot detect unseen defects (its score distribution for unseen defects shifts left, as shown in Fig. 6b), whereas SegAD maintains comparable performance on unseen defects while achieving 96.5% overall. On VisA, the improvement over the strongest one-class baseline EfficientAD is more modest (+0.3 Cl. AUROC, −1.9 FPR@95TPR), which the authors attribute to VisA objects being less structurally complex than VAD. In the low-shot setting (100 bad images), SegAD (All AD + Ours) still leads all baselines by a wide margin (92.7% vs. 78.9% for DRA), though the gap to WRN narrows since WRN degrades more severely with fewer training examples.

## Relation to Prior Work

SegAD repurposes existing one-class anomaly detectors (PatchCore, FastFlow, RD4AD, EfficientAD) as feature extractors whose pixel-level anomaly maps are decomposed via segmentation into local statistical features (quantile, skew, kurtosis, mean), which are then fed to a Boosted Random Forest. This departs from prior supervised anomaly detection methods (DevNet, DRA) that require pixel-level defect masks or extensive data augmentation to generate synthetic defects, and from one-class methods that rely solely on a global maximum-pooling score. The VAD dataset extends the lineage of MVTec AD, LOCO AD, and VisA by providing real-world (non-simulated) defects, logical constraints, and—critically—1000 bad training images, a regime absent from all prior public benchmarks where supervised methods were trained on as few as 10 defective examples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The VAD dataset fills a practical gap by offering a supervised benchmark with sufficient defective examples and real production-line defects (including logical ones), enabling meaningful evaluation of methods that prior datasets could not support. The finding that simple per-segment statistics plus a BRF classifier outperform purpose-built supervised anomaly detectors (DevNet, DRA) by several AUROC points suggests that the field may be over-engineering the final classification stage at the expense of better feature decomposition. Open questions include generalization to unaligned or variable-geometry objects where static segmentation maps fail, whether the approach scales to multi-class or multi-product settings, and whether the distribution mismatch observed with EfficientAD in the one-class setting is a fundamental limitation of using generated defects as training proxies.
