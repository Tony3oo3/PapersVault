## Problem

Existing multimodal benchmarks predominantly test commonsense or daily knowledge rather than expert-level domain expertise and deliberate reasoning. The closest prior benchmark, ScienceQA, covers diverse disciplines but remains at elementary-to-middle-school difficulty, falling short of the 'Expert AGI' standard (90th percentile of skilled adults) proposed by Morris et al. This paper addresses the absence of a comprehensive, college-level multimodal benchmark that demands both advanced perception across heterogeneous image types and step-by-step reasoning grounded in deep subject-specific knowledge.

## Input / Output

Each question presents interleaved text and one or more images (97.5% contain at least one image; 7.4% contain multiple images), with images positioned at the beginning (17.8%), middle (36.9%), or end (50.4%) of the prompt. The 30 image types span photographs, paintings, charts, diagrams, tables, music sheets, chemical structures, medical scans (MRI/CT), and geometric shapes. 94% of questions are multiple-choice (typically 4 options) and 6% are open-ended; evaluation uses micro-averaged accuracy with rule-based regex pipelines to extract final answers from potentially long model responses, falling back to random selection for multiple-choice when no valid answer is detected.

## Method

MMMU is constructed through a three-stage curation process: (1) selecting 30 subjects across 6 disciplines where visual inputs are commonly used in coursework; (2) recruiting 50+ college students as annotators to collect 13K multimodal questions from textbooks, online resources, and lecture materials, with instructions to avoid immediately available answers to mitigate data contamination; (3) quality control via lexical/URL deduplication, format standardization, and difficulty classification (excluding ~10% 'very easy' items), yielding 11.5K final questions split into dev (150), validation (900), and test (10,500) sets. Evaluation is conducted zero-shot across 28 open-source LMMs and proprietary models (GPT-4V, Gemini Ultra, Claude 3 Opus), with text-only LLM baselines augmented by OCR (MMOCR) or LLaVA-1.5 captions to test whether external image-to-text tools suffice. A human expert baseline (90 college seniors, 3 per subject) achieves 88.6% validation accuracy, while GPT-4V reaches only 55.7% test accuracy. Error analysis on 150 GPT-4V failures attributes 35% to perceptual errors, 29% to lack of domain knowledge, and 26% to flawed reasoning, revealing that OCR/captioning augmentation yields negligible gains and confirming the need for genuine joint multimodal interpretation.

## Results

On the MMMU test set (10,500 questions), GPT-4V(ision) achieves 55.7% micro-averaged accuracy, while Gemini Ultra and Claude 3 Opus reach 59.4% on the validation set. The best open-source LMMs at submission time (BLIP-2 FLAN-T5-XXL, LLaVA-1.5-13B) achieve only ~34% test accuracy, though newer models like InternVL-Chat-V1.2 (46.2%) and Qwen-VL-MAX (46.8%) narrow the gap. Human experts score 88.6% (best) on validation, far exceeding all models. GPT-4V's accuracy drops sharply with difficulty: 76.1% on Easy, 55.6% on Medium, and only 31.2% on Hard questions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4V(ision) leads all evaluated LMMs with 55.7% test accuracy, outperforming the best open-source models at submission time (BLIP-2 FLAN-T5-XXL at 34.0%, LLaVA-1.5-13B at 33.6%) by over 20 percentage points. Newer open-source models substantially close the gap: InternVL-Chat-V1.2 (46.2%) and Qwen-VL-MAX (46.8%) trail GPT-4V by roughly 9–10 points. Gemini Ultra and Claude 3 Opus (both 59.4% validation) marginally surpass GPT-4V's 56.8% validation score. Text-only LLMs augmented with OCR or LLaVA captions (e.g., FLAN-T5-XXL + OCR: 31.9% test) show only marginal gains over their text-only counterparts, confirming that MMMU requires genuine joint visual-textual understanding rather than simple image-to-text conversion. Across disciplines, GPT-4V performs best on Humanities & Social Science (76.3%) and Art & Design (65.3%), but drops to 41.7% on Tech & Engineering and 48.4% on Science, where complex diagrams and multi-step reasoning dominate. Human experts (88.6% best, 76.2% worst) remain substantially ahead of all models, underscoring the benchmark's difficulty.

## Relation to Prior Work

MMMU extends the multi-discipline evaluation paradigm established by MMLU and AGIEval from text-only to multimodal settings, while pushing the difficulty from elementary/middle-school (as in ScienceQA) to college-level expert reasoning. It departs from existing multimodal benchmarks (VQA, MMBench, MM-Vet, SEED) that focus on commonsense and daily knowledge by requiring domain-specific knowledge across 30 subjects, 30 heterogeneous image types, and interleaved text-image inputs. The benchmark is explicitly motivated by the Expert AGI taxonomy of Morris et al., positioning multimodal college-level problem-solving as a necessary (though not sufficient) criterion for that milestone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The large gap between GPT-4V (55.7%) and the best human experts (88.6%) on MMMU provides a concrete, reproducible measure of how far current LMMs are from expert-level multimodal reasoning, and the error decomposition (35% perceptual, 29% knowledge, 26% reasoning) gives actionable targets for model improvement. The finding that OCR or captioning pipelines do not meaningfully boost text-only LLMs underscores that true joint multimodal understanding—not just reading text off images—is the bottleneck. The authors acknowledge limitations (manual curation bias, college-level scope may not fully capture Expert AGI), and open questions remain around how to inject domain-specific knowledge, improve complex multi-step reasoning, and reduce the model's documented bias toward prioritizing text over visual evidence.
