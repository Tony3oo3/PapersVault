## Problem

Conventional AMCW-ToF cameras require four sequential quadrature measurements to recover scene depth and intensity, which limits frame rate and introduces motion artifacts in dynamic scenes. No existing CW-ToF approach captures both amplitude and phase in a single snapshot measurement, making real-time depth imaging of moving scenes impractical with standard hardware.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240285/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single CW-ToF snapshot image in which the reference phase θ is varied linearly across rows (or columns) at a rate of k=π/2 per line (R=4 rows per full 2π cycle), and produces a per-pixel depth map (from the recovered phase) and an intensity image (from the recovered amplitude). The spatial resolution is inherently reduced relative to the sensor's native resolution because optical prefiltering (a defocused lens) is required to suppress aliasing and noise folding between the hologram and its twin in the Fourier domain.

## Method

The key insight is expressing the CW-ToF measurement as a complex sinusoid (the 'ToF hologram') whose amplitude and phase encode scene intensity and depth, enabling a direct analogy to off-axis holography. By spatially varying the reference phase linearly during exposure—emulated via a rolling-shutter effect using a Melexis 75027 sensor with hardware ROI and galvanic mirrors—the ToF hologram and its complex-conjugate twin are separated in the Fourier domain. Recovery proceeds by taking the FFT of the snapshot image, bandpass-filtering to isolate the hologram while discarding the twin and DC component, shifting the hologram to the origin, and computing the inverse FFT to extract amplitude and phase. An anisotropic (1D) prefiltering kernel applied only along the rolling-shutter direction minimizes resolution loss, and the technique is shown to be robust to dead/saturated pixels and to outperform the standard N-bucket reconstruction at all phase variation rates.

## Results

The proposed snapshot CW-ToF technique achieves a phase reconstruction SNR of 29.86 dB, slightly exceeding the conventional four-bucket quadrature method (28.56 dB) under matched total exposure duration (T = 100 µs). Critically, the snapshot method requires only a single image, reducing data bandwidth by 4× compared to the four sequential quadrature measurements. Prefiltering with a 1D Gaussian kernel (σ = 1) in the rolling-shutter direction yields a 3 dB SNR gain (2× smaller phase error) over no prefiltering, and the optimal phase variation rate is R = 4 (k = π/2 line⁻¹), which minimizes Fourier-domain overlap between the ToF hologram and its twin.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the conventional four-bucket quadrature method, the proposed snapshot Fourier technique achieves a marginally higher phase SNR (29.86 dB vs. 28.56 dB) while requiring 4× less data bandwidth, and performs uniformly better across all prefilter kernel sizes σ. Against the N-bucket reconstruction applied to the same snapshot image (using neighboring rows as proxies for missing phase offsets), the Fourier technique uniformly outperforms it at all tested phase variation rates and additionally supports non-integer R values, whereas N-bucket is restricted to integer R. The paper also demonstrates that the snapshot technique introduces no additional phase noise relative to the conventional method, and is robust to dead and saturated pixels—capabilities the sequential quadrature approach does not explicitly address. Overall, the snapshot method matches or slightly exceeds conventional accuracy while delivering a 4× temporal resolution improvement, making it suitable for dynamic scenes where motion artifacts degrade sequential capture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws a direct conceptual bridge between off-axis holography (Cuche et al. [7]) and AMCW-ToF imaging by expressing the ToF measurement as a complex sinusoid (the 'ToF hologram'), enabling the Fourier-domain reconstruction pipeline (filter twin, shift hologram, inverse FFT) to be transplanted from holography to depth sensing. It departs from prior snapshot ToF work by Ballester et al. [2,3], which required dual-wavelength superheterodyned interferometry, by showing that a single-wavelength AMCW-ToF sensor with a rolling-shutter (or equivalent spatial phase variation) suffices. The phasor representation of ToF measurements from Gupta [9] and O'Toole [26] provides the mathematical foundation that makes the holography analogy rigorous, while the N-bucket technique [28] serves as the conventional baseline the paper outperforms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 4× reduction in data bandwidth and corresponding 4× improvement in temporal resolution is practically significant as CW-ToF cameras proliferate in AR/VR, robotics, and autonomous navigation, where motion artifacts from sequential quadrature capture are a persistent problem. Key limitations include the spatial resolution sacrifice (mitigated by prefiltering/defocusing), the current firmware lock preventing real-time operation, and the hardware-specific implementation (Melexis 75027 + galvanic mirrors). Open questions include whether ML-based reconstruction (deep image priors, diffusion models) can relax the anti-aliasing constraints, and whether the Fourier-embedding principle extends to non-sinusoidal codes, Doppler ToF, and other coded-exposure modalities.
