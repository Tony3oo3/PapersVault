## Problem

Existing text-based image tone adjustment methods rely on supervised learning with expert-retouched paired datasets, incurring high data collection costs and limiting adjustments to stylistic variants present in training data. No prior approach enables unsupervised, text-guided tone adjustment that generalizes to arbitrary natural language descriptions unseen during training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242426/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image (evaluated at 512×512) and a free-form text description (e.g., 'warm photo', 'cinematic photo') as input. The text is encoded by a frozen CLIP-RN50 text encoder, with a fixed source prompt 'normal photo' representing the input image's tone. The output is a tone-adjusted image of the same resolution, produced by applying an image-text adaptive 3D LUT via trilinear interpolation. A scaling factor s controls the strength of adjustment, enabling smooth interpolation between the original image and a fully adjusted result.

## Method

CLIPtone extends the AdaInt 3D LUT-based enhancement backbone (Yang et al., CVPR 2022) with a lightweight hyper-network text adapter that modulates only the AdaInt module and weight predictor parameters via a Hadamard-product formula θ̂ = θ ⊗ (1 + s·Δθ), while keeping all basis LUTs and CNN features frozen. The text adapter computes a directional vector in CLIP embedding space (target text embedding minus the fixed 'normal photo' source embedding) and passes it through a two-layer MLP to produce Δθ. Training is fully unsupervised, requiring only unpaired source images (4,500 from MIT-Adobe 5K) and text descriptions (30,138 color names from the Color Names Dataset), with no ground-truth adjusted images. The loss combines a CLIP directional loss (adopted from StyleGAN-NADA) that aligns the image-change direction with the text-change direction in CLIP space, a content-preserving MSE loss, and a novel LUT regularization loss (weight L2 penalty plus a sampling-interval loss that penalizes abrupt color transitions in the adaptive 3D LUT). The key insight is that CLIP, pretrained on a vast corpus of image-text pairs, inherently encodes human perception of tonal properties and thus serves as a differentiable perceptual criterion, enabling zero-shot generalization to unseen text descriptions.

## Results

On 500 MIT-Adobe 5K images with 50 tone-related text descriptions, CLIPtone achieves a Grayscale SSIM of 0.858, CLIP image similarity of 0.975, and CLIP text-image directional similarity of 0.083, balancing structure preservation and text alignment. Against pseudo-ground-truth images from Adobe Lightroom filters, CLIPtone attains the highest CLIP directional similarity (0.467) and second-highest CLIP image similarity (0.948). In terms of efficiency, CLIPtone runs in 10.3 ms per 512×512 image with 111.4M parameters, substantially faster than IP2P (7177.4 ms, 1016.9M params) and T2ONet (45.1 ms, 21.1M params).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLIPtone outperforms T2ONet by a large margin on CLIP text-image directional similarity (0.083 vs. 0.021), indicating far more effective tone adjustment, though T2ONet preserves structure slightly better (Grayscale SSIM 0.936 vs. 0.858) because it barely modifies the input. Against CLIPstyler, CLIPtone dramatically improves structure preservation (Grayscale SSIM 0.858 vs. 0.484; CLIP image similarity 0.975 vs. 0.669) while sacrificing only a small amount of text alignment (0.083 vs. 0.095). Compared to IP2P, CLIPtone achieves higher scores on all three metrics (0.858 vs. 0.765 SSIM, 0.975 vs. 0.932 CLIP image sim., 0.083 vs. 0.073 directional sim.) and is roughly 700× faster (10.3 ms vs. 7177.4 ms). On the pseudo-ground-truth evaluation against Adobe Lightroom filters, CLIPtone achieves the best CLIP directional similarity (0.467 vs. 0.359 for T2ONet, 0.310 for IP2P, 0.297 for CLIPstyler), confirming its adjustments most closely match expert-designed tone filters. A 20-participant user study further confirms CLIPtone is preferred over all baselines on structure preservation, text-description alignment, and aesthetic quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

CLIPtone extends the 3D LUT-based image enhancement architecture of AdaInt (Yang et al., CVPR 2022) by introducing a hyper-network text adapter that modulates the AdaInt and weight-predictor parameters, following the modulation strategy from HyperStyle (Alaluf et al., CVPR 2022). For training supervision, it repurposes the CLIP directional loss from StyleGAN-NADA (Gal et al., ACM TOG 2022) as a differentiable perceptual criterion, replacing the paired-image supervision that prior text-based tone adjustment methods (T2ONet, Jiang et al.) require. The key conceptual departure is from both supervised tone adjustment (which needs expert-retouched pairs) and heavy generative-model-based editing (IP2P, CLIPstyler), positioning a lightweight LUT backbone guided by CLIP's pre-trained language-image alignment as a sufficient substitute for either.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for real-time applications: CLIPtone runs in ~10 ms versus ~7.2 s for IP2P while matching or exceeding quality, making text-guided tone adjustment feasible on-device. The zero-shot result—performing nearly identically on unseen keywords—suggests CLIP's pre-trained perceptual knowledge is a viable substitute for task-specific paired data, a finding that could generalize to other perceptual image-editing tasks. However, the method is limited to global tone adjustment, inherits CLIP's biases, and cannot handle styles difficult to express in text; extending to local adjustment or reference-image conditioning (via CLIP's image encoder) are natural next steps that the authors identify.
