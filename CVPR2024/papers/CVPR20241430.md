## Problem

In single-photon LiDAR, packing more pixels into a unit space increases spatial resolution but decreases per-pixel SNR due to the stochastic Poisson nature of photon arrivals. Prior theoretical analyses (e.g., Bar-David 1969, Altmann 2016) characterized only single-pixel estimation error, leaving the fundamental resolution–noise trade-off across a pixel array without a closed-form characterization. This paper asks whether the MSE of the depth estimate can be derived in closed form as a function of the number of pixels per unit space.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a set of photon time stamps per pixel (drawn from an inhomogeneous Poisson process with rate determined by scene reflectivity α and background flux λ_b), a known pulse shape s(t) (Gaussian in the main analysis), and the spatial coordinate x. It produces a piecewise-constant reconstructed depth profile τ̂(x) via per-pixel MLE, and a closed-form MSE expression as a function of pixel count N, total flux α₀, pulse width σ_t, scene slope c, and pixel width σ_x. The 2D extension replaces the scalar slope with the integrated gradient norm ‖∇τ‖² over the unit square.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a three-step approximation chain that makes the multi-pixel MSE analytically tractable: (1) linearize the time-of-arrival function τ(x) as piecewise linear around each pixel midpoint, (2) replace the boxcar pixel indicator with a Gaussian kernel of optimal width σ_x = W/√12 (Lemma 1), and (3) replace the spatial projection integral in the effective return pulse with a spatially invariant convolution. Under these approximations, the effective pulse at pixel n becomes a Gaussian with modified variance σ_n² = c_n²σ_x² + σ_t², where c_n is the local slope of τ(x). Applying the per-pixel Cramer-Rao bound (Theorem 2) and a bias-variance decomposition of the L₂ error between the true τ(x) and the piecewise-constant estimate yields the closed-form MSE = c²/(12N²) + (N/α₀)(c²σ_x² + σ_t²), where the first term is the quantization bias and the second is the photon-noise variance. The result is validated against Monte Carlo simulations in 1D and 2D, and against real 192×128 SPAD data from a picosecond pulsed laser experiment.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 4) provides a closed-form MSE expression: MSE(τ̂, τ) = c²/(12N²) + (N/α₀)(c²σₓ² + σₜ²), where c² is the average squared slope of the time-of-arrival function, α₀ is total scene flux, σₜ is pulse width, and σₓ is the pixel-width proxy. The MSE exhibits a U-shape as a function of pixel count N, with the optimal N ≈ 64 in the 1D simulation. In the real 2D experiment on a 192×128 SPAD sensor, the optimal N exceeds the physical sensor resolution, meaning no binning is beneficial. The theoretical predictions match both Monte Carlo simulations (1D and 2D) and real SPAD data with excellent agreement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without traditional method-to-method baselines. The key empirical comparison is between the full theoretical model (including the c²σₓ² spatial-derivative term in the variance) and a simplified 'lazy' model that omits this term (Eq. 29 vs. Eq. 28). The simplified model, which assumes the pulse width is always σₜ regardless of pixel size, fails to match the simulation, while the full model with c²σₓ² included reproduces the simulated MSE accurately (Fig. 6). The paper also validates against a single-pixel Cramér-Rao bound from Bar-David (1969) and Abshire & McGarry (1987), showing that the per-pixel error in Theorem 2 achieves the CRLB equality. In the real 2D experiment, the measured MSE (computed via bootstrap resampling of 10,000 frames) aligns with the theoretical prediction, confirming the theory's applicability to practical SPAD sensors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the single-pixel Cramér-Rao bound for photon-limited LiDAR (Bar-David 1969; Abshire & McGarry 1987; Altmann et al. 2016) to the multi-pixel array setting, where the fundamental question becomes how MSE scales with the number of pixels packed per unit space. The key methodological departure is a series of approximation techniques—linearizing the time-of-arrival function, replacing the boxcar pixel kernel with a matched-variance Gaussian, and converting spatial projection into convolution—that make the array-level MSE tractable in closed form. Prior single-pixel analyses cannot capture the bias-variance trade-off that emerges when spatial resolution is varied, which is the central phenomenon this paper characterizes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The closed-form MSE expression (Theorem 4) gives sensor designers a first-principles prediction of the optimal pixel count for a given scene slope, pulse width, and total flux, and the U-shaped curve explains why simply maximizing spatial resolution is not always optimal. Limitations include the Gaussian-pulse and zero-background assumptions, the neglect of dead-time and pile-up, and the constant-reflectivity assumption; the authors note that pile-up and non-Gaussian extensions require numerical integration. An open question is whether advanced post-processing (e.g., deep learning denoisers) can outperform the ML-based bound, which would shift the practical operating point. The result also clarifies when binning is unnecessary—when the optimal N exceeds the physical sensor resolution—as observed in their real SPAD experiment.
