## Problem

Replicating specific camera movements and character behaviors across different video clips is difficult: SLAM-based methods produce noisy trajectories in dynamic scenes, and prior NeRF-based pose estimation approaches require manually constructing a scene that closely resembles the original shot. No existing pipeline can automatically extract both the camera trajectory and character motion from a single film shot and transfer them to arbitrary new 2D or 3D content without such manual scene preparation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240400/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single film shot video (a sequence of RGB frames containing one or more characters and complex camera motion) as input, along with the SMPL body model. It produces SMPL tracks in world coordinates (per-frame 3D body poses), an optimized SE(3) camera trajectory, and then either a 2D transfer video (new 3D characters composited over the original background via ProPainter inpainting) or a 3D transfer scene rendered in a game engine with full control over lighting, character identity, and camera path. The method handles various shot types (Arc, Track, Push-In, Pull-Out, Pan, Follow) and supports single, dual, or multiple characters per shot.

## Method

The pipeline first obtains an initial camera trajectory via SLAM and per-frame SMPL parameters, then lifts them to world coordinates using a 4D human reconstruction model (SLAHMR). A dynamic NeRF (D-NeRF) is trained to represent the SMPL tracks as a differentiable scene, and the camera trajectory is then re-optimized by inverting this NeRF with two custom losses: a composition loss comparing color-coded character masks (matched to SMPL vertex colors) and a joint loss comparing ViTPose-predicted 2D joints against reprojected SMPL joints. To ensure temporal smoothness, the per-frame camera correction is parameterized via screw-axis coordinates where two MLPs predict the rotation axis w_t and translation v_t as continuous functions of time, with the initial pose at each step set to the previous frame's optimized pose. The refined camera trajectory is fed back into the 4D reconstruction to produce final SMPL tracks, which are then retargeted to new characters and rendered through a 3D engine workflow for 2D or 3D cinematic transfer.

## Results

Across six shot movement types (Push-In, Pull-Out, Pan, Track, Follow, Arc) evaluated on 100+ film shots, the proposed method achieves the best scores on all three metrics (Pixel Accuracy, IoU, MPJPE) against both DROID-SLAM and iNeRF. The most striking gains are in MPJPE: e.g., 59.6 vs. 404.9 (DROID-SLAM) and 292.6 (iNeRF) for Push-In, and 23.8 vs. 356.2 and 83.9 for Pull-Out. In the user study (7-point Likert scale), the method scores 6.0±0.5 on 2D camera movement restoration vs. 4.7±1.3 for the SLAM+SMPL baseline, and 5.3±0.6 vs. 4.4±1.0 for 3D camera movement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DROID-SLAM, the proposed method wins on every metric across all six shot types, with the largest gaps in MPJPE (e.g., 59.6 vs. 404.9 for Push-In; 130.9 vs. 1046.9 for Follow), indicating that SLAM's camera trajectory is heavily corrupted by dynamic character content. Against iNeRF, the method also dominates all metrics; the MPJPE advantage is 4.9× for Push-In (59.6 vs. 292.6) and 3.5× for Pull-Out (23.8 vs. 83.9), attributed to the composition and joint losses replacing iNeRF's RGB-only supervision. Against JAWS [28], the comparison is qualitative (Fig. 5): the proposed method better preserves the original shot's composition, while JAWS's hand-crafted scene fails to match character framing, especially in the Matrix push-in example. Against PACE [7] and SLAHMR [31] (Fig. 6), the proposed method produces more accurate limb poses in world coordinates, whereas PACE fails on partial-body shots and SLAHMR exhibits open-arm errors due to DROID-SLAM noise on dynamic content. In the user study, the method outperforms the SLAM+SMPL baseline on camera movement restoration in both 2D (6.0 vs. 4.7) and 3D (5.3 vs. 4.4) settings, while character motion scores are comparable (5.8 vs. 5.5 in 2D; 5.0 vs. 4.9 in 3D).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the NeRF-as-differentiable-renderer lineage (iNeRF, BARF) for camera pose estimation, but departs from prior work by eliminating the need for a manually constructed scene: instead of training NeRF on a real or synthetic environment, it trains a dynamic NeRF on SMPL tracks alone and introduces composition and joint losses to replace the RGB supervision that prior methods (iNeRF, JAWS) rely on. For human motion, it extends the SLAM-initialized 4D reconstruction pipeline (SLAHMR, PACE) by re-optimizing the camera trajectory through differentiable rendering, and adds a continuous trajectory parameterization via MLPs over screw-axis parameters to enforce temporal smoothness—addressing a gap where prior NeRF pose estimators optimized each frame independently. The 3D engine-based transfer workflow (retargeting SMPL to new characters, compositing with ProPainter inpainting) positions the work as a production-oriented pipeline rather than a pure estimation method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for digital media production: artists can extract cinematic behavior (camera + character motion) from a reference shot and apply it to entirely new 2D or 3D content without manually rebuilding the scene, which prior methods like JAWS required. Key limitations remain: the method depends on SLAM for an initial trajectory (failing when content changes too rapidly), is designed specifically for human subjects (degrading to a SLAM-like approach for environment-focused shots), and the SMPL approximation loses clothing and fine-grained detail. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to non-human characters, whether the continuous trajectory parameterization scales to very long shots, and whether the 3D engine workflow can be automated end-to-end without artist intervention.
