## Problem

Standard gradient-based saliency maps (simple gradients, integrated gradients) applied to real-world CNN classifiers are typically noisy and dense, lacking desirable structures such as sparsity and connectedness. Existing post-processing remedies (sparsification, SmoothGrad, MoreauGrad) can impose such structures but do so at a significant cost of fidelity to the original gradient map, as demonstrated by sanity-check studies. The paper asks whether structured gradient maps can be obtained through the training process itself, without altering the definition of the interpretation map.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241648/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard RGB images (ImageNette 10-class subset of ImageNet, CUB-GHA bird images, and a synthesized ternary-region dataset) and produces two outputs: the classifier's class prediction and the simple-gradient saliency map ∇_x f_θ(x) for the ground-truth class. The saliency map is the primary object of interest; its structure (sparsity, group sparsity, alignment with expert gaze) is shaped during training rather than modified afterward. For the harmonization variant, an additional non-negative expert attention map A ∈ R^d is provided as a training-time weight.

## Method

The core contribution is a Fenchel-conjugate duality showing that constraining the adversarial perturbation δ with a convex function h(δ) in the AT inner maximization is equivalent (up to O(ε²) first-order approximation) to penalizing the input gradient with the conjugate h*(∇_x L). Concretely, an L∞ ball on δ yields an L1 penalty on the gradient (pixel-level sparsity), an L2,∞ ball yields an L2,1 group-norm penalty (patch-level sparsity and connectedness), a piecewise-quadratic penalty yields an elastic-net penalty (stable sparsity patterns), and a weighted L2 penalty yields a weighted L2 penalty that can suppress background features relative to an expert attention map. During training, the perturbation δ* is obtained by one or more gradient-ascent steps on the regularized adversarial loss, and the network parameters are updated to minimize the resulting regularized objective. Because the regularization acts on the training dynamics rather than on the post-hoc map, the resulting gradient maps retain full fidelity to the simple-gradient definition while exhibiting the desired structural properties.

## Results

On ImageNette, the best-performing variant (elastic net with ε₁=0.01, ε₂=0.05) achieves a DiffROAR score of 3.24% and Gini index of 57.35% while retaining 87.67% clean accuracy, compared to standard training's 1.81% DiffROAR, 46.53% Gini, and 89.04% accuracy. On the synthesized dataset with ground-truth saliency, the harmonization method achieves the highest binary pixel accuracy (93.82%) and recall (67.05%) among all variants. On CUB-GHA, increasing the harmonization coefficient ε from 0 to 5.0 raises the top-5% gaze-saliency overlap from 33.42% to 50.62% with only a 2.6-point drop in classification accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its in-processing adversarial training approach against three post-processing baselines: SmoothGrad, Sparsified-SmoothGrad, and MoreauGrad. On robustness to interpretation attacks (measured by SSIM and top-k intersection ratio), the proposed AT-trained networks display significantly higher robustness than standard training combined with any of the three post-processing methods; standard training remains vulnerable even when augmented with SmoothGrad. On stability (SSIM and top-k overlap across different random initializations and 10% data swaps), adversarial training brings more improvement than the post-processing baselines, with Sparsified-SmoothGrad's high SSIM being misleading since its activated regions actually differ (confirmed by lower top-k overlap). Among the proposed variants, the elastic net regularization achieves the best DiffROAR interpretability score (3.24% vs. 1.81% for standard) and the best stability, while the L₁-norm variant achieves the highest sparsity (Gini up to 65.53%) at the cost of a larger accuracy drop. The elastic net and group-norm variants are also slightly more robust to attacks than the pure L₁-norm variant.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the known connection between L-infinity adversarial training and L1 sparsity in gradient maps (observed empirically by Chalasani et al. and Ross & Doshi-Velez) into a general Fenchel conjugate duality framework, showing that any norm constraint on adversarial perturbations induces the dual norm as a regularizer on the input gradient. It departs from the dominant post-processing paradigm (SmoothGrad, Sparsified-SmoothGrad, MoreauGrad) by proposing an in-processing scheme that regularizes the training objective rather than modifying the already-computed saliency map, thereby preserving fidelity to the original simple gradient. The framework also subsumes the standard FGSM/PGD adversarial training of Madry et al. and Goodfellow et al. as special cases, unifying them under a single convex-analytic lens.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The duality framework gives practitioners a principled recipe: choose the desired structure in the gradient map (pixel sparsity, patch-level group sparsity, stability via elastic net, or alignment with human attention) and the corresponding perturbation norm follows automatically. A key practical finding is that directly adding norm penalties to the training loss fails to produce structured maps, while the adversarial-training formulation succeeds, highlighting the importance of the min-max structure. Limitations include evaluation only on ImageNette (10-class subset) and CUB-GHA, first-order approximation assumptions, and the fact that the framework is restricted to simple gradients rather than integrated gradients, DeepLIFT, or Grad-CAM. Extending the duality to these richer attribution methods and to non-CV domains remains open.
