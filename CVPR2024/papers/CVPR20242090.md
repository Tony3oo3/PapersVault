## Problem

Transformer-based video super-resolution models achieve state-of-the-art accuracy but impose heavy computational and memory costs that prevent deployment on constrained devices. While skip-processing strategies have been explored for high-level video tasks (e.g., object detection), it remains an open question whether such mechanisms can be applied to low-level VSR, which is highly sensitive to minor content changes. This paper demonstrates that feature-level temporal continuity can be exploited to skip redundant computations in VSR without sacrificing PSNR accuracy.

## Input / Output

The model takes a T-frame low-resolution video sequence of shape T×H×W×3 and reconstructs the corresponding high-resolution sequence of shape T×sH×sW×3 (s=4 in all experiments). Processing follows a recurrent bi-directional second-order grid propagation scheme where each frame's feature is enhanced using outputs from the two preceding frames, with 6 or 16/14 frames used for short- and long-sequence training respectively. The final HR output is produced by a pixel-shuffle upsampling layer applied to the refined feature.

## Method

MIA-VSR builds on the BasicVSR++ bi-directional second-order grid propagation framework and introduces two key components. First, an Intra&Inter-frame Attention Block (IIAB) replaces the joint multi-frame self-attention of prior Transformer VSR models: only the current frame's feature generates Query tokens, while past enhanced features and the current frame jointly produce Key and Value tokens, reducing self-attention FLOPs by one-third and avoiding unnecessary re-enhancement of already-mature past features. Second, an adaptive mask prediction module computes L1 feature differences between adjacent frames at each processing stage, passes them through a 1×1 convolution and a Gumbel-softmax gate to produce block-wise binary masks that selectively skip attention and FFN computations in regions with high temporal continuity. The network is trained end-to-end with a Charbonnier reconstruction loss plus an L1 sparsity penalty on the masking features, jointly learning stage-specific masking criteria from data.

## Results

On the long-sequence setting (16/14 frames), MIA-VSR achieves 32.78 dB PSNR and 0.9220 SSIM on REDS4, 38.22 dB on Vimeo-90K-T, and 28.20 dB on Vid4, while requiring only 1.61T FLOPs per frame on REDS4. This represents a 0.06 dB PSNR improvement over PSRT-recurrent (32.72 dB) and 0.03 dB over RVRT (32.75 dB) with approximately 33% and 27% fewer FLOPs, respectively. In the short-sequence setting (6 frames), MIA-VSR reaches 32.01 dB on REDS4, outperforming PSRT-recurrent by 0.13 dB while using 37% fewer FLOPs (1.50T vs. 2.39T). Peak GPU memory is 206 MB, substantially lower than VRT (2149 MB) and RVRT (1056 MB).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MIA-VSR outperforms all Transformer-based baselines (VRT, RVRT, PSRT-recurrent) on PSNR and SSIM across REDS4 and Vid4 while using substantially fewer FLOPs: 1.61T vs. 2.21T (RVRT) and 2.39T (PSRT-recurrent) on REDS4, a 27–33% reduction. On Vid4, MIA-VSR achieves 28.20 dB PSNR, beating PSRT-recurrent (28.07 dB) and RVRT (27.99 dB) by 0.13 and 0.21 dB respectively, with 36% fewer FLOPs than PSRT-recurrent. On Vimeo-90K-T, MIA-VSR (38.22 dB) is slightly below PSRT-recurrent (38.27 dB) by 0.05 dB but uses 31% fewer FLOPs (0.204T vs. 0.297T). Against the CNN-based BasicVSR++ (32.39 dB, 0.39T FLOPs), MIA-VSR gains 0.39 dB PSNR at the cost of higher FLOPs (1.61T), reflecting the inherent trade-off between Transformer expressiveness and CNN efficiency. In the short-sequence setting, MIA-VSR (32.01 dB, 1.50T) surpasses PSRT-recurrent (31.88 dB, 2.39T) by 0.13 dB with 37% fewer FLOPs, confirming the advantage of the IIAB design over joint multi-frame self-attention.

## Relation to Prior Work

MIA-VSR builds directly on the bi-directional second-order grid propagation framework of BasicVSR++ and the Transformer-based recurrent VSR lineage (VRT, RVRT, PSRT), but departs from their joint multi-frame self-attention design by proposing an Intra&Inter-Frame Attention Block (IIAB) that restricts Query generation to the current frame and treats previously enhanced features as read-only Key/Value sources. The adaptive masked processing mechanism extends the skip-convolution idea of Habibian et al. from pixel-level, high-level-task settings to feature-level, block-wise selective skipping for low-level VSR, using Gumbel-softmax gating to learn stage-specific masking criteria. Conceptually, the paper reframes temporal redundancy exploitation from a whole-network skip decision to a per-block, per-position feature-similarity-driven computation budget.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in making Transformer-based VSR deployable on memory-constrained devices: MIA-VSR achieves ~40% FLOPs reduction and substantially lower peak GPU memory (206 MB vs. 1056–2149 MB for RVRT/VRT) while matching or exceeding SOTA PSNR, which is a meaningful step toward real-time video enhancement on edge hardware. A key open question is whether the feature-level masking strategy generalizes to other video restoration tasks (deblurring, denoising) or to longer sequences where temporal drift may invalidate the continuity assumption. The runtime gap relative to RVRT (822 ms vs. 473 ms) highlights that algorithmic FLOPs savings alone are insufficient without kernel-level optimization, leaving room for further systems-level work.
