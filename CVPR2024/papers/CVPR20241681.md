## Problem

Existing adversarial patch detection methods fail to differentiate between the two deep-seated natures of naturalistic adversarial patches (NAPs): aggressiveness (adversarial behavior) and naturalness (deceptive appearance). This results in low precision, because the deceptive appearance misleads models into learning inaccurate patterns, and insufficient generalization, because diverse natural representations introduce varied disturbing forms that current methods cannot handle uniformly.

## Input / Output

The detector takes a 416×416 RGB image containing one or more adversarial patches as input and produces bounding box coordinates localizing each patch. During inference, the raw image is first passed through a feature shield module that performs FFT-based low-frequency extraction, threshold-based region masking, and selective Gaussian blurring before the preprocessed image is fed to the YOLOv5-based single-class detector. The output is a set of bounding boxes with confidence scores, evaluated via AP@0.5.

## Method

NAPGuard is a critical feature modulation framework built on a single-class YOLOv5 detector, combining two complementary strategies. During training, Aggressive Feature Aligned Learning (AFAL) applies a Laplacian operator to enhance high-frequency components, creating aligned images where patches share high-frequency characteristics with their surroundings (mimicking naturalistic adversarial examples); a pattern alignment loss (MSE between feature maps of original and aligned images) plus an auxiliary detection loss on the aligned branch guide the detector to capture accurate aggressive patterns. During inference, Natural Feature Suppressed Inference (NFSI) employs a feature shield module that performs 2D FFT to isolate low-frequency components, generates a binary mask via mean-plus-gamma-sigma thresholding on the low-frequency image, and applies Gaussian blurring only within masked regions to suppress natural features while preserving high-frequency aggressive signals. The combined training objective is (1−α)L_d + αL_a + βL_p, and the overall design amplifies the contrast between natural and aggressive features, enabling precise detection of deceptive patches and generalized detection across diverse NAP representations.

## Results

On the GAP dataset, NAPGuard achieves an average AP@0.5 of 91.00% on NAPs, 90.26% on Non-NAPs, and 92.24% on the mixture set, surpassing the strongest baseline (Ad-YOLO) by 60.24% AP@0.5 on NAPs. The method also outperforms the unmodified Base detector (YOLOv5 trained on GAP) by 15.40% AP@0.5 on the mixture set (92.24% vs. 76.84%). The largest individual gains are on DM-NAP (+44.18% over Base) and LAP (+49.84% over Base), confirming the framework's effectiveness on the most deceptive naturalistic patches.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NAPGuard dominates all five baselines across every patch type. Against Ad-YOLO (the closest competitor, also using YOLOv5), NAPGuard achieves 91.00% average AP@0.5 on NAPs versus Ad-YOLO's 30.76%, a gap of over 60 points; the authors attribute Ad-YOLO's weakness to multi-category training diluting aggressive-pattern learning. Image-analysis methods LGS and APE perform poorly on NAPs (4.95% and 9.90% average AP@0.5 respectively) because they generate numerous false-positive masks in non-patch regions. Segmentation-based methods SAC and PatchZero achieve 0% AP@0.5 on all three NAP types (GNAP, DM-NAP, LAP), completely failing to generalize beyond the Non-NAPs they were designed for. Even compared to the unmodified Base detector, NAPGuard improves the mixture AP@0.5 from 76.84% to 92.24%, with the largest gains on the hardest-to-detect patches (LAP: 36.23→86.07, AdvCloak: 59.94→92.24).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior adversarial patch detection methods (LGS, APE, SAC, PatchZero, Ad-YOLO) by explicitly decomposing the detection problem into two distinct feature natures—aggressiveness (high-frequency adversarial signals) and naturalness (low-frequency camouflage)—rather than treating patches as a monolithic anomaly. It repurposes frequency-domain tools (Laplacian operator, FFT-based low-pass filtering) and a cognitive psychology concept (the pop-out effect) to design a training strategy (pattern alignment loss) and an inference strategy (feature shield module) that were not previously applied to patch detection. The work also introduces the GAP dataset, the first benchmark specifically structured to evaluate generalization across naturalistic and non-naturalistic patches, filling a gap left by prior evaluations that used ad-hoc or single-method test sets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The GAP dataset with its GL1/GL2/GL3 generalization grading provides a much-needed standardized benchmark for adversarial patch detection, and the aggressive/natural feature decomposition offers a reusable conceptual lens for future defense design. However, the evaluation is confined to a single self-constructed dataset and a single architecture (YOLOv5), and the large reported margins (60.24% AP@0.5) are partly inflated by baselines that were never designed for NAPs. Open questions include whether the frequency-domain strategies transfer to transformer-based detectors, whether the approach holds under real-world physical conditions (lighting, occlusion, distance), and whether the feature shield module can be integrated as a lightweight pre-processing step in existing defense pipelines as the authors envision.
