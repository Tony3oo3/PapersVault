## Problem

Neural networks trained on biased datasets with imbalanced attributes rapidly form shortcut correlations based on misleading attributes (e.g., background, color) at the early stage of training, creating a biased feature space that prevents Neural Collapse and severely limits out-of-distribution generalization. Existing debiasing methods rely heavily on auxiliary bias-amplified models, large contrastive sample sets, or pretraining processes, incurring substantial additional training costs. This paper asks whether shortcut learning can be prevented at its root without such extra overhead, by leveraging the geometric structure of Neural Collapse.

## Input / Output

During training, the method consumes an RGB image together with its bias attribute label (e.g., corruption type, background category, gender), which is used solely to retrieve the corresponding prime feature from a pre-constructed simplex ETF; the learnable backbone feature and the fixed prime feature are concatenated before the classifier. At inference, only the RGB image is required—the prime feature slot is filled with a null vector, so the model must rely entirely on intrinsic correlations. The output is a standard softmax class prediction over K categories.

## Method

ETF-Debias constructs a training-free simplex equiangular tight frame (ETF) whose vertices serve as 'perfectly learned' shortcut prime features, one per bias attribute category. During training, the classifier receives the concatenation of the learnable backbone feature and the attribute-specific prime feature, so the model is forced to capture intrinsic class correlations rather than re-deriving the shortcut from scratch. A prime reinforcement regularization term further strengthens the model's dependency on prime features by penalizing the classifier for failing to distinguish bias attributes using primes alone. A gradient analysis shows that because the prime-correlated classifier weights collapse quickly, bias-aligned samples receive implicitly down-weighted pulling/forcing forces while bias-conflicting samples are up-weighted, naturally rebalancing the optimization. At test time, primes are zeroed out, yielding an unbiased classifier that has already converged toward a collapsed, well-separated feature geometry.

## Results

On real-world biased datasets, ETF-Debias achieves up to 6.60% accuracy improvement over the best baseline on Biased FFHQ (94.00% vs. 87.40% at 5% bias ratio), 3.06% on Dogs & Cats (92.18% vs. 89.12%), and 1.66% on BAR (83.66% vs. 82.00%). On synthetic datasets, gains are smaller but consistent: +2.91% on Corrupted CIFAR-10 (65.34% vs. 62.43%) and +0.09% on Colored MNIST (91.36% vs. 91.27%) at the 5% bias ratio. The method requires no auxiliary bias-amplified models, no contrastive pretraining, and no additional optimization of prime features, making it the most training-efficient approach among all compared methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ETF-Debias outperforms all six baselines on nearly every dataset and bias-ratio setting. The largest margins are against reweight-based methods (LfF, LfF+BE) and disentangle-based methods (EnD, SD), which require auxiliary bias-amplified models or large contrastive sample sets; for example, on Biased FFHQ at 5% ratio, ETF-Debias (94.00%) exceeds LfF+BE (87.40%) by 6.60% and SD (84.80%) by 9.20%. Against augmentation-based methods (DisEnt, Selecmix), gains are also consistent: on Corrupted CIFAR-10 at 2% ratio, ETF-Debias (54.64%) beats Selecmix (47.09%) by 7.55%. The only setting where ETF-Debias does not lead is Colored MNIST at 1% bias ratio, where Selecmix (83.34%) edges out ETF-Debias (81.97%) by 1.37%, likely because the extremely simple color bias is already well-handled by contrastive-pair sampling. Overall, ETF-Debias is the only method that does not require bias supervision (marked with ◇) or pre-trained auxiliary models (marked with *), yet still achieves the best or near-best accuracy across all 12 evaluation settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes two previously separate research threads: the Neural Collapse phenomenon (Papyan et al., 2020), which characterizes the terminal-phase geometry of balanced training, and prime training (Wen et al., 2022), which provides fixed supervisory signals to redirect model attention away from shortcuts. The key conceptual move is using a training-free simplex ETF as the prime feature, thereby approximating the 'perfectly learned' shortcut representation without any auxiliary model or additional optimization. This departs from the three dominant debiasing paradigms (reweight-based, augmentation-based, disentangle-based), all of which require substantial extra training cost, by embedding the debiasing mechanism directly into the classifier architecture through a fixed geometric structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a practically appealing debiasing recipe that requires no auxiliary models, no contrastive samples, and no pretraining, making it easy to adopt in existing pipelines. The observation that shortcut learning creates a 'locked-in' biased feature space early in training—which then resists correction even as loss decreases—is a useful diagnostic for understanding why naive training fails on biased data. However, the method still requires bias attribute annotations during training, limiting its applicability to settings where such labels are unavailable. Open questions include whether the ETF prime structure generalizes to multi-attribute or hierarchical biases, and whether the approach extends to non-visual modalities or tasks beyond classification.
