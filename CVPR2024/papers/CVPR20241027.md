## Problem

Pre-trained diffusion models like Stable Diffusion can perform image editing, but existing approaches either fine-tune the model on task-specific datasets or optimize prompts/weights per image, leaving the two most impactful inputs—the Gaussian noise added to the image and the diffusion timesteps—fixed or manually chosen. This forces users into extensive trial-and-error to find settings that balance fidelity to the original image with alignment to the target prompt, and no prior work has automated the joint optimization of these parameters.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241027/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image, a target text prompt describing the desired edit, an original-image description prompt, and optional auxiliary inputs (a reference image, a binary mask, a stroke image, or a user-composed image) depending on the editing mode. It produces a single edited RGB image at the same resolution as the input. All optimization occurs at inference time in the VAE latent space of Stable Diffusion v2.1 (K=10 denoising steps, W=50 optimization iterations), so no fine-tuning of the SD backbone is required, and the method can be swapped onto DreamBooth or Textual Inversion variants without architectural changes.

## Method

TiNO-Edit jointly optimizes the input Gaussian noise N and all K per-step timestep values t_k via gradient descent through the frozen SD UNet, starting from uniform timesteps and standard normal noise. To make this tractable, the authors introduce LatentCLIP (a CLIP visual encoder whose first convolution is replaced to accept 4× downsampled VAE latents) and LatentVGG (analogously adapted VGG), both trained on LAION-5B to match their pixel-domain counterparts, enabling semantic and perceptual losses to be computed entirely in the compact latent space. The composite loss combines a CLIP-based semantic term that equates the image-domain cosine shift to the text-domain cosine shift between original and target prompts, an optional reference-image similarity term, and a latent perceptual term, all weighted and applied only within a mask that localizes the editing region. A key design choice is optimizing all K timesteps independently (rather than a single starting T), allowing non-uniform spacing that adapts to the edit, while the noise is kept within the learned Gaussian distribution via a lower learning rate to prevent generation-quality degradation.

## Results

On the pure text-guided image editing benchmark (200 samples), TiNO-Edit achieves the best scores across all three metrics: CLIP-T 0.328, CLIP-I 0.924, and DINO-I 0.874, outperforming the strongest baselines EDICT (0.327/0.898/0.838) and SINE (0.314/0.912/0.805). For reference-guided editing, TiNO-Edit leads on CLIP-T (0.311), CLIP-I (0.928), and CLIP-Ir (0.674) but trails VCT on DINO-I (0.869 vs. 0.919), which the authors attribute to VCT producing outputs nearly identical to the input. In stroke-guided editing and image composition, TiNO-Edit achieves the highest CLIP-I (0.959, 0.958), CLIP-I* (0.987, 0.978), and DINO-I (0.978, 0.983) scores, with only a tie on CLIP-T for stroke-guided editing (0.298 matching BLD at T=0.5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

TiNO-Edit outperforms all 8 text-guided editing baselines on every metric in Table 1, with the largest margins over Pix2Pix-Zero (CLIP-I: 0.924 vs. 0.793) and Plug-and-Play (DINO-I: 0.874 vs. 0.756), while the closest competitor EDICT trails by only 0.001 on CLIP-T. In reference-guided editing (Table 2), TiNO-Edit beats GLIGEN and Paint-by-Example on all four metrics but loses to VCT on DINO-I (0.869 vs. 0.919); the paper notes VCT's high DINO-I reflects minimal image change rather than successful editing, as VCT also scores the worst CLIP-T (0.267). For stroke-guided editing (Table 3), TiNO-Edit ties BLD (T=0.5) on CLIP-T (0.298) but leads on CLIP-I (0.959 vs. 0.930), CLIP-Is (0.987 vs. 0.986), and DINO-I (0.978 vs. 0.973). In image composition (Table 4), TiNO-Edit achieves the best score on all four metrics, with the most notable gap over SDEdit (T=0.5) on CLIP-I (0.958 vs. 0.903) and CLIP-Ic (0.978 vs. 0.965). Qualitatively, the paper highlights that baselines suffer from incohesive edits, concept leaking, failure to add new objects, and drastic alteration of non-target regions, whereas TiNO-Edit maintains structural fidelity while applying the requested change.

## Relation to Prior Work

TiNO-Edit builds directly on the Stable Diffusion (SD) backbone and the DDIM noisify-then-denoise paradigm introduced by SDEdit, but departs from prior editing methods that optimize text prompts, model weights, or intermediate attention/feature layers. The key conceptual shift is treating the input Gaussian noise and all per-step diffusion timesteps as learnable parameters rather than fixed hyperparameters, which the authors argue has been overlooked. Additionally, the paper introduces LatentCLIP and LatentVGG—encoders trained to operate in the VAE latent space—replacing pixel-domain CLIP/VGG losses used by prior optimization-based editors, and extends the framework to DreamBooth and Textual Inversion variants that encode custom concepts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By automating the selection of noise and timesteps, TiNO-Edit removes a significant source of manual trial-and-error in diffusion-based editing workflows, making results more predictable and controllable across diverse editing tasks (object replacement, style transfer, reference-guided editing, stroke-based editing, composition). The latent-domain losses offer a practical efficiency gain for per-image optimization. However, the method still requires 50 optimization steps per image, limiting real-time or interactive use, and the paper does not explore whether the learned timestep/noise patterns transfer across images or could be distilled into a faster inference procedure. Open questions include generalization to video editing, 3D generation, and whether the optimization landscape is well-behaved enough for convergence guarantees.
