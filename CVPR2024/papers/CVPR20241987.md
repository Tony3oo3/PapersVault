## Problem

Refractive index tomography from a single viewpoint is severely ill-posed because ray paths are nonlinearly determined by the unknown medium itself, and multiple viewing angles are unavailable in many scientific settings (e.g., telescope imaging of dark matter). Existing approaches either require multi-view measurements or rely on linear backprojection that smears structure along the line of sight. This paper asks whether a 3D refractive field can be recovered from a single image by exploiting the known spatial distribution of light sources embedded within the refractive volume.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241987/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single 2D pinhole-camera image (64×64 for gas-flow experiments, 512×512 for the dark matter case study) and a known 3D emission field specifying the positions, amplitudes, and elliptical orientations of point-like light sources distributed throughout the volume. It outputs a continuous scalar refractive index field η(x) ≥ 1 defined over a 3D cube, represented implicitly by a neural network. The emission field is treated as a fixed, known quantity (analogous to a galaxy catalog or BoS calibration pattern), while the image is the sole photometric measurement.

## Method

The refractive field is parameterized as a coordinate-based MLP (NeRF-style) with Fourier positional encoding (degree L=4), eLU activations for smooth spatial gradients, and a Softplus output to enforce η ≥ 1. During each training iteration, rays are traced through the field by numerically integrating Hamilton's eikonal equations with an adaptive Dormand-Prince solver, and the adjoint method is used to backpropagate through the ODE with constant memory. The rendered image is computed by line-integrating the known emission along each curved ray path, and the network is optimized by minimizing MSE against the observed image plus a boundary-condition regularizer enforcing η=1 on the cube faces. The key insight is that light sources distributed throughout the volume (rather than only behind it) provide the 3D localization signal that disambiguates the single-view problem, and the neural field's implicit smoothness prior outperforms explicit TV² regularization on voxel grids for recovering compact refractive structures.

## Results

The paper evaluates reconstruction quality using PSNR (computed on the RI field after subtracting 1) across nine measurement/reconstruction conditions, but reports results primarily through visual comparison (Fig. 4) rather than a single numerical table. The key quantitative finding is that the neural field with light sources distributed throughout the volume (condition A) achieves the most accurate 3D gas flow recovery, while displacement measurements from a single viewpoint fail for all three reconstruction methods. In the dark matter case study, the method recovers a 20³ Mpc³ volume containing 1747 halos from a 512×512 simulated telescope image, where the refractive signal is extremely weak (median deflection of 6×10⁻³ pixels, intensity change of at most ±0.3%). Sensitivity analysis shows reconstruction degrades with sparser light sources but foreground objects remain resolvable even with only 50 sources.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its neural field approach against a linear voxel grid and a TV²-regularized voxel grid under three measurement conditions (Fig. 4). Under image measurements with light sources throughout the volume, the neural field (A) produces the most accurate and compact reconstruction of the gas flow, while the unregularized voxel grid (D) exhibits streak-like artifacts and the TV²-regularized grid (G) still fails to recover structure along the optical axis. Under displacement measurements (the ideal single-view BoS limit), all three methods fail to recover the gas flow shape from a single viewpoint, confirming that image measurements of distributed sources are essential. The comparison with Teh et al. [37] (adjoint nonlinear ray tracing on a voxel grid) highlights that their method requires multiple viewpoints for accurate reconstruction, whereas this paper achieves competitive single-view recovery by leveraging distributed light source information and the implicit regularization of the neural field. The TV²-regularized grid was tuned to maximize PSNR against ground truth (an unrealistic oracle setting), yet still underperforms the neural field, suggesting the neural field's inductive bias is more effective than explicit total-variation regularization for this ill-posed problem.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neural field representation paradigm from NeRF [25] and eikonal ray-tracing for refractive novel-view synthesis [6] to the single-view, continuous-RI setting. While NeRF and related works assume straight ray paths, and prior refractive tomography methods (BoS, ODT, [37]) require multiple viewpoints, this work formulates and solves the inverse problem with a single viewpoint by leveraging the known spatial distribution of light emitters within the volume. The differentiable curved-ray tracing machinery builds directly on the adjoint formulation from [37] and Hamilton's equations, but the key conceptual departure is treating the ray paths as unknowns to be jointly optimized with the field, rather than fixed by a known medium or geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that single-view RI tomography, previously considered intractable without multi-view data, becomes feasible when light sources are distributed throughout the refractive volume rather than behind it—a finding with direct implications for weak gravitational lensing surveys where only one viewing geometry is available. The dark matter case study is compelling but limited to noise-free simulations, and the authors explicitly note sensitivity to errors in light source localization and image measurements as unresolved challenges. Open questions include whether the approach scales to real telescope data with realistic noise, whether more complex emission models (e.g., the full refractive radiative transfer equation) are needed, and how the method performs when the RI field is not smooth.
