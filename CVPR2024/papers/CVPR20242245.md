## Problem

Existing semi-supervised video segmentation methods treat video frames as independent images and apply per-frame classification, ignoring the rich temporal relationships between consecutive frames. This per-frame recognition paradigm is fundamentally different from how humans process video, where distinguished semantic concepts from historical frames are retained as context to aid current-frame recognition. The paper identifies that the classifier component, rather than supervision signal construction or feature extraction, is the overlooked bottleneck for exploiting temporal structure in video.

## Input / Output

During training, the method consumes labeled videos (one annotated frame per video at 512×1024 for Cityscapes or 360×480 for CamVid) alongside unlabeled video clips of T=5 consecutive frames. The TDC operates only during the unsupervised training branch; at inference, the conventional classifier is used for final prediction, keeping the method compatible with existing VSS architectures. The output is a per-frame pixel-level segmentation mask with 19 classes (Cityscapes) or 11 classes (CamVid).

## Method

The Temporal-Dependent Classifier (TDC) replaces the standard per-frame classifier in the unsupervised branch with a prototype-based recognition mechanism that combines two prototype sets: global prototypes (learnable weight vectors, equivalent to a conventional classifier) and context prototypes (computed on-the-fly by averaging pixel features per predicted class from the immediately preceding frame). Recognition is performed via cosine similarity between L2-normalized input features and all prototypes, with separate learnable temperature scalars for each prototype type to prevent the model from shortcutting to context prototypes. A joint-prototypes label relaxation treats the global and context prototype of the same class as a union target in the cross-entropy loss, so a feature is not penalized for being more similar to either representation of the same semantic concept. A bidirectional constraint further computes context prototypes from both the forward (t→t+1) and backward (t+1→t) directions, strengthening the implicit temporal consistency pressure on the feature extractor. The key insight is that requiring context prototypes derived from historical frames to accurately represent the current frame implicitly forces the backbone to learn temporally-consistent semantic representations across frames, providing an effective constraint for semi-supervised learning without explicit consistency losses.

## Results

On Cityscapes validation, TDC achieves 55.81% mIoU with only 1/30 labeled data (100 videos), surpassing the supervised Accel baseline (45.73%) by 10.08% and the previous best SSVS method IFR (52.86%) by 2.95%. Under the 1/16 protocol, TDC reaches 60.97% mIoU, outperforming IFR by 4.58%. On CamVid test, TDC similarly leads all baselines, e.g., 50.95% mIoU at 1/30 vs. 49.50% for IFR and 42.37% for the supervised baseline. The gains are most pronounced in the few-label regime, confirming TDC's effectiveness in label-scarce settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TDC outperforms all five compared semi-supervised methods (CCT, CAC, CPS, IFR) and the supervised Accel baseline across every label budget on both Cityscapes and CamVid. The most direct competitor is IFR [42], the only prior SSVS method, which TDC surpasses by 2.95–4.58% mIoU on Cityscapes and 1.45–2.51% on CamVid. Among the image-based SSIS methods, CPS [4] and CAC [21] are the strongest baselines, but TDC still leads them by 6.84% (1/30 Cityscapes) and 2.12% (1/30 CamVid), respectively. The margin over IFR widens as labeled data decreases (e.g., +2.95% at 1/30 vs. +2.14% at 1/2 on Cityscapes), indicating TDC's temporal-consistency constraint is most valuable in the few-label regime. TDC also improves temporal consistency (TC score) from 73.88% (IFR) to 75.38%, confirming more stable cross-frame predictions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant SSVS paradigm exemplified by IFR, which focuses on constructing supervision signals (inter-frame feature reconstruction) for unlabeled data, and instead targets the classifier as the underexplored component. Conceptually, it repurposes the prototype-based classification idea (where learned weight vectors align with feature cluster centers) by augmenting the conventional global prototypes with on-the-fly context prototypes derived from the previous frame's prediction, drawing an analogy to how humans retain short-term semantic context across frames. The bidirectional constraint further borrows from NLP's bidirectional RNN/BERT design to enforce consistency in both temporal directions. The pseudo-label supervision follows the FixMatch/CPS lineage, keeping the unsupervised loss simple so that the classifier's contribution can be isolated.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a relatively simple classifier modification (adding context prototypes) can outperform more elaborate supervision-signal designs like IFR by a wide margin (up to ~10 mIoU in the fewest-label regime) suggests that the per-frame recognition assumption, inherited from image segmentation, is a meaningful bottleneck in video understanding. A key limitation is that TDC is used only during training while inference still relies on the conventional classifier, leaving open whether the temporal-dependent mechanism could also improve test-time prediction. The approach is validated across three VSS architectures and two datasets, but its interaction with more recent transformer-based or flow-free video segmentation backbones remains unexplored, as does its applicability beyond the pseudo-label supervision scheme.
