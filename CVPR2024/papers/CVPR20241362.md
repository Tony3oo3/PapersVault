## Problem

Existing Graph Contrastive Learning (GCL) methods suffer from two positive sampling limitations: incomplete local sampling (same-class nodes often lie beyond each other's neighborhoods, so neighborhood-based positives miss critical same-class pairs) and blind sampling (homophily-based criteria for extending positives beyond neighborhoods are inherently local and fail on heterophilic graphs). Together, these prevent GCLs from capturing all true positive pairs, degrading performance on both homophilic and heterophilic graphs.

## Input / Output

For graph tasks, HEATS consumes the adjacency matrix and node attribute matrix (e.g., 1,433-dim features on Cora) and produces 64-dimensional node embeddings via a two-layer GCN encoder plus a two-layer MLP projection head, which are then fed to a linear probe for classification. For image tasks, it operates on standard RGB images (32×32 CIFAR-10/100, 96×96 STL-10) through ResNet-18/50 backbones. A distinctive aspect is that the positive sample matrix is constructed globally from node features (independent of graph topology) and then sparsified by thresholding entries below β, so the method is directly transferable to non-graph domains.

## Method

HEATS constructs a block-diagonal, idempotent affinity matrix via a self-expressive objective (minimizing reconstruction error + idempotent penalty + ℓ₂,₁ noise penalty, subject to column-stochasticity, symmetry, non-negativity, and Tr(S)=k) that encodes which nodes are true positives for each anchor. This matrix defines both the positive/negative partition and the per-pair weights in a modified InfoNCE loss, replacing the single cross-view positive of GRACE with a weighted set of same-class positives. The matrix construction and contrastive optimization are updated alternately: the matrix is rebuilt in the evolving feature space each iteration, while the contrastive loss refines the features, creating a feedback loop that progressively sharpens the block-diagonal structure. Theoretically, HEATS yields a tighter lower bound on the mutual information between node attributes and embeddings than neighborhood-based baselines (Theorem 2), and the affinity matrix provably satisfies the block-diagonal property (Theorem 1).

## Results

On six homophilic graphs, HEATS achieves the best unsupervised accuracy across all datasets, reaching 87.10% on Cora, 75.26% on CiteSeer, 85.41% on PubMed, 79.99% on Wiki-CS, 89.29% on Computers, and 94.65% on Photo. On six heterophilic graphs, HEATS shows the largest gains over GRACE: +12.11% on Cornell (66.97% vs 54.86%), +7.29% on Texas (64.86% vs 57.57%), and +15.88% on Wisconsin (65.88% vs 50.00%). For image classification, adding HEATS to SimCLR with ResNet-50 improves CIFAR-100 accuracy by 4.50% (53.81% vs 49.31%) and CIFAR-10 by 2.55% (77.58% vs 75.03%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HEATS outperforms all 14 baselines on every homophilic dataset, with the largest margin over GRACE on CiteSeer (+2.32%, 75.26% vs 72.94%) and over MVGRL on Photo (+2.64%, 94.65% vs 92.01%). On heterophilic graphs, HEATS dominates the small datasets: it beats GRACE by 12.11% on Cornell (66.97% vs 54.86%), 7.29% on Texas (64.86% vs 57.57%), and 15.88% on Wisconsin (65.88% vs 50.00%), while also surpassing the strongest unsupervised baseline SELENE on all three. On the larger heterophilic graphs (Chameleon, Squirrel, Actor), HEATS still leads GRACE and most baselines but trails HomoGCL on Squirrel (36.24% vs 38.71%) and is competitive with SELENE on Actor (29.91% vs 30.12%). For image classification, HEATS consistently improves SimCLR: with ResNet-50, gains are +2.55% on CIFAR-10, +1.07% on STL-10, and +4.50% on CIFAR-100, confirming generality beyond graph domains. Supervised models (GCN, GAT, JKNet) remain competitive on homophilic graphs but degrade sharply on heterophilic ones, where HEATS as an unsupervised method achieves the best overall accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

HEATS builds directly on the GRACE two-channel GCL architecture and the InfoNCE contrastive objective, while drawing a key conceptual ingredient from subspace clustering: the self-expressive learning objective with idempotent and block-diagonal constraints. This departs from the dominant line of GCL positive sampling (HomoGCL, GCA, SELENE) that relies on local neighborhood structure and homophily assumptions, instead constructing global affinity matrices independent of graph topology. The alternating update between matrix construction and contrastive optimization is a novel coupling that has no direct precedent in prior GCL work, though it echoes the iterative refinement strategies in robust subspace clustering.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete structural property (block-diagonal + idempotent) that characterizes optimal positive sample matrices, providing a principled criterion that prior GCL methods lacked. This addresses a real gap: existing positive sampling is both incomplete (missing same-class nodes beyond neighborhoods) and blind (no global criterion), which explains the consistent underperformance on heterophilic graphs. Open questions include scalability of the n×n matrix construction to very large graphs, sensitivity to the number of blocks k, and whether the approach extends to multimodal or dynamic graph settings. The theoretical MI bound (Theorem 2) is a clean contribution but its practical tightness relative to the true MI remains unquantified.
