## Problem

Most open-set recognition (OSR) research evaluates performance under large, coarse-grained semantic shifts, yet real-world fine-grained domains (species monitoring, medicine, art) involve novel categories that are highly visually similar to known ones, creating a 'familiarity trap' where OSR becomes harder, not easier, as semantic distance shrinks. The interplay between label granularity, semantic similarity, and hierarchical structure in the learned representation space remains largely unexplored for this challenging fine-grained regime.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images from the iNat2021 dataset (2.7M images across 10,000 species) and produces a scalar familiarity score indicating whether the image belongs to a familiar (closed-set) or novel (open-set) category. The benchmark provides seven discrete open-set splits per super-category (birds and insects), ranging from 1-hop (fine-grained, within the same taxonomic family) to 7-hop (coarse-grained, entirely different kingdoms), with equal-size closed- and open-set test subsets. A ResNet-50 encoder trained from scratch extracts representations, and the score is computed via maximum logit, KL-disagreement over a 5-model ensemble, or nearest-neighbour distance in L2-normalized feature space.

## Method

The core contribution is a hierarchy-adversarial training strategy that discourages hierarchical structure in the learned representation space to improve fine-grained OSR. A ResNet-50 encoder is trained with a min-max objective: cross-entropy is minimized for the finest-grained classification head while cross-entropy is maximized for all coarser-grained heads, implemented via a gradient reversal layer (adapted from domain adaptation) that flips the sign of back-propagated gradients for coarser heads. The adversarial weight λ is gradually annealed from zero to α during training, and the authors show that a moderate α (0.25) yields the best fine-grained OSR by pushing representations of visually similar categories apart, reducing the familiarity trap. The paper also introduces the iNat2021-OSR benchmark, curating open-set splits along seven taxonomic hop distances for birds and insects, enabling systematic study of OSR from coarse to fine-grained semantic shifts at a scale (10K categories) that avoids pretraining leakage.

## Results

On the iNat2021-OSR benchmark (1-hop fine-grained OSR), the proposed hierarchy-adversarial training with MLS scoring achieves 72.7 AUROC / 70.2 AUPR on Aves and 79.5 AUROC / 77.9 AUPR on Insecta, improving over standard cross-entropy training by +2.6 AUROC / +4.7 AUPR (Aves) and +1.5 AUROC / +1.5 AUPR (Insecta). The paper also shows that MLS is the best scoring rule for fine-grained OSR (1–6 hops), while KL-disagreement outperforms MLS for coarse-grained OSR (7-hop). Fine-grained supervision consistently improves OSR across all semantic distances, and hierarchy-adversarial learning pushes 1-hop open-set category centroids away from familiar categories in representation space, directly addressing the 'familiarity trap.'

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three OSR scoring families—MLS (Vaze et al. [53]), NN (Sun et al. [49]), and KLD (Lakshminarayanan et al. [32])—across three training strategies on 1-hop fine-grained OSR. MLS is the strongest scorer for fine-grained OSR, achieving 72.7 AUROC on Aves with hierarchy-adversarial training, outperforming NN (67.8) and KLD (66.1) by 4.9 and 6.6 AUROC respectively. The proposed hierarchy-adversarial training improves MLS by +2.6 AUROC / +4.7 AUPR on Aves and +1.5 AUROC / +1.5 AUPR on Insecta over standard cross-entropy, and similarly boosts NN by +3.7 AUROC / +0.9 AUPR (Aves) and +3.8 AUROC / +4.1 AUPR (Insecta). However, hierarchy-adversarial training consistently degrades KLD performance (e.g., 66.1 vs. 67.0 AUROC on Aves), suggesting the ensemble disagreement signal is disrupted when hierarchical structure is suppressed. For coarse-grained OSR (7-hop), KLD reverses its ranking and outperforms MLS, while NN remains the weakest scorer at both extremes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the open-set recognition (OSR) literature by shifting focus from the coarse-grained semantic shifts studied by Vaze et al. [53] to progressively finer-grained shifts, using the iNat2021 taxonomy to define controlled 'hop' distances between closed- and open-set categories. Methodologically, it repurposes the gradient reversal layer from domain adaptation [20, 21] into a hierarchy-adversarial training objective, and adopts the hierarchical multi-task loss framework of Wu et al. [58] as a hierarchy-supportive baseline. The scoring methods evaluated (MLS, KL-disagreement, nearest-neighbour) are drawn directly from prior OSR and OOD detection work, positioning the contribution as an empirical study of how these known scores interact with semantic similarity and representation structure rather than a new scoring mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The iNat2021-OSR benchmark (10,000 categories, 2.7M images, seven controlled semantic distances) addresses a critical gap: prior fine-grained OSR benchmarks were too small to train from scratch, risking pretraining leakage and limiting scale. The finding that discouraging hierarchical structure improves fine-grained OSR while impairing coarse-grained OSR suggests that representation geometry matters in ways not captured by closed-set accuracy alone, opening questions about how to optimally balance hierarchical and discriminative features. Limitations include reliance on taxonomy as a proxy for semantic similarity, evaluation on only two taxa (birds, insects), and the hierarchy-adversarial approach's dependence on hierarchical labels rather than more general similarity signals like attributes.
