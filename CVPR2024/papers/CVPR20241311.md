## Problem

Existing human motion prediction methods suffer from dramatic accuracy degradation as the prediction horizon increases, due to two root causes: insufficient numerical stability from high noise and complex distributions in raw 3D pose data, and inadequate modeling stability from unstable parameter updates over long temporal sequences. No prior work has explicitly modeled joint dynamics through a physically grounded representation that simultaneously addresses both issues.

## Input / Output

The method takes an observed 3D pose sequence of T frames (J joints × 3 coordinates, downsampled to 25 FPS) and predicts N future frames. Internally, the input is converted into per-joint, per-axis symplectic trajectories of dimension T×2 (velocity magnitude + direction angle), reducing the effective prediction dimension by a factor of J. The predicted low-dimensional velocity trajectories are then integrated back into full 3D joint positions via a user-defined dynamics model, so the final output is a standard 3D pose sequence.

## Method

SINN comprises two modules. The Symplectic Representation Encoder (SRE) maps each joint into a Hamiltonian mechanical system (location α + momentum β), where momentum is a 4-tuple of velocity magnitude and three direction angles; a symplectic spatial splitting algorithm then decouples the high-dimensional pose into independent per-axis trajectories of dimension T×2, filtering noise and reducing overfitting risk. The Symplectic Temporal Aggregation (STA) module splits long-term prediction into multiple short-term sub-steps handled by a pre-trained GRU-based symplectic operator, whose replicas are temporally concatenated with shared weights and fine-tuned end-to-end. A GAN-based critic (three-layer FCN) provides adversarial supervision during the long-term fine-tuning phase, and a forget unit keeps the input window real-time by dropping old frames and appending newly generated ones. The framework is model-agnostic and can be plugged into existing RNN, GCN, or GAN predictors.

## Results

On Human3.6M, SINN achieves average MPJPE of 7.8 mm at 80 ms and 80.1 mm at 1,000 ms, outperforming the previous best (FDU&FAU: 9.0 mm / 107.0 mm) by 13.3% and 25.1% respectively. On CMU Mocap, the method reaches 62.8 mm at 1,000 ms (vs. 75.4 mm for FDU&FAU), a 16.7% improvement. On 3DPW, SINN achieves 88.2 mm at 1,000 ms (vs. 94.5 mm), a 10.2% gain over the prior state-of-the-art. All reported metrics are MPJPE in millimeters (lower is better).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SINN outperforms all nine baselines across every time horizon on all three datasets. The closest competitor is FDU&FAU [9], which SINN beats by 1.2 mm at 80 ms and 26.9 mm at 1,000 ms on H3.6M (a 25.1% reduction in long-term error). Among RNN-based methods, res-GRU [34] and HPGAN [1] show the largest gaps (22.1 mm and 56.4 mm at 80 ms respectively), indicating that the symplectic representation provides substantial gains over vanilla recurrent architectures. GAN-based BiGAN [17] and HMR [25] are mid-tier, with SINN reducing their 1,000 ms error by 58.2 mm and 39.5 mm. Graph-based methods DMGNN [21] and MSR-GCN [22] do not report 1,000 ms results, but SINN still leads at 400 ms by 36.3 mm and 25.1 mm respectively. The trajectory-based LTD [32] and attention-based HRI [33] are competitive at short horizons but degrade sharply at 1,000 ms (112.3 and 110.9 mm), where SINN maintains 80.1 mm. On CMU Mocap and 3DPW, the same ordering holds: FDU&FAU is the strongest baseline, and SINN improves long-term accuracy by 16.7% and 10.2% respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

SINN repurposes symplectic integration—a classical technique from numerical ODE solving and Hamiltonian mechanics—into the deep learning pipeline for human motion prediction, a task previously dominated by direct 3D position prediction via RNNs, GCNs, and GANs. Conceptually, it departs from the standard paradigm by reformulating joint dynamics in terms of velocity (momentum) rather than raw position, and by splitting the prediction problem spatially (per-joint, per-axis trajectories) and temporally (short-term sub-steps aggregated via a pre-trained GRU operator). The GAN component draws from the HPGAN/BiGAN lineage but is applied to symplectic trajectories rather than raw poses, and the overall framework is explicitly model-agnostic, wrapping around existing architectures rather than replacing them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 20.1% MPJPE reduction on H3.6M and consistent gains across three datasets demonstrate that a simple velocity-based representation with temporal splitting can outperform complex GCN and multi-scale architectures, suggesting that representation design may matter more than architectural complexity for long-horizon prediction. The model-agnostic wrapper (Table 5) offers a practical plug-in improvement for existing pipelines. However, the 'symplectic' framing is somewhat loose—the symplectic operator is a pre-trained GRU without verified symplectic structure preservation, and the theoretical guarantees from Hamiltonian mechanics are not rigorously established. Open questions include whether the velocity representation generalizes to higher-dimensional or multi-agent settings, and whether the GAN-based adversarial loss on trajectories provides genuine distributional benefits beyond MSE minimization.
