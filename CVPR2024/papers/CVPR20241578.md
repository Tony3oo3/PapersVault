## Problem

Existing motion style transfer methods perform well only when the style and content motions share the same action (e.g., both walking), but degrade severely when contents differ (e.g., kick vs. punch) because they fail to disentangle style from content. Additionally, these methods require heuristic post-processing such as copying global translation from the content motion and forcibly fixing foot contacts, which degrades output quality.

## Input / Output

The method takes two motion-capture sequences of up to 200 frames at 60 fps, each specifying 21 joints (from a 31-joint skeleton) with 7-D local joint vectors, 7-D global root translation, and 4-D global velocity per frame. Joints are grouped into P anatomical body parts, and global translation is treated as an additional (P+1)-th 'part' so it participates in attention alongside body parts. The output is a complete stylized motion (pose, global translation, and velocity) produced directly by the generator without any post-processing step.

## Method

MoST uses Siamese transformer encoders (3 blocks each with part-attention and temporal-attention) that simultaneously extract a body-part-specific style feature (via a learnable style token) and a frame-level content dynamics feature (via instance normalization in the final block) from a single motion. A Part-Attentive Style Modulator (PSM) then performs cross-attention between the content features of both motions to determine which body parts in the content motion should receive style from which parts of the style motion, linearly recombining part-specific style vectors accordingly to prevent content leakage. The motion generator (3 transformer blocks) injects the modulated style via AdaIN and reconstructs pose, global translation, and velocity jointly, with attention ensuring consistency between them. Training is adversarial (single-block transformer discriminator) and includes a novel style disentanglement loss that penalizes differences between outputs stylized by two same-style/different-content motions, plus a physics-based loss regularizing velocity, acceleration, and foot-contact timing.

## Results

On the Xia dataset, MoST achieves the lowest Content Consistency (CC) of 8.5 and the lowest Style Consistency++ (SC++) of 63.0, outperforming all four baselines on both metrics. The CC advantage is particularly dramatic: MoST's 8.5 is more than 2× lower than the next-best method (Wen et al., 18.5) and over 6× lower than MotionPuzzle (51.4). Performance is remarkably stable across same-content (CC 8.0, SC++ 62.2) and different-content (CC 8.7, SC++ 63.2) pairs, whereas all baselines degrade substantially on different-content inputs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoST wins on both CC and SC++ against all four baselines on the Xia dataset. Against MotionPuzzle [14], MoST reduces CC from 51.4 to 8.5 (a 6× improvement) and SC++ from 76.0 to 63.0; MotionPuzzle's high CC (especially 66.9 on different-content pairs) reveals that it tends to replicate the style motion rather than transferring disentangled style. Against Aberman et al. [1], MoST cuts CC from 46.0 to 8.5 and SC++ from 189.7 to 63.0, a massive gap reflecting Aberman's frequent complete transfer failures (twisted motions). Against Park et al. [20], MoST improves CC from 38.4 to 8.5 while matching SC++ (65.7 vs 63.0), indicating Park's method preserves style reasonably well but produces twisted poses that compromise content. Against Wen et al. [28], MoST nearly halves CC (18.5 → 8.5) and reduces SC++ from 80.8 to 63.0, showing Wen's method generates smooth but insufficiently stylized motions. Notably, MoST is the only method whose performance remains stable between same-content and different-content pairs, while every baseline degrades significantly on the harder different-content setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

MoST builds on the AdaIN mechanism (Huang et al., ICCV 2017) for style injection in the generator and the transformer-based motion encoding architecture from Kim et al. (ECCV 2022), but departs fundamentally from prior motion style transfer methods (Aberman et al., Park et al., Wen et al., MotionPuzzle) in two ways: it replaces separate style and content encoders with a Siamese encoder that extracts both from a single motion, and it introduces a Part-Attentive Style Modulator that uses cross-attention between content features to route style to the correct body parts. The style disentanglement loss, which penalizes output differences when two style motions share a style label but differ in content, is a new training signal not present in prior work. The paper explicitly positions itself against the well-documented failure of existing methods when style and content motions have different action types, a setting those methods neither designed for nor adequately evaluated.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work addresses a practical gap that limits the applicability of motion style transfer in games, metaverse, and robotics: prior methods degrade sharply or produce incoherent output when the style and content motions depict different actions (e.g., transferring 'old' walking style onto a punch). By eliminating heuristic post-processing (foot-contact fixing, global-translation copying) while outperforming methods that rely on them, MoST offers a cleaner pipeline. Open questions remain: the physics-based loss does not fully eliminate foot penetration, the transformer requires a fixed maximum sequence length, and the authors note the high cost of motion capture data motivates future few-shot extensions. The SC++ metric, while a useful extension of prior evaluation, is still a proxy and the evaluation is confined to two relatively small datasets (8×6 and 16-style configurations), leaving generalization to larger, more diverse motion corpora untested.
