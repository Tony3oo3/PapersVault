## Problem

Standard deep learning assumes independent, shuffled mini-batches, yet humans and animals learn from a single continuous stream of observations. This paper tackles the largely unexplored question of whether and how neural networks can learn effectively from a single continuous video stream at batch size 1, where consecutive frames are highly correlated and standard optimizers like Adam fail. It also asks what pretraining and optimization strategies are needed to match the performance of conventional IID training in this regime.

## Input / Output

The model consumes 4 consecutive RGB frames (12 channels) at 224×224 resolution from a continuous video stream and predicts 4 future frames (12 channels) at a configurable temporal displacement Δ. All task outputs—raw pixels, 40-class semantic segmentation, and depth—are mapped into RGB space via standard color maps, so the architecture and L2 pixel loss remain identical across tasks. Evaluation is performed both in-stream (measuring adaptation to the current video) and out-of-stream on a held-out stream with the optimizer disabled (measuring generalization).

## Method

The paper proposes a unified pixel-to-pixel framework where all prediction tasks share the same model architecture (UNet or ViT-L) and L2 loss, eliminating task-specific decoders. The core technical contribution is an empirical study revealing that momentum in optimizers (e.g., Adam) actively harms single-stream learning due to strong temporal gradient correlations, while RMSProp without momentum is far more robust. A second key finding is that accumulating gradients over multiple steps before updating weights (e.g., every 16 frames) improves generalization at a small cost to adaptation. For pretraining, the authors introduce a family of future-prediction tasks (Guided, Vanilla, and Masked Future Prediction) trained in the standard IID setting on Kinetics-700, which substantially outperform ImageNet MAE pretraining for downstream single-stream performance. The combined 'Baby Learning' (BL) recipe—RMSProp, 16-step gradient accumulation, constant learning rate, and Guided Future Prediction pretraining—matches standard deep learning (Adam + ImageNet MAE) on out-of-stream generalization at batch size 1 while outperforming it in-stream through adaptation.

## Results

The proposed Baby Learning (BL) approach, using a continuous video stream with batch size 1, matches the standard deep learning setup (STDL) with IID data and batch size 1 on out-of-stream generalization while outperforming it in-stream. On ScanNet segmentation (t+1), BL achieves 0.463 in-stream IoU vs. 0.376 for STDL (IID, bs1), and on ScanNet depth (t+1) BL achieves 1.595 logRMSE vs. 1.722 for STDL. Out-of-stream, BL achieves 0.312 IoU on ScanNet Segm (t+1) vs. 0.302 for STDL, and 2.038 logRMSE on ScanNet Depth (t+1) vs. 2.012 for STDL, confirming comparable generalization. The Blind dummy baseline, which exploits temporal correlations in the target stream, reaches 0.547 in-stream IoU on ScanNet Segm (t+1) but has no meaningful out-of-stream performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between BL (continuous stream, batch size 1, RMSProp, guided future prediction pretraining) and STDL (IID shuffled data, batch size 1, AdamW, ImageNet-MAE pretraining). BL matches STDL out-of-stream on all four tasks (e.g., 0.312 vs. 0.302 IoU on ScanNet Segm t+1; 2.038 vs. 2.012 logRMSE on ScanNet Depth t+1) while clearly winning in-stream (0.463 vs. 0.376 IoU on ScanNet Segm t+1; 1.595 vs. 1.722 logRMSE on ScanNet Depth t+1), demonstrating the benefit of adaptation to the specific stream. Against the larger-batch STDL (bs16), BL is competitive out-of-stream but lags in-stream on Ego4D pixel prediction (0.018 vs. 0.023 in-stream, where BL actually wins) while STDL bs16 dominates in-stream on ScanNet Segm (0.495 vs. 0.463). The Blind dummy baseline from Hammoud et al. [15], which simply outputs the mean of previously seen target pixels, achieves surprisingly high in-stream scores (e.g., 0.547 IoU on ScanNet Segm t+1) by exploiting temporal autocorrelation in the target stream, but produces no meaningful out-of-stream results, underscoring the paper's argument that both adaptation and generalization must be measured to avoid misleadingly optimistic evaluations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new problem formulation at the intersection of continual learning, online learning, and self-supervised video pretraining, but deliberately departs from all three. It moves beyond continual learning's typical task-sequence framing (e.g., class-incremental ImageNet) to a single continuous video stream with batch size 1 and high frame rate, and beyond test-time training's short-horizon motivation to multi-hour streams. The closest prior work [33] addressed temporal correlations via replay buffers but never evaluated on the stream itself; the unified pixel-to-pixel framework and the future-prediction pretraining family (guided, vanilla, masked) are novel contributions that repurpose MAE-style objectives for a streaming setting without architectural or loss changes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic study of whether standard deep learning tools even work when learning from a single continuous video stream, a paradigm that mirrors how humans and animals actually acquire knowledge and is essential for embodied AI and personal adaptive assistants. The finding that momentum hurts and that infrequent weight updates trade adaptation for generalization provides concrete, actionable guidance for anyone building streaming learners. Key limitations include the absence of explicit memory modules, the 24-hour stream length, and the remaining gap between continuous-stream and IID performance; open questions include whether these optimization insights generalize to longer streams, other modalities, and whether memory-augmented architectures can close the remaining gap.
