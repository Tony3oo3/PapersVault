## Problem

Existing video INRs exhibit a fundamental trade-off: methods exploiting inter-frame information (e.g., FFNeRV) struggle on static sequences, while those relying on frame-specific content (e.g., HNeRV) falter on high-motion content. Hybrid approaches that combine both are limited to a 2:1 aspect ratio and fail to scale to higher bitrates. No prior video INR has outperformed HEVC across all UVG sequences.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240267/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a video as a sequence of RGB frames (1920×1080 in the UVG evaluation) along with a frame index t, and produces reconstructed frames plus a compressed bitstream. The video state transmitted after training consists of the decoder parameters, per-frame spatial embeddings (e_t), and three multi-resolution GOP feature grids; the encoder is discarded. The latent feature z_t is formed by concatenating the frame-specific embedding with the GOP-specific feature, both at a spatial resolution matching the input frame's aspect ratio.

## Method

T-NeRV is a hybrid video INR whose encoder combines a ConvNeXt-based frame encoder (~100× larger than HNeRV's) producing per-frame embeddings with a three-level multi-resolution feature grid providing GOP-specific features; the embeddings are temporally augmented via a per-frame learnable modulation factor. A flow-based decoder (five T-NeRV blocks with AdaIN temporal injection and PixelShuffle upsampling) predicts an independent frame and optical flow maps, which are used to warp adjacent independent frames and aggregate them into the final output. For compression, the paper extends the entropy-constrained training framework of Gomes et al. to jointly optimize rate and distortion over both model weights and per-frame embeddings, using a single shared entropy model for all embeddings to exploit inter-frame redundancies. This end-to-end rate-distortion objective causes the network to automatically adjust the balance between frame-specific and GOP-specific information during training, effectively fine-tuning itself to the target content's motion characteristics without manual configuration.

## Results

On the UVG dataset, T-NeRV achieves state-of-the-art video representation performance in both evaluation settings: 38.12 dB average PSNR at 720p (12.4M params) and 34.00 dB at 1920×960 (3.0M params), improving over the second-best method by more than 1 dB in each case. On high-motion sequences, gains are most dramatic — e.g., 35.23 dB on 'Ready Set Go' vs. 32.28 dB for FFNeRV (a 2.95 dB improvement). For compression, T-NeRV is the first video INR to outperform HEVC (x265 veryslow, no b-frames) across all UVG sequences in the rate-distortion plot.

## Baselines & Comparisons

T-NeRV outperforms all six INR baselines (NeRV, E-NeRV, C-NeRV, FFNeRV, HNeRV, DNeRV) in both video representation settings. In the 720p setting, it beats the strongest baseline FFNeRV by 1.02 dB on average, with the largest gap on high-motion sequences (2.95 dB on 'Ready Set Go', 1.05 dB on 'Yacht Racing'); HNeRV, the previous hybrid approach, trails by a substantial 8.1 dB on average, confirming that prior hybrid INRs fail to scale beyond 2:1 aspect ratios. In the more challenging 1920×960 setting, T-NeRV leads DNeRV (the second-best) by 1.33 dB on average, with particularly strong gains on static sequences like 'Honeybee' (+1.14 dB) and 'Shake' (+0.50 dB). For compression, T-NeRV surpasses HEVC (x265 veryslow, no b-frames) across the entire BPP spectrum on all UVG sequences — a first for any video INR — and also outperforms C-NeRV, HNeRV, and FFNeRV in the rate-distortion plot. The paper notes that T-NeRV's entropy-constrained training automatically allocates more bits to GOP-specific feature grids for high-motion content and to frame embeddings for static content, explaining its consistent advantage across diverse sequences.

## Relation to Prior Work

T-NeRV synthesizes two previously separate lines of video INR research: the hybrid encoder-decoder paradigm of HNeRV/DNeRV (where per-frame embeddings are extracted by a ConvNeXt-based encoder and transmitted alongside decoder weights) and the parametric multi-resolution feature grid of FFNeRV (which captures GOP-level temporal context). The key architectural departure is fusing these two information sources into a single latent that feeds a flow-based decoder, creating a tunable ratio between frame-specific and GOP-specific information. On the compression side, the paper extends Gomes et al.'s entropy-constrained training framework—which previously only handled INR weights—to also model the entropy of per-frame embeddings and feature grids, enabling joint rate-distortion optimization over all transmitted components.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

T-NeRV is the first video INR to outperform HEVC (veryslow, no B-frames) on all UVG sequences, marking a milestone for the INR-based compression paradigm. The 'lever' concept—where entropy-constrained training automatically allocates more bits to feature grids for high-motion content and to frame embeddings for static content—provides an intuitive, content-adaptive mechanism that eliminates the need for manual per-video configuration. However, the encoding speed remains a critical bottleneck (many hours per sequence), and the method is evaluated only on the 7-sequence UVG dataset at 1080p, leaving open questions about scalability to longer videos, higher resolutions, and diverse content beyond UVG's limited motion range.
