## Problem

Text-to-image models trained on large web-crawled datasets like LAION perpetuate harmful cultural stereotypes and misrepresentations, particularly of Global South cultures. Existing fine-tuning techniques handle low-level adaptations (style, characters) but fail to encode high-level, abstract concepts like culture, and fine-tuning on small representative datasets causes overfitting and memorization of training images.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240521/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a free-form text prompt describing cultural content (e.g., 'a photo of a traditional building, in Korea') and produces a 512×512 RGB image via Stable Diffusion. During fine-tuning, the CCUB dataset provides 150–200 manually captioned image-text pairs per culture as positive examples, while the model's own un-fine-tuned outputs (structurally aligned via ControlNet depth conditioning) serve as negative examples. The fine-tuned model is applied with LoRA adapters, so inference requires only the base Stable Diffusion pipeline plus the learned low-rank weight matrices.

## Method

SCoFT fine-tunes Stable Diffusion's UNet via LoRA using a weighted sum of three losses: the standard latent diffusion noise-prediction loss, a memorization penalty that regularizes outputs under cultural captions toward outputs under generic BLIP captions (preventing overfitting on small data), and a Self-Contrastive Perceptual Loss. The key mechanism decodes latent codes into pixel space over 20 denoising steps (recording gradients only on the first step to limit memory), then computes a triplet loss in a pre-trained perceptual feature space (CLIP convolutional layers or DreamSim) that pulls generated images toward CCUB positives and away from the model's own biased outputs used as negatives. Negative examples are generated by the un-fine-tuned model conditioned on the same depth map (via MiDaS + ControlNet) and a cultural suffix prompt, ensuring structural alignment so the contrast isolates cultural features. This self-contrastive design leverages the model's intrinsic biases as a rich source of counterexamples, shifting the generated distribution away from misrepresentations without requiring large curated datasets.

## Results

SCoFT+MPC (the full method) achieves the best KID on the CCUB test set (19.621 ×10⁻³ vs. 30.355 for un-fine-tuned Stable Diffusion), indicating generated images are closer in distribution to culturally curated data. In the user survey with 51 participants across 5 cultures, SCoFT+MPC attains the best average rank on all four subjective criteria: Best Described (1.83), Most Culturally Representative (1.84), Least Stereotypical (1.91), and Least Offensive (1.78), compared to Stable Diffusion's 3.09, 3.07, 2.98, and 3.07 respectively. On the MS COCO generalization set, Stable Diffusion retains a slight edge in KID (4.396 vs. 4.819) and CLIP Score (0.813 vs. 0.799), which the authors attribute to CLIP's own cultural biases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is un-fine-tuned Stable Diffusion. SCoFT+MPC decisively outperforms it on all culturally-relevant metrics: KID on CCUB drops from 30.355 to 19.621 (a 35% reduction), and user survey average ranks improve by roughly 1.2–1.3 positions on every subjective criterion (e.g., Least Offensive goes from 3.07 to 1.78). In binary pairwise comparisons, SCoFT+MPC was ranked less offensive than Stable Diffusion 62% of the time, and the MMSR consensus algorithm confirmed SCoFT+MPC as the top-ranked method across all five cultural groups. However, Stable Diffusion retains a small advantage on general-purpose metrics (KID-COCO: 4.396 vs. 4.819; CLIP Score: 0.813 vs. 0.799), which the authors argue reflects CLIP's own cultural bias rather than a true quality deficit of SCoFT.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Stable Diffusion's latent diffusion architecture and LoRA-based fine-tuning, but departs from prior fine-tuning work (DreamBooth, Textual Inversion) that targets low-level concepts like character appearance or style. Where prior cultural adaptation efforts (ERNIE-ViLG 2.0, Japanese Stable Diffusion) require millions of curated pairs, SCoFT operates on 150–200 community-collected images per culture by introducing three novel loss components: a memorization penalty using BLIP captions, a perceptual loss computed in decoded pixel space (addressing the technical challenge of backpropagating through multiple UNet denoising steps), and a self-contrastive triplet loss that repurposes the model's own biased outputs as negative examples. The CCUB dataset itself extends the community-curation methodology of MaRVL and RedCaps to vision-language generation with culturally specific categories.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that standard automatic metrics (CLIP Score, KID on COCO) actively favor the un-fine-tuned model over culturally fine-tuned ones, while human evaluators from the target cultures consistently prefer the fine-tuned outputs — a gap that signals the inadequacy of current evaluation tooling for cultural competence. The self-contrastive technique is potentially generalizable to other high-level concepts (disability representation, copyright boundaries) from small curated datasets, but the current scope is limited to five cultures with modest dataset sizes, and the approach does not yet handle generic prompts without explicit cultural context. Open questions include whether the technique scales to dozens of cultures, whether larger community datasets would reduce the need for the memorization penalty, and how to build reliable automatic metrics that align with culturally situated human judgment.
