## Problem

Open-World Few-Shot Learning (OFSL) requires accurate classification from very few support samples whose labels may be corrupted by noise from both known and unseen classes. Existing OFSL methods (metric learning, feature aggregation) degrade significantly under high noise ratios, and the limited sample budget makes even a single mislabeled example disproportionately harmful. The paper asks whether the prior knowledge embedded in frozen vision-language foundation models can be strategically activated to make few-shot classification robust to such label noise.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a C-way K-shot support set (K ∈ {1,2,4,8,16}) of RGB images with category-name text, where a fraction of labels are randomly corrupted (noise ratios from 0 to 1.0), and produces a single class label per query image. The pipeline is three-stage: a frozen pretrainer corrects noisy labels, a frozen DALL-E model generates synthetic images from corrected category names to augment the support set, and a trainable MLP adapter performs final classification. At inference time, only the classification branch of the adapter is invoked, so the inverse-reasoning and data-augmentation components are training-only.

## Method

DeIL introduces a 'Direct-and-Inverse' two-phase strategy: an inverse step (using CLIPN with negation prompts like 'A photo without [CLASSNAME]') first identifies and filters out unlikely categories, and a direct step (using CLIP with standard prompts) then pinpoints the correct class. The frozen DeIL-Pretrainer applies this to detect and correct noisy support labels before any training; the frozen DALL-E model then generates additional images per corrected category to alleviate data scarcity. The trainable DeIL-Adapter fuses CLIP's zero-shot logits with an MLP adapter's output via a CaFo-style logit combination (Eq. 15), and augments cross-entropy with an InfoNCE contrastive loss whose negative samples are selected through CLIPN's inverse reasoning, making the contrastive objective label-independent. Only the two-layer MLP adapter is updated (AdamW, lr=0.001, 40 epochs, batch 256), while CLIP (ResNet-50), CLIPN (ViT-B-16), and DALL-E remain frozen throughout.

## Results

On ImageNet 1-shot, DeIL achieves 62.28% top-1 accuracy at 30% noisy labels and maintains 62.02% even at 100% noise, demonstrating near-constant performance across all noise levels. This contrasts sharply with baselines like CaFo (59.99% at 30% noise, 59.03% at 100%) and CLIP-Adapter (55.19% at 30%, 18.85% at 100%), which degrade substantially as noise increases. DeIL also outperforms the best baseline (CaFo, 63.80%) at zero noise by only 1.18%, but surpasses it by 2.29% at 30% noise and 2.99% at 100% noise. The method is evaluated on 11 datasets (ImageNet, OxfordPets, Caltech101, DTD, Food101, Sun397, UCF101, EuroSAT, FGVC-Aircraft, Flower102, StanfordCars) at 1/2/4/8/16-shot settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DeIL outperforms all seven baselines at every noise level from 30% to 100%, with the largest margins appearing at high noise: at 100% noise, DeIL (62.02%) exceeds CaFo (59.03%) by 2.99%, Tip-Adapter-F (57.73%) by 4.29%, and CLIP-Adapter (18.85%) by 43.17%. At the standard 30% noise setting, DeIL (62.28%) beats CaFo (59.99%) by 2.29%, Tip-Adapter-F (59.97%) by 2.31%, and APE-T (51.02%) by 11.26%. The only case where a baseline edges out DeIL is at 0% noise, where CaFo achieves 63.80% versus DeIL's 62.62% (a 1.18% gap), suggesting DeIL's label-correction machinery introduces a small cost when labels are already clean. The defining advantage of DeIL is its flat performance curve: accuracy stays within a 0.4% range (62.02–62.42%) across all noise levels, whereas every other method degrades by 3–60+ points as noise increases. On the 10 additional datasets (OxfordPets, Caltech101, DTD, Food101, Sun397, UCF101, EuroSAT, FGVC-Aircraft, Flower102, StanfordCars), DeIL uniformly outperforms all compared methods across 1/2/4/8/16-shot settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

DeIL positions itself at the intersection of two trends: the shift from training OFSL models from scratch to leveraging frozen foundation models, and the use of adapter-based fine-tuning (Tip-Adapter, CLIP-Adapter, CALIP). Its key conceptual departure is the 'Direct-and-Inverse' framework, which repurposes CLIPN (a model originally designed for OOD detection) as an inverse classifier to identify noisy labels before CLIP corrects them—a two-stage filtering strategy absent from prior OFSL methods that rely on metric learning or feature aggregation. The logit fusion mechanism in the DeIL-Adapter is directly adapted from CaFo [38], and DALL-E-based data augmentation follows the same design ethos, making the paper's novelty primarily in the orchestration of these components under the Direct-and-Inverse principle rather than in any single architectural innovation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical relevance of OFSL is clear: real-world few-shot deployments rarely have clean labels, and the paper demonstrates that a frozen foundation-model pipeline can maintain near-constant accuracy (~62%) on ImageNet 1-shot even at 100% label noise, where competing methods degrade by 10–50 points. However, the per-component ablation gains are modest (0.1–0.6% each), and the method is tightly coupled to the CLIP/CLIPN/DALL-E ecosystem, leaving open whether the Direct-and-Inverse principle transfers to other foundation model families or to settings beyond image classification. The authors themselves note the need to investigate why foundation models occasionally underperform and to broaden OFSL to more diverse practical tasks.
