## Problem

HDR video reconstruction from alternatingly-exposed LDR sequences has been hindered by the absence of large-scale real-world training datasets, forcing existing models to rely on synthetic data that fails to capture real degradations like noise in under-exposed regions and saturation in over-exposed regions. Additionally, prior alignment methods struggle with global camera motion and produce ghosting artifacts due to inaccurate flow estimation in the presence of exposure-related distortions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241244/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The network consumes three consecutive LDR frames with alternating exposures (e.g., {-3, 0, -3} EV), each mapped to the linear HDR domain via gamma correction and concatenated with the original to form a 6-channel tensor, then predicts a single HDR frame for the center frame at 1500×1000 resolution. The accompanying Real-HDRV dataset provides 500 LDRs-HDRs video pairs where each frame is captured from a 7-image multi-exposure stack (±1, ±2, ±3 EV around reference), with per-frame HDR labels generated via Debevec-Malik fusion, covering indoor/outdoor and daytime/nighttime scenes with global, local, and full motion patterns.

## Method

The proposed two-stage alignment network first applies a Global Alignment Module (GAM) that estimates a weighted sum of 8 pre-defined offset bases (translation, rotation, scale, perspective) via a shared encoder and weight-prediction head, spatially transforming neighboring frames to remove camera motion before local alignment. The Local Alignment Module (LAM) then performs coarse-to-fine feature-level alignment using a 3-level pyramid of adaptive separable convolutions (kernel size 31), where exposure-aware masks (contrast, exposure wellness, saturation) are concatenated with features at each level to guide kernel weight prediction and selectively integrate useful information from neighboring frames. A fusion module suppresses harmful features from under/over-exposed regions and reconstructs the HDR frame through residual blocks with skip connections. The entire pipeline is trained end-to-end with L1 loss between μ-law tonemapped predictions and ground truth, requiring no explicit optical flow estimation.

## Results

On the Real-HDRV test set, the proposed two-stage alignment network achieves the best average performance across all six metrics: PSNR-μ 43.86 dB, SSIM-μ 0.9758, PU-PSNR 38.11 dB, PU-SSIM 0.9792, HDR-VDP-2 63.53, and HDR-VQM 89.71. The closest competitor, Yue23, trails by 0.38 dB in PSNR-μ and 2.09 in HDR-VDP-2. The method also achieves the best per-scene results in both daytime (ID&OD) and nighttime (IN&ON) splits, with a 4.4 dB PSNR-μ gap over the weakest baseline (Kalantari13). In terms of efficiency, the model has 5.98M parameters and 0.34 s/frame inference time, offering a favorable speed-accuracy trade-off compared to Chen21 (0.87 s) and Yue23 (0.76 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all seven baselines on every metric. The closest competitor is Yue23 [45], which uses optical flow plus deformable convolution; the proposed method leads by 0.38 dB in PSNR-μ (43.86 vs 43.48) and 2.09 in HDR-VDP-2 (63.53 vs 61.44), with the gap widening in nighttime scenes (41.58 vs 41.20 PSNR-μ). Among flow-based methods, Chen21 [4] (42.12 dB) and Kalantari19 [18] (41.75 dB) are outperformed by 1.7–2.1 dB in PSNR-μ, while the older Kalantari13 [19] patch-based method lags by 4.45 dB. Attention-based deghosting methods adapted for video—AHDRNet [42] (43.06 dB) and CA-ViT [42] (42.58 dB)—are surpassed by 0.8–1.3 dB, with the paper attributing their inferiority to ghosting artifacts from lack of explicit alignment. LAN-HDR [6], which uses luminance-based attention for alignment, scores 42.53 dB and is outperformed by 1.33 dB; the paper notes it fails to handle under/over-exposed regions properly. Additionally, the dataset evaluation (Table 4) shows that training any of these baselines on Real-HDRV instead of the synthetic Chen21 dataset yields consistent 0.5–1.5 dB PSNR-μ gains, confirming the dataset's value independent of the architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the HDR video reconstruction line from alternating exposures (Kang 2003, Kalantari 2013/2019, Chen 2021, Yue 2023) by explicitly decomposing the alignment problem into a global stage (8-DoF parametric offsets inspired by sparse-to-dense flow and motion basis learning) and a local stage (adaptive separable convolution at the feature level, borrowed from video frame interpolation). It departs from prior flow-based or deformable-convolution alignment approaches, which treat global and local motion uniformly and are vulnerable to noise/saturation in alternating exposures. The dataset contribution fills a gap left by Chen21 (76 dynamic + 49 static pairs), which the authors argue is too small and too limited in motion diversity for supervised training, and by synthetic datasets whose degradation distributions do not match real sensor artifacts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Real-HDRV (500 LDRs-HDRs pairs, ~28k LDR frames, per-frame HDR labels from 7-exposure stacks) is the largest publicly available real-world HDR video reconstruction dataset and is likely to become the standard training/benchmark resource for this task, directly addressing the domain-gap problem that has kept the field stuck on synthetic data. The two-stage alignment is a clean, low-parameter (5.98M) solution that outperforms heavier flow-based methods, but the dataset's frame-by-frame capture protocol (manual motion creation, 40 fps burst) means it does not fully replicate the temporal dynamics of true continuous video, and the 7-image ground-truth stack is impractical for real-time deployment. Open questions include whether the global-then-local decomposition generalizes to very large camera motions or long sequences, and whether the adaptive separable convolution can be replaced by lighter-weight alternatives for mobile deployment.
