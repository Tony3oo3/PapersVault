## Problem

Existing visual foundation models (CLIP, MAE, SAM) operate at the image level or serve narrow subdomains, leaving no unified model capable of simultaneously performing object-level tasks—detection, segmentation, tracking, grounding, and identification—across both images and videos. The absence of a general object representation that generalizes zero-shot to new data and tasks without task-specific fine-tuning is the central gap this paper addresses.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240666/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

GLEE consumes RGB images or video frames alongside flexible text prompts (category lists, free-form referring expressions, captions) and optional visual prompts (points, boxes, scribbles). For large-vocabulary datasets exceeding 100 categories, only the positive categories plus random negatives are fed to the text encoder per image to avoid redundant computation. Outputs include bounding boxes, binary masks at 1/4 resolution (computed via dot product between mask embeddings and a pixel embedding map), object class assignments via text-alignment scores, and object query embeddings that serve directly as track identities through bipartite matching across frames—no dedicated tracking head is required.

## Method

GLEE builds its object decoder on MaskDINO but replaces the FFN classifier with a dynamic class head that computes alignment scores between object embeddings and CLIP-style text embeddings (following DetCLIP), enabling arbitrary vocabularies and free-form descriptions. An early-fusion module (adapted from UNINEXT) performs bi-directional cross-attention between backbone features and prompt embeddings before the Transformer encoder, while text and visual prompt embeddings additionally interact with object queries via self-attention in the decoder. Training is unified across six loss types—focal semantic loss, L1+GIoU box loss, Dice+focal mask loss, confidence loss, contrastive tracking loss (following IDOL for temporal consistency), and L1 distillation loss anchoring the text encoder to frozen CLIP embeddings—jointly optimized over 5M+ images spanning detection, segmentation, referring, and open-world annotations. The key insight is that decoder object embeddings naturally acquire discriminability and temporal consistency sufficient for tracking via simple bipartite matching, eliminating the need for separate tracking modules. Scaling to 10M images by incorporating SA1B masks and GRIT box-caption pairs further strengthens zero-shot generalization at low annotation cost.

## Results

On COCO-val, GLEE-Pro achieves 62.0 APbox and 54.2 APmask, outperforming all generalist models and rivaling specialist detectors like VITDet-H (58.7 APbox). On LVIS, GLEE-Pro reaches 55.7 APbox and 49.9 APmask, surpassing all prior generalist approaches. On open-world instance segmentation (UVO), GLEE-Pro achieves 72.6 AR@100, exceeding ODISE by 8.9 points. On zero-shot video tracking (TAO), GLEE-Pro attains 47.2 TETA, surpassing the previous best OVTrack (34.7) by 36.0%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GLEE-Pro outperforms all generalist models on COCO detection (62.0 vs. 60.6 APbox for UNINEXT-H, 43.4 for Florence-2-L) and LVIS (55.7 APbox vs. no reported LVIS results for most generalists). On referring expression tasks, UNINEXT-H (92.6 P@0.5 on RefCOCO) and Florence-2-L (93.4 P@0.5) still lead GLEE-Pro (91.0), indicating that specialist referring models retain an edge in fine-grained language comprehension. On open-world segmentation (UVO), GLEE-Pro (72.6 AR@100) substantially surpasses ODISE (57.7) by 8.9 points, the largest margin in the table. Among specialist detectors, EVA-02-L (64.2 APbox on COCO) slightly edges out GLEE-Pro (62.0), but GLEE-Pro matches or exceeds all other specialists while simultaneously handling segmentation, grounding, and open-world tasks. On zero-shot video tracking, GLEE-Pro achieves 47.2 TETA on TAO (vs. 34.7 for OVTrack), 31.2 HOTA on BURST (vs. 8.2 for Box Tracker), and 23.9 AP on LV-VIS (vs. 21.1 for OV2Seg-B), demonstrating that image-trained object representations transfer effectively to video without any video-specific training.

## Relation to Prior Work

GLEE builds its object decoder on MaskDINO, adopts the text-embedding classification head from DetCLIP, and uses the early-fusion prompt module from UNINEXT, while borrowing the contrastive tracking loss from IDOL and bipartite-matching tracking from MinVIS. Conceptually, it departs from image-level foundation models (CLIP, MAE, SAM) that learn global representations, and from unified sequence-to-sequence models (OFA, Pix2Seq, UniPerceiver) that handle multiple tasks but focus on image-level understanding with slow autoregressive decoding. The key positioning is as an *object-level* foundation model: rather than producing a generic feature map or a segmentation mask from a visual prompt, GLEE directly predicts object queries with bounding boxes, masks, and class identities from arbitrary text or visual prompts, unifying detection, segmentation, grounding, and tracking under a single detector-based paradigm trained on multi-granularity supervision.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GLEE demonstrates that a single detector-based model, trained jointly on over 5 million images with heterogeneous supervision (boxes, masks, captions, referring expressions, visual prompts), can simultaneously achieve SOTA on detection, instance segmentation, referring comprehension, and open-world segmentation without task-specific fine-tuning, and transfer zero-shot to large-vocabulary video tracking with large margins over specialist trackers. Its demonstrated utility as a drop-in replacement for SAM in the LISA mLLM suggests it can supply the object-level visual grounding that current multimodal LLMs lack. Open questions include whether the zero-shot video advantage persists as video-specific baselines mature, whether the OVIS gap (requiring fine-tuning for SOTA under heavy occlusion) reflects a fundamental limitation of image-centric training, and whether the approach scales to even larger vocabularies or 3D/embodied settings.
