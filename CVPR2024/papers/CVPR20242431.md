## Problem

Existing editable driving scene simulators fail to simultaneously satisfy three requirements: intuitive user interaction (current systems like UniSim and MARS require code-level editing for every trivial change), multi-camera photo-realistic rendering with view consistency, and seamless integration of external 3D digital assets with scene-consistent lighting. No prior system supports natural-language-driven editing of photo-realistic 3D driving scenes while importing arbitrary 3D assets.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242431/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system accepts free-form natural language commands (including mixed, highly abstract, and multi-round instructions with cross-round context dependencies) alongside multi-camera RGB images from the Waymo Open Dataset (front, left-front, right-front cameras at 1920×1280, 40 frames per scene at 10 Hz), camera calibration parameters, a 3D digital asset bank, and lane maps. It outputs a photo-realistic wide-angle driving scene video with edited content, where background is rendered via McNeRF and foreground 3D assets are composited using Blender with McLight-estimated illumination. The multi-camera inputs have asynchronized trigger times and differing exposure times, which the system must reconcile.

## Method

ChatSim employs a multi-agent LLM collaboration framework where a Project Manager agent decomposes user commands into sub-tasks dispatched to specialized agents (view adjustment, background rendering, vehicle deletion, 3D asset management, vehicle motion, foreground rendering), each pairing an LLM for language interpretation with deterministic role functions for execution. For background rendering, McNeRF builds on F2-NeRF but adds two key mechanisms: multi-camera alignment via Agisoft Metashape to correct extrinsic pose noise from asynchronized triggers, and HDR radiance field prediction that multiplies predicted radiance by per-camera exposure time (with a normalization function) to resolve brightness inconsistency at camera-image boundaries. For foreground rendering, McLight estimates scene illumination in two parts: a skydome component using a two-stage autoencoder (LDR-to-HDR panorama reconstruction with a residual connection injecting peak intensity via spherical Gaussian lobe, plus multi-camera self-attention fusion) and a surrounding component that queries McNeRF along hemisphere rays at the object position for spatially-varying lighting; the two are blended by the final transmittance from McNeRF sampling. Vehicle motion is generated by a text-to-motion pipeline that links LLM-extracted motion attributes to lane-map placement and Bezier-curve trajectory planning with DRL-based tracking.

## Results

On the Waymo Open Dataset, McNeRF achieves state-of-the-art background novel view synthesis with PSNR 25.82, SSIM 0.822, and LPIPS 0.378, outperforming the best baseline S-NeRF (PSNR 24.71) by 1.11 dB while running 46× faster (2.5s vs. 114.5s inference). For lighting estimation, McLight reduces peak intensity median error by 57.0% (0.270 vs. 0.628) and peak angular median error by 9.9% (26.5° vs. 29.4°) compared to the prior best method (Wang et al., ECCV 2022), and achieves the highest user preference score (43.1% vs. 37.3%). The multi-agent collaboration framework boosts command execution accuracy from 21.6–71.7% (single agent) to 86.7–98.3% (collaborative agents) across all command categories.

## Baselines & Comparisons

For background rendering (Table 3), McNeRF outperforms all four baselines on all three quality metrics. The closest competitor S-NeRF [69] achieves PSNR 24.71 but requires 114.5s inference; F2NeRF [62] is fast (2.4s) but lags in quality (PSNR 23.26, LPIPS 0.439). McNeRF's gains come from both multi-camera alignment (+2.5 dB over unaligned) and exposure-time compensation (+0.64 dB over no-exposure variant). For lighting estimation (Table 4), McLight beats Wang et al. [64] (ECCV 2022) on peak intensity median error (0.270 vs. 0.628, a 57% reduction) and angular median error (26.5° vs. 29.4°), and substantially outperforms Hold-Geoffroy et al. [31] (CVPR 2019) on all metrics (intensity median 0.270 vs. 0.975; angular median 26.5° vs. 51.6°). In user studies, McLight is preferred 43.1% of the time versus 37.3% for Wang et al. and 19.5% for Hold-Geoffroy et al. For motion generation (Table 5), the proposed text-to-motion method achieves 94–100% success rates across all action types, far exceeding GPT2Code (53.6–89.3%) and GPT2Motion (11.9–89.3%), with perfect within-road constraint satisfaction (100% vs. 21.4% and 27.7%).

## Relation to Prior Work

ChatSim sits at the intersection of three research threads: NeRF-based driving scene reconstruction (building on F2-NeRF as its backbone for unbounded scene rendering), outdoor lighting estimation for virtual object insertion (extending Wang et al.'s skydome autoencoder with a residual peak-intensity connection and multi-camera fusion), and LLM multi-agent collaboration frameworks (inspired by MetaGPT-style role decomposition). It departs from prior scene-editing systems like UniSim and MARS, which required code-level interaction and lacked external asset support, and from 2D image-generation methods (DriveDreamer, MagicDrive) that cannot maintain 3D view consistency. The key conceptual contribution is unifying natural-language-driven orchestration, multi-camera HDR NeRF rendering with exposure compensation, and hybrid skydome-plus-surrounding lighting into a single end-to-end pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ChatSim provides a practical pathway to generate customized, photo-realistic driving perception data (e.g., rare accident scenarios) without costly real-world data collection, validated by measurable 3D detection improvements when used as augmentation. However, the system is currently limited to vehicle-level editing (addition, deletion, motion, viewpoint) and does not yet support weather changes, road topology edits, or pedestrian dynamics, which are critical for comprehensive scenario testing. Open questions include whether the GPT-4-dependent multi-agent pipeline can be replaced by smaller local models for cost and latency, whether McNeRF's per-scene training (40 frames at 10 Hz) scales to longer sequences or dynamic backgrounds, and whether the lighting estimation generalizes to night or heavily overcast conditions where skydome cues are weak.
