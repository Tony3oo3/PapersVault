## Problem

Multi-target multi-camera (MTMC) tracking lacks a large-scale, real-world benchmark: existing datasets are either synthetic game simulations or small-scale controlled camera networks with idealized overlapping views, and the previously largest real-world dataset (DukeMTMC) was withdrawn due to privacy issues. Additionally, no prior dataset provides a thermal modality alongside RGB, leaving the potential of multi-modal learning for multi-camera tracking entirely unexplored.

## Input / Output

The system consumes 16 spatially aligned and temporally synchronized camera streams (RGB at 1920×1080, 23 fps, plus co-located thermal sensors) across 25 scenarios of 5.5 minutes each, spanning indoor/outdoor, day/evening, and multiple seasons. Outputs include per-camera bounding boxes with local track IDs, globally consistent person IDs across all 16 cameras (3,669 unique identities), and tracklet-level feature representations (from QDTrack + BoT) used for cross-camera association. The multi-modal variants additionally produce fused RGB-thermal feature vectors at either the input or feature level.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is the MTMMC dataset itself, annotated via a semi-automatic pipeline that separates single-camera tracking (keyframe labeling + DeepSORT interpolation, corrected by annotators) from multi-camera association (manual labeling with iterative reviewer checks enforcing temporal and spatial consistency constraints). Beyond the dataset, the paper introduces two novel multi-modal learning setups built on QDTrack: (1) modality fusion, which integrates thermal data at the input level (channel concatenation) or feature level (learned fusion) to improve tracking when both modalities are available; and (2) modality drop, which trains on RGB+thermal but evaluates on RGB alone, using knowledge distillation, multi-modal reconstruction, or multi-modal contrastive learning to transfer thermal-informed representations to the unimodal domain. For the full MTMC pipeline, tracklet features from QDTrack and BoT are associated across cameras via hierarchical clustering or optimization-based tracklet-to-target assignment, with thermal features fused at the feature level for the multi-modal variant.

## Results

On the new MTMMC benchmark, ByteTrack achieves the best single-camera tracking performance with IDF1 of 64.8 and MOTA of 89.7 when trained on MTMMC, while QDTrack follows at IDF1 53.0 and MOTA 84.5. MTMMC proves substantially harder than MOT17: JDE drops from IDF1 63.6 on MOT17 to 42.4 on MTMMC, and ByteTrack drops from 76.8 to 40.2 when trained on MOT17 and evaluated on MTMMC. For the full multi-camera tracking task, hierarchical clustering with RGB features achieves IDF1 41.6, and adding thermal via feature fusion improves this to IDF1 43.5. In the modality drop setting (train RGB+T, test RGB only), multi-modal contrastive learning shows the best transfer without fine-tuning (IDF1 59.7 on MOT17).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the four MOT baselines, ByteTrack dominates on MTMMC (IDF1 64.8 vs. QDTrack 53.0, CenterTrack 50.8, JDE 42.4), confirming its strength in handling the dataset's dense, multi-camera scenes. A key finding is that training on MTMMC consistently improves cross-dataset generalization: ByteTrack trained on MTMMC achieves IDF1 69.1 on MOT17, closer to the MOT17-trained upper bound (76.8) than the reverse (40.2 on MTMMC when trained on MOT17). For the multi-camera association step, hierarchical clustering (Kohl et al., IDF1 41.6) outperforms the optimization-based tracklet-to-target assignment (He et al., IDF1 32.8) by 8.8 IDF1 points. In the multi-modal MTMC setting, feature-level RGB-thermal fusion improves the clustering-based MCA from IDF1 41.6 to 43.5. For pre-training on MOT17, combining MTMMC with MOTSynth yields the best QDTrack result (IDF1 72.0 with fine-tuning), surpassing either source alone (68.6 or 70.8), demonstrating complementary value of real-world and synthetic data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on QDTrack (Pang et al., CVPR 2021) as the base multi-object tracker and BoT (Luo et al., 2019) for Re-ID feature extraction, integrating them into a multi-camera association pipeline. It departs from prior MTMC benchmarks (DukeMTMC, MTA, MMPTrack) by providing a real-world, large-scale dataset with 16 cameras across indoor/outdoor settings, filling the void left by DukeMTMC's withdrawal due to privacy concerns. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of a thermal modality alongside RGB, which is unprecedented in MTMC datasets, and the formulation of two new experimental setups—modality fusion and modality drop—that have no direct precedent in the multi-camera tracking literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MTMMC addresses a critical gap in the field: the absence of a large-scale, privacy-compliant, real-world MTMC benchmark after DukeMTMC's retraction. The finding that models trained on MTMMC's diverse, complex scenes generalize better to MOT17 than the reverse suggests that dataset diversity and environmental complexity are more important for robustness than raw annotation volume, a principle that extends beyond tracking. The modality drop experiments open a practically relevant question—whether thermal data during training can improve RGB-only deployment in resource-constrained surveillance settings—but the gains are modest (IDF1 improvements of ~1–2 points for fusion, ~4–5 for contrastive learning without fine-tuning), leaving open how to more effectively exploit the thermal channel. The multi-camera association baselines (hierarchical clustering, optimization-based) remain relatively weak, suggesting substantial room for improvement in cross-camera identity resolution.
