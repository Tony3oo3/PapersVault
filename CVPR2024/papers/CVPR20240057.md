## Problem

Reconstructing HDR video from spiking cameras requires high bit depth, but increasing bit depth multiplies data volume and reduces readout efficiency, creating a fundamental trade-off between HDR quality and achievable frame rate. Existing solutions either accumulate many single-bit spikings (introducing motion blur in high-speed scenes) or use hybrid spiking-RGB systems (requiring cumbersome synchronization and optical alignment). The paper asks whether a standalone spiking camera can simultaneously deliver both HDR and HFR video without the data overhead of pure multi-bit readout.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240057/cropped_page0_idx10.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes rolling-mixed-bit (RMB) spikings from a 500×500 spiking camera at 20,000 Hz, where 4 rows of 3-bit spikings are interleaved within single-bit spikings in a rolling scan (only 1.6% data increase over pure single-bit). At each reconstruction step, RMB-Net receives a temporal window of 7 single-bit frames and 7 upsampled multi-bit frames (accumulation interval N=70), and produces one HDR video frame at 2,000 FPS. The rolling multi-bit rows must first be rectified for shape distortion and upsampled to full-frame resolution before entering the network.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-part system: (1) an RMB readout mechanism that interleaves 4 rows of 3-bit spikings within single-bit spikings in a rolling manner, distributing transmission pressure evenly and increasing data volume by only 1.6% versus 3× for pure 3-bit; and (2) RMB-Net, a reconstruction network with a two-stage fusion design. The first stage uses a cross-bit attention (XBA) block that learns SoftMax-normalized weight masks between each pair of single-bit and multi-bit features to fuse them spatially. The second stage uses a cross-time attention (XTA) block that measures inter-frame correlations across the temporal window to perform weighted temporal merging, with a bias term equal to the temporal average. Three ConvLSTM layers propagate previous states forward for flicker-free output, and a decoder with residual links maps the fused features to the final HDR frame. Training is supervised with l2, SSIM, and LPIPS losses on μ-law tone-mapped ground truth, and Spike2Flow is finetuned on synthetic HDR data for optical flow estimation used in alignment and warping.

## Results

On synthetic data, RMB-Net achieves PSNR 24.06, SSIM 0.895, HDR-VDP-3 8.103, and HDR-QVM 0.096, outperforming all compared baselines across all four metrics. The strongest baseline, GC20 [15], reaches PSNR 19.22 and SSIM 0.800, yielding a 4.84 dB PSNR gap. Notably, pure 3-bit spikings (Pure-M) achieve a higher PSNR of 26.62, but RMB-Net attains comparable quality with less than 2% increase in data volume versus a 3× increase for pure multi-bit spikings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RMB-Net outperforms all four baseline families on every metric. Against the spiking-camera baselines (TFW-S, TFW-L, TFI from [61] and Spk2ImgNet from [57]), the gains are substantial: PSNR improves by 6.4–15.0 dB over TFW-S and by 4.85 dB over the best spiking baseline TFW-L. Against the quanta-image-sensor baselines, RMB-Net surpasses GC20 [15] by 4.84 dB in PSNR and 0.095 in SSIM, and MG20 [36] by 7.90 dB in PSNR. The most informative comparison is with Pure-M (pure 3-bit spikings fed to the same network), which achieves PSNR 26.62 vs. RMB-Net's 24.06; however, RMB-Net delivers this near-comparable quality with only a 1.6% data-volume increase over single-bit spikings, whereas Pure-M triples the data volume. The ablation further shows that removing cross-bit attention (w/o XBA) drops PSNR to 22.29 and removing cross-time attention (w/o XTA) drops it to 21.81, confirming both modules contribute meaningfully.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the spiking-camera-based HDR video reconstruction line (Chang et al. 2023, Han et al. 2020, Yang et al. 2023) by introducing a new input modality: rolling-mixed-bit (RMB) spikings that interleave multi-bit rows within single-bit frames. Conceptually, it repurposes the rolling-shutter readout paradigm (well-studied in HFR video inversion by Fan et al.) to solve a different problem—balancing bit depth against readout bandwidth in spiking sensors. The RMB-Net architecture draws on attention-based fusion (cross-bit and cross-time attention) and temporal state propagation (ConvLSTM), while relying on Spike2Flow for motion compensation, positioning it as a synthesis of spiking-camera reconstruction, QIS-based HDR theory (Gnanasambandam & Chan 2020), and rolling-shutter inversion techniques.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a fundamental hardware-level trade-off in spiking cameras: higher bit depth improves dynamic range but increases data volume, which is detrimental to the high frame rates that make spiking cameras attractive. The RMB mechanism's claim of achieving near-pure-3-bit HDR performance with <2% data overhead (versus 3× for pure 3-bit) is practically significant if the readout mechanism can be implemented in silicon. However, the current evaluation is limited by the absence of a hardware RMB sensor—real data is simulated via spatial-temporal binning—and the 2.5 dB PSNR gap to Pure-M in Table 2 suggests the 'comparable performance' claim is optimistic. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to higher bit depths (4-bit, 8-bit), whether the rolling distortion correction degrades under extreme motion, and whether the theoretical dynamic-range bounds hold under realistic non-ideal sensor conditions.
