## Problem

Current brain decoding is confined to a per-subject-per-model paradigm, where a model trained on one individual's fMRI cannot be applied to others. This limitation stems from three intertwined challenges: variable fMRI signal dimensions across subjects due to brain size differences, subject-specific neural response patterns, and the high cost of acquiring extensive fMRI data for new subjects. No prior method achieves cross-subject decoding with a single model.

## Input / Output

The method takes 1D fMRI voxel vectors from the NSD 'NSDGeneral' ROI as input, where each subject's signal has a different length (approximately 13,000–16,000 voxels) after temporal averaging and spatial flattening of the original 4D (3D+t) scan. The primary output is a reconstructed natural image generated by a Versatile Diffusion model conditioned on predicted CLIP image and text embeddings. A secondary output is synthesized fMRI signals for a target subject, produced by cycling another subject's fMRI through the learned embedder–builder pair while preserving semantic content.

## Method

MindBridge unifies variable-length fMRI inputs via biologically-inspired Adaptive Max Pooling, then projects each subject's signal through a subject-specific MLP brain embedder into an intermediate semantic embedding space. A novel cyclic fMRI reconstruction mechanism enforces subject-invariance: a subject-specific brain builder reconstructs fMRI from the embedding (autoencoder loss), and a cycle loss penalizes embedding mismatch when one subject's fMRI is transformed into another's via the cross-subject embedder–builder pair, effectively simulating shared-viewing conditions absent from the training data. A shared brain translator MLP then maps the invariant embedding to CLIP image and text embeddings, which jointly guide a Versatile Diffusion model for image synthesis. Training combines SoftCLIP contrastive loss, MSE regression loss, reconstruction loss, and cycle loss end-to-end. For new-subject adaptation, a reset-tuning strategy freezes the deep brain translator (retaining cross-subject knowledge) while retraining only the shallow subject-specific embedder and builder, supplemented by pseudo data augmentation that converts other subjects' fMRI into the new subject's space via cycle reconstruction.

## Results

On the NSD dataset (4 subjects, 982 test images), MindBridge with a single model achieves 94.7% CLIP similarity, 92.4% Inception similarity, 95.5% AlexNet(5) similarity, and 87.7% AlexNet(2) similarity, competitive with subject-specific methods that require 4 separate models. In the new-subject adaptation setting with only 500 training samples, MindBridge (via reset-tuning) reaches 86.7% CLIP similarity versus 80.1% for a vanilla per-subject model trained from scratch, a 6.6-point gain. With 4000 samples, the gap narrows but persists: 92.5% vs. 89.8% CLIP similarity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Takagi et al. [38], MindBridge clearly outperforms on every reported metric (e.g., 94.7% vs. 77.0% CLIP, 92.4% vs. 76.0% Inception), though Takagi et al. does not report low-level metrics. Against Brain-Diffuser [23], MindBridge wins on high-level semantic metrics (92.4% vs. 87.2% Inception; 94.7% vs. 91.5% CLIP) but trails on low-level pixel metrics (PixCorr .151 vs. .254; SSIM .263 vs. .356). Against MindEye [33], the strongest baseline, MindBridge is competitive on high-level metrics (94.7% vs. 94.1% CLIP; 92.4% vs. 93.8% Inception) but shows a notable gap on low-level metrics (PixCorr .151 vs. .309; SSIM .263 vs. .323). The critical distinction is that MindBridge achieves these results with a single model handling all 4 subjects, whereas every baseline requires 4 separate per-subject models. In the limited-data adaptation setting (Table 2), MindBridge consistently outperforms the vanilla from-scratch baseline across all three data budgets (500/1500/4000 samples), with the largest margin at 500 samples (86.7% vs. 80.1% CLIP).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the established brain decoding pipeline (fMRI → CLIP embeddings → diffusion-based image reconstruction) pioneered by works like MindEye [33] and Brain-Diffuser [23], but fundamentally departs from their per-subject-per-model paradigm by proposing a single unified model for multiple subjects. It adopts the SoftCLIP loss from MindEye [33] and the Versatile Diffusion [46] model as its generative backbone, while introducing two novel architectural components: an adaptive max-pooling aggregation function to handle variable fMRI dimensions across subjects, and a cyclic fMRI reconstruction mechanism (analogous to cycle-consistency in GANs) to enforce subject-invariant semantic embeddings. The reset-tuning strategy for new-subject adaptation inverts the standard transfer-learning convention of freezing shallow layers, instead resetting subject-specific shallow layers while preserving the cross-subject knowledge in the deep brain translator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MindBridge demonstrates that cross-subject brain decoding is feasible with a single model, which has direct practical implications for reducing the cost and time of fMRI scanning for new subjects (e.g., clinical patients) and for enabling fMRI synthesis as a form of pseudo data augmentation. However, the evaluation is restricted to the NSD dataset with only 4 subjects and 982 common test images, leaving open questions about generalizability to larger, more diverse populations and to different fMRI acquisition protocols. The 1D serialization of fMRI voxels, acknowledged by the authors, discards spatial structure that may be critical for more faithful reconstruction. Future work on 3D-aware cross-subject models and validation on larger multi-site datasets would be needed to confirm the broader applicability of this framework.
