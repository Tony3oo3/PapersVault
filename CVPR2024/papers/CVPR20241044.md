## Problem

Existing music-driven dance generation methods treat the task as a sequence-to-sequence problem, either using autoregressive models that accumulate errors and cause motion freezing after a few seconds, or compressed latent spaces that limit diversity and generalization. They neglect the coarse-to-fine choreographic structure that professional choreographers follow—first designing expressive dance phrases aligned to musical structure, then filling in transitions—making it difficult to generate coherent, expressive dances lasting minutes.

## Input / Output

The input is a long music clip processed via Librosa into a 2D feature map of shape L×35 (envelope, 20-dim MFCC, 12-dim chroma, one-hot peaks, one-hot beats), split into non-overlapping segments of length N (1024 frames ≈ 34 s on FineDance). The output is a continuous 3D dance motion sequence in SMPL format (L×139) comprising 4-dim foot-ground contact labels, 3-dim root translation, and 132-dim 6D-rotation representations for 21 sub-joints, generated in a single parallel inference rather than sequentially.

## Method

Lodge employs a two-stage coarse-to-fine diffusion architecture with characteristic dance primitives (expressive 8-frame key motions with high kinematic energy) as the intermediate representation. The first stage (global diffusion) takes the full music segment and outputs sparse primitives, which are then choreographically augmented by mirroring and beat alignment; these are split into hard-cue primitives (controlling segment boundaries via diffusion inpainting) and soft-cue primitives (guiding expressiveness during early denoising steps). The second stage (local diffusion) runs in parallel across short n-frame segments, using the hard-cue primitives to enforce start/end consistency so segments concatenate seamlessly, while soft-cue primitives enhance motion quality and diversity. A Foot Refine Block addresses the domain gap between linear joint-position contact constraints and nonlinear SMPL rotation space by computing foot contact scores and using cross-attention to generate corrective modifications, and a multi-genre discriminator enforces genre consistency with the music.

## Results

On the FineDance dataset (1024-frame / 34.13 s sequences), Lodge (DDPM) achieves the best motion quality with FID_k = 45.56 and FID_g = 34.29, substantially outperforming the strongest baseline EDGE (FID_k = 94.34, FID_g = 50.38). Lodge also attains the highest Beat Alignment Score (BAS = 0.2397) and the lowest Foot Skating Ratio (5.01% for DDPM; 2.76% for DDIM), compared to 20.04% for EDGE and 39.36% for MNET. In the user study, Lodge (DDPM) wins 42.6% of pairwise comparisons against ground truth, and 80.6–96.7% against competing methods. On AIST++, Lodge (DDPM) achieves FID_k = 37.09, FID_g = 18.79, and BAS = 0.2423, improving over EDGE (FID_k = 42.16, FID_g = 22.12, BAS = 0.2334).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Lodge decisively outperforms all six baselines on motion quality (FID_k and FID_g) and beat alignment (BAS) on FineDance. Against the closest diffusion-based competitor EDGE, Lodge (DDPM) reduces FID_k from 94.34 to 45.56 (−52%) and FID_g from 50.38 to 34.29 (−32%), while improving BAS from 0.2116 to 0.2397. The autoregressive baselines FACT and MNET are far behind on all quality metrics (FID_k > 100) and also suffer from severe foot skating (28–39%). Bailando achieves the best FID_g among baselines (28.17) but still trails Lodge (34.29) on FID_k (82.81 vs 45.56) and BAS (0.2029 vs 0.2397). The one area where Lodge lags is motion diversity: EDGE (Div_k = 8.13, Div_g = 6.45) and Bailando (Div_k = 7.74, Div_g = 6.25) both exceed Lodge (DDPM) (Div_k = 6.75, Div_g = 5.64), suggesting the coarse-to-fine guidance constrains variety somewhat. On AIST++, Lodge improves over EDGE on FID_g (18.79 vs 22.12) and BAS (0.2423 vs 0.2334) but is slightly behind on FID_k (37.09 vs 42.16) and diversity, consistent with the shorter clip lengths in that dataset limiting the advantage of global choreographic modeling.

## Relation to Prior Work

Lodge builds directly on EDGE's diffusion-based dance generation framework, adopting its Sequence Transformer Block and x0-prediction formulation for both its global and local diffusion stages, while borrowing the diffusion inpainting technique from Repaint for boundary consistency. It departs from the autoregressive paradigm of FACT and MNET (which accumulate errors over time) and from EDGE's overlap-and-interpolate strategy (which lacks global choreographic structure) by introducing a two-stage coarse-to-fine architecture where sparse 8-frame 'characteristic dance primitives' serve as an intermediate representation between a global diffusion model and parallel local diffusion models. The multi-genre discriminator follows MNET, and the foot contact optimization draws on LEMO's velocity-decoupling approach, but the Foot Refine Block addresses a domain-gap problem (linear position space vs. nonlinear SMPL rotation space) that prior foot-skating losses did not resolve.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The parallel generation architecture is practically significant: runtime remains stable regardless of sequence length (4.57s with DDIM for 1024 frames), unlike autoregressive methods whose cost scales linearly, making minute-scale dance generation feasible in production pipelines. The explicit encoding of choreographic rules (genre consistency, beat alignment, structural symmetry) through the dance primitives and their augmentation operations represents a principled way to inject domain knowledge into generative models. Key limitations remain: the method cannot generate hand gestures or facial expressions, and the AIST++ results are weaker than FineDance results, suggesting the approach benefits from long-duration training data. Open questions include whether the 8-frame primitive length is optimal, how the method scales to multi-person choreography, and whether the coarse-to-fine decomposition generalizes to other long-sequence motion tasks beyond dance.
