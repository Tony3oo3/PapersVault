## Problem

Implicit Neural Representations (INRs) are widely deployed for video compression, novel view synthesis, and image super-resolution, yet their internal representations remain largely opaque. Existing interpretability tools such as GradCAM and NetDissect do not transfer to the coordinate-to-signal paradigm of INRs, leaving a significant gap in understanding how these networks actually encode the visual signals they represent.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240606/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a trained INR (an FFN or NeRV) along with the image or video it was fitted to (resized to 128×256) and produces a per-neuron contribution map of shape h×w for every neuron in every layer, collectively forming the 'implicit neural canvas.' For NeRV, the contribution maps account for PixelShuffle upsampling and convolutional kernel neighborhoods, so each map reflects the true spatial footprint of a kernel's influence on the final RGB output rather than a single point. The analysis pipeline then consumes these maps to compute variance metrics across instance, RGB, Gabor, and gridcell groupings, track temporal contribution changes, and generate UMAP projections of neuron embeddings.

## Method

XINC computes each neuron's contribution to every output pixel by tracing its activation (weight × input) forward through all subsequent layers. For MLP-based INRs (FFN), this is a straightforward product of the neuron's scalar output with its outgoing weights, summed over all paths to the output. For CNN-based INRs (NeRV), the method treats each convolutional kernel as a neuron, handles PixelShuffle by repeating the mapping r² times and applying a per-block mask so that each r×r spatial block retains contributions from a single filter, and applies a k×k box filter (proximity correction) to account for the fact that intermediate feature-map points feed into neighborhoods in the next layer. The resulting contribution maps are then analyzed through several lenses: variance of normalized contribution deltas across instance/RGB/Gabor/gridcell clusters to quantify what the network attends to, temporal tracking of per-instance contributions to isolate motion-driven changes, and Gabor-filter-based k-dimensional neuron embeddings projected via UMAP to reveal functional neuron types.

## Results

XINC reveals that INR neurons exhibit high contribution variance when pixels are grouped by object instances, RGB color clusters, and Gabor edge clusters, but very low variance for spatial gridcell groupings, indicating INRs are largely space-agnostic while biased toward color and edge features. Representation is highly distributed: nearly 50% of FFN Layer 2 neurons have almost zero contribution to any pixel, and at the 50th-percentile threshold, a large fraction of neurons contribute meaningfully to each pixel. In NeRV, contribution fluctuations across video frames are strongly correlated with optical flow, showing that motion (not object identity) drives temporal changes in neuron contributions. UMAP projection of Gabor-clustered contribution vectors reveals distinct neuron 'types' organized by edge-response characteristics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an interpretability paper that does not propose a new INR architecture or compare against competing methods on a standard benchmark. Instead, it applies the XINC analysis framework to two existing INR architectures — a Fourier Feature Network (FFN, MLP-based) and NeRV (CNN-based) — trained on Cityscapes-VPS and VIPSeg videos. The paper's internal comparisons are between these two architectures (e.g., FFN shows more evenly distributed representations while NeRV shows stronger edge bias and dead neurons) and between different pixel-grouping strategies (instance masks, RGB clusters, Gabor clusters, gridcells) to quantify what features neurons attend to. No quantitative win/lose comparisons against baseline INR methods are reported, as the contribution is the analysis methodology and the qualitative insights it yields rather than a new model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the standard neuron-contribution analysis (weights × inputs) from classical interpretability tools like NetDissect and GradCAM, but adapts it to the fundamentally different coordinate-to-signal paradigm of INRs, where inputs are pixel coordinates rather than image patches. While prior work has touched on MLP-based INR interpretability and hypernetwork effects, the authors position their CNN-based INR analysis (NeRV) as first-of-its-kind, requiring novel handling of PixelShuffle upsampling and the absence of spatial information sharing. The framework thus carves new ground by bridging the gap between established interpretability techniques and the unique architectural constraints of implicit representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As INRs move toward commercial deployment in video compression, understanding their failure modes is critical for real-world adoption, and this paper provides the first systematic lens for doing so. The finding that INRs are nearly space-agnostic yet biased toward edges and colors, with motion driving temporal contribution changes, offers concrete design insights for future architectures. Open questions include whether these findings generalize to higher resolutions, 3D INRs (NeRF), and whether the distributed, low-level nature of representations can be steered toward more semantically meaningful encodings.
