## Problem

Imaging through scattering media is fundamentally limited by the destruction of high-frequency content, and while phase diversity imaging mitigates this by capturing multiple modulated measurements, the modulation patterns have traditionally been chosen randomly or via simple heuristics without explicit optimization for frequency preservation. This paper asks whether wavefront modulations can be learned end-to-end to maximize the information retained in scattered measurements.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240613/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes K=16 scattered image measurements (256×256 pixels in experiments) captured by cycling through 16 learned SLM modulation patterns, each applied to the Fourier plane of the optical system. The modulations themselves are generated by a two-layer MLP that maps a 28-channel Zernike polynomial vector to 16 phase patterns. The output is a reconstructed scene image; critically, the learned modulations are decoupled from the proxy network at deployment and can be paired with any reconstruction algorithm (e.g., the unsupervised iterative NeuWS method), making the acquisition strategy transferable across reconstruction backends.

## Method

The core contribution is an end-to-end differentiable pipeline that jointly optimizes (1) an implicit neural representation of wavefront modulations (a two-layer MLP with leaky-ReLU mapping a 28-channel Zernike vector to 16 modulation patterns) and (2) a lightweight attention U-Net proxy reconstruction network, minimizing MSE between reconstructed and ground-truth scenes over simulated scattering. The optical forward model (PSF computed from phase errors via Fourier optics) is fully differentiable, enabling gradient flow from the reconstruction loss back through the scattering model to the modulation parameters. The key insight is that the proxy network need not generalize well—it serves only as a differentiable surrogate to guide modulation optimization, bypassing the intractable cost of backpropagating through an iterative reconstruction algorithm. The learned modulations are then validated to transfer to the generalizable unsupervised NeuWS iterative method, yielding a best-of-both-worlds deployment where acquisition quality is learned but reconstruction generalizability is preserved.

## Results

In simulation (MIT Places 365, 1000 test images), learned wavefront modulations with the proxy U-Net achieve 30.39 dB PSNR and 0.9082 SSIM, outperforming random modulations (26.44 dB / 0.7980) and no modulations (25.48 dB / 0.7640) by over 3.9 dB. On real-world scattering experiments, learned modulations boost the NeuWS iterative method from 11.26 dB to 14.61 dB PSNR (static scenes) and from 8.90 dB to 12.89 dB (dynamic scenes), with SSIM improving from 0.21 to 0.38 and 0.23 to 0.33 respectively. The learned modulations also improve the proxy network on in-distribution tissue from 17.57 to 19.06 dB PSNR and on out-of-distribution targets from 9.90 to 10.71 dB.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares learned wavefront modulations against two baselines: no modulations (single unmodulated measurement) and randomly sampled modulations (the approach used by NeuWS [8]). Against NeuWS [8] with random modulations, learned modulations yield a 3.35 dB PSNR gain on static scenes (14.61 vs. 11.26) and a 3.99 dB gain on dynamic scenes (12.89 vs. 8.90), with SSIM nearly doubling from 0.21 to 0.38 (static) and 0.23 to 0.33 (dynamic). Against the no-modulation baseline, the proxy network improves from 16.53 to 19.06 dB PSNR on in-distribution tissue. The learned modulations consistently outperform random modulations across all settings—proxy network, iterative method, in-distribution, out-of-distribution, static, and dynamic—demonstrating that the optimization of modulation patterns, rather than the reconstruction algorithm itself, is the key driver of improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends phase diversity imaging (PDI) and the recent NeuWS framework [8] by replacing their randomly or heuristically chosen wavefront modulations with learned, optimized ones. The key conceptual departure is repurposing the differentiable proxy network paradigm from end-to-end optics design: rather than using a proxy to design a better algorithm, the authors use a proxy to design a better physical acquisition system (the modulation patterns). The modulations are parameterized via an implicit neural representation (MLP over Zernike coefficients), and the entire pipeline—optical forward model, modulation generation, and reconstruction—is jointly optimized end-to-end, a formulation not previously applied to scattering media.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central insight—that a proxy network which itself fails to generalize can still produce modulations that transfer to generalizable unsupervised algorithms—opens a practical path for optimizing acquisition hardware without requiring differentiability of the final reconstruction pipeline. This decoupling means the learned modulations can augment any downstream algorithm, not just the one used during training. Limitations include validation only on monochromatic (532 nm) imaging with a single optical setup; extending to broadband or multi-wavelength scenarios would require wavelength-dependent modulation optimization. The approach also suggests a broader template for learning acquisition-time parameters in other computational imaging tasks where the reconstruction algorithm is non-differentiable.
