## Problem

Existing pedestrian animation frameworks for driving simulation either follow 2D trajectories (limited to basic locomotion like walking and running) or track reference video content via per-video optimization (computationally intensive and not on-demand). This restricts the diversity and controllability of simulated pedestrian behaviors, limiting the ability to generate rich, varied motions—such as sudden gestures or upper-body actions—that autonomous vehicles must respond to.

## Input / Output

The controller receives a 2D trajectory (sampled at 0.5 s intervals, 10 steps ahead), a 32×32 local terrain height map, and a reference motion specifying joint positions, rotations, and velocities for selected body parts. A per-joint, per-timestep binary spatial-temporal mask determines which joints are actively tracked at each frame, allowing selective control (e.g., only the left arm or upper body) while the rest of the body is free. The output is a physically plausible 3D pedestrian animation (SMPL joint positions and rotations) running at 30 FPS control / 60 Hz simulation, with the lower body conforming to terrain and trajectory while masked upper-body joints follow the reference motion.

## Method

PACER+ unifies trajectory following and motion tracking into a single goal-conditioned RL policy trained with PPO and Adversarial Motion Prior (AMP). The key mechanism is a per-joint spatial-temporal mask that, during training, is randomly populated at each timestep to indicate which joints should track a reference motion sampled from AMASS, forcing the policy to learn generalizable selective tracking rather than rigid whole-body imitation. The reward combines trajectory-following error (exponential decay of root-position distance), masked motion-tracking error (joint position, rotation, and velocity terms weighted by the mask), and an AMP style reward from a discriminator trained on ~200 motion sequences. Early termination is applied when trajectory deviation exceeds 0.5 m or joint tracking error exceeds 0.3 m, stabilizing training. For real-world video scenarios, the mask is set from ViTPose confidence scores—high-confidence joints are tracked while low-confidence or occluded joints are automatically infilled by the policy without additional optimization.

## Results

On motion quality (Table 1), PACER+ achieves a FID of 6.74 vs. PACER's 7.97 and a diversity score of 1.67 vs. 1.29, with consistent gains under low-speed trajectories (l-FID 7.62 vs. 8.84). On real-world driving scenarios (Table 3), PACER+ reduces E_mpjpe to 77.67 mm and E_gmpjpe to 127.84 mm, outperforming Wang et al. [60] (89.42 / 137.84 mm) while also improving physical plausibility metrics: foot sliding drops to 7.68 mm (vs. 7.87) and foot penetration to 12.12 mm (vs. 14.21). The policy runs at >30 FPS on a single A100 GPU after ~3 days of training in Isaac Gym.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PACER+ is compared against three baselines. Against PACER [48] (its direct predecessor), PACER+ wins on both FID (6.74 vs. 7.97) and diversity (1.67 vs. 1.29) for normal-speed trajectories, and maintains the advantage under low-speed conditions (l-FID 7.62 vs. 8.84; l-Diversity 1.36 vs. 1.24). Against Wang et al. [60] (per-video optimization for driving scenarios), PACER+ achieves lower E_mpjpe (77.67 vs. 89.42 mm) and E_gmpjpe (127.84 vs. 137.84 mm) on real-world Waymo sequences, while also reducing foot sliding (7.68 vs. 7.87 mm), foot penetration (12.12 vs. 14.21 mm), velocity jitter (7.42 vs. 8.21 mm), and acceleration jitter (6.43 vs. 7.42 mm/f²) — all without any per-video optimization. Against NIKI [23] (a pure motion-capture method), PACER+ is not directly comparable on tracking metrics since NIKI does not produce physically simulated output, but NIKI's raw foot sliding (45.32 mm) and penetration (54.21 mm) are an order of magnitude worse than PACER+'s, highlighting the benefit of physics-based simulation. In the motion-tracking ablation (Table 2), PACER+ also outperforms Wang et al. [60] on whole-body tracking (E_mpjpe 72.10 vs. 80.29 mm; E_gmpjpe 123.88 vs. 137.48 mm) while uniquely supporting selective upper-body or single-arm tracking.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends PACER [48] (trajectory-following pedestrian animation) and PHC [29] (perpetual humanoid motion tracking) by unifying both tasks under a single policy network with a novel per-joint spatial-temporal mask that enables selective tracking of specific body parts. It also builds on Wang et al. [60] for real-world driving scenario motion capture, but departs from that work by eliminating the need for per-video optimization and instead achieving zero-shot recreation through the learned policy. The key conceptual departure from prior physics-based character control is the simultaneous, on-demand combination of trajectory following with partial-body motion imitation, rather than treating these as separate or sequential problems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practical gap in autonomous driving simulation where pedestrian animations are limited to basic locomotion, by enabling diverse, controllable upper-body behaviors (e.g., phone calls, waving) while maintaining physical plausibility and trajectory adherence. The zero-shot real-world video recreation capability, including automatic infilling of occluded body parts, has direct utility for populating simulation environments with realistic pedestrian behaviors without per-scene optimization. However, the approach still relies on user-provided trajectories and pre-trained motion generation models, and does not model semantic interactions between pedestrians and the driving environment, leaving open the question of whether a fully autonomous, context-aware pedestrian animation system is achievable within this framework.
