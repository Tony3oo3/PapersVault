## Problem

Despite the widespread adoption of latent diffusion models (LDMs) like Stable Diffusion and Midjourney for high-resolution image synthesis, forensic detection of LDM-generated images remains in its infancy. Existing approaches either require extensive training on generated samples or rely on computationally expensive forward and reverse diffusion processes, making them impractical for real-world deployment. This paper asks whether the autoencoder (AE) that is an inherent component of every LDM can be exploited directly for training-free detection.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image as input and produces a scalar detection score (the minimum LPIPS reconstruction error across a pool of pre-trained AEs) that can be thresholded to classify the image as real or generated. Additionally, by omitting spatial averaging in the LPIPS computation, AEROBLADE yields a spatial reconstruction error map that highlights regions with anomalously low error, enabling qualitative analysis such as localizing inpainted regions within otherwise authentic images. No resolution constraint is imposed beyond what the AE architecture requires, and the method operates on standard image sizes used in the evaluation (e.g., 512×512).

## Method

AEROBLADE exploits the observation that images generated by an LDM lie on the data manifold of its autoencoder and can therefore be reconstructed more faithfully than real images, which are mapped to the nearest manifold point. For a given image x, the reconstruction error is computed as Δ_AE_i(x) = LPIPS(x, D_i(E_i(x))), where E_i and D_i are the encoder and decoder of the i-th LDM's AE. To handle the unknown generator, the method takes the minimum error across a set of AEs: Δ_Min(x) = min_i Δ_AE_i(x), and classifies the image as generated if this value falls below a threshold. The LPIPS distance is computed using the second convolutional layer of VGG16, which the authors find captures the most discriminative fine-grained differences. The entire pipeline is training-free, requiring only the publicly available AE weights, and extends to new models simply by adding their AE to the pool without any retraining.

## Results

AEROBLADE achieves a mean AP of 0.992 across seven state-of-the-art LDMs (SD1.1, SD1.5, SD2.1, KD2.1, MJ4, MJ5, MJ5.1) using LPIPS₂ and Δ_Min, with per-dataset AP ranging from 0.978 to 0.999. On the more realistic TPR@5%FPR metric, AEROBLADE scores between 0.963 and 0.999. The method is entirely training-free, requiring only a forward pass through a pre-trained autoencoder and a single LPIPS computation, yet nearly matches the performance of Corvi et al.'s deep classifier trained on 400,000 generated images (which achieves 1.000 AP on all datasets).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AEROBLADE decisively outperforms all four other baselines on both AP and TPR@5%FPR across nearly all datasets. Against Gragnaniello et al. [13] (720K training samples), AEROBLADE gains 26–47 AP points (e.g., 0.999 vs. 0.526 on KD2.1) and 92–95 TPR@5%FPR points. Against Ojha et al. [26] (720K samples), AEROBLADE leads by 11–31 AP points and 69–79 TPR@5%FPR points. DIRE [53] (80K samples) performs at chance level (AP ≈ 0.50, TPR@5%FPR = 0.000 on all datasets), which the authors attribute to a JPEG/PNG compression inconsistency in the publicly released DIRE representations. SeDID_Stat [23], the only other training-free method, is highly inconsistent (AP 0.376–0.783) and fails on Midjourney (AP ≈ 0.40). The sole method that matches or slightly exceeds AEROBLADE is Corvi et al. [5], a deep classifier trained on 400K LDM-generated images, which achieves 1.000 AP on all datasets; however, AEROBLADE trails by at most 0.021 AP and 0.037 TPR@5%FPR while requiring zero training data or parameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

AEROBLADE carves new ground by repurposing a component of LDMs (the autoencoder) that was originally designed for computational efficiency in generation, rather than building on a prior detection method. The closest conceptual ancestor is DIRE [53], which also uses reconstruction error but requires the full DDIM forward/reverse process and a trained classifier; AEROBLADE shows that the AE alone suffices, eliminating both the expensive denoising steps and the training requirement. It also connects to the broader visual anomaly detection literature [22, 54, 25], where reconstruction residuals reveal out-of-distribution regions, but applies this principle to the specific structure of LDMs. The training-free positioning departs from the dominant paradigm of learning-based detectors (Corvi [5], Ojha [26], Gragnaniello [13]) that require extensive training data and model-specific fine-tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The training-free, modular design means that detecting a new LDM only requires adding its AE to the pool, with no retraining—this is a significant practical advantage in a landscape where new generators appear frequently. The qualitative reconstruction error maps open a path toward localizing inpainted or manipulated regions within otherwise authentic images, extending the method beyond binary detection. Key limitations include sensitivity to image perturbations (JPEG, blur, noise), reduced performance on low-complexity images, and the requirement of access to the generator's AE (though the Midjourney/SD2 similarity mitigates this for closed-source models). Open questions include whether a lightweight classifier on top of reconstruction errors could improve robustness, and whether the approach generalizes to non-LDM architectures or video generation.
