## Problem

Existing adaptive quantization methods for super-resolution networks (e.g., CADyQ, CABM) require extensive quantization-aware training with full LR-HR training pairs, taking 40–70 hours to learn bit-allocation policies. This prohibitive training cost limits the practical deployment of adaptive quantization, as it demands full dataset access and long compute budgets that are often unavailable in real-world settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241909/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a pre-trained 32-bit SR network (EDSR, SRResNet, or RDN at ×4 scale) and a small set of 100 calibration LR images sampled from DIV2K—no ground-truth HR images are required. At inference, a low-resolution input image is processed through the adaptively quantized network to produce a high-resolution output, with per-image bit-widths dynamically adjusted based on the image's complexity. Large test images (e.g., 4K) are split into 96×96 patches for processing.

## Method

The core insight is that image-wise and layer-wise quantization sensitivities are statistically independent (verified via cosine similarity of per-layer MSE across images), allowing the bit-allocation problem to decompose into two simple threshold-based mapping modules rather than K×M per-layer policies. The Image-to-Bit (I2B) module maps an input image's complexity (average gradient density) to a bit factor in {−1, 0, +1} via two learned thresholds, while the Layer-to-Bit (L2B) module maps each layer's activation standard deviation to a bit factor in {−1, 0, +1} via two sensitivity thresholds; the final bit-width is b_base + b_I^j + b_L^k. Both mappings are calibrated from percentile statistics of 100 LR calibration images and then fine-tuned for 10 epochs using STE for rounding, a tanh surrogate for the non-differentiable thresholding, and a bit-width regularization loss—requiring no HR supervision. A bit-aware clipping step (inspired by OMSE) further adjusts activation clipping ranges to minimize L2 distance at each layer's assigned bit-width, and an alternating update schedule (bit mappings → weight clipping → activation clipping) achieves convergence within seconds.

## Results

On EDSR (×4) with 8/6-bit weight/activation quantization, AdaBM achieves 25.96 dB PSNR / 0.782 SSIM on Urban100 with a feature average bit-width (FAB) of 5.7, matching CADyQ (25.98/0.784, FAB 6.6) while using fewer bits. In the more aggressive 4/4-bit setting, AdaBM reaches 25.49/0.759 (FAB 4.3), outperforming both CADyQ (25.12/0.753, FAB 4.9) and CABM (24.98/0.746, FAB 4.4) on Urban100. The key advantage is processing time: AdaBM completes calibration and fine-tuning in 71 seconds versus 40 hours (CADyQ) and 70 hours (CABM), a ~2000× speedup, while requiring only 100 LR calibration images without ground-truth HR pairs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the two adaptive QAT baselines (CADyQ and CABM), AdaBM achieves comparable or better PSNR at lower FAB in most settings: on EDSR 4/4-bit, AdaBM gains +0.37 dB over CADyQ and +0.51 dB over CABM on Urban100 while using fewer average bits (FAB 4.3 vs 4.9 and 4.4). On SRResNet 4/4-bit, AdaBM is slightly behind CABM by 0.10 dB on Urban100 (25.32 vs 25.42) but uses a comparable FAB (4.2 vs 3.8). Against static PTQ baselines (MinMax+FT, Percentile+FT, PTQ4SR) on EDSR 4/4-bit, AdaBM outperforms PTQ4SR by +0.51 dB on Set5 (31.02 vs 30.51) and +0.19 dB on Urban100 (25.11 vs 24.92) at lower FAB (3.8 vs 4.0). On RDN 4/4-bit, AdaBM leads PTQ4SR by +0.39 dB on Set5 (28.71 vs 28.32) and +0.07 dB on Urban100 (23.38 vs 23.31). The dominant advantage across all comparisons is processing time: 71–92 seconds versus 40–93 hours for QAT-based methods, with no requirement for ground-truth HR images.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adaptive quantization line for SR networks initiated by CADyQ (which trains MLPs to predict per-layer quantization function probabilities) and CABM (which builds lookup tables from trained MLPs). The key conceptual departure is the observation that image-wise and layer-wise quantization sensitivities are approximately independent (validated via cosine similarity analysis), allowing the K×M joint bit-allocation problem to decompose into just two simple threshold-based mapping policies. For clipping range optimization, the paper adapts OMSE's L2-minimization approach to handle variable bit-widths per layer, and uses STE for backpropagation through non-differentiable rounding, following standard PTQ practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ×2000 reduction in processing time (from 40–70 hours to ~71 seconds) and the elimination of ground-truth HR images make adaptive quantization practical for real-world deployment pipelines where full training pairs are unavailable or training budgets are tight. The simplicity of the two-threshold mapping (vs. learned MLPs or lookup tables) also reduces inference overhead. Limitations include the restriction to bit factors in {−1, 0, 1}, evaluation only at scale 4 on CNN-based SR models, and slightly lower accuracy than QAT-based methods in most settings. Open questions include whether the independence assumption holds for transformer-based SR or other restoration tasks, and whether finer-grained bit factors would close the remaining accuracy gap.
