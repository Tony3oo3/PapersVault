## Problem

Existing transfer learning evaluation metrics (LogME, GBC, LEEP, H-score, NCE) are designed for classification tasks and do not account for the fundamental differences in retrieval: the asymmetric distribution of relevant versus irrelevant document embeddings, the query-dependent nature of distributional discrepancy, and the long-tail frequency of queries. Without a retrieval-specific metric, practitioners must fine-tune every candidate pretrained model on the target dataset to compare them, which is prohibitively expensive. This paper asks whether one can predict post-fine-tuning retrieval performance directly from pretrained embeddings, without any fine-tuning.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pretrained (unfine-tuned) query and document embeddings together with binary relevance labels, requiring 5 positive and 50 negative documents per query by default. Before MMD computation, embeddings are projected via PCA (typically retaining 90% explained variance) to mitigate the curse of dimensionality. The output is a single scalar RetMMD score per model–dataset pair that ranks models by predicted retrieval performance; a variant (RetMMD-C) outputs a count of statistically significant two-sample tests instead of a raw MMD value, enabling cross-kernel comparison. For dataset transferability, the input shifts to per-query mean embeddings of relevant documents from source and target datasets, producing a distribution distance that correlates with relative fine-tuning gain.

## Method

The key insight is that fine-tuning a retrieval model can be interpreted as a kernel map φ from the pretrained embedding space X into an RKHS H, so that cosine-similarity retrieval in H corresponds to a kernel evaluation in X. RetMMD computes the unbiased MMD between the positive and negative document embedding distributions for each query using a chosen kernel (linear, RBF, polynomial, or cosine), then averages these per-query discrepancies weighted by inverse query frequency to handle long-tail distributions. Kernel selection is automated via a two-sample permutation test (counting significant p-values across queries) or an SVM classifier on positive/negative embeddings, with the kernel maximizing the count of significant tests chosen as default. The approach requires no fine-tuning, no gradient computation, and no pseudo-labels, making it orders of magnitude faster than actually fine-tuning candidate models. A companion dataset-transferability metric computes MMD (or 1-Wasserstein) distance between collections of per-query mean positive-document embeddings from source and target datasets, which empirically correlates with the relative retrieval gain from pretraining on the source before fine-tuning on the target.

## Results

The primary evaluation metric is the weighted Kendall rank correlation between the transferability score and actual retrieval performance (e.g., R@1, MAP@1) after fine-tuning, across seven datasets spanning image, text, and multimodal domains. RetMMD-M achieves the highest correlation on 6 of 7 datasets, reaching 0.94 on NFCorpus, 0.90 on CUB200, 0.89 on CARS196, and a perfect 1.0 on MS-COCO. The only dataset where RetMMD-M is outperformed is FEVER, where GBC achieves 0.67 versus RetMMD-M's 0.52. LogME exhibits negative correlations (i.e., anti-predictive) on four of seven datasets, including NFCorpus (−0.60) and COCO (−0.09).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares RetMMD against two existing transferability metrics that can operate on document embeddings: LogME (logistic-regression-based) and GBC (Bhattacharyya-class-separability). RetMMD-M outperforms both baselines on 6 of 7 datasets. Against GBC, the margin is largest on FiQA-2018 (0.80 vs. 0.33) and COCO (1.00 vs. −0.72), where GBC is actually anti-correlated with retrieval performance. Against LogME, the advantage is even more pronounced: LogME yields negative correlations on NFCorpus (−0.60), FEVER (−0.12), FiQA-2018 (−0.35), and COCO (−0.09), meaning it systematically ranks models in the wrong order on those datasets. The sole exception is FEVER, where GBC (0.67) edges out RetMMD-M (0.52), though both remain positive. The paper notes that other common transferability metrics (LEEP, NCE, H-Score) are designed for classification and require pseudo-labels, making them inapplicable to retrieval settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) and kernel methods—originally developed for two-sample testing and domain adaptation—to construct a transferability metric specifically for retrieval tasks, a setting where existing metrics (LogME, GBC, LEEP, H-score, NCE) are designed around classification assumptions and do not directly apply. The key conceptual move is framing fine-tuning as a mapping from a pretrained embedding space into an RKHS, so that the cosine-similarity-based relevance scoring used in retrieval naturally corresponds to a kernel evaluation on pretrained embeddings. For dataset transferability, the paper adapts the relative-drop formulation of Alvarez-Melis and Fusi (2020) from classification to retrieval by computing distribution distances over per-query mean embeddings of relevant documents rather than individual samples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value is clear: RetMMD can rank candidate pretrained models for a retrieval task without any fine-tuning, saving substantial GPU time (demonstrated on SOP with ViT models), which is especially relevant as retrieval-augmented generation and large-scale search systems multiply the number of model-selection decisions. However, the current formulation treats all relevant documents as equally important and assumes retrieval relies on embedding similarity, leaving open the question of how to extend the metric to rank-aware or hybrid retrieval pipelines. The cross-modal consistency (image, text, multimodal) is encouraging but the strongest correlations (e.g., 1.0 on COCO) rest on only four models, and the method's sensitivity to kernel choice—requiring a separate selection procedure per model—adds practical overhead that limits its turnkey applicability.
