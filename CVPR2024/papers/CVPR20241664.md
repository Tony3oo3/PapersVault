## Problem

Specialist interactive segmentation models achieve high quality but suffer from high latency (re-encoding the image per prompt update) and limited prompt diversity, while generalist models like SAM offer low latency and diverse prompts yet lag behind in segmentation quality despite being trained on ×100 more masks. The paper identifies that the sparse representation of visual prompts in generalist models is the key architectural difference that compromises high-quality segmentation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241664/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 1024×1024 RGB image (encoded once, offline) and visual prompts—clicks, boxes, polygons, scribbles, or masks—unified into a 3-channel dense map at the same spatial resolution (channel 1: positive clicks/scribbles, channel 2: negative clicks/scribbles, channel 3: mask prompt). An optional text prompt is encoded separately via CLIP. The output is a full-resolution binary segmentation mask produced by a lightweight decoder with a simple feature pyramid and two MLP layers.

## Method

SegNext reintroduces the dense prompt representation and fusion paradigm from specialist models into a generalist architecture. A 3-channel dense map (1024×1024) encodes all five visual prompt types and is passed through a convolutional patch-embedding layer; the resulting embeddings are element-wise added to the ViT-B/16 (MAE-pretrained) image embeddings, followed by one or two self-attention blocks for dense fusion. Text prompts are handled sparsely via CLIP vectors queried through SAM-style cross-attention blocks. Training uses only simulated clicks (random + iterative strategies following RITM) with normalized focal loss on COCO+LVIS for 90 epochs, then a single fine-tuning epoch on HQSeg-44K; the model generalizes to unseen prompt types (boxes, scribbles, polygons, masks) at inference without additional training.

## Results

On HQSeg-44K, the best SegNext model (SA×2, fine-tuned on HQSeg-44K) achieves 91.75 5-mIoU, 5.32 NoC90, 9.42 NoC95, and 583 NoF95, outperforming HQ-SAM (89.85 5-mIoU) and all specialist baselines. On DAVIS, it reaches 91.87 5-mIoU and 4.43 NoC90, slightly surpassing HQ-SAM (91.77 5-mIoU, 5.26 NoC90). The SAT latency is 17.6 s, substantially lower than specialist models (SimpleClick: 70.5 s, FocalClick: 51.0 s) while being trained on COCO+LVIS (×100 fewer masks than SA-1B used by SAM).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against specialist models, SegNext (SA×2 + HQ) achieves the best 5-mIoU on HQSeg-44K (91.75 vs. FocalClick 85.45, SimpleClick 85.11, InterFormer 82.62, RITM 77.72) while reducing SAT latency from 51–70 s to 17.6 s. On DAVIS, it matches or slightly exceeds FocalClick (91.87 vs. 91.22 5-mIoU) and SimpleClick (91.87 vs. 90.73) with far lower latency. Against generalist models, SegNext outperforms SAM (91.75 vs. 86.16 on HQSeg-44K; 91.87 vs. 90.95 on DAVIS) and HQ-SAM (91.75 vs. 89.85 on HQSeg-44K; 91.87 vs. 91.77 on DAVIS) despite being trained on ×100 fewer masks than SA-1B. The NoC90 metric on DAVIS shows the clearest advantage: SegNext needs only 4.43 clicks vs. 5.26 for HQ-SAM and 5.14 for SAM. The trade-off is that SegNext's SAT latency (17.6 s) is roughly 2× that of SAM (7.0 s) and HQ-SAM (8.3 s), due to the additional self-attention fusion blocks. On out-of-domain medical datasets (ssTEM, BraTS), SegNext (SA×2) achieves 92.87 and 87.29 10-mIoU respectively, competitive with specialist models that use test-time zoom-in, without any such strategy.

## Relation to Prior Work

SegNext bridges the architectural divide between specialist interactive segmentation models (RITM, FocalClick, SimpleClick) and generalist models (SAM, HQ-SAM). It adopts SAM's decoupled design—encoding the image once and fusing prompts separately—and SimpleClick's lightweight decoder with MAE-pretrained ViT initialization, but departs from both by reintroducing the dense, spatially-aligned representation of visual prompts that specialist models use. The paper's central argument is that SAM's sparse treatment of visual prompts (interpolated positional embeddings or sampled feature vectors) discards the fine-grained spatial information that specialist models preserve through dense maps, and this loss—not the data scale—explains why SAM lags behind specialist models in high-quality segmentation despite 100× more training masks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that dense prompt representation is the critical architectural lever for high-quality interactive segmentation offers a clear design principle for future generalist models, potentially unifying the quality of specialist approaches with the prompt diversity and low latency of generalist ones. However, several open questions remain: whether training with diverse prompt types (rather than clicks only) further improves generalization, whether a downsampled dense map can reduce the computational cost, and whether the approach scales to larger backbones (ViT-Large/Huge) or benefits from the SA-1B-scale data. The method still struggles with thin structures and cluttered occlusions, and the text-prompt integration remains tentative, limiting its practical versatility as a true multi-modal foundation model.
