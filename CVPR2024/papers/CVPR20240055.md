## Problem

Self-supervised audiovisual representation learning typically assumes a one-to-one correspondence between a visual scene and its audio, yet real-world scenes can look nearly identical while containing entirely different speech content. This 'looking similar, sounding different' counterfactual structure acts as a confounder in cross-modal contrastive learning, where linguistically different but otherwise similar audio-video pairs are treated as negatives, potentially degrading learned representations. The impact of such speech variation on audiovisual representation quality had not been previously studied.

## Input / Output

The model consumes temporally segmented video snippets and corresponding audio tracks (as mel spectrograms) from long-form movie and TV content, with each title providing up to seven language-specific audio tracks (EN, ES, FR, JA, DE, IT, KO). During training, dub-augmentation pairs the same video with audio from a different language track, so the two audio views in a quadruplet differ only in spoken language while sharing all non-speech content. After pretraining, the projection heads are discarded and spatio-temporal mean pooling yields a single d-dimensional embedding per modality for downstream probing.

## Method

The core idea is to exploit movie dubs as natural counterfactual cross-modal pairs within a SimCLR-style cross-modal contrastive framework. For each training snippet, the method constructs quadruplets (v_p, a_p, v_s, a_s) where video views are spatio-temporal augmentations and audio views may come from different language dubs of the same scene, ensuring a_p and a_s are similar except in spoken language. Hard negatives are mined by sampling multiple snippets from the same long-form title within each mini-batch, exploiting shared characters, settings, and aesthetics. The cross-modal contrastive loss (Eq. 2) aggregates over both (v_p, a_s) and (v_s, a_p) pairs, implicitly teaching the model to treat language-different audio as positive for the same video. The architecture uses MViT-S for video and a spectrogram-adapted variant for audio, trained from scratch on ~59K video-hours, with optional within-modal loss terms (λ_v, λ_a) explored in ablations.

## Results

On the HEAR benchmark, the best audio model (B.3) achieves 85.60% top-1 accuracy on ESC-50, 53.69 mAP on FSD50K, and 100.0% on GTZAN Music/Speech, all surpassing the top self-supervised leaderboard entries (80.50%, 50.88 mAP, 96.86% respectively). On the LVU video benchmark, the best video model (B.5) sets new state-of-the-art on Genre (64.29% top-1) and Speaking Style (47.62%), and achieves SOTA on Director (69.16%) and Year (55.32%) when excluding Movies2Scenes which uses task-specific movie metadata. Across 15 downstream tasks spanning sound classification, paralinguistic speech, semantic speech, language identification, and long-form video understanding, the dub-augmented models consistently outperform their non-dub-augmented counterparts (e.g., B.4 vs B.3) on non-linguistic tasks while showing only modest degradation on language-sensitive tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GURA (best average HEAR performer), the paper's B.3 model wins on ESC-50 (85.60 vs 74.35), FSD50K (53.69 vs 41.32), LibriCount (69.16 vs 68.34), and GTZAN (100.0 vs 93.59), but loses on CREMA-D (62.79 vs 75.21), Speech Commands (83.82 vs 94.68), and VoxLingua (30.35 vs 71.40). Against PaSST, the paper wins on LibriCount (69.16 vs 66.01), CREMA-D (63.27 vs 61.04), GTZAN (100.0 vs 97.69), Speech Commands (83.82 vs 63.87), and VoxLingua (30.35 vs 25.93), but loses on ESC-50 (85.60 vs 94.75) and FSD50K (53.69 vs 64.09). Against CLAP, the paper is competitive on GTZAN (100.0 tie) but trails on most other HEAR tasks. On LVU, the paper achieves new SOTA on Genre (64.29 vs 58.97 SCALE, 56.62 STCA) and Speaking Style (47.62 vs 42.27 SCALE, 41.62 STCA), and beats all non-metadata baselines on Director (69.16 vs 66.70 STCA) and Year (55.32 vs 53.30 STCA), though it trails Movies2Scenes on Director (69.16 vs 70.90) and Relation (60.98 vs 71.20) where metadata supervision is available.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the cross-modal contrastive learning framework of SimCLR [14] and the movie-based negative sampling strategy of Kalayeh et al. [37] by introducing dub-augmentation: leveraging multilingual dubbed audio tracks as natural counterfactual pairs where the same visual scene is paired with audio differing only in spoken language. Conceptually, it reframes the standard audio-video alignment problem by explicitly accounting for the 'looking similar, sounding different' structure inherent in real-world scenes, which prior contrastive approaches treat as noise or confounders. The synthetic counterfactual pipeline (Demucs → Whisper → SeamlessM4T → LVC-VC) repurposes existing speech processing tools to generate such pairs for arbitrary clips, extending the idea beyond the proprietary Netflix catalog.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that dub-augmentation consistently improves non-linguistic auditory and audiovisual tasks while only modestly degrading linguistic performance validates the hypothesis that speech variation is a confounding factor in scene-level representation learning, and provides a practical, scalable augmentation technique for the field. The open-sourced synthetic pipeline and the LVU-M dataset lower the barrier for the community to study counterfactual cross-modal structure without access to proprietary dub catalogs. Key open questions include whether the approach generalizes to other forms of counterfactual variation (e.g., music, ambient sound), whether the modest linguistic trade-off becomes problematic under fine-tuning rather than probing, and whether the benefits persist at even larger scales or with different backbone architectures.
