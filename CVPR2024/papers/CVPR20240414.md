## Problem

Quality-fitting-based FIQA methods rely on quality anchors from a face recognition model but assume all anchors carry equal confidence, even when intra-class quality variation causes certain classes to receive systematically inflated or deflated anchors. This leads to a fitting bottleneck where the FIQA model over-trusts inaccurate supervision. Additionally, existing deep FIQA approaches do not fully exploit objective quality factors (blur, pose, expression, occlusion, illumination) during training to either guide learning or correct anchor reliability.

## Input / Output

The method takes a 112×112 aligned face image (5-landmark aligned) and a structured textual prompt encoding five quality-factor labels (blur: hazy/blurry/clear; pose: profile/slight angle/frontal; occlusion: obstructed/unobstructed; expression: exaggerated/typical; lighting: extreme/normal) plus a five-level Likert quality descriptor (bad/poor/fair/good/perfect). At inference, the image is matched against all possible textual expressions to produce a quality score in [0,1] computed as the expectation over five fixed anchors {0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9} weighted by the predicted quality distribution.

## Method

CLIB-FIQA builds on CLIP (ResNet50 image encoder + frozen language encoder) and trains in two stages. In stage 1 (5 epochs), a joint learning objective simultaneously fits the quality distribution to CR-FIQA-style angular-space anchors via Earth Mover's Distance and classifies the five quality factors via Focal Loss, with both signals derived by marginalizing a single joint distribution P(S_i|x_i) computed as a temperature-scaled softmax over cosine similarities between the image embedding and all possible text embeddings. In stage 2 (20 epochs), a learned MLP maps the joint factor distribution (R^{3×2×3×2×2}) to a merged-factor distribution (R^5) aligned with the quality distribution's topology; the Jensen-Shannon divergence between these two distributions is passed through a sigmoid to yield a confidence score ρ_i ∈ [0.5, 1], which modulates the shape parameter β of the soft-mapping function to produce a confidence-calibrated quality distribution for continued training. The key mechanism is that when objective quality factors contradict the recognition-model anchor, the confidence drops, reducing the model's reliance on potentially inaccurate supervision and alleviating the fitting bottleneck.

## Results

Under the ArcFace deployment model, CLIB-FIQA achieves an average pAUC of 0.648 and AUC of 0.398 at FMR=1E-3 across eight benchmarks, reducing the average pAUC by 1.81% and AUC by 7.6% compared to the best competitor (CR-FIQA: 0.660 pAUC, 0.431 AUC). Under CosFace, the average AUC drops to 0.419 (vs. CR-FIQA's 0.439, a 3.34% reduction), and under AdaFace, CLIB-FIQA reaches 0.664 pAUC and 0.412 AUC, again outperforming all baselines. The method shows its largest advantage on TinyFace (extreme low-resolution faces), where its EVRC curve significantly deviates from all other methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLIB-FIQA outperforms all eight baselines on average pAUC and AUC across all three deployed recognition models (ArcFace, CosFace, AdaFace). The closest competitor is CR-FIQA [8], which CLIB-FIQA beats by 1.81% in average pAUC (0.648 vs. 0.660) and 7.6% in average AUC (0.398 vs. 0.431) under ArcFace. Among unsupervised methods, SER-FIQ [56] and MagFace [38] are the strongest, but CLIB-FIQA still leads them by 16.6% and 14.5% in average pAUC respectively. Quality-fitting methods like FaceQnet [21], PCNet [64], and SDD-FIQA [44] all trail CLIB-FIQA by 13–23% in average pAUC. The gap is most pronounced on challenging datasets: on TinyFace, CLIB-FIQA achieves 0.827 pAUC vs. 0.900 (FaceQnet) and 0.849 (CR-FIQA), and on CFP-FP it reaches 0.303 vs. 0.315 (CR-FIQA) and 0.345 (FaceQAN). On the standard LFW dataset, performance is comparable across methods, with CLIB-FIQA showing a slight rebound when RUI exceeds 80%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the quality-fitting-based FIQA lineage (FaceQnet, PCNet, SDD-FIQA, CR-FIQA) by replacing the fine-tuned recognition-model regression head with a CLIP-based vision-language model, and by introducing multi-quality-factor joint learning as an auxiliary training signal. It departs from prior methods' assumption that all quality anchors from the recognition model carry equal reliability, instead proposing a confidence calibration step that uses the Jensen-Shannon divergence between a merged-factor distribution (learned from objective quality factors) and the predicted quality distribution to down-weight unreliable anchors. The quality anchor computation itself follows CR-FIQA's angular-space similarity ratio, and the soft-mapping to a five-level Likert distribution draws on prior FIQA work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The confidence-calibration framing addresses a real limitation in quality-fitting-based FIQA: when intra-class quality variation is low, the recognition model produces inflated anchors that the FIQA model over-trusts, creating a fitting bottleneck. Demonstrating that a frozen CLIP backbone with multimodal quality-factor supervision can match or exceed methods that fine-tune the recognition model itself suggests a more modular and potentially more generalizable training paradigm for FIQA. Open questions include whether the approach scales to more quality factors or continuous (rather than discrete) factor representations, and whether the modest 1–3% gains over CR-FIQA would widen with larger training sets or more diverse quality-factor taxonomies.
