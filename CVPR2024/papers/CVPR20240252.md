## Problem

Multi-view perception systems process all N camera views, incurring computational costs that are prohibitive on resource-constrained end devices. Existing efficiency strategies (lower resolution, lighter backbones) sacrifice accuracy, while heuristic next-best-view policies (occlusion-based, FoV-maximizing) ignore the specific target object or task. This paper asks whether a learned, task-aware policy can select only the 2–3 most informative views from N, preserving accuracy while cutting computation proportionally.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240252/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The system receives N RGB images from a fixed camera layout (224×224 for classification, 720×1280 for detection) and the known camera poses. It begins with a randomly chosen initial view, then sequentially selects T−1 additional views (T=2 for classification, T=3 for detection) via the MVSelect policy. The final output is a class label (classification) or a BEV occupancy heatmap (detection), computed only over the T selected views using the standard max-pooling aggregation and task head. The MVSelect module itself is extremely lightweight, sharing the ResNet-18 feature backbone with the task network and adding only a few learnable layers.

## Method

MVSelect is a two-branch controller: a camera branch expands one-hot encodings of already-selected views into learnable camera embeddings (encoding layout prior), and a feature branch processes the max-pooled observation features of those views. The two hidden vectors are combined to produce Q-values over all N candidate cameras, from which the next view is chosen via argmax (or ε-greedy during training). Because unselected views cannot back-propagate gradients, the selection is formulated as a reinforcement learning problem: the state is ⟨one-hot(selected cameras), max-pooled features⟩, the action is the next camera index, and the reward is the final task accuracy (or MODA) given only at the last step. Training uses Q-learning with temporal-difference targets, and the module can be jointly trained with the task network (MVCNN or MVDet) so that the backbone adapts to the reduced view count. The overall design is inspired by CPU instruction pipelining: multi-view computation is broken into sequential steps where only the chosen view is processed, while GPU parallelism across multiple objects is preserved, yielding roughly T/N of the original FLOPs.

## Results

On multi-view classification with T=2 views, MVSelect with joint training achieves 94.3% accuracy on ModelNet40 (12 views) and 94.4% on ModelNet40 (20 views), within 0.2% and 2.1% of the full N-view MVCNN baselines (94.5% and 96.5%), respectively. On multi-view detection with T=3 views, joint training yields 88.6% MODA on Wildtrack and 88.1% on MultiviewX, compared to 90.0% and 93.0% for the full N-view MVDet. Computationally, using 2 out of 20 views reduces FLOPs from 36.5G to 3.6G (≈1/10) and increases throughput from 119.6 to 361.8 instances/s (3.03× speedup).

## Baselines & Comparisons

For multi-view classification with a fixed task network (T=2), MVSelect substantially outperforms existing next-best-view methods: it beats SEE++ [3] by 9.0% (88.2 vs. 79.2 on ModelNet40 12-view) and Potthast & Sukhatme [29] by 11.7% (88.2 vs. 76.5), while also exceeding the dataset-level oracle (85.2) and validation-best policy (85.1). With joint training, MVSelect nearly matches the full N-view system (94.3% vs. 94.5% on ModelNet40 12-view, a 0.2% gap) while using only 2 of 12 views. For multi-view detection (T=3), MVSelect with joint training achieves 88.6% MODA on Wildtrack, trailing the full MVDet (90.0%) by 1.4% and MVDeTr (91.5%) by 2.9%, but surpassing the max-FoV heuristic [35] (78.0%) by 10.6% and SEE++ [3] (76.1%) by 12.5%. On MultiviewX, the joint-training result (88.1%) is 4.9% below MVDeTr (93.7%) but still exceeds all NBV-based baselines. The instance-level oracle (96.5% on ModelNet40 12-view) remains above MVSelect, confirming that per-instance view selection is the key advantage over fixed heuristics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multi-view recognition and detection architectures (MVCNN for classification, MVDet for BEV detection) by introducing a reinforcement-learning-based view selection module (MVSelect) that computes only T < N views rather than all N. Conceptually, it bridges active vision / next-best-view planning (previously studied with 3D sensors for reconstruction) to RGB-camera recognition and detection, where prior view-selection work (RotationNet's random subsets, MVTN's point-cloud-based estimation) either assumed moving cameras or required 3D inputs unavailable in fixed multi-camera RGB systems. The CPU pipelining analogy motivates the sequential, step-by-step selection strategy, and Q-learning with temporal-difference targets handles the non-differentiable nature of discrete view selection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for edge-deployed multi-camera systems: using 2–3 views instead of N yields roughly 1.7×–3× throughput gains while joint training recovers near-full-view accuracy (within 0.2–2.1% for classification, and exceeding the fixed-network instance-level oracle for detection). A notable limitation is that for detection, MVSelect loses its per-instance advantage over a dataset-level oracle because it cannot reason about areas outside the current FoV, suggesting the approach is better suited to tasks where the target is fully observable in at least one view. Open questions include scaling to larger camera arrays, handling moving-camera or dynamic-layout scenarios, and whether the learned selection policies can inform hardware-level camera layout optimization.
