## Problem

Hyperspectral 3D imaging requires simultaneous depth and per-pixel spectral information, but existing systems are either prohibitively expensive and bulky (CASSI, liquid-crystal tunable filters) or sacrifice accuracy, with the best affordable method achieving only 83 nm spectral FWHM. The paper addresses the gap of achieving high spectral resolution and sub-millimeter depth accuracy in a compact, low-cost projector-camera setup.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241095/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system projects 40 binary-code patterns (for depth) and 318 scanline patterns (for hyperspectral) through a sub-millimeter diffraction grating film onto a scene, capturing the reflected light with a standard RGB camera (FLIR GS3-U3-32S4C-C). The output is a depth map and a 46-channel hyperspectral image spanning 430–660 nm at 5 nm intervals, reconstructed per pixel. The entire capture takes approximately 10 minutes, limiting the system to static scenes, and the working depth range is under one meter due to low first-order diffraction intensity.

## Method

The core mechanism places a diffraction grating film (~10 USD) in front of a conventional RGB projector, causing first-order diffraction to spatially disperse projected light by wavelength while zero-order diffraction passes through undistorted. For depth, standard binary-code structured light decoding operates on zero-order diffraction via perspective triangulation. For hyperspectral reconstruction, scanline patterns exploit first-order diffraction to produce narrow-band illumination at each scene point; a per-pixel optimization (Equation 13) inverts the dispersive image formation model across both zero- and first-order diffractions, with a spectral smoothness prior as regularization. The first-order correspondence between camera and projector pixels is modeled data-drivenly (exponential depth dependency plus linear spatial/spectral interpolation) rather than solved analytically, achieving 1 px mean reprojection error in O(1) time. This yields 18.8 nm FWHM spectral accuracy and 1 mm depth error, substantially outperforming the 83 nm FWHM of prior projector-camera methods.

## Results

DSL achieves a spectral FWHM of 18.8 nm across 46 channels (430–660 nm at 5 nm intervals) and an average depth error of 1 mm (relative) / 1.35 mm (absolute) on a translation stage. An ablation removing first-order diffractions degrades spectral FWHM to 50 nm, confirming the critical role of dispersed light. The system requires only a ~10 USD sub-millimeter diffraction grating film added to a conventional projector-camera setup, with a capture time of approximately 10 minutes per scene.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole direct baseline is Li et al. [21] (Pro-Cam SSFM, ICCV 2019), the state-of-the-art practical hyperspectral 3D imaging method using a projector-camera setup with trichromatic primaries. DSL outperforms it by a wide margin on spectral accuracy: 18.8 nm FWHM versus 83 nm FWHM on nine 10-nm bandpass filters (Figure 7), a 4.4× improvement. This gap is attributed to DSL's exploitation of first-order diffractions, which provide narrow-band illumination per wavelength, whereas Li et al. rely on the broad RGB emission spectra of the projector. DSL additionally reports a concrete depth error of 1 mm (relative) and 1.35 mm (absolute), while Li et al. do not emphasize a comparable depth metric in the comparison. The ablation (removing first-order terms) drops FWHM to 50 nm, aligning with Li et al.'s performance level and confirming that the spectral gain is specifically due to the dispersive component.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends structured-light 3D imaging by augmenting a conventional projector-camera setup with a diffraction grating film, repurposing the well-known physics of wavelength-dependent diffraction for simultaneous depth and spectral estimation. It builds on binary-code structured light for depth (Geng [9]) and the spectral smoothness prior from prism-based hyperspectral imaging (Baek [3]), but departs from prior practical hyperspectral 3D methods (e.g., Li et al. [21]) that rely on broadband trichromatic projector primaries and are limited to ~83 nm FWHM. The key architectural departure is exploiting zero-order diffraction for depth triangulation and first-order diffractions for narrow-band spectral illumination within a single structured-light framework, rather than using CASSI, tunable filters, or DOE-based PSF analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DSL demonstrates that a sub-millimeter, ~$10 diffraction grating film can upgrade a standard structured-light system to achieve 18.8 nm spectral FWHM and 1 mm depth error, making accurate hyperspectral 3D imaging accessible for applications in cultural heritage, geology, and food science without expensive instrumentation. Key limitations include a ~10-minute capture time restricting use to static scenes and a working depth range under one meter due to low first-order diffraction intensity. Open questions include whether joint depth-spectrum reconstruction or differentiable optimization of the grating design could address these constraints, and whether the approach extends to infrared or other spectral bands.
