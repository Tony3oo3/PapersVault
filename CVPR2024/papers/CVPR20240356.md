## Problem

Display photometric stereo uses a monitor as a programmable illumination source, offering a large design space for illumination patterns, yet existing methods rely on heuristically designed patterns (OLAT, complementary, gradient) that yield sub-optimal normal reconstruction. No prior work has learned display patterns end-to-end for a specific display-camera system to maximize reconstruction accuracy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240356/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes K display patterns (RGB intensity maps over a 16×9 superpixel grid, each superpixel spanning 240×240 pixels on a curved 4K LCD) and a polarization camera's four-angle captures (0°, 45°, 90°, 135°) per pattern. These are decomposed via Stokes-vector analysis into diffuse and specular components; only the diffuse images feed into reconstruction. The output is a per-pixel surface normal map, with the scene assumed to lie on a plane at a fixed 50 cm distance from the camera.

## Method

DDPS couples a differentiable basis-illumination image formation (I_i = Σ_j B_j · M_{i,j}, a weighted sum of pre-captured basis images by pattern superpixel intensities) with an analytic trinocular photometric-stereo reconstructor (N = (ρ⊙Ml)†I via pseudo-inverse) into a single differentiable chain. Display patterns are optimized end-to-end with Adam using a normalized cosine-distance loss against ground-truth normals from 3D-printed objects, whose poses are fitted via differentiable silhouette rendering in Mitsuba3. A sigmoid constraint keeps pattern intensities in [0,1], and the LCD's inherent linear polarization combined with a polarization camera enables optical diffuse-specular separation, removing specular highlights that would otherwise corrupt the photometric-stereo linear model. The entire pipeline contains no learned neural-network parameters—only the display pattern intensities are optimized—making gradients flow directly and effectively from reconstruction error to pattern design.

## Results

On the 3D-printed test dataset, DDPS learned patterns achieve a reconstruction error of 0.0443–0.0486 regardless of initialization (from flat gray noise to trichromatic random), consistently outperforming all heuristic patterns whose initial errors range from 0.0805 (Group OLAT) to 0.3930 (flat gray). Notably, just 2 learned patterns (error 0.0476) already surpass the best 4-pattern heuristic (Group OLAT, 0.0805), demonstrating improved capture efficiency. DDPS also shows robustness to calibration errors (error 0.0456 with inaccurate superpixel locations vs. 0.0453 with accurate ones) and depth variation (errors 0.0417–0.0561 across 40–100 cm).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DDPS learned patterns outperform all six heuristic illumination patterns (OLAT, Group OLAT, Mono-gradient, Mono-complementary, Tri-gradient, Tri-complementary) by a large margin: the best heuristic (Group OLAT, 0.0805) is roughly 1.7× worse than the best learned result (0.0443). Against learning-based photometric stereo methods (UniPS, SDM-UniPS, Bae et al.), DDPS with its analytic reconstructor produces finer geometric details on leaves, vases, and textiles, while uncalibrated learned methods fail on out-of-distribution examples. Replacing the analytic reconstructor with UniPS degrades error from 0.0475 to 0.0951, confirming that the analytic formulation is critical for effective gradient flow during pattern optimization. The paper also shows that DDPS with only 2 learned patterns beats all 4-pattern heuristic designs, and that even flat gray noise initialization converges to competitive accuracy (0.0466), eliminating the need for domain-specific pattern design.

## Relation to Prior Work

DDPS builds on display photometric stereo systems (Lattas et al., Sengupta et al.) that use off-the-shelf monitors as programmable illumination sources, and on the classic analytic photometric stereo formulation of Woodham. It departs from the prevailing practice of using heuristically designed illumination patterns (OLAT, complementary, spherical gradient) by learning patterns end-to-end via a differentiable framework that chains basis-illumination image formation with analytic normal reconstruction. The paper also repurposes the polarization property of LCDs—previously exploited in light-stage systems—for diffuse-specular separation in a display-camera setup, and draws on differentiable illumination-optimization ideas from active stereo and reflectance capture, but applies them directly to normal-reconstruction loss rather than intermediary metrics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DDPS makes high-quality surface normal estimation accessible with a desk-top LCD and polarization camera, removing the need for expensive light stages or dedicated illumination hardware. The robustness to calibration errors, depth variation, and simplified light-transport models suggests practical deployability, and the finding that two learned patterns outperform four heuristic ones points to significant capture-efficiency gains. Open questions include extending to depth reconstruction (requiring multi-view), handling dynamic scenes (synchronization), generalizing to mobile-phone displays, and incorporating shadows and global illumination into the image-formation model. The 3D-printed training dataset, while clever, limits the diversity of training geometry and material, raising questions about generalization to highly reflective or transparent surfaces.
