## Problem

Current text-to-image diffusion models lack fine-grained, continuous control over abstract 3D attributes such as illumination direction, non-rigid shape deformation, camera parameters, and object orientation. Text prompts are inherently limited to high-level descriptions, and ControlNet-style conditioning requires discrete condition maps that cannot express smoothly varying values like a 11° rotation or a specific dolly-zoom ratio. The paper asks whether a small set of rendered images from a single 3D mesh can teach a diffusion model to accept continuously variable 'words' that control these attributes.

## Input / Output

At inference, the user provides a text prompt augmented with one or more Continuous 3D Word tokens whose values are arbitrary real numbers (e.g., illumination azimuth in degrees, wing-pose angle, dolly-zoom factor) fed through positional encoding into a 2-layer MLP that produces the token embedding. For real-world image editing, a Dreambooth-learned image token is prepended to the prompt alongside the continuous words. The output is a standard-resolution generated or edited image; the continuous words add no extra denoising steps or memory overhead beyond the base Stable Diffusion v2.1 pipeline, and the trained LoRA weights are approximately 6 MB.

## Method

The core mechanism is a continuous mapping g_φ(a): D → T implemented as a 2-layer MLP with positional encoding, which replaces discrete per-value tokens and enables interpolation between training values at inference. Training follows a two-stage strategy: Stage 1 (Dreambooth-style) learns a fixed [Obj] token to anchor object identity across all rendered views, and Stage 2 jointly optimizes the diffusion U-Net (via LoRA) and the MLP g_φ so that attribute tokens are disentangled from identity. To prevent overfitting to the synthetic renderings, ControlNet augmentation generates additional training images with varied backgrounds and textures—using depth maps for shape-changing attributes (wing pose, orientation) and lineart extractions for illumination. At inference, [Obj] is injected as a negative prompt to further suppress the training object's appearance, allowing the learned continuous words to transfer to semantically related but unseen objects (e.g., a dog-trained illumination word applied to a horse or a car).

## Results

In a user study with 20 participants across four control settings (wing pose, illumination, wing pose + orientation, illumination + orientation), Continuous 3D Words achieves an average user preference of 55.4% (range 52.5–61.7%), substantially outperforming ControlNet at full strength (23.6% avg) and half strength (21.0% avg). On average user ranking (1–3 scale, higher is better), the proposed method scores 2.43 ± 0.70, compared to 1.84 ± 0.72 for ControlNet(1.0) and 1.73 ± 0.70 for ControlNet(0.5). The method requires no per-attribute hyperparameter tuning, whereas ControlNet's performance is highly sensitive to its guidance strength setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: ControlNet at full (1.0) and half (0.5) guidance strength, and Zero-1-to-3 for real-world image editing. Against ControlNet, Continuous 3D Words wins decisively on all four control settings in both user preference and average ranking, with the largest margin on illumination (55.0% vs. 16.2% for ControlNet 1.0) and wing pose (61.7% vs. 28.3%). Notably, the two ControlNet strengths each win on different settings (full strength favors wing pose, half strength favors illumination + orientation), indicating that no single ControlNet configuration generalizes well, whereas the proposed method is one-size-fits-all. Against Zero-1-to-3, the comparison is qualitative (Figure 6): Continuous 3D Words produces better-harmonized edits on real photos, while Zero-1-to-3 tends to deform the modified object and requires a multi-step pipeline (segmentation, inpainting, compositing) that compounds errors. The paper also notes that a null-text embedding interpolation baseline failed to capture even the simplest attributes and was omitted from the main comparison.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Dreambooth/Textual Inversion line of work on learning new concepts from few samples, but repurposes the learned token space to encode continuous 3D attributes (illumination, pose, camera parameters) rather than object identity. It departs from Zero-1-to-3 and ViewNETI, which learn viewpoint-specific concepts from large 3D datasets, by showing that a single mesh with a rendering engine suffices to learn transferable 3D-aware attributes. The two-stage training (identity first, then attributes) and ControlNet-based augmentation are practical contributions that build on standard fine-tuning and conditioning pipelines to prevent degenerate solutions and overfitting to rendered backgrounds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that fine-grained 3D attribute control in text-to-image generation is achievable with minimal data (one mesh) and compute (single GPU, ~6MB LoRA weights), making it accessible to non-experts who lack 3D scene authoring skills. Key limitations include failure on style-based prompts, residual overfitting to the training object's shape, and a user-study-observed tradeoff between strict attribute accuracy and physical plausibility. Open questions include whether the approach scales to more complex multi-object scenes, whether the 2-layer MLP generalizes to higher-dimensional attribute spaces, and whether the single-mesh assumption can be relaxed for more diverse attribute learning.
