## Problem

Neural fields have become a dominant representation for complex signals, yet efficient and general-purpose filtering operations for them remain severely limited and largely restricted to Euclidean domains. Existing approaches either require pre-computed filtered ground truth, are confined to frequency decomposition (e.g., anti-aliasing), or incur O(n) Monte Carlo cost per evaluation that scales poorly with filter size and signal dimensionality. This paper addresses the gap by showing how a probabilistic re-interpretation of neural fields enables filter-aware training with O(1) cost, generalizing to non-Euclidean manifolds such as rays and rotations.

## Input / Output

The method consumes discrete signal samples (e.g., 2D pixel grids, sparse ray-color pairs from images) together with a filter kernel specified as a samplable probability distribution (e.g., a Gaussian for image blur, a thin-lens ray distribution for depth of field). The output is a standard neural field network whose forward pass directly yields the filtered signal at any query point, requiring no additional inference-time computation beyond a single network evaluation. A key constraint is that the filter kernel must be non-negative and integrate to one (or be decomposed into such components), and for non-Euclidean domains the kernel must satisfy H-symmetry about the origin to ensure the convolution remains interpretable on the manifold rather than the full Lie group.

## Method

The core insight is to reinterpret neural field training as maximum likelihood estimation where field values are treated as probability densities over sample coordinates, enabling the use of the equivalence between convolution of distributions and addition of random variables. This yields a loss (Eq. 13) in which the network is supervised at shifted locations x+v (where v is sampled from the filter kernel) against the unfiltered ground truth, with a normalization term evaluated over the data support Q rather than the full domain, achieving O(1) cost per evaluation. A balanced variant (Eq. 14) rewrites both loss terms as expectations over the same sample set Q, halving network evaluations and improving convergence. For non-Euclidean manifolds, the derivation is generalized via group convolution on homogeneous spaces G/H of Lie groups, where the group action replaces vector addition and the Haar measure replaces Lebesgue measure, enabling filtering on domains like SE(3) (rigid transformations) and Gr(2,4) (lines in projective space). The practical implementation requires only a modification to the loss function—no architectural changes or extra inference steps—and the filter kernel need only be samplable, with no restriction on its complexity or spatial extent.

## Results

On image filtering (Table 1), the proposed method achieves PSNR of 38.0, 44.2, 48.0, and 50.8 dB at filter scales σ = 0.02, 0.04, 0.08, and 0.16 respectively, outperforming Nsampi et al. [20] at all scales and surpassing the Monte Carlo baseline at the largest scale (σ=0.16: 50.8 vs. 46.6 dB). Inference speed is ~660–700 MP/s, roughly 6× faster than Nsampi et al. (~100–106 MP/s). On Mip-NeRF 360 (Table 3), the depth-of-field-aware model trained with the proposed loss achieves 28.8 dB average PSNR for pinhole rendering, losing only 0.4 dB to Mip-NeRF 360 (29.2 dB) despite learning an entire distribution of lens models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Nsampi et al. [20] (repeated differentiation), the proposed method wins on PSNR at every filter scale in Table 1, with the gap widening from 0.9 dB at σ=0.02 to 4.7 dB at σ=0.16, and is ~6× faster at inference (663–700 vs. 100–106 MP/s). Against the Monte Carlo baseline, the proposed method is competitive at small scales (within 1–2 dB) but clearly superior at the largest scale (σ=0.16: 50.8 vs. 46.6 dB PSNR), where Monte Carlo's O(n) sampling cost degrades quality. On Mip-NeRF 360 (Table 3), the proposed method trained for thin-lens depth-of-field modelling achieves 28.8 dB average PSNR for pinhole rendering, trailing Mip-NeRF 360 by only 0.4 dB (29.2 dB) and 0.038 SSIM, despite learning an entire distribution of aperture and focus settings rather than a single pinhole model. The LPIPS gap is larger (0.309 vs. 0.207 average), suggesting the learned lens distribution introduces some perceptual artifacts in pinhole evaluation. In the learned filter-space experiment (Table 2), the proposed method matches Nsampi et al. closely (42.6 vs. 43.5 dB PSNR) while being 6.5× faster at inference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Mip-NeRF 360 architecture (Barron et al., CVPR 2022) and its integrated positional encoding (Barron et al., ICCV 2021) for the NeRF experiments, and on the Plücker-coordinate light field network of Sitzmann et al. (CVPR 2019) for the light field experiments. Conceptually, it departs from prior neural-field filtering approaches—Nsampi et al.'s repeated-differentiation method (ACM TOG 2023) and INSP's derivative-based composition—by reframing the training objective as a maximum-likelihood estimation problem over probability distributions, which naturally absorbs the convolution into the loss. The key theoretical extension is the generalization from Euclidean convolution to group convolution on homogeneous spaces of arbitrary Lie groups, a step not taken by any of the prior filtering methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a drop-in loss-function modification (no architecture or inference changes) that enables filtering on both Euclidean and non-Euclidean neural fields, unlocking applications like zero-extra-cost depth-of-field simulation and lens-effect modelling that were previously infeasible or expensive. A notable limitation is that the method is not zero-shot: applying a new filter at inference requires retraining or a learned filter space, and complex filter families consume network capacity that degrades reconstruction quality. Open questions include whether architectures can be designed to be more amenable to conditioning on arbitrary filter parameters, and whether the distribution-based formulation extends to non-compact or non-Haar-measurable domains.
