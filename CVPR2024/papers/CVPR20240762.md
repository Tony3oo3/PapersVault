## Problem

Lithic Use-Wear Analysis (LUWA) seeks to identify the worked material (bone, wood, ivory, antler, plants) from microscopic wear traces on stone tools, but no open-source dataset existed to benchmark learning-based methods on this rarely-seen domain. The paper addresses three open questions: how well SOTA pre-trained models generalize to microscopic wear images, how few-shot learning can handle scarce data for newly discovered categories, and how ambiguous magnification and sensing modality choices affect classification accuracy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240762/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Input consists of 865×865 grayscale microscopic images captured via an optical 3D profilometer at 20× (0.65 µm/pixel) or 50× (0.26 µm/pixel) magnification, in two complementary sensing modalities: texture scans (2D grayscale) and 3D surface heightmaps (depth-only). Images are optionally partitioned into 5 or 24 patches to preserve fine-grained detail lost by model resizing, and a majority-voting mechanism aggregates patch-level predictions into a single per-use-wear label. Output is a 9-class worked material label (e.g., beechwood, sprucewood, bone, antler, ivory, various plants).

## Method

The paper's core contribution is the LUWA dataset (23,130 images from 34 stone samples, 2 motion types, 7 wear durations, 9 material classes) combined with a systematic benchmarking protocol. For fully-supervised classification, they deploy ResNet, ConvNeXt, ViT, and DINOv2 under three training strategies (train-from-scratch, full fine-tuning, linear probing) across all combinations of granularity, magnification, and modality (324 total configurations), finding that linear probing on pre-trained weights is most stable and that DINOv2-ViT-g/14 achieves the most consistent performance. For few-shot learning, they use a pre-train + ProtoNet pipeline evaluated over 600 simulated episodes in 9-way-5/20-shot settings, and additionally test GPT-4V with archaeologist-provided prompts for few-shot classification and reasoning. Explainability is assessed via Grad-CAM heatmaps compared against human expert attention annotations, revealing that models and experts focus on similar regions but DINOv2 sometimes misattributes polish regions across categories.

## Results

In fully-supervised classification, the best top-1 accuracy is 67.05%, achieved by linear probing on ImageNet-1K pre-trained ResNet152 with heightmap data, 24-patch granularity, and mixed 20×+50× magnification. In the few-shot setting (9-way-5-shot), DINO-base leads with 61.39% on 50× texture images, rising to 69.67% at 20-shot. All deep learning models substantially outperform human experts (43.75% in-house few-shot accuracy; ~49.5% in published blind tests), while GPT-4V achieves only 37.78% (9-way-5-shot, 20× texture). DINOv2 demonstrates the most stable performance across all granularity, magnification, and sensing modality configurations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DINO (self-supervised ViT) dominates the few-shot setting, with DINO-base reaching 61.39% (50× TEX, 5-shot) and 69.67% (50× TEX, 20-shot), outperforming ResNet152 (57.59% / 64.26%) by 3.8 and 5.4 points respectively. In fully-supervised classification, ResNet152 with linear probing achieves the best single result (67.05%), while DINOv2-ViT-g/14 shows the most stable performance across all granularity, magnification, and modality configurations. ConvNeXt variants (tiny/base/large) cluster in the mid-range (46–57% at 5-shot), consistently below DINO but above ViT-base (41–44%). CLIP-base and DeiT variants underperform relative to ResNet and DINO, suggesting that contrastive language-image pretraining and data-efficient distillation do not transfer well to this niche microscopic domain. GPT-4V (37.78% at 5-shot) and human experts (35–43.75%) are the weakest performers, with all deep learning models exceeding 59.5% under at least one configuration. The traditional SIFT+FVs baseline is outperformed by every deep learning method in its best configuration, though its worst case still exceeds the worst deep learning configurations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of vision-for-science and foundation model generalization. Prior LUWA research relied on blind tests and manual quantification methods without learning-based approaches, and no public dataset existed. Rather than proposing a new architecture, the paper repurposes existing classification backbones (ResNet, ConvNeXt, ViT, DINOv2) and meta-learning pipelines (ProtoNet) to benchmark their transfer to a domain with irregular, discontinuous wear traces that lack clear foreground-background structure. It also connects to the broader microscopic image classification literature (pollen, cells, tissue) while introducing the unique confounders of ambiguous magnification and complementary sensing modalities (texture vs. heightmap).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The LUWA dataset (23,130 images) fills a critical gap as the first open-source benchmark for this domain, enabling reproducible research that was previously impossible due to private data and equipment constraints. The finding that SOTA models (67% top-1) substantially outperform human experts (~49.5%) while still leaving a large accuracy gap suggests both the promise and the difficulty of applying foundation models to niche scientific imaging. Open questions include: which magnification and sensing modality is truly optimal (results partially contradict expert heuristics), how to handle newly discovered material categories with few examples, and why DINOv2's attention sometimes treats identical visual features (polish) as foreground for one material class but background for another. The GPT-4V results highlight a broader limitation: current multimodal LLMs can mimic expert reasoning steps but still fail at domain-specific classification, suggesting the vision encoder remains the bottleneck.
