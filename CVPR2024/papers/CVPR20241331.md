## Problem

Existing MIL-based WSI classification methods suffer from three coupled limitations: high storage and preprocessing costs for thousands of instances, over-fitting due to limited discriminative knowledge, and sensitivity to pseudo-bag grouping priors that disrupt intra-class homogeneity. Critically, the intrinsic relationships among instance sampling, feature representation, and decision-making remain unexploited—past sampling knowledge is not leveraged to guide subsequent decisions, and the sequential nature of clinical diagnosis is ignored.

## Input / Output

The method consumes histopathology WSIs (gigapixel resolution) preprocessed into non-overlapping patches (256×256 at 20× magnification for CAMELYON16, 224×224 at 20× for TCGA Lung), with features extracted by a frozen pre-trained ResNet50 (1024-d) or SimCLR ResNet18 (512-d). Rather than processing all instances simultaneously, PAMIL samples them sequentially in groups of M instances over T = B_i/M steps. The output is a binary bag-level probability (tumor vs. normal) produced by a class-token aggregation, with an auxiliary per-stage sub-bag prediction used for reward computation.

## Method

PAMIL formulates WSI analysis as a Markov Decision Process optimized with PPO, where the state is the remaining instance feature set and the action selects the next group of M instances. The Dynamic Policy Instance Selection (DPIS) module uses an RNN-MLP policy network conditioned on the current fused token to pick instances via spatial proximity and feature-distance similarity to the current representation, guided by a self-supervised reward (prediction-label consistency) and penalty (cosine similarity between the class token and stage tokens) that corrects pseudo-bag biases without requiring instance labels. The Selection Fusion Feature Representation (SFFR) module fuses current patch features with all historical stage tokens through a Transformer encoder and multi-head attention, while a Siamese contrastive loss between adjacent stage tokens enforces temporal consistency and prevents catastrophic forgetting. A Transformer Classification Module (TCM) aggregates all stage tokens via a learned class token for the final bag prediction, and the entire pipeline is trained end-to-end with a combined cross-entropy (bag-level and sub-bag-level) plus Siamese contrastive objective.

## Results

On CAMELYON16, PAMIL (DPIS-LIIS) achieves 96.1% accuracy, 94.7% F1, and 97.7% AUC, surpassing the previous best MHIM (DSMIL) by 3.8% in accuracy. On TCGA Lung Cancer, PAMIL (DPIS-LIIS) reaches 96.5% accuracy, 96.1% F1, and 99.4% AUC, exceeding IAT by 4.4% in accuracy. All three DPIS variants (GMSS, GHSS, LIIS) consistently outperform every baseline across both datasets and all metrics, with LIIS achieving the highest AUC on both benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PAMIL outperforms all 10 baselines on both datasets across every metric. On CAMELYON16, the gap is most pronounced against bag-level methods: MeanPooling (62.6% acc) and MaxPooling (82.6% acc) trail by over 13 and 13.5 percentage points respectively, while attention-based ABMIL (84.5%) and Transformer-based TransMIL (85.8%) lag by roughly 10–12 points. Among pseudo-bags methods, DTFD (90.8% acc) and MHIM-DSMIL (92.5% acc) are the closest competitors, yet PAMIL-LIIS still leads by 5.3 and 3.8 points in accuracy. On TCGA Lung, IAT achieves the highest prior accuracy (92.1%) but with notably lower F1 (84.9%) and AUC (84.9%), whereas PAMIL-LIIS reaches 96.5% accuracy with 96.1% F1 and 99.4% AUC, a 4.4-point accuracy gain. DGMIL reports 92.0% accuracy on TCGA but omits F1, and its K-Means-based grouping introduces prior bias that limits generalizability. The consistent superiority across all three DPIS variants confirms that the dynamic policy-driven sampling and Siamese fusion representation are the primary drivers of improvement rather than a single sampling heuristic.

## Relation to Prior Work

PAMIL carves new ground at the intersection of pseudo-bags-level MIL and reinforcement learning for medical imaging. While prior MIL methods (TransMIL, DSMIL, MHIM, DTFD) treat instance grouping as a static pre-processing step (clustering, random partitioning, or attention-weighted pooling), PAMIL reframes instance selection as a sequential decision problem solved via PPO, where a policy network dynamically samples the next batch of patches conditioned on historical tokens. The feature aggregation side draws on Transformer class tokens and Siamese contrastive learning, but the key departure is the closed-loop feedback: the classifier's prediction and a cosine-similarity penalty jointly reward/punish the sampling policy, creating an adaptive loop absent from both bag-level and pseudo-bags-level baselines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that dynamic, policy-driven instance selection can substantially outperform static grouping strategies (K-Means, random, positional sorting) on challenging WSI datasets where tumor regions are sparse and dispersed, addressing a practical bottleneck in computational pathology. The plug-in DPSF module's ability to boost even simple baselines (MeanPooling +25% accuracy, +38% AUC) suggests the sampling-and-fusion paradigm may generalize beyond the full PAMIL pipeline. However, the method's complexity (RNN policy, PPO training, Siamese contrastive loss, multi-head attention fusion) raises questions about scalability to even larger WSIs or multi-class settings, and the authors acknowledge that spatial/structural information in WSIs is underexploited by the current distance-based sampling strategies.
