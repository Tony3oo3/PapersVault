## Problem

Camera-based sleep staging remains significantly below expert-level performance, with prior video-based methods achieving only κ=0.61 by relying on derived heart and breathing rates rather than underlying waveforms. Additionally, the small size of available video datasets (50 nights) limits the capacity of models that can be trained directly on them, creating a domain-shift problem when transferring from contact-sensor training data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241206/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 850 nm near-infrared video at 20 FPS, from which cardiac waveforms (10 Hz) and respiratory waveforms (5 Hz) are extracted via FDA-cleared software, alongside 90 optical-flow-based motion features computed over head, body, and outer-bed regions with multiple temporal windows and thresholds. During pre-training, the equivalent inputs are ECG-derived cardiac waveforms and thoracic respiratory belt signals. The model outputs four-class sleep stage probabilities (Wake, N1/N2, N3, REM) for each 30-second epoch, processing sequences of 240 consecutive epochs (2 hours) at a time.

## Method

SleepVST patchifies continuous cardiac and respiratory waveforms into non-overlapping 30-second windows, encodes each patch via a shallow 1D ResNet-style convolutional encoder (compressing to 64-dim vectors), concatenates the two modality features, and passes them through a transformer encoder with sinusoidal position encodings to capture long-range sleep-cycle structure. The model is first pre-trained end-to-end on the large SHHS and MESA contact-sensor datasets with a linear classification head. For video transfer, the transformer is frozen as a feature extractor and a new Random Forest classifier is trained on the extracted features augmented with 90 motion features derived from a homography-normalized optical flow field, allowing the classifier to discard sensor-specific features while incorporating motion information unavailable in contact-sensor data.

## Results

On the SHHS and MESA contact-sensor datasets, SleepVST achieves total Cohen's κ of 0.75 and 0.77 (accuracy 83.0% and 85.2%), outperforming all prior cardio-respiratory sleep staging methods. On the OSV video dataset (50 nights), SleepVST achieves a total accuracy of 78.8% and total Cohen's κ of 0.71 in four-class sleep staging, setting a new state-of-the-art for video-based sleep staging. Per-night mean κ is 0.68 on OSV, with the model particularly strong at distinguishing Wake and REM stages.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SleepVST outperforms all seven baselines across both contact-sensor and video-based settings. On SHHS, it beats Bakker et al. [3] (κ_T 0.64) and Sridhar et al. [50] (κ_T 0.66) by 0.11 and 0.09 κ respectively; on MESA, it surpasses Kotzen et al. [22] (κ_T 0.73) by 0.04 κ. For video-based staging on OSV, SleepVST improves over the prior state-of-the-art Carter et al. [6] (κ_T 0.64, Acc_T 74.3%) by 0.07 κ and 4.5 percentage points in accuracy. The gains over motion-only methods (Kamon et al. [21], Nochino et al. [35]) and the single-modality cardiac waveform method (van Meulen et al. [60], κ_T 0.49) are substantial, reflecting the benefit of combining full cardio-respiratory waveforms with motion features. The ablation in Table 3 confirms that the transfer strategy (frozen feature extractor + new classifier with motion features) is critical: directly applying the pre-trained model to video yields only κ_T 0.536, while the full pipeline reaches 0.708.

## Relation to Prior Work

SleepVST adapts the PatchTST-style patchification and transformer encoder architecture to the sleep staging domain, drawing on the success of transformers in EEG-based sleep staging (SleepTransformer, Phan et al.) and the use of raw waveforms over derived rates in wearable-based staging. It extends the authors' own prior video-based sleep staging work (Carter et al., CVPRW 2023), which combined heart rate, breathing rate, and motion features with a Random Forest classifier, by replacing the rate-based inputs with full cardio-respiratory waveforms processed through a transformer. The key conceptual departure is the two-stage transfer learning strategy: pre-training the transformer on large contact-sensor datasets (SHHS, MESA) where abundant labelled data is available, then freezing the model as a feature extractor for the much smaller video dataset, a pattern borrowed from broader transfer learning but applied here across the contact-sensor-to-video domain gap.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper narrows the gap between camera-based and expert polysomnography sleep staging (κ=0.71 vs. inter-scorer agreement of ~0.71–0.81), making non-contact overnight monitoring more clinically viable for longitudinal studies and elderly care settings where repeated PSG is impractical. The transfer learning framework is broadly applicable: any modality that yields cardio-respiratory waveforms (video, wearables, smartmattresses) could potentially leverage pre-training on large contact-sensor corpora. Key limitations include the small video evaluation set (50 nights), a single near-infrared camera at fixed distance, and a measurable performance drop in older adults where cardio-respiratory variability declines. Open questions include whether more expressive motion representations (e.g., learned rather than hand-crafted optical flow features) could enable detection of sleep movement disorders like REM behaviour disorder, and whether the approach generalises to multi-camera or RGB settings.
