## Problem

Existing 3D volumetric lighting estimation methods (e.g., Lighthouse, Wang et al.) represent indoor illumination as dense 3D voxel grids, incurring O(n³) memory and compute costs that are prohibitive for real-time AR/MR applications. These methods ignore the fact that incident radiance in typical indoor scenes originates only from surfaces, which are sparsely distributed in 3D space, leading to substantial waste of storage and computation on empty voxels.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single narrow-FOV LDR RGB image (480×320 in experiments) and produces a 3D voxel octree lighting representation at depth 7 (equivalent to 128³ resolution), where each non-empty node stores an RGB α radiance value. This octree can be rendered into HDR environment maps (256×128) from arbitrary camera poses via the differentiable cone tracing layer, and directly used to shade inserted virtual objects with spatially-coherent lighting. The pipeline also internally predicts per-pixel depth and a global lighting feature vector as intermediate representations.

## Method

The core contribution is a voxel octree lighting representation combined with a U-Net built from O-CNN graph convolution operators that jointly predicts octree subdivision labels and per-node RGB α field values, reducing cost from O(n³) to O(n²). A key architectural innovation is a differentiable voxel octree cone tracing rendering layer that performs distance-adaptive multi-scale sampling: nearby octants are sampled at fine granularity (high frequency) while distant ones are aggregated at coarser levels (mip-map style), enabling efficient feature fusion across octree depths during rendering. The network is trained progressively—first the DenseNet121-based depth network with log-encoded L2 and scale-invariant losses, then the octree-based Light Network supervised by binary cross-entropy on split labels and log-encoded L2 on rendered HDR panoramas from novel views, with an adversarial loss on the global lighting decoder. The entire pipeline is end-to-end differentiable, with the rendering layer providing gradient signals back through the octree structure.

## Results

On the InteriorNet dataset, LightOctree achieves 16.82 dB PSNR for lighting prediction, outperforming single-environment-map methods (Gardner et al. 13.42 dB, NIR 16.59 dB) and per-pixel spherical lobe methods (DeepLight 13.36 dB, Garon et al. 13.21 dB), though it trails the two strongest volumetric baselines Lighthouse (17.29 dB) and Wang et al. (17.37 dB). On the FutureHouse dataset, the proposed spatial-coherence (SC) metric yields 0.226 for LightOctree versus 0.243 for Lighthouse, 0.274 for Li et al., and 0.291 for Gardner et al., indicating the best spatial consistency among all compared methods. In a 20-participant user study, LightOctree was preferred over all three baselines (Gardner et al., Li et al., Lighthouse) with p < 0.001. The method reduces memory and FLOPs from O(n³) to O(n²) relative to uniform 3D voxel grid approaches.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LightOctree clearly outperforms the non-spatially-varying baselines: it gains over 3 dB PSNR on InteriorNet compared to Gardner et al. (13.42) and DeepLight (13.36), and roughly 3.6 dB over Garon et al. (13.21), confirming the benefit of a 3D volumetric representation over a single environment map. Against the per-pixel spatially-varying method of Li et al. [38] (16.66 dB), LightOctree edges ahead by 0.16 dB on PSNR and shows a substantially lower SC metric (0.226 vs. 0.274), reflecting better spatial coherence. The two strongest volumetric baselines—Lighthouse [25] (17.29 dB) and Wang et al. [43] (17.37 dB)—still lead on raw PSNR by 0.47 and 0.55 dB respectively, though the authors note Lighthouse uses stereo input and Wang et al. relies on a full inverse-rendering pipeline with material priors. On the spatial-coherence metric (SC) on FutureHouse, LightOctree (0.226) actually surpasses Lighthouse (0.243), suggesting its octree-based representation captures local lighting variation more faithfully. In the user study, LightOctree was preferred over Gardner et al. (75.2%), Li et al. (68.5%), and Lighthouse (65.2%), all with p < 0.001, indicating perceptual quality that compensates for the small PSNR gap. The key trade-off is efficiency: LightOctree's O(n²) octree structure versus O(n³) uniform grids makes it suitable for interactive AR, at the cost of a modest accuracy gap to the heaviest baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the 3D spatially-coherent lighting estimation line initiated by Lighthouse (Srinivasan et al., 2020) and Wang et al. (ICCV 2021), which use dense 3D volumetric RGB-α representations and 3D CNNs. The key departure is replacing the uniform voxel grid with a sparse voxel octree, drawing on O-CNN graph convolution operators (Wang et al., ACM TOG 2017) and octree-based global illumination techniques (Crasin et al., 2011; Laine & Karras, 2010). A differentiable cone-tracing rendering layer, inspired by real-time GI cone tracing, is introduced to provide multi-scale supervision and enable end-to-end training of the octree-based network, which prior octree architectures could not support for the ill-posed single-image lighting regression task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The O(n²) vs O(n³) memory and compute reduction makes spatially-coherent lighting estimation feasible on performance-constrained AR/MR hardware, addressing a practical bottleneck that has limited deployment of prior volumetric methods. However, the method still trails the best accuracy (16.82 dB vs 17.37 dB PSNR on InteriorNet), and the authors acknowledge that incorporating generative models or joint inverse rendering could close this gap. Open questions include whether the octree sparsity assumption holds in scenes with participating media or complex light transport, and whether the approach generalizes beyond the FutureHouse synthetic training distribution to diverse real-world indoor environments.
