## Problem

Existing multimodal benchmarks rely on Yes/No or multiple-choice formats that allow models to exploit linguistic shortcuts without genuinely reasoning about visual content, and they do not test the ability to detect subtle, out-of-the-ordinary events in complex scenes. This paper asks whether state-of-the-art LMMs can actually identify video game glitches—unusual frames arising from physics violations, rendering errors, or semantic inconsistencies—when given no linguistic hints about what is wrong.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241663/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each test instance is a single RGB game screenshot (one frame extracted from a gameplay video or rendered in Unity) paired with one of three free-text prompts (e.g., 'What is unusual about this image?'). The model produces an unstructured free-text response describing any anomalous aspects, which is then semantically compared against a one-line ground-truth description by a Llama-2-70B-Chat judge. The benchmark comprises 593 glitch frames and 330 glitch-free frames spanning 205 games, with the glitch-free set serving as a control where the expected answer is 'nothing is wrong.'

## Method

GlitchBench is constructed by sampling 1,000 videos from the GamePhysics Reddit dataset, manually filtering for single-frame-detectable glitches to yield 513 real-world samples, and supplementing with 75 synthetic glitches (placeholder textures, mesh distortions, low-res textures) generated in Unity to reduce survivor bias. A key design choice is the free-text response format rather than multiple choice, preventing models from matching answer options without examining the image. Glitch categories are derived through an iterative human-GPT-4 collaborative process that produces four high-level types (Physics/Collision/Spawn, Animation/Pose, Rendering/Texture, Camera/UI/Lighting) mapped to the Lewis et al. taxonomy. Evaluation uses Llama-2-70B-Chat as an LLM judge to assess semantic agreement between model output and ground truth, validated against human labels via Cohen's Kappa (κ = 0.64 mean across models). Eleven LMMs are tested, with GPT-4V achieving the best average accuracy of 43.4% on Q1/Q2 and 64.9% on the extensive captioning question Q3.

## Results

On GlitchBench (593 glitch + 330 glitch-free images from 205 games), GPT-4V achieves the highest average accuracy of 43.4% (Q1: 57.2%, Q2: 29.5%), with the best open-source model LLaVA-1.5-13B reaching 35.5%. In the extensive captioning setup (Q3), GPT-4V's accuracy rises to 64.9%, nearly double that of LLaVA-1.5-13B (30.5%), suggesting a reasoning gap rather than a pure perception gap. GPT-4V scores 91.6% on glitch-free images, indicating the challenge lies specifically in identifying subtle anomalies. The Animation and Pose category is the hardest for all models, while Physics/Collision/Spawn is the easiest.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4V leads all 10 open-source models by a wide margin, outperforming the best open-source model (LLaVA-1.5-13B, 35.5%) by 7.9 pp and the worst (Fuyu, 8.5%) by 34.9 pp on average accuracy. Notably, on standard VQA benchmarks (VQAv2, OK-VQA, AI2D), open-source models like LLaVA-1.5 (80.0%) and Qwen-VL (79.5%) actually surpass GPT-4V (74.2%), yet on GlitchBench they trail by 9.9 and 28 pp respectively, revealing a disconnect between benchmark performance and real-world anomaly detection. Among open-source models, LLaVA-1.5-13B edges out SPHINX-13B (35.5% vs. 27.9%) and OtterHD (24.0%), while the larger InstructBLIP-13B (15.4%) underperforms its 7B counterpart (19.6%), likely due to overconfident 'nothing wrong' responses. Higher resolution consistently helps: SPHINX-13B (448×448) beats SPHINX-7B (224×224) by 2.9 pp, and OtterHD outperforms its base Fuyu model by 15.5 pp. The Q3 extensive-captioning setup reveals that GPT-4V can perceive 64.9% of glitches when asked to describe everything, but drops to 43.4% when asked to identify what is unusual, pointing to a cross-modal reasoning bottleneck rather than a pure visual perception failure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GamePhysics dataset (from the authors' prior work [66]) from a video-level bug identification resource into a frame-level LMM evaluation benchmark, adding 75 synthetic Unity-generated samples to reduce survivor bias. Conceptually, it departs from the Whoops benchmark [9] by using real game screenshots with distracting elements rather than clean synthetic images, and by requiring basic physics/commonsense reasoning rather than cultural knowledge. It also diverges from standard VQA and captioning benchmarks (VQAv2, SEED-Bench, MME) by adopting a free-text 'what is unusual?' formulation that provides no linguistic hints, forcing models to integrate visual scanning with commonsense reasoning without a predefined answer space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that models scoring 70–80% on established LMM benchmarks (VQAv2, SEED-Bench) drop to 8–35% on GlitchBench—reveals a significant gap between benchmark performance and real-world generalization, suggesting current evaluation suites may overstate model capabilities. The Q3-vs-Q1 gap for GPT-4V (64.9% vs. 57.2%) further indicates that models can perceive anomalous details but struggle to selectively attend to them under direct questioning, pointing to a reasoning bottleneck rather than a perception one. Open questions include whether multi-step or chain-of-thought prompting can close the 30–35% headroom, how to handle the long-tail distribution of glitch types, and whether the overrepresentation of open-world games (a noted limitation) skews the difficulty profile.
