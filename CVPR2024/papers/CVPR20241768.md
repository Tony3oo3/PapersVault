## Problem

Absolute Pose Regression (APR) methods rely exclusively on 2D CNN operations at inference, ignoring 3D geometric constraints, which limits their generalization to viewpoints not well covered by training data. Prior test-time refinement approaches either require additional unlabeled data for semi-supervised finetuning or are tightly coupled to a single APR architecture, reducing their practical flexibility.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241768/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Given an RGB query image and a per-scene NeFeS model (trained on ~1000–1500 images per scene), the method iteratively refines a coarse 6-DoF pose from any pretrained APR network over 50 optimization steps. The NeFeS renders dense feature maps (H×W×c) from the current pose estimate, which are compared against features extracted from the query image by a pre-trained 2D feature extractor (from DFNet). When the initial pose originates from a non-differentiable source such as NetVlad image retrieval, the method instead directly optimizes pose parameters via tangent space backpropagation with separate learning rates for rotation and translation.

## Method

NeFeS extends the NeRF-W architecture with a static head that jointly predicts RGB color, N_f features, and density for each 3D position, all rendered via volume rendering quadrature. Two robustness components are introduced: an Exposure-adaptive Affine Color Transformation (ACT) that uses the query image's luminance histogram to predict a 3×3 matrix and bias vector compensating for lighting differences, and a Feature Fusion module (three 3×3 convolutions, a 5×5 convolution, and batch normalization) that combines rendered colors and features into more stable descriptors. Training follows a progressive three-phase schedule—photometric loss only for geometry bootstrapping, then feature loss with stop-gradients on density, and finally the fusion loss—while semantic filtering masks dynamic objects. At test time, a spatially-normalized feature cosine similarity loss between rendered and query features is backpropagated to update either the APR network parameters or the pose directly, with the former yielding lower final errors.

## Results

On Cambridge Landmarks (outdoor), DFNet+NeFeS₅₀ achieves a scene-average median position/orientation error of 0.35 m / 0.77°, improving over the DFNet baseline (1.19 m / 2.90°) by 70.6% in position error and outperforming the next-best method MS-Transformer+PAE (0.96 m / 2.73°) by 63.5%. On 7-Scenes (indoor), the same pipeline reaches 0.02 m / 0.79° average, a 59%+ improvement over DFNet (0.06 m / 1.93°) and roughly 9× better than MS-Transformer (0.18 m / 9.51°). The method also works when the initial pose comes from a non-differentiable retriever (NetVlad), reducing Cambridge error from 3.18 m / 7.74° to 1.15 m / 1.30° after 100 refinement iterations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's method (DFNet+NeFeS₅₀) decisively outperforms all nine compared APR baselines on both Cambridge Landmarks and 7-Scenes. On Cambridge, it reduces the scene-average position error from 1.19 m (DFNet) to 0.35 m and orientation error from 2.90° to 0.77°, a 70.6% position improvement; the closest competitor, MS-Transformer+PAE (0.96 m / 2.73°), is still 2.7× worse in position error. On 7-Scenes, the gap widens further: 0.02 m / 0.79° versus 0.06 m / 1.93° for DFNet and 0.18 m / 9.51° for MS-Transformer, representing roughly a 9× reduction in orientation error over the transformer-based baseline. The method also surpasses the original PoseNet (2.04 m / 6.23° on Cambridge) by a factor of ~6 in position error. When initialized from NetVlad retrieval (a non-APR source), the refinement still achieves 1.15 m / 1.30° on Cambridge, exceeding the accuracy of several standalone APR methods. The paper further shows that optimizing APR network parameters yields slightly lower errors than directly optimizing the pose (0.35 m vs. 0.66 m on Cambridge), but both modes substantially outperform any single-frame baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the NeRF-for-localization line initiated by Direct-PoseNet and DFNet, which used neural radiance fields as a photometric matching module during training or unlabeled finetuning. Here, the authors repurpose the implicit 3D representation as a *test-time* refinement tool: a per-scene Neural Feature Synthesizer (NeFeS) renders dense feature maps from a coarse pose, and the resulting feature-metric error is backpropagated to update the APR network or the pose directly. Conceptually, this bridges the gap between fast but geometry-agnostic APR and slower classical geometry-based refinement (PnP+RANSAC, dense feature-metric alignment), while remaining fully differentiable and requiring no pre-computed SfM model or explicit correspondence search. The feature field distillation idea from Kobayashi et al. and Neural Feature Fusion Fields informs the NeFeS architecture, and the exposure-adaptive ACT borrows from Urban Radiance Fields to handle appearance variation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play test-time refinement framework is practically valuable: it improves any APR architecture (PoseNet, MS-Transformer, DFNet) by 50–70% without requiring additional unlabeled data or architectural changes, and it also works with non-differentiable initializers like NetVlad retrieval. However, the method requires per-scene NeFeS training (a non-trivial offline cost) and has bounded convergence (≤35° rotation, ≤0.6 m indoor / ≤4 m outdoor translation), limiting its applicability to large-scale or highly dynamic environments. Open questions include whether the per-scene training overhead can be amortized across scenes, whether the refinement can be made real-time, and how the approach scales to outdoor scenes with significant appearance change beyond what ACT can compensate.
