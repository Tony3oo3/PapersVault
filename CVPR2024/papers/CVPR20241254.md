## Problem

Global visual geolocation lacks a standard, large-scale, open-access benchmark with reliably localizable images. Existing web-scraped datasets contain significant portions of noisy or non-localizable images, while street-view datasets are proprietary and expensive to acquire, limiting the ability to evaluate whether models learn robust geographical representations or merely memorize training data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241254/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a single street-view image (512 px height, average width 792±127 px) and outputs continuous geographic coordinates (latitude/longitude) along with discrete administrative division labels at four tiers (country, region, area, city). The dataset enforces strict spatial separation (no train image within 1 km of any test image) and sequence separation (no image sequence appears in both splits), and each image carries rich auxiliary metadata including land cover, climate, soil type, driving side, and distance to sea.

## Method

The framework pairs a pretrained ViT-L-14 image encoder (CLIP on DATA_COMP) with an MLP geolocation head, and the key contribution is an extensive ablation over prediction heads, fine-tuning strategies, and contrastive objectives. The best-performing combined model uses a QuadTree spatial partition (11k adaptive cells) with a hybrid prediction head that jointly performs cell classification (cross-entropy) and sub-cell regression (L2 on relative coordinates within the predicted cell), supervised hierarchically at all administrative levels. Full fine-tuning of the encoder is combined with a region-level contrastive loss (MIL-NCE) that pulls representations of images from the same region closer together. This combination yields a Geoscore of 3361 and 68% country accuracy, substantially outperforming both the frozen base model and prior geolocation-specific approaches like GeoEstimator and StreetCLIP.

## Results

The combined model (ViT-L-14 + QuadTree hybrid + hierarchical supervision + fine-tuning + region contrastive loss) achieves a Geoscore of 3361 and an average Haversine distance of 1814 km on the OSV-5M test set, with country/region/area/city classification accuracies of 68.0%/39.4%/10.3%/5.9%. This represents a +1309 Geoscore improvement and a 45% reduction in average distance over the frozen ViT-B/32 base model (Geoscore 2052, distance 2992 km). Over half of the combined model's predictions fall within 250 km of the true location, and the model substantially outperforms human annotators (Geoscore 1009, distance 6407 km) on all metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GeoEstimator [40], the combined model achieves a higher Geoscore (3361 vs. 3331) and a substantially lower average distance (1814 km vs. 2308 km), while matching its region accuracy (39.4%) and slightly exceeding its country accuracy (68.0% vs. 66.8%). However, GeoEstimator retains a clear advantage on area classification (18.4% vs. 10.3%), which the authors attribute to its bespoke geocell architecture with built-in geographical priors. Against StreetCLIP 0-shot [16], the combined model dominates on Geoscore (3361 vs. 2273), distance (1814 vs. 2854 km), country (68.0% vs. 38.4%), and region (39.4% vs. 20.8%), but StreetCLIP achieves a notably higher city-level accuracy (14.8% vs. 5.9%), likely benefiting from its zero-shot text-based retrieval mechanism that can match specific city names. Overall, the combined model is the strongest on the primary geolocation metrics (Geoscore and distance) while GeoEstimator excels at finer-grained administrative classification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against two lineages of geolocation datasets: web-scraped collections (YFCC100M, Im2GPS3k) that are large but biased and predominantly non-localizable, and street-view collections (Google-WS-15k, GMCP, StreetCLIP) that are localizable but proprietary or geographically limited. By leveraging Mapillary's open-licensed crowd-sourced imagery, the authors carve new ground as the first large-scale, globally uniform, open-access street-view dataset purpose-built for geolocation, with enforced spatial and sequence separation to prevent memorization. Methodologically, the benchmark draws on the full spectrum of prior geolocation approaches—retrieval-based (Hays & Efros), classification-based (hierarchical, QuadTree), and hybrid (PIGEON, StreetCLIP)—and systematically evaluates them under a unified protocol.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

OSV-5M addresses a critical gap: prior geolocation benchmarks were either too small, too biased, or too noisy to reliably measure whether models learn genuine geographical representations versus memorizing training-set shortcuts. The strict 1 km spatial separation and sequence-level split make this the first benchmark where test performance truly reflects generalization. Open questions include whether the dataset can support self-supervised pretraining or generative modeling beyond supervised geolocation, and whether the ~4% residual non-localizable images (low-quality, indoor, tunnel shots) will matter at scale. The finding that geolocation fine-tuning degrades retrieval performance relative to the frozen pretrained encoder also raises questions about the trade-off between task-specific adaptation and general-purpose representation quality.
