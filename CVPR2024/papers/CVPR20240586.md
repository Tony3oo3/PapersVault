## Problem

Standard adversarial attacks like PGD, CW, and GAMA produce weaker perturbations on Vision Transformers than on CNNs, yet the fundamental cause of this reduced attack strength has remained unexplained. This paper identifies that softmax operations in every attention block of ViTs cause floating-point underflow errors when pre-softmax outputs have large scale, creating a gradient masking effect that misleads gradient-based attacks. Understanding this mechanism is critical because it also explains why adversarial training of ViTs is notoriously unstable and requires additional tricks like gradient clipping and epsilon warmup.

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 224×224 for ImageNet-100) and a ViT model (primarily ViT-B16) as input, operating under an ℓ∞ threat model with ε=8/255. The AAS attack produces a set of per-attention-block pre-softmax scaling factors S={s₁,...,sₘ} optimized via gradient-based search, which are then applied to the model before generating the actual adversarial perturbation. The AAS-AT training procedure outputs a robust ViT model whose pre-softmax scales are kept within a suitable range throughout training, reducing the need for auxiliary stabilization tricks.

## Method

The core mechanism is to downscale pre-softmax outputs in each attention block (Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(λQKᵀ/√d)V) to prevent floating-point underflow in the exponential computation. The AAS attack finds optimal per-block scaling factors by maximizing LPIPS distance between the original model and a model with perturbed scaling factors, using 10 steps of gradient ascent on the scaling parameters; LPIPS is chosen over cross-entropy or max-margin losses because gradient masking occurs at the feature level, and LPIPS produces perceptually aligned gradients characteristic of robust models. The resulting perturbed model is then attacked with any standard method (PGD, CW, GAMA) to generate stronger adversarial examples. For defense, AAS-AT periodically (every λ epochs) applies the AAS scaling perturbation during adversarial training, keeping pre-softmax magnitudes bounded so that training gradients remain accurate and the model converges to a genuinely robust solution without relying on gradient clipping or warmup heuristics.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, combining AAS-AT with PGD-AT improves AutoAttack robust accuracy from 53.14% to 56.61% (+3.47%), and with Trades from 54.03% to 57.34% (+3.31%). On CIFAR-100, the largest gain is with Trades+AWP, improving AA robust accuracy from 27.03% to 29.41% (+2.38%). For the AAS attack, combining it with GAMA yields up to 2.89% stronger attacks on CIFAR-100 (26.08% vs 28.97% robust accuracy) and 3.63% on ImageNet-100 (26.12% vs 29.75% for PGD-100). The method also improves VIT-specific baselines: XCIT-S12 by 1.28–1.38% and Mo et al. by 1.41–1.76% on AA robust accuracy across CIFAR-10/100.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AAS-AT consistently outperforms all compared adversarial training baselines on AutoAttack robust accuracy. Against PGD-AT [24], it gains +3.47% on CIFAR-10 (56.61% vs 53.14%) and +1.93% on CIFAR-100 (27.71% vs 25.78%). Against Trades [41], gains are +3.31% (CIFAR-10) and +2.05% (CIFAR-100). Against the stronger Trades+AWP [38] baseline, improvements are +1.70% (CIFAR-10) and +2.38% (CIFAR-100). Against VIT-specific methods, AAS-AT improves XCIT-S12 [12] by +1.28%/+1.29% and Mo et al. [26] by +1.76%/+1.41% on CIFAR-10/CIFAR-100 respectively. For the attack side, AAS combined with GAMA [31] achieves 2.89% stronger attacks on CIFAR-100 (26.08% vs 28.97% robust accuracy) and 2.2% on CIFAR-10, while AAS+PGD-100 [24] improves over vanilla PGD-100 by 3.09% on CIFAR-10 and 3.63% on ImageNet-100. The CW [8] and FGSM [17] attacks also show consistent improvements of 1.5–5.4% when combined with AAS across all three datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Yu and Xu's [40] observation that output-space softmax causes floating-point underflow in CNNs to the intermediate attention-block softmax in Vision Transformers, arguing the effect is amplified because it occurs at every layer. The proposed AAS-AT method is built directly on Trades [41], adding a periodic pre-softmax scaling perturbation step, while the AAS attack repurposes LPIPS [43]—originally a perceptual image distance metric—as a feature-level loss for optimizing scaling factors, drawing on the connection between perceptually aligned gradients and adversarial robustness [1, 16, 22]. The work also positions itself against prior ViT adversarial training recipes (Mo et al. [26], Debenedetti [12]) that rely on gradient clipping, epsilon warmup, and high weight decay without explaining why these tricks are necessary.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By identifying intermediate softmax underflow as the fundamental cause of gradient masking in ViTs, the paper offers a unifying explanation for why ViT adversarial training is harder than CNN adversarial training and why various stabilization tricks are needed. The AAS attack is a lightweight, composable module that can be plugged into any existing attack or training method, making it practically accessible. Open questions include whether the effect fully accounts for all ViT robustness phenomena, whether it generalizes to non-softmax attention mechanisms (e.g., linear attention), and whether the gains hold at full ImageNet scale with larger ViT variants. The paper's evaluation is limited primarily to ViT-B16 and CIFAR/ImageNet-100, leaving broader architectural generality partially untested.
