## Problem

Current hybrid self-supervised methods that combine contrastive learning (CL) with masked image modeling (MIM) achieve SOTA on curated single-centric-object datasets like ImageNet but fail to generalize to arbitrary or non-iconic scenarios. The paper identifies the root cause: MAE is theoretically equivalent to patch-level contrastive learning (each patch treated as a distinct category), which directly conflicts with global-level CL (all patches treated as one category), causing representational collapse in heterogeneous scenes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242356/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images, partitions them into non-overlapping ViT-style patches, and randomly masks a subset. Visible patches are fed into the MAE encoder, which produces dual-branch encoded features via patch-level feature augmentation. The decoder outputs per-patch predictions for both branches, and the final learned representations (patch-level features from the encoder) are transferred to downstream tasks including classification, detection, and segmentation. Unlike standard CL, no global data augmentation is applied; instead, augmentation operates at the feature level within the encoder.

## Method

ASL (Arbitrary Self-supervised Learning) builds on the MAE encoder-decoder architecture and introduces explicit patch-level contrastive learning to replace the conflicting global-level CL. Two patch-level feature augmentation strategies generate dual-branch features from the encoder: Dropout in Linear Embedding (DLE) and an Asymmetric Encoder Extension (AEE) that adds a Transformer layer after the encoder. Both branches pass through the decoder to produce per-patch predictions, which are aligned via a symmetric L1 loss (with stop-gradient) rather than cosine similarity or InfoNCE. Crucially, the [cls] token is excluded from alignment, eliminating the global constraint that causes conflicts on non-iconic data. The total training objective is the sum of the standard MAE reconstruction loss and the patch-level semantic alignment loss, with both weighted equally, and the entire framework incurs only ~1.5× the FLOPs of vanilla MAE.

## Results

Pre-trained on COCO (a non-iconic, arbitrary-scenario dataset), ASL achieves 48.6% linear probing accuracy on ImageNet-1K and 50.3% box mAP on COCO detection, outperforming MAE (46.8%/49.3%) by 1.8 and 1.0 points respectively, while all hybrid CL+MIM methods (CMAE, ccMIM, SIM, iBOT) underperform MAE. When pre-trained on ImageNet-100+COCO for 4000 epochs, ASL reaches 85.9% LP and 94.2% fine-tuning accuracy on ImageNet-100, surpassing MAE (74.5%/90.9%) by 11.4 and 3.3 points. On COCO object detection (236k iterations), ASL achieves 51.5% box AP and 45.7% mask AP, and on ADE20K semantic segmentation it reaches 48.4% mIoU, both exceeding MAE pre-trained on the full ImageNet-1K.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ASL consistently outperforms MAE [22] across all evaluated tasks: +1.8% LP on ImageNet-1K (COCO pre-training), +11.4% LP on ImageNet-100 (4000-epoch training), +1.1% box AP on COCO detection, and +0.3% mIoU on ADE20K. All four hybrid CL+MIM baselines—CMAE [27], ccMIM [67], SIM [54], and iBOT [69]—underperform plain MAE when pre-trained on COCO, confirming the paper's central claim that global-level CL conflicts with patch-level MIM in arbitrary scenarios; ASL beats the best hybrid (ccMIM) by 1.9% LP and 1.3% APb on COCO pre-training. On COCO detection, ASL (51.5% APb at 236k iterations) surpasses MAE (50.4% at 499k iterations), ccMIM (50.3% at 249k), and R-MAE (50.8% at 250k), while also exceeding MoCo v3 [8] (47.9%) and BEiT [1] (49.8%) pre-trained on ImageNet-1K. On ADE20K semantic segmentation, ASL (48.4% mIoU) edges out MAE (48.1%), ccMIM (47.7%), and R-MAE (47.0%), and clearly beats SplitMask [16] (45.7%) and MoCo v3 (47.3%). On small-scale CIFAR-10, ASL achieves 91.4% LP, surpassing DINO [3] (89.0%) and MAE (87.3%) by 2.4 and 4.1 points respectively, and on CIFAR-100 it reaches 60.3% LP vs. MAE's 54.0%.

## Relation to Prior Work

ASL builds directly on the MAE encoder-decoder architecture (He et al., 2021) and repurposes two ideas from contrastive learning—dropout-based feature augmentation from SimCSE (Gao et al., 2021) and the asymmetric dual-branch structure from BYOL (Grill et al., 2020)—but transplants them from the global image level to the patch level. The key departure from prior hybrid methods (iBOT, ccMIM, SIM, CMAE) is a theoretical argument that MAE is inherently a patch-level contrastive learner, making its combination with global CL fundamentally conflicting on non-iconic data. Rather than reinforcing global consistency as all prior hybrids do, ASL abandons global constraints entirely and performs explicit patch-level alignment through a decoder, using L1 loss instead of InfoNCE or cosine similarity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of a structural conflict between MAE's patch-level semantics and global CL's uniform-category assumption provides a principled explanation for why SOTA hybrid methods degrade on natural-scene and arbitrary data, a failure mode that had been observed empirically but not theoretically grounded. ASL's practical recipe (a single transformer layer or dropout for feature augmentation + L1 alignment in the decoder) is lightweight (~1.5× MAE FLOPs) and consistently outperforms both MAE and hybrid baselines across classification, detection, and segmentation. Limitations include evaluation restricted to ViT-B, a relatively small number of pre-training datasets, and a theoretical derivation that relies on a Monte Carlo approximation whose tightness is not formally bounded. Open questions include whether the patch-level framing extends to video, 3D, or multimodal settings, and whether the approach scales to ViT-L or larger where the decoder overhead becomes more relevant.
