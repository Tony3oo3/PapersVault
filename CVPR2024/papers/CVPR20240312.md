## Problem

Existing transfer attack methods achieve strong untargeted transferability but remain ineffective at targeted transfer attacks, where perturbations must mislead a black-box model to a specific target label. The paper identifies that adversarial perturbations overfit to source models with mediocre generalization capability, causing them to rely on model-specific features that do not transfer well. The key question is whether enhancing the source model's generalization can produce more transferable targeted perturbations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240312/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a clean RGB image (224×224 from ImageNet) and a target class label as input, and produces an l∞-bounded adversarial perturbation (budget ε_p = 16/255) that, when added to the image, causes a black-box target classifier to predict the target label. The source model is first enhanced via SASD-WS before perturbation generation, and the actual attack uses the TI-DI-MI procedure (translation-invariant, input diversity via random resize-and-pad, and momentum) with 100 iterations and step size 2/255. The enhanced model remains a single model (not an ensemble), making the attack computationally efficient compared to ensemble-based baselines.

## Method

SASD-WS enhances a single source model through two synergistic components. First, Sharpness-Aware Self-Distillation (SASD) fine-tunes an auxiliary model (mirroring the source architecture) using SAM to minimize both cross-entropy loss and loss sharpness, then distills this auxiliary model into the source model while simultaneously minimizing the source model's distillation loss sharpness, effectively flattening its loss landscape. Second, Weight Scaling (WS) approximates an ensemble of randomly pruned SASD models by simply scaling the SASD model's weights by a ratio p (0.93), leveraging the observation that the average of small weight perturbations around a mean weight yields an output close to the ensemble mean. The flattened loss landscape is hypothesized to cover transferable targeted adversarial examples within its low-loss region, while the weight-scaled model captures general features across architectures. The enhanced single model is then used with TI-DI-MI to generate targeted perturbations, outperforming ensemble-based baselines like GhostNet and LGV by 12.2% on average under black-box settings.

## Results

Under the black-box setting with a single source model, SASD-WS achieves a targeted transfer attack success rate that outperforms the state-of-the-art baselines by 12.2% on average across four source models (Inception-v3, ResNet-50, DenseNet-121, VGGNet-16) and four target models. For example, when attacking ResNet-50 from Inception-v3, SASD-WS reaches 44.4% success rate versus 40.5% (LGV), 8.1% (GhostNet), and 3.5% (RAP). With an ensemble of six source models, SASD-WS further improves by 6.6% on average over baselines, reaching 34.1% against ViT-B/16 and 58.7% against CLIP(RN50). Against real-world Google Cloud Vision, SASD-WS achieves 40.2% targeted attack success rate, exceeding the best baseline (LGV at 31.8%) by 8.4%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SASD-WS consistently outperforms all three baselines across nearly every black-box source-to-target pair. Against LGV (the strongest baseline), SASD-WS gains 3.9–10.3 percentage points, e.g., 70.3% vs. 54.5% (Res50→Inc-v3) and 87.5% vs. 80.8% (Dense121→Res50); LGV is unavailable for VGG16 as source. Against GhostNet, the margin is much larger (typically 20–40+ points), e.g., 44.4% vs. 8.1% (Inc-v3→Res50) and 61.6% vs. 10.4% (Dense121→Inc-v3), reflecting GhostNet's reliance on random dropout ensembles that do not flatten the loss landscape. Against RAP, which rectifies the optimization procedure rather than the source model, SASD-WS leads by 10–30+ points in most settings (e.g., 44.4% vs. 3.5% for Inc-v3→Res50), and RAP in particular degrades sharply under ensemble-source settings (8.2% average in Table 2), underscoring the paper's argument that source-model enhancement is essential for targeted transfer attacks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits within the 'enhancing the source model' branch of transferable adversarial attacks, alongside GhostNet (random dropout ensembles) and LGV (additional training epochs), but departs from them by targeting the source model's loss landscape geometry rather than its ensemble diversity. It integrates three previously separate ideas—Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) for flat minima, knowledge distillation for model-to-model knowledge transfer, and the weight-averaging approximation of Izmailov et al.—into a unified self-enhancement pipeline (SASD-WS) that produces a single enhanced model rather than an ensemble. The key conceptual departure is the explicit hypothesis that a source model with mediocre generalization overfits to model-specific features, thereby producing targeted perturbations that fail to transfer, and that flattening the loss landscape directly addresses this.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 12.2% average improvement in black-box targeted transfer attack success rate is substantial for a task where even state-of-the-art methods achieve modest success rates, and the method's practicality (a single weight-scaled model at attack time, no ensemble overhead) makes it broadly applicable. The demonstrated transferability to adversarially trained models, Vision Transformers, and CLIP architectures, as well as the 40.2% success rate against Google Cloud Vision, underscores a real security concern for deployed systems. Open questions include whether the loss-landscape-flattening insight generalizes to untargeted attacks or other modalities, whether the WS approximation degrades at higher pruning ratios, and whether the approach remains effective when the target model is from a fundamentally different architecture family (e.g., diffusion models).
