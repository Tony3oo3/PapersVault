## Problem

Existing implicit neural representations for video suffer from inadequate alignment of intermediate features with target frames during decoding, because AdaIN-based conditioning couples normalization (which suppresses overfitting) with the affine transform. Additionally, GELU activations limit feature diversity, standard L1/SSIM losses fail to preserve high-frequency details, and the separate optimization of pruning, quantization, and entropy coding in the compression pipeline leads to sub-optimal rate-distortion performance due to inconsistent entropy models between training and inference.

## Input / Output

The framework takes RGB video frames (720×1280 or 1080×1920) and a normalized frame index t ∈ (0,1] as input. A video-specific encoder (e.g., ConvNeXt-based) maps each frame to a compact spatial embedding y_t, while the frame index is mapped through sinusoidal positional encoding (b=1.25, l=80) and a small MLP to produce a temporal embedding z_t. Depending on the task, the output is a reconstructed frame, a compressed bitstream (with bpp measured), an inpainted frame (under disperse or central masks), or an interpolated frame (even-numbered frames from odd-frame training).

## Method

The proposed boosting framework introduces a conditional decoder with two key architectural components: (1) a temporal-aware affine transform (TAT) residual block that generates channel-wise (γ, β) parameters from the temporal embedding z_t and applies them directly to intermediate features without normalization, preserving the overfitting capacity that video INRs rely on; and (2) a sinusoidal NeRV-like (SNeRV) block that replaces GELU with SINE activation to produce more diverse feature maps, using two 3×3-kernel blocks in place of one 5×5-kernel block to achieve a more balanced parameter distribution across decoder stages. Training uses a combined distortion loss of L1, MS-SSIM, and FFT-based frequency-domain L1 to preserve high-frequency details. For compression, a consistent entropy minimization (CEM) scheme employs a network-free Gaussian entropy model (transmitting only mean and variance scalars per weight/embedding) that is identical in training and inference, with symmetric quantization for weights and asymmetric quantization for embeddings, eliminating the train/inference entropy model mismatch present in prior work.

## Results

On the UVG dataset (1080p, 7 videos), HNeRV-Boost achieves 35.68 dB average PSNR at 3M parameters, outperforming HNeRV (33.92 dB) by 1.76 dB; E-NeRV-Boost and NeRV-Boost similarly improve over their baselines by 1.08 dB and 1.20 dB respectively. In video compression, HNeRV-Boost surpasses DCVC, H.265, and H.264 across all bitrates in PSNR on the UVG dataset. For video inpainting on DAVIS, the boosted models yield average gains of 3.92 dB (NeRV), 2.43 dB (E-NeRV), and 4.20 dB (HNeRV) under disperse masks, and 2.17/1.37/1.93 dB under central masks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the three INR baselines (NeRV, E-NeRV, HNeRV), the boosted versions consistently win across all model sizes and all four tasks (regression, compression, inpainting, interpolation), with the largest gains in inpainting (up to 4.2 dB for HNeRV-Boost on DAVIS disperse masks). In video compression RD curves (Fig. 5), HNeRV-Boost surpasses both learning-based codecs DCVC and DCVC-TCM, as well as traditional codecs H.264 and H.265, across all tested bitrates in PSNR. For entropy minimization (Fig. 7), the proposed CEM outperforms both Gomes et al. [14] (which uses asymmetric weight quantization and CABAC at inference) and Maiya et al. [28] (which uses a fixed frequency table), achieving lower bitrate at equivalent distortion by maintaining a consistent network-free Gaussian entropy model between training and inference. The boosted models also demonstrate faster convergence: at 300 epochs, HNeRV-Boost on Bosphorus (36.11 dB) exceeds HNeRV at 2400 epochs (34.46 dB). Decoding latency increases modestly (e.g., HNeRV-Boost: 76 ms vs. HNeRV: 41 ms per frame at 1080p) but remains far faster than DCVC (35590 ms).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a universal boosting framework for the implicit neural representation (INR) paradigm for video, building directly on NeRV, E-NeRV, and HNeRV as its primary baselines. It departs from prior conditional modulation approaches (which use AdaIN, coupling normalization with affine transform) by proposing a pure temporal-aware affine transform (TAT) that discards normalization to preserve overfitting capacity. On the compression side, it refines the entropy minimization pipeline of Gomes et al. and Maiya et al. by replacing their neural surrogate entropy models with a network-free Gaussian model, eliminating the train/inference inconsistency that those methods introduce. The sinusoidal activation (SNeRV) draws on the periodic inductive bias literature (SIREN) but applies it specifically to the upsampling blocks of video INRs to achieve more diverse feature maps and balanced parameter distribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework's universality—plugging into multiple existing INR architectures without redesigning them—makes it a practical tool for the INR video community, and the 8× convergence speedup (300 vs. 2400 epochs) is a significant practical gain for per-video fitting workflows. The CEM technique addresses a genuine inconsistency in prior entropy minimization approaches and extends compression to content-relevant embeddings, which prior methods overlooked. However, the decoding latency roughly doubles or triples (e.g., HNeRV-Boost drops from 24 to 13 FPS), and the per-video training paradigm remains a barrier to real-time or streaming applications. Open questions include whether the framework extends to longer video sequences, whether the TAT module generalizes to other conditioning signals beyond frame index, and whether the increased model complexity can be mitigated without sacrificing the reconstruction gains.
