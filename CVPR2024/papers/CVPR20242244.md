## Problem

Existing NeRF-based inverse rendering methods assume scenes are illuminated exclusively by distant light sources (environment maps or collocated flashlights), leaving scenes with multiple emissive sources unaddressed. Two compounding challenges arise: the limited dynamic range of LDR images makes it ambiguous to distinguish emissive source regions from their nearby reflections, and naively backtracing light paths in volume rendering incurs exponential computational cost with each additional ray bounce.

## Input / Output

The method consumes paired LDR multi-view images (200 views per scene in experiments) captured with all emissive sources simultaneously on and off, requiring no prior knowledge of source count, location, color, or intensity. Beyond standard NeRF outputs (SDF, SVBRDF, environment map), it produces a spatially varying emission field E(x) that explicitly identifies which surface regions are self-emitting, enabling per-source color and intensity editing via binary masks in the HSV color space. The approach operates entirely within the 8-bit LDR range, unlike prior mesh-based methods that depend on HDR inputs.

## Method

ESR-NeRF uses six dedicated neural networks (SDF, SVBRDF parameters, emission, environment map, and two outgoing radiance fields for lights-off and lights-on conditions) built on the Voxurf backbone, and introduces a Light Transport Segments (LTS) loss that constrains each network's predicted radiance to be consistent with the radiance achievable from the scene context (direct/indirect environment illumination, emission, and inter-reflection from emissive sources), effectively replacing explicit ray tracing with learned field consistency. A learnable tone-mapper MLP maps unbounded HDR radiance to [0,1] for the rendering loss, resolving the ambiguity between surface opacity and radiance magnitude that degrades SDF reconstruction. The key mechanism for resolving emission-vs-reflection ambiguity is a progressive ray-grouping scheme: training rays are partitioned into 'certain' (confirmed reflective) and 'uncertain' (undetermined) groups, where the certain group enforces reflection-only radiance (suppressing emission) to propagate energy to influential points, while the uncertain group adjusts emission based on the updated context; groups are re-partitioned at fixed intervals by thresholding expected emission strength, iteratively shrinking the uncertain set until only true emissive regions remain.

## Results

ESR-NeRF achieves the best emissive source identification across all 12 synthetic scenes, reaching IoU up to 0.97 and MSE as low as 0.03, substantially outperforming TensoIR (best IoU 0.95, MSE 0.03) and NeILF++ (best IoU 0.95, MSE 0.78). In scene editing under combined intensity and color modification (NV+I+C), ESR-NeRF attains 31.63 PSNR / 0.0199 LPIPS, surpassing TensoIR (25.18 / 0.0531) and NeILF++ (23.24 / 0.0770). On the DTU dataset for surface reconstruction without emissive sources, ESR-NeRF achieves a mean Chamfer Distance of 0.65, outperforming Neural-PBIR (0.68), Voxurf (0.72), and NeuS (0.77).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ESR-NeRF wins against all seven baselines across every evaluation setting. Against TensoIR (CVPR 2023), ESR-NeRF gains +6.45 PSNR on NV+I+C editing (31.63 vs 25.18) and consistently higher IoU in source identification (e.g., 0.81 vs 0.71 on Lego-white). Against NeILF++ (ICCV 2023), the margin is even larger: +8.39 PSNR on NV+I+C (31.63 vs 23.24), with NeILF++ suffering near-zero IoU on several scenes due to emissive source ambiguity. The Twins baseline (separate on/off models) achieves 28.21 PSNR on NV+I+C, still 3.42 PSNR below ESR-NeRF, and introduces physically incorrect color mixing on reflective surfaces. PaletteNeRF (CVPR 2023) lags by 9.05 PSNR on NV+I+C (22.58 vs 31.63) because its palette-based recolorization cannot independently control individual light sources. NeRF-W (CVPR 2021) is excluded from color editing entirely as it lacks per-source control. On DTU surface reconstruction, ESR-NeRF (0.65 CD) outperforms Neural-PBIR (0.68), Voxurf (0.72), and NeuS (0.77), confirming that the LTS loss improves geometry even without emissive sources.

## Relation to Prior Work

ESR-NeRF builds on the Voxurf SDF-based volume rendering architecture and the Disney BRDF model for surface representation, while departing fundamentally from prior NeRF-based inverse rendering methods (TensoIR, NeILF++, Neural-PBIR) that assume scenes are lit exclusively by distant sources like environment maps. It also diverges from mesh-based emissive source reconstruction methods (Azinovic et al., Haefner et al., Philip et al.) by operating within the volume rendering framework and using LDR images rather than HDR inputs with known geometry. Conceptually, the use of neural networks to represent ray-traced fields echoes NeuralRadiosity, but ESR-NeRF repurposes this idea for inverse rendering—specifically to enforce light transport segment consistency and progressively separate emission from reflection, a problem no prior NeRF-based method addresses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method to reconstruct an arbitrary number of emissive sources from LDR multi-view images within a NeRF framework, opening the door to physically-based scene editing (color/intensity control of individual lights) that was previously infeasible without HDR capture or known geometry. The progressive discovery mechanism for resolving the emission-vs-reflection ambiguity in 8-bit images is a practical contribution that could inform other inverse rendering settings with under-exposed or saturated regions. Key open questions include extending to unbounded indoor scenes, disentangling emissive sources from environmental lighting under a single capture condition, and whether LTS-based relighting can faithfully represent novel colors traversing unobserved light paths—limitations the authors acknowledge may require hybrid approaches with explicit path tracers like Mitsuba.
