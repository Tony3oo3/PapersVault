## Problem

Real-world privacy and bias-removal requests arrive as a sequence over time, requiring a pre-trained model to be repeatedly edited to erase specific knowledge while preserving everything else. Existing machine unlearning methods are either computationally prohibitive at scale, require impractical pre-training modifications, or cannot handle multiple sequential erasure requests. This paper formalizes this setting as 'continual forgetting' and identifies two core challenges: efficient deletion of unwanted knowledge and minimal collateral damage to retained knowledge.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a frozen pre-trained vision model (Face Transformer or Deformable DETR), a small forgetting dataset D_f specifying which classes to erase, and a small rehearsal buffer D_r of retained-class samples, with the constraint |D_f| + |D_r| << |D| (original training set). The output is a modified model in which the output FFN layers remain frozen (ensuring backbone-level, hard-to-recover forgetting) and only LoRA modules in the FFN layers carry the update. Evaluation uses H-Mean, which jointly rewards high accuracy on retained classes and large accuracy drop on forgotten classes, plus separate Acc_f/Acc_r or AP_f/AP_r metrics.

## Method

GS-LoRA attaches low-rank LoRA modules to the FFN linear layers of every Transformer block for each forgetting task, keeping the original weights frozen. A group Lasso regularizer treats all LoRA parameters within one Transformer block as a single group, automatically selecting a sparse subset of blocks to modify while zeroing out the rest, thereby limiting the modification to a small, localized region of the network. A stepwise sparsity warmup (alpha = 0 for the first K epochs, then alpha = alpha_K) lets the model escape local minima before the sparsity constraint kicks in. The data loss combines a ReLU-bounded negative loss on forgotten classes (selective forgetting loss) with a standard loss on the rehearsal buffer (knowledge retention loss), weighted by hyperparameter beta. With rank-8 LoRA, fewer than 1% of total parameters are trainable, and the method achieves near-original performance on retained classes while driving forgotten-class accuracy to near zero across face recognition and object detection.

## Results

On face recognition (CASIA-Face100), GS-LoRA achieves H-Mean of 72.2 while forgetting 90% of classes (100-90 setting), retaining 70.5% accuracy on remaining classes (vs. 73.8% pre-train) with only 1.28% tunable parameters. On object detection (COCO 2017), GS-LoRA reaches H-Mean of 49.9 when forgetting 1 class out of 80, maintaining 44.5 AP on remaining classes (vs. 44.3 pre-train) with just 0.62% tunable parameters. In the continual forgetting setting (4 sequential tasks of 20 classes each), GS-LoRA sustains H-Mean of 70.2 after all tasks while keeping old-task accuracy at 0.0%, outperforming all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GS-LoRA outperforms all 11 baselines on H-Mean across every forgetting setting. The closest competitor, SCRUB-S [34], achieves comparable H-Mean on small forgetting (68.6 vs. 69.3 on 100-5) but collapses severely as more classes are forgotten, dropping to 54.8 H on 100-50 and 19.0 on 100-90, while GS-LoRA maintains 71.9 and 72.2 respectively. Regularization-based continual learning methods (EWC [33], MAS [2], LwF [39], DER++ [8], FDR [4]) all degrade substantially on remaining-class accuracy as the forgetting ratio increases (e.g., EWC drops from 68.8 to 33.6 Acc_r from 100-5 to 100-90), whereas GS-LoRA stays within 3–4 points of pre-train performance. On object detection (Table 2), GS-LoRA beats the strongest baseline (Retrain) by 3.3 H-Mean on 80-1 (49.9 vs. 46.6) and by 5.9 on 80-70 (43.7 vs. 37.8), while using only 0.62% tunable parameters versus 100% for retraining. LIRF [74] fails to forget effectively, retaining 51–57% accuracy on forgotten classes, indicating it is unsuitable for this task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) techniques—specifically LoRA—for the inverse task of knowledge erasure rather than adaptation, drawing on Geva et al.'s finding that FFN layers store class-specific knowledge. The group sparse regularization mechanism is adapted from structured sparsity literature (group Lasso) to automatically select which Transformer blocks' LoRA modules should be modified, while the ReLU-bounded forgetting loss follows Du et al. The key conceptual departure from prior machine unlearning work is the framing of forgetting as a lightweight, sequential, parameter-efficient process rather than a computationally heavy retraining or influence-function computation, and the explicit distinction from continual learning (which adds knowledge) by inverting the objective to delete knowledge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The continual forgetting formulation addresses a practically urgent need driven by privacy regulations (GDPR, CCPA) where erasure requests arrive sequentially and must be handled promptly without full retraining. The sub-1% parameter modification and minimal replay data requirements make the approach viable for large deployed models, a significant practical advantage over prior unlearning methods. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to large language models (acknowledged as future work), whether block-level group sparsity is the right granularity for very deep or wide architectures, and how the method behaves when retention data is entirely unavailable for some classes. The reliance on a small rehearsal buffer, while much smaller than full retraining, still represents a data requirement that may be infeasible in strict privacy settings.
