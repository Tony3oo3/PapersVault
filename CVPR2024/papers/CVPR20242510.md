## Problem

Most learned physics simulators rely on graph neural networks that treat 3D geometry as an afterthought, embedding coordinates as additional features rather than as the fundamental structure of the computation. Since physical phenomena like gravity and collision depend critically on spatial relationships in 3D, this paper asks whether point cloud convolutions—which natively operate on 3D coordinates and support continuous interpolation—can outperform GNN-based approaches for modeling multi-object collision dynamics.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242510/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a set of 3D points (either densely sampled surface points or sparse mesh vertices), each carrying a 2-step velocity history and the z-coordinate appended to the feature vector, and produces point-wise velocity or acceleration predictions for the next timestep. For mesh inputs, the network additionally generates features at dynamically selected interaction points on mesh faces that are not part of the original input, enabling face-to-face collision reasoning. Predictions are made autoregressively, and for rigid objects a single rigid-body transformation is estimated from pointwise predictions to preserve shape during rollouts.

## Method

The architecture is a U-Net built from two novel PointConv variants: Object PointConv propagates features within a single object using k-NN or mesh-vertex neighborhoods, while Relational PointConv propagates features across different objects using a distance-filtered neighborhood with normalization by neighbor count. Grid downsampling creates a multi-level hierarchy where bottleneck layers at coarse resolution capture long-range interactions, and the decoder upsamples via PointConv interpolation back to the original point locations. For mesh inputs, a three-layer PointConv sequence computes features at dynamically selected interaction points on nearby mesh faces and propagates them back to the receiver face's vertices, avoiding the need for explicit face nodes. The key mechanistic difference from GNNs is that PointConv generates filter weights multiplicatively from relative coordinates, enabling the network to learn direction-dependent force attenuation that is difficult to capture through simple positional embedding concatenation.

## Results

On the Physion benchmark (contact prediction accuracy), the proposed PointConv U-Net achieves 81.3% average (velocity prediction) and 84.4% average (acceleration prediction) across 7 rigid-body scenarios, outperforming the prior state-of-the-art SGNN (79.4% / 77.0% including Drape). The velocity-prediction variant is especially strong in gravity- and collision-heavy scenarios: Drop (89.8% vs. 74.3% for SGNN), Collide (92.0% vs. 85.3%), and Drape (75.8% vs. 60.6%, a +15.2% gain). On the Kubric dataset (Euclidean distance, lower is better), the PointConv U-Net with dense point-cloud input achieves 1.87 on Movi-A and 2.18 on Movi-C for acceleration prediction, outperforming the GNN U-Net baseline (2.20 / 2.55).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SGNN [14] (the strongest prior GNN baseline), the proposed method wins in 4 of 7 rigid-body scenarios with velocity prediction and 5 of 7 with acceleration prediction, yielding average gains of +1.9% and +5.0% respectively; the largest single-scenario margin is +15.2% on the non-rigid Drape task (75.8% vs. 60.6%). The only scenario where SGNN edges out Ours (vel) is Link (73.3% vs. 55.1%), which the authors attribute to label noise in that training set. Both DPI [17] and GNS [27] are substantially outperformed across nearly all scenarios, with Ours (acc) exceeding DPI by 10+ points on average. An internal ablation (Table 2) further shows that replacing GNN message-passing with PointConv within the same U-Net architecture improves accuracy by 1.6–24.5 points depending on scenario, with the largest gains in gravity-dependent tasks (Support: +24.5, Contain: +17.3), confirming that the multiplicative coordinate–feature interaction in PointConv is the key advantage over positional-embedding-based GNNs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant GNN-based paradigm (GNS, DPI, SGNN, FIGNet) for learning 3D object dynamics by repurposing the PointConv continuous convolution operator as the fundamental building block. It extends PointConv with two specialized variants—Object PointConv for within-object force propagation and Relational PointConv for cross-object collision effects—and wraps them in a U-Net architecture with grid downsampling for hierarchical multi-scale representation. The mesh collision approach, which dynamically generates features at interaction points on faces via PointConv's interpolation property, is a novel departure from prior GNN methods that require explicit face nodes or dense surface sampling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete bridge between the GNN and point cloud communities for physics simulation, showing that the multiplicative coordinate-feature relationship in continuous convolution is particularly effective for gravity and collision reasoning—scenarios where GNNs with concatenated positional embeddings struggle. The finding that explicit face-to-face collision reasoning is unnecessary when meshes are densely sampled (Movi-C) suggests that the interaction-point mechanism may be a targeted solution for sparse representations rather than a universal requirement. Open questions include whether rotation equivariance can be incorporated without sacrificing the architectural advantages, and whether the approach scales to scenes with many more objects or longer prediction horizons.
