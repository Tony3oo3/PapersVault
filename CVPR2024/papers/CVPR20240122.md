## Problem

Prevailing explanations for neural network generalization rely on implicit biases of gradient descent, yet these cannot account for good generalization from gradient-free optimization or the simplicity bias already present in untrained networks. This paper asks what architecture-level inductive biases exist independently of any optimization procedure, and whether the simplicity bias is a universal property of all nonlinear architectures or depends on specific components.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240122/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a fully specified network architecture (activation functions, depth, width, presence of residual connections, layer normalization, multiplicative gating) with weights sampled from a uniform Glorot prior scaled by a tunable magnitude factor α, and evaluates it on a regular grid of points within the unit hypercube [-1,+1]^d. For 2D inputs, the output is a grayscale function map plus three scalar complexity scores: a frequency-weighted average from the discrete Fourier transform, an order-weighted average from Chebyshev/Legendre/Hermite polynomial decompositions, and the Lempel-Ziv dictionary size of the output sequence. For transformer experiments, the output is the LZ complexity of 100-token sequences generated by greedy decoding from a single random prompt token.

## Method

The paper introduces the Neural Redshift (NRS) hypothesis: the parameter space of popular architectures is overwhelmingly concentrated on functions of low complexity (low Fourier frequency, low polynomial order, high compressibility), analogous to a physical redshift biasing observations toward low frequencies. By sampling random weights and evaluating networks on input grids without any training, the authors show that ReLU-like activations, residual connections, and layer normalization each independently push the preferred complexity toward low values, while other activations (TanH, Gaussian, sine) and multiplicative interactions push it higher in a depth- and magnitude-dependent way. They demonstrate that this architecture-level bias predicts trained-model behavior: on modulo-arithmetic tasks of increasing frequency, only architectures whose initialization complexity matches the target's complexity generalize, and a multiplicative prefactor before activations can shift this bias to recover generalization. Finally, they show untrained GPT-2 inherits the same component-level biases (GELU, layer norm scaling, depth) that control sequence compressibility, confirming the NRS extends to transformers.

## Results

The paper's central finding is that ReLU MLPs with random weights exhibit near-zero variance in complexity across samples: virtually all random ReLU networks implement low-frequency, low-order, compressible functions, and this bias is insensitive to depth and weight magnitude. On the modulo-arithmetic task (M=10, 7, 4 of increasing frequency), ReLU MLPs generalize only on the lowest-frequency version (M=10), while Gaussian, TanH, and sine-activated MLPs with a correctly tuned multiplicative prefactor solve all three versions. On Colored-MNIST, digit-recognition accuracy peaks at an intermediate prefactor value (the 'sweet spot' where architectural complexity matches target complexity), while color accuracy is highest at low prefactors. For untrained GPT-2, default GELU activations yield the lowest LZ complexity among tested activations, with ReLU decreasing it further and TanH/sine/SELU increasing it.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baselines but instead contrasts architectural choices as the primary axis of comparison. ReLU (and ReLU-like GELU/Swish/SELU) activations produce the strongest and most stable simplicity bias, maintaining low complexity across all depths and weight magnitudes, whereas TanH, Gaussian, and sine activations show complexity that increases dramatically with depth and weight magnitude. Layer normalization removes the sensitivity to weight magnitude for all activations, and residual connections force complexity to the lowest levels regardless of activation. Multiplicative interactions (gating) increase complexity even for ReLU networks. On the modulo-arithmetic task, ReLU MLPs fail to generalize on higher-frequency versions (M=7, M=4) despite achieving perfect training accuracy, while non-ReLU activations with a tuned prefactor succeed, demonstrating that the simplicity bias is both real and controllable. On Colored-MNIST, low-complexity models (small prefactor) rely on the spurious color feature, while medium-complexity models learn the digit, confirming the architecture–target complexity matching hypothesis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant framing that attributes NN generalization to implicit biases of gradient descent, and from the spectral/simplicity bias literature (Rahaman et al., Arpit et al., Goldblum et al.) which confounds architectural and optimization effects. It extends the random-weight analysis of Chiang et al. (2022) and De Palma et al. (2019) by examining a much wider range of architectures, components, and complexity measures (Fourier, polynomial, LZ), and by showing that the previously assumed universal 'simplicity bias' is actually an emergent property of specific components (ReLU, residual connections, layer normalization) rather than an inherent feature of all nonlinear networks. The 'Neural Redshift' framing explicitly separates parametrization-level biases from optimization-level biases, a distinction prior work had not made cleanly.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a component-level account of why popular architectures generalize well, suggesting that the historical adoption of ReLUs, residual connections, and layer normalization reflects a collective (if implicit) search for low-complexity inductive biases rather than purely numerical stability. This opens a practical avenue for controlling inductive biases at initialization to improve generalization on complex tasks or mitigate shortcut learning, as demonstrated with modulo arithmetic and Colored-MNIST. Key limitations include reliance on small models and 2D visualizations, purely empirical (non-analytical) methodology, and the unresolved question of how architecture-level biases interact with the well-studied implicit biases of (S)GD during training. The fine-grained 'shape' of preferred functions beyond scalar complexity measures remains uncharacterized.
