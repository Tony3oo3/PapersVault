## Problem

Separating direct and global illumination components from a scene is essential for shape recovery and material understanding, but existing multi-frame methods require 20+ synchronized frames with a stationary scene (≥833 ms capture), while single-frame methods are ill-posed and produce lower-quality results. The paper asks whether the high temporal resolution of event cameras can capture the rapid brightness changes from a sweeping shadow fast enough to achieve multi-frame separation quality at single-frame capture speed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242602/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB starting frame (converted to YUV) and a grayscale event stream recorded while a line occluder sweeps across the scene within one interframe interval (~33 ms at 30 fps). The event stream is asynchronous, sparse, and inherently noisy with spatially-varying thresholds. The output is a pair of full-color (RGB) images: the direct illumination component and the global illumination component, both at the resolution of the input frame.

## Method

The pipeline first reconstructs per-pixel maximum and minimum grayscale brightness values from the event stream via event accumulation (Eq. 2), yielding a coarse but noisy separation (Eq. 4). A U-Net-based EvSepNet then refines this coarse result by fusing the starting frame and an event stack in a multi-scale manner, with Separation Correction Blocks (spatial attention via 11×11 convolutions on pooled channel statistics) at each decoder stage to handle high-frequency global illumination violations. A second network, ImColorNet, performs chrominance compensation using Color Correction Blocks built on transposed self-attention (C×C attention maps over channel features) to recover the distinct color signatures of direct and global components that differ from the original frame's U/V channels. Both networks are trained jointly in a supervised manner using Nayar et al.'s multi-frame results as pseudo ground truth, with a combined MSE + Laplacian pyramid + VGG-19 perceptual loss.

## Results

On the EvDiG dataset (230 indoor scenes, 1800 shadow-sweep clips), EvDiG achieves the best PSNR for direct components (30.01 dB) and the best LPIPS for both direct (0.077) and global (0.117) components. For global components, SF-pattern-deep [6] attains slightly higher PSNR (32.96 vs. 31.66) and SSIM (0.867 vs. 0.846), but produces blurry textures with checkerboard artifacts that are penalized by LPIPS. EvDiG also matches the performance of the multi-frame shadow-sweeping method (MF-shadow-classic) while reducing data capture time by approximately 20× (from ~1 s to ~33 ms).

## Baselines & Comparisons

EvDiG outperforms all four baselines on direct-component PSNR (30.01 vs. 28.51 for the strongest baseline SF-pattern-deep) and on LPIPS for both components (0.077/0.117 vs. 0.208/0.228 for SF-pattern-deep), indicating substantially better perceptual quality. Against the multi-frame method MF-shadow-classic [25], EvDiG improves direct PSNR by nearly 10 dB (30.01 vs. 20.59) and global PSNR by over 11 dB (31.66 vs. 19.98), while reducing capture time from ~1 s to ~33 ms. The single-frame deep method SF-scene-deep [26] is surpassed on all three metrics for direct components and on LPIPS for global components, though it retains a slight SSIM edge for global (0.807 vs. 0.846 is actually EvDiG winning; correction: SF-scene-deep scores 0.807 vs. EvDiG 0.846, so EvDiG wins). SF-pattern-deep [6] is the closest competitor, achieving higher global PSNR (32.96) and SSIM (0.867) due to the inherent smoothness of global components, but its checkerboard artifacts and blurry textures result in markedly worse LPIPS scores (0.208/0.228 vs. 0.077/0.117), confirming that EvDiG produces more faithful separations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the high-frequency illumination framework of Nayar et al. [25] for direct-global separation by replacing the multi-frame RGB capture with event camera data, leveraging the event-triggering model to reconstruct min/max brightness from a single starting frame plus an event stream. Conceptually, it sits at the intersection of two lines of work: (1) active-illumination separation methods that require multiple frames and a static scene, and (2) the growing body of event-camera applications in physics-based vision (structured light, shape from polarization, photometric stereo). The two-stage network design (EvSepNet with Separation Correction Blocks for denoising and high-frequency global illumination handling, ImColorNet with transposed self-attention for chrominance compensation) draws on U-Net architectures for event-image fusion and channel-wise attention for color correction, but is the first to combine these for the direct-global separation task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is a 20× reduction in data capture time (from ~1 s to ~33 ms) while matching multi-frame separation quality, which opens direct-global separation to dynamic scenes and real-time applications where keeping a scene static is infeasible. The first real-world event+image dataset for this task (230 scenes, 1800 clips) provides a resource the community lacked. Key limitations remain: the pseudo ground truth is derived from Nayar et al.'s method (circular evaluation), motion-induced events are not modeled, and the smooth global illumination assumption still fails in extreme cases (e.g., disco ball interreflections), though EvSepNet partially addresses this. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to multi-light-source setups, whether learned priors can replace the need for a physical occluder, and how the method performs under significant scene motion.
