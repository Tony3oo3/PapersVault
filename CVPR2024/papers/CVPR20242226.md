## Problem

Existing zero-shot video editing methods rely on DDIM inversion that either ignores temporal context entirely (2D per-frame) or uses only a 2-frame spatial-temporal context, leading to inaccurate reconstruction and flickering during editing. No prior approach captures the full global spatial-temporal structure of a video during inversion without incurring prohibitive computational cost from attending over all frames.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242226/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a video clip of 40–200 frames at 512×512 or 360×640 resolution, a source text prompt describing the input video, and a target text prompt specifying the desired edit. It operates entirely in the latent space of Stable Diffusion 1.5 (VAE-encoded features) with 50 DDIM inversion steps and classifier-free guidance of 7.5, producing a temporally consistent edited video at the same resolution and frame count. No training or fine-tuning is required; the method is a drop-in replacement for the DDIM inversion step in existing editing pipelines.

## Method

STEM inversion formulates the dense video feature (N×H×W×C) as a low-rank representation with K=256 learned bases, estimated via an EM algorithm where the E step computes soft responsibilities (Gaussian-weighted assignment of each pixel to each basis) and the M step updates bases via responsibility-weighted averaging, iterated R=3 times. The converged bases replace the Key and Value projections in every self-attention layer of the UNet (STEM-SA), so each frame attends to a fixed global representation rather than a time-varying 1- or 2-frame context, reducing self-attention complexity from O(N(HW)²) to O(NHWK). This inversion is then plugged directly into existing zero-shot editing methods (TokenFlow, FateZero) by substituting their DDIM inversion step, requiring no changes to the editing or denoising logic itself.

## Results

On the DAVIS dataset, STEM-TokenFlow achieves the lowest warp error (3.5×10⁻³) among all compared methods while maintaining a competitive CLIP score of 0.31, and STEM-FateZero achieves a warp error of 4.3×10⁻³ with a CLIP score of 0.30. In the user study, STEM-FateZero is preferred over FateZero for edit fidelity (83% vs 17%) and temporal consistency (67% vs 33%), while STEM-TokenFlow is preferred over TokenFlow for edit fidelity (75% vs 25%) and temporal consistency (67% vs 33%). For reconstruction quality, STEM inversion consistently outperforms DDIM inversion in both PSNR and SSIM across all frames.

## Baselines & Comparisons

STEM-TokenFlow outperforms TokenFlow [10] on warp error (3.5 vs 4.9×10⁻³) while matching its CLIP score (0.31), and STEM-FateZero outperforms FateZero [31] on both metrics (CLIP 0.30 vs 0.29, warp error 4.3 vs 7.2×10⁻³). Against Tune-A-Video [47], both STEM variants achieve substantially lower warp error (3.5/4.3 vs 32.3×10⁻³) and higher CLIP scores (0.31/0.30 vs 0.28). Compared to Pix2Video [4], STEM-TokenFlow achieves a slightly lower CLIP score (0.31 vs 0.32) but a significantly lower warp error (3.5 vs 6.2×10⁻³), while STEM-FateZero trails on CLIP (0.30 vs 0.32) but leads on warp error (4.3 vs 6.2). Against Text2Video-Zero [19], both STEM methods match or exceed the CLIP score (0.31/0.30 vs 0.31) and achieve dramatically lower warp error (3.5/4.3 vs 22.7×10⁻³). Overall, the STEM inversion drop-in replacement consistently improves temporal consistency (warp error) without sacrificing edit fidelity (CLIP score) across all base editing pipelines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the classical EM algorithm (Dempster et al., 1977) to reformulate the DDIM inversion step in zero-shot video editing, replacing the standard per-frame or two-frame spatial-temporal context with a fixed global low-rank basis set of 256 vectors. It builds directly on the Stable Diffusion + DDIM inversion framework and integrates as a drop-in replacement into two existing zero-shot editing pipelines—TokenFlow (which uses 2D DDIM inversion) and FateZero (which uses naive spatial-temporal DDIM inversion)—without modifying their editing logic. Conceptually, it departs from prior work by arguing that a compact, time-invariant representation learned over the entire video is more stable for temporal consistency than the time-varying local context that existing methods employ.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

STEM inversion offers a practical, training-free upgrade to any DDIM-based video editing pipeline, reducing self-attention complexity from O(N(HW)²) to O(NHWK) while improving reconstruction fidelity and temporal consistency. The main limitation acknowledged by the authors is the fixed basis count (K=256), which may be insufficient for very long or highly diverse videos; an adaptive basis selection strategy is left as future work. More broadly, the finding that a small set of global bases can outperform per-frame or two-frame local context for temporal coherence challenges the prevailing assumption in video diffusion editing that local temporal modeling is the primary driver of consistency.
