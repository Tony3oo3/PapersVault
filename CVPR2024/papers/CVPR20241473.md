## Problem

Open-vocabulary object detectors are routinely evaluated on standard benchmarks (COCO, LVIS) with generic class labels, yet their ability to discern fine-grained object properties—such as color, material, pattern, or transparency—remains unexplored. No prior work systematically probes whether these detectors can correctly assign a detailed natural-language description to an object when confounded by semantically similar hard-negative captions that differ in only one or a few attributes.

## Input / Output

The evaluation protocol takes an RGB image and a dynamic per-object vocabulary of text captions (one positive caption plus N negative captions generated by attribute substitution) as input, and produces bounding boxes with per-caption confidence scores. Inference is performed per object group sharing the same positive caption rather than with a single global vocabulary, and post-processing uses class-agnostic NMS so that an incorrect higher-confidence prediction suppresses the correct one. Evaluation reports COCO mAP and a Median Rank metric that measures where the correct caption falls in the ranked confidence list across all vocabulary entries.

## Method

The paper introduces a benchmark suite built on PACO's structured object annotations (attributes and parts), where positive captions are generated by prompting OpenAssistant-LLAMA-30B with the structured descriptions, and negative captions are produced via targeted attribute substitution to preserve syntactic consistency. Eight benchmarks are constructed: four Difficulty-based (Trivial, Easy, Medium, Hard) that vary the number of substituted attributes (random captions, 3, 2, 1 attributes changed respectively), and four Attribute-based (Color, Material, Pattern, Transparency) that alter only a single attribute type. The evaluation protocol assigns each ground-truth object its own vocabulary, performs separate forward passes per object group, and applies class-agnostic NMS to ensure hard negatives properly penalize incorrect predictions. Eight state-of-the-art open-vocabulary detectors (OWL, OWLv2, Detic, ViLD, GroundingDino, CORA) are evaluated, revealing that even the strongest performers on standard benchmarks drop dramatically in mAP when faced with fine-grained hard negatives, with color being the most recognizable attribute and pattern/transparency the least.

## Results

On the FG-OVD difficulty-based benchmarks (N=5 negatives), the best-performing detector OWL(L/14) achieves only 26.5 mAP on the Hard setting, while Detic — the top performer on standard LVIS Rare (39.9 mAP) — drops to just 11.5 mAP on Hard. On the Trivial benchmark (random negatives), Detic leads with 69.7 mAP, but this advantage reverses completely under hard-negative conditions. Across all detectors, performance degrades sharply as negative difficulty increases, with the gap between Trivial and Hard mAP exceeding 40 points for Detic and ~39 points for OWL(L/14).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates six state-of-the-art open-vocabulary detectors on the proposed FG-OVD benchmarks. OWL(L/14) and OWLv2(L/14) are the most robust under hard-negative conditions, achieving 26.5 and 25.4 mAP respectively on Hard, outperforming Detic (11.5), CORA (13.8), and GDino (16.6) by 10–15 points. Detic, despite leading on LVIS Rare (39.9 mAP) and Trivial (69.7 mAP), suffers the steepest degradation as negatives become harder, indicating its image-level supervision training sacrifices fine-grained attribute discrimination. OWLv2 shows only marginal gains over OWL (e.g., 25.4 vs. 26.5 on Hard), suggesting that scaling to 10B web-scale images adds visual robustness but fails to inject discriminative attribute information. On attribute-specific benchmarks (Table 3), color is the easiest attribute for most detectors (OWLv2(L/14) reaches 53.3 mAP), while pattern and transparency remain challenging for all methods, with OWLv2(L/14) dropping to 23.3 and 12.2 mAP respectively. CORA consistently underperforms across all settings, evidencing that even the most recent architectures struggle with fine-grained label discrimination.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the open-vocabulary object detection (OVD) literature by introducing a fine-grained evaluation protocol that departs from the standard fixed-vocabulary setup used in COCO/LVIS benchmarks. It builds on the PACO dataset's structured object-part-attribute annotations and repurposes them via LLM-generated natural language captions, while drawing on the hard-negative evaluation philosophy of attribute detection benchmarks like OVAD and VAW. Conceptually, it bridges OVD with Referring Expression Comprehension and Phrase Grounding, but explicitly distinguishes itself by requiring discriminative ability among semantically close labels rather than simple grounding of an unambiguous phrase. The dynamic per-object vocabulary with attribute-substituted negatives is the key methodological departure from all prior OVD evaluation protocols.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a critical disconnect: detectors that excel on standard OVD benchmarks (e.g., Detic at 39.9 mAP on LVIS rare) are the worst at fine-grained discrimination (11.5 mAP on Hard), suggesting that current OVD progress may be largely driven by class-level recognition rather than genuine attribute understanding. The near-zero gain from OWLv2's 10B-image self-supervised training over OWL further indicates that scale alone does not inject fine-grained attribute discriminability. This opens concrete research directions—few-shot contrastive fine-tuning, diffusion-model latent probing, and targeted attribute-aware pre-training—that the authors outline but leave unexplored. The benchmark suite itself is a reusable asset for the community to track progress on fine-grained OVD.
