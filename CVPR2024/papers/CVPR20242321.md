## Problem

Procedural models that generate synthetic training data fail to close the domain gap with real-world imagery, yet the field lacks an accessible, high-resolution benchmark to study and iterate on this gap. Existing architectural datasets are limited to 512–1025 pixel resolutions where window details are unresolvable, and large-scale synthetic datasets like SynthIA require engineering resources beyond most academic teams. WinSyn addresses this by providing a constrained (window-only), high-resolution (4K–6K) real-and-synthetic testbed where the procedural-modeling domain gap can be measured and ablated efficiently.

## Input / Output

The procedural model consumes 216–21,735 randomly sampled parameters (governing grammar rule sequences, materials, camera position, and lighting) and produces 21,290 synthetic window images at 4K resolution, each accompanied by per-pixel segmentation labels (10 classes), depth maps, normal maps, and edge maps. For evaluation, the segmentation network takes 512×512 resized RGB window crops as input and outputs per-pixel class predictions. The real dataset comprises 75,739 photographs (up to 4K×6K) yielding 89,318 single-window crops, of which 9,002 carry hand-annotated panoptic segmentation labels at an average cost of $3.90 per image.

## Method

The procedural model combines two grammar layers: a CGA-based split grammar subdivides the building volume into façades, shutters, balconies, and window bounds, while a second Split Shape Grammar with Bézier spline curves generates the window geometry itself (trapezoid, arched, circular shapes), extruding profiles along curves to form frames and glass panes. Scenes are rendered with Blender's Cycles physically-based renderer using procedural PBR shaders, skybox/interior panoramic lighting, and domain randomization to extend beyond typical real-world variation. Evaluation trains a BEiT base model (pre-trained on ImageNet-1k/21k) on 2,048 synthetic or real images and measures mIoU over 10 labels on 4,906 held-out real images. A systematic ablation over 64 variations and 156,903 renders identifies rendering samples-per-pixel (spp), material fidelity, and lighting as the dominant factors, while window geometry shape has minimal impact. The key empirical finding is that synthetic data saturates in performance at ~2,048 samples while real data continues to improve, and no mixture ratio of synthetic and real data closes the ~27-point mIoU gap, indicating the gap is structural rather than a data-volume problem.

## Results

On the WinSyn global test set (4,906 real images), the procedural model's synthetic data achieves 31.23 mIoU, compared to 53.79 mIoU for a model trained on all real data and 58.69 mIoU for the full real-photo evaluation. The synthetic data's performance range across locales (29.33–35.02 mIoU) is notably narrower than real-data training (25.23–61.85 mIoU), indicating the synthetic model does not overfit to any particular region. The ~22.5 mIoU gap between synthetic and global real data is comparable to the gap between training on a single locale (e.g., UK: 37.76) versus the global set, underscoring that the procedural model captures roughly one region's worth of architectural diversity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates four additional approaches to close the synthetic–real domain gap: DeepLabv3+ without pre-training [10], Label Adaptation using real labels during training [39], MIC for unlabeled-data domain adaptation [20], and Histogram Matching for color adjustment [17]. The central finding is that none of these techniques substantially reduce the ~22 mIoU gap between synthetic (31.23) and global real data (53.79); the authors explicitly state that 'the synthetic-real domain gap persists' under all of these approaches. This suggests that the limitation is not in the segmentation architecture or post-hoc adaptation strategy but in the procedural model's inability to replicate the full spatial and material diversity of real windows. The synthetic data does outperform single-locale real training on some test splits (e.g., synthetic→Egypt: 31.34 vs. UK→Egypt: 25.23), confirming it captures a broader but shallower distribution than any one region. The authors conclude that network architecture and data partitioning are orthogonal to the core challenge of improving procedural model fidelity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the procedural building modeling lineage of Split Shape Grammars (Wonka et al., 2003) and the CGA language (Mueller et al., 2006) by applying them to a deliberately constrained sub-domain—individual windows—rather than full urban scenes. It departs from large-scale synthetic-to-real efforts like SynthIA and CARLA, which require massive engineering resources, by arguing that a simpler, focused domain enables academic-scale procedural modeling research. For evaluation, it adopts standard segmentation backbones (BEiT/BEiTv2, DeepLabv3+) and benchmarks against domain-adaptation techniques (label adaptation, MIC, histogram matching) from prior synthetic-data literature, positioning itself as a controlled testbed rather than a new segmentation method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key open question the paper surfaces is whether current procedural modeling techniques can ever replicate the spatial semantics of real-world architecture at high resolution, and the evidence strongly suggests they cannot yet—no architecture, data-mixing ratio, or adaptation technique closes the gap. The finding that the synthetic model's performance is comparable to training on a single geographic locale (e.g., Egypt) but far below the global real-data ceiling (53.79 vs. 31.23 mIoU) provides a concrete, reproducible calibration point for future procedural graphics research. The 4K–6K resolution and 9,002 hand-annotated labels also open avenues for depth estimation, material/reflectance learning, and super-resolution that lower-resolution architectural datasets cannot support.
