## Problem

Reinforcement learning policies for robotic manipulation are sample-inefficient and fail to generalize across diverse object configurations (shape, mass, friction, external disturbances). Rapid Motor Adaptation (RMA) showed that low-dimensional environment embeddings can be inferred from proprioception for locomotion and in-hand rotation, but extending it to general manipulation is non-trivial: proprioception alone cannot capture object identity or geometry before contact, and manipulation tasks involve diverse goals and object-specific behaviors that a single reactive policy cannot handle.

## Input / Output

The deployed system consumes three input streams: (1) proprioceptive state (9-dim joint position/velocity, 7-dim base/TCP pose, 6-dim object position + TCP-to-object distance), (2) a low-resolution depth image from an arm-mounted camera, and (3) a task-specific goal vector (9-dim for pick-and-place, 13-dim for peg insertion, 2-dim for faucet turning). The output is a 7-DOF joint position command for the Franka Emika Panda arm at 20 Hz, executed via a PD controller (Kp=4.0, Kd=0.2). The adapter additionally ingests a history of past observations and actions, enabling it to infer object properties from dynamic response after initial contact.

## Method

The method uses a two-phase training procedure. In phase 1, a 4-layer MLP policy is trained with PPO under heavy domain randomization, conditioned on a privileged environment embedding z produced by a 3-layer MLP encoder μ that takes physical parameters (dimensions, density, friction, impulse magnitudes) plus a 64-dim concatenation of learnable category and instance dictionary vectors serving as geometry proxies. In phase 2, the policy and encoder are frozen, and an adapter (3 2D conv layers for depth + 4 1D conv layers for state-action history + 2-layer MLP) is trained with L2 loss to predict z from only the depth image and proprioceptive history. The key insight is that the category-instance dictionary lets the adapter implicitly classify objects from depth without explicit recognition, while the depth CNN provides pre-contact geometric information that proprioception cannot supply. A curriculum linearly ramps up randomization magnitude during training.

## Results

On the four ManiSkill2 tasks, RMA² achieves the best real-world-deployable success rate across all settings: 73.8% on Pick & Place (YCB), 62.7% on Faucet Turning, 51.6% on Peg Insertion, and 90.5% on Pick & Place (EGAD). The closest real-world baseline, DR+Vi, reaches only 35.2%, 14.7%, 0.0%, and 35.5% respectively. On the EGAD generalization test, RMA² outperforms DR+Vi by a 55-percentage-point margin (90.5% vs. 35.5%), while the gap to the Oracle upper bound is just 1.6–3.8 percentage points per task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RMA² decisively outperforms all four baselines on every task. Against basic Domain Randomization (DR, Tobin et al.), RMA² wins by a wide margin: 73.8% vs. 0.3% SR on Pick & Place (YCB), 62.7% vs. 48.9% on Faucet Turning, 51.6% vs. 45.6% on Peg Insertion, and 90.5% vs. 1.0% on EGAD. The vision-based DR+Vi baseline (Andrychowicz et al.) is the strongest competitor but still trails RMA² substantially—35.2% vs. 73.8% on YCB, 14.7% vs. 62.7% on Faucet, 0.0% vs. 51.6% on Peg Insertion (complete failure due to timeout), and 35.5% vs. 90.5% on EGAD. Automatic Domain Randomization (ADR, Akkaya et al. / Handa et al.) performs comparably to or worse than plain DR on most tasks (1.6% vs. 0.3% on YCB, 51.8% vs. 48.9% on Faucet, 14.0% vs. 45.6% on Peg, 3.0% vs. 1.0% on EGAD), indicating that curriculum-based randomization does not close the gap to adaptation-based methods. The largest relative advantage of RMA² appears on the EGAD generalization test, where the 55-point gap over DR+Vi highlights that RMA²'s depth-conditioned adapter generalizes to unseen object geometries far more effectively than end-to-end visual policies trained with domain randomization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Rapid Motor Adaptation (RMA) from Kumar et al. (quadruped locomotion) and Qi et al. (in-hand rotation) to general robot-arm manipulation tasks, introducing two key modifications: category/instance embedding dictionaries as implicit geometry proxies, and a depth CNN to visually infer object properties prior to contact. It positions itself against domain randomization (DR, ADR) and reactive vision-based policies, arguing that explicit adaptation to a low-dimensional environment embedding outperforms robustness-through-randomization for manipulation. The unified two-phase formalization (Eq. 1 for policy training, Eq. 3 for adapter training) provides a cleaner theoretical framing than prior RMA formulations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that RMA-style adaptation can handle the diverse, object-specific goals of general manipulation (pick-and-place, peg insertion, faucet turning), bridging a gap between locomotion-focused RMA and the broader manipulation literature. The EGAD extrapolation result—where a YCB-trained agent outperforms its in-distribution performance on 2000+ unseen shapes—suggests the object-embedding approach captures transferable geometric structure rather than memorizing specific objects. Key limitations include single-task policies, a persistent Oracle-to-RMA² gap in precision tasks (faucet turning), and no real-world deployment; open questions include multitask generalization, variable object counts, and integration with hierarchical planners.
