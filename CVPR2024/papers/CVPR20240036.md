## Problem

Iterative text-to-image generation models such as Muse require many sampling steps through large Transformer models to ensure that different regions of the image are mutually compatible, making inference computationally expensive. No lightweight mechanism existed to enforce spatial and label compatibility among discrete image tokens without repeatedly invoking the full generative model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240036/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a natural-language text prompt (encoded by T5-XXL) and produces a 512×512 RGB image via a two-stage pipeline: a base model predicts a 16×16 grid of VQGAN tokens (vocabulary size 8192) for a 256×256 image, followed by a super-resolution model that expands to a 32×32 token grid for the final 512×512 output. The MRF operates entirely in the discrete token space, receiving per-token logit distributions from the Transformer backbone and outputting a globally compatible token assignment before detokenization.

## Method

MarkovGen introduces a fully-connected Markov Random Field over the VQGAN token grid, where the energy function combines unary terms (negative Transformer logits) with pairwise terms factored into a learned spatial-similarity matrix W^s and a learned label-compatibility matrix W^c. Mean-field inference iteratively refines marginal distributions via matrix multiplications and softmax operations, and the entire MRF is differentiable so its parameters are trained by backpropagation in a two-stage procedure: self-supervised masked-token prediction pre-training followed by fine-tuning to match Muse's final predictions given an intermediate step. At inference time, the MRF replaces the last 4 of 24 base-model steps and the last 5 of 8 SR steps, fast-forwarding the remaining generation at negligible cost (0.29 ms per MRF inference vs. 10–24 ms per Transformer step), yielding a 1.5× speedup while simultaneously improving FID and human-evaluated quality over full Muse.

## Results

On MS-COCO (256×256), MarkovGen achieves a FID of 12.28 with 18 base-model iterations, outperforming both Full Muse (24 iterations, FID 13.13) and Early Exit Muse (18 iterations, FID 14.37). Inference time drops from 442.05 ms (full Muse) to 281.03 ms (MarkovGen), a 1.5× speedup, while the MRF inference itself costs only 0.29 ms per step. In human evaluation, raters strongly prefer MarkovGen images over both Early Exit Muse and the more expensive Full Muse.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares MarkovGen against two variants of Muse: Full Muse (24 base + 8 SR iterations) and Early Exit Muse (18 base + 3 SR iterations, matching MarkovGen's step budget). On FID, MarkovGen (12.28) beats Full Muse (13.13) by 0.85 points despite using 6 fewer total iterations, and beats Early Exit Muse (14.37) by 2.09 points at the same step count. In human evaluation, MarkovGen is preferred over Early Exit Muse and also over the slower, more expensive Full Muse, confirming that the MRF-based fast-forwarding improves quality rather than merely trading quality for speed. The 1.5× speedup (281 ms vs. 442 ms) comes from replacing 4 base steps and 5 SR steps with near-zero-cost MRF inference (0.29 ms per step).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the fully-connected MRF/CRF framework from image segmentation (Krahenbuhl & Koltun 2011; Zheng et al. 2015) into the discrete VQGAN token space of text-to-image generation, a setting where prior MRF work has not been applied. It departs from segmentation CRFs in two key ways: spatial weights are learned end-to-end rather than fixed to Gaussian potentials, and label compatibility is learned from data rather than assumed to follow a Potts model, since VQGAN tokens lack the smoothness semantics of pixel labels. The backbone is Muse's progressive parallel decoding (masked generative transformer), where the MRF replaces the final sampling steps rather than the entire generation pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is a 1.5× speedup over Muse (already ~3× faster than DALL-E, Parti, Imagen, and Stable Diffusion) with a simultaneous quality gain, and the MRF trains in hours on a single TPUv4, making it easy to bolt onto any pre-trained token-based generator. The paper notes two clear open directions: making spatial/label compatibility weights text-conditioned (turning the MRF into a true CRF), and jointly training the Transformer backbone with the MRF layer so unaries are optimized for structured decoding. A limitation is that the MRF currently receives text guidance only indirectly through the unaries, and the evaluation is confined to a single Muse checkpoint and resolution, leaving open whether the approach generalizes across architectures and scales.
