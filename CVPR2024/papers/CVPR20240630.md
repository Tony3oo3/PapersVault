## Problem

Existing controllable diffusion models like ControlNet and T2I-Adapter require tens of thousands of condition-image pairs to adapt to a new structural modality, making it infeasible for ordinary users. With only ~100 samples, the dominant text-prompt signal in cross-attention layers overwhelms the sparse structural condition, causing generated images to be structurally misaligned with the input condition (e.g., wrong spatial layout, pose, or depth).
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240630/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method accepts a 512×512 structural condition image (sketch, segmentation map, pose skeleton, Midas depth map, or Canny edge) together with a free-form text prompt, and outputs a 512×512 generated image that preserves the spatial structure of the condition. The condition is consumed through a dedicated prompt-free encoder that extracts multi-scale features at resolutions {64, 32, 16, 8}, while the text prompt is encoded by CLIP; during the PCL training stage the text is replaced by a null embedding to suppress its influence.

## Method

The framework has two stages. First, Prompt-free Conditional Learning (PCL) uses a prompt-free encoder—derived from the SD encoder with text-conditional links pruned from the spatial transformers and initialized from pretrained weights—to encode the structural condition into multi-scale features that are injected into the frozen UNet via zero-convolution layers (as in T2I-Adapter). Training replaces all text inputs with a null-text embedding, progressively eliminating the text bias so the encoder learns to align generated content with the condition. Second, Condition-specific Negative Rectification (CNR) introduces a lightweight spatial-transformer block that cross-attends the CLIP image embedding of the condition with the CLIP text embedding of the universal negative prompt, producing a condition-specific negative embedding that replaces the one-size-fits-all negative prompt in the CFG formula, thereby correcting residual content inconsistencies during inference.

## Results

With only 100 training pairs, the proposed method achieves FID of 20.726 and cSSIM of 0.835 on segmentation (COCO), 19.137 FID and 0.803 cSSIM on depth (InstructPix2Pix), and 16.710 FID and 0.475 cSSIM on Canny edge — all surpassing the corresponding 100-sample baselines (ControlNet-100, T2IAdapter-100, PromptDiff-100) on FID. Notably, on segmentation the few-shot method even exceeds the fully trained ControlNet (27.377 FID, 0.820 cSSIM) and T2IAdapter (24.254 FID, 0.811 cSSIM). On pose (Human-Art), the method achieves 32.968 FID and 23.10 AP, outperforming ControlNet-100 (36.659 FID, 19.64 AP) and T2IAdapter-100 (42.601 FID, 13.80 AP) while remaining competitive with the full-trained HumanSD (36.817 FID, 47.51 AP).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ControlNet-100, the proposed method achieves substantially lower FID across all four conditions (e.g., 20.726 vs. 31.109 on segmentation, 19.137 vs. 28.396 on depth, 16.710 vs. 34.596 on Canny), while also matching or exceeding cSSIM on three of four conditions. Versus T2IAdapter-100, the method wins on FID for segmentation (20.726 vs. 22.103), depth (19.137 vs. 20.379), and Canny (16.710 vs. 19.096), with sketch FID essentially tied (21.049 vs. 21.053); cSSIM is higher for the proposed method on every condition. Compared to PromptDiff-100, the method dominates on both FID and cSSIM for depth (19.137/0.803 vs. 23.896/0.721) and Canny (16.710/0.475 vs. 20.148/0.472). The most striking result is that on segmentation the 100-sample method surpasses even the fully trained ControlNet (20.726 vs. 27.377 FID) and T2IAdapter (20.726 vs. 24.254 FID), though it trails full-trained T2IAdapter on depth FID (19.137 vs. 15.856) and full-trained ControlNet on Canny FID (16.710 vs. 13.539). On pose (Table 2), the method achieves 23.10 AP versus 19.64 for ControlNet-100 and 13.80 for T2IAdapter-100, but remains well below the full-trained HumanSD (47.51 AP), indicating that pose is the hardest condition to learn from 100 samples.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the controllable diffusion model lineage (ControlNet, T2I-Adapter) by identifying a specific failure mode in few-shot condition learning: the text-image cross-attention in Stable Diffusion creates a bias that prevents the model from adequately learning structural alignment from sparse data. Rather than simply scaling down training data, the authors architecturally decouple the condition encoder from text (prompt-free encoder with pruned spatial transformer links) and introduce a null-text fine-tuning strategy, departing from the standard joint text-condition training paradigm. The condition-specific negative rectification further modifies the CFG mechanism (Ho et al.) by replacing the universal negative prompt with a condition-conditioned embedding, a departure from prior work that treats the negative prompt as a fixed hyperparameter.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Reducing the data requirement from ~100k to 100 samples per condition modality makes controllable diffusion practically accessible to individual users and small teams who cannot afford large-scale annotation. The conceptual insight that text prompts actively *hinder* structural learning in few-shot regimes challenges the assumption that more conditioning signals are always beneficial. Open questions include whether this approach generalizes to more complex or multi-condition settings, whether the 100-sample regime is truly the floor (could it work with 10 or 50?), and whether the technique transfers to video diffusion or 3D generation. The evaluation is limited to five condition types on three datasets, and the cSSIM metric, while informative, may not fully capture perceptual quality differences.
