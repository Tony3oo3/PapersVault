## Problem

Existing backdoor attacks on CLIP require either poisoning the pre-training data or fine-tuning the entire model with large amounts of additional data, making them inapplicable to data-limited few-shot scenarios. Prompt learning, one of the most widely-used CLIP adaptation techniques, has remained an unexplored attack surface. This paper asks whether a backdoor can be injected during the prompt learning stage with only 16 samples per class, and identifies that influencing both the image and text encoders simultaneously yields a more powerful and generalizable attack than visual-only triggers.

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard RGB image as input; the backdoor is activated by adding a small additive noise trigger δ (ℓ∞-bounded with ε=4 by default) to the image. The trigger-aware context generator consumes the image encoder's feature vector and outputs N=4 continuous context token embeddings (each of dimension e=512) that are prepended to the class name to form the text prompt. For classification, the output is a predicted class label; for the extended retrieval task on Flickr30K, the output is a ranked list of image-text pairs (reported as R@1). Training uses 16 labeled examples per class with the CLIP ViT-B/16 backbone frozen.

## Method

BadCLIP injects a backdoor via trigger-aware prompt learning, consisting of a learnable additive trigger δ on images and a trigger-aware context generator h(θ) (a two-layer fully-connected network) that maps image features to continuous context token embeddings. The key mechanism is that the trigger simultaneously shifts image features (naturally, through the image encoder) and text features (because the context generator produces different prompts for backdoor vs. clean images), closing the cosine-similarity gap between the backdoor image and the target class's text prompt. Training minimizes a combined loss: a trigger loss pushing backdoor images toward the target class and a clean loss preserving accuracy on unmodified images, subject to the ℓ∞ constraint on δ. A trigger warm-up strategy first optimizes δ for T′ iterations with randomly initialized θ, then jointly optimizes both for T″ iterations, which empirically avoids the sub-optimal solutions obtained from simultaneous optimization. The resulting backdoor generalizes to unseen classes, cross-dataset, and cross-domain settings, achieving >99% ASR while matching the clean accuracy of CoCoOp.

## Results

On 11 downstream datasets (16-shot), BadCLIP achieves an average clean accuracy of 79.55% on seen classes and 69.86% on unseen classes, while maintaining an average attack success rate (ASR) of 99.52% (seen) and 99.02% (unseen). Under cross-dataset transfer (trained on ImageNet, tested on 10 other datasets), BadCLIP reaches 100% ASR on 9 out of 10 targets with an average of 99.98%. Under cross-domain transfer (ImageNet → ImageNetV2/Sketch/A/R), ASR ranges from 99.70% to 100.0% (average 99.92%), confirming robustness to domain shift.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against zero-shot CLIP, BadCLIP improves average clean accuracy by 10.21% on seen classes (79.55% vs. 69.34%) while adding a >99% ASR backdoor. Compared to CoOp, BadCLIP trades a small drop in seen-class accuracy (79.55% vs. 82.69%) for a substantial gain on unseen classes (69.86% vs. 63.22%), reflecting the generalizability of trigger-aware prompts. Against CoCoOp, BadCLIP is within ~1% on seen accuracy (79.55% vs. 80.47%) and slightly lower on unseen accuracy (69.86% vs. 71.69%), but uniquely achieves >99% ASR across all settings. Versus BadEncoder, BadCLIP matches clean accuracy (95.13% vs. 94.83%) and achieves 98.57% ASR without any additional data, whereas BadEncoder's ASR collapses to 17.39% when additional data drops from 50K to 1K samples. Against the fine-tuning-on-poisoning-data baseline from CleanCLIP, BadCLIP outperforms on both metrics (67.10% ACC / 98.75% ASR vs. 58.40% / 94.60%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two lines of work: backdoor attacks on CLIP (Carlini et al.'s data poisoning, BadEncoder's fine-tuning) and prompt learning for CLIP adaptation (CoOp, CoCoOp). It departs from prior backdoor attacks by targeting the prompt learning stage rather than pre-training or full fine-tuning, and from prior prompt learning by repurposing CoCoOp's image-specific context generator as an adversarial mechanism. The key conceptual departure is the trigger-aware prompt design, which makes the text encoder respond to the image trigger, thereby influencing both branches of CLIP simultaneously—a mechanism absent from all prior CLIP backdoor attacks that only perturb the image encoder.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work reveals that prompt learning, one of the most widely adopted parameter-efficient adaptation paradigms for CLIP, is itself a viable attack surface, with implications for any deployment where third parties provide prompt-tuned models. The finding that trigger-aware prompts (influencing both image and text features) are essential for generalizable backdoors—yielding 99% ASR on unseen classes versus 2.2% for trigger-agnostic prompts—offers a new design principle for multi-modal backdoor attacks and a corresponding target for future defenses. Open questions include whether defenses can detect trigger-aware prompt manipulation without degrading clean accuracy, and whether this mechanism extends to other multi-modal foundation models beyond CLIP.
