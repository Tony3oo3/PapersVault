## Problem

Existing anomaly detection methods require per-dataset training, and while CLIP-based approaches like WinCLIP show cross-dataset generalization, they depend on handcrafted text prompts tailored to industrial defects, limiting applicability to medical anomalies or semantic anomalies. This paper introduces the Generalist Anomaly Detection (GAD) task: training a single model that detects anomalies across diverse domains without any training or fine-tuning on the target data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241967/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the model receives a query image, K few-shot normal images randomly sampled from the target dataset (serving as in-context prompts, never used for training), and a fixed set of normal/abnormal text prompts. The output is a scalar anomaly score combining a holistic score over a concatenated residual map (patch-level + image-level + text-prompt features) and a max patch-level residual term. The model is trained on auxiliary data (MVTec AD) with binary normal/anomaly labels, and the few-shot prompts are only available at test time.

## Method

InCTRL builds on a frozen CLIP ViT-B/16 backbone with lightweight adapter layers. Its core mechanism is multi-layer patch-level residual learning: at each transformer block, every query patch is compared via cosine distance to its nearest-neighbor patch across all K prompt images, producing a residual map that is averaged over layers. An image-level residual is computed by element-wise subtraction between the adapted query class-token feature and the prototype of adapted prompt features. These visual residuals are fused with text-prompt-guided features (similarity of the query to normal vs. abnormal text prompt prototypes) into a holistic residual map, which is scored by a learned function plus a max-patch residual term. The entire model is trained with a binary classification loss on auxiliary data, with the key insight that larger residuals between a query and its normal-pattern prompts are a domain-agnostic signal of anomaly.

## Results

InCTRL achieves the best AUROC across all nine AD datasets under 2-shot, 4-shot, and 8-shot settings, outperforming the strongest competitor WinCLIP by up to 11.3% AUROC on ELPV (0.839 vs. 0.726, 2-shot) and 6.5% on SDD (0.972 vs. 0.942). On medical datasets, InCTRL reaches 97.3% AUPRC on BrainMRI with only 2 normal prompts, surpassing WinCLIP by 3.9% AUROC. On semantic anomaly detection (MNIST/CIFAR-10), InCTRL achieves 90%+ AUROC where prior SotA methods (RegAD, CoOp) only reach 50–65%, a gap of up to 8.2% AUROC over the best competitor.

## Baselines & Comparisons

InCTRL wins against all six baselines on virtually every dataset and few-shot setting. WinCLIP is the closest competitor, leveraging CLIP's text prompts for zero/few-shot detection, but InCTRL surpasses it by 11.3% AUROC on ELPV, 3.0% on SDD, and 3.9% on BrainMRI (2-shot), with the gap narrowing to 1–3% at 8-shot. Among conventional distance-based methods, PatchCore is the strongest (e.g., 0.837 on ELPV at 8-shot) yet still trails InCTRL by 3.5% there; SPADE and PaDiM generalize poorly across domains, often scoring below 0.60 on medical and semantic datasets. RegAD and CoOp (which share identical numbers, as CoOp uses RegAD's scoring strategy) perform well on industrial defects but collapse on semantic anomalies (0.393–0.612 AUROC on MNIST/CIFAR-10 multi-class), where InCTRL maintains 0.635–0.934. The zero-shot WinCLIP baseline confirms that even without any few-shot prompts, CLIP-based detection is competitive, but InCTRL's in-context residual learning consistently adds 5–15% AUROC over this floor.

## Relation to Prior Work

InCTRL builds on the CLIP-based anomaly detection line initiated by WinCLIP, which demonstrated that VLMs can detect industrial defects via handcrafted text prompts, but departs fundamentally by replacing text-prompt dependence with few-shot normal image prompts and learned in-context residuals. It also redefines in-context learning (originally an NLP concept for task adaptation) for anomaly detection, treating image prompts as prototypes of normal patterns rather than task instructions. The method is positioned against both traditional few-shot AD (SPADE, PaDiM, PatchCore, RegAD) that require per-dataset training, and prompt-learning approaches (CoOp) that adapt CLIP to auxiliary data, by training a single frozen-CLIP-based model on one auxiliary dataset that generalizes without any target-data training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The GAD benchmark (9 datasets spanning industrial, medical, and semantic anomalies under one-vs-all and multi-class protocols) is a practical contribution that addresses a real deployment need: avoiding per-dataset retraining due to privacy constraints or data scarcity. A key limitation is that performance depends on the diversity of the few-shot normal prompts—when normal patterns are heterogeneous (multi-class setting), detection degrades, and failure cases arise when anomalous queries happen to resemble the limited normal prompts. Open questions include whether the approach extends to truly zero-shot settings (no prompts at all), whether it scales to video or 3D medical volumes, and whether the residual-learning formulation can be made prompt-free by learning a universal normality prior.
