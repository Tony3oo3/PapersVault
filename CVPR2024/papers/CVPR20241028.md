## Problem

Traditional epistemic uncertainty quantification relies on Bayesian neural networks, which require retraining or posterior sampling and are incompatible with arbitrary pre-trained deterministic models. Existing gradient-based and perturbation-based alternatives that avoid these requirements lack theoretical justification and often underperform even simple entropy measures. This paper provides the first theoretical grounding for gradient/perturbation-based epistemic UQ and introduces practical improvements that make these methods competitive with or superior to BNN-based approaches.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a frozen pre-trained deterministic classifier (any CNN or ViT architecture) and a single input image, requiring no access to the original training set, validation set, or model retraining. It produces a scalar epistemic uncertainty score per input by computing class-specific gradients of log-probabilities with respect to all parameters, applying layer-wise exponential weights, and averaging over N+1 Gaussian-perturbed copies of the input (σ ≈ 0.02). The entire pipeline is inference-only: one forward pass per perturbed input plus a single backward pass for the averaged probability vector.

## Method

The core method, REGrad, replaces ExGrad's expected gradient norm with an L2 root-normalized sum over classes (√(p_c · ‖∇log p_c‖²)), which mitigates overconfidence when one class dominates. Two further refinements are layered on top: (1) layer-selective gradients apply exponential weights a_l = exp(λl) so deeper layers contribute more strongly to the uncertainty score, reflecting their role in decision-boundary representation; (2) gradient-perturbation integration averages the per-class gradients over N+1 inputs (original plus N Gaussian perturbations with small σ), smoothing noisy raw gradients in the spirit of SmoothGrad. The theoretical contribution establishes, via the Bernstein-von Mises theorem, that small Gaussian parameter perturbations approximate the Bayesian posterior for sufficiently trained models, and proves that the expected gradient norm upper-bounds the perturbation-based KL uncertainty (Proposition 3.5), thereby linking gradient-based UQ to BNNs and justifying the design choices.

## Results

On OOD detection (Table 1), REGrad* achieves an average AUROC of 94.56% across MNIST/C10 source datasets and 85.91% across SVHN/C100 source datasets, outperforming the strongest gradient-based baseline ExGrad (91.86% / 81.02%) by 2.7% and 4.84% respectively. For uncertainty calibration (Table 2), REGrad* attains the best average rAULC of 0.904, marginally ahead of Entropy (0.902) and ExGrad (0.897). In active learning (Table 3), REGrad* achieves the highest average accuracy of 48.83% and lowest NLL of 2.547 across 10 acquisition cycles, surpassing ExGrad (47.11% ACC) by 1.72%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

REGrad* consistently outperforms all gradient-based baselines: it improves over ExGrad (the strongest gradient method) by 2.7% average AUROC on MNIST/C10 sources and 4.84% on SVHN/C100 sources, while achieving 30–60% gains over NEGrad, UNGrad, and GradNorm. Against perturbation-based methods, REGrad* surpasses Perturb x and Perturb θ by 19.82% and 19.12% respectively on the harder SVHN→C100 and C100→LSUN pairs, where these methods degrade severely (AUROC drops to ~40–50%). Compared to entropy-based methods (Entropy, ExGrad V Term), which primarily capture aleatoric uncertainty, REGrad* gains 1.71–17.76% in average AUROC, with the largest margins on C100→SVHN (88.06 vs. 53.38) and C100→LSUN (79.11 vs. 42.28). Against Laplace Approximation (LA), which requires training data access, REGrad* achieves comparable or better results (e.g., 92.32 vs. 91.86 on C10→SVHN, 88.06 vs. 80.09 on C100→SVHN) without needing the original training set. In active learning, REGrad* leads ExGrad by 1.72% ACC and Perturb θ by 1.36% ACC on average, while LA underperforms all other methods (44.06% avg ACC), confirming that the gradient-perturbation approach is more effective under data-limited settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the gradient-based UQ lineage initiated by ExGrad (Igoe et al., 2022) by replacing its expected-norm formulation with an L2 root-normalized class-weighted sum (REGrad), and further incorporates layer-selective weighting and input-perturbation smoothing borrowed from the SmoothGrad (Smilkov et al., 2017) saliency literature. Its most distinctive contribution is a theoretical bridge—via the Bernstein-von Mises theorem—showing that small Gaussian perturbations of a sufficiently trained model approximate the Bayesian posterior, thereby justifying single-model gradient/perturbation scores as valid epistemic UQ proxies without requiring multiple model samples. This departs from the dominant BNN framing (MC-dropout, Laplace approximation) that has treated epistemic uncertainty as inherently requiring a parameter distribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical appeal is clear: a deployed model can self-assess epistemic uncertainty without retraining, extra data, or architectural changes, which is valuable for safety-critical deployment. However, the theoretical guarantees rely on |D|→∞ and mild regularity conditions that are rarely met in practice, and the empirical gains over the strongest gradient baseline (ExGrad) are modest (≈2–5% AUROC in OOD detection, marginal in calibration). Open questions include whether the layer-selective and perturbation components retain their benefit at ImageNet scale or with very deep transformers, and whether the Bernstein-von Mises approximation degrades gracefully when training data is limited or the model is underfit.
