## Problem

Existing multi-rater medical image segmentation methods either merge multiple annotations into a single unattainable 'ground truth,' generate diverse but implicit and unordered predictions, or produce personalized results for individual experts. No prior work simultaneously achieves both diversified (implicit expert opinions) and personalized (explicit per-rater) segmentation, despite both being clinically valuable for quality control and specialist consultation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240361/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 2D medical image slice (center-cropped to 128×128) from MRI or CT, along with n expert annotation masks (n=4 in both experiments), as training input. Stage I outputs a set of diversified segmentation masks sampled from a learned latent space, while Stage II outputs n individual personalized masks, each corresponding to a specific expert rater. The personalized outputs are structured to enable one-to-one matching between predictions and annotations, evaluated via Dice_max and Dice_match set-to-set metrics.

## Method

D-Persona is a two-stage framework built on Probabilistic U-Net. In Stage I, a novel bound-constrained loss supervises the prior distribution by predicting the intersection and union of all expert annotations, relaxing predictions in uncertain regions to increase latent space diversity without adding new parameters. In Stage II, n lightweight attention-based projection heads generate per-expert latent codes from frozen U-Net features, which are then mapped into the learned latent space via cross-attention against a bank of M=100 prior samples, ensuring the expert prompts remain within the diversified distribution. The fixed prediction head from Stage I converts each expert prompt into a personalized segmentation mask, and only the projection heads are trained in Stage II, keeping the overall architecture efficient (22K parameters per head).

## Results

On the in-house NPC-170 dataset, D-Persona Stage I achieves a GED of 0.2359 and Dice_soft of 80.43% (with 50 samples), substantially outperforming the Probabilistic U-Net baseline (GED 0.4465, Dice_soft 75.34%). In Stage II, D-Persona reaches a Dice_mean of 76.19% for personalized segmentation, surpassing the best one-stage baseline Pionono (75.09%) by 1.10%. On LIDC-IDRI, Stage I achieves GED 0.1358 and Dice_soft 90.45%, while Stage II attains Dice_mean 89.17%, which equals the Dice_match upper bound, indicating near-perfect one-to-one expert matching.

## Baselines & Comparisons

D-Persona Stage I dramatically outperforms Probabilistic U-Net [18] on diversity: GED drops from 0.4465 to 0.2359 (a 47% reduction) and Dice_soft rises from 75.34% to 80.43%, confirming the bound-constrained loss effectively expands the latent space. For personalized segmentation, D-Persona Stage II (Dice_mean 76.19%) beats all one-stage baselines: Pionono [36] (75.09%, +1.10%), CM-Global [38] (75.00%, +1.19%), CM-Pixel [48] (74.92%, +1.27%), and TAB [23] (74.32%, +1.87%). Against crowdsourcing heuristics, D-Persona also leads: Random Selection [17] achieves 75.24% and STAPLE [44] achieves 74.47%, both below D-Persona's 76.19%. Notably, on LIDC-IDRI the gap narrows (D-Persona 89.17% vs. Pionono 88.84%), and the paper acknowledges that individually trained U-Nets still outperform D-Persona for specific experts on NPC-170, indicating that fully capturing a single expert's idiosyncratic style remains an open challenge.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Probabilistic U-Net (Kohl et al., NeurIPS 2018) as its generative backbone, extending it with a bound-constrained loss (inspired by pluralistic image completion) to improve latent-space diversity in Stage I. Conceptually, it unifies two previously separate lines of work in multi-rater medical image segmentation: generation-based methods that produce diverse but unordered implicit outputs, and one-stage personalization methods (CM-Global, CM-Pixel, TAB, Pionono) that produce explicit per-expert predictions. The key departure is the two-stage decomposition—first learning a shared latent space of plausible segmentations, then querying expert-specific prompts from it via attention-based projection heads—rather than attempting both objectives in a single training pass.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine clinical gap: in many medical contexts (e.g., GTVp delineation for nasopharyngeal carcinoma), no single ground truth exists, and practitioners need both diverse opinions (for case conferences) and trackable per-expert outputs (for quality control and training). The honest limitation that the model still cannot surpass individually trained U-Nets for specific experts on NPC-170 highlights that capturing idiosyncratic annotation styles from heterogeneous multi-rater data remains an open problem. The two-stage design is modular and could be combined with more powerful generative backbones (e.g., diffusion models), but the moderate performance gains (~1% over the best one-stage baseline) suggest the bottleneck may lie in the personalization mechanism rather than the generative capacity.
