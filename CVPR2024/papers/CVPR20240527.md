## Problem

State-of-the-art vision-language models like CLIP exhibit little to no compositionality, failing to distinguish between semantically swapped descriptions (e.g., 'a girl in white facing a man in black' vs. 'a girl in black facing a man in white'), and prior work shows this deficiency does not resolve with increased model size or training data. The paper asks whether principles from cognitive science—specifically cultural transmission, where the need to teach a new generation incentivizes easier-to-learn (compositional) languages—can be operationalized as a training algorithm for vision-language models.

## Input / Output

The vision agent (ViT-B/32) processes RGB images into patch embeddings, while the language agent (CLIP-style transformer) processes text into token embeddings. Both agents produce 512-dimensional representations by sparsely combining a shared codebook of 16,384 codes via Sparsemax-weighted linear combinations, rather than outputting free-form continuous vectors. The aligned representations support image-to-text and text-to-image retrieval (evaluated on CREPE, SugarCrepe, Cola, Winoground) and zero-shot image classification across 18 datasets.

## Method

The paper reframes CLIP's contrastive learning objective as a Lewis Signaling Game between a vision agent and a language agent, then introduces a shared learnable codebook (16,384 × 512-dim codes) that bottlenecks both modalities' representations into a regulated, finite 'vocabulary.' The core training algorithm—iterated learning—consists of a warmup phase followed by repeated cycles where: (1) the language agent is reinitialized with random weights (simulating a new generation), (2) the old language agent distills into the new one for a fixed number of steps while the codebook is frozen, and (3) both agents interact under standard contrastive loss for a limited duration before the next cycle begins. This 'teaching pressure' from the knowledge gap between the well-trained vision agent and the freshly initialized language agent incentivizes the vision agent to produce smoother, easier-to-learn representations, which empirically reduces the Lipschitz constant over generations and yields more compositional features without sacrificing recognition accuracy.

## Results

On compositionality benchmarks (Table 1), IL-CLIP achieves a mean R@1 of 34.4 on CC3M and 38.0 on CC12M, outperforming standard CLIP (28.3 and 34.9) by 6.1 and 3.1 points respectively. The largest gains appear on CREPE systematicity-compound (47.7 vs. 38.4 on CC3M) and CREPE productivity-replace (14.6 vs. 9.8 on CC3M). On zero-shot image classification across 18 datasets (Table 2), IL-CLIP achieves a mean top-1 accuracy of 24.2 (CC3M) and 38.5 (CC12M), matching or exceeding Codebook-CLIP while substantially outperforming NegCLIP (21.0 and 34.5), confirming that iterated learning does not sacrifice recognition capability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IL-CLIP outperforms standard CLIP on nearly all compositionality splits, with the most dramatic gains on CREPE systematicity-compound (+9.3 on CC3M) and CREPE productivity-replace (+4.8 on CC3M, +5.6 on CC12M). Against Codebook-CLIP, IL-CLIP consistently wins by 1.9–3.2 points in mean score, confirming that the iterated learning paradigm adds value beyond the shared codebook alone. Compared to NegCLIP, IL-CLIP achieves a higher mean on CC3M (34.4 vs. 32.8) and ties on CC12M (38.0 vs. 38.0), while NegCLIP's advantage on the CREPE-swap split (33.3/35.8 vs. 22.3/23.9) is an artifact of its training objective directly targeting swap negatives. On zero-shot classification, IL-CLIP matches Codebook-CLIP (24.2 vs. 24.4 on CC3M; 38.5 vs. 38.8 on CC12M) and substantially outperforms NegCLIP (21.0 and 34.5), showing that iterated learning preserves recognition while NegCLIP's hard-negative mining degrades it. Overall, IL-CLIP is the only method that simultaneously improves compositionality and maintains (or improves) recognition accuracy relative to standard CLIP.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges three research threads: CLIP's contrastive vision-language pretraining, codebook-based discrete representation (Chen et al., 2023), and the iterated learning / cultural transmission framework from evolutionary linguistics (Kirby et al.). The key conceptual departure is reframing contrastive learning as a Lewis Signaling Game between two neural agents and operationalizing cultural transmission as periodic weight resets of one agent, rather than the reinforcement-learning-based emergent communication setups used in prior neural-agent work. Unlike prior iterated learning studies that operated on small, easily-categorizable domains (cubics, balls), this paper applies the paradigm to large-scale real-world image-text data with a standard contrastive objective, raising the question of whether the cognitive science mechanism transfers to modern VLM training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a simple training procedure—periodically reinitializing the language agent—can measurably improve compositionality in VLMs without sacrificing recognition accuracy, offering a practical and computationally cheap intervention for a well-known weakness of CLIP-style models. However, the evidence is limited to ViT-B/32 on CC3M/CC12M, with no scaling experiments to larger architectures or datasets, and the authors acknowledge training instability from the randomness in agent spawning. Open questions include whether the effect persists or grows at scale, whether the codebook is strictly necessary (ablation shows it helps but the IL mechanism alone underperforms), and whether the 'easy-to-learn' property generalizes beyond the specific contrastive setup to other VLM training paradigms.
