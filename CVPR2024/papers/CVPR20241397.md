## Problem

Training AI models for tumor detection and segmentation requires large annotated datasets spanning multiple organs and hospitals, yet early-stage tumor annotations are scarce and models fail to generalize across organs, imaging protocols, and patient demographics. The paper identifies that early-stage tumors (<2 cm) share remarkably similar CT imaging characteristics across parenchymal organs (liver, pancreas, kidney), a property that prior tumor synthesis methods have not exploited to achieve cross-organ generalizability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241397/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The diffusion model takes as input the latent features of a healthy 3D CT sub-volume (compressed by a 3D VQGAN autoencoder) and a binary tumor mask specifying shape and location, and outputs latent features for the tumor region that are decoded back into a full synthetic tumor CT volume. Tumor masks are generated as ellipsoids refined by radiologist feedback, and synthesis runs at ~100 ms per tumor using only 4 DDPM timesteps. The downstream segmentation model consumes these synthetic tumor volumes paired with their masks to produce per-voxel tumor segmentation predictions.

## Method

DiffTumor is a three-stage framework: (1) a 3D VQGAN autoencoder is trained on 9,262 unlabeled CT volumes to learn a compressed latent space with perceptual and adversarial losses; (2) a Latent Diffusion Model (3D U-Net with factorized self-attention over 2D slices and depth) is trained to denoise latent features conditioned on the tumor mask and the healthy-region latent features, requiring as few as one annotated tumor volume; (3) a segmentation network (U-Net, nnU-Net, or SwinUNETR) is trained on large-scale synthetic tumors generated by combining the diffusion model with a repository of healthy CT volumes. The key differentiator is that the diffusion model, trained exclusively on tumors from one organ, generalizes to synthesize realistic tumors in other organs because early-stage tumors share common imaging features, reducing the annotation burden from N×M (organs × hospitals) to 1×M.

## Results

For early-stage tumor detection, DiffTumor achieves the best sensitivity in 7 out of 9 cross-organ transfer settings, with the largest gains when transferring from liver to kidney (76.2% vs. 2.4% for real tumors and 52.4% for Hu et al.) and from pancreas to kidney (71.4% vs. 0% and 52.4%). As a data augmentation method, DiffTumor improves all-stage tumor segmentation DSC by 8.6–10.7% across three backbones (e.g., nnU-Net kidney: 73.8% → 84.5%). On the proprietary FELIX demographics dataset, synthetic data yields an average +16.4% sensitivity and +6.9% DSC, with the largest gain in the 50–60 age group (+18.9% sensitivity, +9.1% DSC). The Visual Turing Test shows radiologists misclassify >50% of synthetic tumors as real (specificity <50% for 3 of 4 radiologists), confirming visual realism.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole external baseline is Hu et al. [37] (Label-Free Liver Tumor Segmentation, CVPR 2023), a modeling-based synthesis method. DiffTumor outperforms Hu et al. in 7 of 9 cross-organ sensitivity settings, with the most decisive wins in liver→kidney (+23.8 pp: 76.2 vs. 52.4), pancreas→kidney (+19.0 pp: 71.4 vs. 52.4), and kidney→kidney (+11.9 pp: 78.6 vs. 66.7). The one setting where Hu et al. prevails is kidney→liver (74.1 vs. 68.8), a 5.3 pp gap. Against the 'real tumors only' baseline, DiffTumor's advantage is overwhelming in cross-organ transfer (e.g., 76.2% vs. 2.4% for liver→kidney), confirming that real-data models essentially fail to generalize across organs. As an augmentation method on same-organ data, DiffTumor consistently improves DSC over real-tumor-only training across all three backbones (U-Net, nnU-Net, SwinUNETR) and all three organs, with the largest gain of +10.7% DSC on kidney (nnU-Net: 73.8→84.5).

## Relation to Prior Work

DiffTumor composes three well-established components—VQGAN-style vector-quantized autoencoders (Esser et al., 2021), Latent Diffusion Models (Rombach et al., 2022), and DDPM training objectives (Ho et al., 2020)—into a three-stage pipeline for 3D CT tumor synthesis. It departs from prior tumor-synthesis work (e.g., liver [36,38,57,93], pancreas [53,80], lung [31,41,86], colon [69]) by explicitly targeting cross-organ generalizability: a diffusion model trained on one organ's tumors is applied to synthesize tumors in other organs, grounded in the empirical observation that early-stage (<2 cm) tumors share imaging characteristics across parenchymal organs. The tumor shape generation strategy (ellipsoids refined by radiologist feedback) is inherited from Hu et al. (2023), and the segmentation backbones (U-Net, nnU-Net, SwinUNETR) are standard, so the contribution is primarily in the synthesis framework and its generalization properties rather than in any single architectural novelty.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: reducing the annotation burden from N×M (N organs × M hospitals) to 1×M could make multi-organ, multi-site tumor-detection AI far more accessible, and the 100 ms/tumor synthesis speed enables large-scale data augmentation during training. Key limitations include validation on only three abdominal organs and only early-stage tumors (<2 cm), leaving open whether the approach extends to later-stage tumors with heterogeneous morphology, to other modalities (MRI, PET), or to organs with very different parenchymal textures. The reliance on a proprietary Hopkins dataset for the demographics experiment limits reproducibility, and the 'one annotated tumor suffices' finding, while striking, is demonstrated under a narrow set of conditions that may not transfer to more complex or heterogeneous tumor populations.
