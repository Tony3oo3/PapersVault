## Problem

Autonomous vehicles are confined to geo-fenced regions because traffic rules and driving norms vary significantly across locations, from left- vs. right-hand traffic to subtle differences like right-turn-on-red permissions. Prior approaches to encoding traffic rules in formal logic (MTL, LTL, STL) are not scalable to the full breadth of local driving handbooks, and no method directly leverages the natural-language driver handbook to adapt driving policies to a new region.

## Input / Output

LLaDA ingests four natural-language inputs: a nominal execution plan (e.g., 'turn right'), the full local driver handbook as raw text, a scene description from the ego perspective, and an optional unexpected-scenario description (e.g., 'someone honks at me'). The scene and scenario descriptions can be produced by a human or a VLM such as GPT-4V. The output is a single adapted motion plan expressed in natural language, which can be consumed by a downstream trajectory planner (e.g., GPT-Driver) or presented directly to a human driver.

## Method

LLaDA is a training-free, two-stage pipeline built entirely on GPT-4 in-context reasoning. First, a Traffic Rule Extractor (TRE) organizes the plan and unexpected scenario into a prompt, asks GPT-4 to produce one or two short keywords, and then retrieves the paragraphs in the local driver handbook containing those keywords—avoiding the context-length and distraction problems of passing the entire handbook. Second, an LLM Planner feeds the extracted relevant paragraphs together with the original plan and scenario into GPT-4 to generate the adapted motion plan. The entire system requires no fine-tuning and runs on a single CPU, making it a drop-in adapter that can be interfaced with any existing AV planning stack or used standalone as a human driver assistant.

## Results

On the nuScenes Boston validation set (planner fine-tuned on Singapore), GPT-Driver+LLaDA reduces average L2 trajectory error from 0.63 m to 0.62 m and average collision rate from 0.58% to 0.56% over a 3-second horizon, with consistent gains at each 1 s, 2 s, and 3 s checkpoint. In a user study with 24 participants, 70.3% rated LLaDA's instructions as strictly following the relevant local law and 82.8% found them very or extremely helpful. A safety ablation across four critical scenarios (stop sign, red light, heavy rain, pedestrian crossing) yielded a 0% error rate over 50 random examples per case.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole quantitative baseline is GPT-Driver [21], a GPT-3.5–based motion planner fine-tuned on the Singapore subset of nuScenes and evaluated on the Boston subset. LLaDA, used as a re-planning guideline layer on top of GPT-Driver, wins on every reported metric: average L2 error drops by 0.01 m (0.63 → 0.62) and average collision rate drops by 0.02 percentage points (0.58% → 0.56%), with the largest relative gain at the 2-second horizon (collision 0.45% → 0.41%). The 1-second L2 and collision values are identical for both methods, indicating that LLaDA's benefit is most pronounced at intermediate-to-longer horizons where traffic-rule differences (e.g., left-hand vs. right-hand driving) have more time to manifest. The paper does not compare against other LLM-based planners (e.g., DriveGPT4, MotionLM) or rule-based baselines, so the scope of the quantitative comparison is limited to a single baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

LLaDA departs from prior traffic-rule-in-planning work (which encodes rules in MTL/LTL/STL formulae) by operating directly on natural-language driver handbooks, and from LLM-for-driving work (GPT-Driver, DriveGPT4, MotionLM) by avoiding any fine-tuning or new model training in favor of a training-free, two-call GPT-4 pipeline (keyword extraction + in-context plan adaptation). Conceptually it is closest to the LLM-for-robotic-reasoning line (Code-As-Policies, ITP), but repurposes the LLM as a policy *adapter* rather than a wholesale planner replacement. The Traffic Rule Extractor (TRE) is a lightweight retrieval step that filters the full handbook down to scenario-relevant paragraphs before the LLM sees them, addressing the context-length and distraction issues that would arise from passing an entire driver's manual to the model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper tackles a genuine deployment barrier—geo-fencing caused by traffic-rule variation—with a modular, plug-in approach that requires no retraining of the underlying planner, making it immediately applicable to existing AV stacks. However, two limitations temper its near-term impact: LLM inference latency makes closed-loop real-time use impractical, and the system is sensitive to the quality of scene descriptions (currently relying on GPT-4V captions that are sometimes inaccurate). Open questions include whether safety certificates can be provided for LLM-generated instructions (via conformal prediction or calibration), how to build an automatic unexpected-scenario detector to avoid running the LLM in every control cycle, and whether an AV-specific VLM could replace the generic GPT-4V for more reliable scene grounding.
