## Problem

Existing image immunization methods against diffusion-based editing, such as PhotoGuard, require backpropagation through the entire multi-step diffusion process, leading to prohibitive GPU memory consumption. Additionally, their perturbations are vulnerable to being masked out during inpainting, making immunization unreliable in common editing scenarios. This paper addresses both limitations by targeting the semantic understanding of the model rather than the full generation pipeline.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242239/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a text prompt specifying the content to protect (e.g., 'woman', 'corgi'), and produces a single immunized image with an imperceptible perturbation bounded by an ℓ∞ norm budget (κ=0.06 in experiments). A content mask is derived from the original image's cross-attention map to identify the region of concern, and the perturbation is optimized to be effective across 10 different diffusion timesteps simultaneously, making it robust to the noise level at which editing is initiated.

## Method

The semantic attack targets the cross-attention mechanism in the denoising U-Net by minimizing an attention-suppressing loss that drives the attention response for a specific text token to zero within the region of interest, effectively distracting the model from identifying where to edit. Rather than backpropagating through the full T-step diffusion chain, the method decomposes the problem into independent denoising steps at different noise levels, reducing the backpropagation path to a single U-Net forward pass per timestep. Timestep universal gradient updating computes gradients at multiple timesteps, averages them, and applies a sign-based PGD update to produce a timestep-agnostic perturbation. This discretization of the diffusion process achieves equivalent or superior immunization with roughly half the GPU memory of the full-process diffusion attack, and the approach remains effective even when the protected content is not explicitly mentioned in the editing prompt.

## Results

On Stable Diffusion V1.4, the proposed semantic attack achieves the lowest PSNR (15.15 dB), SSIM (0.447), VIFp (0.146), and FSIM (0.658) while attaining the highest LPIPS (0.590) among all three methods, indicating the greatest dissimilarity between immunized and original editing outputs. On Stable Diffusion V2.0, the method similarly leads on PSNR (18.06), SSIM (0.472), VIFp (0.101), and LPIPS (0.602), though it trails slightly on FSIM (0.731 vs. 0.745 for Encoder Attack). In terms of memory efficiency, the semantic attack requires nearly half the GPU memory of the diffusion attack when attacking with 10 diffusion timesteps, and its memory cost remains flat as the number of timesteps increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines from PhotoGuard (Salman et al., ICML 2023): the Encoder Attack (targeting the VAE) and the Diffusion Attack (targeting the full diffusion process via PGD). On Stable Diffusion V1.4, the semantic attack outperforms both baselines on all five metrics, with the most dramatic gains in SSIM (0.447 vs. 0.632/0.650) and LPIPS (0.590 vs. 0.413/0.406), indicating substantially more perceptually disruptive editing failures. On Stable Diffusion V2.0, the semantic attack wins on four of five metrics (PSNR, SSIM, VIFp, LPIPS) but is slightly outperformed by the Encoder Attack on FSIM (0.731 vs. 0.745). Beyond quantitative metrics, the semantic attack also demonstrates superior robustness in inpainting scenarios where the perturbation region is not masked out, whereas the Diffusion Attack's efficacy degrades when the mask removes the perturbed region. Additionally, the semantic attack achieves these results with roughly half the GPU memory of the Diffusion Attack, since it backpropagates through a single denoising timestep rather than the entire diffusion chain.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from PhotoGuard (Salman et al., 2023), the first work on image immunization against diffusion-based editing, which attacked either the VAE encoder or the full diffusion process via PGD. Rather than backpropagating through all T denoising steps, the authors reframe the problem as suppressing cross-attention responses for a target content token at individual timesteps, treating the diffusion process as a collection of independent denoising operations. The attention-suppressing loss draws on the interpretability literature showing that cross-attention maps encode text-to-pixel semantic alignment in diffusion U-Nets (Hertz et al., Tang et al.), repurposing this mechanism as an attack surface. The timestep universal gradient updating strategy is a novel PGD variant that aggregates gradients across noise levels to produce a single timestep-agnostic perturbation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The memory-efficiency gain (roughly halving GPU consumption) makes image immunization against diffusion editing more practical for real-world deployment, and the content-specific protection strategy is more robust to inpainting masks than whole-image attacks. However, the evaluation is limited to Stable Diffusion V1.4/V2.0 with 150 images and 3 objects, and the acknowledged vulnerability to noise purification (blurring, JPEG compression) remains an open practical concern. Broader questions include whether the approach generalizes to newer architectures (SDXL, DiT-based models), whether it survives iterative editing pipelines, and how it interacts with future editing methods that may not rely on cross-attention in the same way.
