## Problem

Existing pansharpening methods either use spatially static kernels that cannot adapt to diverse regional content, or generate per-pixel adaptive kernels that are computationally redundant and blind to non-local self-similarity. Graph convolution approaches can access non-local similar patches but lack spatial adaptability and are limited to a small number of nearest neighbors. The paper addresses the gap of simultaneously achieving spatial adaptability and comprehensive non-local self-similarity exploitation in a parameter-efficient manner.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242428/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a low-resolution multispectral image (4, 8, or more spectral bands) and a high-resolution panchromatic image (typically 4× higher spatial resolution, single grayscale channel) as input. The PAN image and upsampled LRMS image are concatenated along the channel dimension to form the input feature map. The output is a high-resolution multispectral image, produced by adding the upsampled LRMS input to the network output following a detail-injection pattern common in pansharpening CNNs.

## Method

CANConv consists of two sub-modules: Similarity Relationship Partition (SRP) clusters pixels in the feature map using K-Means on spatially-pooled neighborhood features, assigning each pixel to one of K clusters that capture non-local self-similarity; Partition-Wise Adaptive Convolution (PWAC) then generates a unique convolution kernel per cluster via a lightweight attention mechanism (Kronecker-product weighting of a global kernel) applied to the cluster's centroid vector. This replaces the per-pixel kernel generation of spatial adaptive convolution with per-cluster generation, drastically reducing redundant parameters while allowing all spatially distant similar pixels to share a single adaptive kernel. CANNet wraps CANConv in a U-Net architecture with CAN-ResBlocks at multiple scales, where cluster index matrices are shared between the two PWACs within each block and reused from downsampling to upsampling stages to maintain multi-scale self-similar relationships. Small clusters below a threshold ratio η are replaced with the global centroid during training to prevent overfitting to outlier pixels.

## Results

On the WV3 dataset (20 reduced-resolution + 20 full-resolution samples), CANNet achieves SAM 2.930, ERGAS 2.158, Q8 0.920, D_λ 0.0196, D_s 0.0301, and HQNR 0.951, setting new bests on Q8, D_s, and HQNR. On QB, it attains the lowest SAM (4.507) and highest Q4 (0.937); on GF2, it achieves the lowest SAM (0.707) and highest Q4 (0.983). The method consistently outperforms the strongest deep-learning baselines (DCFNet, HMPNet, LAGConv) across all three satellite datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CANNet outperforms all 14 baselines on SAM and Q8/Q4 across all three datasets (WV3, QB, GF2), with the closest competitor being DCFNet (SAM 3.038 vs. 2.930 on WV3). On ERGAS, CANNet edges out DCFNet (2.158 vs. 2.165) on WV3 but trails HMPNet (0.564 vs. 0.630) on GF2, making it the one metric where a competitor wins. On full-resolution metrics, CANNet achieves the best D_s (0.0301) and HQNR (0.951) on WV3, while LRTCFPan retains a slight advantage on D_λ (0.0176 vs. 0.0196). Among adaptive-convolution baselines, CANNet surpasses LAGConv by a clear margin (SAM 2.930 vs. 3.104, ERGAS 2.158 vs. 2.300 on WV3), and Table 4 further shows that replacing LAGConv's spatial-adaptive blocks with CAN-ResBlocks yields additional gains (SAM 3.104→3.051). Traditional methods (EXP, TV, MTF-GLLP-FS, BDSD-PC) are consistently 1–3 points worse on SAM and 0.05–0.15 lower on Q8, confirming the advantage of the deep-learning approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: spatial adaptive convolution (PAC, DDF, LAGConv), which generates per-pixel kernels to handle regional variation, and non-local similarity methods (kNN-based graph convolution in CPNet/IGNN, non-local means), which exploit repetitive textures across distant regions. Prior spatial adaptive methods are computationally redundant (one kernel per pixel) and blind to non-local structure, while graph convolution methods capture non-local similarity but apply a single static kernel globally. CANConv departs from both by introducing K-Means clustering as an intermediate granularity: pixels are grouped into K clusters based on local neighborhood features, and one adaptive kernel is generated per cluster, simultaneously achieving spatial adaptability and non-local information propagation without per-pixel kernel overhead.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clustering-based kernel generation offers a principled middle ground between global static convolution and fully per-pixel adaptive convolution, reducing redundant parameters while enabling comprehensive non-local self-similarity exploitation that kNN-based methods (limited to k<3 neighbors) cannot match. The CANConv module demonstrates practical utility as a drop-in replacement for standard convolution in existing pansharpening backbones (DiCNN, FusionNet, LAGConv), suggesting broader applicability. Open questions include whether the non-differentiable K-Means step can be replaced with a learnable clustering mechanism, how the method scales to very large or hyperspectral images where the number of distinct semantic regions grows, and whether the train/test K mismatch (optimal K ≈ 4× training K) indicates a fundamental limitation of fixed-cluster approaches.
