## Problem

While quantum inner product (QIP) has been shown to offer lower theoretical complexity than classical inner product, no explicit quantum circuits for QIP have been constructed, and existing quantum simulators (Qiskit, PennyLane) cannot handle the resulting circuit complexity, making it impossible to integrate QIP into gradient-based ML pipelines. This leaves the practical impact of quantum-mechanism-induced errors on real ML tasks entirely unexplored.

## Input / Output

The QIP operator takes normalized vectors as input in three modes: two vectors (1-to-1), one vector plus N vectors (1-to-N), or M vectors plus N vectors (M-to-N). The output is a discrete approximated inner product value quantized to 2^t levels (where t is the number of measurement qubits), recovered by measuring the output quantum state. A key constraint is that all input vectors must be normalized, and the approximation error depends on both t and the number of sampling repetitions r, with two output strategies (AVG and MODE) trading off accuracy at different qubit counts.

## Method

The paper designs three QIP circuits by composing a Hadamard test (which encodes the classical inner product into a quantum phase θ = arccos(−(x,y))/2) with Quantum Phase Estimation (which decodes θ into a measurable basis state), achieving O(log d/ε), O(log Nd/ε), and O(log MNd/ε) complexity for the three modes. To make simulation tractable on classical hardware, the authors bypass gate-by-gate circuit simulation entirely: they analytically derive the output state probability distribution and introduce a Quantum State Search (QSS) algorithm that starts from the highest-probability basis states and stops upon a hit, reducing sampling complexity from O(2^{2t}) to near O(2^t), with additional speedup from reusing cached probabilities across sampling iterations. This yields a 68,000× speedup over Qiskit's circuit simulator at d=64. The simulated QIP operator is made differentiable by substituting quantum-computed values in the forward pass while using classical chain-rule gradients in the backward pass, packaged as torch_qip for seamless PyTorch integration into neural networks, node2vec, and K-Means.

## Results

On MNIST image classification with ProjUNN (20 epochs), the best quantum variant Q-8-Mode achieves 96.13% test accuracy, marginally exceeding the classical baseline of 95.97%, while Q-8-Avg reaches 95.54%. On K-Means clustering over MNIST, the classical method achieves RI 0.9318, NMI 0.7314, and AMI 0.7211, while the best quantum variant (Q-8-Mode) yields RI 0.9295, NMI 0.7300, and AMI 0.7185, showing a small but consistent gap. The state simulator achieves up to 68,251× speedup over the Qiskit circuit simulator at d=64 while producing numerically identical results (MSE ~9e-4, MAE ~2.5e-2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the classical ProjUNN baseline on MNIST, the quantum QIP variant with 8 qubits and OUTPUT-MODE (96.13%) slightly outperforms the classical model (95.97%), while OUTPUT-AVG at the same qubit count (95.54%) falls just below; at 4 qubits the gap widens to ~2–3% below classical. Against the classical node2vec baseline on PPI and BlogCatalog, quantum variants with higher qubit counts (t=6, 8) approach but do not exceed classical Macro-F1, whereas on BlogCatalog the low-qubit Q-4-Avg variant achieves performance comparable to the classical model, suggesting robustness in some graph-embedding settings. Against the classical K-Means baseline on MNIST, all quantum variants underperform: even the best (Q-8-Mode) trails by ~0.2% in RI and ~0.14% in NMI, and the gap grows substantially at lower qubit counts (e.g., Q-4-Mode drops to RI 0.7866 vs. 0.9318 classical), indicating that ranking-sensitive tasks like K-Means are more vulnerable to quantum measurement noise.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Hadamard test and Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE) as foundational quantum algorithms, combining them into explicit QIP circuits for 1-to-1, 1-to-N, and M-to-N inner product computation. It departs from prior quantum matrix multiplication work (Sracic [29], Zhang [32], Shao [28], Li [18]) by providing the first complete, implementable circuits that use quantum parallelism for speedup, encode results in basis states (avoiding tomography), and support float data. The practical contribution of an efficient state-level simulator (bypassing circuit simulation) and the torch_qip package with automatic differentiation positions this work as a bridge between theoretical QIP complexity results and usable ML pipelines, filling a gap the authors identify as absent in the literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 68k simulation speedup and the differentiable torch_qip package make QIP operators practically integrable into gradient-based ML training, which was previously infeasible with standard circuit simulators. The finding that quantum-induced errors have minimal impact on neural network training and graph embedding with sufficient qubits (t=8) is encouraging for hybrid quantum-classical ML, while the K-Means sensitivity to quantum noise identifies a concrete limitation for error-sensitive ranking tasks. Open questions include whether these results hold under realistic NISQ noise/decoherence (the IBM Brisbane experiments show high variance), whether the normalized-vector constraint can be relaxed, and whether the approach scales to deeper networks or larger datasets before fault-tolerant hardware arrives.
