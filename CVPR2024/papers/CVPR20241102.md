## Problem

End-to-end trained navigation agents in simulators like Habitat rely on instantaneous teleportation for motion, creating a large sim2real gap in actuation that prevents efficient, continuous-motion navigation on real robots. Existing work either uses discrete position commands (stopping the robot between decisions) or ignores dynamics entirely, resulting in halting, jerky behavior or complete failure when transferred to physical platforms. This paper closes both the actuation and sensing sim2real gaps to enable fast, precise point-goal navigation on a real robot.

## Input / Output

The agent receives RGB frames (256×144, downsampled from 1280×720), a fused Lidar-like range vector from four RealSense depth cameras, a static point goal g₀ expressed in the initial reference frame (not updated per step), a dead-reckoning odometry estimate p̂ᵗʳ, and a low-frequency (~0.5–1 Hz, irregular period U(8,12)) absolute pose estimate p̂ᵗᵃ from AMCL. The output is a pair of discretized velocity commands drawn from a 28-action space: 4 linear speeds {0, 0.3, 0.6, 1} m/s crossed with 7 angular speeds {−3,…,3} rad/s, executed asynchronously while the robot is already in motion. An auxiliary head also predicts the dynamic goal compass ĝₜ, supervised only during training from ground-truth pose.

## Method

The core contribution is a second-order asymmetric dynamical model of the combined robot-plus-low-level-controller, with 8 parameters (natural frequency and damping for linear/angular acceleration and deceleration) identified from real robot rollouts. This model runs in a fast 30 Hz physics loop inside Habitat, while sensing, the ResNet-18 visual encoder, a 1D-CNN Lidar encoder, and the GRU recurrent policy operate in a slower 3 Hz decision loop, forcing the agent to anticipate its own motion over the 10 sub-steps between decisions. The agent is trained end-to-end with PPO on 800 HM3D scenes (200 M steps) using a reward combining geodesic-distance gain, a slack efficiency cost, and a collision penalty, with a static PointGoal formulation that requires the agent to internally integrate odometry. Noise models for both drifting odometry and non-drifting but coarser absolute localization are injected during training to match real sensor characteristics, and a rudimentary backward-recovery behavior is layered on top of the learned policy for stuck situations.

## Results

On the HM3D/2.5k simulation benchmark with the identified dynamical model, the proposed agent achieves 97.4% SR, 82.2% SPL, and 51.0% SCT, dramatically outperforming the position-command baseline [43] (58.2% SR, 48.2% SPL, 16.8% SCT) and the velocity-command baseline without dynamics [53] (0.8% SR, 0.1% SPL, 0.1% SCT). On the real Naver Rookie robot (Office/20), the method achieves 40% SR, 28.9% SPL, and 10.1% SCT, compared to 15% SR for [43] and 20% SR for [53]. Against the ROS 2 NavStack planner (Table 4), the finetuned learned agent reaches 55% SR and 11.2% SCT versus 80% SR and 26.2% SCT for the map-based planner, indicating the learned method still trails the classical approach in raw efficiency but offers superior robustness to thin obstacles and dynamic agents.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the position-command baseline of Sadek et al. [43], the proposed method wins decisively on every metric in every setting: on real hardware, SR improves from 15% to 40% and SCT from 2.4% to 10.1%, while in simulation with dynamics the gap is even starker (97.4% vs. 58.2% SR on HM3D). Against the velocity-command baseline of Yokoyama et al. [53] (same 28-action discretized velocity space but no dynamical model in simulation), the proposed method is overwhelmingly superior: the no-dynamics agent is essentially non-functional in simulation (0.8% SR) and only marginally better than the position baseline on the real robot (20% SR vs. 40% SR for the proposed method). Against the ROS 2 NavStack planner (Table 4), the learned agent loses on all three metrics in the real world (55% SR vs. 80% SR; 11.2% SCT vs. 26.2% SCT), but the paper notes the ROS 2 planner suffers frequent collisions with thin obstacles that the learned agent avoids, and the comparison is not fully apples-to-apples since the learned agent was finetuned on a Matterport scan of the same building. The authors also show that rearranging furniture (Office/20-alt) has negligible impact on the learned agent (60% SR vs. 55%), while the ROS 2 planner's advantage persists (90% SR).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Habitat-based embodied navigation line of work by addressing the actuation sim2real gap, which prior work has largely ignored in favor of photorealistic rendering. Rather than using discrete teleportation-style motion commands [43] or velocity commands with instantaneous response [53], the authors identify a second-order dynamical model of the robot's closed-loop differential-drive controller from real rollouts and integrate it into Habitat as a 30 Hz physics sub-loop. The method also repurposes standard robotics localization components (wheel-encoder dead reckoning and AMCL) as noisy inputs to a recurrent policy, bridging the gap between the robotics community's model-based approach and the embodied AI community's end-to-end learning paradigm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the motion sim2real gap is not a minor nuisance but a dominant failure mode: an agent trained with the correct action space but without a dynamical model drops from 97.4% to 0.8% SR when evaluated with realistic dynamics, a finding that should reshape how the field prioritizes simulation fidelity. However, real-world validation is limited to 20 episodes on a single Naver Rookie robot in one office, and the ROS 2 NavStack planner still achieves higher SR (80% vs. 55%) on the same environment, suggesting the learned approach has not yet surpassed well-tuned classical methods in practical settings. Open questions include closed-loop adaptation of the identified dynamics, better noise modeling (the supervision head that helps in simulation hurts in real), and handling rare blocking situations that the recurrent memory misinterprets as permanent.
