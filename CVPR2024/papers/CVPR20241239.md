## Problem

Generative video editing must simultaneously preserve structural fidelity, allow fine-grained appearance control, and harness the creative potential of diffusion models, yet existing methods either lack precise controllability, require expensive per-video tuning, or cannot unify multiple control modalities (structure, text, reference frame) in a single framework. Additionally, the field lacks a standardized benchmark that balances controllability and creativity evaluation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241239/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a video clip (17 frames at 4 fps, 384×576 resolution), a text prompt, per-frame structure representations (depth maps by default, or line drawings/PiDi boundaries), and optionally an edited center key frame as appearance reference. It produces a temporally consistent edited video. For long videos exceeding 17 frames, CCEdit processes them via iterative extension (chaining last edited frame as next reference) and interpolation (editing intermediate frames between two anchors) modes, supporting sequences of 240+ frames at 24 fps.

## Method

CCEdit employs a trident network that decouples structure and appearance control: a main text-to-video branch (Stable Diffusion v1.5 augmented with 1D temporal convolution and attention layers, zero-initialized projection out layers for stable training), a frozen ControlNet structure branch that injects per-frame structural features, and a novel appearance branch (a T2I encoder without text cross-attention) that propagates an edited key frame's features to all frames via temporal modules. Training freezes all spatial weights in the main branch and the entire structure branch, updating only the temporal layers and appearance branch on WebVid-10M plus a private dataset. An anchor prior strategy adds a small scaled latent of the edited key frame (α=0.03) to the initial noise of every frame, providing low-frequency guidance that reduces flickering without altering the noise distribution. The framework also supports plugging in personalized T2I models (DreamBooth, LoRA) into the main branch for domain-specific style control.

## Results

On mini-BalanceCC (50 videos), CCEdit achieves the highest MOS scores for editing accuracy (4.06/5) and aesthetics (4.00/5), and the best overall impression (3.87/5) among all nine methods evaluated. The closest competitor, TokenFlow, scores 3.78 on editing accuracy, 3.61 on aesthetics, and 3.58 overall, while the remaining seven baselines fall between 1.48 and 2.97 on overall MOS. In head-to-head comparisons, CCEdit wins 52.9% of cases against TokenFlow (the strongest baseline) and 64.9%–88.9% against all other methods. CCEdit is marginally behind TokenFlow on temporal consistency (3.74 vs. 3.79), the only metric where it does not lead.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CCEdit outperforms all eight baselines on editing accuracy and aesthetics, with the largest margins over Text2Video-Zero (Edit: 4.06 vs. 2.07) and vid2vid-zero (Edit: 4.06 vs. 3.00). TokenFlow is the closest competitor, trailing CCEdit by 0.28 on editing accuracy and 0.29 on overall MOS, but it edges out CCEdit on temporal consistency (3.79 vs. 3.74); in direct pairwise comparisons, CCEdit still wins 52.9% of cases against TokenFlow. Pix2Video is the second-strongest baseline (overall 2.97), losing to CCEdit in 64.9% of head-to-head comparisons. The remaining six methods (Tune-A-Video, vid2vid-zero, Text2Video-Zero, FateZero, ControlVideo, Rerender A Video) all score below 3.0 on overall MOS and lose to CCEdit in 76.7%–88.9% of pairwise comparisons, with Rerender A Video showing the weakest performance (0% ties, 88.9% losses). Notably, FateZero achieves the second-best temporal consistency among baselines (3.30) but still falls well short of CCEdit on editing accuracy (2.47 vs. 4.06), illustrating the trade-off between temporal smoothness and edit fidelity in prior methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

CCEdit composes three well-established components—Stable Diffusion as the base T2I model, ControlNet for structure injection, and AnimateDiff-style shared temporal layers—into a novel trident architecture that explicitly decouples structure and appearance control into separate side branches. This departs from training-free video editing methods (TokenFlow, vid2vid-zero) that rely on cross-frame attention, and from per-video finetuning approaches (Tune-A-Video) that are expensive at inference time, by performing dataset-level fine-tuning of temporal and appearance modules while freezing spatial weights. The appearance branch, which ingests an edited key frame through a dedicated encoder, is the paper's most distinctive architectural addition, absent from prior video editing pipelines that rely solely on text prompts or per-frame diffusion features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The BalanceCC benchmark (100 videos × 4 prompts, with GPT-4V-assisted annotations and a 'Fantasy Level' axis) fills a gap left by TGVE 2023, whose videos suffer from camera shake and low frame rates, and should become a standard evaluation platform for the field. The trident design is practically attractive because it lets users swap structure representations (depth, line drawing, PiDi) and plug in community T2I models (LoRA, DreamBooth) without retraining, lowering the barrier to creative video editing. A key open limitation is that the method struggles with substantial structural alterations (e.g., rabbit → tiger), since structure is preserved via explicit extraction rather than learned end-to-end, suggesting future work on more flexible structure conditioning.
