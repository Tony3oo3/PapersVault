## Problem

While prior work has shown that Transformers and CNNs differ in robustness to occlusions and perturbations, the underlying decision-making mechanisms remain poorly understood. It is unclear whether the architectural choice (attention vs. convolution) or other design elements (normalization, receptive field, distillation) drive these behavioral differences, and no systematic, dataset-wide statistical framework existed to compare how different backbones actually use visual evidence for classification.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240574/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes RGB images from the ImageNet validation set (first 5,000 images) and a pre-trained visual recognition model as input. Images are partitioned into a 7×7 grid of 49 non-overlapping patches for MSE/sub-explanation analysis, and attribution maps are generated at 28×28 resolution via iGOS++. Outputs include per-image MSE patch sets, sub-explanation counts at four likelihood-ratio thresholds (≥80%, ≥70%, ≥60%, ≥50%), pairwise cross-testing insertion/deletion scores normalized by each model's top-1 confidence on original vs. blurred images, and a 2D Kernel PCA projection of the feature-use landscape.

## Method

The paper proposes two complementary methodologies built on top of Structured Attention Graphs (SAG) beam search. First, sub-explanation counting: after finding all Minimal Sufficient Explanations (MSEs) via beam search (width 5, threshold 0.9) on the 7×7 patch grid, a tree is grown from each MSE by iteratively deleting one patch per child node; the number of surviving nodes above each confidence threshold quantifies compositionality (many sub-explanations = joint use of multiple patches) versus disjunctivism (few sub-explanations but many small MSEs = OR-like reliance on diverse patch subsets). Second, cross-testing: an attribution map generated by model A is used to progressively reveal pixels into a blurred baseline, and the insertion/deletion AUC is measured on model B, yielding a pairwise similarity matrix that is projected to 2D via Kernel PCA to visualize the feature-use landscape. Ablation experiments (replacing layer normalization with batch or group normalization, reducing receptive fields, removing distillation) isolate the specific architectural factors driving compositionality, revealing that normalization type is the dominant factor—batch normalization reduces sub-explanations by ~80% compared to layer/group normalization.

## Results

Across 5,000 ImageNet validation images, ConvNeXt-T and undistilled transformers (Swin-T, Nest-T) exhibit dramatically more sub-explanations than traditional CNNs: ConvNeXt-T has 980.16 sub-explanations at ≥80% likelihood ratio versus 27.45 for VGG19 and 53.68 for ResNet50. At the ≥50% threshold, Swin-T reaches 7,268 sub-explanations per image compared to only 330–600 for CNNs and distilled transformers. Replacing layer normalization with batch normalization in ConvNeXt-T-3 reduces sub-explanations by ~80% (from 7,477 to 673 at ≥50%), while reducing receptive field size alone reduces them by only ~40%. Cross-testing via Kernel PCA reveals three distinct feature-use clusters (older CNNs, newer CNNs/ConvNeXt, transformers), with distillation pulling transformers toward the CNN cluster.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an analysis paper, so 'comparisons' refer to behavioral differences revealed across architectures rather than accuracy wins. Older CNNs (VGG19, ResNet50) are the least compositional, with only 27–54 sub-explanations at ≥80% confidence, indicating a disjunctive strategy of relying on few small patch conjunctions. Newer CNNs trained with modern augmentation (ResNet50-C1/C2/D) increase MSE counts to 9.5–11.0 but remain far less compositional than ConvNeXt-T (980 sub-explanations at ≥80%) or Swin-T (7,268 at ≥50%). Distilled transformers (DeiT-S-dis, PiT-S-dis, LeViT-256) collapse to CNN-like disjunctive behavior with 48–58 sub-explanations at ≥80%, losing the compositional advantage of their undistilled counterparts (DeiT-S: 72, PiT-S: 131). Cross-testing shows older CNNs use a narrower, more singular set of features (high insertion scores when their heatmaps are tested on other models, but low scores when other models' heatmaps are tested on them), while ConvNeXt and undistilled transformers form a distinct feature-use cluster. A simple ensemble of ConvNeXt-T, Swin-T, and ResNet50-C2 achieves 82.9% ImageNet top-1 accuracy, surpassing all individual models (82.1%, 81.2%, 80.0% respectively), confirming that the clusters exploit complementary features.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Structured Attention Graphs (SAG) [30], which introduced beam search to find multiple Minimal Sufficient Explanations (MSEs) per image, and on iGOS++ [13] for generating high-quality attribution maps. Prior work on understanding transformers vs. CNNs [3, 17, 23, 19] focused on per-image robustness properties (occlusion, adversarial perturbations) or representation-level analyses, but did not provide dataset-wide statistical comparisons of decision-making mechanisms. This paper departs from that line by proposing two systematic methodologies—sub-explanation counting and cross-testing—that aggregate explanation statistics across thousands of images to characterize architectural-level behavioral differences, and by identifying normalization (rather than attention vs. convolution) as the dominant driver of compositionality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that batch normalization, not the attention mechanism, is the primary factor reducing compositionality challenges a widely held assumption in the vision community and has direct implications for model design choices in downstream tasks like detection and segmentation where broader feature utilization may be beneficial. The cross-testing methodology offers a practical tool for quantifying feature-use overlap across architectures, with the ensemble result (82.9% from averaging ConvNeXt-T, Swin-T, and ResNet50-C2) suggesting tangible benefits from architectural diversity. Open questions remain: whether compositionality is universally preferable (the authors acknowledge insufficient evidence), whether these findings generalize beyond ImageNet-scale classification, and whether hybrid normalization schemes (e.g., batch-group normalization [43]) can capture the best of both behaviors.
