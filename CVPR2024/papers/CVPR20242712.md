## Problem

Mobile manipulation in cluttered, unstructured environments requires tightly coupling perception, navigation, and whole-body control, yet prior approaches decompose these into separate modular skills (map, plan, execute) that compound errors, introduce latency, and cannot react to dynamic changes. The paper asks whether a single reactive policy can learn to simultaneously decide where to look, how to move its body, and how to manipulate objects without relying on pre-built maps or explicit planning.

## Input / Output

The policy consumes an ego-centric depth image from an actuated head-mounted camera (87° horizontal, 58° vertical FOV), full proprioception (joint angles, velocities, base linear/angular velocity), a goal location, and the object's initial position, and outputs low-level velocity commands for the 10-joint Stretch robot (base translation/rotation, arm lift/extend, 3-DoF wrist, gripper) plus 2-DoF camera joint actions at 10 Hz. During Phase 1 training, the depth image is replaced by 'scandots'—xyz coordinates of obstacle bounding boxes—masked to include only those within the camera's current field of view, forcing the agent to actively reposition its camera to gather environmental information.

## Method

SPIN uses a two-phase teacher-student framework. Phase 1 trains LSTM policies via PPO with backpropagation-through-time in procedurally generated cluttered corridors (IsaacGym, 8192 parallel envs), where the robot receives only visible scandots encoded by a permutation-invariant PointNet and must jointly predict robot and camera actions; two variants are explored—Coupled Visuomotor Optimization (CVO) optimizes both action streams simultaneously, while Decoupled Visuomotor Optimization (DVO) first learns navigation with full scandots access, then distills camera+robot control from that teacher. Phase 2 replaces scandots with a CNN that maps rendered depth images to perception latents, trained via DAgger to match the Phase 1 teacher's actions, yielding a deployable policy that operates purely from ego-depth without any explicit map. The key architectural insight is that the masking function over scandots creates a partially observable MDP where the camera action directly determines future observations, making perception and control a single coupled optimization problem rather than a sequential pipeline.

## Results

On 6 simulation benchmarks (Reach, Pick, Place across Easy/Medium/Hard difficulty), SPIN(CVO) achieves the highest success rates, reaching 0.93–1.00 on all Hard scenarios, compared to 0.20–0.26 for FixCam and 0.57–0.63 for NoPointNet. SPIN(CVO) also achieves the shortest average episode durations on Hard scenarios (e.g., 23.57 s vs. 38.94 s for FixCam on Reach-Hard). In real-world tests, SPIN outperforms the classical mapping-and-planning baseline by 20–40% on static obstacles (0.8 vs. 0.4–0.6 success) and dramatically on dynamic obstacles (0.6 vs. 0.0–0.2 success).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPIN(CVO) outperforms FixCam (fixed forward-looking camera) by approximately 68% on Hard scenarios, confirming that active vision with an actuated camera is essential for navigating cluttered environments. Against NoPointNet (MLP instead of PointNet for scandot encoding), SPIN achieves ~33% higher success rate, showing the benefit of permutation-invariant point encoding. The Mapping baseline (pre-built exteroceptive map) performs competitively on Easy/Medium scenarios but falls below SPIN on Hard cluttered scenes (e.g., 0.90 vs. 1.00 on Place-Hard), as systematic noise in object locations hinders avoidance. In real-world tests (Table 2), SPIN achieves 0.8 success vs. 0.4–0.6 for the classical gmapping + move_base baseline on static obstacles, and 0.6 vs. 0.0–0.2 on dynamic obstacles, where the classical method fails entirely because it relies on a pre-built map. SPIN(DVO) and SPIN(CVO) achieve similar performance in simulation, with CVO slightly ahead on Hard scenarios, suggesting that joint optimization of camera and robot actions helps discover optimal shortcuts.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPIN departs from the dominant classical pipeline (SLAM-based mapping followed by path planning and separate manipulation) by casting simultaneous perception, interaction, and navigation as a single reactive end-to-end policy problem. It also extends prior end-to-end navigation work (e.g., RobustNav, learning exploration policies) by treating the camera as an actuated control variable rather than a fixed sensor, and by coupling navigation with object manipulation in one policy. The teacher-student training framework (RL with privileged scandots distilled into a depth-CNN student via DAgger) borrows from the broader knowledge-distillation literature but applies it to the specific bottleneck of expensive depth rendering in RL. Conceptually, the paper aligns with recent whole-body control work (e.g., Deep Whole-Body Control) but adds the active-perception dimension that those methods lack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a reactive, map-free policy outperforms a classical mapping-and-planning baseline (which has the advantage of a pre-built full map) in cluttered environments challenges the assumption that explicit world models are necessary for robust mobile manipulation. The emergent dynamic obstacle avoidance—never explicitly trained—suggests that large-scale procedural simulation can produce robustness to distribution shifts, though the evidence is limited to two real-world scenes and a single robot platform. Key limitations include reliance on stereo-matched depth rather than RGB (failing on glass/shiny surfaces), a single robot embodiment (Hello Robot Stretch), and the absence of language or high-level task specification. Open questions include whether the approach scales to more dexterous manipulation, whether RGB perception can close the remaining gap, and whether the coupled vs. decoupled training distinction matters at larger scale.
