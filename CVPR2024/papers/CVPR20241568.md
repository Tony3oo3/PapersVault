## Problem

Polarization-based vision (glass segmentation, shape-from-polarization) has never been formally explored for adversarial vulnerability, despite the rapid growth of polarization sensors and applications. Unlike RGB-based physical attacks, which must trade off attack efficacy against human-eye perceivability, polarization is inherently invisible to naked eyes, raising the question of whether polarization-based vision is actually *more* vulnerable in the physical world. This paper demonstrates that it is, by showing that a simple hardware modification to a commercial LCD projector enables contact-free, visually imperceptible attacks on state-of-the-art polarization vision models.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a clean Stokes-parameter image (s0, s1, s2) at 612×512 resolution, a whitebox target model (PGSNet for segmentation or SfP-wild for normal estimation), and a pre-captured set of K=17 candidate polarization images corresponding to uniform projection values discretely spaced from 0 to 255. The output is a grid-based projection pattern (grid sizes 8/16/32 for segmentation, 2 for SfP) where each grid cell is assigned one of the K candidate values; when fed to the modified LCD projector, this pattern produces light of constant intensity but spatially varying polarization angle, which the polarization camera records as a manipulated Stokes image that fools the target model into producing incorrect segmentation masks or fabricated normal maps.

## Method

The key hardware insight is removing the front linear polarizer from a one-chip LCD projector: the liquid crystal cells then rotate the polarization direction of each pixel's light (0 to π/2) while intensity stays constant, so the projected light appears uniformly white to human eyes but carries a programmable polarization pattern. Rather than modeling the full differentiable path from projection pattern v to reflected Stokes image s (which requires unknown scene geometry and material), the authors optimize over a softmax-weighted combination of K pre-captured candidate polarization images, with the weight vector Ω as the sole optimization variable updated via gradient ascent on the adversarial loss. The loss combines Binary Cross Entropy with a custom L_E term (following SegPGD) that explicitly penalizes the model for predicting non-glass pixels as glass and vice versa, amplifying attack efficacy. For physical-world robustness, EOT-style augmentation (Gaussian noise, blur, random background intensity scaling) is applied during optimization, and the grid-based quantization of the projection pattern (each cell assigned one of K discrete values) provides inherent robustness to minor real-world degradations. The same framework extends to SfP-wild with a simpler MAE loss and finer grid (size 2) to fabricate arbitrary object shapes from polarization cues alone.

## Results

In the digital world, the optimized grid-8 perturbation (Ours-8) increases MAE from 0.101 (unpolarized) to 0.719 and drops IoU from 0.715 to 0.200, indicating severe degradation of PGSNet's glass segmentation. In the physical world, the grid-32 perturbation achieves the best attack performance with MAE 0.347 and IoU 0.361, outperforming both random perturbations (MAE 0.257, IoU 0.474) and the unpolarized baseline. For the SfP-wild shape estimation task, the polarizing projection successfully induces the model to estimate a non-existent cube shape from a flat background, with physical-world results closely matching digital simulations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an attack paper, so the 'baselines' are the unpolarized (clean) input and randomly sampled perturbations rather than competing attack methods. The optimized perturbations substantially outperform random ones: in the physical world at grid size 32, Ours-32 achieves MAE 0.347 vs. Random-32's 0.257, and IoU 0.361 vs. 0.474, a clear margin. Ablations show that EOT is critical for physical-world transferability (Ours-8 w/o EOT drops from MAE 0.318 to 0.321 in physical world, but the gap widens at grid 16: 0.399 vs. 0.372), while L_E consistently improves attack efficacy across all grid sizes in both domains. Interestingly, in the physical world, lower-resolution perturbations (grid 32) outperform higher-resolution ones (grid 8), attributed to the inherent robustness of coarser grids against real-world degradations. The paper does not compare against other adversarial attack methods on polarization, as this is the first work to demonstrate physical-world attacks on polarization-based vision.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the physical adversarial attack literature from RGB-based vision to the polarization modality, building on the EOT framework (Athalye et al., 2018) for robust perturbation generation and the optical adversarial attack paradigm (Gnanasambandam et al., 2021) for gradient-based physical-world optimization. The key departure is a hardware-level insight: by removing the front polarizer from a one-chip LCD projector, each pixel's polarization direction becomes independently controllable while intensity remains constant, decoupling the attack channel from the visual channel. This repurposes the LCD's liquid-crystal rotation mechanism (governed by Malus's law) as a polarization steering device, enabling grid-based polarizing perturbations that are invisible to human eyes yet fully captured by polarization cameras.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first formal demonstration that polarization-based vision is vulnerable to physical adversarial attacks, overturning the intuitive assumption that a modality imperceptible to humans would be inherently safer. The attack simultaneously achieves high efficacy and complete visual stealth, eliminating the fundamental efficacy-vs-conceivability trade-off that constrains all prior RGB physical attacks. However, the study is limited to whitebox settings with a fixed co-located camera-projector setup, low projector luminance (restricting applicability to indoor/low-light scenes), and only two target tasks. Open questions include whether blackbox or transferable attacks exist, how defense mechanisms (adversarial training, active illumination) might mitigate the threat, and whether the vulnerability extends to more complex outdoor scenes with arbitrary geometry and materials.
