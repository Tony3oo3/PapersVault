## Problem

Existing interpretable video action recognition methods decompose actions into human-object relationship transitions but suffer from two-stage pipelines (scene graph prediction followed by relation modeling) that yield sub-optimal performance compared to black-box models. The paper addresses how to achieve both high recognition accuracy and built-in interpretability without sacrificing either, by leveraging a language model's logical reasoning to guide a video model during training while using only the video model at inference.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241165/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

During training, the method consumes RGB video frames (T uniformly sampled frames) along with spatio-temporal scene graph annotations (from Action Genome for Charades or re-annotated CAD-120) that provide frame-level human-object relationship labels. At inference, only RGB video frames are required; the model outputs multi-label action class scores plus an interpretable explanation identifying which specific relationship transitions (e.g., 'holding box' → 'not holding box') between selected human-object pairs drove the prediction, obtained by mapping visual relation embeddings to their nearest semantic labels in a learned joint embedding space.

## Method

LaIAR builds two parallel branches—a video model and a language model—that share an identical DT-Former architecture, which combines a Gumbel-Softmax-based adaptive token selector (applied jointly over temporal and spatial dimensions to prune redundant human-object relationships) with a standard transformer encoder that models relationship transitions. The key training mechanism is a three-part cross-modal knowledge transfer scheme: (1) a contrastive loss aligns visual and semantic relation representations in a shared joint embedding space, (2) a token selection supervision loss forces the video model's selection matrix to match the language model's, and (3) a KL-divergence cross-modal loss makes the video model mimic the language model's full output distribution. This redefines interpretability as a visual-language alignment problem: the language model's logical reasoning about relationship transitions steers the video model's learning, and at test time the video model alone produces both the action prediction and the explanation via nearest-neighbor lookup in the joint embedding space.

## Results

On the Charades dataset, LaIAR achieves 45.1 mAP using only RGB input, surpassing the strongest RGB-only baseline OR2G (44.9 mAP) and SGFB (44.3 mAP). In Oracle evaluation mode (using ground-truth bounding boxes), LaIAR reaches 63.6 mAP, outperforming OR2G Oracle (63.3) and VideoMLN Oracle (62.8). On CAD-120, LaIAR attains 0.85 mAR, exceeding VideoMLN (0.83) and Explainable AAR-RAR (0.80). The method also demonstrates superior domain-shift robustness, with a variance of 0.11 across five scene-split subsets versus 0.30 for STIGPN.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LaIAR outperforms all RGB-only baselines on Charades, with a narrow 0.2 mAP margin over OR2G (45.1 vs. 44.9) and a 0.8 mAP lead over SGFB (45.1 vs. 44.3). The gap widens substantially against 3D CNN methods: SlowFast (42.1), LFB (42.5), STRG (39.7), VideoMLN (38.4), and I3D (15.6). In Oracle mode, LaIAR (63.6 mAP) edges out OR2G Oracle (63.3) and VideoMLN Oracle (62.8) while using only bounding boxes rather than full ground-truth scene graphs. On CAD-120, LaIAR achieves 0.85 mAR, beating VideoMLN (0.83), Explainable AAR-RAR (0.80), and Temporal Segment Networks (0.77 with RGB+Flow). For domain-shift robustness, LaIAR shows a variance of 0.11 across five non-overlapping scene splits versus 0.30 for STIGPN, while also achieving a higher average accuracy (57.2 vs. 54.1).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the two-stage interpretable action recognition paradigm (scene graph prediction followed by relation modeling, as in VideoMLN and OR2G) by unifying the process: a language model and a video model share the same DT-Former architecture and are trained jointly, with the language model serving purely as a training-time guide. The cross-modal knowledge transfer mechanism (contrastive joint embedding, token selection supervision, KL-divergence distillation) draws on knowledge distillation literature but repurposes it to transfer logical reasoning structure from semantic to visual representations. The Gumbel-Softmax-based adaptive token selection adapts ideas from efficient transformer inference to the specific problem of identifying salient human-object relationship transitions in video.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The modular design—language model active only during training, video model alone at inference—offers a practical path to interpretable action recognition without the typical performance penalty of purely symbolic or post-hoc methods. However, the approach still depends on scene graph annotations (Action Genome) and a Faster R-CNN detector, limiting its applicability to settings where such supervision is available. Open questions include whether the framework scales to open-vocabulary or long-tail action recognition, whether the interpretability provided by nearest-neighbor semantic labels in the joint embedding space is genuinely faithful to the video model's internal computation, and whether the modest performance gains (≈2.5 mAP on Charades) persist on larger, more diverse benchmarks.
