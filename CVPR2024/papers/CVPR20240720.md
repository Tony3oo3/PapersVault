## Problem

Existing time-resolved 3D reconstruction systems rely on expensive, bulky laboratory-grade single-photon cameras (>$10,000) with high spatial and temporal resolution, making them impractical for applications like wearable computing, drones, and AR. While low-cost SPAD proximity sensors (<$5, <20mm³) have become available, prior work has only recovered simple parametric geometry (e.g., a single plane) from them. This paper addresses the gap by demonstrating that complex, non-parametric 3D shapes can be reconstructed from spatially distributed commodity SPAD sensors with known poses.

## Input / Output

The method takes transient histograms (128 bins each) from N spatially distributed single-pixel SPAD sensors (128–240 viewpoints in real-world experiments, 256 in simulation) along with their known poses. Each sensor pairs a diffuse infrared laser with a wide-FoV (~30°) detector at low temporal resolution (>100 ps), so the total spatial information is extremely sparse (hundreds of pixels per scene) but each histogram encodes rich temporal structure over a large scene patch. The output is a neural signed distance field representing the scene surface, from which a 3D mesh can be extracted.

## Method

The approach uses an analysis-by-synthesis pipeline: a neural SDF (MLP with position encoding, following NeuS) is rendered into transient waveforms via volume rendering that approximates the path-integral transient formation model, then passed through a differentiable sensor model that sequentially applies laser pulse convolution, pile-up distortion, and time-jitter blurring to produce predicted histograms. A key technical contribution is bilevel importance sampling that weights both ray directions (ω) and points along each ray (p(ω,t)) by per-bin density weights, concentrating samples on high-density surface regions. Optimization minimizes an L1 loss between rendered and observed histograms, regularized by an Eikonal term on the SDF and a total-variation penalty against floaters. The method assumes a spatially uniform Lambertian albedo yet proves robust to textured and mildly specular surfaces, and requires only a one-off intrinsic calibration (bin size, delay) per sensor.

## Results

On simulated data (8 objects, 256 sensors, 256 bins), the proposed method achieves an average Chamfer distance below 5 mm, an order of magnitude lower than all baselines (21–69 mm). On real-world captures using the $10 AMS TMF8820 proximity sensor (128–240 viewpoints per object), the method achieves two-way Chamfer distances of 9.8–18.4 mm, compared to 44–99 mm for the best baseline. The method also demonstrates robustness to non-Lambertian surfaces and rich textures despite assuming a spatially uniform albedo.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two families of baselines: reprojection (peak and threshold variants) and space carving. Reprojection methods extract a single depth value per sensor measurement (either the histogram peak or the first bin above a threshold) and back-project to a point cloud; they produce sparse, unevenly distributed points that yield poor GT→Rec Chamfer distances (e.g., 52.5 mm vs. 6.4 mm on Big Box). Space carving marks voxels as occupied/empty based on thresholded depth and takes the union across sensors; it performs reasonably on simulated data with structured poses (21–45 mm) but degrades significantly on real-world captures with varied sensor orientations (44–99 mm). The proposed method outperforms all baselines by a wide margin on every object in both simulated and real-world settings, achieving roughly 5–10× lower Chamfer distance. The advantage is most pronounced in the GT→Rec direction, where the dense SDF-based reconstruction covers the full surface while reprojection leaves large gaps.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the NeuS neural implicit surface framework to render transient histograms rather than RGB images, extending the analysis-by-synthesis pipeline from photometric stereo and NeRF to the time-resolved imaging domain. It builds directly on Jungerman et al. [23] and Sifferman et al. [43], which demonstrated differentiable rendering with low-cost SPAD sensors but were limited to single-sensor, planar-surface recovery; here the authors generalize to multi-sensor, nonparametric scene geometry. The work also draws a conceptual bridge to Transient NeRF [30] and NLOS-NeuS [12], which perform 3D reconstruction from transients but rely on expensive lab-grade hardware with no ambient light, whereas this paper targets commodity proximity sensors operating under real-world conditions with modeled sensor non-idealities (pile-up, time jitter, diffuse laser pulse shape).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a ~$10, 20 mm³ proximity sensor (TMF8820) can reconstruct complex 3D shapes with sub-10 mm accuracy, opening a path toward 3D vision in cost- and power-constrained settings like wearable hand tracking, drone collision avoidance, and mobile robotics. Key limitations remain: the method assumes Lambertian reflectance (failing on specular objects), requires 128–240 views, and takes hours per reconstruction, making it impractical for real-time use. Open questions include whether spatially varying reflectance can be recovered, whether the approach scales to larger scenes or finer details, and whether fast NeRF-style optimizations can reduce reconstruction time to enable SLAM-like applications.
