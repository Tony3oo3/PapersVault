## Problem

Most visually-tuned multimodal language models struggle to comprehend textual content embedded in images, largely because their training data is dominated by natural images with minimal text. Existing text-rich datasets (TextCap, TextVQA, DocVQA) provide relatively short annotations and lack the comprehensive, instruction-style supervision needed to train general-purpose reading assistants.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242355/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

LaRA takes a text-rich RGB image (processed at 336×336 by the CLIP encoder), a natural-language instruction or question, and OCR text extracted from the original high-resolution image via Azure Read API and PaddleOCR. The OCR string is concatenated with the user instruction before being fed to the language decoder, while the image is converted to patch-level visual tokens through a trainable projection. Outputs are free-form text: short extractive answers for concrete questions, longer abstract answers (averaging ~24 words) for reasoning questions, or detailed image captions averaging 65 words.

## Method

LaRA combines a frozen CLIP-ViT-L/14-336 visual encoder, a trainable linear projection into the LLM embedding space, and a Vicuna-1.5-13B language decoder, following the two-stage LLaVA training recipe but initializing the projection from LLaVAR. The key architectural difference is that OCR text extracted from the high-resolution image is merged with the user instruction as an additional text input, giving the LLM direct access to textual content that the low-resolution visual encoder cannot resolve. During the fine-tuning stage, both the projection matrix and the language decoder are updated (learning rate 2e-5, batch size 32, cosine annealing) on a combined set of ~90K TRINS VQA pairs and 158K LLaVA instruction pairs, while the visual encoder remains frozen. The TRINS dataset itself is built semi-automatically: CLIP-based filtering selects text-rich images from LAION-5B, 20 native-English annotators write detailed captions over 2,079 hours, and GPT-4/LLaMA-70B generate balanced extractive and abstract question-answer pairs conditioned on the human captions and OCR results.

## Results

On the primary TRINS-VQA benchmark, LaRA achieves 62.8% extract accuracy and a CIDEr score of 186.6 on abstract questions, substantially outperforming LLaVAR (51.7% / 84.9) and mPLUG-Owl2 (61.0% / 97.2). On OCRBench, LaRA scores 548 overall, trailing only Gemini (651) and GPT-4v (636) while surpassing Monkey (514) and mPLUG-Owl2 (366). On TRINS-Cap captioning, LaRA reaches CIDEr 46.7 and BLEU-1 37.7, nearly doubling the CIDEr of LLaVA 1.5 (15.4). On general visual benchmarks, LaRA matches or exceeds LLaVA (e.g., VizWiz 53.1 vs. 45.0), confirming that TRINS fine-tuning does not degrade natural-image performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LaRA outperforms all open-source baselines on TRINS-VQA: it beats LLaVAR (51.7→62.8 extract acc., 84.9→186.6 CIDEr), mPLUG-Owl2 (61.0→62.8 extract, 97.2→186.6 CIDEr), LLaVA 1.5 (38.8→62.8 extract, 40.7→186.6 CIDEr), and LLaVA (23.7→62.8 extract). Qwen-VL marginally edges LaRA on extract accuracy (63.6 vs. 62.8) but LaRA more than doubles its CIDEr on abstract questions (186.6 vs. 79.4), indicating superior reasoning over simple text extraction. On OCRBench, LaRA (548) surpasses Monkey (514), mPLUG-Owl2 (366), LLaVAR (346), LLaVA 1.5 (331), mPLUG-Owl (297), and MiniGPT-V2 (157), but trails the proprietary Gemini (651) and GPT-4v (636). On TRINS-Cap, LaRA (CIDEr 46.7) dominates InstructBLIP (8.0), MiniGPT-4 (6.3), LLaVA (8.8), and Qwen-VL (16.6). On general benchmarks (OKVQA, GQA, VSR, VizWiz), LaRA matches or slightly exceeds LLaVA, confirming no catastrophic forgetting. For text-to-image generation (TRINS-Gen), TextDiffuser achieves the best OCR accuracy (0.5027) while DeepFloyd IF attains the best CLIP score (0.3917) and lowest FID (49.96), with ControlNet in between.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multimodal instruction-tuning lineage (LLaVA, MiniGPT-4, InstructBLIP) and the text-rich image understanding line (LLaVAR, Monkey) by introducing a semi-automatic annotation pipeline that leverages GPT-4 and CLIP to produce substantially more detailed captions and QA pairs than prior text-rich datasets. Architecturally, LaRA is a minimal departure from LLaVA: it simply prepends OCR-extracted text to the instruction input, building directly on LLaVAR's observation that noisy OCR annotations improve vision-language alignment. The key methodological departure is the human-machine collaborative annotation process, which the authors argue yields higher fidelity than purely machine-generated data (as in LLaVAR) or purely human annotation (as in TextCap, DocVQA).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TRINS addresses a concrete and underexplored gap: most multimodal LLMs are trained predominantly on natural images and fail at reading embedded text, yet real-world applications (documents, posters, book covers) are text-dense. The dataset's significantly longer annotations (65.1 words vs. ~12 for TextCap) and longer answers (23.9 words vs. <4 for related VQA datasets) push models toward deeper comprehension rather than surface-level extraction. Open questions include whether the OCR-augmented approach generalizes to languages/scripts not well-supported by current OCR tools, whether self-alignment or human verification of LLM-generated QA pairs (explicitly left as future work) would further improve quality, and whether a better-trained visual encoder on text-rich images could eventually eliminate the need for an external OCR module entirely.
