## Problem

Fine-grained action analysis in multi-person sports videos is severely hindered by rapid player movements, intense occlusions, and dynamic interpersonal interactions, yet existing sports video datasets lack the fine-grained spatial-temporal annotations needed to benchmark such analysis. No prior dataset provides dense frame-level bounding boxes and temporal boundaries for fine-grained sub-action types in multi-person basketball scenarios, leaving spatiotemporal action localization (STAL) in this setting largely unexplored.

## Input / Output

PoSTAL takes a video snippet (T×H×W×3) and a short text prompt describing the target player's appearance (e.g., 'a player wearing a [Color] jersey number [Number]') as input. It outputs N action tubes—each a sequence of 2D bounding boxes spanning T′ frames—along with a fine-grained sub-action class label per tube from 52 categories. During inference, the descriptive words are automatically filled in from the visual features of the test video rather than being provided externally, and the center frame of each predicted tube is selected as the per-frame detection.

## Method

PoSTAL comprises a Prompt-driven Target Action Encoder (PTA) and an Action Tube-specific Detector (ATD). PTA encodes the text prompt and a predicted sub-action type (obtained from video features via a channel-separated convolutional network, not the ground-truth label) into a shared prompt embedding space using BERT, then fuses this with CSN-152 video features through a spatial-temporal multi-head cross-attention where video features serve as queries and prompt embeddings as keys/values. ATD operates in two parallel branches: a single-level transformer with learnable tube queries (approximated by 3D cuboids) that localizes action tubes in space and time via spatial-temporal MHSA/MHCA layers, and a multi-level transformer that classifies the fine-grained sub-action category. Training uses a five-term multi-task loss (prompt-space classification, binary target presence, tube-level classification, per-frame IoU matching, and L1 box regression) with bipartite matching to assign 6 tube queries to ground-truth tubes, all under a CSN-152 backbone pretrained on Kinetics-400.

## Results

On the FineSports dataset, PoSTAL achieves 21.54 frame-mAP@0.5, 31.18 video-mAP@0.2, and 24.31 video-mAP@0.5, outperforming both baselines across all reported metrics. The overall absolute values are relatively low (e.g., ~21% frame-mAP) due to the inherent difficulty of multi-person basketball scenarios with severe occlusion and rapid player interactions. PoSTAL's largest margin is on video-mAP@0.5, where it surpasses TubeR by 6.55 points (24.31 vs. 17.76), indicating substantially better temporal consistency of predicted action tubes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PoSTAL is compared against two prior spatial-temporal action localization methods: MOC (ECCV 2020) and TubeR (CVPR 2022). Against MOC, PoSTAL improves frame-mAP@0.5 by 2.33 points (21.54 vs. 19.21); MOC does not report video-level metrics. Against TubeR, PoSTAL wins on all three metrics: +2.06 on frame-mAP@0.5 (21.54 vs. 19.48), +2.27 on video-mAP@0.2 (31.18 vs. 28.91), and a substantial +6.55 on video-mAP@0.5 (24.31 vs. 17.76). The authors attribute the frame-level gain over TubeR to the prompt-driven target action encoder (PTA) guiding bounding-box generation more accurately, and the larger video-level gain to the action tube-specific detector (ATD) producing more temporally coherent tubes. Both baselines lack any text-prompt mechanism, which the paper identifies as the key architectural distinction enabling PoSTAL's advantage in multi-person, occlusion-heavy basketball scenes.

## Relation to Prior Work

PoSTAL extends the established spatial-temporal action localization (STAL) paradigm—previously addressed by frame-level methods (MOC, ECCV'20) and video-level tubelet transformers (TubeR, CVPR'22)—by introducing a prompt-driven vision-language cross-attention mechanism that uses textual descriptions (jersey color and number) to explicitly guide target player identification in crowded multi-person sports scenes. The FineSports dataset itself extends MultiSports (ICCV'21) in scale (10,000 vs. 800 basketball videos) and annotation granularity (52 vs. 18 sub-action types), while leveraging the MixSort-OC tracker from SportsMOT (ICCV'23) to streamline spatial annotation. Conceptually, the work repurposes BERT/BLIP-based text encoding and spatial-temporal MHCA from the vision-language literature into a sports-specific target-action encoder, a departure from prior STAL methods that rely solely on visual tubelet queries without explicit language grounding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FineSports provides the first large-scale multi-person basketball benchmark with dense spatial-temporal annotations (123K bounding boxes, 32K temporal boundaries) at a granularity sufficient for fine-grained action tube detection, filling a gap left by prior sports datasets that focus on recognition or temporal localization alone. The prompt-driven formulation opens a path toward language-guided sports analysis where a user can specify a target player and retrieve their action sequence, a capability absent in prior STAL work. However, the dataset is limited to NBA basketball, and the overall performance remains low (F@0.5 ≈ 21.5%), indicating that multi-person occlusion and rapid interaction dynamics remain largely unsolved. Generalization to other team sports (football, volleyball, baseball) and to longer video sequences are natural open questions.
