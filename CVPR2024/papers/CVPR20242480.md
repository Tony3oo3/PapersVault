## Problem

Image classification models exhibit extreme per-class accuracy disparity (e.g., 100% vs. 16% on ImageNet) even when training data is perfectly balanced across classes, a phenomenon distinct from long-tailed recognition where class frequency is the primary driver. The root cause of this 'unfairness' on balanced data—whether it stems from classifier bias or problematic feature representations—remains unexplored, and no systematic analysis exists for how standard training techniques affect per-class fairness.

## Input / Output

The study evaluates standard RGB image classification across 8 balanced datasets (CIFAR-100 at 32×32, ImageNet at 224×224, WIT-400M, and others) using CNNs (ResNet-50/101, WideResNet-34-10), vision transformers (ViT-B), and vision-language models (CLIP, Stable Diffusion). Beyond overall top-1 accuracy, the key outputs are per-class accuracy sorted by difficulty, prediction bias vectors (G_n, G_p), classifier weight norms, class mean angles (CMA), and feature variance norms, enabling diagnosis of where and why individual classes underperform.

## Method

The paper introduces the concept of Model Prediction Bias (Definition 2), decomposing a model's softmax outputs into false-positive frequency (G_n) and true-positive frequency (G_p) components, and proves a gradient convergence condition (Lemma 1) showing G_n + G_p = CF at convergence. By evaluating with k-NN and a fixed ETF classifier, they demonstrate that unfairness originates from problematic representations rather than classifier weight norms or separation angles. They further show that harder classes exhibit higher prediction bias (more other classes confused with them), causing false positives to overwhelm true positives during optimization, which is linked to greater feature diversity (higher class-mean feature variance) in hard classes. Finally, they empirically verify that data augmentations (Mixup, CutMix, AutoAug) and representation learning (contrastive learning, masked modeling) disproportionately improve hard-class accuracy, thereby promoting fairness alongside overall performance, whereas re-weighting sacrifices easy-class accuracy.

## Results

On ImageNet with ResNet-50, the best class ('ostrich') achieves 100% top-1 accuracy while the worst class ('screen') achieves only 16%, revealing extreme unfairness even on balanced data. The Easy/Medium/Hard class averages are 93.1/81.1/59.4% for ResNet-50 and 95.4/86.6/68.9% for ViT-B on ImageNet. On CIFAR-100, WideResNet-34-10 shows a similar pattern (92.2/83.2/70.1% for Easy/Medium/Hard). CLIP models exhibit even more severe disparity, with the worst class at 0% accuracy and a 50% gap between Easy and Hard class averages.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Data augmentation strategies (AutoAug, Mixup, CutMix) consistently yield larger gains on Hard classes than Easy classes: on ImageNet with ViT-B, combining all augmentations gives +4.7% on Hard vs. +1.4% on Easy, while on CIFAR-100 with WideResNet-34-10, CutMix alone gives +3.8% on Hard vs. +0.0% on Easy. Representation learning methods (MoCo pre-training, GPaCo, MAE masked modeling) also favor Hard classes, with MAE pre-training on ViT-B delivering +4.1% on Hard vs. +1.1% on Easy, and GPaCo providing +0.6% on Hard vs. +0.4% on Easy with ResNet-50. In contrast, re-weighting methods (logit adjustment [31], class-balanced loss [14]) improve Hard class accuracy by ~2% but degrade Easy and Medium classes by 1.3–1.5%, resulting in lower overall accuracy than the baseline (77.6% vs. 77.8% on ImageNet). Debiased classifier methods (LfF [32]) and worst-group optimization (JtT [29]) fail to improve fairness on CIFAR-100, with LfF actually reducing Hard class accuracy by 2.4% and overall accuracy by 2.0%. The paper concludes that data augmentation and representation learning are the most effective fairness-promoting techniques, as they improve both overall accuracy and per-class balance simultaneously.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the long-tailed recognition literature (Cao et al., Kang et al., Cui et al.), which attributes per-class accuracy disparity to class frequency imbalance, by demonstrating that extreme disparity persists even on balanced datasets like ImageNet. It repurposes diagnostic tools from long-tailed learning—classifier ℓ₂-norm analysis and separation angle measurement—but finds they do not explain the phenomenon on balanced data, instead pointing to representation quality as the root cause. The introduced concept of Model Prediction Bias (Definition 2) and the gradient convergence lemma (Lemma 1) provide a new analytical framework that unifies the seemingly contradictory observations between long-tailed and balanced settings. The paper also reframes the benefits of data augmentation (Mixup, CutMix, AutoAug) and representation learning (contrastive, masked modeling) in terms of fairness rather than solely overall accuracy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a 100%-to-16% accuracy gap exists on balanced ImageNet challenges the assumption that balanced data eliminates per-class disparity, and the reversal of prediction bias direction (harder classes attract more false positives, opposite of long-tail intuition) provides a mechanistic explanation rooted in data diversity rather than frequency. This reframes the problem as a 'data diversity imbalance' that existing re-weighting and resampling methods cannot address, since they target frequency rather than scenario complexity. Open questions include designing augmentation or self-supervised pre-training strategies explicitly optimized for fairness, handling class co-occurrence and multi-label ambiguity, and whether the findings generalize to video, audio, or other modalities. The paper's diagnostic nature means it raises awareness but leaves the solution space largely unexplored.
