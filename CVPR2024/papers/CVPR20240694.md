## Problem

While generative models produce visually compelling images that fool human observers and signal-based detectors, it remains unclear whether they correctly reproduce the projective geometry of real scenes—vanishing point alignment, perspective consistency, and object-shadow coherence. This paper asks whether systematic geometric discrepancies in generated images can be exploited to reliably distinguish them from real photos, even in cases where conventional pixel-level classifiers fail.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240694/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes standard RGB images but deliberately strips away pixel intensity information before classification. Three derived geometric representations are extracted: binary object-shadow instance masks, perspective field vector maps (encoding per-pixel orientation relative to vanishing points and horizon), and unordered line segment sets. Each representation is fed to a dedicated classifier that outputs a single real-vs-generated score. A critical preprocessing step uses a pixel-based ResNet-50 prequalifier to partition the test set into easy, unconfident, and misclassified subsets, so that the geometry classifiers are evaluated specifically on images where signal-based detection is at chance level.

## Method

Three independent classifiers are trained on Kandinsky-v3-generated images, each consuming only one type of derived geometric cue and never seeing raw pixels. The object-shadow classifier is a ResNet-50 trained on binary masks produced by a pretrained object-shadow instance detector; the perspective-field classifier is a ResNet-50 trained on vector field maps from a pretrained Perspective Fields model; and the line-segment classifier is a PointNet trained on unordered line segment coordinates from DeepLSD. The key experimental design is the prequalification filter: a signal-based ResNet-50 is first trained to separate real from generated images, and the geometry classifiers are then evaluated on the 'hard' subsets (unconfident and misclassified) where the prequalifier's AUC drops to ~0.5 or 0.0. This ensures that high AUCs (0.72–0.97) on the hard sets reflect genuine geometric reasoning rather than residual signal artifacts. Grad-CAM visualizations confirm that the classifiers attend to specific geometric errors such as inconsistent shadow directions, mismatched vanishing points, and distorted building facades, and the approach generalizes across unseen generators including DALL-E 3, Adobe Firefly, SDXL, and PixArt-α.

## Results

On the hard 'unconfident' test set (where the signal-based ResNet-50 prequalifier achieves only AUC ≈ 0.51), the geometry-based classifiers reach AUCs of 0.82 (line segments, indoor), 0.84 (perspective fields, outdoor), and 0.80 (perspective fields + object shadows, combined). On the 'misclassified' test set (prequalifier AUC = 0.00), the geometry classifiers still achieve AUC up to 0.82. Across all settings, the three geometry cues (object-shadow, perspective fields, line segments) yield AUCs ranging from 0.72 to 0.97, and the Kandinsky-trained prequalifier itself reaches AUC 0.99 / 97.43% accuracy on its own test set before hard-example filtering.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not include a head-to-head numerical comparison against other published AI-image detectors (e.g., DIRE, C2P-Net, or the SOTA local signal-based detectors mentioned in the abstract). The primary internal comparison is between the pixel-based ResNet-50 prequalifier and the three geometry-only classifiers on the same hard-example subsets: on the unconfident set the prequalifier is at chance (AUC ≈ 0.51) while the geometry classifiers reach 0.80–0.84, and on the misclassified set the prequalifier is at AUC 0.00 while geometry classifiers still reach up to 0.82. The authors also demonstrate cross-generator generalization: classifiers trained solely on Kandinsky-v3 images generalize to Stable Diffusion XL, PixArt-α, DALL-E 3, and Adobe Firefly (Fig. 7), though DeepFloyd IF is an exception requiring its own training set. The Kandinsky-based prequalifier itself outperforms SDXL- and PixArt-based prequalifiers in generalization (AUC 0.97–0.98 vs. lower values on cross-model tests), motivating its use as the training source for the geometry classifiers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes three pretrained geometric analysis tools—Perspective Fields (Jin et al., CVPR 2023), DeepLSD line detection (Pautrat et al., CVPR 2023), and an object-shadow instance detector—as inputs to lightweight classifiers (ResNet-50, PointNet) that never see raw pixels. It departs from the dominant signal-based detection literature (JPEG artifacts, resampling traces, GAN fingerprints) by introducing a 'prequalifier' stage that explicitly removes images already separable by pixel-level features, then shows the remaining 'hard' images are still identifiable through geometry alone. Conceptually, it extends shadow-inconsistency detection from photo-manipulation forensics (Kee et al., Boháček et al.) into a systematic evaluation of generative models, and it contrasts with prior work showing that diffusion models implicitly encode scene geometry (Bhattad et al., Du et al., Zhan et al.) by demonstrating that this latent understanding does not translate into pixel-accurate projective structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes projective-geometry correctness as a distinct and currently unmet axis of generative-model evaluation, complementing pixel-fidelity metrics (FID, CLIPScore) and semantic alignment scores. The practical implication is that a geometry-only detector (no pixel access) can flag generated images that pass all signal-based checks, which is relevant for content provenance and for diagnosing where generators fail. Open questions include whether architectural innovations (explicit geometric reasoning, new loss functions) can close the gap, whether the findings extend to video and 3D generation, and whether the prequalifier-based curation introduces a selection bias that limits the generality of the reported AUCs. The DeepFloyd non-generalization result also hints that generator-specific geometric failure modes may not be fully captured by a single training distribution.
