## Problem

Existing fine-tuning methods for transfer learning focus on re-weighting source-domain knowledge to reduce empirical risk, but overlook structural risk minimization (SRM). Due to the large data-scale disparity between pre-training and fine-tuning, the model inherits excessive complexity from the pre-trained network, and this redundant complexity is not reduced by standard fine-tuning, leading to suboptimal generalization on the target task.

## Input / Output

The method operates as a plug-in regularization on top of any existing fine-tuning pipeline. During training, a Gaussian noise vector η ~ N(0, φ²) is added to the input image, and the forward pass is run twice (with and without noise) to compute per-block noise sensitivity. The output remains task-specific (class labels for classification, segmentation masks for TransUNet, bounding boxes for DETR), and inference is unchanged since the noise is only used during training.

## Method

TSRS constrains the stable rank of the fine-tuned network by minimizing noise sensitivity at each block. Concretely, during each forward pass, Gaussian noise is added to the input, and the relative L2 change in each block's feature output (from block l to D) is computed as a per-block noise sensitivity loss, summed and weighted by a hyper-parameter α. The theoretical justification is that noise sensitivity provides an upper bound on the stable rank of the weight matrices (per Arora et al.), and the generalization bound of a network scales with the product of spectral norms and stable ranks across layers. By adding L_TSRS to the standard fine-tuning objective, the model is optimized toward lower stable rank and thus lower structural risk, complementing knowledge-reweighting methods that only address empirical risk. The approach is architecture-agnostic, working identically for ResNet (where a block is a stage of bottlenecks) and ViT (where a block is a transformer unit).

## Results

TSRS consistently improves top-1 accuracy across all fine-tuning methods (Baseline, DELTA, BSS, Co-Tuning, UOTS) on CUB, Stanford Cars, and FGVC Aircraft at all sampling rates (15%–100%) for both ResNet50 and ViT-B. Gains are most pronounced under data scarcity: on Cars at 15% sampling, BSS+TSRS reaches 47.22% vs. 44.00% for BSS alone (+3.22 pp), and the improvement grows with model size (ResNet152: +4.81 pp). In the regularization comparison on Cars 15%, TSRS achieves 46.30% top-1 accuracy, outperforming L2 (44.00%), SRN (44.20%), ART (43.83%), and Dynamic regularization (43.30%). TSRS also transfers to segmentation (TransUNet: +1.14 DSC on Synapse) and detection (DETR: +3.76 AP on LIDC at 15%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

TSRS is evaluated as an add-on to five fine-tuning methods (vanilla fine-tuning, DELTA, BSS, Co-Tuning, UOTS) and consistently improves each one across all datasets and sampling rates for both ResNet50 and ViT-B; the largest gains appear at 15% sampling where model complexity redundancy is most harmful. Against dedicated regularization baselines on Cars 15%, TSRS (46.30%) beats L2 (44.00%), SRN/stable-rank normalization (44.20%), ART (43.83%), Dynamic regularization (43.30%), and L1 (42.96%), with the margin over L2 being +2.30 pp. The improvement scales with model size: +0.50 pp for ResNet18, +3.22 pp for ResNet50, and +4.81 pp for ResNet152, confirming that larger models benefit more from structural risk reduction. In the case study, TSRS also outperforms simple input-noise-only regularization (ANO) on Lipschitz constant (robustness) and Hessian eigenvalue spectra (convexity/smoothness), achieving lower Lipschitz bounds and suppressing both large and negative Hessian eigenvalues more effectively. For downstream tasks, TSRS embedded in TransUNet improves DSC by +1.14 (Synapse), +0.51 (ISIC2018), and +0.71 (Spine), and in DETR improves AP by +3.76 (LIDC 15%), +3.65 (30%), +2.28 (50%), and +1.80 (100%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the theoretical link between noise sensitivity and stable rank established by Arora et al. (ICML 2018), which shows that noise sensitivity upper-bounds the stable rank of a network's weight matrices. It extends Sanyal et al.'s (2019) use of stable rank normalization for generalization, which was limited to the final classification layer, by applying the constraint across all network blocks during fine-tuning. Conceptually, the paper departs from the dominant fine-tuning literature (BSS, DELTA, Co-Tuning, UOTS) that focuses exclusively on re-weighting source-domain knowledge to reduce empirical risk, arguing that structural risk minimization has been systematically overlooked in this setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete gap in transfer learning: existing fine-tuning methods reduce empirical risk but leave model complexity (as measured by noise sensitivity/stable rank) essentially unchanged, which the authors show empirically limits generalization. TSRS is a simple, architecture-agnostic regularization that can be plugged into any fine-tuning pipeline with one extra forward pass, making it practically accessible. A stated limitation is that when source and target data scales are comparable, the structural risk disparity vanishes and TSRS offers no benefit. Open questions include whether the noise-sensitivity proxy for stable rank remains tight in very deep or heterogeneous architectures, and whether the optimal starting block l and noise intensity φ can be learned automatically rather than tuned per dataset.
