## Problem

Existing 2D-keypoint-based mesh refinement methods (e.g., SMPLify, CLIFFr) rely on gradient descent over a reprojection loss, which suffers from two fundamental issues: (1) inherent depth ambiguity in 2D projection makes the optimal solution hard to find and can actually increase depth error, and (2) divergent gradients from distal joints backpropagated through the kinematic chain complicate and even harm the refinement of proximal joints. No prior method explicitly models joint depth or the kinematic-tree structure during refinement.

## Input / Output

The method takes initial SMPL parameters (pose θ ∈ R^{24×3}, shape β ∈ R^{10}, camera translation t ∈ R³) from any HMR model plus 2D keypoints j ∈ R^{24×2} as input, and iteratively (M=10 iterations) produces refined pose, shape, and camera translation. It uses a full-perspective camera model with focal length estimated as f=√(H²+W²) rather than the common weak-perspective assumption, and the camera rotation is absorbed into the global body orientation. The method is plug-and-play: it can be applied on top of any HMR base model (CLIFF, SPIN, EFT, PRoM) or even on top of other refinement methods, and generalizes to other parametric models like MANO for hands.

## Method

KITRO's key insight is that given 2D keypoints, the parent joint's depth, and the 3D bone length, the child joint position can be solved in closed form, yielding exactly two solutions (bone pointing toward vs. away from the camera). These binary choices per bone naturally form a decision tree along each kinematic chain; edge weights are computed via softmax of cosine similarity between each closed-form bone direction and the original HMR prediction, and the optimal full-body pose is selected by maximizing the product of edge weights along the path. The pose update is soft (weighted by the edge certainty) and applied via swing rotation computed with Rodrigues' formula, while camera translation is refined via SVD with a moving-average regularizer and shape is optimized with an L1 bone-length projection loss. This explicit depth modeling and tree-based global selection avoids the gradient conflicts of prior methods, improving both proximal and distal joints simultaneously.

## Results

On 3DPW, KITRO achieves 27.67 mm PA-MPJPE, 43.53 mm MPJPE, and 53.44 mm PVE, reducing errors by 4.3, 12.3, and 18.5 mm respectively over the CLIFFb baseline. On Human3.6M, it reaches 21.04 mm PA-MPJPE and 34.50 mm MPJPE. The 27.67 mm PA-MPJPE on 3DPW is close to the 26 mm accuracy achieved during the dataset's own creation process. KITRO also generalizes to the MANO hand model on Freihand, achieving 4.06 PA-MPJPE vs. 4.66 for SMPLify.

## Baselines & Comparisons

KITRO outperforms all compared refinement methods across every dataset and metric. On 3DPW, it beats the SOTA refinement CLIFFr [18] by 4.37 mm in PA-MPJPE (27.67 vs. 32.04), 12.30 mm in MPJPE (43.53 vs. 55.83), and 18.51 mm in PVE (53.44 vs. 71.95). Against SMPLify [1], the margin is even larger: 8.44 mm in PA-MPJPE and 23.14 mm in MPJPE on 3DPW. On Human3.6M, KITRO improves over CLIFFr by 4.84 mm in PA-MPJPE (21.04 vs. 25.88) and 8.29 mm in MPJPE (34.50 vs. 42.79). The plug-and-play nature is confirmed in Table 3: applied on top of SPIN, EFTb, and PRoM, KITRO consistently outperforms SMPLify by 5–15 mm in PA-MPJPE and 15–35 mm in MPJPE. On the MANO hand model (Freihand), KITRO also edges out SMPLify (4.06 vs. 4.66 PA-MPJPE), demonstrating generalization beyond SMPL.

## Relation to Prior Work

KITRO sits within the established line of 2D-keypoint-driven human mesh refinement (SMPLify, CLIFF, EFT, SPIN), which all rely on iterative gradient descent to minimize a 2D reprojection loss. It departs from this paradigm by replacing gradient-based parameter optimization with a closed-form geometric calculation of bone directions via swing-twist decomposition (borrowed from Hybrik) and Rodrigues' formula, then resolving the resulting per-bone depth ambiguity through a decision tree over the kinematic chain. The camera model follows prior full-perspective approaches (SPEc, Zolly), and the plug-and-play design is validated across multiple HMR backbones (CLIFF, SPIN, EFT, PRoM) and even the MANO hand model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical impact is that KITRO is a lightweight, plug-and-play refinement that can be stacked on top of any existing HMR model or even on top of other refinement methods (CLIFFr), yielding consistent 15–25% error reductions without retraining. The conceptual contribution—showing that depth ambiguity decomposes into independent binary choices per bone, and that a global decision-tree search outperforms both greedy and gradient-based selection—offers a cleaner geometric alternative to the field's default optimization loop. Limitations include dependence on the quality of the initial HMR estimate (person misidentification cases) and on accurate 2D keypoints, both of which remain open challenges for fully in-the-wild deployment.
