## Problem

Neural image compressors are typically trained once and deployed with fixed parameters, but real-world applications require adapting to new image sources or target bitrates over time. Standard end-to-end fine-tuning breaks backward compatibility—the ability to decode bitstreams produced by the original model—because changes to the entropy model make old bitstreams undecodable. This paper asks whether both the encoder and decoder can be updated for new data or rates while preserving the decoder's ability to reconstruct images from previously encoded bitstreams.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained variable-rate neural compressor (encoder, decoder, and entropy model) along with new training data (e.g., CelebA-HQ at 256×256) and/or an extended λ range, and produces a fine-tuned model that simultaneously decodes old bitstreams and compresses new data at new rates. The entropy model is frozen throughout fine-tuning, so the output bitstream format and entropy coding parameters remain unchanged. The architecture produces hierarchical latent variables at four spatial scales (32×32, 16×16, 8×8, 4×4 for 256×256 inputs), each entropy-coded separately via asymmetric numeral systems, and the decoder reconstructs the image top-down from these latents.

## Method

The core insight is that freezing the entropy model guarantees lossless recovery of old latent variables from old bitstreams, reducing the problem to continual learning of the decoder without forgetting. The knowledge replay training strategy addresses this by computing a weighted loss: the standard rate-distortion loss on new data (ℓ_new) plus a replay loss (ℓ_KR) that feeds old training data through the frozen old encoder and trains the current decoder to reconstruct those images from the resulting latents, with a hyperparameter α controlling the trade-off. The proposed architecture decouples the entropy model and decoder into two parallel branches (rather than the sequential dependency in Hyperprior models), using ConvNeXt blocks with conditional convolution for variable-rate operation. This design keeps the entropy model to only 14% of total parameters (vs. 34–84% in existing methods), leaving the majority of parameters learnable during fine-tuning and enabling more effective adaptation to new data and rates.

## Results

The primary metric is BD-Rate (%) w.r.t. VTM 22.0 (lower is better), evaluated on both old bitstreams (backward compatibility) and new data (CelebA). The proposed model with knowledge replay (KR) achieves 0.87% BD-Rate on old Kodak bitstreams and −16.6% on new CelebA data, yielding an average of −7.87%—the best across all model architectures and fine-tuning strategies. In contrast, fine-tuning both encoder and decoder without KR causes backward compatibility to collapse (262.9% BD-Rate on old bitstreams for the proposed model), while the KR strategy keeps old-bitstream performance within 1% of the pre-trained baseline. For rate-incremental learning (low→high), the proposed model with KR achieves 0.96% on old bitstreams and 0.70% on new rates, again outperforming all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two learned image compression baselines—MSH-VR (Mean & Scale Hyperprior [Minnen et al., NeurIPS 2018]) and GMA-VR (Gaussian Mixture & Attention [Cheng et al., CVPR 2020])—each adapted to variable-rate compression. The proposed model with KR outperforms GMA-VR with KR by 3.46 percentage points on old bitstreams (0.87% vs. 4.33%) and by 2.2 points on new CelebA data (−16.6% vs. −14.4%), and beats MSH-VR with KR by a wide margin (19.2 points on old, 26.2 points on new). The critical comparison is between fine-tuning strategies: end-to-end fine-tuning of encoder and decoder (FT Enc. & Dec.) achieves the best new-data BD-Rate (e.g., −19.0% for the proposed model) but catastrophically breaks backward compatibility (262.9% vs. 1.87% pre-trained), whereas KR preserves backward compatibility (0.87%) while still achieving −16.6% on new data—only 2.4 points behind the incompatible fine-tuning baseline. The encoder-only fine-tuning baseline (FT Enc.) is the safest for backward compatibility but provides minimal new-data gains (−14.6% vs. −16.6% for KR on the proposed model). Overall, KR consistently dominates: it is the only strategy that simultaneously maintains backward compatibility and delivers strong new-data performance across all three architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the knowledge replay principle from class-incremental learning (iCaRL [45]) to the unique constraints of learned image compression, where backward compatibility is governed by entropy coding sensitivity rather than the typical catastrophic forgetting of vision tasks. Architecturally, it draws on hierarchical residual coding (QARV [14], NVTc [18]) but deliberately decouples the entropy model from the decoder into parallel branches, reducing the frozen parameter fraction from 47–84% in existing Hyperprior-based models to 14%. The variable-rate conditioning via conditional convolution follows Choi et al. [12] and QARV [14], and the overall framing departs from content-adaptive compression (per-image optimization) by requiring a one-time training procedure with no test-time cost.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant for deployment: neural compressors in storage or streaming applications must decode previously encoded bitstreams, and this paper shows that both encoder and decoder can be updated (not just the encoder, as in prior encoder-side adaptation) while preserving that guarantee. The observation that only the entropy model needs standardization—not the full architecture—offers a concrete direction for JPEG AI and related standardization efforts. Limitations include the requirement to retain access to the old training set for replay, validation on only two base architectures and three scenarios, and the open question of whether the approach extends to video compression or multi-stage continual learning (more than one fine-tuning round).
