## Problem

Neural SDF optimization from raw point clouds without normals frequently converges to poor local minima when the target shape's geometry differs substantially from the standard spherical initialization. The paper identifies the root cause: the standard loss terms (zero level set, Eikonal, neural pull) fail to provide a good descent direction when the network's current normal directions are misaligned with the target shape's normals, a situation quantified by the proposed SION metric. This is especially severe for shapes with thin parallel surfaces or large concavities with small openings.

## Input / Output

The method takes a raw point cloud (100k uniformly sampled surface points) with no per-point normals or scanner metadata as input. It produces a neural SDF implemented as a small coordinate network with Fourier feature embeddings and a dense parametric hash grid (17.5M parameters total), whose zero level set defines the reconstructed watertight surface. The network is queried at arbitrary 3D coordinates to obtain signed distances, enabling meshing via marching cubes and downstream editing or generation tasks.

## Method

PG-SDF introduces guiding points Y that start on a high exterior level set (radius 16δ_X from the input) and are incrementally moved toward the input points X in small steps along the inward normal direction, with the target level set radius δ halved iteratively from 4δ_X down to δ_X. At each step, the network is optimized to interpolate the updated Y positions using a composite loss combining zero level set loss on Y, an input-distance loss, Eikonal regularization, an approximate SDF loss (leveraging the network's own normals to assign signs), and a neural pull loss. The key insight is that each small movement keeps SION(Y; R) low, ensuring the loss provides a clear descent direction and avoiding the local minima that trap direct optimization. The entire procedure is formalized as a homotopy method where the guiding points parameterize a continuous deformation from an easy problem (fitting a distant, simple level set) to the hard problem (fitting the input points), with the SION metric providing a principled criterion for when the optimization is well-conditioned.

## Results

On the ShapeNet watertight subset (260 shapes), PG-SDF achieves a mean squared chamfer distance of 3.72e-5 and mean IoU of 0.9729 (std 0.0676) in the normal-free setting, outperforming the next-best method OG-S (3.75e-5 chamfer, 0.9615 IoU) and substantially beating DiGS (1.32e-4 chamfer, 0.9390 IoU) and SAP (4.09e-4 chamfer, 0.9118 IoU). When ground-truth normals are available, PG-SDF+N achieves 2.43e-5 chamfer and 0.9932 IoU (std 0.0076), surpassing SPSR (5.44e-5, 0.9926) and SDF+N (3.97e-5, 0.9915). On the 20 highest-SION (most geometrically challenging) shapes, the advantage is decisive: PG-SDF reaches 0.9153 mean IoU versus 0.7651 for OG-S and 0.7108 for DiGS, while SDF collapses to 0.3413.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the normal-free setting, PG-SDF outperforms all competing methods on both squared chamfer and IoU. The closest competitor is OG-S (0.9615 mean IoU, 0.1048 std), which PG-SDF surpasses by 1.1 IoU points with roughly 35% lower variance; DiGS (0.9390) and SAP (0.9118) trail further behind, while SIREN (0.3085), SAL (0.4030), and the vanilla SDF baseline (0.5490) fail catastrophically on challenging shapes. The classical SPSR method (0.7187 IoU) is also clearly outperformed. When ground-truth normals are provided, PG-SDF+N (0.9932 IoU, 0.0076 std) beats SPSR (0.9926, 0.0105) and SDF+N (0.9915, 0.0141) on both mean IoU and consistency. The gap widens dramatically on the 20 highest-SION shapes: PG-SDF achieves 0.9153 mean IoU versus 0.7651 (OG-S), 0.7616 (SAP), 0.7108 (DiGS), and 0.3413 (SDF), confirming that the point-guided homotopy specifically addresses the failure mode of existing methods on geometrically challenging shapes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the neural SDF loss formulations of IGR (zero-level-set + Eikonal) and Neural Pull, identifying a fundamental failure mode: when the target shape's geometry differs substantially from the spherical initialization, these losses do not provide a valid descent direction toward the true SDF. It departs from prior optimization-fixing approaches (DiGS's regularization annealing, OGINR's discrete pre-solve, SAP's chamfer-based deformation) by reframing the problem as a homotopy: guiding points are moved in small steps through a sequence of exterior level sets, ensuring each sub-problem is easy to solve. The SION metric, which quantifies geometric dissimilarity between a target surface and a surrounding surface, is a new diagnostic tool not present in prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The SION metric provides a practical, computable way to predict which shapes will be difficult for any neural SDF method, enabling practitioners to anticipate and address failures before they occur. The homotopy interpretation gives theoretical justification for the small-step strategy and clarifies why prior methods fail, which could inform future loss design. Limitations noted by the authors include sensitivity to high outlier fractions and inability to handle large occlusions gracefully (treated as concavities to explore); the authors suggest anisotropic per-point sampling radii as a potential fix. An open question is whether the level-set stepping strategy generalizes to other implicit representation tasks beyond surface reconstruction.
