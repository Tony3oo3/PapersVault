## Problem

Existing deep-learning-based mesh denoising networks apply a single set of parameters uniformly to all facets, ignoring the diverse geometric characteristics (smooth, curved, small-edge, large-edge) and varying noise intensities that differ across a mesh. This uniform treatment limits denoising performance because regions with different geometric and noise properties require different denoising strategies.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240514/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a noisy triangle mesh and processes each facet by extracting its r-ring patch, which is normalized (translated to origin, scaled to unit bounding box, rotated so the mean normal aligns with [0,0,1]). The hyper-network additionally receives two per-facet angles: α^n (angular gap between the noisy normal and a filtered-mesh normal, encoding noise intensity) and α^g (maximum angular difference among filtered normals in the 2-ring patch, encoding geometric characteristics). The output is a denoised normal per facet, which is then used to update vertex positions via a standard projection scheme.

## Method

Hyper-MD combines a graph-convolutional denoising network (with static and dynamic EdgeConv layers for feature extraction and FC layers for normal regression) with a hyper-network that dynamically customizes the denoising parameters per facet. The hyper-network's input is the two angles (α^n, α^g), and its output is constructed as a coarse-grained base selected from 12 pre-trained candidate models (one per combination of 3 noise-intensity bins × 4 geometric-category bins) plus fine-grained offsets predicted by a multi-branch fully connected network. Training proceeds in three stages: (1) pre-train the full denoising network on all data, (2) freeze the feature extractor and fine-tune the normal-inference head separately on each of the 12 facet categories to produce the candidate pool, and (3) freeze the denoising network and train the hyper-network to predict the fine-grained offsets. This design means only the small FC head parameters are customized at inference, improving both efficiency and stability over directly regressing all parameters.

## Results

On the synthetic dataset (SynData), Hyper-MD achieves the best average E_a (4.50°) and E_v (7.85×10⁻⁴) on complex meshes, outperforming the closest competitor GCN-Denoiser (5.25°, 8.21×10⁻⁴) by 14% and 4.4% respectively. On simple meshes, Hyper-MD attains the best E_v (2.20×10⁻³) while TGV achieves a slightly lower E_a (3.84° vs. 4.32°). On real-scanned Kinect datasets, Hyper-MD consistently achieves the minimum E_a and E_v across Kv1Data, Kv2Data, and K-FData, with the sole exception of E_v on K-FData where it is second only to GCN.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Hyper-MD outperforms all five baselines on complex meshes for both E_a and E_v, with the largest margins over TGV (E_a: 4.50° vs. 5.43°; E_v: 7.85 vs. 10.95×10⁻⁴) and GNF (E_a: 4.50° vs. 6.85°). On simple meshes, TGV achieves the best E_a (3.84° vs. Hyper-MD's 4.32°), but Hyper-MD wins on E_v (2.20 vs. 3.01×10⁻³), indicating better geometric fidelity. The closest deep-learning competitor, GCN-Denoiser, is beaten by Hyper-MD on all four average metrics (e.g., complex E_a: 4.50° vs. 5.25°), confirming the benefit of hyper-network-based parameter customization. Conventional filters (BMF, BNF, GNF) are consistently outperformed, with BMF showing the weakest results overall (E_a ≈ 7° on both subsets). On real-scanned data, Hyper-MD achieves the lowest E_a on all three Kinect datasets and the lowest E_v on Kv1Data and Kv2Data, while being marginally behind GCN on E_v for K-FData.

## Relation to Prior Work

Hyper-MD builds directly on GCN-Denoiser [23] for its denoising network architecture (EdgeConv-based feature extraction and FC-based normal inference) and vertex updating scheme, but departs from the single-fixed-network paradigm by introducing a hyper-network that dynamically customizes the normal inference module's parameters per facet. The hyper-network concept is borrowed from image recognition [3], super-resolution [10], and point cloud upsampling [37], but this is the first application to mesh denoising. The key methodological novelty is the two-angle input (α^n for noise intensity, α^g for geometric characteristics) computed via a filtered-mesh agent, combined with a coarse-grained base (selected from 12 category-specific candidate models) plus fine-grained offsets predicted by a multi-branch FC network.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine limitation of existing deep-learning MD methods—treating all facets identically regardless of local noise level or geometric type—and demonstrates consistent (if modest) improvements across synthetic and real-scanned benchmarks. However, the practical cost is significant: training takes ~80 hours and denoising is ~2.4× slower than GCN-Denoiser (920s vs 384s), limiting immediate adoption. Open questions include whether the facet-wise iterative paradigm can be parallelized or replaced with a single-pass formulation, whether the 12-category discretization is optimal, and whether the hyper-network approach transfers to other geometry processing tasks (e.g., inpainting, remeshing) where per-region parameter adaptation would similarly benefit.
