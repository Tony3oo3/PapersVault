## Problem

Parkinson's disease assessment relies on the UPDRS, a manual ordinal scoring system that is subjective, coarse, and exhibits high inter-rater variability (Kappa as low as 0.37). While machine learning could automate and refine these assessments, no benchmark video dataset exists for PD motor exams, and prior video-based studies are limited to single-camera 2D recordings covering only one or two activities, fundamentally constraining kinematic measurement precision.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240195/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The system ingests synchronized 6-camera video (1920×1200 px, 80 fps) of subjects performing 25 UPDRS motor exam activities in a 6.3×3 m hexagonal space. 2D keypoints are extracted per-view (MediaPipe 21-point hands for finger tapping; MMPose Halpe26 for body), then triangulated into metric-unit 3D poses via direct linear transformation. Outputs are ordinal UPDRS component scores (0–4) and a binary PD/Healthy diagnosis, with the 3D metric coordinates enabling physical quantities (velocity, distance) unavailable from 2D projections.

## Method

The pipeline extracts hand and body 2D poses per camera view, triangulates them into 3D via direct linear transformation, and smooths outliers. For finger tapping, features are computed per 10-event window from the thumb–index angle (angular speed, acceleration, amplitude) plus 3D-only wrist coordinates and finger opening/closing velocities; for gait, spatiotemporal (double support time, step length) and kinematic (joint angles) features are derived per stride after projecting 3D poses onto the best-fitting walking plane. Each raw feature is summarized by seven statistics (mean, min, max, median, IQR, std, entropy/CV), yielding 20 (3D) or 14 (2D) features for tapping and 57 (3D) or 45 (2D) for gait after correlation pruning (r > 0.85). Classification uses traditional ML models (MLP, Random Forest, SVM, LightGBM, etc.) under leave-one-subject-out validation, with clinician bias matrices (Tanno et al., CVPR 2019) applied to handle noisy ordinal labels. The key empirical finding is that 3D-feature models consistently outperform 2D-feature counterparts (e.g., 0.69 vs. 0.37 F1 for finger-tapping UPDRS prediction), demonstrating the value of multi-camera 3D reconstruction over single-view 2D analysis.

## Results

On the TULIP dataset, the best model for UPDRS score prediction (ordinal 0–4) is a 3D-feature MLP for finger tapping (0.69 weighted F1) and a 3D-feature Random Forest for gait (0.72 weighted F1). For binary PD/Healthy diagnosis, the 3D-feature MLP on finger tapping achieves 0.87 F1, approaching the best clinician (0.90 F1) who had access to all 25 activities. Across both tasks, 3D features consistently outperform 2D features by 0.25–0.45 F1, and the 3D-feature models surpass the ResNet50 pose-sequence baseline (Mehta et al.) by 0.23–0.45 F1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Islam et al. (2023), who used LightGBM on 2D features, the TULIP 3D-feature MLP wins on both finger-tapping tasks: UPDRS prediction improves from 0.36 to 0.69 F1 (+0.33), and PD/Healthy diagnosis improves from 0.77 to 0.87 F1 (+0.10). Against Mehta et al. (2021), who applied a ResNet50 directly to 3D pose sequences, the TULIP hand-crafted 3D features dominate across all four sub-tasks: finger-tapping UPDRS (0.69 vs 0.30), gait UPDRS (0.72 vs 0.27), finger-tapping PD/Healthy (0.87 vs 0.64), and gait PD/Healthy (0.72 vs 0.59), with margins of 0.13–0.45 F1. The authors attribute the ResNet50's underperformance to the small TULIP cohort (15 subjects) limiting deep-learning generalization. For gait PD/Healthy, the TULIP 3D Random Forest (0.72 F1) is essentially tied with Rehman et al. (2019) at 0.73 F1, a near-parity result the authors note may reflect the increased natural heterogeneity of gait. Overall, the 3D-feature models match or exceed the least-accurate clinician on UPDRS scoring and approach the best clinician on binary diagnosis, while outperforming all prior computational baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of PD video-based assessment (Islam et al. 2023, Mehta et al. 2021, Lu et al. 2021) by moving from single-camera 2D recordings to a multi-camera markerless 3D setup, and from one or two UPDRS activities to all 25 Part III motor exam tasks. Methodologically, it adopts the clinician bias-matrix framework of Tanno et al. [65] to handle noisy ordinal UPDRS labels, and combines off-the-shelf pose estimators (MediaPipe, MMPose) with direct linear transformation triangulation for 3D reconstruction. The key conceptual departure is that prior 3D PD kinematics required marker-based motion capture, whereas TULIP demonstrates that markerless multi-camera triangulation achieves sufficient precision (21 mm for finger tapping, 56 mm for gait) for clinical-grade analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TULIP fills a critical gap as the first publicly available multi-view 3D benchmark for PD motor exams, enabling the community to develop and compare markerless 3D assessment methods that were previously impossible without expensive motion capture rigs. The finding that 3D hand-crafted features roughly double UPDRS prediction F1 over 2D equivalents (0.69 vs 0.37 for finger tapping; 0.72 vs 0.41 for gait) provides strong empirical motivation for multi-camera acquisition in clinical settings. However, the cohort of 15 subjects with limited demographic diversity, the analysis of only 2 of 25 recorded activities, and the large-room recording environment all constrain generalizability. Open questions include whether the 3D advantage persists at scale, whether unsupervised representations can discover PD signatures beyond UPDRS, and how the paradigm adapts to smaller clinical or home environments.
