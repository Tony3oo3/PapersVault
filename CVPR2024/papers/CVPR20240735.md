## Problem

Previous 3D hand pose estimation methods were constrained by limited training data (typically a single or few datasets) and relatively small network architectures, resulting in poor robustness on in-the-wild images with diverse viewpoints, occlusions, and interactions. Additionally, evaluation was largely confined to controlled studio benchmarks, leaving a gap in measuring real-world performance. This paper asks whether the scaling philosophy successful in NLP and other vision domains—large data plus high-capacity models—can similarly transform hand mesh recovery.

## Input / Output

At test time, HaMeR receives a hand bounding box crop from an RGB image (detected by a separate detector) and returns MANO pose parameters (θ ∈ R⁴⁸), shape parameters (β ∈ R¹⁰), and a 3D camera translation (t ∈ R³) that together define the full 3D hand mesh. Only a right-hand network is trained; left hands are handled by mirroring the input image and output mesh. Although the model is single-frame, it produces temporally smooth reconstructions when applied sequentially to video frames without any explicit temporal modeling.

## Method

HaMeR uses a fully transformer-based architecture: a ViT-H backbone processes image patches, and a transformer decoder head with a single query token cross-attends to the ViT output tokens to regress the MANO and camera parameters. The key design choice is scaling: training on 2.7M examples consolidated from 10 datasets (4× larger than FrankMocap's training set), combining 3D-annotated studio data with 2D-keypoint in-the-wild data. Training supervision combines 3D parameter and joint losses, 2D reprojection losses, and adversarial losses (separate discriminators for shape, pose, and each joint angle) to enforce natural hand geometry when only 2D supervision is available. Ablations confirm that both data scale and model capacity independently improve performance, and their combination yields the largest gains—achieving 2–3× improvement in PCK@0.05 on in-the-wild benchmarks over prior state-of-the-art.

## Results

On the HO3Dv2 benchmark, HaMeR achieves a PA-MPJPE of 7.7 mm and AUC_J of 0.846, outperforming the previous best (AMVUR: 8.3 mm, 0.835) and MobRecon (9.2 mm). On FreiHAND, HaMeR reaches a PA-MPJPE of 5.7 mm and F@15 of 0.990, matching MobRecon on PA-MPJPE but surpassing it on mesh metrics. On the newly introduced HInt in-the-wild benchmark, HaMeR achieves PCK@0.05 scores of 49.4% (New Days), 44.4% (VISOR), and 40.3% (Ego4D), representing a 2–3× improvement over the strongest prior baseline (MeshGraphormer at 19.1%, 16.8%, and 14.7% respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HaMeR outperforms all 15 compared baselines across every benchmark and metric. On HO3Dv2, HaMeR (PA-MPJPE 7.7 mm, AUC_J 0.846, F@5 0.635) beats the closest competitor AMVUR (8.3 mm, 0.835, 0.608) by ~0.6 mm and 0.011 AUC, and surpasses MobRecon (9.2 mm, 0.538 F@5) by a wider margin. On FreiHAND, HaMeR (PA-MPJPE 5.7 mm, F@15 0.990) ties MobRecon on PA-MPJPE but leads on F@5 (0.785 vs 0.784) and F@15 (0.990 vs 0.986), while clearly outperforming MeshGraphormer (6.0 mm, 0.986) and AMVUR (6.1 mm, 0.987). The most dramatic gains appear on the HInt in-the-wild benchmark, where HaMeR's PCK@0.05 of 49.4/44.4/40.3% across New Days/VISOR/Ego4D exceeds the best prior method (MeshGraphormer at 19.1/19.1/14.7%) by roughly 2.5–3×, and surpasses FrankMocap (16.1/16.8/13.2%) by over 3×. HandOccNet, despite its occlusion-aware design, performs worst on HInt (9.1/8.1/7.7% PCK@0.05), suggesting its specialized architecture does not generalize as well as HaMeR's scaled transformer. The ablation (Table 5) confirms that both large-scale data (4× more examples) and the ViT-H backbone contribute independently, with their combination yielding the largest jump (e.g., 16.9% → 49.4% PCK@0.05 on New Days).

## Relation to Prior Work

HaMeR directly extends the HMR2.0 architecture (Goel et al., ICCV 2023) — a ViT-H backbone with a single-token transformer decoder — from full-body human mesh recovery to the hand-specific setting. It departs from the dominant hand-reconstruction literature (FrankMocap, METRO, MeshGraphormer, HandOccNet, AMVUR), which relied on CNN backbones and hand-specific architectural innovations (occlusion-aware modules, probabilistic attention, graph convolutions), by instead applying the 'scale up data and model capacity' philosophy that has driven progress in NLP and general vision. The paper consolidates 10+ datasets (2.7M examples, 4× FrankMocap's training set) and introduces HInt, a new in-the-wild 2D keypoint benchmark with occlusion labels, to complement the controlled 3D benchmarks (FreiHAND, HO3D) that had become somewhat saturated.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 2–3× PCK@0.05 gap on in-the-wild data (e.g., 49.4 vs. 16.1 on New Days) reveals that prior methods were substantially underfitting to real-world conditions, and that simple scaling of data and model capacity is more effective than hand-specific architectural complexity — a methodological insight that may transfer to other pose-estimation tasks. HInt, with its occlusion annotations and egocentric coverage, fills a genuine evaluation gap and will likely become a standard benchmark. Limitations include the single-frame design (no explicit temporal modeling), reliance on the MANO parametric model, and the fact that in-the-wild evaluation is limited to 2D reprojection accuracy rather than true 3D ground truth. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to other body parts, whether temporal modeling would further close the gap on video, and whether the MANO model itself is a bottleneck for extreme poses.
