## Problem

Existing 3D facial NeRF editing methods control appearance through text prompts, lighting parameters, or reference images, which limits precise local manipulation and interactivity. Color strokes offer a natural interactive tool for specifying local appearance changes, but applying them to 3D faces is challenging due to the absence of explicit texture, the coarse geometry representation in NeRF, and the difficulty of preserving unchanged geometric details during editing.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242540/cropped_page0_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes three inputs: a user-drawn color stroke image (a low-frequency, simplified version of the face produced by bilateral filtering and semantic clustering), an optional 2D mask delineating the editing region (either auto-derived from stroke shape or hand-drawn), and the original face latent code in EG3D's StyleGAN2 latent space (obtained via random sampling or PTI inversion of a real image). The output is an edited latent code that, when passed through EG3D's triplane generator and volume renderer, produces multi-view-consistent edited facial images from arbitrary camera poses, with the 3D mask volume enabling per-region blending of original and edited features.

## Method

The core architecture operates in EG3D's latent space: a Color Encoder (structured similarly to pSp) maps the edited stroke image to a latent code w_c, and a cross-attention transformer-based Latent Fusion Network then fuses w_c (as query tokens) with the original latent code w (as key/value tokens) to produce w_edit, implicitly extracting color from the stroke while preserving geometry from the original. A novel geometry density loss enforces 3D consistency by sampling points near the facial surface via depth-guided ray offsets and minimizing the L1 difference in decoded density between original and edited triplanes. For precise local editing, a volumetric fusion module lifts the 2D mask into a 256×256×256 3D mask volume (via depth-guided point sampling, dilation, and Gaussian filtering), then blends original and edited triplane features per-sample before decoding, ensuring unedited regions remain pixel-identical. Training is supervised on 94,268 paired samples (original face, edited face, stroke map) constructed via semantic segmentation augmentation and StyleGAN2 style-mixing, with a combined loss of density, L2 image, LPIPS, ArcFace identity, and regularization terms.

## Results

On 1000 facial editing examples from FFHQ, StrokeFaceNeRF achieves FID 16.86, KID 0.83×10⁻², identity consistency 0.85±0.10, and E-SSIM 84.30×10⁻², outperforming all baselines on every metric. The closest competitor, pSp-mixing, scores FID 31.32, KID 2.00×10⁻², ID 0.73±0.09, and E-SSIM 81.50×10⁻². The 2D methods (SC-FEGAN and SDEdit) lag substantially, with FID values of 47.18 and 37.61 respectively, and the lowest identity consistency scores (0.50 and 0.41).

## Baselines & Comparisons

StrokeFaceNeRF outperforms all four baselines on every reported metric. Against the strongest 3D baseline (pSp-mixing, which applies style-mixing to pSp's predicted latent code by replacing layers 7–14), the proposed method reduces FID from 31.32 to 16.86 (46% improvement) and KID from 2.00 to 0.83 (59% improvement), while also improving identity consistency from 0.73 to 0.85 and E-SSIM from 81.50 to 84.30. The 2D methods SC-FEGAN and SDEdit produce noticeably smoother, less realistic edits with the worst identity scores (0.50 and 0.41), reflecting their inability to preserve 3D geometry. The plain pSp baseline (without mixing) globally alters the entire face including background, whereas pSp-mixing partially mitigates this but still introduces undesirable changes in unedited regions. The paper notes that no prior 3D-aware stroke-based synthesis method exists, so pSp was retrained on EG3D with the authors' dataset to enable a fair 3D comparison.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: 2D stroke-based face editing (pSp, SC-FEGAN, SDEdit) and 3D face NeRF generation/editing (EG3D, FE-NeRF, IDE-3D, NeRFFaceEditing). It departs from prior 3D face editing work, which relies on reference images or random latent sampling for global appearance changes, by introducing interactive color strokes as the editing interface. The color encoder borrows the pSp architecture, while the triplane-based NeRF backbone and volume rendering pipeline come directly from EG3D. The key conceptual departure is the transformer-based latent fusion network that explicitly separates color information (from strokes) from geometry (from the original latent code), combined with a novel density loss and volumetric fusion for local editing—neither of which appears in prior stroke-based or NeRF editing literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method to enable interactive, stroke-driven local appearance editing on 3D face NeRFs, opening a practical workflow for character design and virtual production where users can precisely control hair color, skin tone, or facial patterns from arbitrary viewpoints. The geometry density loss and volumetric fusion are transferable techniques that could benefit other NeRF editing tasks beyond faces. However, the method struggles with highly detailed regions (eyeballs) and complex multi-color strokes (e.g., rainbow hair), and the evaluation is limited to a single 3D backbone (EG3D) with relatively weak baselines (2D methods and a retrained pSp). Extending the approach to geometry editing via line drawings and to non-facial 3D models remains open.
