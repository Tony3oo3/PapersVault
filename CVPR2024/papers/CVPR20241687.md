## Problem

Affordance learning traditionally requires large-scale datasets with dense pixel-level annotations and can only reason within a closed vocabulary of predefined affordance categories. Foundation vision-language models like CLIP can recognize novel objects but fail to understand fine-grained affordances and object parts. This paper asks whether a model trained with merely one example per base object category can still segment affordances on unseen objects and recognize novel affordance vocabularies (e.g., 'chop' for 'cut').

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image (resized to 256×256, cropped to 224×224) and a set of affordance text labels as input, producing a dense pixel-wise affordance segmentation mask of shape H×W×N. Training requires only N_b image-mask pairs (one per base object category, e.g., 50 for AGD20K or 54 for UMD), while at inference the model accepts arbitrary novel object images and can substitute semantically similar affordance words not seen during training. The output is a per-pixel probability map over all affordance classes, optimized with binary cross-entropy.

## Method

The framework pairs a frozen DINOv2 vision encoder (chosen for its part-aware representations and part-level semantic correspondence) with a CLIP text encoder and a lightweight transformer decoder. Three key designs address specific bottlenecks: (1) CoOp-style text prompt learning inserts shared learnable context vectors before the CLS token to handle CLIP's sensitivity to affordance phrasing; (2) multi-layer feature fusion adaptively combines the last j DINOv2 layers via learned weighted summation to capture multiple granularity levels; (3) a CLS-guided masked cross-attention decoder uses the vision encoder's [CLS] token to produce a foreground mask that constrains cross-attention, promoting alignment between visual and text embeddings without full fine-tuning. The decoder uses text embeddings as queries and visual features as keys/values, with the final prediction obtained by matrix-multiplying the last decoder output with the original DINOv2 features to preserve part-aware structure. Training uses SGD (lr 0.01) for 20k iterations with binary cross-entropy loss on the linear layers, embedder, and decoder only.

## Results

On the UMD dataset, the proposed OOAL method achieves 74.6 mIoU (seen), 59.7 mIoU (unseen), and 66.4 hIoU using only 54/76 images (0.36% of full training data), matching or exceeding fully-supervised methods trained on 14,823/20,874 images (e.g., PSPNet: 72.0/60.8/66.0; SegFormer: 74.6/57.7/65.0). On AGD20K, the method achieves 0.740 KLD, 0.577 SIM, and 1.745 NSS (seen) and 1.070 KLD, 0.461 SIM, 1.503 NSS (unseen) with only 50/33 images (0.22%/0.21% of training data), outperforming the best weakly-supervised method LOCATE (1.226 KLD, 0.401 SIM, 1.177 NSS seen) by a wide margin. The method also generalizes to novel affordance vocabularies (e.g., 'saw' for 'cut', 'grab' for 'grasp') without additional training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against 10 baselines across two datasets. On AGD20K, the method decisively outperforms all four weakly-supervised affordance grounding (WSAG) methods: LOCATE (1.226 KLD seen) is the strongest WSAG baseline but still trails Ours (0.740 KLD) by ~40% in KLD and ~50% in SIM. Among CLIP-based open-vocabulary methods, ZegCLIP (1.413 KLD) and SAN (1.435 KLD) are the closest but remain far behind Ours (0.740 KLD), while MaskCLIP is essentially non-functional (5.752 KLD). On UMD, the headline result is that Ours (74.6/59.7/66.4 hIoU) matches or exceeds all three fully-supervised methods (PSPNet 72.0/60.8/66.0, SegFormer 74.6/57.7/65.0, DeepLabV3+ 70.5/57.5/63.3) using only 0.36% of their training data. When those same segmentation architectures are retrained in the OOAL setting, they degrade by ~10% (e.g., SegFormer drops from 74.6 to 64.6 seen mIoU), confirming that the architecture alone is insufficient without the proposed vision-language alignment. The CLIP-based methods (MaskCLIP, SAN, ZegCLIP) all perform poorly under OOAL, with MaskCLIP collapsing to near-random (4.25 mIoU), validating the paper's analysis that CLIP lacks fine-grained affordance understanding.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of affordance learning and foundation model adaptation. It builds directly on DINOv2's self-supervised part-aware visual features and CLIP's text encoder, combining them with CoOp-style prompt learning, multi-layer feature fusion, and a CLS-guided transformer decoder. It departs from prior one-shot methods (OSSS, OS-AD) that rotate support images each iteration, and from open-vocabulary affordance methods (OpenAD, AffCorrs) that require large annotations or explicit support images, by framing a stricter setting: one fixed example per base category with zero-shot inference on both novel objects and novel affordance vocabularies. The empirical analysis of why DINOv2 outperforms CLIP and DeiT III for fine-grained affordance grounding provides a principled justification for the architectural choice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that foundation models can be adapted for fine-grained affordance understanding with under 1% of conventional training data, matching or exceeding fully-supervised segmentation methods. The finding that self-supervised DINOv2 features are more suitable than vision-language CLIP features for part-level affordance recognition challenges the default assumption that CLIP is the go-to backbone for open-vocabulary dense prediction. Open questions include whether this approach scales to 3D affordance maps for robotics, whether the one-shot-per-category requirement can be further relaxed, and how the method performs in cluttered real-world scenes beyond the two curated benchmarks. The CLS-guided mask's role in improving generalization to unseen objects suggests a broader principle about constraining cross-modal attention for robustness.
