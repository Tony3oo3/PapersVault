## Problem

GANs trained on limited data suffer from discriminator overfitting and unstable training, yet Batch Normalization—despite its proven generalization benefits—has been largely excluded from GAN discriminators because it empirically degrades performance. The paper identifies the root cause: BN's centering step drives feature cosine similarity to zero (causing gradient amplification) and its scaling step introduces a Lipschitz constant inversely proportional to the minimum batch standard deviation (causing gradient explosion). This gap between BN's theoretical generalization promise and its practical failure in GAN discriminators remains unaddressed.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB images ranging from 32×32 (CIFAR) to 1024×1024 (few-shot datasets with as few as 64 training images), taking a noise vector z ~ p_z and a small real dataset μ̂_n as input to produce generated images. CHAIN is applied exclusively within the discriminator's convolutional blocks (after each conv layer C₁, C₂, C_S), processing feature maps of shape B×d×H×W. The adaptive interpolation parameter p is dynamically updated during training based on the sign of the discriminator's mean output on real samples relative to a threshold τ, requiring no additional input.

## Method

CHAIN replaces BN's centering step with a zero-mean regularization term (λ·p·‖μ‖²) added to the discriminator loss, and replaces the scaling step with a Lipschitz-constrained RMS normalization that multiplies Y/ψ by ψ_min to cap the Lipschitz constant at 1. The normalized features are then adaptively interpolated with unnormalized features via a Bernoulli-distributed mask M ~ B(p), yielding the ARMS operation: (1−M)⊙Y + M⊙(Y/ψ)·ψ_min. The parameter p simultaneously controls regularization strength and interpolation ratio, and is updated adaptively (p_{t+1} = p_t + Δ_p·ε) when the discriminator's mean output on real data exceeds a threshold τ, signaling overfitting. Theoretically, the paper proves CHAIN reduces the squared gradient norm of latent features by a factor ≤1 minus a non-negative term, and reduces weight gradients by lowering the largest eigenvalue of pre-layer features, thereby improving both training stability and generalization per a PAC-Bayesian error bound.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with 10% data, OmniGAN+CHAIN achieves tFID 6.81 and vFID 10.64, dramatically outperforming the OmniGAN baseline (tFID 53.02, vFID 57.68) and surpassing OmniGAN+DA (tFID 19.45) by 2.8×. On ImageNet with only 2.5% training data, BigGAN+ADA+CHAIN reaches tFID 23.01 and IS 16.57, beating BigGAN+ADA+KDDLGAN (tFID 28.79) and BigGAN+MaskedGAN (tFID 38.62). On 100-shot StyleGAN2 tasks, DA+CHAIN achieves FID 6.93 on Panda and 30.88 on Dog, outperforming FakeCLR (8.42/42.02) and KDDLGAN (8.41/50.22). On seven 1024×1024 few-shot datasets, FastGAN-Dbig+CHAIN reduces FID by 30–55% over the FastGAN baseline (e.g., Shells: 78.62 vs. 138.50).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CHAIN consistently outperforms all 21 baselines across every dataset and architecture tested. On CIFAR-10 (10% data), OmniGAN+CHAIN (tFID 6.81) beats OmniGAN+DA (19.45) by 2.8×, OmniGAN+ADA (40.05) by 5.9×, and even LeCam+DA+KDDLGAN (13.86 vFID) on vFID. On ImageNet (2.5% data), BigGAN+ADA+CHAIN (tFID 23.01) surpasses BigGAN+ADA+KDDLGAN (28.79) and BigGAN+MaskedGAN (38.62), while BigGAN+CHAIN alone (30.66) already beats BigGAN+DA (86.07) by 2.8×. On 100-shot StyleGAN2 tasks, DA+CHAIN (Panda FID 6.93, Dog 30.88) outperforms FakeCLR (8.42/42.02), KDDLGAN (8.41/50.22), GenCo (9.49/49.63), and AugSelf (8.36/48.19) on all five datasets. On the seven 1024×1024 few-shot datasets, FastGAN-Dbig+CHAIN reduces FID by 43% on Shells (78.62 vs. 138.50), 49% on Skulls (82.47 vs. 165.64 for FastGAN-Dbig), and 30% on Pokemon (31.94 vs. 45.70) compared to the FastGAN baseline. Notably, CHAIN also outperforms standard normalization alternatives (BN, IN, LN, GN) in ablation Table 6, with BN actually degrading performance (tFID 37.37 vs. baseline 22.24 on CIFAR-10), confirming the paper's theoretical diagnosis of gradient explosion in BN's centering and scaling steps.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Batch Normalization (Ioffe & Szegedy, 2015) but identifies a mechanistic reason why BN fails in GAN discriminators: the centering step drives inter-feature cosine similarity to zero (Theorem 3.1) and the scaling step can have Lipschitz constant exceeding 1 when σ_min < 1 (Theorem 3.2). It departs from the dominant data-efficient GAN strategies—massive augmentation (DA, ADA, MaskedGAN) and knowledge distillation (KDDLGAN, TransferGAN)—by instead modifying the discriminator's internal normalization, placing it in the discriminator-regularization lineage alongside spectral normalization, gradient penalties (WGAN-GP, DigGAN), and LeCam. The PAC-Bayesian generalization bound (Prop. 3.1) connects GAN generalization to discriminator weight gradient norms, providing a theoretical bridge between normalization design and generalization that prior GAN theory (Zhang et al. 2018, Ji et al. 2021) did not explicitly establish.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CHAIN offers a simple, architecture-agnostic drop-in module that outperforms augmentation-heavy baselines without requiring any external data or pre-trained models, which is practically significant for privacy-sensitive or resource-constrained settings (medical imaging, rare species). The clear mechanistic diagnosis of BN's failure mode in GAN discriminators—rather than merely observing it empirically—gives practitioners a principled basis for normalization design in adversarial settings. Open questions include whether the adaptive p mechanism and running cumulative statistics are truly necessary (ablations show only marginal gains), whether the approach transfers to diffusion-based generators, and whether the PAC-Bayesian bound's emphasis on weight gradients generalizes beyond the specific Hessian-based derivation.
