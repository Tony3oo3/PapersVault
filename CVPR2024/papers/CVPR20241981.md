## Problem

Prior data reconstruction attacks in federated learning (gradient inversion and linear layer leakage) have been evaluated solely on reconstruction quality metrics like PSNR and SSIM, or on whether any images are identifiable. No prior work has examined whether the leaked data is actually useful for downstream model training, leaving open the question of whether an attacker can leverage reconstructed data to build models that outperform the federated learning baseline itself.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241981/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The attacks consume client gradient updates (weight and bias gradients of a modified fully-connected layer for LLL, or full model gradients for GI) along with the model architecture to produce reconstructed images. For GI (Inverting Gradients), labels are recovered from the final-layer gradient before optimization and matched to images during the iterative reconstruction. For LLL (LOKI), images are recovered by solving a linear equation from FC-layer gradients but arrive as an unordered set with no label correspondence, requiring either full label knowledge or semi-supervised learning (CoMatch) for downstream training. The final output is a trained ResNet-18 (CIFAR-10, Tiny ImageNet) or DNN (MNIST) classifier evaluated on standard test/validation splits.

## Method

This is an empirical study that applies two existing attack families—Inverting Gradients (GI) and LOKI (LLL)—across MNIST, CIFAR-10, and Tiny ImageNet, then trains models from the leaked data under multiple settings: fully supervised from scratch, semi-supervised with CoMatch (to handle LLL's label-matching gap), and fine-tuning a pre-trained FL model with only 2–5% of leaked images. The key finding is that reconstruction quality metrics are poor predictors of training utility: images with PSNR < 14 still yield 58.29% test accuracy on CIFAR-10, and LLL with only 36.48% leakage rate achieves 88.86% accuracy, still 20% above the FedAvg baseline. For GI, batch size is the critical bottleneck—increasing from 4 to 16 on CIFAR-10 drops accuracy from 90.34% to 76.83%—while LLL performance is remarkably stable across FC layer sizes. The paper also shows that initializing from a trained FL model and fine-tuning on a small subset of leaked data yields 13–29% accuracy gains over training from scratch on the same data.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, linear layer leakage (LOKI) with FC factor 8 achieves 93.16% test accuracy, and gradient inversion (Inverting Gradients) with batch size 4 achieves 90.34%, both substantially outperforming the FedAvg baseline (72.76% with 10 clients) while remaining close to centralized training (94.38%). On MNIST, LOKI nearly matches centralized accuracy (98.82% vs. 98.89%), while GI slightly underperforms FedAvg. On Tiny ImageNet, LOKI with FC factor 8 reaches 46.70% top-1 validation accuracy versus 37.00% for FedAvg (10 clients) and 48.55% for centralized.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the centralized training baseline (94.38% on CIFAR-10), LOKI with FC factor 8 comes within 1.22% (93.16%) and Inverting Gradients with batch 4 within 4.04% (90.34%), showing leaked data can nearly replicate full-dataset training. Against the FedAvg baseline (72.76% with 10 clients, 68.71% with 50 clients), both attacks achieve large gains: LOKI improves by 20.40% and GI by 17.58% on CIFAR-10. On MNIST, LOKI (98.82%) essentially ties centralized (98.89%) and beats FedAvg (96.17–96.18%), while GI (94.29–95.96%) actually falls slightly below FedAvg, likely due to noise sensitivity in sparse MNIST images. On Tiny ImageNet, LOKI outperforms FedAvg for all FC factors ≥ 2 (41.15–46.70% vs. 35.06–37.00%), but at FC factor 1 (35.20%) it is nearly on par with FedAvg 50 clients (35.06%), highlighting that the total number of leaked images matters more on harder datasets. The key takeaway is that LLL attacks are far more effective for downstream training than GI attacks, as reconstruction quality degrades sharply with batch size for GI but remains high for LLL regardless of FC layer size.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes two established data reconstruction attack families—gradient inversion (Inverting Gradients) and linear layer leakage (LOKI)—not as novel methods but as data sources for a new evaluation question: can the leaked data actually train competitive models? Prior work on these attacks measured success exclusively through image-similarity metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS) or leakage proportion, never assessing downstream training utility. The paper also draws on semi-supervised learning (CoMatch) to address the label-matching gap inherent in LLL, and uses standard FL baselines (FedAvg, FedSGD) and centralized training as reference points, but introduces no new attack or training algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical implication is that the field's standard metrics for evaluating data reconstruction attacks (PSNR, SSIM, leakage rate) are poor proxies for the actual harm of a breach: a model trained on leaked data can substantially outperform federated learning even when reconstructions look like noise. This reframes the threat model for FL privacy—defenses that optimize for reconstruction quality may be insufficient if even low-quality leaks enable strong downstream models. Open questions remain around label matching for LLL at scale, the effect of non-IID client data on reconstruction, interaction with differential privacy and secure aggregation, and whether findings generalize to larger datasets like ImageNet where manual labeling is infeasible.
