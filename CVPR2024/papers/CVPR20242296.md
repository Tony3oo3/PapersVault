## Problem

Neural implicit surface reconstruction methods assume straight-line light transport, which breaks down for transparent objects where refraction and reflection bend light paths. Existing approaches for transparent objects rely on controlled camera setups (gray-coded backgrounds, turntables) to obtain ray-location correspondences and typically require a known refractive index, limiting their use in unconstrained real-world scenes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242296/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-view RGB images (800×800 for synthetic scenes; variable resolution for real captures) with known camera poses and intrinsic matrices, and requires no special hardware or pre-specified refractive index. It outputs a refined signed distance function (extractable as a 3D mesh) and novel view renderings. The refractive index is estimated post-hoc by rendering novel views with the pre-trained model and selecting the value that maximizes PSNR, rather than being a training input.

## Method

TNSR operates in two stages: first, NeuS is trained under the standard opacity assumption to obtain an initial SDF; second, the surface is refined by casting rays through the object using sphere tracing and Snell's law to compute reflected and refracted directions, then rendering colors along these piecewise-linear rays with the learned radiance field and combining them via Fresnel equations. The key technical contribution is a differentiable nested optimization: the ray-surface intersection distance is formulated as a constrained optimization problem, and Proposition 4.6 from Deep Declarative Networks yields closed-form gradients (implicit differentiation of φ_SDF = 0) that backpropagate the photometric color-consistency loss through the intersection to the SDF network parameters. This connects surface refinement with volume rendering in an end-to-end differentiable pipeline, requiring only multi-view RGB supervision. The method considers one reflection and up to two refractions per ray, and the refractive index is searched over a range to maximize novel-view PSNR without additional training.

## Results

On novel view synthesis across six transparent-object datasets, TNSR achieves the best or near-best PSNR on all six scenes, with standout results on Ellipsoid (25.24 dB) and Optical Ball (24.07 dB). On geometry reconstruction (Chamfer L1 × 10⁻³), TNSR outperforms NeuS on all four synthetic objects, e.g., 0.80 vs 1.05 on Ellipsoid and 18.05 vs 19.28 on the complex Kitty shape, while UNISURF and VolSDF fail entirely on Kitty. TNSR also achieves the lowest LPIPS on most datasets (e.g., 0.061 on Ellipsoid, 0.084 on Bottle), indicating sharper renders than baselines that produce blurrier outputs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the closest baseline NeuS+Ref. (NeuS surface rendered with analytic refraction/reflection but no refinement), TNSR wins on all six datasets across PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS, confirming that the differentiable surface refinement step is essential. Compared to Eikonal Fields, TNSR achieves dramatically higher PSNR on all four synthetic scenes (e.g., 24.07 vs 13.17 on Optical Ball, 23.20 vs 13.82 on Bottle), where Eikonal Fields fails to estimate the refractive field, while the two methods are more competitive on the real Ball and Glass scenes. Against MS-NeRF, TNSR achieves lower LPIPS on Ball (0.105 vs 0.105) and Glass (0.120 vs 0.119) and higher PSNR on Optical Ball (24.07 vs 21.36) and Bottle (23.20 vs 21.35), though MS-NeRF edges ahead on PSNR for Ball (22.35 vs 21.80). For geometry, TNSR achieves the lowest Chamfer L1 on all four synthetic objects, beating NeuS by 0.15–1.51 × 10⁻³ and outperforming UNISURF and VolSDF, which fail to reconstruct the complex Kitty shape at all. Standard NeRF baselines (TensoRF, Instant-NGP, Nerfacto) are consistently outperformed by TNSR on PSNR and LPIPS across most datasets, with the largest gaps on the synthetic scenes where refraction effects are most pronounced.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on NeuS [35], which combines SDF-based implicit surface representation with volume rendering for multi-view reconstruction, but departs from its straight-ray assumption by incorporating physical refraction and reflection tracing via Snell's law and Fresnel equations. It contrasts with controlled-setup methods (e.g., NETO [16], Lyu et al. [17]) that require custom hardware and known refractive indices to obtain ray-location correspondence supervision; instead, TNSR leverages the learned opaque-background radiance field as a photometric supervision signal. The differentiable nested optimization for ray-surface intersections draws on the implicit differentiation framework of Gould et al. [11], applied here to piecewise-linear refracted and reflected ray paths within a volume rendering pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the need for controlled camera setups and known refractive indices, TNSR makes transparent object reconstruction applicable to unconstrained real-world scenes, which is a significant practical step forward. The differentiable nested optimization technique for computing gradients through ray-surface intersections is a reusable component that could benefit other differentiable rendering and inverse graphics tasks. However, the method is limited to at most two refractions and one reflection, cannot handle translucent objects, and shows degraded performance on complex geometries with multiple light bounces (e.g., Kitty's ears), leaving open the question of how to scale to arbitrary bounce counts and mixed transparency.
