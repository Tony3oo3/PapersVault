## Problem

Existing IMU-based human pose estimation methods underutilize acceleration data, ignore spatial relationships between body parts and sensor placements, and rely heavily on synthetic virtual IMU data that introduces a domain gap with real measurements. These limitations cause ambiguities in complex motions (e.g., distinguishing sitting from standing during arm raises) and poor generalization to unseen subjects and unconstrained movements.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242082/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a temporal sequence of 72-dimensional IMU readings (6 sensors × 9-dim orientation + 3-dim acceleration) from fixed body locations (root, head, left/right lower arms, left/right ankles), along with initial leaf-joint velocities and initial pose for RNN initialization. It produces full-body SMPL joint rotations structured as three part-based outputs (upper limbs, torso, lower limbs) plus intermediate pseudo-velocity estimates for each sensor-equipped joint, with the root joint velocity retaining only its vertical component.

## Method

DynaIP employs a two-stage, part-based architecture: Stage I (Velocity Regression Network) uses an MLP to process initial velocities and a 2-layer LSTM to regress pseudo-velocities from raw IMU data, converting noisy acceleration into continuous motion indicators; Stage II (Pose Regression Network) concatenates the estimated velocities with IMU inputs and feeds them through a 2-layer LSTM to predict joint rotations. The body and sensors are partitioned into three local regions (upper limbs, torso, lower limbs), each processed by an independent sub-model to exploit local joint dependencies while minimizing interference from weakly associated joints. A global feature extractor coarsely captures full-body motion from all six IMUs and is concatenated into each local branch to maintain global coherence. Training data is unified across skeleton formats (SMPL and Xsens) via a global bone-orientation mapping that aligns corresponding joints one-to-one, enabling the model to leverage diverse real inertial mocap datasets without the virtual-to-real domain gap.

## Results

On the DIP-IMU test set, DynaIP* (trained on unified real mocap data) achieves 13.78° SIP error and 7.07° global angular error, reducing SIP error by 8% and angular error by 19% compared to the prior state-of-the-art PIP (15.02° / 8.73°). Across five public datasets, DynaIP (trained on Xsens data only) achieves the best or near-best results on all metrics, with a 28% relative SIP error reduction on Natural Motion (15.78° vs. PIP's 19.62°) and an 18% angular error reduction on CIP (4.54° vs. 5.52°). On the challenging DIP-IMU sitting sequences, real mocap training reduces SIP error by 28% over the virtual-to-real AMASS baseline (29.28° vs. 40.72°).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DynaIP outperforms all four baselines (DIP, TransPose, TIP, PIP) on every dataset and metric in Table 3. Against the strongest baseline PIP, DynaIP reduces SIP error by 1.8% on DIP-IMU (17.31° vs. 17.62°), 6% on AnDy (8.93° vs. 9.49°), 18% on UNIPD (7.29° vs. 8.90°), 10% on CIP (11.42° vs. 12.68°), and 20% on Natural Motion (15.78° vs. 19.62°). When augmented with the DIP-IMU training set (DynaIP*), the advantage over PIP widens to 8% SIP and 19% angular error reduction on the DIP-IMU test set (Table 2). DynaIP also surpasses TIP and TransPose by larger margins on all datasets, with the gap most pronounced on Natural Motion where DynaIP's SIP error is 28% lower than PIP's and 29% lower than TIP's. The box-plot analysis on Natural Motion further shows DynaIP has the lowest maximum SIP error and fewer outliers, indicating more consistent performance across diverse motions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DIP→TransPose→TIP→PIP lineage of real-time IMU-based human pose estimation, inheriting the RNN initialization strategy from PIP [46] and the sparse-IMU-to-SMPL formulation from DIP [14]. It departs from the dominant virtual-to-real training paradigm (AMASS synthetic data + fine-tuning) by instead unifying multiple real inertial mocap datasets across different skeleton formats (SMPL, Xsens) via a simple global-orientation mapping. The part-based decomposition into three body regions draws conceptually from part-based 3D dynamics learning in vision-based pose estimation [20, 36], repurposing that spatial-locality principle for the IMU setting where it had not been applied.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that abundant real mocap data, when properly unified across skeleton formats, can replace the virtual-to-real pipeline that has constrained the field, yielding a 19% pose-error reduction over the prior SOTA (PIP) on DIP-IMU. The part-based modeling addresses a practically important failure mode—ambiguity between similar limb motions in different postures (e.g., arm raises while sitting vs. standing)—that monolithic architectures struggle with. Open questions include whether the three-region decomposition is optimal, whether the simple one-to-one skeleton mapping generalizes to more structurally dissimilar formats, and whether the pseudo-velocity intermediate stage transfers to other sparse-sensor configurations (e.g., fewer than six IMUs).
