## Problem

Current adiabatic quantum computing (AQC) approaches for clustering discard all but the best measurement, throwing away information contained in the remaining samples that could quantify uncertainty. This paper addresses how to exploit the full set of noisy AQC measurements to compute calibrated posterior probabilities over balanced k-means solutions, enabling identification of ambiguous data points and alternative clusterings at little additional cost.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242005/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of I feature vectors (e.g., 1000-dim VGG embeddings for images or 4-dim tabular features for IRIS), a number of clusters K, and per-cluster size constraints s_k, and encodes them into a QUBO/Ising Hamiltonian. It produces a set of feasible binary assignment matrices Z with calibrated posterior probabilities P(Z|X), from which a CoresetSearch algorithm extracts progressively smaller high-confidence point subsets. Unlike standard k-means which returns a single partition, the output is a ranked distribution over solutions, with the MAP solution as the top entry.

## Method

The paper formulates balanced k-means as an energy-based model where the data likelihood is a mixture of Gaussians with identity covariance, yielding a quadratic energy E(X|Z) over binary one-hot assignment variables that maps directly to the Ising Hamiltonian of a D-Wave quantum annealer. Cluster-size and one-hot constraints are enforced via quadratic Lagrangian penalties, and the annealer is run at a sufficiently high temperature to sample the Boltzmann distribution. The key insight is a posterior recomputation step: rather than trusting the AQC's implicit (miscalibrated) sampling temperature, the paper evaluates the analytic energy of every feasible measured solution and renormalizes over them to obtain calibrated P(Z|X), avoiding the need to match the physical annealing temperature exactly. A greedy CoresetSearch algorithm then iteratively intersects high-probability solutions to extract subsets of points whose assignment is unambiguous, providing a practical uncertainty signal for downstream tasks.

## Results

On synthetic balanced k-means tasks (15 points, 3 clusters, 2D), the QA solver on D-Wave Advantage 2 achieves 56.1% accuracy, 79.4% completeness, and 74.6% adjusted Rand index, matching SIM (56.4%) and exhaustive search (56.4%) while outperforming balanced k-means (51.3% accuracy, 75.0% completeness). On the IRIS dataset (3 clusters, 15 points), QA and SIM achieve 47.2%/47.1% accuracy and 81.7% completeness, slightly exceeding k-means (47.0% accuracy, 80.6% completeness). The calibrated posterior probabilities are well-calibrated for problems up to 15 points, with the sparsification plots showing near-oracle ordering of tasks by predicted confidence. The key differentiator is the probabilistic output: calibrated confidence scores and coresets identifying ambiguous points at negligible additional cost beyond the 5000 AQC measurements.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole clustering baseline is balanced k-means [Malinen & Franti, 2014]. On the 15-point, 3-cluster synthetic task, the QA/SIM/exhaustive formulations outperform k-means by ~5 percentage points in accuracy (56.1–56.4% vs. 51.3%), ~4.5 points in completeness (79.4–79.5% vs. 75.0%), and ~6 points in adjusted Rand index (74.6–74.7% vs. 68.8%). On IRIS, the gap narrows to a marginal 0.2-point accuracy advantage (47.2% vs. 47.0%) and 1.1-point completeness advantage (81.7% vs. 80.6%), with QA and SIM essentially tied. However, the critical distinction is that k-means provides only a single point estimate, whereas the proposed method yields calibrated posterior probabilities over all sampled solutions and coresets that flag ambiguous points—information k-means cannot produce. At larger scales (45 points, 3 clusters), k-means actually surpasses SIM in accuracy (52.8% vs. 50.8%), suggesting the QUBO formulation's advantage diminishes as problem complexity grows beyond what the current QA can reliably solve.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the AQC-based balanced k-means formulation of Arthur and Date [4] and the one-hot QUBO encoding of Date et al. [27] by repurposing the quantum annealer from a pure optimizer into a probabilistic sampler. While prior AQC clustering work (including Nguyen et al. [65]) discards all but the best measurement, this paper retains all feasible samples and recomputes calibrated posterior probabilities via the Boltzmann distribution, following the energy-based inference framework established in Birdal et al. [12]. The classical balanced k-means of Malinen and Franti [56] serves both as the baseline and as the initialization for Lagrangian multiplier estimation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is showing that the 'waste' measurements from a quantum annealer carry calibratable probabilistic information at negligible additional cost, enabling uncertainty quantification for clustering that is otherwise expensive (MCMC) or unavailable (greedy k-means). This is particularly relevant for downstream tasks like multi-object tracking and autonomous driving where ambiguous assignments must be flagged. However, the approach is currently limited to very small problem sizes (≤20 points) on the D-Wave Advantage 2, and the calibration depends on sufficient sampling temperature, which constrains real-world applicability until quantum hardware scales. Open questions include whether the coreset strategy generalizes to high-dimensional learned features without the identity-covariance assumption, and how the method behaves as QA fidelity improves.
