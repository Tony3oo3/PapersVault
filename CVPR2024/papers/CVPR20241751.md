## Problem

Current open-set supervised anomaly detection (OSAD) methods treat the limited training anomaly examples as drawn from a single homogeneous distribution and are trained in a closed-set setting. This ignores the inherent heterogeneity of real-world anomalies, which can arise from vastly different distributions (e.g., tumors varying in appearance, shape, size, and position), causing these methods to fail on unseen anomalies whose distributions differ from the seen ones.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241751/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images and a small set of labeled anomaly examples (1 or 10 per dataset) alongside normal samples. Features are extracted by a pretrained OSAD backbone (DRA or DevNet), and the framework operates on these low-dimensional feature vectors rather than raw pixels. The output is a scalar anomaly score per image, with higher scores indicating greater abnormality. The framework is plug-and-play: any existing OSAD model's features and loss function can be substituted as the base model.

## Method

AHL consists of two components. HADG generates T heterogeneous anomaly distribution datasets by k-means clustering normal features into C clusters, pairing each cluster with randomly sampled anomaly examples and pseudo anomalies (via CutMix, CutPaste, or DRAEM Mask), then splitting each dataset into disjoint support and query sets to simulate surrogate open-set environments. CDL then performs collaborative differentiable learning: T base models are trained on their respective support sets using a deviation loss, and a unified model is iteratively optimized on the query sets (unseen during base-model training) to synthesize richer anomaly heterogeneity. A self-supervised BiLSTM sequentially predicts next-epoch anomaly scores to estimate each base model's generalization error, which is inverted into importance weights that adaptively reweight the collaborative loss, down-weighting poorly generalizing base models at each training step.

## Results

Under the general setting with 10 anomaly examples, AHL(DRA) achieves the best AUC on 7 out of 9 datasets, averaging 0.970 on MVTec AD (vs. 0.966 for DRA) and 0.925 on AITEX (vs. 0.892 for DRA). With only 1 anomaly example, AHL(DRA) maintains a clear advantage, averaging 0.901 on MVTec AD (vs. 0.889 for DRA) and 0.888 on Optical (vs. 0.880 for DRA). Overall, AHL enhances DRA by up to 4% AUC and DevNet by up to 9% AUC across the nine real-world datasets spanning industrial, planetary, and medical domains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AHL(DRA) consistently outperforms the DRA baseline across all nine datasets in both 10-shot and 1-shot settings, with average AUC gains of ~0.4% (10-shot) to ~1.2% (1-shot) on MVTec AD and up to 3.3% on AITEX (10-shot: 0.925 vs. 0.892). Similarly, AHL(DevNet) improves over DevNet by up to 9% AUC (e.g., Optical 1-shot: 0.547 vs. 0.513; ELPV 1-shot: 0.828 vs. 0.810). Against the other OSAD baselines (MLEP, FLOS) and the semi-supervised SAOE, AHL(DRA) achieves substantially higher AUC on nearly all datasets, with the largest margins on Hyper-Kvasir (0.880 vs. 0.666 for SAOE) and Optical (0.976 vs. 0.941 for SAOE). In the hard setting (single anomaly class), AHL(DRA) improves DRA by up to 3.2% AUC, confirming gains come from generalization to unseen anomaly classes rather than memorizing seen ones. In cross-domain evaluation, AHL(DRA) achieves near-perfect AUC (≥0.973) on all five MVTec texture subsets, compared to DRA's more variable cross-domain scores (0.833–0.998). Against the fully-supervised FS-DRA upper bound, AHL(DRA) is competitive on 7/9 datasets and actually surpasses it on AITEX (0.925 vs. 0.919) and SDD (0.991 vs. 0.991, tied), demonstrating effective use of few-shot examples without overfitting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the OSAD line established by DevNet and DRA, which introduced deviation-based losses and disentangled anomaly representations for detecting both seen and unseen anomalies with few training examples. The key conceptual departure is rejecting the homogeneous-distribution assumption: prior OSAD methods treat all training anomalies as drawn from a single distribution in a closed-set setting, whereas AHL simulates multiple heterogeneous anomaly distributions via k-means clustering of normal samples combined with random anomaly subsets and pseudo-anomaly generation (CutMix, CutPaste, DRAEM). Rather than proposing a standalone detector, AHL is positioned as a generic meta-framework that wraps existing OSAD models (DRA, DevNet) with a collaborative differentiable learning loop and a self-supervised BiLSTM-based importance estimator, distinguishing it from simple ensemble approaches that the authors show to be suboptimal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work addresses a practical and well-motivated limitation in OSAD—overfitting to the specific anomaly classes seen during training—and provides a plug-and-play mechanism that consistently improves multiple SOTA baselines across nine diverse datasets (industrial, medical, planetary). The cross-domain transfer results, where AHL(DRA) achieves near-perfect AUC on several MVTec texture subsets when fine-tuned with only normal samples, suggest the learned heterogeneity captures transferable anomaly structure. However, the approach's reliance on k-means clustering quality, the fixed number of simulated distributions (T=6), and the observation of slight cross-domain degradation on some targets leave open questions about scalability to highly diverse or high-dimensional anomaly spaces and whether the simulated heterogeneity truly approximates the unbounded real-world anomaly distribution.
