## Problem

Point tracking methods can follow individual scene points through long videos with occlusions, but are too slow and memory-intensive to track every pixel densely. Classical optical flow methods are fast and spatially coherent but struggle with large displacements and long occlusion events. The paper bridges this gap by showing that a small set of sparse tracks can bootstrap a dense, accurate flow-and-visibility prediction between arbitrary frame pairs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241569/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

DOT takes a video of RGB frames and a small set of N≈10³ sparse point tracks (position + visibility per frame) extracted by a frozen off-the-shelf tracker, with tracks sampled preferentially near motion boundaries detected via Sobel filtering of a pre-trained flow. For any source–target pair (s,t), it outputs a dense flow field F_{s→t} ∈ ℝ^{W×H×2} and a soft visibility mask M_{s→t} ∈ [0,1]^{W×H} (thresholded at 0.8 for binary use), initially computed at reduced resolution W/4 × H/4 and upsampled to full resolution. The number N is a tunable knob trading accuracy for speed, ranging from 256 to 8192 tracks.

## Method

DOT has three modules: (1) an off-the-shelf point tracker (e.g., CoTracker) produces N sparse tracks with visibility, sampled near flow discontinuities; (2) nearest-neighbor (Voronoi) interpolation assigns each pixel the displacement and visibility of its closest visible track, yielding coarse flow F⁰ and mask M⁰ at quarter resolution; (3) a RAFT-inspired network refines these estimates using 4D correlation volumes between source and target features, iteratively (K=4) updating the flow via a GRU that reads local neighborhoods around the current correspondence, while jointly refining the mask. Unlike RAFT's zero initialization, DOT starts from the track-based estimate, and unlike pure tracking methods it exploits full-frame spatial context. Training is supervised on Kubric synthetic videos with L1 flow loss and binary cross-entropy mask loss, using 2048 CoTracker tracks as input and 2048 ground-truth tracks for supervision, with the tracker frozen.

## Results

On the CVO (Final) test set, DOT with N=2048 tracks achieves an EPE of 1.38 (all/vis/occ: 1.38/0.82/4.10) and IoU of 80.2%, outperforming CoTracker (EPE 1.52, IoU 75.3%) while running ~100× faster (1.65s vs 173.5s per video). On the more challenging CVO (Extended) set with 48-frame videos, DOT (N=2048) achieves EPE 5.07 and IoU 71.0%, matching CoTracker (EPE 5.20, IoU 70.4%) at 166× lower inference time. On the TAP benchmark, DOT achieves AJ of 65.9 on DAVIS (Strided) and 83.4 on RGB-Stacking (Strided), surpassing the best single-point tracker CoTracker(★) (64.8 and 73.6) despite not being optimized for specific test queries.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DOT substantially outperforms all optical flow baselines: on CVO (Extended) with large motions, it reduces EPE by a factor of ~4 compared to RAFT (5.07 vs 28.6) and GMA (5.07 vs 30.0), and improves IoU by over 8% relative. Against the best point tracking method CoTracker, DOT matches or slightly beats EPE (1.38 vs 1.52 on Final; 5.07 vs 5.20 on Extended) while being 100–166× faster, and improves IoU by 5 points (80.2 vs 75.3 on Final). DOT clearly surpasses TAPIR (EPE 1.38 vs 4.19) and PIPs++ (EPE 1.38 vs 9.49) on CVO, and outperforms MFT (EPE 1.38 vs 3.16) and AccFlow (EPE 1.38 vs 1.73). On the TAP benchmark, DOT beats OmniMotion by 14.2 AJ on DAVIS (Strided) (65.9 vs 51.7) and by 5.9 AJ on RGB-Stacking (Strided) (83.4 vs 77.5), and slightly edges out the single-query-optimized CoTracker(★) on DAVIS (Strided) (65.9 vs 64.8 AJ) while being far more efficient for dense prediction.

## Relation to Prior Work

DOT sits at the intersection of two mature research threads—optical flow estimation and point tracking—and unifies them in a way neither family has previously achieved. Architecturally, it builds directly on RAFT's iterative correlation-volume refinement and GRU-based update, but departs by replacing RAFT's zero-motion initialization with a meaningful estimate derived from sparse point tracks via nearest-neighbor (Voronoi) interpolation, and by jointly predicting a visibility mask. On the tracking side, it extends the multi-point tracking idea of CoTracker to its logical extreme (all pixels simultaneously) while avoiding the quadratic cost by tracking only ~10³ points and letting a lightweight flow network densify the result. The conceptual departure from prior work is thus methodological: rather than applying expensive per-pixel tracking or chaining frame-to-frame flows, DOT treats a small set of long-range tracks as a coarse prior for a standard optical flow estimator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: DOT makes dense long-term tracking (flow + visibility between arbitrary frame pairs) feasible at optical-flow-level latency (~1 s per video vs. hundreds of seconds for dense point tracking), unlocking downstream applications like frame interpolation, inpainting, and video compression that were previously bottlenecked by tracking cost. The paper also introduces an Extended CVO benchmark with longer, higher-FPS videos to better stress-test long-range and occlusion-heavy scenarios. Limitations include dependence on an off-the-shelf tracker (so gains are coupled to future tracker improvements), training exclusively on Kubric synthetic data (raising generalization questions for real-world textures and lighting), and acknowledged failure modes under extreme occlusions or rapid appearance changes. An open question is whether the simple Voronoi interpolation step can be replaced by learned sparse-to-dense modules that better exploit the spatial structure of tracks.
