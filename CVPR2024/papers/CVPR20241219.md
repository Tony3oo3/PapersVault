## Problem

Despite widespread evidence that generative models leave characteristic traces on their samples, no formal definition of a generative model's 'fingerprint' or 'artifact' has been provided in the literature. Moreover, existing fingerprinting methods are largely limited to binary real-vs-synthetic detection, leaving the harder multi-class problem of attributing a sample to its specific source model (among GANs, VAEs, Flows, and Score-based models) largely unexplored.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single generated image and a reference set of real images (the training data of the generative models under study) as input. Both are mapped into a chosen embedding space—raw RGB, channel-wise FFT frequency domain, or the 2048-d feature space of a pretrained ResNet50 (SL) or Barlow Twins (SSL) encoder—and the generated sample's artifact is computed as its vector difference from the nearest real-image point in that space. The resulting artifact vector (same dimensionality as the embedding space) is the sole input to the attribution classifier, which outputs a multi-class label identifying the source generative model.

## Method

The paper formalizes an artifact as the Euclidean difference between a generated sample and its nearest neighbor on the true data manifold (estimated from real training images in a chosen embedding space), and a fingerprint as the set of all such artifacts for a model. In practice, the manifold is approximated by the finite set of real-image embeddings, and each generated sample's artifact is its residual after projecting onto this set. These artifact vectors are then fed to a ResNet50-based attribution network fine-tuned with cross-entropy loss over the multi-class model-identity labels. The authors provide theoretical justification showing that a zero fingerprint is equivalent to unit Precision (or Recall) and that non-zero fingerprints imply non-zero IPMs (MMD, Wasserstein). They further introduce four benchmark datasets (GM-CIFAR10, GM-CelebA, GM-CHQ, GM-FFHQ) spanning 30+ models across all four major generative families, and show that the artifact representation outperforms hand-crafted color, frequency, and learned-feature baselines on attribution accuracy, cross-dataset generalization, and feature-space separability.

## Results

On the four proposed benchmark datasets, ManiFPT (in its best embedding-space variant) achieves 72.0% accuracy on GM-CIFAR10, 74.7% on GM-CelebA, 64.8% on GM-CHQ, and 66.1% on GM-FFHQ for multi-class model attribution. The best baseline (Wang20) reaches 62.2%, 62.2%, 59.5%, and 64.2% respectively, yielding average improvements of 5.73% across datasets. ManiFPT also achieves the highest Fréchet Distance Ratio (FDR) on three of four datasets (e.g., 168.0 on GM-CelebA with SL embedding), confirming superior inter-class separability of the artifact feature space.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ManiFPT outperforms all eight baselines on every dataset. The largest margins appear on GM-CIFAR10 and GM-CelebA, where the best ManiFPT variant (SL/SSL) exceeds the strongest baseline (Wang20 at 62.2%) by roughly 10–12 percentage points. On GM-CHQ, the gap narrows to ~3.7% (ManiFPT_FREQ 64.8% vs. Yu19 61.1%), and on GM-FFHQ to ~1.9% (ManiFPT_FREQ 66.1% vs. Wang20 64.2%), consistent with the paper's observation that SoTA hybrid models (e.g., DDGAN) produce more mixed artifacts. Among baselines, hand-crafted color features (McCloskey18, Nataraj19) and frequency features (Durall20, Dzanic20) consistently underperform learned CNN-based methods (Wang20, Yu19, Marra18/19). Wang20 is the strongest single baseline on CIFAR10 and FFHQ, while Yu19 leads on CHQ; neither matches ManiFPT's FDR, which reaches 168.0 on CelebA (SL) versus Yu19's 111.4.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new conceptual ground by formalizing what a 'fingerprint' of a generative model actually is, rather than proposing yet another specific feature (frequency spectrum, color histogram, learned CNN embedding) to measure one. Prior works (Durall et al., Dzanic et al., McCloskey et al., Yu et al.) each identified a particular artifact signature and built classifiers on top of it, but none provided a unifying definition of the fingerprint itself. The paper repurposes manifold learning theory—treating real data as lying on a lower-dimensional manifold—to define an artifact as the deviation of a generated sample from its nearest real-data point, and a fingerprint as the set of all such deviations. It then connects this definition theoretically to Precision/Recall and IPMs, grounding it in established generative-model evaluation metrics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The formal definition provides a principled, model-agnostic framework for studying generative model limitations, moving the field beyond ad-hoc feature engineering. The four new benchmark datasets (GM-CIFAR10, GM-CelebA, GM-CHQ, GM-FFHQ) covering all four major generative families (GAN, VAE, Flow, Score-based) fill a clear gap in the literature and enable systematic multi-class attribution studies. Open questions include whether the manifold-based definition extends to video, 3D, or text-to-image models, how sensitive results are to the quality of the manifold estimate, and whether the approach scales to the rapidly growing zoo of new architectures (e.g., diffusion transformers, autoregressive image models). The finding that loss function choice and upsampling type dominate fingerprint structure confirms community intuition but also suggests that architectural innovations targeting these components will be most visible in attribution settings.
