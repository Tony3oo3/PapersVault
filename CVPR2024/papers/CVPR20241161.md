## Problem

Despite the rapid adoption of vision-language (VL) foundation models in medical imaging, their fairness across demographic groups remains unexplored, largely due to the absence of medical VL datasets with comprehensive demographic attributes and ground-truth labels. Existing public VL datasets (e.g., CheXpert, MIMIC-CXR) rely on noisy auto-extracted labels and limited demographic fields, making rigorous fairness analysis infeasible. This paper addresses both the data gap and the methodological gap by introducing a purpose-built fair VL medical dataset and a fairness-aware training objective.

## Input / Output

The model takes a pair of inputs: an SLO fundus image and a de-identified clinical note (11–332 words, mean 147) that includes both image descriptions and non-imaging clinical information such as medications, test results, and family history. Because standard VL text encoders (e.g., CLIP's 77-token limit) cannot accommodate full clinical notes, the notes are summarized via LLMs (GPT-4, PMC-LLAMA, MED42) to ≤180 characters before training. The output is a binary glaucoma diagnosis (glaucoma vs. non-glaucoma, defined by visual field mean deviation thresholds), evaluated via linear probing or zero-shot transfer, with fairness measured across six protected attributes (age, gender, race, ethnicity, preferred language, marital status).

## Method

FairCLIP augments CLIP's standard symmetric contrastive loss with a Sinkhorn-distance-based fairness regularizer computed during pre-training. For each batch, it computes the empirical distribution of diagonal similarity scores M_{i,i} (image–text cosine similarities) for the full batch and for each demographic subgroup, then minimizes the Sinkhorn distance between the overall distribution and each subgroup's distribution. The Sinkhorn distance is preferred over KL divergence because it is a true probability metric (symmetric, satisfies the triangle inequality), making it a more principled measure of distributional alignment. The resulting combined loss is backpropagated through both the vision and text encoders, encouraging the model to produce similar image–text correlation structures across demographic groups without sacrificing overall classification accuracy. The approach is evaluated in both zero-shot and linear-probing settings across ViT-B/16 and ViT-L/14 architectures, and compared against adversarial-debiasing baselines.

## Results

On Harvard-FairVLMed, FairCLIP (ViT-B/16) achieves zero-shot ES-AUC of 65.50 on Race, 65.39 on Gender, 63.33 on Ethnicity, and 62.31 on Language, consistently outperforming CLIP (61.67, 63.21, 63.09, 60.19 respectively) while also reducing Demographic Parity Difference (e.g., Race DPD drops from 15.35 to 6.07; Gender DPD drops from 4.34 to 0.84). In the linear probing fairness analysis (Table 2), medical pre-training improves ES-AUC by up to 8.38 points on ethnicity, and CLIP-FT reaches 80.27 AUC vs. 77.27 for natural-pretrained CLIP. Across all four protected attributes, Asian, Male, Non-Hispanic, and Spanish subgroups are consistently the highest-performing, revealing systematic bias in both CLIP and BLIP2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CLIP (Radford et al., 2021), FairCLIP consistently improves ES-AUC across all four protected attributes and both architectures: for ViT-B/16, ES-AUC gains range from +2.12 (Ethnicity: 63.09→63.33) to +3.83 (Race: 61.67→65.50), while AUC improves by 1.5–2.4 points. Fairness metrics also improve substantially in most cases—Race DPD drops from 15.35 to 6.07 and Gender DPD from 4.34 to 0.84 (ViT-B/16)—though a few individual fairness metrics (e.g., Race DPD for ViT-L/14) slightly worsen, indicating the Sinkhorn objective does not uniformly minimize every fairness criterion. Against BLIP2 (Li et al., 2023), the paper does not directly compare FairCLIP to BLIP2 in the same evaluation protocol; however, in the linear probing analysis (Table 2), medical pre-training closes the gap between BLIP2 and CLIP (80.10 vs. 80.27 AUC), and BLIP2-FT outperforms CLIP-FT on ethnicity and language ES-AUC. Against the adversarial fairness baseline (Beutel et al., 2017, 'CLIP w/ Adv'), FairCLIP consistently outperforms both standard CLIP and the adversarial variant across all attributes (Figure 3c), whereas CLIP w/ Adv does not reliably surpass plain CLIP, suggesting that adversarial debiasing is less effective than the Sinkhorn-based distributional alignment in the VL setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the medical imaging fairness literature (which has focused almost exclusively on vision-only chest X-ray models like CheXpert and MIMIC-CXR) to the vision-language setting, arguing that the human-written nature of clinical text could compound biases already present in visual encoders. Methodologically, FairCLIP departs from the dominant adversarial-debiasing and KL-divergence approaches by adopting Sinkhorn distance (a regularized Wasserstein variant) to align the overall sample distribution with per-group distributions in the CLIP similarity space. The dataset itself is positioned against existing medical VL datasets (CheXpert, MIMIC-CXR, PadChest) by providing ground-truth labels, richer demographic attributes (six protected attributes vs. two or three), and clinical notes that include non-imaging information, addressing the noisy-label and limited-demographic limitations the authors identify in prior resources.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The public release of Harvard-FairVLMed fills a concrete gap: no prior dataset combined fundus imaging, detailed demographics, and clinical notes with verified ground-truth labels for fairness evaluation, making systematic VL fairness studies in ophthalmology (and by extension, other specialties with similar note structures) feasible for the first time. However, the scope is narrow—single disease (glaucoma), single imaging modality (SLO fundus), and a relatively small 10K-sample cohort—so whether the Sinkhorn-based fairness objective and the observed bias patterns generalize to other medical VL tasks (radiology, pathology, dermatology) remains an open question. The finding that medical-domain pre-training improves fairness for underrepresented subgroups (Hispanic, Black) but not for language minorities suggests that domain adaptation alone is insufficient and that targeted interventions like FairCLIP are still needed.
