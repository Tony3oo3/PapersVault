## Problem

Existing deep learning-based image restoration methods process all regions of an image through a unified pipeline of repeated feature computation, treating diverse content patterns (smooth regions vs. high-frequency textures) identically. This leads to an overfitting-like phenomenon where certain regions are wrongly restored because the model lacks the capacity to adapt its processing to varying content complexity. The paper addresses the gap of explicitly decoupling and separately restoring different content components within a single end-to-end framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241997/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a single low-quality RGB image as input and produces a restored high-quality RGB image as output. Internally, the LQ image is decomposed via whole-image DCT into n frequency sub-bands (n=3 for SR and deblurring, n=2 for denoising), each processed by a dedicated sub-network of different computational cost, and the n restored sub-images are pixel-wise summed to form the final HQ output. The framework is task-agnostic and wraps around any existing IR network without altering its architecture.

## Method

CoDe consists of three components: (1) a Content Decoupling Module that applies whole-image type-II DCT (avoiding 8×8 block artifacts) and splits the spectrum into n sub-bands using learnable circular frequency masks with radii determined by trainable α parameters, then inverse DCT yields n LQ sub-images each containing one frequency component; (2) an IR Network Container that streamlines the original IR network into n cascaded sub-nets of progressively increasing width and depth via parameter sharing, where each sub-net processes one content component and a Reconstruction Layer Pool maps features back to RGB space; (3) a Content Consistency Loss that replaces the standard pixel-wise reconstruction loss with a weighted L1 loss computed on DCT coefficients of each sub-image, using task-specific weights β_i to emphasize more complex content components. The key insight is that reducing computational cost for simpler (low-frequency) content while allocating more capacity to complex (high-frequency) content alleviates the overfitting that causes wrong restorations in a unified pipeline.

## Results

On classical image SR (Table 1), CoDe applied to DAT achieves 33.42 dB PSNR on Set5 at ×4 (vs. 33.08 dB original), a 0.34 dB gain, and GRL+CoDe reaches 38.82 dB on Set5 at ×2 (vs. 38.67 dB original). For image denoising, GRL+CoDe improves over GRL by up to 0.15 dB on Urban100 at σ=15 (35.69 vs. 35.54). For motion deblurring, GRL+CoDe gains 0.12 dB on GoPro (34.05 vs. 33.93) and 0.11 dB on HIDE (31.76 vs. 31.65). The framework consistently improves every baseline across all 14 evaluation datasets without altering the underlying network architecture.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CoDe is applied as a plug-in framework on top of 15 existing IR methods across three tasks, and it improves every single baseline in every comparison. For SR, the largest gain is 0.34 dB (DAT ×4 on Set5: 33.42 vs. 33.08), while GRL+CoDe leads all methods at both ×2 (38.82 dB Set5) and ×4 (33.21 dB Set5). For denoising, GRL+CoDe outperforms GRL by 0.07–0.15 dB across CBSD68, Kodak24, McMaster, and Urban100 at all noise levels (σ=15/25/50), and also surpasses SwinIR, DRUNet, and RNAN. For real-world denoising, MIRNet-v2+CoDe gains 0.11 dB over MIRNet-v2 on SIDD (39.95 vs. 39.84) and 0.15 dB over AINDNet on DND (39.99 vs. 39.37). For deblurring, GRL+CoDe improves over GRL by 0.12 dB on GoPro (34.05 vs. 33.93) and 0.11 dB on HIDE (31.76 vs. 31.65), while also outperforming Stripformer and BANet. No baseline is matched or beaten by the original (un-enhanced) version of any method; the framework consistently yields positive gains of 0.05–0.34 dB in PSNR across all 14 evaluation datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

CoDe positions itself as a task- and architecture-agnostic wrapper that can be applied to any existing IR network (CNN or Transformer), rather than proposing a standalone model. It departs from prior frequency decoupling methods (OctConv, Haar wavelet, FFT-based approaches) by replacing fixed sliding-window or fixed-subband operations with whole-image type-II DCT and learnable circular frequency masks, enabling an arbitrary number of content components. It also distinguishes itself from earlier DCT-based IR methods (which use 8×8 block-wise DCT à la JPEG) by operating on the full image spectrum to avoid block-boundary artifacts. The divide-and-conquer strategy of assigning sub-nets with graduated computational costs to different frequency bands is a novel repurposing of the parameter-sharing idea from NAS literature into the IR setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework's architecture- and task-agnostic nature makes it a practical plug-in that can immediately boost a wide range of existing IR models, which is valuable for practitioners who do not want to redesign their pipelines. However, the performance gains, while consistent, are modest (typically 0.05–0.34 dB), and the added cost of multiple DCT/iDCT operations plus cascaded sub-nets may be non-trivial for very high-resolution or video restoration. Open questions include whether the number of content components (n) and the weighting coefficients (β) can be learned end-to-end rather than set per task, whether the approach extends naturally to generative or video restoration settings, and whether the DCT-based decoupling is truly optimal compared to learned or adaptive transforms.
