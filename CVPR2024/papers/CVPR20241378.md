## Problem

Dynamic vision tasks like structure-from-motion and optical flow require trackable features, but textureless objects lack them. Physical patterns (e.g., QR codes) provide trackability but require direct contact and alter appearance, while projected structured-light patterns can be applied remotely yet do not persist on the surface, making them useless across frames. No prior method combines remote applicability with temporal persistence for dynamic tracking.

## Input / Output

The PDR network consumes a 3-channel input: the raw thermal frame (640×512, 30 Hz, scaled to [0,1]), a spatial uncertainty heat map G(P_{f,m}) encoding where K=2–4 newly projected Halton-sequence dots are expected (with projection-order-dependent blur to account for motion), and a constant channel 0.1m encoding the diffusion magnitude. The network outputs a correction image that, when subtracted from the current frame, yields a PDR-corrected frame compatible with standard vision pipelines (COLMAP for SfM, RAFT for optical flow, Lucas-Kanade for point tracking). Downstream outputs include camera trajectories, sparse 3D point clouds, optical flow fields, and 3D object poses.

## Method

A coaxial thermal camera and dual-axis galvo-steered laser project discrete Halton-sequence heat dots (K=2–4 per frame) onto scene surfaces, creating transient thermal patterns that 'stick' to objects for several frames. The key challenge is that heat diffusion and the continuous addition of new dots violate the brightness-constancy assumption underlying standard tracking. To address this, a 6-block ResNet is trained as a projection-diffusion reversal (PDR) operator that simultaneously removes newly projected points and reverses the diffusion of existing ones, producing a frame whose appearance is consistent with the reference frame. Training uses 13 static-scene video sequences with L2 loss between the corrected frame and the ground-truth reference, augmented with random spatial shifts proportional to |t_n − t_{f+m}| to simulate motion-induced dot displacement. The coaxial (zero-baseline) geometry ensures each camera pixel maps to a unique projector ray, enabling predictable dot placement regardless of scene depth.

## Results

For structure-from-motion on a velvet-like styrofoam head, the PDR-corrected camera trajectory fits a circle with R² = 0.99996, compared to R² = 0.94 and 0.99 without PDR for two different scan configurations. On a black plastic head (~160° rotation), the recovered mesh achieves a mean Hausdorff distance of 1.5 mm (0.8%) and a maximum of 7.1 mm (3.7%) against a painted-texture COLMAP reference; without PDR, the reconstruction fails entirely. In the indoor localization cart experiment (~20 m loop), the mean absolute position error is ~14 cm relative to a GoPro-based COLMAP reference, with successful loop closure despite no shared features between the first and last frames. Optical flow on a rotating stage fits the expected circular model with R² = 0.98.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against other structured-light or thermal-vision methods; instead, the primary comparison is an ablation of the projection-diffusion reversal (PDR) network. With PDR, the styrofoam-head SfM trajectory achieves R² = 0.99996 versus 0.94–0.99 without it, and the black-plastic-head reconstruction succeeds (7.1 mm max error) whereas without PDR the pipeline fails to register all frames or produce a recognizable face. The system is also implicitly compared to standard RGB-based SfM (COLMAP), which fails outright on the low-albedo, textureless objects used in the experiments, demonstrating that the thermal-pattern approach enables reconstruction where visible-light methods cannot. In the cart localization experiment, the thermal system's ~14 cm mean error is measured against a GoPro + COLMAP reference, confirming practical accuracy for indoor navigation. The PDR network's contribution is most pronounced on rapidly diffusing materials or with larger frame gaps (m > 1), while it has negligible effect on slow-diffusing surfaces where individual points remain sharp.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of structured light, thermal imaging, and dynamic vision. Prior thermal imaging work (depth estimation, segmentation, person re-identification) treated thermal cameras as passive sensors, while active thermal illumination studies (Dashpute et al. for material classification, Tanaka et al. for light transport decomposition, Erdozain et al. for 3D scanning) used IR sources for static reconstruction tasks. The key departure here is exploiting the *persistence* of laser-induced heat as a trackable feature that 'sticks' to surfaces, enabling dynamic tasks (SfM, optical flow, object tracking) on textureless objects — a capability absent from both the physical-pattern (QR codes) and projected-pattern (structured light) literatures. The PDR network, a 6-block ResNet trained to reverse heat diffusion, is a lightweight addition that allows standard vision pipelines (COLMAP, RAFT, Lucas-Kanade) to operate on thermally-projected features without modification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work opens a practical pathway for autonomous agents to 'paint their own texture' on otherwise featureless surfaces, enabling navigation in challenging environments (tunnels, pipelines, textureless hallways) without physically altering objects. Key limitations remain: thermal patterns are transient (preventing feature-based loop closure), performance is material-dependent (glass, metals degrade), the output is a sparse point cloud not easily densified with standard MVS, and laser safety constrains deployment around humans. Open questions include whether digital codes can be encoded into thermal patterns for inter-agent communication, whether dense 3D recovery is feasible in the thermal domain, and how the approach handles rapid scene motion that smears projected dots into curves.
