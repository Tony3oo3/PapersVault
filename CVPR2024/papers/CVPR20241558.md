## Problem

No established method exists for reconstructing dense, photorealistic 3D scene representations from spike camera data, whose binary spike stream output is fundamentally incompatible with standard NeRF training that assumes clean RGB images under optimal illumination. While event-camera-based NeRFs (Ev-NeRF, EventNeRF) have been explored, they suffer from inherent lack of texture detail; spike cameras offer ultrahigh temporal resolution (up to 40 kHz) with full visual detail, but their noisy, threshold-based firing mechanism makes direct photometric supervision infeasible.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241558/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a continuous binary spike stream (spatial resolution 250×400 for real data, 400×400 for synthetic; temporal resolution 20,000 Hz) and camera poses (from COLMAP for real data, Blender for synthetic). A key unusual aspect is that the rendered output is not compared directly to pixel intensities but is fed through a differentiable spiking neuron layer to regenerate a synthetic spike stream, which is then compared to the ground-truth spike stream over 256 time steps. A per-pixel threshold variation matrix R(x,y), calibrated from a uniform-light capture, is required to model response nonuniformity.

## Method

SpikeNeRF appends an integrate-and-fire (IF) spiking neuron layer (256 time steps) after the standard NeRF coarse-fine MLP, converting rendered ray intensities back into a synthetic spike stream that emulates the physical spike camera's sampling process. The core loss is a long-term spike rendering loss that measures the L2 distance between the generated spike stream (with threshold variation and noise) and the observed spike stream over 256 timesteps, rather than comparing short-term intensity estimates that are corrupted by temporal sampling noise and fixed-pattern noise. Training uses Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) through the spiking neuron layer, with a surrogate gradient (1/V_th) replacing the non-differentiable Heaviside step function to update the last MLP layer weights. The framework leverages NeRF's inherent multi-view consistency as robust self-supervision, enabling coherent 3D structure recovery even under diverse and non-ideal illumination conditions without requiring any RGB supervision.

## Results

On the synthetic NeRF dataset (6 scenes), SpikeNeRF achieves PSNR 20.78/22.07 dB, SSIM 0.209/0.916, and LPIPS 0.617/0.053 (full image / object region), outperforming the best two-stage baseline TFP(256)+NeRF by over 5 dB in PSNR and 0.05 in SSIM. On real-world spike data evaluated with no-reference metrics, SpikeNeRF attains BRISQUE 35.94 and NIQE 6.09, beating TFP+NeRF (42.64/6.16), TFI+NeRF (47.92/7.49), and the event-camera baseline EventNeRF (83.98/12.71). The method also demonstrates robustness across illumination levels, with PSNR ranging from 18.72 (intensity 16) to 23.89 (intensity 64) on synthetic data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SpikeNeRF outperforms all four baselines on every metric in both synthetic and real-world evaluations. Against TFP(32)+NeRF and TFP(256)+NeRF (both from [46]), the gains are most dramatic: +4.6 dB PSNR over TFP(32) and +5.7 dB over TFP(256) on the object region, with SSIM improving from 0.648/0.864 to 0.916. TFI+NeRF [46] achieves comparable PSNR to TFP(256) on the object region (19.34 vs. 19.24) but still trails SpikeNeRF by 2.7 dB. Spk2img+NeRF [45], the learning-based baseline, performs worst overall (PSNR 14.05 on object region), confirming that its design for ideal illumination degrades sharply under noisy spike conditions. On real-world data (Table 4), SpikeNeRF achieves the lowest BRISQUE (35.94) and NIQE (6.09), beating the event-camera EventNeRF [29] by a wide margin (83.98/12.71) and the standard-camera Image+NeRF [22] baseline (52.07/9.06), demonstrating that the spike camera's high temporal resolution yields sharper novel views than both event and conventional frame-based sensors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the NeRF line of work (Mildenhall et al.) to a fundamentally different input modality: continuous binary spike streams from neuromorphic spike cameras, rather than RGB images or event streams. It departs from prior event-camera NeRF efforts (Ev-NeRF, EventNeRF) by exploiting the spike camera's integrate-and-fire mechanism and higher temporal resolution to preserve texture detail that event data inherently lacks. The method repurposes spiking neuron models (integrate-and-fire layers) from the spike-camera image-reconstruction literature (Zhu et al. TFI/TFP, Zhao et al. Spk2imgNet) not as a preprocessing stage but as a differentiable loss-computation module embedded directly within the NeRF training loop, coupled with BPTT and surrogate gradients for backpropagation through the non-differentiable firing threshold.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that a dense volumetric 3D representation can be learned purely from noisy, binary spike streams without any intermediate image reconstruction, opening a path for 3D scene understanding in high-speed and low-light regimes where conventional cameras fail. The long-term spike-rate loss with threshold-variation simulation provides a principled way to handle the non-idealities of real spike hardware, and the multi-view consistency argument offers a general recipe for self-supervision from inherently noisy neuromorphic sensors. Key limitations include the reliance on no-reference quality metrics for real-world evaluation (no ground-truth novel views), only five real scenes, and 20-hour training times; open questions include scalability to dynamic scenes, extension to other neuromorphic modalities, and whether the approach can operate in a single forward pass rather than iterative optimization.
