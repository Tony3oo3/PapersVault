## Problem

Pixelwise (conditional) calibration of segmentation models via inductive conformal prediction treats each image as a single calibration sample, requiring large calibration sets that are often unavailable in data-scarce domains like medical imaging. Marginal (imagewise) calibration is data-efficient but provides no per-pixel guarantees. The paper asks whether the spatial structure of natural images can be exploited to achieve pixel-level calibration with far fewer calibration images.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242648/cropped_page4_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained U-Net segmentation model (53.5M parameters) and a labeled calibration set of images (320×240 RGB for MS-COCO, 384×384 CT slices for Medical Decathlon). It produces, for each test pixel, a prediction set C_α whose threshold is drawn from the non-conformity curve of the spatial cluster containing that pixel, rather than from a single global curve (imagewise) or a per-pixel curve (pixelwise). The clustering itself is a one-time calibration step that maps every pixel coordinate to a cluster ID.

## Method

Kandinsky calibration is an intermediate between marginal and conditional conformal calibration: it first computes noisy per-pixel non-conformity curves, then clusters spatially nearby pixels with similar curves, and finally pools all non-conformity scores within each cluster to obtain a more stable cluster-level curve used for prediction sets. Three clustering strategies are proposed: (1) k-means on a fixed set of quantile values of the per-pixel curves, (2) GenAnn, a differential-evolution genetic algorithm that optimizes the center and radii of concentric annuli to minimize within-cluster variance, and (3) FCC, which parameterizes annular boundaries as finite Fourier series and optimizes the coefficients via BFGS with a nesting regularization. The key insight is that spatially proximate pixels share similar non-conformity behavior (e.g., objects are more centered, borders are harder), so pooling scores across a cluster reduces variance in the estimated quantiles without sacrificing the conditional validity guarantee that pixelwise calibration would require.

## Results

The paper evaluates calibration quality via mean coverage error (CE₂₀) across four experimental settings. In the low-data MS-COCO-S setting (100 calibration images), FCC-ANN achieves the lowest coverage error of 0.060, followed by GenAnn (0.068) and K-Means (0.086), while pixelwise calibration degrades to 0.131 and imagewise to 0.091. In the low-data Decathlon-S setting (27 patients), GenAnn achieves the best coverage error of 0.152, compared to 0.249 for pixelwise and 0.175 for imagewise calibration. In the high-data MS-COCO-XL setting (20,000 images), pixelwise calibration is best (0.055) with Kandinsky methods close behind (0.060–0.067), confirming that the advantage of Kandinsky calibration is most pronounced when calibration data are scarce.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three Kandinsky clustering methods (K-Means, GenAnn, FCC) against two standard calibration baselines: pixelwise (conditional) calibration and imagewise (marginal) calibration. In all three low-data settings (MS-COCO-S, Decathlon-L, Decathlon-S), every Kandinsky method outperforms both baselines. The advantage is most dramatic in Decathlon-S, where GenAnn reduces coverage error from 0.249 (pixelwise) to 0.152, a 39% relative improvement, and from 0.175 (imagewise) to 0.152, a 13% improvement. In MS-COCO-S, FCC-ANN achieves 0.060 versus 0.131 for pixelwise (a 54% reduction) and 0.091 for imagewise (a 34% reduction). Among the Kandinsky methods themselves, GenAnn and FCC (which incorporate spatial priors via annuli) consistently outperform K-Means in low-data regimes, while K-Means performs best in Decathlon-L where the spatial structure is more amenable to data-driven clustering. In the high-data MS-COCO-XL setting, pixelwise calibration (0.055) slightly edges out all Kandinsky methods (0.060–0.067), confirming that the data-efficiency gain of Kandinsky calibration diminishes when calibration data are abundant.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the inductive conformal prediction framework (Papadopoulos 2008; Vovk 2012) for model calibration, applying it to the specific setting of image segmentation where the model is a collection of per-pixel classifiers. It positions the proposed Kandinsky method as an intermediate between two existing extremes—marginal (imagewise) calibration, which pools all pixels into one calibration set, and conditional (pixelwise) calibration, which treats each image as a single sample—by clustering spatially similar pixels to share non-conformity scores. The clustering strategies draw on k-means, differential evolution (genetic algorithms), and Fourier-based concentric decomposition, with the geometric prior of concentric annuli motivated by the spatial structure of natural and medical images. The work is most closely related to class-conditional conformal prediction [12] and prior calibration analyses [8, 16], but departs by exploiting spatial correlations specific to segmentation rather than class-conditional structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical relevance is strongest in medical imaging, where annotated calibration data is expensive and scarce; the demonstrated coverage-error reductions (e.g., ~50% lower than pixelwise on MS-COCO-S) could translate into more reliable clinical decision support. A key limitation is that the cluster geometry (concentric circles/ellipses) must be chosen by hand based on task-specific prior knowledge, which restricts generalizability to tasks where such spatial structure exists and is known. Open questions include whether cluster shapes can be learned automatically, whether other non-conformity scoring functions improve performance, and whether the approach extends to 3D volumetric segmentation or multi-class settings without per-class manual geometry specification.
