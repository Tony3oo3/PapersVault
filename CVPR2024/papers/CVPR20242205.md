## Problem

Collecting high-quality demonstrations for dexterous manipulation is expensive and labor-intensive, and the prevailing assumption is that real-world demos are the gold standard for real-world tasks. Existing sim2real approaches either train policies exclusively in simulation or rely on RL with domain randomization requiring millions of interaction samples. This paper challenges the assumption by asking whether simulated human demos, augmented extensively in a physics simulator, can actually outperform equivalent real-world demonstrations when transferred to a real dexterous robot.

## Input / Output

The policy consumes a single RGB image and robot proprioception (joint positions) at 30 Hz, and outputs action chunks—sequences of 22-dimensional actions comprising a 6D Cartesian velocity for the XArm6 end effector and 16D finger joint position targets for the Allegro hand—via ACT. Teleoperation data is collected using a RealSense camera for vision-based hand tracking, requiring no additional sensors. The fine-tuning stage uses only 3 minutes of real-world trajectories per task, while the simulation dataset is augmented to hundreds of trajectories.

## Method

CyberDemo collects a modest set of human teleoperation demos in the SAPIEN simulator, then augments them along four trajectory-level dimensions: random camera views (re-rendering replayed states from new viewpoints), random lighting and object textures, diverse object geometries (perturbing original actions with Gaussian noise until success), and random object poses via a novel Sensitivity-Aware Kinematics Augmentation that computes per-segment robustness to action noise and distributes pose corrections proportionally across the trajectory. Training follows an Automatic Curriculum Learning schedule with four levels of increasing augmentation complexity, advancing only when the policy's success rate exceeds a threshold, and generating additional augmented data on-demand when it does not. The policy architecture is ACT (Action Chunking with Transformers), augmented with Action Aggregation to merge small-motion steps and filter teleoperation noise. Finally, the model is fine-tuned on 3 minutes of real-world demos to close the remaining controller-level sim2real gap, particularly critical for non-quasi-static tasks like valve rotation.

## Results

On the real robot, CyberDemo achieves an average 31.67% success-rate boost over all baselines in the in-domain setting (Level 1) across four dexterous manipulation tasks. For quasi-static pick-and-place tasks, CyberDemo is 35% higher than baselines; for the non-quasi-static rotate task, it is 20% higher. Under out-of-domain conditions (random lighting, displaced objects, or both), baselines frequently drop to 0/20 successes while CyberDemo maintains 5–13/20, demonstrating robustness to visual and positional perturbations. In the most challenging generalization test (rotating novel tetra/penta-valves under random light and new positions), CyberDemo achieves 30% success versus at most 2.5% for any baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CyberDemo is compared against three vision-pretraining baselines—R3M (time-contrastive + video-language alignment on Ego4D), PVR (MoCo-v2 on ImageNet), and MVP (Masked Autoencoder on human interaction video)—all fine-tuned on the same 3-minute real-world demonstration set. CyberDemo wins on every task and every difficulty level; the margin is largest in out-of-domain settings where baselines collapse (e.g., R3M scores 0/20 on Pick-and-Place Bottle at Levels 2–4, while CyberDemo scores 5–8/20). In the in-domain (Level 1) setting, CyberDemo leads by 31.67% on average across tasks. For the rotate task specifically, CyberDemo achieves 15/20 in-domain versus 11/20 (R3M), 8/20 (PVR), and 8/20 (MVP), and extends to 17/20 under random light (Level 3) where the best baseline (MVP) reaches only 10/20. In the novel-object generalization test (tetra/penta-valve rotation), CyberDemo attains 30% success while the best baseline manages only 2.5% by chance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on ACT (Action Chunking with Transformers) as its policy backbone but fundamentally repositions the data collection paradigm: rather than treating real-world teleoperation demonstrations as the gold standard for imitation learning, it argues that simulated human demos augmented via a physics simulator can surpass them. It extends domain randomization—previously a reinforcement learning technique requiring millions of interaction samples—into the imitation learning setting by applying it to a small set of human trajectories replayed in simulation. The Sensitivity-Aware Kinematics Augmentation for object pose randomization is a novel trajectory-level technique that departs from both image-level augmentation (color jitter, cropping) and the simple pose-reset strategies common in RL, distributing pose corrections across trajectory segments weighted by their robustness to action noise.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a deeply held assumption in robotic manipulation that in-domain real-world demonstrations are the most effective training signal, showing that simulation-based augmentation can yield policies that are more robust to lighting, object geometry, and initial pose variations than policies trained on equivalent real data. The generalization to novel objects (rotating tetra- and penta-valves when only tri-valve demos were collected) suggests a path toward reducing the data collection burden for dexterous manipulation. Key limitations include the need to build a task-specific simulated environment and the evaluation being limited to three tasks with 20 trials per condition; open questions include whether the approach scales to longer-horizon or multi-object tasks and whether the sim2real gap narrows further with more sophisticated simulators.
