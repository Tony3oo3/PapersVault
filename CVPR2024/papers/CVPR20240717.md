## Problem

Neural field training requires extensive SGD iterations to overfit a single signal, making practical deployment costly. Prior work has focused on architectural modifications (Fourier features, sinusoidal activations) or meta-learned initializations to counteract spectral bias, but has not explored whether transforming the data itself could make the optimization bias work in favor of faster convergence.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240717/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 2D grayscale image (512×512, converted from sRGB to Linear RGB) as input and produces a trained neural field (SIREN or Instant-NGP) that approximates the image. The image is first passed through a data transformation (e.g., random pixel permutation), the neural field is trained on the transformed signal via full-batch gradient descent, and the inverse transformation is applied at inference to recover the original signal. Training cost is measured as the number of SGD steps to reach PSNR 50 dB, with per-image learning rate tuning.

## Method

The paper systematically evaluates seven elementary data transformations—random pixel permutation (RPP), zigzag permutation, inversion, standardization, linear scaling, centering, and gamma correction—across two neural field architectures (SIREN with sinusoidal activations and Instant-NGP with multi-resolution hash encoding) and three image datasets. RPP is the only transformation that consistently accelerates training (1.08×–1.50×) across all setups, despite increasing the image's high-frequency content, which spectral bias theory would predict to be detrimental. The authors explain this via a 'blessings of no pattern' hypothesis: RPP removes smooth, easy-to-fit patterns that dominate early training but create structured error artifacts (sinusoidal wavy patterns in SIREN, axis-aligned blocks in Instant-NGP) that impede reaching high fidelity. Loss landscape analysis reveals that RPP training, after a slow start, discovers a 'linear expressway' connecting moderate-PSNR to high-PSNR parameters with a low loss barrier (28.3 dB vs. 15.3 dB for original), while error variance across pixels is up to 5.8× lower for RPP-trained models, indicating more uniform fitting.

## Results

Random pixel permutation (RPP) provides a consistent 1.08×–1.50× speedup in SGD steps to reach PSNR 50 dB across all six architecture–dataset combinations (SIREN and Instant-NGP on Kodak, DIV2K, CLIC). Zigzag permutation achieves dramatic 17×–22× speedups on SIREN but slows down Instant-NGP (0.78×–0.86×), while inversion consistently degrades training (0.07×–0.72×). On average, the original data requires ~30% more SGD steps than RPP-transformed data to reach the same fidelity level.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but instead benchmarks seven data transformations against the identity (original) transformation as the baseline. RPP is the only transformation that accelerates training in every single experimental setup (all 6 architecture–dataset pairs), making it the most robust choice. Zigzag permutation is the second-best on SIREN (17×–22× speedup) due to its low-frequency structure aligning with SIREN's sinusoidal bias, but it actually slows down Instant-NGP (0.78×–0.86×), likely because Instant-NGP's spatial grid encoding already imposes an axis-aligned inductive bias. Inversion is the worst performer, consistently slowing training by 28%–93%, suggesting that the natural brightness distribution of images is already well-suited for optimization. Standardization and linear scaling show architecture-dependent effects: they speed up SIREN (1.36×–2.36×) but slow down Instant-NGP (0.45×–0.61×), indicating that the benefit of normalizing intensity ranges is architecture-specific.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant strategy of fighting spectral bias through architectural priors (Fourier features, sinusoidal activations, multi-resolution hash encodings, meta-learned initializations) by asking whether the optimization bias can be exploited via data-space transformations instead. It connects conceptually to the random-label memorization literature (Arpit et al., Maennel et al.), but applies the insight to the overfitting-specific context of neural fields without any fine-tuning step. The work is complementary to encoding-based acceleration methods (positional encodings, spatial grids, tree structures) and positions data transformation as an orthogonal, additive lever for reducing training cost.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 'blessings of no pattern' hypothesis offers a concrete, testable explanation for why removing easy-to-fit structure can paradoxically speed up convergence, supported by loss-landscape visualization (linear expressway) and error-pattern analysis. However, the practical speedup is modest (1.08×–1.50×) and RPP destroys spatial structure, limiting direct applicability to tasks requiring interpolation or generalization. The most impactful open question is whether one can design transformations that retain enough spatial coherence for generalization while still removing the specific easy patterns that slow late-stage optimization—a balance the authors identify but do not resolve.
