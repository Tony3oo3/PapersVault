## Problem

Blind image restoration without knowledge of the degradation process remains challenging: supervised methods generalize poorly to unseen degradation types, while model-based approaches require the degradation to be known at inference. This paper asks whether a pre-trained diffusion model can be directly repurposed for restoration without any degradation assumption or paired training data, while still preserving the identity and content of the input image.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240284/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single degraded RGB image (e.g., 256×256 face, dog, or cat image with arbitrary real-world degradation such as noise, motion blur, or low resolution) and optionally a personal album of ~20 clean images of the same subject. The degraded image is first corrupted with Gaussian noise at a chosen timestep K before being fed into the diffusion model; the output is a single restored high-quality image sampled from the constrained generative space. No degradation model, paired data, or explicit conditioning signal is required at inference.

## Method

The core insight is that adding sufficient Gaussian noise to a degraded image y₀ makes it statistically indistinguishable from a clean image x₀ at the same noise level (y_K ≈ x_K), so a pre-trained DDPM can denoise y_K directly. To prevent the unconstrained generative space from producing outputs that drift away from the input identity, the diffusion model is fine-tuned on a small set of anchor images that constrain the posterior p(x₀|y_K) to a narrow subspace. For single-image restoration, these anchors form a 'generative album' of 16 images produced by denoising y_K with periodic L₁ skip guidance (applied every n steps rather than every step, preserving generation quality while loosely following the input). At inference, the degraded image is noised to timestep K and denoised for K steps with the fine-tuned model, with no further guidance. For personalized restoration, the generative album is replaced by a real personal album, enabling recovery of high-frequency details absent in the degraded observation.

## Results

On four real-world blind face restoration benchmarks, the method achieves the best FID and MUSIQ scores across all datasets without any supervised training on degradation pairs. On Wider-Test, it reaches FID 46.38 and MUSIQ 58.73, outperforming the strongest baseline CodeFormer (FID 48.57, MUSIQ 55.70). The advantage is most pronounced on the out-of-distribution Deblur-Test (real motion blur), where the method achieves FID 135.33 versus 163.47 for CodeFormer, since supervised baselines were never trained on motion blur. For personalized restoration, identity scores (ArcFace cosine similarity) reach 0.731, 0.716, and 0.664 on three subjects, substantially exceeding all single-image and exemplar-based baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method outperforms all five baselines on every metric across all four datasets. Against CodeFormer (the strongest supervised baseline), it improves FID by 2.2 on Wider-Test, 2.1 on WebPhoto-Test, 10.0 on LFW-Test, and 28.1 on Deblur-Test, while also achieving higher MUSIQ in every case. The gap widens dramatically on Deblur-Test (real motion blur), where all supervised methods (GFPGAN, CodeFormer, VQFR, DR2) degrade to FIDs of 163–178 because their synthetic training pipelines do not model motion blur, whereas the degradation-agnostic approach maintains FID 135.33. DR2(+VQFR), which combines a diffusion model with a supervised face restoration network, consistently performs worst among the baselines (e.g., FID 143.96 on WebPhoto-Test), suggesting that coupling a diffusion model with a task-specific supervised network is less effective than the proposed pure generation-by-denoising strategy. For personalized restoration (Table 2), the method surpasses ASFFNet (the only other exemplar-based method) by 0.037–0.142 in ArcFace identity score, and exceeds single-image methods (CodeFormer, VQFR, DR2) by 0.09–0.33, confirming that constraining the generative space with a personal album recovers high-frequency identity details that single-image approaches lose.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes blind image restoration as a generation problem by building on the DDPM forward/reverse process (Ho et al., 2020) and adapting the guidance mechanism from diffusion posterior sampling (Chung et al., 2022) into a periodic 'skip guidance' for constructing a generative album. It departs fundamentally from both supervised restoration methods (GFPGAN, CodeFormer, VQFR, DR2), which require paired training data and model specific degradations, and model-based optimization methods (Kawar et al., Chung et al.), which assume the degradation operator is known at inference. The key conceptual departure is that no degradation model or paired data is needed at all: the degraded image is simply noised to a level where it becomes indistinguishable from a clean image, and the pre-trained diffusion model's reverse process performs the restoration, with a fine-tuned generative space (via anchor images) ensuring identity preservation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The degradation-agnostic nature of the approach is practically significant: it generalizes to real-world images with unknown, mixed, or out-of-distribution degradations (e.g., motion blur) where supervised methods fail, as demonstrated on the Deblur-Test set. The personalized restoration application—using a personal album to recover high-frequency details like wrinkles and fur that are absent in the degraded input—addresses a real user need that prior single-image methods cannot. However, the per-image fine-tuning requirement makes the method slow compared to feed-forward baselines, and evaluation is limited to class-specific domains (faces, dogs, cats) due to the absence of a strong general-purpose diffusion model. Open questions include whether the generative space can be constrained without fine-tuning (e.g., via adapter layers or prompt-based methods) and whether the approach transfers to a diverse, general-purpose generative space.
