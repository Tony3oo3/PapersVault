## Problem

Existing video anomaly detection methods are confined to closed-set anomaly categories and cannot handle unseen anomalies in open-world deployment. Recent open-set VAD work can detect but not categorize anomalies, leaving detection results ambiguous. This paper introduces open-vocabulary VAD (OVVAD), the first formulation that requires both detecting and categorizing seen and unseen anomaly categories under weak supervision.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241025/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a video clip as input, sampling 1 out of every 16 frames (up to 256 frames during training) and encoding each frame with a frozen CLIP ViT-B/16 image encoder. It produces two outputs: frame-level anomaly confidence scores (via a binary detector) and a video-level anomaly category label (via cross-modal alignment between aggregated video features and text embeddings of category names). During fine-tuning, the model also consumes LLM-generated text prompts and AIGC-generated pseudo-anomaly videos as auxiliary training signals.

## Method

The architecture disentangles OVVAD into class-agnostic detection and class-specific categorization, jointly optimized. For detection, a nearly weight-free Temporal Adapter (graph convolution with a distance-based adjacency matrix) captures temporal dependencies without the overfitting risk of parameter-heavy temporal transformers, and a Semantic Knowledge Injection module retrieves scene/action words from LLMs (ChatGPT), encodes them via CLIP's text encoder, and fuses them into visual features through sigmoid-weighted cross-modal attention. For categorization, video-level features are aggregated via soft attention over frame confidences and matched against learnable-prompted text embeddings (CoOp-style). A Novel Anomaly Synthesis module uses LLMs to generate scene descriptions and AIGC models (DALL-E, Gen-2) to produce pseudo novel anomaly videos, which are inserted into normal clips to provide frame-level supervision during a fine-tuning stage that improves generalization to unseen categories.

## Results

On UCF-Crime, the proposed method achieves 86.40% AUC for frame-level detection (93.80% on base categories, 88.20% on novel categories), outperforming the best open-set baseline DMU by 1.26%. On XD-Violence, it reaches 66.53% AP (57.10% base, 76.03% novel), surpassing DMU by 2.63% AP. On UBnormal, it attains 62.94% AUC, exceeding RTFM by 2.00%. Notably, the method's novel-category performance (88.20% AUC on UCF-Crime, 76.03% AP on XD-Violence) substantially exceeds all prior open-set approaches, demonstrating strong generalization to unseen anomaly types.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the fair open-set setting (trained only on base anomalies + normal), the proposed method outperforms all prior open-set VAD baselines across all three benchmarks. On UCF-Crime, it beats DMU [62] (85.14% AUC) by 1.26% and RTFM [42] (84.47%) by 1.93%, while the first open-set VAD work Zhu et al. [67] lags significantly at 78.82%. On XD-Violence, the gap widens: the method achieves 66.53% AP versus DMU's 63.90% and Zhu et al.'s 64.40%, with a particularly large margin on novel categories (76.03% vs. 71.63% for DMU). On UBnormal, it leads RTFM [42] (60.94%) and DMU [62] (59.91%) by 2.00% and 3.03% AUC respectively. Remarkably, the method's overall UCF-Crime AUC (86.40%) is within 1.18% of the best full-training-set baseline (CLIP-TSA† at 87.58%), despite never seeing novel categories during training, underscoring the effectiveness of the NAS module and semantic knowledge injection for open-vocabulary generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the open-set video anomaly detection line (Zhu et al. 2022, Ding et al. 2022) by adding the requirement to not only detect but also categorize anomalies, both seen and unseen, which prior open-set VAD works explicitly overlook. It builds on CLIP's cross-modal alignment for zero-shot generalization, borrows the CoOp prompting strategy for category embeddings, and introduces LLM-generated semantic knowledge (SKI) and AIGC-generated pseudo-anomalies (NAS) as novel augmentation mechanisms. The nearly weight-free temporal adapter departs from the common practice of adding parameter-heavy temporal transformers, motivated by the observation that extra parameters harm generalization to novel categories.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

OVVAD is a practically critical capability for video surveillance systems that must identify not just whether something is anomalous but what specific type of anomaly it is, yet the paper's categorization accuracy remains low (41–64% TOP-1, dropping to 30–37% for novel categories), limiting immediate deployment. The detection results are strong—matching or exceeding full-training-set methods without ever seeing novel anomalies—but the gap between detection and categorization performance suggests the task is far from solved. Open questions include whether better AIGC video generation (rather than image animation) could close the categorization gap, and whether the LLM-derived semantic knowledge generalizes beyond the specific anomaly vocabularies tested.
