## Problem

Most generative 3D approaches are object-centric, making it non-trivial to edit existing photorealistic NeRF scenes or insert new objects at controlled spatial locations. Prior methods like Instruct-NeRF2NeRF and DreamEditor rely on complex iterative pipelines with intertwined diffusion and NeRF updates, lack fine-grained positional control, and cannot reliably generate new objects at user-specified positions within a scene.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240895/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained NeRF scene (30–300 input images), a text prompt, and a spatial constraint (either an axis-aligned bounding box for region selection or an arbitrary proxy mesh for object insertion). Rendered RGB, depth, and inpainting mask images from the NeRF are arranged into a 2D grid (reference sheet) that is downscaled to fit the diffusion model's memory and attention limits. The output is a fine-tuned NeRF representing the edited scene, with the reference sheet serving as an immediate visual preview before committing to the full image-set generation.

## Method

The key insight is that depth-conditioned ControlNet inherently produces multi-view consistent images when applied to a grid of views in a single diffusion pass, eliminating the need for iterative NeRF–diffusion update loops. The pipeline operates in two stages: first, a small set of reference cameras (M << N) around the edit region is rendered from the original NeRF, arranged into a grid with one intentionally empty slot, and processed through ControlNet with depth conditioning and an inpainting mask to produce the reference sheet; second, each target view's rendered image, depth, and mask are placed into the empty slot while the remaining reference sheet tiles stay fixed, propagating the edit consistently across all N views. Spatial control is enforced through two selection modes—shape selection (bounding-box depth comparison) and proxy selection (mesh depth compositing)—which generate per-camera depth conditions and inpainting masks. The edited image set is then used to fine-tune the original NeRF in one pass, with an optional second iteration for improved 3D consistency on complex edits.

## Results

Across 10 scenes, SIGNeRF achieves a CLIP text-to-image directional similarity of 0.23 (image) and 0.2125 (NeRF render), outperforming Instruct-NeRF2NeRF (0.1603 / 0.1600) and DreamEditor (0.1849 NeRF-only). For background preservation, SIGNeRF reaches PSNR 32.22 and SSIM 0.83, compared to Instruct-NeRF2NeRF's 30.09 / 0.64, thanks to its masked update strategy. The method also runs in roughly half the time of the baselines on a single GPU and provides an immediate reference-sheet preview before committing to full image-set generation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SIGNeRF outperforms both baselines on every reported metric. Against Instruct-NeRF2NeRF, it improves NeRF CLIP directional similarity from 0.1600 to 0.2125 (+33%) and background preservation from PSNR 30.09 / SSIM 0.64 to 32.22 / 0.83, while also producing more consistent multi-view geometry (e.g., a coherent bear snout vs. the Janus effect in Instruct-NeRF2NeRF). Against DreamEditor, SIGNeRF raises NeRF CLIP similarity from 0.1849 to 0.2125 and generates more detailed, less over-smoothed objects, since DreamEditor's reliance on score-distillation sampling with high classifier-free guidance tends to simplify geometry. Qualitatively, Instruct-NeRF2NeRF fails entirely to insert new objects at user-specified positions (Fig. 7), whereas SIGNeRF embeds them consistently via proxy-mesh or bounding-box selection. In terms of speed, SIGNeRF completes a single-iteration edit in roughly half the time of the other two methods on one GPU, and its image-set update step is embarrassingly parallel, unlike the sequential NeRF–diffusion loops of the baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

SIGNeRF builds on the NeRF representation and depth-conditioned ControlNet diffusion, but departs sharply from the iterative NeRF/diffusion update loops used by Instruct-NeRF2NeRF and DreamEditor. Its core conceptual contribution is the observation that ControlNet, when applied to a grid of views in a single pass, inherently produces multi-view consistent outputs, enabling a two-step reference-sheet-then-propagate strategy that replaces repeated optimization cycles. The method also extends the editing task to controlled object generation via proxy mesh placement, going beyond the text-only semantic control of prior generative NeRF editors like DreamEditor and Instruct-NeRF2NeRF.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The reference-sheet grid trick offers a practical, low-overhead mechanism for multi-view consistency that could be adopted by other diffusion-based 3D pipelines, and the preview capability (inspecting the reference sheet before committing to full generation) is a meaningful usability improvement over black-box iterative methods. However, the approach is constrained by the need to downscale images to fit the grid, by a bias toward center-positioned objects, and by its unsuitability for large-scale background modifications, leaving open the question of whether grid-based consistency can be generalized to arbitrary scene extents. The method's reliance on a single ControlNet pass also means it inherits the quality ceiling of the underlying diffusion model.
