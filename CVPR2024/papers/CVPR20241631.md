## Problem

Generalized Category Discovery (GCD) requires clustering a partially labeled image collection into an unknown number of classes, yet prior methods typically exploit the ground-truth class count K for both model selection during training and final clustering, making them impractical for real-world deployment. Additionally, most existing approaches rely on K-means or pseudo-labeling pipelines that are computationally expensive or introduce extra trainable modules. This paper asks whether a classic non-parametric mode-seeking technique (mean shift) can be integrated into contrastive representation learning to achieve strong clustering without ever accessing the true K.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a collection of RGB images split into a labeled-known subset (80% on CIFAR-100, 50% elsewhere) and an unlabeled set that may contain both known and unknown classes. A pretrained DINO ViT-B/16 encoder (with a 768-dim projection head) produces l2-normalized embeddings; kNN retrieval (k=8) is performed over the full training set, which is re-extracted and detached each epoch. The output is a per-image cluster assignment obtained via agglomerative clustering (ward linkage) on iteratively mean-shifted embeddings, together with an estimated cluster count K selected by maximizing labeled-image accuracy on the validation set during training.

## Method

CMS learning performs a single-step mean shift on each image embedding by aggregating its k nearest neighbors (k=8) with a simple weight schedule (1−α for the query, α/k for each neighbor, α=0.5), then l2-normalizes the result to keep it on the unit hypersphere. The contrastive objective (InfoNCE-style) pulls the mean-shifted embeddings of an image and its augmented version together while pushing apart mean-shifted embeddings of other images in the batch; a supervised contrastive loss on the labeled subset is added with weight λ. At inference, the mean-shift step is iterated multiple times (retrieving kNNs from the currently updated embedding space each round) until labeled validation accuracy plateaus, after which agglomerative clustering with ward linkage assigns final clusters. The number of clusters K is jointly estimated during training by running agglomerative clustering on the small validation set across a range of K values and selecting the one with the best labeled accuracy, avoiding any post-hoc K estimation or ground-truth K access. No extra trainable modules are introduced—only the last encoder block and projection heads (≈6.3 M parameters) are fine-tuned.

## Results

On the GCD benchmark with the ground-truth number of classes K given, CMS achieves state-of-the-art 'All' accuracy on 5 of 6 datasets: 82.3% on CIFAR-100, 84.7% on ImageNet-100, 68.2% on CUB-200, 56.9% on Stanford Cars, and 56.0% on FGVC-Aircraft. Without access to GT K (estimated jointly during training), the method still achieves 79.6% on CIFAR-100, 81.3% on ImageNet-100, 64.4% on CUB, and 55.2% on FGVC-Aircraft, outperforming methods that do exploit GT K on CUB and FGVC-Aircraft. The only dataset where CMS does not achieve the best 'All' accuracy is Herbarium19 (36.4% vs. SimGCD's 44.0%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CMS outperforms all 10 baselines on 5 of 6 datasets in the standard GCD setup with GT K. The largest margins are on CUB-200 (+4.7% over DCCL, the next best) and Stanford Cars (+3.1% over SimGCD), while on CIFAR-100 and ImageNet-100 it edges out PromptCAL by 1.1% and 1.6% respectively. On Herbarium19, CMS (36.4%) trails SimGCD (44.0%) and PIM (42.3%), the only clear losses. In the more practical setting without GT K (Table 1b), CMS still leads on most datasets and notably surpasses methods that do use GT K on CUB (64.4% vs. PIM's 62.0%) and FGVC-Aircraft (55.2% vs. GPC's 43.3%), demonstrating that the agglomerative-based K estimation during training is effective. The training-free Agglomerative+DINO baseline is substantially weaker (e.g., 37.0% on CUB vs. 68.2%), confirming that the contrastive mean-shift learning objective is the primary source of gain rather than the backbone alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper revisits the classic mean-shift algorithm (Comaniciu & Meer, 2002) and integrates it into a contrastive representation learning framework for Generalized Category Discovery (GCD), a task formalized by Vaze et al. (2022). Prior GCD methods (GCD, DCCL, PromptCAL, SimGCD, PIM, GPC) predominantly rely on K-means clustering at inference and exploit the ground-truth number of classes K for model selection or classifier design, often in a two-stage train-then-estimate pipeline. CMS departs from this by using a single-step kNN-based mean shift during training to shape embeddings, iterative mean shift at inference for clustering, and agglomerative clustering to jointly estimate K during training—introducing zero extra trainable modules beyond the pre-trained DINO ViT backbone and its projection head.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in eliminating the need for ground-truth K throughout both training and evaluation, which prior GCD work routinely exploits but is unavailable in real deployment. The finding that a non-parametric, 20-year-old mode-seeking algorithm, when paired with modern contrastive learning, matches or exceeds SOTA methods that use heavier machinery (teacher-student frameworks, pseudo-labeling, parametric classifiers) suggests that representation quality via neighborhood aggregation may matter more than architectural complexity for clustering-oriented tasks. Limitations include the kNN approximation violating the continuity and radial-symmetry assumptions required for strict mean-shift convergence, and the K estimation still shows non-trivial error on some datasets (e.g., 20.4% error on Stanford Cars). Open questions include whether the approach scales to very large datasets where kNN retrieval becomes costly, and whether the single-step mean shift during training could be replaced by multi-step shifts for further gains.
