## Problem

Existing NeRF-based reflection methods rely on spatially invariant directional encodings (Fourier features, spherical harmonics) that assume distant, uniform lighting. Under near-field indoor lighting where the environment map varies spatially, these encodings produce noisy geometry, 'faked' reflections placed behind surfaces, and poor view interpolation. The paper addresses this gap by introducing a spatially varying encoding mechanism tailored to near-field specular modeling.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240279/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-view RGB images (downsampled to 854×1280) with calibrated camera poses, plus monocular normal estimates from OmniData used only as early-training supervision (first 4K iterations). Beyond the final novel-view rendering, the pipeline outputs a physically meaningful decomposition: diffuse color, specular color, specular tint, surface roughness, and surface normals. Notably, the specular component is evaluated once per ray at the volumetric depth surface rather than at every sample point, reducing computation while reflecting that reflections occur only at the surface.

## Method

The core contribution is a 3D Gaussian directional encoding that embeds a 5D ray space (origin and direction) using a set of 256 learnable 3D Gaussians, each parameterized by position, anisotropic scale, and quaternion rotation. For each ray, the encoding computes the maximum Gaussian value along the ray via a closed-form solution, producing spatially varying features that naturally mimic how preconvolved incident light changes with position under near-field lighting. Roughness is modeled by scaling each Gaussian's extent, enabling on-the-fly glossiness editing. Training proceeds in two stages: first, the Gaussians and specular decoder are initialized by fitting a pseudo incident light field constructed from Gaussian-blurred image pyramids; then they are jointly fine-tuned with the NeRF. A monocular normal loss (OmniData) is applied only during the first 4K iterations to bootstrap geometry and resolve the shape-radiance ambiguity before being removed.

## Results

On the Eyeful Tower dataset (near-field indoor lighting), SpecNeRF achieves 32.58 PSNR, 0.9328 SSIM, and 0.1445 LPIPS, outperforming all baselines by 0.7–0.9 dB in PSNR. On the NISR+Inria dataset, it reaches 30.77 PSNR and 0.8909 SSIM, marginally ahead of Ref-NeRF (30.65 PSNR). On the far-field Shiny dataset, SpecNeRF scores 26.56 PSNR and 0.7277 SSIM, again leading all baselines, demonstrating that the Gaussian directional encoding generalizes beyond its near-field design target.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SpecNeRF outperforms all three baselines on every metric across all three datasets. The largest margin is on the Eyeful Tower dataset (near-field lighting), where it gains +0.73 dB PSNR over NeRF, +0.93 dB over Ref-NeRF, and +0.87 dB over MS-NeRF, with a notable LPIPS improvement (0.1445 vs. 0.1561–0.1626). On NISR+Inria, the advantage is smaller (+0.02 to +0.55 dB PSNR), which the authors attribute to fewer reflective surfaces in that dataset. On the far-field Shiny dataset, SpecNeRF still leads by +0.06 to +0.10 dB PSNR, confirming that the Gaussian encoding can approximate spatially invariant behavior by placing Gaussians far away. Qualitatively, the paper notes that baselines resort to 'faking' reflections by placing emitters behind surfaces, producing incorrect normals and foggy geometry, whereas SpecNeRF models specular highlights directly on the surface.

## Relation to Prior Work

SpecNeRF extends the Ref-NeRF framework, which introduced structured diffuse/specular decomposition and integrated directional encoding for NeRFs, by replacing the spatially invariant directional encoding (Fourier or spherical harmonics) with a learnable 3D Gaussian directional encoding that captures spatially-varying near-field lighting. The volumetric rendering backbone draws on NeRF, mip-NeRF 360 (density proposal networks, distortion loss), and VR-NeRF (LOD-aware hash encoding). A key conceptual departure is treating the ray origin as part of the encoding space (5D ray space) rather than encoding only the 2D reflection direction, which introduces an inductive bias toward near-field lighting that prior object-level methods under distant environment-map assumptions could not capture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Gaussian directional encoding offers a practical, drop-in replacement for existing directional encodings in NeRF pipelines, enabling more physically meaningful specular/diffuse decomposition in room-scale indoor scenes — a setting where prior methods produce 'faked' reflections and noisy geometry. However, the method is limited to relatively low-frequency specular reflections (it cannot reconstruct mirror-like reflections due to the finite number of Gaussians), and the per-pixel traversal of all Gaussians introduces non-trivial computational cost. Open questions include whether more efficient traversal (e.g., rasterization or spatial indexing) could scale the approach to higher frequencies, and whether the monocular normal bootstrapping strategy generalizes beyond the specific OmniData model used here.
