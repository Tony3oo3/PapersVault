## Problem

Post-hoc OOD detection methods have been extensively benchmarked on cleanly labeled training data, yet real-world classifiers are routinely trained on noisy labels from crowd-sourcing, web-scraping, or imperfect annotation pipelines. This paper asks how 20 state-of-the-art OOD detectors perform when the underlying classifier is trained on unreliable labels, revealing that most methods fail to separate incorrectly classified in-distribution samples from true OOD inputs — a critical bottleneck for safe deployment that prior work has overlooked.

## Input / Output

The 20 OOD detectors operate on RGB images at varying resolutions (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 256×256 for Clothing1M, 448×448 for CUB/FGVC-Aircraft) and produce a scalar OOD score per image, while the underlying classifier simultaneously outputs a class label. Beyond the standard AUROC_ID vs. OOD metric, the paper introduces a decomposed evaluation: AUROC_correct vs. OOD and AUROC_incorrect vs. OOD, which isolates how well a detector separates genuine OOD samples from the classifier's own mistakes on ID data. Evaluation spans 7 OOD test sets (MNIST, SVHN, Textures, EuroSAT, Food-101, Stanford Online Products subset, ImageNet subset) with minimal semantic overlap to the ID data.

## Method

This is a large-scale empirical study rather than a new detector: 396 classifiers (ResNet-18, MLP-Mixer, Compact Transformer × 22 training label sets × 3 seeds × 2 checkpoints) are trained under clean, real noisy (CIFAR-N, Clothing1M), and synthetic noisy (uniform NCAR, class-conditional) label conditions, and 20 post-hoc OOD methods from the OpenOOD benchmark are applied on top. The key analytical contribution is showing that the well-known correlation between ID accuracy and OOD AUROC is largely an artifact: methods like MSP achieve high AUROC_ID vs. OOD on clean data simply because few ID samples are misclassified, but their AUROC_incorrect vs. OOD hovers near 0.5 (random), meaning they cannot distinguish the classifier's own errors from OOD inputs. Distance-based methods operating in feature space (GRAM, MDS, KNN, VIM, MDSEnsemble) are the most robust because their scores depend on feature geometry rather than the predicted class probability. The study further shows that uniform synthetic noise is a worst-case benchmark, that early stopping helps accuracy but can accelerate OOD degradation, and that for class-statistic-based methods, using the full (noisy) training set for parameter setup often outperforms a smaller clean validation set.

## Results

Across 396 trained classifiers (3 architectures × 22 datasets × 3 seeds × 2 checkpoints), the best OOD detector (GRAM) achieves 94.45% AUROC_ID vs. OOD on clean CIFAR-10 but drops to 88.82% under 40% real label noise (CIFAR-10 Worst). Even at just 9% noise (CIFAR-10 Agg), the median AUROC across all 20 methods drops by over 5 percentage points. Only 5 of 20 methods (GRAM, KNN, MDS, MDSEnsemble, VIM) reach 90%+ AUROC on at least one noisy dataset, while the widely-used MSP baseline falls to 82.48% on CIFAR-10 Worst and 66.02% on Clothing1M-Noisy. The critical finding is that AUROC_incorrect vs. OOD (separating misclassified ID samples from OOD) is near-random (~50%) for most methods, including MSP, regardless of classifier accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This empirical study benchmarks 20 post-hoc OOD detection methods rather than proposing a new one. Distance-based methods operating in feature space (GRAM, MDS, VIM, KNN, MDSEnsemble) consistently outperform softmax-based (MSP, ODIN, TempScale) and logit/energy-based (EBO, MLS, ASH, DICE, REACT) methods under label noise, with GRAM achieving the highest overall AUROC (94.45% clean CIFAR-10, 88.82% at 40% noise). The MSP baseline, the most widely adopted OOD score in the literature, degrades most severely: it drops from 91.34% to 82.48% on CIFAR-10 Worst and to 66.02% on Clothing1M-Noisy, and its AUROC_incorrect vs. OOD is near-random (~50%), meaning it cannot distinguish misclassified ID images from true OOD inputs. MDSEnsemble and GRAM, the only methods incorporating multi-depth features, show the flattest accuracy–AUROC trade-off curves, making them most robust when the underlying classifier is inaccurate. Among logit-space methods, REACT and DICE (which apply activation sparsification) are the most promising for separating ID mistakes from OOD, while KLM and GradNorm perform worst under high noise. The paper also shows that synthetic uniform noise (SU) is the most damaging label noise model for OOD detection, more so than real instance-dependent noise at the same overall rate, and that early stopping helps OOD detection for nearly all methods but can accelerate the rate of AUROC decline per unit accuracy loss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-studied areas—post-hoc OOD detection and label noise robustness—but is the first to systematically evaluate 20 OOD detection methods from the OpenOOD benchmark under noisy training labels. It builds on the CIFAR-N real-noise dataset and Clothing1M, and extends the observation (from Vaze et al. and Hendrycks et al.) that ID accuracy correlates with OOD detection performance by dissecting *why* that correlation holds and when it breaks down. The closest prior work is Pan et al. [42], who studied MC-dropout and deep ensembles under synthetic uniform noise, and Olmin & Lindsten [40], who examined calibration under label noise; this paper broadens the scope to post-hoc scoring functions, real instance-dependent noise, and multiple architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the widely cited accuracy–OOD-detection correlation is largely an artifact of methods failing to separate ID misclassifications from OOD samples (AUROC_incorrect vs OOD ≈ 0.5 for MSP) reframes a core assumption in the field and has direct implications for safe deployment where labels are inherently imperfect. Distance-based feature-space methods (GRAM, MDS, KNN, VIM) emerge as the most robust, offering practitioners a concrete design guideline. Open questions remain: whether training-based OOD methods, noise-robust learning techniques, or pre-training strategies can close the gap, and whether the findings generalize beyond the post-hoc, closed-set setting studied here.
