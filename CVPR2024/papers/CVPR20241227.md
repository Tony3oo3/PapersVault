## Problem

Night photography images suffer from the joint degradation of low luminance and motion blur, but existing methods either cascade separate enhancement and deblurring pipelines (losing information at each stage), rely on paired synthetic training data with limited real-world generalization (e.g., LEDNet), or depend on assumed degradation models that fail on unknown real-world degradations. No prior zero-shot method can simultaneously restore both brightness and sharpness without paired data or explicit degradation assumptions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241227/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single low-light blurry RGB image (256×256, matching the pre-trained unconditional ImageNet diffusion model) and produces a normal-light sharp RGB image. No paired ground truth or degradation parameters are required at inference time. A user-adjustable brightness level E (default 0.5) controls the output exposure, and for extremely dark inputs a practical exposure correction (PEC) warm-start is applied to preserve content guidance before diffusion sampling begins.

## Method

FourierDiff embeds Fourier-domain priors into a pre-trained diffusion model by exploiting the observation that luminance information concentrates in amplitudes while structure and content reside in phases. In each reverse diffusion step, the estimated clean sample x_{0|t} is FFT-decomposed, and its amplitude (carrying natural-image luminance priors) is recombined with the phase of the input image (preserving content) via IFFT to guide the next denoising step, with a learnable factor γ controlling brightness. A spatial-frequency alternating optimization strategy periodically (every N=200 steps) refines the input phase by estimating a blur kernel from the autocorrelation of the phase, solving a regularized deblurring problem, and updating the kernel using the progressively clearer diffusion samples. This creates a synergistic loop where the sampling process provides increasingly visible details for kernel estimation, while the refined phase supplies sharper structural guidance back to the sampling process, all without any paired training data or assumed degradation model.

## Results

On the LOL-Blur dataset, FourierDiff achieves the best no-reference scores across all four metrics: NIQE 3.80, PI 3.88, BRISQUE 33.13, and MUSIQ 62.46, outperforming the best cascade baseline (GRL→GDP: NIQE 4.32, PI 4.90, BRISQUE 43.25, MUSIQ 58.92) and the joint LEDNet (NIQE 3.99, PI 5.07, BRISQUE 42.59, MUSIQ 59.64). On full-reference metrics, LEDNet (trained on paired data) achieves higher PSNR (25.74 vs. 20.53) and SSIM (0.85 vs. 0.71), but FourierDiff still surpasses all cascade baselines. On the real-world RealBlur dataset, FourierDiff leads all methods on every no-reference metric (NIQE 3.25, PI 3.36, BRISQUE 26.39, MUSIQ 52.24), with a particularly large margin over LEDNet (NIQE 3.72, PI 5.03, BRISQUE 42.31, MUSIQ 49.45), confirming superior generalization without paired training data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FourierDiff outperforms all seven baseline configurations on every no-reference metric (NIQE, PI, BRISQUE, MUSIQ) on both LOL-Blur and RealBlur. Against the best cascade baselines, the gains are substantial: on RealBlur, FourierDiff reduces BRISQUE from 28.96 (GDP→GRL) to 26.39 and improves MUSIQ from 39.35 to 52.24, indicating markedly better perceptual quality. Against the only dedicated joint method LEDNet, FourierDiff wins decisively on real-world RealBlur (e.g., PI 3.36 vs. 5.03, BRISQUE 26.39 vs. 42.31) but trails on synthetic LOL-Blur full-reference metrics (PSNR 20.53 vs. 25.74, SSIM 0.71 vs. 0.85), which is expected since LEDNet is supervised on paired data. The cascade baselines consistently underperform the joint methods, confirming that sequential enhancement-then-deblurring or deblurring-then-enhancement loses critical information at each stage. A user study with 40 participants further confirms that FourierDiff results are subjectively preferred over all baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

FourierDiff builds on the DDPM reverse diffusion framework (Ho et al., 2020) and the posterior-sampling formulation of DDNM (Chung et al., 2023) for the x_{0|t} estimation and joint-distribution sampling step. It departs from prior diffusion-based restoration methods (DDRM, DDNM, GDP) that require an explicit degradation model—either a fixed linear operator or a learnable degradation network—by instead using amplitude-phase decomposition in the Fourier domain to implicitly handle luminance and structural degradations without any degradation assumption. The spatial-frequency alternating optimization component repurposes the phase-only blur kernel estimation technique of Pan et al. (2019) but embeds it iteratively within the diffusion sampling loop, creating a feedback between the generative prior and the deblurring process. Conceptually, the paper carves new ground by being the first to exploit Fourier-domain amplitude/phase separation as a guiding mechanism for zero-shot diffusion-based image restoration, rather than as a training-time representation (as in DiffLL) or a post-hoc analysis tool.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FourierDiff demonstrates that a zero-shot method requiring no paired training data and no degradation model can outperform supervised joint methods (LEDNet) on real-world images across all no-reference quality metrics, suggesting that the generative prior of large-scale diffusion models, properly guided in the frequency domain, is sufficient to handle complex real-world degradations. The amplitude-phase decomposition provides an intuitive and modular framework that could potentially extend to other joint degradation tasks (e.g., low-light + rain, low-light + noise). However, the method inherits the slow inference of diffusion models (1000 steps) and degrades in extremely dark scenes where content guidance is lost, leaving open the question of whether the approach can be accelerated (e.g., via latent-space or few-step diffusion) while preserving the Fourier-guided refinement. The finding that supervised methods trained on synthetic pairs underperform on real data also highlights a broader gap in the field between synthetic training distributions and real-world degradation diversity.
