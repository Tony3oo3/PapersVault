## Problem

Existing data curation strategies for vision-language models select filtering thresholds independently of the available training compute, implicitly assuming that a fixed quality ranking remains optimal regardless of how many epochs the model trains. This paper demonstrates that high-quality filtered data rapidly loses utility through repetition, eventually underperforming lower-quality but less-repeated data, and that prior scaling laws treat web data as homogeneous, ignoring the heterogeneous quality structure of real scraped datasets.

## Input / Output

The method takes individually trained scaling parameters (utility b, half-life τ, normalizer a, irreducible loss d) for each data quality bucket (e.g., Top 10%, Top 10%-20%, etc., ranked by CLIP or T-MARS scores) along with a target compute budget, and produces predicted zero-shot accuracy curves for any mixture of those buckets without requiring actual training on the mixture. The output is a Pareto frontier mapping compute budgets (32M–640M iterations) to optimal filtering thresholds, validated against held-out test points from models trained on the corresponding mixtures.

## Method

The paper extends the standard power-law scaling form y = a·n^b + d by introducing an exponential decay of the utility parameter b with each repetition epoch: b_{k+1} = b·(1/2)^{k/τ}, where τ is a per-pool half-life capturing data diversity. Theorem 1 then shows how to combine p heterogeneous pools into an effective utility b_eff^{(k)} = (1/p)Σ b_i·δ̂_i^k and an adjusted half-life τ̂ = p·τ (further scaled by pool-size ratio for contrastive O(n²) comparisons), enabling prediction of mixture performance purely from individual pool parameters. Parameters are fitted via grid search over a, b, d, and τ for each bucket trained for 10 epochs on the DataComp medium pool (128M samples), and the resulting scaling curves are validated at 34B compute using publicly available ViT checkpoints from Cherti et al.

## Results

At 128M compute, LAION-style filtering (retaining top 10% by CLIP score) outperforms no-filtering by 7.5% average zero-shot accuracy across 18 downstream tasks. However, beyond ~450M training iterations, unfiltered Common Crawl surpasses LAION-filtered data, demonstrating that aggressive filtering becomes counterproductive at high compute. The proposed scaling laws predict the Pareto-optimal filtering threshold across compute budgets from 32M to 640M: Top 10% is optimal below 100M samples, Top 20% between 100M–350M, and Top 30% beyond 350M. These predictions are validated at 34B compute using publicly available ViT checkpoints from Cherti et al., where the proposed formulation corrects the high errors of prior scaling curves at small data scales.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LAION filtering (Schuhmann et al.), the paper shows a clear compute-dependent reversal: LAION's fixed CLIP-score threshold (>0.28, retaining 10%) wins by 7.5% at 128M compute but loses to no-filtering beyond 450M iterations, because each LAION sample is seen ~32 times and its utility decays. Against T-MARS (Maini et al.), the originally proposed Top 20% retention is shown to be optimal only at moderate compute; at 32M, Top 10% is superior, while at 640M, Top 30% outperforms the T-MARS default. Against Cherti et al.'s scaling laws for CLIP, the proposed formulation incorporating repetition decay and heterogeneous data pools predicts final error with substantially higher accuracy, particularly for models trained on small datasets where Cherti et al.'s curves exhibit 'extremely high errors.' The paper's scaling curves for data mixtures (Figure 5) are estimated purely from individual pool parameters without any joint training on mixtures, and the predicted test points align closely with actual performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the standard neural scaling law formulation (y = a·n^b + d) from Kaplan et al. and Cherti et al. by introducing two critical modifications: a per-pool utility parameter that captures heterogeneous data quality, and an exponential decay of that utility with repeated epochs. It departs from Muennighoff et al., who modeled diminishing returns via an 'effective dataset size' reduction, by instead modeling decay in the utility exponent b itself, which the authors argue is necessary to capture the interplay between data quality and repetition in contrastive VLM training. The work also positions itself against the data curation literature (LAION, T-MARS, C-SSFT), which has treated filtering thresholds as fixed constants independent of training compute.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: widely adopted curation strategies like LAION's 10% CLIP-score threshold can be actively harmful at high compute budgets, and the optimal filtering aggressiveness shifts as a function of total training iterations. The scaling-law framework provides a principled, compute-aware method to select filtering thresholds without exhaustive training sweeps, which is especially valuable for web-scale pre-training where each experiment is extremely expensive. Open questions remain about how the diversity parameter τ behaves when mixing pools of different diversity, whether scaling parameters transfer across orders of magnitude in pool size, and how batch size interacts with the repetition decay model.
