## Problem

Existing image quality metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, DISTS) are designed for LDR images and fail to align with human perception of HDR image quality, while the HDR-VDP family, though perceptually grounded, contains complex non-differentiable modules that prevent its use as a loss function in perceptual optimization. There is no simple, differentiable, and empirically validated HDR quality metric that both correlates well with human judgments and can serve as an optimization objective.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242009/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For quality assessment, the method takes a pair of uncalibrated HDR images (reference and test) in linear luminance and produces a single scalar quality score. The HDR images are decomposed into K overlapping LDR windows (default K=3, each covering ~2.64 stops of a 7.64-stop display range), and the final score aggregates per-window LDR metric scores with configurable global weights G^(k) that allow emphasis on specific luminance ranges. For novel view synthesis, the method takes 18 training HDR views (400×400) per scene and outputs rendered HDR views at 17 held-out viewpoints, with the quality metric used as a differentiable training loss.

## Method

The core mechanism is an inverse gain-offset-gamma display model that maps an HDR image into a stack of K LDR images by sliding a fixed dynamic-range window (7.64 stops) across the scene's luminance range, with each window position determined by an exposure value v^(k). Each LDR image pair is then scored by a standard LDR quality metric (MAE, PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, or DISTS) producing a local quality map, which is pooled using a well-exposedness weighting that suppresses under- and over-exposed pixels. An optional luminance shift compensation step optimizes the test image's exposure values {v̂^(k)} to maximize the aggregated score, mitigating bias from global luminance offsets. The resulting metric Q* is differentiable and reduces to the base LDR metric when applied to LDR images, enabling seamless transfer of LDR-IQA advances to HDR. For HDR novel view synthesis, Q_MAE* is used as the training loss for a simplified HDR-NeRF (without the tone mapper), directly optimizing the radiance field in linear luminance space.

## Results

On the weighted average across four HDR-IQA datasets, the proposed Q_DISTS* achieves the best SRCC of 0.869 and PLCC of 0.873, outperforming the strongest model-based baseline HDR-VDP-3-Q (SRCC 0.801, PLCC 0.808) by 0.068 in SRCC. Q_LPIPS* also leads among LPIPS-based variants with SRCC 0.840 and PLCC 0.843. On the largest UPIQ dataset (HDR subset), Q_DISTS* reaches SRCC 0.853 and PLCC 0.857, surpassing HDR-VDP-3-Q (0.845/0.843) and PU21-DISTS (0.801/0.822). The inverse display model consistently improves every base LDR metric over its PU/PU21-encoded counterpart, confirming that multi-exposure decomposition is a more effective HDR-to-LDR bridge than perceptually uniform encoding.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against 19 baselines spanning model-based (HDR-VDP-3-Q, HDR-VDP-3-D, HDR-VQM, NLPD), encoding-based (PU/PU21 + MAE/PSNR/SSIM/LPIPS/DISTS), and raw LDR metrics. Q_DISTS* wins on all four datasets and the weighted average, beating the best model-based method HDR-VDP-3-Q by 0.068 SRCC (0.869 vs. 0.801) and the best encoding-based method PU21-DISTS by 0.043 SRCC (0.869 vs. 0.826). The inverse display model provides consistent gains over PU/PU21 encoding for every base metric: e.g., Q_DISTS* (0.869) > PU21-DISTS (0.826) > DISTS (0.680), and Q_LPIPS* (0.840) > PU21-LPIPS (0.825) > LPIPS (0.765). On the UPIQ HDR subset specifically, Q_DISTS* (0.853) surpasses HDR-VDP-3-Q (0.845) by 0.008 SRCC, while PU21-DISTS drops to 0.801, showing the encoding approach degrades on true HDR content. For the novel view synthesis task (Table 3), HDR-NeRF† guided by Q_MAE* outperforms HDR-NeRF+ (tone-mapped supervision) by 1.4 dB in PSNR (28.75 vs. 27.36) and 0.039 in SSIM (0.933 vs. 0.929), and by 0.23 in HDR-VDP-3-Q (9.86 vs. 9.63), confirming the metric's utility as a training loss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two dominant paradigms in HDR quality assessment: model-based approaches (HDR-VDP family) that explicitly simulate the human visual system but are complex and non-differentiable, and encoding-based approaches (PU, PU21, PQ) that map HDR luminances into a perceptually uniform range but suffer from overflow or contrast compression. Instead, it repurposes the simple inverse gain-offset-gamma display model from Mantiuk & Heidrich [26]—originally designed for web-based HDR visualization—as a decomposition mechanism that splits an HDR image into a stack of LDR images at different exposures, onto which any standard LDR metric (SSIM, LPIPS, DISTS, etc.) can be directly applied. The approach is conceptually inspired by Munkberg et al.'s multi-exposure HDR texture compression [38], and the novel view synthesis component builds on HDR-NeRF [17] by replacing its learned tone mapper with the proposed metric as a direct optimization objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is a training-free, differentiable HDR quality metric that automatically inherits improvements from the rapidly evolving LDR-IQA literature (e.g., DISTS, LPIPS), removing the need for expensive re-calibration on HDR perceptual data. The luminance shift compensation (Eq. 8) addresses a well-known practical pitfall in HDR comparison that prior metrics handle poorly. A stated limitation is that the metric does not model the reduced sensitivity of the visual system at low absolute luminances, meaning predictions are display-luminance-agnostic—a gap that PU/PU21 and HDR-VDP explicitly address. Open questions include whether the fixed three-window decomposition is optimal for scenes with extreme dynamic ranges (>8 stops) and whether the approach extends naturally to video or to display-adaptive assessment.
