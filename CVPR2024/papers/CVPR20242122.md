## Problem

Virtually all deep network image classification work uses images in the sRGB format processed for human viewing, with almost no exploration of alternative input spaces. The paper asks whether the human-optimized display pipeline is also optimal for machines, and specifically whether log of linear RGB — which aligns with the logarithmic sensors of the human visual system and the physics of light-matter interaction — provides performance gains and natural invariance to illumination changes that are orthogonal to network architecture choices.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images at 480-pixel short edge in three representations: 8-bit sRGB JPG, 16-bit linear RGB TIFF, and float32 log RGB EXR with range [0, 11.1]. A critical constraint is that log data must not be normalized to [0,1] or resized via interpolation, as these operations corrupt the logarithmic relationships; all geometric and photometric operations must be performed in linear space before the log transform. The output is a 10-way class label (apples, tomatoes, bananas, recycle bins, stop-signs, plates, books, spatulas, mugs, pens) from the newly introduced RAW10 dataset of ~10,000 RAW images.

## Method

The core contribution is an empirical study showing that feeding log of linear RGB (derived from RAW sensor data via de-Bayer, white balance, and natural log) into standard CNN backbones (ResNet18, DenseNet121, and a custom 11-layer CNN) improves classification accuracy and yields invariance to random intensity and color balance perturbations without additional augmentation. The physical basis is the Bi-Illuminant Dichromatic Reflection model, where the log transform separates material reflectance from illumination (log I = log R_B + log(L_A + γL_D)), making local features like gradients stable across lighting changes. A key practical finding is that log-trained networks require different meta-parameters (Adam optimizer, higher learning rate, polynomial scheduler) than sRGB or linear-trained networks (SGD with momentum, step scheduler), and that a simple substitution of log for sRGB in an existing pipeline will fail. The paper further demonstrates that applying an inverse sRGB transform followed by a log to 8-bit JPG images (pseudo-log) recovers most of the performance gains, implying existing large-scale sRGB databases can be reprocessed to benefit from this input space.

## Results

On the RAW10 dataset, log RGB inputs consistently outperform sRGB and linear RGB across both ResNet18 and DenseNet121. DenseNet121 trained on log RGB achieves 91.9% top-1 accuracy on the original test set (vs. 91.4% sRGB, 91.6% linear), with the largest gains appearing on modified test sets: 88.0% under random color balance (vs. 84.7% for both sRGB and linear) and 86.9% under combined intensity and color balance variation (vs. 81.0% sRGB, 82.0% linear). ResNet18 shows even more dramatic improvements under perturbation, reaching 86.9% on random color balance (vs. 72.6% sRGB, 75.7% linear) and 83.9% on combined variation (vs. 67.3% sRGB, 71.9% linear).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's baselines are the same network architectures (ResNet18, DenseNet121) trained on sRGB or linear RGB inputs rather than log RGB. Log RGB wins on every test condition for both architectures, with the most substantial margins under perturbation. For ResNet18, log RGB gains 14.3 points over sRGB on random color balance (86.9% vs. 72.6%) and 16.6 points on combined variation (83.9% vs. 67.3%), while DenseNet121 gains 3.3 and 5.9 points respectively. On the unmodified test set, the advantage is smaller (1.5–3.7 points), suggesting the primary benefit of log RGB is robustness to illumination variation rather than raw classification accuracy. The pseudo-log experiments (Table 3) further show that even 8-bit sRGB-JPG images, after inverse sRGB transform and log, recover most of the log RGB gains (e.g., DenseNet121 pseudo-log achieves 91.70% vs. 91.94% true-log on the original test set), indicating the preprocessing benefit extends to existing web-scale datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Maxwell et al. (2023) [30], which demonstrated log RGB invariance with a small 5-layer CNN, to standard backbone architectures (ResNet18, DenseNet121) on a substantially larger 10k-image dataset. It also relates to Cotogni and Cusano's equivariant networks [5] and Funt and Zhu's Laplacian-of-log approach [11], both of which achieved invariance through architectural modifications or fixed preprocessing; here, the claim is that log RGB input alone, with no architectural changes, yields both improved accuracy and natural invariance. The physical motivation draws on the Bi-Illuminant Dichromatic Reflection model [28], which predicts that log space separates illumination from reflectance, making body-reflection cylinders parallel and uniform in orientation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The most practically significant finding is that pseudo-log data (inverse sRGB transform + log on 8-bit JPG) recovers most of the benefit of true log data, implying that millions of existing annotated images in sRGB format could be reprocessed to improve network performance without new data collection. The invariance to intensity and color balance without augmentation is useful for deployment in variable lighting. However, the evidence is limited to a single 10-category, 10k-image dataset, and the meta-parameter sensitivity (log networks require different optimizers, learning rates, and schedulers) complicates adoption. Open questions include whether gains persist on larger, more diverse benchmarks like ImageNet, whether custom architectures exploiting log-space structure could yield further improvements, and whether the effect is truly orthogonal to network design at scale.
