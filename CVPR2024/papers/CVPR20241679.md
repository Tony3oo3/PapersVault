## Problem

Conventional burst photography pipelines treat pixel motion as noise and blend frames into a single image, discarding the parallax and motion cues that could separate scene layers. Existing layer-separation approaches that do exploit motion rely on generic flow models that overfit without expensive regularization penalties, or on backward-projection camera models ill-suited to multi-layer composition. The paper asks whether a compact, physically grounded representation can simultaneously fuse a burst into a high-resolution image and decompose it into transmission and obstruction layers without learned priors or post-processing.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241679/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes an unstabilized 42-frame, two-second RAW burst (12 MP Bayer data) along with calibrated camera intrinsics and gyroscope measurements, all available natively on modern smartphones. It produces a high-resolution fused scene decomposed into a transmission plane, an obstruction plane, a per-pixel alpha matte, and refined camera poses (translation and rotation). The two image planes are locked to the z-axis at fixed depths, with their relative ordering (obstruction in front vs. behind) determining whether the model performs occlusion removal or reflection removal.

## Method

The core contribution is a neural spline field (NSF) flow model that splits motion estimation into a spatial network h(u,v) mapping image coordinates to spline control points, followed by cubic Hermite spline interpolation S(t; P) at the target timestamp. This parametrization inherently bounds temporal dynamics—flow is continuous in time up to second derivatives—eliminating the need for total-variation or Jacobian-based regularization that generic flow networks require. The full model uses a forward projective camera model (ray-plane intersection rather than backward splatting) with translation learned from scratch via splines and rotation initialized from gyroscope data as a small-angle offset. Spatial complexity is controlled through multi-resolution hash encoding levels (L^γ=8 for flow, 12–16 for image content), and training proceeds coarse-to-fine with a sinusoidal schedule that progressively activates higher-resolution hash grids. The two layers are alpha-composited along each ray with a temperature-scaled sigmoid, and the entire system is trained end-to-end with a relative photometric loss plus a light alpha-sparsity penalty, converging in ~3 minutes on a single RTX 4090.

## Results

On synthetic scenes with paired ground truth (Fig. 8), the method reports PSNR/SSIM improvements across all test scenes compared to baselines, with camera motion simulated from real hand-shake data. The paper emphasizes that these gains are achieved with no post-processing steps or learned priors, using only a relative photometric loss and a simple alpha regularization term. Qualitative comparisons on in-the-wild captures (Figs. 6, 9) show the method recovers occluded and reflected content that multi-view and single-image baselines fail to separate, while also jointly producing high-quality alpha mattes. Training takes approximately 3 minutes on a single RTX 4090 GPU for a full 42-frame, 12-megapixel burst sequence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against six baselines spanning single-image, multi-view, and neural radiance field approaches. Multi-view methods (Liu et al. [42] and NIR [50]) fail to achieve meaningful occlusion or reflection removal in the small-baseline burst setting, as the occluder maintains high self-overlap across the short sequence. OCC-NeRF [70] and NeRFReN [18] struggle to converge on sharp reconstructions for the respective occlusion and reflection tasks. The single-image inpainting method Lama [57] can fill occluded regions but cannot faithfully recover lost fine details (e.g., carvings in the Door scene) and requires a hand-annotated mask rather than producing an alpha matte. DSR-Net [24] is the only single-image reflection method able to suppress even small reflection components, but still falls short of the proposed method's near-complete reflection-free transmission layer. The proposed NSF method outperforms all baselines by distilling information across all 42 input frames, jointly estimating camera poses, layer flows, and alpha mattes without any learned priors or regularization penalties on the flow model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes three distinct threads: the projective camera model and long-burst imaging setting from Chugunov et al. [8], the two-layer alpha-composited neural image representation from Nam et al. [50], and the spatio-temporal spline parametrization from Ye et al. [67]. The key departure from [50] is replacing its unregularized generic flow model with a neural spline field whose temporal behavior is constrained by construction (cubic Hermite spline), eliminating the need for total-variation or Jacobian-based regularization penalties. Unlike [8], which treats the neural field as 'a vehicle for depth optimization,' this work targets high-fidelity image reconstruction as a first-class output alongside layer decomposition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified two-layer model handles occlusion, reflection, shadow, haze, and motion segmentation with a single architecture and no task-specific learned priors, which is practically valuable for smartphone computational photography pipelines. A notable finding is that existing multi-view obstruction-removal methods (Liu et al., OCC-NeRF, NeRFReN) fail entirely on small-baseline burst sequences, highlighting a gap that this projective-model approach fills. Limitations include the two-layer restriction, the ~3-minute per-scene test-time optimization cost, and dependence on RAW data plus gyroscope metadata; open questions raised by the authors include incorporating learned segmentation/flow features, task-specific physical priors, and extending beyond burst data to other multi-image modalities.
