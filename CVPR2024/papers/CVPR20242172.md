## Problem

Existing CNN explainability methods produce pixel-level saliency maps, which are meaningless for human-drawn sketches where the fundamental semantic unit is a stroke, not a pixel. Moreover, the rasterisation step that converts vector strokes into pixel images is non-differentiable, blocking gradient-based attribution back to strokes. The paper also asks a broader question: beyond visualising saliency, what concrete downstream impact does sketch explainability have on retrieval, generation, assisted drawing, and adversarial robustness?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a vector sketch as a sequence of 5-element points (x, y, and one-hot pen-state flags for pen-down, pen-up, end-of-drawing) along with any pre-trained sketch model (no retraining required). Outputs are task-dependent: a per-stroke importance score (SLA) or per-coordinate importance score (P-SLA) that can be used to rank strokes, filter noisy strokes from a sketch, identify which strokes a generator prioritised, or select the smallest stroke whose removal flips a classifier's prediction. For assisted drawing, the input additionally includes a target photo, and the output is a cleaned sketch with low-attribution strokes removed via Gumbel-Softmax gating.

## Method

The core technical contribution is making the non-differentiable vector-to-raster conversion amenable to backpropagation via two complementary mechanisms. SLA (coarse stroke-level attribution) rasterises each stroke independently using Bresenham line drawing, then composes the full sketch as a differentiable sum of raster strokes; a per-stroke binary weight mask (1 on Bresenham pixels, 0 elsewhere) prevents the degenerate solution where all strokes receive identical gradients. P-SLA (partial stroke-level attribution) replaces Bresenham with a differentiable distance transform: each pixel's intensity is a sigmoid of the minimum distance to any line segment in the vector sketch, with a large multiplicative penalty (×10⁶) applied at pen-up states to suppress rendering between disconnected strokes. Both attributions are computed post-hoc as ∂F_θ(X)/∂X propagated through the differentiable rasterisation, requiring no retraining of the downstream model. The approach is demonstrated as a lightweight plugin across four tasks: SBIR reliability (correlating predicted stroke order with human drawing order), assisted drawing (removing strokes with low cosine-similarity attribution to a target photo), interactive sketch-to-photo generation (identifying strokes with minimal influence on StyleGAN latent codes), and adversarial attacks (removing the smallest stroke or fewest points that maximally increase classification loss).

## Results

For sketch-based image retrieval, sketches with high correlation (≥0.5) between predicted stroke order and human temporal drawing order achieve 57.6% mAP (category-level) and 17.6% Acc.@1 (fine-grained), versus 50.1% and 12.7% for low-correlation (≤0.1) sketches, validating that attribution captures meaningful stroke importance. In noisy stroke removal, SLA and P-SLA improve fine-grained SBIR accuracy over ground-truth sketches (36.1%/36.5% vs. 33.4% Acc.@1 on Shoe-V2; 54.9%/56.5% vs. 53.3% on Chair-V2), with SLA-filtered sketches preferred by users 78.5% of the time in a human study. In adversarial attacks, removing a single stroke of length ≤5 drops classification accuracy by 2.1% (QuickDraw) and 3.4% (TU-Berlin), while length ≤15 causes drops of 2.7% and 6.4% respectively. In the human transparency study, participants identify correct/incorrect model predictions 75.9–76.3% of the time using SLA/P-SLA attributions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's only direct quantitative baseline is Bhunia et al. [15] (CVPR 2022), which uses reinforcement learning (actor-critic PPO) to train a stroke subset selector for noise-tolerant SBIR. On Shoe-V2, Bhunia et al. achieves 43.7% Acc.@1, outperforming SLA (36.1%) and P-SLA (36.5%) by 7.6% and 7.2% respectively; on Chair-V2, the gap is 9.9% and 8.3%. The authors attribute this performance gap to the fundamental difference in approach: Bhunia et al. trains the baseline model end-to-end with RL, whereas SLA/P-SLA operate purely post-hoc without any retraining. However, in the human study (MOS), SLA-filtered sketches are strongly preferred over ground-truth sketches (78.5% vs. 21.5%), while P-SLA-filtered sketches are preferred only 57.1% of the time due to broken-line artifacts. The paper positions its contribution not as outperforming RL-based filtering on retrieval accuracy, but as providing a lightweight, training-free explainability tool that also enables novel applications (adversarial attacks, interactive generation) that the baseline does not address.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the well-established post-hoc explainability literature (GradCAM, input gradients, saliency maps) from pixel-level attribution in photographs to stroke-level attribution in human-drawn sketches, arguing that strokes—not pixels—are the natural semantic unit for sketch interpretation. It builds directly on pre-trained sketch encoders (CLIP-based SBIR from Sain et al. [77] and ResNet-50/StyleGAN generation from Koley et al. [55]) as plug-in targets, requiring no re-training. The key technical departure is solving the non-differentiability of rasterisation via two differentiable pipelines (additive stroke composition for SLA, distance-transform thresholding for P-SLA), which prior sketch explainability work (Qu et al. [70], Lin et al. [61]) addressed only through customised training pipelines rather than a portable, model-agnostic solution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The stroke-level attribution framework provides a practical, zero-training explainability tool that can be attached to any pre-trained sketch model, lowering the barrier for interpretability in deployed sketch systems. The adversarial attack finding—that removing a single stroke of ≤5 pixels can flip a classifier's prediction—reveals a fragility mode distinct from pixel-noise attacks and has direct safety implications for deployed sketch-based services. Limitations include the white-box-only adversarial setting, a small human study (7 participants), and the fact that the assisted-drawing results trail the only prior RL-based method by ~7%, suggesting post-hoc attribution may have a ceiling compared to task-specific training. Open questions include whether stroke-level attribution generalises to other sparse or sequential modalities (e.g., handwriting, musical notation) and whether black-box stroke-removal attacks transfer across architectures.
