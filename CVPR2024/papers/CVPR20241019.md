## Problem

3D Gaussian Splatting produces strong artifacts when the rendering sampling rate differs from the training sampling rate (e.g., zooming in or out). The paper identifies two root causes: the absence of 3D frequency constraints, which allows degenerate Gaussians to exceed the Nyquist limit and cause erosion/high-frequency artifacts when zooming in, and the use of a fixed 2D dilation filter that spreads radiance incorrectly across pixels when zooming out.

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-view RGB images with known camera poses and intrinsics, and outputs novel-view rendered images at arbitrary resolutions. A key distinction from prior 3DGS is that the 3D smoothing filter is computed per-primitive from the training views' sampling rates (focal length and depth), making it an intrinsic, viewpoint-independent part of the scene representation. The 2D Mip filter has a fixed variance of 0.1 (approximating one pixel), so rendering at any scale requires no retraining or re-encoding.

## Method

Mip-Splatting introduces two modifications to the 3DGS pipeline. First, a 3D smoothing filter band-limits each Gaussian primitive by convolving it with a 3D Gaussian whose variance is s/ν̂_k², where ν̂_k is the maximal sampling rate across all training views in which the primitive is visible (computed via the Nyquist-Shannon theorem and recomputed every 100 iterations). This ensures no Gaussian encodes frequencies above half the maximum sampling rate, eliminating high-frequency artifacts at higher resolutions. Second, the 2D screen-space dilation is replaced by a 2D Mip filter—a 2D Gaussian with variance 0.1 that approximates the box filter of physical pixel integration—mitigating aliasing and dilation artifacts at lower resolutions. Unlike EWA splatting, which empirically sizes its filter to the rendered image, the Mip filter targets a fixed single-pixel approximation, and unlike Mip-NeRF's integrated position encoding, it operates in closed form on the Gaussian representation without requiring multi-scale training data.

## Results

On the Blender dataset with single-scale training and multi-scale testing (zoom-out), Mip-Splatting achieves an average PSNR of 31.97 dB, substantially outperforming 3DGS (24.84 dB) and 3DGS+EWA (29.40 dB), with the gap widening at lower resolutions (28.67 vs. 17.69 PSNR at 1/8 resolution). On Mip-NeRF 360 with single-scale training and multi-scale testing (zoom-in), Mip-Splatting reaches 27.37 dB average PSNR versus 23.25 dB for 3DGS and 25.43 dB for 3DGS+EWA. In the standard same-scale setting on Mip-NeRF 360, Mip-Splatting matches 3DGS+EWA (27.79 vs. 27.77 PSNR) and slightly exceeds 3DGS (27.79 vs. 27.21 PSNR), confirming no performance regression in-distribution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Mip-Splatting decisively outperforms 3DGS [18] at all non-training scales: on Blender zoom-out, PSNR drops from 33.33 to 17.69 for 3DGS at 1/8 resolution, while Mip-Splatting maintains 28.67; on Mip-NeRF 360 zoom-in, 3DGS falls to 19.59 at 8× while Mip-Splatting holds 26.22. Against 3DGS+EWA [59], Mip-Splatting wins at every non-training scale (e.g., 28.67 vs. 24.63 PSNR at 1/8 res on Blender; 26.22 vs. 22.81 at 8× on Mip-NeRF 360), though 3DGS+EWA edges ahead at full training resolution on Blender (33.51 vs. 33.36). Compared to Mip-NeRF [1] and Tri-MipRF [17], Mip-Splatting achieves comparable or slightly better PSNR at training scale but pulls ahead at lower resolutions (28.67 vs. 27.97 and 23.87 respectively at 1/8 res), and avoids the over-smoothing that affects EWA-based methods. On the Mip-NeRF 360 zoom-in setting, Mip-Splatting surpasses Mip-NeRF 360 [2] (26.22 vs. 24.10 at 8×) and Zip-NeRF [3] (26.22 vs. 20.27 at 8×), which suffer from MLP extrapolation failure at out-of-distribution frequencies. In the standard same-scale Mip-NeRF 360 benchmark (Table 4), Mip-Splatting (27.79 PSNR, 0.827 SSIM, 0.203 LPIPS) is essentially tied with 3DGS+EWA (27.77/0.826/0.206) and slightly ahead of 3DGS (27.21/0.815/0.214), confirming no in-distribution regression. Against older baselines (NeRF, TensoRF, Instant-NGP, Plenoxels, NeRF++), Mip-Splatting dominates across all scales and metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [18], introducing two targeted modifications: a 3D smoothing filter that constrains Gaussian primitive frequencies via the Nyquist-Shannon theorem, and a 2D Mip filter that replaces 3DGS's screen-space dilation with a Gaussian approximation of the physical pixel box filter. It departs from prior anti-aliasing approaches (Mip-NeRF, Zip-NeRF, Tri-MipRF) that rely on cone tracing and multi-scale training data, instead achieving alias-free rendering through closed-form modifications to the 3D representation that require only single-scale training. The 2D Mip filter is conceptually related to EWA splatting [59], but differs in targeting an exact single-pixel approximation rather than an empirically-sized bandwidth limiter, and in being applied during inverse rendering (reconstruction) rather than forward rendering only.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Mip-Splatting addresses a fundamental practical limitation of 3DGS: its inability to render faithfully at sampling rates different from training, which is critical for applications like VR, cinematography, and robotics where zoom and camera distance vary. The simplicity of the fix (two Gaussian filters) and the fact that the 3D smoothing becomes intrinsic to the representation (zero rendering overhead) make it highly adoptable. Open questions include whether the Gaussian-to-box-filter approximation can be improved for extreme zoom-out, whether the per-primitive sampling rate computation can be made more efficient via CUDA or precomputation, and whether the approach extends to dynamic scenes or 4D representations.
