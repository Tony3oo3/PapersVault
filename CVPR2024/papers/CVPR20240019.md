## Problem

Existing model-based multi-fidelity HPO methods (BOHB, A-BOHB, Hyper-Tune) rely on the successive-halving assumption that learning curves of different configurations rarely intersect, which does not hold in practice. This leaves a fundamental open question: what is the appropriate fidelity level for each configuration to fit the surrogate model? Prior approaches either use fixed fidelities for all configurations, fit separate models per fidelity, or naively use the last observed fidelity, none of which adaptively match the fidelity to each configuration's learning curve characteristics.

## Input / Output

The method takes a hyperparameter search space Λ, a descending objective function (e.g., validation loss), and a set of discrete resource/fidelity levels r ∈ {r_min, ..., r_max} (e.g., training epochs or data subset ratios). It outputs the best-found configuration λ* with its performance y*, along with per-configuration efficient points (used for surrogate fitting) and saturation points (used for final evaluation). A key design choice is that the warm-up point w is set at 20% of the total resource budget, and the method treats each fidelity evaluation as an individual task for parallel scheduling rather than evaluating an entire configuration end-to-end.

## Method

FastBO adaptively identifies two key points per configuration from its estimated learning curve: the efficient point e_i (the smallest r where doubling resources yields less than δ₁ improvement), which serves as the fidelity for surrogate model fitting, and the saturation point s_i (the smallest r beyond which performance varies by less than δ₂), which approximates the final fidelity for post-processing. Learning curves are modeled as a weighted linear combination of three parametric families (power-law, exponential, logarithmic) fitted via maximum likelihood estimation on early observations collected during a warm-up stage. The warm-up stage also performs early-termination detection by monitoring for consecutive performance drops exceeding a ratio α, and the post-processing stage promotes the top-k configurations to their saturation points. The surrogate is a Gaussian Process with Expected Improvement acquisition, and the adaptive fidelity strategy generalizes to extend any single-fidelity method (e.g., CQR, BORE, REA) to the multi-fidelity setting by simply replacing full evaluations with efficient-point evaluations.

## Results

On the three expensive datasets (Covertype, ImageNet16-120, Slice), FastBO identifies configurations with comparable or better performance while saving 10%–87% wall-clock time relative to all baselines. Specifically, FastBO achieves 22.9±0.2 val. error on Covertype in 0.7h (vs. 2.9h for BO, 3.9h for PASHA), 55.3±0.2 val. error on ImageNet16-120 in 2.2h (vs. 6.6h for BO, 2.5h for PASHA), and 26.3±2.6 val. loss on Slice in 0.4h (vs. 3.1h for BO, 1.2h for PASHA). In terms of relative efficiency (FastBO = 1.00), the closest competitor is PASHA at 0.90 on ImageNet16-120, while single-fidelity BO drops to 0.13–0.34 across datasets. FastBO also achieves top-2 final performance on 8 out of 10 datasets in the anytime experiments.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FastBO outperforms all 11 baselines on wall-clock time for configuration identification. Against single-fidelity BO (GP-based), FastBO is 3–8× faster (e.g., 0.7h vs. 2.9h on Covertype, 0.4h vs. 3.1h on Slice) while matching or slightly improving performance. Among model-free multi-fidelity methods, PASHA is the closest competitor (0.90 relative efficiency on ImageNet16-120) but still lags on Covertype (0.18) and Slice (0.35); ASHA and Hyperband show weaker anytime performance, particularly on NAS-Bench-201 and FCNet. Among model-based multi-fidelity methods, DyHPO (0.41–0.51) and Hyper-Tune (0.40–0.67) are the strongest but remain 2–6× slower than FastBO; BOHB (0.29–0.68), A-BOHB (0.20–0.38), and A-CQR (0.17–0.37) are consistently the slowest. For the generality experiments, the adaptive fidelity extension improves all three single-fidelity methods (CQR, BORE, REA) over their original versions, with REA (an evolutionary algorithm) showing particularly notable gains, confirming the strategy's broad applicability beyond GP-based BO.

## Relation to Prior Work

FastBO builds on the Bayesian Optimization framework (GP surrogate + Expected Improvement) and the multi-fidelity evaluation paradigm established by SHA, Hyperband, and ASHA. It departs from prior model-based multi-fidelity methods (BOHB, A-BOHB, Hyper-Tune, DyHPO) that either fit separate per-fidelity surrogates, impose strong tractability assumptions on joint models, or naively use the last-observed fidelity. The key conceptual departure is replacing the SHA assumption that learning curves rarely intersect with an adaptive, per-configuration fidelity choice derived from parametric learning curve modeling (power-law, exponential, logarithmic families), and further positioning the efficient-point strategy as a general bridge from any single-fidelity method to the multi-fidelity setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 10–87% wall-clock time savings for identifying good configurations directly lower the barrier to HPO and NAS, particularly for practitioners with limited compute. The generality claim—that the efficient-point strategy can extend arbitrary single-fidelity methods (demonstrated on CQR, BORE, and evolutionary REA)—opens a practical pathway for the community to retrofit multi-fidelity efficiency onto existing toolkits without redesigning the core optimizer. Open questions include how well the three parametric learning curve families generalize to highly non-standard training dynamics (e.g., large-scale transformers with complex schedules), whether the 20% warm-up budget is optimal across problem scales, and whether the approach extends naturally to continuous (rather than discrete) fidelity levels.
