## Problem

Commercially available XR headsets (AR glasses, VR goggles) have cameras at extreme viewpoints where the body is frequently clipped out of frame, and their 6DoF headset pose alone lacks fine-grained hand and foot information. Existing approaches either require unrealistic dedicated body cameras, rely on kinematic pose as a fragile intermediate representation between vision and physics, or produce physically implausible motion (floating, penetration) because they ignore underlying dynamics. This paper addresses the gap of learning a single end-to-end controller that maps raw XR sensor streams to physically plausible full-body motion without any intermediate pose representation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes monochrome SLAM camera images (2 views at 120×160 for Aria glasses, 4 views at 120×160 for Quest 2) and a 6DoF headset pose (global rotation and translation) at each frame. The headset pose is treated as a virtual joint: the rotation and translation difference between the simulated head joint and the headset pose forms a tracking signal. The output is a vector of PD-controller target angles (23 or 24 actuated joints) that drives a physics-simulated humanoid; the full-body 3D pose is then read from the simulation state rather than regressed directly.

## Method

SimXR uses a Siamese ResNet18 (with group normalization replacing batch normalization) to extract shared features across all camera views, which are concatenated with the headset-pose tracking signal and proprioception, then fed through a 6-layer MLP to produce joint actions. The key training mechanism is online distillation from a pre-trained RL motion imitator (PHC): the student policy is rolled out in Isaac Gym physics simulation, and at each collected state the teacher PHC is queried to produce a reference action given the ground-truth reference pose, yielding a DAgger-style supervised loss on action matching. This offloads the sample-inefficient RL (billions of interactions) to the low-dimensional motion-imitation task while the high-dimensional image-to-action mapping is learned with sample-efficient supervised learning. The approach requires only ~0.1B environment interactions (vs. ~2B for PHC's RL training) and runs at ~30 FPS at inference, enabling real-time avatar control from off-the-shelf XR hardware.

## Results

On the synthetic Quest 2 test set (458k frames), SimXR achieves 94.3% success rate, 40.0 mm Epa-mpjpe, 6.5 mm/frame² acceleration error, and 8.3 mm/frame velocity error. On the real-world Quest 2 dataset (40k frames), it achieves 10/10 success rate and 73.0 mm Epa-mpjpe. On the Aria Digital Twin test set (25k frames), SimXR reaches 100% success rate with 47.7 mm Epa-mpjpe, 4.6 mm/frame² acceleration error, and 7.3 mm/frame velocity error. The model is compact (59.7 MB) and runs at ~30 FPS in real-time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against UnrealEgo (a pure vision-based method without physics), SimXR achieves substantially lower pose error (40.0 vs 47.2 mm Epa-mpjpe on synthetic; 73.0 vs 81.0 mm on real-world) and dramatically lower acceleration/velocity errors (6.5 vs 54.5 mm/frame²; 8.3 vs 32.5 mm/frame), reflecting the benefit of physics simulation as a prior. SimXR also uses ~9x fewer parameters (59.7 MB vs 554.5 MB). Against KinPoly-v (a two-stage method that estimates kinematic pose then feeds it to a pre-trained imitator), SimXR wins on success rate (94.3% vs 82.2%), Epa-mpjpe (40.0 vs 42.8 mm synthetic; 73.0 vs 83.0 mm real-world), and real-world success (10/10 vs 9/10). KinPoly-v has slightly lower acceleration (4.4 vs 6.5) and velocity (5.8 vs 8.3) errors on synthetic data, but this comes at the cost of worse overall pose accuracy and lower success rate, attributed to the fragility of the intermediate kinematic pose representation and the difficulty of providing reference velocities to PHC in a two-stage pipeline. On the Aria ADT dataset, SimXR (47.7 mm Epa-mpjpe) outperforms both KinPoly (60.8 mm) and UnrealEgo (71.5 mm), with the headset-only ablation (85.8 mm) confirming that vision input is critical for hand tracking.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of physics-based humanoid control for pose estimation (KinPoly, SimPOE, QuestSim) by removing the kinematic pose intermediate representation and instead learning an end-to-end mapping from raw XR headset sensor input (monochrome SLAM images + 6DoF pose) to joint actuation signals. The training strategy adapts the online distillation paradigm from PULSE, but with a critical departure: the student and teacher operate on drastically different input modalities (images + headset pose vs. 3D pose), rather than sharing the same I/O. It also departs from prior egocentric pose estimation work (UnrealEgo, SelfPose, Mo2Cap2) that relies on purpose-built downward-facing fisheye cameras, instead targeting the constrained camera geometries of commercially available headsets (Quest 2 side-facing, Aria forward-facing).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is one of the first methods to demonstrate real-time, end-to-end simulated avatar control from off-the-shelf XR headset sensors without custom camera rigs, making it directly applicable to consumer AR/VR applications and potentially humanoid robot teleoperation. The large-scale synthetic dataset (2.2M frames) for Quest 2's SLAM camera configuration fills a notable gap in the literature and enables future research on this hardware. Key limitations include failure on unseen body parts (hands/feet), motion lag when limbs enter/exit the field of view, and the absence of temporal modeling or scene-level context. Open questions include whether auxiliary losses, temporal architectures, or scene-awareness can close the remaining gap to ground-truth pose, and whether the distillation framework generalizes to other humanoid morphologies or sensor configurations.
