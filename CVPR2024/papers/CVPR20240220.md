## Problem

Knowledge distillation of text-to-image diffusion models reduces inference to 1–5 steps but typically yields lower sample quality than the teacher, limiting practical adoption. This paper asks whether the student can be leveraged as a collaborator rather than a replacement, after discovering that roughly 30% of student samples are actually preferred over the teacher's for the same prompt and noise.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240220/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes a text prompt (CLIP-tokenized) and an initial noise vector as primary inputs; for editing it additionally accepts a reference image, and for controllable generation it accepts Canny edges or semantic segmentation masks alongside the prompt. The student model produces a latent sample in 1–5 multistep consistency-sampling steps, which is decoded to a 512×512 (SD1.5) or 1024×1024 (SDXL) RGB image. If the adaptive step rejects the sample, the teacher refines it by adding Gaussian noise at a rollback level σ ∈ [0.3, 0.75] and denoising with DPM-Solver or UniPC in 5–20 additional steps, or regenerates from scratch.

## Method

The proposed adaptive teacher-student pipeline has three stages: (1) the distilled student (trained via consistency distillation) generates an initial sample; (2) an oracle—ImageReward, a learned human-preference scorer—evaluates the student sample against a k-th percentile threshold τ tuned on a hold-out set, approximating a full-reference teacher-vs-student comparison without running the teacher; (3) samples below τ are sent to the teacher for either refinement (corrupting the student output with noise at level σ and denoising) or regeneration (sampling from scratch). The key empirical insight is that student-teacher divergence (measured by DreamSim) correlates strongly with quality gaps in both directions, and that the student tends to outperform the teacher precisely on complex, high-curvature trajectories where the teacher's own samples are more variable. The approach is model-agnostic and plugs into existing distillation frameworks (CD, ADD) and downstream tasks (SDEdit, ControlNet) without retraining.

## Results

On text-to-image synthesis with SD1.5, the adaptive approach matches the teacher's human preference with 5× fewer steps (vs. DDIM-50) and 2.5× fewer steps (vs. DPM-25), and improves generative quality by up to 19% over the teacher and 20% over non-adaptive refinement at the same average step budget. For SDXL, the adaptive pipeline with CD-SDXL achieves SDXL-Base quality at 5× lower average cost, and boosts ADD-XL by 14% in human preference at 4 steps. In text-guided editing (SDEdit, strength 0.6), the method attains an ImageReward of 0.281 with DINOv2 reference preservation of 0.669 at 30 average steps, outperforming the CD-SD1.5 student (IR 0.192) and matching or exceeding the full teacher (IR 0.183) while using far fewer steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the SD1.5 teacher (DDIM-50 / DPM-25), the adaptive method wins on human preference by up to 19% at the same average step count and matches teacher quality at 5× fewer steps; on automated metrics (Figure 10), the adaptive refinement strategy achieves lower FID while the adaptive regeneration strategy achieves higher CLIP score than the teacher at equivalent budgets. Compared to the non-adaptive refinement baseline (applying the teacher to all student samples), the adaptive step yields a 20% human-preference gain, confirming that selective teacher invocation is critical. Against CD-SD1.5 (consistency distillation, 5 steps), the adaptive pipeline substantially improves ImageReward (0.281 vs. 0.192 in editing) and FID in synthesis, while preserving reference similarity. For SDXL, the method with CD-SDXL matches the 50-step DDIM teacher at 5× lower cost, and improves ADD-XL (adversarial distillation, 4 steps) by 14% in human preference. In controllable generation with ControlNet, the adaptive approach (9–11 steps) outperforms the 20-step teacher by 19% for Canny edges and 4% for semantic masks. Restart Sampling is included as an additional baseline in the synthesis comparison, where the adaptive method also prevails on human preference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant distillation paradigm—where the goal is to replace the expensive teacher with a faster student—by proposing an adaptive collaboration in which the student generates first and the teacher selectively intervenes. It builds directly on consistency distillation (Song et al., 2023) as the student training framework and on ImageReward (Xu et al., 2023) as the quality oracle, while drawing a conceptual analogy to FrugalGPT's cascade for LLMs. The refinement step borrows from SDXL's two-stage base-plus-refiner design (Podell et al., 2023), but the key departure is the adaptive gating that skips teacher inference when the student sample is already strong.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The empirical discovery that roughly 30% of distilled student samples are human-preferred over their teacher challenges the assumption that distillation is purely lossy and opens a practical route to reducing average inference cost while matching or exceeding teacher quality. The pipeline is model-agnostic (validated on SD1.5 and SDXL with both consistency and adversarial distillation) and transfers to editing and controllable generation, suggesting broad applicability. Open questions include whether a better oracle (or a learned one) could push the acceptance rate higher, how the approach interacts with distribution diversity (a known weakness of adversarial distillation), and whether the threshold-tuning overhead generalizes across prompt distributions without re-tuning.
