## Problem

Existing VideoQA models employ goal-free vision perception that fails to interact vision with language during answer generation, leading to poor handling of long-term dependencies, multi-event reasoning, and multi-scale semantics in long-form videos. Additionally, directly fusing question text into visual representations via cross-attention introduces language bias, where models exploit textual shortcuts rather than performing genuine visual reasoning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242324/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes N=100 densely sampled video frames (encoded by frozen CLIP into patch and CLS embeddings) and a question text (encoded by CLIP's text encoder into word-level and sentence-level embeddings). Notably, the final answer is generated exclusively from question-related visual embeddings without any additional textual input, since text-vision interaction is completed during the semantic distillation stage. For open-ended QA an MLP head predicts the answer; for multiple-choice the VTM head selects the option with maximum logit.

## Method

The core contribution is the Language-Aware Gate (LA-Gate), a multi-head cross-gating mechanism that computes cosine similarity between visual queries and a language key to produce per-token importance scalars, applied as element-wise multiplicative gates on visual values—exciting question-relevant patches while suppressing irrelevant ones without injecting text information into the visual stream. Two modules are built on LA-Gate: a Differentiable Sparse Sampling module using stacked frame-sampling blocks with Gumbel-Softmax to select K=16 question-relevant frames from N=100, and a Vision Refinement module that iteratively applies LA-Gate combined with spatial-temporal self-attention to distill multi-scale question-related semantics into a single video-level CLS token. Pretraining employs Video-Text Matching and Vision-Guided Masked Language Modeling (where masked tokens are predicted from visual context with stop-gradient on text embeddings to enforce visual reliance), along with a contrastive loss on matched pairs.

## Results

On EgoTaskQA (normal split), VideoDistill achieves 45.02% overall accuracy, surpassing the previous SOTA HCRN (42.20%) by 2.82%, with particularly large gains in the object category (54.64% vs. 45.31%, +9.33%) and multi-agent category (36.95% vs. 31.36%, +5.59%). On AGQA v2, VideoDistill reaches 55.80% overall accuracy, outperforming MIST-CLIP (54.39%) by 1.41%, with the largest margin in object-relation questions (56.32% vs. 51.68%, +4.64%). The model also demonstrates strong robustness to the number of input frames, maintaining competitive accuracy even with only 4 densely sampled frames thanks to its differentiable sparse sampling module.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VideoDistill outperforms all VideoQA baselines on both long-form benchmarks. On EgoTaskQA, it beats the previous SOTA HCRN [30] by 2.82% overall (45.02% vs. 42.20%), with the largest margins in object (+9.33%) and multi-agent (+5.59%) categories; it also surpasses ClipBERT [31] (39.87%), PSAC [36] (38.90%), HME [15] (40.16%), HGA [26] (36.77%), and VisualBERT [35] (37.93%). On AGQA, VideoDistill (55.80%) edges out MIST-CLIP [16] (54.39%) by 1.41% overall and AIO [50] (48.59%) by 7.21%, though MIST-CLIP retains advantages in relation-action (67.18% vs. 55.09%) and object-action (68.99% vs. 55.12) categories, which the authors attribute to language bias exploitation. The only category where VideoDistill underperforms a baseline is action on EgoTaskQA (16.35% vs. ClipBERT's 22.91%), which the authors explain as a consequence of avoiding language shortcuts that other methods exploit. In the language-only ablation (Table 3), VideoDistill shows the largest performance drop when vision is removed (e.g., −100% on object, −81.6% on predictive), confirming it relies far less on language bias than HCRN or BERT [13].

## Relation to Prior Work

VideoDistill departs from the dominant paradigm in VideoQA where language and vision are fused via cross-attention (as in PSAC, HCRN, PMT, MCAN) by proposing LA-Gate, a gating mechanism that modulates visual representation importance based on language without directly mixing text embeddings into visual features. For long-form video understanding, it contrasts with segment-level sampling approaches (ClipBert, MIST) by performing goal-driven, frame-level differentiable sparse sampling using Gumbel-Softmax, and with hierarchical temporal attention methods (LF-VILA, TALLFormer) by reducing the input rather than expanding receptive fields. The design draws inspiration from human goal-driven perception studies and from gated attention literature (Squeeze-and-Excitation, Selective Kernel Networks), repurposing these ideas into a unified semantic distillation framework built on frozen CLIP encoders.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses the well-documented language bias problem in VideoQA with a principled architectural solution (LA-Gate) that is potentially applicable beyond VideoQA as a general cross-modal fusion alternative to cross-attention. The differentiable sparse sampling module offers a practical efficiency gain for long-form video understanding by making performance insensitive to the number of input frames. However, limitations remain: the action category still underperforms, the approach depends on CLIP's frozen visual encoder, and the language-bias mitigation is validated primarily through a single proxy metric (performance drop under static/noise video input). Open questions include whether LA-Gate generalizes to other multimodal tasks, whether the sparse sampling strategy scales to videos with hundreds of relevant frames, and whether the 'purer' visual embeddings truly improve out-of-distribution robustness beyond the tested benchmarks.
