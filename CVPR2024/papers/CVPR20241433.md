## Problem

Decomposing composite hypervectors in Hyperdimensional Computing (HDC) requires identifying which elementary vectors from known codebooks were bound (and possibly bundled) to form a target vector. Existing classical approaches, including resonator networks, scale linearly with the combinatorial search space O(N^F), making them intractable for large codebooks or many factors. The paper further identifies that the more common 'bound-then-bundled' structure in practice (the Memorized-Factorization Problem) has not been addressed by any efficient algorithm.

## Input / Output

The method takes a D-dimensional composite hypervector (bipolar ±1 in the MAP scheme, typically D=50–5000) and F codebooks each containing N random elementary hypervectors, and produces the indices of the F elementary vectors whose binding best reconstructs the target. For the memorized-factorization extension, the target is a bundling of k bound hypervectors, and the output is the set of k factorizations. The algorithm operates in an approximate regime: similarity values are noisy (Gaussian with width ∝ √(kM/D)), so the output is the most probable factorization rather than an exact match, and the search is over N^F × k^M candidate combinations.

## Method

HDQMF encodes each D-dimensional hypervector as D qubits (|0⟩=+1, |1⟩=−1) and prepares a uniform superposition over all N^F factorizations using a polynomial-complexity state-preparation circuit. The key modification to Grover's algorithm replaces the binary oracle phase flip with a continuous phase shift e^{iπδ} proportional to the similarity between the candidate binding and the target, and alternates between U_o and U_o^{-1} in successive iterations to enable exact analytical treatment of arbitrary similarity distributions. The oracle computes the binding via MXOR gates (D auxiliary qubits) and induces the similarity-dependent phase using a sequence of angle-parameterized Pauli Z-gates, requiring only local operations with no entanglement. The additive property of quantum phase naturally extends the algorithm to the memorized-factorization problem: M parallel factorization systems accumulate phases additively, so the total phase encodes the fraction of correctly matched bundled components. The optimal number of iterations is N_itr ≈ (π/4)√(N^F/(kM)), yielding a quadratic speedup over classical exhaustive search, with θ=1 (full phase range) proven optimal.

## Results

HDQMF achieves a dramatically lower effective step count (N_S = N_I / P_s) than the Resonator Network across all tested configurations. At D=50, K=10, HDQMF requires only 72 effective steps (2 iterations, P_s=0.025) versus 338 for the Resonator Network (20 iterations, P_s=0.06, P_f=0.15). At D=500, K=20, the gap narrows but HDQMF still leads with N_S=47 versus 195 for the Resonator Network. The quantum algorithm also exhibits zero non-convergence probability, whereas the Resonator Network fails to converge in 12–19% of trials.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the Resonator Network [5,15], the state-of-the-art classical method for high-dimensional vector factorization. HDQMF wins decisively on the effective-step metric N_S: at D=50, K=10, HDQMF needs 72 effective steps versus 338 for the Resonator Network (a ~4.7× reduction), and at D=500, K=10, the gap is 44 vs. 338 (~7.7×). The Resonator Network also suffers a non-convergence probability of 12–19% (P_f), while HDQMF has no such failure mode. In the use-case demonstration (Fig. 6), at D=50 the Resonator Network fails to converge entirely while HDQMF correctly identifies the marked state within a few iterations; at D=500 both succeed but the Resonator Network requires more iterations. The paper attributes the advantage to the quadratic speedup of the modified Grover's algorithm, which reduces the search from O(N^F) classical steps to O(√(N^F/(kM))) quantum iterations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the high-dimensional vector factorization problem formalized by Frady et al. [5,15] and the resonator network approach by introducing the HDC Memorized-Factorization Problem, which generalizes from decomposing a single bound hypervector to decomposing a bundle of bound hypervectors (the 'bind-then-bundle' scheme used in most practical HDC models). Methodologically, it departs from classical iterative/optimization-based solvers by adapting Grover's algorithm [8] with a key modification: replacing the binary phase inversion with a continuous phase shift proportional to hypervector similarity, and alternating the oracle with its inverse to enable analytical tractability. The additive property of quantum phase is exploited to handle the bundling dimension without increasing circuit complexity, a design choice not present in prior quantum search applications to structured data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Hypervector decomposition is a fundamental bottleneck in HDC applications—without it, composite representations cannot be interpreted or used for downstream symbolic reasoning, limiting the practical utility of neuro-vector-symbolic architectures like [10]. The quadratic speedup is theoretically significant and the analytical framework for approximate Grover search with continuous phases is a reusable tool. However, practical impact is currently constrained by NISQ-era hardware limitations (qubit count, coherence, error rates), and the paper itself acknowledges HDQMF will likely operate in a hybrid classical-quantum regime. Open questions include whether the speedup persists under realistic quantum noise, how the algorithm scales to very large codebooks (N >> 100), and whether the low per-run success probabilities (2–6% in Table 1) are acceptable for real-time cognitive pipelines.
