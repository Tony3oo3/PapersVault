## Problem

3D Gaussian Splatting achieves real-time rendering but fails on reflective and specular surfaces because it does not explicitly model light-surface interactions, missing view-dependent effects like specular highlights. Existing methods that handle reflections (Ref-NeRF, ENVIDR) rely on implicit representations with time-consuming optimization (hours) and slow rendering, making them impractical for interactive use. The paper tackles the unexplored problem of integrating a shading function into the 3D Gaussian Splatting framework while preserving its training and rendering efficiency.

## Input / Output

The method takes multi-view RGB images with known camera parameters and a sparse SfM point cloud as input, initializing a set of anisotropic 3D Gaussian spheres. Beyond the rendered novel-view image, the model outputs per-Gaussian shading attributes (diffuse color, roughness, specular tint, normal, and a 3rd-order spherical-harmonic residual color) and a global 6×64×64 trainable environment light cube map. Training runs for 30,000 Adam iterations on a single RTX 3090, and the final model renders at ~97 FPS, roughly 3× slower than vanilla Gaussian Splatting but orders of magnitude faster than MLP-based reflective methods.

## Method

GaussianShader augments each 3D Gaussian sphere with a simplified shading function c(ωo) = γ(cd + s ⊙ Ls(ωo, n, ρ) + cr(ωo)), where the specular term integrates a GGX NDF over a differentiable mipmap-based environment light map (following NVDiffRec), and the residual color term (3rd-order SH) captures complex indirect reflections that direct specular alone cannot explain. The key technical challenge is normal estimation on discrete Gaussians: the method uses the shortest axis direction of each Gaussian ellipsoid as an approximate normal (motivated by the observation that Gaussians flatten during optimization) and adds a learnable normal residual with sign ambiguity resolved per viewing direction. A novel normal-geometry consistency loss enforces agreement between the rendered normal map and Sobel-gradient normals derived from the rendered depth map, avoiding expensive KNN neighborhood searches. A sparsity loss pushes Gaussian opacities toward 0 or 1 to encourage thin-plate geometry, and a small L2 penalty keeps normal residuals close to the shortest axis.

## Results

On the Shiny Blender and NeRF Synthetic datasets (averaged), GaussianShader achieves 32.76 PSNR, outperforming Gaussian Splatting (32.05), Ref-NeRF (31.73), and ENVIDR (30.04). Training takes only 0.58 hours versus 23 hours for Ref-NeRF and 6 hours for ENVIDR, while rendering at 97 FPS compared to 0.03 FPS (Ref-NeRF) and 1.33 FPS (ENVIDR). On the Shiny Blender reflective-object benchmark specifically, GaussianShader improves over Gaussian Splatting by +1.57 dB PSNR (31.94 vs. 30.37) and reduces LPIPS from 0.083 to 0.068. On general (non-reflective) NeRF Synthetic scenes, performance is on par with Gaussian Splatting (33.38 vs. 33.30 PSNR).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Gaussian Splatting [21], GaussianShader wins on reflective surfaces (Shiny Blender: 31.94 vs. 30.37 PSNR, +1.57 dB) and matches it on general NeRF Synthetic scenes (33.38 vs. 33.30 PSNR), at the cost of roughly 3× slower rendering (97 vs. 274 FPS) and 2.3× longer training (0.58 h vs. 0.25 h). Against Ref-NeRF [45], GaussianShader achieves higher PSNR (32.76 vs. 31.73) while training ~40× faster (0.58 h vs. 23 h) and rendering ~3,200× faster (97 vs. 0.03 FPS). Against ENVIDR [27], GaussianShader leads in both quality (32.76 vs. 30.04 PSNR) and speed (0.58 h vs. 6 h training; 97 vs. 1.33 FPS). Against NeRO [29] and NVDiffRec/NVDiffMC [34,18] on Shiny Blender, GaussianShader achieves 31.94 PSNR versus 29.84, 28.70, and 28.88 respectively, with substantially lower LPIPS (0.068 vs. 0.072, 0.119, 0.147). Against VolSDF [52] on NeRF Synthetic, GaussianShader improves PSNR by 5.42 dB (33.38 vs. 27.96). The only scenario where a baseline wins is ENVIDR on Shiny Blender PSNR (32.88 vs. 31.94), attributed to SDF's naturally smoother normals, though ENVIDR is ~10× slower to train and ~73× slower to render.

## Relation to Prior Work

GaussianShader extends 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023) by replacing its spherical-harmonic-only appearance model with a simplified physically-motivated shading function (diffuse + GGX specular + residual color), and adopts the mipmap-based environment light integration framework from NVDiffRec (Munkberg et al., 2022). It departs from prior reflective-surface methods like Ref-NeRF and ENVIDR, which embed shading within implicit MLP or SDF representations, by instead attaching per-Gaussian material attributes and a novel normal estimator (shortest-axis direction plus learnable residual) to the explicit discrete Gaussian representation. The residual color term, parameterized by spherical harmonics, is a deliberate departure from Ref-NeRF's direct-reflection-only model, allowing capture of indirect and scattered light without full rendering-equation evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in closing the efficiency gap between physically-based reflective rendering and real-time Gaussian Splatting: a ~40× training speedup over Ref-NeRF (0.58 h vs. 23 h) while retaining competitive PSNR on specular benchmarks makes interactive reflective-scene rendering feasible for the first time in the Gaussian Splatting paradigm. However, the simplified shading model (no full rendering equation, no inter-reflection) still trails SDF-based methods like ENVIDR on the most specular objects, and the normal estimation, while clever, relies on the empirical observation that Gaussians flatten during optimization—a property that may not hold for all scene types. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to dynamic or large-scale reflective scenes, whether the residual color can be made more physically interpretable, and whether the depth-gradient normal-geometry loss remains stable under sparse or noisy depth.
