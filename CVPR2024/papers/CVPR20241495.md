## Problem

Large multi-modal models like CLIP are widely deployed in assistive technologies for blind and low vision (BLV) users, yet their performance on data actually captured by BLV users has never been systematically evaluated. Existing evidence of disparities for this population is either anecdotal or not specific to LMMs, leaving a critical gap in understanding whether these models will serve one of their most important user groups equitably.

## Input / Output

The primary evaluation feeds RGB images (from BLV-captured ORBIT and VizWiz datasets or web-crawled MS-COCO and Open Images) alongside text prompts into 25 CLIP variants, producing zero-shot class label predictions over standardized N-way episodic tasks. Text prompts vary in specificity—raw object names, color adjectives, material adjectives, or combinations—to probe CLIP's sensitivity to tactile versus visual language. The few-shot mitigation variant additionally consumes K training images per class to compute prototype embeddings, and the downstream analysis extends outputs to bounding boxes (OWL-ViT), segmentation masks (CLIPSeg), and generated images (DALL-E2).

## Method

The paper constructs a standardized episodic zero-shot classification protocol (inspired by meta-learning) that holds task parameters constant across four datasets, enabling direct comparison of CLIP's accuracy on BLV versus web-crawled images across 25 variants spanning architecture size (ViT-B/16 to ViT-g/14) and pre-training scale (80M to 3.8B pairs). To isolate causal factors, logistic regression with interaction terms disentangles the marginal effect of each image quality issue (blur, occlusion, lighting, viewpoint, framing) and disability-object status on classification accuracy. A textual analysis of noun phrases in LAION-400M, LAION-2B, and DataComp-1B captions quantifies that disability objects appear ~17× less and materials ~4× less frequently than their non-disability/color counterparts, linking pre-training data composition to the observed disparities. A ProtoNets-based few-shot approach (5–40 shots) is tested as a mitigation, showing it reduces the disability/non-disability accuracy gap from 17 to 2 percentage points on clean images but only to ~15 points on cluttered scenes. Finally, example-based analyses on 180 BLV images demonstrate that the disparities propagate to three CLIP-based downstream models: OWL-ViT (object detection), CLIPSeg (semantic segmentation), and DALL-E2 (text-to-image generation).

## Results

Across 25 CLIP variants, zero-shot classification accuracy is 15.0 percentage points lower on BLV datasets (ORBIT, VizWiz-Classification) than on web-crawled datasets (MS-COCO, Open Images), with the gap ranging from 6.7 pp (ViT-g/14 LAION-2B) to 22.8 pp (ViT-B/32 DataComp-M). Disability objects are recognized 25.3 pp less accurately than non-disability objects (36.5% vs 58.9% on ORBIT Clean). A 5-shot ProtoNets approach reduces the disability/non-disability accuracy gap from 17.1 pp to just 2.1 pp on ORBIT Clean, outperforming both LLM-generated description baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the 5-shot ProtoNets approach against two zero-shot prompt-engineering baselines: Menon & Vondrick [39] (LLM-generated object descriptions) and Pratt et al. [51] (customized prompts via GPT-4). On ORBIT Clean, 5-shot ProtoNets achieves 86.2% accuracy on disability objects, substantially outperforming [39] (48.3%) and [51] (50.1%), while also matching or exceeding their non-disability accuracy (88.3% vs. 57.0%). The accuracy gap between disability and non-disability objects drops from 17.1 pp (0-shot) to 2.1 pp with 5-shot, compared to 8.7 pp for [39] and 6.9 pp for [51]. On the more realistic ORBIT Clutter, the 5-shot advantage in absolute accuracy persists (54.5% vs. 32.1%/34.2% for disability objects), though the gap reduction is less dramatic (14.6 pp vs. 18.1/15.2 pp). Both prompt-engineering baselines slightly reduce non-disability accuracy relative to vanilla 0-shot, whereas 5-shot improves both categories simultaneously.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the growing literature on LMM fairness and quality-of-service disparities—previously studied for gender, race, and age—to the specific and under-examined case of blind/low vision (BLV) users. It builds on CLIP's zero-shot classification paradigm and the episodic evaluation setup from meta-learning, but departs from prior disparity studies by being the first to systematically quantify performance gaps on real BLV-captured data (ORBIT, VizWiz) across 25 CLIP variants, and by tracing the root cause to underrepresentation in pre-training corpora via textual analysis of LAION and DataComp. The few-shot mitigation draws on ProtoNets, while the downstream impact analysis connects to the broader ecosystem of CLIP-based models (OWL-ViT, CLIPSeg, DALL-E2).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a 15-percentage-point accuracy gap persists regardless of architecture size or pre-training dataset scale challenges the implicit assumption that 'more data and bigger models' will close equity gaps, and the 16–17× underrepresentation of disability objects in pre-training captions provides a concrete, actionable target for dataset curation. The practical mitigation (5-shot ProtoNets reducing the gap from 17 to 2 points in clean settings) offers a low-cost path for application developers, though it degrades significantly in cluttered, realistic scenes. Open questions include whether few-shot adaptation generalizes to the full range of BLV assistance tasks, how to integrate application-level mitigations (camera stabilization, personalized augmentation), and whether dataset transparency norms (datasheets) will be adopted for proprietary pre-training corpora.
