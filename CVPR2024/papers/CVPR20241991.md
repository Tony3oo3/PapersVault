## Problem

Existing turbulence restoration methods are designed for static or minimally dynamic scenes, and fail in highly dynamic environments where object motion compounds with atmospheric turbulence, disrupting optical flow and breaking multi-frame registration. This paper presents the first segment-then-restore pipeline that explicitly separates dynamic foreground from static background before applying targeted enhancement to each.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241991/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes RGB video sequences (1080p, up to 56 frames per clip) degraded by atmospheric turbulence and produces restored frames with reduced geometric distortion and enhanced high-frequency sharpness. Internally it generates a binary foreground/background segmentation mask per frame, an enhanced background via weighted stacking, and a final sharpened composite. The Restormer transformer operates on patches of 128–384 pixels, and the full pipeline runs at approximately 5.71 s per 1080p frame on an A100 GPU.

## Method

The pipeline has five stages: (1) global camera-motion stabilization via normalized 2D cross-correlation against a reference frame; (2) unsupervised motion segmentation using average optical flow (AOF) computed with pre-trained RAFT over an adaptively chosen number of neighboring frames N, where N is selected to maximize separation between near-zero (static) and near-one (dynamic) normalized flow magnitudes; (3) adaptive Gaussian-weighted image stacking for the static background, with window size governed by the turbulence strength parameter C_n² estimated from the video; (4) Poisson pyramid blending to merge the enhanced background with the segmented foreground; and (5) a Restormer transformer trained on paired clean/turbulent images generated by a novel procedural simulator that uses 8-octave 3D simplex noise for temporally coherent tilt and 3D Perlin noise for adaptive blur, enabling rapid generation of 100K training clips at up to 1024×1024 resolution.

## Results

On the CLEAR dataset, Turb-Seg-Res achieves PSNR of 26.48/26.50/24.82 dB and SSIM of 0.853/0.867/0.828 for low/medium/high turbulence, substantially outperforming AT-Net (22.91/23.37/22.62 dB) and TurbNet (18.86/18.99/19.15 dB). On the novel line deviation metric, the method scores 1.89 (±0.39), compared to 3.33 (±0.33) for AT-Net and 5.79 (±0.20) for TurbNet, confirming superior geometric fidelity. For segmentation on URG-T, the AOF-based approach achieves 0.627 mean IoU, ranking second only to RGA (0.696) while operating at significantly lower latency. Per-frame latency at 1080p is 5.71 s on an A100 GPU, versus 80 s for AT-Net and 4200 s for TCI.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Turb-Seg-Res decisively outperforms both single-frame deep learning baselines on the CLEAR dataset: it gains roughly 3.5–7.6 dB PSNR over AT-Net and 7.3–7.6 dB over TurbNet across all turbulence levels, with SSIM improvements of 0.09–0.29. On the line deviation metric, the method nearly halves AT-Net's score (1.89 vs. 3.33) and reduces TurbNet's by a factor of three (1.89 vs. 5.79), confirming better preservation of straight structural edges. Against the multi-frame TCI method, the comparison is qualitative: TCI removes distortion well but fails on moving objects (e.g., a missing car), whereas Turb-Seg-Res preserves both static and dynamic content. For segmentation on URG-T, the proposed AOF method (0.627 IoU) trails RGA (0.696) by ~7 points but substantially beats TMO (0.468), Deformable Sprites (0.334), and DSNet (0.277), while running at far lower latency than RGA. In terms of speed, Turb-Seg-Res (5.71 s/frame on A100) is comparable to TurbNet (5 s on RTX 3090) but 14× faster than AT-Net and ~735× faster than TCI.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in turbulence restoration, where prior methods either target static scenes (lucky imaging, multi-frame stacking) or single frames (AT-Net, TurbNet), by introducing a segment-then-restore pipeline specifically for dynamic video sequences. It repurposes the Restormer transformer (originally for general image restoration) and RAFT optical flow (originally for video understanding) within a novel pipeline that first separates static and dynamic content before applying targeted enhancement. The procedural noise-based turbulence simulator (simplex + Perlin) carves new ground by replacing physics-based Zernike-coefficient simulators with a fast, temporally coherent alternative suitable for large-scale video dataset generation, while the adaptive Gaussian-weighted stacking guided by C_n² statistics bridges classical multi-frame fusion with turbulence-aware parameterization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first pipeline to jointly handle geometric distortion correction and sharpness recovery in dynamic turbulent video, addressing a gap that limits long-range surveillance and imaging systems in real-world conditions. The procedural noise simulator (100ms per frame, 100K+ clips) lowers the barrier to training turbulence restoration models, and the open-sourcing of code, simulator, and augmented URG-T data provides a practical foundation for the community. Key limitations include the 5.71s/frame latency at 1080p (not real-time), the segmentation bottleneck via RAFT, and the gap between noise-based and physics-based simulation fidelity. Open questions include whether the pipeline generalizes to extreme turbulence beyond tested C_n² ranges, whether real-time operation is achievable on embedded hardware, and whether the simple AOF segmentation scales to scenes with multiple overlapping moving objects.
