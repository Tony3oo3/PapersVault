## Problem

Standard image quality assessment (IQA) metrics such as PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS are the de facto standard for evaluating lighting estimation algorithms, yet it is unclear whether they actually reflect human preference when estimated lighting is used to relight virtual objects into real photographs. This paper demonstrates through a controlled psychophysical study that no single IQA metric consistently agrees with human perception across different tasks (accuracy vs. plausibility) and materials (diffuse vs. glossy), calling into question the validity of current evaluation practice.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240065/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The learned metric takes a pair of rendered images (each produced by a different lighting estimation method on the same scene) along with a ground truth render, and outputs a scalar preference score indicating which image a human observer would likely prefer. All 15 constituent IQA metrics are computed on tonemapped RGB stimuli (γ=2.4) as seen by observers, except HDR-VDP3 which operates on pre-tonemapped HDR renders. The method is trained on 25 indoor and 25 outdoor scenes with 5 indoor and 3 outdoor lighting estimation methods, yielding 640 training and 160 validation pairwise comparisons per experiment.

## Method

The core contribution is a learned combination of 15 existing IQA metrics (11 Full-Reference and 4 No-Reference) using ε-Support Vector Regression (ε=0.1, C=1) to predict human preference scores. The SVR takes as input the vector of per-metric score differences between two candidate images relative to ground truth (i.e., {ℓ_k(I_a, I*) − ℓ_k(I_b, I*)} for k=1..15), making it order-agnostic, and outputs a continuous preference score approximating the binary 2AFC choices of human observers. Four separate SVR models are trained—one for each combination of task (accuracy matching vs. plausibility judging) and material (diffuse vs. glossy)—since human perception differs systematically across these conditions. The psychophysical ground truth is generated from 49 observers performing 2AFC comparisons across 25 scenes and 8 lighting estimation methods, with z-scores computed via the Thurstone Case V model. The approach generalises to unseen lighting estimation methods, achieving agreement scores of 0.786–0.889 on a held-out method and on a second user study with novel methods, substantially outperforming the best individual IQA metric.

## Results

The proposed learned metric combination (SVR over 15 IQA metrics) achieves Spearman's ρ of 0.689–0.753 and Kendall's τ of 0.572–0.628 across four indoor experiments (Task 1/2 × diffuse/glossy), consistently outperforming the next-best single metric by 0.16–0.57 in ρ. On the generalization study with unseen lighting estimation methods, the learned metric reaches agreement scores of 0.786–0.889 (diffuse/glossy, Task 1/2), surpassing the best standard metrics (VIF, NIQE, RGB Ang. Err., BRISQUE) which score 0.670–0.809. No single IQA metric exceeds the expected observer agreement in more than one experiment, and several (e.g., ΔE, PieAPP, HDR-VDP3) perform at or near chance level (~0.5) for glossy and plausibility tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 13 IQA metrics (10 Full-Reference: RGB angular error, PSNR, RMSE, si-RMSE, SSIM, VIF, ΔE, LPIPS, PieAPP, FLIP, HDR-VDP3; 4 No-Reference: BRISQUE, NIQE, UNIQUE, HyperIQA) against human perceptual data from 49 observers. Only si-RMSE, SSIM, LPIPS, and FLIP (indoor) or PSNR, RMSE, si-RMSE, SSIM, and LPIPS (outdoor) match or exceed expected observer agreement, and only for Task 1 with diffuse materials. For Task 1 glossy and all Task 2 (plausibility) experiments, most metrics perform at or near chance level (~0.5), with ΔE, PieAPP, and HDR-VDP3 being particularly poor. The learned SVR combination dominates all individual metrics in every experiment, with the largest gaps in Task 2 (plausibility) where the next-best single metric (BRISQUE, ρ=0.145) is far below the learned metric (ρ=0.711). On the holdout test (Khan et al. 2006 excluded from training), the learned metric still outperforms all standard metrics, confirming generalisation to unseen lighting estimation methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not propose a new lighting estimation algorithm but instead interrogates the evaluation methodology that underpins the entire field. It repurposes psychophysical tools from vision science (Thurstone Case V scaling, 2AFC paradigms) to systematically test whether standard IQA metrics (LPIPS, SSIM, FLIP, HDR-VDP3, etc.) actually track human preference when lighting estimates are used for virtual object insertion. Conceptually, it draws on prior observations in colour constancy and gamut mapping where metrics disagreed with observer choices, but extends this critique to the lighting estimation domain for the first time. The learned metric combination (SVR over 15 IQA metrics) follows the strategy of Cadik et al. [6] for localised distortion prediction, adapted here to a global perceptual preference task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a near-universal practice in lighting estimation research: using full-reference IQA metrics as the primary quantitative evaluation. By showing that most metrics perform at or near chance level for glossy materials and plausibility judgments, it provides strong motivation for the community to adopt perceptually grounded evaluation. The released PerceptionMetric benchmark and learned metric combination offer a concrete tool for future work, and the generalisation experiments (holdout method, new methods including Stable Diffusion outpainting) suggest practical utility. Limitations include the restricted stimulus set (sphere-on-plane only), modest outdoor observer count (12), and correlation scores below the 0.8 reliability threshold, leaving open whether findings extend to complex multi-object scenes and diverse geometries.
