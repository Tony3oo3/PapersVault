## Problem

Industrial 6DoF pose estimation requires sub-millimeter accuracy at 150–200 cm working distances, but existing benchmarks lack the camera diversity, multi-exposure HDR data, and polarization modalities needed to co-design camera systems, HDR pipelines, and pose algorithms for factory deployment. Additionally, standard ground-truth-based evaluation is too imprecise (2–3 mm annotation error at industrial distances) to reliably measure the <3 mm MVD accuracy that industrial applications demand.

## Input / Output

The dataset provides, per scene, synchronized captures from 13 calibrated cameras: 8 Basler RGB cameras (2 MP and 8 MP, baselines 10 cm–1 m), 4 FLIR mono-polarization cameras (5 MP, baselines 50 cm–1 m), and 1 Photoneo XL structured-light scanner (~500 μm depth accuracy at 2 m). Each scene is recorded at 4 exposures (1, 30, 80, 200 ms) under 3 lighting conditions (100–100,000 lux), and the robot's kinematically calibrated transforms (<100 μm) serve as the evaluation reference. The pose estimation pipeline outputs a 6DoF rigid transform per object, scored by median Maximum Vertex Distance (MVD) in millimeters at 99% recall.

## Method

The paper contributes two coupled components: (1) the Industrial Plenoptic Dataset (IPD) of 20 industrial parts across 2,300 robot-mounted scenes, enabling ablation over camera type, HDR algorithm, and modality; and (2) Robot Consistency, an evaluation protocol that exploits the known relative rigid transforms between N robot configurations to estimate pose error without relying on potentially biased ground-truth annotations. The pose estimation pipeline under test combines Mask R-CNN detection, HRNet-based 2D keypoint regression, and either multi-view PnP (with Ceres re-projection minimization) or ICP for structured-light depth, optionally followed by edge refinement on grayscale or polarization (AOLP/DOLP) edges. Theoretically, the authors prove Robot Consistency is a consistent and unbiased estimator under spherical camera-pose distributions, and empirically validate it on synthetic data showing it tracks true model performance more accurately than mm-level human annotations, particularly in the high-precision regime relevant to industry.

## Results

On the Industrial Plenoptic Dataset under challenging spotlight lighting, Mertens HDR achieves the best median MVD of 3.061 mm with 0.77 recall and 0.963 precision on Basler-HR cameras, outperforming No HDR (3.271 mm MVD, 0.72 recall), Debevec (3.300 mm, 0.74 recall), and Robertson (3.351 mm, 0.74 recall). Across all lighting conditions, high-resolution multi-view cameras (Basler-HR) consistently outperform other camera types, achieving median MVD of 2.640–3.078 mm and 0.77–0.83 recall. The Robot Consistency evaluation method achieves a mean absolute difference of 0.13 mm from true synthetic ground truth, compared to 0.47 mm for 1 mm annotation jitter, validating its suitability for industrial-grade sub-millimeter accuracy assessment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three standard HDR algorithms (Debevec, Robertson, Mertens) against a no-HDR baseline on the best-performing Basler-HR cameras under the most challenging spotlight lighting condition. Mertens [50] achieves the best median MVD (3.061 mm) and highest recall (0.77), improving over No HDR by 0.21 mm in MVD and 0.05 in recall. Debevec [19] and Robertson [56] show only marginal recall gains over No HDR (0.74 vs. 0.72) while slightly degrading MVD (3.300 and 3.351 mm vs. 3.271 mm), indicating that naive HDR fusion can be counterproductive. Mertens also achieves the highest precision (0.963), suggesting its exposure-fusion strategy best preserves the contrast needed for pose estimation in high-dynamic-range industrial scenes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by identifying the co-design of camera systems, HDR imaging, and pose estimation algorithms as the missing ingredient for industrial-grade 6DoF pose estimation, a gap not addressed by any prior benchmark. It departs from existing industrial datasets (T-LESS, HomebrewedDB, MVTec ITODD, DIMO, StereOBJ-1M) which use limited camera counts, single lighting conditions, no HDR, and no polarization, and from household-object datasets (LineMOD, YCB-Video, PhoCaL) that don't target sub-millimeter accuracy. The Robot Consistency evaluation method is inspired by a prior patent [35] but is formalized here with theoretical guarantees (consistency, unbiasedness under spherical camera pose distributions) and validated on synthetic data, distinguishing it from all prior ground-truth- or annotation-based evaluation approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This dataset and evaluation framework directly target the gap between academic 6DoF benchmarks (10% object diameter tolerance at 50–100 cm) and industrial requirements (<3 mm MVD at 150–200 cm, 99% recall), which has kept vision-based pose estimation out of most factory deployments. The finding that even the best-performing camera (Basler-HR) achieves only ~2.6–3.1 mm MVD on average, with five parts exceeding 3 mm (thin, small, dark-reflective), underscores that no single camera or algorithm suffices and that co-design is genuinely necessary. Open questions include whether the Robot Consistency framework scales to more complex multi-object scenes, whether the polarization and HDR gains observed here transfer to other industrial settings, and whether the 20-part, 2,300-scene scale is sufficient to drive meaningful algorithmic progress.
