## Problem

Most underwater image enhancement methods operate in raw pixel space, leaving the frequency-domain structure of degradation largely unexplored and underutilizing deep models' representational capacity. The paper identifies that color degradation resides primarily in the low-frequency amplitude component while texture/detail degradation resides in high-frequency wavelet sub-bands, and that standard diffusion models struggle to focus on fine-grained information while introducing sampling artifacts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242439/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a degraded underwater RGB image as input, decomposes it via Haar DWT into one low-frequency (LL) and three high-frequency (LH, HL, HH) sub-bands at half resolution, processes each branch independently, and reconstructs the final enhanced image via inverse DWT. Training uses 256×256 patches with paired ground-truth images from UIEBD and LSUI; evaluation also includes the non-reference U45 benchmark. The two networks (WFI2-net and FRDAM) are detachable, so WFI2-net alone can serve as a standalone enhancement model.

## Method

WF-Diff consists of two detachable stages. First, WFI2-net performs frequency preliminary enhancement: the high-frequency branch uses a Wide Transformer Block combining self-attention and channel attention over multi-scale depthwise convolutions to model global structural details, while the low-frequency branch uses a Spatial-Frequency Fusion Block that jointly processes spatial embeddings and Fourier-domain amplitude/phase components; a Cross-Frequency Conditioner (CFC) enables mutual information exchange between the two branches via cross-attention. Second, FRDAM applies two separate diffusion models (LDFB and HDFB) in wavelet space to learn the residual distribution between ground truth and the initial enhanced sub-bands, rather than generating full images from noise, which focuses the generative capacity on fine-grained corrections and mitigates sampling diversity artifacts. Training uses L1 amplitude loss for the low-frequency branch, L2 loss for high-frequency branches, a Wasserstein GAN adversarial loss, and standard DDPM noise-prediction losses for the diffusion branches.

## Results

On the UIEBD dataset, WF-Diff achieves the best performance across all four metrics: PSNR 23.86 dB, SSIM 0.8730, LPIPS 0.1248, and FID 27.85, outperforming the strongest baseline DM-water (PSNR 21.88, SSIM 0.8194, LPIPS 0.1436, FID 31.07). On LSUI, WF-Diff attains the best FID (26.75) and SSIM (0.9437), while DM-water edges ahead on PSNR (27.65 vs. 27.26) and U-shape leads on LPIPS (0.1028 vs. 0.1096). On the no-reference U45 benchmark, WF-Diff achieves the highest UIQM score (3.181) but trails DM-water on UCIQE (0.619 vs. 0.634).

## Baselines & Comparisons

WF-Diff achieves the best scores on all four full-reference metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, FID) on the UIEBD dataset, with the largest margin over DM-water [36] (the closest diffusion-based competitor) of +1.98 dB in PSNR and +0.0536 in SSIM. On LSUI, WF-Diff leads in FID (26.75 vs. 27.91 for DM-water) and SSIM (0.9437 vs. 0.8867), but DM-water slightly outperforms it on PSNR (27.65 vs. 27.26) and U-shape [18] achieves a lower LPIPS (0.1028 vs. 0.1096). On the no-reference U45 benchmark, WF-Diff attains the highest UIQM (3.181) but DM-water leads on UCIQE (0.634 vs. 0.619). Traditional methods (UIEWD, UWCNN, UIEC²-Net, Water-Net) are consistently outperformed by a wide margin across all datasets, with PSNR gaps exceeding 8 dB on UIEBD. Overall, WF-Diff demonstrates the strongest aggregate performance, winning 8 out of 10 metric-dataset combinations, with the two losses being narrow margins against DM-water and U-shape.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the diffusion-based UIE line initiated by DM-water (Tang et al., 2023) by relocating the diffusion process from pixel space to wavelet sub-bands and learning residual distributions rather than full images, drawing on DDPM (Ho et al., 2020) and DDIM (Song et al., 2021) for the forward/reverse process formulations. It departs from the dominant pixel-space paradigm of prior UIE methods (U-shape, U-color, SCNet, etc.) by explicitly decomposing degradation into low-frequency (color) and high-frequency (texture) components via DWT and Fourier analysis, then addressing each with dedicated architectures (SFFB for low-freq, WTB for high-freq). The cross-frequency conditioner (CFC) introduces a novel interaction mechanism between the two branches, and the overall two-stage design (deterministic preliminary enhancement followed by stochastic diffusion refinement) is a structural departure from single-network UIE approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The empirical finding that color degradation resides primarily in low-frequency amplitude while texture degradation lives in high-frequency sub-bands provides a principled decomposition that could inform other restoration tasks beyond underwater imaging. FRDAM's plug-and-play design for residual frequency refinement is a potentially reusable component. However, the inference cost (0.28 s per image, ~19× slower than SCNet) limits real-time deployment, and the paper acknowledges this as a key limitation. Open questions include whether the wavelet-space residual diffusion generalizes to other degradation types (e.g., fog, motion blur) and whether the 1000-step diffusion schedule can be compressed without quality loss.
