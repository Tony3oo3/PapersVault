## Problem

Continual learning methods are designed to prevent catastrophic forgetting, yet their vulnerability to adversarial attacks that deliberately *induce* forgetting remains largely unexplored. This paper asks whether an attacker can poison a single task in a CL stream to maximally degrade performance on all previously learned tasks, under the realistic constraint that the attacker has no access to prior task data and does not know which CL algorithm the victim employs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242671/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The attacker receives the victim's trained CL model (backbone parameters plus per-task classification heads), the clean images and labels for the current task, and the batch normalization running statistics embedded in the model. The output is an additive perturbation δ added to each current-task image, constrained by an ℓ∞ norm bound (ε ∈ {0.1, 0.3}) and truncated to the [0,1] pixel range. The attacker does not require knowledge of the victim's CL algorithm, its regularization coefficient λ, or any data from prior tasks.

## Method

BrainWash operates in two stages. First, the attacker performs an optimization-based model inversion on the victim's CL model to reconstruct proxy samples for each prior task, using a loss that combines classification accuracy on target one-hot labels with total-variation, ℓ2-norm, and feature-statistics regularization (matching batch-norm running means and variances). Second, the attacker formulates a bi-level optimization: the outer loop maximizes the classification loss on the inverted prior-task data (and, for the 'cautious' attacker, subtracts a weighted loss on clean current-task data to preserve accuracy), while the inner loop simulates the victim's fine-tuning on the poisoned current task. This intractable bilevel problem is solved via a first-order approximation that unrolls only k=1 SGD step of the inner optimization, following the MetaPoison framework. Notably, the attacker simply fine-tunes the backbone and a randomly initialized head on the poisoned data in the inner loop, without replicating the victim's specific CL regularizer, yet the resulting noise remains effective across EWC, MAS, RWALK, AFEC, ANCL, and replay-based methods.

## Results

BrainWash dramatically increases catastrophic forgetting across all five regularization-based CL methods and three benchmark datasets. On CIFAR-100 with EWC, BWT drops from −5.2 (clean) to −29.1 (reckless, ε=0.3), a ~5.6× increase in forgetting, while last-task accuracy falls from 68.3% to 25.5%. On miniImageNet, the worst case (MAS, reckless, ε=0.3) drives BWT to −38.4 from a clean −6.7. The 'cautious' attacker variant preserves higher last-task accuracy (e.g., 42.2% vs. 25.5% for EWC on CIFAR-100) at the cost of somewhat less forgetting (−24.7 vs. −29.1 BWT).

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the head-to-head comparison on 10-split CIFAR-100 with EWC (Table 3), BrainWash induces the most forgetting (BWT = −29.1) among all four methods, outperforming Meta-Poison (−22.94), Unlearnable Examples (−21.4), and Deep-Confuse (−19.42). BrainWash also maintains a substantially higher last-task accuracy (25.5%) than Unlearnable Examples (3.7%) and Deep-Confuse (10.0%), indicating the poisoned data remains functional for the current task while still degrading prior knowledge. Meta-Poison achieves the highest last-task accuracy (35.3%) but induces less forgetting than BrainWash by ~6 BWT points. Overall, BrainWash is the only method that simultaneously achieves the strongest forgetting and a non-trivial level of current-task performance, making it the most effective attack in the CL setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

BrainWash sits at the intersection of data poisoning (bi-level optimization with first-order approximation, following MetaPoison [29] and MAML-style unrolling [22]) and continual learning security. It departs from prior CL adversarial work—backdoor attacks [57] and targeted poisoning [27,39]—by specifically optimizing for maximum forgetting of all prior tasks rather than inserting a backdoor or degrading specific classes. The model inversion component borrows the feature-statistics regularization from Yin et al. [68] to reconstruct proxy data for previous tasks, enabling the attacker to operate without access to historical training data. The key conceptual departure is formulating forgetting maximization as the outer objective of a bilevel program, with the inner loop being a simple fine-tune (not the victim's CL algorithm), yet still transferring effectively across diverse regularization methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work demonstrates that regularization-based CL methods, which are widely deployed for their stability-plasticity balance, remain vulnerable to forgetting-inducing poisoning even under a realistic threat model where the attacker lacks access to prior task data and is agnostic to the specific CL algorithm. The finding that model-inversion proxies achieve nearly identical forgetting to real data (28.8 vs. 28.16 in Table 4) suggests that even without any data access, a model-inversion step is sufficient, broadening the attack surface. Open questions include whether architectural CL methods (progressive networks, parameter isolation) offer stronger resistance, whether defenses exist beyond simply increasing λ (which the ablation shows is ineffective without sacrificing plasticity), and how the attack scales to foundation-model-scale continual training where data is non-i.i.d. and compute-constrained.
