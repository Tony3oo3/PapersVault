## Problem

Prior video-language alignment methods face two key limitations: they either fail to capture both short-range and long-range temporal dependencies while remaining compatible with pretrained image-text encoders, or they rely on small-scale datasets with noisy ASR transcripts that lack visual grounding. This paper addresses both by proposing a hierarchical temporal attention mechanism that integrates directly with CLIP-style encoders and by curating the largest video-language dataset to date (~800M pairs) with LLM-generated captions for improved semantic alignment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241237/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The video encoder takes a clip uniformly sampled to 12 frames at 224×224 resolution (via multi-scale random crop with ratios 1.0 and 0.8), split into P×P patches yielding TN patch tokens plus UV Multi-Scale Temporal tokens and a [CLS] token. The text encoder processes either the ASR subtitle or the BLIP-2-generated caption (both LLM-summarized to fit CLIP's context window). The model outputs a video embedding (linear projection of the final [CLS] token) and a text embedding, which are aligned via symmetric info-NCE loss over both text sources simultaneously.

## Method

VidLA extends CLIP's two-tower architecture by replacing the vision encoder's standard self-attention with a two-stage hierarchical temporal attention: (1) Spatially Local Temporal Attention, where each patch token attends only to patches at the same spatial position across all frames to capture fine-grained motion; and (2) Global Spatio-Temporal Attention, where UV Multi-Scale Temporal (MST) tokens at U=3 hierarchy levels (V=4 tokens each, temporal scale r=2) attend to patch tokens at progressively coarser frame strides, enabling multi-scale temporal summarization. A [CLS] token then aggregates information from all MST and patch tokens. The model is initialized from pretrained CLIP ViT-B/32 or ViT-B/16 checkpoints (with zero-initialized projection matrices for the new attention), trained for 3 epochs on YT-VidLA-800M using 128 A100 GPUs with batch size 4096, and supervised by dual info-NCE losses against both subtitle and caption text representations.

## Results

On MSR-VTT text-to-video retrieval, VidLA-B/32 with dual-softmax achieves R@1 of 60.9, outperforming the previous best two-tower method CLIPViP* (55.9) by 5.0 points. On the longer-video benchmarks, gains are even larger: VidLA-B/32* reaches R@1 of 62.2 on DiDeMo (+8.4 over CLIPViP*) and 69.2 on ActivityNet Captions (+10.1 over CLIPViP*). For video-to-text retrieval on MSR-VTT, VidLA-B/16* achieves R@1 of 63.1, a 6.9-point improvement over the prior best. On classification, VidLA-B/16 attains 84.9% top-1 on Kinetics-400 and 69.9% on Something-Something-v2, competitive with dense-pretraining methods like VideoMAE-v2 (81.5%/71.2%) despite using only contrastive alignment pretraining.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VidLA outperforms all two-tower baselines across every retrieval benchmark. On MSR-VTT, VidLA-B/32* (R@1=60.9) surpasses CLIPViP* (55.9) by 5.0 points and UATVR* (49.8) by 11.1 points in text-to-video retrieval; the gap widens to 8.4 points on DiDeMo (62.2 vs. 53.8) and 10.1 points on ActivityNet Captions (69.2 vs. 59.1), confirming that the hierarchical temporal attention is most beneficial for longer videos. On VATEX, VidLA-B/32* (73.7) beats DRL (63.5) by 7.2 points. For video-to-text on MSR-VTT, VidLA-B/16* (63.1) exceeds UATVR* (51.1) by 12.0 points. Among two-stage re-ranking methods, VidLA-B/32* (60.9) still surpasses InternVideo ViT-L (55.2) and UMT (51.0) on MSR-VTT T2V without requiring a separate cross-modal fusion stage. On classification, VidLA-B/16 (84.9% K400, 69.9% Sth-v2) is competitive with VideoMAE-v2 (81.5%/71.2%) and VideoSwin (82.7%/69.6%) despite using only contrastive alignment pretraining rather than dense reconstruction objectives.

## Relation to Prior Work

VidLA extends the CLIP two-tower architecture from image-text to video-text alignment, departing from prior video-language approaches that either employ complex hierarchical space-time attention (making pretrained image-text initialization difficult) or factorized space-time attention (which over-aggregates redundant local information). Rather than modifying the backbone architecture, it introduces Multi-Scale Temporal (MST) tokens that operate at different temporal resolutions within a simple transformer, preserving compatibility with pretrained CLIP encoders. On the data side, it departs from the dominant practice of relying on ASR subtitles (which have poor visual grounding) by leveraging multi-modal LLMs (BLIP-2) to generate visually grounded captions and LLMs to summarize texts, creating the largest video-language dataset to date (~800M pairs) with clips of varying durations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses two persistent bottlenecks in video-language alignment: the architectural tension between temporal modeling and pretrained image-text initialization, and the scarcity of large-scale semantically aligned video-text data. The 800M dataset with multi-scale temporal clips and LLM-curated captions is likely to become a standard resource for the community. However, the approach remains limited to contrastive alignment pretraining, and its classification results (Table 7) are competitive but not state-of-the-art compared to dense-pretraining methods like VideoMAE, suggesting that alignment-focused objectives may not capture all fine-grained features needed for dense prediction tasks. Open questions include whether the hierarchical temporal attention generalizes to very long-form videos (minutes to hours) and whether the LLM-generated captions introduce systematic biases that affect downstream fairness.
