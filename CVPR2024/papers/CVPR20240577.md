## Problem

Monocular depth estimation models pretrained on terrestrial datasets suffer severe performance degradation on underwater scenes due to light attenuation, backscatter, and water turbidity, yet acquiring large-scale real underwater image-depth pairs is prohibitively expensive and limited in diversity. Existing synthetic alternatives (GAN-based style transfer) produce unrealistic images with a large domain gap, while real datasets like Sea-thru and SQUID are small and have sparse, unreliable depth from stereo pairs. The paper addresses this by asking whether diffusion-based generation can produce photorealistic underwater scenes that are both visually convincing and paired with accurate depth, enabling supervised training of terrestrial depth models for underwater use.

## Input / Output

The generation pipeline takes terrestrial depth maps (from DIODE-outdoor, 400 images) and text prompts (e.g., 'an underwater view of Atlantis') as input, producing photorealistic underwater images that preserve the scene layout of the depth map; each depth map is sampled four times per prompt, yielding 3,200 image-depth pairs in the final Atlantis dataset. For the downstream depth estimation task, a single underwater RGB image is the input and a per-pixel depth map (capped at 20 m, consistent with backscatter-dominated radiance) is the output. The intermediate ControlNet training set consists of 700 {underwater image, MiDaS-estimated depth, BLIP2 caption} triplets from the UIEB dataset.

## Method

The core pipeline has three stages: (1) construct 700 {Underwater, Depth, Text} triplets by running MiDaS for depth and BLIP2 for captions on UIEB underwater images; (2) train a Depth2Underwater ControlNet (only the ControlNet branch is trainable; the pretrained Stable Diffusion v1.5 backbone is frozen) so that it can condition SD generation on a depth map to produce underwater imagery; (3) generate the Atlantis dataset by feeding 400 DIODE-outdoor depth maps with text prompts through the trained ControlNet + SD, sampling four times per prompt to obtain 3,200 diverse underwater scenes whose depth is inherited directly from the terrestrial source. The key distinction from prior GAN-based synthesis is that SD + ControlNet creates entirely new, non-existent underwater scenes rather than applying a style filter to existing terrestrial content, which the authors verify via LLaVA v1.5 (100% vs. 18% underwater-scene agreement). Terrestrial depth models (iDisc, NeWCRFs, IEBins, VA-DepthNet, all using Swin-L encoders) are then trained from scratch on Atlantis and evaluated on unseen underwater benchmarks (Sea-thru, SQUID, UIEB), showing consistent large improvements over their KITTI/NYU-pretrained counterparts.

## Results

On the Sea-thru dataset (D3+D5), all four depth models trained on Atlantis achieve dramatic improvements over their KITTI- or NYU Depthv2-pretrained counterparts. For example, VA-DepthNet's RMSE drops from 7.842 (KITTI) to 1.204 (Atlantis), and its δ1 rises from 0.025 to 0.648. On SQUID, similar gains are observed: iDisc's RMSE drops from 7.265 (KITTI) to 2.663 (Atlantis) and δ1 jumps from 0.156 to 0.637. The improvements are consistent across all models and both test datasets, confirming that Atlantis effectively closes the terrestrial-to-underwater domain gap.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is not between different architectures but between the same depth model trained on terrestrial data (KITTI or NYU Depthv2) versus the same model trained from scratch on the proposed Atlantis dataset. All four models (iDisc, NeWCRFs, IEBins, VA-DepthNet) show consistent and large improvements when trained on Atlantis: RMSE drops by 50–85% and δ1 increases by 3–25× across both Sea-thru and SQUID test sets. For instance, VA-DepthNet trained on Atlantis achieves δ1=0.648 on Sea-thru versus 0.025 for its KITTI-pretrained version, and δ1=0.705 on SQUID versus 0.148. NYU Depthv2 pretraining generally performs slightly better than KITTI pretraining on underwater scenes (smaller domain gap for indoor scenes), but both are far outperformed by Atlantis training. In the domain-gap analysis (Section 4.4), the paper also compares against UW-GAN [24,51] using LLaVA v1.5: 100% of Atlantis-generated images are correctly identified as underwater scenes by the LLVM, versus only 18% for UW-GAN, confirming a substantially smaller domain gap for the diffusion-based synthesis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Stable Diffusion and ControlNet—previously used for open-ended text-to-image generation—as a targeted data synthesis engine for underwater depth estimation. It departs from the dominant GAN-based underwater synthesis paradigm (UW-GAN, Hambarde et al.) by generating entirely novel underwater scenes conditioned on terrestrial depth maps rather than style-transferring existing terrestrial images, thereby preserving scene layout while changing content. The depth estimation models evaluated (iDisc, NeWCRFs, IEBins, VA-DepthNet) are existing terrestrial architectures retrained from scratch on the generated dataset, so the contribution is the data pipeline rather than a new estimation architecture. Auxiliary tools (MiDaS for depth, BLIP2 for captioning) are used off-the-shelf to construct the intermediate training triplets for the Depth2Underwater ControlNet.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a fundamental bottleneck in underwater depth estimation—data scarcity—by showing that a small (3,200-pair) diffusion-generated dataset can dramatically close the domain gap for multiple existing depth models, with practical downstream utility demonstrated via Sea-thru enhancement. The 100% vs. 18% LLVM agreement rate against UW-GAN provides a compelling, if single-model, validation that diffusion-generated scenes are semantically more faithful to the underwater domain than GAN style transfers. Open questions include whether scaling the dataset (currently limited to 400 source depth maps) or hybrid training with real data yields further gains, whether the MiDaS-derived depth in the intermediate dataset introduces systematic errors that cap performance, and whether the approach generalizes to other domain-shift problems (e.g., adverse weather, low-light).
