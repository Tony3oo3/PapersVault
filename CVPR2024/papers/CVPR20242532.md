## Problem

Existing pose refinement methods require training scene-specific features or implicit representations optimized with a camera pose-based loss, raising the question of whether such specialized training is truly necessary. The paper investigates whether generic, off-the-shelf pre-trained features can serve as an effective cost function for pose refinement without any per-scene training or fine-tuning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242532/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a query RGB image, an initial 6-DoF pose hypothesis (from retrieval or a matching pipeline), and a renderable scene model (a textured mesh or 3D Gaussian Splatting) as input, and outputs a refined camera pose parameterized as a 3D center and unit quaternion. The scene model must support efficient rendering at arbitrary poses; in practice, low-detail compressed meshes render in ~500 µs and Gaussian Splatting in 600–900 µs on an RTX 4090. The method is agnostic to the specific scene representation, requiring only that a view can be generated for any candidate pose.

## Method

MCLoc operates within a render-and-compare framework: candidate poses are perturbed via a multi-beam particle filter on the SO(3)×T(3) manifold, rendered against the scene model, and scored by pixelwise L2 distance between normalized dense features extracted from an off-the-shelf CNN (ResNet-18 fine-tuned for place recognition). A coarse-to-fine strategy exploits the hierarchical structure of deep features—deeper layers with larger receptive fields and wider convergence basins are used in early iterations to handle large initial errors, while shallower layers with higher spatial resolution are switched in for fine-grained alignment. The particle filter employs multi-hypothesis beam tracking with periodic resampling to explore the hypothesis space in breadth, gradually reducing beam count and increasing image resolution as optimization converges. The key insight is that pre-trained dense features exhibit nicely shaped convex basins around each pose, making them unreasonably effective as a pose-similarity metric without any task-specific training.

## Results

On Cambridge Landmarks, MCLoc (no per-scene training) achieves median translation/rotation errors of 0.31/0.42° (King's), 0.39/0.73° (Hospital), 0.12/0.45° (Shop), and 0.26/0.88° (St. Mary's), outperforming all pose regressors and most per-scene pose refiners. On the large-scale Aachen Day-Night v1.1 benchmark, MCLoc starting from retrieval achieves 55.8/73.3/89.7% recall at (25cm,2°)/(50cm,5°)/(5m,10°) for Day queries, and when applied as post-processing on top of hloc, improves Day recall to 87.9/94.9/98.9% and Night recall to 73.8/88.5/97.9%. On 7scenes (indoor), MCLoc achieves competitive but slightly weaker results (e.g., 5/1.8 cm/° on Chess) compared to per-scene refiners like NeFeS (2/0.8), reflecting the challenge of textureless indoor surfaces.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MCLoc outperforms all pose regressors (MS-Transformer, DFNet, LENS) by a wide margin on Cambridge Landmarks, e.g., 0.31/0.42 vs. 0.33/0.50 (LENS, best regressor) on King's. Among per-scene pose refiners, MCLoc beats CROSSFIRE and NeFeS on most scenes but trails FQN slightly on King's (0.31 vs. 0.28 m translation). On Aachen Day-Night, MCLoc from retrieval (55.8/73.3/89.7% Day) is competitive with PixLoc (63.2/67.8/75.5%), winning on the two coarser thresholds but losing on the finest (25cm,2°) for Day queries. As a post-processing step on hloc, MCLoc improves Day recall from 87.4 to 87.9% at (25cm,2°) and Night recall from 71.7 to 73.8%, confirming its complementary role to matching-based SOTA. On 7scenes, MCLoc (5/1.8 cm/° on Chess) is competitive with NeFeS (2/0.8) and CROSSFIRE (1/0.4) but lags, as indoor textureless surfaces reduce the discriminative power of perceptual similarity. The paper also shows MCLoc can serve as preprocessing for matching pipelines (Table 4), improving LoFTR+MeshLoc Night recall from 71.2 to 74.3% at (25cm,2°) while reducing top-K from 20 to 10.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the render-and-compare framework for pose refinement by replacing the learned, task-specific features used by prior methods (PixLoc, FQN, NeFeS, CROSSFIRE) with generic, off-the-shelf pre-trained CNN features as a pixelwise similarity cost. The optimization mechanism draws from Monte Carlo particle filtering on Lie groups [22, 23], which had been applied to localization with radiance fields [66, 69] but not yet combined with mesh-based rendering and dense feature scoring. Conceptually, the work builds on the LPIPS finding [116] that deep features are robust perceptual similarity estimators, extending that observation from image quality assessment to pose discrepancy measurement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that pose refinement can be achieved without per-scene training or fine-tuning, removing a significant deployment barrier and enabling the method to scale to arbitrarily large scenes where implicit field approaches fail. However, matching-based methods (hloc, AS) still hold state-of-the-art accuracy, and indoor scenes with textureless, repetitive surfaces remain challenging for the perceptual-similarity signal. Open questions include whether task-specific feature training (e.g., RoMA) can close the remaining gap, whether the approach transfers to other 3D representations beyond meshes and Gaussian Splatting, and whether the particle filter can be replaced by more efficient optimizers once the convergence basin is well understood.
