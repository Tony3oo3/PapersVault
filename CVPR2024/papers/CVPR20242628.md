## Problem

Facial-based depression recognition (DR) inherently captures biometric identity information, creating serious privacy risks for patients. Existing privacy-preserving approaches (e.g., landmark extraction) still require capturing and processing visually identifiable facial images, leaving a gap at the data acquisition stage. This paper asks whether identity information can be erased at the optical imaging level—before any digital image is formed—while retaining the depression-relevant features needed for accurate DR.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242628/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The input is a facial scene imaged through a learnable single thin lens whose surface profile is parameterized by Zernike polynomial coefficients; the resulting sensor-plane image is a wave-based convolution of the scene with the lens PSF, not a standard camera capture. The output is a continuous depression score on the BDI-II scale (0–63), predicted by a fusion head that combines emotion and depression features. Critically, no identifiable facial image is ever stored or transmitted—the privacy protection is enforced at the hardware (optical) level rather than by post-hoc anonymization.

## Method

OpticalDR couples a differentiable optical model (a single thin lens whose PSF is computed from Zernike-parameterized surface profiles via wave-based image formation) with a Spatial Attention Network (ResNet backbone + SA module) in an end-to-end pipeline. Training follows a four-stage progressive strategy: (1) the lens is pretrained to maximize visual distortion and identity confusion (inverse triplet loss + MSE) while preserving facial landmarks; (2) the SANet is fine-tuned for emotion classification on the lens-captured images; (3) a second SANet is fine-tuned from the emotion model for coarse depression-level classification using a histogram loss over BDI-II bins; (4) a multimodal transformer fuses the emotion and depression feature vectors to regress the final continuous score. The key insight is that the lens acts as a task-specific, irreversible feature extractor that discards identity while preserving depression-relevant spatial patterns, and the progressive curriculum leverages large-scale identity and emotion datasets to bootstrap the DR model without requiring a single dataset containing all labels.

## Results

On AVEC 2013, OpticalDR achieves MAE 7.53 and RMSE 8.48; on AVEC 2014, it achieves MAE 7.89 and RMSE 8.82. In terms of privacy preservation, the learned optical lens reduces facial recognition AUC to 0.51 across all four tested models (Facenet, IR152, IRSE50, ArcFace), effectively erasing identity to chance level. This is achieved while maintaining competitive DR accuracy, outperforming other privacy-preserving variants (ViT+lens, STA-DRN(SA)+lens) by 0.6–1.1 MAE points on both datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among privacy-preserving methods, OpticalDR clearly outperforms ViT+lens (MAE 7.53 vs. 8.16 on AVEC 2013; 7.89 vs. 8.25 on AVEC 2014) and STA-DRN(SA)+lens (MAE 7.53 vs. 8.62 on AVEC 2013; 7.89 vs. 8.67 on AVEC 2014), demonstrating that the progressive multi-task training strategy is critical when combining optics with deep networks. LQGDNet+lens degrades severely (MAE 10.56 on AVEC 2013), confirming that simply bolting a lens onto an existing architecture without co-optimization is ineffective. OpticalDR does not surpass LQGDNet without privacy (MAE 6.38 on AVEC 2013), which relies on visible facial images and quaternion-based local feature extraction; the ~1.15 MAE gap reflects the inherent cost of erasing identity information. However, OpticalDR's RMSE of 8.48 on AVEC 2013 is actually lower than the non-privacy STA-DRN(SA) (9.57) and ViT (10.67), suggesting the learned lens acts as a beneficial feature filter for depression-relevant signals. The privacy guarantee is strong: AUC drops to 0.51 (chance level) on all four face recognition models, whereas Gaussian blur (σ=50) and defocus (d=275) achieve similar AUC but leak additional sensitive cues like skin color and gender.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Deep Optics framework (Sitzmann et al., 2018), which parameterizes optical elements as learnable layers co-optimized with downstream networks, to the domain of privacy-preserving depression recognition. Prior DR approaches either use raw facial images (STA-DRN, LQGDNet) or extract features like landmarks from visible images (AVEC 2017 protocol), both of which still require capturing identifiable facial data. OpticalDR departs from these by placing a learnable Zernike-parameterized thin lens in front of the sensor so that no identifiable facial image is ever generated, and introduces a progressive multi-task training strategy (identity → emotion → depression → fusion) to compensate for the lack of a single dataset with all required labels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

OpticalDR demonstrates that hardware-level privacy protection (no facial image captured or stored) is compatible with competitive depression recognition, addressing a real gap in medical AI where patient facial data is highly sensitive. However, the entire optical model is simulation-based with no physical lens deployment, and the DR performance (MAE 7.53 on AVEC 2013) still trails the best non-privacy method LQGDNet (MAE 6.38), suggesting a meaningful accuracy-privacy trade-off remains. Open questions include whether the Zernike-parameterized lens can be physically fabricated, whether the approach generalizes to video/temporal depression cues, and whether the progressive learning strategy transfers to other privacy-sensitive biometric tasks.
