## Problem

While prior computational work on gender representation in films has quantified screen time and speaking time, it has not addressed the complex temporal and visual patterns that produce the perception of character objectification (the 'male gaze'). No existing dataset or computational approach operationalizes this high-level interpretive construct for video data, leaving a gap between film-studies theory and machine-learning practice.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240909/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes movie clips of 1–5 minutes (1914 clips from 12 films, drawn from the MovieGraphs dataset) as input and produces a binary objectification label (Sure vs. Easy/Hard Negative) along with presence labels for eight visual concepts (Type of shot, Look, Body, Posture, Clothing, Appearance, Expression of emotion, Activity). A key design choice is the inclusion of Hard Negative clips—scenes containing objectification elements from the thesaurus that do not produce an overall objectification effect—enabling fine-grained error analysis that distinguishes compositional sufficiency from mere element presence.

## Method

The paper evaluates frozen pre-trained video encoders (X-CLIP trained on Kinetics, ViViT-B/16) with a lightweight MLP adapter (adaptive max pooling → 2 dense layers, 128 hidden units, ReLU, 2-way softmax) for binary objectification classification. For interpretability, it implements Post-hoc Concept Bottleneck Models: a linear SVM is trained per concept to extract a Concept Activation Vector in the X-CLIP embedding space, and clips are projected onto the 8-dimensional CAV subspace before feeding an interpretable classifier (decision tree or logistic regression). The central empirical finding is that objectification is compositional—the average number of annotated concepts per clip rises from 1.26 (Hard Negative) to 2.6 (Sure)—and that Hard Negative examples significantly improve F1-scores, confirming that distinguishing element presence from objectification effect requires fine-grained annotation. Error analysis via logistic regression on clip descriptors identifies Type of shot, Look, Posture, and Appearance as the concepts most poorly captured by current pre-trained embeddings.

## Results

On the ObyGaze12 dataset, the best model (X-CLIP with MLP adapter) achieves an F1-score of 0.82 on the hardest binary classification setting (distinguishing Sure from Easy+Hard Negatives, trained on Hard Negatives), compared to 0.73 for ViViT-B/16. The task is confirmed feasible as all models substantially outperform the random baseline (F1 ≈ 0.55) and the all-positive baseline (F1 ≈ 0.06–0.08). Including Hard Negative examples in training consistently improves F1 by 0.09–0.11 over Easy Negative-only training. The interpretability-focused PCBM variants (DT and LR classifiers on CAV projections) sacrifice 0.10–0.44 F1 points relative to direct X-CLIP classification, reflecting the known accuracy-interpretability tradeoff.

## Baselines & Comparisons

X-CLIP (Ni et al., ECCV 2022) outperforms ViViT-B/16 (Arnab et al., ICCV 2021) across all four evaluation settings, with the largest gap on the hardest configuration: 0.82 vs. 0.73 F1 when trained on Hard Negatives and tested on all negatives. On the simpler EN-vs-S setting, X-CLIP leads by 0.26 F1 (0.79 vs. 0.53) when trained on Easy Negatives, though the gap narrows to 0.09 when both are trained on Hard Negatives (0.71 vs. 0.62). The paper's own PCBM variants (DT and LR classifiers over CAV projections) consistently underperform direct X-CLIP classification by 0.10–0.44 F1, confirming the expected accuracy-interpretability tradeoff of concept bottleneck models. Notably, PCBM performance drops sharply (to 0.37–0.44 F1) when the task requires distinguishing Sure from Hard Negatives, revealing that X-CLIP embeddings cannot linearly separate the subtler concepts (Type of Shot, Look, Posture, Appearance) that differentiate objectifying from non-objectifying scenes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior film-bias work (Guha et al., Somandepalli et al., Mazieres et al., Jang et al.) that quantified gender representation through presence metrics (screen time, speaking time, one-face frames) by introducing a high-level interpretive construct—objectification—operationalized through dense concept annotations over video. It builds on the MovieGraphs dataset for clip selection and social-reasoning annotations, and on X-CLIP/ViViT for video embeddings, but the key methodological departure is the use of Post-hoc Concept Bottleneck Models with Concept Activation Vectors to perform interpretable, concept-level error analysis on a subjective video-interpretation task. The annotation protocol draws on NLP propaganda-detection practices (Da San Martino et al.) and medical dense-annotation approaches (Daneshjou et al.), adapted to the temporal and multi-concept nature of film objectification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first computational benchmark for detecting a high-level, theory-driven construct (objectification) in video, filling a gap where prior work addressed only presence quantification or still-image framing. The finding that objectification is compositional—average annotated concepts per clip rises from 1.26 (Hard Negative) to 2.6 (Sure)—corroborates recent neuroscience evidence that no single visual cue suffices, and suggests future models must capture multi-concept temporal interactions rather than single-frame features. Key open questions include how to scale dense, expert-level annotation beyond 12 films, how to improve representation of subtle concepts (Type of Shot, Look, Posture, Appearance) that current embeddings fail to linearly separate, and whether the moderate F1 scores (~0.82) will improve with larger, more diverse datasets or task-specific pre-training.
