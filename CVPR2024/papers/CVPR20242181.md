## Problem

SOTA image deblurring networks employ heavily designed encoders but are forced to use lightweight decoders due to GPU memory constraints, capping their performance at a saturation point that additional training iterations cannot surpass. The paper asks whether inheriting a well-trained encoder and refactoring the decoder with substantially higher capacity can push beyond this ceiling, and how to do so in a memory-efficient manner.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242181/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB blur image as input and produces a deblurred sharp image as output, computed as the blur image plus a predicted residual (blur pattern). During training, 256×256 patches are used with batch size 16; at test time, a 384×384 sliding window with 32-pixel overlap handles full-resolution images. A key structural detail is that each sub-decoder independently produces a full sharp prediction from the same encoder features, so the network has K parallel output heads rather than a single sequential decoder path.

## Method

AdaRevD freezes a pre-trained encoder (from UFPNet or NAFNet) and trains K reversible sub-decoders, each composed of four Level modules (FuseBlock + FourierBlock) that take the encoder's degradation representation as input and produce a sharp prediction. The reversible architecture (following RevCol) allows all K sub-decoders to be trained simultaneously with GPU memory comparable to a single decoder, since intermediate activations are recomputed via inverse operations rather than stored. A lightweight classifier (DownSample + NAFBlock + LayerNorm + MLP) predicts the degradation degree of each patch from encoder feature e4, and an early-exit signal based on per-class incremental PSNR thresholds determines which sub-decoder each patch exits at, reducing compute for easy patches. Training proceeds in two phases: decoder training with L1 + 2D Fourier loss applied uniformly across all sub-decoders, followed by classifier training with cross-entropy loss. The reversible structure is shown via CKA analysis to progressively disentangle high-level degradation degree (at deeper levels) from low-level blur pattern (at shallower levels) across successive sub-decoders.

## Results

On the GoPro dataset, AdaRevD-L achieves 34.60 dB PSNR and 0.972 SSIM, surpassing the previous SOTA FFTformer (34.21 dB) by 0.39 dB and UFPNet (34.06 dB) by 0.54 dB. On HIDE, AdaRevD-L reaches 32.35 dB PSNR, outperforming UFPNet (31.74 dB) by 0.61 dB. On RealBlur-R and RealBlur-J, AdaRevD-B/L achieve 36.53–36.60 dB and 30.12–30.14 dB respectively, exceeding UFPNet by 0.28–0.35 dB and 0.25–0.27 dB. The reversible architecture also reduces training memory: RevD-L uses 5,087 MB vs. 10,960 MB for a non-reversible 8-sub-decoder model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AdaRevD-L (34.60 dB) outperforms all prior SOTA methods on GoPro, beating FFTformer (34.21 dB) by 0.39 dB, UFPNet (34.06 dB) by 0.54 dB, and MRLPFNet (34.01 dB) by 0.59 dB. On HIDE, AdaRevD-L (32.35 dB) surpasses UFPNet (31.74 dB) by 0.61 dB and DeepRFT+ (31.66 dB) by 0.69 dB. On RealBlur-R, AdaRevD-B (36.56 dB) and RevD-L (36.60 dB) exceed UFPNet (36.25 dB) by 0.31–0.35 dB, while on RealBlur-J, AdaRevD-B (30.12 dB) beats UFPNet (29.87 dB) by 0.25 dB. The adaptive patch-exiting variant (AdaRevD) maintains near-identical performance to the full reversible decoder (RevD) while reducing sub-decoder utilization to ~84%, confirming that the classifier-based early exit introduces only a 0.014 dB PSNR drop. Notably, the paper also shows that simply continuing to train UFPNet with more iterations actually degrades performance (34.09 → 33.71 dB), validating the claim that the bottleneck is decoding capacity rather than training duration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the reversible column architecture from RevCol (Cai et al., ICLR 2023), which was designed for encoder-side classification, into a decoder-side mechanism for image deblurring. Rather than following the dominant trend of designing heavier encoders (as in UFPNet, NAFNet, MPRNet), it argues the performance ceiling is set by the lightweight decoder and proposes to inherit a frozen SOTA encoder while scaling up the decoder with multiple reversible sub-decoders. The adaptive patch-exiting strategy draws from super-resolution work (APE-SR, ClassSR) but is adapted to the UNet-based deblurring architecture, where early exit is structurally difficult without the multi-decoder design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a useful empirical argument that SOTA deblurring networks are decoder-limited rather than encoder-limited, supported by the CKA analysis showing gradual disentanglement of degradation degree and blur pattern across sub-decoders. The reversible architecture offers a practical ~2× memory saving at scale (5,087 MB vs 10,960 MB for 8 sub-decoders), making larger decoders feasible on consumer GPUs. However, the absolute performance gain over UFPNet is modest (~0.5 dB on GoPro), and the adaptive exiting saves compute with only marginal quality loss, leaving open whether this decoder-scaling paradigm generalizes to other restoration tasks or to encoder-side improvements.
