## Problem

Multi-task learning for dense prediction tasks typically requires fully labeled datasets where every sample carries ground-truth for all tasks, but curating such data is prohibitively expensive (e.g., 1.5+ hours per Cityscapes segmentation mask). Existing partially-labeled MTL methods either apply single-task consistency regularization that ignores cross-task relations, or use pair-wise task mappings that scale quadratically with the number of tasks. The paper asks whether all tasks can be regularized jointly in a single learned latent space with linear complexity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242570/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image (H×W) and produces dense predictions for K tasks simultaneously (segmentation masks, depth maps, surface normal maps, edge occlusion, reshading, 2D keypoints, edge texture), each with task-specific output channels O_t. The key structural detail is that all K task predictions are concatenated along the channel dimension into a joint tensor of shape (ΣO_t)×H×W, which serves as the unit of computation for the JTR encoder. For partially labeled samples, the target tensor substitutes the model's own detached predictions in place of missing ground-truth labels, so the encoder always sees a complete multi-task representation.

## Method

JTR introduces a learned encoder-decoder (g_θ1 ∘ g_θ2) that maps the stacked multi-task prediction tensor into a single joint-task latent space and back. The training objective combines a supervised loss on labeled tasks with a distance loss between the encoded prediction tensor and an encoded target tensor (ground-truth where available, detached predictions otherwise), plus a reconstruction loss to prevent the encoder from collapsing to a trivial single-point mapping. Because all tasks are encoded into one shared latent space simultaneously, gradients from the distance loss propagate through all task decoders at once, capturing higher-order cross-task relations that pair-wise methods cannot model. This yields linear complexity in the number of tasks versus the quadratic scaling of pair-wise approaches like MTPSL, while the learned metric space outperforms naive pixel-wise distances.

## Results

On NYU-v2 'randomlabels', JTR achieves 37.08 mIoU (segmentation), 0.5541 aErr (depth), and 29.44 mErr (normals), yielding a +21.92% mean improvement over Supervised MTL—outperforming MTPSL (+19.66%) and Perceptual Map (+10.80%). On Cityscapes 'onelabel', JTR reaches 72.33 mIoU and 0.0163 aErr (+8.22% over baseline), surpassing MTPSL (+6.70%). On Taskonomy 'halflabels' (7 tasks), JTR achieves +5.08% mean improvement versus MTPSL's +3.16% and Consistency Reg's +2.33%, while also avoiding the large per-task degradations (e.g., −20.7% on keypoints) that MTPSL suffers under 'randomlabels'.

## Baselines & Comparisons

JTR consistently outperforms MTPSL [33] (the strongest prior method) across all three datasets and all partially labeled settings: on NYU-v2 'randomlabels' JTR gains +21.92% vs. MTPSL's +19.66%, on Cityscapes 'onelabel' +8.22% vs. +6.70%, and on Taskonomy 'halflabels' +5.08% vs. +3.16%. Against the cross-task consistency baselines from Zamir et al. [74] (Direct Map and Perceptual Map), JTR achieves substantially larger gains—e.g., +13.97% vs. +1.84% (Perceptual Map) and −13.55% (Direct Map, which actually degrades performance) on NYU-v2 'onelabel'. JTR also clearly surpasses Consistency Regularization [19, 29, 48, 57] by a wide margin in every setting (e.g., +21.92% vs. +5.30% on NYU-v2 'randomlabels'), confirming that cross-task joint regularization is far more effective than per-task input-augmentation consistency. Finally, the MuST pseudo-labeling framework [21] underperforms even naive Supervised MTL on NYU-v2 'randomlabels' (−2.38% for STL teachers, +8.58% for the MTL student), while JTR reaches +21.92%, demonstrating that teacher-student pseudo-labeling is ill-suited to the partially labeled MTL regime where labeled subsets are too small to train reliable per-task teachers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the pair-wise cross-task regularization paradigm established by MTPSL [33] and cross-task consistency methods [74], which model inter-task dependencies by learning a separate mapping for every pair of tasks (quadratic in the number of tasks). JTR instead introduces a single joint-task latent space learned via an autoencoder, regularizing all K tasks simultaneously with a single distance loss (linear complexity). The approach also builds on consistency regularization [19, 29, 48, 57] as the baseline semi-supervised strategy and contrasts with pseudo-labeling frameworks like MuST [21], which the authors argue are difficult to combine across multiple tasks. Conceptually, JTR repurposes the autoencoder bottleneck as a learned metric space for cross-task alignment, avoiding the architectural modifications (FiLM layers, per-pair optimizers) that MTPSL requires.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The linear-scaling property of JTR becomes practically significant as the number of tasks grows: on Taskonomy (7 tasks), JTR cuts training time roughly in half (105h vs 223h) and VRAM by ~30% compared to MTPSL, making partially labeled MTL more tractable for large task suites. The method's resilience to task imbalance (depth-extended NYU-v2) without additional weighting tricks is a useful practical property. However, the paper is limited to dense prediction tasks on single-domain data, and extending JTR to heterogeneous output types (classification + regression + pixel-level) or multi-domain settings remains open. The moderate performance margins over MTPSL (typically 1–5 points in Δ%) suggest the joint-task space captures cross-task relations that pair-wise methods miss, but the gap is not large enough to suggest a fundamental limitation of the pair-wise approach.
