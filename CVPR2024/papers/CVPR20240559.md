## Problem

The dominant paradigm for video-language understanding transfers pre-trained image encoders to video via shallow temporal fusion, which limits the number of frames that can be processed due to quadratic memory growth and degrades spatial capabilities on noisy video-language data. No systematic recipe exists for scaling video-first encoders with early temporal aggregation beyond the typical 16-frame window to minutes-long videos without prohibitive memory costs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240559/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The video encoder consumes up to 256 frames at 1 FPS (4.3 minutes), patchified into 3D spatiotemporal tubelets via a 3D convolution, with up to 75% of tubelet tokens randomly masked during training. The text side accepts captions, queries, or QA prompts. Outputs include a pooled video-text embedding for retrieval, and autoregressive text generation (captions, summaries, multiple-choice answers) produced by a frozen 400M–1B LM conditioned on the frozen video encoder via cross-attention layers and a Perceiver resampler.

## Method

The paper proposes a two-stage recipe: (1) contrastive pre-training of a ViViT-based encoder with joint space-time attention and 3D tubelet patchification using NCE loss, and (2) video-to-text tuning with a frozen LM, Perceiver resampler, and randomly initialized cross-attention layers. The key finding is that random masking of up to 75% of input video tokens during contrastive pre-training is the most robust and memory-efficient scaling strategy, yielding only ~1% Recall@1 drop on retrieval and no captioning degradation while providing 2–3× memory savings. This outperforms factorized attention, parameter-efficient adaptation (LoRA, MLP Adapters), and multi-resolution TubeViT patchification, which all suffer 25% relative performance drops on temporally rich benchmarks. For long-video adaptation (SHORTVIVIT → LONGVIVIT), the paper further freezes most encoder parameters and tunes only the last four layers, combining high masking with widthwise tuning to achieve an additional 3× memory reduction without performance loss.

## Results

On long video benchmarks (256 frames), LONGVIVIT achieves 20.3 Rouge-L on YouCook2 summarization and 56.8% accuracy on EgoSchema QA (zero-shot), substantially outperforming SHORTVIVIT (7.0 / 47.9) and modular LLM-assisted approaches (PALI-to-Bard: 19.9 / 44.8). On short video benchmarks, SHORTVIVIT-L matches or exceeds VideoCoCa-L on YouCook2 retrieval (20.4 vs. 18.9 T2V Recall@1) and significantly outperforms it on YouCook2 captioning (21.0 vs. 20.7 CIDEr zero-shot). The efficient variant with 75% input masking (Efficient SHORTVIVIT-L) incurs only ~1% absolute Recall@1 drop while providing 2–3× memory savings, and fine-tuned LONGVIVIT reaches 30.6 Rouge-L on YouCook2, approaching the SoTA VLTint (34.6) which uses specialized multi-network features.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LONGVIVIT decisively outperforms all modular LLM-assisted baselines on temporally rich benchmarks: it beats PALI-5B-to-Bard by 11.8 points on EgoSchema (56.8 vs. 44.8) and by 0.4 on YouCook2 (20.3 vs. 19.9), and surpasses SeViLA-to-SHORTVIVIT by 16.1 points on YouCook2 (20.3 vs. 4.2) and 7.2 on EgoSchema (56.8 vs. 49.6). Even with oracle event segmentation, Bard-based aggregation (18.2 Rouge-L on YouCook2) cannot match LONGVIVIT's direct 256-frame encoding (20.3). On ActivityNet, which has weaker temporal dependencies, modular approaches (SHORTVIVIT-to-Bard: 19.3) slightly outperform LONGVIVIT (15.2) in zero-shot, and fine-tuned VLTint (36.9) still leads over LONGVIVIT (24.0). On short video benchmarks, SHORTVIVIT-L matches VideoCoCa-L on YouCook2 retrieval (20.4 vs. 18.9 T2V) and outperforms it on captioning (21.0 vs. 20.7 CIDEr), though the much larger VideoCoCa-2.1B (34.3 CIDEr) and Flamingo-3B (55.8 CIDEr on YouCook2) remain stronger on captioning due to their larger LM components.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the two-step video-language recipe established by Flamingo and BLIP-2 (pre-train a vision encoder via contrastive learning, then fuse frozen visual representations into a frozen LM), but departs from the dominant image-first paradigm (VideoCoCa, Flamingo) by adopting video-first encoders with joint space-time attention from ViViT. It systematically evaluates memory-efficient alternatives drawn from multiple lines of work—factorized attention, parameter-efficient adaptation (LoRA, MLP Adapters), input masking (inspired by VideoMAE), and multi-resolution patchification (TubeViT)—to identify which enable scaling beyond 16 frames. The key conceptual departure is from the modular long-video approach of captioning segments and aggregating via LLMs, showing that a direct video encoder can outperform this on temporally rich benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that masking up to 75% of video tokens during contrastive pre-training incurs negligible performance loss provides a simple, broadly applicable recipe for scaling video encoders to minutes-long inputs without architectural changes, which is practically important given memory constraints. The demonstration that a ~1B parameter video-first model outperforms modular approaches using much larger LLMs (Bard) for long-video summarization and QA challenges the assumption that LLM-based aggregation over frame captions is necessary for long-range temporal understanding. Open questions include whether the masking robustness generalizes to higher FPS or even longer contexts, whether the HowTo100M Summary data quality limits further gains, and whether the identified temporal-dependency benchmarks (YouCook2, EgoSchema) are sufficient or whether the community needs more fine-grained temporal evaluation.
