## Problem

Existing inverse rendering methods rely on implicit neural representations (e.g., NeRF) that suffer from low expressive capacity and high computational cost, preventing interactive-rate rendering. While 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) achieves real-time novel view synthesis, it has never been adapted for inverse rendering due to two fundamental incompatibilities: 3DGS does not natively produce reliable surface normals, and its forward-mapping rasterization cannot trace occlusions the way NeRF's backward ray marching can.

## Input / Output

The method consumes calibrated multi-view RGB images captured under static but unknown natural illumination, along with camera extrinsic and intrinsic matrices. The output is a set of 3D Gaussians each storing per-point attributes (spherical harmonic color coefficients, opacity, surface normal, albedo, metallic value, and roughness), plus a learned environment map and precomputed occlusion/illumination volumes. This representation directly supports novel view synthesis, relighting under arbitrary lighting, and material editing at real-time rates.

## Method

GS-IR is a three-stage pipeline built on 3DGS with differentiable forward-mapping splatting. Stage 1 optimizes Gaussians for geometry using a depth-derivation-based normal regularization: depth is computed as a linear interpolation of per-Gaussian distances to the image plane (avoiding both the floating problem of volumetric accumulation and disc aliasing of peak selection), and the resulting depth gradient supervises normals stored in the Gaussians via a penalty loss plus TV smoothing. Stage 2 bakes occlusion into spherical harmonic (SH) coefficients by rendering six depth cubemaps per occlusion volume via forward-mapping passes, converting them to binary occlusion cubemaps, and convolving with SH bases—enabling efficient online indirect illumination without ray tracing. Stage 3 combines the splatting renderer with a Cook-Torrance PBR pipeline using split-sum approximation and image-based lighting, jointly optimizing material parameters, the environment map, and illumination volumes under a decomposition loss that includes shade, material-TV, and lighting-TV terms.

## Results

On the TensoIR Synthetic dataset, GS-IR achieves 35.333 PSNR and 0.039 LPIPS for novel view synthesis, outperforming TensoIR (35.088 PSNR) and significantly beating NeRFactor (24.679 PSNR) and InvRender (27.367 PSNR). For albedo reconstruction, GS-IR reaches 30.286 PSNR, surpassing all baselines including TensoIR (29.275) and NVDiffrec (29.174). On relighting, GS-IR achieves 24.374 PSNR, ranking second behind TensoIR (28.580) but well ahead of NeRFactor (23.383) and NVDiffrec (19.880). Training time is under 1 hour, a 5× speedup over TensoIR (5 hrs) and >100× faster than NeRFactor (>100 hrs).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GS-IR outperforms TensoIR [22] on novel view synthesis PSNR (35.333 vs. 35.088) and albedo PSNR (30.286 vs. 29.275), while TensoIR retains an edge on normal MAE (4.100 vs. 4.948) and relighting PSNR (28.580 vs. 24.374). Against NeRFactor [46], GS-IR improves NVS PSNR by over 10 dB (35.333 vs. 24.679) and reduces training time from >100 hours to under 1 hour. Compared to InvRender [47], GS-IR gains 7.96 dB in NVS PSNR (35.333 vs. 27.367) and 2.95 dB in albedo PSNR (30.286 vs. 27.341). NVDiffrec [32] is competitive on NVS PSNR (30.696) but lags significantly on albedo (29.174) and relighting (19.880). On Mip-NeRF 360, GS-IR (25.381 PSNR) surpasses NeRF++ (25.112) and Plenoxels (23.079) but trails dedicated NVS methods 3DGS (27.21) and Mip-NeRF 360 (27.569), which is expected since those methods do not perform material decomposition. The key advantage is that GS-IR achieves competitive NVS quality while simultaneously recovering geometry, materials, and illumination in under 45 minutes.

## Relation to Prior Work

GS-IR extends 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) from its original novel-view-synthesis role into the inverse rendering pipeline, departing from the dominant NeRF-based lineage (NeRFactor, TensoIR, InvRender) that relies on backward-mapping ray tracing for occlusion and normal computation. The key conceptual shift is replacing ray marching with forward-mapping splatting, which necessitates two novel adaptations: a depth-derivation-based regularization to produce reliable normals from the inherently loose 3DGS geometry, and a baking-based occlusion cache (inspired by real-time rendering techniques like Irradiance Volumes and Light Probes) to approximate indirect illumination without ray tracing. The PBR formulation (Cook-Torrance BRDF with split-sum approximation) follows standard graphics practice, while the spherical-harmonics occlusion volumes represent a new application of SH caching to the inverse rendering setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first 3DGS-based inverse rendering framework, GS-IR demonstrates that the efficiency gains of explicit Gaussian representations (5× faster training than TensoIR, real-time rendering) can be brought to physically-based scene decomposition without sacrificing novel-view-synthesis or albedo quality, and it extends the scope from object-level (TensoIR's limitation) to unbounded real scenes. The baking-based occlusion strategy is a practical and likely reusable solution to the fundamental incompatibility between forward-mapping rendering and per-pixel visibility queries. A stated limitation is that SH-based occlusion only captures low-frequency diffuse indirect illumination, leaving specular indirect lighting unmodeled—a gap the authors suggest could be addressed with screen-space global illumination techniques, pointing to an open direction for the field.
