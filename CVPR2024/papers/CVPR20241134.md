## Problem

Existing adversarial attacks on trajectory prediction models in autonomous driving exclusively target inference-time vulnerabilities, leaving training-time susceptibility to data poisoning entirely unexplored. No backdoor attack has been designed for trajectory prediction, despite these models being critical for safe motion planning. This paper addresses the gap by proposing the first adversarial backdoor attack that injects naturalistic, stealthy triggers into training data to force malicious predictions at inference time.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241134/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The attack consumes standard trajectory prediction inputs: past 2D agent coordinates over an observation horizon and vectorized HD map lane points (stacked 2D coordinates). It produces k poisoned training samples where the trigger is a perturbed trajectory of the closest vehicle to the AV and the disguise is a differentiable transformation applied to the road layout. The attack is model-agnostic and black-box: the surrogate model used to craft poisoned samples need not share architecture or even map encoding (vector vs. raster) with the victim model.

## Method

The attack proceeds in two phases. First, trigger generation applies an adversarial perturbation to the attacking vehicle's trajectory, constrained by a kinematic bicycle model for physical feasibility, optimizing a loss combining ADE, FDE, and lateral deviation metrics with dynamic bounds. Second, backdoor disguising solves a bi-level optimization that transforms the road layout via a differentiable point transformation (cosine-based curvature) so that the malicious future trajectory appears as a correct ground-truth (no off-road, heading aligned with the altered lane). The bi-level problem is approximated using gradient alignment: the lane transformation is optimized with signed Adam to align the full training gradient with an adversarial gradient computed only on the k poisoned samples, with periodic retraining of the surrogate network. Gradient-norm-based sample selection identifies the most potent benign samples for trigger injection, and the entire pipeline operates without access to the victim model's parameters or architecture.

## Results

On Argoverse, the proposed backdoor attack achieves tASR values of 83–93% and tCA values of 84–97% across four victim models (HiVT, TNT, MMTransformer, LaneGCN), demonstrating both high effectiveness and stealth. On nuScenes, tASR reaches 74–93% with tCA of 86–91% for the PGP and LaPred models. The attack succeeds with as little as 5% poisoning budget, and gradient-based sample selection improves tASR by up to 19% over random selection at low budgets. Existing gradient-shaping defences reduce tASR by only 2.6–4.6%, leaving the attack above 70% success rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates the backdoor attack across 6 victim models (PGP, LaPred on nuScenes; HiVT, TNT, MMTransformer, LaneGCN on Argoverse) using 2–4 different surrogate models per victim, all in a black-box setting. HiVT is the most vulnerable victim, reaching tASR of 93.12% (FDE) with MMTransformer as surrogate while maintaining 96.88% tCA, making the attack nearly undetectable. TNT shows the most variable tCA (73–92%) depending on the surrogate, with HiVT-as-surrogate producing the lowest tCA (73.16%), suggesting some surrogate-victim pairs generate harder-to-learn poison patterns. MMTransformer is the least vulnerable victim, with tASR ranging only 77.89–83.44%, yet still well above 70%. LaneGCN is consistently the most robust to increasing the number of attacking vehicles (q), with the smallest tCA drop (17% vs. 24% for HiVT at q=4). The attack transfers across representation encodings: vector-based surrogates (PGP) successfully attack rasterized-map victims (Trajectron++, AgentFormer) on nuScenes with comparable tCA/tASR trends. Gradient-shaping defences [23] reduce tASR by only 2.6% (HiVT) and 4.6% (MMTransformer), confirming the attack's resilience to existing mitigations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the backdoor/data-poisoning paradigm from image classification into trajectory prediction for autonomous driving, a domain where prior adversarial work (AdvDO, Bahari et al., Zhang et al.) has exclusively targeted inference-time vulnerabilities. The trigger generation step adapts the kinematic-constrained adversarial perturbation scheme from AdvDO, while the disguising step repurposes the lane transformation function from Bahari et al. and solves the resulting bi-level optimization via the gradient-alignment technique from Fowl et al. and Geiping et al. The key conceptual departure is the two-stage design—first crafting an effective trigger, then disguising it as a naturalistic road-layout change—combined with model-agnostic black-box operation, which had no precedent in the trajectory prediction security literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that a small fraction (5%) of stealthily poisoned training samples can force state-of-the-art prediction models to generate malicious trajectories with >83% success while remaining nearly undetectable (tCA > 80%), the paper raises a concrete and previously unaddressed security risk for deployed AD systems. The finding that gradient-shaping defences reduce attack success by only 2–6% suggests that current training-time protections are insufficient against naturalistic, dynamically-feasible poisoning, motivating the development of more robust detection or certification methods. Open questions include whether the attack generalizes to end-to-end driving stacks (perception + prediction + planning jointly), whether real-world data pipelines (sensor noise, map updates) would degrade the crafted triggers, and whether the bi-level optimization cost is practical at the scale of production training datasets.
