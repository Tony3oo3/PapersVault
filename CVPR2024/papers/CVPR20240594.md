## Problem

Existing diffusion-based video editing models focus on texture and attribute manipulation (appearance, background, style) but largely ignore motion, the most distinctive feature of video over images. Transferring motion from a reference video to a source while preserving the original protagonist's appearance and dynamic background remains unexplored, and naive ControlNet-based approaches fail because reference pose signals conflict with the source motion encoded in the inverted noise, producing ghosting and blurring artifacts.

## Input / Output

The method takes a source video (≥70 frames at 512×512), a reference video providing target motion, and a text prompt describing the scene. Skeletons are extracted via OpenPose and foreground masks via SAM from both videos. The output is a motion-edited video where the protagonist performs the reference motion while retaining the source's appearance and background. Inference requires one-shot learning (300 steps) on the source video followed by DDIM inversion, taking approximately 10 minutes per video on a single A100 GPU.

## Method

MotionEditor builds on Stable Diffusion's U-Net with ControlNet, augmented by a content-aware motion adapter that performs cross-attention between pose features (query) and source latent features (key/value) to resolve the conflict between reference motion and source content, alongside temporal attention and temporal convolution blocks for temporal consistency. A two-branch architecture separates reconstruction (preserving source appearance) from editing (applying target motion), connected by a high-fidelity attention injection mechanism that decouples keys and values into foreground and background components using a segmentation mask before injecting them into the editing branch's Consistent-Sparse Attention and Temporal Attention layers. The Consistent-Sparse Attention uses the current frame as query and concatenates preceding and current frames as key/value, avoiding the first-frame bias of prior sparse attention designs. A skeleton alignment algorithm resizes and translates the reference skeleton to match the source protagonist's size and position. Only the motion adapter and temporal attention are trained (one-shot, 300 steps); the attention injection is entirely training-free and active only at inference.

## Results

On 20 in-the-wild video editing cases, MotionEditor achieves the best CLIP score (28.86) and the lowest LPIPS values across all three variants: LPIPS-S (0.273), LPIPS-N (0.124), and LPIPS-T (0.082). The closest competitor, FateZero, scores 28.07 on CLIP and 0.308/0.176/0.124 on the three LPIPS metrics, while GAN-based methods (LWG, MRAA) lag furthest behind. In a user study, MotionEditor is preferred over all baselines in motion alignment (78.9%–96.3%), appearance alignment (74.5%–98.8%), and textual alignment (74.7%–92.6%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MotionEditor outperforms all seven baselines on every reported metric (CLIP, LPIPS-S, LPIPS-N, LPIPS-T). The closest competitor is FateZero (CLIP 28.07 vs. 28.86; LPIPS-T 0.124 vs. 0.082), which still exhibits overlapping frames of the protagonist's legs due to its attention fusion strategy being ill-suited for motion editing. GAN-based motion transfer methods (LWG, MRAA) perform worst overall, with CLIP scores below 27 and LPIPS-T above 0.20. Follow-Your-Pose fails to preserve source appearance (LPIPS-S 0.337) and suffers from ghosting due to motion conflict between inverted noise and reference pose. Tune-A-Video and ControlVideo show dramatic appearance changes (hairstyle, clothing texture, background) because their single-branch architecture cannot interact with original video features when pose conditions are introduced. MasaCtrl produces excessive blurry noise because its cross-attention-derived masks are unreliable and temporally inconsistent.

## Relation to Prior Work

MotionEditor builds on the Latent Diffusion Model (Stable Diffusion) as its backbone and ControlNet for pose-conditioned generation, while adopting the one-shot learning paradigm from Tune-A-Video to adapt temporal attention to a specific source video. It departs from human motion transfer (LWG, MRAA, FOMM), which animates still images, and from pose-guided video generation (Follow-Your-Pose, ControlVideo), which synthesizes new videos without preserving the source appearance. The key conceptual departure is the diagnosis that ControlNet's pose-derived control signals inherently conflict with the source motion encoded in DDIM-inverted noise, necessitating a content-aware adapter that bridges reference poses with source latent features rather than relying on pose conditioning alone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first diffusion-based method to perform motion editing on video while simultaneously preserving the dynamic per-frame background, camera movement, and protagonist appearance—capabilities that neither motion transfer (still-image-based) nor pose-guided generation (appearance-agnostic) provides. Practical limitations remain significant: the 10-minute inference time per video on a single A100, the requirement for one-shot learning per source video, and observed temporal inconsistencies in foreground subjects all constrain real-world deployment. Open questions include whether the two-branch attention injection generalizes to multi-subject scenes, whether the approach scales to longer videos beyond 70 frames, and whether the content-aware adapter can be made training-free to eliminate the per-video adaptation step.
