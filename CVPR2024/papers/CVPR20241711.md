## Problem

Existing training-free acceleration methods for diffusion models (e.g., DDIM, DPM-Solver) reduce inference steps by applying a single predicted noise direction over large timestep intervals, which introduces truncation error that accumulates progressively and severely degrades sample quality—especially at very low NFEs (≤10). The paper identifies that the root cause is using an inaccurate integral direction: the noise predicted at the interval's initial timestep is suboptimal for the entire interval, and no prior method corrects this without retraining the base model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241711/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

TimeTuner takes a frozen pre-trained noise prediction model ε_θ and a fixed discretization trajectory (0 = t_0 < t_1 < … < t_K = T) as input, and outputs a set of K optimized scalar timesteps τ_i that replace the original t_i as conditioning inputs to ε_θ during sampling. No architectural modification or weight update to the base DPM is required; the only learned parameters are the K scalars. For conditional generation, a label or text prompt is additionally passed through the standard conditioning pathway, and the method is compatible with classifier-free guidance at various scales.

## Method

The key insight, motivated by the mean value theorem, is that for a denoising interval [t_{i-1}, t_i], the noise predicted at some intermediate timestep τ_i (rather than at t_i) provides a more accurate integral direction. TimeTuner defines a generalized DE solver f_{θ,τ} that feeds τ_i instead of t_i into ε_θ, and trains each τ_i by minimizing a loss measuring the L2 discrepancy between ε_θ(f_{θ,τ}(x̃_{t_i}, τ_i), t_{i-1}) and ε_θ(x̃_{t_i}, t_i)—i.e., how well the tuned direction at the next step agrees with the original direction. Theorem 1 provides an upper bound on the distribution gap showing this loss directly controls the accumulative truncation error. Training is extremely lightweight (only scalar optimization, ~1 hour on A100 for NFE=10) and can be done sequentially (better quality) or in parallel (faster, slightly worse). The method is fully plug-in: it composes with any existing sampler (DDIM, DPM-Solver-2, EDM, Consistency Distillation) and any trajectory choice without modifying the base model.

## Results

On LSUN Bedroom (256×256, unconditional, NFE=10 via DDIM on LDM), TimeTuner reduces FID from 9.46 to 5.85 and sFID from 12.02 to 9.44, while also improving Recall from 0.34 to 0.44. On FFHQ, FID drops from 23.58 to 14.80 (a 37% reduction), and on ImageNet (label-conditioned), FID improves from 10.13 to 9.63 with sFID halved from 12.52 to 7.29. The improvement is most pronounced at low NFE: on FFHQ, the FID gap between DDIM and DDIM+Ours shrinks from 8.77 at NFE=10 to only 1.51 at NFE=25, confirming that TimeTuner primarily corrects the large truncation error inherent in few-step sampling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TimeTuner is a plug-in booster, so it is evaluated by adding it on top of each baseline rather than replacing it. Against DDIM [38] and DDPM [8], it consistently lowers FID across all seven datasets and all NFEs tested (e.g., CIFAR-10 quadratic NFE=10: 13.65→11.77; LSUN Bedroom: 9.65→6.07). Against Analytic-DDIM/DDPM [1] and DPM-Solver-2 [25], the same pattern holds in Figures 3–5, with the largest relative gains at NFE≤10. Against the high-order EDM solver [14] on label-conditioned ImageNet, FID improves from 10.13 to 9.63 at NFE=10, and the gain persists across CFG scales 3/5/7 (Table 3). Against the extreme-NFE consistency distillation baseline CD [40], TimeTuner reduces FID from 8.14 to 7.14 at NFE=1 and from 5.91 to 5.18 at NFE=2 on LSUN Bedroom. In the head-to-head comparison with dedicated schedule-optimization methods (Table 4, CIFAR-10), TimeTuner outperforms OMS-DPM [22] (11.77 vs 12.34 at NFE=10 quadratic), RS-DDIM [42] (15.76 vs 16.12 at NFE=10 linear), and GGDM [43] (>40 at NFE=16), confirming that optimizing the per-step timestep condition is more effective than re-optimizing the global trajectory.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the line of training-free diffusion acceleration methods (DDIM, DPM-Solver, Analytic-DPM, EDM) that reduce inference steps by using larger discretization intervals. Rather than proposing a new trajectory or higher-order solver, it identifies the root cause of quality degradation as an inaccurate integral direction: the noise prediction at the interval's initial timestep is suboptimal for the entire span. The key conceptual departure is replacing the conditioning timestep t with an optimized scalar τ for each interval, drawing an analogy to the mean value theorem of integrals. This is orthogonal to and composable with existing trajectory choices (quadratic, uniform, log-SNR) and solver orders, positioning TimeTuner as a plug-in booster rather than a replacement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for practitioners who need fast diffusion sampling: a ~1-hour scalar optimization on a single GPU yields large FID improvements (e.g., 9.65→6.07 on LSUN Bedroom at 10 NFE) without modifying the pre-trained model. The theoretical error bound (Theorem 1) provides a principled justification, and the plug-in design means it can be layered onto any existing acceleration pipeline. Open questions include whether the parallel training strategy's slight performance loss can be eliminated, whether the approach extends to video or 3D generation, and whether the optimized timesteps transfer across different base models or noise schedules without re-optimization.
