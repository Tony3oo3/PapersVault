## Problem

Existing talking face generation systems require an audio source as input, and text-driven approaches rely on cascade pipelines (TTS → TFG) that suffer from error accumulation and computational bottlenecks. No prior work jointly synthesises both a talking face video and natural speech from a single portrait and text while generalising to unseen identities. Two specific challenges remain: generating diverse, natural head poses without a driving video, and maintaining consistent speaker voice characteristics (prosody, timbre, accent) when the input portrait varies in facial motion.

## Input / Output

The system takes a single static portrait image (in-the-wild, arbitrary pose) and a text string as input, and simultaneously produces a talking face video and a synthesised speech audio clip. Notably, no audio encoder is needed—the TFG model consumes intermediate TTS representations (text embedding, upsampled text feature, and energy) as its driving signal, and the TTS model is conditioned on a motion-removed identity feature extracted from the portrait. The framework is trained on LRS3 (indoor TED talks) and evaluated on the more challenging outdoor datasets LRS2 and VoxCeleb2 in a one-shot setting.

## Method

The TTSF framework unifies a StyleGAN-based TFG generator (from LIA) with a Matcha-TTS (OT-CFM) speech synthesiser into a single jointly-trained model. For variational head pose generation, an OT-CFM motion sampler predicts motion codes from a prior network, with an auto-encoder-based noise reducer compressing and reconstructing latent features to mitigate the temporal instability that direct regression on StyleGAN decoder channels produces. A key cross-modal insight is that the TFG motion extractor subtracts motion features from the visual encoding to yield a pure identity feature f_id, which conditions both the TTS encoder and decoder—removing pose-dependent variation so the same person's voice remains consistent regardless of head angle in the portrait. The audio mapper aggregates text embedding, upsampled text feature, and energy via a Multi-Receptive Field Fusion module and injects them into specific decoder layers (6 and 7) to drive lip synchronisation, replacing the conventional audio encoder entirely. Training combines GAN, L1/LPIPS reconstruction, identity preservation, SyncNet lip-sync, OT-CFM, auto-encoder, prior, and TTS losses in a single objective.

## Results

On LRS2 (one-shot setting), TTSF achieves the best FID of 18.348 and ID-SIM of 0.864, outperforming all cascade baselines (MakeItTalk: FID 25.168, SadTalker: FID 20.729, Audio2Head: FID 42.262). On VoxCeleb2, TTSF attains the highest ID-SIM (0.876) and competitive LSE-C (5.721) while maintaining the best diversity score (DIV 0.143). In the user study, TTSF receives substantially higher MOS scores across all three criteria (Lip Sync 4.09, Motion Naturalness 3.85, Video Realness 3.87) compared to the next-best method (SadTalker: 2.78, 2.80, 2.90).

## Baselines & Comparisons

TTSF outperforms MakeItTalk on every metric: FID (18.348 vs. 25.168), ID-SIM (0.864 vs. 0.850), LSE-C (5.686 vs. 3.487), and DIV (0.143 vs. 0.095), indicating substantially better video quality, identity preservation, lip synchronisation, and head-motion diversity. Against Audio2Head, TTSF achieves a dramatically lower FID (18.348 vs. 42.262) and much higher ID-SIM (0.864 vs. 0.225), while Audio2Head edges out TTSF only marginally on DIV (0.145 vs. 0.143). Compared to SadTalker, TTSF wins on FID (18.348 vs. 20.729), ID-SIM (0.864 vs. 0.859), and DIV (0.143 vs. 0.111), but SadTalker records a higher LSE-C (6.256 vs. 5.686); however, the user study shows TTSF is perceived as significantly better in lip sync (4.09 vs. 2.78 MOS). For the TTS component, TTSF (w/o motion) surpasses Face-TTS on WER (14.56 vs. 18.02), C-SIM (0.593 vs. 0.272), and F0 RMSE (48.52 vs. 52.33), though Face-TTS has a slightly lower MCD (6.85 vs. 7.23), confirming that motion-removed identity conditioning yields more consistent speaker voices.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate pipelines—Talking Face Generation (TFG) and Text-to-Speech (TTS)—into a single bidirectional architecture, departing from the cascade approach used by prior text-driven TFG works (e.g., [68, 69, 72]) that chained TTS output into an audio-driven TFG model. The TFG backbone inherits the motion extractor and StyleGAN-style generator from LIA [67], while the TTS backbone is Matcha-TTS [40]; the key architectural departure is the cross-conditioning: TTS intermediate features replace the audio encoder in TFG, and motion-removed identity features from TFG condition the TTS speaker embedding. The OT-CFM motion sampler [37] with an autoencoder noise reducer is a novel combination tailored to the discrete, semantically-structured latent space of a StyleGAN decoder, addressing temporal instability that direct regression of motion codes produces.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first unified system that jointly produces both a talking-face video and natural speech from a single portrait and text, eliminating the error accumulation and computational overhead of cascade pipelines. The practical insight that facial motion in a source portrait actively degrades speaker-identity extraction for TTS (C-SIM drops from 0.593 to 0.451 when motion features are retained) is a useful finding for any face-conditioned speech synthesis. Limitations include reliance on indoor TED-talk training data (LRS3), a lower LSE-C sync score than SadTalker on VoxCeleb2, and evaluation restricted to single-speaker, single-portrait scenarios; open questions include scalability to multi-speaker dialogue, longer-form generation, and real-time inference on consumer hardware.
