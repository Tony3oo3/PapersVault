## Problem

Despite SAM's empirical success in improving generalization by seeking flat minima, the specific components of its adversarial perturbation responsible for this improvement remain unexplained. This lack of mechanistic understanding limits principled development of improved sharpness-aware optimizers. The paper asks: which part of the minibatch gradient used in SAM's perturbation actually drives generalization gains?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240686/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

F-SAM consumes the same inputs as vanilla SAM: a minibatch of RGB images with labels, current model weights, and a differentiable loss function. The key additional state is an exponentially moving average (EMA) vector m_t of historical minibatch gradients, updated each iteration with a momentum factor λ. The output is updated model weights via a base optimizer (SGD or AdamW), identical in form to SAM but with a modified perturbation direction that explicitly subtracts the estimated full-gradient component from the current minibatch gradient.

## Method

F-SAM decomposes the minibatch gradient ∇L_B(w) into a full-gradient component (projection onto ∇L(w)) and a batch-specific stochastic gradient noise component (residual projection). Empirical analysis shows the full-gradient component degrades generalization while the noise component drives SAM's benefits, because the full-gradient perturbation increases sharpness across the entire dataset, creating inconsistency with the subsequent minimization step that only uses the current minibatch. To exploit this, F-SAM estimates the full gradient via an EMA of historical stochastic gradients (m_t = λm_{t-1} + (1-λ)∇L_B(w_t)) and sets the perturbation direction to d_t = g_t - σ·m_t, effectively removing the full-gradient component. The resulting perturbation ε_t = ρ·norm(d_t) is 'friendly' in that it maximizes current-minibatch loss while minimizing impact on the full-dataset loss, yielding consistent sharpness minimization. The paper proves the EMA approximates the full gradient with error O(γ^{1/3}) and establishes O(log T / √T) convergence for F-SAM on non-convex problems.

## Results

On CIFAR-100, F-SAM achieves 81.29% (ResNet-18) and 85.16% (WRN-28-10) test accuracy, outperforming SAM by 0.41% and 0.28% respectively. On CIFAR-10, F-SAM gains 0.1–0.2% over SAM across all architectures (e.g., 96.75% vs 96.58% for ResNet-18). On ImageNet with ResNet-50, F-SAM reaches 77.35% top-1 accuracy, a 0.21% improvement over SAM (77.14%). In transfer learning with DeiT-small, F-SAM consistently beats SAM by 0.1–0.7% across five fine-tuning datasets, with the largest gain on Flowers102 (75.15% vs 74.38%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

F-SAM consistently outperforms SAM across all tested settings: by 0.1–0.2% on CIFAR-10, 0.2–0.4% on CIFAR-100, 0.21% on ImageNet, and 0.1–0.7% in transfer learning. Against ASAM, F-SAM wins on CIFAR-100 (e.g., 81.29% vs 81.68% for ResNet-18 is actually ASAM winning slightly, but F-SAM wins on WRN-28-10 at 85.16% vs 84.99%) and on ImageNet (77.35% vs 77.10%). Against FisherSAM, F-SAM achieves higher accuracy on CIFAR-100 for ResNet-18 (81.29% vs 80.99%) and WRN-28-10 (85.16% vs 84.91%), though FisherSAM edges ahead on VGG16-BN (76.90% vs 77.08% — actually F-SAM wins). Compared to plain SGD, F-SAM provides substantial gains (e.g., 81.29% vs 77.90% for ResNet-18 on CIFAR-100). In transfer learning, F-SAM also outperforms AdamW by 0.1–0.7%, and notably rescues SAM's collapse on Flowers102 where SAM (74.38%) underperforms AdamW (74.82%) while F-SAM reaches 75.15%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on SAM (Foret et al., ICLR 2020) and its adaptive variants (ASAM, FisherSAM), which formulate generalization improvement as a min-max problem over a neighborhood of the current weights. Prior analyses of SAM's success relied on PAC-Bayes bounds, Hessian eigenvalue minimization, or noise-prevention arguments in simplified settings, but none isolated which component of the minibatch gradient actually drives the generalization gain. F-SAM departs from this lineage by decomposing the perturbation direction into a full-gradient component and a batch-specific stochastic noise component, identifying the latter as the effective driver, and proposing an EMA-based practical modification that removes the former. The 'friendly adversary' framing also connects to the m-sharpness observation in the original SAM paper, providing a new mechanistic explanation for why small sub-batches help.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the full gradient component in SAM's perturbation actively degrades generalization reframes how practitioners should think about sharpness-aware optimization: the 'noise' in stochastic gradients is not merely a byproduct but the essential signal for flat-minima seeking. Practically, F-SAM's reduced sensitivity to the perturbation radius ρ addresses a well-known tuning pain point of SAM, and the 28-point recovery at 80% label noise demonstrates robustness gains that could matter in noisy-data regimes. Open questions include whether the EMA approximation quality becomes a bottleneck for very large models or long training runs, whether the 'friendliness' principle generalizes to other sharpness-based or adversarial-perturbation methods, and whether the modest 0.1–0.4% accuracy gains over SAM persist in the most competitive large-scale training settings.
