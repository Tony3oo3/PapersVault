## Problem

Fast DPM-solvers (DDIM, DPM-Solver++) reduce sampling steps from 1000 to 10–50, but this makes exact inversion—recovering the initial noise from a generated image—challenging because the naive DDIM inversion (forward Euler in reverse) breaks down with large time steps. Prior exact inversion methods either only work for first-order solvers, cannot handle high-order multistep methods, or fail to converge under large classifier-free guidance (ω > 1), which is standard practice for quality enhancement.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single generated image (pixel-space 64×64 or latent-space from LDM/Stable Diffusion) along with the text prompt (required for LDMs) and produces the initial noise vector x_T (or z_T in latent space) that would have generated the image. The inversion is performed step-by-step along the denoising schedule, and the final output can be re-forwarded through the DPM to verify reconstruction fidelity. For LDMs, an additional decoder-inversion step (gradient descent on ‖x − D(z)‖²) is applied to avoid the information loss inherent in using the encoder as a proxy for the decoder's inverse.

## Method

For first-order DDIM, the paper replaces the forward Euler used in naive DDIM inversion with a backward Euler (implicit) method, solved at each step via gradient descent or a forward step method on ‖ẑ_{t_i} − z'_{t_i}‖². This implicit formulation is robust to large classifier-free guidance because the step size can be reduced to ensure convergence, unlike fixed-point iteration (FPI) which requires the model to satisfy a Lipschitz condition that is violated when ω > 1. For high-order DPM-solvers (e.g., DPM-Solver++(2M)), the multistep structure requires past states (x_{t_{i-2}}, …) that are unknown during inversion; the paper approximates these high-order terms by running a fine-grained naive DDIM inversion to estimate the missing past states, then treats those approximated terms as constants while solving the backward Euler for the current state. The combination of backward Euler with high-order term approximation (Algorithm 2) achieves near-exact inversion of 10-step DPM-Solver++(2M) sampling, and the decoder-invariant formulation (Algorithm 1) extends the approach to latent diffusion models.

## Results

On the background-preserving editing task (60×5=300 images, LDM with classifier-free guidance ω=7.5), Algorithm 1 achieves a background NMSE of 12.8±2.0 (×10⁻³), approaching the oracle upper bound of 11.0±1.7 while the naive DDIM inversion yields 30.4±4.8. For reconstruction, both Algorithm 1 (DDIM, 50 steps) and Algorithm 2 (DPM-Solver++(2M), 10 steps) substantially reduce noise and image NMSE compared to naive DDIM inversion across pixel-space DPM (ImageNet64) and LDM settings. Algorithm 2 also enables tree-ring watermark classification (100 images per watermark) where naive DDIM inversion fails to detect one of three watermarks. Runtime increases from 59 s (naive DDIM, 1000 steps) to 79 s (Alg. 1) and 159 s (Alg. 2) in LDM.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the naive DDIM inversion (Song et al. [31]), both proposed algorithms achieve substantially lower reconstruction NMSE for both noise and image across all settings (DDIM 50 steps, DPM-Solver++(2M) 10 steps, pixel-space and latent-space). In background-preserving editing, Algorithm 1 reduces background NMSE from 30.4±4.8 to 12.8±2.0, a more than 2× improvement, and approaches the oracle (11.0±1.7) without requiring the original denoising trajectory. Against Pan et al. [21] (FPI-based inversion), the paper's methods are more robust to large classifier-free guidance (ω>1): FPI exhibits poor noise reconstruction in LDM (Fig. 3c) due to non-convergence of the fixed-point iteration, while Algorithm 1 using gradient descent/forward step remains stable. Against the oracle of Patashnik et al. [23], Algorithm 1 narrows the gap from 19.4 NMSE (naive DDIM) to just 1.8 NMSE, demonstrating that exact inversion can nearly replicate oracle-level editing quality. The trade-off is runtime: Algorithm 1 costs 79 s vs. 59 s for naive DDIM (1000 steps) and Algorithm 2 costs 159 s, reflecting the iterative implicit solves at each step.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as the missing exact inverse for fast ODE-based diffusion sampling. DDIM (Song et al.) samples via forward Euler, and the widely-used naive DDIM inversion is itself a forward Euler step in reverse — not the true inverse. Pan et al. (2023) addressed this gap for first-order DDIM using fixed-point iteration, but their method degrades under the large classifier-free guidance (ω > 1) that is standard practice for quality. Crucially, no prior exact-inversion method handles higher-order solvers like DPM-Solver++(2M), whose multistep structure depends on past states unavailable during inversion. The authors fill both gaps: backward Euler (via gradient descent or forward-step) for DDIM, and backward Euler with a fine-grained naive-DDIM approximation of the high-order terms for DPM-Solver++(2M).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Exact inversion of fast solvers unlocks applications that were previously infeasible or inaccurate: reliable watermark detection and even watermark *classification* in the noise space, and background-preserving image editing without access to the original generation trajectory. The practical limitation is computational cost — Algorithm 2 takes ~159 s versus ~3 s for naive DDIM inversion in LDM — and the method still requires knowing the text prompt. The authors acknowledge that on accelerated schedulers and in latent space the inversion is 'near-exact' rather than perfectly exact, and leave joint prompt-and-noise estimation as future work. Whether the high-order-term approximation generalizes beyond second-order multistep solvers (e.g., DPM-Solver++(3M)) remains an open question.
