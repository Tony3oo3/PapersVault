## Problem

Despite deep networks achieving strong performance across diverse training objectives, their internal computational layout—how layers, spatial regions, and feature scales organize hierarchically—remains opaque. No existing tool explicitly maps the full 4D structure of network features (space, layer, scale, channel) onto the brain to reveal how different training paradigms shape this internal organization, nor to diagnose how scaling or fine-tuning disrupts it.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images (processed through a frozen pre-trained ViT backbone into L×C×H×W features) and 3D brain voxel coordinates from the NSD fMRI dataset. The primary training target is per-voxel fMRI beta weights (hemodynamic response amplitude), but the key analytical outputs are the learned factorized selectors: a 2D spatial coordinate per voxel, a probability distribution over layers, a scalar local-vs-global token weight, and a linear channel weight vector—collectively forming an explicit, interpretable brain-to-network mapping.

## Method

FactorTopy factorizes the brain-to-network feature selection into four independent, low-dimensional selectors (space, layer, scale, channel) rather than learning a full 4D mask per voxel. The space selector linearly maps 3D voxel coordinates to 2D image coordinates (analogous to retinotopy), the layer selector produces a softmax over ViT layers, the scale selector outputs a scalar blending local patch tokens with the global class token, and a learned per-layer channel alignment matrix projects all layers into a shared D-dimensional space before a linear channel selector picks the best channels. Topological smoothness is enforced via Lipschitz continuity constraints using sinusoidal position encoding on adjacent brain voxels, which significantly improves selection confidence and spatial coherence. The entire model is trained end-to-end to minimize fMRI prediction error, and the resulting selectors are then used to color the brain surface by layer, cluster voxels by channel co-occurrence, and compute a hierarchy slope metric quantifying brain-network alignment.

## Results

On the NSD fMRI dataset, CLIP achieves the lowest root sum squared difference to the model-wise maximum across all six brain ROIs (e.g., 0.005 on FFA, 0.006 on PPA), indicating the best overall brain encoding. For hierarchy alignment, CLIP's slope increases with model scale (0.32 for B/32-14M → 0.50 for B/16-140M → 0.53 for L/14-1B), while all other models (ImageNet, SAM, DiNOv2, MAE, MoCov3) show decreased hierarchy slope when scaled up. After fine-tuning on 50 samples per class, CLIP's brain score drops only 0.016 (0.131→0.115 on ISIC) versus 0.043 for DiNOv2 (0.128→0.085) and 0.025 for SAM (0.111→0.086), confirming less catastrophic forgetting for hierarchically organized networks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLIP dominates the brain encoding comparison, achieving the lowest root sum squared difference to the max model in 5 of 6 ROIs (V1, V2V3, OPA, FFA, PPA), with particularly strong performance on semantic regions (FFA: 0.005, PPA: 0.006). DiNOv2 is the closest competitor, matching CLIP on EBA (0.013 vs 0.015) and PPA (0.007 vs 0.006), but is weaker on early visual regions (V1: 0.033 vs 0.032) and OPA (0.021 vs 0.011). MAE and MoCov3 perform well on early visual cortex (V1: 0.031–0.032) but are substantially worse on body and face regions (EBA: 0.029–0.031, FFA: 0.015–0.017). SAM and ImageNet (supervised) show the weakest overall encoding, with SAM particularly poor on EBA (0.065) and FFA (0.056). Stable Diffusion at T=40 is competitive on early visual regions (V1: 0.031) but lags on semantic regions (FFA: 0.019, PPA: 0.013). For hierarchy alignment, CLIP uniquely improves its slope with scale (0.32→0.53), whereas DiNOv2's slope actually increases (0.67→0.76) but its R² drops, and SAM/MAE/MoCov3 all show degraded alignment at larger sizes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper inverts the mainstream direction of brain-encoding research: rather than using deep networks to explain brain function (as in gradient-based V1 orientation analysis or category-selective region studies), it uses known brain functional organization to diagnose and visualize deep network computation. Methodologically, it extends the authors' own Memory Encoding Model (Algonauts 2023 winner) by introducing FactorTopy—a factorized, topologically-smooth selection across space, layer, scale, and channel dimensions—replacing the per-voxel independent 2D spatial masks used in prior brain encoding approaches. The factorized representation with Lipschitz continuity constraints is the key methodological departure, making the brain-to-network mapping statistically more stable and interpretable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that CLIP uniquely improves brain-hierarchical alignment with scale while all other training objectives (supervised, self-supervised, segmentation) degrade it challenges the assumption that larger models should be more organized, and suggests image-text contrastive learning may produce representations closer to biological visual processing. The practical implication—that hierarchically organized networks suffer less catastrophic forgetting after fine-tuning on small datasets—offers a concrete diagnostic for practitioners choosing pre-trained backbones. Limitations include dependence on the single publicly available NSD dataset (3 subjects), restriction to ViT architecture, and the somewhat coarse hierarchy-slope metric; whether these findings generalize to non-brain-like architectures or other dataset scales remains open.
