## Problem

Existing face de-identification methods operate entirely at the software level, leaving a security gap where an attacker can intercept the original high-fidelity image before the de-identification algorithm processes it. This paper closes that gap by moving privacy protection to the hardware (optical) level, so that identity-sensitive information is removed during image acquisition itself rather than after the fact.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240429/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two inputs: a privacy-preserving image captured through a learned optical lens (256×256 RGB, with high-frequency identity information optically filtered out) and a reference face image from a public dataset that supplies the style for the new identity. It produces a de-identified synthetic face image that preserves the source person's global geometry (head pose, eye/nose/mouth positions) while adopting the reference image's appearance, along with a face heatmap extracted from the privacy-preserving capture. Each source image is transformed using 10 randomly sampled reference images during evaluation.

## Method

The framework operates in two stages. Stage I jointly optimizes a phase mask (parameterized by Zernike polynomial coefficients) and a Hourglass-based heatmap regression network end-to-end: the optics loss minimizes similarity between the acquired and original image to force identity-concealing distortion, while the heatmap loss ensures geometric landmarks remain recoverable from the degraded capture. Stage II freezes the camera and regression model, then trains a StarGAN2-based conditional GAN that maps the privacy-preserving image (via an inversion encoder), the extracted heatmap, and a style code from the reference image to a new synthetic face. Two novel losses are introduced: a facial expression consistency loss that aligns masked regions between source and generated images to preserve subtle expressions like smiles, and an LPIPS perceptual loss to improve generation quality. The discriminator is modified to use the reference image rather than the source image, preventing identity leakage through the adversarial signal.

## Results

On CelebA-HQ, the proposed method (Ours) achieves DIS scores of 0.734 (FR), 1.071 (CASIA), and 1.251 (VGGFace2), with the best VGGFace2 score among all learned-optics variants. The low-resolution camera variant (Ours-LR) achieves higher DIS (0.795/1.123/1.294) but is vulnerable to reverse-engineering attacks. On utility metrics, Ours achieves FID of 29.218, face detection rate of 1.000 (MtCNN) / 0.991 (Dlib), and landmark error of 2.298 (MtCNN) / 2.716 (Dlib), trading some generation quality for stronger privacy. In hardware experiments with a prototype deformable-mirror camera, Ours achieves DIS of 0.792/1.156/1.334, outperforming RiDDLE (0.798/0.958/1.224) on CASIA and VGGFace2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the primary identity-protection metric (DIS), Ours outperforms RiDDLE on all three face-recognition backbones (VGGFace2: 1.251 vs 1.129; CASIA: 1.071 vs 1.033; FR: 0.734 vs 0.776) and CIAGAN on all three, but trails IDeudemon (FR: 0.734 vs 0.819; CASIA: 1.071 vs 1.228) and FIT (FR: 0.734 vs 0.812; CASIA: 1.071 vs 1.207), while edging out both on VGGFace2. On utility metrics (Table 3), RiDDLE clearly dominates with FID 15.389 vs Ours' 29.218 and substantially lower bounding-box and landmark errors, reflecting the information loss inherent in the privacy-preserving optics. Ours is competitive with DeepPrivacy on utility (FID 29.218 vs 23.713; LM 2.298 vs 3.280 on MtCNN) and substantially outperforms CIAGAN (FID 29.218 vs 32.611; LM 2.298 vs 8.042). The paper notes that Ours-LR (fixed low-resolution optics) achieves higher DIS than the learned lens but is susceptible to deconvolution attacks, whereas the learned phase mask resists SOTA diffusion-based deconvolution (DDRM) recovery. In the hardware prototype, Ours matches or exceeds RiDDLE on CASIA (1.156 vs 0.958) and VGGFace2 (1.334 vs 1.224) DIS, validating the approach in a real optical setup.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established research threads: (1) learned optics for privacy-preserving imaging, pioneered by Hinojosa et al. (ICCV 2021) and PrivHar (ECCV 2022), which jointly optimize a Zernike-parameterized phase mask with a downstream task network; and (2) GAN-based face de-identification (StarGAN2, DeepPrivacy, CIAGAN, RiDDLE), which replaces identities in already-captured images. The key departure is that prior optics-learning papers were task-specific (pose estimation, action recognition) and did not combine the degraded capture with a generative model, while prior face de-identification methods all operate at the software level on full-fidelity images, leaving a security gap vulnerable to sniffing attacks. This work is the first to close that gap by learning optics specifically for face de-identification and pairing the privacy-preserving capture with a StarGAN2-style generator conditioned on a face heatmap and a reference image.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that hardware-level privacy (optical encoding at capture time) is meaningfully more robust to deconvolution attacks than simpler fixed-optics approaches like low-resolution sensors, as validated by a SOTA diffusion-based restoration model (DDRM) that recovers identity from LR images but fails on the learned-optics images. However, the practical impact is tempered by the deformable mirror's limitation to 15 Zernike coefficients, the modest (and sometimes inferior) quantitative performance relative to software-only SOTA like RiDDLE on detection and landmark metrics, and the acknowledged artifacts in generated faces. Open questions include whether the approach scales to higher-order optics, whether the two-stage training can be unified, and whether the framework generalizes beyond face de-identification to other identity-sensitive tasks (e.g., full-body anonymization) while maintaining utility.
