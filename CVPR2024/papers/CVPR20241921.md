## Problem

Existing robustness and OOD detection benchmarks simulate distribution shifts by digitally perturbing already-acquired images, but real-world covariate shifts arise during the image acquisition process itself—through variations in ambient lighting and camera sensor parameters (ISO, shutter speed, aperture). This 'eyes' component of the vision pipeline has remained unexplored, leaving it unclear whether robustness gains observed under synthetic corruptions generalize to physically captured images, and whether OOD detection methods can handle covariate-shifted data that is neither purely in-distribution nor semantically out-of-distribution.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241921/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes RGB images captured by a physical camera in a controllable dark-room testbed (ES-Studio), where reference images from Tiny-ImageNet (200 classes, >375×500 px) are displayed on a screen and photographed under 54+ manual sensor configurations (combinations of ISO, shutter speed, aperture) plus auto-exposure, with light toggled on/off. The output is a 200-class label and an OOD score computed by various detection methods. The dataset comprises 202k images (138k validation, 64k test) derived from 2,000 reference samples, with each image cropped to the valid screen region and validated for consistency.

## Method

The paper constructs ES-Studio, a reproducible testbed with a dark room, display screen, adjustable camera (ISO/shutter/aperture), and controllable ceiling lamps, to directly capture physically perturbed images rather than applying digital corruptions. Using this testbed, they curate ImageNet-ES (202k images) and conduct three empirical studies: (1) OOD detection evaluation under both semantics-centric and Model-Specific OOD (MS-OOD) frameworks, revealing that SOTA methods like ViM and ASH fail on covariate-shifted data while older methods like MSP and ODIN perform better; (2) domain generalization experiments showing that augmenting training with environmental/sensor-domain perturbations from ImageNet-ES improves robustness on both ImageNet-ES and conventional ImageNet-C; and (3) sensor parameter control analysis demonstrating that well-tuned camera settings can boost a lightweight EfficientNet-B0 to outperform a 120× larger OpenCLIP-h model, with the optimal settings being model-specific rather than aligned with human aesthetic preferences.

## Results

On ImageNet-ES (C-OOD), older OOD detection methods MSP and ODIN consistently outperform newer SOTA methods ViM and ASH in F1 score, while ViM remains best on S-OOD benchmarks (Texture, iNaturalist, OpenImages-O). For domain generalization, adding ImageNet-ES environment/sensor augmentations to ResNet-50 finetuning improves ImageNet-ES accuracy from 49.6% to 55.8% and IN-C from 51.0% to 51.8%. Most strikingly, well-tuned sensor parameters (Best) improve accuracy over auto exposure by 9.9–47.9 points across models; EfficientNet-B0 with Best sensor settings (83.8%) surpasses OpenCLIP-h (632M params) under auto exposure (79.1%) and all-params (77.6%) conditions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

For OOD detection on C-OOD (ImageNet-ES), MSP and ODIN (older baselines) achieve higher F1 scores than the newer SOTA methods ViM and ASH across all 54 manual sensor settings, while ViM leads on S-OOD benchmarks (Texture, iNaturalist, OpenImages-O, SSB-hard, NINCO). This reveals that recent OOD method progress has been biased toward semantic shifts and has regressed on covariate shifts. For domain generalization, AugMix and DeepAugment improve IN-C accuracy (57.4% vs. 51.0% baseline) but degrade ImageNet-ES accuracy (49.1% vs. 49.6%), whereas adding ImageNet-ES sensor/environment augmentations improves both (55.8% on ImageNet-ES, 51.8% on IN-C). In the sensor control comparison, EfficientNet-B0 (5M params) with Best sensor settings (83.8%) outperforms OpenCLIP-h (632M params, ~120× more parameters) under auto exposure (79.1%) and all-params (77.6%) conditions, demonstrating that input quality via sensor control can exceed the benefit of model scale and DG. DINOv2-g (1.1B params) achieves the highest All-params accuracy (79.6%) but still drops 14.6 points from its Best setting (94.2%), confirming that no model size fully compensates for poor sensor configuration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in robustness and OOD evaluation, where distribution shifts are simulated via digital perturbations on already-captured images (ImageNet-C, ImageNet-P, AugMix, DeepAugment). Instead, it introduces a physically grounded evaluation axis—environmental lighting and camera sensor parameters (ISO, shutter speed, aperture)—captured through a real camera in a controllable testbed (ES-Studio). Conceptually, it builds on the MS-OOD framework of Averly and Chao for fine-grained C-OOD labeling and on the domain generalization literature for augmentation strategies, but repositions the problem from 'how to make the brain more robust' to 'how to control the eyes.' The paper explicitly frames the image acquisition process as an unexplored source of covariate shift that existing benchmarks cannot capture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a critical blind spot: SOTA OOD detection methods (ViM, ASH) that excel on semantic shifts actually underperform older baselines (MSP, ODIN) on physical covariate shifts, suggesting the field's recent progress may have been narrowly optimized for S-OOD. The finding that well-tuned sensor parameters let a 5M-parameter EfficientNet-B0 outperform a 632M-parameter OpenCLIP-h under auto-exposure reframes the robustness problem as partly an input-quality problem rather than purely a model-capacity problem, with direct implications for resource-constrained and mobile deployments. Key limitations include the use of screen displays rather than real 3D objects (missing material/light interaction), the restriction to 200 Tiny-ImageNet classes, and the need to combine sensor control with digital post-processing for full practical coverage. Open questions include whether these findings generalize to video, multi-sensor systems, and whether differentiable sensor control can be trained end-to-end.
