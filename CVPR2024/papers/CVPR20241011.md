## Problem

Motion prediction models for autonomous driving degrade significantly when deployed in new driving environments, yet conventional update strategies are impractical: joint training demands ever-growing compute as data accumulates, while transfer learning suffers catastrophic forgetting of previously learned environments. The continual learning literature has largely focused on image classification benchmarks, leaving the problem of sequentially updating trajectory prediction models across non-stationary, real-world driving distributions largely unaddressed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241011/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes structured vehicle states (positions, velocities) and HD-map road geometry encoded via a DenseNet-121 backbone, organized into sequential driving scenes from nuScenes where each scene yields 10–30 trajectory prediction examples for one or more target vehicles. During the adaptation phase, incoming data is split into 50 adaptation samples (merged with memory buffer contents) and 20 held-out evaluation samples per stage. The output is a predicted future trajectory per target vehicle, evaluated by average displacement error (ADE).

## Method

The framework operates in two phases. In pre-training, an OML-inspired inner/outer loop trains a Representation Learning Network (DenseNet-121) and Prediction Learning Network (MLP) to produce sparse, interference-resistant representations: the inner loop sequentially updates the PLN on sampled sequences while freezing the RLN, and the outer loop updates both on fresh samples. A modulation network, sharing features from the RLN backbone, gates the final RLN layers via a dot product, and an L1 penalty on its output vector explicitly enforces per-input sparsity (58% instance sparsity vs. 15% for OML). In the adaptation phase, an Optimal Memory Buffer Retention Strategy replaces uniform reservoir sampling: at each stage, the model computes representation-vector similarity between incoming data and current buffer contents, and only triggers buffer updates (via reservoir sampling) when similarity drops below a threshold, thereby preserving diverse coverage of the full data distribution within a limited buffer and reducing gradient interference across stages.

## Results

On the nuScenes continual motion prediction benchmark (400 scenes pre-training, 50 scenes sequential adaptation), the proposed method achieves the best Retained Accuracy (RA) and Backward Transfer & Interference (BTI) across all memory buffer sizes. At M=40, it reaches 2.06±0.11 m ADE (RA) and 0.13±0.09 (BTI), approaching the joint-training upper bound of 1.92±0.03 m ADE while using only a fraction of the data. Even at the smallest buffer (M=20), the proposed method (2.24±0.23 RA, 0.51±0.14 BTI) outperforms all other pre-training strategies, demonstrating robustness to limited replay capacity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all five baselines on both RA and BTI at every memory buffer size. Against OML [20], the closest meta-learning competitor, the proposed method reduces RA from 2.35 to 2.06 m ADE (M=40) and BTI from 0.14 to 0.13, with a larger gap at smaller buffers (2.75→2.24 RA at M=20). MAML-Rep [20] shows the worst BTI among meta-learning baselines (0.77 at M=30), which the proposed method reduces to 0.24. EWC [22] achieves competitive RA at M=40 (2.31) but suffers high BTI variance (0.36±0.76), indicating unstable forgetting behavior. Joint training [25] sets the performance ceiling at 1.92 m ADE but is impractical for real deployment due to ever-growing data and compute costs; the proposed method closes this gap to within 0.14 m ADE at M=40. In the adaptation-strategy ablation (Table 2), the proposed adaptive sampling also beats reservoir sampling [23] (2.25 vs 2.48 RA, 0.22 vs 0.45 BTI at M=40), confirming that representation-similarity-based buffer curation is more effective than uniform proportional storage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the meta-representation learning framework of OML (Javed & White, NeurIPS 2019) to the motion prediction domain, adding a modulation network with L1 sparsity regularization to explicitly encourage sparse, input-specific representations that resist catastrophic forgetting. It departs from standard reservoir sampling for memory buffer management by introducing an adaptive retention strategy that uses representation-vector similarity to detect distribution shifts and selectively store diverse samples. The work carves new ground by being the first to formulate continual learning specifically for trajectory prediction in autonomous driving, where prior continual learning research has focused almost exclusively on image classification benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically critical gap: commercial autonomous driving systems must continuously adapt to new road geometries and traffic patterns without retraining from scratch, yet no prior work has provided a dedicated continual learning framework for motion prediction. Key limitations include the reliance on a single dataset (nuScenes) with a synthetic scene-by-scene task sequence that may not capture the truly non-stationary, overlapping distribution shifts of real deployment, and the modest absolute improvements over strong baselines (e.g., RA 2.06 vs. 2.35 at M=40). Open questions include whether the sparsity-inducing modulation network generalizes to other prediction tasks, how the adaptive buffer strategy performs under genuinely adversarial or rapidly shifting distributions, and whether the framework scales to the hundreds of distinct environments a production system would encounter.
