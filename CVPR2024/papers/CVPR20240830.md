## Problem

Conventional depth completion methods are trained and tested with a fixed number of sparse depth measurements, but in practice sparsity varies significantly due to transparent surfaces, mirrors, black objects, and sensor defects. When the number of sparse depths deviates from the training configuration, completion performance degrades severely because erroneous depth values propagate to neighboring pixels during refinement. This paper addresses the gap by formulating sparsity-adaptive depth refinement (SDR), where a single model must handle a variable number of sparse depth points at test time.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240830/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a set of sparse depth measurements whose count varies at test time (10–1000 points on NYUv2, 4–64 LiDAR lines on KITTI) and produces a dense depth map at the same spatial resolution. Sparse depth values are treated as ground-truth anchors that remain unchanged throughout refinement; only unmeasured pixels are updated. The framework is built on an off-the-shelf monocular depth estimator (Jun et al. [37]) that generates an initial dense depth map from the RGB image alone, which is then refined using the sparse measurements.

## Method

The SDR framework comprises three components: a monocular depth estimator producing an initial depth map, a guidance network (PVT-Base encoder + U-Net decoder with NAF blocks) that fuses image, sparse depth, and initial depth features into a guidance feature map, and the proposed Masked Spatial Propagation Network (MSPN). MSPN introduces a propagation mask initialized to 1 at sparse depth locations and 0 elsewhere; at each iteration, a pixel-to-window attention mechanism computes attention scores between a query feature and masked key features within a 13×13 window, refining only pixels whose mask is active while the mask itself expands outward via the same attention weights. The number of refinement iterations is computed adaptively from the sparsity level so the mask can spread across the full image. Two MSPN layers are stacked in series: the first performs coarse refinement over the entire image with an adaptive iteration count, while the second re-initializes the mask to the original sparse locations and performs six fixed iterations to intensively correct regions near sparse depths, reducing monocular depth errors around those anchors.

## Results

On NYUv2 ordinary depth completion (500 sparse points), MSPN achieves the best RMSE of 0.089 m, REL of 0.012, and δ₁.₂₅ of 99.6%, marginally outperforming NLSPN, DySPN, and CompletionFormer (all at RMSE 0.090). On KITTI, MSPN achieves RMSE 1212.7 mm and iRMSE 2.6 (16 lines) and RMSE 835.7 mm and iRMSE 2.1 (64 lines), both best among compared methods. In the sparsity-adaptive setting, MSPN maintains consistent RMSE across 10–1000 sparse points on NYUv2 and 4–64 LiDAR lines on KITTI, whereas baselines trained for a fixed sparsity degrade sharply when the number of measurements changes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On NYUv2, MSPN achieves the lowest RMSE (0.089 m), narrowly beating NLSPN [54], DySPN [44], and CompletionFormer [79] (all 0.090 m), while substantially outperforming earlier methods like S2D [51] (0.204 m), GuideNet [64] (0.101 m), and TWISE [33] (0.097 m). On KITTI, MSPN wins on RMSE and iRMSE at both 16 lines (1212.7 mm / 2.6 vs. 1218.6 / 3.0 for CompletionFormer) and 64 lines (835.7 mm / 2.1 vs. 848.7 / 2.5 for CompletionFormer), though it trails CompletionFormer slightly on MAE at 16 lines (341.8 vs. 337.4 mm). In the sparsity-adaptive (SDR) evaluation (Figs. 5–6), MSPN is the only method whose RMSE curve remains flat across all sparsity levels; baselines such as NLSPN [54] and DySPN [44] trained for a fixed number of points degrade sharply when tested at different sparsities, while sparsity-agnostic methods like Conti et al. [12] and Eldesokey et al. [18,19] remain consistently worse than MSPN at every sparsity level. On the cross-dataset SUN RGB-D test, MSPN matches or exceeds NLSPN [54] and CompletionFormer [79] without fine-tuning, confirming generalization. For the depth-hole-filling task (Table 4), MSPN outperforms CompletionFormer [79] on all five metrics (e.g., RMSE 0.325 vs. 0.348 m, δ₁.₂₅ 0.936 vs. 0.925).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the spatial propagation network (SPN) lineage—CSPN, NLSPN, DySPN—which iteratively refines depth maps via local convolutions, by replacing convolution-based propagation with a pixel-to-window attention mechanism and introducing a propagation mask that gradually expands from sparse depth points. It departs from the conventional depth-completion formulation (predicting an initial depth map from sparse depths) by instead using an off-the-shelf monocular depth estimator as the initial estimate and reframing the task as sparsity-adaptive refinement, making the number of sparse depths a variable rather than a fixed training hyperparameter. The guidance network borrows the U-Net encoder-decoder structure with PVT-Base as encoder and NAF blocks in the decoder, while the attention design draws on the Neighborhood Attention Transformer's relative-position-bias formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation is strong: real LiDAR and ToF sensors produce highly variable sparsity due to transparent surfaces, sensor defects, and operating conditions, yet all prior SPN-based methods are trained and tested at a fixed sparsity and degrade sharply when it changes. The SDR framework's modularity (swappable monocular depth estimator, cross-dataset validation on SUN RGB-D) suggests broad applicability, but the method still depends on the quality of the monocular prior and the adaptive iteration count is a heuristic lower bound. Open questions include whether the mask-based propagation remains effective at extreme sparsity (single-point or near-zero measurements) and whether the two-layer MSPN design generalizes to multi-frame or video depth completion.
