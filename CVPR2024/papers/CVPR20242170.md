## Problem

3D Gaussian Splatting suffers from over-reconstruction during Gaussian densification, where high-variance image regions are covered by only a few large Gaussians, producing blur and artifacts in rendered images. The root cause is that pixel-wise L1 loss yields small gradients in these regions, preventing the densification mechanism from splitting large Gaussians. This paper is the first to address the over-reconstruction problem from a spectral perspective, showing that the deficiency manifests clearly as frequency spectrum discrepancy between rendered and ground truth images.

## Input / Output

The method takes multi-view RGB scene images and a sparse point cloud from structure-from-motion as input, and produces rendered novel view images at arbitrary camera poses. Training begins at one-quarter resolution for 500 iterations before upsampling to full resolution, and Gaussian densification (cloning and splitting) is terminated after 15,000 iterations, after which only the frequency and spatial losses continue to refine Gaussian properties. The frequency regularization operates on the 2D DFT of the rendered image versus the ground truth at each training step.

## Method

FreGS introduces progressive frequency regularization via a frequency annealing technique that performs coarse-to-fine Gaussian densification. It computes amplitude and phase discrepancies between the 2D DFT of the rendered image and the ground truth in Fourier space, then decomposes these into low-frequency and high-frequency components using a fixed low-pass filter H_l and a dynamic high-pass filter H_h. The dynamic high-pass filter progressively expands its passband from D_0 to the full spectrum D over iterations [T_0, T], so that early training regularizes only low-frequency (large-scale structure) discrepancies while later training incorporates high-frequency (fine detail) discrepancies. This progressive schedule is combined with the standard pixel-level L1 loss and D-SSIM term, and the frequency loss terminates once Gaussian densification ends at iteration 15,000. The key insight is that frequency-space regularization increases pixel gradients in over-reconstructed regions, enabling the densification mechanism to correctly split large Gaussians where spatial-domain L1 loss alone fails to trigger splitting.

## Results

On Mip-NeRF360, FreGS achieves PSNR 27.85, SSIM 0.826, and LPIPS 0.209, outperforming 3D-GS (27.21 / 0.815 / 0.214) and Mip-NeRF360 (27.69 / 0.792 / 0.237). On Tanks&Temples, FreGS reaches PSNR 23.96, SSIM 0.849, and LPIPS 0.178, surpassing 3D-GS (23.14 / 0.841 / 0.183) by a notable margin of +0.82 dB in PSNR. On Deep Blending, FreGS attains PSNR 29.93, SSIM 0.904, and LPIPS 0.240, again leading 3D-GS (29.41 / 0.903 / 0.243). FreGS consistently outperforms all NeRF-based baselines (Plenoxels, INGP-Base, INGP-Big, Mip-NeRF360) by significant margins across every dataset and metric.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FreGS is compared against five baselines: Plenoxels, INGP-Base, INGP-Big, Mip-NeRF360, and 3D-GS. Against the closest competitor 3D-GS, FreGS wins on all three metrics across all three datasets, with the largest PSNR gain of +0.82 dB on Tanks&Temples (23.96 vs. 23.14) and a consistent LPIPS improvement (e.g., 0.178 vs. 0.183 on T&T). Against Mip-NeRF360, FreGS leads by +0.16 dB PSNR on Mip-NeRF360 (27.85 vs. 27.69) and +0.53 dB on Tanks&Temples (23.96 vs. 22.22), while also achieving higher SSIM and lower LPIPS. Against the hash-grid methods INGP-Base and INGP-Big, FreGS gains over 2 dB PSNR on Mip-NeRF360 (27.85 vs. 25.30/25.59) and over 2 dB on Tanks&Temples (23.96 vs. 21.72/21.92). Against Plenoxels, the margins are even larger, with FreGS exceeding it by more than 4 dB PSNR on Mip-NeRF360. In summary, FreGS achieves the best score on every metric in every dataset, with no losses or ties to any baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

FreGS builds directly on 3D Gaussian Splatting (3D-GS) by Kerbl et al., inheriting its anisotropic Gaussian representation, differentiable splatting, and adaptive densification pipeline, while adding a frequency-space regularization loss to address the over-reconstruction problem that 3D-GS exhibits during densification. The frequency-domain perspective draws on prior work in NeRF variants (BARF, WaveNeRF, FreeNeRF) that exploit Fourier or wavelet decompositions for learning, but FreGS is the first to apply progressive frequency regularization specifically to guide Gaussian densification in an explicit 3D representation. The frequency annealing schedule—progressively widening the high-pass filter band from low to high frequency—repurposes the coarse-to-fine training philosophy common in multi-scale learning but applies it in the spectral domain rather than spatial or resolution scales.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Over-reconstruction is a well-documented weakness of 3D-GS that produces visible blur and artifacts, and FreGS provides a principled, frequency-space diagnostic (spectrum discrepancy) and remedy that complements the standard pixel-level L1 loss. The frequency annealing schedule is simple to implement yet effectively decouples large-scale structure learning from fine-detail refinement, which could be adopted in other Gaussian-based or explicit-representation methods. Limitations include evaluation on only 11 static scenes, the added per-iteration Fourier transform cost, and the fact that matching 3D-GS's Gaussian count requires manually increasing the gradient threshold. Open questions include whether the annealing schedule can be learned, how the approach extends to dynamic or 4D scenes, and whether the frequency regularization interacts well with other recent 3D-GS improvements (e.g., 4D-GS, 2D-GS).
