## Problem

Existing VPR methods produce each image's global feature using only that image itself, neglecting cross-image variations (viewpoint, illumination, season) that limit robustness in challenging scenes. Additionally, directly applying pre-trained visual foundation models to VPR fails to focus on discriminative landmarks and is susceptible to dynamic foreground interference, while full fine-tuning causes catastrophic forgetting of previously learned representations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242359/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 224×224 RGB images and produces a 14×768-dim global descriptor (from a spatial pyramid with 1×1, 2×2, and 3×3 levels, where the 1×1 level uses the class token and remaining levels use GeM pooling), which is L2-normalized and optionally PCA-reduced to 512–4096 dimensions. During training, features are computed in batches of 288 images (72 places × 4 images) so that cross-image correlation can be modeled; at inference, a single image's global feature is used for nearest-neighbor retrieval, with inference batches of 8–16 images.

## Method

CricaVPR uses a frozen DINOv2/ViT-B/14 backbone with inserted multi-scale convolution (MulConv) adapters—lightweight modules with parallel 1×1, 3×3, and 5×5 convolution paths placed in parallel to the MLP layer of each transformer block—to introduce multi-scale local priors while preserving pre-trained knowledge. The backbone's patch tokens are reshaped into spatial feature maps and processed through a spatial pyramid to yield 14 regional features per image. A cross-image encoder (two stacked transformer encoder layers with post-LN) then correlates the i-th regional feature across all images in a batch via attention, allowing each image to harvest condition- and viewpoint-invariant information from same-place images and discriminative contrast from different-place images. The 14 output regional features are concatenated and L2-normalized into the final global descriptor. Training uses the multi-similarity loss with online hard mining on GSV-Cities (560k images, 67k places) for 10 epochs, requiring only 3.5 hours on two RTX 3090 GPUs.

## Results

CricaVPR achieves 94.9% R@1 on Pitts30k, 93.0% R@1 on Tokyo24/7, 90.0% R@1 on MSLS-val, and 69.0% R@1 on MSLS-challenge, setting new state-of-the-art among global-retrieval-based VPR methods. On the more challenging datasets, it reaches 90.7% R@1 on Nordland (seasonal changes), 64.7% R@1 on AmsterTime (very long-term), and 85.1% R@1 on SVOX-Night (extreme illumination), outperforming the previous best by 14.5%, 15.8%, and 20.7% absolute R@1 respectively. The method uses only 4096-dim PCA-reduced descriptors and requires just 3.5 hours of training on two RTX 3090 GPUs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CricaVPR outperforms all eight compared baselines on every dataset in the main comparison. Against the strongest global-retrieval competitors, it beats EigenPlaces (ICCV 2023) by 2.4% R@1 on Pitts30k (94.9 vs 92.5), ties on Tokyo24/7 (93.0 vs 93.0), leads by 0.9% on MSLS-val (90.0 vs 89.1), and by 1.6% on MSLS-challenge (69.0 vs 67.4). Against MixVPR (WACV 2023), the margins are larger: +3.4% on Pitts30k, +7.9% on Tokyo24/7, +2.0% on MSLS-val, and +5.0% on MSLS-challenge. The two-stage methods (Patch-NetVLAD, TransVPR) that rely on local-feature re-ranking are also surpassed by CricaVPR on all four datasets, despite CricaVPR using only a single global descriptor without any re-ranking step. On the extreme-condition datasets (Table 3), the advantage widens dramatically: CricaVPR leads MixVPR by 14.5% on Nordland, 24.5% on AmsterTime, and 20.7% on SVOX-Night, indicating that cross-image correlation awareness is particularly effective under severe appearance changes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the DINOv2 self-supervised vision transformer as a frozen backbone and extends the adapter-based parameter-efficient transfer learning paradigm (originally from NLP, popularized in vision by Convpass) to the VPR domain. It departs from prior VPR methods (NetVLAD, MixVPR, EigenPlaces, CosPlace) that produce each image's global descriptor independently, by introducing a cross-image encoder that uses attention to correlate regional features across all images in a training batch. The multi-scale convolution adapter (MulConv) is a deliberate departure from Convpass's single-scale 3×3 convolution, motivated by the observation that VPR discriminative landmarks occupy local regions of uncertain size, requiring multi-scale local priors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that cross-image correlation during training can substantially improve the robustness of global VPR descriptors without any re-ranking or local feature matching at inference time, achieving SOTA on all seven benchmark datasets with only 3.5 hours of training (vs. full-day for CosPlace/EigenPlaces). The finding that a single-scale convolution adapter (Convpass-style) actually degrades VPR performance relative to a vanilla adapter, while a multi-scale variant recovers and surpasses it, provides useful guidance for adapting foundation models to tasks with spatially heterogeneous discriminative regions. Open questions include whether the cross-image correlation mechanism can be extended to inference-time batch processing for further gains, how it scales to databases with millions of images, and whether the approach generalizes beyond street-level imagery to indoor or aerial VPR.
