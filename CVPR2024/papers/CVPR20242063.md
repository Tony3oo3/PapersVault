## Problem

Exemplar-free class-incremental learning (EFCIL) methods rely on estimating semantic drift of old class prototypes after the feature extractor is updated on new tasks, but existing drift estimation approaches (e.g., SDC) degrade sharply in small-start settings where the initial task is small and the feature space shifts considerably. Without stored exemplars, there is no direct way to measure where old prototypes have moved in the new embedding space, leading to large accuracy drops on later tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242063/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each task boundary, the method consumes the current task's training images (e.g., 32×32 for CIFAR-100, 224×224 for ImageNet-Subset/CUB-200), the stored old class prototypes, and both the old and newly trained feature extractors (ResNet-18). It produces a set of adversarially perturbed images per old class (100 samples, 3 perturbation iterations) that serve as pseudo-exemplars, and outputs updated (resurrected) old prototypes in the new feature space. At test time, classification is performed via a Nearest-Class-Mean (NCM) classifier over all prototypes in the current feature space.

## Method

Adversarial Drift Compensation (ADC) generates pseudo-exemplars for old classes by applying targeted adversarial attacks to current-task images: for each old class k, the m closest current samples to the old prototype P_{t-1}^k are iteratively perturbed (3 steps, α=25) in the direction that minimizes L2 distance to P_{t-1}^k in the old feature space, so the perturbed images are misclassified as class k by the NCM classifier. The drift for class k is then computed as the mean difference between the new and old feature extractor outputs on these adversarial samples, and the old prototype is updated by adding this drift vector. The key enabling insight is continual adversarial transferability: because knowledge distillation (LwF on logits) keeps the new model close to the old one, adversarial samples crafted in the old feature space remain effective in the new feature space. Training itself is a simple LwF baseline (cross-entropy on new classes + logits distillation from the frozen previous-task teacher) with an NCM classifier, and the entire ADC procedure adds only ~3% computational overhead per task.

## Results

On the small-start EFCIL setting, ADC achieves 59.14% last-task accuracy and 69.62% average incremental accuracy on CIFAR-100 (T=5), outperforming the second-best method SDC by 4.2% and 4.8% respectively. On the more challenging 10-task split, ADC reaches 46.48% A_last on CIFAR-100 and 32.32% on TinyImageNet, with gains of 5.12% and 5.17% over SDC. On fine-grained datasets, ADC reports particularly large margins: 64.46% A_last on CUB-200 (T=5) and 54.86% on Stanford Cars (T=7), exceeding the runner-up by 5.78% and 9.68% respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ADC outperforms all seven baselines across every dataset and task split in both A_last and A_inc. The closest competitor is SDC [52], which ADC beats by 4.2–5.12% on CIFAR-100, 0.95–5.17% on TinyImageNet, and 1.72–2.58% on ImageNet-Subset. The NCM baseline (LwF training + NCM classifier) is a surprisingly strong competitor, outperforming PASS, SSRE, FeTrIL, and FeCAM in several settings, but ADC still leads it by 5.6% (CIFAR-100 T=5 A_last) to 5.7% (TinyImageNet T=10 A_last). On fine-grained datasets (Table 2), the margins widen substantially: ADC exceeds FeCAM (the best baseline on CUB-200 T=10) by 6.19% and SDC (best on Stanford Cars T=7) by 9.68%. Notably, ADC also surpasses the exemplar-based NME method (20 exemplars/class on CIFAR-100, 50/class on TinyImageNet) in last-task accuracy, demonstrating that adversarial pseudo-exemplars can replace stored data for drift estimation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the semantic drift compensation line of work (SDC [52], NAPA-VQ [28], Prototype Reminiscence [42]) by replacing the use of raw current-task data or topological augmentation with adversarially perturbed samples that serve as pseudo-exemplars for old classes. It repurposes targeted adversarial attacks [22, 27]—a tool from the security literature—as a drift estimation mechanism, exploiting the observation that adversarial perturbations transfer from the old to the new feature space in continual learning due to knowledge distillation. The training backbone is a simple LwF [24] distillation with an NCM [36] classifier, which the authors show is a stronger baseline than most prior EFCIL methods (PASS, SSRE, FeTrIL, FeCAM) in the small-start setting they introduce.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper highlights that existing EFCIL methods, designed for big-start settings where the first task contains half the dataset, degrade substantially in the more practical small-start setting, and that a simple LwF+NCM baseline often outperforms them—this is a useful negative result for the field. ADC's ~3% computational overhead and ability to outperform exemplar-based NME (20 samples/class) on CIFAR-100 make it a practical option where storing data is infeasible. However, the method requires known task boundaries and a sufficient quantity of current-task data, limiting its applicability to online continual learning or few-shot settings, which the authors identify as open directions.
