## Problem

Existing deep learning halftoning methods produce halftones via image-to-image black-box transformations, treating the output as a binary image rather than a collection of individual ink dots. This prevents direct manipulation of dot positions to fix local artifacts, regulate the number of dots for consumable savings, or achieve dot-level dithering control that is essential for practical printing workflows.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242531/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a continuous-tone grayscale image C and a set of k randomly initialized ink dot coordinates Q = {q_0, …, q_{k-1}} (decimal 2D positions) as input, where k is set via a linear function of the image's grayscale statistics. It produces a binary halftone matrix H (0/1 pixels) along with the final optimized dot positions, enabling post-hoc local refinement. Experiments use 64×64 resolution with 20,000–30,000 dots for 256×256 images, and the dot count is directly adjustable at inference time.

## Method

The key insight is decomposing the halftone matrix H as a Hadamard product of k sparse binary matrices, each formed by the outer product of one-hot encodings of a single dot's x and y coordinates (Eq. 2), reducing the discrete-to-continuous differentiability problem to approximating one-hot encodings. O-Net, a 4-layer fully-connected network trained with dense coordinate sampling (step 0.01), learns to map continuous coordinates to one-hot vectors, enabling gradient backpropagation from the halftone matrix to dot positions. P-Net, a lightweight CNN, concatenates the current halftone H with the continuous-tone image C, applies stacked convolutions with downsampling for global context and upsampling for local detail, and outputs per-pixel 2D displacement vectors that move each dot. Training follows an iterative loop (Algorithm 1): dots are displaced by P-Net predictions, the halftone is regenerated via O-Net, and P-Net is updated using a combined tone loss (Gaussian-smoothed L2), structural loss, and inverse-halftoning loss with a frozen reconstruction network.

## Results

On 64×64 test images from DIV2K and UTKFace, the proposed method achieves an average PSNR of 43.04 dB, outperforming the best traditional baseline Ostromoukhov (41.18 dB) by ~1.9 dB and the deep learning baseline Xia et al. (33.83 dB) by ~9.2 dB. In terms of structural similarity (SSIM), the method reaches 0.1709 average, surpassing Floyd-Steinberg (0.1687) and Ostromoukhov (0.1628). The method also demonstrates unique controllability: it can reduce ink dot count from 18K to 10K while maintaining visually pleasing halftones, and can locally refine artifact regions by repositioning individual dots (improving PSNR from 37.24 to 44.43 in a targeted refinement experiment).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all four baseline categories on both PSNR and SSIM. Against traditional error-diffusion methods, it beats Ostromoukhov (41.18 dB) by 1.86 dB and Floyd-Steinberg (39.52 dB) by 3.52 dB in average PSNR, while also achieving the highest SSIM (0.1709 vs. 0.1687 for Floyd-Steinberg and 0.1628 for Ostromoukhov). Against the deep learning baseline Xia et al. [35], the improvement is substantial: 43.04 dB vs. 33.83 dB PSNR (~9.2 dB gap), with the deep learning baseline also showing a much narrower PSNR range (29.89–35.94) indicating less robustness across diverse images. Qualitatively, the paper shows that traditional methods produce striped dot distributions or regular artifacts, while Xia et al. introduces visual artifacts in flat regions; the proposed method better preserves tonal details and allows local artifact removal by repositioning individual ink dots—a capability none of the baselines support.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from both traditional halftoning (Floyd-Steinberg, Ostromoukhov, and other error-diffusion/ordered-dithering methods) and prior deep learning approaches (notably Xia et al. [35]) that treat halftoning as a global image-to-image transformation. The key conceptual shift is reframing halftoning as an iterative dot-placement optimization problem, where individual ink dot coordinates are the optimization variables rather than pixel values. The O-Net's use of Hadamard product decomposition to make discrete dot positions differentiable is a novel architectural contribution, while P-Net borrows the iterative refinement paradigm from diffusion models and point cloud generation, adapted to the high-density, fine-grained demands of halftone dot motion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dot-level controllability enables two practically important capabilities absent in prior methods: regulating ink dot quantity for consumable savings and locally refining artifact-prone regions without regenerating the entire halftone. However, quantitative experiments are limited to 64×64 images, the ~2–3% dot collision rate remains unaddressed, and the O-Net must be pre-trained and frozen, adding a two-stage training burden. Open questions include whether the approach scales to high-resolution or color halftoning (mentioned only as a future extension), how to handle collisions more gracefully, and whether the iterative refinement loop remains tractable as dot counts grow for larger prints.
