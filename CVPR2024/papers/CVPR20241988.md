## Problem

As deep learning shifts to pre-training/fine-tuning paradigms, models form complex generational dependency networks, yet no method exists to identify which parent model a given child model was fine-tuned from among a set of candidates. Unlike IP protection (a binary piracy check) or generic model similarity (which ignores the fine-tuning relationship), neural lineage detection is a multi-class provenance task that must account for the specific parameter and feature changes induced by fine-tuning.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the full parameter vectors and intermediate-layer feature matrices of both the child model and each candidate parent model, with parameters reshaped into 2D matrices (H_θ × W_θ) and features into (H_F × W_F) before being stacked along a new leading axis to align parent and child element-wise. The output is a scalar score per parent–child pair, converted via softmax into a probability distribution over all M candidate parents, from which the argmax identifies the predicted parent. The approach also extends to sub-network lineage detection, where individual transformer layers of a hybrid model are traced back to their source ViT-B models.

## Method

The paper proposes two complementary approaches. The learning-free method leverages NTK-based linearization: it approximates the fine-tuned child as f_p(x) + ∇_θp f_p(x)(θ_c − θ_p) and derives closed-form linearized versions of six similarity metrics (l1, l2, lp, log-sum-exp, CKA, DC) that fold the Jacobian computation into a single backpropagation pass, reducing memory from O(N·K·|θ|) to O(|θ|). The learning-based Lineage Detector uses shared CNN encoders (1×1 first-layer kernels for element-level parent–child alignment) to produce weight and feature tokens, which are concatenated with a learnable CLS token and processed by a multi-layer transformer whose attention captures the interdependent weight–feature changes of fine-tuning; a linear head on the CLS output yields the final score, trained with cross-entropy over the candidate set.

## Results

On the primary classification benchmark (Table 1), the learning-based Lineage Detector achieves 99.11% accuracy on CIFAR-10 with FC networks and 99.87% with ResNet-18, outperforming the best learning-free method (l₂ with approximation: 80.03% and 90.47% respectively) and the best direct-similarity baseline (l∞ without approximation: 77.32% and 90.19%). The learning-free method with approximation improves over the direct-similarity baseline by an average of 3.17% (up to 28% on CKA for FC networks). Across-generational detection (Table 2) shows the Lineage Detector maintains 97.75–99.11% accuracy even at 3-generation gaps, while direct similarity drops to 38–67%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two families of baselines: (1) direct parent–child similarity metrics (l₁, l₂, l∞, CKA, DC) computed without any fine-tuning approximation, and (2) the same metrics augmented with the proposed linearized fine-tuning approximation. The learning-based Lineage Detector dominates all baselines across every dataset and architecture, achieving 97.86–99.94% on FC networks and 99.21–99.87% on ResNet-18, with statistically significant gains (p<0.05) on most settings. Among learning-free methods, the l₁ metric with approximation is the strongest for ResNet-18 (94.64–98.92%), while l₂ with approximation leads for FC networks (80.03% on CIFAR-10 vs. 77.32% for the best direct baseline). CKA and DC consistently underperform norm-based metrics by 15–40 points, attributed to their invariance to rotation/projection that discards discriminative fine-tuning signals. The approximation step yields the largest relative gains for CKA (e.g., 4.39→5.71% on FMNIST-FC, 68.45→70.23% on FMNIST-ResNet) and DC (3.51→14.48% on FMNIST-FC), confirming that integrating the fine-tuning approximation is most beneficial for metrics that otherwise lose signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new task—neural lineage detection—by repurposing two established threads: NTK-based neural network linearization (first-order Taylor approximation of fine-tuning dynamics) and neural network representation similarity metrics (CKA, Distance Correlation, norm distances). It explicitly departs from model IP protection (watermarking, fingerprinting), which relies on pre-embedded external media and frames fine-tuning as an attack, whereas here fine-tuning is the signal to be detected. The learning-based method further extends this by replacing the fixed linear approximation with a transformer-based detector that learns the fine-tuning signature end-to-end from stacked weight and feature encodings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Neural lineage detection has direct practical implications for model provenance, intellectual property verification without watermarks, and regulatory traceability in an era of massive model repositories. The learning-free method's model-agnosticism (applicable to any architecture at any time without training) makes it immediately deployable, while the learning-based detector's 99%+ accuracy on classification tasks demonstrates the task is highly tractable. Open questions include scalability to very large candidate pools, robustness to adversarial parameter perturbations designed to obscure lineage, and the significant performance gap observed on dense-prediction tasks (segmentation accuracy drops to ~37%), suggesting the fine-tuning signal is weaker or more entangled in multi-scale architectures.
