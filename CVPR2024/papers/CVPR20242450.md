## Problem

Accurate and robust camera angle estimation from fisheye images in Manhattan world scenes remains an open challenge because general urban images often lack the geometric constraints (lines, arcs, vanishing points) that geometry-based calibration methods rely on. Existing learning-based methods either operate in a non-Manhattan world (and thus cannot estimate pan angle) or use fully-connected regressors that are sensitive to domain shift and do not exploit the geometric structure of vanishing points.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242450/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single rectangular fisheye image (center-cropped for the VP estimator) and produces five camera parameters: focal length f, distortion coefficient k₁, and the three rotation angles (pan, tilt, roll) in the Manhattan world frame. These parameters are then used to backproject 13 detected VP/ADP points onto a unit sphere and solve the absolute orientation problem to recover the full rotation, enabling remapping of the fisheye image into an undistorted view with front/side directions distinguished by the Manhattan frame.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-estimator pipeline: a VP estimator built on HRNet that performs heatmap regression to detect 13 keypoints (5 vanishing points + 8 auxiliary diagonal points) in the image, and a distortion estimator (adapted from Wakai et al. 2022) that predicts intrinsics. The key insight is introducing Auxiliary Diagonal Points at the eight cubic-corner directions, which together with the 6 VPs form an arrangement with regular octahedral symmetry and maximal 3D spatial uniformity, compensating for the frequent absence of visible VPs in real images. The HRNet loss is modified to penalize detection failures (unlike standard pose estimation), and DARK post-processing is applied to heatmaps for sub-pixel precision. At inference, the 2D VP/ADP detections are backprojected using the estimated intrinsics, and the rotation is recovered via the optimal linear attitude estimator (Rodrigues vector), yielding pan, tilt, and roll angles without requiring any arc or line detection.

## Results

On the SL-MH test set, the proposed method (HRNet-W32, 13 VP/ADP points) achieves mean absolute errors of 2.20° (pan), 3.15° (tilt), and 3.00° (roll), with a reprojection error of 5.50 pixels and a 100% executable rate. This substantially outperforms the best prior learning-based method (Wakai et al., ECCV'22: 4.13° tilt, 5.21° roll, 7.39 px REPE) and the best geometry-based method (Lochman et al., WACV'21: 22.36° pan, 44.42° tilt, 33.20° roll, 59.1% executable rate). The method also runs at 12.3 fps, at least 280× faster than the geometry-based solvers. Cross-dataset results (Tables 8–9) confirm consistent superiority across StreetLearn, SP360, and HoliCity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method wins decisively against all five baselines on every reported metric. Against the strongest learning-based baseline (Wakai et al., ECCV'22), it reduces tilt error from 4.13° to 3.15°, roll error from 5.21° to 3.00°, and REPE from 7.39 to 5.50 px, while additionally estimating pan (2.20°) which that method cannot. Against the geometry-based methods (Pritts et al., CVPR'18 and Lochman et al., WACV'21), the improvement is dramatic: pan error drops from 22–25° to 2.20°, tilt from 42–44° to 3.15°, and roll from 18–33° to 3.00°; moreover, the method achieves a 100% executable rate versus 59.1% for Lochman et al., meaning it handles images with few arcs that cause geometry-based solvers to fail. Against the earlier learning-based methods (López-Antequera, CVPR'19 and Wakai & Yamashita, ICCVW'21), REPE improves by 15× and 5.5× respectively, and tilt/roll errors are reduced by roughly 8× and 3.5×. The ablation in Table 7 further shows that adding the 8 auxiliary diagonal points (ADPs) to the 5 VPs reduces average rotation error by 15.4° compared to using VPs alone, confirming the critical role of the ADP design.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two prior lines of work: learning-based single-image camera calibration (López-Antequera et al. CVPR'19, Wakai et al. ECCV'22) that estimates tilt, roll, and distortion but ignores the Manhattan world (and thus cannot recover pan angle), and geometry-based Manhattan-world calibration (Pritts et al. CVPR'18, Lochman et al. WACV'21) that recovers full rotation but depends on detecting sufficient arcs and vanishing points. The key departure is repurposing heatmap regression from human pose estimation (HRNet, DARK) to detect vanishing points and newly introduced auxiliary diagonal points (ADPs), replacing the fragile arc-detection pipeline of geometry-based methods with a pixel-wise regression that is robust to scenes lacking explicit line structure. The ADP concept—placing eight diagonal keypoints at the vertices of a cube to complement the six octahedral VPs—has no direct precedent in prior calibration literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method enables robust, fast (280× faster than geometry-based solvers) single-image calibration of fisheye cameras in urban Manhattan-world scenes, which is a prerequisite for downstream recognition tasks (object detection, lane detection, action recognition) on undistorted wide-FOV imagery. The ablation showing that ADPs alone (10.54° pan error) outperform VPs alone (19.38°) is a useful conceptual insight: spatially uniform diagonal directions provide stronger rotational constraints than axis-aligned vanishing points when the latter are occluded or absent. However, the method still fails when fewer than two unique axes are recoverable from the detected points, and cross-domain generalization degrades substantially (pan error jumps from ~2° in-domain to ~10° on HoliCity), leaving open the question of whether multi-image or video-based inputs can close this gap.
