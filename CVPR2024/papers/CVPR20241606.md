## Problem

Visual biases in image classifiers are typically identified indirectly through visualization or sample statistics, requiring human supervision to interpret and express in an explainable form. Prior language-based approaches either retrieve from a predefined vocabulary (limiting discovery to known biases) or generate detailed captions that are informative but impractical for downstream debiasing. The paper addresses the gap of providing a compact, explainable, and directly actionable representation of visual bias.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241606/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a trained image classifier and its class-wise validation set, partitioned into correctly and incorrectly predicted subsets. A pretrained captioning model (ClipCap by default) generates text descriptions for mispredicted images, from which the YAKE algorithm extracts candidate keywords. The output is a ranked list of bias keywords with CLIP scores (the difference in average cosine similarity between the keyword and wrong vs. correct images), which can be further converted into sample-wise bias group labels via a CLIP zero-shot classifier for downstream debiasing.

## Method

B2T (Bias-to-Text) operates in two steps: (1) it generates captions for class-wise mispredicted images using a pretrained captioning model and extracts common keywords via the YAKE algorithm, and (2) it validates each keyword by computing a CLIP score defined as sim(keyword, D_wrong) − sim(keyword, D_correct), where sim is the average cosine similarity between the keyword's text embedding and image embeddings in each subset. Keywords with high positive CLIP scores are confirmed as biases because they align more strongly with misclassified images than correct ones. The keyword form is the key design choice: it provides a natural group name for each bias, enabling direct integration with debiasing techniques such as DRO (by inferring per-sample bias labels via CLIP zero-shot classification), CLIP prompt augmentation (inserting fine-grained keywords like 'bamboo' instead of coarse group names like 'land'), and cross-model comparison. The approach is zero-shot with respect to bias discovery—no predefined bias vocabulary or iterative training is required—and is robust across different captioning and scoring models.

## Results

On CelebA blond, DRO-B2T achieves 90.4±0.9% worst-group accuracy and 93.2% average accuracy, surpassing ERM (47.7% worst-group) and even DRO with ground-truth bias labels (90.0% worst-group). On Waterbirds, DRO-B2T reaches 90.7±0.3% worst-group and 92.1% average accuracy, again exceeding DRO with GT labels (89.9% worst-group). For CLIP zero-shot prompting, augmenting prompts with B2T positive keywords improves worst-group accuracy from 50.3% (base CLIP) to 61.7% on Waterbirds and from 76.2% to 80.0% on CelebA blond.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DRO-B2T outperforms all unsupervised debiased training baselines on worst-group accuracy: it beats CNC (88.8% on CelebA, 88.5% on Waterbirds) by ~1.6–2.2 points, JTT (81.5%, 83.8%) by ~8.9–6.9 points, LfF (77.2%, 78.0%) by ~13.2–12.7 points, and GEORGE (54.9%, 76.2%) by ~35.5–14.5 points. Notably, DRO-B2T also slightly exceeds DRO with ground-truth bias labels (90.4 vs. 90.0 on CelebA; 90.7 vs. 89.9 on Waterbirds), suggesting that B2T-inferred labels are more reliable than the noisy GT annotations. For bias discovery, B2T achieves substantially higher AUROC than Domino [17] and Failure Direction [30] on both CelebA and Waterbirds (Figure 5), confirming superior sample-wise bias labeling. In CLIP zero-shot prompting (Table 2), B2T-pos keywords improve worst-group accuracy over the base group-name prompt [85] by 3.3 points on CelebA (80.0 vs. 76.7) and 8.0 points on Waterbirds (61.7 vs. 53.7), while B2T-neg keywords degrade performance, validating that the CLIP score correctly identifies bias-relevant keywords.

## Relation to Prior Work

B2T carves new ground by repurposing pretrained captioning models (ClipCap, BLIP, etc.) and CLIP scoring in a pipeline that was not previously assembled for bias discovery. Prior bias-discovery methods either retrieve mispredicted samples and require human interpretation (JTT, GEORGE), match outliers to a predefined vocabulary in CLIP embedding space (Domino, Failure Direction), or generate full-sentence descriptions of individual failure cases (Wiles et al.) that are informative but impractical for downstream debiasing. B2T's key conceptual departure is the intermediate keyword form: free-text captions are reduced to short, group-naming keywords via YAKE, which are then validated by a CLIP-score gap between mispredicted and correctly predicted images, yielding a practical handle for DRO training, prompt augmentation, and model comparison.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The keyword explanation form bridges the gap between interpretable bias identification and actionable debiasing, enabling practitioners to plug discovered keywords directly into DRO training, CLIP prompting, and cross-model failure analysis without additional annotation. The paper's limitations are tied to the quality of upstream captioning and scoring models, which may underperform in uncommon domains (medical, satellite imagery), and the approach is validated primarily on classification tasks. Open questions include whether the keyword form generalizes to multi-label or open-vocabulary settings, whether more advanced captioners (GPT-4-class) yield meaningfully different or more reliable bias keywords, and how the method scales when biases are entangled across multiple attributes simultaneously.
