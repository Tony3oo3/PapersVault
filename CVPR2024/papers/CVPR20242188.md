## Problem

Real-time rendering super-resolution must simultaneously preserve sharp texture details, maintain temporal stability, and avoid ghosting artifacts, yet existing RRSR methods produce blurry results due to limited texture information in low-resolution inputs, and ghosting persists when motion vectors become unreliable in occluded regions of dynamic scenes. Video super-resolution methods that could recover details are too expensive (relying on optical flow or bidirectional propagation) to run in real time.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242188/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The network consumes a low-resolution lighting component (demodulated from the rendered radiance), a high-resolution material component, traditional and dual motion vectors, and G-buffers (normal and depth maps), all at the LR resolution except the HR material component. It outputs a high-resolution super-resolved radiance image at 1920×1080 (default 4×4 upsampling). Unusually, the SR network operates only on the smooth lighting component in LR space and performs upsampling after reconstruction via pixel-unshuffle, then multiplies the result with the HR material component to recover full texture detail.

## Method

The key insight is radiance demodulation: the rendered radiance is decomposed into a smooth lighting component and a detailed material component (following the BRDF pre-integration factorization of Zhuang et al.), so SR is applied only to the smoother lighting signal while the HR material component is used directly for re-modulation, bypassing the need to hallucinate texture. A motion mask is constructed by subtracting the dual motion vector from the traditional motion vector and binarizing the result, explicitly marking previously-occluded regions; this mask feeds a gated convolution that learns to suppress unreliable temporal information in those regions. The reconstruction backbone is a lightweight frame-recurrent network combining a ConvLSTM (to exploit intermediate feature states across frames) with a U-shaped module containing residual channel attention blocks, trained with a composite loss of smooth L1, SSIM, and a temporal consistency loss that penalizes warping error between adjacent reconstructed frames. The entire pipeline runs in ~14 ms with 1.61M parameters, achieving over 3× speedup over direct HR ray-traced rendering.

## Results

On the seven Unity-rendered scenes at 4×4 SR (target 1920×1080), the proposed method achieves PSNR of 21.58–37.03 dB and SSIM of 0.7306–0.9828, outperforming all six deep-learning baselines on nearly every scene. For example, on the challenging Bistro scene it reaches 26.43 dB / 0.8739 SSIM (vs. 25.37 / 0.8108 for TTVSR) and 0.141 LPIPS (vs. 0.278 for RVRT). The method uses only 1.61 M parameters and runs in 12.41 ms (TensorRT FP16), compared to 7.32 M / 98.69 ms for BasicVSR++ and >10.78 M / >100 ms for TTVSR and RVRT, meeting real-time budgets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method is compared against six deep-learning VSR/RRSR baselines (FRVSR, TecoGAN, NSRR, BasicVSR++, TTVSR, RVRT) and two industry solutions (DLSS 2.0, FSR 2.0). It wins on PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS across all seven scenes except Pica, where RVRT edges it by 0.06 dB PSNR (37.09 vs. 37.03) and 0.49 VMAF (85.92 vs. 85.43), though the proposed method still leads on SSIM (0.9828 vs. 0.9821) and LPIPS (0.029 vs. 0.030). The largest margins appear on texture-heavy scenes: Bistro gains +1.01 dB PSNR over the best baseline (TTVR) and San_M gains +1.17 dB, while LPIPS drops from ~0.28 to 0.141 on Bistro. NSRR, the closest RRSR-specific baseline, shows visible ghosting artifacts in dynamic scenes that the proposed motion mask eliminates. In terms of efficiency, the method uses 1.61 M parameters and 12.41 ms inference—roughly 6× faster than BasicVSR++ (98.69 ms) and 8× faster than TTVSR/RVRT (>100 ms)—while NSRR, though smaller (0.53 M), is still 2× slower (23.94 ms). Against DLSS 2.0 and FSR 2.0 (evaluated at 2×2 since they do not support 4×4), the proposed method avoids DLSS's severe aliasing and FSR's over-blurring, preserving sharper texture detail.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes radiance demodulation—a technique established in real-time rendering denoising (Zhuang et al., CGF 2021)—into the super-resolution domain, which is the core conceptual departure from prior work. It sits at the intersection of two lines of research: video super-resolution methods (BasicVSR++, TTVSR, RVRT) that are too computationally heavy for real-time use, and real-time rendering SR methods (NSRR, DLSS, FSR) that cannot recover fine texture details from low-resolution inputs. The motion mask construction directly leverages the dual motion vector formulation from Zeng et al. (CGF 2021), and the recurrent architecture draws on the ConvLSTM and frame-recurrent design lineage from FRVSR and BasicVSR++. The key positioning is that by SR-ing only the smooth lighting component and re-modulating with a directly available HR material component, the method sidesteps the fundamental information bottleneck that limits all prior RRSR approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for game and VR developers: the method achieves over 3× speedup compared to direct high-resolution ray-traced rendering while producing visually superior results, and the 14 ms total inference time meets real-time budgets. The radiance demodulation insight—that texture detail lives in the material component which can be captured at full resolution without global light transport—opens a clean decomposition that could inform other rendering post-processing tasks. However, the approach is tightly coupled to rendering pipelines that expose G-buffers and material components, limiting its applicability to natural video or captured footage. Open questions include whether the method generalizes to more complex view-dependent materials (e.g., clearcoat, subsurface scattering) and whether the per-scene training requirement can be relaxed for production use.
