## Problem

Intrinsic image decomposition (separating albedo from shading) for Lambertian scenes is fundamentally ill-posed under arbitrary shape and illumination, requiring handcrafted or learned spatial priors. This paper identifies that the light lost to absorption—previously unobservable in the visible spectrum—can be measured via a co-located thermal camera, and proves that access to this absorbed light renders the decomposition a pixel-wise well-posed problem.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240541/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method requires a single visible HDR image (composited from a 15-exposure stack, 600×800) and a short thermal video (~4 sec, 200 frames at 60 Hz, 512×640) captured immediately after turning on illumination, with the scene at thermal equilibrium beforehand. The two cameras are co-located via a gold dichroic beamsplitter and aligned by homography. Output is a per-pixel albedo map (grayscale or wavelength-dependent RGB), a shading map, and an absorbed light intensity estimate, all computed independently at each pixel with no spatial regularization.

## Method

The approach has two stages grounded in energy conservation. First, absorbed light intensity is estimated from the thermal video by modeling heat transport without conduction (valid in the short transient immediately after light onset), yielding a closed-form 2-parameter exponential solution; per-pixel gradient descent fits this curve to recover a constant c₁ proportional to absorbed power. Second, albedo and shading are computed in closed form: for grayscale albedo, ρ = πI_v / (πI_v + ζS̃) and η = πI_v + ζS̃, where ζ is a calibrated relative scale factor; for wavelength-dependent albedo, the reflectance spectrum is parameterized in a low-dimensional basis (M=3 for RGB), reducing the problem to solving K+1 linear equations in M+1 unknowns via non-negative least squares. The key insight is that absorbed light mirrors reflected light in functional form but scaled by (1−ρ), so the sum of the two equals irradiance at every pixel, providing the constraint that makes the decomposition well-posed without any shape or illumination model.

## Results

On the painted mask scene, the proposed method achieves a si-MSE of 0.084 for albedo and 0.0005 for shading, substantially outperforming RGB-Retinex (0.253 / 0.0030) and Ordinal Shading (0.399 / 0.0080). On the color chart under four different illuminations, the mean albedo error is 0.020 for the proposed method versus 0.034 for Retinex and 0.080 for Ordinal Shading. Qualitatively, the method correctly separates shape-related shading (e.g., concave nose region, embossed patterns) from flat albedo across four diverse scenes with complex geometry and unknown illumination.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: the classical Retinex algorithm (Belhumeur et al., 1999) and the SOTA learning-based Ordinal Shading method (Careaga & Aksoy, 2023). The proposed physics-based method wins decisively on both metrics. For albedo si-MSE, the proposed method (0.084) is roughly 3× better than Retinex (0.253) and nearly 5× better than Ordinal Shading (0.399). For shading si-MSE, the gap is even larger: 0.0005 vs. 0.0030 (Retinex) and 0.0080 (Ordinal Shading), a 6× and 16× improvement respectively. On the color chart, the mean albedo error is 0.020 vs. 0.034 (Retinex) and 0.080 (Ordinal Shading). The key advantage is that the proposed method operates per-pixel using energy conservation, while both baselines rely on spatial priors (hand-crafted for Retinex, learned for Ordinal Shading), which leave residual albedo in their estimated shading maps.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously disconnected fields: intrinsic image decomposition (a decades-old ill-posed problem in vision) and computational thermal imaging. It departs from prior intrinsic decomposition work (Retinex, learned priors, photometric stereo) by introducing a new physical input—absorbed light intensity estimated from a thermal video—rather than relying on spatial priors or learned models. The closest prior work, Tanaka et al. [32], also uses thermal videos but performs direct-global light transport decomposition in the infrared spectrum, not visible-spectrum albedo-shading separation. The low-dimensional albedo subspace assumption [10, 14–16] is borrowed from color constancy literature to handle wavelength-dependent reflectance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core contribution is a clean theoretical result: energy conservation at each pixel, combined with a thermal measurement of absorbed light, makes single-view albedo-shading separation pixel-wise well-posed for Lambertian scenes under arbitrary shape and illumination—eliminating the need for spatial priors or learned models. However, practical applicability is constrained by the requirement for a co-located thermal camera, a controlled light-on/off protocol, thermal equilibrium before illumination, and negligible heat conduction (short time window). Open questions include extending the theory to general BRDFs, translucent materials, and subsurface scattering, as well as handling scenes where conduction cannot be ignored or where the background also heats up significantly.
