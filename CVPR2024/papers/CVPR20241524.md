## Problem

Replay-free architectural methods for continual learning are largely ineffective in the class-incremental learning (CIL) setting because they require task identifiers at test time to select the correct network subregion, a signal unavailable in CIL. The few architectural approaches that do operate without task identifiers (e.g., SpaceNet, AFAF) significantly underperform even simple replay baselines. This paper asks whether a replay-free architecture can match or exceed replay methods in CIL by leveraging the sequential structure of learning within the network itself.

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images (28×28 for MNIST/FashionMNIST/EMNIST, 32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 for Tiny ImageNet) and outputs a class label from all classes seen so far. Unusually, at test time the network first produces a binary activation vector (thresholded at mean + 1 std per layer) across all layers, which is fed to a context-detector of chained logistic regressions to infer the originating episode; only then are output neurons masked to the relevant age group before the final softmax, making the prediction a two-stage process rather than a single forward pass.

## Method

NICE assigns each neuron a maturation age (0 = surplus, 1 = plastic, 2+ = frozen) inspired by adult hippocampal neurogenesis. Every p epochs, age-1 neurons are pruned back to age-0 by solving a greedy set-cover that retains the smallest subset contributing ≥95% of layer activation; at episode boundaries, surviving age-1 neurons mature to age-2 and all older neurons increment. To prevent interference, incoming connections to age-2+ neurons are frozen and connections from younger to older neurons are pruned, guaranteeing zero forgetting in mature subnetworks. A context-detector stores binary activation patterns for recent samples and fits chained logistic regressions to predict which episode a test sample belongs to, enabling the network to mask out irrelevant output neurons without any external task signal. The only additional hyperparameter is the pruning interval p, and the total parameter count is lower than a standard dense network due to pruning.

## Results

On the standard memory budget, NICE achieves 83.4% on MNIST, 73.9% on Fashion-MNIST, 66.3% on EMNIST, 55.1% on CIFAR-10, 20.3% on CIFAR-100, and 11.8% on Tiny-ImageNet (average accuracy across all classes after the final episode). NICE outperforms all eight replay baselines on four of six datasets (MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, EMNIST, CIFAR-10) and is competitive on the remaining two. With a doubled memory budget (×2), NICE extends its lead on most datasets (e.g., 85.9% MNIST, 75.1% Fashion-MNIST, 69.4% EMNIST, 56.5% CIFAR-10), though x-DER surpasses it on CIFAR-100 (25.7% vs. 22.0%) and Tiny-ImageNet (14.8% vs. 12.2%). At the quadrupled budget (×4), replay methods such as x-DER and iCaRL begin to outperform NICE, suggesting replay dominates when ample memory approximates i.i.d. conditions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NICE outperforms all eight replay baselines on the standard memory budget for MNIST (83.4% vs. best replay 81.1% TAMiL), Fashion-MNIST (73.9% vs. 68.7% iCaRL), EMNIST (66.3% vs. 65.3% iCaRL), and CIFAR-10 (55.1% vs. 54.2% iCaRL). On CIFAR-100, x-DER leads with 24.7% versus NICE's 20.3%, while on Tiny-ImageNet NICE edges out x-DER (11.8% vs. 11.6%). iCaRL is the strongest replay baseline overall, ranking in the top-3 on every dataset at standard and ×2 budgets, thanks to its nearest-mean-of-exemplars classifier and knowledge distillation. TAMiL and x-DER, despite being more recent, underperform iCaRL substantially on CIFAR-10 (26.8% and 37.2% respectively vs. iCaRL's 54.2%). At the ×4 budget, x-DER (28.5% CIFAR-100, 16.8% Tiny-ImageNet) and iCaRL (25.8% CIFAR-100, 15.8% Tiny-ImageNet) surpass NICE, indicating that with very large memory the setting approaches i.i.d. training where replay naturally excels.

## Relation to Prior Work

NICE carves new ground by combining biological neurogenesis (adult-born granule cells in the hippocampus) with a fixed-architecture DNN, introducing neuron aging, iterative pruning, and weight freezing to create isolated subnetworks per episode. It departs from prior architectural CIL methods (SpaceNet, AFAF) that require task identifiers at test time, and from regularization methods that fail in CIL, by using a learned context detector (chained logistic regressions on thresholded binary activations) to infer which subnetwork to activate without any external signal. The context-detection mechanism draws a conceptual parallel to OOD detection via ReACT, where neurons exhibit distinctive activation signatures for in-distribution versus out-of-distribution inputs, repurposing that phenomenon for episodic context discrimination.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NICE is the first replay-free architectural method to match or exceed replay baselines in CIL at standard and 2× memory budgets across six datasets and three architectures, challenging the long-held assumption that replay is indispensable for CIL. However, the gains are modest (typically 1–5 points), replay methods reclaim the lead at 4× budget, and NICE faces a hard ceiling from neuron exhaustion as episodes accumulate. Open questions include how to integrate limited-window replay with NICE's freezing schedule, whether the logistic-regression context detector can be replaced by richer sequential models, and how the method behaves under significant domain shifts that would require reactivating early-layer capacity.
