## Problem

Existing jigsaw puzzle solvers rely on discriminative CNN classifiers to predict piece positions or permutations, which scale poorly to large numbers of pieces, cannot handle a variable number of elements, and fail when pieces are missing because they prioritize learning order over capturing the full data structure. This paper addresses the gap by formulating puzzle solving as a generative problem that can simultaneously determine positions of available pieces and synthesize content for missing ones.

## Input / Output

The method takes unordered image patches (e.g., 3×3 grids of 64×64 crops from 255×255 images, or 96×96 fragments with 48-px gaps) or shuffled video frames/clips (single frames up to 8-frame segments), with an arbitrary subset optionally masked. It outputs 2D positional encodings (32-dim: 16 for x, 16 for y) for image patches or 1D encodings (16-dim) for video frames, plus reconstructed visual content for any missing pieces. For high-resolution video (e.g., QST at >1024×1024), frames are first compressed into a latent space before diffusion is applied, and the model operates on these latent variables rather than raw pixels.

## Method

JPDVT represents a solved puzzle as a set of (visual-content embedding, positional-encoding) pairs and formulates reassembly as a conditional diffusion denoising problem: the forward process gradually adds Gaussian noise to positional encodings (and to visual embeddings of missing pieces), while the reverse process, implemented by a diffusion transformer, denoises them conditioned on the observed visual content. The transformer backbone is a modified ViT with adaptive normalization layers before and after multi-head attention and MLP blocks to inject the diffusion timestep, and it uses relative positional embeddings anchored to the first patch to prevent collapse into a simple classification task. For missing pieces, the model jointly generates both their positions and their visual content embeddings in a single reverse-diffusion pass, enabling inpainting-free reconstruction. For video, a modified U-Net (2D spatial convolutions only, no temporal convolutions or temporal positional embeddings) performs the denoising, with latent-space diffusion for high-resolution inputs. A linear noise schedule with T=1000 steps is used throughout, and the training objective is standard noise-prediction MSE on the positional encodings.

## Results

On 3×3 image puzzles, JPDVT achieves 68.7% puzzle-level accuracy on ImageNet-1k (vs. 47.3% for SAJ and 48% for Deepzzle) and 71.3% piece-level accuracy on JPwLEG-3 (vs. 59.7% for Siamese). For large-scale 150-piece puzzles with 7% erosion, JPDVT reaches 45% puzzle-level accuracy versus only 10% for SJP-ED. On temporal puzzles, JPDVT achieves the lowest Normalized Kendall Distance across MovingMNIST, CLEVRER, and UCF101 for all piece counts tested, and is second-best on QST (where STP uses ground-truth feature correspondences).

## Baselines & Comparisons

JPDVT outperforms all image-puzzle baselines: it beats SAJ [31] by 21.4 points in puzzle-level accuracy on ImageNet-1k (68.7% vs. 47.3%), Deepzzle [19] by 20.7 points (68.7% vs. 48%), and SJP-ED [3] by 35 points on 150-piece puzzles (45% vs. 10%). On JPwLEG-3, JPDVT leads Siamese [24] (71.3% vs. 59.7%), Puzzlet [25] (71.3% vs. 58.2%), SJP-ED [3] (71.3% vs. 55.2%), and Deepzzle [19] (71.3% vs. 52.3%) in piece-level accuracy. For temporal puzzles, JPDVT achieves the best Normalized Kendall Distance on MovingMNIST, CLEVRER, and UCF101 across all piece counts, substantially outperforming J-VAD [30] (e.g., 2.6 vs. 268.2 on CLEVRER 1×32) and VCOP [33] (e.g., 0.3 vs. 14.5 on UCF 8×4). The only setting where JPDVT does not achieve the best score is QST, where STP [6] reports zero distance; however, STP relies on ground-truth feature correspondences for structured videos and PCA decomposition for dynamic textures, whereas JPDVT operates directly on raw pixels without auxiliary inputs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes jigsaw puzzle solving from a discriminative classification problem (predicting positions or permutations via CNN backbones) into a conditional generative diffusion problem, where positional encodings are denoised given visual content embeddings. Architecturally, it builds on diffusion transformers (Peebles et al.) with adaptive normalization for timestep conditioning, ViT backbones (Beyer et al.), and latent diffusion for high-resolution video (Rombach et al.), departing from the CNN-based feature extraction and pairwise classification pipelines that dominate prior jigsaw solvers. The key conceptual departure is leveraging the transformer's equivariance to shuffling and the generative nature of diffusion to simultaneously handle missing pieces—a capability absent from all prior discriminative approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ability to solve puzzles with missing pieces (both spatial and temporal) opens applications in document restoration, archaeology, and video frame interpolation that were previously intractable with discriminative methods. The unified framework for image and video puzzles, scaling to 150 pieces, suggests the diffusion-based positional encoding paradigm could generalize to other reassembly or ordering tasks. However, inference is an order of magnitude slower than discriminative baselines (~10s vs ~1s per video), and the comparison set is limited (3–4 baselines per dataset), leaving open whether the advantage persists against more recent or stronger discriminative approaches at scale.
