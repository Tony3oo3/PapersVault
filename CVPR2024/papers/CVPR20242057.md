## Problem

Video-to-video synthesis struggles to maintain temporal consistency across frames. Prior approaches either rely on implicit spatial-temporal attention that only loosely captures motion, or treat estimated optical flow as a rigid constraint (e.g., inpainting occluded regions), which propagates flow estimation errors into the output. The paper addresses how to leverage optical flow's structural guidance while gracefully handling its inherent imperfections.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242057/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an input video (N frames at 512×512), a target text prompt, and derives spatial conditions (depth maps or canny edges) and estimated optical flow (via UniMatch) from the input. The first frame is edited by any off-the-shelf I2I model, then edits are propagated to subsequent frames. Output is a temporally consistent edited video; by default 16 keyframes are generated at interval 4 and interpolated to 32 FPS via RIFE, with autoregressive batching (last frame becomes next batch's first frame) for longer clips.

## Method

FlowVid inflates a spatially-controlled I2I diffusion model (ControlNet-based U-Net) to video by replacing 2D convolutions with pseudo-3D layers and adding spatial-temporal self-attention that attends to the first and previous frames. The key architectural contribution is encoding optical flow as a soft temporal condition: the first frame is warped to each subsequent frame using estimated flow, and the resulting flow-warped video (with occluded regions marked) is concatenated with the noisy latent as an additional input channel, alongside spatial conditions, occlusion masks, and the first frame itself. Training uses v-parameterization and a joint loss over all four condition types. At generation, an edit-propagate pipeline first edits frame 1 with any I2I model, warps the edit through the input video's flow, and feeds the flow-warped edited video plus spatial controls into the trained model for DDIM denoising. Self-attention features from DDIM inversion of the input video are fused during generation to preserve original structure, and a global color calibration step prevents graying in autoregressive evaluation.

## Results

On 25 DAVIS videos with 115 manually designed prompts (5 participants), FlowVid achieves a 45.7% human preference rate, outperforming TokenFlow (40.4%), Rerender (10.2%), and CoDeF (3.5%). In terms of efficiency, FlowVid generates a 4-second 512×512 video in 1.5 minutes on a single A-100 GPU, making it 3.1×, 7.2×, and 10.5× faster than CoDeF, Rerender, and TokenFlow respectively. The ablation study confirms that combining all four condition types (spatial controls, flow-warped video, flow occlusion masks, and first frame) yields the best results, with the full model outperforming any subset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FlowVid outperforms all three baselines on both quality and speed. Against TokenFlow (the closest competitor at 40.4% preference), FlowVid achieves a 5.3 percentage-point higher preference rate while being 10.5× faster (1.5 min vs. 15.8 min), as TokenFlow requires ~500 DDIM inversion steps per frame. Against Rerender (10.2%), FlowVid wins by a large margin of 35.5 percentage points and is 7.2× faster, since Rerender generates frames sequentially and suffers from color shifts and bright flickering. Against CoDeF (3.5%), FlowVid dominates by 42.2 percentage points and is 3.1× faster; CoDeF's per-video optimization for canonical image construction leads to significant blurriness under large motion. The paper also notes that TokenFlow sometimes fails to sufficiently alter the video per the prompt, while Rerender struggles with large motions and CoDeF degrades with significant camera/object movement.

## Relation to Prior Work

FlowVid sits at the intersection of three research threads: spatially-conditioned I2I diffusion (ControlNet, T2I-Adapter), video diffusion with spatial-temporal attention (Tune-A-Video, Pix2Video, FateZero), and optical-flow-guided V2V synthesis (Rerender, CoDeF, VideoControlNet). Its key conceptual departure is treating estimated optical flow as a soft, supplementary reference rather than a hard constraint for inpainting or canonical-image construction, and pairing it with spatial conditions (depth/canny) to compensate for flow inaccuracies. The decoupled edit-propagate pipeline—editing the first frame with any off-the-shelf I2I model and then propagating via a trained video diffusion model—is architecturally distinct from prior methods that jointly generate all frames or require per-video optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 3–10× runtime advantage over competing V2V methods makes high-quality video editing practically accessible, and the plug-and-play compatibility with arbitrary I2I editors broadens the range of achievable modifications without retraining. The autoregressive extension with color calibration opens a path to longer video generation, though the reliance on first-frame structural alignment and vulnerability to large occlusions remain open challenges. A key open question is whether the soft-flow formulation generalizes to more complex multi-object scenes or camera motions where both flow and spatial conditions degrade simultaneously.
