## Problem

Decomposing an object's appearance into materials and illumination is fundamentally ambiguous (a shiny object under blurry light is indistinguishable from a diffuse object under sharp light) and severely ill-conditioned for diffuse surfaces, which act as low-pass filters on incoming light. Prior methods either recover only the BRDF-convolved lighting without explicit material decomposition or exploit only internal self-shadowing among observed objects. This paper shows that unintended shadows cast by unobserved moving occluders (e.g., the camera operator) provide a strong signal that resolves both the ambiguity and the conditioning problem.

## Input / Output

The method takes T multi-view RGB images (400×400, 256 views in experiments) with known camera poses and optionally a known or NeRF-reconstructed object mesh; the occluders themselves are never directly observed, existing only as their shadow effects on the object. It jointly recovers three outputs: a 50×100 equirectangular environment map with exponential nonlinearity for HDR, a spatially-varying BRDF (RGB albedo, scalar roughness, scalar specular reflectance) represented by a coordinate-based MLP, and per-image occluder masks as spherical-harmonic functions on radius-r_t spheres centered at the scene origin. Notably, the occluder shapes are treated as unknowns to be optimized rather than as noise to be discarded.

## Method

The core contribution is a differentiable Monte Carlo ray tracing pipeline that jointly optimizes three components via gradient descent: (i) occluder masks parameterized as spherical harmonics (degree P) mapped through a sigmoid to [0,1] on per-image spherical shells, (ii) an environment map as an image pyramid with exponential nonlinearity, and (iii) a spatially-varying BRDF (Lambertian diffuse + GGX specular) encoded by a coordinate-based MLP with positional encoding. Rendering uses multiple importance sampling and models both occluder shadows and self-occlusions while neglecting global illumination. The key insight is that explicitly modeling occluders makes the inverse problem *more* well-conditioned rather than less: the sharp angular discontinuities at occlusion boundaries inject high-frequency signal into the per-image illumination, counteracting the low-pass filtering of diffuse BRDFs. Optimization uses Adam with a variance-reduced L2 loss (two independent Monte Carlo renders per pixel) and separate learning rates (3×10⁻³ for environment/materials, 1 for occluders), implemented entirely in JAX.

## Results

On the Stanford-ORB salt004 scene, the method recovers albedo at 19.3 dB PSNR with occluder modeling versus 14.4 dB without, demonstrating a ~5 dB improvement from exploiting unintended shadows. In the parameterization ablation (Table 1), the full model achieves the lowest environment-map RMSE across all four synthetic scenes (e.g., 0.011 on potatoes vs. 0.015 for direct environment map optimization and 0.997 for direct spherical harmonics). Figure 9 shows that the relative MSE of recovered environment maps consistently decreases when occluders are modeled, with the gap widening as illumination frequency increases, material roughness increases, and occluder size grows.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external inverse-rendering methods; instead, it ablates its own components to isolate the contribution of each design choice. The most important comparison is with-occluders vs. without-occluders: modeling unobserved occluders consistently reduces environment-map RMSE across all tested illumination frequencies, material roughness levels, and occluder sizes (Figure 9), and improves albedo PSNR from 14.4 dB to 19.3 dB on the real Stanford-ORB capture (Figure 8). For parameterization, spherical-harmonic occluder representation is critical—replacing it with direct per-pixel mask optimization degrades RMSE by roughly 2–3× (e.g., 0.038 → 0.831 on the chair scene). The image-pyramid environment map representation also outperforms direct optimization of pre-exponentiated values, yielding smoother and more accurate illumination reconstructions (Figure 10). These internal comparisons establish that both the occluder modeling and the specific parameterizations are necessary for the method's performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the inverse rendering literature by introducing unobserved external occluders as a joint optimization variable alongside materials and illumination. It builds directly on Swedish et al.'s linear formulation for recovering high-frequency lighting from self-shadowing among known diffuse objects, replacing it with differentiable Monte Carlo ray tracing to handle arbitrary spatially-varying BRDFs and external occluders. The occlusion-based light recovery idea also draws from passive non-line-of-sight imaging (Baradad et al., Torralba & Freeman), which exploits known occluder shapes on planar surfaces; Eclipse generalizes to arbitrary, time-varying, and unknown occluder geometries on non-planar objects. The rendering backbone relies on modern differentiable Monte Carlo ray tracers (Li et al., Mitsuba 2) for gradient computation through visibility discontinuities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key insight—that shadows cast by camera operators, typically treated as nuisance artifacts, carry exploitable signal for disambiguating materials from lighting—reframes a common capture problem as an asset. This is particularly impactful for diffuse objects where inverse rendering is severely ill-conditioned and prior methods can only recover convolved (blurred) lighting. However, the method currently assumes known or NeRF-approximated geometry, far-field illumination, and neglects global illumination, limiting real-world applicability. Open questions include whether the approach scales to scenes with multiple simultaneous occluders, dynamic lighting, or unknown geometry without a NeRF proxy, and whether the spherical-harmonic occluder parameterization is sufficient for complex real-world occluder shapes like human bodies.
