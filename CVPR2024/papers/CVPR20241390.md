## Problem

Passive depth estimation methods such as stereo and defocus fundamentally rely on scene texture to establish correspondences, making textureless surfaces (e.g., a uniform white wall) widely considered impossible to reconstruct without active illumination. This paper challenges that assumption by demonstrating that the spatial coherence of natural light itself can serve as a texture cue, enabling fully passive depth recovery of textureless objects.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20241390/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system captures four phase-shifted interference intensity images (I_t) of a scene under spatially incoherent natural illumination, with a narrowband filter (633 nm, 5 nm FWHM) ensuring effective monochromaticity. A scanning lens translates laterally to acquire one vertical column at a time, and the full 3D map is assembled from multiple column scans. The output is a per-pixel depth map in millimeters and a 3D surface reconstruction, with measurable depth range and resolution bounded by the coherence length of the illuminant (approximately 8 mm range at M=1 magnification in the prototype).

## Method

The core mechanism is a self-interference Michelson interferometer in which one 4f arm images the scene via a mirror and the other arm uses a hollow-roof retroreflector to produce a horizontally flipped copy at the sensor. Because natural light is spatially incoherent, only the scene point on the central axis—where the point coincides with its own flipped image—yields a non-zero interference signal, effectively isolating a single coherent 'texture' point per image row. A tilted orthographic camera, realized by shifting the aperture in the Fourier plane of a second 4f system, converts the depth of that isolated point into a measurable horizontal disparity shift, with depth computed as z = x_p / β. Phase-shifting interferometry (T ≥ 3 equally spaced phase delays) extracts the complex interference component J from the captured intensity images, and the full 3D reconstruction is obtained by translating the scanning lens to sweep across the scene and combining per-column depth estimates.

## Results

The lab prototype successfully reconstructs textureless targets under natural illumination. For a metallic stair target, the average measured distance between stair planes is ~0.99 mm versus a ground truth of 0.9 mm. For a cylindrical metal pen, the reconstructed depth map fits the expected circular shape with an average error of ~0.1 mm. The system achieves a depth range of approximately 8 mm at unit magnification and can resolve up to ~38 depth planes under indoor illumination (Δc ≈ 4 μm) or ~500 depth planes under sunlight (Δc ≈ 60 μm).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not perform quantitative comparisons against existing depth estimation methods, as its primary contribution is demonstrating that passive reconstruction of textureless objects is possible at all—a task previously considered impossible with passive techniques. The paper positions itself against the conventional wisdom that stereo, defocus, and shading-based methods fundamentally cannot recover depth of textureless surfaces. Rather than benchmarking against structured light or time-of-flight systems (which are active and thus not directly comparable in a passive setting), the authors validate their approach through physical measurements: linear disparity-vs-depth response (Fig. 8c), contrast degradation with depth (Fig. 8b), and geometric accuracy on known shapes (stair target, cylindrical pen). The swept-angle laser illumination serves as a controlled reference to verify that the system's behavior matches theoretical predictions before testing under truly natural (sunlight and indoor) illumination.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the self-interference framework of incoherent digital holography (Rosen et al.) and the specific 4f mirror/retroreflector optical configuration previously used by Kotwal et al. to visualize source coherence. It departs from prior self-interference methods (FINCH, COACH) that convolve the scene with a complex PSF—useless for uniform scenes—by instead using self-interference to isolate individual coherent wavefronts and measure their disparity shift. Conceptually, it reframes the stereo correspondence problem: rather than requiring intensity-based texture, it exploits the spatial coherence of natural light as an implicit per-point identifier, effectively turning coherence into a texture feature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a long-standing assumption in passive vision that textureless depth recovery is fundamentally impossible, demonstrating a physically grounded workaround. However, practical applicability is severely constrained: the method requires sufficient coherence length (sunlight works, many indoor sources do not), a narrowband spectral filter (long exposures), column-by-column scanning, and performs poorly on subsurface-scattering or specular surfaces. Open questions include whether the approach can be accelerated (e.g., via computational scanning), extended to non-metallic objects, or made robust to the wide range of coherence lengths encountered in real indoor environments.
