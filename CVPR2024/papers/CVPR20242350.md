## Problem

Structured light 3D scanning faces a longstanding tradeoff: achieving sub-millimeter accuracy traditionally requires projecting many patterns, making acquisition slow and restrictive, while reducing to the theoretical minimum of three patterns causes existing pixelwise decoders to produce dense noise and outliers. The paper asks whether a global, neural inverse-rendering formulation can recover dense, sub-pixel-accurate geometry from just three or four grayscale SL frames captured from a single viewpoint.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20242350/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 3–4 synchronized grayscale images from a single camera (up to 60 fps) and the corresponding known projection patterns, all from a rectified stereo camera-projector pair. It outputs a megapixel-resolution sub-pixel disparity map, per-pixel surface normals and reflectance, a 3D mesh extracted from the SDF zero level set, a residual map capturing ambient and indirect illumination, and a no-reference disparity error estimate obtained by subtracting front-to-back and back-to-front reconstructions. Training takes approximately 12 minutes on an RTX 6000 GPU with 10 GB VRAM per scene.

## Method

TurboSL formulates few-pattern SL decoding as a neural inverse rendering problem solved over the entire image rather than independently per pixel. Geometry is represented by a signed distance field (SDF) parameterized by a feature-based hash-encoding network, converted to an opaque density field for volume rendering; a 2D MLP encodes reflectance and a residual term for ambient/indirect light. The key architectural choice is bidirectional rendering: forward rendering marches camera rays to synthesize images (Eq. T4), while reverse rendering marches projector rays to synthesize the projected patterns (Eq. T5), jointly enforcing consistency of the scene and a learnable projector blur kernel. The SDF's inductive bias acts as an effective outlier-rejection mechanism that exploits the high inlier accuracy of a la carte patterns, which are otherwise unusable with pixelwise decoders. A no-reference uncertainty proxy is obtained by training a second model with back-to-front volume scanning and subtracting the two disparity maps, yielding a conservative error estimate correlated with ground-truth disparity error.

## Results

TurboSL achieves sub-pixel-accurate disparity estimation at megapixel resolution from as few as 3 grayscale structured-light patterns, with acquisition at up to 60 fps using a standard off-the-shelf projector (LG PH550) and grayscale camera. Training takes 12 minutes and 10 GB of VRAM on an RTX 6000 GPU. The method also recovers per-pixel surface normals, reflectances, ambient/indirect light residuals, and a no-reference disparity uncertainty proxy. The paper does not report a single numerical error metric (e.g., RMSE in mm) in a tabular form; instead, accuracy is demonstrated qualitatively against ground-truth geometry and via the bidirectional-scan uncertainty proxy (Figure 6), which shows strong correlation with true disparity error to within a fraction of a pixel.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the ZNCC pixelwise decoder [31], described as the state-of-the-art for pattern-agnostic SL decoding. In the direct visual comparison (Figure 3), ZNCC reconstructions are described as 'poor' across all three tested pattern families (a la carte, MPS, and Hamiltonian) for n=3 patterns, producing noisy and outlier-ridden point clouds. TurboSL, by contrast, yields clean, detailed surfaces from the same 3-pattern inputs, with the a la carte patterns showing 'far better accuracy' than the other families. The paper notes that the gap between n=3 and n=4 patterns is relatively small for TurboSL with a la carte patterns, whereas ZNCC degrades significantly at n=3. The comparison is qualitative (visual mesh quality) rather than a single scalar metric, but the visual evidence strongly favors TurboSL in the few-pattern regime where pixelwise decoding is most challenged.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper recasts the classical pixelwise structured-light decoding problem as a global neural inverse rendering problem, drawing on the SDF-based surface representation lineage (NeuS, Neuralangelo, Instant-NSR) and the NerfAcc training framework. It departs from prior SL work in two key ways: (1) it treats the entire image as a joint inference problem rather than decoding each pixel independently, and (2) it introduces bidirectional rendering (forward camera-to-projector and reverse projector-to-camera) as a mechanism for both improving fidelity and extracting a no-reference uncertainty proxy. The paper also repurposes a-la-carte SL patterns—previously problematic due to their outlier-prone decoding—by leveraging the SDF's inductive bias as an implicit outlier rejection mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TurboSL breaks the longstanding structured-light tradeoff between acquisition speed, spatial density, and sub-pixel accuracy, demonstrating megapixel-resolution 3D capture from as few as three patterns at 60 fps with commodity hardware. The bidirectional scanning uncertainty proxy is a novel no-reference metric that could inform downstream geometry processing, though the authors acknowledge it needs further exploration. Open questions include whether the approach extends to dynamic scenes beyond the brief 3-frame window, how it handles more complex reflectance (specular, subsurface), and whether the SDF-over-density-field advantage generalizes to other data-starved inverse rendering settings beyond SL.
