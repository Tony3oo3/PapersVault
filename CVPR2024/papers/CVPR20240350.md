## Problem

Medical image segmentation suffers from domain shifts across imaging modalities, sequences, acquisition sites, and patient age, yet no prior framework systematically addresses all four shifts while remaining deployable in centralized, federated, and test-time UDA settings. Previous methods either require co-located source and target data, synchronous adaptation, or target labels for model selection, limiting their practical value in multi-institutional studies constrained by HIPAA/GDPR.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240350/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 3D volumetric scans at 96³ voxels in two forms: local patches randomly sampled from the volume and the entire scan downsampled to the same 96³ size. Source-domain scans carry per-voxel labels for 7 subcortical brain regions or 4 cardiac structures; target-domain scans are fully unlabeled. The output is a per-voxel segmentation mask at the local patch resolution, with the global scan used only as contextual input rather than a separate prediction target.

## Method

MAPSeg combines three components: (1) a 3D multi-scale masked autoencoder that pretrains a 3D-ResNet encoder by reconstructing 70% masked regions at both local (8³ patches) and global (4³ patches) scales; (2) a masked pseudo-labeling (MPL) stage where a teacher model (EMA of the student) generates pseudo-labels on complete target images while the student learns to segment masked target images against those labels; and (3) a global-local collaboration (GLC) module that concatenates local and global encoder features in latent space with a cosine-similarity regularization to exploit anatomical priors. The key insight is that random masking acts as an ideal strong perturbation for consistency regularization, and the MAE-pretrained encoder can infer semantics of missing regions, making MPL stable even under large domain shifts. The unified loss decomposes naturally into a supervised server loss and a self-supervised client loss, enabling the same framework to operate in centralized, federated (with federated averaging of EMA teacher parameters), and test-time (asynchronous, source-free) UDA without architectural changes.

## Results

On the private infant brain MRI dataset, MAPSeg achieves 77.7% average Dice for cross-sequence, 73.1% for cross-site, and 75.2% for cross-age UDA segmentation, outperforming the best baselines by 9.5, 10.4, and 10.5 Dice points respectively. On the public cardiac CT→MRI task, MAPSeg reaches 81.2% average Dice without using any target labels for validation, surpassing the previous SOTA (FSUDA-V2, 75.5%) by 5.7 Dice. In the federated setting, Fed-MAPSeg achieves 69.9/73.6/71.0 Dice across the three brain tasks, far exceeding FAT (27.6/63.8/69.0) and DualAdapt (28.4/66.1/54.8). Test-time UDA incurs less than 3% Dice drop on the target domain relative to centralized UDA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the brain MRI tasks, DAR-UNet [70] (image-translation-based) is the strongest baseline in cross-sequence (68.2%) but degrades substantially in cross-site (57.6%) and cross-age (58.8%), likely because translation alters brain size and introduces segmentation errors. Among pseudo-labeling methods, HRDA [30] leads in cross-site (62.7%) and MIC [31] leads in cross-age (64.7%), yet both fail entirely on small structures (pallidum, accumbens) in the cross-sequence task. AdvEnt [63] (adversarial entropy minimization) is consistently the weakest baseline, scoring only 21.0% in cross-site. On the cardiac CT→MRI benchmark, MAPSeg (81.2% without target labels) surpasses the previous best FSUDA-V2 [43] (75.5%) by 5.7 Dice, and notably outperforms all 11 prior methods that used target labels for model selection. In the federated setting, Fed-MAPSeg dominates both FAT [50] and DualAdapt [69] by large margins (e.g., 69.9 vs. 27.6/28.4 in cross-sequence), while maintaining performance within ~1–2 Dice of centralized UDA.

## Relation to Prior Work

MAPSeg synthesizes three established threads—3D masked autoencoding (extending He et al.'s 2D MAE to volumetric medical scans), EMA-based teacher-student pseudo-labeling (from Mean Teacher), and global-local feature collaboration (from Chen et al.'s ultra-high-resolution segmentation)—into a single pipeline where MAE pretraining provides the representational foundation that makes pseudo-labeling stable under random masking. It departs from prior UDA-for-medical-imaging work, which has typically addressed one domain-shift type (cross-sequence, cross-site, cross-age, or cross-modality) in a single deployment setting, by unifying all four shifts under one framework and extending it to federated and test-time scenarios where source and target data cannot be co-located. The use of random masking as the strong perturbation for consistency regularization, rather than geometric or photometric augmentations, is a deliberate design choice justified by the MAE pretraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is substantial: in multi-institutional medical imaging, HIPAA/GDPR constraints and annotation costs make centralized UDA infeasible, and MAPSeg's ability to operate in federated and test-time settings with <3% target-domain performance loss addresses a real deployment gap. The large margins over prior SOTA (10.5 Dice on infant brain MRI, 5.7 on cardiac CT→MRI) and the demonstration that target labels are unnecessary for model selection strengthen its applicability to real clinical pipelines. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to more complex multi-organ or whole-body segmentation, how it scales to larger federated networks with heterogeneous client data distributions, and whether the source-domain degradation observed in test-time adaptation can be mitigated without sacrificing target performance.
