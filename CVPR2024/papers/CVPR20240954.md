## Problem

Existing diffusion-based video prediction methods treat videos as collections of independent images and enforce temporal coherence through external constraints like temporal attention blocks, which are computationally expensive and overlook the inherent continuity in video data. Moreover, transitions between consecutive frames do not uniformly contain the same amount of motion, so modeling them with a single-step process leads to suboptimal sampling quality.

## Input / Output

The model takes a small set of context frames (e.g., 4–5 past frames at 64×64 or 128×128 resolution) as input and autoregressively generates future frames one at a time, producing 15–40 predicted frames depending on the dataset. Notably, the model uses only the last few context frames (e.g., 4 out of 10 in the KTH setup) and requires no auxiliary annotations such as pose or class labels, relying solely on pixel-level information. The U-Net backbone processes each intermediate state x_t along with the timestep t to predict the next frame y.

## Method

The core contribution is a Continuous Video Process (CVP) that models the transition between two consecutive frames x and y as a continuous multi-dimensional process with a novel noise schedule g(t) = −t·log(t) that vanishes at both endpoints, ensuring the data distribution exists at t=0 and t=1. The forward process interpolates between endpoints via x_t = (1−t)x + ty − (t·log(t)/√2)·z, and the reverse process is a Markov chain with learned Gaussian transitions parameterized by a U-Net that predicts y from (x_t, t). Training minimizes a simplified weighted MSE loss derived from a novel variational bound, requiring only one future frame per training step rather than a block of k frames. At inference, the model iteratively refines from x toward y in N steps, needing 75% fewer sampling steps than diffusion-based baselines while maintaining temporal coherence without any explicit temporal attention mechanism.

## Results

On KTH (10→30 frames), CVP achieves FVD 140.6, PSNR 29.8, and SSIM 0.872, outperforming the best baseline RIVER (FVD 180) by 22% on FVD while using only k=1 training frames versus k=10 for baselines. On BAIR (2→14 frames), CVP reaches FVD 65.1, beating MCVD (87.9) by 26%. On Human3.6M (5→30), CVP achieves FVD 144.5 versus Grid-keypoint's 166.1, and on UCF101 (5→16), CVP reaches FVD 245.2 versus RaMViD's 356. Notably, CVP requires 75% fewer sampling steps per frame compared to diffusion-based baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CVP achieves the best FVD across all four datasets, with the most dramatic gains on UCF101 (245.2 vs. 356 for RaMViD, a 31% improvement) and Human3.6M (144.5 vs. 166.1 for Grid-keypoint). On KTH 10→30, CVP beats RIVER by 22% on FVD (140.6 vs. 180) and SRVP by 37% (140.6 vs. 222), though RIVER retains a slight PSNR edge (30.4 vs. 29.8). On BAIR, CVP (k=1) outperforms MCVD (k=5) by 26% on FVD for 2→14 prediction (65.1 vs. 87.9) and by 28% for 2→28 (85.1 vs. 118.4), but VDM (66.9) narrowly edges out CVP (70.1) on the 1→15 setting. A critical distinction is that CVP trains on only k=1 future frame per block and generates all future frames autoregressively, whereas baselines like MCVD, RIVER, and SRVP train on k=5 or k=10 future frames simultaneously, making CVP's per-frame training cost substantially lower. The paper also reports that CVP requires 75% fewer sampling steps per frame than diffusion-based baselines, addressing a key efficiency concern.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the variational bound and Markov chain reverse process framework of denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPM, Ho et al. 2020), but fundamentally reinterprets the diffusion process for video: instead of treating a video as a sequence of independent frames connected by temporal attention, it models the transition between two consecutive frames as a continuous multi-dimensional interpolation with a novel noise schedule g(t) = −t·log(t) that vanishes at both endpoints. This departs from prior diffusion-based video predictors (MCVD, VDM, RaMViD) that rely on external temporal attention blocks for coherence, and from flow-matching approaches (RIVER) that also operate on frame pairs but without the continuous-process framing. The U-Net backbone and autoregressive single-frame training (k=1) further distinguish it from multi-frame diffusion baselines that train on blocks of 5–15 future frames simultaneously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical implication is that temporal coherence in video prediction can emerge from the continuous-process formulation itself, eliminating the need for temporal attention mechanisms and reducing training to a single future frame per step — a simplification that lowers both architectural complexity and data requirements. However, the evaluation is confined to relatively small-scale benchmarks (64×64 for KTH, BAIR, Human3.6M; 128×128 for UCF101) and modest hardware (2× A6000 GPUs), leaving open whether the approach scales to high-resolution, long-horizon, or highly dynamic video. The limited context window (4–5 frames) and the multi-step-per-frame sampling cost remain bottlenecks for real-time or long-sequence applications, and the paper itself flags these as directions for future work.
