## Problem

Existing multi-modal large language models for video understanding are limited to processing very few frames (typically under 100), making them infeasible for long videos exceeding one minute due to prohibitive computation complexity, GPU memory cost, and weak long-term temporal connectivity. Additionally, no standardized benchmark existed to evaluate long video understanding capabilities in a question-answering setting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240573/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system consumes a long video as a sequence of T RGB frames (each H×W, tested at 224×224) processed incrementally via a sliding window of C frames, along with a text question. It supports two inference modes: global mode (understanding the entire video) and breakpoint mode (understanding a specific timestamp, combining long-term memory, current short-term memory, and the frame at that moment). Output is a free-form text answer or generated description, with the system capable of handling over 10K frames on a single 24GB GPU.

## Method

MovieChat uses a frame-wise visual encoder (EVA-CLIP ViT-G/14 + BLIP-2 Q-former) to extract N tokens per frame, fed sequentially into a fixed-length short-term memory buffer via a sliding window. When the buffer reaches capacity, a parameter-free memory consolidation step iteratively merges the most similar adjacent frame tokens (following ToMe's weighted averaging) to produce sparse long-term memory tokens, reducing redundancy while preserving information. The key architectural insight, inspired by the Atkinson-Shiffrin memory model, is that short-term memory acts as a rapidly updated buffer that periodically consolidates into a compact long-term store, with merged tokens also reinitializing the short-term buffer to maintain cross-window information flow. A hierarchically decomposed positional encoding extends the absolute position range from n to n² to accommodate the growing token count. The final video representation passes through a Q-former and linear projection before being fed into an LLM for question answering.

## Results

On the proposed MovieChat-1K long video benchmark (>10K frames), MovieChat processes 2048 frames and achieves 62.3% accuracy (score 3.23/5) in global mode and 48.3% accuracy (score 2.57/5) in breakpoint mode, outperforming all baselines that can only handle 32–100 frames. On short video QA, MovieChat reaches 75.2% accuracy on MSVD-QA, 52.7% on MSRVTT-QA, and 45.7% on ActivityNet-QA, surpassing the strongest baseline (Video-ChatGPT) by 10.3, 3.4, and 10.5 points respectively. In long video generation quality (Table 4), MovieChat leads on all five GPT-assisted metrics (CI 3.11, DO 2.93, CU 3.24, TU 3.17, CO 3.25) versus the best baseline VideoChat (CI 3.04, DO 2.75, CU 3.09, TU 3.00, CO 3.21).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MovieChat outperforms all five baselines on every reported metric. Against the closest competitor Video-ChatGPT [40] (100 frames), MovieChat gains +4.5 points in global accuracy (62.3 vs 47.6) and +0.3 in breakpoint accuracy (48.3 vs 48.0), while also improving scores by 0.68 and 0.12 respectively. VideoChat [34] (32 frames) is beaten by +4.5 global accuracy and +2.2 breakpoint accuracy, with a notable 0.94-point score gap in breakpoint mode. Video LLaMA [78] (32 frames) trails by the largest margin: +10.6 global accuracy and +9.2 breakpoint accuracy. LLaMA Adapter [79] and FrozenBiLM [72] appear only in short-video tables, where MovieChat leads by 10.3 and 43.0 points on MSVD-QA accuracy respectively. A key practical advantage is that MovieChat ingests 20–64× more frames than baselines, yet still fits within 24 GB VRAM, whereas prior methods saturate at ~100 frames.

## Relation to Prior Work

MovieChat extends the MLLM paradigm established by Flamingo, BLIP-2, VideoChat, and Video-LLaMA, which pair a visual encoder with a frozen LLM via a Q-former or projection layer. Its key architectural departure is the Atkinson-Shiffrin-inspired memory mechanism: a sliding-window FIFO short-term buffer whose overflow is consolidated into a compact long-term store via ToMe-style token merging, repurposing an image-efficiency technique for temporal compression. Unlike prior video MLLMs that process all frames simultaneously (limiting them to ~100 frames), MovieChat processes frames sequentially and discards redundancy, enabling >10K-frame understanding on a single 24GB GPU. The paper also introduces MovieChat-1K, the first benchmark specifically targeting long-video QA with manual annotations, filling a gap the authors identify in prior datasets like MovieQA and MovieNet.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is substantial: MovieChat demonstrates that long-video understanding (10K+ frames) is feasible on consumer hardware, opening the door to interactive dialogue over full-length films and TV episodes. The memory mechanism (sliding window + FIFO + ToMe consolidation) is a simple, parameter-free recipe that could be adopted by other video MLLMs. However, the authors acknowledge limitations—perception is bottlenecked by the underlying short-video pretrained encoder, and temporal reasoning remains coarse. Open questions include whether the simple concatenation in breakpoint mode is optimal, whether the memory mechanism scales to even longer videos without degradation, and whether the approach generalizes beyond the MovieChat-1K distribution.
