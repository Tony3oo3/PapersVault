## Problem

Camera exposure control under dynamic lighting changes requires rapid convergence to avoid over/under-exposed frames that degrade downstream vision tasks. Existing methods—built-in AE controllers and optimization-based approaches—need 10–30+ frames to converge, produce non-optimal parameter pairs (e.g., long exposure with high gain), or incur heavy computation, making them unsuitable for real-time, on-board deployment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/CVPR20240301/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent consumes a grayscale RoI patch (full image or a specific region) that is resized to a fixed resolution, vectorized along the x-axis into a 128-dim intensity vector, and stacked over 3 consecutive frames to form a 384-dim state. It outputs two continuous scalar values representing relative adjustments to exposure time and gain, which are sent directly to the camera hardware. Because the input is always resized before vectorization, inference takes a constant 1 ms on CPU regardless of the original image resolution.

## Method

DRL-AE trains a Soft Actor-Critic agent with a lightweight MLP (2 hidden layers, 256 units) to jointly control exposure time and gain via a continuous relative action space. The state is a vectorized intensity history rather than CNN features, avoiding heavy convolution computation and the intensity-agnostic generalization issues of learned features. The reward combines three terms: a midtone-brightness objective (target 0.5), a flickering penalty on frame-to-frame intensity change, and a Sobel-based noise penalty to regularize gain. Training uses a static-to-dynamic lighting curriculum (fixed moderate → fixed extreme → dynamically changing LED brightness) in a light-controlled darkroom, combined with spatial domain randomization (random flip, crop, rotate, resize per episode) to enable zero-shot generalization to real-world scenes without fine-tuning.

## Results

DRL-AE converges to a well-exposed image within 3–5 frames under sudden lighting changes, compared to ~30 frames for the Nelder-Mead-based method of Shin et al. [21] and 10–30 frames for the camera's built-in AE. Inference takes only 1 ms on a CPU (Ryzen 5950x) regardless of input resolution, versus 108.7 ms for Shin et al. at 1600×1200. On a real-time driving benchmark (5,355 image pairs), DRL-AE yields 38% more SIFT features on average (1,306 vs. 946) and detects 5% more objects with YOLOv5 compared to the built-in AE controller.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Shin et al. [21] (Nelder-Mead optimization with noise-aware image quality assessment), DRL-AE wins decisively on both convergence speed and latency: it reaches a well-exposed image in 3 frames versus ~30 frames, and runs in 1 ms versus 108.7 ms (1600×1200) or 18.2 ms (800×600) — a 109× speedup at the larger resolution. Against the camera's built-in AE controller (Muramatsu [14], Sampat [18]), DRL-AE converges in 3–5 frames after a sudden lighting change, while the built-in AE still requires 10 frames (outdoor) or 30 frames (indoor). On downstream vision tasks over 5,355 real driving images, DRL-AE extracts 38% more SIFT features on average (1,306 vs. 946; median 1,157 vs. 711, a 62% gap) and detects 5% more cars and pedestrians with YOLOv5, with objects appearing earlier in the sequence — a critical advantage for high-speed autonomous driving. Notably, DRL-AE achieves all of this as a zero-shot transfer from a light-controlled darkroom, without any fine-tuning on the real-world scenes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is the first to apply deep reinforcement learning to the camera exposure control task, departing from both the optimization-based line (built-in AE, Bayesian optimization, Nelder-Mead search) and the data-driven line (supervised exposure prediction with ground-truth labels). Conceptually, it repurposes the SAC actor-critic framework for a traditionally heuristic or black-box-search problem, replacing iterative parameter search with a learned policy that outputs continuous relative adjustments to exposure time and gain. A notable methodological departure is the use of a trivially simple state representation—vectorized intensity history from a small RoI patch—instead of the CNN features adopted in related visual control work, motivated by the need for sub-millisecond CPU inference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in achieving 1 ms CPU inference and 5-frame convergence, making learned exposure control viable for embedded robotics and autonomous driving where built-in AE is too slow under sudden lighting changes. The zero-shot transfer from a controlled darkroom to real-world driving scenarios is encouraging but rests on a single training environment and a relatively weak built-in AE baseline. Open questions include extending to aperture control, motion-aware exposure (to avoid blur), hardware generalization across camera models, and whether the simple intensity-history state scales to more complex multi-sensor or HDR settings.
