## Problem

In ill-posed inverse problems, the full posterior distribution encodes all plausible solutions and their likelihoods, but high-dimensional posteriors are practically impossible to visualize. Existing approaches either generate many posterior samples (computationally expensive with diffusion-based samplers), produce per-pixel heatmaps that ignore inter-pixel correlations, or traverse along posterior principal components without modeling the actual probability density within the subspace. This leaves users without a tool to inspect the effective support and likelihood structure of the projected posterior.

## Input / Output

The method receives a degraded measurement y (e.g., a noisy, inpainted, or colorized image) together with an input-adaptive affine subspace defined by an origin point x₀(y) (from an MMSE predictor) and K orthonormal directions W(y) (from NPPC, typically K=2 for 2D visualization). It outputs the projected posterior density p(v|y) as a continuous function over the K-dimensional coordinate space, which can be evaluated at any queried point v to produce contour plots or heatmaps. The subspace is measurement-specific, meaning the origin and directions change per input, and the density is a marginal (integrated over all orthogonal dimensions) rather than a slice of the full posterior.

## Method

PPDE learns the conditional projected posterior using an energy-based model trained with a multi-level contrastive divergence variant. The architecture is split into a heavy CNN feature extractor that processes the measurement y and the subspace definition {x₀(y), W(y)} to produce a compact feature vector h(y), and a lightweight MLP that, conditioned on h(y) via adaptive shift-and-scale (AdaIN-style), outputs the unnormalized log-density E(v, h(y); θ) = −log p̃(v|y). This separation allows the expensive feature extraction to run once per input while the cheap MLP is queried many times for grid evaluation or MCMC contrastive sampling. The key conceptual distinction is that the method learns the true projected posterior (marginalizing over orthogonal coordinates) rather than a sliced posterior (evaluating the high-dimensional density at u=0), which would miss high-probability regions since natural images concentrate near a low-dimensional manifold. Training uses Langevin dynamics to generate contrastive samples, with the CD objective pushing up likelihood on data projections and down on MCMC-generated negatives.

## Results

The method (PPDE) is evaluated via negative log-likelihood (NLL, lower is better) of the projected posterior distribution across six restoration tasks. PPDE achieves the best NLL in every task: 3.72 on MNIST inpainting, 3.04 on MNIST denoising, 5.37 on CelebA eye inpainting, 7.39 on CelebA colorization, 2.84 on CelebA 8× super-resolution, and 6.86 on ImageNet colorization. The largest gains are on ImageNet colorization (6.86 vs. 10.15 for NPPC) and CelebA super-resolution (2.84 vs. 5.12 for NPPC), where the Gaussian approximation is particularly poor. PPDE is also orders of magnitude faster than the KDE baseline, which requires 100 posterior samples per image from a diffusion-based sampler.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PPDE is compared against two baselines: (1) NPPC [41], which approximates the projected posterior as a 2D Gaussian using the posterior PCs and variances, and (2) a KDE baseline that projects 100 posterior samples (generated by DDNM [64] or DPS [10]) onto the top-2 PC subspace and estimates the density via kernel density estimation. PPDE outperforms NPPC on all six tasks, with the most dramatic improvement on ImageNet colorization (6.86 vs. 10.15 NLL, a 32% reduction) and CelebA super-resolution (2.84 vs. 5.12, a 45% reduction), indicating that the Gaussian assumption is severely inadequate for these tasks. Against the KDE baseline, PPDE also wins on all four tasks where both are reported (MNIST inpainting 3.72 vs. 3.9, MNIST denoising 3.04 vs. 3.26, CelebA colorization 7.39 vs. 7.7), while being orders of magnitude faster since it avoids iterative posterior sampling. Notably, on CelebA eye inpainting the KDE baseline (8.28) is actually worse than the simple Gaussian NPPC (6.38), likely due to insufficient sample size, whereas PPDE achieves 5.37, beating both by a wide margin.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on NPPC [41], which provides the input-adaptive affine subspace (origin and posterior principal components) that PPDE uses as its projection target, and on the multi-level contrastive divergence training scheme from Yair & Michaeli [65] for stable EBM training. Conceptually, it departs from two dominant paradigms in posterior uncertainty: (i) per-pixel heatmaps that ignore inter-pixel correlations, and (ii) posterior sampling (e.g., DDNM, DPS) that is too slow for practical visualization. The key departure is that PPDE learns the *projected* posterior density (marginalizing over orthogonal dimensions) rather than *slicing* the high-dimensional posterior, a distinction the paper argues is critical for meaningful low-dimensional visualization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PPDE offers a practical, orders-of-magnitude faster alternative to diffusion-based posterior sampling for visualizing uncertainty in 2D, with consistent NLL improvements over both Gaussian (NPPC) and KDE-from-sampling baselines across diverse tasks. The main limitation is that meaningful visualization is restricted to 2–3 dimensional subspaces, which may be insufficient for severely ill-posed problems requiring many principal components to capture posterior structure. An open question is how to extend this approach to higher-dimensional or adaptive subspaces, or to learn a condensed representation of the full posterior that remains navigable by a user.
