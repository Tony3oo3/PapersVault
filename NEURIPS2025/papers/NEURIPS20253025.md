## Problem

There is no objective, automatic, and scalable method to evaluate the memory capability of LLM-based personal assistants. Existing approaches either rely on costly manual annotation or generate QAs directly with LLMs, whose hallucinations cause ground-truth correctness to drop below 90% in most scenarios and below 40% in complex ones. Additionally, LLM-generated user profiles tend to lack diversity, producing overly plausible and homogeneous personas.

## Input / Output

The simulator takes a predefined BRNet structure (11 entities, 73 attributes, causal edges, and conditional probability distributions for the daily-life scenario) and uses LLMs purely for surface-level rewriting. It outputs structured evaluation trajectories ξ = (M, q, a, a′, h), where M is a list of ~15 user messages, q is a question, a is a textual answer, a′ is a single-choice answer with LLM-generated distractors, and h is the set of target messages needed for retrieval. The MemDaily dataset comprises 2,954 trajectories across six QA sub-types, and the benchmark scales difficulty by injecting question-irrelevant social-media posts at controlled ratios (MemDaily-vanilla through MemDaily-200).

## Method

MemSim combines two mechanisms. First, the Bayesian Relation Network (BRNet) is a two-level DAG over entities and their attributes; it encodes scenario-specific prior knowledge as causal edges and conditional distributions, and generates diverse hierarchical user profiles via ancestral sampling (topological ordering + conditional draws), avoiding the intractable joint distribution. Second, the causal generation mechanism extracts structured 'informative hints' (entity, attribute, value) from the sampled profile and uses them as the shared causal source for both user messages and QAs: LLMs only rewrite hints into natural language without performing any reasoning, so factual content in messages and answers is guaranteed consistent and hallucination-free. Five QA types (single-hop, multi-hop, comparative, aggregative, post-processing) are constructed by selecting different combinations of hints, and two noise types (entity-side and attribute-side) are concatenated into messages to simulate real-world distractors. This design creates an asymmetric difficulty: construction is deterministic from the profile, while solving requires the agent to retrieve and reason over noisy message sequences.

## Results

On MemDaily-vanilla, FullMem achieves the highest accuracy across most question types (0.976 on Simple, 0.982 on Conditional, 0.848 on Post-processing), while Aggregative questions remain the hardest for all methods (0.25–0.38 accuracy). OracleMem, which receives only the ground-truth target messages, reaches 0.966–0.988 on most types but still only 0.376 on Aggregative, indicating a fundamental bottleneck in textual memory for aggregation tasks. Under the noisier MemDaily-100 setting (100× irrelevant messages), FullMem drops to 0.586 on Comparative and ReceMem collapses to 0.104, while RetrMem maintains relatively stable performance (0.706 on Comparative). For retrieval, LLM-based retrieval achieves the best Recall@5 in short contexts (0.888 on Simple), while embedding-based retrieval (FAISS + Llama-160m) outperforms it in longer contexts (0.698 vs. 0.612 on Simple in MemDaily-100).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark paper, so the 'baselines' are the memory mechanisms being evaluated rather than competing dataset-construction methods. FullMem (concatenating all messages) consistently outperforms RetrMem (FAISS-based retrieval) by 5–10 points on Simple and Conditional questions in MemDaily-vanilla, but RetrMem becomes more competitive under noise (MemDaily-100), where FullMem's Comparative accuracy drops to 0.586 while RetrMem holds at 0.706. ReceMem (sliding window of k recent messages) degrades catastrophically with noise, falling from 0.832 to 0.500 on Simple and from 0.631 to 0.104 on Comparative. NonMem and NoisyMem serve as lower bounds, with NoisyMem paradoxically exceeding NonMem on vanilla (0.512 vs. 0.508 on Simple) but falling below it on MemDaily-100 (0.458 vs. 0.508), suggesting LLMs benefit from medium-length context. OracleMem (perfect retrieval) establishes the ceiling, revealing that even with ideal retrieval, Aggregative questions cap at ~0.37–0.38 accuracy, pointing to a reasoning bottleneck beyond retrieval.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of Bayesian network sampling and LLM-based dataset construction for agent evaluation. Prior work on evaluating LLM agent memory relies on subjective human scoring or semi-automatic QA construction that suffers from hallucination-induced unreliability (correctness below 90%, sometimes below 40% in complex scenarios). The key methodological departure is the causal generation mechanism, where LLMs are restricted to rewriting pre-sampled structural hints rather than generating factual content, thereby decoupling diversity (handled by BRNet ancestral sampling) from reliability (guaranteed by the causal chain from profiles → hints → messages/QAs). This is distinct from knowledge-graph-based QA datasets for RAG evaluation, which target common-sense questions and lack the textual user-message context needed for personal assistant memory assessment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MemDaily fills a clear gap as the first fully objective and automatic benchmark for evaluating factual memory in LLM-based personal assistants, providing the community with a scalable, reproducible evaluation protocol. A notable open question surfaced by the experiments is that even with perfect retrieval (OracleMem), aggregative questions plateau around 37% accuracy, pointing to a fundamental bottleneck in how LLMs process and combine multiple retrieved facts in textual form. The paper's scope is limited to factual information in a single daily-life scenario and excludes dialogue-based interactions and higher-level preferences, leaving substantial room for extension. Preventing misuse of the generated synthetic user data is also flagged as an unresolved concern.
