## Problem

Shapley values provide a principled framework for explaining reinforcement learning agents, but exact computation scales as O(2^|F| · |S|), making it infeasible for real-world problems. Existing scalable approximations from supervised learning (Monte Carlo sampling, parametric amortization) do not address RL-specific challenges: temporal dependencies across multi-step trajectories, the need to explain from off-policy data, and the requirement that explanations track an evolving policy in real time.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a trained policy π, a state s decomposed into n features, an action a, and a feature subset C (with features outside C replaced by a masking value outside the state-space support). It produces a vector of n Shapley values per state-action pair attributing each feature's contribution to behaviour, outcome, or prediction, plus the underlying characteristic function values. The outcome characteristic additionally requires a modified policy μ that selects actions via the behaviour characteristic at the explained state s_e and the original policy elsewhere, making it a sequential quantity estimated through bootstrapped value functions rather than a single-step regression target.

## Method

FastSVERL amortizes Shapley estimation across states using two coupled parametric models: (1) a characteristic model π̂(s,a|C;β) trained by masked-input regression to predict the conditional expectation E[π(S,a)|S^C=s^C], and (2) a Shapley model φ̂(s,a;θ) trained via a weighted least-squares loss (following FastSHAP) over subsets C drawn from the combinatorial Shapley weighting, with a post-hoc additive correction enforcing the efficiency constraint. For outcome explanations, a single conditioned policy π̂(a|s; s_e,C) that switches between the behaviour characteristic and the original policy at the explained state is paired with a parametric value function V(s|s_e,C;β) trained via TD updates, amortizing the 2^|F|×|S| separate return estimations into one model. The framework further supports off-policy explanation via importance-sampling reweighting of replay-buffer transitions, continual explanation by jointly updating explanation models alongside the agent's policy with configurable update ratios, and a single-sample variant that replaces the characteristic model with one Monte Carlo draw per loss evaluation, halving total training time while eliminating error propagation from upstream approximations.

## Results

In Mastermind-222 (8 features, 53 states), all three FastSVERL components—behaviour characteristic, outcome characteristic, and outcome Shapley values—converge to MSE below 0.01. In large-scale Mastermind domains (24–36 features, up to 2.8×10¹¹ states), both characteristic and Shapley models converge reliably with ~1.1×10⁶ and ~7.3×10⁵ training updates respectively, achieving final losses of ~3.7×10⁻³ and ~1.3×10⁻³. In the Hypercube domain, training cost scales approximately polynomially with the number of states while remaining largely insensitive to the number of features. The single-sample characteristic approximation (Section 5) halves total training time versus the model-based approach while matching the accuracy of using exact characteristic values.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external Shapley approximation methods; instead it compares internal design choices. For the outcome characteristic, off-policy training converges faster than on-policy training because it reuses the original agent's experience buffer rather than collecting new transitions from the conditioned policy. For off-policy explanation learning, importance sampling (Eq. 17) reduces approximation error compared to using the training buffer without reweighting, though it does not match the accuracy of a dedicated on-policy baseline. The single-sample characteristic approximation (Section 5) outperforms the model-based characteristic in both speed and accuracy: it converges before the model-based setup even begins its Shapley updates, matches the performance of using exact characteristic values, and halves total training time. For continual learning, higher explanation-to-policy update ratios (10:1, 50:1) maintain low Shapley approximation error throughout training, whereas low ratios (1:1, 2:1) exhibit error spikes coinciding with large policy updates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalises the SVERL framework (Beechey et al., 2023/2025), which defined rigorous Shapley-based characteristic functions for explaining RL behaviour, outcomes, and predictions but was computationally intractable due to exponential scaling. It adapts the parametric amortisation strategy from FastSHAP (Jethani et al., 2021) to RL's unique challenges—temporal dependencies, off-policy data, and evolving policies—by training Shapley models and characteristic models with RL-specific loss functions. A key departure from both SVERL and FastSHAP is the single-sample characteristic approximation (Section 5), which eliminates the need for a separate characteristic model entirely by injecting one Monte Carlo sample directly into the Shapley loss, a technique not present in either prior line of work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FastSVERL is the first scalable method that makes principled Shapley-based RL explanations feasible, addressing the practical gap between SVERL's theoretical guarantees and real-world deployment. The single-sample extension is a promising direction that halves training cost and prevents error propagation, and it extends naturally to supervised learning. However, the framework is validated only on small discrete-action domains (Mastermind, Gridworld, Hypercube), the outcome characteristic lacks a formal convergence guarantee under function approximation, and no user studies or real-world deployment are presented—leaving open whether the approximations remain reliable and interpretable at the scale and complexity of practical RL systems.
