## Problem

Rule-based reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) for multimodal LLMs has widely adopted explicit chain-of-thought reasoning before verifiable answers, following the DeepSeek-R1 paradigm, yet no prior work has systematically examined whether this thinking process is actually necessary or beneficial during the fine-tuning phase itself. The paper challenges the assumption that explicit thinking is a prerequisite for RFT success, asking whether it can hinder reward convergence, produce inconsistent responses, and waste compute—particularly for smaller models and perception-oriented tasks.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image (resized to 328×328 for classification; up to 4096-token prompts for math) paired with a text question and, for classification, a sampled subset of class labels (40% for few-shot, 80% for base-to-new). Outputs are structured as either a thinking block in <thinking>...</thinking> followed by an answer in <answer>...</answer> (Thinking-RFT), or a bare answer string (No-Thinking-RFT). The reward is strictly binary: 1 if the extracted answer exactly matches the ground-truth label or numeric result, 0 otherwise, with no partial credit.

## Method

The paper introduces No-Thinking-RFT, which removes the format reward and the thinking prompt, replacing them with a direct-answer instruction and a strict equality accuracy reward that forces the model to output only the final answer, thereby eliminating the thinking phase entirely during GRPO training. To diagnose why thinking can hurt, the authors propose Think-After-Answer, which reorders the output so the verifiable answer precedes the reasoning, testing the hypothesis that thinking before the answer slows reward convergence. They further introduce Adaptive-Thinking, where the model is prompted to self-select between a thinking and a no-thinking response format, and both formats receive full format reward. All variants are trained with GRPO in an R1-zero setting (no SFT warmup) on Qwen2-VL 2B/7B and InternVL2.5 models across six task families (classification, spatial reasoning, math, puzzles, referring grounding, detection), revealing that No-Thinking-RFT matches or beats Thinking-RFT on perception and puzzle tasks with 4–12× faster training, while Thinking-RFT retains an edge only on computation-heavy math problems for 7B models.

## Results

On CVBench spatial reasoning (2B), No-Thinking-RFT achieves 76.76% overall accuracy vs. 70.36% for Thinking-RFT (+6.4%), while on 7B the gap narrows to 80.67% vs. 80.36%. On PuzzleVQA, No-Thinking-RFT outperforms Thinking-RFT by 18.35% (2B: 70.85% vs. 52.50%) and 14.05% (7B: 80.65% vs. 66.60%). On MathVista (7B), Thinking-RFT reverses the trend at 64.60% vs. 59.10% for No-Thinking-RFT. In few-shot classification across 11 datasets (2B), No-Thinking-RFT achieves 84.39% average accuracy vs. 81.25% for Thinking-RFT, while training time drops from 599 min to 139 min on CVBench.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between the paper's four RFT variants (Thinking-RFT, Think-After-Answer, No-Thinking-RFT, Adaptive-Thinking). No-Thinking-RFT decisively outperforms Thinking-RFT on visual perception (CVBench 2B: +6.4%) and puzzle tasks (PuzzleVQA 2B: +18.4%, 7B: +14.1%), but loses on 7B math reasoning (MathVista: 59.10% vs. 64.60%). Against SFT on few-shot classification (2B, 11 datasets), No-Thinking-RFT achieves 84.39% average vs. SFT's 70.65%, a +13.74% gain. Against VLM-R1 [60] on referring grounding (RefCOCO val, 600 steps), No-Thinking-RFT scores 90.83% vs. VLM-R1's 90.55% in-domain, but trails on LISA-Grounding OOD (61.76% vs. 63.14%). In the DPO comparison (Appendix H.2), both DPO variants (with model-generated and random negatives) perform substantially worse than both Thinking-RFT and No-Thinking-RFT on FGVCAircraft and MathVQA, confirming that online GRPO sampling is critical for the no-thinking paradigm to work.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DeepSeek-R1 rule-based RFT paradigm (GRPO with verifiable rewards and explicit thinking tags) to multi-modal LLMs, building directly on prior MLLM RFT efforts like Vision-R1 and VLM-R1 that aimed to replicate the 'aha moment' and length-increasing effects. However, it departs from the field's prevailing assumption that explicit chain-of-thought is a necessary component of RFT success, drawing instead on the overthinking literature (Sprague et al., Jiang et al.) to question whether thinking during the *training* phase (not just inference) is beneficial. The paper introduces No-Thinking-RFT (strict equality reward, no format reward), Think-After-Answer, and Adaptive-Thinking as controlled ablations of the thinking process, positioning itself as a mechanistic study of what RFT actually learns rather than a new SOTA pursuit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that removing explicit thinking from RFT matches or beats Thinking-RFT on most visual perception and puzzle tasks while reducing training time by 4–10× provides practitioners with a concrete, efficient alternative for fine-tuning small MLLMs. The free-lunch cross-dataset generalization phenomenon suggests rule-based RFT teaches transferable classification knowledge rather than memorization, which could reduce the need for large-scale labeled data. Key open questions include whether these findings hold for models larger than 7B (where thinking may become genuinely useful), whether Adaptive-Thinking can be refined to problem-level granularity, and whether the inconsistency between thinking-tag and answer-tag content can be explicitly penalized to improve reasoning-task performance.
