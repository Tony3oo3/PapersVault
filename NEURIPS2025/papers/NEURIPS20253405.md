## Problem

Multi-Agent Debate (MAD) is widely adopted to improve LLM reasoning, yet the relative contributions of its two core components—multi-agent ensembling and iterative inter-agent communication—have remained unexamined. This paper asks whether the performance gains typically attributed to debate are actually driven by the simpler act of aggregating multiple independent outputs via majority voting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253405/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a natural language question (multiple-choice or open-ended) and N=5 LLM agents (e.g., Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Llama3.1-8B-Instruct) that each generate stochastic responses using temperature 1.0, nucleus sampling 0.9, and a 512-token cap. The output is a final answer selected by majority voting over initial responses (T=0) or after T∈{2,3,5} rounds of simultaneous-talk debate where each agent observes its neighbors' prior-round responses and revises its answer. For open-ended summarization tasks, the best-performing agent's output per round is reported in lieu of a discrete vote.

## Method

The paper empirically disentangles MAD into Majority Voting (ensembling, T=0) and inter-agent Debate, showing across seven benchmarks that majority voting alone matches or exceeds all MAD variants (Decentralized, Sparse, Centralized). Theoretically, each agent is modeled as a Dirichlet-Compound-Multinomial (DCM) distribution where the Dirichlet prior encodes internal belief over answer options and the Multinomial captures stochastic generation; each debate round then corresponds to a Bayesian posterior update under Dirichlet-Multinomial conjugacy. The authors prove that this update induces a martingale over agents' belief in the correct answer (Theorem 2), formally establishing that debate alone does not improve expected correctness, and derive a concentration bound on majority voting's success probability (Theorem 1). Guided by the martingale insight, they propose targeted interventions—MAD-oracle (locking correct answers), MAD-Conformist (retaining responses matching the majority), and MAD-Follower (stochastically adopting the majority)—that bias belief updates toward correct signals and break the neutral martingale, yielding consistent improvements over vanilla MAD.

## Results

Across seven NLP benchmarks, Majority Voting achieves an average accuracy of 0.7691 (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) and 0.7242 (Llama3.1-8B-Instruct), matching or exceeding all Multi-Agent Debate variants. The best MAD configuration (Decentralized, T=2) reaches only 0.7377 and 0.6929 respectively, while Centralized MAD degrades to 0.6551 and 0.6094. On individual benchmarks, Majority Voting leads on Arithmetics (0.9900 vs. 0.7600 for Decentralized MAD) and GSM8K (0.9400 vs. 0.8867), while MAD variants occasionally match it on MMLU and HellaSwag. The paper further shows that theory-informed interventions (MAD-oracle) can push average accuracy to 0.8236–0.8364, confirming that biasing belief updates toward correct signals is the key to improving debate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Majority Voting outperforms Decentralized MAD [2] (Du et al., ICML 2024) by 3.1 points in average accuracy on Qwen2.5-7B (0.7691 vs. 0.7377 at T=2) and by 3.1 points on Llama3.1-8B (0.7242 vs. 0.6929), with the gap widening as debate rounds increase (Decentralized MAD drops to 0.7050 at T=5). Against Sparse MAD [10] (Li et al., EMNLP 2024), Majority Voting leads by 3.6 points on Qwen (0.7691 vs. 0.7330) and 2.5 points on Llama (0.7242 vs. 0.6990). The gap is most pronounced against Centralized MAD [14] (Guo et al., IJCAI 2024), where Majority Voting exceeds it by 11.4 points on Qwen (0.7691 vs. 0.6551) and 11.5 points on Llama (0.7242 vs. 0.6094), indicating that a central aggregator actively degrades performance. The paper's theoretical analysis (martingale property) explains why: debate alone does not shift expected correctness, so the ensembling effect of multiple independent agents—captured entirely by Majority Voting—accounts for essentially all gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new analytical ground within the rapidly growing Multi-Agent Debate (MAD) literature rather than building on a specific prior method. While prior work has focused on designing better communication topologies (sparse, centralized, decentralized), agent personas, and debate protocols, this paper steps back to ask a more fundamental question: how much of MAD's measured gains actually come from the debate component versus simple ensembling? The theoretical contribution draws on Bayesian conjugacy and the Pólya Urn scheme to formalize debate dynamics, providing a principled lens that prior empirical MAD studies lacked. The paper also connects to the broader ensemble learning literature by showing that the 'Multi-Agent' component is essentially a standard voting/ensembling mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work challenges a core working assumption in the MAD research community—that iterative inter-agent communication is the primary driver of performance gains—and provides both empirical and theoretical evidence that majority voting accounts for most of the benefit. The martingale proof offers a clean, general explanation for why debate alone is neutral in expectation, redirecting future research toward designing interventions that break the martingale (e.g., biasing belief updates toward correct signals). Open questions remain: whether the findings hold for truly heterogeneous model populations (different architectures, not just personas), whether the conformist/follower interventions scale to open-ended generation tasks, and whether more sophisticated non-Bayesian update rules (e.g., confidence-weighted or retrieval-augmented) can escape the martingale constraint. The practical implication is significant: practitioners may save substantial compute by replacing multi-round debate with simple majority voting in many settings.
