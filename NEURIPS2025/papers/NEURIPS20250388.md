## Problem

In high-stakes human-AI decision-making, existing approaches either always use a cheap model (risking errors) or always use an expensive one (inflating costs), and static deferral thresholds cannot adapt to shifting task difficulty. The paper addresses how to jointly balance prediction correctness, computational cost, and the decision to abstain to a human expert in a principled, adaptive manner across a cascade of LLMs of varying capacity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250388/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system consumes multiple-choice questions (grade-school science, 57-subject MMLU, US medical board exams, Indian medical entrance exams) and produces a final predicted answer, an abstention signal routing the query to a human expert, and per-tier calibrated confidence and uncertainty scores. Verification is performed by prompting the same or a larger model with the generated chain-of-thought response to output a YES/NO correctness token or a scalar confidence value, which is then passed through a Bayesian logistic regression calibrator fitted on 100 samples.

## Method

The framework is a two-tier cascade (e.g., Qwen-2.5-1.5B → Qwen-2.5-7B) governed by two policies: a deferral policy that regenerates the answer with the larger model when the base model's calibrated confidence (via surrogate token probability, self-verification, or Monte Carlo variants, all calibrated with Bayesian logistic regression and a non-linear log-odds transform) falls below a threshold φ_base, and an abstention policy that escalates to a human expert when the posterior predictive standard deviation exceeds ξ_base or ξ_large. To satisfy the adaptability desideratum, the three thresholds are parameterised as sigmoid-transformed free variables and optimised online via Adam on a differentiable system-risk objective (error + λ_c·cost + λ_a·abstention) using a replay buffer populated only by abstention feedback, with Boolean decision masks replaced by soft logistic steps for gradient flow. The cost model scales linearly with model size and input/output token counts (ρ=5 output-to-input ratio), and the online loop is evaluated over 1,000 streamed queries tracking cumulative regret against single-model baselines.

## Results

The cascaded LLM system (Qwen-2.5-1.5B → 7B) is evaluated using the Incremental Benefit per Cost (ΔIBC) metric, where higher values indicate better cost-efficiency over the single-model baseline. The best configuration—large-model verification with Surrogate Token Probability (STP, n=1)—achieves ΔIBC of 242.7±36.3 on ARC2-Easy and 89.3±26.2 on ARC2-Challenge, substantially outperforming base-model verification (which yields negative ΔIBC across all datasets). On MMLU, gains are marginal (ΔIBC = 2.5±5.2), and on the medical datasets (MedQA: 1.7±16.3; MedMCQA: −10.0±10.1), the cascade provides no consistent advantage. In the online learning setting, the cascaded system achieves lower cumulative regret than either single model on 4 of 5 datasets over 1,000 streamed queries.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the cascaded system against two single-model baselines: using only the base model (Qwen-2.5-1.5B) and only the large model (Qwen-2.5-7B), each with a single abstention threshold. On ARC2-Easy and ARC2-Challenge, the cascade with large-model verification (STP n=1) achieves ΔIBC of 242.7 and 89.3 respectively, far exceeding the base-model-only baseline (which yields negative ΔIBC, e.g., −16.9 on ARC2-Easy) and indicating the cascade captures accuracy gains at a fraction of the large model's cost. On MMLU, the cascade's advantage is marginal (ΔIBC = 2.5±5.2 for large-STP), and on both medical datasets (MedQA, MedMCQA) no verification method produces a statistically significant positive ΔIBC, suggesting the cascade offers no cost-benefit advantage in these harder domains. In the online learning experiment (Figure 4), the cascaded system achieves lower cumulative regret than both single-model baselines on ARC2-Easy, ARC2-Challenge, MMLU, and MedMCQA, but not on MedQA, where poor confidence estimation undermines the deferral policy. The paper also shows that a gradient-based threshold optimisation outperforms a grid-search approach in the online setting, confirming the benefit of the proposed differentiable risk formulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the cascaded LLM decision framework of Zellinger et al. (2025) and Zellinger & Thomson (2024) by adding an online learning mechanism that adapts deferral and abstention thresholds from human feedback, drawing on the online decision mediation paradigm of Jarrett et al. (2022). For confidence estimation, it adopts surrogate token probability from Kadavath et al. (2022) and wraps it in a Bayesian logistic regression calibration (a Bayesian analogue of Platt scaling). Unlike prior multi-LLM routing work (FrugalGPT, AutoMix, Hybrid LLM) that optimises purely among automated models, this framework introduces a human expert as a third escalation tier and explicitly models the cost–accuracy–abstention trade-off as a differentiable system risk optimised online.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a principled, deployable recipe for organisations that must balance LLM inference cost against error risk and the need for human oversight, with the online learning component addressing the practical problem of shifting task distributions. Key open questions include whether theoretical guarantees exist for the cascaded system's regret bounds, how the approach scales beyond two tiers, and whether the Bayesian calibration step (which the authors note is more complex than standard Platt scaling) is necessary in practice. The honest negative result on MedQA—where the cascade fails to beat a single large model—highlights that confidence estimation quality remains a bottleneck on high-difficulty domains, and the sensitivity to imperfect human feedback underscores a practical deployment risk.
