## Problem

The reflow procedure in k-rectified flow causes the learned velocity field to drift away from the target data distribution, producing a growing reconstruction error gap between real and fake samples. This drift is compounded by the need for prohibitively large numbers of generated pairs (600K–4M) and leads to degraded image quality in full-step generation. The paper identifies this distributional drift as an innate consequence of supervising the flow with samples from a shifted distribution rather than the true target.

## Input / Output

The model takes random Gaussian noise vectors as input and produces synthetic RGB images via ODE integration (Euler for 1-step, RK45 adaptive for full-step). Experiments span CIFAR-10 (32×32), ImageNet 64×64, and LSUN Bedroom (256×256). A distinguishing feature is that during training the model consumes both fake pairs (noise, generated image) and real pairs (numerically inverted noise of a real image, the real image itself), with the real pairs augmented by Slerp-perturbed conic neighbors to cover the local noise-space neighborhood.

## Method

Balanced conic reflow replaces the standard fake-pair-only reflow with an alternating scheme that interleaves conic reflow steps (using real pairs) and original reflow steps (using fake pairs). For each real image X₁, the method computes its inverse noise Z₀,ᵣ = v⁻¹(X₁) via the previously learned 1-rectified flow, then constructs conic neighbors by Slerp-interpolating Z₀,ᵣ with a random Gaussian ε at ratio ζ, forming trajectories Conic(X₁, ε, ζ, t) = tX₁ + (1−t)·Slerp(Z₀,ᵣ, ε, ζ). The Slerp noise magnitude ζ follows a scheduled decay (ζ_max·2t′²/(1+t′²)) and is periodically refreshed by recomputing inverse noises, with ζ_max selected to maximize the reconstruction-error gap between real and fake samples. The combined training objective (Eq. 11) applies indicator functions to alternate between the standard MSE velocity-matching loss on fake pairs and the conic-pair loss on real pairs, anchoring trajectories to the true data manifold while still covering the full domain distribution.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 one-step generation (Euler, N=1), the method reduces FID from 12.21 to 5.98 for 2-rectified flow and from 8.15 to 5.48 for 3-rectified flow, while using only 300K fake pairs (7.2% of the 4M used by the original). Under full-step RK45 sampling, it achieves FID 3.24 (2-rectified) and 3.70 (3-rectified) with fewer NFEs (104 and 98 vs. 110 and 104). On ImageNet 64×64, FID improves from 31.2 to 28.2 (RK-45) and from 39.7 to 37.8 (Euler). On LSUN Bedroom 256×256, one-step Euler FID drops dramatically from 139.98 to 26.54.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the original k-rectified flow [27], the method wins on every metric across all settings: on CIFAR-10 one-step, FID drops from 12.21→5.98 (2-rectified) and 8.15→5.48 (3-rectified) with IS improving from 8.08→8.79 and 8.47→8.84; under RK45 full-step sampling, FID improves from 3.36→3.24 and 3.96→3.70 while also reducing NFEs. On ImageNet 64×64, FID improves from 31.2→28.2 (RK-45) and 39.7→37.8 (Euler), and recall rises from 0.5264→0.6032 (RK-45). On LSUN Bedroom, one-step Euler FID collapses from 139.98 to 26.54. Against RF++ [24], the paper applies its conic reflow as a plug-in and improves FID from 4.43 to 4.22 while using fewer synthetic pairs (600K fake + 50K real vs. 800K synthetic), confirming generalizability to other reflow-based training schemes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the k-rectified flow framework of Liu et al. [27] by identifying a previously under-characterized failure mode in the reflow procedure: a progressive distributional drift away from the target data distribution, distinct from the known issues of error accumulation and model collapse [56]. While concurrent works like Simple Reflow [18] also incorporate real data pairs and RF++ [24] modifies the time distribution, this paper's specific contribution is the Slerp-based conic perturbation around real-sample inversions, which provides localized geometric supervision in noise space. The method is positioned as a drop-in replacement for the fake-pair reflow step, compatible with existing rectified flow pipelines including RF++ and potentially SD3/Flux-style architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of distributional drift as a first-order problem in iterative reflow provides a useful diagnostic lens for the broader flow-matching community, and the conic reflow technique is a simple, plug-and-play modification that reduces synthetic pair requirements by over 90% while substantially improving one-step FID on CIFAR-10. Open questions include whether the approach scales effectively to high-resolution conditional generation (text-to-image), whether the Slerp perturbation schedule generalizes across architectures, and whether the drift phenomenon has a clean theoretical characterization beyond the empirical KL-divergence and reconstruction-gap evidence presented here. The paper notes that 60K real images may be insufficient for ImageNet, suggesting a data-efficiency frontier that remains to be explored.
