## Problem

Most human-robot collaboration research evaluates robots on short, predefined episodes with closed-form task sets, ignoring the reality that humans have persistent psychological traits, long-term preferences, and spontaneous, environment-conditioned intentions that evolve over days. No existing framework enables the study of continual, open-ended HRC where a robot must personalize its assistance to an individual's traits and temporal routines across multiple days without explicit task instructions.

## Input / Output

The robot receives uniformly sampled video frames (1024×768) of the human's first task, a text-based 3D scene dictionary mapping objects to rooms, an inferred Big-5 trait profile, and retrieved intention/task history from prior hours and days. The output is a filtered set of assistive tasks: the robot-VLM first generates an intention superset, then a task superset per intention, and binary classifiers (yes/no) select the most appropriate tasks. At day's end, the human provides binary feedback with reasoning, which updates the trait profile fed back into subsequent days' prompts and classifier inputs.

## Method

The framework has two coupled components. For human simulation, an LLM (Llama-3.1-8B) generates whole-day intention sequences conditioned on Big-5 traits, 3D environment, and prior-hour history, with Reflexion-based self-correction and recency/relevance memory retrieval to handle long contexts; motions are rendered via SMPL-X whole-body sequences from Motion-X/AMASS. For robot assistance, the paper decouples task inference into a two-stage chain: a VLM (Llama-3.2-11B) imagines multiple candidate intentions and tasks from the observation video, and LoRA-finetuned Mistral-7B classifiers score each candidate against the inferred human profile and temporal context. The key design choice is optimizing the VLM via prompting (incorporating the evolving trait profile and retrieved history) while training classifiers via supervised learning on end-of-day feedback, enabling the robot to capture correlations between traits, time-of-day, and specific intentions without overfitting to a fixed task set.

## Results

The main assistive agent achieves predicate-based success rates of 0.505 (Setting 1: same human, same scene), 0.465 (Setting 2: same human, different scenes), 0.439 (Setting 3: different humans, same scene), and 0.344 (Setting 4: different humans, different scenes) on the final day of collaboration. The method shows the strongest within-day improvement and the strongest across-day improvement (second only to the Oracle upper bound) among all approaches. The simulated human model achieves 99.5% intention classification accuracy, 83.0% task classification accuracy, and 78.9% temporal dependence accuracy, with user study accuracies of 76.4% (MCQ) and 71.2% (trait matching).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the main method against six baselines: Direct Prompting, Direct Finetuning, Oracle, Random, Intention Agnostic, and Human & Context Agnostic. The main method achieves the highest within-day improvement and the strongest across-day improvement, second only to the Oracle upper bound (which receives ground-truth human intentions). Direct Prompting and Direct Finetuning show minimal improvement over days, as they establish rigid 1-to-1 mappings between time and intentions/tasks and struggle with the varying distribution of human behavior. Random degrades over time due to the absence of learning or validation. Intention Agnostic shows moderate improvement but lags behind the main method because it skips intention inference, losing the stronger temporal dependencies that exist between intentions (validated by the 78.9% next-intention prediction accuracy). Human & Context Agnostic captures only superficial time-task correlations and ignores human traits, leading to the weakest within-day gains. Removing human traits inference (No Traits) causes the largest drop in Settings 3 and 4 (0.239 and 0.206 vs. 0.439 and 0.344), confirming that personalization to individual humans is critical for multi-human collaboration.

## Relation to Prior Work

COOPERA extends the Watch-and-Help paradigm [50] from episodic, predefined-task collaboration to a continual, multi-day, open-ended setting where humans spontaneously generate intentions driven by psychological traits and temporal context. For human simulation, it draws on the memory retrieval mechanism from Generative Agents [46] (recency × relevance scoring) and the Big-5 personality framework, but departs from prior simulated-human work by grounding LLM-driven behavior in 3D environments with expressive whole-body motion (SMPL-X, Motion-X) rather than remaining purely language-based. The assistive agent chains a VLM (Llama-3.2-11B) for intention/task superset generation with LoRA-finetuned binary classifiers (Mistral-7B), positioning itself between the direct-prompting and oracle baselines common in open-world robot planning [8, 29].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

COOPERA provides the first benchmark for studying how robots can personalize assistance to individual humans over multiple days in open-ended household settings, a gap left by prior episodic HRC work. The finding that generalizing to a new human (SR 0.343) is substantially harder than to a new scene (SR 0.465) highlights that person-specific trait modeling remains the core bottleneck, and the midday performance dip reveals that trait-driven behavioral diversity is the hardest regime for current agents. Key limitations include single-human scope, reliance on relatively small open-source models (8B–11B), and simulation-only evaluation, leaving open questions about multi-human collaboration, communication-based trait inference, and transfer to physical robots.
