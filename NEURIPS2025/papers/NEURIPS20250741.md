## Problem

Prior research on LLM moral reasoning has focused almost exclusively on individual agents, leaving a critical blind spot: how collective deliberation among multiple LLM agents alters moral judgments in high-stakes domains. This paper asks whether LLM multi-agent systems exhibit a 'Utilitarian Boost'—a systematic shift toward endorsing norm violations that maximize overall welfare—mirroring the well-documented effect in human groups, and whether the underlying mechanisms match or diverge from those in humans.

## Input / Output

The system takes moral dilemma scenario text (drawn from Greene's personal/impersonal trolley dilemmas, Oxford Utilitarianism Scale items, and CNI action-omission scenarios) and feeds it to 1–3 LLM agents who either reason solo or engage in 6 rounds of structured multi-turn discussion before producing a final private reflection. Each agent outputs a 1-7 moral acceptability rating accompanied by a detailed argument; downstream analysis extracts CNI latent parameters (Consequences, Norms, Inaction preference), OUS subscale scores (Impartial Beneficence, Instrumental Harm), and emotion tags via pretrained RoBERTa classifiers. Ratings are externally validated through stratified Prolific crowdsourcing (r = 0.581 with LLM scores) to confirm coherence between arguments and numeric scores.

## Method

The paper tests six LLMs (Llama3.3:70B, QwQ, Qwen3:32B, Gemma3:27B, Qwen2.5:32B, GPT-4.1) under Solo and Group (dyad/triad) conditions using a cumulative-link ordinal mixed-effects regression with random intercepts for dilemmas and random slopes for repetitions, selected via likelihood-ratio model comparison. The key finding is a significant Utilitarian Boost (β = 0.31, p < .0001) that is specific to personal (direct-harm) dilemmas and absent in impersonal or non-moral controls. Mechanism analysis via the CNI model reveals that, unlike humans whose boost is driven solely by heightened consequence sensitivity, LLM groups show diverse profiles—some become more impartial (GPT-4.1, Llama3.3), others more action-focused (Qwen3, QwQ), and some more norm-tolerant (Gemma3). Post-hoc probing experiments demonstrate that the boost can be attenuated by model-family heterogeneity (β = −0.30), reversed by capacity heterogeneity (strong × weak pairs, β = 1.40), or countered by mixing deontological and utilitarian role prompts (ΔU = −0.323), while uniform deontological prompting fails to prevent the shift.

## Results

Across six LLMs (Gemma3, GPT-4.1, Llama3.3, Qwen2.5, Qwen3, QwQ), group deliberation produces a significant Utilitarian Boost of β = 0.31 (SE = 0.046, z = 6.81, p < .0001) on a 1–7 moral acceptability scale. The boost is concentrated in personal dilemmas involving direct harm (β = 0.64 overall; 0.74 for pairs, 0.55 for triads, all p < .001), while impersonal dilemmas show no significant shift (β = −0.02, p = .975). Model-specific boosts range from 0.57 (GPT-4.1) to 1.65 (Gemma3), with Qwen3 (1.23) and Llama3.3 (0.80) in between. Post-hoc experiments show that mixed moral-framework prompts (utilitarian + deontological) reverse the boost (ΔU = −0.323, p < .0001), and strong–weak model pairings flip it toward deontological outcomes (β = 1.40, p < .001).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to Keshmirian et al. (2022) human group data, LLM Solo baselines sit lower (≈2.7–3.6 vs. human ≈3.8/7), yet the mean LLM boost (~+0.42) closely matches the human +0.5-point uptick, with Gemma3 exceeding the human average at +0.85. Compared to Rokosz et al. (2025), the key mechanistic divergence is that human groups become more utilitarian solely through heightened sensitivity to consequences (C), whereas LLM groups exhibit heterogeneous CNI profiles: Gemma3 acts as a norm-aligned optimizer, GPT-4.1 and Llama3.3 shift via enhanced impartiality, and Qwen3/QwQ adopt an action-focused utilitarian stance. The LLM boost is also more restricted in scope—significant only for personal (direct-harm) dilemmas—whereas the human Utilitarian Boost generalizes across dilemma types. Emotionally, LLM groups shift from disgust-dominant solo language toward neutral or fear-labeled arguments, a pattern that correlates with boost magnitude (Gemma3 shows the highest fear proportion and the largest boost), partially mirroring human findings that fear and disgust track utilitarian responding in personal dilemmas.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the established line of work on the Utilitarian Boost in human group moral deliberation (Keshmirian et al. 2022; Rokosz et al. 2025) to multi-agent LLM systems, applying the CNI computational model (Gawronski & Ng, 2024) and the Oxford Utilitarianism Scale (Kahane et al., 2018) as measurement instruments to LLM group responses. It also builds on the emerging LLM-MAS literature on emergent collective distortions (conformity, belief congruence, social irrationality) but is the first to systematically characterize moral-reasoning-specific group dynamics. The key departure from prior LLM moral reasoning work, which has focused on single-agent comparisons with humans, is the structured multi-turn group deliberation protocol with ordinal mixed-effects modeling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LLM groups become more utilitarian specifically in personal (direct-harm) dilemmas—the very contexts where society most needs norm protection—while showing no boost in impersonal scenarios, inverts the human pattern and raises urgent alignment concerns for multi-agent deployments in medicine, law, and safety engineering. The mechanism divergence from humans (diverse CNI profiles vs. a single C-driven shift) means that debiasing strategies validated on individual models or human groups may not transfer. Open questions include whether the effect persists in larger panels or committee topologies, whether mixed human-LLM groups exhibit the same dynamics, and whether the exploratory mitigation levers (model diversity, prompt diversity, capacity heterogeneity) generalize beyond the small-scale dyad/triad experiments reported here.
