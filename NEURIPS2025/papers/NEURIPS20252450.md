## Problem

Machine learning-based SAT solvers applied to cryptanalysis lack a unified evaluation framework: existing methods use inconsistent input formats (CNF, ANF, raw bitstrings), varying ML integration points, and no standardized cryptographic datasets, making fair cross-method comparison impossible. Cryptographic SAT instances also exhibit domain-specific structures—high symmetry and algebraic dependencies—that differ fundamentally from general SAT benchmarks, and no prior benchmark systematically isolates how input representation and ML integration level affect solver performance on these instances.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252450/cropped_page3_idx5.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark provides each cryptographic instance in three parallel representations: ANF (polynomial equations over GF(2) with tripartite variable-monomial-clause graphs), CNF (bipartite literal-clause or variable-clause graphs), and raw plaintext-ciphertext bitstrings. The three evaluation levels produce distinct outputs: standalone distinguishers predict satisfiability labels (SAT/UNSAT), heuristic-enhanced solvers report average solving time per instance, and hyperparameter optimization yields tuned parameter tuples (e.g., restartint, reduceint, decay for Kissat). BASIN uniquely consumes raw bitstrings directly and outputs a recovered key assignment via neural-guided bitwise enumeration.

## Method

SAT4CryptoBench organizes evaluation into three ML-integration levels—standalone distinguisher (end-to-end GNN classification on ANF or CNF graphs), heuristic enhancement (internal learnable modules like BMM or external neural networks like NeuroBack guiding CDCL branching), and hyperparameter optimization (HEBO/EasyNAS tuning solver parameters on cryptographic distributions)—and provides matched datasets across Simon, Cipher, MD4, SHA-1, and SHA-256 at multiple rounds and key sizes in all three input formats. The key architectural contribution is BASIN, a bitwise solver that bypasses formula encoding entirely: it takes plaintext-ciphertext bitstrings, uses an improved CryptoANFNet to predict a likely key assignment from the ANF structure, and then performs guided bitwise enumeration from that initialization. Experiments reveal that ANF-based GNNs (CryptoANFNet) achieve near-perfect distinguisher accuracy on 3-round Simon while CNF-based GNNs (GCN, GIN, GGNN, Neurosat) remain near chance level, and that BASIN's direct bitstring input outperforms both CNF and ANF solvers on 12-round Simon (29.67s vs. 371.78s and 364.96s respectively), demonstrating that input representation is a first-order determinant of solver effectiveness on cryptographic instances.

## Results

On the Standalone Distinguisher task, CryptoANFNet (ANF-based) achieves 100% prediction accuracy on 3-round Simon (8-bit) and 99.5% on 16/32-bit, while all CNF-based GNNs (GCN, GIN, GGNN, Neurosat) remain near random (50–52%). For Heuristic Enhancement, BMM-enhanced MapleLCMDist solves Cipher-12 in 368.26s vs. 382.92s for its base, and MapleSat-BMM reduces Simon-11-32-64 from 73.85s to 41.57s. Hyperparameter optimization via HEBO cuts Kissat's solving time on Cipher-12 by 46.5% (974.35s→521.42s) and MapleSAT on MD4 by 70.4% (1.435s→0.425s). On high-round Simon-12-32-64, the proposed BASIN solver (plaintext-ciphertext input) achieves 29.67s, outperforming the best CNF solver (MapleSat+HO-EasyNAS, 371.78s) and best ANF solver (WDSat+MVC, 364.96s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CNF-based GNNs (GCN, GIN, GGNN, Neurosat) all achieve near-random accuracy (50–52%) on Simon distinguisher tasks, while the ANF-based CryptoANFNet reaches 100% on 3-round and 67–70% on 6-round, establishing a clear representation gap. Among internal ML-enhanced solvers, BMM variants (MapleSat-BMM, MapleLCMDist-BMM) consistently outperform their base counterparts on complex block-cipher instances (e.g., MapleLCMDist-BMM: 368.26s vs. 382.92s on Cipher-12; MapleSat-BMM: 41.57s vs. 73.85s on Simon-11-32-64), but offer no advantage or even regress on simpler instances like MD4. External ML modules (NeuroBack, NeuroCadical, Graph-Q-Sat) show substantial overhead: NeuroBack inflates Kissat's time from 974s to 2184s on Cipher-12, and Graph-Q-Sat worsens Minisat from 570s to 2438s, indicating poor generalization from their training distributions to cryptographic structures. For hyperparameter optimization, HEBO consistently outperforms EasyNAS (e.g., Kissat-H: 521.42s vs. Kissat-E: 583.55s on Cipher-12; MapleSAT-H: 0.425s vs. MapleSAT-E: 0.276s on MD4 where EasyNAS actually wins), with gains of 46–70% over default settings. The proposed BASIN solver (plaintext-ciphertext input) decisively outperforms both the best CNF solver (MapleSat+HO-EasyNAS: 371.78s) and best ANF solver (WDSat+MVC: 364.96s) on Simon-12-32-64, achieving 29.67s — a 12× speedup — while WDSat alone (ANF) is fastest on Simon-10-32-64 (0.08s) but degrades sharply at higher rounds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on CryptoANFNet (Zheng et al., NeurIPS 2024), which first demonstrated that ANF-based graph neural networks can learn cryptographic SAT structure, and extends it into a comprehensive benchmark framework. It sits at the intersection of three established lines: ML-assisted CDCL solvers (NeuroSAT, NeuroBack, Graph-Q-Sat, BMM-enhanced MapleSat), SAT-based cryptanalysis toolkits (CryptoSAT, CDCL-Crypto), and GNN-based SAT solving (GCN, GIN, GGNN). The key departure is unifying these fragmented efforts under a single evaluation protocol with standardized cryptographic datasets in multiple input representations (CNF, ANF, plaintext-ciphertext bitstrings), and introducing BASIN as a bitwise solver that bypasses both CNF and ANF formulations entirely.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SAT4CryptoBench fills a critical gap: prior ML-SAT benchmarks (G4SATBench, SATzilla) target general SAT problems and cannot capture the algebraic structure and symmetry unique to cryptographic instances. The finding that ANF-based networks achieve near-perfect accuracy where CNF-based networks fail at chance level, and that raw bitstring input (BASIN) outperforms both structured representations on high-round ciphers, provides concrete guidance for future solver design. However, the authors acknowledge that ML models struggle to generalize across algorithms and instance scales, and that computational overhead can negate benefits on simpler instances—open questions that will require more adaptive, lightweight architectures. The benchmark is most impactful for researchers at the SAT/cryptanalysis/ML intersection who need a standardized, reproducible evaluation platform.
