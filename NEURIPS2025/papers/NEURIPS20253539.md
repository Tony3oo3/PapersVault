## Problem

LLMs for optimization modeling frequently produce syntactically invalid, infeasible, or mathematically incorrect formulations, and existing approaches—whether prompt-based agents or offline fine-tuning on curated datasets—lack a mechanism to inherently guarantee functional correctness and solver compatibility. This paper is the first to apply Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) directly to optimization modeling, using classical solvers as objective oracles to provide precise, instance-level feedback during training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253539/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a natural language optimization problem description and generates a structured chain-of-thought response segmented into three parts: free-form reasoning steps, a concise mathematical formulation, and executable Python code (using GurobiPy). The code is deterministically executed by an optimization solver, which returns the optimal objective value, feasibility status, and a .lp file encoding the instance-level model structure (objective direction, binary/integer variable counts). The .lp file serves as a solver-agnostic, implementation-independent artifact that enables both reward computation and structural consensus scoring during data synthesis.

## Method

SIRL trains an LLM policy via REINFORCE++ (a PPO-clipped policy gradient) with two key design choices. First, a Partial KL surrogate applies the KL-divergence penalty selectively only to the mathematical formulation and solver code segments while omitting it from reasoning steps, balancing exploratory reasoning diversity with syntactic and structural stability in the critical output segments. Second, a two-stage verifiable reward is computed by executing the generated code: Stage 1 rewards format compliance, successful execution, and objective-value accuracy against ground truth; Stage 2 adds a bonus for incorporating advanced modeling techniques (e.g., Big-M, nonlinear formulations) verified via .lp file statistics. For training data, an instance-enhanced self-consistency method scores multi-role LLM responses using weighted consensus over four .lp-derived features (objective value, optimization direction, binary variable count, integer variable count) rather than relying solely on final-value majority voting.

## Results

SIRL-Qwen2.5-32B achieves a Macro Average pass@1 accuracy of 68.3% across five optimization modeling benchmarks (NL4OPT, MAMO Easy, MAMO Complex, IndustryOR, OptMATH), surpassing the 671B-parameter DeepSeek-V3.1 (65.0%), DeepSeek-R1 (64.6%), and OpenAI-o3 (57.1%). The smaller SIRL-Qwen2.5-7B model reaches 60.6% Macro Average, outperforming all offline-learning baselines (OptMATH-Qwen2.5-7B at 55.4%, LLMOpt-Qwen2.5-14B at 51.1%) and the agent-based OptiMUS (49.4%). On the most challenging OptMATH benchmark, SIRL-32B achieves 45.8% pass@1, exceeding DeepSeek-V3.1 (43.9%) and OpenAI-o3 (44.0%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SIRL-32B outperforms DeepSeek-V3.1 (65.0% Macro AVG) by 3.3 points and OpenAI-o3 (57.1%) by 11.2 points on the overall macro average, winning on 4 of 5 individual benchmarks against OpenAI-o3 (losing only on IndustryOR: 42.0% vs 44.0%) and 3 of 5 against DeepSeek-V3.1 (losing on MAMO Complex: 61.1% vs 63.5% and IndustryOR: 42.0% vs 44.0%). Against the strongest offline-learning baseline OptMATH-Qwen2.5-32B (61.1% Macro AVG), SIRL-32B leads by 7.2 points, with particularly large gains on IndustryOR (42.0% vs 31.0%) and OptMATH (45.8% vs 34.7%). The 7B SIRL model dominates all same-scale offline-learning competitors: it beats OptMATH-Qwen2.5-7B by 5.2 Macro AVG points (60.6% vs 55.4%), winning on every individual benchmark including IndustryOR (33.0% vs 20.0%) and OptMATH (30.5% vs 24.4%). Against the agent-based OptiMUS, SIRL-7B achieves a 11.2-point Macro AVG improvement (60.6% vs 49.4%), with the largest gap on OptMATH (30.5% vs 20.2%). Notably, SIRL-32B also surpasses the much larger DeepSeek-R1 (64.6%) by 3.7 Macro AVG points despite being roughly 20x smaller in parameter count.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is the first to apply Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) to optimization modeling, drawing a direct analogy to how Lean verifies mathematical proofs and compilers verify code: classical solvers (Gurobi, COPT, CPLEX) serve as domain-specific oracles. Methodologically, it builds on REINFORCE++/PPO for the policy gradient, Chain-of-Thought for structuring the reasoning-to-code pipeline, and ORLM's data synthesis pipeline, but departs from prior offline-learning approaches (SFT, DPO in ORLM, OptMATH, LLMOpt) by replacing imitation-based objectives with solver-verified reward signals. The key conceptual departure is the Partial KL surrogate, which selectively regularizes only the mathematical formulation and code segments while leaving the reasoning segment free to explore, and the instance-enhanced self-consistency that leverages .lp file structural features (variable counts, optimization direction) beyond simple value-based majority voting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Partial KL design offers a practical recipe for tool-augmented RL tasks where diverse reasoning exploration must coexist with strict syntactic/format constraints on the executable output — a tension likely relevant beyond optimization to any domain requiring verifiable structured generation. The .lp file as a solver-agnostic, instance-level verification artifact is a clean mechanism that could generalize to other formal modeling languages. However, the evaluation benchmarks are relatively small (100–652 instances), performance on the hardest sets (IndustryOR, OptMATH) remains modest even for the 32B model, and the authors acknowledge persistent reward hacking. Open questions include whether the framework scales to truly complex, multi-stage industrial problems and whether the two-stage curriculum generalizes to non-convex or stochastic optimization settings.
