## Problem

Graph transformer expressivity results are typically tied to specific architectural choices (modified attention mechanisms, particular PE designs), and empirical evaluations are conducted on small-scale datasets where implementation details dominate. This prevents generalizable insights about what actually drives GT performance across diverse domains and model scales, leaving a gap between theoretical expressivity guarantees and practical design guidance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255637/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The GDT consumes graphs with node and edge feature embeddings plus structural positional encodings (RWSE, RRWP, LPE, or SPE) computed from the graph Laplacian or random-walk matrices. It supports both node-level tokenization (one token per node) and edge-level tokenization (one token per node and per edge, equivalent to a line-graph transformation), with a [cls] token treated as a virtual node connected to all others. Predictions are produced at graph level (via the [cls] token), node level, or edge level through an MLP head, and for few-shot transfer the raw token embeddings are used directly with k-NN without fine-tuning.

## Method

The GDT uses unmodified standard scaled dot-product attention with an additive attention bias B that encodes edge embeddings (via a learned projection ρ) and relative PEs (via a learned weight W_U), while absolute PEs are added to the initial token embeddings. The key theoretical contribution proves that this standard-attention GDT can simulate the Generalized-Distance Weisfeiler-Leman (GD-WL) algorithm by leveraging the Lindemann-Weierstrass theorem to show softmax attention can injectively encode multisets, decoupling expressivity from attention design and tying it solely to PE choice. The paper further establishes a fine-grained PE expressivity hierarchy (RWSE ≺ RRWP ≺ SPE, LPE ≺ SPE, with RWSE incomparable to 1-WL) and validates these results empirically at scale—over 8M graphs and ~270M tokens across molecular property prediction, object detection, code summarization, and algorithmic reasoning—showing that PE efficiency varies greatly while predictive differences are modest, and that the GDT supports effective few-shot transfer and 16× size extrapolation.

## Results

On 16M-parameter models, RRWP achieves the best performance on 4 of 6 tasks: PCQ (90.4 MAE), FLOW (1.45 MAE), MST (96.04 F1), and BRIDGES (99.21 F1). LPE and RWSE are competitive and more efficient, with LPE leading on COCO (44.83 F1) and CODE (19.48 F1), while RWSE is strongest on FLOW (1.49 MAE) among the efficient PEs. On the BREC expressivity benchmark, GDT with RWSE matches Graphormer-GD exactly (203/203 samples distinguished), and with LPE achieves 180 vs. 183. Scaling to 90M and 160M parameters consistently improves performance, e.g., MST F1 rises from ~93 (15M) to 95.80 (160M, RWSE), and few-shot transfer from COCO to PASCAL surpasses prior SOTA with fewer than 10% of training samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary architectural comparison is against Graphormer-GD [Zhang et al., 2023] on the BREC expressivity benchmark. GDT with RWSE achieves 203/203 correctly distinguished samples, exactly matching Graphormer-GD with RWSE (203/203). With LPE, GDT scores 180 vs. Graphormer-GD's 183, a negligible 3-sample gap. The authors attribute this near-equivalence to Theorem 1, which proves both architectures can simulate GD-WL. Within the GDT framework, RRWP dominates on algorithmic reasoning tasks (MST 96.04, BRIDGES 99.21 F1) but is significantly slower and more memory-hungry (e.g., 19.2 GB vs. ~9.8 GB on CODE). LPE and RWSE offer the best efficiency-performance tradeoff, with LPE leading on COCO (44.83 F1) and RWSE on FLOW (1.49 MAE), while NoPE and SPE underperform on BRIDGES (~55 F1) due to insufficient structural information for edge-level tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

The GDT deliberately departs from the dominant GT design paradigm of modifying the attention mechanism (as in Graphormer, GRIT, or GraphGPS) by retaining standard scaled dot-product attention and instead characterizing expressivity entirely through positional encodings. It builds on the GD-WL framework of Zhang et al. (2023) but proves, for the first time, that standard softmax attention (not saturated softmax or hardmax) can simulate GD-WL, using the Lindemann-Weierstrass theorem from number theory to establish injectivity of multiset encoding. The edge-embedding strategy is adapted from Graphormer and Bechler-Speicher et al. (2025), and the PE expressivity hierarchy extends results from Black et al. (2024) and Zhang et al. (2024) with new incomparability and approximation results for RWSE, RRWP, LPE, and SPE.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By decoupling GT expressivity from attention design, the paper provides a clean theoretical lens through which practitioners can reason about PE choices without re-deriving results for each architecture variant. The large-scale evaluation (8M+ graphs, 270M tokens) and few-shot transfer results (surpassing PASCAL SOTA with <10% of training data) offer practical guidance that is hard to obtain from small-scale benchmarks. Key limitations include incompatibility of the learnable attention bias with FlashAttention2/FlexAttention at inference time, failure to exploit attention sparsity for very large graphs, and the gap between the fine-grained theoretical PE hierarchy and the less pronounced empirical differences. Open questions include whether the Lindemann-Weierstrass-based expressivity argument extends to linear-attention GTs and how to reconcile the theoretical PE ordering with the empirically compressed performance gaps.
