## Problem

While both reinforcement learning and optimal control offer paths to learning from offline trajectories, their relative strengths in the reward-free setting—where agents must learn purely from suboptimal, unlabeled data—remain poorly understood. Prior work has focused on RL methods trained on expert or exploratory data without isolating how specific data quality factors (diversity, trajectory length, spatial coverage) affect performance. This paper systematically evaluates both paradigms across 23 datasets of varying quality to identify which approaches generalize best to unseen layouts, new tasks, and degraded data conditions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes top-down RGB images (64×64×3 for Diverse PointMaze; 64×64×2 for Two-Rooms) along with a goal observation and, where applicable, agent velocity, and produces a sequence of 2D action vectors (displacement or acceleration) via test-time planning. No reward signal is used at any point—neither during training nor during planning—making this a pure optimal control method. The planner re-solves the action sequence every step (or every 4 steps for efficiency) using MPPI over a 16-step horizon in the learned latent space, and adapting to a new task (e.g., state avoidance) requires only changing the planning cost, not retraining.

## Method

PLDM learns a latent dynamics model using the JEPA architecture with a reconstruction-free self-supervised objective: an encoder maps observations to latent representations, and an ensemble of K predictors (GRU or CNN) forecasts future latents conditioned on the current latent and action. Training minimizes the L2 distance between predicted and encoded latents, augmented with VICReg-inspired variance/covariance regularization, inverse dynamics modeling, and temporal smoothness terms to prevent representation collapse. At test time, MPPI searches over action sequences to minimize a combined cost of latent-space goal distance and ensemble prediction uncertainty, which regularizes the plan to stay within the training distribution. The key differentiator from reconstruction-based world models (e.g., DreamerV3) is that predicting latents rather than reconstructing pixels yields features that are significantly more informative for planning, and the entire pipeline requires no reward annotations whatsoever.

## Results

On the Two-Rooms environment with 3M high-quality transitions, all methods reach strong performance (HILP 100%, GCIQL 98.0%, PLDM 97.8%, HIQL 96.4%), but PLDM is the only method that generalizes to unseen maze layouts, maintaining high success rates even when trained on just 5 layouts while all model-free baselines fail. PLDM also generalizes zero-shot to a state-avoidance (chasing) task simply by inverting the planning cost, outperforming HILP on average agent-chaser distance. In data-efficiency experiments, PLDM and GCIQL achieve ~80% success rate with only a few thousand transitions, while HILP requires substantially more data to reach comparable performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PLDM is the only method that generalizes to unseen obstacle layouts (Section 4.8) and to a new task (state avoidance, Section 4.6), where it outperforms HILP by maintaining greater separation from the chaser. Against HILP, PLDM trades off data efficiency (HILP is more data-hungry, needing more transitions to reach peak performance) for superior layout and task generalization; both tie on trajectory stitching and random-policy robustness. Against GCIQL, PLDM wins decisively on layout generalization (GCIQL fails on held-out mazes) and task transfer, while GCIQL matches PLDM on data efficiency and stitching. Against HIQL, CRL, and GCBC, PLDM dominates on nearly all stress tests: HIQL drops to 26.3% on no-door-passing data vs. PLDM's 34.4%, CRL drops to 14.7%, and GCBC to 8.4%, while all three fail completely on unseen layouts. On best-case in-distribution performance with abundant high-quality data, all methods are competitive (96–100% success), confirming that PLDM's advantages emerge specifically under distribution shift and data-quality stress.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of reward-free offline RL (HILP, HIQL, GCIQL, CRL) and model-based optimal control, but departs from both in a key way: rather than learning a policy via trial-and-error or value iteration, it learns a latent dynamics model using a reconstruction-free JEPA objective (LeCun, 2022) and performs test-time planning with MPPI (Williams, 2015). It explicitly contrasts with reconstruction-based world models (DreamerV3, TD-MPC2), showing that predicting latents rather than pixels yields substantially better planning features. The paper also extends the OGBench benchmark (Park et al., 2024) with two new navigation environments and 23 controlled datasets, enabling a systematic stress-test of generalization properties that prior reward-free RL work had not isolated.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic head-to-head comparison of model-free RL versus model-based planning for reward-free offline learning, yielding clear practitioner guidance: model-free GCRL excels with abundant high-quality data, while latent-dynamics planning is the only approach that simultaneously handles suboptimal data, short trajectories, unseen layouts, and task transfer. The finding that reconstruction-free JEPA objectives dramatically outperform pixel-reconstruction objectives (97% vs. 24–26% success) for downstream planning is a strong argument for the JEPA paradigm in control. Key limitations include restriction to navigation (no manipulation or POMDPs), deterministic dynamics assumption, and ~4× slower inference than model-free baselines, though replanning every 4 steps recovers most performance. Open questions include extending PLDM to long-horizon reasoning with compounding prediction errors, partially observable settings, and amortized planning to close the inference gap.
