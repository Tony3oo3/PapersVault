## Problem

While state entropy maximization is well-understood as an unsupervised pre-training objective in single-agent RL, its multi-agent counterpart lacks a principled formulation, theoretical characterization, and practical algorithm. The paper asks how different multi-agent entropy objectives (joint, disjoint, mixture) relate, how hard the problem is in theory versus practice, and whether a scalable decentralized algorithm can explicitly optimize finite-trial entropy objectives for downstream task transfer.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252524/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each agent receives only its own local state observation (decentralized information), not the full joint state, and the environment is a finite-horizon Markov Game with discrete states (continuous states are discretized into 10 bins per dimension for the plug-in entropy estimator). The algorithm consumes N trajectories of length T per epoch and outputs a decentralized joint policy — one parametric policy per agent — that can be directly deployed as a pre-trained initialization for sparse-reward downstream tasks via standard multi-agent policy optimization (e.g., MA-TRPO).

## Method

The paper formalizes multi-agent state entropy maximization as a convex Markov Game (cMG) and identifies three distinct objectives: joint entropy over the product state space, disjoint (per-agent marginal) entropy, and mixture entropy over the uniform average of marginals. Theoretically, it proves that these objectives are ordered with logarithmic gaps in the number of agents (Lemma 4.1) and that the finite-trials deployment mismatch for mixture objectives scales as 1/√(|N|K), making more agents beneficial rather than harmful (Theorem 4.2). Algorithmically, it introduces Trust Region Pure Exploration (TRPE), a fully decentralized off-policy method that optimizes the single-trial (K=1) objective via an importance-sampling surrogate function with per-agent KL-divergence trust regions, enabling each agent to update its policy independently using only its own trajectory data and the plug-in entropy estimate of the empirical state distribution.

## Results

On the Secret Room environment (Env. i, T=50), pre-training via mixture entropy objectives leads to faster learning and higher worst-case return in sparse-reward downstream tasks compared to uniform initialization, while disjoint-objective pre-training is actively harmful (producing near-deterministic policies overfitted to uncoordinated states). On the MaMuJoCo Reacher environment (Env. ii, T=100), both joint and mixture pre-training guarantee zero-shot performance compatible with MA-TRPO after 2×10⁴ samples, whereas disjoint pre-training fails to achieve this. Mixture objectives consistently show lower variance and more stable zero-shot generalization than joint objectives, which exhibit extremely high variance with worst-case performances no better than random initialization. The theoretical bounds (Lemma 4.1) are corroborated: mixture entropy is a tight lower bound on joint entropy (gap scales as log|N|), while disjoint entropy can be a poor approximation of joint entropy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three objective formulations (Joint, Disjoint, Mixture) all optimized via the same TRPE algorithm, against standard initializations (Uniform for Env. i, Zero-Mean for Env. ii) as baselines for downstream sparse-reward tasks. Mixture objectives win on all practical metrics: they enforce a clustering behavior where agents specialize in different regions, yielding superior sample efficiency in downstream learning and the most stable zero-shot generalization. Joint objectives lose in practice despite being theoretically tractable (Fact 4.1), because the exponential growth of the joint state space (|S|^|N|) causes poor concentration (Theorem 4.2) and high-variance zero-shot performance. Disjoint objectives lose most decisively: agents over-optimize over a restricted reachable space without coordination, producing near-deterministic policies that are harmful for downstream tasks (Fig. 3b shows disjoint pre-training performing worse than no pre-training at T=50). The key practical takeaway is that mixture entropy provides the best trade-off between estimation tractability (support size |S̃| independent of N) and exploration quality, with the deployment mismatch scaling as 1/√(|N|K) rather than 1/√K.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends single-agent state entropy maximization (Hazan et al., 2019; Mutti et al., 2021) to multi-agent settings by casting the problem within the convex Markov Games framework (Gemp et al., 2024), which prior work had not equipped with entropy functionals or finite-trials objectives. It adapts the finite-trials convex RL analysis of Mutti et al. (2023) from cMDPs to cMGs, and builds a decentralized trust-region algorithm (TRPE) on the TRPO blueprint (Schulman et al., 2015) using importance-sampling-based off-policy updates (Metelli et al., 2020). The key departure from prior MARL exploration work, which relied on ad hoc entropic reward-shaping heuristics, is the provision of a principled problem formulation with three distinct objectives (joint, disjoint, mixture) and formal performance bounds linking them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first principled framework for unsupervised multi-agent pre-training via state entropy maximization, providing practitioners with a clear taxonomy of objectives and theoretical guidance on which to choose. The finding that mixture entropy offers the best tractability-performance trade-off—particularly that the finite-trials mismatch scales as 1/√(N·K), meaning more agents actually reduce deployment risk—is practically actionable for teams deploying multi-robot systems. Key limitations include restriction to discrete state spaces (plug-in entropy estimator), Monte-Carlo sample inefficiency in high dimensions, and validation on only two small-scale environments with four seeds. Open questions include scalable continuous-domain entropy estimation, guarantees for decentralized-information policies, and whether the mixture-objective advantage persists at larger agent counts and more complex tasks.
