## Problem

LLM-based decompilers lack a large-scale, publicly accessible benchmark of real-world binary-source function pairs. Existing benchmarks are either contest-style with simple code, synthetic mappings that diverge from real-world distributions, or fragment-level (only variable names or line-level DWARF traces), none of which provide complete function-level pairs from release-level binaries at the scale needed for modern LLM training.

## Input / Output

The decompilation task takes a compiled binary function (x86-64 assembly at optimization levels O0–O3) and produces the corresponding complete C/C++ source function including signature, body, and user-defined types. The benchmark itself consists of 2 million such binary-source pairs extracted from 450 GB of executables across 85K binaries. For evaluation, source functions are compiled at four optimization levels, decompiled back, and verified via unit tests (re-executability), AST-based readability (R2I), and Levenshtein-based edit similarity.

## Method

The core contribution is the CTF (Compile-Trace-Filter) pipeline. First, a forked Clang compiler forces consistent -O{0,1,2,3} and -g flags across all builds, while missing dependencies are auto-resolved by parsing CMakeLists.txt and querying GPT for install commands. Second, the Source-Trace algorithm maps each binary function to its source function by extracting DWARF-reported line locations, using Tree-sitter to recover the full enclosing source function for each line, and selecting the candidate with the maximum line-overlap intersection—this corrects the fragmentation caused by inlining and reordering. Third, a three-stage filter (project-scope removal, in-binary template-instantiation deduplication, and cross-binary MinHash-LSH near-duplicate elimination) reduces 100 million raw pairs to 2 million high-quality ones. For evaluation, Decompile-Bench-Eval combines manually translated HumanEval/MBPP binaries with GitHub2025 (repos published after 2025) to prevent data leakage, and the authors fine-tune LLM4Decompile-End (1.3B/6.7B) via sequence-to-sequence training on 10% of the benchmark, achieving a 20%+ re-executability improvement over prior benchmarks.

## Results

On re-executability (the primary metric), LLM4Decompile-DCBench-6.7b achieves 39.48% average on HumanEval and 43.67% on MBPP, outperforming the base LLM4Decompile-End-1.3b (16.22%/20.54%) by over 20% relative improvement. The 1.3B variant (LLM4Decompile-DCBench-1.3b) reaches 20.89%/24.93%, a 28.8% and 21.4% relative gain over its base. On R2I readability, the 6.7B model scores 69.12 (HumanEval) and 71.80 (MBPP), while on edit similarity it achieves 54.72 (HumanEval) and 56.70 (MBPP), consistently leading all open-source baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LLM4Decompile-DCBench-6.7b substantially outperforms all open-source and traditional baselines: it beats GPT-4.1-mini (13.42%/19.89%), IDA (18.22%/24.49%), Idioms-1.3b (17.91%/22.47%), and the base LLM4Decompile-End-1.3b (16.22%/20.54%) by 15–27 percentage points on average re-executability across HumanEval and MBPP. The only model that surpasses it is Claude-Sonnet-4-reasoning (46.79%/55.68%), which benefits from trillions of training tokens versus the 0.2B tokens used here. The 1.3B DCBench model already exceeds IDA and GPT-4.1-mini, confirming that even a small model fine-tuned on 10% of Decompile-Bench (200K pairs) surpasses general-purpose LLMs and traditional decompilers. On R2I readability, the 6.7B model (69.12/71.80) leads all baselines including Claude-Sonnet-4 (56.74/62.16), and on edit similarity it achieves 54.72/56.70 versus Claude's 50.68/55.99, indicating superior structural fidelity to the original source.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on LLM4Decompile (Tan et al., EMNLP 2024) as the base decompiler model, extending its training data from the synthetic ExeBench to a new real-world corpus. It departs from the four existing categories of binary-source benchmarks—code-contest (Decompile-Dataset, HumanEval-Decompile), synthetic (AnghaBench, ExeBench, CSMith), fragment-level (Dire, Assemblage), and small real-world function-level (CodeCMR 60K, Idioms 154K)—by introducing the first million-scale, real-world, function-level benchmark. The CTF framework (Compile-Trace-Filter) is the key methodological departure: rather than relying on DWARF line-level mappings that break under inlining and optimization, it uses a patched Clang toolchain plus a Tree-sitter-based Source-Trace algorithm to recover complete function-level correspondences, then applies a three-stage filter (project-scope, in-binary dedup, cross-binary MinHash-LSH) to reduce 100M raw pairs to 2M high-quality ones.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first open-source, million-scale, real-world binary-source benchmark, Decompile-Bench removes a major bottleneck for LLM decompilation research that was previously locked behind closed commercial corpora (BinaryAI, MLM, ReCopilot). The 20% re-executability gain from fine-tuning on just 10% of the data, and the ablation showing that unfiltered raw data actually degrades performance, underscore that data quality and real-world distribution matter more than raw volume. Open questions include whether the CTF pipeline generalizes to other architectures (ARM, RISC-V) and languages (Rust, Go), whether the 6.7B model's remaining gap to Claude-Sonnet-4 (39.5% vs 46.8% re-executability) can be closed with the full 2M pairs, and how the benchmark performs under adversarial or obfuscated binaries. The paper is limited to C/C++ on x86-64 with Clang 19, and the evaluation set (HumanEval/MBPP translated to C/C++) remains relatively small in function count.
