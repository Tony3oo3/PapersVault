## Problem

Existing remote sensing vision-language datasets target general geospatial tasks, leaving no comprehensive resource for disaster-specific assessment that spans multiple hazard types, sensor modalities, and the full task spectrum required in real-world response. State-of-the-art VLMs fail on disaster scenes due to the absence of a disaster-focused corpus, a cross-sensor gap between optical and SAR imagery, and insensitivity to fine-grained damage object counting. DisasterM3 fills this gap as the first multi-hazard, multi-sensor, multi-task remote sensing vision-language dataset for global-scale disaster assessment.

## Input / Output

The model consumes co-registered bi-temporal image pairs (pre- and post-disaster) standardized to 0.8 m resolution, where the post-disaster image is either optical (WorldView series) or single-polarization SAR (Capella/Umbra), paired with a natural-language instruction. Outputs span nine tasks: multiple-choice labels for disaster/land-use/bearing-body recognition and building/road damage counting, pixel-level segmentation masks for referring segmentation, free-text spatial relationship descriptions for relational reasoning, and long-form structured reports (disaster captions plus restoration advice) scored on a 5-point GPT-4.1 rubric. The 26,988 images and 123,010 instruction pairs are split into an Instruct set (92,968 pairs) for training and a Bench set (30,042 pairs) for evaluation.

## Method

DisasterM3 is built from 36 historical disaster events (10 types) across 5 continents, sourced from xBD, BRIGHT, and Maxar Open Data, with a construction pipeline that combines GPT-4o-generated prompt variations, expert annotation of instance-level damage masks and spatial relationships, and multi-round verification; for SAR images with degraded semantics, annotations are transferred from co-registered optical counterparts. The benchmark evaluates 14 VLMs under four settings (optical-optical and optical-SAR, for both QA and referring segmentation), revealing that even GPT-4.1 achieves only ~42% average QA accuracy. Four models (Qwen2.5-VL-7B, InternVL3-8B, LISA-7B, PSALM-1.3B) are fine-tuned on the Instruct set, showing that injecting disaster-specific corpus yields stable gains (up to 10.4% QA, 2.1 report, 40.8% segmentation mIoU) with cross-sensor and cross-disaster generalization. A key architectural finding is that PSALM's Mask2Former-style multi-mask-token decoder (100 tokens) substantially outperforms LISA's single fixed mask token, because disaster scenes demand robust mask proposals across highly diverse object scales and damage severities.

## Results

On the DisasterM3 Bench set (optical-optical QA), the best open-source model InternVL3-78B achieves 39.3% average accuracy, while GPT-4.1 leads all baselines at 42.3%. Fine-tuning Qwen2.5-VL-7B and InternVL3-8B on the DisasterM3 Instruct set yields substantial gains: +9.2% and +10.4% average QA accuracy respectively, with the fine-tuned InternVL3-8B reaching 41.7% AVG (narrowly trailing GPT-4.1). For referring segmentation, fine-tuned PSALM-1.3B achieves 50.5% mIoU (a +40.8% absolute gain over its zero-shot score of 9.7%), and fine-tuned LISA-7B reaches 44.8% mIoU (+17.3%), both substantially outperforming all un-fine-tuned segmentation baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among open-source VLMs, the fine-tuned InternVL3-8B (41.7% AVG) surpasses all zero-shot open-source baselines including InternVL3-78B (39.3%) and Qwen2.5-VL-72B (40.5%), and slightly trails GPT-4.1 (42.3%) by 0.6%. The fine-tuned Qwen2.5-VL-7B (40.4%) also exceeds its 72B counterpart (40.5%) and all smaller open-source models, with the largest relative gain on bearing body recognition (BBR: 4.7%→21.5%) and disaster type recognition (DTR: 66.6%→83.6%). Remote sensing VLMs (GeoChat 10.7%, TeoChat 23.0%, EarthDial 22.9%) perform substantially worse than generic VLMs, confirming a domain gap that DisasterM3-specific fine-tuning addresses. For referring segmentation, fine-tuned PSALM-1.3B (50.5% mIoU) and LISA-7B (44.8%) dramatically outperform all zero-shot segmentation baselines (best: LISA-7B at 27.5%), with PSALM's multi-token Mask2Former architecture proving more effective than LISA's single fixed mask token despite 5× fewer parameters. GPT-4o and GPT-4.1 remain the strongest zero-shot baselines on QA (39.3% and 42.3% AVG) and report generation, but the fine-tuned models close the gap to within 0.6% on QA while substantially exceeding them on report quality (3.83–3.90 vs. 2.57–2.27 on a 5-point scale).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of remote sensing VLMs and disaster response. While prior RS vision-language datasets (EarthVQA, VRSBench, GeoChatSet, TeoChatlas) target general geospatial tasks and existing disaster datasets (FloodNet, xBD, BRIGHT) focus on single-hazard detection or classification, DisasterM3 is the first to unify multi-hazard (10 types), multi-sensor (optical + SAR), and multi-task (9 progressive tasks from recognition to report generation) in a bi-temporal vision-language instruction format. Methodologically, it repurposes existing VLM architectures (Qwen2.5-VL, InternVL3, LISA, PSALM) through fine-tuning on the new disaster corpus rather than proposing a new model, positioning itself as a data-driven contribution that exposes domain gaps in current VLMs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even GPT-4.1 achieves only ~42% average accuracy on disaster QA tasks, and performance drops sharply on SAR imagery, highlighting a critical cross-sensor gap that limits real-world deployment during cloud-covered disaster events. The identification of counting insensitivity and the overfitting dilemma in fine-tuned models point to concrete open problems (object-sensitive encoders, numerical-enhanced optimization) for the community. As the first comprehensive disaster VLM benchmark spanning 5 continents and 36 events, it establishes a reference standard that will likely shape future disaster-response AI research, though its single-polarization SAR data and 0.8 m resolution constraint limit operational generalizability.
