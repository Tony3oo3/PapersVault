## Problem

The loss landscape of 3D Human Pose Estimation is highly complicated, containing multiple disconnected local minima that can cause models to converge to sharp, poorly generalizing solutions. Despite the well-known link between loss landscape flatness and generalization, no prior 3D HPE work has explicitly analyzed or leveraged this structure. This paper is the first to visualize the 3D HPE loss landscape and to design training and inference strategies that directly exploit its geometry.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254992/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 2D pose keypoints (typically from a cascaded pyramid network detector, so inherently noisy) as input and regresses 3D joint positions in camera coordinates. The adaptive scaling mechanism introduces an input-dependent scalar denominator that modulates the prediction magnitude per sample, and at inference the final 3D pose is the arithmetic mean of M head predictions, each independently scaled by its own learned factor.

## Method

The core mechanism is an adaptive scaling layer that divides the network's raw 3D pose prediction by a learned, input-dependent scalar σ(h_ψ(g_φ(x)))+1, where g_φ is the shared encoder and h_ψ is a small per-head regressor. This introduces intentional parameter redundancy (multiple (θ, ψ) pairs can produce the same output), which empirically flattens the loss landscape and eliminates high-loss barriers between local minima. On top of this, M regression heads (each with distinct f_θi and h_ψi) are attached to the shared encoder and trained jointly with averaged loss, with different random initializations to encourage diverse solutions on the now-smooth landscape. At inference, the M scaled predictions are averaged. The paper shows that ensembling without the adaptive scaling mechanism fails because heads converge to different disconnected minima with conflicting gradients, while SAM-based flattening is biased and does not improve performance.

## Results

On H36M, the full method (adaptive scaling + ensemble) reduces MPJPE by 1.2–4.9 mm and PA-MPJPE by 0.4–2.9 mm across four representative architectures (MLP, CNN, GCN, Transformer). The largest gains appear on SemGCN (MPJPE 64.8→59.9, PA-MPJPE 51.2→48.3) and BaselineNet (MPJPE 55.4→53.6, PA-MPJPE 44.2→42.7). In cross-dataset evaluation on 3DPW, the method improves PCK by 1.2–6.2 points, AUC by 0.5–7.3 points, and MPJPE by 2.8–15.6 mm over the unmodified baselines, with the most dramatic improvement on SemGCN (MPJPE 104.3→88.7).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method is applied as a plug-in to four representative 3D HPE architectures and consistently outperforms each unmodified baseline on both MPJPE and PA-MPJPE on H36M. The largest relative improvement is on SemGCN (GCN), where MPJPE drops from 64.8 to 59.9 mm (−7.6%) and PA-MPJPE from 51.2 to 48.3 mm (−5.7%), suggesting the loss-landscape smoothing is most beneficial for architectures that struggle with the complex landscape. On the cross-dataset 3DPW benchmark, SemGCN again shows the biggest gain (MPJPE 104.3→88.7 mm, PCK 78.2→84.4), while BaselineNet and PoseFormer show moderate but consistent improvements. Compared to Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM), the adaptive scaling mechanism achieves lower MPJPE (54.2 vs. 56.1 for BaselineNet; 54.9 vs. 57.3 for VPose) and PA-MPJPE (43.1 vs. 44.4; 43.4 vs. 45.7), indicating that SAM's global flattening is biased and less effective for the multi-modal 3D HPE landscape. Ensembling without the adaptive scaling mechanism degrades performance severely (e.g., BaselineNet MPJPE jumps to 73.3), confirming that the smoothing is a prerequisite for the ensemble to function.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the flat-minima/generalization connection established in broader deep learning (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber 1994; SAM by Foret et al. 2020; mode connectivity by Garipov et al. 2018) and applies it to 3D HPE for the first time, arguing that prior 3D HPE methods (multi-hypothesis estimation, elastic architectures, data augmentation) have overlooked the geometric structure of the loss landscape. The adaptive scaling mechanism is a novel architectural trick that introduces input-dependent redundancy to flatten local loss landscapes, and the multi-head ensemble is positioned as a parameter-efficient alternative to full deep ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al. 2017) or snapshot ensembles (Huang et al. 2017), specifically designed to exploit the smooth region created by the scaling mechanism. The paper explicitly departs from SAM-style explicit flattening, showing it is biased and ineffective for this task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The loss landscape analysis reveals that 3D HPE suffers from disconnected local minima driven by depth ambiguity, a structural issue that prior methods addressed only indirectly. The finding that SAM actively degrades performance while a simple architectural redundancy trick improves it across four architectures suggests that task-specific loss geometry should guide optimization strategy choices. Open questions include whether the approach extends to video-based 3D HPE, whether the DAR-based partitioning generalizes to other one-to-many regression tasks, and whether the modest 1–2 MPJPE gains persist at larger model scales or with more aggressive ensembling. The method's architecture-agnosticism and comparable training cost make it a practical plug-in, but the reliance on ground-truth 2D poses during cross-dataset evaluation limits the real-world applicability of the reported gains.
