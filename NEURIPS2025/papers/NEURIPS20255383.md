## Problem

While prior DPO improvements have focused on modifying the learning objective, the data-centric aspect of preference learning remains largely unexplored: what characteristics of preference data most contribute to model alignment? The paper identifies that noisy reward signals in preference pairs cause parameter shrinkage during DPO training, degrading model performance, yet no principled criterion existed for selecting informative samples while filtering toxic ones.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255383/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a full preference dataset of (prompt, chosen, rejected) triples, an external reward model (Skywork-Reward-Llama-3.1-8B-v0.2), and a small in-distribution language model (Llama-3.2-3B) that is briefly DPO-tuned on a 2,000-sample seed to serve as the implicit reward source. It outputs a filtered high-margin subset (typically 10% of the original, e.g., 6,000 samples from UltraFeedback's 61,135) used for DPO training, producing an aligned model that outperforms full-dataset training. The selection threshold is set to guarantee exclusion of samples with negative aggregated margins.

## Method

The paper first proves theoretically that exogenous noise in reward labels causes parameter shrinkage toward zero, and that selecting high-margin data induces compensatory parameter inflation. Driven by this, it proposes BeeS (Bayesian Aggregation for Preference data Selection), a three-step procedure: (1) in-distribution pre-DPO training of a small model on a random seed subset to obtain an implicit reward; (2) computing both external reward margins and implicit DPO reward margins for every preference pair; (3) aggregating K margin sources via a Bayesian product-of-probabilities formula that projects each margin into a unified [0,1] preference probability via a clipped linear mapping, so that any single low-margin source deprioritizes the pair. The key design insight is that external and implicit margins are weakly correlated (as shown by joint distribution visualizations), making their Bayesian combination more robust than either alone, while implicit margins from different model sizes are strongly correlated, enabling weak-to-strong transfer. The selected subset is then used for standard DPO training, and the framework extends naturally to iterative DPO by re-applying the three steps at each round of online data generation.

## Results

On AlpacaEval 2.0, BeeS-selected 10% subsets (6,000 samples) consistently outperform full-dataset DPO training by approximately 2–4 percentage points in length-controlled (LC) win rate across Llama-3-8B Base and Instruct models on both UltraFeedback and Llama-UltraFeedback. For example, on Llama-3-Instruct(8B) with Llama-UltraFeedback, BeeS achieves 36.36% LC / 36.47% WR versus 32.31% / 32.44% for full-set training. In the iterative DPO setting, BeeS with only 25% of online data reaches 48.49% LC win rate after three rounds, surpassing the full-data iterative DPO baseline (44.51% LC). Across TL;DR, HH, and UltraFeedback with 2,000 samples, BeeS achieves the best or near-best GPT-4 win rate on all three tasks (83.25%, 90.25%, 91.68% respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BeeS outperforms all single-source margin baselines (External Margin, Implicit Margin, IFD/Low-Gap) and Random selection across nearly all model–dataset combinations on AlpacaEval 2.0, with the largest gaps over Random (up to ~7% LC on Llama-3-Base with Llama-UltraFeedback) and over M-Ex (up to ~5.5% LC). Critically, BeeS also beats full-dataset DPO training by 2–4% LC win rate, demonstrating that 10% carefully selected data surpasses 100% unfiltered data. In the iterative DPO comparison against the standard online iterative DPO baseline (Xiong et al., ICML 2024), BeeS with 25% of online data achieves 48.49% LC / 54.99% WR after three rounds versus 44.51% / 53.12% for the full-data baseline, confirming high redundancy in on-policy data construction. Against DPO loss-function variants (SimPO, β-DPO, α-DPO), BeeS is unique in improving both raw and length-controlled win rates simultaneously, whereas those variants primarily boost LC win rate to a lesser degree. On the classic preference datasets (TL;DR, HH, UF) with 2,000 samples, BeeS achieves the top win rate on all three tasks, while individual margin strategies (especially M-Ex and IFD) can perform significantly worse than Random on at least one task, highlighting BeeS's robustness to noisy or toxic samples.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of DPO improvements that modify the objective function (IPO, KTO, SLiC, SimPO, β-DPO, α-DPO) by instead targeting the data selection dimension, which the authors identify as 'largely overlooked.' Theoretically, it builds on the parameter shrinkage analysis from Zhu et al. (2023) to formalize why noisy preference labels degrade learned parameters, then introduces a margin-maximization principle as the corrective. The Bayesian aggregation mechanism (Eq. 3) is adapted from multi-view probabilistic clustering (Liu et al., CVPR 2022; Deng et al., 2024), repurposed here to fuse heterogeneous reward margin sources into a unified preference probability. The iterative DPO extension builds directly on Xiong et al. (ICML 2024), applying the same selection logic to on-policy generated data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: using only 10% of UltraFeedback data yields 3–8% AlpacaEval 2.0 improvements across Llama, Mistral, and Qwen models, and 25% of on-policy data suffices for iterative DPO, revealing substantial redundancy in presumed high-quality data construction. The parameter shrinkage/inflation framework provides a principled, model-agnostic explanation for why data quality matters in preference learning, potentially guiding future work beyond DPO. Key limitations include evaluation only up to 14B parameters, absence of error bars or multiple seeds, and reliance on a linear approximation for the Bayesian aggregation that may not capture complex margin distributions. Open questions include whether the approach transfers to truly human-annotated preferences (vs. model-scored), whether it scales to 70B+ models, and whether the weak-to-strong implicit reward estimation (using a 3B proxy for an 8B target) remains valid at larger scale gaps.
