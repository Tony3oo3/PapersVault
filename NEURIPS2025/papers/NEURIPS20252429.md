## Problem

Neuron duplication in neural networks creates symmetry-induced saddle lines in the loss landscape, which are known to be benign non-convexities. However, the structure of the landscape *near* these saddle lines remained uncharacterized: gradient flow trajectories that escape the saddle line were not understood to converge to a distinct class of solutions. This paper identifies and characterizes 'channels to infinity'—extremely flat regions asymptotically parallel to saddle lines where gradient-based optimization converges to minima at infinity, a phenomenon previously unreported for regression settings.

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on single-hidden-layer (and multi-layer) MLPs with smooth activations (softplus, erf, sigmoid) trained via full-batch gradient flow on scalar regression targets (modified Rosenbrock functions and Matérn-32 Gaussian processes with varying kernel scales). Channel identification requires three simultaneous criteria: parameter norm exceeding 10³, cosine distance between a pair of input weight vectors below 10⁻³, and >90% of parameter updates confined to the channel subspace. The key outputs are the gated linear unit parameters (c, a, w, Δ) recovered in the ε→0 limit, the Hessian eigenvalue spectrum showing simultaneous flatness (min eigenvalue → 0) and sharpening (max eigenvalue ~ 1/ε²), and the geometric relationship between channels and their parent saddle lines.

## Method

Starting from Fukumizu & Amari's neuron-duplication theorem, the authors construct saddle lines and then perturb along the most negative Hessian eigendirection to discover channels to infinity, where two neurons' output weights diverge to ±∞ while their input weights converge. The central technical insight is a reparameterization of the neuron pair via central finite difference: aᵢσ(wᵢ·x) + aⱼσ(wⱼ·x) = (c/2)[σ((w+εΔ)·x)+σ((w−εΔ)·x)] + (a/2ε)[σ((w+εΔ)·x)−σ((w−εΔ)·x)], which in the ε→0 limit yields the gated linear unit cσ(w·x) + a(Δ·x)σ'(w·x). A Taylor expansion proves the loss scales as L(θ₀) + ε²h(θ₀) + O(ε⁴), explaining the asymptotic flatness (slope ~ ε³ along the channel) and the coexistence of extreme flatness with increasing sharpness in orthogonal directions. Empirically, gradient flow, SGD, and ADAM all reach these channels with high probability from standard initializations, but finite-step-size optimizers stall at the 'edge of stability' where the maximum Hessian eigenvalue hits 2/η, making the channels appear as flat local minima with finite parameter values.

## Results

Across 4,420 trained networks spanning multiple architectures (1–3 hidden layers, 2–16 neurons, input dimensions 2–16) and datasets (modified Rosenbrock, four Gaussian Process kernels with s ∈ {0.1, 0.5, 2, 10}), channels to infinity are reached with substantial probability from standard Glorot initializations, with frequency positively correlated with target function roughness (GP kernel scale s). The loss along a channel scales as ε² (where ε = ‖wᵢ − wⱼ‖/2), the gradient slope decays as ε³, and the maximum Hessian eigenvalue diverges as 1/ε², confirming the coexistence of extreme flatness along the channel and increasing sharpness in orthogonal directions. In the analytically solvable toy example (2-neuron erf network, 4 parameters), the population loss has stable fixed points at ε = 0 (i.e., at infinity), with h(w*) > 0 at both local minima, proving the existence of minima at infinity. SGD and ADAM get stuck at the edge of stability (λ_max = 2/η) early in the channel, with ε_min ~ √η.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing optimization methods or architectures; it is a characterization study of a previously unidentified loss-landscape structure. The closest comparison is between finite-norm local minima and channel minima: their training losses are statistically indistinguishable (Figure 4d), suggesting channels do not produce worse solutions, but channels exhibit larger maximum Hessian eigenvalues (sharper) and smaller minimum eigenvalues (flatter) than finite-norm minima as training progresses. Relative to the prior theory of Fukumizu & Amari [1], the paper shows that while symmetry-induced saddle lines are benign (confirmed empirically), the channels running asymptotically parallel to them represent a distinct and previously uncharacterized structure that gradient flow reaches with high probability. The paper also contrasts its minima at infinity with the well-known separable-classification minima at infinity [9], noting that channels involve converging input weights at finite values rather than diverging all parameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neuron-duplication framework of Fukumizu & Amari (2000) and Fukumizu et al. (2019), which established that duplicating a neuron creates a line of critical points (saddle line) in the loss landscape. While prior work characterized the stability of these saddle lines (plateau saddles vs. strict saddles) and identified minima at infinity for separable classification problems, this paper discovers a new structural element: channels to infinity that are asymptotically parallel to saddle lines but distinct from them, along which output weights diverge while input weights converge. The functional interpretation via central finite differences connecting to gated linear units, and the connection to the edge of stability phenomenon (Cohen et al., 2021), are novel syntheses not present in prior loss-landscape literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The discovery that gradient-based training reaches these channels with substantial probability (10–30%+) across diverse architectures and datasets, and that they are easily misidentified as flat local minima, has direct implications for how practitioners interpret converged models and for the design of regularization and stopping criteria. The functional role as gated linear units suggests that the network's effective capacity is larger than its nominal architecture implies, with potential consequences for generalization and model compression. Key open questions include whether channels to infinity persist in large-scale transformer or convolutional architectures, how multi-neuron channels (involving higher-order derivatives) behave, and whether the edge-of-stability trapping within channels can be exploited or avoided in practice. The analysis is currently limited to small networks (≤16 neurons) and smooth activations, leaving the ReLU case and large-scale settings as important future directions.
