## Problem

Adaptive learning requires both understanding students' evolving knowledge states and generating tailored learning resources, yet existing Knowledge Tracing (KT) methods reduce this to binary correctness prediction without interpretable descriptions, and Exercise Recommendation (ER) methods match students to static, one-size-fits-all exercise pools while ignoring knowledge states. This paper introduces the unified task of Knowledge-aware Exercise Generative Recommendation (KEGR), which dynamically perceives knowledge states from exercise responses and customizably generates new exercises, bridging the gap between diagnosis and personalized recommendation in a closed loop.

## Input / Output

The system consumes a student's sequential exercise history (each record containing the exercise text, associated knowledge concept, and binary correctness label), a domain-specific knowledge graph providing prerequisite and mapping relations among concepts, and configurable parameters specifying exercise type (single-choice, multi-choice, or judgment only—subjective types are excluded) and quantity (default 10). It produces a natural-language knowledge state description identifying mastered and weak concepts with reasoning, a set of newly generated exercises each with a stem, associated concept ID, and standard answer, and a multi-dimensional quality evaluation report. All processing is prompt-based via GPT-4o with no model training or fine-tuning.

## Method

ExeGen coordinates four specialized LLM agents—Recommendation Manager (orchestrator), Knowledge Perceiver, Exercise Generator, and Quality Evaluation Expert—through an adaptive loop pipeline of monitoring → perception → generation → refinement. Two key mechanisms differentiate the approach: (1) a human-simulated knowledge perception mechanism that mimics the cognitive sequence of knowledge retrieval → analysis → solving, using RAG over a domain knowledge graph to extract structured knowledge triples, zero-shot CoT to generate per-exercise explanations, and a dual-matching strategy (exact concept matching plus BERT-based semantic similarity) to retrieve relevant demonstrations for ICL-based knowledge state inference; and (2) an exercise generative-adversarial mechanism inspired by GANs, where a group of quality experts (linguistic fluency, knowledge concept coverage, correctness/reasonableness) iteratively evaluate and provide structured feedback to the Exercise Generator for up to 10 refinement rounds, enforcing multi-dimensional pedagogical constraints before final delivery.

## Results

On the MOOCCubeX dataset, ExeGen consistently outperforms all four LLM-based baselines across all three exercise types (single-choice, multiple-choice, judgment) and all eight evaluation metrics. For multiple-choice exercises, ExeGen achieves the highest scores: Knowledge Relevance 4.82, Answer Accuracy 4.79, Difficulty Appropriateness 4.80, and Error Hit Rate 0.79, compared to the best baseline ICL+CoT (KR 4.25, AA 4.39, DA 4.28, HR 0.21). The Error Recall metric shows the largest gap, with ExeGen reaching 0.82 versus 0.40 for ICL+CoT on multiple-choice exercises, indicating substantially better coverage of students' weak knowledge concepts. Zero-shot prompting performs worst across all settings, while ICL and CoT each provide moderate improvements, and their combination (ICL+CoT) yields the strongest baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ExeGen wins decisively against all four baselines on every metric and every exercise type. The strongest baseline, ICL+CoT, trails ExeGen by 0.57 in Knowledge Relevance (4.25 vs. 4.82) and by a factor of nearly 4× in Error Hit Rate (0.21 vs. 0.79) on multiple-choice exercises, revealing that prompt-level techniques alone cannot match the multi-agent pipeline's ability to target weak concepts. Zero-shot prompting (Liu et al., 2023) is consistently the weakest, scoring below 3.8 on most GPT-based metrics and below 0.12 on HR, confirming that raw LLM generation without structured context is poorly aligned with individual knowledge states. ICL (Dong et al., 2022) and CoT (Wei et al., 2022) each improve over zero-shot by roughly 0.3–0.5 on GPT-based metrics, but neither alone closes the gap to ExeGen; their combination (ICL+CoT) is the best single-prompt strategy yet still lags ExeGen by 0.4–0.6 on GPT-based scores and by 0.35–0.42 on Recall. The ablation further confirms that the multi-agent coordination (particularly the Knowledge Perceiver and the generative-adversarial refinement loop) is responsible for the majority of the gain over the best single-prompt baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by unifying two previously isolated tasks—Knowledge Tracing (KT) and Exercise Recommendation (ER)—into a single closed-loop problem (KEGR) where interpretable knowledge-state descriptions directly drive *generative* (not retrieval-based) exercise creation. Methodologically, it repurposes RAG and ICL from the NLP literature into a human-simulated cognitive pipeline for knowledge perception, and transplants the GAN generator-discriminator dynamic into a multi-agent LLM setting for iterative exercise refinement. The multi-agent orchestration draws on recent LLM agent frameworks (e.g., AgentCF, LLM-ESR) but applies them to a domain-specific educational workflow with a domain knowledge graph as grounding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By producing interpretable, natural-language knowledge-state descriptions and generating entirely new exercises (rather than selecting from a static pool), ExeGen addresses a practical gap in adaptive learning systems where black-box KT and one-size-fits-all recommendation fail to form a coherent feedback loop. Key open questions remain: the domain KG has only 0.06% prerequisite-relation coverage, LLM reasoning degrades on long interaction histories, subjective exercise types are excluded, and human evaluation is limited to 20 students and 3 teachers—so generalizability beyond the MOOCCubeX computer-science domain is untested. The $0.18/student API cost and modular agent design suggest practical deployability, but large-scale A/B testing in real classrooms is still needed.
