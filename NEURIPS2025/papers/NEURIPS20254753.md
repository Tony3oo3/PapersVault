## Problem

Existing adversarial attacks that elicit LLM hallucinations produce unrealistic prompts: token-level methods like GCG insert gibberish tokens, while LLM-agent and manual-prompting methods alter the original question's meaning. No prior method generates attacks that are simultaneously semantically equivalent to the original prompt and semantically coherent, leaving a gap in understanding how hallucinations arise from realistic, meaning-preserving prompt variations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254753/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an original multiple-choice question prompt (filtered from MMLU to 347 samples where all target LLMs initially answer correctly), a target incorrect answer token (the most likely wrong choice among A/B/C/D), and three LLMs: a target LLM to attack, a proposer LLM (GPT-4.1-Nano) for generating rephrasings, and a feasibility checker LLM (GPT-4.1-Mini) for verifying semantic equivalence. It outputs a semantically equivalent and coherent adversarial prompt, evaluated by whether the target LLM produces both the incorrect token and a hallucinated explanation classified as factuality or faithfulness by a separate hallucination evaluator (GPT-4.1).

## Method

SECA formulates hallucination elicitation as a constrained optimization problem: maximize the log probability of generating a target incorrect token, subject to semantic equivalence (verified by a binary feasibility checker LLM using five criteria including mutual entailment and same ground-truth answer) and semantic coherence (ensured by restricting candidates to outputs of an instructed proposer LLM). Rather than classical projection-based constraint enforcement, SECA uses a constraint-preserving zeroth-order loop: at each iteration, the proposer LLM generates M semantically equivalent rephrasings per candidate (with randomized verb/style/template instructions to avoid semantic collapse), candidates are ranked by adversarial strength (log P of target token), filtered by the feasibility checker, and the top-N most adversarial feasible prompts are retained for the next round. This iteratively narrows the combinatorial prompt space to a small feasible set while monotonically increasing the objective, converging in roughly 30 iterations without requiring gradients or exhaustive search.

## Results

On a filtered MMLU subset (347 samples, 16 subjects), SECA achieves ASR@30 of 80.29% on Llama-3-3B, 81.24% on Llama-3-8B, and 36.86% on Qwen-2.5-7B, substantially outperforming both Raw prompts (48.20%, 63.52%, 10.19%) and GCG (6.26%, 9.86%, 0.57%). Crucially, SECA maintains near-zero semantic equivalence error (SEE = 0.00) and semantic coherence error (SCE ≤ 1.06), while GCG produces severely incoherent prompts (SCE = 307–1255) and near-total semantic equivalence violations (SEE ≈ 0.96–0.98). Across all 7 evaluated LLMs (including GPT-4o-Mini, GPT-4.1-Nano, Llama2-13B, Qwen-2.5-14B), SECA raises ASR@30 by at least 20 percentage points over raw prompts on most subjects.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SECA decisively outperforms both baselines on all three target LLMs in Table 2. Against Raw prompts (the original MMLU questions used directly), SECA improves ASR@30 by 32.09 points on Llama-3-3B (80.29 vs. 48.20), 17.72 points on Llama-3-8B (81.24 vs. 63.52), and 26.67 points on Qwen-2.5-7B (36.86 vs. 10.19), while maintaining comparable or better semantic coherence (SCE ≤ 1.06 vs. 1.08). Against GCG, the gap is even more dramatic: SECA achieves 12.8× higher ASR@30 on Llama-3-3B (80.29 vs. 6.26), 8.2× on Llama-3-8B (81.24 vs. 9.86), and 64.7× on Qwen-2.5-7B (36.86 vs. 0.57). The semantic quality gap is stark—GCG produces prompts with SCE of 307–1255 (severely incoherent gibberish) and SEE of 0.96–0.98 (nearly all prompts violate semantic equivalence), whereas SECA achieves SCE ≤ 1.06 and SEE = 0.00, matching the original prompts' coherence. The paper attributes GCG's poor performance to its initialization with a gibberish suffix that drastically lowers the objective, from which subsequent optimization steps cannot recover.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws a direct analogy from computer vision adversarial attacks (where perturbations must be imperceptible to humans) to formulate hallucination elicitation as a constrained optimization problem requiring both semantic equivalence and coherence. It departs from prior hallucination-elicitation methods—GCG (token-level optimization producing gibberish), LLM-agent-based approaches (Investigator Agent, Adaptive Evaluation), beam search (BEAST), and manual prompting (ICD, Answer Assemble Ace)—by arguing that all of these violate at least one of the two constraints, yielding either incoherent or meaning-shifted prompts. The method itself is a zeroth-order search that repurposes LLMs as both candidate proposers and constraint enforcers, a design choice motivated by the intractability of classical constrained optimization in discrete, black-box prompt spaces.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that even frontier commercial LLMs (GPT-4o-Mini, GPT-4.1-Nano) are vulnerable to realistic, semantically equivalent rephrasings, raising practical robustness concerns for high-stakes deployments. The finding that more verbose and lexically diverse prompts are more susceptible to hallucination offers a concrete, actionable insight for both red-teaming and defense. Key limitations include evaluation restricted to a single dataset (MMLU) in an MCQA setting, reliance on LLM-based semantic equivalence checking (validated but not perfect), and the use of a small filtered subset (347 samples). Open questions include extension to free-form generation, reasoning models, and untargeted hallucination discovery.
