## Problem

Benchmark prediction methods aim to estimate full-benchmark performance from only a small subset of evaluation points, but it remains unclear whether these methods generalize beyond the interpolation regime where source and target models are drawn from the same distribution. This paper systematically evaluates 11 benchmark prediction methods across 19 diverse benchmarks to determine whether careful core-set selection is necessary and whether these methods remain reliable when target models outperform all source models — the typical scenario at the evaluation frontier.

## Input / Output

For each benchmark, the method receives full per-data-point performance scores (binary correct/incorrect) for M source models across all N data points (N ranges from 277 to 50,000), and per-data-point scores for each target model on only n data points (n ∈ {10, 20, 50, 100, 200}). The output is a single scalar estimate of each target model's mean accuracy over the full benchmark. The estimation gap is measured as the mean absolute difference between the true full-benchmark accuracy and the predicted value, averaged over all target models across 100 random trials.

## Method

The paper introduces six new benchmark prediction methods and evaluates them alongside five existing ones under both interpolation (random source-target split) and extrapolation (bottom 50% as sources, top 30% as targets) settings. The key new contribution is applying Augmented Inverse Propensity Weighting (AIPW) to benchmark prediction: for each target model f, a Ridge regression g is trained to predict point-wise performance s(f,z) from the vector of source model performances s(F^(s),z) on the core set, and the final estimate debiases the core-set mean using the predicted scores on the complement. The central empirical finding is that a simple baseline — random sampling plus Ridge regression (RANDOM-SAMPLING-LEARN) — outperforms most sophisticated core-set selection methods in interpolation, while AIPW is the only method that consistently beats random sampling in extrapolation, owing to its consistency as an estimator. The paper further quantifies the dependence on model similarity via a chance-corrected agreement metric, showing a strong negative correlation between similarity and estimation gap for all learning-based methods.

## Results

Under the interpolation setting (source and target models identically distributed), RANDOM-SEARCH-LEARN achieves the lowest average estimation gap of 2.7% (n=50), a 42.1% reduction over the RANDOM-SAMPLING baseline (4.8%), while the simpler RANDOM-SAMPLING-LEARN achieves 2.9% (37.2% reduction). Under the extrapolation setting (target models are the top-30% performers, sources are the bottom-50%), nearly all learning-based methods collapse: RANDOM-SEARCH-LEARN's gap inflates to 12.8% (185% worse than RANDOM-SAMPLING's 4.6%). The only method that consistently beats RANDOM-SAMPLING in extrapolation is AIPW, achieving 4.0% (a 12.6% reduction), though its advantage shrinks from 30.4% in interpolation to 12.6% in extrapolation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the simple RANDOM-SAMPLING baseline, most learning-based methods (RANDOM-SAMPLING-LEARN, RANDOM-SEARCH-LEARN, LASSO, PCA, ANCHOR-POINTS-PREDICTOR) achieve 20–42% lower estimation gaps in interpolation but suffer 100–260% *increases* in extrapolation, making them worse than just averaging a random sample. AIPW is the sole exception, beating RANDOM-SAMPLING in 18/19 benchmarks under extrapolation (4.0% vs 4.6%) and 19/19 under interpolation (3.3% vs 4.8%). Against the previous state-of-the-art GP-IRT (Polo et al., 2024), RANDOM-SEARCH-LEARN and RANDOM-SAMPLING-LEARN achieve lower gaps in interpolation (2.7–2.9% vs 3.3%), but in extrapolation GP-IRT (4.7%) and P-IRT (5.1%) substantially outperform the regression-based methods (12.8–13.6%), though AIPW (4.0%) still edges them out. Against ANCHOR-POINTS-WEIGHTED (Vivek et al., 2023), the clustering-based approach is competitive in extrapolation (5.6%) but is outperformed by AIPW (4.0%) and RANDOM-SAMPLING itself (4.6%). The paper's central finding is that core-set selection strategies (clustering, optimization) provide no meaningful advantage over random sampling; the key driver of performance is the regression model, and even that fails when target models are more capable than any source model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a systematic empirical audit of the benchmark prediction literature, which has been dominated by core-set selection methods (Anchor Points k-medoids clustering, IRT-based predictors from tinybenchmarks). It departs from this paradigm by showing that the *manner* of subset selection is largely irrelevant: a random sample plus Ridge regression (RANDOM-SAMPLING-LEARN) matches or beats methods that invest in clustering, optimization, or IRT modeling. The one genuinely new method, AIPW, repurposes the augmented inverse propensity weighting estimator from causal inference (Robins & Rotnitzky, 1995) and prediction-powered inference (Angelopoulos et al., 2023) into the benchmark prediction setting, bringing the property of consistency that other learning-based methods lack. The paper also draws on the model similarity literature (Mania et al., 2019; Goel et al., 2025) to formalize *why* methods fail: they interpolate among similar models and extrapolate poorly.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical finding is that benchmark prediction methods fail precisely at the evaluation frontier — the setting where new, better models are being assessed — with estimation gaps increasing by up to 185% over random sampling. This cautions the field against over-relying on efficient evaluation techniques for the most important use case. Even the best method (AIPW) yields only modest gains (−12.6% in extrapolation vs. −30.4% in interpolation), and random sampling with twice the data outperforms all methods. Open questions include whether fundamentally different approaches (e.g., active evaluation that adapts to the target model's behavior) can break the similarity dependence, and whether the modest AIPW advantage justifies its per-model regression training cost at scale.
