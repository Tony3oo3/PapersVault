## Problem

Knowledge distillation enables unauthorized cloning of proprietary teacher models, yet no method exists to detect whether an open-weight student was distilled from a specific teacher when only the student's weights and the teacher's API are available. Prior work on membership inference, OOD detection, and data auditing either requires teacher weights, assumes architectural similarity, or targets training-data provenance rather than distillation provenance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253999/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an open-weight student model (full parameter access) and API access to K candidate teacher models, with no access to training data, teacher weights, or the distillation algorithm. For classification, the student and teachers output probability vectors over C classes; for text-to-image, they output generated images from a shared prompt (the empty string, exploiting classifier-free guidance). The output is a predicted teacher index via argmax over aggregated alignment scores, with the multiple-choice formulation eliminating the need for an absolute detection threshold.

## Method

The framework is a three-stage, model-agnostic pipeline: (1) input construction via a data-free generator G_φ trained with a mixup-based objective and batch-normalization-statistics alignment loss to produce class-conditional synthetic images where the student is confident (or empty-string prompts for diffusion models); (2) score computation using either point-wise measures (KL divergence on logits for classification, LPIPS on generated images for text-to-image) or set-level measures (Aligned Cosine Similarity on projected logits, or CKA with RBF kernels on CLIP image embeddings); (3) prediction by selecting the teacher with the highest aggregated score across N synthetic inputs. The key design choice is training the generator to maximize student confidence on synthetic inputs, which amplifies the score gap between the true distillation teacher and unrelated candidates, enabling detection even with as few as 5 inputs.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, the proposed KL-based method achieves 81.0% accuracy and 0.89 AUC on average across input sizes, while the ACS variant reaches 83.0% accuracy and 0.75 AUC. On ImageNet, the KL variant attains 65.0% accuracy and 0.86 AUC, and the ACS variant reaches 67.0% accuracy and 0.74 AUC. For text-to-image generation, the LPIPS-based point-wise score achieves 96.0% accuracy and 1.00 AUC on average, reaching perfect detection (100% accuracy) from N=10 inputs onward. These results represent improvements of 59.6%, 71.2%, and 20.0% over the strongest baselines on CIFAR-10, ImageNet, and text-to-image respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all baselines across every dataset and input size. On CIFAR-10, the strongest baselines (MIA Filter+KL at 51% Acc / 0.75 AUC and MIA Filter+CKA at 52% Acc / 0.74 AUC) are surpassed by the KL variant (81% Acc / 0.89 AUC) and ACS variant (83% Acc / 0.75 AUC), with the method even exceeding the Oracle that uses real training data (70% Acc / 0.84 AUC). On ImageNet, the gap is even more pronounced: baselines plateau around 33–36% accuracy and 0.47–0.76 AUC, while the KL variant reaches 65% accuracy and 0.86 AUC. For text-to-image generation, the best captioning-based baselines (GPT-2+DINO at 80% Acc / 0.92 AUC, Blip-Base+DINO at 79% Acc / 0.92 AUC) are clearly outperformed by the LPIPS point-wise score (96% Acc / 1.00 AUC), which achieves perfect detection from N=10 inputs. The MMD-FUSE and CKA set-level baselines perform poorly on ImageNet (≈33% Acc, ≈0.50 AUC), indicating that kernel-based two-sample tests lack discriminative power in this setting. The ACS baseline (Hamilton et al.) shows unstable performance on text-to-image (57% Acc on average with high variance), while the DINO and CLIP embedding baselines saturate near 0.97–1.00 similarity across all candidates, making teacher identification infeasible.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new task—knowledge distillation detection—by repurposing data-free input synthesis techniques (mixup-based generators from Qimera and Dreaming to Distill) and kernel-based similarity measures (CKA, ACS, MMD-FUSE) that were previously used for model compression, quantization, or representation comparison. It explicitly distinguishes itself from membership inference, data auditing, and memorization detection, which either require teacher weights, assume architectural similarity, or target training-data membership rather than distillation provenance. The multiple-choice formulation (selecting among K candidate teachers) is a deliberate departure from binary detection, avoiding threshold calibration and aligning with real-world provenance questions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

With open-weight models proliferating and high-profile distillation controversies (e.g., DeepSeek/OpenAI), the ability to audit whether a released model was distilled from a proprietary teacher has direct IP and accountability implications. The method's data-free, model-agnostic design makes it practical in settings where training data and teacher weights are unavailable. Key limitations include degraded performance when distillation strength is low (λ < 0.5) and when student-teacher architectures differ substantially; extending the framework to LLMs and free-form diffusion inputs remains open.
