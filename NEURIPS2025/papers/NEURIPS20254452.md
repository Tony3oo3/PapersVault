## Problem

Large Language Models fail at combinatorial problems like Sudoku and SAT, achieving 0% board accuracy even with reasoning-focused variants, because they cannot backtrack from incorrect inferences. Prior neuro-symbolic solvers rely on custom architectures, and the closest imitation-learning baseline (Shah et al., 2024) is restricted to puzzles solvable by a fixed set of human-crafted strategies without any guessing or backtracking, capping accuracy well below 100%. This paper asks how to teach a vanilla decoder-only Transformer to solve NP problems through informed trial-and-error with backtracking, and then to minimize the number of guesses needed.

## Input / Output

The input is a partially filled 9×9 Sudoku grid (or a 1-in-3 SAT instance) encoded as a sequence of 3-digit action tokens (row, column, value), preceded by a 'start' token. The model generates a transcript of rule applications, guess tokens, and backtracking steps, terminated by an 'end' token; the final solution is extracted by taking the last assigned value for each cell. The action-level tokenization compresses each move into a single token (vs. three in prior work), reducing sequence length by a factor of 3. The framework generalizes to any NP problem by swapping the verifier and transcript generator.

## Method

The method trains a 42M-parameter GPT-2 causal Transformer on DFS-generated transcripts that alternate between applying four basic Sudoku rules (lone/hidden singles) and making informed guesses with backtracking on dead ends, mirroring the DPLL algorithm. Two key design choices accelerate learning: (i) action-level tokenization encoding each (row, col, value) move as a single 3-digit token, and (ii) a multi-target loss that sums cross-entropies over all valid next actions rather than a single target. Beyond imitation learning, the paper formulates the guessing step under depth-1 non-adaptive policies as a contextual Min-Sum Set Cover problem and derives a novel loss L₁ = (Σ pᵢ)⁻¹ that directly minimizes the expected number of trials (a geometric-distribution objective), outperforming the standard multi-target cross-entropy L₂. Training uses only successful trajectories, concentrating learning on high-value backdoor guesses rather than encouraging continuation of incorrect paths.

## Results

The proposed Causal Transformer with trial-and-error DFS achieves 98.90% board accuracy on both the Random and Kaggle unfiltered test sets, 99.50% on Kaggle filtered, and 99.40% on RRN. The depth-1 guessing variant (beyond imitation learning) further improves to 99.8% accuracy on Random puzzles, with a median of only 1.5 guesses needed to find the correct backdoor. Rule-logic accuracy (correctly filling all cells resolvable by basic Sudoku rules) reaches near-perfect levels, and the multi-target loss with action-level tokenization accelerates learning substantially compared to the single-target baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all four baselines across every test set. Against the closest prior LLM-style approach, the Causal Transformer of Shah et al. (2024), the gains are largest: +31.7 pp on Kaggle unfiltered (98.90% vs. 67.16%), +12.3 pp on Kaggle filtered (99.50% vs. 87.18%), and +4.6 pp on RRN (99.40% vs. 94.80%). Against the Masked Diffusion Model of Kim et al. (2025), the improvement is +23.9 pp on Kaggle unfiltered (98.90% vs. 74.97%) and +10.0 pp on Kaggle filtered (99.50% vs. 89.49%). Against the domain-specific RRN (Palm et al., 2018) and Recurrent Transformer (Yang et al., 2023) retrained on the same Random generator, the method still leads by 6.3–6.5 pp on Random and Kaggle unfiltered (98.90% vs. 92.40–92.60%), with smaller but consistent margins on the filtered and RRN sets (0.2–1.5 pp). Notably, the Causal Transformer baseline is restricted to puzzles solvable by seven human-crafted strategies (only 1.9M of Kaggle's 3M puzzles), whereas the proposed method generalizes across the full distribution without such restrictions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Shah et al. (NeurIPS 2024), who trained a GPT-2 variant to solve Sudoku by imitating seven human-crafted strategies, by replacing those complex heuristics with only the four basic Sudoku rules and adding a DFS exploration loop with informed guessing and backtracking (analogous to DPLL). It departs from prior neuro-symbolic solvers (RRN, Recurrent Transformer) that rely on custom architectures by using a vanilla decoder-only Transformer with action-level tokenization and a multi-target soft-label loss. The 'beyond imitation learning' phase introduces a novel connection to the Min-Sum Set Cover problem, yielding a geometric-distribution-based loss (L1) that directly optimizes expected solution length, which has no direct precedent in the Sudoku-solving literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework demonstrates that a 42M-parameter vanilla GPT-2 can solve NP-complete problems (Sudoku, 1-in-3 SAT) at 99% accuracy using only basic rules plus trial-and-error, without external tools or custom architectures—offering a general recipe for teaching LLMs combinatorial reasoning. The structural finding that ~99.8% of random Sudoku instances are solvable with at most one backdoor guess is of independent interest and suggests that the search space for many NP instances is far shallower than worst-case analysis implies. Key limitations include the restriction to depth-1 non-adaptive guessing policies; extending the Min-Sum Set Cover optimization to multi-level adaptive search (where the model decides when to backtrack without a dead-end signal) remains an open and non-trivial challenge.
