## Problem

Conventional RAG systems perform a single retrieval step before generation, which limits their ability to handle complex multi-hop queries where the necessary information is spread across multiple documents and the initial query may not retrieve all relevant passages. The paper addresses this by asking whether an LLM can be explicitly trained to iteratively retrieve, reason over intermediate results, and dynamically reformulate its query based on the evolving state of the reasoning process.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252003/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a natural-language query and a large retrieval corpus (English Wikipedia, ~36M passages) as input. At inference, it produces a structured retrieval chain—a sequence of sub-queries and sub-answers generated autoregressively—followed by a final answer. The chain length L and the number of sampled chains N are tunable at test time, allowing practitioners to trade off latency (total token consumption) against answer quality along a log-linear Pareto frontier.

## Method

CoRAG augments existing QA-only datasets with intermediate retrieval chains via rejection sampling: an LLM iteratively generates sub-queries conditioned on prior sub-answers, retrieves top-k documents for each sub-query, and produces sub-answers, with the highest-likelihood chain selected for training. A Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct model is then fine-tuned with a unified multi-task objective that simultaneously predicts the next sub-query, the sub-answer given retrieved documents, and the final answer given the full chain. At test time, three decoding strategies—greedy, best-of-N sampling (using a 'No relevant information found' penalty score for chain selection), and breadth-first tree search—control the test-time compute budget. The key mechanism is that the model learns to decompose complex queries into simpler sub-queries and to reformulate when a retrieval step yields no useful information, effectively performing trial-and-error exploration over the retrieval space.

## Results

On multi-hop QA, CoRAG-8B (L=10, best-of-8) achieves 72.5 EM on 2WikiMultihopQA, 56.3 EM on HotpotQA, 54.4 EM on Bamboogle, and 30.9 EM on MuSiQue, surpassing the strongest baseline Search-o1-32B by 14.5, 11.1, and 14.3 EM points respectively (Bamboogle is the sole exception where Search-o1-32B leads at 56.0 vs. 54.4). On the KILT benchmark, CoRAG-8B sets new state-of-the-art scores on 8 of 9 tasks, with the largest gain on HoPo (60.6 vs. previous best 50.6) and WnCw (76.7 vs. 71.2), while marginally trailing Atlas-11B on FEVER (93.1 vs. 93.5). The Pareto frontier between EM score and token consumption follows an approximate log-linear relationship up to 128k tokens, with gains diminishing as chain length L increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CoRAG-8B substantially outperforms all baselines on multi-hop QA despite using an 8B model, compared to Search-o1-32B (32B) and RA-DIT 65B. On MuSiQue, the most challenging dataset, CoRAG achieves 30.9 EM versus 16.6 for Search-o1-32B and 26.1 for ITER-RETGEN, a 14.3-point margin over the strongest competitor. On 2WikiMultihopQA, CoRAG leads Search-o1-32B by 14.5 EM points (72.5 vs. 58.0) and the fine-tuned Llama-8B baseline by 17.4 points. The sole exception is Bamboogle, where Search-o1-32B (56.0 EM) edges out CoRAG (54.4 EM), likely due to Bamboogle's reliance on knowledge more recent than the Wikipedia corpus and its small 125-instance test set. On the KILT benchmark, CoRAG-8B surpasses the previous best on 8 of 9 tasks, with particularly large gains on HoPo (+10.0 over Atlas-11B) and WnCw (+5.5 over previous best), while trailing Atlas-11B by only 0.4 on FEVER. The paper also shows that CoRAG's advantage over the fine-tuned Llama-8B baseline (without chain-of-retrieval) is consistent across all four multi-hop datasets, confirming the contribution of the iterative retrieval mechanism itself.

## Relation to Prior Work

CoRAG sits at the intersection of two research threads: iterative retrieval-augmented generation (FLARE, ITER-RETGEN, IRCoT, Self-RAG) and test-time compute scaling (Chain-of-Thought, Tree-of-Thought, STaR, OpenAI o1). Prior iterative RAG methods rely on few-shot prompting or distillation from proprietary models to elicit multi-step retrieval behavior, while test-time scaling work has focused on pure reasoning without retrieval. CoRAG departs from both by explicitly fine-tuning open-source LLMs on rejection-sampled retrieval chains, treating the retrieval process as a learnable sequence of sub-queries and sub-answers rather than a prompting artifact. The rejection sampling data augmentation strategy is borrowed from STaR and Llama 3 post-training, but applied here to the retrieval chain generation problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CoRAG demonstrates that an 8B model with explicit chain-of-retrieval training can match or exceed much larger systems (32B Search-o1, 65B RA-DIT) on knowledge-intensive tasks, offering a practical and efficient recipe for grounded RAG deployment. The characterized log-linear scaling relationship between token budget and performance gives practitioners a principled way to allocate test-time compute, and the finding that gains are concentrated on multi-hop tasks (marginal on single-hop NQ/TriviaQA) points toward adaptive compute allocation as a natural next step. Key limitations include evaluation restricted to short-answer tasks (long-form generation remains open), dependence on a fixed Wikipedia corpus, and the observation that iterative self-improvement via re-sampling yields mixed results, suggesting diminishing returns from the current data augmentation loop.
