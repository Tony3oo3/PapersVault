## Problem

Existing ML-based methods for Rectilinear Steiner Minimum Tree (RSMT) and its obstacle-avoiding variant (OARSMT) suffer from excessive search-space exploration, reliance on heuristic post-processing, or inability to handle multi-degree GPU parallelization. Critically, no prior ML-based approach can avoid obstacles at inference time without being explicitly trained on obstacle configurations, limiting practical deployment in VLSI routing where obstacle layouts vary per net.

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of n pin coordinates (x, y) as input during training, and at inference additionally accepts m rectangular obstacles (each defined by 4 corner vertices). Inputs are padded to a fixed tensor of shape (batch × (n̂ + 4m̂) × 2) with -1 for missing elements, enabling batched GPU processing of variable-degree instances. The output is a rectilinear edge sequence (RES): an ordered list of n−1 non-commutative edge pairs (v_i, h_i) specifying vertical-then-horizontal paths that form a spanning tree, from which total Manhattan wirelength is computed in linear time.

## Method

OAREST is an actor-critic RL framework built on pointer networks that sequentially generates a RES, with the key theoretical contribution being a proof that an optimal RES over pins plus a subset of obstacle corner vertices always yields an optimal OARSMT (Theorem 2.5). The core innovation is a four-component dynamic masking strategy (input, obstacle, visited, activation) that simultaneously enables multi-degree GPU parallelization and obstacle avoidance purely at inference: the activation mask starts by blocking all obstacle vertices, then selectively unblocks corner vertices of obstacles that a newly generated edge overlaps with, guiding the agent to route around them. Training uses REINFORCE policy gradient for the actor and MSE for the critic, operating exclusively on pin-only instances with no obstacle data, yet the model generalizes to obstacle-avoiding inference by treating dynamically activated obstacle corners as additional connection targets.

## Results

On the RSMT task (Table 4), OAREST achieves 0.00% average wirelength error on R5, 0.06% on R20, and 0.36% on R50, matching or slightly beating REST (T=8) across R5–R40 and tying at R50. On the OARSMT task, OAREST achieves >98% and >96% zero-overlap success rates for 5 and 10 obstacles respectively, without any obstacle training. On the ICCAD19 real-world benchmark, OAREST attains the second-best wirelength (behind only the exact GeoSteiner) while running at roughly half the time of REST (T=8) and 1/7 the time of GeoSteiner.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the exact solver GeoSteiner [16], OAREST incurs a small wirelength gap (0.00–0.36% on R5–R50) but runs orders of magnitude faster, with near-linear scaling versus GeoSteiner's exponential growth. R-MST [17] is dramatically outperformed, with OAREST achieving ~0.06% error on R20 versus R-MST's ~12.4%. BGA [18] is consistently beaten by OAREST across all degrees (e.g., 0.15% vs. 0.60% on R30). FLUTE (A=18) [19] is the closest traditional competitor; OAREST edges it out on R5–R40 (e.g., 0.10% vs. 0.18% on R25) but FLUTE (A=18) slightly wins on R45–R50 (0.30% vs. 0.31% and 0.29% vs. 0.36%). REST (T=8) [5], the direct predecessor, is marginally outperformed by OAREST on R5–R45 (e.g., 0.19% vs. 0.21% on R35) and tied on R50, while OAREST additionally supports multi-degree GPU parallelization that REST lacks. For OARSMT, OAREST beats the heuristic OARST [20] in wirelength and the exact ObSteiner [21] in runtime by a large margin, while achieving >98% zero-overlap success without any obstacle training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RES (Rectilinear Edge Sequence) representation and actor-critic RL framework introduced in REST [5] from vanilla RSMT to the obstacle-avoiding setting (OARSMT), a nontrivial extension that requires proving RES optimality under obstacles (Theorem 2.5). It departs from prior ML-based OARSMT methods (Chen et al. [7,9], Lin et al. [8]) that rely on large action spaces, heuristic post-processing (maze routing, OASG+SPF), or Steiner-point prediction, by maintaining an end-to-end RES-based formulation. The dynamic masking strategy is the key architectural departure, enabling multi-degree GPU parallelization (unavailable in REST/EPST) and inference-time obstacle handling without any obstacle-specific training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ability to avoid obstacles at inference time without training on them is practically significant for VLSI design, where obstacle configurations vary per layout and retraining is infeasible. The focus on multi-degree parallelization for small instances aligns with real-world ICCAD19 statistics showing >95% of nets have <10 pins, making GPU-parallel inference the dominant use case. Limitations include increasing error beyond 50 pins (training ceiling), residual overlaps in extreme dense-obstacle scenarios (1-4% failure rate), and evaluation primarily on synthetic random obstacles rather than complex industrial layouts. Open questions include whether training on obstacles further improves wirelength, and whether the approach extends to multi-layer routing with overflow constraints.
