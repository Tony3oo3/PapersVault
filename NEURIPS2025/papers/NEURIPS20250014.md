## Problem

Existing motion-controllable video generation methods suffer from coarse control granularity and limited scalability: sparse signals (boxes, masks) cannot capture local motion, while dense signals (optical flow, pixel-level point tracks) either require auxiliary estimation models or lack surrounding spatial context. Moreover, all prior approaches introduce extra motion encoders or fusion modules (e.g., ControlNet), which degrade the motion signal and complicate scalable fine-tuning of the video-generation backbone.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250014/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt, a first-frame RGB image, and a set of sparse or dense 2D point trajectories (1 to 1024 tracks, each a sequence of (x,y) pixel coordinates over 1+T frames) as input, and produces a 5-second, 832×480 motion-controlled video. During training, trajectories are extracted from a dense 32×32 grid via CoTracker; at inference, users can specify any number of tracks. The model retains its original I2V capability when no trajectories are provided (5% dropout during training).

## Method

Wan-Move injects motion guidance by directly editing the first-frame condition features in latent space, eliminating all auxiliary motion encoders and fusion modules. Pixel-space point trajectories are deterministically mapped to latent coordinates via spatial division by the VAE compression ratios (Eq. 2), and the first-frame latent feature at each trajectory origin is replicated to its corresponding position in subsequent zero-padded frames (Eq. 3), leveraging the translation equivariance of VAE features. This updated condition feature is concatenated with noisy latents along the channel dimension and fed into the unmodified Wan-I2V-14B DiT backbone, which is fine-tuned with a flow-matching objective on 2M curated 720p videos. The key advantage over prior work (e.g., Motion Prompting's random track embeddings or ControlNet-based fusion) is that the replicated features carry rich local texture and semantic context, yielding finer motion control with only a 3-second inference overhead versus 225 seconds for ControlNet.

## Results

On MoveBench, Wan-Move achieves FID 12.2, FVD 83.5, PSNR 17.8, SSIM 0.64, and EPE 2.6, outperforming all baselines on every metric. On the public DAVIS benchmark, it similarly leads with FID 14.7, FVD 94.3, PSNR 16.5, SSIM 0.61, and EPE 2.5. In a 2AFC human study, Wan-Move wins over 96% of comparisons against Tora and LeviTor, and reaches competitive parity with the commercial Kling 1.5 Pro Motion Brush (53.4% win rate on motion quality).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Wan-Move decisively outperforms ImageConductor on all metrics, with EPE dropping from 15.6 to 2.6 on MoveBench, reflecting ImageConductor's weakness in pixel-level trajectory injection. Against the closest academic competitors LeviTor (EPE 3.4), Tora (EPE 3.3), and MagicMotion (EPE 3.2), Wan-Move achieves a lower EPE of 2.6 while also improving FID by 5–10 points and PSNR by 2–3 dB, demonstrating that latent feature replication captures richer local context than ControlNet-based or adaLN-based fusion. In the multi-object setting (Table 2), Wan-Move further widens the gap, achieving FVD 226.3 vs. 350.0 (Tora) and 764.5 (ImageConductor). Against the commercial Kling 1.5 Pro Motion Brush, Wan-Move is roughly competitive in the 2AFC human study (47.8% / 53.4% / 50.2% win rates on motion accuracy / quality / visual quality), marking the first research model to match a commercial product's motion control quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

Wan-Move builds directly on the Wan-I2V-14B image-to-video diffusion backbone and departs from the dominant paradigm of motion-controllable video generation, which typically introduces auxiliary motion encoders (ControlNet, adaLN, or dedicated trajectory embedding modules) to inject motion signals. The closest prior work, Motion Prompting, also uses point trajectories but encodes them as random pixel-space embeddings and fuses them via a separate ControlNet branch; Wan-Move instead maps trajectories into latent coordinates and replicates first-frame latent features along those trajectories, eliminating all extra modules. The paper also introduces MoveBench, a new benchmark that addresses the limited scale, short duration, and sparse annotations of existing evaluation sets like DAVIS, VIPSeg, and MagicBench.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core practical contribution is a drop-in motion-control mechanism that requires no architectural modification to the I2V backbone, making it trivially scalable to larger models and longer sequences without the engineering overhead of training and maintaining separate motion encoders. MoveBench provides the field with a more rigorous and diverse evaluation standard (1018 clips, 54 categories, 5-second duration, hybrid human+SAM annotations). Open questions include how the approach handles prolonged occlusions, whether it generalizes to higher resolutions and longer durations beyond 5s/480p, and whether the latent-replication trick transfers to other I2V architectures with different VAE compression ratios.
