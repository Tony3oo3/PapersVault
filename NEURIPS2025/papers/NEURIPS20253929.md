## Problem

While general MLLMs like GPT-4o-mini achieve competitive MSA performance, their large parameter counts and high inference costs do not always yield significant gains over smaller task-specific models, raising cost-effectiveness concerns. The paper asks whether the generalized knowledge of MLLMs can be transferred to task-specific MSA models during training, enabling them to achieve state-of-the-art performance without relying on MLLMs at inference time.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253929/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes preprocessed multimodal sequences: language tokens (via BERT), facial action units (via OpenFace), and audio features (via Librosa), with sequence lengths of 50, 50–55, and 375–500 frames respectively. MLLM prompts are generated once before training from three uniformly sampled video frames and the text transcript, then embedded via BERT plus a two-layer Transformer encoder to match the feature dimension. The output is a single continuous sentiment score (regression), evaluated via MAE, correlation, and derived binary classification metrics (Acc-2, F1).

## Method

MMSLF is a two-stage teacher-student framework. In the teacher stage, GPT-4o-mini generates context-aware prompts highlighting key sentiment cues per modality; these prompts are embedded and injected as a conditional query into a modified cross-attention module, where the standard attention map (Q from target modality, K/V from source modality) is element-wise multiplied with a prompt-derived shifted attention map before softmax, producing a conditional attention map that guides visual-to-language and audio-to-language alignment. The teacher is trained with a simple L1 regression loss. In the student stage, the same architecture is trained without MLLM prompts, supervised by three losses: ground-truth regression, attention transfer (L1 distance between student's attention maps and teacher's conditional attention maps), and fused representation matching (L1 distance between student and teacher fusion tokens). This allows the student to internalize the MLLM-guided alignment patterns and perform inference independently with only 0.82M parameters.

## Results

On SIMS, MMSLF-Teacher achieves F1 of 84.06±0.43 and MAE of 0.370±0.50, while MMSLF-Student (0.82M parameters) reaches F1 of 81.85±1.41 and MAE of 0.382±1.39. On MOSI, the Teacher attains F1 of 85.15/86.69 and MAE of 0.734, and the Student achieves F1 of 83.68/85.50 and MAE of 0.746. On MOSEI, the Teacher reaches F1 of 85.55/86.71 and MAE of 0.539, while the Student achieves F1 of 84.20/84.74 and MAE of 0.548. The Student model outperforms all task-specific baselines (ALMT, MulT, MISA, TETFN, etc.) on most metrics across all three datasets while using only 0.82M parameters and 6.39s inference time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MMSLF-Student (0.82M params) outperforms all task-specific baselines on SIMS: it beats ALMT by +1.68 F1 (81.85 vs. 80.17) and −0.039 MAE (0.382 vs. 0.421), and surpasses TETFN by +2.51 F1 and MulT by +2.19 F1. On MOSI, the Student exceeds ALMT by +0.68 F1 (83.68 vs. 83.00) and Self-MM by +0.73 F1, while on MOSEI it beats ALMT by +0.10 F1 (84.20 vs. 84.10) and TETFN by +0.14 F1. Against MLLMs, MMSLF-Teacher outperforms GPT-4o-mini on SIMS F1 (84.06 vs. 82.51) and MAE (0.370 vs. 0.453), and beats Video-LLaMA2 and GPT-4V on all metrics across all three datasets. However, Gemini-2.0-Flash remains the strongest overall (e.g., 85.12 Acc-2, 0.381 MAE on SIMS; 87.76/89.49 Acc-2 on MOSI), exceeding both Teacher and Student. The concurrent work DeepMLF achieves the best MAE on SIMS (0.362) and MOSEI (0.510) and best Corr on MOSEI (0.800), but MMSLF-Teacher surpasses it on F1 (84.06 vs. 83.09 on SIMS) and Acc-2 (83.06 vs. 82.89). The Student's key advantage is efficiency: it matches or exceeds task-specific baselines with only 0.82M parameters and 6.39s inference, versus >22 min for Gemini-2.0-Flash and >27 min for GPT-4o-mini.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the MulT (Tsai et al., 2019) cross-modal attention architecture by introducing a conditional attention mechanism that injects MLLM-generated prompts as an additional query signal, and wraps the whole system in a teacher-student knowledge distillation framework (building on Hinton et al. and Zagoruyko et al.). It departs from the concurrent line of work that directly applies MLLMs (e.g., GPT-4V, Video-LLaMA2) to MSA by instead using the MLLM only during training to produce context-aware prompts, so that a small task-specific student model performs inference independently. The approach also sits alongside DeepMLF, which embeds a task-specific LLM with learnable tokens for fusion, but MMSLF keeps the MLLM entirely external to the trainable network.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a 0.82M-parameter student can approach or match the performance of >7B-parameter MLLMs on MSA while running in ~6 seconds versus >22 minutes, offering a concrete efficiency-performance trade-off for deployment. However, the gains over strong task-specific baselines (ALMT, DeepMLF) are modest and not uniformly significant (p-values for Acc-2 and F1 exceed 0.05), and the teacher suffers severe overfitting on small datasets like SIMS and MOSI. Open questions include whether replacing traditional feature extractors (OpenFace, Librosa) with more powerful pretrained encoders—as partially explored in Appendix B.3—can unlock substantially larger gains, and whether the framework generalizes beyond the three relatively small benchmarks used here.
