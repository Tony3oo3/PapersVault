## Problem

Existing worldwide image geolocalization pipelines retrieve candidate locations and then select the best match using naive cosine similarity between independently encoded query and candidate embeddings. This point-wise, decoupled scoring fails to model spatial relationships among candidates—such as relative distances and Tobler's First Law of Geography—causing the model to confuse visually similar but geographically distant scenes, even when the correct candidate is present in the top-k retrieval results.

## Input / Output

At inference, GeoRanker takes a single query image and a pool of candidates formed by union of retrieved entries (12–14, each carrying GPS coordinates, city/country text, and a reference image) and LVLM-generated entries (3–5, GPS-only). Each query–candidate pair is formatted into a structured prompt that also appends 5 negative examples (textual GPS + descriptions only, to limit GPU memory). The model outputs a scalar distance score per candidate; the GPS coordinates of the highest-scoring candidate become the final prediction.

## Method

GeoRanker feeds the structured query–candidate prompt into a Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct backbone (fine-tuned via LoRA on q/k/v projections, rank 16) and reads the final position token's hidden state through a bias-free linear value head to produce a scalar geographic-distance score. The core training signal is a multi-order distance objective built on the partial Plackett-Luce distribution: a first-order term ranks the absolute geodesic distances from the query to each of the top-k₁ candidates, while a second-order term ranks the pairwise *gaps* between those distances, forcing the model to learn not just which candidate is closest but how much closer it is relative to others. The two terms are combined as a weighted sum (λ = 0.7) and optimized with AdamW over 100 K triplets (2 M query–candidate pairs) for one epoch on 4×L40S GPUs. At inference the model scores all retrieved and generated candidates in parallel and returns the GPS of the argmax, achieving +12.9 % street-level accuracy over G3 on IM2GPS3K and +37.3 % on YFCC4K.

## Results

GeoRanker achieves state-of-the-art accuracy across all geographic thresholds on both benchmarks. On IM2GPS3K, it reaches 18.79% at the 1 km (street-level) threshold, 45.05% at 25 km, and 89.29% at 2500 km, improving over the previous best (G3) by +12.9%, +10.0%, and +4.7% respectively. On YFCC4K, the gains are even larger at fine granularity: 32.94% at 1 km (+37.3% relative over G3's 23.99%) and 43.54% at 25 km (+21.3%). The method also outperforms all baselines at coarser scales (200 km, 750 km, 2500 km) on both datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GeoRanker outperforms all 10 baselines across every threshold on both IM2GPS3K and YFCC4K. The closest competitor is G3 (NeurIPS'24), the prior SOTA that also uses a two-stage retrieve-then-select pipeline with LVLM-based candidate generation; GeoRanker beats it by +12.9% at 1 km on IM2GPS3K and a striking +37.3% at 1 km on YFCC4K, with consistent 5–21% gains at coarser scales. Among RAG-based methods, Img2Loc (SIGIR'24) is the second-strongest baseline (15.34% / 19.78% at 1 km), but GeoRanker surpasses it by 3.45 and 13.16 points respectively. Classification-based methods (PlaNet, CPlaNet, ISNs, Translocator, GeoDecoder, PIGEON) and retrieval-based methods ([L]kNN, GeoCLIP) trail substantially, with the largest gaps at the 1 km threshold (e.g., GeoCLIP at 14.11% vs. GeoRanker's 18.79% on IM2GPS3K). The paper also includes a direct ranking comparison (Table 3) where GeoRanker's learned distance scores beat both a prompting-based LVLM selector (16.62% vs. 18.79% at 1 km) and simple top-1 embedding similarity (13.31%), confirming that the multi-order distance loss provides meaningful re-ranking benefit over heuristic selection.

## Relation to Prior Work

GeoRanker extends the RAG-based geolocalization pipeline established by G3 (NeurIPS 2024) and Img2Loc (SIGIR 2024), which retrieve candidates and then select the best match via LVLM prompting or cosine similarity. The key departure is replacing this selection step with a dedicated distance-aware ranking model that explicitly models query-candidate spatial interactions through a shared LVLM (Qwen2-VL with LoRA) and optimizes a novel multi-order Plackett-Luce loss over both absolute and relative geographic distances. This borrows the listwise ranking formulation from the learning-to-rank literature (Plackett-Luce, pairwise/listwise objectives) and adapts it to the spatial domain, where the 'relevance' signal is geodesic distance rather than binary relevance. The retrieval backbone (CLIP encoders, GeoCLIP GPS encoder, InfoNCE alignment) is inherited directly from G3, making GeoRanker a drop-in replacement for the final selection stage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete and well-motivated bottleneck: retrieval already surfaces good candidates (Figure 1), but naive similarity-based selection fails to exploit them, particularly for visually similar yet geographically distant scenes. The multi-order distance loss and the GeoRanking dataset (100K samples, 2M query-candidate pairs) provide both a principled training signal and the first dedicated resource for geographic ranking, which could benefit GeoAI, information retrieval, and LVLM research beyond geolocalization. Open questions include whether the ranking stage can be adaptively skipped when retrieval is already confident (the authors suggest this as a practical optimization), how the approach scales to much larger candidate pools, and whether the gains persist when the retrieval stage itself is weaker or the database is less comprehensive.
