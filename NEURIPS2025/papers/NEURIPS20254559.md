## Problem

Inequality proving demands a blend of tight-bound discovery, strategic theorem application, and precise symbolic manipulation, making it a demanding frontier for LLMs. Existing benchmarks are either too small, synthetic, or locked into formal proof assistants (Lean, Isabelle), leaving LLMs' informal reasoning abilities unmeasured. This paper asks: can current LLMs construct rigorous, step-by-step informal proofs of Olympiad-level inequalities, and where exactly do their deductive chains break down?

## Input / Output

Each problem is presented as a natural-language + LaTeX statement with explicit domain constraints (e.g., a,b,c > 0, abc = 1), decomposed into either a bound-estimation subtask (find the extremal constant C) or a relation-prediction subtask (select from ≤, ≥, =, <, >, or 'None of the above'). The model produces a free-form step-by-step informal proof ending in a boxed final answer. Evaluation is not a single string match: a five-judge LLM-as-judge pipeline verifies answer equivalence (handling notational variants like 1/√2 vs √2/2) and independently checks every reasoning step for four specific flaw types, with a solution counted correct only if it passes all five judges.

## Method

The paper introduces an informal-yet-verifiable task formulation that recasts inequality proving into two automatically checkable subtasks (bound estimation and relation prediction), avoiding the overhead of formal proof assistants while preserving the core reasoning challenge. On top of this, INEQMATH provides 200 IMO-medalist-authored test problems, 100 development problems, and 1,252 training problems (each with up to four step-wise solutions and annotations linking to 83 named theorems across 29 categories). The evaluation framework is the key technical novelty: a modular LLM-as-judge system comprising a final-answer judge (two-stage extraction + equivalence checking) and four specialized step-wise judges—Toy Case (unjustified generalization from specific instances), Logical Gap (skipped or unsupported transitions), Numerical Approximation (improper decimal substitution in symbolic derivations), and Numerical Computation (arithmetic errors verified via SymPy/Python code generation). Each judge achieves F1 > 0.9 against human labels, and the system is validated on a manually annotated development set. The paper then systematically evaluates 29 LLMs (chat and reasoning models, open-source and proprietary) in zero-shot, revealing that overall accuracy drops by up to 65.5% relative to final-answer accuracy, with even o1 achieving only 8.0% overall accuracy, and that scaling model size or test-time tokens yields diminishing returns while theorem-guided hints and critic-based self-refinement show modest but consistent gains.

## Results

On the INEQMATH benchmark of 200 Olympiad-level inequality problems, the best-performing model o3 achieves only 21.0% Overall Accuracy (requiring both a correct final answer and step-wise sound reasoning), while its Answer Accuracy is 18.7%. The average Overall Accuracy across all 29 evaluated LLMs is just 5.0%, compared to an average Answer Accuracy of 42.9%—a dramatic gap exposing fragile deductive chains. The most common step-wise errors are logical gaps (85.0% average failure rate) and unjustified toy-case generalization (59.7%), followed by numerical approximation errors (26.9%) and computation errors (6.8%). Even top reasoning models like o1 (8.0% Overall Acc) and o3-mini (9.5% Overall Acc) fall far below the Answer Accuracy they achieve (55.2% and 62.5% respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 29 LLMs spanning open-source chat models (Qwen2.5, Llama-4), proprietary chat models (GPT-4o, Gemini 2.0, Grok 3), and specialized reasoning LLMs (o1, o3, o4-mini, DeepSeek-R1, QwQ). Reasoning LLMs consistently outperform chat models on Answer Accuracy—o3-mini (62.5%), Grok 3 mini (71.5%), and DeepSeek-R1 (63.5%) all exceed 60%, while chat models like GPT-4o (38.5%) and GPT-4.1 (31.3%) trail significantly. However, this advantage collapses under step-wise scrutiny: Grok 3 mini's Overall Accuracy drops by 65.5 percentage points (71.5% → 6.0%), and o3-mini drops by 53.0 points (62.5% → 9.5%). o3 achieves the best Overall Accuracy at 21.0%, but even this is far below its 18.7% Answer Accuracy, indicating that no current model reliably constructs rigorous proofs. The most prevalent failure mode across all models is logical gaps (73.1% average step accuracy), followed by toy-case overgeneralization (40.3%) and numerical approximation errors (15.0%), while computation errors are relatively rare (73.1% step accuracy). Scaling model size improves Answer Accuracy but yields diminishing returns on Overall Accuracy, and extending test-time computation (e.g., o1 from 5K to 40K tokens) produces no measurable gain in Overall Accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant formal theorem-proving paradigm (Lean/Isabelle-based benchmarks like MiniF2F, ProofNet, INT, AIPS) by reformulating inequality proving into two informal, automatically verifiable subtasks—bound estimation and relation prediction—closer to how humans reason about inequalities. It also extends the LLM-as-judge evaluation literature (e.g., EIC-Math, Omni-MATH) by introducing four specialized step-wise judges targeting specific reasoning flaws (toy-case overgeneralization, logical gaps, numerical approximation, computation errors) rather than a single holistic correctness check. The dataset curation strategy (IMO-medalist-authored test problems, textbook-sourced training problems with theorem annotations) bridges the gap between the small curated collections (ChenNEQ) and the large but template-generated synthetic datasets (INT, AIPS).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a fundamental and quantified gap in current LLMs: even the strongest reasoning models (o1, o3) achieve under 21% overall proof correctness despite 50–70% final-answer accuracy, revealing that finding an answer and constructing a rigorous derivation are largely decoupled skills. The finding that neither model scaling nor extended test-time computation meaningfully closes this gap challenges the prevailing assumption that more compute will fix reasoning fragility, and points toward structural interventions like theorem-guided retrieval and self-critique as more promising directions. Open questions include whether the strict all-judges-must-pass metric over-penalizes valid but non-standard proofs, whether the informal formulation captures the full difficulty of inequality proving, and whether the identified failure modes (logical gaps at 85% average error rate) can be addressed through targeted training data or architectural changes.
