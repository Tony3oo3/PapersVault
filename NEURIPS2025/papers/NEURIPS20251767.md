## Problem

LLMs exhibit notably limited proficiency in graph-structured reasoning, with even state-of-the-art models like OpenAI o1 achieving only ~58% on basic graph connectivity tests. Prior approaches—pretraining graph foundation models or supervised fine-tuning on curated datasets—are bottlenecked by the scarcity of large-scale, universally represented graph data and the cost of human annotation. This paper asks whether reinforcement learning on synthetic graph-theoretic tasks with verifiable rewards can elicit latent graph reasoning abilities already present in pretrained LLMs.

## Input / Output

The model receives a graph encoded as a unified edge list (e.g., (1,2),(2,3),...) concatenated with a natural-language task question, where graphs are subgraphs of 5–35 nodes sampled via random walk from real-world networks in the Network Repository. The model produces a chain-of-thought reasoning trace followed by a final answer in \boxed{} format; answer types span boolean, integer, float, node list, edge list, and node mapping across 50 graph-theoretic tasks ranging from simple attribute queries to NP-hard problems like Hamiltonian path and maximal independent set.

## Method

G1 applies Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to train LLMs on 100k synthetic graph-theoretic problems from the newly constructed Erdős dataset, using rule-based outcome rewards that combine strict value matching, Jaccard-index set matching, and algorithmic verification for multi-solution problems. An optional supervised fine-tuning warmup (either direct QA fitting or CoT-SFT via rejection sampling from Qwen2.5-32B) provides a cold start for challenging tasks where base-model accuracy is near zero. The RL phase runs for only 300 steps on 8×A800 GPUs with a KL penalty to the reference policy and an entropy bonus for exploration. The key design choice is that all reward signals are computed by deterministic NetworkX-based verifiers rather than LLM judges, enabling scalable, bias-free training without human annotation. The resulting G1-7B model achieves 66.16% average accuracy on Erdős (vs. 32.06% for the base model), outperforming GPT-4o-mini and rivaling o3-mini, while generalizing zero-shot to unseen graph encodings, real-world node classification/link prediction, and general reasoning benchmarks without degradation.

## Results

On the Erdős benchmark (50 graph-theoretic tasks, 5k test examples), G1-7B achieves 66.16% average accuracy, surpassing GPT-4o-mini (47.60%) by 18.56% and reaching competitive performance with OpenAI o3-mini (64.90%). The smaller G1-3B model (59.76%) outperforms Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct (47.16%) and Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct (42.28%) despite having 20× fewer parameters. G1-7B also generalizes zero-shot to larger graphs (36–100 nodes), unseen benchmarks (GraphWiz: 57.11%, GraphArena: 41.10%), and real-world node classification/link prediction (Cora/PubMed: 87.29% avg), while even improving general reasoning (GSM8K: 87.49%, MATH: 71.80%, MMLU-Pro: 48.56%) over its base model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

G1-7B (66.16%) decisively outperforms all open-source baselines: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (32.06%, +34.10%), DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B (51.64%, +14.52%), and Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct (47.16%, +19.00%). Against proprietary models, G1-7B beats GPT-4o-mini (47.60%) by 18.56% and narrowly trails o3-mini (64.90%) by only 1.26% despite using a 7B model versus o3-mini's unknown (likely much larger) parameter count. The comparison with GraphWiz is particularly striking: GraphWiz-7B-RFT (7.70%) and GraphWiz-7B-DPO (10.59%)—models specifically trained on graph instruction data—collapse to near-random performance on Erdős, while G1-7B achieves 66.16%, demonstrating that RL on diverse synthetic tasks generalizes far better than instruction-tuning on a narrow graph dataset. G1-3B (59.76%) also surpasses the 72B Qwen2.5-Instruct by 12.60%, highlighting the parameter efficiency of the RL approach. On transfer benchmarks, G1-7B leads on GraphWiz (57.11% vs. 51.86% for DeepSeek-R1-Distill) and GraphArena (41.10% vs. 28.84% for Qwen2.5-7B base), confirming zero-shot generalization to unseen tasks and encodings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the RLVR (Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards) paradigm popularized by DeepSeek-R1 for math and coding to the domain of graph-theoretic reasoning, using GRPO with rule-based rewards on synthetic tasks. It departs from prior graph-focused LLM work (GraphWiz, GraphInstruct, InstructGraph) that relied on instruction tuning or preference tuning on curated datasets, and from graph foundation model pretraining efforts, by arguing that LLMs already possess latent graph reasoning that can be elicited through self-play RL without human-annotated supervision. The Erdős dataset itself extends prior graph benchmarks (NLGraph, GraphArena, GraCoRe) in scale (100k training examples, 50 tasks) and in sourcing graphs from real-world networks rather than purely random models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates a data-efficient, scalable path to strong graph reasoners that circumvents the scarcity of large-scale, universally represented graph data that has limited prior graph foundation model efforts. The finding that RL on synthetic graph tasks preserves and even improves general reasoning (GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-Pro) while SFT degrades it reinforces the 'SFT memorizes, RL generalizes' narrative in a new domain. Open questions include scaling to graphs with thousands of nodes (context window bottleneck), dynamic difficulty scheduling to address sample inefficiency on NP-hard tasks, and whether the approach transfers to other structured data modalities (tabular, time-series, molecular).
