## Problem

While Transformer research has extensively analyzed attention mechanisms, the final pooling step that aggregates token-level representations into a fixed-size vector for downstream prediction remains theoretically uncharacterized. No principled framework exists to explain how different pooling choices (average, sum, max, last-token) affect a model's ability to distinguish semantically similar from dissimilar inputs, despite empirical evidence showing pooling significantly impacts downstream accuracy.

## Input / Output

The method operates on sequences of n token embeddings of dimension d (bounded in [0,B]^{n×d}) produced by a frozen Transformer backbone, and outputs a single d-dimensional pooled vector consumed by a lightweight linear head. Across modalities, inputs are image patches (ViT/LipsFormer), tokenized text sequences (GPT-2, BERT, Mistral-7B, Llama-3, Qwen-2.5), or time-series segments (MOMENT), all padded or truncated to fixed lengths. The pooled representation is then mapped to task-specific outputs: class labels, pixel values for inpainting, per-pixel segmentation masks, cosine similarity scores, next-token logits, or forecast/imputed values.

## Method

The paper introduces an expressivity measure E_ε[f] that quantifies the probability two inputs within an ε-neighborhood are mapped to outputs differing by more than σ, then derives closed-form Lipschitz-based bounds γ for four standard pooling strategies. The key insight is that each pooling function introduces a distinct multiplicative scaling factor on the backbone's Lipschitz constant: Average pooling is contractive (1/√n), Sum pooling is expansive (√n), Last-token pooling is neutral (1), and Max pooling is adaptive (√min(n,d)), explaining why contractive pooling suits global-context tasks while expansive pooling captures fine-grained local distinctions. The bounds are shown to extend to alternative attention formulations (L2-MHA with tied Q/K matrices, and LipsFormer's scaled cosine similarity in a Swin windowed architecture) by substituting architecture-specific Lipschitz constants. Empirically, the theory is validated across 28+ datasets in vision, NLP, and time series using six pooling methods (four fixed plus learnable Attention and Weighted Average pooling), confirming that no single strategy dominates and that learnable Weighted Average pooling converges to weight distributions matching the theoretically optimal fixed strategy for each task.

## Results

Across computer vision tasks with ViT-small, Average pooling achieves the best inpainting MSE (0.239 on CelebA) and segmentation accuracy (71.88% on PascalVOC-Cls), while Last-token (CLS) pooling leads on classification (90.35% CIFAR-10, 77.45% CUB-200-2011). In NLP, Average/Sum pooling outperform Last-token on global-context tasks (e.g., STSB Spearman 0.713 vs. 0.587 for BERT), whereas Last-token dominates next-token prediction (84.57% Top-10 on TinyStories with GPT-2 vs. 38.83% for Avg). For time series, Attention pooling achieves the best classification accuracy (93.60% on FordA with MOMENT-small) and imputation MSE (0.076 on ETTh1), while Last-token pooling is best for forecasting (0.082 MSE on ETTh1). No single pooling strategy dominates across all tasks and modalities.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares six pooling strategies (Last-token/CLS, Average, Sum, Max, Weighted Average, and Attention pooling) as the primary comparison axis, rather than comparing against external methods. In computer vision, Average pooling consistently outperforms Sum and Max on global-context tasks (inpainting, segmentation) by 5–15% in MSE or accuracy, while CLS pooling leads on fine-grained classification (e.g., 77.45% vs. 71.48% on CUB-200-2011). In NLP, the gap is stark for next-token prediction: Last-token achieves 84.57% Top-10 on TinyStories with GPT-2 versus 38.83% for Average and 29.36% for Sum, confirming the theoretical expansive/contractive distinction. For global-context NLP tasks (STS, Banking77), Average and Sum pooling match or exceed Last-token by 10–25 points (e.g., 0.713 vs. 0.587 Spearman on STSB with BERT). In time series, Attention pooling dominates classification and imputation (93.60% FordA, 0.076 ETTh1 imputation MSE) but underperforms Last-token on forecasting (0.106 vs. 0.082 ETTh1 MSE). Weighted Average pooling emerges as the most robust non-learnable alternative, matching or approaching the best fixed strategy across nearly all tasks, while Max pooling is consistently the weakest performer across all three modalities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously disconnected research threads: (1) theoretical analyses of Transformer expressivity and Lipschitz continuity that focus on the attention backbone (e.g., L2-MHA, LipsFormer, LipschitzNorm) but stop before the pooling layer, and (2) empirical studies of pooling strategies in ViT, BERT, and LLM-based models that report task-dependent performance differences without a unifying theoretical explanation. By importing the expressivity notion from graph representation learning and applying it to the full pipeline including pooling, the authors provide the first closed-form bounds on how each pooling operation (Avg, Sum, Last-token, Max) scales the model's ability to distinguish similar from dissimilar inputs. The framework is shown to extend to alternative attention formulations (L2-kernel, scaled cosine similarity), positioning pooling as an architectural component on par with attention in theoretical treatments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers practitioners a principled, task-driven guideline for selecting pooling: contractive methods (Avg) for global-context tasks, expansive methods (Last-token, Sum) for local-detail tasks, and learnable methods (W-Avg, Attn) when sufficient data permits adaptive weighting. Key limitations include the restriction to frozen backbones (joint end-to-end adaptation of pooling and encoder remains unexplored), the fact that the derived bounds are necessary but not sufficient for optimal performance, and the single-layer theoretical analysis (though argued to extend multiplicatively to multi-layer models). Open questions include developing hybrid pooling that dynamically balances global smoothing with token-level sensitivity, deriving scaling laws for pooling performance as datasets grow, and tightening bounds to identify when specific strategies are provably optimal.
