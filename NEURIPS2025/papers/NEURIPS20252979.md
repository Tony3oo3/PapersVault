## Problem

Existing LLM-based scientific hypothesis generation produces coarse-grained hypotheses that lack the methodological specifics and experimental configurations needed for direct laboratory implementation. This paper formalizes fine-grained hypothesis discovery as a combinatorial optimization problem and investigates the upper limits of LLMs' capacity to navigate the resulting reward landscape, asking four foundational questions about how best to harness LLM heuristics, whether LLM-judged quality aligns with ground truth, and how ensemble composition affects the reward signal.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252979/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a research background (comprising a research question and a survey of established methodologies) plus a coarse-grained hypothesis direction—ranging from a brief sentence to a preliminary hypothesis with partial details—as input, and produces a fine-grained hypothesis enriched with precise reagents, concentrations, temperatures, characterization methods, and step-by-step experimental protocols. The coarse hypothesis is intentionally ambiguated (key details removed or abstracted) to prevent answer leakage while preserving the research direction. All processing is text-only, and the output is a single coherent hypothesis document rather than a ranked list.

## Method

The core contribution is Hierarchical Heuristic Search (HHS), which partitions the implicit edit space into p domain-specific hierarchy levels (e.g., reaction mechanism → general components → specific reagents/conditions) and searches each level sequentially, exploiting optimal substructure to reduce combinatorial complexity. At each level, an LLM proposes one edit at a time, and a pairwise-comparison module serves as the gradient signal; the search terminates at a local optimum after k consecutive non-improving steps, and three independent search traces are recombined via an LLM summarization step to interpolate a superior optimum. Theoretically, the hierarchy smooths the reward landscape by acting as a low-pass filter—attenuating high-frequency irregularities at finer levels—enabling convergence to stronger local optima than flat greedy search. The framework operates entirely in-context (no fine-tuning), uses GPT-4o-mini as both proposer and evaluator, and is evaluated on a post-January-2024 chemistry benchmark with expert-annotated fine-grained hypotheses to avoid data contamination.

## Results

On the TOMATO-Chem benchmark (51 post-2024 chemistry papers), HHS achieves 40.35% Soft Recall and 23.04% Hard Recall in recovering ground-truth methodological and experimental details, substantially outperforming the strongest baseline (Greedy Search + In-context RL + Self-consistency) at 31.53% / 17.73%. In LLM-based pairwise comparisons, HHS wins 73.53% of overall judgments against Greedy Search and 53.43% against Greedy Search + Self-consistency, with expert evaluations confirming a 76.47% win rate over Greedy Search. The method requires ~282 reasoning steps on average, compared to ~68 for the strongest baseline, reflecting the cost of hierarchical refinement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HHS (40.35% Soft / 23.04% Hard Recall) decisively outperforms all five external baselines. Over ChemCrow (Bran et al., 2024; 12.28% / 7.20%), HHS gains +28.07 / +15.84 points. Over Qi et al. (2024; 19.57% / 11.15%), the margin is +20.78 / +11.89. Over SciMON (Wang et al., 2024; 18.57% / 10.09%), HHS leads by +21.78 / +12.95. Over MOOSE (Yang et al., 2024; 20.04% / 11.76%) and MOOSE-Chem (Yang et al., 2025; 19.99% / 11.98%), the improvements are +20.31 / +11.28 and +20.36 / +11.06 respectively. Internally, HHS also beats its own flat-search ablation (Greedy + In-context RL + Self-consistency; 31.53% / 17.73%) by +8.82 / +5.31 points, confirming that the hierarchical decomposition—not merely additional search steps or self-consistency aggregation—is the primary driver of the gain.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the MOOSE-Chem line of work (Yang et al., ICLR 2025), which introduced the TOMATO-Chem dataset and a framework for rediscovering coarse-grained chemistry hypotheses, by formalizing the substantially harder task of generating fine-grained, experimentally actionable hypotheses. Methodologically, it draws on coarse-to-fine refinement strategies from computer vision (Tian et al., 2024), self-consistency aggregation (Wang et al., 2023), and the evolutionary-unit design from MOOSE-Chem, but reframes the entire process as a combinatorial optimization over an LLM-defined reward landscape with hierarchical decomposition. The key conceptual departure from prior hypothesis-generation work (SciMON, ChemCrow, MOOSE) is the explicit treatment of hypothesis refinement as a search problem with a smoothable reward landscape, rather than a single-shot generation task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic study of how to elicit fine-grained, lab-ready hypotheses from LLMs, releasing a post-2024 benchmark specifically designed to prevent data contamination—a practical resource for the scientific-discovery community. The finding that repeated instances of the strongest model outperform diverse ensembles of similar-capacity models (Q3) challenges the common assumption that model diversity improves ensemble quality, though the evidence is limited to three models in one domain. Significant open questions remain: the 60% divergence rate from ground-truth hypotheses and the ~50% coverage of experimentally critical details suggest that current LLMs still fall well short of expert-level hypothesis specification, and generalization beyond chemistry (requiring domain-specific hierarchy design) is untested.
