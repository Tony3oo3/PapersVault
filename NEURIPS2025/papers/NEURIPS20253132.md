## Problem

Hamiltonian flow for optimization (HF-opt), which simulates energy-conserving dynamics and periodically resets velocity to zero, achieves only non-accelerated convergence rates (matching gradient descent) for general strongly and weakly convex functions when using fixed short integration times. While Wang (2024) showed acceleration for strongly convex quadratics using Chebyshev-based integration times, no general accelerated method based on pure Hamiltonian flow (without damping) existed for broader function classes. This paper asks whether randomizing the integration time—mirroring the randomized Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (RHMC) strategy from sampling—can yield Nesterov-level accelerated rates for general convex objectives.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a differentiable objective f with known smoothness constant L and (optionally) strong convexity constant α, an initial point x_0, a step size h constrained by h ≤ 1/(4√L) (strongly convex) or h ≤ 1/(7√L) (weakly convex), and a refreshment rate γ_k that is either constant (γ_k = √α) or decaying (γ_k = 17/(2(k+9)h)). The output is an ε-accurate minimizer x_K satisfying E[f(x_K) − f(x*)] ≤ ε, with the guarantee holding in expectation over the Poisson-driven random refreshment events rather than deterministically. In practice, the smoothness constant L can be handled via adaptive line search, and the strong convexity constant α can be misspecified without destabilizing the algorithm.

## Method

The core idea is to replace the fixed integration time in HF-opt with a random draw from an exponential distribution, producing the randomized Hamiltonian flow (RHF). In continuous time, RHF is a piecewise deterministic process: between random jump times governed by an inhomogeneous Poisson process with rate γ(t), the state (X_t, Y_t) evolves under the energy-conserving Hamiltonian ODE; at each jump, the velocity Y_t is reset to zero. This randomization is the key mechanism that converts the non-accelerated O(1/t) or O(exp(−αt)) rates of fixed-step HF-opt into accelerated O(1/t²) and O(exp(−√α·t)) rates, matching the optimal accelerated gradient flow (AGF) up to constants. For discrete-time implementation, the paper discretizes RHF as RHGD: each step applies a backward-Euler integrator (approximated by a gradient descent step on the proximal subproblem) to evolve the Hamiltonian dynamics over time h, then probabilistically refreshes the velocity to zero with probability min(γ_k·h, 1). The convergence proof uses a Lyapunov function that tracks both the objective gap and the kinetic energy, showing E_{k+1} ≤ E_k with the accelerated rate, while bounding the error introduced by approximating the proximal step with a single gradient evaluation.

## Results

Theoretically, RHGD achieves Õ(√κ log(1/ε)) iterations for α-strongly convex L-smooth functions and O(√(L/ε)) iterations for weakly convex L-smooth functions, matching the optimal accelerated rates of Nesterov's AGD. In continuous time, RHF achieves O(exp(−√(α/5)·t)) for strong convexity and O(1/t²) for weak convexity, matching accelerated gradient flow (AGF) up to constants. Numerically, on quadratic minimization (d=100, L=500, κ=10⁷), RHGD with a misspecified α̂=0.01 (vs. true α=5×10⁻⁵) maintains stable convergence while AGD and CAGD degrade significantly; in the weakly convex (α=0) setting, RHGD achieves the fastest convergence among all tested algorithms.

|---|---|---|

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against AGD (Nesterov 1983), RHGD matches the optimal O(√κ log(1/ε)) iteration complexity in theory. Empirically on quadratic minimization with exact α, AGD converges slightly faster in early iterations, but when α is overestimated (α̂=0.01 vs. true α=5×10⁻⁵ at κ=10⁷), AGD degrades substantially while RHGD remains stable and outperforms AGD in later stages. In the weakly convex quadratic setting (α=0), RHGD consistently outperforms AGD throughout all iterations. Against CAGD (Even et al. 2021), the pattern is similar: CAGD is competitive with exact parameters but suffers under misspecification, while RHGD is more robust. On logistic regression with adaptive stepsizes, RHGD (with γ_k=2√α) is faster than GD in late iterations and comparable to both AGD and CAGD, with all accelerated methods clearly outperforming GD. Overall, RHGD trades a small constant-factor slowdown under ideal parameter knowledge for significantly improved robustness to hyperparameter misspecification and a clear advantage in the weakly convex regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper translates the randomized Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (RHMC) sampling algorithm of Bou-Rabee and Sanz-Serna (2017) into an optimization setting, building on the Hamiltonian flow for optimization (HF-opt) framework of Teel et al. (2019) and extending Wang's (2024) Chebyshev-based acceleration from strongly convex quadratics to general strongly and weakly convex functions. The key conceptual departure from prior Hamiltonian-based optimization methods is the use of pure energy conservation (no damping) combined with randomized integration time, rather than the damped accelerated gradient flow (AGF) that underlies most existing Hamiltonian optimization approaches. The paper also introduces the Lift-Conserve-Project (LCP) scheme as a unifying principle connecting HF-opt and HMC, and contrasts its approach with the continuized AGD (CAGD) of Even et al. (2021), which uses randomization over a linear ODE rather than Hamiltonian dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous proof that randomized Hamiltonian flow achieves accelerated convergence rates (matching AGD/AGF up to constants) for both strongly and weakly convex functions, in both continuous and discrete time. This is significant for the optimization-sampling connection: the authors explicitly hope their analysis provides theoretical tools toward validating the long-standing conjecture that RHMC achieves accelerated mixing in KL divergence for log-concave targets, a result that remains unproven in discrete time for general distributions. Limitations include convergence guarantees only in expectation (not deterministic), somewhat larger constants than AGD, and empirical validation restricted to synthetic quadratic and logistic regression problems at d=100. Open directions include stochastic variants, non-convex extensions (e.g., quasar-convexity), and exploration of alternative integrators like leapfrog.
