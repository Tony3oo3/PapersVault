## Problem

Computing attention flow in Transformers via max-flow on the full layered attention graph has O(L^2.5 N^3) complexity, making it infeasible for modern LLMs and LMMs with dozens of layers and hundreds of tokens. The existing approximation, Attention Rollout, reduces complexity to O(LN^3) but violates flow conservation and global capacity constraints, potentially producing misleading interpretability results in safety-critical applications.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254475/cropped_page3_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-computed layered attention graph (nodes = token embeddings per layer, edges = normalized attention weights as capacities) and a set of source-destination token pairs. It outputs approximated max-flow values for those pairs, a compressed attention graph, token importance rankings, and attention heatmaps. The edge pruning threshold is fixed at 1×10⁻⁶, and a user-specified retain rate (e.g., 25%–80%) controls how many of the L layers are preserved; the first and last layers are always retained.

## Method

FlowPrune prunes the attention graph before applying the HLPP max-flow algorithm, guided by the Max-Flow Min-Cut Theorem and two Transformer structural properties (softmax sparsity and strict layering). Edge Pruning removes all edges with capacity below a threshold, exploiting the fact that softmax produces many near-zero attention weights. Layer Compression uses a sampling-based heuristic: random source-to-sink paths are drawn, the lowest-capacity edge on each path is flagged as a potential min-cut edge, and layers containing relatively few such edges are removed. Removed layers are bypassed by multiplying binary adjacency matrices of adjacent layers to create direct cross-layer edges with capacity 1 (which preserves max-flow since all original weights are in [0,1]). A dynamic programming procedure in the appendix selects which layers to compress to maximize retained min-cut edge coverage, and the compressed graph is then solved with HLPP at reduced complexity O(L^2.5 N^3 / R^2.5) where R is the compression ratio.

## Results

FlowPrune achieves substantial speed-ups in attention flow computation: at a 50% layer retain rate, Llama's per-case time drops to ~0.27 s (from 0.55 s), and at extreme compression (3 layers) it requires only 5.31% of the original runtime; LLaVA reaches ~35% of original time at 50% retention. Fidelity remains high even under aggressive compression: Top-K token retention (Jaccard IoU) stays around 0.90 when only 3 of 32 layers are kept, and First Rank Divergence exceeds 0.4 at 50% retention. In application tasks, FlowPrune's Max-n overlap with the original Attention Flow reaches 98.5% (n=3, MRPC pretrained) and 100% (n=10, ViT heatmap), far surpassing Attention Rollout's 63.4% and 28.18% respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares FlowPrune against two baselines: the original (uncompressed) Attention Flow [Abnar & Zuidema, 2020] and Attention Rollout (the recursive matrix-multiplication approximation from the same paper). Against the original Attention Flow, FlowPrune is an approximation that preserves fidelity: at 50% layer retention, absolute error is small for high-flow values and Top-K IoU remains above 0.90 even at 3 layers. Against Attention Rollout, FlowPrune wins decisively on every application metric. On MRPC paraphrasing verification, FlowPrune achieves 98.5% Max-3 overlap with the original flow (pretrained) versus only 63.4% for Attention Rollout; on ViT heatmaps (DeiT-Small, ILSVRC2012), the gap widens to 100% vs. 28.18% at n=10; and on QNLI question-answer verification, FlowPrune reaches 98.05% vs. 64.68% at n=3. The paper attributes Attention Rollout's inferiority to its neglect of global capacity constraints and flow conservation, which causes it to produce diffuse, less localized attribution maps.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Abnar & Zuidema's (2020) formulation of attention flow as a max-flow problem on a layered DAG, and on their Attention Rollout approximation. It departs from both by introducing a graph-pruning step—guided by the Max-Flow Min-Cut Theorem and two structural properties of Transformers (softmax-induced sparsity and strict layering)—before applying the HLPP max-flow algorithm. The pruning is decomposed into Edge Pruning (removing near-zero-capacity edges) and Layer Compression (removing layers with few potential min-cut edges, identified via a sampling heuristic and a dynamic-programming selection), which is a novel combination not present in prior attention-flow work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Attention flow is a powerful but computationally prohibitive interpretability tool (O(L^2.5 N^3)), making it impractical for modern LLMs and LMMs with hundreds of layers and thousands of tokens. FlowPrune offers a principled, theoretically grounded acceleration that preserves top-token fidelity (≈0.90 Jaccard even at 3/32 layers), making cross-layer interpretability feasible at scale. A key open limitation is that relative error grows for very small flow values, which could distort analysis of low-salience tokens. Future work could explore adaptive thresholds, integration with sparse-attention architectures, and validation on models with 100+ layers and 4K+ token contexts.
