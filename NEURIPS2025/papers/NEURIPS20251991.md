## Problem

Training multimodal agents for complex tool-use tasks requires extensive human-annotated trajectories and task-answer pairs, which are prohibitively expensive and often biased toward specific environments. Existing SFT and RL approaches cannot scale to diverse multimodal settings where ground-truth trajectories are unavailable, leaving a gap in how agents can autonomously refine their tool-usage strategies without any pre-collected annotation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251991/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each step, the agent receives a natural language query, one or more multimodal files (images, PDF, XLSX, DOCX, PPTX), and the history of prior thoughts, codes, and observations, then outputs a thought and executable code snippet that invokes a tool from a fixed toolkit. Tasks are not drawn from a fixed benchmark but are synthesized on-the-fly by an LLM from seed queries, with files generated via code or retrieved from image datasets. The final output is the task answer, but the training signal is the step-wise preference between candidate actions rather than end-to-end correctness.

## Method

SPORT is an iterative self-exploration loop with four components: (1) task synthesis via LLM-generated queries and files, (2) step sampling where the controller produces n candidate actions (thought + code) per step and executes them to collect observations, (3) step verification where a multimodal LLM verifier selects the best action from the n candidates based on execution results, and (4) preference tuning via flexible DPO that updates the controller using the resulting (preferred, dispreferred) action pairs. The key design choice is step-level rather than trajectory-level preference, which is easier to verify with pre-trained LLMs and extracts useful signal even from partially failed trajectories. The process is iterative: the updated controller guides sampling in the next round, progressively refining tool-usage behavior. The method builds on a MAT-style SFT stage (20K trajectories) with Qwen2-VL-7B as the base controller, fine-tuned via LoRA (rank 32) on 8×A100 GPUs, and requires no human annotation beyond the initial SFT alignment.

## Results

On the GTA benchmark, the SPORT Agent (Tuned-Qwen2-VL-7B) achieves 60.26% AnsAcc, 72.41% ToolAcc, and 91.87% CodeExec, surpassing the SFT-based T3-Agent (MAT-Qwen2-VL-7B) by 6.41% on AnsAcc, 7.78% on ToolAcc, and 7.55% on CodeExec. On the GAIA benchmark, SPORT Agent reaches 20.61% AnsAcc, improving over MAT-Qwen2-VL-7B by 3.64% and over the untrained Qwen2-VL-7B baseline by 10.91%. The SPORT Agent also outperforms closed-source GPT-4o-based HF Agent on GTA ToolAcc (72.41 vs. 63.41) while achieving comparable CodeExec (91.87 vs. 95.12).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the SFT-based T3-Agent (MAT-Qwen2-VL-7B), SPORT wins on all GTA metrics (AnsAcc 60.26 vs. 53.85, ToolAcc 72.41 vs. 64.63, CodeExec 91.87 vs. 84.32) and on GAIA AnsAcc (20.61 vs. 16.97), confirming that iterative step-wise preference tuning yields consistent gains over supervised fine-tuning alone. Compared to the untrained HF Agent with Qwen2-VL-7B, SPORT improves GTA AnsAcc by 17.95 points (60.26 vs. 42.31) and GAIA AnsAcc by 10.91 points (20.61 vs. 9.70), demonstrating the value of both SFT and self-exploration. On GTA, SPORT surpasses the closed-source GPT-4o-based HF Agent in ToolAcc (72.41 vs. 63.41) and AnsAcc (60.26 vs. 57.05), though GPT-4o retains a slight edge in CodeExec (95.12 vs. 91.87). On GAIA, SPORT still trails the GPT-4o-based HF Agent (20.61 vs. 33.40 AnsAcc), which the authors attribute to the larger model size and training data of closed-source systems. Against the Lego Agent (GPT-4, GTA AnsAcc 46.59) and Warm-up Agent (GPT-4-turbo, GAIA AnsAcc 17.60), SPORT achieves higher scores on both benchmarks, validating the effectiveness of the self-exploration framework even with a 7B open-source controller.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPORT builds on the ReAct agent framework and the MAT/T3-Agent line of work for multimodal tool-use agents, but departs from their reliance on human-annotated or API-distilled trajectories by introducing a fully self-exploration loop. It extends step-wise preference optimization (STEP-DPO, SCDPO, SDPO), which has been applied to code generation and math reasoning where correct trajectories are readily available, to the harder multimodal tool-use setting where no ground-truth trajectories exist. The AI-feedback-based verifier draws on the RLAIF/self-refine paradigm but is specifically designed to evaluate intermediate tool-calling steps (thought + code + observation) rather than final answers, and the iterative task-synthesis component borrows from self-instruction methods adapted to multimodal file generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SPORT addresses a practical bottleneck—prohibitively expensive human annotation for multimodal agent trajectories—by showing that a 7B VLM can self-improve its tool-use planning through iterative exploration and step-wise DPO, releasing a 16K preference dataset in the process. The key open question is the verifier: it currently relies on hand-crafted prompts and rules, limiting generalization to out-of-distribution tasks, and the authors themselves flag learning-to-verify as future work. A broader implication is that the online-exploration-over-static-DPO gap (5.13% vs. 1.28% AnsAcc gain) suggests that in-distribution, iteratively refined preference data matters more than raw data volume, a finding that could inform other agent-training pipelines.
