## Problem

While instruction-based image editing models produce visually plausible outputs, their ability to perform knowledge-grounded reasoning (e.g., understanding that sodium reacts violently with water, or that red cabbage turns red in acid) remains largely unmeasured. Existing reasoning-aware benchmarks such as RISEBench and SmartEdit categorize tasks along coarse axes (temporal, causal, spatial) without a formal cognitive or knowledge-based framework, leaving it unclear which specific types of knowledge a model must internalize and apply for different editing goals.

## Input / Output

Each test case consists of one or more input images paired with a natural-language editing instruction; temporal prediction tasks supply multiple sequential frames, and multi-element composition tasks supply several source images to be integrated. The evaluated model produces a single edited image, which is then scored by a VLM judge (GPT-4o or Qwen2.5-VL-72B) on four 1-to-5 metrics—Visual Consistency, Visual Quality, Instruction Following, and the novel Knowledge Plausibility—normalized to a 100-point scale. Knowledge Plausibility is only computed for tasks in Natural Science, Social Science, and Logical Reasoning, and is supported by a manually curated one-sentence knowledge hint that tells the judge the expected real-world outcome.

## Method

KRIS-Bench is built on a top-down taxonomy drawn from the revised Bloom taxonomy of educational objectives (Anderson & Krathwohl), decomposing editing knowledge into three levels—Factual (perceptual attributes, spatial relations, temporal cues), Conceptual (social-science commonsense, natural-science principles across biology/chemistry/geography/math/medicine/physics), and Procedural (logical reasoning, multi-step instruction decomposition)—which further yield 7 reasoning dimensions and 22 concrete tasks spanning 1,267 annotated instances. The evaluation protocol introduces Knowledge Plausibility as a fourth metric alongside the standard Visual Consistency, Visual Quality, and Instruction Following, with carefully crafted VLM prompts that jointly assess instruction compliance and knowledge consistency to avoid scoring inconsistencies. For knowledge-intensive cases, a concise human-written knowledge hint (e.g., 'adding purple cabbage indicator to acidic water should result in a red color change') is supplied to the VLM judge, and a user study with 12 annotators confirms that these hints significantly improve the correlation between VLM scores and human judgments (higher Pearson r, lower MAE). The benchmark is evaluated on 10 state-of-the-art models (3 closed-source, 7 open-source), with BAGEL-Think demonstrating that integrating an explicit reasoning step into the editing pipeline yields measurable gains on knowledge-plausibility tasks.

## Results

On KRIS-Bench (1,267 instances, 22 tasks, 7 reasoning dimensions), GPT-4o achieves the highest overall average score of 80.09/100, followed by Gemini 2.0 (62.41) and Doubao (60.70). Among open-source models, BAGEL-Think leads with 53.36, outperforming its non-reasoning counterpart BAGEL (47.76) by ~5.6 points, demonstrating the critical role of explicit reasoning. Knowledge Plausibility scores are consistently the lowest metric across all models (e.g., GPT-4o: 51.00 on Logical Reasoning vs. 59.25 on Instruction Following), revealing persistent gaps in applying real-world knowledge during editing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o dominates across all three knowledge types, scoring 79.80 (Factual), 81.37 (Conceptual), and 78.32 (Procedural), with a 17.7-point margin over the second-best closed-source model Gemini 2.0 (62.41 overall). Among open-source models, BAGEL-Think (53.36) is the clear leader, surpassing BAGEL (47.76) by 5.6 points and Step1X-Edit (43.29) by 10.1 points, with its advantage most pronounced on Conceptual Knowledge (59.44 vs. 48.01). BAGEL-Think even outperforms closed-source Gemini 2.0 and Doubao on Knowledge Plausibility for Biology and Chemistry tasks. The weakest open-source models are InsPix2Pix (22.82) and OmniGen (28.85), which score near zero on Temporal Prediction and Multi-element Composition due to single-image input limitations. All models show their largest performance drop on Procedural Knowledge (Logical Reasoning), where even GPT-4o scores only 71.56 and Knowledge Plausibility drops to 51.00, indicating that symbolic and multi-step reasoning remains the hardest challenge for current editing systems.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior reasoning-aware editing benchmarks (RISEBench, AURORA-Bench, SmartEdit) that categorize tasks by coarse reasoning axes (temporal, causal, spatial, logical) by instead grounding the taxonomy in Anderson and Krathwohl's revised Bloom's taxonomy of educational objectives, decomposing editing demands into Factual, Conceptual, and Procedural knowledge types. The instruction-based image editing task itself builds on the diffusion-model and VLM editing literature (MagicBrush, InstructPix2Pix, Step1X-Edit, BAGEL), but the paper's contribution is diagnostic rather than methodological: it introduces a knowledge-centric evaluation lens and a novel Knowledge Plausibility metric that prior benchmarks lack. The use of VLM-as-judge for evaluation follows the broader LLM-as-a-judge paradigm, with the specific addition of manually curated knowledge hints to calibrate plausibility scoring.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

KRIS-Bench reveals that even the strongest closed-source models (GPT-4o, Gemini 2.0) score below 70 on Knowledge Plausibility for scientific and logical reasoning tasks, exposing a systematic gap between visual plausibility and knowledge-grounded correctness that prior task-oriented benchmarks fail to surface. The counterintuitive finding that factual perception tasks are not consistently easier than conceptual ones challenges the assumption that current models have robust low-level grounding. Limitations include the modest 1,267-instance scale, uneven task distribution, cultural biases in task design, and reliance on VLM-based scoring whose reliability, while validated, remains approximate. Open questions include whether integrating explicit reasoning chains (as in BAGEL-Think) can close the knowledge-plausibility gap, and how to scale the benchmark cross-culturally.
