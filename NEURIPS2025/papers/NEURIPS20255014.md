## Problem

Continuous-time online learning theory previously only addressed the realizable setting, where the data stream is guaranteed to be generated by a function in the hypothesis class. This paper tackles the more challenging agnostic setting, where the stream may be arbitrary, noisy, or non-stationary, and may even adapt in response to the learner's past queries. The central questions are: how frequently must a learner sample a continuous stream to maintain accurate predictions, and can effective learning still occur when the environment reacts to the learner's actions?

## Input / Output

The learner receives a continuous-time data process {(X_t, Y_t)} over a finite horizon T but only observes labels at S sparse, strategically chosen query times; between queries the deployed predictor is frozen and processes all incoming features without feedback. The output is a sequence of predictors {h_s} that make point predictions on X_t at every time step, with performance measured by the integral of loss over [0,T] relative to the best fixed hypothesis in H. A key structural constraint is that the query strategy and the learning algorithm are decoupled: the query strategy selects times without access to labels, while the learning algorithm updates only at those discrete instants.

## Method

For oblivious streams, the paper provides a black-box reduction to classical discrete-time online learning: the horizon is partitioned into S fixed epochs of length Δ=T/S, one query is sampled uniformly per epoch, and the key insight is that the loss at a uniformly sampled point is an unbiased estimate of the continuous-time risk over that epoch, yielding regret T·R(S)/S for any class with discrete-time regret R(S). For adaptive streams, fixed epochs provably fail (Proposition 1), so the paper switches to dynamic random epochs and introduces a novel importance-weighting scheme with factor α_s = 1 − (t_s − t_{s−1})/Δ that restores unbiasedness despite the complex randomness of epoch boundaries; this weighting is realized algorithmically within a weighted Exponential Weights Algorithm (Algorithm 3). The resulting adaptive regret for classes of finite Littlestone dimension is Õ(T·√(Ldim(H)/S)), and matching lower bounds are established via Khintchine-type inequalities and Littlestone-tree-based hard process constructions, confirming optimality in all parameters T, S, and Ldim(H).

## Results

The paper establishes tight minimax regret bounds for agnostic continuous-time online learning. For oblivious data streams, the regret scales as Õ(T·√(Ldim(H)·log S)/√S) via a black-box reduction to classical online learning (Theorem 1, Corollary 1). For adaptive data streams, a novel importance-weighting scheme with dynamic epochs achieves the same Õ(T·√(Ldim(H)·log S)/√S) rate (Theorem 2). A matching lower bound of Ω(T·√(Ldim(H)/S)) is proven for both settings (Theorem 3), confirming optimality in all parameters T, S, and Ldim(H). Empirical validation (Appendix A) confirms the predicted O(1/√S) regret scaling across four data processes (one oblivious, three adversarial) with a threshold-expert class of 201 hypotheses over 30,000 time steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a primarily theoretical paper without direct comparisons to prior methods on shared benchmarks; the contribution is establishing the first quantitative regret characterization for agnostic continuous-time online learning. In the empirical validation (Appendix A), three algorithmic variants are compared: Uniform Epoch (fixed intervals), Dynamic Epoch (random intervals), and Dynamic + Reweighting (random intervals with importance weights α_s = 1 − (t_s − t_{s−1})/Δ). Under adversarial A1 (bit-after-query) and A2 (post-query shift), the dynamic schedule outperforms uniform updates, confirming the theoretical prediction that fixed epochs fail under adaptive adversaries (Proposition 1). Under adversarial A3 (refresh random bits), the reweighting variant outperforms the unweighted dynamic variant, demonstrating the effectiveness of the importance-weighting correction. Under the oblivious drift setting, all three algorithms achieve comparable O(1/√S) scaling, differing only by constants, consistent with the black-box reduction in Theorem 1.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the continuous-time online learning framework of Devulapalli and Hanneke [7], which established the update-and-deploy protocol but only for the realizable setting, to the more challenging agnostic regime where no perfectly consistent hypothesis is guaranteed. The black-box reduction for oblivious streams (Theorem 1) directly imports classical discrete-time regret bounds via the Littlestone dimension characterization of Ben-David et al. [1], while the adaptive-stream analysis introduces a novel importance-weighting scheme that transforms queried losses into unbiased continuous-time risk estimators under random epoch boundaries—a technique not present in either prior line of work. The lower bound constructions draw on Khintchine-type inequalities and phase-based Littlestone tree arguments from [1], adapted to the continuous-time integral risk formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first quantitative characterization of fundamental limits for agnostic learning from continuous data streams with limited interaction, establishing tight Θ(T·√(Ldim(ℋ)/S)) regret bounds in both oblivious and adaptive settings. The result that optimal query strategies differ between the two settings (fixed vs. dynamic epochs) yet yield the same regret order is a clean structural insight. Open questions include: designing query strategies tailored to specific data-generating processes (e.g., i.i.d. or Markovian streams) that could beat the worst-case rate, extending to non-binary or real-valued hypothesis classes with sharper dependence on complexity measures, and understanding computational efficiency of the oracle-based reductions in practice.
