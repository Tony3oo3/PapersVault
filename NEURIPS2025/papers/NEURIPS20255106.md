## Problem

Existing imitation learning benchmarks (CartPole, MountainCar, continuous control tasks, Atari) fail to support meaningful generalisation assessment because training and evaluation conditions are too similar, optimal actions are unknown, or state spaces lack precise structural control. This makes it impossible to determine whether an agent has truly learned the underlying task or merely memorised training trajectories, as demonstrated by the paper's analysis showing that 100,000 random initialisations in standard environments remain close to training data in state space.

## Input / Output

The agent receives either a full labyrinth image (coloured tiles for start, goal, agent, and walls) or a vector encoding of agent position, start/goal coordinates, and wall structure; a partially observable variant restricts the view to tiles in the agent's immediate vicinity while preserving start and goal visibility. The output is a single discrete action from {up, down, left, right}, with the constraint that no action is valid once the goal tile is reached. The reward function normalises accumulated reward by the shortest-path length so that an AER of 1 always corresponds to optimal navigation regardless of labyrinth size or structure.

## Method

Labyrinth is a discrete, fully observable grid environment formally defined as a graph (nodes = tiles, edges = connections) that enables verifiably distinct training, validation, and test splits by varying wall structures, start/goal positions, or both, with uniqueness enforced via structure hashing. It supports four task settings—standard navigation, partial observability, key-and-door (sequential sub-goals), and ice-floor (safety constraints)—and uses Johnson's algorithm to enumerate all optimal paths for ground-truth per-state evaluation. The paper benchmarks six imitation learning methods (BC, DAgger, GAIL, BCO, SQIL, IUPE) using CNN encoders (Atari DQN-based and ResNet-18) trained for 1,000–10,000 epochs, finding that all methods fail to generalise to unseen structures, with success rates dropping from 37–75% on training to 0–21% on validation/test splits. The key design insight is that the discrete graph structure guarantees known optimal actions for every state, enabling fine-grained per-state error analysis and controlled isolation of generalisation factors (structure change vs. position shift vs. action distribution shift) that are infeasible in continuous or visual benchmarks.

## Results

On the 5×5 Labyrinth (biased setting), all six imitation learning methods fail to generalise beyond the training split. On the test split, success ratios range from 0% (SQIL) to 5% (IUPE), with average episodic rewards between −3.80 and −4.00 (where 1.0 indicates optimal shortest-path navigation). IUPE is the only method exceeding 10% SR on validation (21%), but this does not transfer to test (5%). An extended BC experiment with ResNet-18 and 10,000 epochs improves test SR to 53%, yet the authors argue this reflects better encoding rather than true task generalisation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks six imitation learning methods on the new Labyrinth environment rather than proposing a new algorithm. IUPE (Gavenski 2020) is the strongest performer, achieving 75% SR on train and 21% on validation, but drops to 5% on test, suggesting it memorised validation-specific patterns rather than learning navigation. BCO (Torabi 2018) achieves the highest train SR (70%) but collapses to 2–3% on unseen structures. GAIL (Ho 2016) and DAgger (Ross 2011) show moderate train performance (61% and 57% SR) but similarly fail to generalise (9% and 5% on validation). BC (Pomerleau 1988) and SQIL (Reddy 2019) are the weakest, with SQIL achieving only 4% train SR and 0% on test. The authors conclude that no existing method truly generalises: even the best test SR (5%) is far below the 100% achievable by a BFS solver, and the extended BC+ResNet-18 result (53% test SR) still falls short of reliable generalisation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not extend a specific prior method but rather carves new ground in the evaluation infrastructure for imitation learning. It departs from the standard RL-derived benchmarks (CartPole, MountainCar, MuJoCo continuous control, Atari) by arguing they cannot meaningfully separate training from test conditions, and instead introduces a discrete, fully observable grid environment with known optimal actions. The implementation builds on the authors' own IL-Datasets toolkit for standardised training/evaluation pipelines, and the environment is integrated into the gymnasium ecosystem for broad accessibility. Conceptually, it repurposes the classical maze-navigation problem as a controlled probe for generalisation, leveraging graph-theoretic structure (Johnson's algorithm for path enumeration) to guarantee verifiably distinct train/eval/test splits.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that every tested IL method (BC, DAgger, GAIL, BCO, SQIL, IUPE) achieves near-zero success on unseen labyrinth structures, even with ResNet-18 and 10,000 epochs—challenges the field's implicit assumption that current methods learn transferable navigation skills rather than state-action correlations. Labyrinth provides a reproducible, fine-grained tool for isolating specific generalisation failures (structural change vs. start/goal shift vs. action-distribution shift), which is difficult in continuous or visual benchmarks. Key limitations include discrete-only actions (excluding methods like OPOLO, MAHALO, CILO) and the relatively small grid sizes tested; open questions include whether any architecture can truly internalise the topological reasoning required, and whether the benchmark's difficulty is appropriate or artificially inflated by the image-based state representation.
