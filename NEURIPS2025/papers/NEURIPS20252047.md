## Problem

Existing bias evaluation methods for language models require explicitly specifying social groups in prompts (e.g., 'a woman doctor'), yet most real-world generative AI usage does not mention demographic groups. This leaves a critical gap: it is unknown whether representational biases in *how* people are described persist when gender is unprompted, and whether naive interventions that increase the *quantity* of representation (who is represented) inadvertently proliferate harmful stereotypes in the *quality* of representation (how they are represented).

## Input / Output

The method consumes occupation-based text prompts (e.g., 'Generate a persona of a [OCCUPATION]') sent to LLMs both with and without gender specification, producing 100+ generations per occupation/gender/prompt combination. A rule-based Gender Association Method then labels each unprompted generation as female, male, or non-binary using pronoun and honorific frequency counts (achieving >99.6% accuracy on a validation set). The final output is a per-occupation, per-model SRB Score (a signed scalar) quantifying whether statistically significant descriptive words for associated women cluster closer to specified-women embeddings than specified-men embeddings, with p-values confirming significance across all occupation-model pairs.

## Method

The GAS(P) methodology operates in three stages. First, a Gender Association Method (Algorithm 1) assigns gender to unprompted generations by counting female/male/neutral pronouns and gendered honorifics, with a non-binary branch triggered by explicit non-binary terms. Second, a Calibrated Marked Words method (Algorithm 4) identifies statistically significant words per occupation-gender-model triple by adapting the Fightin' Words/Marked Personas framework with two key modifications: a hybrid prior mixing the English language corpus (NLTK Brown) with the generated text corpus via a mixing parameter α=0.15, and regularizing terms (r₁, r₂) computed from calibration word counts to suppress spurious significance of common words. Third, the Subset Representational Bias (SRB) Score (Algorithm 6) computes the Chamfer distance (using cosine distance as the metric) between word-embedding sets of associated-gender significant words and specified-gender significant words, yielding Δ(S‖T_F, T_M) = CH(S, T_F) − CH(S, T_M); a negative score indicates the associated set is closer to specified women, a positive score to specified men, and a t-test across occupations confirms whether the gender gap in SRB scores is statistically significant.

## Results

Across 63 occupations and three models (GPT-3.5, GPT-4o-mini, Llama-3.1-70b), the SRB Score reveals statistically significant distribution-level differences in how associated women vs. men are described, with all per-model p-values below 0.05. Women's representation exceeds BLS benchmarks in most occupations (e.g., 90–100% in female-dominated roles), yet 89.7% of 1,500 K-means++ clusters contain words at least 50% more prevalent in one gender. The SRB Score increases from GPT-3.5 to GPT-4o-mini (e.g., 100% increase for 'software engineer,' 'cook,' and 'chef'), indicating that representational biases between associated and specified genders have strengthened in newer models. The Gender Association Method achieves >99.6% accuracy on its validation set, capturing 80–98% of generations depending on model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's Calibrated Marked Words method is built on and compared to Marked Personas (Cheng et al., ACL 2023). The original Marked Personas method flags common words (e.g., 'the,' 'be,' 'one,' 'being') as statistically significant, while the Calibrated Marked Words method—using a hybrid English+topic prior with regularizing terms—eliminates these spurious detections and surfaces higher-quality, gender-differentiating words (e.g., 'trailblazing,' 'imposter syndrome' for women; 'github,' 'startup,' 'kubernetes' for men). On the SRB Score, the paper does not benchmark against a competing bias-detection method but instead compares across model generations: GPT-4o-mini shows a statistically significant increase in SRB magnitude relative to GPT-3.5, meaning associated-gender generations more closely mirror specified-gender stereotypes in the newer model. Llama-3.1-70b exhibits patterns intermediate between the two GPT models, with slightly lower capture rates (98.2% vs. 94.3% for GPT-4o-mini) but similar directional biases. The key takeaway is that no model eliminates the representational gap; the 'improvement' in female representation (who is represented) coincides with stronger stereotypical language patterns (how they are represented).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Marked Personas framework (Cheng et al., 2023) and its underlying Fightin' Words statistical machinery (Monroe et al., 2008) to a setting those methods were not designed for: measuring representational differences when the social group is not specified in the prompt. Prior work on occupational gender bias (Rudinger et al., Zhao et al., Kirk et al., Mattern et al., Kotek et al.) uniformly required explicit gender specification via pronouns, names, or template slots, leaving the more realistic unprompted setting unexplored. The GAS(P) methodology and the Subset Representational Bias Score are the paper's core methodological contributions, bridging the gap between who is represented (distributional analysis) and how they are represented (statistical word-level comparison) without requiring the group to appear in the input.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central caution—that naively increasing the proportion of women in generated personas may proliferate stereotypical depictions rather than reduce harm—has direct implications for how model developers design and evaluate bias mitigation. The finding that SRB Scores increased from GPT-3.5 to GPT-4o-mini suggests that representation-focused interventions may inadvertently strengthen the very stereotypes they aim to counter. Open questions include whether these unprompted representational biases transfer to downstream tasks (e.g., hiring recommendations, creative writing), whether the methodology generalizes beyond gender and occupation to other axes (race, disability), and how to design interventions that address both who and how simultaneously. Limitations include reliance on U.S. BLS baselines, high computational cost of generating sufficient samples, and limited non-binary analysis due to low representation.
