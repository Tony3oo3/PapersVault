## Problem

The conventional wisdom holds that finetuning excels at style/tone customization but fails at knowledge injection, yet this dichotomy is taxonomically ambiguous—teaching a tone is arguably a form of knowledge injection, and vice versa. The paper asks what concrete, controllable factors actually explain the large performance gap observed between these two canonical finetuning use-cases, rather than attributing it to an inherent categorical difference.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253335/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a base Gemini v1.5 Pro or Flash model and finetunes it via LoRA on artificially engineered datasets of ~10,000–100,000 tokens (roughly 10,000 characters per fact), spanning five training formats: direct QA pairs, multi-turn conversations, Wikipedia-style articles, multi-step reasoning examples, and role-play dialogues. The finetuned model is then evaluated on six task types (direct QA, conversational QA, role-play QA, Wikipedia fill-in-the-blank, Wikipedia sentence completion, and reasoning problems), with all evaluation tasks pre-filtered to be solvable via in-context learning to control for task feasibility. Datasets are constructed from matched pairs of real-world and fictional Wikipedia entities plus generated personas, each carrying 120 mutually exclusive facts in numerical, categorical, and emotional forms.

## Method

The paper conducts a grid search of nearly 4,000 LoRA finetuning experiments on Gemini v1.5 Pro and Flash, systematically varying five axes: entity type (real-world, fictional, or persona), information quantity (20, 200, or 4,000 facts), information type (numerical, categorical, emotional), training data format (QA, multi-turn, Wikipedia, reasoning, role-play), and evaluation task type. Key findings include: (1) QA-pair training data consistently outperforms Wikipedia-article-style data across nearly all evaluation tasks, even when the evaluation format mismatches the training format; (2) numerical facts are significantly harder to finetune than categorical or emotional facts; (3) models struggle to apply finetuned knowledge during multi-step reasoning even when they can recall the same facts in direct QA, a limitation the authors attribute to the reversal curse and random-access constraints of autoregressive models; (4) accuracy degrades roughly as a power law with increasing fact count; and (5) entity type (real vs. fictional vs. persona) does not significantly affect finetuning difficulty once other factors are controlled. The authors conclude that the 'knowledge injection vs. task customization' distinction is a distinction without a difference, and that the observed performance gap is fully explained by the correlated differences in data format, information type, quantity, and evaluation alignment.

## Results

In the canonical tone-teaching experiment, finetuned Gemini v1.5 Pro achieves 96.3% average tone-identification accuracy and 94.7% exact-match accuracy (vs. 30% random baseline), while the Wikipedia knowledge-injection experiment yields only 11% exam-question accuracy. Across ~4,000 grid-search finetuning experiments, question-answer training data consistently outperform Wikipedia-article-style data across nearly all evaluation tasks, and numerical facts are significantly harder to retain than categorical or emotional facts. Accuracy degrades roughly as a power law as the number of injected facts scales from 20 to 4,000, and entity type (real-world, fictional, or persona) does not significantly affect finetuning difficulty after controlling for other factors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but instead performs a controlled grid search over training data formats, information types, entity types, and evaluation tasks. Question-answer (QA) pairs emerge as the dominant training format, outperforming Wikipedia-article-style data on all evaluation tasks including Wikipedia fill-in-the-blank, despite a style mismatch; the reverse is not true (Wikipedia training does not improve QA evaluation). Reasoning-based training data yield the best performance on reasoning evaluation tasks but underperform on direct QA, and vice versa, a pattern the authors attribute to the reversal curse and random-access limitations of autoregressive models. Numerical facts show consistently lower finetuning accuracy than categorical or emotional facts across all evaluation tasks and training formats. Entity type (real-world vs. fictional vs. persona) shows no significant difference in finetuning difficulty, contradicting the intuition that learning about external entities is inherently harder than learning a persona. Scaling from 20 to 4,000 facts causes a power-law decline in accuracy even with proportionally more training data and epochs, identifying information volume as a key failure mode for large-scale knowledge injection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the prevailing 'show-not-tell' framing (OpenAI, 2024; Zhang et al., 2024) that treats style customization and knowledge injection as fundamentally different finetuning regimes, arguing instead that the distinction is a 'distinction without a difference.' It builds on prior empirical observations that finetuning is poor at knowledge injection (Ovadia et al., 2024; Gekhman et al., 2024; Ghosal et al., 2024) and on mechanistic accounts of autoregressive model limitations—the reversal curse (Berglund et al., 2024) and random-access failures (Zhu et al., 2024)—but reframes the problem as one of identifying concrete, controllable factors (data format, information type, quantity, evaluation alignment) rather than accepting a binary success/failure taxonomy. The study also connects to the knowledge-editing literature (De Cao et al., 2021; Meng et al., 2023) and to findings that QA-format data improves knowledge acquisition (Jiang et al., 2024; Khashabi et al., 2020), but operates exclusively in the finetuning regime rather than weight-level editing or pretraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practitioners with actionable, well-controlled guidance: preprocess finetuning data into QA format, expect numerical facts to be harder to retain than categorical ones, and do not expect finetuned knowledge to transfer to multi-step reasoning. The finding that Wikipedia-style articles—the most natural document format for knowledge—are among the least effective finetuning data types is directly counterintuitive and explains why many prior knowledge-injection experiments underperform. Open questions remain about the mechanisms behind the Wikipedia-article underperformance and the numerical-vs-categorical gap, and the results are specific to the Gemini v1.5 family in the 10K–100K token application regime, leaving generalizability to other architectures and to the continued-pretraining limit untested.
