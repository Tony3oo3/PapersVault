## Problem

Despite rapid development of graph condensation (GC) methods for node classification, no unified evaluation framework exists to fairly compare them. Existing methods use inconsistent validation protocols (some leak test-set information, others validate at different frequencies), neglect critical dimensions like privacy preservation, denoising ability, and NAS effectiveness, and the impact of key design choices—initialization, structure synthesis, and graph property preservation—remains poorly understood.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes original graphs with node features, edge structures, and node labels across seven datasets (Cora, Citeseer, Pubmed, Arxiv, Flickr, Reddit, Yelp) spanning both transductive and inductive settings, and produces condensed synthetic graphs at controlled reduction rates (r = #synthetic nodes / #training nodes, ranging from 0.02% to 2.6%). Evaluation outputs are multi-dimensional: test accuracy and F1-macro, efficiency metrics (preprocessing time, per-epoch time, peak GPU/disk memory), MIA accuracy for privacy, performance drop under three noise types, NAS correlation coefficients (Pearson accuracy and rank), and cross-GNN transferability scores across six architectures.

## Method

GC4NC establishes a fair evaluation protocol that unifies the validation model (2-layer GCN with 256 hidden units, 300 epochs, no early stopping), restricts validation frequency to total_epochs/10, and selects condensed graphs by best validation accuracy rather than test accuracy. It then systematically benchmarks ten GC methods (GCond, DosCond, GEOM, SFGC, MSGC, SGDD, GDEM, GCDM/GCondX, GCSNTK) alongside coreset (Random, K-Center, Herding, Cent-D, Cent-P) and coarsening (Averaging, VNG, VN) baselines across five evaluation dimensions: performance/scalability, privacy preservation via membership inference attacks, denoising under feature/structural/adversarial noise, NAS effectiveness via APPNP architecture search, and transferability across GCN, SGC, APPNP, Cheby, GraphSage, and GAT. The framework further isolates the impact of design choices by varying data initialization strategies (five coreset/coarsening-based initializations), comparing structure-free versus structure-based condensation, and measuring preservation of five graph properties (Density, Max Eigenvalue, DBI, DBI-AGG, Homophily) to identify which properties correlate with downstream performance.

## Results

Across 7 datasets (Cora, Citeseer, Pubmed, Arxiv, Flickr, Reddit, Yelp) at three reduction rates each, trajectory-matching (TM) methods GEOM and SFGC consistently achieve the highest node classification accuracy among all condensation methods, e.g., GEOM reaches 82.82% on Cora (r=2.6%) and 73.03% on Citeseer (r=1.8%), closely matching whole-graph training (81.5% and 72.6% respectively). All GC methods substantially outperform coreset selection (Random, K-Center, Herding) and coarsening (Averaging, VNG) baselines, with gaps of 5–20+ points at low reduction rates. Structure-free variants (e.g., GCondX) are more efficient and scalable but sacrifice 1–3% accuracy compared to their structure-based counterparts (e.g., GCond). On large datasets like Arxiv (r=0.5%), the best GC method (GEOM, 69.59%) still lags whole-graph training (71.4%) by ~1.8 points, indicating room for improvement at scale.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark compares 14+ graph reduction methods across five categories: coreset selection (Random, K-Center, Herding, Cent-D, Cent-P), coarsening (Averaging, VNG, VN), gradient matching (GCond, DosCond, MSGC, SGDD, GCondX), trajectory matching (GEOM, SFGC), and distribution matching (GCDM, GDEM, GCSNTK). TM methods (GEOM, SFGC) dominate on accuracy across nearly all datasets and reduction rates, e.g., GEOM achieves 82.82% on Cora (r=2.6%) vs. GCond (80.54%), DosCond (81.15%), and SGDD (80.94%). Among GM methods, DosCond and GCond are competitive with each other (within 1–2 points), while SGDD and MSGC trail by 1–3 points on most datasets. Coarsening methods (Averaging, VNG) are the strongest non-GC baselines, reaching 75–76% on Cora but still 4–7 points below the best GC methods. Coreset selection methods (Random, K-Center, Herding) are consistently the weakest, with Herding (73.77% on Cora r=2.6%) still 9 points below GEOM. Notably, on Yelp, the simple Averaging coarsening baseline (55.04% at r=0.05%) outperforms most GC methods, suggesting GC's advantage diminishes on certain dataset characteristics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as the first unified, multi-dimensional benchmark for graph condensation (GC) on node classification, extending the dataset distillation paradigm from the image domain (DC-bench) to graphs. Rather than proposing a new condensation method, it systematically evaluates existing GC methods (GCond, DosCond, GEOM, SFGC, MSGC, SGDD, GDEM, GCDM, GCSNTK) alongside coreset selection and graph coarsening baselines under a fair, consistent protocol. It departs from prior GC papers by adding evaluation dimensions they neglected—privacy preservation via membership inference attacks, denoising ability under structural and feature noise, NAS effectiveness, and cross-model transferability—while also connecting GC to the broader graph reduction literature (coresets, coarsening) that prior GC work treated in isolation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides practical guidance for practitioners choosing among GC methods: trajectory matching wins on accuracy but is the least efficient, structure-free methods scale better but lose denoising ability, and inner optimization or trajectory matching is essential for NAS and transferability. Key open questions remain: GC methods hit OOM at larger reduction rates (unlike image condensation which scales gracefully), the privacy gains are modest (5–10% MIA reduction) and untested against stronger attacks like differential privacy, and the denoising ability against feature noise is essentially absent. The finding that homophilous graphs condense into heterophilous ones while preserving performance also challenges the assumed importance of homophily for GNN success, suggesting the field needs to reconsider which graph properties actually matter.
