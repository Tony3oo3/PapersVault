## Problem

Integrating historical control data with experimental data can substantially improve A/B testing efficiency, but distributional shifts—covariate, policy, and posterior—between the two datasets bias the resulting treatment effect estimator. Existing methods either assume no posterior shift or require it to be either zero or sufficiently large for consistent data selection, failing in the practically important intermediate regime of moderate posterior shifts. This paper seeks a data integration method that robustly handles all three shift types simultaneously, including moderate posterior shifts and heavy-tailed reward residuals.

## Input / Output

The method takes two i.i.d. context-action-reward triplet datasets: an experimental dataset where actions are randomized between treatment and control, and a historical dataset generated exclusively under the control policy with a potentially different covariate distribution and reward function. It produces a covariate-dependent weight function w(s) ∈ [0,1] that adaptively blends the two datasets' estimating functions per context, yielding a weighted ATE estimate. The weight is learned via sample-splitting: half the data estimates w and nuisance functions (propensity scores, density ratios, reward models), and the other half constructs the final ATE estimator, enabling valid asymptotic inference.

## Method

The key insight is to reframe the weight selection problem as offline policy learning: each value of w(s) is treated as an action in a contextual bandit, and the goal is to find the policy minimizing the MSE of the resulting weighted ATE estimator. Following the pessimistic principle, the method minimizes an upper bound on the estimated MSE (squared bias + variance) rather than the point estimate, where the upper bounds are constructed via concentration inequalities or Wald-type confidence intervals. This pessimism is critical for adaptivity: it ensures the excess MSE over the oracle depends only on estimation errors at the true optimal weight w*, not at the estimated weight ŵ, so the estimator automatically adapts to whichever scenario (heavy-tailed rewards, small/moderate/large posterior shifts) is present. The base estimators are IS or DR estimators for the control-group mean, and the covariate-dependent weighting (as opposed to the constant weight in prior work) allows the method to exploit historical data in contexts where the posterior shift is small while discarding it where the shift is large.

## Results

In the toy example (Table 2), the proposed estimator achieves MSE 1.394 (95% CI: 1.312–1.476), outperforming EDO (MSE 1.701) and HDB (MSE 2.372) by striking a bias-variance balance (bias 0.221, variance 1.345). Across five theoretical scenarios (heavy-tailed historical/experimental rewards, small/moderate/large posterior shifts), the proposed estimator satisfies the oracle property in all five, meaning its MSE is asymptotically equivalent to the scenario-specific optimal estimator. In synthetic and ridesharing-data-based simulations (100 replications), the proposed method achieves the lowest MSE in most settings, particularly as the posterior shift parameter μ_diff increases from 0 to 5.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed pessimistic estimator dominates all five baselines (EDO, HDB, MVE, CWE, NonPessi) by achieving the oracle property across all five scenarios, whereas each baseline is optimal in only one or two scenarios and fails in the rest. EDO is optimal under heavy-tailed historical rewards and moderate/large posterior shifts but underperforms when historical data is useful (scenarios ii–iii). HDB is optimal only under heavy-tailed experimental rewards and fails in four of five scenarios due to uncorrected posterior-shift bias. MVE (minimum variance estimator) is optimal only under small posterior shifts and suffers large bias under moderate-to-large shifts. CWE (constant-weight pessimistic estimator from Li et al. 2024) is sub-optimal under small posterior shifts because it restricts weights to be context-independent, and under heavy-tailed rewards because it does not fully apply the pessimistic principle to the variance term. NonPessi (non-pessimistic variant) fails under moderate posterior shifts because the bias is undetectable from data without pessimistic upper bounds, and can also degrade under heavy-tailed rewards. In the quantitative toy example, the proposed method reduces MSE by 18% over EDO (1.394 vs. 1.701) and 41% over HDB (1.394 vs. 2.372).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the pessimistic data integration framework of Li et al. (ICML 2024), which combined experimental and historical ATE estimators using a fixed constant weight, by replacing it with a covariate-dependent weight function learned via counterfactual risk minimization. It also subsumes the minimal-variance estimator (MVE) of Li et al. (Biometrics 2023) as a special case (w=1 with no bias penalty), while departing from both by explicitly accounting for posterior shift—a limitation that causes MVE to fail under moderate-to-large shifts and causes the constant-weight pessimistic estimator to be suboptimal under context-dependent shifts. The conceptual bridge between data integration and offline policy learning (treating the weight as a policy) is the paper's key methodological departure from prior data-fusion approaches that rely on penalized selection (e.g., LASSO-type methods) requiring the posterior shift to be either zero or sufficiently large.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The oracle property—achieving near-optimal MSE across five distinct scenarios (heavy-tailed rewards, small/moderate/large posterior shifts) without knowing the scenario in advance—provides practitioners with a single robust estimator for A/B testing under distributional shift, eliminating the need to diagnose which type of shift is present. The moderate posterior shift regime (Scenario iv), where the bias is undetectable from data yet non-negligible, is particularly important because prior methods (NonPessi, CWE) fail there while the pessimistic approach succeeds. Limitations include the non-dynamic (single-step) setting, bounded-reward assumption, and sample-splitting requirement; open questions include extension to sequential treatments with carryover effects, unbounded outcomes, and more general RL objectives such as fitted Q-evaluation.
