## Problem

Existing reward models apply nearly uniform computational resources across all inputs, lacking the adaptability to allocate additional test-time compute to complex queries that require multi-step reasoning. This inflexibility limits their effectiveness when evaluating responses where appropriate rewards are not immediately apparent, and no prior work has systematically explored how to scale test-time compute for reward estimation.

## Input / Output

Each RRM input consists of a query paired with exactly two candidate responses (ties are not allowed), with the strict two-response constraint reserving output token budget for the reasoning phase. The model autoregressively generates a chain-of-thought analysis followed by a final judgment in the format \boxed{Assistant 1} or \boxed{Assistant 2}. For multi-response scenarios, the pairwise interface is extended via ELO round-robin (O(n²) comparisons) or knockout tournament (O(n) comparisons, O(log n) sequential rounds), both composable with majority voting over multiple RRM samples.

## Method

RRMs reframe reward modeling as a reasoning task: the model first produces an explicit chain-of-thought analysis of both responses before emitting its preference, enabling adaptive allocation of test-time compute. Training uses Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with a simple binary rule-based reward (+1 if the RRM selects the ground-truth response, −1 otherwise), requiring no explicit reasoning traces as supervision—the model self-evolves its reward reasoning capabilities within this rule-based environment. Base models are DeepSeek-R1-distilled Qwen2 variants (7B and 32B), trained on ~420K preference pairs spanning Skywork-Reward, Tulu 3, and verifiable QA sources. A key emergent behavior is adaptive compute allocation: the model can terminate its reasoning phase early via a special token, producing shorter traces for simple chat queries and longer traces for complex reasoning tasks, without any explicit length conditioning.

## Results

On RewardBench, RRM-32B (voting@16) achieves 91.9% overall accuracy, surpassing GPT-4o (86.7%), DeepSeek-R1 (84.9%), and RM-R1-32B (90.9%), though trailing the scalar model Skywork-Reward-Gemma-2-27B (94.3%). On PandaLM Test, RRM-32B (voting@16) reaches 80.2% agreement and 81.9% F1, outperforming all baselines including Skywork-Reward (76.6%/76.4%) and DeepSeek-R1 (78.7%/72.5%). On reward-guided best-of-N inference (PPE), RRM-32B (voting@5) achieves 83.0% on MMLU-Pro, 91.8% on MATH, and 64.3% on GPQA, substantially exceeding Skywork-Reward (67.0/56.3/44.0) and GPT-4o (64.8/56.9/46.3). RRM-32B also achieves 98.3% on the RewardBench Reasoning subset, the highest among all evaluated models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RRM-32B (voting@16) outperforms GPT-4o (86.7%), DeepSeek-R1 (84.9%), RM-R1-32B (90.9%), and DirectJudge-32B (90.4%) on RewardBench Overall, but falls short of the scalar Skywork-Reward-Gemma-2-27B (94.3%), which benefits from a dedicated regression head. On PandaLM Test, RRM-32B (voting@16) achieves the best agreement (80.2%) and F1 (81.9%), beating Skywork-Reward (76.6/76.4) by ~3.6/5.5 points and DeepSeek-R1 (78.7/72.5) by ~1.5/9.4 points. The gap between RRM and DirectJudge (trained on identical data) is most pronounced on the Reasoning subset (98.3 vs. 90.9), confirming that explicit chain-of-thought reasoning adds substantial value for complex queries. On best-of-N inference (Table 2), RRM-32B (voting@5) dominates Skywork-Reward by 16.0 points on MMLU-Pro (83.0 vs. 67.0) and 35.5 points on MATH (91.8 vs. 56.3), while GPT-4o trails both. In post-training experiments (Table 4), RL with RRM-32B feedback yields stable MMLU-Pro gains (50.86 at 600 steps) whereas RL with Skywork scalar rewards degrades to 34.76, suggesting reward hacking with scalar scores.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the generative reward model line (JudgeLM, DeepSeek-GRM, RM-R1) by explicitly framing reward modeling as a chain-of-thought reasoning task and training with GRPO-based reinforcement learning rather than supervised fine-tuning on reasoning traces. It departs from scalar reward models (Skywork-Reward) and LLM-as-a-Judge prompting by introducing adaptive test-time compute allocation: the model decides how much reasoning to invest per query. The multi-response rewarding strategies (ELO rating, knockout tournament) repurpose established competition structures from chess and sports into a reward-modeling context, building on the ELO system [17] and the pairwise tournament idea from Liu et al. [41].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Reward models are the bottleneck in RLHF and post-training pipelines, and this paper demonstrates that a 7B reasoning-based reward model can match or exceed 27B+ scalar models, offering a practical path to higher-quality preference signals without proportional compute increases. The RL-Skywork degradation (49.5→34.8 on MMLU-Pro) versus RL-RRM stability (50.6→50.9) highlights a concrete reward-hacking risk in scalar-reward RL that practitioners should be aware of. Open questions include whether the 4× GPU-hour overhead of RRM-based RL is acceptable at scale, whether the emergent adaptive compute allocation generalizes beyond the tested domains, and how the approach performs with truly novel or out-of-distribution queries where the RL-trained reasoning patterns may not transfer.
