## Problem

Competitive coevolutionary algorithms (CoEAs) are widely used for game playing but lack rigorous theoretical guarantees on when and why they succeed. A key open question is how the distribution of opponents—specifically their diversity and challenge level—affects the speed of coevolutionary learning, and whether pathological behaviors like cycling and forgetting can be provably avoided. This paper provides the first detailed runtime analysis of a CoEA on a combinatorial game where the previous general bound was O(n⁵ log² n), showing that careful analysis of how individuals exploit opponents' mistakes yields a much tighter O(n² log³ n) bound.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes as input a combinatorial game graph G (for RLO, a directed acyclic graph with 4n+1 vertices and n 'challenges') and a binary payoff function f_G: X_G × X_G → {-1,1}, where the strategy space X_G = ∏_{v∈Int(G)} F(v) is isomorphic to {0,1}^{3n}. Key parameters are population size μ = Θ(n log n), mutation rate c ≤ 1/600, and boundary γ = 1/(100n). The output is an optimal strategy (a complete assignment of preferred moves at every interior vertex) discovered within the population, with runtime measured as the total number of payoff queries (game evaluations) until such a strategy first appears.

## Method

The paper introduces a novel MUTATE(c, p_t, x) operator applied to tournament winners before they enter the next population, which probabilistically resamples each position uniformly with probability proportional to c·∏(1−p_t(i)(s)), nudging the frequency vector toward uniformity. This transforms Tournament UMDA from a balanced EDA into a stable one, preventing premature convergence on positions not yet relevant while still allowing convergence where selective pressure is strong. The proof analyzes the 3D probability vector q_t = (p(c_i,a_i), p(a_i,c_{i-1}), p(b_i,c_{i-1})) tracking a single gadget, showing via a sequence of contracting cuboids that the stochastic process follows deterministic trajectories toward a target set Â = [1/8,7/8]×[1−2γ,1−γ]². The MUTATE operator is critical to this trajectory-following argument because it biases q_t toward the cube's center where trajectories move most directly and fluctuations are least erratic. A complementary black-box lower bound (Theorem 6.1) shows that any EA evaluating against uniformly random opponents requires exponential time on RLO, confirming that the coevolutionary arms race—not merely diversity—is essential for efficient learning.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 5.2) proves that Tournament UMDA with the novel MUTATE operator finds an optimal strategy for Reciprocal LeadingOnes (RLO) in O(n² log³ n) game evaluations with high probability, improving the previous best bound of O(n⁵ log² n) by a factor of roughly n³/log n, with a matching lower bound of Ω(n² log n). Theorem 6.1 further establishes that any non-coevolutionary EA evaluating against uniform random opponents requires exponential time (≥ 2^(n/8)) to find an optimal RLO strategy. Experimentally, Tournament UMDA with c=0.05 outperforms c=0 on all six combinatorial games (e.g., RLO: 5.53×10⁴ vs 1.06×10⁴ mean runtime; Tic-Tac-Toe: 3.13×10⁵ vs 6.48×10⁵), with all improvements statistically significant (p < 10⁻³). The log-log regression slope for RLO runtime is 2.136 for c=0 (matching the n² term in the theory) and 1.543 for c=0.05, suggesting potentially better asymptotic scaling for smaller populations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Tournament UMDA (both c=0 and c=0.05) is the best-performing algorithm on all six combinatorial games, outperforming all seven baselines: pollackblair1998 (one-pop. CoEA with crossover), chellapillafogel1999 (one-pop. CoEA with random opponents), panaitluke2002 (one-pop. tournament CoEA), rosinbelew1997 (two-pop. CoEA with archives), sjlk2015 (two-pop. CoEA with fitness sharing), PDCoEA (two-pop. game-theoretic CoEA), and RankDivCoEA (two-pop. ranking-diversity CoEA). The best non-EDA baselines (panaitluke2002 and rosinbelew1997) both use crossover and archives to promote diversity, yet still trail Tournament UMDA substantially. The MUTATE operator (c=0.05) provides a consistent and statistically significant speedup over c=0 on every game, with the largest relative gains on RLO (~1.9×), Turning Turtles (~1.9×), Chomp (~2.2×), and Tic-Tac-Toe (~2.1×). The theoretical contribution improves the runtime bound from O(n⁵ log² n) (via [4]) to O(n² log³ n), and the exponential lower bound (Theorem 6.1) confirms that the coevolutionary arms race is essential—diversity alone (random opponents) is insufficient.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the first runtime analysis of a coevolutionary algorithm on impartial combinatorial games (Benford & Lehre, GECCO 2025), which gave a general O(n^{5} log^{2} n) bound via a switchability-based framework, by performing a much more detailed trajectory analysis on a specific game to achieve O(n^{2} log^{3} n). The algorithm analysed is a direct descendant of the coevolutionary UMDA studied on symmetric zero-sum games (Benford & Lehre, GECCO 2024), itself adapted from the original UMDA (Muhlenbein & Paaß, 1996). The key conceptual departure is the introduction of a MUTATE operator that transforms the EDA from 'balanced' to 'stable' in the sense of Krejca's EDA theory, enabling a trajectory-following proof technique (sequences of contracting cuboids) that avoids the limitations of standard drift analysis in higher dimensions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first result to provide matching (up to polylog) upper and lower bounds for a CoEA on a combinatorial game, establishing that the coevolutionary arms race is provably essential: evaluating against uniform random opponents requires exponential time even though the opponent distribution is maximally diverse. The RLO benchmark and the stability-based MUTATE operator offer concrete tools for future CoEA design and analysis. An open question highlighted by the authors is whether the empirically observed O(n^{1.5}) scaling for smaller populations (slope 1.543) can be matched theoretically, which would require case-by-case arguments beyond the current framework.
