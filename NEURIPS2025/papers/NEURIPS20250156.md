## Problem

What features neural networks learn and how they emerge during training remains an open question. Prior saddle-to-saddle analyses of two-layer networks rely on disparate simplifying assumptions (linear activations, orthogonal inputs, layer-wise learning), making it difficult to establish a unified, scalable theory of feature learning that predicts the order, timing, and magnitude of loss drops.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250156/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

AGF takes a two-layer network specification (activation function σ, data representation g_i(x), number of hidden neurons H), a supervised training task (ground-truth function y and input distribution), and a small initialization scale α ≪ 1. It produces a discrete sequence of saddle-to-saddle events: for each iteration, which neuron activates, the jump time τ at which it crosses the activation threshold, the loss level after the subsequent cost-minimization phase, and the learned feature direction. The learning rate is rescaled as η = E[c_i] (or η_α = -log α for κ=2, η_α = α^{-1} for κ>2) to capture meaningful dynamics in the α→0 limit.

## Method

AGF approximates gradient flow at small initialization as an alternating two-step process. In Step 1 (utility maximization), dormant neurons independently follow projected gradient flow on the unit sphere to maximize their utility U_i(θ;r) = E_x[⟨f_i(x;θ), r(x)⟩], where r is the residual determined solely by active neurons; the first neuron whose accumulated utility S_i(t) crosses a threshold c_i/η activates. In Step 2 (cost minimization), all active neurons collectively follow gradient flow to minimize the MSE loss until convergence, after which a new residual is computed for the next iteration. The key insight is the separation of timescales between fast directional and slow radial dynamics of dormant neurons near the origin (governed by the homogeneity degree κ of the activation), which justifies treating alignment as instantaneous for κ=2 and motivates the utility-based race condition. AGF provably converges to gradient flow for diagonal linear networks (Theorem 3.1), recovers greedy low-rank learning in fully connected linear networks and principal component regression in attention-only transformers, and predicts sequential Fourier feature emergence in quadratic networks trained on modular addition.

## Results

The paper's main results are theoretical rather than benchmark-based. Theorem 3.1 proves that AGF converges pointwise to the Pesme–Flammarion algorithm (and hence to gradient flow) in diagonal linear networks as α→0. Conjecture 4.1 predicts the exact sequence of loss levels ℓ⁽ᵏ⁾ = ½Σᵢ>ₖ μᵢ and jump times for stepwise singular value decomposition in fully connected linear networks, matching experiments in Figure 4. For modular addition (p=20), AGF predicts that a quadratic network learns Fourier frequencies in decreasing order of |x̂[ξ]|, with loss after k frequencies ℓ⁽ᵏ⁾ = Σᵢ>ₖ |x̂[ξᵢ]|²/p, and Figure 6 confirms this matches gradient flow dynamics. On CIFAR-10 (Figure 7), AGF tracks gradient flow loss curves closely at small hidden widths but the correspondence weakens as width increases and cost-minimization phases overlap.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical unification paper rather than a method-comparison paper, so there are no standard baseline comparisons. AGF provably recovers the Pesme–Flammarion [18] saddle-to-saddle algorithm for diagonal linear networks (Theorem 3.1), matching gradient flow exactly in the vanishing initialization limit. For fully connected linear networks, AGF reduces to the Greedy Low-Rank Learning (GLRL) algorithm of Li et al. [20], and Conjecture 4.1 extends their analysis to the non-commuting covariance case (Figure 4 shows good agreement). For attention-only linear transformers, AGF recovers the principal component regression sequence of Zhang et al. [32] (Equation 7, Figure 5). In the commuting case, Conjecture 4.1 reduces to the sequence proposed by Gidel et al. [19]. The novel contribution is the modular addition setting (Section 5), where AGF provides the first complete theoretical characterization of Fourier feature emergence, predicting both the order (decreasing |x̂[ξ]|) and the dynamics (jump times scaling as p^{3/2}/|x̂[ξ_{k+1}]|³), which prior work had only established empirically.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces Alternating Gradient Flows (AGF), a unified algorithmic framework that subsumes previously disparate saddle-to-saddle analyses: the sparse regression algorithm of Pesme and Flammarion for diagonal linear networks, the Greedy Low-Rank Learning procedure of Li et al. for fully connected linear networks, and the sequential principal component regression of Zhang et al. for attention-only linear transformers. Rather than deriving each result in isolation under architecture-specific assumptions, AGF provides a single two-step recipe (utility maximization over dormant neurons, cost minimization over active ones) that recovers all three as special cases. The framework is motivated by Bakhtin's theory of noisy heteroclinic networks and extends the directional-radial decomposition used in prior work on ReLU networks near the origin to a general setting with arbitrary homogeneous activations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AGF offers the first complete theoretical characterization of training dynamics for modular addition, showing that quadratic networks learn Fourier features in decreasing order of coefficient magnitude — a result that had only been observed empirically in mechanistic interpretability work. The unification is conceptually valuable, but the framework's generality remains unproven: convergence to gradient flow is established only for diagonal linear networks, and on natural data tasks (e.g., CIFAR-10) the stepwise structure that AGF predicts becomes invisible as width increases. Key open questions include whether AGF extends to deeper architectures, whether it captures population-level transitions in teacher-student or multi-index settings, and whether the vanishing-initialization limit is the right regime for understanding feature learning at practical scales.
