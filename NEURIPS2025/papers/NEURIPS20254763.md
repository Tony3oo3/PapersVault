## Problem

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods like LoRA suffer significant performance degradation under out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions, yet their robustness to distributional shifts remains largely unexplored. Existing Mixture-of-LoRA-Experts (MoPE) approaches using token-level routing overfit to surface-level patterns and fail to generalize, while sentence-level routing sacrifices in-distribution accuracy. The paper asks how to design a PEFT framework that simultaneously achieves strong ID performance and OOD robustness.

## Input / Output

The method operates on text inputs at two granularities: token-level hidden states from a frozen LLM backbone and sentence-level representations obtained via average pooling over those tokens. For NER, inputs are biomedical sentences producing structured JSON entity annotations; for SA, social-science text yields sentiment labels; for EQA, question-passage pairs yield extracted answer spans. The hierarchical router consumes both the pooled sentence embedding and individual token embeddings to compute per-token expert gating weights, with top-k sparsity (k=2) enforced at inference.

## Method

HiMoLE organizes LoRA experts into N Knowledge Competition Groups (KCGs), each containing M Knowledge Collaboration Experts (KCEs), where each KCE is a standard low-rank LoRA module. A two-level routing mechanism first uses a sentence-level linear router to compute coarse KCG relevance scores, then a token-level linear router refines per-token expert selection; the final gating is a softmax-normalized element-wise product of the two score vectors followed by top-k selection. Training proceeds in two stages: Stage 1 initializes each KCG by fine-tuning on a K-means-clustered sub-dataset to establish domain-specific inductive biases, and Stage 2 jointly optimizes all experts and routers with a composite loss combining task loss, an auxiliary load-balancing term, and a novel diversity loss that penalizes pairwise cosine similarity between KCG outputs to reduce redundancy. The paper further provides a theoretical analysis showing that hierarchical routing strictly increases expected pairwise gradient similarity (reducing gradient conflicts) compared to token-only routing, which in turn yields a tighter generalization error bound via gradient variance.

## Results

HiMoLE achieves the best performance across all three tasks (biomedical NER, social-science SA, and general-domain EQA) on both in-distribution and out-of-distribution data. On OOD NER, HiMoLE reaches 65.3 F1, outperforming the strongest baseline HydraLoRA (62.9 F1) by 2.4 points and MixLoRA (61.0 F1) by 4.3 points. On OOD SA, HiMoLE scores 38.9 EM versus 38.3 for MixLoRA/HydraLoRA and 35.5 for LoRA. On OOD EQA, HiMoLE attains 32.8 EM and 28.7 ROUGE-2, exceeding MixLoRA (31.4 EM) and HydraLoRA (30.4 EM). The paper reports improvements of up to 3.0% on ID and 5.0% on OOD over the best baseline, with the largest OOD gains in knowledge-intensive domains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HiMoLE consistently outperforms all three baselines (LoRA, MixLoRA, HydraLoRA) on every task and both ID and OOD splits. HydraLoRA is the strongest single baseline, reaching 77.3 F1 on NER ID and 62.9 F1 on NER OOD, but HiMoLE surpasses it by 0.6 and 2.4 F1 points respectively. MixLoRA, despite its multi-task design, shows notable OOD degradation: on NER OOD it scores 61.0 F1, actually below standard LoRA (61.9 F1), suggesting overfitting to token-level patterns. On SA OOD, HiMoLE's 38.9 EM edges out MixLoRA and HydraLoRA (both 38.3 EM) by 0.6 points, while on EQA OOD it leads MixLoRA by 1.4 EM points. The paper attributes the OOD gap to the lack of sentence-level semantic integration in token-level routing baselines, which causes expert misallocation under distributional shift. Notably, the OOD advantage of HiMoLE is most pronounced in knowledge-intensive domains (NER), where the improvement over the best baseline reaches 4.3 F1 points, versus 0.6 points in the less knowledge-dense SA task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Mixture-of-Parameter-Efficient-Experts (MoPE) paradigm—exemplified by MixLoRA and HydraLoRA—by introducing a hierarchical structure at both the expert level (Knowledge Competition Groups containing Knowledge Collaboration Experts) and the routing level (sentence-level coarse allocation followed by token-level refinement). It departs from prior MoPE work, which relies exclusively on token-level routing, by arguing that local token features are insufficient for robust expert selection under distributional shift. The two-stage training strategy with K-means-based subdomain initialization and a diversity-promoting loss further distinguishes HiMoLE from single-stage MoPE training, while the theoretical analysis connects hierarchical routing to gradient conflict reduction via a generalization bound.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap: PEFT methods like LoRA are widely deployed but their OOD fragility has been largely unexamined, particularly in knowledge-intensive domains like biomedicine. The hierarchical routing design principle—using global sentence semantics for coarse expert allocation and local token features for fine-grained refinement—offers a reusable architectural pattern for improving distributional robustness in sparse expert systems. However, the method still fails to outperform the base model on the hardest OOD subset (biomedical NER), and evaluation is limited to three NLP tasks with 7B/13B models, leaving open whether the approach scales to larger models or more complex multi-domain shifts. The moderate gains (3–5% over best baselines) suggest the hierarchical structure helps but is not a complete solution to OOD robustness in PEFT.
