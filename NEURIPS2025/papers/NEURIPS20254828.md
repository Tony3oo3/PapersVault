## Problem

In-context learning in LLMs is unstable and scales poorly with the number of demonstrations: performance is sensitive to demonstration selection and ordering, long prompts inflate inference costs and hit context-window limits, and existing latent-shift disentanglement methods rely on linear-attention approximations that manipulate individual attention heads or hidden states rather than capturing the full architectural dynamics of the transformer. The paper asks whether demonstration-induced latent shifts can be disentangled from query-induced shifts in a functional, output-level manner that avoids architectural intervention while remaining stable and composable.

## Input / Output

During training, the teacher model receives a concatenation of n labeled demonstrations and the query, while the student model receives only the query and a LoRA adapter (0.1–0.3% of total parameters). At inference, the student processes the query alone—no demonstrations in the prompt—producing a classification label or full token probability distribution over the vocabulary. Optionally, additional in-context demonstrations can be prepended at inference time to compose additively with the adapter's encoded shift, and multiple adapters trained on different demonstration subsets can be merged by summing their parameters to handle demonstration sets exceeding the context window.

## Method

WILDA (Weakly-supervised In-context Learning Disentanglement via Adapters) frames ICL as a source of weak supervision: a teacher LLM generates pseudo-labels on unlabeled queries by processing demonstrations + query jointly, and a student LLM with a lightweight LoRA adapter learns to reproduce those pseudo-labels using only the query input, minimizing cross-entropy over the full vocabulary distribution. Rather than approximating latent shifts through linear-attention decompositions of individual attention heads, WILDA captures the demonstration effect implicitly in the adapter weights, which encode the combined influence of all transformer components (attention, feed-forward, residual paths). Three training variants control demonstration dynamics—fixed (WILDA-F), order-shuffled per epoch (WILDA-S), and resampled per epoch (WILDA-R)—with WILDA-S providing the best stability. The method exhibits weak-to-strong generalization: the student surpasses the teacher through pseudo-label correction (refining noisy ICL outputs via local consistency, evidenced by a lower Lipschitz constant) and coverage expansion (generalizing beyond the demonstration-supported region in representation space). Adapter arithmetic enables scalable composition: separate adapters for demonstration subsets are merged by parameter summation, and the resulting adapter composes additively with additional in-context demonstrations at inference time.

## Results

On the 16-shot GLUE and MMLU benchmarks with Llama 3 (8B), WILDA-S achieves the best in-domain generalization across all nine datasets, with scores ranging from 64.3 (COLA) to 96.1 (SST), outperforming standard 16-shot ICL by 2.6–11.9 points (e.g., RTE: 86.0 vs. 75.1; MATH: 49.5 vs. 43.5). WILDA-S also exhibits the lowest standard deviation across 10 runs (e.g., 0.6 on RTE vs. 6.5 for n-shot ICL), indicating substantially improved stability. On the ARC-Challenge reasoning benchmark, WILDA-S reaches 59.2% accuracy versus 54.5% for 16-shot ICL. Adapter arithmetic with 8×16 demonstrations pushes Llama 3 to 92.8% on RTE and 93.0% on MISC, surpassing 32-shot ICL (75.3% RTE, 84.5% MISC).

## Baselines & Comparisons

WILDA-S outperforms all three baselines across every GLUE and MMLU dataset for both Llama 3 and Phi 3. Against PBFT [33], WILDA-S gains 2.8–12.5 points (e.g., RTE: 86.0 vs. 73.2; MATH: 49.5 vs. 44.0), with the largest margins on MMLU tasks. Against ICV [28], the advantage is even more pronounced on MMLU (MISC: 88.0 vs. 67.0; MATH: 49.5 vs. 41.5) and moderate on GLUE (RTE: 86.0 vs. 72.9). Against Batch-ICL [42], WILDA-S leads on 8 of 9 datasets (RTE: 86.0 vs. 77.8; MATH: 49.5 vs. 36.2), with Batch-ICL only competitive on MNLI (73.1 vs. 70.9). In terms of stability, WILDA-S reduces standard deviation by 50–90% relative to n-shot ICL (e.g., RTE: 0.6 vs. 6.5; QNLI: 2.2 vs. 5.5), a statistically significant improvement on all datasets. On the OOD setting (Table 3), WILDA-S also leads, e.g., QNLI→RTE: 69.0 vs. 66.3 (n-shot) and 65.3 (Batch-ICL), confirming that the gains are not limited to in-domain evaluation.

## Relation to Prior Work

WILDA departs from prior latent-shift disentanglement methods (ICV, Batch-ICL, function vectors) that intervene directly on attention heads or hidden states under linear-attention approximations, instead treating ICL outputs as weak supervision signals to train a LoRA adapter end-to-end. It builds on the mechanistic decomposition of ICL into zero-shot and demonstration-induced components (Dai et al., Todd et al.) but realizes the disentanglement functionally through a teacher-student objective rather than architecturally. The weak-to-strong generalization framing connects to the theoretical work of Lang et al. and Wei et al., while the adapter arithmetic for fusing demonstration subsets extends task-arithmetic ideas (Ilharco et al., Chitale et al.) to the ICL setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

WILDA offers a practical, parameter-efficient (0.1–0.3% of model parameters) route to stable, long-context ICL that avoids reprocessing demonstrations at inference time, with the added benefit of composable adapters for scaling beyond context-window limits. The empirical demonstration of weak-to-strong generalization—where a student trained on noisy ICL pseudo-labels consistently outperforms its teacher—provides concrete evidence for a theoretically predicted phenomenon in a practical LLM setting. Open questions include scalability to very large demonstration sets (beyond 128), applicability when unlabeled data is scarce, and whether the W2S effect persists for more complex, multi-step reasoning tasks beyond GLUE-style classification.
