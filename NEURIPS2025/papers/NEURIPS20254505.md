## Problem

Existing methods for constructing confidence intervals on covariate-response associations in spatial settings (OLS, sandwich estimators, spatial regression, debiasing) fail to achieve nominal coverage when the model is misspecified and inference is needed at fixed, nonrandom locations that differ from the observed data — a combination that is essentially always present in spatial problems. No prior method guarantees valid frequentist coverage under both misspecification and nonrandom locations simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254505/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes source triplets (spatial location, covariate vector, response) and target pairs (spatial location, covariate vector) along with a user-specified Lipschitz constant L and confidence level 1−α; the noise variance σ² is optional and estimated via a quadratic program when unknown. Notably, the source covariate values are not used in the inference for the target-conditional OLS coefficient — only the spatial locations enter the nearest-neighbor weight matrix and the Wasserstein bias bound. The output is a single confidence interval for the p-th coefficient of the target-conditional least-squares estimand, whose width reflects both a Wasserstein-based bias bound and a Gaussian randomness term.

## Method

The paper defines a target-conditional OLS estimand (the best linear approximation to the response at target locations) and estimates it by replacing unobserved target responses with 1-nearest-neighbor spatial imputations from source responses. The key insight is decomposing the estimation error into a deterministic bias term (from the spatial mismatch between source and target) and a mean-zero Gaussian term, then bounding the bias via the L-Lipschitz smoothness assumption using Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality, which reduces the supremum over L-Lipschitz functions to a Wasserstein-1 distance solvable as a linear program. A custom lemma then constructs the narrowest symmetric confidence interval valid for a normal distribution with unknown mean in [−B, B], where B is the Wasserstein bias bound. When σ² is unknown, it is estimated consistently by solving a quadratic program that minimizes squared residuals over all L-Lipschitz functions at the source locations, justified by the Kirszbraun extension theorem.

## Results

In the single-covariate simulation (N=300, M=100), the proposed method and GP BCIs are the only two methods to consistently achieve the nominal 95% coverage across all shift values, with the proposed method yielding substantially narrower confidence intervals than GP BCIs. In the three-covariate simulation (N=10,000, M=100), only the proposed method achieves nominal coverage; no competing method exceeds 30% coverage at any shift value. On the real tree-cover dataset (170 source, 133 target locations, 250 seeds), the proposed method is the only method to reach 95% coverage for all three regression coefficients (Aridity Index, Elevation, Slope), while for the Slope parameter every other method achieves at most 54% coverage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed Lipschitz-driven method decisively outperforms all five baselines on the primary metric of frequentist coverage. OLS and the sandwich estimator achieve near-zero coverage under any non-zero spatial shift because they assume the estimator is unbiased at target locations, producing extremely narrow (overconfident) intervals. The importance-weighted least squares (KDEIW) baseline, following Shimodaira's covariate-shift framework, approaches nominal coverage only at zero shift but degrades sharply under distribution shift because finite-sample density-ratio estimation cannot fully debias the estimator. Generalized least squares with restricted spatial regression (Hodges & Reich) also fails catastrophically under shift, again due to overconfident intervals. Gaussian process Bayesian credible intervals (GP BCIs) are the only competitor that also achieves nominal coverage in the single-covariate simulation, but their intervals are substantially wider than the proposed method's, and they do not scale to the N=10,000 three-covariate experiment. On the real tree-cover data, the gap is most dramatic for the Slope coefficient, where the proposed method reaches ~95% coverage while the best competing method (GP BCIs) reaches at most ~54%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the KNN imputation framework (Troyanskaya et al.) for point estimation and the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality / Wasserstein-1 distance machinery (Peyré & Cuturi) for bias bounding, combining them in a novel way to construct confidence intervals for spatial associations. It departs fundamentally from the i.i.d. assumption underlying OLS, sandwich estimators, importance weighting, and semiparametric debiasing, replacing it with a Lipschitz smoothness assumption on the spatial domain. The Kirszbraun extension theorem justifies reducing the infinite-dimensional Lipschitz constraint to a finite quadratic program for noise variance estimation. The target-conditional OLS estimand is explicitly noted as not previously proposed in spatial linear regression, positioning the work as a new formulation rather than an incremental improvement on existing spatial regression methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method to guarantee nominal frequentist coverage for spatial associations under both model misspecification and nonrandom, fixed locations — a combination that is essentially always present in real spatial problems (e.g., EPA monitoring stations, municipal health data). The practical impact is significant for environmental science, epidemiology, and economics, where policy decisions depend on valid uncertainty quantification for covariate-response relationships at locations without data. Key limitations include the requirement for a domain-informed Lipschitz constant, the homoskedastic Gaussian noise assumption, and the restriction to linear associations; the authors note extensions to GLMs and multivariate responses as future work. The method's reliance on a quadratic program for σ² estimation limits scalability to very large N, though a scalable 1-NN-based alternative is provided.
