## Problem

Existing EEG-based neural decoders suffer from two coupled limitations: they fail to generalize to unseen subjects due to individualized neural activity patterns, and they are locked to a single visual task (video or image), requiring separate architectures and retraining for each. No prior framework achieves zero-shot transfer across both subject and task boundaries simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255761/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes raw EEG recordings (62-channel at 1000 Hz from SEED-DV, or 32-channel from THINGS-EEG) preprocessed to 0.5–40 Hz and resampled to 200 Hz, along with a discrete task-type token (video or image). It produces either a multi-frame video reconstruction or a single static image, generated through an SDXL-based diffusion pipeline conditioned on four fused guidance signals: predicted keyframes via ControlNet, BLIP-2-derived text captions, learned dynamic motion patterns, and residual EEG features. The same trained weights handle both output modalities without architectural modification.

## Method

NEED comprises three tightly coupled components. First, the Individual Adaptation Module (IAM) normalizes subject-specific EEG patterns through a four-level hierarchical pipeline (signal, spectral, temporal, semantic) combined with adversarial subject disentanglement and KL-divergence regularization, pretrained on SEED, DEAP, and EEGEYENET. Second, the Dual-Stream EEG Encoder (DSGNet) processes spatial topology via spherical-harmonic embeddings and graph convolutions in parallel with temporal dynamics captured by multi-scale Riemann-Liouville fractional transforms and dilated convolutions, fused through cross-stream attention. Third, a dual-pathway understanding stage splits features into a Perception module (low-level motion/scene/temporal coherence via a Spatiotemporal Memory-Augmented Recurrent Network and Bidirectional Dynamic Contrastive loss) and a Semantic module (high-level concepts via CLIP-ViT-G/14 keyframe alignment, an LLM adapter, and Hierarchical Semantic Contrastive loss). The unified inference mechanism performs task-adaptive diffusion with conditional layer modulation (γ, β per layer indexed by task token) and softmax-weighted multi-guidance integration, enabling zero-shot cross-task generation by simply switching the task encoding while keeping all parameters fixed.

## Results

On SEED-DV video reconstruction, NEED achieves 0.898 2-way and 0.405 40-way classification accuracy, and 0.356 SSIM, outperforming the best EEG baseline EEG2video (0.852 / 0.340 / 0.300) and all fMRI-based methods. For zero-shot image reconstruction on THINGS-EEG (no fine-tuning), NEED attains 0.352 SSIM and 0.795 CLIP score, surpassing EEG-based baselines CognitionCapturer (0.347 SSIM, 0.715 CLIP) and ATM-S (0.330 SSIM, 0.786 CLIP). In the EEG classification benchmark (Table 1), DSGNet achieves 14.23% 40-class top-1 accuracy, a 101.8% relative improvement over the best baseline (BraVL at 7.05%), and 81.34% on the Human binary task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NEED outperforms all EEG-based video reconstruction baselines: EEG2video/GLMNet [26] (0.852→0.898 2-way, 0.340→0.405 40-way, 0.300→0.356 SSIM) and also surpasses fMRI-based video methods including Wang [47] (0.773), Kupershmidt [46] (0.771), MinD-Video [30] (0.839), and NeuroClips [45] (0.834) on 2-way accuracy, though NeuroClips retains a higher SSIM (0.390 vs. 0.356) due to fMRI's superior spatial resolution. For zero-shot image reconstruction on THINGS-EEG, NEED beats both EEG baselines—CognitionCapturer [44] (0.347 SSIM, 0.715 CLIP) and ATM-S [45] (0.330 SSIM, 0.786 CLIP)—on SSIM and CLIP, while trailing ATM-S slightly on SwAV (0.575 vs. 0.582). Against fMRI-based Mind's Eye [25], NEED wins on SSIM (0.352 vs. 0.317) and SwAV (0.575 vs. 0.382) but loses on CLIP (0.795 vs. 0.918) and Inception (0.742 vs. 0.929), reflecting the inherent information gap between EEG and fMRI. In the classification benchmark, DSGNet dominates all 14 baselines including ShallowNet/DeepNet [6], EEGNet [10], TSCNet [8], Conformer [48], BraVL [2], EEGPT [13], TSCInv [19], and SVM [58], with the largest margin (+101.8%) on 40-class top-1 accuracy. META-MEG [40] is outperformed on all four image reconstruction metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate research threads in EEG-based visual decoding: cross-subject generalization (drawn from domain adaptation work like Personal-zscore and plug-and-play adaptation) and visual reconstruction (from the EEG2video and Mind Reader lineages). Prior EEG reconstruction methods were either task-specific (image-only or video-only) or subject-specific, requiring retraining for new individuals or stimulus types. NEED repurposes components from the broader neural decoding literature—CLIP, SDXL, ControlNet, BLIP-2—within a novel EEG-specific encoder (DSGNet with spherical harmonic spatial embeddings and fractional-calculus temporal processing) and a hierarchical subject-adaptation module (IAM) pretrained across multiple datasets, positioning itself as the first framework to jointly address both subject and task generalization in a single zero-shot pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that a single model can generalize to unseen subjects (93.7% retention) and transfer from video to image reconstruction without fine-tuning (SSIM 0.352), this work points toward practical brain-computer interfaces that don't require per-user calibration or per-task retraining—two major barriers to real-world deployment. However, reconstruction quality remains far from photorealistic, the framework's complexity (IAM + DSGNet + PU + SU + task-adaptive diffusion with multiple guidance signals) raises questions about which components are truly necessary, and the zero-shot cross-task claim is validated on only one image dataset. Open questions include whether the approach scales to lower-density consumer EEG, whether the many novel components (fractional calculus, spherical harmonics, SMARN) each contribute meaningfully, and whether the cross-subject gains hold across recording sessions and experimental paradigms.
