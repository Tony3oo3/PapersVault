## Problem

In open-ended multi-agent environments without predefined roles or coordination rules, self-interested LLM agents face social dilemmas where individual optimization leads to suboptimal collective outcomes. Existing approaches—rule-oriented, role-oriented, and model-oriented—either compromise agent autonomy, limit adaptability, or assume goal alignment, leaving the critical challenge of credit assignment and incentive-aware pricing for heterogeneous objectives unaddressed.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes agent local states, planned actions, and task environment rules during real-time execution, and the full completed trajectory (states, actions, rewards over T steps) post-task. Outputs are structured as machine-readable negotiation messages using <s>...</s> tags for intent, pricing proposals, and accept/reject/counter responses, culminating in a cooperative strategy agreement and a final reward redistribution allocation expressed as percentage shares per agent. The Short-Term CoT produces qualitative externality indicators (positive/negative) and proposed price adjustments, while the Long-Term CoT produces quantitative Shapley value estimates and concrete redistribution offers.

## Method

Shapley-Coop is a zero-shot cooperative workflow integrating three modules: a structured negotiation protocol with standardized message formats, a Short-Term Shapley Chain-of-Thought that qualitatively assesses whether an agent's planned action creates positive or negative externalities for others (determining whether pricing is needed), and a Long-Term Shapley Chain-of-Thought that retrospectively computes Shapley values from the observed trajectory to determine how much price is owed. The key insight is decomposing the pricing mechanism into a forward-looking 'whether to price' heuristic (qualitative externality sign estimation via LLM reasoning) and a backward-looking 'how much to price' calculation (formal Shapley value aggregation over all coalition orderings). This creates a closed-loop where short-term pricing aligns heterogeneous goals during task execution, and long-term fair credit assignment sustains trust and incentive alignment across repeated interactions, all without any additional training on the target task.

## Results

In the Raid Battle game (Level 1), Shapley-Coop (LLM+SC) achieves reward allocation accuracy with maximum deviation of +0.48% from the Shapley-value-based expected allocation, compared to LLM+STS which shows a −4.52% deficit for the primary taunter. Across all three difficulty levels, LLM+SC keeps per-agent allocation errors within ±2%, while LLM+STS exhibits systematic underestimation of up to −6.26% (Level 2) and −3.70% (Level 3). In the ChatDEV software development simulation, the gap between data-driven Weighted Earned Value (WEV) and human-assigned rewards is below 6% for most roles, with hands-on roles (Programmer, Reviewer) showing near-perfect alignment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four configurations: LLM-only (no cooperation), LLM+NEG (standard negotiation), LLM+STS (short-term Shapley CoT only), and LLM+SC (full Shapley-Coop). LLM+SC decisively outperforms LLM+NEG in the Raid Battle game: while LLM+NEG agents default to damage-dealing (ignoring taunt/heal), LLM+SC achieves balanced role distribution with all four agents contributing to taunt and healing. In credit assignment accuracy, LLM+SC reduces maximum per-agent allocation error from 4.52–6.26% (LLM+STS) to 0.17–1.93% across Raid Battle levels. In the Escape Room, LLM+NEG enables only occasional cooperation with unfair first-mover advantage, while LLM+SC achieves consistent Pareto-superior outcomes with equal 4.5/4.5 splits. A Banzhaf-value ablation (Appendix F) confirms that Shapley is essential: in Raid Battle Level 2, Banzhaf under-allocates Agent 4 by 11.63% (14% vs. 25.63% expected), and in ChatDEV it misassigns the Programmer's share from 35% down to 25%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Shapley value from cooperative game theory [4] as a principled credit-assignment tool, but departs from prior LLM multi-agent work by introducing a 'pricing-based perspective' that explicitly frames coordination as an economic transaction problem. It positions itself against three existing paradigms—rule-oriented, role-oriented, and model-oriented methods—arguing that none adequately handle heterogeneous goals in open-ended settings. The framework also contrasts with MARL credit-assignment approaches (e.g., LIO, LOPT) by operating in a zero-shot, reasoning-based paradigm rather than requiring extensive training, and draws on economic mechanisms (Pigovian taxes, Coase theorem) to motivate its negotiation protocol design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap in LLM multi-agent systems: how to align self-interested agents without imposing rigid roles or assuming goal alignment, which is increasingly relevant as LLMs enter open-ended collaborative workflows. Key limitations include the inability to dynamically adjust pricing during collaboration, the communication/token overhead of negotiation, and the scalability trade-off where the Shapley approximation may not satisfy the efficiency axiom. Open questions include whether adaptive real-time incentive mechanisms can be developed, how the approach scales beyond four agents, and whether the negotiation protocol's token cost can be reduced without sacrificing fairness.
