## Problem

Sample-wise learning curves are widely assumed to be well-behaved (monotone and convex), yet prior estimates from LCDB 1.0 suggested only ~8% of curves deviate from this assumption. The authors argue this conclusion was premature because LCDB 1.0 suffered from low anchor resolution, no feature scaling, missing data, and a data-leakage issue, all of which can mask or create spurious ill-behavior. The paper asks: how many learning curves are *significantly* ill-behaved when measured with proper statistical rigor, and which learners are responsible?

## Input / Output

The database ingests 265 OpenML tabular classification datasets (72 from the curated CC-18 benchmark) and trains 32 learners at training-set-size anchors defined by n_k = ⌈16·2^(k/8)⌉, yielding four times the resolution of LCDB 1.0. Each learner is evaluated under three feature-scaling settings (none, min-max, standardization) and two data-leakage variants (preprocessor fitted on full data vs. train-only), with 5×5 inner/outer seeds for 25 repeats per anchor. Outputs include per-anchor performance (error rate, F1, AUC, log-loss), binary ill-behavior labels (non-monotone, non-convex, peaking, dipping) with Bonferroni-corrected significance, continuous violation-error magnitudes (ε_mono, ε_conv), parametric curve-fitting MSE, and Successive Halving model-selection regret.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a statistically rigorous detection framework for learning-curve ill-behavior that differs from LCDB 1.0 in three key ways: (1) all anchor pairs (not just consecutive ones) are compared, catching violations over longer ranges; (2) convexity is evaluated on the log-scale anchor spacing rather than treating anchors as linearly spaced; and (3) each candidate violation is confirmed via a paired one-sided t-test with Bonferroni correction across all N(N−1)/2 pairs (monotonicity) or N(N−1)(N−2)/6 triplets (convexity), ensuring detections are significant at α=0.05. Violation errors ε_mono and ε_conv quantify the magnitude of the worst deviation. The database itself adds four modern learners (CatBoost, TabNet, RealMLP, TabPFN v2), mixed Naive Bayes variants, and documents all missing values. Downstream analyses use parametric models (POW4, MMF4, WBL4) for curve fitting and Successive Halving for multi-fidelity model selection to demonstrate practical impact of ill-behavior.

## Results

On LCDB 1.1 CC-18 (72 datasets, no feature scaling), 14.9% of learning curves are significantly ill-behaved (non-monotone or non-convex), with 9.9% non-monotone and 11.5% non-convex. Peaking accounts for 5.0% and dipping for 6.1% of curves. Using the less conservative Holm's method, this rises to 18.2%. Downstream, parametric curve fitting (POW4) shows mean log-MSE over 10× larger on ill-behaved curves, and Successive Halving model selection fails to select the top-2 learner in ~80% of cases when curves frequently cross (vs. near-perfect selection when they rarely cross).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is against LCDB 1.0 (Mohr et al., ECML PKDD 2022), which reported that 'the large majority of learning curves is largely well-behaved.' LCDB 1.1 nearly doubles the estimated ill-behaved fraction: 14.9% (CC-18, no FS) vs. 8.1% (LCDB 1.0, interpolated to match resolution). The authors attribute this gap to three methodological improvements: (1) 4× higher anchor resolution (n_k = ⌈16·2^{k/8}⌉ vs. ⌈16·2^{k/2}⌉), (2) comparing all anchor pairs rather than only consecutive ones, and (3) incorporating the logarithmic anchor spacing into the convexity test. LCDB 1.0's 8.1% barely exceeds the 5% false-positive floor, whereas LCDB 1.1's 14.9% is clearly significant. Feature scaling (min-max, standardization) reduces ill-behavior only marginally (e.g., 14.9% → 11.2% with standardization on CC-18), and for some learners (Nearest Centroid, certain Naive Bayes variants) scaling actually increases ill-behavior. The most ill-behaved learners are Sigmoid SVM (58.1%), QDA (45.7%), LDA (37.7%), and TabNet (74.3%), while tree-based ensembles (CatBoost 1.5%, Decision Tree 1.5%) and TabPFN v2 (1.5%) are the most well-behaved.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends LCDB 1.0 (Mohr et al., ECML PKDD 2022), the first large-scale database of sample-wise learning curves, by increasing anchor resolution fourfold, adding modern tabular learners (CatBoost, TabNet, RealMLP, TabPFN v2), incorporating feature scaling variants, and fixing data-leakage and missing-data issues. It departs from LCDB 1.0's detection methodology by comparing all anchor pairs (not just consecutive ones), incorporating the logarithmic anchor scale into convexity checks, and applying Bonferroni-corrected paired t-tests to ensure statistical significance. The broader positioning draws on the learning curve shape literature (Viering & Loog 2022), the peaking/dipping phenomena identified in toy settings, and multi-fidelity optimization (Successive Halving), but the paper's central argument is that LCDB 1.0's conclusion of 'largely well-behaved' curves was premature due to insufficient resolution and statistical rigor.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that approximately 15% of learning curves are significantly ill-behaved—nearly double the prior estimate—challenges the widely-held assumption that sample-wise learning curves are monotone and convex, with direct consequences for parametric curve fitting (MSE increases >10× on ill-behaved curves) and multi-fidelity model selection (crossing curves degrade Successive Halving performance). LCDB 1.1, with 265 datasets, 32 learners, three feature scalings, and 25 repeats per curve, provides a challenging and reproducible benchmark for future work on learning curve modeling and model selection. Key open questions include whether ill-behavior persists under hyperparameter tuning (computationally expensive to verify), the root causes of ill-behavior (particularly phase transitions in TabNet and MLP), and whether findings generalize beyond classification error rate to other metrics and regression settings.
