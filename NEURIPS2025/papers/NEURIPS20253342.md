## Problem

Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) have proven effective at decomposing LLM intermediate representations into interpretable, monosemantic features, but no comparable analysis existed for text-to-image diffusion models. This paper asks whether SAEs can learn interpretable features from the internal representations of a few-step diffusion model (SDXL Turbo) that causally influence image generation, and whether features learned on a single denoising step generalize to multi-step settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253342/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The SAEs are trained on 16×16 spatial feature maps (384M vectors per block) extracted from the residual updates of four cross-attention transformer blocks in SDXL Turbo's U-net, collected over 1.5M LAION-COCO prompts in 1-step mode. At inference, interventions specify a feature index, a binary spatial mask (drawn interactively in SAEPaint), and a scalar strength, which are added to or subtracted from the block's update at each spatial location. For RIEBench evaluation, features are selected via grounded SAM2 segmentation masks and transferred between paired forward passes, with performance measured by LPIPS distance to the original and CLIP similarity increase with the edit prompt.

## Method

The paper trains one SAE (encoder + decoder pair) per transformer block on the residual update ΔD = D^out − D^in, using TopK activation (k=160) with 5120 features (expansion factor 4) and an auxiliary loss for dead features, following the methodology of Gao et al. [19]. SAEs are applied to four blocks (down.2.1, mid.0, up.0.0, up.0.1) identified via ablation as having the highest causal impact on generation. The key finding is that SAEs trained exclusively on 1-step SDXL Turbo activations retain high explained variance and semantic feature overlap across 4-step Turbo and 20-step vanilla SDXL, and also transfer to FLUX Schnell (a diffusion transformer), demonstrating that one-step denoising suffices for learning meaningful features. For classifier-free guidance in multi-step SDXL, features are added to the text-conditioned pass and subtracted from the unconditional pass to avoid cancellation. The authors introduce RIEBench, a representation-based image editing benchmark built on PIEBench's nine edit categories with grounded SAM2 masks, to quantitatively measure feature sensitivity, specificity, and causality.

## Results

On RIEBench (9 edit categories from PIEBench), SAE features (40–640 transported) achieve LPIPS-vs-CLIP-similarity tradeoffs comparable to neuron interventions (12,800–204,800 neurons) and activation steering, while requiring orders of magnitude fewer features. SAE feature specificity (CLIP similarity among top-activating images) ranges from 0.70–0.76 across the four transformer blocks, significantly above the 0.57 random-image baseline. Features trained on SDXL Turbo's 1-step process generalize to 4-step and 20-step SDXL without retraining, retaining high explained variance and causal influence. For FLUX Schnell, multi-layer SAE interventions (layers 18–56) outperform single-layer interventions across most edit categories.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three intervention methods—SAE features, individual neurons, and raw activation steering—across nine PIEBench edit categories. In most categories (change object, add object, delete object, background), all three methods achieve similar LPIPS/CLIP tradeoffs with no statistically significant differences. However, for change content, change color, change material, and change style, both SAE features and steering are significantly more effective than neurons. For change object at higher LPIPS budgets, neurons slightly dominate. The critical practical advantage of SAEs is efficiency: they match or exceed neuron performance while transporting 40–640 features versus 12,800–204,800 neurons (2–3 orders of magnitude fewer). On the change pose task, none of the methods succeed, which the authors attribute to the inherent difficulty of pose editing via constant-vector interventions on masked regions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the sparse autoencoder (SAE) methodology developed for LLM interpretability (Bricken et al., Cunningham et al., Gao et al.) into the text-to-image diffusion model setting, applying it to residual-stream updates of cross-attention transformer blocks in SDXL Turbo's U-net. It builds on PIEBench for edit-category prompts and grounded SAM2 for spatial feature selection, and is conceptually adjacent to GAN dissection (Bau et al.) which performed neuron-level interventions on GANs, and to prior layer-specialization studies in diffusion models (Voynov et al., Agarwal et al., Zhang et al.) that used handcrafted attributes rather than learned sparse features. The key departure is that no prior work had trained SAEs on the intermediate representations of a modern text-to-image diffusion model, and the paper introduces RIEBench as a dedicated benchmark for evaluating representation-level image editing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that SAEs trained on SDXL Turbo's 1-step generation generalize without retraining to 4-step SDXL Turbo, vanilla multi-step SDXL, and even FLUX Schnell is practically significant: it means interpretable features can be extracted at a fraction of the computational cost and then deployed across a large family of models (the authors note ~8,700 SDXL and ~36,000 FLUX adaptations on HuggingFace). RIEBench provides the first quantitative benchmark for representation-based image editing in diffusion models, and the revealed block specialization (composition → detail → style) offers a concrete mechanistic hypothesis for how distilled diffusion models organize their forward pass. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger diffusion-transformer architectures with more layers, how to capture cross-block compositional features, and whether the one-step-to-multi-step generalization holds for non-distilled training from scratch.
