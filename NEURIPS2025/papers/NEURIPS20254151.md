## Problem

Two-player zero-sum partially observable stochastic games (zs-POSGs) lack a principled connection to dynamic programming techniques developed for fully observable stochastic games. Prior reduction attempts—the occupancy Markov game and the public-belief alternating Markov game—each violate at least one of the three criteria for a lossless reduction (value preservation, equilibrium correspondence, information structure equivalence), leaving the field restricted to ad hoc algorithms with no systematic pathway for transferring DP theory and scalable solution methods.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a finite-horizon zs-POSG specification: a set of hidden states, per-player private actions and observations, a public observation channel, a joint transition-observation function, a stage reward, an initial belief distribution, a discount factor, and a planning horizon. It produces occupancy-set dependent focal policies (mappings from each player's local occupancy set to a decision rule over private histories), the corresponding value function over uninformed occupancy states, and an explicit exploitability bound ε that quantifies worst-case suboptimality against a best-responding opponent. The exploitability bound is expressed in closed form as a function of the ℓ₁-density of sampled marginal occupancy states, the reward magnitude, the discount factor, and the horizon length.

## Method

The paper introduces the first lossless reduction from any zs-POSG to a transition-independent zero-sum stochastic game, where each player's local state is an occupancy set (the collection of uninformed occupancy states consistent with their own decision-rule history) and the global state is the intersection of both players' occupancy sets. Because each local state evolves independently of the opponent, the reduced game admits a hierarchy of planners—focal, uninformed, informed, and marginal—each operating at a different level of information access, with the value function proven to be uniformly continuous across uninformed occupancy states. This uniform continuity enables a point-based value iteration algorithm that samples marginal occupancy states, extracts greedy decision rules via linear programs whose size scales with the number of sampled points and reachable histories, and monotonically improves the value function at each backup. Two pruning strategies (dominated linear-function removal and redundant informed-state discarding) are incorporated for scalability, and the resulting focal policy's exploitability is bounded by O(δ/(1−γ)²) where δ is the maximum ℓ₁-distance from any reachable marginal state to the sample set.

## Results

Across all six benchmark families (Adversarial Tiger, Competitive Tiger, Recycling, Mabc, Matching Pennies, and three Pursuit-Evasion variants) at horizons ℓ ∈ {2,3,4,5,7,10}, at least one PBVI variant achieves the lowest exploitability in every setting where a baseline completes. PBVI₃ is the most reliable, e.g. reaching ε = 0.00 on Adversarial Tiger (ℓ=4,7) and Competitive Tiger (ℓ=4), while HSVI times out (OOT) on all benchmarks beyond ℓ=4 and CFR+ runs out of memory (OOM) on most instances at ℓ ≥ 4. On Matching Pennies (ℓ=10), all three PBVI variants converge to v(b) = 1.80, matching CFR+ (ε = 0.06) while HSVI fails to complete.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three PBVI variants (PBVI₁ without pruning, PBVI₂ and PBVI₃ with bounded pruning) against two baselines: HSVI [Delage et al., 2023] and CFR+ [Tammelin, 2014]. HSVI's backup operator grows exponentially with the horizon, causing it to time out (2-hour limit) on every benchmark at ℓ ≥ 4 except Matching Pennies, where its compact structure allows completion; on Matching Pennies (ℓ=4) it ties PBVI₃ at ε = 0.01. CFR+ is sensitive to history-space size: it performs competitively on compact games like Matching Pennies (ε = 0.01 at ℓ=4, 0.06 at ℓ=10) but exhausts memory on all other benchmarks at ℓ ≥ 4, including Competitive Tiger at ℓ=4 where the local history set is already large. In every completed comparison, at least one PBVI variant achieves strictly lower exploitability than both baselines; PBVI₃ is the most consistent winner, achieving ε = 0.00 on Adversarial Tiger (ℓ=4,7), Competitive Tiger (ℓ=4), and Mabc (ℓ=4,5,7), while the baselines either fail to complete or report ε ≥ 0.01. The only notable exceptions are Competitive Tiger at ℓ=4,5 where PBVI₂ (not PBVI₃) achieves the lowest ε, and Mabc at ℓ=7 where all three PBVI variants tie at ε = 0.00 with CFR+ (which also reports 0.00 before OOM at larger horizons).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the successful line of lossless reductions from common-payoff POSGs to fully observable surrogates (Szer et al. 2005; Dibangoye et al. 2013a, 2016; Oliehoek et al. 2008, 2013) into the adversarial zero-sum setting, where all prior attempts (OMG by Wiggers et al. 2016; PuB-AMG by Sokota et al. 2023) failed to satisfy the equilibrium correspondence or value preservation criteria. It also generalises the transition-independence principle, previously exploited in cooperative Dec-MDPs (Becker et al. 2003, 2004; Dibangoye et al. 2012, 2013b, 2014), to zero-sum stochastic games, and adapts point-based value iteration from POMDPs (Pineau et al. 2003) and one-sided POSGs (Horák et al. 2017; Horák and Bošanský 2019) to the fully adversarial two-player case via a novel planner hierarchy over occupancy sets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The lossless reduction provides the first principled bridge for transferring the full apparatus of stochastic-game dynamic programming (Bellman equations, minimax theorems, value iteration) into partially observable adversarial settings, dissolving a long-standing gap that had confined zs-POSG solvers to ad hoc algorithmic designs. The uniform continuity of the value function and the explicit exploitability bound (Theorem 7) give practitioners a concrete handle on solution quality. Key limitations include the worst-case growth of linear-program size with the planning horizon and the weakening of formal guarantees when both pruning strategies are combined; open questions include compact representations, regret-minimisation-based local updates, and whether the framework can be unified across cooperative, competitive, and mixed-motive POSGs.
