## Problem

The Ecosystem Demography (ED) model is a theory-based carbon cycle model widely used in the Global Carbon Budget and NASA's Carbon Monitoring System, but its ~812 CPU-day computational cost for a single global 0.5° run over 40 years severely limits high-resolution forecasting. While ML emulators have accelerated weather forecasting thanks to open benchmark datasets like WeatherBench, no publicly available ML-ready dataset exists for global forest carbon dynamics, and the 100+ heterogeneous input variables required by ED make independent data construction prohibitively complex for ML researchers.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250261/cropped_page4_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The dataset provides 812,280 independent time-series sequences (54,152 global land locations × 15 seed tree ages) at 0.5° resolution spanning 40 years (1981–2021), with 12 monthly sub-steps per year and 136 input variables per step (meteorological forcings from MERRA-2, hourly CO₂ from NOAA CarbonTracker, and soil hydraulic properties from ROSETTA). The forecasting task takes the input tensor X ∈ R^(N×T×M×D) plus an initial target vector v ∈ R^(N×7) and sequentially predicts 7 output variables per year (vegetation height, AGB, soil carbon, LAI, GPP, NPP, Rh). Sequences are kept independent rather than organized as spatial images to preserve consistency with the ED model's per-site propagation, and three evaluation scenarios (overall 1/16-sampling, Köppen-Geiger climate zones, and forest-age strata) plus two new metrics (cumulative error at the final step and year-over-year delta error) are defined to reflect downstream monitoring needs.

## Method

CarbonGlobe is constructed in three stages: (1) collecting and preprocessing 100+ input variables from MERRA-2, NOAA CarbonTracker, and ROSETTA at 0.5° global resolution; (2) running the calibrated ED v3.0 model on an HPC cluster (~812 CPU days) to generate 40-year output sequences for 15 seed tree ages per location; and (3) formatting the data into ML-ready tensors with standardized train/test splits and evaluation scenarios. For benchmarking, eight time-series forecasting models (LSTM, LSTNet, DeepED, Transformer, Informer, DLinear, Crossformer, TimeXer) are trained under a unified protocol (Adam, lr=1e-4, batch 1200, 60 epochs, 1/16 spatial sampling for training) with a shared noise-perturbation strategy that injects small random-walk noise (σ=1e-4) into initial target variables during training to mitigate error accumulation in long-horizon autoregressive forecasting. The paper introduces two problem-driven metrics—cumulative error (L2 norm at the final step) and delta error (mean L2 norm of year-over-year prediction differences)—designed with domain experts to capture error accumulation and inter-annual variability that standard RMSE/MAE obscure, and evaluates models across overall, climate-zone, and forest-age scenarios.

## Results

On CarbonGlobe (0.5° global, 40-year, 812,280 sequences), DeepED achieves the best RMSE on four of seven target variables: Height (1.900), AGB (0.491), SC (0.660), and LAI (0.416), substantially outperforming the next-best models (e.g., 1.900 vs. 2.887 for Height). Transformer and Informer lead on the three flux variables: GPP (0.217/0.234), NPP (0.107/0.116), and Rh (0.121/0.124). DLinear performs worst across all variables (e.g., Height RMSE 5.731, ~3× worse than DeepED), while TimeXer shows particularly large errors on flux variables (GPP RMSE 0.972, ~4.5× worse than Transformer). Inference time for all ML emulators is 1–1.5 hours on a single RTX A4500 GPU, versus ~812 CPU days for the ED model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks eight time-series forecasting models on CarbonGlobe, revealing a clear split: the ED-specific emulator DeepED dominates the four 'stock' variables (Height, AGB, SC, LAI) with RMSE values roughly 30–50% lower than the next-best generic models (e.g., Height 1.900 vs. LSTNet 2.887; AGB 0.491 vs. Transformer 1.154), while Transformer and Informer lead on the three 'flux' variables (GPP, NPP, Rh) where seasonality and climate-driven variability matter more (e.g., GPP 0.217 vs. DeepED 0.258; NPP 0.107 vs. DeepED 0.127). Crossformer achieves several runner-up positions but never clearly surpasses Transformer or Informer. DLinear, despite its simplicity, is the worst performer across all seven variables (Height RMSE 5.731, ~3× DeepED), and TimeXer is particularly poor on flux variables (GPP 0.972, ~4.5× Transformer), suggesting its exogenous-variable attention mechanism is ill-suited to this problem. The authors note that the complementary strengths of DeepED (monotonic stock variables) and Transformer/Informer (periodic flux variables) reflect the heterogeneous temporal patterns in carbon dynamics, and that the proposed delta and cumulative error metrics sometimes reorder model rankings relative to standard RMSE/MAE.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by constructing the first global-scale, ML-ready dataset for forest carbon dynamics forecasting, drawing conceptual inspiration from ML-ready benchmark datasets in weather and climate (WeatherBench 2, ClimSim, ClimateSet) and the broader paradigm of deep learning emulators for computationally expensive theory-based models (FourCastNet, GraphCast). The only prior deep learning emulator for forest carbon dynamics (DeepED, Wang et al. 2023) was limited to the Northeastern US and did not release a dataset. CarbonGlobe repurposes the well-established Ecosystem Demography (ED) model as a ground-truth generator, integrating 100+ heterogeneous input variables (MERRA-2 meteorology, NOAA CO2, ROSETTA soil) into a standardized time-series format, and positions standard time-series forecasting architectures (LSTM, Transformer, Informer, Crossformer, DLinear, TimeXer) as candidate emulators rather than proposing a new architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CarbonGlobe addresses a critical gap: the ED model, which underpins the Global Carbon Budget and NASA's Carbon Monitoring System, requires ~812 CPU days for a single global 40-year run, making high-resolution or scenario-based forecasting impractical. By providing a ready-to-train dataset with problem-driven metrics (cumulative and delta error) and three evaluation scenarios (overall, climate-zone, forest-age), the paper enables the ML community to develop emulators that reduce inference to ~1–1.5 hours on a single GPU. Key open questions include whether emulators trained on historical data (1981–2021) can generalize to future climate scenarios (CMIP-6), whether the 1/16 training sampling ratio is optimal for spatial generalization, and whether the current autoregressive formulation can be replaced by non-autoregressive approaches to further reduce error accumulation over multi-decade horizons.
