## Problem

Existing neural forecasting architectures require extensive depth and hyperparameter search for each new dataset, embed interpretable structures (e.g., Koopman eigenmodes) inaccessibly within network layers, and treat time series forecasting, chaos prediction, and fluid dynamics as separate problems. No single constrained design simultaneously eliminates depth as a hyperparameter, provides directly extractable spatiotemporal eigenmodes, and generalizes across statistical and physical domains.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252970/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

FlowMixer takes a multivariate time series tensor X ∈ R^{n_t × n_f} (padded with historical data to ensure square mixing matrices), a 32-step chaotic state vector, or 64 consecutive 2D vorticity snapshots (400×160 grid) as input, and produces a same-shape output tensor whose last h rows contain the forecast. For chaotic systems, 32-step predictions are compounded to 1024 steps (~9–10 Lyapunov times). A distinguishing output is the set of Kronecker-Koopman eigenmodes Φ_{i,j} = q_i ⊗ p_j, directly extractable via standard eigendecomposition of W_t and W_f, enabling algebraic horizon modification (interpolation/extrapolation) without retraining.

## Method

FlowMixer is a single-layer architecture F(X) = φ⁻¹(W_t φ(X) W_fᵀ) where φ is a reversible normalization (RevIN or time-dependent TD-RevIN), W_t is a non-negative quadratic time-mixing matrix (αI + W⊙W, analogous to fractional differencing), and W_f is a stochastic feature-mixing matrix derived from static attention. The key theoretical property is semi-group closure: composing any number of FlowMixers with the same φ yields an equivalent single-layer FlowMixer, eliminating depth as a hyperparameter entirely. In vectorized form, the mixing operation becomes (W_f ⊗ W_t) vec(X), whose eigendecomposition yields Kronecker-Koopman eigenmodes that couple spatial and temporal patterns, enabling continuous algebraic horizon scaling via eigenvalue exponentiation. For chaotic systems, a Semi-Orthogonal Basic Reservoir (SOBR) replaces φ with a random semi-orthogonal dimensional lifting S∘φ, expanding the representation space while preserving the architectural constraints.

## Results

On the seven standard long-horizon forecasting benchmarks (ETTh1, ETTh2, ETTm1, ETTm2, Weather, Electricity, Traffic) across horizons 96–720, FlowMixer achieves best or second-best MSE in the majority of 28 dataset-horizon combinations using a single mixing layer. Headline MSE values include 0.159 (ETTm2, h=96), 0.143 (Weather, h=96), 0.131 (Electricity, h=96), and 0.434 (Traffic, h=720). Wilcoxon signed-rank tests confirm statistically significant MSE improvements (p<0.05) over all six primary baselines, with average improvements ranging from 0.93% (vs. Chimera) to 12.78% (vs. iTransformer). For chaotic attractors (Lorenz, Rössler, Aizawa) and 2D turbulent flows (cylinder Re=150, NACA airfoil Re=1000), FlowMixer achieves ~4–5% relative l² error on vorticity fields and maintains consistent trajectory accuracy over ~10 Lyapunov times.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the six primary baselines in Table 1, FlowMixer achieves statistically significant MSE superiority (p<0.05, Wilcoxon) over all: it wins 28/28 MSE comparisons vs. TimeMixer++ (avg. +4.98%), iTransformer (+12.78%), and CycleNet (+10.62%), 27/28 vs. Ada-MSHyper (+6.20%), 23/28 vs. TSMixer (+3.17%), and 18/28 vs. Chimera (+0.93%, the closest competitor). On MAE, FlowMixer is significant vs. iTransformer, Ada-MSHyper, CycleNet, and TSMixer but not vs. Chimera (p=0.099) or TimeMixer++ (p=0.236), where the two methods are near-tied. In the extended Table 2, FlowMixer dominates older architectures (N-HITS, FEDformer, Autoformer, DLinear) by wide margins (e.g., 0.355 vs. 0.475 MSE on ETTh1 h=96 vs. N-HITS) and substantially outperforms Koopman-based methods KooPA and KNF (e.g., 0.355 vs. 0.371/0.975 on ETTh1 h=96). For chaotic attractor prediction (Figure 3), FlowMixer with SOBR matches or slightly outperforms Reservoir Computing [Pathak 2018] and N-BEATS [Oreshkin 2019] across Lorenz, Rössler, and Aizawa systems, with N-BEATS showing notable divergence on Rössler and RC struggling on the Lorenz z-component. For 2D turbulent flows, FlowMixer achieves ~3.3e-3 MSE (cylinder) and 2e-3–8e-3 MSE (airfoil), competitive with physics-informed approaches in the literature while using standard SGD without explicit conservation constraints.

## Relation to Prior Work

FlowMixer extends the all-MLP mixing paradigm of TSMixer by imposing non-negativity and shape-preserving constraints that yield a semi-group composition property, effectively eliminating depth as a hyperparameter. The Kronecker-Koopman eigenmode framework draws on Dynamic Mode Decomposition and Koopman operator theory but departs from prior Koopman-based neural methods (Koopa, KNF) by making eigenmodes directly extractable through standard eigendecomposition rather than embedded within network layers. The SOBR component repurposes reservoir computing principles (Pathak et al.) into a simplified, non-trainable semi-orthogonal projection that integrates with the mixing architecture, while the reversible normalization extends RevIN with time-dependent parameters enabled by the shape-preserving design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The semi-group property offering a principled elimination of depth search addresses a genuine deployment bottleneck across all existing forecasting architectures, and the directly extractable Kronecker-Koopman eigenmodes provide an interpretability layer that Koopman-based neural methods lack. However, the cubic computational complexity in sequence length and the absence of explicit physical conservation limits practical scaling to very long sequences or high-resolution fluid simulations. Open questions include whether sparsification can preserve the semi-group structure at scale, whether the algebraic horizon modification (Eq. 12) remains accurate under distribution shift, and how the architecture performs when the single-layer assumption is stressed by highly non-stationary or multi-scale phenomena beyond the tested benchmarks.
