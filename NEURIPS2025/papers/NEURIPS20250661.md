## Problem

Existing goal-conditioned RL methods for learning temporal distances achieve either stitching (finding paths shorter than those in the data) or long-horizon estimation, but not both: Q-learning-based methods stitch but accumulate compounding errors, Monte Carlo contrastive methods handle long horizons but cannot find optimal paths, and quasimetric network methods stitch architecturally but fail in stochastic or off-policy settings. The paper asks whether an additional invariance beyond the triangle inequality can be identified to recover optimal temporal distances from Monte Carlo estimates within a quasimetric architecture.

## Input / Output

The method consumes offline datasets of (state, action, next_state, goal) tuples where goals are sampled K steps ahead with K ~ Geom(1−γ), supporting both low-dimensional state vectors (up to 21-dim) and RGB images processed through an impala-small visual encoder. It produces a goal-conditioned policy mapping (state, goal) → action, and a learned quasimetric temporal distance d_θ parameterized by an MRN with 8 ensemble components over 512-dim latent representations. The distance is used both for policy extraction (minimizing d_θ((s,π(s,g)), g)) and as a learned representation of goal-reaching difficulty.

## Method

TMD learns a quasimetric distance d_θ via MRN parameterization that enforces the triangle inequality architecturally, then applies two additional invariance constraints on top of a backward NCE contrastive initialization: (i) action invariance, which forces d(s,(s,a))=0, and (ii) temporal invariance, a SARSA-style Bellman backup that regresses e^{−d((s,a),g)} onto E_{P(s'|s,a)}[e^{log γ − d(s',g)}] using a custom Bregman divergence D_T(d,d')=exp(d−d')−d for stable gradients. The key theoretical insight is that the SARSA (on-policy) backup operator, when combined with the quasimetric projection and action invariance, converges to the optimal successor distance d_SD* even when initialized from a suboptimal behavioral policy's contrastive estimate, effectively turning on-policy SARSA into an off-policy algorithm through the metric constraints. Policy extraction minimizes the learned distance over actions with a behavioral cloning regularization term, and the overall loss weights the contrastive and invariance components via a hyperparameter ζ.

## Results

On the OGBench benchmark (16 environments, 6 seeds, success rate aggregated over 5 goals each), TMD achieves the best or near-best performance on 13 of 16 tasks. On stitching tasks, TMD outperforms CRL by 2–3× (e.g., 68.5% vs. 36.2% on humanoidmaze_medium_stitch) and QRL by 3–4× (68.5% vs. 18.0%). On stochastic teleport environments, TMD outperforms both CRL and QRL by over 3× (29.3% vs. 4.1% and 8.6% on pointmaze_teleport_stitch). On visual tasks, TMD dramatically outperforms QRL (38.5% vs. 1.4% on visual_antmaze_teleport_stitch) while remaining competitive with CMD and CRL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TMD is compared against six baselines on OGBench. Against CMD (Myers et al., ICML 2024), which also learns a quasimetric temporal distance but without the T and I invariance constraints, TMD is consistently slightly better or comparable, with the largest gaps on long-horizon stitching (23.0% vs. 9.3% on humanoidmaze_large_stitch) and stochastic exploration (49.6% vs. 0.2% on antmaze_teleport_explore). Against CRL (Eysenbach et al., NeurIPS 2022), TMD substantially outperforms on all stitching and stochastic tasks (e.g., 68.5% vs. 36.2% on humanoidmaze_medium_stitch; 29.3% vs. 4.1% on pointmaze_teleport_stitch), though CRL edges out TMD on antmaze_medium_navigate (94.9% vs. 93.6%) and visual_antmaze_giant_navigate (47.2% vs. 40.1%). Against QRL (Wang et al., ICML 2023), which enforces the triangle inequality but fails in stochastic settings, TMD wins by large margins on all stochastic and visual tasks (e.g., 38.5% vs. 1.4% on visual_antmaze_teleport_stitch) and on stitching tasks. Against GCBC (Ding et al., NeurIPS 2019), GCIQL (Kostrikov et al., 2021), and GCIVL (Park et al., ICLR 2025), TMD outperforms on the majority of tasks, with the most dramatic wins on stitching and stochastic environments; however, GCIVL achieves the best score on pointmaze_teleport_stitch (44.4% vs. 29.3%) and scene_noisy (26.4% vs. 19.6%), and GCBC slightly edges TMD on pointmaze_teleport_stitch (31.5% vs. 29.3%).

## Relation to Prior Work

TMD unifies two previously separate frameworks for goal-conditioned RL: contrastive/successor-feature methods (CRL, CMD) that learn temporal distances via Monte Carlo estimation but cannot stitch, and quasimetric geometry methods (QRL, MRN) that enforce the triangle inequality architecturally to find shortest paths but fail in stochastic or off-policy settings. The key conceptual departure is showing that the standard Bellman optimality operator (max over actions) can be weakened to a SARSA-style expectation over dynamics without losing optimality, provided the triangle inequality and action invariance are simultaneously enforced. This reframes the quasimetric constraint not merely as a shortest-path mechanism but as the structural property that compensates for the absence of the max operator, turning an on-policy SARSA update into an off-policy optimal one.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TMD is the first method to simultaneously achieve architectural stitching, optimal policy recovery, and robustness to stochastic dynamics in offline GCRL, addressing a gap where prior methods could only satisfy two of these three properties. The clean theoretical guarantee (Theorem 3) that the invariance constraints converge to the optimal successor distance from any fully-covering policy provides a principled foundation rather than an ad hoc combination of losses. Open questions include whether the ζ hyperparameter (balancing contrastive vs. invariance losses) can be set more principledly, whether the policy extraction step can be integrated more tightly into distance learning, and whether more expressive quasimetric architectures (e.g., IQE) would further improve performance. The method's reliance on the MRN parameterization and its evaluation limited to OGBench-scale environments (≤21-dim states, small visual inputs) leave open how it scales to higher-dimensional or more complex robotic settings.
