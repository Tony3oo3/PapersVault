## Problem

Deriving scaling laws for large language models requires exhaustively training many models across different sizes and compute budgets, making the process prohibitively expensive. While learning curve prediction has been studied, the hierarchical structure inherent in NLP learning curve datasets (e.g., model size decomposed into embedding parameters and layers, or translation organized by source and target language) has not been exploited for zero-shot prediction. This paper asks whether modelling these bi-level hierarchies and cross-task correlations enables accurate zero-shot learning curve prediction and probabilistic scaling law estimation at a fraction of the computational cost.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a grid of observed learning curves (e.g., a 5×6 matrix of loss-vs-compute curves for nanoGPT, or source-language × target-language ChrF/BLEU curves for translation), z-score-normalized with logarithmic x-axis values, typically subsampled to 11 points per curve due to computational constraints. It produces full posterior predictive distributions (multivariate Gaussians) over unseen learning curves, from which probabilistic scaling law parameters (β₀, β₁ for the log-log form l(c) = (c/c₀)^(-γ)) are estimated via Monte Carlo averaging over R runs, accompanied by uncertainty quantification. The hierarchical structure is exchangeable—swapping which dimension indexes tasks versus data instances yields comparable performance.

## Method

The core model is the latent variable multi-output Gaussian Process (MaGP) from Ma et al., adapted to NLP learning curve prediction. Each task t (e.g., a fixed embedding size) contains multiple data instances d (e.g., different layer counts), forming a bi-level hierarchy; a shared GP mean function g(x) encodes common prior information across all tasks, while task-specific GPs l_t^d(x) model individual curves, and a latent variable h_t ~ N(0, I) captures inter-task correlations. For probabilistic scaling laws, the model is trained R times on observed curves, zero-shot predicts missing curves for unseen model sizes, fits the compute-efficient frontier scaling law to the union of observed and predicted curves in each run, and averages the resulting β₀ and β₁ estimates. An active learning strategy queries the learning curve with highest predictive variance at each iteration, substantially reducing uncertainty in the predicted scaling law compared to random or size-ordered querying strategies.

## Results

On the nanoGPT_large dataset, MaGP achieves MSE of 0.03±0.01 (Quad), 0.02±0.01 (Tri), and 0.04±0.05 (T1) for zero-shot learning curve prediction, consistently outperforming all baselines by a wide margin. For probabilistic scaling law estimation, the predicted law from the Tri split is (-0.052±0.005)c+(3.352±0.202) with an Area between Curves (AbC) of 0.160±0.172 to the ground truth (-0.056c+3.51), at a compute cost of 2.06×10⁵ PetaFLOPs (vs. 6.081×10⁵ for the full dataset). Active learning further reduces AbC uncertainty, with the most confident predictions achieved at lower query counts compared to random or largest-first strategies.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MaGP outperforms the Deep Hierarchical GP (DHGP, Hensman et al. [23]) across all train-test splits: on Quad, MaGP achieves MSE 0.03 vs. DHGP's 0.07 (2.3× lower), and on Tri, 0.02 vs. 0.07 (3.5× lower). The gap narrows on the smaller nanoGPT_small dataset (T1^small: 0.08 vs. 0.11), where fewer training curves limit both methods. Against the Bayesian Neural Network baselines adapted from Klein et al. [18], MaGP is orders of magnitude better: on Quad, MaGP's MSE of 0.03 compares to BNN(LC) at 10.69 and BNN(orig) at 13.96, a gap of roughly 350–470×. The BNN variants struggle due to the very small dataset size (29 curves × 11 points vs. 256 configurations in the original BNN paper), and their fixed basis functions cannot capture the hierarchical structure. For scaling law prediction, MaGP with the Tri split recovers a slope of -0.052 (ground truth: -0.056) at only 34% of the full compute cost, while the Quad split is most cost-efficient (21% of full cost) but yields a less accurate slope (-0.043). Active learning further improves reliability, producing consistently lower AbC than random or largest-first querying strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the latent variable multi-output Gaussian Process (MaGP) model of Ma et al. [24], originally developed for hierarchical biological datasets, to the domain of NLP learning curve prediction. It departs from prior zero-shot learning curve prediction work (Klein et al. [18] with BNNs, Swersky et al. [17] with GPs) by explicitly modelling the two-layer hierarchical structure inherent in NLP model configurations (e.g., embedding size × layer count, or source language × target language) and leveraging cross-task correlations for prediction. The paper also extends the Deep Hierarchical GP of Hensman et al. [23] as a baseline, positioning MaGP's latent-variable approach as superior when inter-task correlations are present. The probabilistic scaling law derivation via Monte Carlo simulation over predicted curves is a new application of these predictive models to the scaling law literature (Hoffmann et al. [3], Kaplan et al. [4]).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that scaling laws can be estimated with substantially reduced compute (e.g., the Quad split uses ~21% of the full dataset's compute) while maintaining reasonable accuracy, and that active learning strategies further reduce predictive uncertainty at each query step. This offers a practical pathway for researchers to derive probabilistic scaling laws without exhaustively training all model configurations. Key limitations include the very small dataset sizes (≤30 curves, 11 sample points each), the fixed architectural grid that prevents interpolation to unseen embedding sizes, and the variance-based active learning criterion that ignores compute cost. Open questions include whether the hierarchical structure assumption generalises to larger, more diverse model families, and how cost-aware query strategies might improve the compute-vs-uncertainty trade-off.
