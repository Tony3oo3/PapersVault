## Problem

Existing attention-based Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layers require specialized expert designs that differ from FFN-MoE and demonstrate suboptimal performance under matched computational budgets. The paper asks whether the attention mechanism can be reformulated to reveal an underlying FFN-like structure, enabling a unified expert architecture and parameter sharing across attention and FFN modules without sacrificing attention's expressiveness.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251243/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a sequence of n token hidden states (each d-dimensional) and produces next-token predictions via standard autoregressive language modeling. A key structural detail is that in the pre-mixing formulation, the token mixing operation operates on full d-dimensional hidden states rather than low-dimensional value vectors, and each expert receives a weighted combination of all token hidden states (a_i · X) as input rather than a single token. Evaluation is reported as perplexity on FineWeb-Edu and WikiText-103, plus zero-shot accuracy across eight downstream benchmarks.

## Method

UMoE reformulates multi-head attention by grouping the value projection W_v and output projection W_o into a single two-layer FFN-like expert E(x) = xW_vW_o, revealing that attention decomposes into a token mixing step (weighted summation over hidden states) followed by token-wise expert processing. In the pre-mixing variant, token hidden states are first aggregated into contextualized representations before being processed by the expert, making the expert input a weighted combination of all tokens rather than a single token. This structural alignment allows the same fine-grained FFN experts (two-layer MLPs with intermediate size d_v and a non-linear activation) to serve both attention and FFN layers, with parameter sharing across modules. Expert-specific query vectors are generated via low-rank projections (rank r) added to a shared query, while keys and values are shared across all experts. The top-k router dispatches tokens to the most relevant experts, and the only architectural distinction between the attention-MoE and FFN-MoE sub-layers is the expert input: individual token hidden states for FFN versus attention-weighted combinations for attention.

## Results

On FineWeb-Edu, UMoE achieves the lowest perplexity among all MoE variants at both scales: 20.44 (base, 540M params) and 15.95 (large, 3.6B params), outperforming the best FFN-MoE baseline (21.19 and 16.09 respectively). On WikiText-103, UMoE also leads with 26.67 (base) and 25.44 (large) PPL. In zero-shot evaluation, UMoE attains the highest average accuracy across eight downstream tasks: 40.06% (base) and 47.58% (large), surpassing the next-best FFN-MoE by 0.51 and 0.46 points respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the fine-grained FFN-MoE baseline (DeepSeek-V3 style), UMoE achieves lower perplexity on both FineWeb-Edu (20.44 vs 21.19 base; 15.95 vs 16.09 large) and WikiText-103 (26.67 vs 27.94 base; 25.44 vs 25.47 large), while using fewer total parameters (540M vs 535M base; 3.6B vs 3.8B large) thanks to shared experts between attention and FFN. Against MoA, UMoE wins decisively on FineWeb-Edu at both scales (20.44 vs 22.28 base; 15.95 vs 16.72 large) but MoA retains a slight edge on WikiText-103 for the large model (25.14 vs 25.44), which the authors attribute to the small corpus size. Against SwitchHead, UMoE dominates on both datasets at both scales (e.g., 20.44 vs 22.91 on FineWeb base; 15.95 vs 16.48 on FineWeb large). In zero-shot evaluation, UMoE achieves the best average accuracy (40.06% base, 47.58% large), edging out FFN-MoE (39.55%, 47.12%) and clearly surpassing MoA (38.49%, 45.55%) and SwitchHead (38.30%, 46.27%). The computational overhead of UMoE's pre-mixing attention is modest: ~1.17× MACs over dense for base models, shrinking to ~1.03× for large models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the fine-grained expert design paradigm established by DeepSeekMoE, XMoE, and scaling-law analyses of fine-grained MoE, which show that many small experts outperform fewer large ones. It departs from prior attention-MoE work (MoA, SwitchHead) that required specialized expert designs incompatible with FFN-MoE, by reformulating multi-head attention as a 'pre-mixing' operation followed by token-wise two-matrix processing — structurally identical to an FFN expert. The low-rank query projection (borrowed from LoRA) is used to keep per-expert parameter counts aligned with the two-matrix FFN convention, enabling direct parameter sharing between attention and FFN MoE layers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified architecture demonstrates that attention-MoE can match or exceed FFN-MoE performance while enabling parameter sharing, offering a cleaner scaling path for sparse Transformers. However, the pre-mixing formulation introduces a modest computational overhead (1.17× for base models, shrinking to 1.03× at scale) and the expert-allocation ablation (all experts in attention outperforming mixed allocation) is only validated on small models trained on 20B tokens. Open questions include whether more efficient token-mixing alternatives (linear attention, state-space models) can eliminate this overhead, whether shared experts suffer from knowledge conflicts at scale, and whether the architecture's advantages persist in reasoning-heavy or code domains not covered by the evaluation.
