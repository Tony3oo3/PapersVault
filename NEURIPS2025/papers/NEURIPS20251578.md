## Problem

In hierarchical VLA frameworks, the high-level VLM planner must be finetuned on demonstrations segmented into sub-tasks, but existing segmentation methods (human annotation or heuristic rules) produce sub-tasks that can deviate significantly from the low-level visuomotor policy's training distribution. This misalignment causes the planner to generate instructions the policy was never optimized for, degrading long-horizon task performance. The paper asks: can sub-tasks be automatically identified from a video demonstration in a way that is explicitly aligned with the visuomotor policy's existing capabilities?

## Input / Output

The method takes a video demonstration (RGB frames from front-view and gripper-view cameras, concatenated per frame) and the pre-existing visuomotor policy training dataset (e.g., 1,908 RLBench demonstrations decomposed into ~12,700 sub-task intervals). It produces a set of sub-task partitioning intervals (start/end frame indices) whose visual features are nearest-neighbor aligned with the training set, which are then augmented with language labels (via Gemini-1.5-flash) to form the planner finetuning dataset. The interval representation is a concatenation of visual encoder embeddings of the beginning and ending frames, searched via approximate nearest neighbor (Annoy with angular distance).

## Method

RDD formulates sub-task identification as an optimal partitioning problem over the demonstration's frame sequence, where the interval-wise additive scoring function measures alignment with the visuomotor training set via retrieval: for each candidate interval, it retrieves the nearest-neighbor interval from the training database and scores it by negative visual distance plus a temporal duration penalty (weighted by α). The dynamic programming solver (adapted from Jackson et al.) finds the globally optimal partition in O(N²) evaluations, reducible to O(N) when the maximum sub-task length is bounded. A key property (Proposition 3.1) ensures the scoring is invariant to how many sub-intervals of equal similarity are merged, preventing degenerate over-segmentation. For out-of-distribution sub-tasks, the similarity measure is augmented with a general change-point detection term (UVD) weighted by β to balance alignment with known skills versus discovery of novel sub-tasks.

## Results

On RLBench (13 manipulation tasks), RDD achieves 74.9±6.9% average success rate, matching the expert heuristic upper bound (75.1±4.7%) within 0.2% while outperforming UVD (71.4±5.1%) and uniform decomposition (71.3±5.4%) by ~3.5%. On real-world AgiBotWorld data, RDD achieves 0.706 mIoU against ground-truth segmentations versus 0.506 for UVD. Under OOD sub-task settings on RoboCerebra/LIBERO, RDD (β=0.10) reaches 0.630 mIoU compared to UVD's 0.598. RDD also outperforms Gemini-2.5-pro (72.6±4.7%) as a VLM-based decomposer by 2.3% in average success rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RDD outperforms UVD [25] (the state-of-the-art task-agnostic visual change-point decomposer) by 3.5% in average success rate on RLBench (74.9% vs. 71.4%) and by a large margin on real-world AgiBotWorld data (0.706 vs. 0.506 mIoU), indicating that heuristic visual change-point detection does not reliably align with the visuomotor policy's training distribution. Against the Expert heuristic decomposer from RACER [13] (the same task-specific rule used to build the visuomotor training set, serving as an upper bound), RDD achieves near-oracle performance with only a 0.2% gap in average success rate (74.9% vs. 75.1%), demonstrating that retrieval-based alignment can effectively recover expert-level decomposition without task-specific rules. RDD also surpasses Gemini-2.5-pro [42] as a VLM-based decomposer by 2.3% in average success rate (74.9% vs. 72.6%), showing that general-purpose video understanding alone is insufficient without explicit alignment to the low-level policy's capability space. Notably, UVD performs nearly identically to the naive uniform decomposition baseline (71.4% vs. 71.3%), suggesting that visual feature change points are not meaningfully aligned with the policy's training sub-tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of hierarchical VLA frameworks (Hi Robot, π0.5, RACER) and sub-task decomposition methods. It builds directly on the optimal partitioning algorithm of Jackson et al. [31] for the dynamic programming solver, but departs fundamentally from prior decomposition approaches: rather than detecting change-points in visual features (UVD) or relying on task-specific heuristics and human annotation, RDD reframes sub-task identification as a retrieval problem that explicitly aligns proposed segments with the low-level visuomotor policy's training distribution. The conceptual shift is from 'where does the video change?' to 'which segments look like things the policy already knows how to execute?'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck in deploying hierarchical VLA systems: the planner must be finetuned on sub-task-labeled demonstrations, but existing labeling methods (human annotation, heuristics, UVD) produce segments that may be outside the visuomotor policy's competence, degrading end-to-end performance. The finding that a retrieval-based method outperforms both heuristic change-point detection and a powerful general-purpose VLM (Gemini-2.5-pro) suggests that alignment with the policy's training distribution is more important than general video understanding for this task. Open questions include how RDD behaves when the visuomotor policy's training data is noisy or incomplete (the paper suggests integration with data curation like CUPID), whether the single-frame visual representation suffices for tasks requiring temporal context (e.g., navigation), and how the approach scales to truly open-world settings where the policy has never seen the target task.
