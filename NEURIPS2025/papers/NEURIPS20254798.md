## Problem

The difficulty of interpreting the privacy budget ε is frequently cited as a fundamental limitation of differential privacy, used to discourage its adoption or to promote alternative privacy methods. This paper argues that this criticism misattributes the challenge: the difficulty lies not in DP's definition but in the inherent complexity of quantifying privacy risk in context, a problem faced by any rigorous privacy assessment framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254798/cropped_page9_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the work takes as its analytical subject the current landscape of privacy-preserving machine learning, including DP mechanisms, alternative privacy metrics (k-anonymity, similarity-based metrics, label DP), and real-world deployments (Apple's DP, federated learning, LLM training). It produces a structured argument with practical recommendations for how researchers and practitioners should interpret, communicate, and set privacy budgets, along with a recommended decision-making process for selecting privacy-preserving algorithms.

## Method

The paper builds its case through four connected arguments. First, it demonstrates that privacy risk estimation is inherently difficult for humans—citing anonymization failures, probability intuition studies, and the Contextual Integrity framework—so no metric should be expected to be directly interpretable by end users. Second, it highlights DP's formal advantages (post-processing robustness, adaptive composition, worst-case guarantees) that alternatives like k-anonymity or similarity-based metrics lack, and shows that ε has clear interpretability in simple cases like randomized response. Third, it applies Goodhart's Law to show that treating ε minimization as an objective leads to unfair accounting (public pretraining, local vs. central DP, label DP) and rejection of valid research with larger but meaningful budgets. Fourth, it warns that the 'ε is hard to set' narrative hinders adoption by promoting weaker alternatives and enabling privacy-washing deployments, and provides a recommended decision-making process emphasizing order-of-magnitude reasoning and trust-assumption scrutiny over precise budget values.

## Results

This is a position paper with no empirical results table. The paper uses illustrative numbers to support its arguments: a fully DP ImageNet model at ε=8 achieves 39.2% top-1 accuracy versus ~90% for the non-private SOTA [41], and DP LLMs at ε=2 lag non-private versions by roughly five years [29]. For randomized response with K=3790 (emoji), ε=log(3) yields a 99.92% incorrect-response rate, while matching the 75% true-answer guarantee of binary RR requires ε≈9.33. The Apple macOS deployment is cited as a cautionary example where a per-day budget of ε=16, advertised as ε=1, degraded rapidly under composition [48].

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not run controlled experiments or report head-to-head benchmark comparisons. Instead, it qualitatively contrasts differential privacy against several alternatives: k-anonymity and its extensions (l-diversity, t-closeness) are shown to fail under composition; similarity-based privacy metrics are argued to provide no real protection and can even increase leakage [18, 10]; label DP is criticized for excluding features from the budget while those features act as strong proxies for the private label; and local-vs-central DP in federated learning is discussed to show that architectural trust assumptions, not the raw ε value, determine the meaningful privacy guarantee. The paper also critiques the 'public pretraining + private fine-tuning' paradigm [50] as a form of unfair accounting that artificially lowers reported ε without improving end-to-end privacy. No method is declared a winner; the paper's conclusion is that DP's formal properties (post-processing robustness, composition, worst-case guarantees) make it the only framework with sufficient theoretical grounding, and that the difficulty of interpreting ε reflects the inherent hardness of privacy risk estimation rather than a flaw in DP itself.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes threads from the DP literature (Dwork et al. 2006), the history of anonymization failures (Sweeney, Netflix Prize), Contextual Integrity (Nissenbaum 2004), and the hypothesis-testing interpretation of DP (Wasserman & Zhou 2010; Balle et al. 2020) to reframe a common criticism. Rather than building on a specific technical line, it departs from the prevailing narrative that 'no one knows how to set ε' by arguing this difficulty is inherent to privacy quantification in high-dimensional, context-dependent settings, not a flaw in DP's definition. The Goodhart's Law framing of ε-minimization as a harmful research incentive, and the critique of public-pretraining and label-DP as accounting games, extend the discussion from technical adequacy to research-culture and trust-assumption concerns.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central practical implication is that the 'ε is hard to set' narrative actively discourages DP adoption and enables privacy-washing by making weakly-private alternatives seem more tractable. It raises the open question of when a task is fundamentally incompatible with strong privacy guarantees (e.g., predicting exactly the private signal one claims to protect), which has no clean resolution within any current framework. The call to reverse the burden of proof—requiring non-DP methods to demonstrate robustness rather than expecting DP to be disproven—could shift how the community and regulators evaluate privacy claims, though the paper offers no formal mechanism for doing so.
