## Problem

Existing steering methods for multimodal LLMs (MLLMs) apply a single fixed steering vector regardless of the input, which is inadequate when the desired behavior is input-dependent. For example, a safe response to an illegal-activity query should refuse engagement, while a safe response to a medical query should defer to an expert—yet mean-steering cannot distinguish between these cases. This paper investigates whether input-specific steering vectors can be learned with negligible overhead to enforce such context-dependent behaviors.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255341/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the method takes a standard multimodal query (an RGB image and a text instruction) and produces a steered text response, identical in format to the unsteered model's output. No contrastive prompts or additional inputs are needed at test time; the only extra computation is a forward pass through a 2-layer MLP (bottleneck size 100) on the hidden representation at one intermediate layer, followed by a single vector addition to the residual stream at one decoder layer for every generated token. The steering magnitude α is a scalar hyperparameter chosen per application to balance behavior enforcement against response quality degradation.

## Method

The core idea is to replace the single static steering vector with an input-specific one predicted by a lightweight auxiliary network. First, an oracle method (P2S) computes per-sample steering vectors as the difference of hidden representations at layer L* obtained by running the MLLM in teacher-forcing mode with input-specific positive and negative contrastive prompts. Second, L2S trains a 2-layer MLP (Tanh activation, bottleneck 100) to reconstruct these P2S vectors from the input context—the hidden representation of the last input token at a separate layer L'—using a combined L2, L1, and cosine-similarity loss. The MLP is initialized with a dictionary-learning basis for stability and trains in under a minute on a single GPU. At inference, the predicted steering vector is added to the residual stream at layer L* for all generated tokens, yielding input-dependent behavior correction with no modification to the base MLLM weights.

## Results

On MMSafetyBench (safety enforcement), L2S reduces the average Unsafe-score (p≥0.5) from 0.276 (No-steering) to 0.082 on LLaVA-v1.5 and from 0.287 to 0.058 on Qwen2-VL, while simultaneously improving the Expert-Deferring score from 0.250 to 0.395 (LLaVA) and 0.184 to 0.592 (Qwen2-VL). On POPE (hallucination mitigation), L2S achieves 86.46% accuracy on the Random subset for LLaVA-v1.5 (vs. 82.73% No-steering, 84.29% Mean-S) and 92.53% for Qwen2-VL (vs. 91.75% No-steering). On COCO captioning, L2S reduces CHAIR_s from 17.31 to 16.10 and CHAIR_i from 52.80 to 51.80 while improving Gemini win-rate from 35.80% to 64.20%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is Mean-S, which corresponds to the contrastive activation addition (CAA) paradigm of Panickssery et al. [39] applied with a single averaged steering vector across all inputs. L2S consistently outperforms Mean-S on both applications: on MMSafetyBench, L2S reduces Unsafe-score(p≥0.5) by ~49% relative to Mean-S on LLaVA (0.082 vs. 0.161) and by ~38% on Qwen2-VL (0.058 vs. 0.093), while also improving ED-score by 20% (0.395 vs. 0.329) and 45% (0.592 vs. 0.408) respectively. On POPE hallucination, L2S beats Mean-S by 2.2–1.4 percentage points in accuracy across the three subsets for LLaVA and by 0.8–2.1 points for Qwen2-VL. The key advantage of L2S over the static CAA-style Mean-S is its ability to adapt the steering direction per input, which is critical when the desired behavior instantiation varies (e.g., refusing illegal activity vs. deferring to a financial expert). L2S also outperforms the Prompt baseline (appending a safety instruction) and the Norm-Rnd baseline (random-direction noise injection) across all metrics, confirming that the learned input-dependent direction is substantially more informative than either fixed prompting or isotropic perturbation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the activation-steering / representation-engineering line of work (CAA, contrastive activation addition, mean steering) from LLMs to MLLMs, and crucially departs from the static single-vector paradigm by making the steering direction input-dependent. It builds most directly on CAA-style contrastive prompting [39] and on recent MLLM steering efforts (Khayatan et al. [18] for concept-level mean-difference steering, Wang et al. [56] for adaptive per-token steering, Li et al. [27] for safety-probe-driven interventions), but goes further by learning a per-input steering vector via a lightweight auxiliary network rather than averaging or fixing a direction. The key conceptual departure is that the *instantiation* of a desired behavior (e.g., refuse vs. defer-to-expert) depends on the input, so a single vector is fundamentally insufficient.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

L2S offers a practically attractive middle ground between expensive fine-tuning and crude static steering: a 2-layer MLP trained in seconds on extracted representations, with negligible inference overhead, that adapts the steering direction per input. This opens the door to input-conditioned alignment (safety, hallucination, potentially personalization) without retraining the base MLLM. Open questions include whether single-layer linear shifts are sufficient for more complex behaviors, how to automate contrastive-prompt selection, and whether the approach generalizes beyond the two 7B models and two applications tested. The paper also acknowledges that the contrastive prompts used to generate training targets are hand-crafted per scenario, limiting full automation.
