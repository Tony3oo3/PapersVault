## Problem

Existing kernel-based relative similarity tests require manually specifying an alternative hypothesis (e.g., 'Q is closer to U than P') before selecting a kernel, creating a circular dependency: once the hypothesis is fixed, one can always find a kernel that supports it, rendering the test ill-defined. This oriented approach also guarantees a probability α of missing the true relationship regardless of sample size, and restricts kernel selection to heuristics like median bandwidth that lack guarantees on complex data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes three independent sample sets of equal size m (Z from anchor U, X from P, Y from Q) using a data-splitting protocol: training samples drive Phase I kernel optimization, while a separate set of testing samples (Z', X', Y') is used in Phase II for significance assessment. The output is a pair: a binary significance indicator h ∈ {0,1} at level α, and a direction indicator F ∈ {+1, −1} identifying which of P or Q is closer to U. The deep kernel is parameterized by a neural network φ_ω with two Gaussian kernels G₁ and G₂, making the method applicable to high-dimensional inputs such as RGB images.

## Method

The paper defines the Anchor-based Maximum Discrepancy (AMD) as sup over a deep kernel space K of |⟨μ_U − (μ_P+μ_Q)/2, μ_P − μ_Q⟩_{H_κ}|, which geometrically measures the maximum discrepancy between the MMD distances d(U,P) and d(U,Q). Phase I estimates AMD by optimizing the deep kernel in two separate branches (maximizing for F=+1, minimizing for F=−1) with a data-augmentation regularization term that enforces E[d̂(Z^aug, X^aug, Y^aug)]=0 to prevent overfitting; the final kernel is chosen by highest absolute statistic. Phase II performs a unified one-sided test on F·d^{κ*}(U,P,Q) > 0, deriving the critical threshold via wild bootstrap (exponential weights on U-statistic kernel evaluations) under the least-favorable boundary of the composite null. Theoretically, the estimator is proven consistent (Theorem 3), type-I error is bounded at α (Lemma 4), and test power exceeds that of any uniformly chosen alternative hypothesis whenever the learned direction F is better than random (Theorem 5).

## Results

On MNIST and CIFAR-10, the AMD test achieves high rejection rates (test power) across the full range of ν ∈ [0,1], correctly identifying which distribution is closer to the anchor for both ν < 0.5 and ν > 0.5, whereas all baselines degrade sharply when ν > 0.5. When ν = 0.5 (no true relative similarity), all methods including AMD control the type-I error at the nominal level α = 0.05. With data augmentation, AMD reaches 96.9% test power at m = 340 on MNIST and 96.8% at m = 160 on CIFAR-10, outperforming the no-augmentation variant (93.1% and 95.2% respectively). In practical applications on ImageNet variants and adversarial CIFAR-10, AMD achieves higher test power than MMD-D, particularly in cases where the closer distribution contradicts the prespecified alternative hypothesis.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares AMD against five baselines: MMD-D and MMD-H (Bounliphone et al., ICLR 2016), KLFI (Gerber et al., NeurIPS 2023), UME (Jitkrittum et al., NeurIPS 2018), SHCE (Lopez-Paz & Oquab, ICLR 2017), and LBI (Cheng & Cloninger, IEEE TIT 2022). All baselines share a critical limitation: they test a prespecified alternative hypothesis (e.g., Q is closer to U than P), so they achieve high test power only when ν < 0.5 (where Q is indeed closer) but their rejection rates drop near zero when ν > 0.5 (where P is closer). AMD, by learning the hypothesis direction F in Phase I, maintains high test power in both regimes, effectively doubling the range of detectable relative similarity relationships. At ν = 0.5, all methods including AMD correctly fail to reject (type-I error ≈ 0.05), confirming no spurious detections. In the ImageNet variant evaluation (Figure 4), MMD-D fails entirely on the ImageNet-SK vs. ImageNet-A comparison because it assumes Q is closer, while AMD correctly identifies the closer variant. Similarly, in adversarial perturbation detection (Figure 5), MMD-D cannot detect perturbation levels below 4/255 (where P is closer to U), whereas AMD handles all perturbation levels symmetrically.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the relative similarity testing framework of Bounliphone et al. (2016) and Gerber et al. (2023), which use MMD with a manually specified alternative hypothesis (e.g., Q is closer to U than P). The key departure is identifying that this manual specification creates an ill-defined kernel selection problem: once the hypothesis is fixed, one can always find a kernel that supports it, undermining the test's validity. The paper draws on deep kernels from Liu et al. (2020) for expressive function spaces and wild bootstrap for U-statistics to propose AMD, which learns the hypothesis direction and the kernel simultaneously rather than sequentially.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of the oriented kernel selection problem is a clean methodological contribution that explains why existing relative similarity tests can be misleading, particularly when the true relationship contradicts the prespecified alternative. The two-phase approach (estimate AMD to infer the relationship, then test significance with wild bootstrap) provides a principled solution with controlled type-I error. However, the method is limited to three distributions (one anchor, two candidates), and extending to multiple distributions remains open. The data augmentation regularization adds computational overhead, and the theoretical guarantees rely on standard but non-trivial assumptions (covering numbers, bounded kernels).
