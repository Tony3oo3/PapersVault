## Problem

While jailbreak vulnerabilities of LLMs and MLLMs via text and visual modalities are well-studied, the susceptibility of end-to-end Large Audio Language Models (LALMs) to audio-specific hidden semantics—such as emphasis, speed, intonation, tone, background noise, celebrity accent, and emotion—remains largely unexplored. This gap is critical because end-to-end LALMs directly perceive and interpret these paralinguistic features, making them potentially more vulnerable to modality-specific manipulations than cascaded ASR+LLM pipelines.

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes jailbreak text prompts (sourced from AdvBench, MM-SafetyBench, RedTeam-2K, and SafeBench), converts them to audio via gTTS, and applies 20 parameterized editing operations across 7 semantic categories to produce 157,782 audio samples. Evaluation outputs are Attack Success Rates (ASR) judged by Llama Guard 3, where a successful attack requires the response to be classified as harmful AND exceed 50 words in length. The benchmark reports ASR separately for Explicit and Implicit jailbreak subtypes, with relative changes (±%) compared to unedited baselines for each editing type.

## Method

Jailbreak-AudioBench comprises three components: a Toolbox that performs TTS conversion and injects hidden semantics via STFT, SoX, Coqui TTS, and Dia-1.6B across 7 editing types (emphasis, speed, intonation, tone, background noise, celebrity accent, emotion); a Dataset construction pipeline that categorizes 4,700 questions into Explicit (2,497) and Implicit (2,203) subtypes via GPT-4o plus human review, then generates original and edited audio variants; and a Benchmark evaluating 10 LALMs (BLSP, SpeechGPT, Qwen2-Audio, SALMONN-7B/13B, VITA-1.5, R1-AQA, MiniCPM-o-2.6, GPT-4o-Audio, Gemini-2.5-Flash). Beyond single-editing evaluation, the paper introduces a Query-based Audio Editing Jailbreak method that systematically combines 32 editing configurations (2×Speed × 2×Emphasis × 2×Background Noise × (2×Celebrity Accent + 2×Emotion)) to overwhelm model normalization, raising ASR from 13.3% to 48.8% on Qwen2-Audio and from 0.7% to 8.4% on GPT-4o-Audio. A lightweight prompt-based defense prepends a TTS-rendered safety instruction to each audio sample, and t-SNE/UMAP analyses of internal representations reveal that vulnerable models (SALMONN) maintain editing-based clustering throughout all transformer layers while robust models (Qwen2-Audio) normalize to semantic clustering by mid-depth layers.

## Results

On the Explicit Subtype dataset, original (unedited) ASR ranges from 0.7% (GPT-4o-Audio) to 47.5% (BLSP), while on the Implicit Subtype it ranges from 0.8% (GPT-4o-Audio) to 18.25% (BLSP). Audio editing induces the largest ASR increases in SALMONN-13B (up to +28.6% with machine noise) and SALMONN-7B (up to +21.4% with volume×10 emphasis), whereas GPT-4o-Audio and Gemini-2.5-Flash remain largely robust with changes under 2%. Under the proposed Query-based Audio Editing Jailbreak (32 editing combinations), ASR surges dramatically: Qwen2-Audio from 13.3% to 48.8%, SALMONN-7B from 31.6% to 85.1%, GPT-4o-Audio from 0.7% to 8.4%, and Gemini-2.5-Flash from 8.1% to 49.4%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark evaluates 10 LALMs under 20 audio editing conditions. SALMONN-7B and SALMONN-13B are the most vulnerable, with ASR increases of +13% to +29% across emphasis, speed, tone, background noise, celebrity accent, and emotion editing, far exceeding all other models. GPT-4o-Audio is the most robust, maintaining ASR below 2.5% even under the most aggressive single edits, though its Query-based ASR still rises to 8.4%. Gemini-2.5-Flash shows moderate robustness to single edits (≤3.4% change) but is surprisingly vulnerable to the combinatorial Query-based attack, jumping from 8.1% to 49.4%. Open-source models BLSP (47.5% original ASR) and Qwen2-Audio (16.8%) occupy opposite ends: BLSP is highly vulnerable at baseline but relatively resistant to editing, while Qwen2-Audio is moderately robust to single edits yet jumps to 48.8% under the 32-variant query-based attack. The prompt-based defense (prepended audio instruction) reduces ASR across all models but leaves residual vulnerability, confirming that current safeguards are insufficient against audio editing jailbreaks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the well-established jailbreak evaluation paradigm for LLMs and vision-language models (via benchmarks like AdvBench, MM-SafetyBench, RedTeam-2K, and SafeBench) to the audio modality, where prior work was limited to a single preliminary study [65] using only 350 samples without audio editing. It carves new ground by introducing audio hidden semantics—prosody, tone, emotion, celebrity accent, background noise—as a distinct attack vector, leveraging standard audio editing tools (SoX, STFT, Coqui TTS, Dia-1.6B) to generate adversarial variants. The query-based audio editing approach, which systematically combines multiple editing types to overwhelm model normalization, represents a novel attack formulation not present in prior text or vision jailbreak literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As voice assistants (Siri, Alexa, Google Assistant) become ubiquitous, the demonstrated vulnerability of end-to-end LALMs to audio editing jailbreaks represents a concrete and previously unquantified security risk. The query-based editing results—particularly Gemini-2.5-Flash jumping from 8.1% to 49.4% ASR and GPT-4o-Audio from 0.7% to 8.4%—suggest that even state-of-the-art closed-source models have meaningful audio-specific blind spots. However, the defense proposed (a prepended audio instruction) leaves residual ASR concerningly high, and the reliance on TTS-generated audio rather than natural speech limits real-world generalizability. Open questions include whether more effective defenses exist, how findings transfer to natural human speech with organic prosodic variation, and whether the Llama Guard 3 evaluation metric (which misclassifies prompt-repetition responses) adequately captures true jailbreak success.
