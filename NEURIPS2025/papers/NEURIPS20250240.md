## Problem

Existing AI safety systems operate reactively, requiring users to explicitly ask for help before receiving assistance. However, people often lack the safety knowledge or awareness to recognize risks in real time, meaning they may never know what to ask. This paper asks whether current LLMs and MLLMs can proactively observe a user's behavior and environment and flag potential dangers without any user query.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250240/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each sample presents either a structured text log (4–8 segments with time, location, environmental observation, and behavioral observation fields, averaging ~547 words) or a sequence of 1–4 AI-generated sub-images (GPT-4o-image), paired with a system prompt instructing the model to act as a silent 'Reminder Agent.' The model must output either a risk alert that both identifies the specific dangerous behavior and explains why it is hazardous, or simply 'None.' Evaluation runs N=16 trials per sample at temperature 0.7 with a think-then-answer CoT format, and GPT-4.1 serves as an automated judge checking whether the response matches the annotated safety knowledge and risk reason.

## Method

PaSBench is constructed via a human-in-the-loop pipeline: 288 safety knowledge points are extracted from Chinese safety-education books and government websites, filtered through dual-annotator cross-checking (from 495 initial candidates), then instantiated as 128 image sequences (GPT-4o draft prompts → human refinement → sequential GPT-4o-image generation with up to 10 quality-check retries) and 288 text logs (DeepSeek-R1 generates a realistic user profile and a 500–1500-word third-person observation log ending before the incident). The benchmark evaluates 36 models (32 LLMs, 22 MLLMs) under three metrics—Accuracy (avg-of-N), Potential (best-of-N), and Robustness (worst-of-N). A key diagnostic experiment converts knowledge points into multiple-choice questions, revealing that models retain >80% safety knowledge yet still miss 45–55% of risks in the proactive setting, pinpointing unstable proactive reasoning rather than knowledge deficits as the primary bottleneck. A reactive ablation (supplying the safety knowledge explicitly) recovers 68–93% of previously missed cases, and the strong Pearson correlation (r=0.897) between text and image detection rates indicates the limitation is modality-independent.

## Results

On PaSBench, the top-performing model Gemini-2.5-pro achieves 71% average accuracy on the image set (128 samples) and 64% on the text set (288 samples), but its robustness (Worst-of-16) drops to 55% on images and 50% on text (Gemini-2.0-pro), meaning it misses 45–55% of risks across repeated trials. Weaker models perform far worse: Qwen2.5-VL-3B reaches only 13% image accuracy, GPT-4.1-nano 20% text accuracy, and Llama-3.1-8B 24% text accuracy, with robustness below 5% for most small models. In a multiple-choice knowledge probe covering the same 128 knowledge points, all models exceed 80% accuracy (Gemini-2.5-pro: 87–94.5%), indicating the bottleneck is proactive reasoning rather than safety knowledge.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 36 models (32 LLMs on text, 22 MLLMs on image) rather than comparing a single proposed method against baselines. Among proprietary models, Gemini-2.5-pro leads on both modalities (71% image / 64% text accuracy), while Gemini-2.0-pro achieves the highest text robustness (0.50). Notably, non-reasoning models (Gemini-2.0-pro, Claude-3.5-sonnet, GPT-4.1) outperform large reasoning models (o1) on the proactive detection task, suggesting that extended chain-of-thought reasoning does not help when the core challenge is recalling and applying safety knowledge proactively. Across all model families (Gemini, Claude, GPT/O-series, Qwen, Llama, DeepSeek), larger models consistently outperform smaller counterparts on accuracy, robustness, and potential. In a controlled reactive experiment (giving the model the safety knowledge explicitly), 68–93% of proactive failures are resolved, confirming that the gap is in proactive analytical ability rather than modality understanding or knowledge absence.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in LLM safety evaluation, where prior benchmarks (SafeText, RESPONSE, HealthBench, MSSBench, LabsafetyBench) assume the user already recognizes a risk and explicitly asks for help. It also distinguishes itself from LLM safety-alignment work (jailbreak resistance, toxic content refusal) and from proactive dialogue research (clarifying questions, ProAgent), which target conversational quality rather than hazard detection. By formulating a task where the model must observe a sequence of text logs or images and flag risks without any user query, the paper carves out a genuinely new evaluation axis: proactive risk awareness as distinct from both reactive safety advice and self-safety alignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central diagnostic finding—that models possess the safety knowledge (>80% on multiple-choice probes) yet fail to reliably activate it proactively (45–55% miss rate across 16 trials even for Gemini-2.5-pro)—reframes the engineering challenge from knowledge acquisition to stable proactive reasoning, pointing toward training-data augmentation and propose-then-verify pipelines rather than simply scaling model size. Open questions remain: whether performance degrades on longer or continuous observation streams, how to calibrate risk severity to avoid alert fatigue, and whether the GPT-4.1-as-judge protocol introduces systematic bias. The benchmark's modest size (416 samples) and single-language (English) scope also limit generalizability claims.
