## Problem

Instruction fine-tuning implicitly assumes a single coherent supervisory signal behind all human-written instructions, but real annotators exhibit distinct stylistic and semantic preferences (terse vs. verbose, formal vs. colloquial, strict vs. creative) that introduce preference bias into training data. This bias causes gradient updates to be pulled in conflicting directions, forcing models to memorize idiosyncrasies rather than learn a consensus, and no existing PEFT method explicitly reconciles conflicting annotator preferences within the model's internal representations during fine-tuning.

## Input / Output

DCPC operates on pairs of instruction texts flowing through a frozen LLM backbone (LLaMA-2 7B/13B or Mistral-7B) equipped with P-Tuning v2 prefix adapters, consuming layer-wise hidden states (d=4096 or 5120) and per-layer prefix embeddings (m=16 tokens) as its working signals. It produces dynamically corrected prefix slices injected back into the frozen backbone, yielding aligned hidden representations that steer the LM head toward a consensus-style generated response. The entire pipeline is label-free and adds only ~9.6M tunable parameters, plugging into any existing PEFT adapter without modifying backbone weights.

## Method

DCPC comprises three interacting modules. First, a Preference-Sensitive Similarity detector flags high-similarity/low-agreement instruction pairs by combining cosine similarity of hidden states with symmetric KL divergence of soft label distributions, aggregated across layers with exponential weighting that emphasises deeper layers where preference drift magnifies. Second, Cross-Layer Prefix Alignment cross-mixes prefixes of the two instructions across consecutive layers and minimises the mean-squared distance between the resulting composite representations, with a proven geometric contraction guarantee (d^{l+k} ≤ L^{2k} d^l) under L-Lipschitz transformer blocks. Third, a lightweight Preference Correction Module (PCM) parameterises a diagonal Gaussian over each instruction's embedding, samples a soft preference distribution, and synthesises corrected prefix slices from a shared meta-matrix that are injected back into the model to nudge predictions toward a consensus style. The total loss combines an ambiguity term, the alignment term, and a KL penalty between the two preference distributions, all optimised in a single extra backward pass per flagged pair.

## Results

On six preference-shifted benchmarks (BoolQ-PS, COPA-BS, ReCoRD-R, SST-2-P, RTE-E, Alpaca-IS), DCPC achieves 86.1/91.7/91.9/92.8/83.7 accuracy (F1-EM) and 9.4 GPT-score, outperforming the strongest PEFT baselines by 4.0–6.7 points and Full-FT by 2.7–3.7 points. On the original (unshifted) datasets, DCPC also leads all baselines, reaching 88.9% on BoolQ, 93.5% on COPA, 92.2 F1-EM on ReCoRD, 95.0% on SST-2, 84.7% on RTE, and 9.5 GPT-score on Alpaca with only 9.6M tunable parameters. The method cuts inter-seed variance by up to 35% under synthetic preference shifts compared to the best PEFT baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DCPC outperforms all 18 baselines on every preference-shifted benchmark. Against the strongest PEFT methods (AdaLoRA, PEDRO, Learned-Adapter), DCPC gains 4.0–6.7 points in accuracy/F1-EM; for example, on BoolQ-PS it reaches 86.1% versus 79.4% for AdaLoRA (+6.7) and 79.1% for PEDRO (+7.0). Full-parameter fine-tuning (Full-FT), despite using 7B trainable parameters, drops to 82.4% on BoolQ-PS and 80.7% on RTE-E, still trailing DCPC by 3.7 and 3.0 points respectively. WizardLM's curriculum approach is the closest full-model competitor (82.9% on BoolQ-PS, 8.9 GPT-score on Alpaca-IS) but remains 3.2 and 0.5 points below DCPC. Noise-regularization baselines (NeFTune, SymNoise, RobustFT) show moderate degradation under shifts (e.g., RobustFT: 81.4% on BoolQ-PS) and are consistently outperformed by 4.7+ points. In the noisy/inconsistent-label comparison (Figure 4), DCPC also surpasses dedicated methods such as Co-teaching, NoiseBox, RSVMI, LAWMV, AALI, and the LRA diffusion model, confirming that its cross-layer alignment and PCM mechanism provide a more effective correction than task-level reweighting or label-cleansing heuristics.

## Relation to Prior Work

DCPC extends the P-Tuning v2 prefix-tuning framework by adding two new mechanisms—cross-layer prefix alignment and a Preference Correction Module—that operate on the model's internal representations rather than on the training data or loss function. It departs from prior work on inconsistent labels (Constitutional AI, WizardLM curriculum) which impose external constraints or task-level heuristics, and from noisy-label methods (NeFTune, SymNoise, RobustFT) that inject or remove noise at the data level, by instead detecting and correcting preference drift within the transformer's layer-wise hidden states in a self-supervised, label-free manner. The theoretical grounding draws on standard Lipschitz contraction and VC-dimension arguments under class-conditional noise, positioning the method within the broader PEFT literature while introducing a representation-level correction step that prior prefix/prompt tuning methods lack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete and underexplored failure mode of instruction fine-tuning—inter-annotator stylistic and semantic preference bias—and provides both a diagnostic (high cosine similarity coexisting with growing KL divergence in deep layers) and a practical, plug-and-play remedy that adds only ~9.6M tunable parameters. However, the evaluation relies on synthetic preference shifts generated by GPT-3.5, raising circularity concerns, and the method is validated on only two backbone families (LLaMA-2, Mistral-7B) without code release. Open questions include whether the approach transfers to dialogue, multimodal, and RLHF settings, and whether the Lipschitz contraction assumption (L < 1 per block) holds in practice for modern transformer architectures.
