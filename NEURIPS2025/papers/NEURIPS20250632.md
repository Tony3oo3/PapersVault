## Problem

Watermark detection in LLMs is structurally a goodness-of-fit (GoF) testing problem—under the null hypothesis, pivotal statistics are i.i.d. from a known distribution—yet classical GoF tests have received almost no attention in the watermarking literature. Prior work has focused on designing new watermarking schemes or ad hoc sum-based detectors, with only a single divergence-based GoF test (Li et al., 2025) studied for one specific watermark. This paper asks: how well do the full suite of classical GoF tests perform for modern watermark detection across diverse schemes, models, and editing conditions?

## Input / Output

The method takes a token sequence w_{1:n}, the watermark decoder S, and the secret key as input, and computes per-token pivotal statistics Y_t = Y(w_t, ζ_t) where ζ_t is derived from a hash of the preceding m tokens and the key. These are transformed to p-values p_t = 1 − F_0(Y_t) and fed into permutation-invariant GoF test statistics (e.g., the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance D_n), so the ordering of tokens does not affect the result. The output is a binary decision at a chosen significance level α (default 0.01), with critical values obtained from asymptotic null distributions or Monte Carlo calibration rather than closed-form finite-sample quantiles.

## Method

The paper systematically evaluates eight classical GoF tests—Tr-GoF (Phi), Kuiper, Kolmogorov-Smirnov, Anderson-Darling, Cramér-von Mises, Watson, Neyman smooth, and Chi-squared—against five sum-based baselines across three unbiased watermarking schemes (Gumbel-max, Inverse-transform, SynthID), three open-source LLMs (OPT-1.3B, OPT-13B, Llama 3.1-8B), two datasets (C4, ELI5), four temperatures, and three edit types (deletion, substitution, information-rich). The key insight is that GoF tests compare the full empirical CDF of pivotal statistics against the known null CDF using richer nonlinear operations (sorting, maximization, polynomial expansion), capturing distribution-level deviations that sum-based statistics compress away. A novel finding is that text repetition, prevalent at low temperatures, introduces step-like structure in the empirical CDF that GoF tests exploit to maintain strong detection power even when watermark signals are information-theoretically weak. Under information-rich edits that selectively remove the most extreme pivotal statistics, GoF tests remain robust because they assess global distributional fit rather than relying on a few high-magnitude values.

## Results

On the C4 dataset (Type II error, lower is better), GoF tests consistently outperform the best sum-based baseline across all three watermarking schemes. At high temperature (T=0.7, n=400), GoF tests achieve 0.2–0.3% Type II error versus ~0.7% for the baseline under Gumbel-max. The advantage is most dramatic at low temperature (T=0.3, n=400), where Chi reduces Type II error from 15.1% (baseline) to 2.9% for Gumbel-max, from 27.1% to 3.6% for Inverse-transform, and from 44.4% to 10.4% for SynthID. Type I errors remain well-controlled near the nominal α=0.01 level across all methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares eight GoF tests against five sum-based baselines: Aaronson's score [1] and log score [29,13] for Gumbel-max, negative-sum score [29] for Inverse-transform, identity-sum score [9] for SynthID, and the least-favorable minimax test [31] applied to all three schemes. The 'Baseline' column reports the best among these five. GoF tests win decisively at low temperature: at T=0.3, n=400, Chi outperforms the best baseline by 12.2 percentage points (Gumbel-max: 2.9% vs 15.1%), 23.5 points (Inverse-transform: 3.6% vs 27.1%), and 34.0 points (SynthID: 10.4% vs 44.4%). At high temperature (T=0.7, n=400), GoF tests still lead but by a smaller margin (e.g., 0.2% vs 0.7% for Gumbel-max). Under information-rich edits at 50% token modification (Table 3), the gap widens dramatically: for SynthID, the baseline degrades to 94.5% Type II error while Kui, Wat, and Chi maintain 0.1%, 0.6%, and 0.9% respectively, demonstrating that GoF tests' distribution-level sensitivity makes them far more robust to targeted attacks than sum-based methods that rely on extreme values.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the pivotal-statistic hypothesis-testing framework for watermark detection established by Li et al. [31, 33], which introduced the least-favorable detection rule and a single truncated phi-divergence GoF test for the Gumbel-max watermark. The key departure is systematic: rather than proposing one new detection rule, the authors evaluate eight classical GoF tests (Kolmogorov-Smirnov, Anderson-Darling, Cramér-von Mises, Kuiper, Watson, Neyman smooth, Chi-squared, and the truncated phi test) across three watermarking schemes (Gumbel-max, inverse-transform, SynthID), treating GoF testing as a general-purpose detection tool rather than a scheme-specific construction. The paper also identifies text repetition at low temperatures as a structural factor that GoF tests exploit but sum-based baselines miss, a phenomenon not analyzed in prior watermarking or GoF literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that classical, off-the-shelf GoF tests provide a simple drop-in improvement over the sum-based detectors that dominate the watermarking literature, with consistent gains across models, temperatures, text lengths, and edit types. The identification of repetition as a detection signal at low temperatures reframes a known LLM pathology as a statistical advantage, opening questions about whether other structural artifacts of autoregressive generation can be similarly exploited. Open problems include a theoretical account of which GoF test is optimal under which NTP distribution class, adaptive test selection based on input characteristics, and extension to high-dimensional pivotal statistics (e.g., SynthID's k-dimensional g-values) where classical GoF tests face the curse of dimensionality.
