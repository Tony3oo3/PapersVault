## Problem

Existing dynamic GNNs for EEG-based seizure analysis rely on temporally fixed static graphs, failing to capture the evolving brain connectivity that characterizes seizure progression. Additionally, the relative expressivity of different spatio-temporal architectures (graph-then-time, time-and-graph, time-then-graph) for EEG graph modeling has not been formally characterized, leaving model design choices largely heuristic.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254837/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-channel EEG recordings (19 channels on TUSZ, 22 on CHB-MIT) that are first transformed via short-time Fourier transform into log-amplitude frequency snapshots, then z-normalized across the training set to form a tensor of shape N×T×d. It produces a binary classification label: seizure vs. non-seizure for detection, or pre-ictal (defined as the 1-minute window before onset) vs. normal for the more challenging prediction task, evaluated over 12-second and 60-second epochs.

## Method

EvoBrain is a time-then-graph architecture built on explicit dynamic graph modeling. For each time snapshot, a sparse directed weighted graph is constructed from pairwise normalized cross-correlations between channels (retaining only top-τ edges), so both node features and adjacency matrices evolve over time. A two-stream Mamba (selective state space model) independently processes the temporal sequences of node features and edge weights, using input-dependent gating to implement selective forgetting and updating analogous to neuromodulatory synaptic dynamics. The resulting static graph is then encoded by a GCN augmented with Laplacian Positional Encoding, which injects eigenvector-based positional codes to preserve the spatial identity of individual brain regions that standard GNN message-passing would otherwise collapse. The paper provides formal proofs (Theorems 1 and 2) establishing that explicit dynamic modeling is strictly more expressive than static graphs, and that time-then-graph is strictly more expressive than both time-and-graph and graph-then-time under 1-WL GNNs.

## Results

On the TUSZ dataset (12-second window, seizure detection), EvoBrain achieves an AUROC of 0.877 and F1 of 0.539, outperforming the strongest dynamic GNN baseline GRU-GCN (AUROC 0.856, F1 0.505) and the large-scale foundation model LaBraM (AUROC 0.825, F1 0.472) by 14% and 12% in AUROC and F1 respectively. For the 60-second detection window, EvoBrain reaches AUROC 0.865 and F1 0.483, a 23% AUROC and 30% F1 improvement over the EvolveGCN dynamic GNN baseline (0.670 / 0.340). On the more challenging seizure prediction task (12-second window), EvoBrain attains AUROC 0.675 and F1 0.470, maintaining a 13.8% AUROC advantage over the best dynamic GNN baseline. EvoBrain is also 17× faster in training and 14× faster in inference than the SOTA time-and-graph model DCRNN.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EvoBrain outperforms all nine baselines across both detection and prediction tasks on TUSZ. Against the strongest dynamic GNN baseline GRU-GCN (time-then-graph), EvoBrain gains +2.1 AUROC points (0.877 vs 0.856) and +3.4 F1 points (0.539 vs 0.505) on 12s detection. Against the time-and-graph model DCRNN, the gap widens to +6.0 AUROC (0.877 vs 0.817) and +12.4 F1 (0.539 vs 0.415). Against the graph-then-time EvolveGCN, EvoBrain achieves a dramatic +23% relative AUROC improvement (0.865 vs 0.670) and +30% F1 improvement (0.483 vs 0.340) on 60s detection. Notably, EvoBrain surpasses the large-scale pretrained foundation model LaBraM by +5.2 AUROC (0.877 vs 0.825) and +6.7 F1 (0.539 vs 0.472) on 12s detection while using 30× fewer parameters. On the harder prediction task, EvoBrain leads all dynamic GNN baselines (best prior: GRU-GCN at 0.659 AUROC) by +1.6 AUROC points, though LaBraM slightly edges it on F1 (0.482 vs 0.470) at 12s, likely benefiting from large-scale pretraining. Conventional methods (SVM, Random Forest, LSTM, CNN-LSTM) and the BIOT transformer all trail EvoBrain by 10+ AUROC points on detection.

## Relation to Prior Work

EvoBrain sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) dynamic GNNs for EEG, where prior work (DCRNN, EvolveGCN, GRAPHS4MER) typically fixes a single static adjacency matrix across all time steps; (2) the architectural taxonomy of Gao and Ribeiro (2022), which distinguishes time-then-graph, graph-then-time, and time-and-graph paradigms but does not provide EEG-specific expressivity analysis; and (3) selective state space models (Mamba), which the paper repurposes as a biologically motivated temporal encoder with input-dependent gating. The key departure is the combination of explicit per-snapshot dynamic graph construction (via normalized cross-correlation) with a time-then-graph pipeline that applies the GNN only once after temporal encoding, rather than at every time step as in recurrent GNN approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical proof that time-then-graph is strictly more expressive than time-and-graph for dynamic EEG graphs provides a principled justification for a simpler, faster architecture, and the 17× training speedup makes real-time clinical deployment more feasible. The clinical visualization of evolving graph structures (focal vs. generalized seizures) offers a tangible interpretability angle for neurosurgeons planning interventions. However, the seizure prediction task remains challenging: pre-ictal patterns are weaker and more spatially diffuse, and the larger pretrained LaBraM model still outperforms EvoBrain on prediction, suggesting that scale and pretraining remain important. Open questions include generalization beyond seizure tasks, handling demographic bias in EEG data, and whether the dynamic graph construction (top-τ cross-correlation) is optimal or could be replaced by learned connectivity.
