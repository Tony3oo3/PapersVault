## Problem

Large language models are static after pretraining: they consume new data 'as-is' via finetuning or in-context learning without any mechanism to restructure, augment, or configure how they learn from it. This paper asks whether an LLM can self-adapt by generating its own finetuning data and optimization directives, effectively learning how to learn from new inputs rather than passively ingesting them.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252483/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives either a text passage (knowledge incorporation) or a set of few-shot input-output demonstrations (ARC tasks) as context. It produces a 'self-edit'—a natural-language generation that specifies synthetic training sequences (e.g., logical implications, rewrites, QA pairs) and, in the few-shot setting, a JSON configuration of data augmentations and optimization hyperparameters (learning rate, epochs, loss token selection). The self-edit is not a final answer but a directive: it is fed back into the same model as SFT training data (with LoRA), producing updated weights that are then evaluated on a held-out task to compute the RL reward.

## Method

SEAL uses two nested loops: an outer RL loop that optimizes the self-edit generation policy, and an inner update loop that applies each generated self-edit via supervised finetuning (LoRA) and evaluates the resulting model on a downstream task. Because the reward depends on the model's own (changing) parameters, the RL problem is non-stationary; the authors handle this with on-policy ReST^EM, where the model samples multiple self-edits per context, keeps only those whose application improves downstream accuracy (binary reward), and performs SFT on the retained prompt-completion pairs. No separate adaptation module or hypernetwork is needed—the model's own autoregressive generation directly parameterizes the weight update. In the knowledge-incorporation domain, the self-edit is a list of passage implications finetuned via LoRA; in the few-shot ARC domain, the self-edit is a structured JSON specifying which augmentation tools to invoke and what training hyperparameters to use, enabling the model to autonomously configure its own test-time training pipeline.

## Results

On the knowledge incorporation task (no-context SQuAD QA with Qwen2.5-7B), SEAL achieves 47.0% accuracy in the single-passage LoRA setting, surpassing GPT-4.1-generated synthetic data (46.3%) and the base model (32.7%). In the continued pretraining regime (n=200 passages), SEAL reaches 58.2%, approaching GPT-4.1's 59.4%. On few-shot ARC-AGI (Llama-3.2-1B), SEAL achieves a 72.5% adaptation success rate, substantially outperforming in-context learning (0%) and untrained self-editing (20%), though remaining below the Oracle TTT upper bound (100%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GPT-4.1 synthetic data, SEAL wins the single-passage setting (47.0% vs. 46.3%) despite using a much smaller 7B model, but trails slightly in the CPT regime (58.2% vs. 59.4% at n=200; 46.4% vs. 49.2% at n=2067). Against Oracle TTT (Akyürek et al., 2025) on ARC, SEAL achieves 72.5% success rate versus the 100% upper bound, confirming room for improvement but demonstrating that RL-learned self-edits substantially close the gap over untrained self-editing (20%). Against Generative Adapter (Chen et al., 2025), SEAL loses the single-passage comparison (47.0% vs. 66.8%) but decisively wins CPT (58.2% vs. 28.0%), suggesting that parameterizing updates through synthetic data generation scales better than direct LoRA weight prediction. Against Entigraph (Yang et al., 2025), SEAL edges out the pairs+triples variant at n=200 (58.2% vs. 56.0%) but falls slightly behind at n=2067 (46.4% vs. 48.6%), indicating that structured heuristic generation remains competitive at larger scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

SEAL builds on the deductive closure training line (Akyürek et al., Lampinen et al.) that showed finetuning on model-generated implications of a passage improves knowledge retention, and on test-time training (Akyürek et al. 2025) for few-shot adaptation. The key departure is wrapping these in a reinforcement learning outer loop (ReST-EM) that trains the model to generate self-edits optimized for downstream utility, rather than relying on static prompts or manually tuned heuristics. This positions SEAL as a meta-learning framework where the model's own generation parameterizes its weight updates, contrasting with hypernetwork approaches (Generative Adapter) that predict LoRA weights directly and with prior synthetic-data pipelines that lack a feedback signal tying generation quality to adaptation success.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SEAL offers a concrete path toward the 'data wall' problem: as human-generated text is exhausted, models that can generate high-utility training signal for themselves become essential. The demonstration that a 7B model after two RL rounds matches or exceeds GPT-4.1 at producing effective synthetic data for self-adaptation is a notable result, though the margin is thin and reverses at larger CPT scales. Significant open questions remain: catastrophic forgetting under sequential self-edits is clearly present, the computational cost of the inner-loop reward (30–45 s per evaluation) limits scalability, and the current setup requires paired evaluation tasks that prevent training on unlabeled corpora. The framework's generality—applicable to knowledge incorporation, few-shot learning, and potentially agentic continual learning—makes it a promising foundation, but the small model sizes (1B, 7B) and narrow benchmarks leave its behavior at frontier scale untested.
