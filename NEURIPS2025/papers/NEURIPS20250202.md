## Problem

Schrödinger bridge (SB) methods achieve state-of-the-art style transfer on natural images but introduce unwanted anatomical shifts when applied to medical image harmonization. The paper identifies the root cause: the SB's entropy-regularized optimal transport formulation uses squared Euclidean distance as the transport cost, which is inappropriate because medical images sharing the same anatomy but differing in contrast need not be close in pixel intensity space. This makes direct SB application unreliable for tasks where anatomical fidelity is paramount.

## Input / Output

The method takes 2D OCT B-scans from either a Zeiss Cirrus (1024×512, 2 μm/pixel axial) or Heidelberg Spectralis (496×1024, 4 μm/pixel axial) scanner, cropped and resized to a common 128×512 resolution, and produces a harmonized B-scan in the target scanner's contrast. No paired images are required; training uses only unpaired datasets from both scanners plus automatically generated segmentation label maps as auxiliary targets for the invertible network. The pipeline operates on individual B-scans independently within volumes.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage anatomy-guided latent metric Schrödinger bridge (LMSB). First, a volume-preserving invertible neural network (INN) built from affine coupling layers with U-Net backbones and a checkerboard split maps both Cirrus and Spectralis B-scans into a shared latent Euclidean metric space, trained to minimize distance to a common 'latent contrast' (segmentation label map) so that images with identical anatomy but different contrast are pulled together. The volume-preserving constraint (unit Jacobian determinant) and Gaussian noise augmentation ensure the INN retains invertibility and preserves fine structures like vessels and shadows. Second, a diffusion Schrödinger bridge matching (DSBM) procedure is trained in this learned latent space to transport the source distribution to the target distribution, using iterative Markovian fitting with 30 steps and 100 diffusion steps. Because the latent metric compresses distances along the contrast direction, the SB's Euclidean transport cost no longer conflates contrast change with anatomy change, reducing anatomical shift during harmonization.

## Results

On the OCT harmonization task (1,500 test B-scans per direction), LMSB with ODE sampling achieves the best average retinal boundary MAE of 0.43 px (Spectralis→Cirrus) and 0.38 px (Cirrus→Spectralis), outperforming DSBM ODE (0.49 / 0.47) and DDIB ODE (0.89 / 1.08). For structural similarity, LMSB ODE attains the highest LNCC of 0.66 and 0.69, versus 0.63/0.67 for DSBM ODE and 0.65/0.63 for DDIB ODE. In terms of domain consistency (FID), LMSB ODE scores 25.34 (Spectralis→Cirrus) and 32.97 (Cirrus→Spectralis), slightly higher than DSBM ODE (24.26 / 31.39) but far better than DDIB ODE (48.48 / 50.97), with the pre-harmonization FID being 140.53.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares LMSB against two baselines: DSBM (Diffusion Schrödinger Bridge Matching, Shi et al. 2023) applied directly in image space, and DDIB (Dual Diffusion Implicit Bridge, Su et al. 2023). On anatomy preservation (MAE), LMSB ODE wins on 7 of 10 boundary rows in each direction, achieving the best average MAE (0.43 vs. 0.49 for DSBM ODE and 0.89 for DDIB ODE in Spectralis→Cirrus; 0.38 vs. 0.47 and 1.08 in Cirrus→Spectralis). On structural similarity (LNCC), LMSB ODE also leads (0.66/0.69 vs. 0.63/0.67 for DSBM ODE and 0.65/0.63 for DDIB ODE). However, on domain consistency (FID), DSBM ODE slightly outperforms LMSB ODE (24.26 vs. 25.34 for Spectralis→Cirrus; 31.39 vs. 32.97 for Cirrus→Spectralis), which the authors note is expected since LMSB operates in a learned latent space rather than directly between the two original domains. Both DSBM and LMSB substantially outperform DDIB on FID (48.48/50.97), and ODE sampling consistently preserves anatomy far better than SDE sampling across all methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Diffusion Schrödinger Bridge Matching (DSBM) framework of Shi et al. (2023) and the underlying DSB formulation of De Bortoli et al. (2021), but departs from them by identifying a fundamental geometric flaw: the Euclidean transport cost implicit in SB is misaligned with anatomical structure in medical images. The key novelty is inserting an invertible neural network (a normalizing flow with affine coupling layers) between the image domain and the SB, creating a latent Euclidean metric space where same-anatomy images are pulled closer via a pullback metric. This combines the normalizing-flow literature (Dinh et al., Kingma & Dhariwal) with the Schrödinger bridge literature in a way that prior medical harmonization work (CycleGAN, UNIT, MUNIT, CUT, DDIB) has not attempted, and the paper provides a Riemannian-geometric analysis (Fig. 1) explaining why the Euclidean metric introduces anatomy shifts that the latent metric reduces.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Anatomy-preserving harmonization is a prerequisite for reliable multi-center and longitudinal OCT studies, and the paper demonstrates that the widely-used DSB/DSBM approach silently corrupts retinal boundaries — a problem that would invalidate downstream thickness measurements. The theoretical connection between SB and entropy-regularized OT with squared Euclidean cost, combined with the geometric decomposition into anatomy and contrast directions, gives a principled (if approximate) fix rather than an ad-hoc regularizer. Open questions include whether the segmentation-label-map latent contrast is optimal, whether the approach generalizes to more complex anatomies (brain MRI, cardiac CT), and whether the residual anatomy inconsistency (which the authors note actually helps reduce inter-device bias in paired measurements) can be further controlled.
