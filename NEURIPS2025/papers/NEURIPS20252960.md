## Problem

Membership inference (MI) tests are the de facto method for determining whether a data point was used to train a language model, but their reliability has been questioned under exact-match definitions. Recent work proposed relaxing membership to a neighborhood-based notion (semantic neighbors count as members), yet no prior work examined whether MI tests remain trustworthy under this relaxation when the training data is subject to adversarial poisoning. This paper shows that even under neighborhood-based membership, an adversary can poison the training set to flip MI test predictions while preserving the ground-truth membership label, and establishes a fundamental theoretical trade-off between a test's clean-data accuracy and its poisoning robustness.

## Input / Output

Given a target text sequence x_t, a training dataset D, a pretrained LLM θ, and a neighborhood definition (n-gram overlap, embedding cosine similarity, normalized edit distance, or exact match), PoisonM produces a single poison text sequence x_poison that must lie in the same membership class (neighbor or non-neighbor) as the clean point it replaces. The attack is MI-test agnostic: one poison per neighborhood definition works across all evaluated tests (LOSS, Min-K%, zlib, perturbation-based, reference-based) without the adversary knowing which test will be used. For false positives, the adversary needs b₂=10 poison non-neighbors; for false negatives, only b₁=1 poison neighbor suffices.

## Method

PoisonM exploits the misalignment between the geometric neighborhood boundary (defined by a distance metric) and the superlevel sets of the MI test (regions in sequence space that elicit high membership scores). The attack samples a point from the 'wrong' side of the neighborhood boundary (e.g., a true neighbor when constructing a poison non-neighbor) and then iteratively substitutes random tokens via greedy coordinate descent, choosing replacements that maximize cosine similarity of last-layer model activations with the target while simultaneously pushing the sequence across the neighborhood boundary (e.g., breaking n-gram overlap, reducing embedding similarity, or increasing edit distance). Theoretically, Theorem 5.1 proves that the sum of a test's clean-data advantage and its robust advantage is upper-bounded by the expected PoisonM mapping error δ*, meaning a test that is highly discriminative on clean data is inherently more vulnerable to poisoning. Empirically, PoisonM degrades AUC of all five MI tests well below random (0.5) across four neighborhood definitions, two datasets, and three model sizes (2.7B–12B Pythia), and remains effective under perplexity filtering and cross-model transfer.

## Results

PoisonM consistently degrades membership inference test AUC well below random (0.5) across all four neighborhood definitions, five MI tests, and both datasets. The most dramatic drop is the reference-based test under 7-gram neighborhood on AI4Privacy, falling from 0.647 natural AUC to 0.089 under PoisonM. Even in the least affected setting (Edit Distance, kmin, AGNews), PoisonM reduces AUC from 0.540 to 0.432. The paper also validates a theoretical trade-off: the test with the highest natural AUC (reference-based) consistently ranks lowest under poisoning, confirming that stronger clean-data performance increases vulnerability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares PoisonM against three baselines adapted from Liu et al. [13] (token dropouts and casing flips) and Panaitescu-Liess et al. [31] (chunk insertion), which were originally designed to force verbatim completion or elicit copyrighted content, not to manipulate MI tests. PoisonM outperforms all three baselines in every setting across both datasets. For example, under 7-gram neighborhood on AI4Privacy with the reference test, PoisonM achieves 0.089 AUC versus 0.250 (token dropouts), 0.398 (casing flips), and 0.618 (chunk) — the chunk baseline barely degrades performance. In the exact-match setting where all methods are restricted to poisoning non-neighbors only, PoisonM still dominates: 0.022 AUC for the reference test versus 0.044 (dropouts), 0.036 (casing), and 0.037 (chunk). The paper attributes PoisonM's advantage to two factors: its ability to generate poisons for both neighbors and non-neighbors (baselines only handle non-neighbors), and its targeted optimization of the MI test score rather than generic text perturbation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neighborhood-based membership inference framework formalized by Zhang et al. [10] by introducing robustness-to-poisoning definitions (RSens, RSpec) and proving a fundamental accuracy-robustness trade-off (Theorem 5.1). It departs from prior work questioning MI reliability (Duan et al. [9], Das et al. [11]) by showing that even the relaxed neighborhood-based definition—proposed as a fix for exact-match ambiguity—remains fundamentally vulnerable. The poisoning attack is distinct from traditional data poisoning targeting model behavior [15] and from Wen et al. [22], which showed poisoning can amplify MI scores; here the adversary specifically inverts MI test predictions while preserving ground-truth membership labels. The attack mechanism draws an explicit analogy to adversarial examples (misalignment between l_p balls and decision boundaries [25]), but applied to the training dataset rather than input perturbations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The trade-off theorem reveals that the very property making MI tests useful—strong separation of member/non-member score distributions—is what makes them vulnerable to poisoning, meaning no test can be simultaneously accurate and robust without additional assumptions. This has direct practical implications for copyright enforcement (a disgruntled author could plant poisons to trigger false positives) and fairness auditing (adversaries could plant poisons to create false negatives for minority-class points). Open questions include whether model-dependent or context-aware membership definitions could close the gap between neighborhood boundaries and test superlevel sets, whether the attack extends to pretraining (not just finetuning), and whether the b₂=10 budget for non-neighbor poisons can be reduced. The paper's scope is limited to finetuning and specific datasets, and the attack requires model access (though transferability is demonstrated).
