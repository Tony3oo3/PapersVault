## Problem

Computing SHAP values requires summing over all 2^n feature subsets, making exact computation intractable for moderate n. Existing black-box methods (KernelSHAP, LinRegSHAP) approximate this sum via stochastic sampling and per-instance optimization, while tree-specific methods (TreeSHAP) exploit structure but don't generalize. No prior approach leverages the fact that many real-world predictors—neural networks with spectral bias and tree ensembles—admit compact sparse Fourier representations that could eliminate the exponential sum entirely.

## Input / Output

The method requires binary (zero-one) features; continuous features must be discretized into quantiles or one-hot encoded categoricals. It consumes a query instance x*, a background dataset D (used to define the value function via marginal averaging), and either query access to a black-box predictor or full structural access to a tree ensemble. The Fourier approximation is computed once (amortized cost), after which each new query point only requires evaluating the closed-form summation. Output is the full n-dimensional SHAP value vector, exact when the Fourier representation is exact (trees) or accurate to the approximation quality of the sparse Fourier fit (black-box).

## Method

FOURIERSHAP is a two-stage algorithm. Stage 1 computes a k-sparse Fourier (Walsh-Hadamard) representation of the predictor: exactly and recursively for tree ensembles (degree ≤ depth, sparsity O(T·4^d)), or approximately for black-box models via sparse WHT algorithms with query complexity O(nk). Stage 2 exploits a key combinatorial result (Lemma 1) showing that the SHAP value of a single Fourier basis function Ψ_f(x) = (−1)^{⟨f,x⟩} admits a closed-form expression that collapses the exponential sum over all subsets S into a factor depending only on the parity of |A|+1, where A is the set of positions where the background sample disagrees with the query within the support of f. By linearity of SHAP values, the full SHAP vector is a simple double summation over k frequencies and |D| background points with complexity Θ(n·|D|·k), which is fully parallelizable across cores/GPUs via JAX vmap. This yields 10–10000× speedups over KernelSHAP/LinRegSHAP and outperforms DeepLift and FastSHAP in accuracy while maintaining a tunable speed-accuracy trade-off controlled by the sparsity parameter k.

## Results

FOURIERSHAP achieves 10–10,000× speedup over LINREGSHAP across all four dataset/model combinations (Entacmaea, SGEMM, GB1, avGFP) while maintaining competitive R² accuracy against ground-truth SHAP values. In the neural network setting, it outperforms the white-box DeepLift by 10–100× in runtime while also achieving higher R², despite assuming only black-box query access. In the tree setting, FOURIERSHAP delivers order-of-magnitude speedups over TREESHAP, FASTTREESHAP, GPU-TREESHAP, and PLTREESHAP on Entacmaea (n=13) and SGEMM (n=40), with diminishing but still significant gains on GB1 (n=80) and avGFP (n=236) as tree depth increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LINREGSHAP (variance-reduced KernelSHAP), FOURIERSHAP is 10–10,000× faster on every dataset/model variation, since LINREGSHAP still requires solving a constrained least-squares optimization per instance. Against DeepLift (white-box, requires layer activations), FOURIERSHAP is 10–100× faster and more accurate in the neural network setting, while operating under the stricter black-box (query-only) assumption. Against FASTSHAP (amortized MLP explainer), FOURIERSHAP achieves higher R² in 3 out of 4 settings at comparable speedup, and offers a reliable, fine-grained speed–accuracy knob via the Fourier sparsity parameter k, whereas FASTSHAP's accuracy depends unpredictably on MLP capacity. Against TREESHAP (the standard exact tree method), FOURIERSHAP achieves order-of-magnitude speedups on Entacmaea and SGEMM across all depths, with the gap narrowing on GB1 and avGFP at higher depths due to the growing number of Fourier frequencies. Against FASTTREESHAP, GPU-TREESHAP, and PLTREESHAP (the fastest available tree-SHAP implementations), FOURIERSHAP is faster in most settings, though the advantage diminishes as tree depth and feature count increase the sparsity k of the Fourier representation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) the spectral bias literature showing that neural networks and tree ensembles have low-degree, sparse representations in the Walsh-Hadamard (Fourier) basis [Yang & Salman 2019, Kushilevitz & Mansour 1993], and (2) the SHAP value computation literature that has produced increasingly efficient sampling-based (KernelSHAP, LinRegSHAP), learned-explainer (FastSHAP), and tree-specific (TreeSHAP, FastTreeSHAP, PLTreeSHAP) algorithms. FOURIERSHAP departs from all of these by exploiting the Fourier structure to derive a closed-form expression for SHAP values of each basis function, thereby replacing the exponential coalition sum with a linear summation over Fourier coefficients and background data points. The sparse WHT algorithms of Amrollahi et al. [2019] provide the first-stage approximation, while the combinatorial identity in Lemma 1 is the paper's own theoretical contribution that makes the second stage tractable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The exponential cost of exact SHAP computation is a recognized bottleneck in industrial pipelines with millions of instances, and FOURIERSHAP's amortized design (compute Fourier approximation once, then evaluate in O(n·|D|·k) per query) addresses this directly with 10–10000× speedups over sampling-based baselines. The tunable sparsity parameter k gives practitioners a reliable, fine-grained speed-accuracy knob that learned-explainer methods like FastSHAP do not offer. Key limitations include the requirement for binary (or discretized) features, the one-time cost of the sparse WHT step which grows linearly in k, and the fact that for very high-degree functions the required sparsity k can become large. Open questions include whether the approach extends naturally to continuous features without lossy discretization, and whether the Fourier approximation quality degrades for models with strong high-order interactions.
