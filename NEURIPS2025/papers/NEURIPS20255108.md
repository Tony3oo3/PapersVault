## Problem

Large reasoning models (LRMs) trained with reinforcement learning often overthink, generating redundant reasoning paths regardless of task difficulty, and rely on fixed reasoning strategies that cannot adapt to problem complexity. Existing compression methods either apply uniform length penalties or depend on offline difficulty estimation, limiting their adaptability. This paper asks whether LRMs can learn to dynamically switch between fast (intuitive) and slow (deliberate) thinking modes based on task complexity, inspired by dual process theory in cognitive science.

## Input / Output

The model takes a mathematical reasoning question as input and produces a structured reasoning path where each step is explicitly wrapped in either <fast_think></fast_think> or <slow_think></slow_think> tokens, followed by a final answer in boxed format. The fast/slow token annotations make the model's cognitive allocation transparent and interpretable, and the proportion of each mode in the output is directly used as a reward signal during training. Evaluation uses pass@1 over 16 sampled responses with temperature 0.6, reporting accuracy, average token count, and the ACU metric (accuracy per computation unit).

## Method

ACPO is a two-stage training framework. Stage 1 (cold start) fine-tunes the model on 745 annotated samples where GPT-4 compares short and long correct responses to label essential reasoning steps as slow thinking and trivial/omitted steps as fast thinking. Stage 2 applies GRPO with a three-component reward: (1) an accuracy reward (+1/−1), (2) an online Token Length Budget (TLB) reward that computes the budget L_budget = p·L_r + (1−p)·L_max directly from the current GRPO sampling group's success rate p, penalizing deviation from this difficulty-adaptive target via tanh, and (3) a system pattern reward that encourages a higher proportion of fast thinking tokens when p > 0.5 (easy) and a higher proportion of slow thinking tokens otherwise (hard). The final reward is clipped to remain strictly positive for correct and strictly negative for incorrect responses, and the policy is optimized via the standard GRPO clipped surrogate objective with KL regularization.

## Results

On MATH 500, ACPO-7B reduces average token count from 3,977 to 1,405 (64.7% reduction) while maintaining 91.6% accuracy (vs. 92.8% baseline), achieving an ACU of 0.93 (2.8× improvement over the base model's 0.33). On AIME 2024, ACPO-1.5B achieves 30.0% accuracy with 6,670 tokens (60.5% reduction from 16,894), outperforming the base model's 28.9% accuracy. On the simpler GSM8K dataset, ACPO-7B improves accuracy from 86.5% to 88.3% while reducing tokens from 445 to 413, demonstrating that the method avoids over-compression on easy problems.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the DeepSeek-R1 base models, ACPO consistently achieves 50–65% token reduction with only 1–3% accuracy drop, yielding 2–3× ACU improvements across all three model sizes. Compared to ThinkPrune (fixed 2k token limit), ACPO-1.5B achieves higher ACU (3.22 vs. 2.35 on MATH 500) and better AIME accuracy (30.0% vs. 27.0%) while using fewer tokens (1,679 vs. 2,356). Against L1-Exact and L1-Max, which impose rigid length penalties, ACPO-1.5B maintains higher accuracy (81.0% vs. 79.8%/81.8% on MATH 500) and substantially better AIME performance (30.0% vs. 16.7%/23.3%), showing that fixed-length constraints cause over-compression on hard problems. Versus DAST's SimPO_DAST (offline difficulty-adaptive preference optimization), ACPO-7B achieves comparable MATH 500 accuracy (91.6% vs. 92.6%) while halving the token count (1,405 vs. 2,802) and improving AIME ACU (0.17 vs. 0.12). Against DeepScaleR-1.5B-Preview (GRPO-trained), ACPO-1.5B trades 6.8% MATH 500 accuracy for a 57% token reduction, reflecting the inherent accuracy-efficiency trade-off of aggressive compression.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on GRPO as its RL backbone and extends the Token Length Budget concept from DAST by computing it online during training rather than offline, eliminating the need for pre-sampled preference pairs. It departs from prior efficient-reasoning methods (L1, ThinkPrune, TokenSkip) that apply uniform length penalties by introducing system-aware reasoning tokens (<fast_think>, <slow_think>) inspired by Kahneman's dual-process theory, making cognitive mode allocation an explicit, trainable signal. The two-stage SFT-then-RL pipeline and the three-component reward (accuracy + online TLB + system pattern) represent a novel combination, but each individual ingredient—GRPO, TLB estimation, and fast/slow thinking—has precedent in the literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a practical, interpretable mechanism for reducing LRM inference cost: the explicit thinking-mode tokens make the model's cognitive allocation transparent and auditable, which is valuable for deployment where latency matters. However, the approach is constrained to verifiable tasks (math) where correctness is binary, and the accuracy trade-offs on MATH500 (e.g., 92.8→91.6 for 7B) suggest the reward design still struggles to perfectly balance compression and correctness. Open questions include whether online TLB generalizes to open-ended or multi-step tasks without ground-truth labels, and whether the system pattern reward can be replaced by a learned difficulty estimator to reduce reliance on the sampling success rate proxy.
