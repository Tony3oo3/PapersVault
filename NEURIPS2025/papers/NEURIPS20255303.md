## Problem

Existing reliable autoevaluation methods (R-AutoEval) that use prediction-powered inference to correct LLM-judge bias can degrade sample efficiency compared to conventional human-labeled evaluation (R-Eval) when the autoevaluator is inaccurate. No prior method simultaneously provides finite-sample reliability guarantees and a provable sample-efficiency guarantee that is no worse than R-Eval, leaving practitioners without a principled way to decide how much to trust synthetic data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes n human-labeled pairs (X_i, Y_i), N unlabeled inputs X~_i (partitioned into groups of r = ⌊N/n⌋ per round), a pre-trained autoevaluator f, and hyperparameters α, δ, S candidate reliance factors {ρ_s}, and initial weights {w_{s,0}}. It produces a sequential e-value E_n and a binary test decision T_n = 1{max_i E_i ≥ 1/δ} indicating whether the model's risk satisfies R ≤ α, with the test valid at any stopping time via Ville's inequality. The effective observations ℓ^f_{s,i} are bounded in [−ρ_s, 1+ρ_s], and the method requires the autoevaluator to be evaluable on the same real inputs X_i for bias correction.

## Method

R-AutoEval+ constructs S effective observations per round, each using PPI++ with a different reliance factor ρ_s ∈ [0,1] that scales the contribution of autoevaluator data relative to real data. It then builds a single e-value as a convex combination of S betting strategies (one per ρ_s), with weights w_{s,i} updated via exponential weights (online convex optimization) based on the accumulated log-e-value E_{s,i−1} for each candidate. This adaptive mechanism causes weights to concentrate on ρ_s = 0 (reverting to R-Eval) when the autoevaluator is unreliable and on ρ_s = 1 (R-AutoEval) when it is accurate, as demonstrated by the sublinear-regret guarantee in Lemma 1. Theorem 3 proves that the asymptotic sample complexity is bounded by min_s {1/g_{s,★}}, ensuring R-AutoEval+ is never worse than either R-Eval or R-AutoEval, with strict improvement when the autoevaluator is sufficiently accurate.

## Results

On the GSM8K reasoning budget allocation task, R-AutoEval+ saves up to 127 tokens over R-Eval and up to 66 tokens over R-AutoEval on average across six autoevaluators (e.g., 856.13 tokens with GPT-4.1 vs. 883.99 for R-AutoEval). On the TriviaQA quantization task, R-AutoEval+ selects a smaller (lighter) quantized Llama-3.1-8B model than both R-Eval and R-AutoEval when the LLM judge is sufficiently accurate (BF16/MX6), while recovering R-Eval's selection when the judge quality degrades (MX4). On the Instruct-Induction prompting task, R-AutoEval+ consistently selects shorter prompt templates than both baselines across all autoevaluator configurations. All methods maintain the target reliability guarantee (no performance drop exceeding α=10% or accuracy below 1−α).

## Baselines & Comparisons

R-AutoEval+ is compared against R-Eval (testing-by-betting on real data only) and R-AutoEval (PPI-based correction using synthetic data). On the GSM8K reasoning task, R-AutoEval+ outperforms R-AutoEval in every autoevaluator setting (e.g., 856.13 vs. 883.99 tokens with GPT-4.1; 866.22 vs. 893.39 with DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B), and outperforms R-Eval when the latter is available (941.20 vs. 983.34 with Qwen3-32B). On the TriviaQA quantization task, R-AutoEval+ selects a smaller model than both baselines when the LLM judge is accurate (BF16/MX6), but reverts to matching R-Eval when the judge is poor (MX4), confirming the theoretical guarantee that it is never worse than either baseline. On the Instruct-Induction prompting task, R-AutoEval+ consistently selects shorter prompts than both R-Eval and R-AutoEval regardless of autoevaluator quality. Notably, R-AutoEval can be worse than R-Eval (e.g., 1007.45 vs. 983.34 tokens with Qwen3-32B), while R-AutoEval+ avoids this degradation by adaptively reducing reliance on synthetic data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established frameworks: the testing-by-betting approach for mean estimation (Waudby-Smith & Ramdas, 2024) and prediction-powered inference (PPI/PPI++, Angelopoulos et al., 2023–2024). R-Eval applies testing-by-betting to real data only, while R-AutoEval (Einbinder et al., 2024) extends it to incorporate synthetic data via PPI bias correction but without sample-efficiency guarantees. R-AutoEval+ departs from both by introducing an adaptive exponential-weights mechanism over a discrete grid of PPI++ regularization coefficients ρ, allowing the e-value to dynamically shift between R-Eval (ρ=0) and R-AutoEval (ρ=1) based on accumulated evidence about autoevaluator quality. The universal portfolio betting strategy (Cover, 1991) provides the sublinear-regret betting strategy required for the theoretical guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first autoevaluation method with a provable finite-sample efficiency guarantee (at least no worse than both R-Eval and R-AutoEval, strictly better when the autoevaluator is sufficiently accurate), closing a gap where prior PPI-based methods either offered only asymptotic reliability or lacked efficiency guarantees. Practically, this matters for LLM deployment pipelines where LLM-as-judge evaluation is standard but costly to calibrate. Key limitations include the requirement for unlabeled real data, the a priori discrete grid of ρ values, and the efficiency guarantee holding only for sufficiently high reliability levels (small δ). Open directions include combining adaptive synthetic-data weighting with active real-data selection and extending to continuous ρ optimization.
