## Problem

Pre-trained language models achieve strong zero-shot accuracy but suffer from two deployment obstacles: their decision process is opaque (predictions emerge from billions of latent parameters with only indirect saliency explanations), and autoregressive inference is computationally expensive, typically requiring GPU-class hardware. No existing method extracts an explicit, interpretable, and CPU-efficient classifier directly from a frozen PLM without any gradient-based fine-tuning.

## Input / Output

The method takes natural-language sentences or sentence pairs as input, which are embedded via a frozen text-embedding model (text-embedding-ada-002, 1536-dim) to form a positive-definite language kernel. At test time, classification reduces to a weighted vote over at most 32 natural-language support vectors, and the dual SVM coefficients serve as exact per-sentence influence scores, yielding faithful sentence-level rationales alongside the predicted class label. For sentence-pair tasks, the kernel operates on embedding differences κ(φ(x₂)−φ(x₁), φ(x₄)−φ(x₃)).

## Method

SVG is a training-free, two-stage procedure that converts a frozen PLM into an explicit kernel classifier. Stage 1 runs a Metropolis-Hastings sampler whose proposal distribution is the PLM's completion API, synthesising candidate sentences near the decision boundary; the acceptance probability is approximated using the current SVM dual coefficients and forward/backward completion probabilities. Stage 2 solves the dual SVM objective (hinge loss with box constraints) restricted to the generated candidates via SMO, retaining only sentences with non-zero dual weights as the explicit support set. The key theoretical insight is that the PLM's negative log-likelihood induces a positive-definite language kernel k_θ(x,x′)=exp[−log p_θ(x)−log p_θ(x′)], embedding all sentences in an RKHS whose dimensionality is independent of |θ|, so the optimal large-margin classifier is a finite weighted sum of kernel evaluations. The paper proves SVG minimises empirical hinge loss within the span of its supports and derives a generalization bound that scales with the support count (≤32) rather than the PLM parameter count, enabling CPU-only training in under three minutes per GLUE task.

## Results

On the GLUE zero-shot benchmark, SVG achieves 91.7% accuracy on SST-2, 72.9 F1 on QQP, 64.0% on QNLI, and 57.9% on RTE, all computed on a single 3 GHz CPU core with 1 GB RAM in under 49 seconds per task. The method surpasses the prompting baseline on all seven tasks, with the largest gains on QQP (+23.2 F1) and QNLI (+13.2 Acc.). An ablation without the MCMC sampling stage drops SST-2 to 88.9% and QQP to 65.7 F1, confirming that Metropolis-Hastings support-vector generation is critical for the performance lift.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole external baseline is the prompting method of Gao et al. [13] ("Making pre-trained language models better few-shot learners"), which uses manually constructed label prompts with a frozen PLM. SVG outperforms this baseline on all seven GLUE tasks: SST-2 91.7 vs. 83.6 Acc. (+8.1), CoLA 9.1 vs. 2.0 Matt. (+7.1), QQP 72.9 vs. 49.7 F1 (+23.2), MRPC 63.7 vs. 61.9 F1 (+1.8), RTE 57.9 vs. 51.3 Acc. (+6.6), QNLI 64.0 vs. 50.8 Acc. (+13.2), and MNLI 51.8 vs. 51.7 3-way Acc. (+0.1). The most substantial margins appear on sentence-pair tasks (QQP, QNLI), where the kernel's ability to capture inter-sentence similarity via the difference-of-embeddings formulation (Eq. 7) provides a clear advantage over flat prompting. The MRPC margin is small and the SVG variance (±12.1) is notably high, suggesting that paraphrase detection remains the weakest task for this approach. Overall, SVG matches or exceeds the prompting baseline across the board while additionally providing explicit sentence-level rationales and running entirely on CPU.

## Relation to Prior Work

SVG sits at the intersection of classical kernel methods (SVMs in an RKHS, Cortes & Vapnik 1995) and modern PLM-based zero-shot classification. Rather than fine-tuning a transformer or using fixed prompt templates (Gao et al., 2020), the paper repurposes a frozen GPT-3 model as a positive-definite language kernel and uses Metropolis-Hastings MCMC to iteratively propose sentences near the emerging decision boundary, then solves the SVM dual on those candidates. This 'teacher-student communication' framing—where the PLM generates support vectors and a non-parametric kernel machine learns the classifier—departs from both the prompting paradigm (which uses a small fixed set of label descriptions) and the fine-tuning paradigm (which requires gradient updates over billions of parameters). The theoretical contribution ties the generalization bound to the number of support vectors rather than to |θ|, echoing the dual-space analysis of Zhang et al. (2016) but in a generative, sampling-driven setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical appeal is clear: a classifier distilled to ≤32 natural-language support vectors is inherently interpretable, requires no GPU, and trains in under three minutes per task. However, the approach remains tightly coupled to a proprietary cloud API (OpenAI GPT-3) for both the kernel and the MCMC proposals, so the 'CPU-only' claim applies only to the SVM solver, not the full pipeline. Scalability is a genuine open question—kernel methods are O(n²) in the number of samples, and the paper itself acknowledges this limitation for many-shot or high-dimensional settings. The observed 'few-shot double descent' (accuracy peaking at 2 labeled examples, dipping, then recovering) is intriguing but left unanalyzed, and the evaluation is confined to the GLUE suite with only three runs, leaving open whether the gains over prompting generalize to newer benchmarks or larger model families.
