## Problem

AI-driven therapeutic design methods benchmarked on in silico tasks like GuacaMol ignore real-world constraints such as mutagenicity, hERG blockade, and ADME properties, because this experimental knowledge remains locked in natural-language scientific literature rather than structured databases. The authors show that over 60% of molecules proposed by existing design methods have a high probability of being mutagenic, and fewer than 10% survive even basic safety filtering. This paper addresses the gap by constructing Medex, a large-scale dataset of 32.3M (entity, fact) pairs distilled from the literature, and demonstrating that models pretrained on it can incorporate these priors into both prediction and design.

## Input / Output

The method consumes canonical SMILES strings (Kekulé form, tokenized via a SMIRK-like tokenizer) for small molecules, amino acid sequences for proteins, and short natural-language fact sentences (e.g., 'detectable in blood and brain') as the two modalities to be aligned. It produces a shared 128-dimensional joint embedding space in which structure and text co-occur; downstream, a single-hidden-layer MLP head maps these embeddings to binary classification scores (AUROC/Accuracy), continuous regression values (MAE/PCC/Spearman), or feasibility constraints for Bayesian optimization of de novo molecules. The contrastive pretraining uses only the 32.3M (SMILES/RefSeq, fact) pairs from Medex, while supervised fine-tuning uses the corresponding TDC task datasets.

## Method

MedexCLIP is a CLIP-style contrastive model that learns to align frozen structure encoders (a T5 with hidden size 256 for molecules, ProtT5-XL for proteins) and a frozen text encoder (stella_en_1.5B) through two 2-layer MLP adapters projecting into a shared 128-dim space, trained with a noise-contrastive loss and a learned temperature parameter. The Medex dataset itself is built via an entity-first pipeline: PubChem and UniProt link entities to ~43M papers, GROBID parses PDFs into 400M+ paragraphs, a Llama 405B model distilled into Llama 3.1 8B via LoRA tags entities per paragraph (90.5% precision), and GPT-4.1 distilled into Gemma 3 4B extracts concise, context-independent facts. Entity normalization converts chemical names to canonical SMILES via PubChem titles and OPSIN, and gene names to NCBI IDs via GNorm2. Two alternative architectures—MedexLLaVA (projecting structure embeddings into a Qwen 0.5B LM's token space) and MedexLM (text-only instruction tuning on fact-derived multiple-choice questions)—are also evaluated, with MedexCLIP consistently outperforming them. For zero-shot evaluation, the model uses prototypical networks: GPT-4.1 generates ten positive and ten negative fact descriptions per task, and unseen molecules are classified by inner-product similarity to the averaged class prototypes without any parameter updates.

## Results

On 35 TDC classification tasks, MedexCLIP (15M trainable parameters) achieves a mean AUROC/Accuracy of 0.771, outperforming the 2B-parameter TxGemma generalist (0.768) and beating task-specific SOTA specialists on 10/35 tasks. On 28 regression tasks, MedexCLIP surpasses TxGemma-2B on 23/28, with particularly large gains on drug synergy MAE (5.215 vs. 9.724) and drug-target interaction PCC (0.654 vs. 0.525). In zero-shot evaluation, MedexCLIP achieves a mean AUROC of 0.718 across 9 design-relevant endpoints, a 74% relative improvement over the Gemma-2B baseline (0.411). On GuacaMol, constrained Bayesian optimization using MedexCLIP as a safety filter produces molecules with scores close to unconstrained BO while eliminating predicted toxic candidates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MedexCLIP (15M trainable parameters) is compared against two baselines: TxGemma 2B (a Gemma-based generalist fine-tuned on all TDC tasks) and TDC LM (a Qwen 500M model fine-tuned exclusively on TDC labels without Medex). Against TxGemma 2B, MedexCLIP wins on the majority of tasks: it leads on toxicity (0.837 vs. 0.822 AUROC), pharmacokinetics classification (0.830 vs. 0.805 AUROC), drug synergy (5.215 vs. 9.724 MAE), drug-target interaction (0.654 vs. 0.525 PCC), and reaction yields (0.921 vs. 0.661 PCC), while trailing on a few tasks including clinical trial outcome (0.631 vs. 0.679 AUROC), protein interaction (0.857 vs. 0.861 AUROC), and antibody affinity (2.338 vs. 1.066 MAE). Against TDC LM, MedexCLIP shows consistent and often large advantages, most notably on pharmacokinetics AUROC (0.830 vs. 0.726), high-throughput screening (0.737 vs. 0.620), and developability MAE (3.672 vs. 6.144), though TDC LM edges ahead on clinical trial outcome (0.683 vs. 0.631) and protein interaction AUROC (0.868 vs. 0.857). The key takeaway is that a 15M-parameter model pretrained on literature-distilled facts matches or exceeds a 133× larger generalist on most TDC benchmarks, demonstrating that the knowledge priors in Medex are the primary driver of performance rather than raw model scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

MedexCLIP directly adapts CLIP's noise-contrastive loss to align molecular structure embeddings (SMILES via T5, proteins via ProtT5) with natural-language fact embeddings, while MedexLLaVA follows LLaVA's projection-into-token-space paradigm for soft-prompting a language model with structure representations. The zero-shot evaluation borrows the prototypical-networks framework for class-prototype matching. Conceptually, the paper departs from prior therapeutic instruction datasets (SMolInstruct, Mol-Instructions, DrugChat) that rely on template-based examples from curated databases like PubChem and ChEMBL; instead, Medex mines free-form factual statements from 43M+ scientific papers via LLM-based entity tagging and fact distillation, positioning unstructured literature as the primary training signal rather than structured bioactivity tables.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 32.3M-fact dataset is a substantial new resource that makes literature-derived priors accessible to small multimodal models, and the demonstration that 15M trainable parameters can match or exceed 2B-parameter generalists on TDC benchmarks lowers the computational barrier for academic drug-design groups. The GuacaMol safety analysis (>60% of top-scoring molecules flagged as potentially mutagenic) provides a concrete, widely-applicable motivation for incorporating literature priors into de novo design pipelines. Key open questions include how to weight facts by provenance and experimental quality, whether the implicit cross-document graph structure (corroboration, contradiction) can be exploited, and whether the contrastive pretraining approach generalizes to entity types beyond small molecules and proteins.
