## Problem

The paper challenges the entrenched narrative that AI regulation and innovation are in opposition, arguing that this false dichotomy has already produced measurable harm through algorithmic bias, synthetic misinformation, and unaccountable high-stakes decision-making. It asks whether AI systems that systematically violate fundamental rights can genuinely be called 'innovative,' and identifies the Collingridge Dilemma—the tension between acting early enough to prevent harm and flexibly enough to sustain progress—as the core governance challenge that current deregulatory approaches fail to address.

## Input / Output

As a position paper with no computational pipeline, the analytical input consists of regulatory texts (EU AI Act, GDPR, the revoked US Executive Order 14110), historical case studies (thalidomide, Dutch SyRI, aviation safety records), and empirical evidence of AI harms (Slovak election deepfakes, biased credit scoring, predictive policing). The output is a structured argument reframing regulation as an enabler of responsible innovation, operationalized through Table 1, which maps eight perceived regulatory burdens to corresponding benefits across sandboxes, SME support, and competitive-advantage categories.

## Method

The paper employs a multi-layered policy-analysis framework built on three analytical moves. First, it draws historical analogies from aviation, pharmaceuticals, and welfare systems to demonstrate that robust regulation has consistently fostered rather than hindered technological progress (e.g., a 1,000-fold improvement in aviation safety post-FAA). Second, it applies the Collingridge Dilemma as a theoretical lens to argue that early, adaptive governance is necessary to prevent AI from becoming too deeply embedded to regulate, and grounds the economic case in the Porter hypothesis that well-designed regulation stimulates innovation. Third, it conducts a detailed case study of the EU AI Act's risk-based architecture, dissecting specific mechanisms—regulatory sandboxes (Art. 57), real-world testing (Art. 60), SME support (Art. 62), and Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment (Art. 27)—to show how each converts a perceived compliance burden into a trust-building or competitive advantage. The analysis is anchored in the OECD's responsible innovation framework and the Council of Europe's AI Convention, culminating in a structured comparison (Table 1) that systematically pairs common deregulatory objections with corresponding regulatory benefits.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experimental results. Its central argument is qualitative: the EU AI Act's risk-based regulatory framework (sandboxes, FRIA, SME support, real-world testing) transforms perceived compliance burdens into competitive advantages such as legal certainty, consumer trust, and ethical differentiation. The paper supports this claim with historical analogies (aviation safety improved >1,000-fold post-regulation; thalidomide led to Kefauver-Harris Amendments) and documented AI harms (Slovak deepfake election interference, Dutch SyRI welfare scandal, COMPAS predictive-policing bias). No metrics, benchmarks, or ablations are reported.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not compare methods against empirical baselines. Instead, it contrasts two regulatory philosophies: the EU's risk-based, rights-anchored approach (EU AI Act) versus the US deregulatory turn (revocation of Executive Order 14110, AI Action Plan 2025). The authors argue the EU model wins on long-term viability by preventing the Collingridge Dilemma (entrenchment before harm is recognized), while the US approach risks repeating failures documented in aviation (pre-FAA accident rates of 1-in-25,000), pharmaceuticals (thalidomide), and welfare (SyRI). Historical sector comparisons serve as the paper's de facto 'baselines': regulated sectors (aviation, pharma) achieved >1,000× safety improvements and sustained innovation, whereas unregulated or under-regulated AI deployments have produced documented harms (deepfake election interference, algorithmic discrimination in credit and policing). The paper also engages alternative scholarly views (Draghi report, Ridley, Braben, Castro et al., Gikay, Wachter) as intellectual counter-arguments, acknowledging their concerns while contending that the EU's adaptive mechanisms (sandboxes, FRIA, SME provisions) address the over-regulation critique without sacrificing innovation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper repurposes the Collingridge Dilemma (1980) and the Porter Hypothesis from their original contexts (general technology governance, environmental regulation) to the specific AI regulatory landscape, synthesizing them with the OECD's 'responsible innovation' framework. It draws on historical regulatory case studies—aviation (FAA), pharmaceuticals (Kefauver-Harris Amendments), and welfare systems (Dutch SyRI)—to build an analogy-based argument, while positioning explicitly against the deregulation narrative embodied in the revoked US Executive Order 14110 and the 2025 AI Action Plan. The EU AI Act serves as the primary case study, building on prior legal scholarship about risk-based regulation (De Gregorio & Dunn, 2022) and the GDPR's compliance-driven innovation effects, but the paper's contribution is integrative rather than extending a specific prior research line.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a structured, multi-sector argument that well-designed AI regulation is a precondition for sustainable innovation rather than a barrier, which is timely given the active policy debate in both the US and EU. However, as a position paper, it lacks empirical evidence that the EU AI Act's mechanisms (sandboxes, FRIA, SME support) actually produce the claimed competitive advantages—the Act is still in its implementation phase, so the Porter-hypothesis-style benefits remain hypothetical. Open questions include whether the risk-based classification is granular enough to avoid both under- and over-regulation, how sandboxes will function in practice across 27 member states, and whether the framework can adapt to rapid technological shifts without becoming obsolete.
