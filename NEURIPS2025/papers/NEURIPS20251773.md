## Problem

Equivariant neural networks are known to be difficult to optimize, and prior work has shown small empirical benefits from relaxing equivariance constraints, but the underlying mechanism remains unexplained. This paper asks a more foundational question: do equivariance constraints themselves introduce fundamental obstacles to optimization by hiding parameter symmetries of the larger unconstrained model class, and can these hidden symmetries provably create spurious minima that prevent learning?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251773/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The theoretical framework takes an equivariant network built with permutation representations (viewed as a linearly constrained subspace of unconstrained MLP parameters), a loss function, and the global parameter symmetry group of the unconstrained model. It produces a characterization of hidden fixed point subspaces, existence proofs for spurious minima under specific dimensionality and regularity conditions, and a classification of which p-rep reductions are problematic. Empirically, the experiments use 2-layer ReLU teacher-student networks with S3 permutation symmetry (5-dimensional input: one π₃ rep plus two trivial reps) and spherical signal models with SO(3) irreps, sweeping over the two free equivariant linear map parameters to visualize the exact loss landscape.

## Method

The core contribution is the 'hidden symmetry' framework: global parameter symmetries of the unconstrained model (e.g., permuting neurons) may map the constrained equivariant subspace to itself only partially, making them invisible to the constrained model while still shaping its loss landscape. The paper proves (Lemma 3.2, Theorem 3.3) that when a hidden symmetry's fixed point subspace intersects the constrained space in a codimension-1 subspace, it splits the constrained parameter space into two halves that gradient descent cannot cross, guaranteeing a spurious minimum on the 'wrong' side. For p-rep equivariant networks, Theorems 3.4 and 3.5 characterize exactly when this occurs: a transitive p-rep block becomes reducible (collapsing to a smaller transitive p-rep) precisely when neurons within the block share input weights, and two distinct transitive p-reps sharing neurons must be the same representation. Empirically, the paper demonstrates that completely relaxing equivariance converts the spurious minimum into a saddle, allowing the network to escape into a symmetrically related equivariant subspace corresponding to a different choice of group representation in the hidden layer.

## Results

In a 2-layer ReLU teacher-student setup with S₃ permutation equivariance, the paper demonstrates that the equivariantly constrained loss landscape contains two distinct minima: a global minimum and a spurious minimum, separated by the hidden fixed point subspace (θ₁ = θ₂). Training from the 'bad' side of this subspace consistently converges to the spurious minimum, while training from the 'good' side reaches the global minimum. Upon complete relaxation of equivariance constraints, the network escapes the spurious minimum (which becomes a saddle point in the unconstrained space) and converges to a global minimum in a symmetrically related equivariant subspace, with final weights corresponding to a permuted version of the teacher weights and a different choice of group representation in the hidden layer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baseline methods on standard benchmarks. Instead, the key comparison is between the equivariantly constrained network and the fully relaxed (unconstrained) network in a controlled teacher-student setting. The constrained network, when initialized on the 'bad' side of the hidden fixed point subspace, gets trapped at a spurious local minimum and cannot escape via gradient descent. The relaxed network, initialized at this same spurious minimum, successfully escapes because the spurious minimum becomes a saddle point in the unconstrained parameter space (confirmed by a negative Hessian eigenvalue). The relaxed network traverses through a region of elevated equivariance error before arriving at a global minimum in a different equivariant subspace. The paper also notes that rescaling the parameterization (using √(n−1)θ₁I + θ₂(11ᵀ−I) instead of θ₁I + θ₂(11ᵀ−I)) makes the boundary between convergence basins a straight line corresponding exactly to the hidden fixed point subspace, but no rescaling can eliminate the barrier entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the loss landscape analysis of overparameterized networks (Simsek et al. 2021, Brea et al. 2019) to the constrained setting of equivariant networks, introducing the concept of 'hidden symmetries'—parameter symmetries of the unconstrained parent model that are broken by the choice of constraint enforcement. It builds on the principle of symmetric criticality (Palais 1979) and the irreducible neuron framework from Simsek et al. to prove that these hidden symmetries induce critical points and spurious minima in the equivariant subspace. The work departs from prior equivariance relaxation literature (Pertigkiozoglou et al. 2024, van der Ouderaa et al. 2022), which motivated relaxation through model misspecification, by identifying a more fundamental optimization obstacle: the constraint enforcement choice itself can create barriers to reaching global minima even when the true solution is perfectly equivariant.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first theoretical explanation for why equivariant networks can be difficult to optimize beyond the well-known sample efficiency benefits, and suggests that effective relaxation may require rethinking the fixed choice of group representation in hidden layers rather than merely adding unconstrained components. The empirical observation that a relaxed network 'jumps' between equivariant subspaces corresponding to different representation choices opens a concrete direction for designing better equivariant architectures. Key limitations include the restrictive conditions (C2–C4) needed for the spurious minima theorem, the requirement to view the model as a subspace of a larger class (unclear for tensor-product equivariant networks), and the fact that all experiments are 2-layer teacher-student setups with small S3 symmetry, leaving open whether these effects persist in deeper, more realistic equivariant architectures.
