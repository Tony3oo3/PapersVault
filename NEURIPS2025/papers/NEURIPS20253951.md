## Problem

Covert advertisements on social media disguise themselves as ordinary product-sharing posts to deceive consumers, yet no publicly available dataset or benchmark exists to evaluate whether MLLMs can detect them. Existing social media moderation research focuses on other harmful content (fake news, cyberbullying, hate speech) and completely overlooks this deceptive advertising threat, which violates advertising disclosure laws in multiple jurisdictions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253951/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each sample is a multimodal RedNote post comprising a title, body text (avg. ~197 characters), a set of images (avg. 5.28 per post), and associated comments (avg. ~25 characters each). The model must output a single binary label (1 = covert advertisement, 0 = not), but the decision requires integrating cues that may be hidden in any modality—purchase links embedded in images, shop names in comments, or promotional language in the post text. The dataset is heavily imbalanced (12.3% positive), and a challenging subset of 1,127 non-advertisement product-sharing posts is deliberately included to stress-test false-positive rates.

## Method

The paper introduces CHASM, a 4,992-instance manually curated dataset built through a three-stage pipeline: collection from RedNote via brand-new accounts to avoid behavioral bias, filtering of explicitly sponsored posts, and anonymization of personal information in both text and images. Annotation employs a dynamic quality-control workflow where non-product posts receive a single label while product-sharing posts require three-annotator majority voting, with 50 covertly embedded gold-standard questions monitoring quality (94% accuracy achieved). For evaluation, 15 MLLMs are tested under zero-shot prompting, in-context learning, and LoRA fine-tuning (rank 8, α=32, 3 epochs, 5-fold cross-validation). The key finding is that no model is reliably accurate in zero-shot or ICL settings (best F1 = 0.597 for GPT-4o), but fine-tuning Qwen2.5-7B on CHASM raises F1 to 0.756, surpassing all proprietary baselines. Error analysis reveals that even fine-tuned models remain weakest at detecting subtle cues in comments and recognizing structural differences (single-brand focus vs. multi-brand sharing) between covert ads and genuine product posts.

## Results

On the CHASM dataset (4,992 multimodal posts from RedNote), GPT-4o achieves the best zero-shot F1-score of 0.597, while DeepSeek-V3 achieves the best in-context learning F1-score of 0.592. After fine-tuning with LoRA, Qwen2.5-7B reaches an F1-score of 0.756 (AUC 0.852), surpassing GPT-4o's zero-shot performance by 15.9 F1 points. The authors conclude that no current MLLM is sufficiently reliable for covert advertisement detection without task-specific training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among proprietary MLLMs, GPT-4o leads in zero-shot (F1 0.597) while DeepSeek-V3 leads in in-context learning (F1 0.592); the two are within 0.5 F1 points of each other. Reasoning MLLMs (Gemini 2.5 Pro, Step-R1-V-Mini, QvQ-Max) do not significantly outperform non-reasoning models on F1, with Gemini 2.5 Pro achieving only 0.422 F1 zero-shot despite the highest recall (0.921). Large open-source models (DeepSeek-V3 at 0.571, Llama-4 at 0.511) approach proprietary performance, while small open-source models (LLaVA 0.242, DeepSeek-VL2 0.272) lag substantially. The key finding is that fine-tuning Qwen2.5-7B on CHASM yields F1 0.756, beating GPT-4o zero-shot by 15.9 points and DeepSeek-V3 ICL by 16.4 points, demonstrating that task-specific training is essential. In-context learning provides minimal gains over zero-shot for most models, and adding detailed evaluation criteria to prompts actually degrades performance (e.g., GPT-4o drops from 0.597 to 0.562 F1 with detailed prompts).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of social media content moderation and advertisement detection. While prior moderation work has addressed hate speech, fake news, cyberbullying, and toxic content, and prior advertisement datasets have focused on explicit or traditional ads (e.g., Facebook ad revenue prediction, ad image understanding, illegal ad classification), no prior work has systematically defined, curated, and benchmarked the specific task of detecting *covert* advertisements that deliberately disguise themselves as organic product-sharing posts. The closest prior effort, AdspectorX [51], is acknowledged but critiqued for biased data collection and insufficient alignment with real-world covert advertising behaviors. Methodologically, the paper repurposes existing MLLMs (GPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3, Qwen2.5-VL, InternVL, Llama-4, Gemini 2.5 Pro, etc.) as evaluation targets rather than proposing a new architecture, positioning itself as a benchmark and empirical study rather than a methodological contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that even state-of-the-art MLLMs (best F1 ≈ 0.60) are unreliable for covert advertisement detection out of the box, and that in-context learning and reasoning capabilities provide no meaningful advantage—findings that temper expectations for deploying general-purpose LLMs in this moderation role. The practical takeaway that a fine-tuned 7B open-source model (Qwen2.5-7B, F1 = 0.756) surpasses GPT-4o zero-shot offers a cost-effective deployment path for platforms. Key open questions include the dynamic/temporal nature of covert ads (clues evolving in comment sections), the role of user behavior signals and creator profiling, and generalizability beyond the single Chinese platform (RedNote) and language studied. The dataset's deliberate inclusion of challenging product-sharing negatives (22.6% of samples) makes it a more realistic and harder benchmark than prior ad-detection datasets.
