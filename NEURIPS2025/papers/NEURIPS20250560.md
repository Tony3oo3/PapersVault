## Problem

Graph Foundation Models (GFMs) suffer from severe instability and inefficiency during few-shot prompt fine-tuning: performance fluctuates heavily due to randomness in the selection of limited support samples and structural discrepancies between pre-trained and target graph domains. No existing paradigm simultaneously addresses generating transferable structural patterns and ensuring stable adaptation under arbitrary few-shot support sets.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250560/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For pre-training, GRAVER consumes multi-domain source graphs (adjacency matrices and node feature matrices of heterogeneous dimensions) along with LLM-enhanced raw text for semantic alignment. At fine-tuning, it takes a target graph with m randomly selected labeled support samples per class (m ≪ total pre-training nodes) and a pre-computed vocabulary bank of graphon-based structure and feature tokens organized by source domain and class. The output is a predicted class label for each query node or ego-graph, with the augmented support samples formed by overlapping generated vocabulary subgraphs onto the original support samples at their max-degree node.

## Method

GRAVER is a three-stage framework. In pre-training, it aligns heterogeneous multi-domain features via LLM-enhanced text and truncated SVD, then disentangles each node's ego-graph into K factor-aware subgraph vocabularies through iterative soft-routing with a mutual information regularizer (InfoNCE-based) to enforce semantic independence across channels; a universal contrastive link-prediction objective trains the encoder. In vocabulary modeling, nonparametric graphon experts (a structure token W^A over [0,1]² and a feature token W^X over [0,1]) are estimated per class per domain, enabling conditional generation of new vocabulary subgraphs that inherit both structural priors and semantic traits. At fine-tuning, a hierarchical MoE-CoE network routes knowledge: the MoE layer selects relevant source domains via softmax over domain-specific vocabulary banks, while the CoE layer composes class-level token experts within each selected domain; the resulting generated vocabulary is overlapped onto each support sample, and a lightweight learnable graph prompt adapter is optimized on the augmented samples with a contrastive classification loss plus a sparsity-encouraging MoE-CoE routing loss.

## Results

On one-shot node classification, GRAVER achieves the best accuracy across all 7 target datasets, with average gains of 2.8% over the runner-up (SAMGPT) and over 4% over MDGPT. On one-shot graph classification, GRAVER leads by an average of 3.2% over the runner-up, with the largest margin on Wiki-CS (55.90% vs. 52.10%). Critically, GRAVER reduces the standard deviation by an average of 54.0% (node) and 54.6% (graph) compared to baselines, demonstrating substantially more stable fine-tuning under random support set selection. The advantage is most pronounced under low-shot conditions (m ≤ 4) and in cross-domain settings where structural discrepancies are largest.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GRAVER outperforms all 15 baselines on every target dataset for both node and graph classification. Against the strongest multi-domain pre-training baselines, GRAVER exceeds SAMGPT by 2.8% (node) and 3.2% (graph) on average, with the gap widening to over 5% on harder targets like ogbn-arXiv (49.25% vs. 45.27% node) and Wiki-CS (55.90% vs. 52.10% graph). Compared to prompt-based methods (GPPT, GraphPrompt, GPF, ProNoG), GRAVER gains 4–12% on node classification, indicating that generative vocabulary augmentation provides substantially more informative in-context signals than static prompts. Against self-supervised pre-training baselines (GCC, DGI, GraphCL, DSSL, GraphACL), the advantage is even larger (10–20%), confirming that multi-domain pre-training with transferable vocabularies is essential for cross-domain few-shot adaptation. Most notably, GRAVER's standard deviations are roughly half those of all baselines (e.g., 2.44 vs. 6.98 on Wiki-CS node), demonstrating that the generative augmentation stabilizes fine-tuning regardless of which random support samples are selected.

## Relation to Prior Work

GRAVER synthesizes three previously separate threads: ego-graph disentanglement via soft-routing (Ma et al., ICML 2019; Liu et al., AAAI 2020), nonparametric graphon-based generative modeling (G-Mixup, Han et al., ICML 2022), and prompt-based few-shot fine-tuning (GPPT, GraphPrompt). It departs from prior generative augmentation work (G-Tuning, UniAug) by conditioning generation on class-specific transferable subgraph patterns extracted during pre-training rather than producing task-agnostic structures, and from multi-domain pre-training baselines (GCOPE, MDGPT, SAMGPT) by introducing a hierarchical MoE-CoE routing mechanism that selectively composes source-domain vocabulary tokens at fine-tuning time. The LLM-enhanced feature alignment follows ZeroG (Li et al., KDD 2024) but is integrated as a preprocessing step rather than the core contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 54% average reduction in standard deviation across 20 random support-set draws addresses a practical deployment concern that prior GFMs have largely ignored: fine-tuning reliability under limited supervision. The theoretical guarantees (Propositions 1–2) provide justification for why disentangled ego-graph vocabularies are transferable units and why graphon-based generation converges to the empirical distribution, lending credibility beyond empirical validation. Limitations include dependence on GPT-4 for semantic alignment (cost and access), the assumption that graphon estimators are bounded and Lipschitz (which may not hold for highly heterogeneous graphs), and moderate GPU memory overhead during fine-tuning. Open questions include whether the vocabulary approach extends to regression or link-prediction tasks, and whether the graphon estimation remains stable as vocabulary size grows in very large-scale settings.
