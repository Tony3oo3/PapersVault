## Problem

Existing sign language video generation (SLVG) methods rely on a single coarse condition (e.g., skeleton sequences) to bridge text translation and video generation, limiting the naturalness and expressiveness of outputs—particularly in capturing facial expressions and fine hand movements. While incorporating multiple fine-grained conditions (dense poses, 3D hands) could improve fidelity, directly translating such high-dimensional conditions from text is error-prone and unstable. The paper asks how to effectively integrate multiple fine-grained conditions into SLVG without suffering from the translation errors inherent in predicting them directly.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single reference signer image (for identity preservation) and a spoken language text as input, and produces a temporally coherent sign language video whose frame count is determined autoregressively by an [EOS] token rather than a fixed length. Videos are generated at 272×224 (RWTH-2014T) or 512×512 (How2Sign) resolution, with the signer's appearance locked to the reference image while body pose, facial expression, and hand geometry follow the text semantics. The intermediate representation is a sequence of discrete multi-condition tokens (vocabulary size 625 = 5⁴) that encode fine-grained poses and 3D hand geometry per frame.

## Method

SignViP introduces a discrete tokenization paradigm with three jointly designed components. (1) A Sign Video Diffusion Model (SD v1.5 U-Net with AnimateDiff temporal attention, a Reference Net for identity, and a Condition Guider for multi-condition embeddings) is trained with denoising loss alongside a multi-condition encoder that maps fine-grained poses and 3D hands into continuous embeddings. (2) An FSQ Autoencoder compresses these embeddings into discrete tokens (4 latent channels, 5 quantization levels each) via Finite Scalar Quantization, avoiding codebook collapse issues of VQ. (3) A GPT-2-based Multi-Condition Token Translator, initialized with CLIP text embeddings, autoregressively predicts frame-level discrete tokens from spoken language text, using parallel prediction heads per frame and scheduled sampling (40% replacement) to mitigate exposure bias. At inference, text → discrete tokens → continuous embeddings → diffusion-guided video generation, with condition augmentation (p=0.001) during training to improve robustness to temporal discontinuities.

## Results

On RWTH-2014T, SignViP achieves FID 508.91, CLIP-FID 154.10, FVD 1025.45, and IDS 0.571, outperforming all baselines. On How2Sign, it reaches FID 575.67, CLIP-FID 109.61, FVD 2207.67, and IDS 0.624. In video back-translation, SignViP attains BLEU-4 of 8.65 (RWTH-2014T) and 5.04 (How2Sign) with ROUGE-L of 28.85 and 16.99 respectively, substantially exceeding SignGAN (BLEU-4: 3.16/1.97) and SignGen (BLEU-4: 0.51/0.41). In the generative capability comparison, SignViP's diffusion model achieves FVD 275.22 and Hand SSIM 0.614 on RWTH-2014T, surpassing AnimateAnyone (FVD 365.42, Hand SSIM 0.505) and ControlNet (FVD 556.63, Hand SSIM 0.483).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SignViP consistently outperforms all SLVG baselines across both datasets. Against SignGAN [61] and its AnimateAnyone-enhanced variant, SignViP reduces FVD by 28–42% and improves IDS by 0.10–0.11 on RWTH-2014T, while on How2Sign it cuts FVD by 20–28% and boosts IDS by 0.09. SignGen [47], the end-to-end multi-condition approach, is the weakest baseline (FVD 1715.32/3538.49), and SignViP improves FVD by 40% and 37% on the two datasets respectively. In the generative capability comparison (Table 4), SignViP's diffusion model beats both ControlNet [88] and AnimateAnyone [29] on all frame-level metrics, with the largest gap in Hand SSIM (0.614 vs. 0.505 and 0.483 on RWTH-2014T). In pose back-translation (Table 2), SignViP outperforms SLP methods ProTran [58], Adversarial [56], and MDN [60] on most metrics, though MoMP [59] slightly edges ahead on BLEU-1 (20.55 vs. 21.94 on RWTH-2014T); however, when MoMP is combined with a pose-to-video generator (ControlNet or AnimateAnyone), SignViP clearly wins in the full SLVG pipeline (Table 1). Human evaluation (Table 6) confirms these findings, with SignViP receiving 70.0% and 81.8% of votes on RWTH-2014T and How2Sign respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

SignViP extends the SLVG pipeline established by SignGAN and SignGen, which decompose the task into text-to-condition translation followed by condition-to-video generation. It departs from prior work in two key ways: (1) replacing the single coarse skeleton condition with multiple fine-grained conditions (dense poses and 3D hands), and (2) introducing a discrete tokenization paradigm via an FSQ Autoencoder that compresses continuous condition embeddings into a compact discrete vocabulary, which a GPT-2-based autoregressive translator then predicts from text. The video generation backbone is assembled from Stable Diffusion v1.5 (U-Net), AnimateDiff (temporal attention), and AnimateAnyone (Reference Net for identity preservation), while the quantization mechanism draws directly from FSQ as an alternative to VQ-VAE.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete gap in SLVG: single-condition methods lack expressiveness (especially for facial expressions and hand details), while naively adding multiple high-dimensional conditions introduces severe translation errors. The discrete tokenization approach provides a principled solution that could generalize to other multi-condition video generation tasks beyond sign language. Key limitations include the inability to support multiple sign languages simultaneously (requiring separate model training per language) and the computational cost of iterative diffusion sampling. Open directions include multi-language unification, faster inference via consistency models or efficient ODE solvers, and whether the tokenization paradigm transfers to other structured-condition video generation settings.
