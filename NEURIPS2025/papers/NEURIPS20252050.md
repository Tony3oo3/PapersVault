## Problem

Craig interpolants in linear integer arithmetic (LIA) are essential for generating inductive invariants in program verification, but existing solvers often produce interpolants with large coefficients and complex structures that overfit to particular approximations of reachable and error states. Simpler interpolants are more likely to generalize as valid inductive invariants, yet no learning-based method has been applied to guide their generation in LIA. This paper introduces a lightweight neural model that learns to select minimal disjunct subsets from input formulas, reducing interpolant complexity by up to 47.3% (Z3) and 69.1% (CSIsat) without modifying the underlying solver.

## Input / Output

The model receives two LIA formulas A and B, each expressed in disjunctive normal form as a sequence of disjunction-free (conjunctive) subformulas. It outputs a binary selection over the disjuncts of A and B, yielding subsets S_A ⊆ A and S_B ⊆ B; a single SMT solver invocation on these subsets then produces the simplified interpolant. The model does not generate the interpolant directly but acts as a pre-filter, and the selection must be solver-aware since different solvers may produce different interpolants from the same subset pair.

## Method

The architecture combines a message-passing GNN encoder with a self-attention selector. The GNN converts each disjunction-free formula into an AST graph with node features (type, token embedding, depth, raw constant value), applies L rounds of message passing with residual connections and layer normalization, and aggregates to a formula-level embedding via combined mean and sum pooling. The selector concatenates all formula embeddings from A and B, augments them with learnable set-membership vectors (e_A, e_B), and applies multi-head self-attention so each disjunct can attend to all others across both formulas; a feedforward scoring head then produces per-disjunct scores for ranking. Training is supervised with ground truths obtained by exhaustive enumeration over all valid (S_A, S_B) subset pairs, selecting the minimal one by set size, token count, and constant magnitude. The key design choice is top-down lazy sampling—directly predicting the full subset in one forward pass—rather than iterative bottom-up counterexample-guided sampling, which requires only a single SMT solver call at inference time.

## Results

On the 532 challenging test problems (where a simpler interpolant exists), Z3 guided by the learned model reduces interpolant complexity from 5.38 to 2.83 (a 47.3% reduction) and plain-text length from 186.7 to 108.6, while also cutting solver time by 30% (0.012s → 0.009s). The model achieves a 72.9% valid rate on this subset, with a simple fallback to the unguided solver for the remaining cases. When applied to the older CSIsat solver (original average complexity 11.5), the guided model reduces complexity to 3.57 (CSIsat-trained) or 5.13 (Z3-trained), yielding up to a 69.1% reduction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Z3, the model-guided approach wins decisively on interpolant complexity (2.83 vs. 5.38, a 47.3% reduction) and solver time (0.009s vs. 0.012s), at the cost of a 72.9% valid rate on the challenging subset (mitigated by falling back to unguided Z3). Against CSIsat, the improvement is even more dramatic: complexity drops from an original average of 11.5 to 3.57 (CSIsat-trained model, 69.1% reduction) or 5.13 (Z3-trained model, 55.4% reduction), effectively elevating the legacy solver to near-modern quality without code changes. Against CVC5, the comparison is limited because CVC5 fails 85% of problems within a 5-second timeout; on the 15% it solves, the guided version reduces solver time from 0.301s to 0.107s (~28% reduction) but cannot further simplify already-minimal interpolants (complexity 1). Cross-solver transfer is partial: each solver performs best with its own dedicated model (~74% valid) versus ~53–59% with the other solver's model, indicating the learned heuristics capture both shared and solver-specific knowledge.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the lazy sampling method of Albarghouthi and McMillan (2013), which iteratively samples disjuncts from input formulas to construct simple interpolants via counterexample-guided refinement. The key departure is replacing the bottom-up, counterexample-driven selection with a top-down, learned selection: a message-passing GNN encodes each disjunct's AST, and a self-attention selector directly predicts which subsets of disjuncts suffice, requiring only a single SMT solver call. The architecture draws on standard GNN message-passing and Transformer-style attention, but is deliberately kept lightweight (no LLMs) to meet the latency demands of downstream verification pipelines. The paper also contributes a 66K-problem dataset (Interp(4,4)) constructed from real verification tool traces, filling a gap the authors note has no prior equivalent in the literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value lies in the ability to simplify interpolants from both modern (Z3) and legacy (CSIsat) solvers without modifying their codebases, which is particularly useful when a simpler interpolant is needed to close an inductive invariant proof that the unguided solver cannot produce. However, the evaluation is constrained to small formulas (at most 4 disjuncts per side, max complexity 31), no error bars are reported, and the 72.9% success rate for Z3-guided runs means the method is best used as a best-effort pre-filter with fallback. Open questions include whether the approach extends to non-linear arithmetic or array theories, whether it generalizes to larger disjunct counts, and whether task-specific training signals (from the downstream verification loop) could further improve interpolant quality beyond syntactic simplicity.
