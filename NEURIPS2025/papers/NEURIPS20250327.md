## Problem

Previous theoretical lower bounds for one-layer softmax transformers on compositional reasoning tasks (function composition, Match3) relied on communication complexity and only applied to transformers using sub-linear precision bits, leaving open whether infinite-precision arithmetic could circumvent these limitations. Additionally, existing higher-order attention mechanisms that can in principle solve these tasks (third-order, triangular) suffer from cubic-time complexity, limiting their practical scalability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250327/cropped_page8_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The four tasks consume different input structures: function composition takes a (2n+1)-length sequence encoding two functions g,h:[n]→[n] and a query x; Match3 takes n integers from [m-1]; binary relation composition takes n tokens indexed by pairs (i,j)∈[√n]² encoding two Boolean matrices A,B; and quotient binary relation composition adds a coloring function col:[m]→[m] to the matrix inputs. In all cases, the output is a real-valued score per position whose sign encodes the binary answer (e.g., whether a triplet sums to 0 mod m, whether a composed relation entry is 1), with the auxiliary or target token carrying the final prediction.

## Method

The paper introduces two complementary contributions. First, a new complexity measure called split-VC dimension: for a Boolean function f:Σⁿ→{0,1}, one splits the n input positions into 'input' and 'parameter' sets, constructs a Boolean matrix, and takes the maximum VC dimension over all such splits. The main theorem shows that any one-layer standard-attention transformer computing f (even with infinite-precision arithmetic) must have size ≥ split-VC(f)^Ω(1), yielding lower bounds for function composition, Match3, and binary relation composition. Second, the paper proposes Strassen attention, where the attention score for triplet (i,j,k) is computed as a sum of three pairwise dot-products (f_i·g_j + g_j·h_k + h_k·f_i) rather than coordinate-wise products of all three vectors. This structure allows the entire attention layer to be decomposed into a constant number of n×n matrix multiplications, achieving O(n^ω·d) time (sub-cubic) while still capturing three-token interactions. The paper proves that a one-layer constant-size Strassen attention transformer can solve all four tasks, and empirically validates this with supervised training on synthetic datasets and the COGS benchmark.

## Results

In parameter-matched comparisons, 1-layer Strassen attention (2.5k params) outperforms 2-layer standard attention (3.6k params) on Binary Relation Composition (100% vs 92%) and Quotient Binary Relation Composition (98% vs 82%). On the COGS compositional generalization benchmark, 3-layer Strassen attention achieves 72% accuracy versus 65% for standard attention at matched parameter count (~99k). Across all four synthetic tasks, Strassen and third-order attention are the only mechanisms reaching high accuracy, while Strassen consistently trains faster than third-order attention and matches standard attention's speed on Function Composition and Quotient Binary Relation Composition.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against standard attention (Vaswani et al., 2017), Strassen attention wins on every evaluated task: 100% vs 92% on Binary Relation Composition, 98% vs 82% on Quotient Binary Relation Composition, and 72% vs 65% on COGS, all while using fewer or equal parameters. Against triangular attention (Bergen et al., 2021), Strassen has a clear advantage on Quotient Binary Relation Composition, where triangular attention cannot exploit its structural shortcut; however, triangular attention trains faster on Binary Relation Composition because the matrix structure is natively embedded in its architecture, and triangular attention cannot be applied to Function Composition or Match3 without reformulating the input as a matrix. Against third-order attention (Sanford et al., 2023), Strassen achieves comparable or slightly higher accuracy on Function Composition and Match3 while consistently reaching peak performance in fewer training epochs and exhibiting more stable learning dynamics, particularly on Function Composition and Quotient Binary Relation Composition where third-order attention shows significant instability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends two complementary lines of research on transformer expressivity. On the theoretical side, it departs from the communication-complexity-based lower bounds of Peng et al. (2024) and Sanford et al. (2023), which required assuming limited numerical precision, by introducing split-VC dimension—a new combinatorial complexity measure that yields lower bounds even for transformers with infinite-precision arithmetic. On the architectural side, it builds on the observation that compositional tasks require tracking interactions among three or more tokens (motivating triangular attention of Bergen et al. and third-order attention of Sanford et al.), but departs from both by proposing Strassen attention, whose score decomposes into pairwise dot-products enabling an O(n^ω) implementation via fast matrix multiplication, in contrast to the O(n³) cost of prior triplet-interaction mechanisms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The split-VC dimension provides a reusable tool for proving expressivity limits of transformers that are immune to the 'just use more bits' objection, potentially applicable to other architectures and tasks. The Strassen attention mechanism offers a concrete, sub-cubic path to higher-order reasoning in transformers, but its practical impact remains untested beyond small-scale synthetic tasks and the COGS benchmark; the authors themselves flag that learnability at scale, generalization to masked language modeling, and extension to interactions beyond triplets are open. A key open question is whether the smoother loss landscape observed empirically for Strassen versus third-order attention reflects a fundamental optimization advantage or an artifact of the small experimental regime.
