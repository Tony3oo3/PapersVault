## Problem

Complex motion systems arise from coordinated interactions among simpler components, yet existing methods to model such dynamics rely on manually-defined or heuristic hierarchies with fixed motion primitives, limiting generalizability across tasks. No prior approach can automatically discover interpretable, transferable parent-child motion dependencies directly from observed data without assuming domain-specific structure, dimensionality, or predefined kinematic chains.

## Input / Output

The method takes a sequence of N observed positions X^t ∈ R^{N×d} over T timesteps (or, for 3D scenes, per-Gaussian parameters {μ, Σ, σ, c} with temporal deformation fields) and produces a binary DAG adjacency matrix H ∈ {0,1}^{N×N} encoding the learned parent-child hierarchy, reconstructed absolute motions, and—when applied to 3D Gaussian splatting—a deformed scene rendered from updated Gaussian parameters. Parent candidates are restricted to k nearest neighbors in Euclidean space, and during training the hierarchy is sampled stochastically via Gumbel-Softmax, while at inference the maximum-likelihood assignment is used. For 3D deformation, user-specified handle positions (translation or rotation) on selected Gaussians drive the ARAP-based deformation of the full scene.

## Method

HEIR formulates hierarchy inference as a differentiable graph learning problem on a k-NN proximity graph, where a single graph attention layer computes edge weights representing parent probabilities via softmax-normalized attention over each vertex's k nearest neighbors. The encoder predicts relative motion δ^t as the residual between each node's absolute velocity and the weighted sum of its parent candidates' velocities, while the decoder reconstructs absolute motion by accumulating relative velocities along the hierarchy via a truncated Neumann series (Σ H^l δ^t). Discrete hierarchy matrices are sampled from the learned edge weights using the Gumbel-Softmax straight-through estimator, enabling end-to-end gradient flow through the discrete parent assignment. Training minimizes two L1 objectives: a reconstruction loss matching predicted to ground-truth absolute motion, and a regularizer penalizing relative motion magnitude to prevent the trivial star-topology solution. For rotational motion, the encoder decomposes relative velocity into radial and angular components in polar coordinates, and a graph-Laplacian algebraic-connectivity hinge loss enforces a well-formed, non-fragmented hierarchy.

## Results

On the 1D hierarchical motion benchmark, the method recovers a valid hierarchy in 73% of 1,000 independent trials, compared to a Monte-Carlo random baseline of ~0.00005%. On the synthetic planetary orbit task, it achieves 100% correct hierarchy reconstruction without noise and 73.6% with Gaussian noise (σ=0.05). On the D-NeRF 3D Gaussian splatting deformation task, the method outperforms SC-GS across all four scenes on every metric (PSNR, SSIM, CLIP-I, LPIPS), with the largest PSNR gains on Hook (+2.6 dB) and Excavator (+1.65 dB), and LPIPS reductions of roughly 40–50% relative to the baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against a single baseline, SC-GS [Huang et al., CVPR 2024], the only recent method with available code for dynamic Gaussian splat deformation. The proposed method wins on all four D-NeRF scenes across all four metrics. The most striking gap is on the Hook scene, where PSNR improves from 15.7 to 18.3 (+2.6 dB) and LPIPS drops from 0.0954 to 0.0617 (−35%). On Excavator, PSNR rises from 19.91 to 21.56 and LPIPS falls from 0.065 to 0.0383 (−41%). CLIP-I is essentially tied on Excavator (0.978 vs. 0.978) but the proposed method leads by 0.013–0.020 on the remaining three scenes. Qualitatively, the paper reports that SC-GS introduces unnatural bending, exaggerated body distortions, and physically implausible limb deformations, whereas the learned hierarchy preserves structural rigidity and produces more coherent, physically plausible deformations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes ideas from graph attention networks, Gumbel-Softmax discrete sampling, and Neumann-series motion aggregation into a unified framework for learning parent-child motion hierarchies from data. It departs from prior hierarchical motion work (motion programs, hierarchical VAEs, FineGym) that assumes fixed or hand-crafted hierarchies, and from 3D deformation methods (SC-GS, HiMoR, MB-GS) that use predefined motion bases or per-object control structures, by instead inferring the hierarchy matrix itself as a differentiable graph learning problem. The ARAP solver for 3D scene deformation is borrowed from classical mesh processing and repurposed for Gaussian splat manipulation under learned structural constraints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method offers a principled, interpretable alternative to heuristic motion decompositions, with the learned hierarchy providing a natural interface for structured scene editing. However, the single-parent-per-element assumption limits expressiveness for systems with overlapping motion influences, and the method cannot recover latent semantics absent from observed trajectories. Experimental validation is constrained by a single real-world baseline (SC-GS) due to code unavailability of other concurrent methods, and the synthetic benchmarks, while clean, do not stress-test scalability to the hundreds of thousands of Gaussians in realistic scenes. Open questions include whether the k-NN proximity graph suffices for long-range dependencies and whether multi-parent or weighted hierarchies would improve fidelity.
