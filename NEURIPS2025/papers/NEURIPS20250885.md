## Problem

Optimization formulations stored as images (whiteboard photos, scanned pages, handwritten notes) cannot be automatically interpreted and solved by machines because they lack semantic structure, and no dataset or end-to-end pipeline exists for the optimization domain specifically. Existing mathematical expression recognition datasets contain only short, single-line expressions from general mathematics, not the multi-line, structurally complex programs that characterize optimization problems.

## Input / Output

The framework accepts a single RGB image of a mathematical optimization formulation (handwritten, typeset, or mixed), which is resized to fit a 768×1024 canvas with aspect-ratio preservation and white center-padding, then contrast-enhanced and unsharp-masked. It produces a three-stage output: LaTeX code of the formulation, a corresponding PYOMO modeling-language script, and the numerical solution to the optimization problem. The accompanying dataset (AutoOpt-11k) provides LaTeX labels for all 11,554 images and PYOMO scripts for a 1,018-image subset, with image widths ranging from 159 to 3,611 px and LaTeX lengths from 14 to 1,620 characters.

## Method

Module M1 uses a hybrid vision encoder that fuses ResNet-101 local feature vectors (projected via a learnable gating scalar α initialized to zero) with Swin Transformer patch embeddings, feeding a mBART autoregressive decoder initialized with NOUGAT weights to generate LaTeX token-by-token. Module M2 fine-tunes the 1.3B-parameter DeepSeek-Coder causal decoder-only transformer on instruction-style LaTeX-to-PYOMO pairs. Module M3 implements a Bilevel Optimization-based Decomposition (BOBD) method that introduces a Logistic Regression-based Variable Classification Model (LR-VCM) to automatically partition decision variables into upper and lower levels; a genetic algorithm then samples upper-level variables while a classical solver (interior-point or LP) resolves the nested lower-level subproblem, with the classification re-learned every 10 generations. The key architectural insight is that combining CNN local pattern capture with Transformer global spatial reasoning, gated by a learnable scalar, outperforms either component alone for two-dimensional mathematical notation.

## Results

On the AutoOpt-11k test set, AutoOpt-M1 achieves a Character Error Rate (CER) of 0.0286 on the combined handwritten+printed split, outperforming Nougat (0.0440), GPT-4o (0.1017), and Gemini 2.0 Flash (0.1338). For handwritten expressions specifically, AutoOpt-M1 reaches CER 0.0412 versus Nougat's 0.0752. The full AutoOpt framework (M1→M2→M3) achieves a 94.20% end-to-end success rate on 500 held-out problems, with module-level reliabilities of 97.14% (M1) and 91.75% (M2). The M2 module (LaTeX-to-PYOMO) attains a BLEU score of 88.25 and CER of 0.0825.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AutoOpt-M1 is compared against three baselines: Nougat (fine-tuned on AutoOpt-11k), GPT-4o, and Gemini 2.0 Flash (both used via API with prompting). On the combined handwritten+printed CER, AutoOpt-M1 (0.0286) substantially outperforms Nougat (0.0440) by ~35% relative improvement, and dominates the LLM baselines by a wide margin (GPT-4o: 0.1017, Gemini: 0.1338). On handwritten CER, the gap over Nougat is even larger (0.0412 vs. 0.0752, a 45% relative reduction). The only metric where AutoOpt-M1 trails Nougat is printed CER (0.0176 vs. 0.0168), a negligible 0.0008 difference. In terms of BLEU score (reported in Figure 4), AutoOpt-M1 outperforms Nougat on all splits, confirming that the hybrid ResNet-101 + Swin Transformer encoder provides a consistent advantage over the pure-Transformer Nougat architecture. The LLM baselines (GPT-4o, Gemini) are significantly larger models yet perform far worse, highlighting the benefit of task-specific fine-tuning on a domain-relevant dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

The paper extends the NOUGAT document-understanding architecture by replacing its vision encoder with a hybrid ResNet-101 + Swin Transformer design, motivated by the need to capture both local stroke-level features (handwritten symbols) and global spatial structure (fractions, matrices, multi-line layouts) simultaneously. For the optimization-solving module, it builds on Sinha et al.'s (2024) bilevel decomposition strategy by automating the variable-classification step with a logistic regression model, removing the need for manual decomposition. Conceptually, the paper departs from prior MER work—which targets single-line expressions from general mathematics—by framing the task as recognizing multi-line, interlinked mathematical programs specific to the optimization domain, a setting the authors argue is absent from existing datasets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AutoOpt-11k fills a concrete gap: no prior image dataset was purpose-built for optimization formulations, and the 11,554-image collection (with LaTeX and partial PYOMO labels) is now publicly available, enabling the community to develop and benchmark domain-specific MER and code-generation models. The end-to-end pipeline (image → LaTeX → PYOMO → solution) with a reported 94.2% out-of-distribution success rate demonstrates that full automation of optimization problem solving is practically achievable with modest model sizes. Open questions include handling ill-defined or multi-page formulations, scaling to very large-scale problems, and whether the BOBD approach with ML-based variable classification generalizes beyond the 10 test problems evaluated. The framework's reliance on a small fine-tuned LLM (DeepSeek-Coder 1.3B) for LaTeX-to-PYOMO conversion also raises questions about robustness to novel or non-standard modeling language constructs.
