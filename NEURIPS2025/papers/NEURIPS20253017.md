## Problem

AI-powered attacks on Learning with Errors (LWE) match or outperform classical cryptanalytic methods under certain parameter settings, yet AI practitioners lack accessible, large-scale datasets to study and improve these attacks. Prior open-source LWE datasets contained only ~4M samples and covered limited parameter regimes, while generating new reduced LWE data requires hours-to-days of compute and expertise in lattice reduction algorithms. This gap deters AI researchers from contributing to post-quantum security analysis.

## Input / Output

The pipeline starts from 4n eavesdropped LWE samples (a, b) ∈ Z_q^n × Z_q with specified lattice dimension n, modulus q, and secret/error distributions, then produces five reduced datasets (RA, Rb) of 40M–400M samples each, hosted on HuggingFace for off-the-shelf use. Each reduced sample is a row of the transformed matrix with smaller norms than the original, and the datasets span five (n, log₂q) settings from (256, 20) to (1024, 26) with varying reduction quality ρ (0.3992–0.9036). The output also includes the cliff structure in column standard deviations that distinguishes 'cruel' (unreduced) from 'cool' (reduced) secret positions.

## Method

Dataset construction leverages the subsampling trick to generate millions of LWE matrices from 4n initial samples, then embeds each into a q-ary lattice Λ_i = [[0, qI_n], [ωI_m, A_i]] and applies interleaved lattice reduction alternating between BKZ2.0 (blocksize β) and flatter (strength α) with a polishing step after each iteration. Algorithm switching is triggered when reduction progress stalls (mean decrease over s steps falls below threshold γ), and the process terminates when the standard-deviation reduction ρ drops below a per-setting threshold τ. For benchmarking, the paper trains 4-layer encoder-only transformers (embedding dim 512, 8 heads, angular embeddings) for the SALSA attack and uses brute-force + linear regression for Cool & Cruel, both on single NVIDIA Titan X GPUs, reporting maximum recoverable Hamming weight for binary and ternary secrets across all five settings.

## Results

The paper establishes baseline performance for two AI attacks (SALSA and Cool & Cruel) on five TAPAS LWE datasets, reporting the maximum Hamming weight (h) of binary or ternary secrets successfully recovered. The strongest result is SALSA recovering a binary secret with h=63 at n=512, log₂(q)=41, while the weakest settings (n=512, log₂(q)=12 and n=1024, log₂(q)=26) yield only h=4–7. Datasets range from 40M to 400M reduced LWE samples, with reduction quality (ρ) varying from 0.3992 to 0.9036, and computational cost spanning 349K to 40.5M total CPU hours per dataset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks two existing AI attacks—SALSA (transformer-based, from [35,20,21,34]) and Cool & Cruel (brute-force + linear regression, from [23])—using the open-source implementations from [36]. On binary secrets, SALSA outperforms Cool & Cruel at n=256/log₂(q)=20 (h=33 vs. 22) and n=512/log₂(q)=41 (h=63 vs. 60), while Cool & Cruel wins at n=512/log₂(q)=28 (h=12 vs. 9) and n=1024/log₂(q)=26 (h=6 vs. 5), with a tie at n=512/log₂(q)=12 (h=6). For ternary secrets, Cool & Cruel leads in four of five settings (e.g., h=22 vs. 20 at n=256; h=13 vs. 11 at n=512/log₂(q)=28), while SALSA recovers a notably higher ternary weight at n=512/log₂(q)=41 (h=45 vs. 37). These results confirm that both attacks remain competitive in the recoverable range and establish the performance floor that future work on the TAPAS datasets must exceed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SALSA line of work (Wenger et al., NeurIPS 2022; Li et al., ACM CCS 2023; Stevens et al., 2024) and the Cool & Cruel attack (Nolte et al., AFRICACRYPT 2024), which established that transformer-based models can recover LWE secrets from reduced lattice data. The preprocessing pipeline—subsampling 4n initial samples and applying interleaved BKZ2.0/flatter lattice reduction with polishing—directly follows Li et al. (2023) and Nolte et al. (2024). The key departure is scale and accessibility: prior open-source datasets contained only ~4M samples in a single parameter regime, whereas TAPAS provides 40M–400M reduced samples across five distinct (n, q) settings, explicitly designed as off-the-shelf training data for the broader AI community rather than as a vehicle for a new attack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LWE underpins NIST-standardized post-quantum cryptosystems like CRYSTALS-KYBER, and AI attacks already match or outperform classical lattice-reduction attacks in certain parameter regimes, making systematic study of these attacks a security-critical task. The 10–100× data scale enables scaling-law investigations and larger-model training that were infeasible with prior 4M-sample releases, while the five parameter settings allow controlled study of how n, q, and reduction quality (ρ) affect attack performance. Key open questions include whether simply training on more data can compensate for the limited reduction quality (which is on par with prior work), whether other architectures or training paradigms (e.g., reinforcement learning) can improve beyond the transformer baselines, and whether the problem can be reformulated (e.g., as decision-LWE or multi-secret learning) to yield better generalization. The 40M+ CPU-hour cost of dataset generation remains a significant barrier, motivating future work on data augmentation and synthetic generation.
