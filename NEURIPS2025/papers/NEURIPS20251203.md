## Problem

Diffusion-based inverse problem solvers face a fundamental trade-off: zero-shot methods handle arbitrary degradations but rely on score approximations that reduce accuracy, while training-based methods model the posterior exactly but cannot be informed of the degradation operator at test-time, forcing them into a blind restoration setting. No prior training-based approach can accept the specific degradation H as a conditioning signal, limiting each trained model to a fixed set of degradations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251203/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a degraded measurement vector y (e.g., a blurred, masked, or downsampled RGB image) together with the corresponding linear degradation operator H and its pseudo-inverse H†, which must have computationally efficient implementations. Rather than feeding H as a data tensor, the operator is applied architecturally to internal feature maps. The model produces posterior samples (63 NFEs), single-step MMSE estimates, or Neural Posterior Principal Components, all from the same trained network. Experiments use 64×64 and 256×256 RGB images from FFHQ and ImageNet.

## Method

InvFusion introduces a Feature Degradation Layer that applies H to un-patched network activations, concatenates the result with the measurements y, passes through a learned channel-wise linear transform, and maps back via H†, producing a degradation-informed feature representation. These features are injected into the main processing stream through joint-attention (InvFusion Blocks), where keys and values from the Feature Degradation Layer are concatenated with the self-attention keys and values before computing attention. The base architecture is HDiT (Hourglass Diffusion Transformer), whose attention layers preserve spatial dimensionality so that H can be applied at every resolution. Training uses the standard diffusion denoising loss conditioned on both y and H, with H sampled from a distribution over degradation families (strided motion blur, patch in-painting, random matrix operators). The key insight is that the degradation operator is embedded into the architecture's computation graph rather than treated as an input, enabling a single model to handle diverse degradations while maintaining the correctness guarantees of supervised posterior sampling.

## Results

On FFHQ64, InvFusion achieves the best CFID (4.2, 5.0, 7.1) across all three degradation tasks (strided motion blur, in-painting, matrix operator) among both training-based and zero-shot methods, while using only 63 NFEs. The InvFusion MMSE estimator (1 NFE) achieves the highest PSNR of all methods: 25.0 dB (motion blur), 19.8 dB (in-painting), and 17.9 dB (matrix operator). On ImageNet64, InvFusion similarly leads in CFID (5.6, 5.9, 6.3) and its MMSE variant achieves the best PSNR (23.4, 18.4, 17.3 dB). The advantage over the closest training-based baseline (Palette) is most dramatic on the matrix operator task, where CFID drops from 25.5 to 7.1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

InvFusion outperforms all six zero-shot baselines (DDRM, DDNM, DPS, ΠGDM, DAPS, MGPS) on CFID across every degradation, with the largest margins over DDRM and DDNM (e.g., CFID 4.2 vs. 23.2/42.3 on motion blur). Among zero-shot methods, ΠGDM is the closest competitor on FID (6.3 vs. 4.2 on motion blur) but still trails InvFusion on CFID and requires 100 NFEs with network derivatives. Against the training-based baseline Palette, InvFusion wins on CFID in all three tasks (4.2 vs. 5.8, 5.0 vs. 5.5, 7.1 vs. 25.5) and on PSNR for motion blur (22.7 vs. 21.0) and matrix operator (15.1 vs. 12.5); the gap is smallest on in-painting where the degradation is easily inferred from the input. InDI, the other training-based baseline, is substantially worse on CFID (22.8–58.7 vs. 4.2–7.1) despite using fewer NFEs (50). For MMSE estimation, InvFusion beats Palette by 1.5–2.4 dB PSNR across all tasks, and the single-NFE MMSE variant outperforms the 63-NFE posterior sampler on PSNR, confirming the architecture's efficiency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of two established paradigms for diffusion-based inverse problem solving: training-based conditioning (Palette, InDI) and zero-shot score decomposition (DDRM, DDNM, DPS, PiGDM). Rather than building directly on either lineage, it repurposes the algorithm-unrolling principle of applying the degradation operator H within network layers, but executes it through a novel Feature Degradation Layer integrated via joint-attention in an HDiT transformer backbone. The architectural constraint that deep feature resolution must match input image dimensions (enabling H to be applied to activations) is a key design departure from standard diffusion architectures, and the use of attention rather than explicit iterative optimization distinguishes it from classical deep unfolding methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By being the first training-based method that accepts the degradation operator at test time, InvFusion resolves the fundamental flexibility-accuracy trade-off that has constrained the field: zero-shot methods are flexible but approximate, while training-based methods are accurate but blind to H. The demonstrated OOD generalization (Palette fails completely on out-painting while InvFusion succeeds) provides strong evidence that explicit degradation knowledge is not merely helpful but critical for correct restoration. Open questions include whether the architecture extends to non-linear degradations (enabling latent diffusion), how it scales to the full space of possible linear operators, and whether the computational overhead of repeated H/H† applications becomes prohibitive at higher resolutions or with more complex operators.
