## Problem

Despite extensive empirical characterization of LLM scaling laws, hallucinations, and knowledge acquisition dynamics, no unified theoretical framework explains why these phenomena occur or how they are interconnected. The paper addresses this gap by asking: what data structure, when compressed by a model of limited capacity, naturally produces the observed power-law scaling, frequency-ordered learning, and capacity-dependent hallucination?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255613/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The theoretical framework takes a data-generating process (a parametric syntax model with n_s clusters of dimension d_syn, plus a nonparametric Pitman-Yor CRP knowledge model with discount parameter α and cluster dimension d_knw) and a model capacity constraint C (in bits) as inputs. It produces closed-form bounds on per-sentence Bayesian redundancy (yielding data and model scaling exponents), a frequency-ordered acquisition schedule for knowledge elements, and a sharp threshold: knowledge elements appearing below a frequency proportional to C^(1/α) are hallucinated regardless of training epochs. The 'data' is synthetically generated from the model itself, so the theory predicts LLM behavior on data drawn from this specific hierarchical process rather than on arbitrary corpora.

## Method

The core insight is interpreting LLM training as a two-part coding process in the Kolmogorov structure function: the model parameters form the first part (describing the compressor) and the arithmetic-coded data forms the second part, so minimizing perplexity is equivalent to minimizing total description length. They propose the Syntax-Knowledge model, a hierarchical generative framework where syntax is captured by a finite-dimensional parametric model (learned at the fast parametric rate Õ(N^{-1})) and factual knowledge is modeled by a nonparametric Pitman-Yor Chinese Restaurant Process (learned at the slower rate Õ(N^{α-1})), motivated by Heap's and Zipf's laws. Under the Bayesian sequential prediction framework, the optimal redundancy equals the mutual information I(X_{1:N}; φ_data), which they bound as Õ(d_knw/N^{1-α} + n_s·d_syn/N), yielding the data scaling law. For model scaling, they formulate a constrained optimization where an omniscient model allocates C bits of capacity across knowledge clusters with power-law mixing probabilities p_i ∝ i^{-1/α}, solving it via a distortion-rate analysis to obtain Red_M(C) = Θ(C^{-1/α+1}), which predicts that lower-frequency knowledge is acquired only at larger model sizes and is hallucinated below a capacity-dependent threshold.

## Results

The paper derives a data scaling law bound showing the optimal Bayesian redundancy per sentence is $\tilde{O}(d_{\text{knw}}/N^{1-\alpha} + n_s d_{\text{syn}}/N)$, where the syntax component decays at the parametric rate $\tilde{O}(N^{-1})$ and the knowledge component decays more slowly at $\tilde{O}(N^{\alpha-1})$. For model scaling, the optimal redundancy under a capacity constraint $C$ bits is characterized as $\text{Red}_M(C) = \Theta(C^{-1/\alpha+1})$, with individual knowledge cluster contributions scaling as $\Theta(\min\{k^{-1/\alpha}, C^{-1/\alpha}\})$. Experimentally, models trained on power-law-distributed data exhibit clear power-law loss scaling with both data size and model size, while uniformly distributed data deviates significantly from power-law behavior. For a 7.2M-parameter model, knowledge elements occurring fewer than 508 times are consistently hallucinated regardless of pretraining epochs, confirming the theoretical frequency threshold for hallucination.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that validates its predictions against empirical observations rather than comparing against competing methods. The key empirical comparison is between power-law-distributed and uniformly-distributed training data: power-law data yields smooth power-law loss decay with both data size and model size, while uniform data produces a flat-then-sharp-drop-then-plateau loss curve, confirming that frequency structure in data drives the observed scaling exponents. The paper also contrasts its model scaling prediction with Allen-Zhu & Li [3], who found that acquired knowledge (in bits) scales linearly with model size on uniformly distributed data; the authors note this is consistent with their theory but that the cross-entropy loss follows a very different (non-power-law) scaling in the uniform case. The theoretical loss decomposition by frequency class (Figure 1) closely matches empirical observations: smaller models capture only the most frequent knowledge first, while larger models progressively acquire rarer knowledge, with each frequency class plateauing at a model-size threshold that marks its irreducible loss component.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies the Kolmogorov structure function framework (Li & Vitányi; Rissanen & Tabus) with Bayesian nonparametric modeling (Pitman-Yor Process) to interpret LLM training as a two-part coding process, extending the prediction-compression equivalence established by Cover & Thomas and Hutter's universal AI theory. It departs from prior scaling-law explanations (Kaplan et al., Chinchilla, Bahri et al., Hutter's learning curve theory) by explicitly separating a finite-dimensional parametric syntax model from an infinite-dimensional nonparametric knowledge model, and by grounding the power-law scaling exponents in the Zipf/Heap structure of knowledge frequencies rather than in architectural or optimization assumptions. The Bayesian redundancy analysis builds on the classical coding-game framework of Clarke & Barron and Rissanen, while the model-scaling derivation via rate-distortion under capacity constraints connects to the Kolmogorov structure function's relationship with Shannon's rate-distortion theory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a single theoretical lens (Kolmogorov structure function + hierarchical syntax-knowledge decomposition) that simultaneously explains data scaling laws, model scaling laws, the frequency-ordered acquisition of knowledge, and capacity-induced hallucination—phenomena previously addressed in isolation. A key open question is whether the framework extends beyond factual knowledge to compositional reasoning, multi-hop inference, and the diverse knowledge structures in real corpora. The experiments validate predictions on small models (≤7.2M parameters) with synthetic power-law data, leaving open whether the qualitative predictions (e.g., hallucination thresholds, frequency-ordered learning) hold at the scale of modern frontier LLMs. The connection to the Solomonoff predictor and the question of how practical LLMs approximate universal predictors under computational constraints remain important directions.
