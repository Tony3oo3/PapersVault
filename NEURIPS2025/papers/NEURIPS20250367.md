## Problem

Real-world GUI environments (PC software, mobile apps) are complex and proprietary, making it infeasible to obtain the comprehensive screen layout and inter-screen navigation graphs needed to systematically train and benchmark GUI agent navigation. Existing SFT-based agents memorize trajectories but generalize poorly to unseen screens, while recent single-turn RL methods only correct individual actions rather than learning multi-step navigation strategies through interaction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250367/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent receives a textual task instruction (e.g., 'From page_0 to page_151'), a textual history of prior observation-action pairs, and a current screen screenshot containing randomly named, visually unfamiliar icons. It outputs a ReAct-style response pairing a natural-language explanation with a precise action command (e.g., 'click Hotel icon on page_0; Action: click(835,65)'), constrained to a maximum of 12 interaction rounds with total context under 1k tokens (each image encoded as 729 tokens). The action space is limited to click and complete, and icons are intentionally drawn from an uncommon library with random names to prevent the model from exploiting prior semantic knowledge.

## Method

The paper introduces GE-Lab, a simulation environment engine that models GUI navigation as a customizable tree-structured graph where nodes are screens and edges are clickable transitions, formalized as a POMDP. Training follows a three-stage paradigm on Qwen2.5-VL-7B: (1) SFT on synthesized trajectory data memorizes fundamental page-transition knowledge; (2) ST-RL (GRPO-based) operates on pre-constructed trajectories with a composite reward (action type, coordinate accuracy, intent matching, format) to improve generalization to unseen paths; (3) MT-RL generates observation-action sequences online through interactive rollouts with a sparse goal-based reward (+1 only upon reaching the target screen), encouraging exploration and backtracking. A key empirical finding is that initializing RL from an early SFT stage (1 epoch) yields higher final rewards than later stages, as the model retains greater plasticity for policy adaptation. MT-RL further reduces dependence on annotated data by relying solely on environment feedback, and the full pipeline generalizes to real-world benchmarks (ScreenSpot, AndroidWorld) after continual training on 24k real-world samples.

## Results

On the GE-Lab simulation environment, MT-RL achieves the highest interactive benchmark scores (Pass@1: 17.47, Pass@5: 25.16) and matches ST-RL on OOD overall accuracy (63.25 vs. 63.06), while SFT achieves the best in-distribution overall accuracy (98.89). On real-world benchmarks after continual training, MT-RL-Continue-Train attains the highest average score (72.03%), with particularly strong gains on ScreenSpot (86.08%), VisualWebBench AG (76.70%), and AndroidWorld (15.51%). The staged paradigm shows SFT memorizes navigation (ID Overall 98.89), ST-RL improves OOD generalization (63.06 vs. 55.45), and MT-RL further boosts interactive task success through exploration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against non-fine-tuned frontier models (GPT-4o, Claude-3.7-Sonnet, Gemini-2.0-flash-thinking), all fine-tuned variants achieve dramatic improvements: MT-RL's OOD overall of 63.25 surpasses GPT-4o's 25.85 by 2.4×, and its interactive Pass@5 of 25.16 exceeds GPT-4o's 2.49 by over 10×. Compared to SFT, MT-RL trades in-distribution accuracy (67.33 vs. 98.89 overall) for substantially better OOD generalization (63.25 vs. 55.45, +7.8 points) and interactive performance (Pass@5: 25.16 vs. 20.86, +4.3 points), confirming that RL-based training reduces overfitting to training trajectories. Against ST-RL, MT-RL achieves a marginal OOD edge (63.25 vs. 63.06) but a clear interactive advantage (Pass@1: 17.47 vs. 17.22; Pass@5: 25.16 vs. 22.34), with the gap widening on longer paths (Path@7: 2.92 vs. 0.83 for Pass@1). On real-world benchmarks after continual training, the same hierarchy holds: MT-RL-Continue-Train (72.03 avg) > ST-RL-Continue-Train (70.92) > SFT-Continue-Train (70.87) > Base (67.15), validating that the simulation-trained paradigm transfers to real GUI environments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GUI agent training literature by combining three previously separate threads: SFT-based navigation (UI-TARS, OS-ATLAS), single-turn RL with GRPO (UI-R1, GUI-R1), and multi-turn RL from non-GUI agent systems (RAGEN, VAGEN). The key departure is GE-Lab, a simulation environment engine that provides full access to screen layouts and navigation graphs—something unavailable in proprietary real-world GUIs—enabling systematic study of how each training stage contributes to navigation. Unlike prior GUI RL work that corrects single-step actions against ground-truth trajectories, this paper introduces sparse goal-based rewards in an interactive multi-turn setting, bringing the agent-environment interaction paradigm from general RL into GUI navigation for the first time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes a practical, reproducible training paradigm (SFT → ST-RL → MT-RL) with clear empirical evidence for each stage's role, and GE-Lab offers the community a flexible, low-cost environment for GUI navigation research that circumvents the proprietary-data bottleneck. However, the current action space is limited to click/complete, and the simulated environment is substantially simpler than real GUIs (no text, no scrolling, no complex layouts), so the degree to which these findings transfer to production-grade agents remains an open question. The finding that moderate SFT (1 epoch) is a better RL initialization than more SFT is a useful practical insight that challenges the common assumption that more supervised pre-training always helps downstream RL.
