## Problem

Traditional neural SDE models require costly numerical solvers (e.g., Euler-Maruyama with many small steps) to sample between arbitrary time points, with computational cost scaling linearly in the time gap. This makes real-time applications such as high-frequency trading, robotic control, and digital twins prohibitively expensive. The paper asks whether one can learn SDE transition laws directly as conditional distributions while preserving the mathematical properties of stochastic flows (identity at zero time, Markov property, and Chapman-Kolmogorov self-consistency) without any numerical integration.

## Input / Output

For the base NSF, the model takes a current state vector x_s ∈ ℝⁿ, an elapsed time Δt = t−s, and (for non-autonomous systems) the absolute time s, and produces a single sample from p(x_t | x_s) in one forward pass through the flow, with the transition log-density available in closed form via the change-of-variables formula. For the latent NSF, the input is a sequence of irregularly-sampled noisy observations o_{t_i} with non-uniform time intervals Δt_i, and the output is a sequence of future observation predictions with associated uncertainty. The maximum one-shot prediction horizon H_pred is a design choice: predictions beyond H_pred are obtained by recursive application of the one-step transition, trading a small number of flow evaluations for arbitrary-length forecasts.

## Method

NSF parameterises the SDE weak solution p(x_t | x_s) as a conditional normalising flow whose base distribution is a state-dependent Gaussian following the Euler-Maruyama form (mean shifted by Δt·μ(c), noise scaled by √Δt·σ(c)), followed by L affine coupling layers whose shift and log-scale parameters are explicitly multiplied by Δt, guaranteeing the identity property at Δt=0 by construction. A bidirectional KL-divergence flow loss enforces Chapman-Kolmogorov consistency between one-step and two-step transitions; because the two-step marginal is intractable, a Brownian-bridge-parameterised auxiliary variational distribution b_ξ(x_{t_j} | x_{t_i}, x_{t_k}) with factor α(τ)=τ(1−τ) provides tractable variational upper bounds in both KL directions. The latent NSF extension embeds NSF transitions into a variational state-space model with a GRU encoder, adding a skip-ahead KL loss that directly compares the posterior at time t_j against the NSF-predicted marginal from t_i, enabling efficient long-horizon inference without recursive solver steps. Training combines negative log-likelihood (or β-NELBO for the latent model) with the flow loss, optimised via alternating gradient updates on flow parameters θ and bridge parameters ξ.

## Results

On the Stochastic Lorenz Attractor, NSF (H_pred=1.0, single-step) achieves the lowest KL divergence across all time horizons (0.2±0.6 at t=1.0) while requiring only 53 kFLOPs per sample, compared to 959–737,354 kFLOPs for solver-based baselines. On CMU Motion Capture, Latent NSF achieves state-of-the-art MSE of 3.41±0.27 on the beyond-horizon extrapolation task (Setup 2), outperforming the next best method (SDE matching, 4.26±0.35) and being >2 orders of magnitude faster than Latent SDE (3.5 ms vs 75 ms per 100 samples). On Stochastic Moving MNIST, Latent NSF (one-step) achieves the best Static FD (1.67±0.47) and competitive Frame-wise FD (0.63±0.15) versus Latent SDE (2.66±0.87 and 0.58±0.21).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the Stochastic Lorenz Attractor, NSF (single-step, H_pred=1.0) achieves the best KL divergence at every time horizon, with the largest margins at longer horizons: at t=1.0 it reaches 0.2±0.6 KL versus 1.5±0.5 for Latent SDE [33], 6.0±0.3 for SDE matching [3], and 14.1–53.1 for the Stable Neural SDE variants [38], while using 53 kFLOPs (18–13,700× fewer than baselines). On CMU Motion Capture Setup 2 (beyond-horizon extrapolation), Latent NSF achieves SOTA MSE of 3.41±0.27, beating SDE matching [3] (4.26±0.35), NCDSSM [2] (4.74±0.01), ARCTA [9] (9.92±1.82), Latent SDE [33] (9.52±0.21), and Latent ODE [43] (31.62±0.05); on Setup 1 it is competitive (8.62±0.32) but trails NCDSSM (5.69±0.01) and SDE matching (5.20±0.43). On Stochastic Moving MNIST, Latent NSF (one-step) achieves the best Static FD (1.67 vs 2.66 for Latent SDE) with comparable Frame-wise FD (0.63 vs 0.58), while the recursive variant matches Latent SDE on Static FD (2.36 vs 2.66) at roughly half the runtime. Across all three benchmarks, the solver-based baselines (Latent SDE, Stable Neural SDE variants) incur 18–13,700× more FLOPs and 20–400× more runtime per 100 samples than NSF, with the gap widening for longer prediction horizons.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the solver-free philosophy of neural flows (Biloš et al., 2021) — which learns ODE solution maps directly via architecturally constrained networks — into the stochastic setting, where the target is the SDE transition distribution p(x_t|x_s) rather than a deterministic flow map. The architectural backbone is conditional normalising flows (Rezende & Mohamed, 2015; Winkler et al., 2019), with a Gaussian initialisation scaled by Δt and √Δt (mirroring Euler–Maruyama) followed by affine coupling layers whose parameters vanish at Δt=0 to enforce the identity property. The latent extension follows the variational state-space model framework (Krishnan et al., 2017), replacing discrete-time Gaussian transitions with continuous-time NSF kernels. A key departure from prior neural SDE work (Li et al., 2020; Oh et al., 2024) is that NSF never learns drift or diffusion coefficients; it learns the weak solution directly, and a bidirectional KL regularisation (via a Brownian-bridge variational distribution) enforces Chapman–Kolmogorov consistency without any numerical solver at training or inference time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is a two-orders-of-magnitude reduction in inference cost for distant time-point sampling while maintaining distributional accuracy comparable to iterative solvers, which opens the door to real-time stochastic modelling in trading, robotics, and digital-twin applications where per-step solver cost is prohibitive. The beyond-horizon extrapolation result on CMU Motion Capture (SOTA MSE) suggests that directly modelling the Markov transition kernel avoids the error accumulation inherent in recursive solver-based prediction. Open questions include: whether the approximate Chapman–Kolmogorov enforcement (variational upper bounds only) degrades outside the training horizon, how the maximum one-shot horizon H_pred should be chosen in practice, and whether more expressive flow parameterisations (e.g., transformer-based flows) can relax the affine-coupling constraint without sacrificing tractable Jacobians.
