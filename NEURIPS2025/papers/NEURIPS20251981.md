## Problem

Reward models in RLHF are primarily evaluated through accuracy (correct ranking of output pairs), yet empirical evidence shows that more accurate reward models do not necessarily produce stronger language models after RLHF. It remains unclear what property beyond accuracy determines whether a reward model is an effective teacher for policy gradient optimization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251981/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a reward model r_RM, an initial language model policy π_θ(0), and a prompt set S as inputs, and produces (i) the reward variance induced by r_RM over on-policy outputs sampled from π_θ(0) for each prompt, and (ii) a lower bound on the time t_γ required for the expected ground truth reward to increase by an additive constant γ. Crucially, reward variance is computed over outputs drawn from the current policy distribution (on-policy), not from fixed off-policy benchmarks, making it a joint property of the reward model and the specific language model being aligned.

## Method

The paper proves that low reward variance — the degree to which r_RM separates rewards assigned to outputs probable under the policy — induces a flat RLHF objective landscape, causing both proxy and ground truth rewards to increase at a rate bounded below by Ω(Var^{-1/3}), regardless of accuracy. This builds on the vanishing-gradient connection between reward variance and the policy gradient norm [64], extended here by showing that higher-order derivatives also vanish, preventing rapid gradient growth during training. Two key corollaries follow: (i) a perfectly accurate reward model can lead to arbitrarily slow ground truth reward maximization compared to a less accurate one with higher variance, and (ii) the same reward model can be an effective teacher for one policy while inducing a flat landscape for another. The theoretical analysis uses gradient flow (small learning rate limit) with general autoregressive policies for the main lower bound and tabular policies for the separation results. Experiments with Pythia and Llama-3.2 models (up to 8B) trained via RLOO on UltraFeedback and AlpacaFarm confirm that reward variance strongly correlates with reward maximization rate (Pearson r = 0.98 for proxy reward), and that a perfectly accurate but low-variance reward model underperforms less accurate alternatives.

## Results

Reward variance induced by the reward model for the initial policy strongly correlates with the rate of reward increase during policy gradient: Pearson correlation of 0.982 for proxy reward and 0.834 for ground truth reward (Table 2). A perfectly accurate reward model with low reward variance (0.111) leads to significantly slower ground truth reward increase compared to less accurate models with higher variance (e.g., 0.630 for the 100% on-policy RM). The combined measure of reward variance and accuracy achieves the highest correlation with ground truth reward increase (Pearson 0.940, Spearman 0.828), outperforming either metric alone. In cross-model experiments (Section 4.2), the reward model yielding the highest ground truth reward varies across initial policies, confirming that no single reward model is universally optimal.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares proxy reward models of varying accuracy and variance against direct optimization with the ground truth reward (ArmoRM [Wang et al., 2024]). Strikingly, in the first few epochs of policy gradient, proxy reward models with higher reward variance (e.g., 0.630) outperform direct optimization with ArmoRM (variance 0.256), despite ArmoRM being perfectly accurate. The perfectly accurate but low-variance RM (variance 0.111) underperforms all less accurate models, confirming Theorem 2. In the cross-model experiment (Section 4.2), GRM-Llama-3.2-3B and GRM-Gemma-2-2B [Yang et al., NeurIPS 2024] (RewardBench scores 90.9 and 88.4) and RM-Tulu-V2-8B [Ivison et al., NeurIPS 2024] (score 74.5) are compared across Pythia and Llama-3.2 initial policies. RM-Gemma-2B (lowest RewardBench score, 65.4) induces the highest reward variance for all policies and yields the largest proxy reward increase, but its inaccuracy prevents this from translating into ground truth reward gains. The best-performing reward model for ground truth reward differs across initial policies, with no single model dominating all settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Razin et al. [64], which established that the gradient of the RLHF objective vanishes when reward variance is low for softmax-parameterized policies. Here, that vanishing-gradient result is leveraged to derive lower bounds on reward maximization time and to characterize what makes a reward model an effective teacher. The work also connects to the reward shaping literature [53, 31, 19, 34] and the broader RLHF optimization literature, but departs from prior analyses that focused on specialized algorithms or sample complexity by studying the standard policy gradient pipeline (PPO, RLOO, GRPO) and the interaction between reward model properties and the specific policy being aligned. The finding that more accurate reward models are not necessarily better teachers parallels the 'stronger teachers do not always provide better supervision' phenomenon in knowledge distillation [58, 86], though the mechanism here is distinct: it stems from reward variance rather than inherent weakness of the teacher signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work formalizes a fundamental limitation of current reward model benchmarks (RewardBench, RMB, etc.) that produce universal accuracy-based rankings independent of the language model being aligned, showing that reward variance—which depends on the RM-policy interaction—is equally critical for efficient optimization. Open questions include how to best train or modify reward models to induce higher variance (naive scaling amplifies gradient noise), how to incorporate policy-specific variance into evaluation protocols, and whether these insights extend to verifiable-reward settings (math, coding) and alignment methods beyond policy gradient (e.g., DPO, Best-of-N). The practical implication is that reward model selection should be policy-aware, and that a single 'best' reward model does not exist across all language models.
