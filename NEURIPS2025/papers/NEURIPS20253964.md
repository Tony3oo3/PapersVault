## Problem

Reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) has dramatically improved LLM reasoning, but its impact on vision-language models has been muted, largely because prior VLM reasoning pipelines depend on costly knowledge distillation and supervised fine-tuning from larger or closed-source models. The paper asks whether a VLM can self-improve via RFT alone—without any distillation—and identifies that the critical missing ingredient is selecting training samples at the right difficulty level for the base model.

## Input / Output

Each training and evaluation instance pairs an RGB image with a text question (math, chart, or natural-image reasoning). During MCTS-based filtering, the VLM generates step-by-step reasoning chains where each step is delimited by an <end> tag, and the final answer is enclosed in \boxed{}. The critic model (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) then judges correctness against the ground-truth answer to decide whether the MCTS search terminates. For RFT, the model is prompted to produce an internal monologue inside <think>\n tags followed by the final answer in \boxed{}.

## Method

The core contribution is repurposing Monte Carlo Tree Search as an offline difficulty estimator: for each of 70k open-source samples, the base VLM (Qwen2.5-VL-7B or 72B-Instruct) acts as the policy in an MCTS loop (selection by visitation count, expansion with k=3 temperature-0.5 rollouts, simulation to a final answer), and the number of iterations K needed to reach a correct answer serves as the difficulty score. Samples with K > 5 or unsolved after 50 iterations are retained, yielding 11k (7B) or 7.5k (72B) training instances. These are then used directly for RFT with the GRPO objective (no SFT, no distillation, no process reward model), where the VLM's own rollout accuracy provides the reward signal. Ablations show that the combination of medium-difficulty (K>5) and unsolved samples outperforms either subset alone, random sampling, self-consistency filtering, or training on the full 70k set, and that model-specific difficulty calibration (7B-selected data for 7B, 72B-selected for 72B) is essential—cross-model transfer degrades performance significantly.

## Results

ThinkLite-VL-7B achieves an average score of 64.18 across eight visual reasoning benchmarks, improving over its base model Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct (59.69) by 4.49 points and surpassing GPT-4o (65.21 avg) on MathVista (75.1 vs. 63.8) and o1 (73.9). ThinkLite-VL-72B pushes the SoTA further to 72.67 average, reaching 79.7 on MathVista and 64.3 on MathVerse—new records among all evaluated VLMs. Both models use only 11k (7B) and 7.5k (72B) training samples via MCTS-guided selection, with no knowledge distillation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ThinkLite-VL-7B (64.18 avg) outperforms all 7B-level reasoning baselines: MM-EUREKA-Qwen-7B (62.15), OpenVLThinker-7B (61.71), Vision-R1-7B (61.46), LLaVA-Cot-11B (52.85), and Mulberry-7B, while using substantially less training data (11k vs. 15k–260k). On MathVista specifically, ThinkLite-VL-7B (75.1) surpasses GPT-4o (63.8), o1 (73.9), and Qwen2.5-VL-72B-Instruct (74.8). ThinkLite-VL-72B (72.67 avg) beats QvQ-72B (67.37), InternVL2.5-78B (67.99), and LLaVA-Onevision-72B (61.75) by 4.4–10.9 points on average, and sets new SoTA on MathVista (79.7) and MathVerse (64.3). The only benchmarks where ThinkLite-VL-7B trails are MMBench and MM-Vet, where InternVL2.5-8B leads by 1.3 and 1.0 points respectively. Compared to its own base model, ThinkLite-VL-7B gains +4.49 avg and ThinkLite-VL-72B gains +4.42 avg, with the largest per-benchmark gains on MathVision (+9.3 and +8.6) and MathVerse (+7.6 and +11.0).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Monte Carlo Tree Search from its traditional role as an inference-time reasoning aid (as in Tree of Thoughts, DeepSeek-Prover) into a training-time data selection mechanism, using the number of MCTS iterations to solve a problem as a difficulty proxy. It builds directly on GRPO (from DeepSeekMath) as the RL objective and extends the RFT-for-VLM-reasoning line (Vision-R1, OpenVLThinker, MM-Eureka) by eliminating the knowledge distillation and SFT stages that those pipelines rely on. The key departure from concurrent work like MM-Eureka is the use of a search-based, fine-grained difficulty signal rather than a binary accuracy threshold, and the finding that unsolved-but-informative samples are valuable training signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: achieving SoTA visual reasoning with 11k/7.5k samples and no distillation dramatically lowers the barrier to training competitive reasoning VLMs, making self-improvement accessible without access to large teacher models. The finding that model-specific difficulty calibration matters (cross-model sample transfer underperforms) suggests that difficulty-aware data curation should be a standard step in any RFT pipeline. Open questions include whether the MCTS-based difficulty signal generalizes to other modalities or task types, how the approach scales as base models improve (the difficulty distribution shifts), and whether the computational cost of running MCTS over 70k samples can be reduced without losing selection quality.
