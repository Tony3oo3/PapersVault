## Problem

Real-world LLM deployments must balance competing objectives (e.g., helpfulness vs. harmlessness, cost vs. accuracy), but existing solutions either require expensive weight-level retraining (RLHF, DPO, multi-objective fine-tuning) or are tailored to specific trade-offs like cost-accuracy cascades. No lightweight, post-hoc, weight-agnostic method exists that provides finite-sample, distribution-free guarantees on an arbitrary guardrail metric while maximizing a secondary objective, and that works with closed, black-box APIs without access to logits or model internals.

## Input / Output

Given a context x with a finite action set A(x) (e.g., four multiple-choice options), the method consumes per-action real-valued scores from a Primary model p(x,a) and a Guardian model g(x,a), plus a user-specified risk budget α ∈ (0,1). It outputs a single selected action a* and an implicit routing decision: if the Primary's top action is sufficiently dominant (the λ-relaxed candidate set is a singleton), the Primary's choice is returned directly; otherwise the Guardian selects from the candidate set. No access to model logits, weights, or internal probabilities is required—only the opaque scores returned by each API call.

## Method

Conformal Arbitrage (CA) learns a single scalar threshold λ̂ via conformal risk control (CRC) on a calibration set of n exchangeable examples. For a given λ, the λ-relaxed candidate set C_λ(x) contains all actions whose Primary scores are within λ of the Primary's top score; the per-sample loss L_i(λ) measures the gap between the Guardian's best action overall and the Guardian's best action within C_λ(x_i), capturing residual guardrail risk. CRC selects the smallest λ̂ satisfying (n/(n+1))·R̂_n(λ) + B/(n+1) ≤ α, yielding the finite-sample, distribution-free guarantee E[L(x, C_λ̂(x))] ≤ α. At deployment, if C_λ̂(x) is a singleton the Primary acts autonomously; otherwise the Guardian refines the choice from the filtered candidate set. A utility-optimality theorem (Theorem 1) shows that among all score-gap routers, CA achieves expected primary utility within O(n⁻¹) of the optimal threshold under the same risk budget, requiring only a few hundred calibration examples and no model retraining.

## Results

On TruthfulQA (cost vs. accuracy), Conformal Arbitrage traces an efficient frontier: tightening the risk budget α from 0.25 to 0.05 raises accuracy from 0.621 to 0.806 while increasing cost from $0.188 to $0.521 per 1000 examples, compared to the Guardian-only baseline of 0.833 accuracy at $0.620. On PKU-SafeRLHF (helpfulness vs. harmlessness), CA at α=0.30 halves the Primary's safety violations (severity-loss 0.245 vs. 0.676) while retaining 63% of its helpfulness (0.325 vs. 0.519), and at the strictest budget α=0.10 it more than doubles the Guardian's helpfulness (0.214 vs. 0.156) while keeping severity-loss within the prescribed limit. Across all settings, CA meets the finite-sample guarantee E[L] ≤ α at every risk level.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Conformal Arbitrage against three baselines: the Primary-only model, the Guardian-only model, and a cost-matched random router that selects the acting model uniformly at random while matching CA's average cost. On TruthfulQA, CA beats the cost-matched random router at every risk level except α=0.25 (where it is marginally below by 0.011), with the largest advantage of +0.031 accuracy at α=0.10. On PKU-SafeRLHF, CA's mean performance at every risk budget strictly dominates the linear interpolation between Primary and Guardian achievable by randomized routing, and it meets the finite-sample severity-loss bound E[L] ≤ α at all tested budgets. The ablation with gpt-4.1-mini as Primary (narrowing the capability gap) shows CA still gains +2 pp accuracy over the random router at α=0.05 while invoking the Guardian on only 24% of queries, though the margin narrows as the two models become more similar in capability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies Conformal Risk Control (CRC; Angelopoulos et al., 2024) to the model-routing problem, calibrating a single score-gap threshold that determines when a Primary model's prediction is confident enough to act on versus when to defer to a more conservative Guardian. Conceptually, the score-gap router mirrors Chow's (1970) Bayes-optimal reject-option classifier, with the 'reject' action replaced by escalation to a second model. The closest prior work is Cascaded Selective Evaluation (CSE; Jung et al., 2025), which also uses conformal calibration for LLM routing but is restricted to binary disagreement risk, requires logit access, and uses per-judge thresholds; Conformal Arbitrage generalizes to arbitrary bounded guardrail losses, operates on opaque API scores, and uses a single global threshold. The broader cascade and selective-prediction literature (FrugalGPT, AutoMix, SelectiveNet) informs the routing paradigm, but those methods either optimize for a specific trade-off (cost-accuracy) or lack distribution-free finite-sample guarantees on the guardrail metric.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Conformal Arbitrage offers a lightweight, weight-agnostic, post-hoc mechanism for dialing the trade-off between any two competing objectives (accuracy vs. cost, helpfulness vs. harmlessness) in deployed LLM systems, with finite-sample, distribution-free guarantees that require only a few hundred calibration examples. This is practically valuable for organizations using closed-API models where retraining or logit access is unavailable. Key limitations include the focus on multiple-choice settings (free-text extension is sketched but not deeply validated), the single-step two-model architecture (no multi-level cascades), and the absence of direct comparison against purpose-built cascade systems like CSE. Open questions include whether adaptive CRC can tighten guarantees on heterogeneous prompt distributions, how the method scales to large action spaces in open-ended generation, and whether the score-gap threshold remains the optimal routing statistic in richer settings.
