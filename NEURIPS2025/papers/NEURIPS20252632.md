## Problem

Self-supervised neural combinatorial optimization struggles to enforce discrete constraints coherently: training typically relies on penalty terms in the loss that require careful tuning, while inference uses separate rounding heuristics that treat the two phases differently and may degrade solution quality. No general framework existed that simultaneously provides a differentiable loss encoding the constraints and a rounding procedure with a quality guarantee, in a unified end-to-end pipeline.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a bipartite graph G=(U,V,E) encoding a set system (elements U and candidate subsets V) together with a cardinality constraint k, processed by a 3-layer GraphSage encoder that outputs a perturbation vector mapped into the (k,n)-hypersimplex Δ_{n,k}. At inference, the decomposition yields a small support of O(n) feasible sets of size exactly k, from which the best set is selected; the rounding guarantee ensures the returned solution's objective is at least as good as the continuous extension value F(x). The framework extends beyond cardinality constraints to partition matroids, spanning trees, and independent sets by swapping the vertex-finding oracle in the decomposition.

## Method

The core mechanism is an iterative GLS-based decomposition that expresses any point x in the feasible polytope as a convex combination of at most n+1 vertices (feasible sets), where each iteration selects a vertex via a strong optimization oracle and computes the largest coefficient keeping the next iterate in the polytope. The resulting vertex-probability pairs define a distribution D_C(x) over feasible sets, and the expected objective E_{S~D_C(x)}[f(S)] serves as the self-supervised loss, which is almost everywhere differentiable and thus amenable to standard backpropagation. The neural network predicts a perturbation of a known interior point (e.g., the uniform vector k/n·1 for the hypersimplex) to guarantee the output remains in the polytope without requiring explicit projection. At inference, the same decomposition produces candidate feasible solutions, and selecting the best one provides a rounding guarantee that the discrete solution is no worse than the continuous relaxation. A rescaling variant (multiplying coefficients by b∈(0,1]) allows controlled approximation with more sets in the support, improving exploration during training.

## Results

The paper evaluates on the Maximum Coverage problem across four datasets (Random500, Random1000, Twitch, Railway) with metrics of inference time (lower is better) and objective value (higher is better). In the no-test-time-optimization (TTO) setting, the short version of the method consistently outperforms all learning baselines (CardNN, EGN, RL) in both inference time and objective value. With TTO (medium/long variants), the method surpasses most TTO-based baselines across datasets except Twitch, where all learning-based methods underperform due to encoder architecture limitations. The method also outperforms the greedy algorithm on multiple benchmarks (e.g., Random500 for larger k) and UCOM2 on Random1000 and Railway, while scaling to instances with hundreds of thousands of nodes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CardNN [88] (with and without TTO), the proposed method achieves superior inference time and objective value in the no-TTO setting across all three CardNN variants; with TTO, the medium/long variants surpass CardNN-TTO on most datasets except Twitch, where CardNN's direct Adam optimization on the test sample overcomes the encoder architecture weakness. Against the greedy algorithm [61], the method is competitive overall and outperforms it on Random500 for larger values of k, though greedy retains its (1−1/e) approximation guarantee. Against UCOM2 [10], the method outperforms on Random1000 and Railway but loses on Twitch, where UCOM2's incremental greedy de-randomization performs well. Against the naive EGN baseline [35] and the RL (Actor-Critic) baseline [45, 69], the method consistently wins in both speed and solution quality. The main exception across all comparisons is the Twitch dataset, where the authors attribute poor performance of all learning-based methods (including their own) to the encoder architecture and data distribution characteristics rather than the decomposition/loss design.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges convex geometry (specifically the constructive GLS/Carathéodory decomposition) with self-supervised neural combinatorial optimization, building directly on Karalias et al.'s (NeurIPS 2022) framework of using continuous extensions of discrete set functions as differentiable losses. Prior self-supervised CO methods handle constraints through penalty terms in the loss or separate rounding heuristics, creating a train/inference mismatch; this work departs by embedding constraints directly into the distribution via polytope decomposition, eliminating the need for tuned constraint penalties and providing a built-in rounding guarantee. The general template requires only a strong optimization oracle for the feasible-set polytope, unifying treatment of cardinality constraints, partition matroids, spanning trees, and independent sets under one algorithmic framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a principled, general recipe for constrained neural CO that is applicable whenever a strong optimization oracle exists, potentially covering a very large class of combinatorial problems. The rounding guarantee (best feasible set in the decomposition is at least as good as the continuous extension value) is a practical advantage over heuristic rounding used in prior methods. Key limitations include sensitivity to encoder architecture (notably poor performance on the Twitch dataset) and dependence on polytope structure for efficiency; the paper acknowledges that model architecture design is an important but underexplored direction. Open questions include extending the approach to problems where no efficient oracle exists (e.g., TSP polytope) and whether the decomposition-based loss design generalizes to non-convex or non-polyhedral feasible sets.
