## Problem

Federated learning under non-IID data causes the global model to overfit a small number of client-specific features, leading to poor generalization on unseen data. Existing remedies (correction terms, knowledge distillation, client clustering, multi-model searching) introduce extra communication overhead, require public data, or are incompatible with secure aggregation. The paper asks whether one can improve FL generalization by directly disrupting overfit features in the global model without any of these additional costs.

## Input / Output

Each client receives a stochastically reset copy of the global CNN (ResNet-18, VGG-16, or MobileNet-V1) and trains it on local RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 for Tiny-ImageNet, variable for Office-31) to produce class-label predictions. The reset operation acts exclusively on convolutional-layer kernels, resampling a fraction θ of them from a Gaussian matched to the layer's current mean and variance, so the dispatched model is a valid but partially 'destroyed' version of the global model. The server aggregates the K locally trained copies by weighted averaging to produce the next global model.

## Method

FedPhoenix duplicates the global model into K copies and, before dispatch, stochastically resets ⌊θ·n_i⌋ kernels in each convolutional layer of each copy by resampling them from N(μ_i, σ_i²), the layer's own statistics. A dynamic stabilization strategy gates which layers are eligible for reset: layer i is only reset while round r ≤ i·r_s/|S_l|, so shallower layers stop being perturbed earlier and the model progressively stabilizes. The core insight is that destroying a few features forces the network to distribute its inference reliance across many generalized features (which get restored through cross-client aggregation) rather than a handful of client-specific ones (which do not). The reset ratio θ is kept small (1/32 or 1/64) and the entire overhead is server-side, making the method compatible with secure aggregation. A convergence analysis under standard FL assumptions shows the perturbation variance decays to zero as r → r_s, recovering the O(1/T) rate of vanilla FedAvg.

## Results

FedPhoenix achieves up to 20.73% higher test accuracy than FedAvg on CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18 under IID settings (84.85% vs. 64.12%). Under the most heterogeneous non-IID setting (β=0.3), FedPhoenix reaches 80.12% on CIFAR-10 (ResNet-18), 56.58% on CIFAR-100, and 59.04% on Tiny-ImageNet, outperforming the best baseline (FedMut) by 16.08%, 12.13%, and 8.53% respectively. Across all 27 experimental configurations (3 datasets × 3 models × 3 heterogeneity levels), FedPhoenix consistently achieves the highest accuracy, with the largest gains observed on CNN-heavy architectures like ResNet-18 and MobileNet-V1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FedPhoenix outperforms all five baselines across every dataset, model architecture, and heterogeneity setting tested. Against FedAvg [1], the most standard FL baseline, FedPhoenix achieves the largest absolute gains—up to 20.73% on CIFAR-10 (ResNet-18, IID) and 16.15% on CIFAR-100 (ResNet-18, β=0.3). FedMut [26], the strongest baseline in most settings, is surpassed by FedPhoenix by 8.5–21.1% depending on the configuration, with the gap widening under higher heterogeneity (β=0.3). FedProx [21] and CluSample [28] show only marginal improvements over FedAvg and are consistently outperformed by FedPhoenix by 10–25%. FedGen [23] (knowledge-distillation-based) performs competitively on Tiny-ImageNet with VGG-16 but is still surpassed by FedPhoenix by 5.5–12.5% across all settings. Notably, FedPhoenix's advantage is most pronounced on architectures with many convolutional layers (ResNet-18, MobileNet-V1) compared to VGG-16, which has more fully-connected layers that are not targeted by the reset mechanism.

## Relation to Prior Work

FedPhoenix builds on the federated learning literature addressing non-IID client data, positioning itself against correction-term methods (FedProx, SCAFFOLD), knowledge distillation approaches (FedGen), client clustering (ClusteredSampling), and gradient mutation (FedMut). Its key conceptual departure is a heuristic parameter-reset mechanism that stochastically destroys a subset of convolutional kernels before dispatching model copies to clients, rather than adding regularization terms, generating synthetic data, or perturbing gradients. The dynamic stabilization strategy—progressively reducing resets in shallower layers over training rounds—distinguishes it from FedMut's uniform random mutations, and the use of layer-statistics-based resampling (rather than zeroing or Kaiming initialization) is a deliberate design choice to bound perturbation variance for convergence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FedPhoenix offers a simple, server-side-only intervention that requires no additional communication, public data, or client-side computation, while remaining compatible with secure aggregation—a combination that prior methods (clustering, KD, correction terms) cannot simultaneously achieve. The t-SNE and feature-masking diagnostics providing a mechanistic explanation for why FedAvg overfits to a small number of features add interpretive value beyond the accuracy gains. Key open questions include whether the reset strategy transfers to Transformer architectures and NLP tasks, whether the convergence guarantees extend to partial-participation settings, and whether the large observed improvements (15–20%) persist at larger scale (e.g., ImageNet) or with more aggressive non-IID splits.
