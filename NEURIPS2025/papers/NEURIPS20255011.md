## Problem

Imposing group fairness constraints on resource allocation problems can reduce social welfare, but the magnitude of this loss (the Price of Fairness) was previously uncharacterized for matroid allocation problems under a fairness notion that accounts for each group's inherent capacity. This paper introduces opportunity fairness—requiring each group's allocation to be proportional to its maximum feasible allocation in isolation—and provides the first tight PoF bounds across adversarial, parametric, and random settings.

## Input / Output

The method takes a C-colored matroid (E, I) with a rank function r and, in random settings, a probability vector p ∈ Δ^C governing how agents are assigned to groups. The output is a closed-form PoF bound expressed as a combinatorial optimization over subsets of groups (Corollary 3.3), which in the semi-random setting reduces to a one-dimensional optimization over the independence index ρ. All results are stated for fractional (polymatroid) allocations, with a randomized rounding guarantee (Proposition 2.4) showing that integral allocations approximate the fractional optimum at cost O(C/min_c r(c)) in fairness and O(C) in L1 deviation.

## Method

The key technical insight is that the set of feasible per-group allocations of a colored matroid is itself a polymatroid (Proposition 3.2), which yields a simple characterization of PoF as a ratio involving the rank function over subsets of groups (Corollary 3.3). Leveraging this, the paper proves a worst-case PoF of C−1 (tight for any matroid class containing graphic or partition matroids), a parametric bound interpolating between 1 and C/4 via the independence index ρ = r([C])/Σr(c), and—most notably—a semi-random bound (Theorem 4.8) that reduces an infinite-dimensional combinatorial optimization over all concave 1-Lipschitz functions R to a one-variable problem, showing PoF→1 whenever no group exceeds fraction 1/(C−1) of the population. For Erdős–Rényi random graphs (both graphic and transversal matroids), PoF converges to 1 with high probability away from the critical edge-density threshold.

## Results

The paper establishes that the worst-case Price of Opportunity Fairness (PoF) for a C-colored matroid is exactly C−1 (Theorem 4.1), and that for two groups the PoF is always 1 (Corollary 4.2), meaning no welfare loss. In the semi-random setting where agents are randomly partitioned into C groups, the PoF converges to 1 whenever the largest group has probability mass at most 1/(C−1) (Corollary 4.9). For Erdős–Rényi random graphs (both graphic and transversal matroids), the PoF converges to 1 with high probability whenever the edge probability is sufficiently far from the critical threshold (Propositions 4.10–4.11).

**Summary of Price of Opportunity Fairness (PoF) bounds across settings.**

| Setting | PoF Bound | Key Condition |
|---|---|---|
| Adversarial (worst-case) | C − 1 (tight) | Any C-colored matroid |
| Two groups (C = 2) | 1 (exact) | Always |
| Equal group ranks | ≤ C/4 + O(1/C) | r(c) = r(c′) ∀ c,c′ |
| Independence index ρ ∈ [1/C, 1/(C−1)] | 1 (exact) | Full competition or full independence |
| Semi-random, max p_c ≤ 1/(C−1) | → 1 (in probability) | No dominant group |
| Semi-random, max p_c = π | ≤ min(C − 1/π, 1) | Theorem 4.8 |
| Random graphic matroid, q ≤ 1/(ωn) or q ≥ ω/n | → 1 w.h.p. | Any p ∈ Δ^C |
| Random transversal matroid, q ≤ 1/(ωn^{3/2}) or q ≥ ω log n / n | → 1 w.h.p. | Any p ∈ Δ^C |

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no experimental baselines; the 'comparisons' are between fairness notions and between analytical settings. The paper contrasts opportunity fairness with proportional fairness (demographic parity), showing that proportional fairness can yield an unbounded PoF when irrelevant agents are added to a group, while opportunity fairness remains bounded by C−1 (Section 2.4). Opportunity fairness is also compared to Kalai–Smorodinsky fairness: every maximum-size opportunity-fair allocation satisfies Kalai–Smorodinsky fairness, but the converse does not hold, making opportunity fairness more restrictive (Appendix A). The paper further positions its beyond-worst-case analysis (semi-random and fully random settings) against the classical adversarial worst-case bound of C−1, demonstrating that realistic group structures and random matroid structures reduce the PoF to 1, i.e., no welfare loss. No direct algorithmic comparison is made with prior fair-matroid-allocation works (e.g., Chierichetti et al. [18], Bandyapadhyay et al. [9]), which focus on approximation algorithms rather than the quality of the optimal fair solution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Price of Fairness framework (Bertsimas et al. 2011; Caragiannis et al. 2012) to a new fairness notion—opportunity fairness—drawn from Equality of Opportunity in ML (Hardt et al. 2016) and Kalai-Smorodinsky bargaining (1975), applied to matroid allocation problems. While prior work on fair matroid allocation (Chierichetti et al. 2019; Bandyapadhyay et al. 2023) focuses on efficiently *computing* fair allocations via approximation algorithms, this paper instead characterizes the *quality gap* of the optimal fair allocation through a polymatroid representation (building on Edmonds 1970). The analysis also departs from purely adversarial worst-case bounds by adopting the beyond-worst-case paradigm, introducing semi-random (random coloring) and fully random (Erdős-Rényi graph) settings that were previously unexplored for this problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides tight, interpretable PoF bounds that reveal a counterintuitive structural insight: welfare loss under opportunity fairness is driven by the presence of a *dominant* group, not by small protected groups that must be 'catered to.' This reframes how practitioners should think about the fairness-efficiency trade-off in allocation systems (job matching, refugee resettlement, university admissions). Key limitations include the large-market assumption (fractional allocations approximate integral ones only when group ranks are large) and the restriction to unweighted social welfare; extending to individual-level weights would break the polymatroid structure. Open questions include the intermediate regime for random bipartite graphs near the phase transition, and whether the no-loss guarantee extends to other matroid classes or intersectional fairness settings with many colors.
