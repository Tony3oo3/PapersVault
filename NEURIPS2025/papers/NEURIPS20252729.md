## Problem

Publicly available ransomware detection datasets are limited in both scale (≤27 TiB combined) and ransomware variant coverage (≤20 samples), and critically, all label data only at the stream level rather than per individual I/O command. This stream-level labeling misclassifies benign commands within infected streams and prevents the development of high-granularity sequential models or tasks like selective data recovery of only ransomware-corrupted bytes.

## Input / Output

The input is a raw NVMe I/O command stream where each command carries four fundamental attributes (timestamp, opcode R/W, size in bytes, disk offset) plus eight derived overlap and time-lapse features (OV and Δt for WAR, RAR, RAW, WAW pairs). Commands are quantized into 18 bits and split into a pair of 9-bit tokens (with an added index bit yielding a 1024-token vocabulary), and models consume sequences of 1000 commands (or the full stream for the continuous uLSTM). The output is a per-command binary prediction (ransomware vs. benign), enabling direct measurement of missed ransomware traffic volume without aggregation.

## Method

The paper introduces the CLEAR dataset (1,045 TiB, 137 ransomware variants, 23 billion commands) with per-command labels derived via a four-step algorithm combining process-tree ancestry, post-encryption path normalization, and System-process attribution. To exploit this labeling, the authors train command-level sequential models—Command Level Transformer (3-layer self-attention), biLSTM, and a continuous unidirectional uLSTM—that predict a label for every command in a 1000-command window, contrasting with aggregative baselines (Random Forest, DeftPunk, UNET, Patch Level Transformer) that classify entire chunks. The key finding is that performance scales with context length rather than architecture choice, with the continuous uLSTM (infinite history) achieving 0.36% MDR and 50 MB MBD₃, outperforming the best aggregative model by up to 82% in data loss prevention. A feature ablation study confirms the derived overlap features contribute meaningfully, and a TLSH-clustered unseen-ransomware benchmark demonstrates that sequential models degrade less than tabular models on out-of-distribution variants.

## Results

The best-performing model, the continuous uLSTM (530K parameters), achieves a Missed Detection Rate (MDR) of 0.36% and a MegaBytes-to-Detection (MBD₃) of 50 MB at a fixed False Alarm Rate of 1 per 50 GiB, with an AUC of 0.999664. This represents up to 82% reduction in data loss (MBD₃) compared to the state-of-the-art DeftPunk baseline (282 MB) and roughly 3× improvement over the best aggregative model (PLT, 157 MB), despite using orders of magnitude fewer parameters (530K vs. 17M for PLT, 153M for UNET). All three command-level models (CLT, biLSTM, uLSTM) outperform every aggregative baseline on MBD₃, confirming that per-command labeling enables substantially earlier detection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the state-of-the-art DeftPunk baseline (Decision Tree + XGBoost, 260K params), the uLSTM reduces MBD₃ from 282 MB to 50 MB (an 82% reduction in data lost before detection) and improves MDR from 6.74% to 0.36%, while also achieving a higher AUC (0.999664 vs. 0.990172). The Random Forest baseline performs worst overall (9.07% MDR, 286 MB MBD₃), confirming that simple tabular aggregation is insufficient. Among the paper's own aggregative baselines, the Patch Level Transformer (PLT, 17M params) is the strongest, achieving 1.16% MDR and 157 MB MBD₃, but the much smaller command-level models (400K–530K params) still outperform it on MBD₃ by 2–3×. The UNET (153M params) sits between PLT and the command-level models on MBD₃ (195 MB) but has the highest parameter count, highlighting the efficiency advantage of the command-level approach. In the unseen-ransomware benchmark (Section 6), all models degrade on out-of-distribution variants, but the sequential command-level models degrade less than the tabular models, suggesting better generalization to novel ransomware families.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in storage-level ransomware detection by introducing command-level labeling, a granularity absent from all prior public datasets (RanSAP, DeftPunk, RanSMAP) that label only at the stream level. Conceptually, it extends the I/O-trace detection lineage (Redemption, SSD-insider, MimosaFTL) by treating each NVMe command as an individually classifiable unit rather than aggregating thousands of commands into statistical chunks. The derived overlap features (OV, Δt) follow ideas from prior work [4,5], but the combination of per-command labels, 137 ransomware variants, and 1,045 TiB of traffic represents a scale and granularity jump that no prior dataset achieves. The sequential modeling approach (Transformer, LSTM) applied at the command level departs from the tabular/decision-tree paradigm dominant in the literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The command-level labeling enables tasks impossible under stream-level annotation, most notably selective data recovery (reverting only ransomware commands while preserving benign I/O), and the 82% reduction in data loss (MBD3) demonstrates practical security impact. The unseen-ransomware benchmark, built on TLSH clustering of 137 variants into 47 families, addresses a critical deployment concern that prior datasets cannot support due to their small variant counts. Key limitations include Windows-only data, absence of traffic entropy features, and collection in a controlled (emulated) environment rather than in the wild, all of which constrain generalizability. Open questions include whether the long-range sequential dependencies (evidenced by uLSTM's superiority) persist under Linux workloads or with additional feature types, and whether the command-level approach scales to real-time detection on production storage hardware.
