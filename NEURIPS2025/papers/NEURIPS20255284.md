## Problem

Decision-focused learning (DFL) for linear optimization problems is computationally expensive because gradient-based training requires solving the optimization problem with predicted parameters at every loss evaluation. This bottleneck limits scalability and makes DFL impractical for large or frequently retrained problems. The paper asks whether one can train a predictive model to produce decision-quality-optimal cost predictions without any solver calls during training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255284/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a feature vector x (e.g., 8-dimensional California housing features or 5-dimensional synthetic features) along with the fixed constraint matrix A and right-hand side vector b that define the feasible polytope. It outputs a predicted cost vector ĉ of dimension n (matching the number of LP variables). Crucially, the loss evaluation at training time requires only the ground-truth optimal solution z*(c) and its precomputed adjacent vertices — no solver call is made on ĉ, and the true cost vector c is not needed, enabling use in the incomplete information setting where only (x, z*(c)) pairs are available.

## Method

The core insight (Proposition 4.1) is that a vertex of the feasible polytope is optimal for a cost vector c if and only if c^T z ≤ c^T z_adj for all adjacent vertices z_adj. The LAVA loss exploits this by summing hinge penalties max(ĉ^T z*(c) − ĉ^T z_adj, −ε) over all adjacent vertices, penalizing the predicted cost vector whenever an adjacent vertex would be preferred over the true optimum. Adjacent vertices are precomputed once before training via simplex pivots; for degenerate vertices, the Transition Node Pivoting (TNP) rule ensures all adjacent vertices are found without exhaustive basis enumeration. Because the loss involves only dot products between ĉ and precomputed solution vectors, training is entirely solver-free, convex, and differentiable almost everywhere, enabling standard gradient-based optimization with the Adam optimizer.

## Results

On the three predict-then-optimize benchmarks, LAVA achieves normalized test regrets of 0.016±0.001 (Random LP), 0.086±0.002 (Multi-dimensional Knapsack), and 0.060±0.012 (Shortest Path). These are within 1–8% of the best solver-based methods (SPO+ and PFYL) while eliminating all solver calls during training. LAVA is Pareto-efficient on all benchmarks, meaning no other method simultaneously achieves lower training time and lower test regret.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SPO+ and PFYL (the strongest solver-based baselines), LAVA incurs a small regret penalty—e.g., 0.086 vs. 0.082 on Multi-dim. Knapsack and 0.060 vs. 0.052 on Shortest Path—but eliminates all LP solve calls during training, yielding substantial wall-clock savings (SPO+ takes 180 s and PFYL 106 s on Random LPs, while LAVA's training is dominated by a one-time adjacent-vertex precomputation). Against NCE, LAVA is substantially better on every benchmark (0.016 vs. 0.025 on Random LP; 0.086 vs. 0.159 on Knapsack; 0.060 vs. 0.151 on Shortest Path), despite NCE also being designed for efficiency. Against CaVE, which requires solving a QP per training step, LAVA again wins on all three benchmarks (0.016 vs. 0.017, 0.086 vs. 0.111, 0.060 vs. 0.080) while being fully solver-free. The gap to SPO+/PFYL widens on the highly degenerate Shortest Path benchmark, where LAVA's adjacent-vertex precomputation becomes expensive, but even there LAVA remains Pareto-efficient.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits within the surrogate-loss branch of decision-focused learning (DFL), alongside SPO+, NCE, and CaVE, but departs from all of them by eliminating solver calls entirely during training. The core geometric insight—that a vertex is optimal iff it outperforms its adjacent vertices on the feasible polytope—transforms the DFL loss into a set of simple dot-product comparisons against precomputed vertices, rather than requiring an LP or QP solve at each gradient step. The method also builds on the Transition Node Pivoting (TNP) rule from Gal and Geue [15] to handle the nontrivial problem of enumerating adjacent vertices for degenerate solutions, a line of work that had remained relatively obscure since the early 1990s.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By removing the need for solver calls during training, LAVA addresses the primary scalability bottleneck of DFL for linear programs, making it practical for larger instances and for hyperparameter tuning where adjacent vertices are computed once and reused. The method works in both complete and incomplete information settings and scales favorably with the number of constraints. Key limitations include degraded performance on highly degenerate problems (e.g., network flow / shortest path), where precomputing adjacent vertices becomes expensive, and the reliance on LP relaxation for integer problems, which may be conservative. Open directions include more principled extensions to MILPs, leveraging ground-truth cost vectors when available, and problem-specific heuristics for degenerate vertex enumeration.
