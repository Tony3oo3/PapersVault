## Problem

Existing learning-based preconditioners built on GNNs inherit the triangular-solve structure of Incomplete Cholesky factorization, which is inherently sequential and poorly parallelizable on GPUs, while also imposing long-range dependencies along the elimination tree that local message-passing GNNs cannot effectively capture. Traditional SPAI preconditioners are GPU-friendly (requiring only SpMV per CG step) but rely on sequential construction algorithms and often yield suboptimal spectral approximations. The paper asks whether a GNN can directly learn a high-quality SPAI preconditioner that is both GPU-parallel and well-suited to local graph propagation.

## Input / Output

The GNN consumes the sparse SPD matrix A as a graph: node features encode geometric/physical properties (e.g., 3D position and density for PDE problems, position and boundary indicator for hyperelasticity, or row-average for synthetic matrices), and edge features carry the corresponding nonzero entries or b×b blocks A_ij. The output is a sparse matrix G sharing A's sparsity pattern, from which the preconditioner M^{-1} = GG^T + εI is assembled; applying it at each CG iteration requires only two sparse matrix-vector products and a vector addition. The framework supports blocked matrices (nb×nb with block size b) and the GNN has ~24k parameters with 4 message-passing layers and hidden dimension 24.

## Method

The core contribution is a GNN (encoder–processor–decoder, adapted from Gilmer et al.) that predicts the entries of a sparse factor G such that M^{-1} = GG^T + εI approximates A^{-1}, replacing the sequential SPAI construction with a single differentiable forward pass. The key architectural insight is that the SPAI application s_j depends only on the two-hop neighborhood of node j (mediated through intermediate nodes l), which naturally aligns with GNN message passing, whereas IC's forward/backward substitution propagates information globally along the elimination tree. For training, the paper introduces the Scale-invariant Aligned Identity (SAI) loss, which normalizes A by its mean nonzero-entry magnitude before computing ‖(A/‖A‖)M^{-1} − I‖_F² via a stochastic trace estimator, making the objective invariant to the absolute scale of A and matching CG's scale-invariant convergence behavior. This eliminates the need for ground-truth solution vectors in the dataset and enables robust training across matrices of varying magnitudes. The GNN is trained for 500 epochs with AdamW on a single A100 GPU, and the resulting preconditioner is applied in a standard PCG loop where each iteration costs two SpMV operations plus fixed CG overhead.

## Results

On GPU, the proposed GNN-based SPAI preconditioner achieves total CG solve times of 36 ms (Heat), 26 ms (Poisson), 51 ms (Hyperelasticity), and 253 ms (Synthetic) at rtol=10⁻⁸, outperforming all traditional baselines by 68%–113% in relative time savings. The method requires only ~197 iterations on the Heat problem (vs. 520 for Diagonal and 204 for IC) while maintaining a construction cost of just 0.18 ms—comparable to the Diagonal preconditioner. On the Synthetic System, the advantage is most dramatic: 253 ms vs. 5024 ms for traditional AINV (a 20× speedup). The SAI loss further reduces iterations on the hardest problem (Synthetic) from 2110 (L₂ loss) to 1122, a 47% reduction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the three traditional GPU baselines, the proposed method wins on all four datasets: it beats Diagonal by 2.1×–20× in total time (Diagonal has the lowest construction cost but requires 2–25× more iterations), beats IC by 1.7×–5.5× (IC matches iteration counts on Heat but its triangular solves cost 0.80 ms/iteration on GPU vs. 0.04 ms for the proposed SpMV-based application), and beats traditional AINV by 1.4×–20× (AINV's 19 ms construction cost and poor spectral approximation make it the weakest baseline). Against prior learning-based methods, the proposed approach outperforms Neural PCG [Li et al., 4] on GPU (26 ms vs. 51 ms on their dataset) and Neural IF [Häusner et al., 5] by 7.9× on GPU (132 ms vs. 1040 ms), with the gap widening under out-of-distribution conditions (167 ms vs. 268 ms for Neural PCG; 168 ms vs. 1222 ms for Neural IF). Against AMG (AMGX), the proposed method loses on simple Heat/Poisson problems (AMG+CG: 20 ms vs. 36 ms on Heat) but wins decisively on Hyperelasticity (51 ms vs. 286 ms) and Synthetic (253 ms vs. non-convergent within 10 s), where AMG's parameter sensitivity becomes a bottleneck.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the classical Sparse Approximate Inverse (SPAI) preconditioner framework (Benzi 1996; Kolotilina & Yeremin 1993) by replacing its sequential construction algorithm with a message-passing GNN adapted from Gilmer et al. (2017). It deliberately departs from the dominant line of learning-based preconditioners (Li et al. 2023; Häusner et al. 2024; Trifonov et al. 2025), which train GNNs to predict Incomplete Cholesky factors and inherit the sequential triangular-solve bottleneck. The key conceptual shift is recognizing that SPAI's two-hop locality in the preconditioner application naturally matches GNN message passing, whereas IC's elimination-tree propagation creates long-range dependencies that are ill-suited to local aggregation. The SAI loss further distinguishes this work by eliminating the need for ground-truth solution vectors during training and enforcing scale invariance aligned with CG's convergence theory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper closes a practical gap: IC preconditioners, while spectrally strong, are bottlenecked by triangular solves on GPUs, and prior learning-based methods inherited this limitation. By restricting the preconditioner to SpMV operations, the approach achieves 40–53% wall-clock reductions on GPUs with a tiny 24k-parameter model, making learned preconditioning viable in GPU-native scientific computing pipelines. Open questions include whether dynamic sparsity patterns (beyond matching A's structure) could further improve condition numbers, whether the framework extends to non-SPD systems (GMRES) or multigrid smoothers, and how it scales to multi-GPU settings. The fixed sparsity pattern constraint and single-GPU memory limit are acknowledged limitations that bound near-term applicability.
