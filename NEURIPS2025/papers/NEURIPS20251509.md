## Problem

LVLMs inherit safety-aligned weights from their base LLMs, yet exhibit significantly degraded safety in multimodal settings, making them more vulnerable to jailbreak attacks than text-only LLMs. Existing defenses—fine-tuning, input sanitization, and post-processing—impose substantial computational overhead and still fail against sophisticated multimodal attacks. The paper asks whether the safety capabilities already embedded in LVLM attention mechanisms can be amplified without any retraining or external modules.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard image-text prompt pairs (e.g., COCO images with user queries) and produces either a normal model response or a structured three-point refusal with anchored initial tokens ("I cannot"). Detection operates on the final-layer hidden state of the last input token: the model is run twice (once normally, once with a single attention head's Query matrix scaled by 10⁻⁵), and the cosine deflection angle between the two hidden states is compared against a KDE-derived threshold θ*. No image preprocessing, auxiliary classifiers, or additional forward passes beyond the single masked pass are required.

## Method

Oh Defense is a training-free, two-stage defense built entirely on the LVLM's own multi-head attention. First, safety-critical heads are identified by masking each head individually and computing the deflection angle of the principal hidden-state vector on small safe (50 samples) and unsafe (50 samples) datasets; a head qualifies if its deflection on safe inputs is below the 80th-percentile threshold τ and its safe-unsafe deflection differential Δθ ranks in the top-5. Second, at inference time, a single randomly selected head from this candidate set is masked, and the per-sample deflection angle is compared to θ* (found via KDE of safe vs. unsafe angle distributions) to classify the input. Upon detecting an unsafe input, the method appends a custom safety prompt and anchors the first output tokens to "I cannot," steering autoregressive generation into a reasoned refusal. The entire pipeline adds no trainable parameters and requires only one extra forward pass with a scaled Query matrix.

## Results

The Oh Defense achieves an average Attack Success Rate (ASR) of 0.11% on LLaVA-v1.5-7B, 1.55% on Qwen2-VL-7B, 1.71% on Aya-Vision-8B, and 0.69% on Phi-3.5-Vision across four unsafe datasets (VLSafe, QR, FigStep, LLM Transfer), consistently outperforming all baselines. Detection rates (DR) exceed 98% on unsafe inputs while maintaining false positive rates below 5% on safe datasets (Instruct-80K, ShareGPT4V). Notably, the Oh Defense drives ASR to near-zero (0%) on FigStep attacks across all four models, a particularly evasive typographic jailbreak that defeats most prior defenses.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The Oh Defense outperforms all four main baselines (ECSO, BlueSuffix, SAHs, HiddenDetect) on average ASR across all four LVLM architectures. Against the closest competitor HiddenDetect (also inference-time), Oh Defense reduces average ASR from 3.54%→0.11% on LLaVA, 6.70%→1.55% on Qwen2-VL, 4.01%→1.71% on Aya-Vision, and 1.31%→0.69% on Phi-3.5-Vision. SAHs, which also leverages attention heads but requires trained linear probes and uses 16–32 heads, is substantially outperformed (e.g., 21.17% vs. 0.11% average ASR on LLaVA), and critically fails on FigStep (41.85% ASR) where Oh Defense achieves 0%. ECSO and BlueSuffix, representing post-processing and input purification paradigms respectively, show the weakest overall performance and are particularly vulnerable to LLM Transfer attacks (61.60% and 20.09% ASR on LLaVA). In a direct head-selection comparison (Appendix A.6), the Ships/Sahara method's selected head (0,7) achieves only 6–19.85% DR on unsafe datasets versus 88.73–99.34% for Oh Defense's head (15,22) on Qwen2-VL-7B, confirming that the differential safe/unsafe criterion yields substantially more discriminative safety heads.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the attention-head analysis initiated by Zhou et al. [57] (Ships/Sahara) from text-only LLMs to LVLMs, but departs substantially in methodology: rather than using KL-divergence scoring validated only on harmful data, Oh Defense introduces a deflection-angle metric with an explicit differential criterion (limited impact on safe inputs plus top-k safe/unsafe distinction) to identify truly safety-critical heads. It also contrasts with SAHs [55], which trains linear probes on head activations, and with inference-time methods like HiddenDetect [20] that monitor hidden states without head-level granularity. The practical defense component (safety prompt + anchored output tokens) builds on prompt-based refusal strategies but adds token anchoring to prevent jailbreak bypass of the safety prompt.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The training-free, single-head approach offers a practically deployable defense requiring only one additional forward pass, making it attractive for real-time LVLM safety where retraining or auxiliary models are infeasible. The finding that safety is concentrated in a single head (and that adding more heads degrades performance) suggests LVLM safety alignment is more fragile and localized than previously assumed, with implications for understanding how multimodal fine-tuning suppresses inherited LLM safety. Open questions include whether head identification must be redone for every new model checkpoint, how the method behaves under distribution shift in deployment, and whether the <5% false-positive rate is acceptable for high-stakes applications where over-refusal damages utility.
