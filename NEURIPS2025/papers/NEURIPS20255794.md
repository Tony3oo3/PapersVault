## Problem

In multi-objective optimization, selecting the most preferred solution from the Pareto set is a fundamental but under-theorized problem: the Pareto set is implicitly defined, non-smooth, and non-convex even when all objectives are strongly convex quadratics, and no prior notion of stationarity is both necessary for preference optimality and verifiable from local information. This paper asks how to define a meaningful optimality/stationarity concept on the Pareto set and how to efficiently approximate it, including in the practically important setting where the preference function is only accessible through noisy pairwise comparisons (as in RLHF).

## Input / Output

The method takes n strongly convex, L-smooth objective functions F = (f_1, …, f_n) over R^d and a preference function f_0 (or, in the dueling-feedback variant, a comparison oracle returning noisy binary preferences between pairs of decision vectors under a Bradley-Terry-style link function). It outputs a decision vector x and scalarization weights β ∈ Δ^{n-1} that are (ε_0, ε)-preference stationary. A key structural detail is that the constraint set is never explicitly parameterized: the Pareto set is recovered implicitly as the projection of the Pareto manifold, and the algorithm only ever queries local gradient/Hessian information at the current iterate rather than solving for the exact Pareto point x_β.

## Method

The central insight is to lift the problem from the non-smooth Pareto set in R^d to the Pareto manifold P(F) ⊂ R^d × Δ^{n-1}, which is a smooth submanifold diffeomorphic to the (n−1)-simplex via the map β ↦ (x_β, β) where x_β = argmin f_β. This reduces the Pareto-constrained optimization to a smooth problem over the convex simplex: min_β (f_0 ∘ x*)(β). The algorithm, Pareto Majorization-Minimization (PMM), iteratively constructs a quadratic majorizing surrogate for f_0 ∘ x* using a local estimator ∇̂x*(x,β) = −∇²f_β(x)⁻¹∇F(x)ᵀ (which converges to the true ∇x*(β) as x → x_β), minimizes the surrogate over the simplex to update β, and then solves the strongly convex scalarized subproblem to update x. For dueling feedback, ∇f_0 is estimated via zeroth-order comparisons along random unit directions with a clipped Bernoulli inversion. The paper proves O(ε_0^{-2}) last-iterate convergence to an (ε_0, ε)-preference stationary point under both first-order and dueling feedback, and shows that any first-order-only stationarity condition that is necessary for preference optimality must be trivial.

## Results

The paper provides the first finite-time convergence guarantee for Pareto-constrained optimization. Theorem 10 establishes that PMM with first-order preference feedback reaches an (ε₀, ε)-preference stationary point in K ≤ 2μ_g(f* − f_*)/(c₁²·ε₀²) iterations, i.e., O(ε₀⁻²) iteration complexity (dimension-independent). Theorem 11 extends this to the dueling feedback setting, also achieving K = O(ε₀⁻²) in expectation, with the dueling gradient estimator requiring m ≍ d⁴ log(d/ε₀)/ε₀⁴ samples per batch and b ≍ d/ε₀² batches. The rate is dimension-independent for first-order feedback, though dueling feedback incurs unavoidable dimension dependence in the sample complexity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its PMM algorithm against Pareto Navigating Gradient (PNG) from Ye and Liu (2022), the only prior first-order method for this problem. The comparison is both theoretical and visual: Figure 2 shows that on two strongly-convex quadratic instances, PMM (orange) converges to the ground-truth preference-optimal solution (black square) while PNG (blue) converges to a suboptimal point. More fundamentally, Appendix D.1 (Example D.1) constructs an explicit counterexample where the unique preference-optimal point is the origin, yet the (c,ε)-PNG stationary point is bounded away from it and converges to e₁ as ε→0. The paper further proves (Proposition D.1) that any first-order stationarity condition that is necessary for preference optimality must be trivial—it must accept all preference-generic gradient configurations—explaining why PNG's first-order condition cannot be both non-trivial and necessary. PMM overcomes this by leveraging second-order information (the Hessian of the scalarized objective) through the Pareto manifold structure, which is essential for a meaningful stationarity notion.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the majorization-minimization framework (Lange et al., 2000; Marumo et al., 2023) to handle the implicit constraint structure of the Pareto set, and extends the classical optimization-over-the-efficient-set problem (Bolintinéanu, 1993b; Ye and Liu, 2022) from restricted settings (linear preferences, linear objectives) to general nonlinear, nonconvex preference functions. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of the Pareto manifold—a smooth lifting of the Pareto set into R^d × Δ^{n-1} that is diffeomorphic to the simplex—which resolves the non-smoothness and non-convexity that plague the projected Pareto set. The dueling feedback extension draws on the Bradley-Terry preference model and zeroth-order gradient estimation techniques from the RLHF and derivative-free optimization literatures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to provide a geometrically meaningful, verifiable notion of (approximate) preference stationarity for the general nonlinear setting, along with the first finite-time convergence guarantee O(ε_0^{-2}) for Pareto-constrained optimization. The result that prior stationarity conditions (e.g., Ye and Liu, 2022) are not necessary for preference optimality—meaning existing algorithms can provably avoid optimal points—is a significant correction to the field. Key limitations include the strong convexity requirement on objectives (which excludes many practical ML settings) and dimension-dependent sample complexity under dueling feedback. Open directions include extending to nonconvex objectives, scaling to many objectives, and incorporating deterministic dueling feedback for practical RLHF deployment.
