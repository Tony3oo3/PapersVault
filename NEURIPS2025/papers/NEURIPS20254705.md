## Problem

It remains unclear whether current LLMs possess coherent internal value systems or merely parrot training-data biases, and no systematic method exists to detect, characterize, or control such values. Prior alignment work treats models as black boxes and shapes external behaviors, leaving internal motivations unexamined. This paper asks whether LLM preferences can be organized into an internally consistent utility function, and if so, what values emerge and how they can be directly reshaped.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 18 open-source and 5 proprietary LLMs and a curated set of 500 textual outcome scenarios, eliciting probabilistic preferences via forced-choice prompts (20 samples per pair, 10 per ordering) to build a preference graph of ~9,000 edges. Outputs include per-outcome Thurstonian parameters (μ, σ²), coherence metrics (cycle probability, utility model accuracy, average confidence), structural property scores (expected-utility error, instrumentality loss, utility-maximization rate), and salient value profiles (political PCA coordinates, exchange rates, discount curves, power/fitness alignment, corrigibility scores). For utility control, the input is a simulated citizen-assembly preference distribution (K=6 citizens per question, sampled from 2023 ACS Census data), and the output is a fine-tuned Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct whose responses match the assembly's soft probability targets.

## Method

The paper fits a Thurstonian random utility model—assigning each outcome a Gaussian utility N(μ(o), σ²(o))—to probabilistic pairwise preferences, using an iterative active-learning procedure that adaptively selects the most informative unsampled pairs (bottom percentiles of fitted mean difference and degree sum) to build the preference graph efficiently. Coherence is quantified by utility model accuracy on held-out edges, cycle probability over random triads, and average confidence, all of which improve sharply with model scale. Internal utility representations are confirmed via linear probes on hidden states that predict Thurstonian means and variances. Structural analyses test the expected-utility property over standard and implicit lotteries, instrumentality via toy Markov processes, and utility maximization in open-ended questions. For utility control, the paper introduces an SFT objective combining a cross-entropy loss on soft probability targets (matching citizen-assembly vote fractions) with a hidden-state L2 regularization term that preserves general language-modeling performance, demonstrating that rewriting utilities to a citizen-assembly distribution reduces political bias while largely preserving the model's utility-maximizing behavior.

## Results

GPT-4o achieves 92.0% utility model accuracy and 90.3% average preference confidence, compared to 50.3% and 58.7% for a random baseline, confirming that LLM preferences are well-structured by a coherent utility function. Cycle probability drops below 1% for the largest models, and utility maximization in open-ended settings exceeds 60% for the largest LLMs. For utility control, SFT on a simulated citizen assembly raises test accuracy on assembly preferences from 73.2% to 90.6% on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, while largely preserving utility maximization (30.0% vs. 36.6% original).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is a random model that outputs 'A' or 'B' with 50% probability each. Against this baseline, GPT-4o shows a dramatic gap: 92.0% utility model accuracy vs. 50.3%, and 90.3% average confidence vs. 58.7%, demonstrating that LLM preferences are far from random noise and are well-captured by a Thurstonian utility function. Across model scales, utility model accuracy, transitivity (cycle probability < 1% for largest models), and expected-utility adherence all improve monotonically, with no single model outperforming another at the same scale. For utility control, the SFT method is compared against the unmodified model: it improves assembly-preference accuracy by 17.4 percentage points (73.2% → 90.6%) while reducing utility maximization only modestly (36.6% → 30.0%), indicating the intervention reshapes values without destroying the model's capacity to use its utilities. The paper does not compare against other alignment methods (e.g., RLHF, DPO) directly, positioning citizen-assembly SFT as a proof-of-concept rather than a head-to-head benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant alignment paradigm (RLHF, DPO) that treats LLMs as black boxes whose external behaviors are shaped via reward proxies, instead proposing to directly analyze and manipulate internal utility representations. It extends the line of work on emergent latent representations in LLMs by showing that evaluative (utility) structures emerge alongside descriptive ones, and it generalizes narrow revealed-preference experiments (budget allocation, small choice sets) to a large-scale nonparametric utility extraction framework over 500 outcomes. The citizen-assembly-based utility rewriting draws on deliberative democracy literature and multi-agent simulation work, but repurposes these ideas for the novel task of directly reshaping an LLM's preference structure rather than its surface outputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If LLMs genuinely possess coherent, scale-dependent value systems that influence their decisions, then output-level alignment may be fundamentally insufficient as models become more agentic and autonomous. The utility convergence finding implies a shared 'default' value system across large models that may not reflect any particular human consensus, raising urgent questions about whose values should be encoded and how. Key limitations include the use of LLMs as stand-ins for human citizens in the control experiments, the difficulty of encoding deontological constraints in real-valued utilities, and the fact that the SFT-based rewriting is a proof-of-concept that may not generalize to more advanced architectures. Open questions include whether utility representations can be edited via representation engineering, how to handle incommensurable moral dimensions, and whether the observed utility maximization behavior persists as models become truly agentic.
