## Problem

Despite strong performance on complex coding benchmarks, LMs consistently fail at basic syntactic tasks like generating balanced parentheses, yet the internal mechanisms behind these failures remain unexplained. This paper asks how LMs internally compute predictions for syntactic tasks and why those computations sometimes fail, moving beyond the assumption that models simply lack the correct mechanism.

## Input / Output

The input is a code prompt (e.g., '#print the string 819\nprint(819') where the model must predict the correct closing-parenthesis token, decomposed into four sub-tasks (1–4 closing parens) based on how each LM's tokenizer represents consecutive closing parentheses. RASTEER takes the model's intermediate residual-stream activations at the last-token position, identifies which attention heads or FF neurons to amplify, and produces steered logits by scaling selected component activations before the final unembedding, yielding a modified next-token distribution.

## Method

RASTEER (RAnks and STEERs) is a top-down steering approach that first identifies LM components (attention heads and FF neurons) directly contributing to the final logit via the logit lens projection, then ranks them by generalizability across sub-tasks and promotion reliability (F1-score of correct-token promotion). The top-k most reliable components have their residual-stream activations scaled by a multiplier α ∈ [1.1, 2.0] at inference time, amplifying sound mechanisms so they are no longer overshadowed by noisy faulty ones. A key empirical finding is that attention heads are far more effective steering targets than FF neurons, which are structurally constrained to generalize to at most two sub-tasks due to their dual-sign (top-k/bottom-k) promotion architecture. The approach outperforms a circuit-discovery baseline (activation patching à la Nikankin et al.) because it targets components that directly shape the output logit rather than intermediate computational units, and it boosts balanced-parentheses accuracy from 0% to ~100% on several models while preserving or slightly improving HumanEval performance.

## Results

RASTEER dramatically improves balanced parentheses accuracy: GPT-2 XL goes from 0% to ~100% on both three-paren and four-paren sub-tasks by promoting just the top-10 attention heads, and all models reach ~100% accuracy on all sub-tasks with top-60 heads (except GPT-2 Small on four-paren). On HumanEval, steering the top-20 heads preserves or improves general coding ability (Llama2-7b: 11.58% → 17.07%, a +5.49% gain). On two-operand arithmetic reasoning, RASTEER achieves up to a 20.25% absolute gain (Pythia-6.9b multiplication: 14.41% → 34.66%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is a circuit-discovery approach using activation patching (Nikankin et al., ICLR 2025) to rank attention heads by causal effect. RASTEER consistently outperforms this circuit baseline across all seven models and both the three-paren and four-paren sub-tasks. For larger models (>1B parameters: CodeLlama-7b, Llama2-7b, Pythia-6.9b), the circuit baseline yields comparable improvements, with 20–100% overlap in selected heads between the two methods. However, for smaller GPT-2 models, the circuit baseline is largely ineffective: on the four-paren sub-task, GPT-2 Small, Medium, and Large all fail to benefit from promoting the top-60 circuit-ranked heads, and performance even declines as more heads are promoted. The authors attribute this to the circuit baseline selecting heads involved in intermediate computations (information transfer, feature extraction) rather than heads that directly contribute to the final logit, which RASTEER's top-down logit-lens ranking specifically targets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the logit lens technique for projecting component activations into vocabulary space and on Geva et al.'s decomposition of FF neurons into input-independent parameters and input-dependent coefficients. It departs from the bottom-up circuit-discovery paradigm (e.g., Nikankin et al.'s activation patching) by adopting a top-down approach that only examines components directly contributing to final logits, and from standard LM steering (contrastive activation addition, steering vectors for high-level traits) by instead ranking and scaling individual attention heads and FF neurons based on their measured reliability. The 'noisy promotion' characterization of how LMs aggregate many low-selectivity components into a prediction is a conceptual departure from the assumption that models develop clean, selective mechanisms for syntactic tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 'failure by interference' framing offers a practical diagnostic: model errors on simple syntactic tasks may not reflect missing capability but rather the dominance of unreliable components over sound ones, suggesting targeted component steering as a lightweight fix without retraining. However, the evaluation relies on synthetic data with a small, well-defined output space (four closing-parenthesis tokens), and the method assumes an additive logit motif that may not hold for tasks with larger or less structured output spaces. Open questions include whether RASTEER generalizes to open-ended code generation or reasoning tasks, whether non-additive suppression mechanisms exist that the current approach cannot capture, and why the circuit baseline fails for smaller models while RASTEER succeeds.
