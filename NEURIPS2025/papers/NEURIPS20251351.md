## Problem

Traditional SNN training methods either rely on biologically implausible backpropagation or on hand-designed, fixed plasticity rules (e.g., STDP, BCM) that cannot adapt to changing environments or multi-task demands. The paper argues that the core limitation is the rigid application of observed biological plasticity mechanisms without abstraction, and proposes instead that networks should learn the plasticity rules themselves as the primary object of optimization, rather than directly adjusting synaptic weights.

## Input / Output

For the working-memory task, the network receives a 3D input vector per timestep encoding a binary stimulus sequence, a delay indicator, and a reproduction cue, and outputs a scalar prediction of the original stimulus after a variable delay. For multi-task RL (Brax continuous control), the network receives environment sensor readings concatenated with a task-goal observation (target direction, speed, or position) and outputs continuous motor actions. A critical design choice: during testing, plasticity-trained agents have their synaptic weights reset to zero while their learned plasticity parameters (A, B, C, D) remain fixed, forcing the network to reconstruct its function purely through local plasticity dynamics rather than stored weights.

## Method

The Plasticity-Driven Learning Framework (PDLF) replaces direct weight optimization with a parametric, per-synapse plasticity rule that is a two-component expansion of the Spiking BCM rule: Synaptic Cooperation Plasticity (SCP), A_ij·x_i·(x_j − C_ij), which modulates weight change by the joint pre- and post-synaptic spike traces, and Presynaptic-Dependent Plasticity (PDP), B_ij·x_j + D_ij, which adds a pre-synaptic-driven term with a stabilizing bias. All four parameters (A, B, C, D) are learnable per synapse, allowing each connection to develop its own heterogeneous plasticity profile. These parameters are optimized via Parameter-Exploring Policy Gradients (PEPG), an evolutionary strategy that maintains a population of agents with perturbed plasticity parameters and selects for fitness across multiple tasks simultaneously. The key distinction from prior work is that plasticity is the central object of learning rather than a fixed mechanism for weight updates, enabling the network to encode information in synaptic weights during the delay phase of working memory, adapt dynamically across conflicting tasks, and generalize to unseen directions, speeds, and even turning trajectories without additional training.

## Results

On six continuous-control RL tasks (ant_dir, swimmer_dir, halfcheetah_vel, hopper_vel, ur5e, fetch), PDLF (SCP+PDP) achieves 6904±801 on ant_dir and 10531±827 on swimmer_dir, compared to only 1069±98 and 31±8 for directly trained weights—a 6.5× and 339× improvement respectively. PDLF also outperforms the Meta-Hebb ANN baseline on all four overlapping tasks, e.g., 6904 vs. 5646 on ant_dir and 10531 vs. 7605 on swimmer_dir. In the working-memory copying task, PDLF-based SNNs show faster convergence, longer memory retention, and lower firing rates than directly trained SNNs. Ablation confirms both SCP and PDP are essential: removing PDP causes complete divergence due to loss of the equilibrium mechanism.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares PDLF against two baselines: (1) direct weight optimization via PEPG evolutionary strategy, and (2) Meta-Hebb [Najarro & Risi, NeurIPS 2020], a state-of-the-art ANN meta-learning method that also learns plasticity rules. Against direct weight training, PDLF wins decisively on all six tasks—most dramatically on swimmer_dir (10531 vs. 31, a ~339× gap) and ur5e (86 vs. 7, a ~12× gap)—while directly trained agents collapse to near-chance or trivial solutions like maintaining immobility. Against Meta-Hebb, PDLF (SNN) outperforms the ANN baseline on all four overlapping tasks: ant_dir 6904 vs. 5646 (+22%), swimmer_dir 10531 vs. 7605 (+38%), halfcheetah_vel −549 vs. −735 (+25% closer to zero), and hopper_vel 869 vs. 794 (+9%). Notably, PDLF additionally generalizes to unseen directions and speeds (72 test directions vs. 8 training directions) and recovers from complete synaptic weight resets, capabilities that neither baseline demonstrates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Spiking BCM rule (Bekolay et al., 2013) as its mathematical foundation, expanding it into a parametric framework with two learnable components: Synaptic Cooperation Plasticity (SCP) and Presynaptic-Dependent Plasticity (PDP). It departs from prior SNN training paradigms—surrogate gradient methods, ANN-to-SNN conversion, and fixed-rule plasticity (STDP, three-factor rules)—by treating the plasticity rule itself as the object of optimization rather than a fixed mechanism for weight updates. Unlike differentiable plasticity and neuromodulated plasticity approaches that maintain a separation between weight and plasticity-rule optimization, PDLF unifies them: the network's synaptic weights are shaped entirely by the learned plasticity dynamics at inference time, while an evolutionary strategy (PEPG) optimizes the plasticity parameters offline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that learning plasticity rules rather than directly optimizing weights yields meaningful gains in working memory capacity, multi-task RL performance, and generalization to unseen task parameters in SNNs, and notably enables recovery from complete synaptic weight loss—a property with implications for robust, deployable neuromorphic systems. However, the experimental scope is limited to small-scale networks (128 hidden neurons) and six Brax continuous-control tasks, and the reliance on evolutionary strategies for training raises scalability questions. Open questions include whether PDLF generalizes to larger, deeper architectures, whether gradient-based or hybrid optimization can replace ES for plasticity parameter learning, and how the framework performs in substantially more diverse or high-dimensional environments.
