## Problem

Prior work on fairness in matching under biased evaluations (e.g., Celis et al., ICML 2024) assumes a centralized model where all institutions evaluate candidates identically. In practice, institutions rely on overlapping but independently processed signals, producing inter-institutional correlation that compounds with group bias to skew allocation outcomes. This paper asks how group-dependent bias and inter-institutional correlation jointly determine group-level representation in a decentralized two-institution stable matching market.

## Input / Output

The model takes two i.i.d. uniform [0,1] latent attributes per candidate, a multiplicative bias parameter β ∈ (0,1] applied to the disadvantaged group, a correlation parameter γ ∈ [0,1] controlling the convex combination of attributes used by Institution 2, and symmetric institutional capacities c < 1/2. Outputs are deterministic equilibrium thresholds s₁* and s₂*(γ) in the n→∞ continuum limit, a piecewise closed-form representation ratio R(β,γ) partitioned into four regimes by analytically derived γ-thresholds, a normalized fairness metric N(β,γ), and a Pareto frontier of (β, γ) pairs achieving a target fairness level τ. The analysis is purely analytical (no training or data fitting); finite-n validation uses 30 Monte Carlo trials at n = 100 and 250.

## Method

The paper models a two-institution deferred-acceptance market where Institution 1 ranks by v₁ and Institution 2 ranks by γv₁ + (1−γ)v₂, with group G₂ scores multiplicatively downscaled by β. In the continuum limit, the unique stable matching is governed by two capacity equations whose solution (s₁*, s₂*) is derived via a regime-reduction technique: the 16 possible geometric case pairs for the decision boundaries of the two groups collapse into exactly four contiguous γ-regimes separated by three closed-form thresholds γ₁, γ₂, γ₃ (Theorem 3.1). Within each regime, s₂*(γ) satisfies a specific algebraic equation (Theorem 3.2), and the representation ratio R(β,γ) admits a piecewise closed-form expression (Theorem 3.3). A key structural result is that although s₂*(γ) is unimodal (non-monotone), R(β,γ) and its normalized variant N(β,γ) are monotonically increasing in both β and γ (Corollary 4.1, Theorem F.2), enabling Pareto-frontier-based intervention planning: given current parameters (β₀, γ₀) and a fairness target τ, the designer can read off the minimal adjustment to either parameter needed to achieve N(β,γ) ≥ τ.

## Results

The paper derives a piecewise closed-form expression for the representation ratio R(β,γ) across four γ-regimes separated by analytically derived thresholds γ₁, γ₂, γ₃ (Theorem 3.3). The headline finding is that R(β,γ) increases monotonically in γ (Corollary 4.1), but the dependence on β shifts from linear (when γ≈1, matching the prior centralized result of [15]) to quadratic (when γ≤γ₁), meaning even modest losses in evaluator correlation cause sharp drops in representation. In the highly selective regime (c small, β≈1−c), R drops from ≈1/3 at γ=1 to ≈c/4 at γ≤γ₁. Finite-sample validation (Table 2, c=0.2, β=0.9) confirms the continuum predictions: representation ratios range from ~0.44 to ~0.73 for n=100 and ~0.41 to ~0.67 for n=250, with standard deviations decreasing as n grows.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper with no empirical baselines; the primary comparison is against the centralized matching model of Celis et al. [15] (ICML 2024), which assumes both institutions evaluate candidates identically (γ=1). Under that model, the representation ratio R(β,1) scales approximately linearly with β. The present paper shows that this linear relationship breaks down sharply once evaluator correlation drops: for γ≤γ₁, R(β,γ)≈β² (quadratic decay), and in the highly selective regime the gap between R(1−c,1)≈1/3 and R(1−c,γ₁)≈c/4 can be an order of magnitude. The paper also contrasts with [12] (Castera et al., EC 2022), which studies correlation-induced statistical discrimination without explicit group bias, and with [57,58] (Peng & Garg), which analyze noise correlation in matching but without stability constraints or explicit bias parameters. The key structural contribution over all prior work is the joint characterization of how bias β and correlation γ interact through the γ-thresholds γ₁, γ₂, γ₃, revealing that the two parameters compound rather than act independently on representation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the centralized biased-evaluation matching model of Celis et al. (ICML 2024) [15], which assumed all institutions evaluate candidates identically, to a decentralized two-institution setting where evaluations are partially correlated via a convex-combination parameter γ. It also connects to the line of work on inter-institutional correlation in stable matchings (Castera et al. [12], Arnosti [4]) and to the optimization-based bias model of Celis et al. (NeurIPS 2024) [14], but is the first to jointly analyze group-dependent multiplicative bias and evaluator correlation within a single stable-matching framework. The mean-field/continuum-limit technique for characterizing equilibrium thresholds follows the standard approach of Azevedo & Leshno [7] and Arnosti [4].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first closed-form characterization of how bias and evaluator correlation jointly determine group representation in decentralized matching, identifying critical γ-thresholds where selection behavior undergoes discrete regime transitions. The Pareto-frontier framework for intervention planning (choosing between bias reduction vs. evaluator alignment to hit a fairness target) offers a concrete design tool for system operators. Key limitations include the restriction to two institutions, uniform attribute distributions, and the symmetric capacity case; the authors note that strategic behavior, dynamic multi-cycle effects, and strong attribute dependence remain open. The finding that structural phase transitions persist even at β=1 (no bias) suggests evaluator disagreement alone can generate nontrivial fairness losses, a point with broader implications for multi-institution selection design.
