## Problem

Existing survival calibration methods rely on heuristic binning (D-cal, X-cal), Kaplan-Meier estimation (SFM, CSD), or bootstrap sampling (CSD-iPOT), which limits adaptability to continuous-time settings, introduces variance in tail regions, and often degrades discriminative performance. The paper addresses this by introducing a bin-free, sampling-free post-hoc calibration framework grounded in the Kolmogorov-Smirnov metric, which provides a global, data-adaptive measure of calibration error without discretization.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-computed CDF values F̂_θ(Y_i|z_i) from any trained survival model, along with censoring indicators δ_i and observed times Y_i, and uses a held-out validation set to optimize three scalar parameters (a, b, α). It outputs a transformed CDF F̂*_θ = {G^{-1}(a·G(F̂_θ)+b)}^α evaluated at the same time points, where G is a logit link function. The constraint a>0 and α>0 ensures the transformation is strictly monotone, preserving the ordering of CDF values and thus the time-independent C-index.

## Method

KSP applies a three-parameter parametric transformation to the estimated CDF, F̂*_θ = {G^{-1}(a·G(F̂_θ)+b)}^α, where G is a strictly monotone increasing link function (logit by default), and optimizes (a, b, α) via ADAM gradient descent to minimize the KS-cal metric on a validation set. KS-cal is defined as sup_x |F̃(x) − x|, where F̃(x) is a censored-data-adjusted empirical CDF that accounts for right-censoring through a linear interpolation term (1−δ_i)(x−U_i)/(1−U_i) at censored points. The three parameters control distinct aspects: a adjusts tail behavior, b shifts the distribution relative to the identity line, and α introduces nonlinearity. The approach is analogous to Platt scaling in classification but adapted to the censored survival setting, with computational complexity O(B·N) where B is the number of optimization iterations. The paper also provides a theoretical guarantee (Theorem 4.1) that calibration holds if and only if KS-cal converges to zero, and an in-processing variant KS-cal(k) that aggregates the top-k squared deviations as a differentiable training penalty.

## Results

Across 60 experiments (6 survival models × 10 datasets), KSP outperforms the non-calibrated baseline in approximately 70% of pairwise comparisons on calibration metrics, with statistically significant wins in 45/60 cases on KS-cal and 43/60 on D-cal(20). Against CSD, KSP wins 51/60 on KS-cal and 48/60 on D-cal(20), while CSD retains a slight C-index advantage (34 vs. 13 wins). The ablation on MIMIC-III with DeepSurv shows progressive improvement: adding the shift parameter b alone reduces D-cal(20) from 0.0645 to 0.0420 and KS-cal from 0.2509 to 0.1832, while the full (a, b, α) parameterization further reduces D-cal(20) to 0.0070 and KS-cal to 0.0911, all with negligible C-index change (0.95839 → 0.95826).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CSD (Qi et al., 2024a), KSP clearly dominates on distributional calibration metrics—winning 51/60 on KS-cal and 48/60 on D-cal(20)—but CSD retains a C-index edge (34 vs. 13 wins) due to its explicit guarantee of preserving time-independent C-index. Against CSD-iPOT (Qi et al., 2024b), KSP achieves comparable C-index (21 vs. 25 wins, with 14 ties) while substantially outperforming on all calibration metrics (46/60 on D-cal, 44/60 on KS-cal, 45/60 on KM-cal). Against the in-processing baselines X-cal (Goldstein et al., 2020) and SFM (Chapfuwa et al., 2020), evaluated on MIMIC-III with CRPS, both methods reduce calibration error as the regularization λ increases but degrade C-index; KSP achieves a better calibration–discrimination trade-off with stable performance across all metrics and no hyperparameter tuning required. Overall, KSP's key advantage is its bin-free, sampling-free design, which avoids the tail-region mismatches and quantile-crossing issues observed in CSD and CSD-iPOT, while its O(B·N) computational complexity is lower than CSD's O(N·|P|·R).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Park et al. (2025), which introduced the KS-cal metric as a bin-free calibration measure for survival models, and on the D-calibration framework of Haider et al. (2020), from which KS-cal, D-cal, and S-cal are all derived. It departs from the dominant post-processing approaches—conformal prediction methods (CSD, CSD-iPOT) that rely on KM-based quantile adjustments and bootstrap sampling, and in-processing penalty methods (X-cal, SFM) that require binning or surrogate losses—by proposing a simple parametric logit transformation with three hyperparameters that minimizes KS-cal directly. The key conceptual move is treating calibration as a global, data-adaptive optimization problem rather than a bin-wise or quantile-wise correction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

KSP offers a practical, computationally efficient alternative to conformal post-processing for survival calibration, with the advantage of preserving C-index by construction (monotone transformation) and avoiding the variance and quantile-crossing issues of sampling-based methods. The theoretical grounding (Theorem 4.1) provides a clean characterization of when KS-cal converges to zero, linking it to the standard calibration definition. Open questions include extending KSP to conditional (subgroup-level) calibration, handling datasets with heavy ties or severe skewness where performance degrades, and whether the three-parameter logit family is expressive enough for all miscalibration patterns. For practitioners deploying survival models in clinical or infrastructure settings, the method's simplicity and lack of tuning (beyond the optimization loop) make it an attractive drop-in calibration step.
