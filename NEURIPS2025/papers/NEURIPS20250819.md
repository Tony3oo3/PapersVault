## Problem

Fully-Corrective Frank-Wolfe (FCFW) achieves strong convergence and sparsity by minimizing the objective over the convex hull of the active set, but this subproblem can be as computationally hard as the original problem, making FCFW impractical for large-scale quadratic objectives. Existing corrective variants (AFW, BCG, BPCG) lack a unified framework with tight convergence guarantees and face-identification properties, and algorithms like Split Conditional Gradient (SCG) and Second-Order Conditional Gradient Sliding (SOCGS) suffer from suboptimal convergence rates or limited function-class applicability.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a convex differentiable function f (quadratic for the QC steps, i.e., f(x) = ½⟨x, Ax⟩ + ⟨b, x⟩ + c with A ⪰ 0) and accesses the compact convex feasible set X exclusively through a Linear Minimization Oracle, never requiring projections. The quadratic corrections solve a linear program or linear system whose dimension equals the active set size |S| rather than the ambient dimension n, with cached scalar products ⟨v, Aw⟩ and ⟨b, v⟩ to accelerate setup. The output is an approximate minimizer x* with a primal gap certificate, and under strict complementarity on polytopes the method identifies the optimal face and converges to an exact solution in finite time.

## Method

The paper introduces Corrective Frank-Wolfe (CFW), a unified framework where each iteration compares the local pairwise gap ⟨∇f(x_t), a_t − s_t⟩ against the global FW gap to decide between a corrective step on the active set and a standard FW step, with corrective steps required to be either descent steps (sufficient primal progress) or drop steps (active-set reduction). For quadratic objectives, two efficient corrections are designed: QC-LP enforces non-negativity of barycentric weights directly via a linear program over the affine hull, while QC-MNP solves the affine-hull linear system and applies a single Wolfe-style ratio test to project back onto the simplex, dropping at most one atom per iteration. The key relaxation replaces the intractable minimization over conv(S) with the tractable minimization over aff(S), converting the quadratic problem into a linear system of size |S|. These corrections are then applied as inner solvers within SCG (yielding an improved O(1/√t) rate) and SOCGS (enabling efficient quadratic subproblem solving and extending convergence to generalized self-concordant functions without global Lipschitz smoothness).

## Results

The paper presents convergence plots (not numerical tables) across four experimental settings. In sparse regression over the K-Sparse polytope (K∈{5,20}), both QC-LP and QC-MNP converge linearly and reach optimality significantly faster than all baselines, with the advantage more pronounced for smaller K where the optimal face is larger. In entanglement detection (a∈{0.25,0.5}), QC-MNP achieves a 'drastic initial acceleration,' solving the problem to optimality in a fraction of the iterations and wall-clock time of the other methods. For projection onto Birkhoff polytope intersections (n∈{300,500}), QC-MNP and QC-LP outperform BPCG at n=500 and match it at n=300. In SOCGS for logistic regression over the ℓ₁-ball (k∈{50,200}), QC methods show substantial improvements over BPCG once the iterate is sufficiently close to the optimum, with QC-MNP yielding more significant acceleration at the cost of longer wall-clock time per correction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares QC-LP and QC-MNP against four FW baselines (FW, AFW, PFW, BPCG) in the first two experiments, and against BPCG and the original SCG/SOCGS variants in the remaining two. FW is consistently the weakest baseline: it does not converge linearly in sparse regression and performs poorly in wall-clock time for entanglement detection due to high LMO call counts. AFW and PFW improve over FW but are still clearly outperformed by both QC methods in convergence speed. BPCG is the strongest baseline and the closest competitor; QC methods beat it in sparse regression (reaching optimality faster), entanglement detection (QC-MNP in particular), and Birkhoff projection at n=500, though QC-LP's LP is rarely feasible in the entanglement setting, causing it to fall back to pairwise steps and track BPCG's trajectory. In the SOCGS experiment, QC methods match AFW for longer inner runs (k=200) but outperform both BPCG and AFW for shorter runs (k=50), with QC-MNP performing more corrections per interval than QC-LP at the cost of additional wall-clock time.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies a family of active-set-based Frank-Wolfe variants—AFW, BCG, BPCG, and FCFW—under a single Corrective Frank-Wolfe (CFW) framework where each iteration either performs a standard FW step or a corrective step on the active set, with a clean gap-comparison criterion deciding which. The key departure from prior work is the introduction of two quadratic correction steps (QC-LP and QC-MNP) that exploit the structure of quadratic objectives to replace the computationally expensive FCFW subproblem with a linear program or a linear system solve, drawing on Wolfe's Minimum-Norm Point algorithm. The paper also extends the applicability of two existing algorithms—Split Conditional Gradient and Second-Order Conditional Gradient Sliding—by plugging these quadratic corrections into their inner subproblems and strengthening their convergence guarantees (a tighter O(1/√t) rate for SCG and global linear convergence for SOCGS on generalized self-concordant functions).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The CFW framework provides a clean, modular template for designing new corrective steps without re-proving convergence, lowering the barrier for future algorithmic variants. The quadratic corrections are practically significant because they reduce the corrective subproblem from a general convex program to an LP or linear system whose size depends only on the active set, not the ambient dimension—yielding substantial wall-clock speedups across sparse regression, entanglement detection, Birkhoff polytope projection, and logistic regression. Open questions include whether the finite-time convergence guarantee can be extended beyond strict complementarity or to non-quadratic objectives, and whether the single-truncation design of QC-MNP (versus the iterative MNP-Correction) is always optimal or whether multi-step truncation could further improve sparsity. The framework's reliance on an LMO also limits its applicability to settings where linear minimization is tractable.
