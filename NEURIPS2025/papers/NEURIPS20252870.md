## Problem

Deep ensembles provide state-of-the-art uncertainty quantification but are too computationally expensive for practical deployment. Existing knowledge distillation methods for ensembles either lose significant uncertainty when compressing large DNNs into smaller ones (one-to-one distillation) or suffer from numerical instability in parameter estimation (Dirichlet distribution distillation). The paper addresses the gap of a distribution distillation method that is both numerically stable and superior in preserving ensemble uncertainty.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252870/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained teacher ensemble of n DNNs and a set of m design points (typically validation data) as input. At each design point, the n teacher models produce prediction vectors that are treated as independent realizations of a Gaussian process. At inference, given a new input x, the student DNN outputs a mean vector μ_θ(x) and a factor loading matrix Φ_θ(x) ∈ R^{q×c}; ensemble members are then generated by sampling latent factors z ~ N(0, I_q) and computing f(x) = μ_θ(x) + Φ_θ(x)ᵀ z, yielding a full predictive distribution with both mean and covariance for uncertainty quantification.

## Method

The core contribution is the Deep Latent Factor (DLF) model, a Gaussian process where f(·) = μ_θ(·) + Φ_θ(·)ᵀ Z with Z ~ N_q(0, I_q), and both the mean function μ_θ and factor loading Φ_θ are parameterized by a single student DNN with q+1 output nodes. Training uses an EM algorithm: the E-step computes the posterior of latent factors z_i given observed teacher predictions at design points, and the M-step updates DNN parameters via stochastic gradient descent on mini-batches. A key technical contribution is an MMD-regularized pretraining step that maximizes a penalized complete log-likelihood to find a good initial solution, addressing the local-optimum and identifiability issues inherent in factor loading estimation. For the multivariate (classification) case, the model extends to a matrix-variate Gaussian with Kronecker-product covariance structure, handled via vectorization. The approach also introduces a noise parameter σ_f² in the observation model for numerical stability and uses an inverse-gamma distribution to distill regression noise variance σ_ε².

## Results

On CIFAR-10, DLF achieves 93.40% accuracy, 0.2246 NLL, and 2.79% ECE, outperforming all distillation baselines and approaching the teacher ensemble (94.24%, 0.1539, 0.9%). On CIFAR-100, DLF reaches 79.68% accuracy and 0.8974 NLL with 9.45% ECE, substantially beating Hydra (77.42%, 1.2912, 12.70%) and LBE (79.58%, 1.0110, 13.42%). On UCI regression benchmarks, DLF achieves the best or second-best RMSE, NLL, and CRPS across all six datasets, with 95% coverage probabilities closest to the teacher ensemble (e.g., 0.9240 vs. 0.8995 for Hydra on Boston housing). On GLUE/SuperGLUE language tasks, DLF outperforms Hydra and LBE by 4–10 percentage points in ECE (e.g., 9.74% vs. 23.31% on RTE).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DLF consistently outperforms Hydra [11] and LBE [14] (one-to-one distillation) on both accuracy and uncertainty metrics across all datasets: on CIFAR-100, DLF gains +2.26% accuracy and −3.25% ECE over Hydra, and +0.10% accuracy and −3.97% ECE over LBE. Against Proxy-EnD² [18] (Dirichlet-based distribution distillation), DLF achieves substantially higher accuracy (79.68% vs. 67.62% on CIFAR-100) while Proxy-EnD² has a slight ECE edge on CIFAR-10 (2.08% vs. 2.79%), confirming the paper's claim that Dirichlet parameter estimation is unstable. EDFM [45] (flow-matching-based distribution distillation) is dominated by DLF on every metric for both CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 (e.g., 79.68% vs. 64.17% accuracy on CIFAR-100). On UCI regression, DLF beats both Hydra and BE [12] on RMSE, NLL, and CRPS in most cases, with notably better 95% coverage (e.g., 0.9240 vs. 0.8995 for Hydra on Boston housing), indicating that deterministic weight-sharing distillation underestimates ensemble uncertainty. On GLUE/SuperGLUE language tasks, DLF's ECE advantage over Hydra and LBE is largest (e.g., 9.74% vs. 23.31% on RTE), suggesting the gap widens with teacher–student complexity differences.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the standard linear factor model [Bartholomew et al., 30] by replacing its fixed mean and factor-loading parameters with functions parameterized by a student DNN, yielding the Deep Latent Factor (DLF) model. It also builds on the distribution distillation framework of Malinin et al. [17], which treats ensemble members as draws from an input-dependent Dirichlet distribution, but replaces the Dirichlet with a Gaussian process whose mean and covariance are learned via EM. Conceptually, the key departure from one-to-one distillation methods (Hydra, Batch Ensemble, LBE) is treating ensemble-member-specific weights as random effects integrated out in the likelihood rather than fixed effects optimized jointly, and the departure from Dirichlet distillation is the use of a Gaussian process that applies to both regression and classification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The DLF model achieves competitive or superior uncertainty quantification with dramatically fewer parameters (e.g., ~1,862 vs. ~6,650 for Hydra on Boston housing), making it attractive for on-device deployment where deep ensembles are infeasible. The EM-based estimation provides numerical stability that Dirichlet-based methods lack, and the learned DLF body transfers to distribution-shift settings by fine-tuning only the latent-factor head. Open questions include extending the framework to Bayesian DNN teachers, jointly distilling pretrained language models and their LoRA adapters, and using the DLF as a posterior approximation for online Bayesian learning.
