## Problem

Existing visual captioning benchmarks are outdated for modern MLLMs: traditional ones rely on short ground-truth sentences and N-gram metrics (BLEU, CIDER) that are style-sensitive and unreliable, while newer benchmarks (Dream-1K, DetailCaps, VDC, CompreCap) improve annotation quality but remain limited to single-view analyses (vague-view or object-view) with incomplete visual element coverage. No prior benchmark simultaneously evaluates both the correctness and thoroughness of captions across a comprehensive set of visual dimensions for both images and videos.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes RGB images (for 9 static dimensions) and video clips (for 4 dynamic dimensions) as visual input, paired with a generated caption from an MLLM. For video evaluation, the full video is sent to Gemini while GPT receives 50 uniformly sampled frames due to API limits. Each sample is annotated with a single randomly selected visual element per dimension (the 'One Represents All' strategy), and GPT-4 Turbo judges the caption into one of three states (MIS/COR/INC) per dimension, yielding per-dimension precision, hit, and KT scores across 12 dimensions on nearly 11K human-annotated samples.

## Method

CAPability organizes evaluation into 6 views (object, global, text, camera, temporal, knowledge) spanning 12 dimensions (9 static shared by images and videos, 4 dynamic exclusive to videos). The annotation pipeline uses SOTA MLLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini-1.5-pro, Qwen-VL-Max) plus PaddleOCR to pre-annotate candidate elements, then randomly selects one element per sample to preserve unbiased coverage, followed by data balancing to suppress long-tail distributions and human verification achieving >97% accuracy. Evaluation uses GPT-4 Turbo as a judge to classify each caption-dimension pair as MIS (missed), COR (correct), or INC (incorrect), from which precision (COR/(COR+INC)) measures correctness and hit (COR/ALL) measures thoroughness. A key additional contribution is the KT (know-but-cannot-tell) metric, which converts annotations into QA pairs and measures the fraction of cases where a model answers the question correctly but fails to include the information in its free-form caption, exposing a gap between instruction-following and autonomous description capabilities.

## Results

On CAPability, Gemini-2.0-flash and GPT-4o achieve the highest precision (79.3% and 79.2% average), indicating their captions are most factually accurate. For thoroughness (hit), Gemini-1.5-pro leads with 60.4% average, outperforming GPT-4o (56.5%) by 3.9 points, while among 72B open-source models Qwen2.5VL-72B achieves the best hit (53.4%). All models struggle significantly on dynamic dimensions: object number (hit ~25–45%), camera movement (hit ~12–35%), and action (hit ~12–35%), revealing a persistent gap between correctness and thoroughness. The KT metric further shows that Gemini-1.5-pro has the lowest 'know but cannot tell' gap (25.2%), while LLaVA-OV-72B, Qwen2VL-72B, and InternVL2.5-78B exceed 40%, meaning they frequently know the answer in QA but fail to include it in free-form captions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among closed-source models, GPT-4o leads on precision (79.2% avg) and excels specifically on camera angle (67.0% on both precision and hit, 9.6% above the second place) and dynamic object number (96.4% precision). Gemini-1.5-pro dominates on hit (60.4% avg, +3.9 over GPT-4o), with a particularly large advantage in object counting (40.0% hit for image, 45.0% for video, 10–16.3% above the runner-up) and character identification (36.5% hit vs. 28.1% for GPT-4o). Gemini-2.0-flash edges out GPT-4o on overall precision (79.3% vs. 79.2%) and leads on OCR (95.3%) and camera movement precision (35.4%). Among 72B open-source models, Qwen2.5VL-72B achieves the best hit (53.4%) with competitive precision (75.9%), while LLaVA-OV-72B and InternVL2.5-78B trail on both metrics. On the KT metric, Gemini-1.5-pro has the lowest gap (25.2%), meaning it most reliably translates its QA knowledge into captions, whereas LLaVA-OV-72B (41.3%), Qwen2VL-72B (42.6%), and InternVL2.5-78B (40.8%) all exceed 40%, indicating they frequently know the correct visual detail but fail to mention it spontaneously in a caption. All models, regardless of size or source, perform poorly on action (hit 12–35%), camera movement (hit 12–35%), and object number (hit 18–45%), confirming these as the most challenging dimensions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in visual caption benchmarking by departing from both traditional sentence-matching approaches (MS-COCO, BLEU/CIDER) and the intermediate keyword-extraction methods (Dream-1K, DetailCaps, VDC) as well as the object-centric scene-graph evaluation of CompreCap. Rather than relying on ground-truth caption sentences at any level of abstraction, CAPability uses complete visual element annotations across 6 views and 12 dimensions, drawing its dimension taxonomy from visual generation benchmarks (GenEval, VBench, T2V-CompBench) and VQA benchmarks (MMBench, MVBench). The evaluation framework introduces a dual correctness-thoroughness paradigm (precision + hit) and a novel KT metric that converts annotations into QA pairs to measure the gap between a model's ability to answer targeted questions versus spontaneously including information in a free-form caption.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The KT metric reveals a previously unmeasured and substantial disconnect: models achieve similar QA accuracy (~67-70%) across the board, yet their KT values range from 25% (Gemini-1.5-pro) to over 40% (LLaVA-OneVision, Qwen2VL, InternVL2.5), meaning they 'know but cannot tell' in a large fraction of cases. The large precision-hit gap (~75% vs. ~50%) across all models shows that thoroughness has been systematically under-evaluated in prior benchmarks. Open questions include whether the GPT-4-Turbo-as-judge pipeline introduces systematic biases, whether the 'One Represents All' random sampling strategy adequately captures compositional complexity, and whether the 12 dimensions are sufficient or whether additional axes (e.g., emotional tone, narrative structure) should be added. The benchmark is likely to become a standard evaluation tool for MLLM captioning as it provides per-dimension diagnostics rather than a single aggregate score.
