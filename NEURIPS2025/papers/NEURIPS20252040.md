## Problem

GRPO-based reasoning model training is computationally expensive because it requires sampling a group of completions per question and performing three forward passes (policy, reference, old policy) over all of them, scaling training time multiplicatively with group size. The paper identifies that not all completions contribute equally to the policy gradient—completions with low absolute advantage provide negligible training signal—yet GRPO processes them all indiscriminately. The gap is a principled mechanism to discard low-contribution completions before the expensive forward computation without degrading final accuracy.

## Input / Output

During training, the policy model receives a math question (text prompt) and generates G=16 candidate completions, each structured as a chain-of-thought reasoning block enclosed in think tags followed by a final answer in answer tags. A rule-based reward function scores each completion on format compliance (0 or 1) and answer correctness (0, 1.5, or 2), from which group-normalized advantages are computed. The trained model then produces a single reasoning chain and boxed final answer at inference time, evaluated via Pass@1 accuracy on benchmarks like GSM8K, MATH, AMC2023, and AIME2024.

## Method

CPPO introduces two mechanisms to accelerate GRPO. First, completion pruning: after computing group-normalized advantages for all G completions (a cheap prior-forward step), only the top-k completions by absolute advantage value are retained for the three forward passes and gradient update, reducing computation from 3G to 3k forward passes per question. The theoretical justification decomposes the GRPO gradient into a probability-ratio term, an advantage term, and a policy-gradient term, showing that the advantage is the only component available before the forward pass and that near-zero-advantage completions contribute negligibly to the update. Second, dynamic completion allocation: since pruning leaves GPU memory underutilized, the strategy pre-samples b/(1−p) questions per batch (where p is the pruning rate) so that after pruning, the retained completions from the extra questions fill the freed capacity, maximizing GPU parallelism and reducing total training steps. Experiments on Qwen2.5-1.5B/7B/14B and Llama-3.2-1B show up to 7.98× speedup on GSM8K and 3.48× on MATH while matching or exceeding GRPO accuracy, and the approach generalizes to DAPO and Dr.GRPO.

## Results

On GSM8K with Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, CPPO at 87.5% pruning rate achieves 80.01% accuracy (vs. GRPO's 77.38%) while reducing training time from 23,500s to 4,516s (5.22× speedup); at 93.75% pruning, it reaches 7.98× speedup (2,946s) with 78.99% accuracy. On MATH with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, CPPO at 87.5% pruning achieves 75.95% accuracy (vs. GRPO's 75.26%) with a 3.48× speedup (9,722s vs. 33,795s). Out-of-distribution evaluation on AMC2023 and AIME2024 confirms CPPO preserves generalization, with CPPO at 50% pruning scoring 53.12% on AMC2023 and 10.00% on AIME2024 versus GRPO's 46.88% and 5.83%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), CPPO wins on both accuracy and speed: on GSM8K, CPPO at 87.5% pruning scores 80.01% vs. GRPO's 77.38% while training 5.22× faster; on MATH, CPPO at 87.5% scores 75.95% vs. 75.26% at 3.48× faster. Against DAPO (Yu et al., 2025), combining CPPO with DAPO (DAPO+CPPO) maintains the same 78.01% accuracy but cuts training time from 3,800s to 2,134s (1.78× speedup). Against Dr.GRPO (Liu et al., 2025), Dr.GRPO+CPPO improves accuracy from 79.45% to 80.14% while reducing training time from 7,991s to 5,122s (1.56× speedup). Against REINFORCE++ (Hu et al., 2025), CPPO achieves higher accuracy (78.92% vs. 78.54%) with substantially less training time (5,192s vs. 9,043s). Against PPO without KL (Hu et al., 2025), CPPO achieves 78.92% vs. 74.45% accuracy in comparable time (5,192s vs. 5,465s), demonstrating that the completion-pruning strategy is orthogonal to and compatible with other RL algorithm choices.

## Relation to Prior Work

CPPO builds directly on GRPO (from DeepSeek-R1/DeepSeekMath), which itself eliminates the PPO critic by estimating baselines from group scores. The paper's key departure is the observation that completion contributions to the policy gradient are proportional to their absolute advantage, motivating a pruning mechanism that discards low-advantage completions before the expensive forward passes of the policy, reference, and old-policy models. A complementary dynamic completion allocation strategy fills freed GPU capacity with completions from additional questions, addressing the underutilization that pruning would otherwise cause. The method is shown to be composable with other GRPO variants (DAPO, Dr.GRPO), positioning it as a general acceleration layer rather than a standalone algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Training cost is a primary bottleneck for RL-based reasoning models, and CPPO's up-to-8× speedup with preserved or improved accuracy makes large-scale RL training substantially more accessible. The counterintuitive finding that removing low-advantage completions improves accuracy (by filtering out noisy partially-correct signals) challenges the implicit assumption that more sampled completions always yield better training. However, the evaluation is limited to models under 14B parameters and math-only benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, AIME, AMC), leaving open whether the pruning benefit generalizes to larger models, non-mathematical reasoning tasks, or settings where completion generation dominates wall-clock time.
