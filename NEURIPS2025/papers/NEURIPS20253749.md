## Problem

Eye tracking data during reading is rich in cognitive signal yet strikingly underexploited in machine learning, partly because datasets are released in heterogeneous formats, lack standardized preprocessing, and have no consistent evaluation protocol that accounts for their structured non-i.i.d. nature (each reader reads multiple texts, each text is read by multiple readers). No prior benchmark provides a unified framework for comparing predictive models across diverse tasks, datasets, and generalization regimes in this domain.

## Input / Output

Each prediction instance is a single experimental trial: a stimulus passage T (sequence of words), the reader's eye movement scanpath E_T^R (time-stamped sequence of fixations and saccades with spatial coordinates and durations, linked to words), and optionally a task-specific text T_task (e.g., a comprehension question or claim) with its own eye movements. Outputs are either a reader-level property (continuous score for comprehension skill or vocabulary knowledge, binary label for dyslexia) or a reader-text interaction (binary correctness for comprehension/claim verification, continuous Likert rating for perceived difficulty, binary label for domain expertise). Evaluation is stratified into three generalization regimes—unseen reader, unseen text, and unseen reader & text—rather than a single train/test split.

## Method

EyeBench is a benchmark infrastructure rather than a single novel architecture. It provides a modular data-harmonization pipeline that downloads six public datasets (OneStop, SB-SAT, PoTeC, MECO L2, CopCo, IITB-HGC) and transforms them into a unified format with aligned text and gaze sequences, word-level and trial-level eye movement measures, and linguistic features. Twelve models from prior literature are implemented and evaluated across seven tasks: nine neural models spanning fixation-level RNNs/CNNs (AhnRNN, AhnCNN), word-level LSTMs (BEyeLSTM), pre-trained language model variants (PLM-AS, PLM-AS-RM), and RoBERTa-based multimodal transformers (RoBERTEye-W, RoBERTEye-F, MAG-Eye, PostFusion-Eye), plus three traditional classifiers (Logistic Regression, SVM, Random Forest) on aggregated gaze features. Six baselines (chance, majority class, mean/median, reading speed, text-only RoBERTa) provide informative lower bounds. Training uses k-fold cross-validation (k=10 for OneStop, k=4 for smaller datasets) with per-split hyperparameter tuning, and the key methodological contribution is the three-regime evaluation protocol that separately measures generalization to new readers, new texts, and both simultaneously.

## Results

EyeBench v1.0 benchmarks 12 models (9 neural, 3 traditional ML) and 6 baselines across 7 tasks on 6 datasets, using AUROC/balanced accuracy for classification and RMSE/MAE/R² for regression, aggregated via Average Normalized Score and Mean Rank. The headline finding is that no single model consistently outperforms the others across tasks, datasets, or the three generalization regimes (Unseen Reader, Unseen Text, Unseen Reader & Text). In several settings, simple baselines such as Reading Speed and Text-Only RoBERTa perform competitively with the most complex state-of-the-art architectures (e.g., PostFusion-Eye, RoBERTEye-F). Critically, none of the tasks reach a performance level deemed viable for real-world deployment, underscoring the benchmark's role as an open challenge.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark compares 12 predictive models against 6 non-eye-movement baselines (Random/Chance, Majority Class, Mean/Median, Reading Speed, and Text-Only RoBERTa). The central empirical finding is that no model family dominates: traditional ML baselines (Logistic Regression, SVM, Random Forest using trial-aggregated gaze features) and simple neural models (AhnRNN, AhnCNN using only fixation-level features) are competitive with multi-granularity transformer-based models (RoBERTEye-F, PostFusion-Eye, MAG-Eye) in several task-dataset-regime combinations. The Text-Only RoBERTa baseline, which uses no eye movement data at all, is particularly notable for matching or approaching eye-movement-informed models on some tasks, suggesting that textual features alone capture a substantial portion of the predictive signal. The Reading Speed baseline (total reading time, no eye tracking required) also proves surprisingly strong, indicating that global temporal signals remain underexploited. Overall, the results highlight that the field lacks a clear best-practice architecture and that the gap between baselines and SOTA is smaller than expected, leaving substantial room for modeling innovation.

## Relation to Prior Work

EyeBench does not build on a single prior method but rather consolidates the fragmented landscape of eye-movement-plus-text predictive modeling into the first standardized benchmark. It repurposes 12 previously published models (ranging from logistic regression and SVM to transformer-based architectures like RoBERTEye and MAG-Eye) and 6 publicly available datasets into a unified evaluation infrastructure with harmonized data formats and three non-i.i.d. generalization regimes (unseen reader, unseen text, unseen both). The key conceptual departure from prior work is the explicit treatment of the structured non-i.i.d. nature of eye-tracking-while-reading data—where each sample is tied to both a reader and a text—through dedicated evaluation splits, a dimension that existing multimodal ML benchmarks have not addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

EyeBench opens a previously underexplored multimodal domain to the broader ML/NLP community by providing an accessible, reproducible entry point with standardized data loading, 12 reference models, 6 baselines, and evaluation code. The finding that no single model consistently dominates and that simple baselines (Reading Speed, Text-Only RoBERTa) remain competitive with state-of-the-art neural approaches signals that the field is far from solving these tasks, leaving substantial room for modeling innovation. Open questions include how to better exploit the temporal structure of scanpaths, how to achieve real-world-viable predictive performance, and how to extend the benchmark to naturalistic settings (glasses, webcams, VR) as low-cost eye tracking matures.
