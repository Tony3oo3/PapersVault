## Problem

Existing privileged knowledge distillation (PKD) methods for target sound extraction provide overly rich privileged information (e.g., raw target sound or timestamps) to the teacher model, causing the teacher to overfit to training data and produce high-variance outputs that impair student generalization. The paper addresses how to regulate both the amount and the flow of target sound information in the teacher to enable more effective knowledge transfer without increasing inference complexity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254409/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the student model takes a 5.9-second audio mixture at 44.1 kHz and an n-hot target class label vector (M=41 classes) and outputs the extracted target sound waveform. During training only, the teacher additionally receives the ground-truth target sound signal, from which a pretrained DAC neural codec extracts compressed codec representations (dimension 1024) that are further disentangled into static and dynamic factors. The dynamic factors condition the teacher's later layers, while the n-hot label conditions its early layers, forming a coarse-to-fine information flow that is entirely absent at student inference time.

## Method

DCKD first compresses the target sound via a frozen DAC neural codec encoder and quantizer to regulate the amount of privileged information. A Disentangled Static & Dynamic Encoder (DSDE) then separates the codec representations into a time-invariant static factor (modeled with a channel attention module) and time-variant dynamic factors (modeled with a Bi-LSTM plus instance normalization to strip class-specific global features). Disentanglement is enforced through mutual information objectives: vCLUB minimizes MI between static and dynamic factors, InfoNCE maximizes MI between the static factor and the target sound, and a cosine distance loss anchors the static factor to the n-hot class embedding. The n-hot label conditions the teacher's early layers while the class-independent dynamic factors condition its later layers, creating a regulated coarse-to-fine conditioning pipeline that prevents the teacher from over-relying on PI. After teacher training, its intermediate feature representations are frozen and used to supervise the student via a feature-level MSE distillation loss combined with a result-level TSE loss, with the student initialized from teacher weights for stable fine-tuning.

## Results

On the FSD Kaggle + TAU synthetic dataset (41 classes, 3–5 sources per mixture), DCKD improves the Sound Selector student from 8.80 dB SDRi / 7.90 dB SI-SDRi to 10.40 dB / 9.42 dB (+1.60 / +1.52 dB), and the lightweight Waveformer student from 5.50 dB / 4.67 dB to 6.48 dB / 5.80 dB (+0.98 / +1.13 dB). Gains are consistent across 1-target (+2.21 dB SI-SDRi), 2-target (+1.30 dB), and 3-target (+1.01 dB) selection conditions for Sound Selector. On the ESC-50 dataset (50 classes), DCKD yields a smaller but positive gain of +0.33 dB SDRi and +0.20 dB SI-SDRi over the Sound Selector baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DCKD outperforms the Sound Selector student baseline [5] by +1.60 dB SDRi and +1.52 dB SI-SDRi, and the Waveformer student baseline [8] by +0.98 dB SDRi and +1.13 dB SI-SDRi, confirming gains across both a large (6.55 M) and a compact (1.67 M) architecture. Against prior PKD methods, DCKD surpasses the timestamp-based PKD [12] (9.30 / 8.46 dB) by +1.10 / +0.96 dB and the pitch-based PKD [13] (9.61 / 8.71 dB) by +0.79 / +0.71 dB in SDRi / SI-SDRi, demonstrating that disentangled codec representations transfer more effectively than raw timestamps or pitch cues. Notably, even the raw target sound as PI (9.82 / 8.89 dB) underperforms DCKD, validating the paper's claim that overly rich PI harms student generalization. On the language-query AudioSep model [51], DCKD yields only a marginal SI-SDRi gain (+0.05 dB) with a negligible SDRi drop (−0.01 dB), which the authors attribute to AudioSep's FiLM-based conditioning throughout the network conflicting with DCKD's coarse-to-fine design. Overall, DCKD wins or ties on every comparison, with the largest margins observed on the Sound Selector architecture and under multi-target (2- and 3-target) selection conditions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the privileged knowledge distillation (PKD) framework for target sound extraction introduced by Kim et al. (ICASSP 2024), which used timestamps as privileged information. Rather than feeding raw or minimally processed PI to the teacher, DCKD introduces a two-stage regulation: a pretrained DAC neural codec compresses the target sound to control PI information content, and a Disentangled Static & Dynamic Encoder (DSDE) — drawing on the static/dynamic factorization of Bai et al. (NeurIPS 2021) and instance normalization from Chou & Lee (Interspeech 2019) — separates class identity from temporal detail. The key departure from prior PKD work is the coarse-to-fine conditioning scheme: class labels condition early layers while class-independent dynamic factors condition later layers, structuring the information flow to prevent teacher overfitting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled solution to the teacher-overfitting problem in PKD, showing that regulating both the amount and the flow of privileged information yields more effective knowledge transfer than simply providing richer PI. This has practical implications for any PKD-based audio system where training-time information (timestamps, reference signals, pitch) is unavailable at inference. Key limitations include the substantial computational overhead of the DAC codec during teacher training and the assumption that isolated target signals are available during training, which restricts applicability to real-world recordings. Open questions include whether lighter-weight compression can replace DAC, and whether the framework extends to unsupervised or weakly supervised settings where clean target references are unavailable.
