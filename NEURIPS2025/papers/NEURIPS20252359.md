## Problem

Machine learning researchers routinely scrape, use, and redistribute nude images for nudity detection tasks without consent from the depicted subjects, constituting a form of image-based sexual abuse (IBSA) that is almost entirely unacknowledged in the literature. Despite the sensitivity of the data, norms around consent, data handling, and ethical justification are virtually absent, and existing venue ethics guidelines have failed to prevent these harmful practices.

## Input / Output

The systematic review takes as input 150 computer science publications (2002–2024) that use datasets containing real, non-generated nude images, collected via keyword search on Google Scholar and filtered to DBLP-indexed CS venues. The output is a quantitative and qualitative characterization of data collection practices (sources, scale, consent), three distribution pathways (published example images, annotator access, public dataset/model release), and research framing (stated social goals, ethical considerations, classification criteria).

## Method

The authors conducted a multi-stage systematic review: keyword search on Google Scholar yielded 1,204 papers, filtered to 379 at DBLP-indexed CS venues, then manually screened to 150 papers using real nude images. Each paper was annotated with pre-defined questions covering dataset details (source, size, public availability), published example images (count, censorship level), and research practices (data handling, annotation protocols, security). A subset of 74 papers received supplemental annotation on framing, social goals, and terminology, coded via inductive coding until thematic saturation. Quantitative analysis produced descriptive statistics on collection scale (over 8 million images across 126 datasets) and distribution, while qualitative analysis identified patterns in how researchers frame and justify their work. The authors further applied consequentialist and deontological ethical frameworks to evaluate whether stated research objectives adequately justify the harms of nonconsensual image collection and redistribution.

## Results

Across 150 papers (2002–2024) spanning 110 venues, the authors identified 126 unique datasets containing over 8 million nude images, with 98 papers creating new datasets (~2 million images) and 87 papers embedding 816+ example nude images in their manuscripts (9 fully uncensored, 28 with only bodies censored but faces visible). Only 4 of 74 fully annotated papers mentioned any ethical or privacy consideration, and only 2 reported IRB approval. Of the 98 papers that created new datasets, 60 did not specify a data source, and 18 explicitly scraped adult content platforms; one A* paper (Paper6) included content depicting sexual violence collected from since-banned subreddits.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper and systematic review rather than a methodological contribution, so it does not propose a new model or compare against baselines. The closest analogue to a 'comparison' is the contrast between current research practices (nonconsensual scraping, uncensored publication, open dataset release) and the ethical standards the authors advocate (consent-based data trusts, restricted distribution, IRB oversight). The paper documents that existing venue ethics guidelines (NeurIPS, ACM, IEEE, CVPR) already contain principles sufficient to regulate nude image use, yet 5 of 8 A*-venue papers in the corpus were published in 2023 or later—after those guidelines were in place—indicating that reviewer enforcement has not translated into changed practice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the 2016 Gehl et al. review of 102 computer vision papers on pornography filtering, which surfaced gender and sexual politics in how researchers frame their work, by shifting focus to the concrete harms of data collection, distribution, and handling practices. It builds on prior documentation of nonconsensual content in large-scale datasets (ImageNet, LAION-400M, Moments in Time) and broader critiques of extractive scraping norms, but is the first to systematically quantify the scope of nonconsensual nude image use across 150 papers and explicitly frame these practices as constituting image-based sexual abuse (IBSA). The paper also connects to data governance literature (Scheuerman et al. on traceability, Gebru et al. on datasheets) while arguing that existing consent and privacy frameworks are insufficient for the specific harms of nude content redistribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that zero of 74 fully annotated papers discussed consent, and that researchers intentionally collected abusive content (upskirt images, CSAM-adjacent material from banned subreddits), reveals a systemic ethical failure that existing IRB and venue ethics guidelines have not addressed. The paper's proposed interventions—banning identifiable nude images in publications, establishing participatory data trusts, and requiring explicit data source disclosure—offer concrete pathways but raise open questions about feasibility (verifying consent from images alone, balancing research needs with subject dignity, whether AI-generated alternatives truly reduce harm given their own nonconsensual training data). For the broader ML community, this work challenges the default assumption that scraping 'public' data is ethically neutral and demands that nudity detection research be held to fundamentally different standards than general image classification.
