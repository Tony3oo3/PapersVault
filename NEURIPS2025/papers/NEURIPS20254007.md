## Problem

Drone-based search and rescue relies on audio to detect human presence when vision fails, but drone ego-noise drives SNR below -10 dB, masking human vocal and non-vocal cues. Existing drone audition datasets are either too small and limited in SNR diversity (DREGON: 6.12 hours, -16 to -10 dB) or synthetic with unrealistic 0 dB mixing, and no standardized setup exists for studying how microphone placement, throttle, and drone size affect recording quality.

## Input / Output

The dataset provides 23.5 hours of multi-channel audio at 16 kHz: two 8-channel circular microphone arrays (M_up above and M_down below the drone) plus a single standalone microphone (M_center), across 216 unique configurations varying drone type, throttle, mic-drone distance (25/50 cm), source type, source distance (1–9 m), source loudness (60/90 dB), and room acoustics. The evaluation pipeline consumes these multi-channel recordings along with source (x,y,z) coordinates and drone-only reference signals, and outputs enhanced audio (measured via SI-SDR) and per-segment class labels among human vocal, human non-vocal, non-human ambient, and silence.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a systematically collected real-world dataset where drones (DJI F450 and F330) are affixed to a fixed aluminum frame to emulate hovering, with 17 microphones deployed in two 8-channel arrays and one standalone mic to capture genuine acoustic interactions between drone noise and source sounds at SNRs from -57.2 dB to -2.5 dB. For benchmarking, a two-stage pipeline is evaluated: (1) noise suppression via three baselines—traditional (MVDR beamforming + NoiseReduce spectral gating), neural (MPSENet magnitude-and-phase enhancement on the lowest-RMS channel), and hybrid (beamformed input fed to MPSENet); and (2) audio event classification using SSLAM, a self-supervised audio transformer pre-trained on AudioSet, with its 527 labels mapped deterministically to four classes. The key empirical finding is that all suppression methods degrade sharply below -30 dB SNR, and non-vocal and ambient sounds suffer far worse classification F1-scores than vocal sounds, revealing that current speech-centric enhancement and classification models are inadequate for the full range of human-presence cues in drone audition.

## Results

On DRONEAUDIOSET, neural noise suppression (MPSENet) achieves the best SI-SDR across all SNR groups, improving HV sound quality from −17.03 dB (unprocessed) to −12.37 dB at SNR >−10 dB, and from −29.50 dB to −23.44 dB at SNR <−30 dB. For downstream classification, SSLAM on MPSENet-enhanced audio reaches F1=0.874 for human vocal sounds at SNR >−10 dB (vs. 0.968 for clean recordings), but drops sharply to F1=0.232 for human non-vocal and F1=0.192 for non-human sounds. All methods degrade substantially below −30 dB SNR, with SI-SDR gains shrinking to ~6 dB over unprocessed audio, highlighting a fundamental limitation of current approaches under extreme drone ego-noise.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks three noise suppression strategies—Traditional (MVDR beamforming + spectral gating [24,26]), Neural (MPSENet [13]), and Hybrid (beamforming + MPSENet)—followed by SSLAM classification. MPSENet (Neural) consistently outperforms the Traditional pipeline by 2–8 dB in SI-SDR across all SNR groups and source types, with the largest gap at extreme low SNR (<−30 dB: −23.44 vs. −29.54 dB for HV). The Hybrid approach matches or slightly exceeds Neural alone at low SNRs (e.g., −22.76 vs. −23.44 dB at <−30 dB for HV) but underperforms at high SNRs, suggesting beamforming introduces distortion when the source is already relatively clean. For classification, Neural-enhanced audio yields the best F1 for HV (0.874 at >−10 dB, approaching the 0.968 clean-recording ceiling), while HNV and NH F1 scores remain very low (≤0.28) across all methods, indicating that current speech-optimized enhancement and AudioSet-pretrained classifiers are poorly suited for non-vocal and ambient sound detection under drone noise. The Traditional method provides negligible SI-SDR improvement over unprocessed audio at SNR <−30 dB (−29.54 vs. −29.50 dB), effectively failing in the most challenging regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in drone audition for search-and-rescue by introducing the first large-scale, publicly available real-world dataset (23.5 hours) that systematically captures the full range of ego-noise conditions (SNR from −57.2 dB to −2.5 dB). It departs from prior drone audio datasets—DREGON and AVQ, which focused on source localization with limited SNR diversity, and Morito et al., which relied on synthetic convolution at 0 dB SNR—by recording genuine acoustic interactions across two drone types, multiple throttle levels, 17 microphones in varied placements, and three indoor environments. For evaluation, the paper repurposes established speech-enhancement tools (MVDR beamforming, spectral gating, MPSENet) and an audio-event-detection transformer (SSLAM) from the broader audio ML literature, applying them to the previously unstudied regime of extreme drone ego-noise.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset fills a critical gap: no prior resource captured the full distribution of real drone-noise conditions relevant to SAR, making systematic study of noise suppression and human-presence detection under extreme low SNR impossible. The empirical finding that all evaluated SOTA methods degrade severely below −30 dB SNR, and that non-vocal human sounds (knocks, footsteps) and ambient sounds are far harder to classify than vocal sounds, highlights a fundamental limitation of current speech-centric enhancement pipelines. Open questions include whether end-to-end training, drone-specific noise models, or hardware co-design (suspended microphones, adaptive throttle) can close this gap, and how findings transfer to outdoor environments and true in-flight hovering dynamics that the fixed-frame setup approximates but does not fully replicate.
