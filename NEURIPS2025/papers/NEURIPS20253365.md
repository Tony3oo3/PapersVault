## Problem

Existing algorithmic collusion research focuses on reinforcement learning agents discovering collusive equilibria through repeated-game dynamics and credible threats of retaliation, leaving open whether the mere correlation of predictions arising from homogeneous model development practices is itself sufficient to sustain supra-competitive prices. This paper isolates correlation as a standalone collusion mechanism in personalized pricing and asks when adopting correlated (but potentially less accurate) algorithms constitutes a rational firm strategy, with implications for US antitrust law.

## Input / Output

The theoretical model consumes binary algorithmic predictions p1, p2 (segmenting consumers into high/low willingness-to-pay types) along with five parameters (θ, a1, a2, σ, ρ) and produces equilibrium pricing strategies, consumer welfare, and firm utility as explicit functions of the correlation parameter. The empirical study uses ACSIncome (2018 US Census) tabular data to predict income >$50k, where each firm selects between logistic regression (lower accuracy, lower variance) and random forest (higher accuracy, higher variance), and outputs best-response matrices identifying pure Nash equilibria in the model-selection game over 15 random seeds.

## Method

The paper constructs a two-stage game-theoretic duopoly model analyzed under Bayes-Nash equilibrium rather than repeated-game or RL dynamics. In the second stage, two firms set personalized prices (H or L) based on correlated binary classifier predictions; the key mechanism is that correlation ρ between predictions eliminates the strategic uncertainty that drives undercutting—when both firms predict the same consumer type, they price identically, sustaining higher equilibrium prices. The first stage models firms' strategic choice between correlated (shared data or model class) and independent model development, revealing that firms may rationally prefer a less accurate but more correlated model when consumers are price-sensitive (Theorem 5.3). The empirical validation on ACSIncome demonstrates this trade-off concretely: logistic regression's lower variance yields more correlated predictions than random forest, and equilibrium analysis shows firms prefer the correlated-but-worse model under high price sensitivity and large H/L ratios.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical results establish that (1) consumer welfare is strictly decreasing in algorithm correlation ρ (Theorem 4.1), and (2) firms prefer higher correlation when consumer price sensitivity is high, specifically when σ < σ*(θ,R) = (Rθ−1)/(2θ(R−1)) (Theorem 4.2). The key strategic result (Theorem 5.3) shows that firms can achieve strictly higher utility from a correlated but less accurate model than from an independent but more accurate one. In the empirical study on ACSIncome, when θ=0.75, H/L=3, σ=0.1, and ρ_c=1, firms prefer a correlated logistic regression model at accuracy a_c=0.6 over an independent random forest model at accuracy a_i=0.72, demonstrating a willingness to sacrifice 12 percentage points of accuracy for coordination.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baselines but instead contrasts two strategic regimes: correlated models (shared data, same model class) versus independent models (separate data, different model classes). In the empirical study, random forests (RF) outperform logistic regression (LR) on all standard metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, TNR, AUC), yet firms prefer the LR-LR equilibrium over RF-RF when H/L is large and σ is low (consumers are price sensitive). This preference for the worse-performing but more correlated model is the paper's central empirical finding. In the data-procurement experiment (Appendix C.2), increasing the overlap parameter γ from 0 to 1 monotonically increases model correlation while decreasing accuracy, and firms prefer intermediate-to-high γ values (e.g., γ=0.9) over full independence (γ=0) under high price sensitivity. The theoretical results confirm that this preference is robust: Lemma 5.1 shows independence is always a PNE when a_i > a_c, but Theorem 5.3 shows both equilibria can coexist with correlation yielding higher firm utility.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the personalized pricing competition literature (Rhodes & Zhou, Baik & Larson) by introducing correlated algorithmic predictions as a strategic variable, moving beyond the assumption of independent or perfect information. It builds on Kleinberg & Raghavan's algorithmic monoculture framework but shifts the welfare analysis from correlated errors to their competitive pricing consequences. Crucially, it departs from the dominant algorithmic collusion literature (Klein 2021, Possnig 2023, Arunachaleswaran et al. 2024) that relies on repeated-game dynamics and reinforcement learning to find collusive equilibria; here, correlation alone in a one-shot Bayesian game suffices to sustain supra-competitive prices, requiring no threats or adaptive learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a mechanism for tacit collusion—algorithmic correlation—that is largely unaddressed by current US antitrust law, which typically requires evidence of explicit agreement or traditional plus factors. The counterintuitive finding that firms may rationally prefer less accurate models to increase correlation with competitors (Theorem 5.3) has direct implications for how regulators should interpret model selection decisions. Key limitations include the stylized duopoly setting with binary pricing and a specific consumer friction parameter; open questions include whether these effects persist in n-firm markets, how to empirically detect correlation-driven collusion in real pricing data, and whether the legal framework can be adapted to treat public model announcements as plus factors.
