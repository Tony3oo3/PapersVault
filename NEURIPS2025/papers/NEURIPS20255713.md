## Problem

Vanilla DPO for video diffusion suffers from a motion bias: annotators systematically prefer slow-motion clips with fewer artifacts over dynamic ones, causing the model to suppress motion after alignment. Additionally, assigning a single binary preference to an entire 5-second video ignores temporal variation in quality, yielding a sparse and imprecise learning signal that misattributes credit when artifacts appear at different timestamps in each video.

## Input / Output

The model takes a text prompt as input and generates a 5-second horizontal video at 512×288 resolution using a DiT-based latent flow model with a MAGVIT-v2 autoencoder (8× spatial, 4× temporal downsampling) and T5 text embeddings. For training, preference pairs are constructed by partially noising a ground-truth video (guidance level η ∈ [0.65, 0.8]) and denoising with two independent noise seeds, then labeling preferences on 1-second temporal segments rather than the full clip. Inference uses 40 rectified-flow sampling steps with classifier-free guidance scale 8 and timestep shifting of 5.66.

## Method

DenseDPO combines two key modifications to standard DPO for video. First, StructuralDPO replaces independent-noise pair generation with guided denoising: a ground-truth video is corrupted at step n = round(η·N) with two different Gaussian noises, then denoised to produce structurally aligned pairs that share motion trajectories but differ in local details, neutralizing the motion bias. Second, the single whole-video preference label is replaced with segment-level labels (s = 1s), yielding a vector l ∈ {−1,+1}^F where F = ⌈T/s⌉. The DPO loss (Eq. 7) sums the implicit reward differences over all non-tie segments within a pair, providing denser and more temporally precise supervision. This temporal alignment also enables off-the-shelf VLMs (e.g., GPT-o3) to reliably label segment-level preferences, achieving DPO performance close to human labels without task-specific fine-tuning.

## Results

On VideoJAM-bench, DenseDPO achieves a Dynamic Degree of 85.38 (VBench) and 0.680 (VisionReward), significantly outperforming Vanilla DPO (80.25 / 0.535) while matching it in Text Alignment (0.863 vs. 0.867), Visual Quality (0.376 vs. 0.371), and Temporal Consistency (0.632 vs. 0.636). On MotionBench, DenseDPO reaches a Dynamic Degree of 84.73 (VBench) and 0.858 (VisionReward) versus Vanilla DPO's 72.55 / 0.709, while maintaining comparable Motion Smoothness (97.33 vs. 97.43). Notably, DenseDPO achieves these gains using only one-third of the labeled video pairs (10k vs. 30k) compared to Vanilla DPO.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Vanilla DPO (Liu et al. [46]), DenseDPO wins decisively on Dynamic Degree (85.38 vs. 80.25 VBench; 0.680 vs. 0.535 VisionReward) while essentially tying on Text Alignment, Visual Quality, and Temporal Consistency, all while using only 10k labeled pairs versus 30k. Against the VLM-based labelers, GPT o3 Segment + DenseDPO (TA 0.842, VQ 0.368, TC 0.598, DD 0.672) substantially outperforms VisionReward + StructuralDPO (TA 0.785, VQ 0.347, TC 0.521, DD 0.622), LiFT, and VideoScore in DPO alignment quality. In raw preference prediction accuracy (Table 3a), task-specific models like VisionReward (72.45%) and VideoReward (71.89%) slightly edge out GPT o3 (70.03%) on 1s segments, but GPT o3 Segment's majority-voting aggregation over segments achieves 70.59% on 5s videos—far surpassing all single-pass VLMs (51–62%). GPT o3 alone on long videos (53.45%) is near chance level, confirming that segment-level decomposition is critical for VLM-based labeling. Overall, DenseDPO with GPT o3 Segment labels approaches human-labeled DenseDPO (TA 0.863, VQ 0.376, TC 0.632, DD 0.680) while using 5.5× more video pairs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DPO-for-diffusion lineage (Rafailov et al. → Wallace et al. → VideoAlign) by identifying a video-specific failure mode—motion bias in preference pairs generated from independent noise—and addressing it with SDEdit-style guided generation to produce structurally aligned video pairs. The key conceptual departure is shifting from whole-video binary preferences to segment-level dense preferences, drawing an analogy to sentence-level DPO in LLMs. The guided-pair construction also connects to the broader SDEdit/controlled-diffusion literature, repurposing partial denoising not for editing but for bias-neutralized preference data curation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The motion-bias diagnosis and its fix via guided generation are practically important for anyone applying DPO to video models, as the bias silently degrades dynamic content in a way that standard metrics may not fully capture. The demonstration that off-the-shelf VLMs (GPT-o3) can produce competitive segment-level preference labels—approaching human-annotated DenseDPO performance—opens a path toward scaling preference data without proportional annotation cost, though the 5.5× data overhead relative to human labels remains a significant gap. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to longer videos (>5s), other video architectures, and whether the VLM labeling quality improves sufficiently with next-generation models to close the remaining performance gap.
