## Problem

Randomized experiments are costly and often underpowered, while in silico experiments leveraging foundation models can improve precision but produce invalid statistical inferences when model predictions are biased. The open question is whether foundation model predictions can be safely integrated into experimental analysis to reduce variance while preserving valid confidence intervals under minimal assumptions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255328/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a randomized experiment dataset (covariates X, binary treatment A, bounded outcome Y), known propensity scores π_a by design, and black-box predictions from k foundation models (e.g., GPT-4o, LLaMA 3 70B, Claude 3.5 Haiku) that map (X, A) to a predicted outcome. It outputs a single ATE point estimate and an asymptotically valid confidence interval. A key design constraint is that the standard AIPW estimator (outcome regression fitted on the experimental sample via cross-fitting) is always included in the ensemble, guaranteeing the combined estimator never has worse asymptotic variance than the standard baseline.

## Method

H-AIPW constructs k+1 AIPW estimators: one standard AIPW with a cross-fitted outcome regression from experimental data, and k AIPW estimators each plugging in a different foundation model as the outcome regression function. These are combined as a weighted average with weights λ* = Σ^{-1}1/(1^T Σ^{-1}1) that minimize the asymptotic variance of the combined estimator, where Σ is the covariance matrix of the influence function values estimated empirically. The key theoretical guarantee (Theorem 2) is that the resulting estimator is consistent, asymptotically normal, and has asymptotic variance no larger than any individual estimator in the ensemble—even when foundation models are arbitrarily biased—because the optimal combination automatically down-weights or eliminates biased models. This distinguishes H-AIPW from PPCT, which omits the standard AIPW and thus can have higher variance than the experimental-data-only baseline, and from PROCOVA, which requires structured observational data rather than black-box model predictions.

## Results

Across eight randomized experiments (n=100 and n=200 subsamples, R=10k repetitions), H-AIPW achieves the lowest scaled variance among all estimators in nearly every setting, with variance reductions of approximately 5–11% relative to the standard AIPW estimator. The headline claim is an equivalent reduction of up to 20% in required sample size to match the confidence interval width of the standard AIPW estimator (Figure 2, n=75). Gains are most pronounced in the small-sample regime (n=100), where H-AIPW outperforms AIPW(standard) by 4–11% in variance across all eight studies.

## Baselines & Comparisons

H-AIPW outperforms the standard AIPW estimator (linear outcome regression) in all eight studies at both sample sizes, with variance reductions of roughly 5–11% (e.g., 10.39 vs. 11.72 in Melin 2022; 4.49 vs. 4.82 in Haaland 2023). Against PPCT [Poulet et al., 2025], H-AIPW achieves lower variance in 15 of 16 study–sample-size combinations; PPCT is only guaranteed asymptotically to match the difference-in-means estimator, not AIPW, and indeed shows higher variance than AIPW(standard) in several settings (e.g., 11.00 vs. 11.72 in Melin 2022 at n=100). Against PROCOVA [Liao et al., 2023; Schuler et al., 2022], H-AIPW again wins in nearly all cases, with the gap widening in small-sample settings (e.g., 5.88 vs. 6.41 in Caprariello 2013). AIPW with a boosting function class suffers substantially higher variance (e.g., 23.09 vs. 17.09 in Kennedy 2020) because the small experimental samples cannot support complex models. The Shuman et al. (2024) study, which uses image-based treatment and post-dates GPT-4o's training cutoff, confirms that H-AIPW's gains are not an artifact of data contamination: H-AIPW (8.46) still beats PPCT (9.08) and PROCOVA (9.12) using only a single model (GPT-4o).

## Relation to Prior Work

H-AIPW is built directly on the AIPW estimator (Robins et al., 1994) and the optimal estimator combination framework of Lavancier and Rochet (2016), applying inverse-covariance weighting to a set of AIPW estimators whose outcome regression functions are replaced by black-box foundation models. It departs from PPCT (Poulet et al., 2025), which uses a single foundation model and omits the standard AIPW from the combination, thereby lacking a safety guarantee against increased variance. It also differs from PROCOVA (Liao et al., 2023; Schuler et al., 2022) and related observational-data-augmentation methods by not requiring well-structured external data, instead leveraging arbitrary black-box model predictions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is a variance guarantee: H-AIPW is provably no less precise than standard AIPW even when all foundation models are arbitrarily biased, which is a critical safety property for adoption in regulated domains like clinical trials where FDA guidance demands valid inference under minimal assumptions. The empirical 5–20% sample-size reduction across eight social science experiments is meaningful for cost-constrained research, though the gains depend on foundation models being reasonably accurate for the target domain. Open questions include finite-sample undercoverage when combining many models (addressed partially via cross-fitting), extension to settings with unknown propensity scores, and whether the approach transfers to clinical trial outcomes where LLM predictions may be less reliable.
