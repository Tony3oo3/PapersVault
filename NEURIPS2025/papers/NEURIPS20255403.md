## Problem

The standard model for average-case analysis of deterministic game-solving algorithms samples leaf values independently, causing root values to asymptotically collapse to a single fixed value and rendering all algorithms (including AlphaBeta) provably globally optimal. This independence assumption strips away the ancestor dependencies and sibling correlations that make real games challenging, producing trivial instances where algorithm comparisons lose meaning. The paper asks whether a simpler model that enforces structural dependencies can generate non-trivial, difficulty-tunable instances while remaining analytically tractable.

## Input / Output

The forward model takes a branching degree b, tree height h, and a categorical distribution μ over the discrete value set {-n,...,n} as parameters, and generates a complete b-ary game-tree by sampling values top-down from root to leaves. The analysis then computes, for each algorithm A (TEST, AlphaBeta, Scout), the expected number of leaf evaluations I_A(h) and the asymptotic branching factor r_A = lim_{h→∞} I_A(h)^{1/h}. A key structural constraint is that at every internal node, exactly one uniformly-chosen 'special child' must inherit the parent's negated value (enforcing the negamax constraint), while the remaining b−1 children are drawn from μ truncated to respect the minimax bound, making the tree generation a conditional, level-wise process rather than an independent leaf assignment.

## Method

The core contribution is the forward model (Algorithm 1), which constructs game-trees incrementally from the root downward: at each node with value x, one of b children is uniformly selected to inherit −x, and the other b−1 children are sampled from a fixed distribution μ truncated to {−x,...,n}. This top-down construction makes intermediate node values conditionally known during tree generation, which the authors exploit to derive closed recursive systems (Equations 7–11) for the average-case complexity of TEST, AlphaBeta, and Scout. These recurrences are cast in matrix form, allowing efficient numerical computation of I_A(h) via matrix iteration and extraction of the branching factor as the spectral radius of the transition matrix. The key theoretical results show that AlphaBeta and TEST provably share the same asymptotic branching factor (Theorem 5.1), and Scout is at most as expensive as TEST-bruteforce (Theorem 5.2), yet finite-depth analysis reveals that AlphaBeta incurs a substantially larger multiplicative constant than Scout, mirroring the performance of the naive TEST-BRUTEFORCE baseline.

## Results

Under the forward model, all analyzed algorithms (TEST, ALPHA-BETA, SCOUT) share the same asymptotic branching factor r, as proven in Theorem 5.1 (r_AB = r_TEST) and numerically confirmed for SCOUT (Theorem 5.2). However, finite-depth analysis (b=10, n=5, h up to ~5000) reveals that ALPHA-BETA incurs a significantly larger sub-exponential multiplicative constant than SCOUT and TEST-BISECTION, making it the worst-performing algorithm across all distribution parametrizations (uniform, triangular, power-law, bimodal-uniform). Remarkably, ALPHA-BETA's performance mirrors that of the naive TEST-BRUTEFORCE baseline, suggesting its α/β windows rapidly collapse to null-window situations equivalent to exhaustive threshold testing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares five algorithms under the forward model: ALPHA-BETA, SCOUT, TEST-BRUTEFORCE, TEST-BISECTION, and TEST-HARDEST. SCOUT consistently outperforms ALPHA-BETA across all four distribution parametrizations and all tree depths, achieving the smallest multiplicative constant. ALPHA-BETA is the worst performer, closely tracking TEST-BRUTEFORCE (which calls TEST for every threshold s ∈ {-n+1,...,n}), suggesting ALPHA-BETA's recursive α/β updates effectively reduce to exhaustive threshold testing. TEST-BISECTION (binary search over thresholds, analogous to MTD(f)) also outperforms ALPHA-BETA, supporting prior empirical claims of MTD(f)'s practical superiority. Surprisingly, SCOUT even outperforms TEST-HARDEST (the single most expensive TEST call) for deeper trees, indicating SCOUT's adaptive threshold updating provides an advantage over fixed-threshold testing. These findings contrast with the standard (independent-leaf) model, where all algorithms provably achieve the optimal √b branching factor asymptotically, masking the practical differences revealed here.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard model of average-case analysis for game-solving algorithms (Knuth & Moore 1975, Pearl 1980, Baudet 1978), where leaf values are independently sampled, by introducing the 'forward model'—a top-down, level-wise generation process that enforces ancestor dependencies between sibling nodes. Conceptually, it is closest to the Prefix Value Game Tree Model of Furtak & Buro (2009) and earlier forward-generation ideas of Scheucher & Kaindl (1998), but differs in its mechanism (one child inherits the parent's negated value rather than additive increments) and, crucially, provides the first full recursive-complexity analysis under this type of model. The paper also builds on prior critiques of the independence assumption (Nau 1982) and on the algorithmic landscape of AlphaBeta, Scout, PVS, and MTD(f) established in the 1980s–1990s literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The forward model offers a tractable yet structurally richer benchmark that reveals finite-depth performance differences invisible to asymptotic analysis: AlphaBeta incurs a substantially larger multiplicative constant than Scout or TEST-BISECTION, challenging its status as a default strong baseline. Open questions include deriving closed-form branching-factor expressions, extending the analysis to PVS and MTD(f) (which require modeling certificate-value distributions), and generalizing to variable branching degrees and continuous value spaces. The model's simplicity (uniform trees, fixed conditional distribution, single inheritance mechanism) limits its fidelity to real games, so its findings should be interpreted as evidence about algorithmic structure rather than direct predictions for chess or Go.
