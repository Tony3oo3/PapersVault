## Problem

Existing LLM benchmarks (MBU, MFU) assume all model parameters participate in computation, which grossly overestimates resource utilization for sparse MoE systems—by up to 3× when only a subset of experts is active. Additionally, there is no principled framework to understand how MoE systems trade off cost, accuracy, and performance, leaving practitioners without guidance for deployment decisions across heterogeneous hardware.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252717/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes an MoE model checkpoint, a hardware specification (GPU/CPU/DRAM/HBM/SSD/interconnect details), an evaluation dataset, and a serving framework (vLLM, SGLang, MoE-Infinity, K-Transformers, HuggingFace Transformers, or Accelerate). At runtime, a lightweight expert-activation profiler instruments the router in each MoE layer to record per-token, per-layer activation patterns, which are then used to compute sparsity-aware metrics. Outputs include a CAP radar diagram, per-token cost in USD (combining purchase and energy costs over deployment lifetime), S-MBU and S-MFU percentages, accuracy scores (exact match, F1, win rate), and latency/throughput metrics (TPOT, tokens/s, TTFT).

## Method

MoE-CAP introduces two sparsity-aware performance metrics that replace the full model size with the actually-activated parameter size: S-MBU (Eq. 4) computes achieved bandwidth as (S_activated + S_KV)/TPOT divided by peak bandwidth, where S_activated sums only experts flagged by a boolean indicator 1[l,i] traced from router outputs; S-MFU (Eq. 5) computes FLOPs per token as F_attn + 2·N_router + 2·k_expert·N_expert, accounting for top-k routing and shared experts without runtime tracing. A comprehensive cost model (Eqs. 1–3) decomposes hardware purchase cost into computation (GPU+CPU), communication (PCIe, NVLink, C2M), and memory (HBM, DRAM, SSD) components, and combines it with energy cost over deployment lifetime R to yield a per-token dollar cost. The CAP Radar Diagram visualizes each system's normalized scores on cost, accuracy, and performance axes, revealing that MoE systems typically optimize two dimensions at the expense of the third (categorized as PA, PC, or CA systems). An automated evaluation pipeline supports six serving frameworks across five benchmarks (MMLU, GSM8K, MATH, Arena-Hard, LongBench) and multiple hardware platforms from edge devices to data-center clusters.

## Results

MoE-CAP validates its sparsity-aware metrics against hardware profilers: S-MFU matches profiler-measured values within 0.05% across all batch sizes (Table 2), and S-MBU stays within 1% of profiled values on multi-node H20 clusters (Table 3), while vanilla MBU overestimates memory access by over 260% on Mixtral-8x7B. The CAP radar analysis reveals that no current MoE system optimizes all three dimensions simultaneously: SGLang achieves the lowest decoding latency (0.058 s/token) and 91.1% exact match on GSM8K but at the highest hardware cost; MoE-Infinity preserves the same 91.1% accuracy at ~60% lower purchase cost but incurs a 2.6× latency penalty (0.15 s/token). Full expert activation of DeepSeek-R1 requires 18,901 GB/s bandwidth (only feasible on DGX-H100-class hardware), whereas at batch size 1 the requirement drops to 1,040 GB/s, making it deployable on consumer GPUs like the RTX 4090 (450W) with offloading.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoE-CAP benchmarks three representative MoE serving systems—SGLang (performance-accuracy optimized), K-Transformers (performance-cost optimized), and MoE-Infinity (cost-accuracy optimized)—on Qwen3-30B-A3B using the CAP radar. MoE-Infinity [49] matches SGLang's exact-match accuracy (91.1% on GSM8K) while reducing purchase cost by ~60%, but at the cost of 2.6× higher decoding latency (0.15 vs. 0.058 s/token); in the stress test under Azure request-trace replay, MoE-Infinity sustains a steady 14–17% S-MBU whereas SGLang peaks at 54.5% before degrading under saturation due to PagedAttention token eviction. vLLM/PagedAttention [28] (used as the 'vllm_moe' baseline in Table 4) achieves comparable prefill throughput to SGLang on A100 clusters (e.g., 1776 vs. ~18932 T/s for Mixtral-8x22B vs. 8x7B, reflecting model size differences) but the paper's CAP analysis shows that vLLM-based deployments without offloading require significantly more HBM and thus higher hardware cost than MoE-Infinity's DRAM-offloading approach. The key finding is that no single system dominates all three CAP dimensions: SGLang wins on latency and throughput, MoE-Infinity wins on cost at equal accuracy, and K-Transformers offers a middle ground with lower cost than SGLang but reduced accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM benchmarking lineage (MLPerf, Open-LLM-Leaderboard, LLM-Perf) by introducing sparsity-aware metrics—S-MBU and S-MFU—that directly modify the MBU formula from Databricks [1] and the MFU formula from PaLM [8] to account for selective expert activation in MoE layers. It departs from prior benchmarks in two key ways: (i) the cost model generalizes beyond GPU-only accounting to include heterogeneous compute (CPU, GPU), multi-tier memory (HBM, DRAM, SSD), and interconnect costs, and (ii) the CAP Radar Diagram provides a principled framework for categorizing MoE systems into performance-accuracy, performance-cost, and cost-accuracy optimized types. The benchmark evaluates existing serving systems (SGLang, vLLM, MoE-Infinity, K-Transformers) rather than proposing a new serving architecture, positioning itself as an evaluation layer atop the current MoE systems ecosystem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The sparsity-aware metrics address a concrete and previously unquantified problem: standard MBU/MFU overestimate resource utilization by up to 3× for MoE systems, leading operators to over-provision expensive hardware. The practical implication—that DeepSeek-R1 at batch size 1 requires only 1,040 GB/s (feasible on an RTX 4090 with offloading) versus 18,901 GB/s for full activation—challenges the assumption that trillion-parameter MoE models are exclusively data-center workloads. Key limitations include inference-only coverage (no training or post-training scenarios), a relatively small set of evaluated models, and the absence of a standardized hardware reference platform. Open questions include whether the CAP trade-off taxonomy holds as MoE architectures evolve (e.g., dynamic expert selection, learned sparsity), and how the metrics behave under real-world cloud elasticity and spot-instance pricing.
