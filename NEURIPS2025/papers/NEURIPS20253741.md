## Problem

Current multimodal alignment methods (CLIP, ImageBind, LanguageBind, VAST) rely on pairwise cosine similarity with an anchor modality, which guarantees alignment only between each modality and the anchor but provides no geometric indicator that non-anchor modalities are aligned with each other. This leaves the third modality (e.g., audio in video-text retrieval) effectively unexploited, causing failures when that modality carries discriminative information absent from the other two.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 8 randomly sampled video frames, a single 10-second audio clip (converted to a 64-dimensional log Mel filterbank spectrogram with a 25 ms Hamming window), and a natural-language caption, each encoded by dedicated encoders (EVA-CLIP ViT-G, BEATs, and BERT-B respectively) into unit-norm vectors in a shared latent space. The output is a triangle-area similarity score computed from three dot products in that higher-dimensional space, which drives retrieval rankings (R@1, R@10) or classification decisions without any fusion MLP or pairwise projection.

## Method

TRIANGLE replaces pairwise cosine similarity in contrastive losses with the area of the triangle formed by three unit-norm embedding vectors x, y, z in R^n, defined as A = ½√(⟨u,u⟩⟨v,v⟩ − ⟨u,v⟩²) where u = x−y and v = x−z; smaller area indicates tighter three-way alignment. This area-based score is plugged directly into InfoNCE-style contrastive objectives (Eqs. 5–6) so that all three modalities are jointly aligned in their natural higher-dimensional space rather than projected onto 2D pairwise planes. For downstream retrieval tasks, a cosine regularization term (Eq. 9) is subtracted from the area to specifically control alignment of the two task-relevant modalities, and a data-text-matching (DTM) loss from VAST is added to the total objective. The model reuses VAST's 1.3B-parameter encoder stack (with fusion layers removed) and is pretrained for 10k steps on a 150k-sample subset of VAST27M, requiring no new datasets or architectural additions.

## Results

On zero-shot video-text retrieval, TRIANGLE achieves R@1 of 55.2 (T2V) and 52.5 (V2T) on MSR-VTT, 54.9/53.1 on DiDeMo, 59.7/54.1 on ActivityNet, and 83.9/80.9 on VATEX, setting new state-of-the-art across all four benchmarks. Compared to VAST (same encoders, parameters, and pretraining data), TRIANGLE improves R@1 by up to 9 points (e.g., +8.8 on MSR-VTT V2T, +8.3 on ActivityNet T2V). On audio tasks, TRIANGLE reaches 32.2 R@1 / 77.1 R@10 on AudioCaps text-to-audio retrieval and 44.8 R@1 / 80.0 R@10 on VGGSound audio-visual classification, outperforming VAST by up to 11.7 points on R@10.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TRIANGLE outperforms VAST (the strongest same-architecture baseline with identical encoders and pretraining data) by 3.6–8.8 R@1 points across all eight video retrieval metrics, with the largest gains on V2T tasks where audio disambiguation is most critical. Against the general n-modal methods GRAM and Symile, TRIANGLE wins on every three-modal benchmark: GRAM trails by 0.7–3.7 R@1 on video retrieval and by 1.0 R@1 on AudioCaps, while Symile collapses to near-chance (0.3 R@1) in the training-from-scratch setting. Among two-modal baselines, TRIANGLE surpasses the strongest vision-text methods (VideoPrism-b at 51.4 T2V on MSR-VTT, mPLUG-2 at 47.1) by 3.8–8.1 points, confirming that the third audio modality contributes meaningfully when properly integrated. On audio tasks (Table 2), TRIANGLE beats GRAM by 1.0 R@1 on AudioCaps and by 4.2 R@1 on VGGSound, and exceeds VAST's T-AV configuration by 5.2 R@1 on VGGSound. In the Touch-Vision-Language experiment (Appendix), TRIANGLE improves over the original TVL model from 36.7 to 83.1 Acc@1, demonstrating generality beyond video-audio-text.

## Relation to Prior Work

TRIANGLE extends the CLIP-style contrastive learning paradigm by replacing the pairwise cosine similarity with a triangle-area measure computed directly in the higher-dimensional space spanned by three modality embeddings. It departs from the anchor-based pairwise alignment strategy used by ImageBind, LanguageBind, and VAST (which fuse modalities via MLPs before computing cosine), and from general n-modal objectives like GRAM (parallelotope volume) and Symile (total-correlation bound), by proposing a geometry-specific similarity tailored to exactly three vectors. The method reuses VAST's encoder stack (BERT-B, BEATs, EVA-CLIP ViT-G) and DTM loss, isolating the contribution to the similarity measure itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The triangle-area formulation offers a rare combination of geometric interpretability (area directly quantifies three-way alignment) and practical simplicity (no fusion layers, negligible compute overhead), which prior anchor-based or fusion-based methods lack. A key open question is generalization beyond three modalities: the authors sketch a polygon-decomposition strategy but do not implement or validate it, leaving the method's applicability to four-plus modalities untested. Additionally, the need for a cosine regularization term (α) in edge cases (flat triangles) suggests the pure area measure is not always sufficient, and the optimal α appears task-dependent, complicating deployment. The consistent 4–9 point R@1 gains over the same-architecture VAST baseline across six benchmarks make this a practically useful drop-in replacement for three-modal contrastive training.
