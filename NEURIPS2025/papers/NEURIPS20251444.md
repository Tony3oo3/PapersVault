## Problem

Despite significant progress in causal representation learning (CRL) for identifying latent causal structures, it remains unclear what makes learned representations actually useful for downstream causal tasks and how to evaluate them properly. Standard CRL evaluation metrics like R² and mean correlation coefficient (MCC) fail when latent variables are causally related, as they measure predictability or correlation rather than whether a representation exclusively measures the intended causal variable, leading to misleading conclusions about identification quality.

## Input / Output

The method consumes paired samples of ground truth causal variables Z ∈ R^N and learned measurement variables (CRL encoder outputs) Ẑ_{A_j} ∈ R^{D_j}, along with a binary adjacency matrix V ∈ {0,1}^{N×M} encoding the measurement model (i.e., which causal variables each measurement should exclusively depend on). The measurement model can be specified by a CRL algorithm's identifiability guarantees, assumptions for a particular causal estimand, or ground-truth knowledge. The output is a scalar T-MEX score (a Hamming distance) plus the individual conditional independence test decisions forming the empirical adjacency matrix Ŵ.

## Method

The paper reinterprets CRL through a measurement model framework (inspired by Silva et al., 2006), where each learned representation is a proxy measurement of one or more latent causal variables, and introduces the Test-based Measurement EXclusivity (T-MEX) score to quantify alignment between the learned representation and the hypothesized measurement model. T-MEX tests the null hypothesis H₀(i,j): Ẑ_{A_j} ⊥ Z_i | Z_{[N]\{i}} for every causal variable–measurement pair, constructs an empirical adjacency matrix Ŵ from the test rejections, and computes the Hamming distance between Ŵ and the expected adjacency matrix V. The score is non-parametric and agnostic to the specific conditional independence test (the authors use the projected covariance measure test), and Proposition 3.1 provides an upper bound on the expected T-MEX when the measurement model holds, depending only on the test's significance level α and power β. The paper demonstrates that T-MEX reliably tracks absolute ATE bias in both numerical simulations and real-world ecological video analysis (ISTAnt), while R² and MCC produce misleadingly high scores for entangled representations under causal dependencies.

## Results

In the numerical simulation (§5.1), T-MEX correctly distinguishes model quality: the sufficiently trained Model A achieves T-MEX = 0.12 ± 0.33, while the insufficiently trained Model B (1.18 ± 0.39) and corrupted Model C (2.00 ± 0.00) score significantly higher. In contrast, R² remains high (0.67–0.99) across all three models, failing to reflect the ATE bias. On the real-world ISTAnt ecological benchmark (§5.2), models with T-MEX = 0 achieve both higher classification accuracy and lower absolute ATE bias than T-MEX = 1 models, with a Mann-Whitney U test yielding p = 0.0047, confirming statistically significant separation in causal validity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against the two standard CRL evaluation metrics: coefficient of determination (R²) and mean correlation coefficient (MCC). In the numerical simulation, R² fails to distinguish the well-trained Model A (R² = 0.998 for Z₁) from the corrupted Model C (R² = 0.939 for Z₁), yet T-MEX clearly separates them (0.12 vs. 2.00), aligning with the actual ATE bias. MCC is shown to be equally inadequate: even when Z₂ and Z₃ remain entangled in the representation, MCC can still report a perfect score of 1. On the ISTAnt benchmark, T-MEX = 0 models achieve consistently higher accuracy and lower ATE bias variance than T-MEX = 1 models, with the difference in absolute ATE bias being statistically significant (p = 0.0047 via Mann-Whitney U test). The paper also notes that SHD (Structural Hamming Distance) for causal graph evaluation can be misleading when representations are entangled, as a perfect SHD of zero can be achieved with entangled representations using methods like LiNGAM.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reinterprets Causal Representation Learning (CRL) through the measurement model formalism of Silva et al. (2006), adapting it from a latent-variable discovery context to the evaluation of learned representations. It builds on the block-identifiability notion from von Kügelgen et al. (2021) as the formal criterion for when a representation 'measures' a causal variable, and positions T-MEX as a principled alternative to the R² and MCC metrics that dominate the CRL evaluation literature. The key departure is treating the learned representation as a proxy measurement whose alignment with a hypothesized measurement model can be tested via conditional independence, rather than as a regression target whose predictability score (R²) or correlation (MCC) suffices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper exposes a concrete and practically important gap: the evaluation metrics used across the CRL literature (R², MCC) can give deceptively high scores when latent variables are causally related, leading to misinterpretation of identification quality. T-MEX offers a test-based, non-parametric alternative that requires no parametric assumptions on the measurement function and can be computed from observational data alone, making it accessible in settings where ground-truth ATE bias is unavailable. Open questions include how T-MEX scales to very high-dimensional latent spaces (CI test power degrades), whether the framework extends naturally to temporal or hierarchical causal structures, and how to handle cases where the measurement model itself is uncertain or only partially specified.
