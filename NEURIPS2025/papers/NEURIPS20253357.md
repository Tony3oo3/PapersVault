## Problem

Existing uncertainty bounds for kernel-based regression either require (conditional) independence and Gaussianity of the noise sequence, are overly conservative, or demand solving a constrained convex program whose dimension scales with the number of training points. This paper asks whether a tight, non-asymptotic, distribution-free bound can be obtained under the weaker assumption of energy-bounded (possibly correlated or biased) noise, while remaining computationally tractable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253357/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes N noisy input-output pairs at pairwise distinct locations, a test point x_{N+1}, two positive-semidefinite kernels (k^f for the latent function, k^w for the noise), and known RKHS-norm upper bounds Γ_f and Γ_w. It produces exact point-wise upper and lower bounds f̄(x_{N+1}) and f̲(x_{N+1}) that characterize the worst-case latent function value within the hypothesis class. Unusually, the bound's width depends on a test-point-specific noise parameter σ* obtained by a 1-D minimization, so the uncertainty envelope is not a single fixed-width band but adapts locally to the data geometry.

## Method

The core contribution is reducing an infinite-dimensional worst-case optimization over two RKHSs to a scalar unconstrained minimization over a noise scale σ ∈ (0,∞). A relaxed problem that merges the two RKHS-norm constraints into a single weighted sum admits a closed-form solution identical in structure to the GP posterior mean plus β_σ·√Σ_σ^f, where β_σ and Σ_σ^f are computed from the sum kernel k^f + σ²k^w. The optimal bound is then the infimum of this relaxed expression over σ, which is a smooth 1-D problem amenable to gradient descent or bisection. The derivation proceeds via the Representer Theorem (finite-dimensional reduction), an SVD-based coordinate change to handle kernel-matrix rank deficiency, and a QR decomposition that isolates the data-determined subspace from the adversarial degrees of freedom, ultimately yielding a linear program with a norm-ball constraint. In the two degenerate cases (σ→∞ or σ→0), closed-form solutions recover classical kernel-interpolation and Fogel-style linear-regression bounds, confirming the result as a strict generalization.

## Results

The paper's main contribution is theoretical: Theorem 1 shows the optimal non-asymptotic uncertainty bound for kernel regression under energy-bounded noise reduces to a scalar minimization over a noise parameter σ of a GP-posterior-mean-plus-width expression (Eq. 8). Numerically (Fig. 2), in the low-data regime (N < ~50), the proposed energy-bounded bounds are significantly less conservative than the standard high-probability GP bound of Abbasi-Yadkori [2013]; however, as N grows beyond ~200, the proposed bounds plateau while the probabilistic bound continues to shrink by leveraging noise independence. In the safe-control application (Fig. 3), the proposed bound with full data achieves the highest feasibility success rate, while a nearest-10-points variant matches the probabilistic bound's success rate at substantially lower solve time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary numerical baseline is the high-probability GP error bound of Abbasi-Yadkori [2013] (also used in Srinivas et al. 2012, Fiedler et al. 2021). In the uncertainty-region-area comparison (Fig. 2, N = 1–1000, squared-exponential kernel, Dirac noise, Γ_w² = Nε² with ε = 0.01), the proposed optimal and relaxed bounds are markedly tighter in the low-data regime but do not improve further with additional data, whereas the probabilistic bound asymptotically dominates as N → ∞ by exploiting conditional independence. For the safe-control task (Fig. 3, 500 test points, 20 random noise realizations), the proposed bound with all training data achieves the highest success rate (share of feasible QP instances) but at the highest computational cost; the nearest-10-points variant of the proposed bound reaches a success rate comparable to the probabilistic bound while being significantly faster, since its solve time is independent of N. The paper also notes that under the stronger point-wise-boundedness assumption, the optimal bounds of Scharnhorst et al. [2023] are tighter than Theorem 1, but require solving a constrained convex program with O(N) variables to optimality, whereas the proposed σ-optimization returns a valid (though potentially suboptimal) bound at every iteration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies and generalizes three previously separate lines of work: Fogel's (1979) set-membership bounds for linear regression under energy-bounded noise, the Weinberger–Golomb kernel interpolation error bounds, and the high-probability GP regression bounds of Srinivas et al. (2012) and Abbasi-Yadkori (2013). The key conceptual departure is from probabilistic bounds, which require (conditional) independence and distributional assumptions on the noise, and from deterministic bounds (Scharnhorst et al., 2023), which require solving an N-variable constrained convex program. By formulating the problem as a scalar optimization over a noise parameter σ, the paper recovers both the linear-regression and kernel-interpolation results as special cases while handling correlated and biased noise sequences without distributional assumptions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The bound is tight (worst-case optimal within the hypothesis class), non-asymptotic, and computationally tractable—any σ yields a valid upper bound, enabling iterative refinement and integration into streaming or safe-control pipelines. The safe-control experiment demonstrates practical value: the subset-of-data variant achieves near-full-data success rates at a fraction of the compute cost, and the deterministic nature allows rigorous certification of data-selection strategies. Open questions include robustness to kernel mis-specification and unknown RKHS-norm bounds (addressed only empirically), and whether the approach extends effectively to Bayesian optimization or model-based reinforcement learning. The trade-off identified—superior performance in the low-data regime but no asymptotic improvement with more data, unlike probabilistic bounds—highlights a fundamental tension between distribution-free and distributional assumptions.
