## Problem

Rectified flow models face two critical limitations in text-to-image editing: inversion-based methods suffer from cumulative errors and a reconstructability-editability trade-off, while existing inversion-free approaches like FlowEdit still produce suboptimal results when target prompts contain multiple semantic attributes, because a single editing trajectory leads to entangled gradients and conflicting directions in latent space.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255124/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a source RGB image, its corresponding source text prompt, and a target text prompt describing the desired edit. An LLM (Mistral-7B) decomposes the target prompt into N sub-target prompts (typically N≤3) at inference time, adding roughly 20 minutes of overhead. The pipeline operates on SD3/SD3.5 rectified flow backbones with T=50 sampling steps, skipping the first third (η_max=33) and performing the decomposition phase over 5 steps (η_dec=28) before switching to a unified flow for the remaining trajectory.

## Method

SplitFlow decomposes the editing flow into independent sub-target flows, one per semantically decomposed sub-prompt, each governed by its own velocity-difference ODE (extending the FlowEdit formulation). At the end of the decomposition phase, two aggregation mechanisms recombine the sub-flows: Latent Trajectory Projection (LTP) projects each sub-target latent onto the normalized target latent direction to enforce global semantic consistency, and Velocity Field Aggregation (VFA) adaptively weights sub-target velocity fields using a softmax over pairwise cosine similarities, suppressing redundant directions while emphasizing distinct semantic components. This design is inspired by gradient conflict resolution in multi-task learning, and the paper provides a formal proof (via Gibbs' and Jensen's inequalities) that VFA yields a higher inner product with the average flow than simple averaging, guaranteeing improved fidelity. After aggregation, the unified latent follows the standard target-prompt ODE to completion, balancing fine-grained attribute manipulation during decomposition with holistic prompt alignment during the final phase.

## Results

On the PIE-Bench benchmark (700 images), SplitFlow with SD3 achieves a Structure Distance of 25.96×10³, PSNR of 22.45, LPIPS of 102.14×10³, and CLIP Edited similarity of 23.83, outperforming the FlowEdit baseline (SD3) on all metrics. The fidelity-enhanced variant SplitFlow† (SD3) further reduces Structure Distance to 14.55×10³ and LPIPS to 68.53×10³ while maintaining CLIP Edited at 23.01, surpassing FTEdit (SD3.5) in both CLIP Whole (26.23 vs. 25.74) and CLIP Edited (23.01 vs. 22.27). With SD3.5, SplitFlow achieves the best background preservation among flow-based methods (Distance 11.68×10³, LPIPS 52.93×10³, SSIM 89.76×10²).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SplitFlow (SD3) outperforms FlowEdit (SD3) on every reported metric: Structure Distance improves from 27.24 to 25.96 (×10³), LPIPS from 105.46 to 102.14 (×10³), and CLIP Edited from 23.67 to 23.83. Against iRFDS (SD3), the gap is even larger, with Distance dropping from 62.72 to 25.96 and CLIP Edited rising from 21.67 to 23.83. The fidelity-enhanced variant SplitFlow† (SD3) beats FTEdit (SD3.5) on Structure Distance (14.55 vs. 18.17), LPIPS (68.53 vs. 80.55), and both CLIP metrics (26.23/23.01 vs. 25.74/22.27), despite FTEdit using a stronger backbone. Compared to Flux-based inversion methods (RF-Inversion, RF-Solver, FireFlow), SplitFlow† achieves substantially better background preservation (e.g., LPIPS 68.53 vs. 120–185) and higher CLIP Edited scores. On SD3.5, SplitFlow marginally edges out FlowEdit (SD3.5) across all metrics (Distance 11.68 vs. 11.80, LPIPS 52.93 vs. 53.68, CLIP Whole 26.29 vs. 26.18), confirming consistent gains from flow decomposition. Overall, SplitFlow wins or ties on all metrics against every flow-based baseline, with the largest margins over inversion-based methods (RF-Inversion, iRFDS) and the smallest over the direct FlowEdit baseline it extends.

## Relation to Prior Work

SplitFlow directly extends FlowEdit's inversion-free velocity-difference ODE formulation for rectified flow editing, replacing the single unified editing trajectory with a decompose-then-aggregate pipeline. The decomposition step leverages an LLM to split complex target prompts into semantically localized sub-prompts, while the aggregation mechanism (Latent Trajectory Projection and Velocity Field Aggregation) is explicitly inspired by gradient conflict resolution techniques from multi-task learning (CAGrad, Nash-MTL). Conceptually, the paper departs from the broader inversion-based editing lineage (DDIM, RF-Inversion, RF-Solver, FireFlow) by remaining fully inversion-free, and from prior inversion-free work (FlowEdit, InfEdit) by introducing flow decomposition as a means to disentangle multi-attribute gradients.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical pain point in multi-attribute text-to-image editing where a single editing trajectory suffers from gradient entanglement, and the flow decomposition strategy offers a principled way to isolate and recombine semantic directions. However, the method's reliance on an LLM for prompt decomposition introduces a quality dependency and increases inference time (~83 min vs. ~57 min for FlowEdit on 700 images), limiting immediate practical adoption. Open questions include whether decomposition can be learned end-to-end rather than delegated to an LLM, whether the aggregation strategy generalizes to video or 3D editing, and whether the modest quantitative gains over FlowEdit (e.g., 25.96 vs. 27.24 structure distance on SD3) would hold under more diverse or adversarial prompt distributions.
