## Problem

Diffusion model inference requires hundreds to thousands of function evaluations, and existing acceleration strategies face a trade-off: fast numerical solvers degrade sharply below ~10 NFEs, while distillation methods introduce complex training procedures, computational overhead, and instability (e.g., model collapse, over-smoothing). This paper seeks an intermediate strategy that balances generation quality and step count without the fragility of either extreme.

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard Gaussian noise sample (and optionally a class label for conditional generation) and produces a synthetic RGB image in 1–10 steps, operating on the PF-ODE trajectory in either pixel space (EDM on CIFAR-10, 32×32) or latent space (SiT-XL/2 on ImageNet-256×256). The network's input is a noised image x_t together with two time points (t, s), and it outputs the secant f(x_t, t, s) — the average velocity over the interval [t, s] — which is then used to jump directly from x_t to x_s in a single evaluation.

## Method

The core idea is to replace the tangent (instantaneous derivative v(x_t, t)) learned by standard diffusion models with a secant function f(x_t, t, s) that represents the time-averaged derivative over [t, s], enabling multi-step jumps in a single network call. The training loss is derived by recognizing the secant as a Monte Carlo expectation of tangents over the interval, and by using Picard iteration to estimate the unobserved intermediate state from the observed one, yielding four variants (SDEI, STEI, SDEE, STEE) that differ in whether they estimate the interior or endpoint and whether they use a teacher model or direct data supervision. Crucially, the loss target is either the diffusion model itself or the same regression target as standard diffusion training, which the authors show provides significantly greater target stability than consistency-model losses that involve explicit derivatives. Theorems 2 and 3 guarantee that under L-Lipschitz continuity, the optimal solution of the secant loss recovers the true secant in a sufficiently small neighborhood, with accuracy extending to larger intervals via a bootstrapping process.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, the SDEI variant achieves a 10-step FID of 2.14, outperforming all fast samplers (best: LD3 at 2.38) while remaining competitive with few-step training methods. On ImageNet-256×256, the STEI variant with interval CFG attains a 4-step FID of 2.27 and an 8-step FID of 1.96, while STEE achieves 8-step FID of 1.96, both surpassing the IMM baseline (2.51 at 4 steps, 1.99 at 8 steps) and significantly outperforming fast samplers (Flow-DPM-Solver: 3.76 at 8 steps). The teacher SiT-XL/2 requires 250 steps for FID 2.15, so the secant version achieves comparable quality in 4–8 steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On CIFAR-10, SDEI (FID 2.14, 10 steps) outperforms all fast samplers—DPM-Solver-v3 (2.51), LD3 (2.38), and DPM-Solver++ (2.91)—but trails the best few-step training methods: sCT (2.06, 2 steps), ECT (2.11, 2 steps), and IMM (1.98, 2 steps). On ImageNet-256×256, the advantage is more pronounced: STEI with interval CFG (2.27, 4 steps) beats IMM (2.51, 4 steps) and Shortcut Models (7.80, 4 steps) by a wide margin, while STEE with CFG (1.96, 8 steps) matches IMM (1.99, 8 steps). Notably, STEI at 1 step (FID 7.12) surpasses both IMM at 1 step (7.77) and Shortcut Models at 4 steps (7.80). The paper reports that consistency models (iCT, sCT, sCD) diverge on ImageNet in their experiments, whereas secant losses maintain stable convergence throughout training. Overall, the method positions itself as a stable, low-overhead alternative that degrades more gracefully than numerical solvers at low NFEs while avoiding the training instability and computational overhead of adversarial or distribution-matching distillation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as an intermediate strategy between fast ODE samplers (which degrade sharply below ~10 NFEs) and diffusion distillation methods (which introduce complexity, instability, or model collapse risks). Conceptually, it is the integral counterpart to consistency models: where consistency losses use explicit differentiation (or finite-difference approximations) to enforce the PF-ODE trajectory, secant losses use Monte Carlo integration and Picard iteration to learn the average velocity (secant) between two time points. The method is formulated within the flow matching framework and applied to EDM (UNet) and SiT (DiT) as teacher models, inheriting their parameterizations while replacing the tangent (instantaneous velocity) prediction with a secant (average velocity) prediction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is that secant losses achieve training stability that consistency models lack at scale—consistency models diverge on ImageNet-256×256 in the authors' experiments, while secant losses converge reliably in only 1% of the original training budget (50K–100K vs. 7M iterations). This makes few-step distillation/fine-tuning of large pretrained diffusion models substantially more accessible. Open questions include the theoretical interaction between classifier-free guidance and secant losses (the ImageNet results rely heavily on CFG), the significant 1-step vs. 8-step performance gap, and whether the approach extends to data-limited or non-image modalities without access to training data.
