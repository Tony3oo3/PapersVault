## Problem

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) improves LLM reasoning but often leads to 'superficial self-reflection,' where models fail to reliably identify errors in their own outputs. Existing approaches decouple problem-solving from verification training or lack direct, contemporaneous reward feedback for the verification skill itself within the RL loop, leaving models unable to robustly self-assess their reasoning.

## Input / Output

The method takes two types of input: (1) raw math problems for solution generation, and (2) problem-solution pairs formatted via a predefined verification prompt template that explicitly states verification criteria and requests a critique with a score. Outputs are chain-of-thought solutions ending in a \boxed{} final answer, and natural-language verification critiques ending in a \boxed{} score. The reward signal is discrete and rule-based: +1 for a correctly formatted and matched answer, −0.5 for a boxed but incorrect answer, and −1 for an unboxed response, applied identically to both generation and verification tasks.

## Method

RISE is an online RL framework built on PPO that jointly trains an LLM's problem-solving and self-verification abilities within a single unified policy-gradient objective. In each iteration, the model first generates K solutions per problem, then constructs verification prompts from its own on-policy generations using a template; the same rule-based outcome verifier that scores solutions also provides ground-truth supervision for the verification task. Both the generation batch and the verification batch are concatenated into a single training batch, with GAE advantages computed independently per trajectory and a shared critic learning a unified value function across tasks. The critical design choice is that verification data is collected online from the current policy (not from a frozen or distant policy), ensuring the model learns to verify the very solutions it is simultaneously learning to produce. The verification batch size (default 128, i.e., 12.5% of the 1024 generation batch) acts as a compute-scaling knob that trades off verification training intensity against problem-solving training.

## Results

On mathematical reasoning benchmarks (MATH500, AIME 2024, AMC 2023, Minerva, OlympiadBench), RISE-7B achieves 42.9% average Pass@1 reasoning accuracy and 69.2% average self-verification accuracy, while RISE-3B reaches 33.5% and 74.3% respectively. The self-verification gains are dramatic: RISE-1.5B jumps from 26.8% (Zero-RL) to 74.5% verification accuracy, a 47.7-point improvement. At test time with k=4 sampling, RISE-3B and RISE-7B outperform standard majority voting by +0.2% and +1.9% respectively when using verification-weighted selection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Zero-RL (identical PPO setup without the verification objective), RISE consistently wins on self-verification by large margins—74.5% vs. 26.8% (1.5B), 74.3% vs. 35.8% (3B), and 69.2% vs. 46.6% (7B) average accuracy—while also achieving modest reasoning gains (+0.4, +1.0, +1.2 points respectively). Against SFT baselines, RISE dominates overwhelmingly: RISE-7B scores 42.9% average reasoning vs. 11.3% for SFT-7B, a 31.6-point gap. Compared to instruction-tuned Qwen2.5 models, RISE-3B improves reasoning by 3.7 points (33.5% vs. 29.8%) and self-verification by 33.4 points (74.3% vs. 40.9%). RISE models also outperform off-the-shelf verifiers (GPT-4o at 57.8% and Math-Shepherd-7B) on verification accuracy, with RISE-3B reaching 74.3% average. On cross-domain tasks (MMLU-Pro, GPQA, HumanEval), RISE-7B edges out Zero-7B (47.4 vs. 46.3 on MMLU-Pro; 64.1 vs. 63.7 on HumanEval), confirming transferable benefits.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards) paradigm, exemplified by DeepSeek-R1 and Tulu 3, by repurposing the same outcome-verifier reward signal to simultaneously train a model's self-verification capability alongside its problem-solving ability within a single PPO loop. Prior work on solution verification (Math-Shepherd, Lightman et al.) and self-critique (Xi et al., Xie et al.) typically decouples the two tasks or trains them in separate stages, while self-play approaches (Lin et al., Ma et al.) use constructed trajectories rather than on-policy verifiable rewards. RISE's key departure is the tight online coupling: the model critiques its own on-policy generated solutions, and the identical rule-based verifier that scores the solution also provides ground-truth supervision for the verification task, all within one unified policy-gradient update.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete and well-motivated failure mode—'superficial self-reflection'—and shows that a simple architectural addition to existing RLVR pipelines (adding a verification batch with the same reward signal) yields a 2.8× improvement in verification accuracy with only modest reasoning gains. The clean ablation showing that online verification is essential (offline verification collapses in verification accuracy while preserving reasoning) provides actionable guidance for practitioners building self-improving reasoners. Open questions include whether the trained self-verifier can bootstrap further improvement on unlabeled data, whether a discriminative verifier head would be preferable, and whether the approach generalizes beyond mathematics to other verifiable-reward domains. The modest reasoning improvements (1–3 points over Zero-RL) and the absence of error bars temper the practical impact somewhat.
