## Problem

Data-driven ocean forecasting lacks a standardized benchmark comparable to WeatherBench in atmospheric science, making it difficult to assess model skill, diagnose failure modes, or enable fair comparison across architectures. Critical operational data—first-guess trajectories, nowcasts, and atmospheric forcings from assimilation cycles—are typically unavailable in public reanalyses, further hindering reproducible research in this domain.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250303/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

All candidate models receive identical initialization from weekly GLO12 nowcast snapshots (1/12°, 50 vertical levels, every Tuesday in 2024) and are forced with the same 10-day ECMWF IFS atmospheric forecast fields (1/10°: wind, radiation, precipitation, heat/freshwater fluxes). Models produce 10-day forecasts of 3D temperature, salinity, and currents plus 2D sea surface height; evaluation yields RMSE scores across three tracks (reanalysis, analysis, observations) and process-oriented diagnostics (mixed layer depth, geostrophic currents, Lagrangian drift deviation) computed at native model resolution to avoid unfair penalization of coarser grids.

## Method

OceanBench constructs a curated dataset from operational Mercator Ocean systems—GLO12 nowcasts and IFS forcings that are not publicly distributed—paired with matched near-real-time observations (Argo, altimetry, drifters, SST buoys) for the full year 2024. The evaluation framework defines three complementary tracks: Model-to-Reanalysis against GLORYS12 (1/12°, 50 levels), Model-to-Analysis against GLO12's observation-constrained analysis, and Model-to-Observations via the IV-TT CLASS-4 protocol for resolution-agnostic validation. Beyond pointwise RMSE, process-oriented diagnostics (MLD from density thresholds, geostrophic currents from SSH gradients, and Lagrangian particle advection) test whether forecasts maintain physical consistency without explicit dynamical constraints. Baselines include the physics-based GLO12 system and three ML architectures spanning autoregressive (GLONET with FNO+CNN+Transformer, WenHai with Swin Transformer and bulk formulae) and direct-prediction (XiHe with 20 independent per-lead-time transformers) strategies, all initialized and forced identically to ensure fair comparison.

## Results

OceanBench evaluates four forecasting systems (GLO12 physics-based, GLONET, XiHe, WenHai ML-based) on 10-day global ocean forecasts, reporting absolute RMSE against the GLORYS12 reanalysis across five state variables (thetao, so, uo, vo, zos) at six depths plus process diagnostics (MLD, geostrophic currents, Lagrangian drift). ML models outperform the GLO12 physics baseline on surface velocity components (uo, vo) but underperform on potential temperature (thetao), with salinity (so) performance being variable and sometimes competitive. WenHai, which embeds bulk formulae for surface fluxes, consistently ranks lowest among ML models across most metrics, while XiHe's direct (non-autoregressive) prediction strategy yields the most stable error evolution across lead times. In the models-to-observations track, GLONET achieves the lowest errors on surface currents at 15 m depth, and ML temperature skill improves with depth (100–300 m) while salinity skill degrades.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the physics-based GLO12 baseline, ML models (GLONET, XiHe) win on dynamical surface velocity variables (uo, vo) but lose on the scalar tracer thetao, with salinity (so) being a mixed result where some ML models are competitive or slightly superior. Among the three ML baselines, WenHai (Cui et al., Nature Comms 2025) consistently ranks lowest across most metrics, suggesting its embedded bulk formulae constrain generalization despite adding physical realism. XiHe (Wang et al., 2024) benefits from its direct prediction strategy, showing stable error evolution across lead times, whereas the autoregressive GLONET (Aouni et al., 2024) and WenHai accumulate errors over the 10-day horizon. In the models-to-observations track, GLONET achieves the lowest surface-current RMSE at 15 m depth, outperforming both GLO12 and the other ML models. In the models-to-analysis track, all ML models underperform GLO12 (as expected, since the analysis inherits GLO12's dynamical structure), but WenHai's error patterns most closely mirror GLO12's due to shared physical assumptions. The paper concludes that architectural inductive biases aligned with physical principles (as in XiHe's design) are more effective for dynamical fidelity than external forcing parameterizations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the limited 'OceanBench: The SSH Edition' (Johnson et al., NeurIPS 2023), which covered only sea surface height and temperature over the Gulf Stream, into a comprehensive global, multi-variable benchmark with three complementary evaluation tracks. It adapts the benchmarking philosophy of WeatherBench/WeatherBench2 from atmospheric science to the ocean domain, but introduces a critical new element: operational-grade data (GLO12 nowcasts and ECMWF IFS atmospheric forcings) that was previously unavailable in public datasets due to assimilation cycles. The benchmark evaluates existing ML ocean models (GLONET, XiHe, WenHai) alongside the physics-based GLO12 system, positioning itself as the missing standardized infrastructure that has slowed progress in data-driven ocean forecasting relative to its atmospheric counterpart.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

OceanBench fills a critical gap by providing the first standardized, reproducible framework for evaluating data-driven global ocean forecasting, complete with operational initialization data, atmospheric forcings, and process-oriented diagnostics (MLD, geostrophic currents, Lagrangian trajectories) that test physical consistency beyond pointwise RMSE. Key open questions remain: the benchmark is limited to deterministic 1–10 day forecasts, lacks regional breakdowns and biogeochemical variables, and does not yet support probabilistic or ensemble evaluation. The finding that physically-inspired forcing (WenHai's bulk formulae) actually degrades ML performance relative to more flexible architectures challenges the assumption that embedding operational parameterizations always helps, and the clear dynamical-vs-tracer skill split highlights where ML models still lag physics-based systems.
