## Problem

As generative AI produces near-photorealistic videos, existing detection methods struggle to generalize across rapidly evolving generative models. Image-based detectors miss temporal inconsistencies, while video-based detectors require extensive task-specific training and still degrade on unseen or future generators. The paper asks whether the geometric structure of neural representations—specifically, the straightness of temporal trajectories—can serve as a general, training-light signal for distinguishing natural from synthetic video.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a 2-second video segment from which 24 frames are uniformly sampled, each resized to 224×224 and normalized to [0,1]. Each frame is encoded by a frozen DINOv2 ViT-S/14, extracting the 384-dim CLS token and 196 patch embeddings (16×16 grid) from the final transformer block (block.11), concatenated into a single 75,648-dim vector per frame. The output is a binary real/fake label from a two-layer MLP (64→32) operating on a 21-dim feature vector, with end-to-end inference of ~48 ms on a single GPU.

## Method

ReStraV exploits the perceptual straightening hypothesis by measuring how straight a video's trajectory is in DINOv2's representation space. For each video, it computes per-step displacement vectors between consecutive frame embeddings, then derives two geometric signals: stepwise distance (magnitude of displacement) and curvature (angle between successive displacement vectors, Eq. 1). These per-frame time series are summarized into 8 statistical descriptors (mean, min, max, variance for each signal), concatenated with 7 raw distance and 6 raw curvature values to form a 21-dim feature vector. A lightweight MLP is trained on this vector to classify real vs. AI-generated video. The key empirical finding is that DINOv2 differentially straightens natural videos (aligning with its learned priors) while leaving AI-generated trajectories curved, producing a large positive curvature gap (Δθ = 45.46°) that does not exist in pixel space or in HVS-inspired encoders—making the geometric signal both interpretable and highly discriminative without any fine-tuning of the backbone.

## Results

On the VidProM benchmark, ReStraV (MLP classifier) achieves 97.06% average accuracy and 98.81% average mAP across four AI generators (Pika, VideoCraft2, Text2Video-Zero, ModelScope), with per-generator accuracies of 90.90–99.50%. On GenVidBench, it reaches 93.01% average accuracy on the Main task and 96.96% on the challenging Plants task, outperforming all video-based baselines. In the zero-shot Veo3 test, ReStraV attains 83.2% accuracy and 86.9% AUROC without any Veo3 training data. End-to-end inference takes approximately 48 ms per video on a single RTX 2080 GPU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ReStraV substantially outperforms all eight image-based detectors on VidProM, achieving 97.06% average accuracy versus a best baseline of 63.64% (Gram-Net) and most baselines hovering near chance level (45–52%). Against the video-based VideoSwin baseline, ReStraV wins in every generalization scenario: seen generators (97.05% vs. 77.91%), unseen generators (89.45% vs. 62.44%), and future generators/Sora (80.05% vs. 60.70%). On GenVidBench, ReStraV dominates both the Main task (93.01% avg vs. best baseline TimeSformer at 75.09%) and the Plants task (96.96% avg vs. best baseline TimeSformer at 75.09%), with particularly large margins over I3D, SlowFast, and VideoSwin which collapse to near-chance on several generators. In the one-to-many DeMamba benchmark, ReStraV achieves the highest F1 scores in two of three training conditions (0.827 and 0.898) and remains competitive with DeMamba on AP. On the Physics-IQ matched-pair 2AFC task, ReStraV (using only mean curvature as a threshold, no classifier) reaches 97.5–99.0% across Pika, Runway, and Sora, dramatically surpassing Gemini 1.5 Pro (55.6–80.5%), with the largest gap on Sora where the VLM is near chance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the perceptual straightening hypothesis from neuroscience (Henaff et al., 2019, 2021)—which showed that biological visual systems transform curved pixel-space video trajectories into straighter neural representations—into a detection signal for AI-generated video. It departs from both image-based detectors (which exploit spatial/frequency artifacts in individual frames) and dedicated video detectors (which learn temporal inconsistencies via task-specific training) by instead measuring the geometric curvature and stepwise distance of a frozen DINOv2 representation trajectory, then applying a lightweight classifier. The key conceptual departure is the finding that detection performance does not correlate with absolute perceptual straightening capability; rather, it hinges on the *differential* straightening of natural versus synthetic trajectories, a property that self-supervised encoders like DINOv2 exhibit but HVS-inspired models do not.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~48 ms inference cost and strong zero-shot generalization (83.2% on Veo3, 97–99% on Physics-IQ matched pairs where Gemini 1.5 Pro scores 55.6% on Sora) make this a practically deployable detection tool that could complement watermarking and provenance standards. However, the authors rightly flag Goodhart's law: adversarially trained generators could optimize to flatten their representation trajectories, and future sufficiently capable models may eliminate the curvature gap entirely. Open questions include whether the signal persists for longer videos, whether time-reversal invariance (a noted limitation) can be broken to add a complementary signal, and whether the differential straightening effect is specific to DINOv2 or generalizes to other self-supervised encoders as they evolve.
