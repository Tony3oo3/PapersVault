## Problem

Higher-order common neighbors (CNs) in link prediction suffer from two critical issues: redundancy (different-order CNs of the same node pair overlap heavily, reducing their incremental information) and over-smoothing (as CN order increases, nodes become CNs for nearly all node pairs, homogenizing pairwise representations). Existing GNN-based link prediction methods either ignore higher-order CNs or use them without addressing these problems, limiting their ability to capture complex pairwise structures.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an undirected graph (symmetric adjacency matrix and node feature matrix) along with a target node pair, and outputs a scalar link-existence probability via a sigmoid-activated MLP. A key design choice is that the MPNN backbone runs exactly once over the entire graph for all target links (MPNN-then-SF paradigm), ensuring scalability on large graphs like ogbl-ppa (576K nodes, 30M edges). Higher-order CNs are defined via walks (not shortest paths), and the target link is removed from the adjacency matrix during training but retained during validation/test.

## Method

OCN combines two techniques to make higher-order CNs informative. First, coefficient orthogonalization applies Gram-Schmidt to the CN coefficient vectors (each in R^n, encoding how many walks of a given length pass through each intermediate node) to eliminate linear correlation across orders; a polynomial-filter variant (OCNP) approximates this with orthonormal polynomial bases (e.g., Chebyshev) at lower cost. Second, path-based normalization divides each node's CN coefficient by the total number of k-hop walks it participates in, which provably tightens the latent-space distance upper bound as k increases (unlike unnormalized CNs, whose bound is independent of k). The final prediction is the element-wise product of MPNN node embeddings plus a weighted sum over k of OCN^k · MPNN(A,X), following the NCN architecture. Theoretical analysis on random graphs and Barabási-Albert models confirms that normalized high-order CNs yield strictly decreasing proximity bounds for positive pairs, justifying the normalization.

## Results

OCN achieves state-of-the-art link prediction across seven benchmarks, with headline results of 97.42 HR@20 on DDI (vs. 84.11 for the strongest baseline NCNC), 72.43 HR@50 on Collab, and 69.79 HR@100 on PPA. On average, OCN improves by 7.7% over NCNC on the five datasets where it leads, by 7.2% over NCN, and by 12.4% over BUDDY. The polynomial-filter variant OCNP maintains comparable overall performance (e.g., 97.65 HR@20 on DDI, 74.87 HR@100 on PPA) while reducing computational overhead.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OCN decisively outperforms all traditional heuristics (CN, AA, RA) and GAE-based models (GCN, SAGE) by large margins across every dataset. Among GNN-based baselines, OCN surpasses the SF-then-MPNN methods SEAL and NBFnet (which OOMs on three datasets) and the SF-and-MPNN methods Neo-GNN and BUDDY by an average of 12.4% over BUDDY. The closest competitor is NCNC (the MPNN-then-SF architecture OCN builds upon), which OCN beats on five of seven datasets, with the largest gaps on DDI (+13.31 HR@20), Collab (+5.82 HR@50), and PPA (+8.37 HR@100); NCNC retains a slight edge on Citation2 (89.12 vs. 88.57 MRR). On PPA, OCN also surpasses the large-scale GraphGPT model (69.79 vs. 68.76 HR@100). The polynomial-filter variant OCNP trades a small drop on Collab and Citation2 for a notable gain on PPA (74.87 vs. 69.79) while reducing inference time and GPU memory.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on NCN (Wang et al., ICLR 2024), adopting its MPNN-then-SF architecture where structural features guide pooling of pre-computed MPNN representations. The key conceptual departure is diagnosing two specific pathologies in higher-order common neighbors—redundancy across orders and over-smoothing—and addressing them via Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization and path-count normalization, respectively. The polynomial-filter variant (OCNP) draws on spectral GNN theory (Wang and Zhang, ICML 2022) to approximate orthogonalization at lower cost, while the normalization trick is shown to degenerate to the classic Resource Allocation heuristic at k=1, connecting the method to the long lineage of CN-based link prediction heuristics (CN, AA, RA).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical result that, without normalization, the latent-space distance bound does not tighten with increasing k (while with normalization it does) provides a principled explanation for why prior methods using higher-order CNs have not consistently improved performance—a gap that has been empirically observed but not formally explained. The 7.7% average improvement over the strongest baseline (NCNC) on OGB datasets is substantial for link prediction, and the scalability analysis showing OCN/OCNP match NCN's single-pass MPNN cost makes the approach practical for large graphs. Open questions include whether the instability observed with 3-hop CNs can be resolved, whether the theoretical bounds are tight in practice on real-world graphs, and whether the orthogonalization strategy generalizes to other pairwise structural features beyond common neighbors.
