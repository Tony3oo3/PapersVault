## Problem

The overwhelming majority of LLM benchmarks evaluate models on independent, i.i.d. samples in a parallel paradigm, measuring only static abilities. This leaves a critical dimension unexplored: a model's capacity to learn and adapt within a specific task (learning capability) and the speed at which this learning occurs (learning efficiency)—fundamental indicators of human-like intelligence that the prevailing evaluation paradigm is inherently unable to capture.

## Input / Output

The model receives a sequence of seven interrelated problems from the same task type, solving them one at a time. In the feedback learning setting, each subsequent problem's prompt includes all prior problems, the model's own previous solutions, and detailed rubric-based feedback from a judge (GPT-4o); in the demonstration setting, it receives prior problems with canonical answers instead. Outputs are scored as binary correctness per problem via instance-level rubrics evaluated by an LLM judge, then aggregated into six learning metrics (Acc, slope k, P_first, P_offset, N_consec, Acc_pw-K) and a position-wise accuracy curve across the seven positions.

## Method

EvaLearn introduces a sequential evaluation paradigm where 648 hand-crafted problems are organized into 182 sequences of seven, spanning six task types (summarization, classification, extraction, logical reasoning, mathematical reasoning, sequential reasoning). Each problem is accompanied by a human-written instance-level rubric, and correctness is assessed by GPT-4o as a judge following the rubric (validated at >95% agreement with human annotators). The key design principle is that the six evaluation metrics are decoupled from the specific learning method employed—whether the model learns from demonstrations or from feedback—making the benchmark extensible to arbitrary learning strategies. The metrics jointly capture learning speed (slope of the fitted position-wise accuracy curve), initial competence (P_first), true learning onset after discounting pre-existing knowledge (P_offset), stability of consecutive correct solutions (N_consec), and post-warmup adaptation (Acc_pw-K), providing a multi-faceted quantification of dynamic learning that is not reducible to a single aggregate accuracy score.

## Results

On EvaLearn's overall accuracy (averaged across 182 sequences × 7 problems), Gemini-2.5-Pro achieves the highest zero-shot performance at 68.5%, while OpenAI-o3-mini shows the largest improvement from sequential feedback learning, jumping from 54.3% to 64.8% (+10.5 pp). Five of eleven models benefit from sequential solving (o3-mini +10.5%, Claude-3.7-Sonnet +7.2%, Claude-3.7-Sonnet-Thinking +6.2%, Qwen2.5-32b-Instruct +1.7%, Gemini-2.5-Flash +1.2%), while four show declines, most notably DeepSeek-R1 (−9.3 pp) and Doubao-1.5-Thinking-Pro (−5.5 pp). On the slope metric k (learning efficiency), Claude-3.7-Sonnet achieves the highest value (k=2.08), and thinking-based models generally show steeper, more stable accuracy curves than non-thinking counterparts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks eleven frontier LLMs (five non-thinking, six thinking-based) on EvaLearn's sequential problem-solving paradigm. OpenAI-o3-mini is the standout learner, achieving the largest overall gain (+10.5 pp) and the best logical-reasoning improvement (+14.1 pp), outperforming DeepSeek-R1 (which drops 9.3 pp overall) despite R1's higher zero-shot score. Claude-3.7-Sonnet (non-thinking) shows the highest learning efficiency slope (k=2.08) and strong gains in sequential reasoning (+16.3 pp) and mathematical reasoning (+15.6 pp), while its thinking variant Claude-3.7-Sonnet-Thinking improves classification by +13.5 pp. GPT-4o shows minimal overall change (+0.1 pp) but a notable +18.0 pp jump in mathematical reasoning, whereas DeepSeek-R1 and Doubao-1.5-Thinking-Pro suffer the largest overall declines (−9.3 pp and −5.5 pp respectively), indicating negative transfer from sequential context. Gemini-2.5-Pro maintains the highest absolute accuracy (68.5% zero-shot) but shows only a slight −1.3 pp drop, suggesting its strong static ability partially compensates for limited learning gains. Across all models, mathematical reasoning and classification tasks consistently show the largest positive learning effects, while summarization tends to degrade (7 of 9 models decline), and sequential reasoning remains the hardest task for most models to improve on.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant parallel (i.i.d.) evaluation paradigm used by benchmarks like MMLU, HellaSwag, and TruthfulQA, which measure only static model abilities. It also distinguishes itself from interactive/multi-turn evaluation (WebArena, AgentBench, τ-bench) by requiring models to solve a fixed sequence of same-type problems and measuring how performance evolves across positions, rather than evaluating multi-turn dialogue or agent actions. The evaluation mechanism builds on LLM-as-a-judge with instance-level rubrics, while the learning paradigms tested (demonstration learning and feedback learning) draw on in-context learning and Reflexion-style verbal reinforcement, respectively. The core conceptual contribution is reframing LLM evaluation from a static snapshot to a dynamic learning-curve measurement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

EvaLearn introduces a genuinely new axis of model comparison—learning capability and efficiency—that is empirically decoupled from static benchmark scores, suggesting that current leaderboards miss an important dimension of model potential. Key open questions include what architectural or training factors drive learning-capability differences, whether the 7-problem sequences are long enough to capture meaningful learning dynamics, and how the benchmark extends to open-ended tasks where rubric-based evaluation is harder to construct. The finding that summarization performance often *declines* with feedback (7/9 models) and that the strongest zero-shot model (DeepSeek-R1) suffers the largest sequential drop (−9.3%) raises important questions about when and why experience transfer fails.
