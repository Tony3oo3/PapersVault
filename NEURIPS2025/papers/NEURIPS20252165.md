## Problem

Prior theoretical work on Transformers has focused on representational power (approximation capacity), but the purely statistical question—whether Transformers need fewer samples than feedforward or recurrent networks even with infinite compute—remained open. This paper asks: what function class can Transformers learn with fewer samples compared to feedforward and recurrent networks, and what architectural mechanism (attention) enables this advantage?

## Input / Output

The input is a prompt consisting of N d-dimensional tokens x_i paired with index vectors t_i ∈ [N]^q that specify which q tokens are relevant at each position. The output is a sequence of N scalars y_i = g(x_{t_{i1}}, …, x_{t_{iq}}), where g is a link function approximated by a two-layer ReLU network. The critical structural feature is that the sparsity pattern is dynamic: the relevant token positions change from prompt to prompt and are described in the input itself, making this a sparse retrieval problem rather than a fixed-subset regression.

## Method

The paper introduces the q-Sparse Token Regression (qSTR) model and establishes a three-way sample complexity hierarchy. For Transformers, they prove that H ≥ q attention heads suffice to retrieve the q relevant tokens at each position (each head targets one token via a sparse query-key matrix), yielding sample complexity polylog in N through covering-number and Rademacher complexity bounds; conversely, H < q heads provably fail even with infinite samples. For RNNs, they develop a metric-entropy argument showing that approximating qSTR requires output-projection norms scaling as √N, which—combined with a min-norm ERM analysis—yields an N^Ω(1) sample complexity lower bound, though RNNs can still learn the simplified case (constant t across positions) with polylog(N) samples. For FFNs, they exploit the stationarity condition of first-order optimization to show that first-layer weights must lie in the span of the n training examples, producing an Ω(Nd) lower bound even for the simplified model. The core insight is that attention provides an input-dependent, adaptive routing mechanism that neither fixed-weight feedforward projections nor sequential hidden-state updates can replicate.

## Results

The paper establishes a sample complexity hierarchy across three architectures on the q-Sparse Token Regression (qSTR) model. A single-layer Transformer with H ≥ q attention heads achieves a sample complexity of Õ(√(m_g·q(d+q) + q³ + qd²)/n), which is almost independent of sequence length N (only polylogarithmic dependence). In contrast, RNNs require Ω(N^c) samples for some constant c > 0 to learn general qSTR (Theorem 7), and FFNs require Ω(Nd) samples even for the simpler simple-qSTR variant (Theorem 9). Additionally, Transformers with H < q heads provably cannot learn qSTR even with infinitely many samples (Proposition 4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three architectures — single-layer multi-head Transformers, bidirectional RNNs, and fully-connected feedforward networks — on two variants of the qSTR sequence-to-sequence regression task. Transformers dominate on the full qSTR model: they achieve near-N-independent sample complexity with H ≥ q heads, while RNNs provably require N^Ω(1) samples (Theorem 7) and FFNs require Ω(Nd) samples (Theorem 9), regardless of model size. On the simpler simple-qSTR variant (where the relevant token positions are fixed across all output positions), RNNs close the gap with Transformers, achieving polylog(N) sample complexity (Theorem 5), while FFNs still require Ω(Nd) samples. This establishes a strict three-way hierarchy: Transformer ≥ RNN > FFN in statistical efficiency. The empirical validation (Figure 1) confirms these predictions: on 1STR, both FFNs and RNNs show large sample complexity growth with N, while on simple-1STR, RNNs perform closer to Transformers with a much milder N-dependence than FFNs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of work on Transformer expressivity (SHT23, SHT24, WWHL24), which established *representational* separations showing Transformers can approximate certain sequence functions with logarithmic size while other architectures require polynomial size. Here, the authors repurpose the q-Sparse Token Regression (qSTR) model—introduced in SHT23 as a representational benchmark—for a purely *statistical* separation, asking how sample complexity scales with sequence length N under infinite compute. The generalization analysis builds on covering-number and Rademacher-complexity techniques from EGKZ22, TT23, and Tru24, but refines them to handle the dynamic sparsity structure. The RNN lower bound introduces a new metric-entropy argument for norm-based representational cost, going beyond the bit-complexity lower bounds of SHT23.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes a clean three-way hierarchy (Transformer ≻ RNN ≻ FFN) in sample complexity for dynamic sparse retrieval, showing that the advantage of attention is fundamentally statistical rather than merely computational—FFNs need Ω(Nd) samples and RNNs need N^Ω(1) samples even with infinite width, while Transformers achieve polylog(N). This provides a theoretical grounding for the observed length-generalization gap in practice and suggests that the attention mechanism's inductive bias toward sparse retrieval is what makes long-context learning sample-efficient. Key open questions include whether the bounds are tight (the information-theoretic limit is Ω(qd)), how optimization dynamics affect the realized sample complexity, and whether depth in Transformers further improves the N-dependence.
