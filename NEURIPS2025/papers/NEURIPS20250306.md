## Problem

Audio-Language Models (ALMs) introduce new vulnerability vectors through the audio modality that existing defenses—whether transferred from traditional audio adversarial attacks or text-based LLM jailbreak defenses—fail to adequately address. The paper tackles the gap of having no dedicated, principled defense framework for ALM-specific jailbreaks, motivated by the observation that the behavioral diversity and inherent complexity of ALMs require modality-aware protection strategies.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250306/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on 30-second audio waveforms (16 kHz sampling rate) that are first converted to Mel-spectrograms via the Whisper encoder. The output is a universal Shortcut Activation Perturbation (SAP) of the same 30-second duration, constrained to an ℓ∞ budget of 0.5, which is added to the Mel-spectrogram at inference time. The perturbation is applied only to a sparse subset of k=48 Mel-frequency bins (out of 128 total) as determined by the M-GSM mask, and the ALM then produces a safe text refusal response. No model retraining is required; the SAP is a single fixed signal applied uniformly across all inputs.

## Method

ALMGuard hypothesizes that well-aligned ALMs inherently possess safety-aligned shortcuts—latent input sensitivities that, when triggered, steer the model toward safe behavior. It discovers a universal Shortcut Activation Perturbation (SAP) by optimizing a single acoustic signal via PGD to minimize a cross-entropy safety loss (driving outputs toward a fixed refusal template) over a training set of jailbreak examples. The key technical innovation is the Mel-Gradient Sparse Mask (M-GSM), which computes per-bin gradient sensitivity ratios s_f = g_f^safe / (g_f^ASR + ε) to identify frequency bands that are highly influential for jailbreak mitigation yet largely inconsequential for benign speech understanding, then restricts the perturbation to only the top-k such bins. This targeted masking indirectly preserves model utility without requiring an explicit ASR auxiliary loss. The paper further provides theoretical guarantees: a Hoeffding-based generalization bound showing low empirical safety risk implies low population risk on unseen attacks, and a Lipschitz-based deviation bound showing benign-task loss change is proportional to ε·d_k·L_enc·G_max.

## Results

ALMGuard reduces the average Success Rate of Attack (SRoA) to 14.6% across four SOTA ALMs (Qwen2-Audio, LLaMA-Omni, Lyra-Base, Qwen2.5-Omni) and six jailbreak attacks, outperforming all five baselines. Against the two SOTA ALM-specific attacks, AdvWave and Gupta et al., ALMGuard achieves average SRoA of 4.6% and 1.9% respectively (vs. 53.5% and 29.3% undefended). On benign tasks, ALMGuard increases WER by only 1.85% on Qwen2-Audio (LibriSpeech) and decreases AIR-Bench-Chat RQS by 0.56, while Self-Reminder and ICD degrade WER by 26.27% and 8.98% respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ALMGuard outperforms Self-Reminder [51] on 3 of 4 models in average SRoA (16.1% vs 27.6% on Qwen2-Audio; 8.1% vs 35.0% on LLaMA-Omni; 20.7% vs 24.5% on Qwen2.5-Omni), with a near-tie on Lyra-Base (13.5% vs 13.9%). Against ICD [47], ALMGuard achieves substantially lower SRoA on Qwen2-Audio (16.1% vs 37.6%) and LLaMA-Omni (8.1% vs 18.2%), but ICD edges out ALMGuard on Lyra-Base (9.1% vs 13.5%), likely due to Lyra-Base's sensitivity to in-context prompts. On benign-task utility, ALMGuard is far superior: it increases LibriSpeech WER by only 1.85% on Qwen2-Audio, whereas Self-Reminder inflates WER to 33.12% (+26.27%) and ICD to 15.83% (+8.98%), because both text-based defenses trigger over-refusal on normal ASR inputs. ALMGuard also achieves the best overall average SRoA (14.6%) compared to ICD (21.6%) and Self-Reminder (25.3%), and it is the only method that reduces AdvWave-P SRoA to 0% on Qwen2.5-Omni.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the shortcut-learning concept from computer vision (where shortcuts are typically harmful artifacts) to identify *beneficial* safety-aligned pathways in Audio-Language Models, framing ALM jailbreak defense as the activation of these latent pathways via a universal acoustic perturbation (SAP). It explicitly departs from two prior defense lineages—traditional audio adversarial defenses (Gaussian noise, smoothing, downsampling) and text-based LLM jailbreak defenses (Self-Reminder, ICD)—which the authors show are largely ineffective against ALM-specific attacks like AdvWave. The M-GSM mechanism, which uses a gradient-sensitivity ratio between safety and ASR objectives to select sparse Mel-frequency bins, is a novel contribution that has no direct analogue in prior audio or text defense literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As ALMs are deployed in mission-critical systems (robotics, real-time assistants), the absence of dedicated jailbreak defenses is a significant safety gap that this paper addresses as the first ALM-specific framework. The near-zero-cost, plug-and-play nature (a single precomputed perturbation applied at inference) makes it practically deployable without retraining. Key limitations remain: defense against semantic-based attacks (PAIR-Audio, PAP-Audio) is weaker than against acoustic-based ones, and the approach relies on the assumption that safety shortcuts are shared across models and attacks. Open questions include whether the safety-shortcut hypothesis generalizes to future ALM architectures, how to extend the framework to explicitly target semantic-level adversarial intent, and whether combining SAPs with complementary text-level defenses yields a more comprehensive guardrail.
