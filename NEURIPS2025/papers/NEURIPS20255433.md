## Problem

Most OOD detection methods either discard training data after deployment or assume centralized access to both training and test data, an assumption that rarely holds in medical imaging where privacy, size, and proprietary constraints prevent shipping training databases to deployment sites. This paper asks whether OOD detection can directly compare test samples against the original training data without transferring any raw data or embeddings to the point of deployment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255433/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single target medical image (224×224 for dermatology, ultrasound, and chest X-ray; 50×50 patches for histopathology) at the deployment node, a pre-trained ResNet18 classifier for the primary task, and the full source training dataset held on a separate remote node. Only model parameters are exchanged between nodes; no raw images or feature embeddings are shared. The output is an OOD score equal to the number of communication rounds R required for a binary isolation classifier to converge, with a fixed threshold s* producing a binary ID/OOD label. In the class-conditional variant, the predicted class from the primary model is also transmitted to condition the source-node sampling.

## Method

DIsoN trains a binary classifier to isolate a single target sample from the source training data, using the convergence speed (number of communication rounds) as the OOD score: OOD samples are easier to separate and converge in fewer rounds, while ID samples require more rounds. The training is decentralized across two nodes — a Source Node holding the training data and a Target Node holding the single test sample — which exchange only model parameters over multiple rounds, with each node performing local SGD updates followed by weighted aggregation (θ^(r+1) = α·θ_S + β·θ_T). A key theoretical result (Proposition 3.1) proves that with E=1 local step and aggregation weights α=|B_s|/(|B_s|+N), β=N/(|B_s|+N), the decentralized update exactly replicates the centralized Isolation Network gradient step. The class-conditional variant (CC-DIsoN) further restricts source-node sampling to only the predicted class of the target, reducing intra-class variability and making ID isolation harder. Practical design choices include Instance Normalization (instead of Batch Normalization) to handle the single-sample target node, stochastic data augmentation to prevent trivial memorization, and a ResNet18 backbone initialized from the pre-trained primary model.

## Results

On the three artifact-detection benchmarks (Chest X-Ray, Dermatology, Breast Ultrasound), CC-DIsoN achieves an average AUROC of 80.0% and FPR95 of 59.2%, outperforming the best baseline CIDER (70.2% AUROC, 72.8% FPR95) by 9.8% AUROC and 13.6% FPR95. On the MIDOG histopathology benchmark, CC-DIsoN achieves 69.6% average AUROC in the challenging near-OOD setting (vs. 61.2% for PALM and 62.4% for ViM) and near-perfect 98.3% AUROC / 4.4% FPR95 in the far-OOD setting. Across all 12 OOD detection tasks, CC-DIsoN consistently achieves the best or second-best AUROC and the best FPR95.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CC-DIsoN outperforms all seven baselines on average across the three artifact-detection benchmarks. Against the strongest baseline CIDER [30] (70.2% avg AUROC, 72.8% FPR95), CC-DIsoN improves AUROC by 9.8 points and reduces FPR95 by 13.6 points. Post-hoc methods (MSP, MDS, fDBD, ViM) all cluster around 61–64% average AUROC with FPR95 above 78%, indicating they struggle to detect domain-specific artifacts like rulers, pacemakers, and text annotations. Deep iForest [40] performs worst overall (53.4% AUROC), consistent with prior findings that tree-based isolation methods degrade on high-dimensional imaging data. On the MIDOG histopathology benchmark, CC-DIsoN leads in the near-OOD setting (69.6% avg AUROC vs. PALM's 61.2% and ViM's 62.4%) and matches PALM in the far-OOD setting (both ~98.3% AUROC) while achieving substantially lower FPR95 (4.4% vs. 11.6%). Notably, CC-DIsoN's advantage is largest on Dermatology (89.5% AUROC vs. CIDER's 82.0%) and Chest X-Ray (84.9% vs. 70.5%), where artifact patterns are visually distinct from the training distribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

DIsoN repurposes the core intuition of Isolation Forests—measuring how easily a sample can be separated from training data—but replaces decision trees with a neural network binary classifier whose convergence rate serves as the OOD score. The decentralized training framework draws on Federated Learning's parameter-exchange paradigm, but differs fundamentally from FL-based OOD detection (e.g., [44, 24]) in that it trains a per-sample throwaway classifier rather than a generalizable model across distribution-shifted nodes. The paper departs from the dominant OOD detection paradigm (post-hoc scoring or embedding-space regularization) by arguing that direct comparison with the original training data is more informative than indirect representations like summary statistics or prototypes, and achieves this without violating data-sharing constraints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DIsoN addresses a practical gap that most OOD detection methods ignore: training data cannot be shipped to deployment sites due to privacy, legal, or size constraints, yet direct comparison with training data is arguably the most informative signal for OOD detection. The paper envisions a new service model where ML providers offer remote, secure OOD detection using their training data, which could become a standard component of safety-critical ML deployment. Key limitations include the per-sample runtime (40s–4min), the requirement for a persistent remote node holding training data, and the single-target-at-a-time design; extending to batch processing and reducing communication rounds are natural next steps. Whether the convergence-based scoring generalizes beyond the tested medical imaging domains and to non-CNN backbones remains open.
