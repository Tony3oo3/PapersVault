## Problem

Existing LLM benchmarks for scientific capability either evaluate single isolated skills or one-shot problem-solving, failing to test the iterative loop of experiment design, data collection, and result interpretation that defines real scientific discovery. Wet-lab experimentation is prohibitively expensive in expertise, time, and equipment, making it infeasible to generate in-the-loop experimental data for benchmarking. The paper asks: how can we create a scalable, automated environment where an LLM agent must iteratively design perturbations, observe simulated responses, and refine its mechanistic hypothesis?

## Input / Output

The agent receives a partial SBML model containing all species, compartments, units, and non-reaction-specific parameters, but with all reactions and reaction-specific parameters removed and identifiers anonymized to 4-character strings. Across up to 20 iterations, the agent can (1) write Python code in a persistent shell (numpy, pandas, scipy, sklearn, libsbml) to analyze accumulated time-series data, (2) request experiments by specifying initial-concentration perturbations to any non-boundary species, and (3) submit a final SBML model. The output is a complete SBML model scored on three complementary metrics: Network Topology Score (F1 over pairwise species interactions), Reaction Matching Score (precision/recall/F1 over matched reactant-product-modifier sets), and Simulation Trajectory Error (SMAPE between predicted and true species concentration time-series).

## Method

SciGym uses 350 peer-reviewed SBML models from BioModels as a 'dry lab': when the agent requests a perturbation, the environment modifies the hidden reference SBML, simulates it with Tellurium/libRoadRunner, and returns the resulting time-series data. The agent is a ReAct-style LLM operating in a Thoughts-Actions-Observations loop, deliberately not told the evaluation metrics to preserve open-endedness. The benchmark is split into 'small' (137 models, <10 reactions) and 'large' (213 models, up to 400 reactions) to probe scaling with system complexity. A key design choice is the perturbation interface: agents can only change initial concentrations of species, mirroring a common and cost-effective biological intervention, rather than having access to arbitrary system modifications. The paper evaluates six frontier LLMs (Gemini-2.5-Pro/Flash, GPT-4.1/mini, Claude-3.7-Sonnet/3.5-Haiku) and finds that while pro models outperform mini counterparts, all models degrade sharply with increasing reaction count, overfit to observed initial conditions, and struggle substantially with modifier relationships compared to reactant-product connections.

## Results

On SciGym-small (137 SBML systems), Gemini-2.5-Pro achieves the best overall performance with a Simulation Trajectory Error (STE) of 0.3212 and Reaction Matching Score (RMS) F1 of 0.3383 (without modifiers) / 0.1817 (with modifiers). Claude-3.7-Sonnet follows with STE 0.3615 and RMS F1 of 0.3047/0.1688, while GPT-4.1 trails at STE 0.4611 and RMS F1 of 0.3038/0.1740. All mini variants (Gemini-2.5-Flash, GPT-4.1-mini, Claude-3.5-Haiku) underperform their pro counterparts, with Claude-3.5-Haiku showing the weakest results (STE 0.6281, RMS F1 0.0987). Performance degrades substantially as system complexity increases, and all models struggle markedly with modifier relationships (NTS precision <0.12) compared to reactant-product edges (NTS precision 0.37–0.45).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but instead benchmarks six frontier LLMs (three families × pro/mini) against each other and against a zero-shot prompting baseline. All six agents outperform their respective zero-shot baselines on both STE and RMS, confirming that iterative experiment design and data analysis provide genuine gains over one-shot model recovery. Within each family, pro variants consistently beat mini variants: Gemini-2.5-Pro reduces STE by 23% over Gemini-2.5-Flash (0.3212 vs. 0.4181) and nearly doubles RMS F1 without modifiers (0.3383 vs. 0.2005); GPT-4.1 improves STE by 23% over GPT-4.1-mini (0.4611 vs. 0.6007); Claude-3.7-Sonnet reduces STE by 42% over Claude-3.5-Haiku (0.3615 vs. 0.6281). Gemini-2.5-Pro is the clear leader overall, outperforming Claude-3.7-Sonnet by 11% in STE and 10% in RMS F1 (w/o modifiers). A key finding across all models is the severe modifier gap: NTS precision for reactant-product edges ranges 0.37–0.45, while reactant-modifier and modifier-product edges drop to 0.00–0.12, indicating that identifying regulatory relationships remains a major bottleneck for LLM scientific reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the ReAct agent framework (Yao et al., 2023) for the LLM's reasoning-action loop, and repurposes the BioModels repository of peer-reviewed SBML models as a 'dry lab' simulator to generate experimental data in the loop. It departs from prior AI-scientist benchmarks (DiscoveryWorld, BioDiscoveryAgent, Lab-Bench) that either evaluate single skills, use fixed observed datasets without perturbation, or rely on predefined genetic perturbations, by introducing an open-ended iterative loop where the agent designs its own experiments, writes analysis code, and progressively refines a mechanistic hypothesis. It also distinguishes itself from causal discovery benchmarks (CausalBench) by requiring active intervention design rather than passive analysis of static data, and from equation-discovery benchmarks (LLM-SR, ChemReasoner) by using structured biological models with rich species relationships rather than scalar reward functions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SciGym is the first benchmark to evaluate the full scientific discovery cycle—hypothesis formation, experiment design, data analysis, and model refinement—in an open-ended loop with simulated biological systems, filling a clear gap where wet-lab costs previously made in-the-loop evaluation infeasible. Key open questions include how performance changes with realistic measurement noise and sparse sampling, whether more powerful or biologically diverse perturbations (beyond initial-concentration changes) substantially improve recovery, and whether the observed overfitting to specific experimental conditions reflects a fundamental limitation in LLM mechanistic reasoning or simply insufficient interaction budget. The benchmark is also extensible as BioModels grows, and the released 350-model dataset provides a reusable testbed for future agent architectures.
