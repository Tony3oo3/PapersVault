## Problem

Standard supervised fine-tuning treats each training error as a transient signal, consumed and discarded immediately after the gradient update, so the final model retains no explicit memory of where, how, or why errors occurred during its learning trajectory. This paper asks whether retaining and leveraging the model's own internal learning signals during fine-tuning can close the train-test misalignment gap without modifying the training objective, data distribution, or requiring external feedback.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255345/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The Copilot model consumes three signals from the Mistake Log: the Pilot's input representations, its layer-wise hidden states (mean-pooled across all decoder layers), and the token-level probability discrepancies (ground-truth minus predicted distribution). It outputs raw, unnormalized logits in vocabulary space that are additively fused with the Pilot's softmax-normalized logits via a tunable scaling factor λ, producing a rectified probability distribution for next-token decoding. Notably, the Copilot bypasses the softmax layer entirely, operating as a regression head that predicts distributional error rather than a valid probability distribution, which is why an RMSE loss is used instead of cross-entropy.

## Method

The Transformer Copilot framework introduces a Mistake Log that systematically records the Pilot model's input representations, layer-wise hidden states, and token-level prediction errors throughout the entire fine-tuning trajectory. A separate Copilot model, initialized from the Pilot's decoder, is jointly trained each round to predict these token-level discrepancies using an RMSE loss, with a modified cross-attention mechanism that lets it attend to both the Pilot's input context and internal processing dynamics stored in the Mistake Log. During inference, the Copilot's output is additively fused with the Pilot's logits (scaled by λ) to produce a rectified distribution for collaborative auto-regressive generation. The paper proves that under mild conditions (Copilot error bounded by the Pilot's bias-variance sum), the rectified prediction achieves strictly lower expected error than the Pilot alone, and that the Copilot need not match the Pilot's accuracy in isolation. Empirically, a lightweight Copilot (e.g., 1B) paired with a larger Pilot (e.g., 7B) outperforms frontier models with 4B more parameters while adding only ~4% computational overhead, and the Copilot transfers to new Pilot models of the same architecture without additional training.

## Results

T-Copilot consistently improves Pilot model performance across 10 commonsense and arithmetic reasoning tasks by 2.0%–34.5% relative improvement. The headline result is that Qwen2.5-7B+T-Copilot-3B (10B total params) achieves 88.2% average commonsense accuracy and 81.7% average arithmetic accuracy, surpassing Qwen2.5-14B (87.4% / 80.8%) with 4B fewer parameters. Similarly, LLaMA-3.2-3B+T-Copilot-3B (6B) outperforms LLaMA-3.1-8B (8B) on both task categories (82.3% vs 82.1% commonsense; 66.5% vs 65.9% arithmetic). The method incurs only ~4% marginal time overhead during fine-tuning and inference compared to the Pilot alone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LLaMA-3.1-8B [21], LLaMA-3.2-3B+T-Copilot-3B (6B total) wins on both commonsense (82.3% vs 82.1%) and arithmetic (66.5% vs 65.9%) with 2B fewer parameters. Against Qwen2.5-14B [90], Qwen2.5-7B+T-Copilot-3B (10B total) wins on commonsense (88.2% vs 87.4%) and arithmetic (81.7% vs 80.8%) with 4B fewer parameters. Against LLaMA-Pro-8B and Mistral-Pro-8B [84], LLaMA-3.2-3B+T-Copilot-3B (6B) outperforms both on commonsense (82.3% vs 80.8%/79.8%) and arithmetic (66.5% vs 62.9%/61.7%). Against the MoE baseline Ministral-8B [71], the same 6B T-Copilot configuration wins on both metrics (82.3% vs 81.7% commonsense; 66.5% vs 65.9% arithmetic). Against MergeKit-9B [26], LLaMA-3.1-8B+T-Copilot-1B (9B) achieves 84.0% commonsense and 67.8% arithmetic, beating MergeKit-9B's 82.1% and 66.9% at the same parameter budget.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard SFT paradigm where each training error is treated as a transient gradient signal consumed and discarded. While self-refinement methods (Self-Refine, Reflexion, REST) improve generation through iterative prompting or external feedback at inference time, T-Copilot instead captures the model's own internal learning signals—hidden states and token-level probability discrepancies—during fine-tuning and trains an auxiliary Copilot model to rectify logits at inference. The conceptual lineage draws from human reflective learning (maintaining a mistake log) and from interpretability tools like the Logits Lens, but the specific mechanism of jointly training a transduction network on the Mistake Log with a modified cross-attention over Pilot hidden states is new. The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 4.1) formalizes when this rectification strictly reduces expected error, providing a principled justification absent in prior heuristic self-correction approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical, low-overhead (≈4%) way to close the train-test gap in SFT without modifying the training objective, data distribution, or requiring external feedback—making it broadly applicable to existing fine-tuning pipelines. The transferability result (a Copilot trained on one Pilot model improves a different Pilot of the same architecture without retraining) is practically valuable for organizations maintaining multiple model checkpoints. Open questions include how the approach interacts with RLHF/DPO post-training, whether the Mistake Log buffer size (128 rounds) is sufficient for long training runs, and whether the modest 2–6% gains on 7B+ models will grow with further scaling or remain bounded by the Pilot's inherent capacity.
