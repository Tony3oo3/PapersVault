## Problem

Policy gradient methods have achieved remarkable practical success in imperfect-information extensive-form games (Poker, Stratego, Hanabi), yet no prior work provides a provable polynomial-time convergence guarantee to Nash equilibrium for such games. The two fundamental obstacles are that value iteration (the standard subroutine in Markov game RL) fails under imperfect information, and the policy optimization landscape is highly nonconvex. The paper asks whether policy gradient methods can provably converge to an ε-approximate Nash equilibrium in a polynomial number of iterations and trajectory samples.

## Input / Output

The method takes a two-player zero-sum imperfect-information EFG specified by a game tree of height D, information sets partitioning each player's histories, per-infoset action sets, and a terminal payoff function, along with a policy parametrization choice (direct truncated-simplice parameters or softmax parameters restricted to bounded polytopes X_R, Θ_R). A decentralized exploration scheme (Assumption 2) requires each player to reach every subsequence with probability at least γ/|H|, which directly controls the gradient-domination modulus. The output is a last-iterate ε-approximate Nash equilibrium policy profile, with the number of iterations and per-step trajectory samples polynomial in 1/ε, 1/ε_trunc, 1/γ, |H|, |S_1|, |S_2|, A, B, and 2^{D(T)}.

## Method

The central insight is hidden concavity: EFG utilities are concave in sequence-form strategies, and after adding a bidilated regularizer (which upgrades concavity to strong concavity) combined with a uniform lower bound on information-set reachability, each player's utility satisfies the proximal Polyak-Lojasiewicz (pPL) condition in behavioral-strategy space. This is established by bounding the Lipschitz modulus of the inverse sequence-form-to-policy map, which depends polynomially on the exploration parameter γ. Under the pPL condition, alternating gradient descent-ascent with tuned asymmetric stepsizes (one player updates faster) converges in the last iterate via a Lyapunov-function argument, bypassing the regret-minimization framework typical of EFG analysis. Three algorithmic variants are proven: (1) direct parametrization with Euclidean bidilated regularization, (2) softmax parametrization with entropic bidilated regularization, and (3) natural policy gradient (Fisher-preconditioned mirror descent) with softmax and entropy regularization, the last of which reduces to multiplicative-weight policy updates in policy space.

## Results

The paper evaluates Alt-RegNPG (with two variants: diminishing regularization and discounted KL from a moving reference policy) on four imperfect-information EFGs—Kuhn Poker, Leduc Poker, 2×2 Abrupt Dark Hex, and Liar's Dice—measuring exploitability over training iterations. The key finding is that exploitability (defined as max_{π1'} V^{π1',π2} − min_{π2'} V^{π1,π2'}) decreases monotonically over time for all three Alt-RegNPG variants, confirming the theoretical last-iterate convergence guarantee. The method's exploitability curves are competitive with both CFR and MMD across all four games, with the paper noting it "compares well" with these baselines. No specific numerical exploitability values are reported in the text; results are presented as convergence curves in Figure 1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Alt-RegNPG against two baselines: CFR (Counterfactual Regret Minimization, Zinkevich et al. 2007) and MMD (Magnetic Mirror Descent, Sokota et al. 2022). On all four games (Kuhn Poker, Leduc Poker, 2×2 Abrupt Dark Hex, Liar's Dice), Alt-RegNPG achieves exploitability curves that are competitive with both CFR and MMD, with the paper stating the method "compares well" with these baselines. A key differentiator is that Alt-RegNPG provides the first provable last-iterate convergence guarantee with polynomial dependence on 1/ε and game parameters, whereas CFR only guarantees average-iterate convergence and MMD has no convergence guarantee in EFGs. The alternating update scheme (as opposed to simultaneous updates in both baselines) is central to achieving the last-iterate convergence result. The paper does not report a clear numerical win or loss on any specific game; rather, the contribution is the theoretical guarantee combined with empirically competitive performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate research threads: policy gradient convergence theory for Markov games/MDPs (Mei et al. 2020, Cen et al. 2022a, Kalogiannis et al. 2025) and equilibrium computation in imperfect-information EFGs (Liu et al. 2024, Sokota et al. 2022). The key conceptual bridge is showing that EFG utilities, while nonconvex in behavioral-strategy space, are hidden-concave via the sequence-form reparametrization—a structural analogy to occupancy-measure concavity in MDPs that had not been exploited for policy gradient convergence. The bidilated regularizer from Liu et al. (2024) is repurposed as the mechanism that upgrades weak gradient domination to the strong proximal-PL condition, and the convergence proof departs from the regret-minimization arguments standard in EFG literature in favor of a Lyapunov-based last-iterate analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first provable polynomial-time, last-iterate convergence guarantee for policy gradient methods in imperfect-information EFGs, closing a gap that has limited the theoretical understanding of why practical MARL algorithms succeed in games like Poker and Stratego. The hidden-concavity/gradient-domination framework provides a principled template for extending these guarantees to neural-network-parameterized policies in large-scale EFGs where tabular CFR is infeasible. Key limitations include the exponential-in-depth (though polynomial-in-game-size) dependence, the requirement for mini-batching, and the authors' own admission that the analysis is loose; open questions include variance-reduced variants, limit-point characterization, and whether the benign-nonconvexity lens refines results for general nonconvex games.
