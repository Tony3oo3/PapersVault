## Problem

All meta-learning approaches operate under the mini-batch episodic training framework, which naturally exposes task identity information, yet this signal has not been systematically exploited as additional supervision to improve generalizability. The paper asks whether a meta-learner can be trained to align models from the same task while discriminating models from different tasks—emulating the alignment and discrimination abilities intrinsic to human fast learning—and whether such a contrastive objective can be made universal across problem types and meta-learner architectures.

## Input / Output

During meta-training, ConML consumes a batch of B tasks, each split into a support set D^tr and a query set D^val. For each task, it additionally samples K subsets κ_k from D^tr ∪ D^val to form positive pairs, while cross-task pairs serve as negatives. The model representation ψ(g(D;θ)) is task-type-specific: updated weights for optimization-based learners, concatenated class prototypes for metric-based learners, hypernetwork outputs for amortization-based learners, and a probe-token forward pass for ICL models. The output is a combined loss L_ConML = L_e + λ(d^in − d^out) that backpropagates through the meta-learner, producing task-specific predictions (class labels for classification, scalar outputs for regression/ICL) at meta-test time.

## Method

ConML introduces a contrastive meta-objective in model space: a projection function ψ maps each meta-learner's output h = g(D;θ) to a fixed-length vector e ∈ R^d, and a distance function φ (cosine or Euclidean) measures intra-task distance d^in (between representations from different subsets of the same task) and inter-task distance d^out (between representations from different tasks in the batch). The total training loss is L_e + λ(d^in − d^out), where L_e is the original episodic validation loss. The framework is learner-agnostic because ψ is trivially specified per meta-learner category (e.g., updated weights for MAML, concatenated prototypes for ProtoNet, hypernetwork output for CNAPs, probe-token output for ICL), and problem-agnostic because it only requires the episodic batch structure. A theoretical result (Theorem 1) proves that minimizing the contrastive objective L_c achieves the tightest possible upper bound on excess risk, making it a worst-case generalization safeguard that can be plugged into any meta-learner without architectural changes.

## Results

ConML consistently improves all tested meta-learners on both miniImageNet and tieredImageNet (5-way 1-shot and 5-shot). The largest gains appear for optimization-based methods: MAML improves from 48.75% to 56.25% (+7.5%) and FOMAML from 48.12% to 57.64% (+9.5%) on miniImageNet 5-way 1-shot. Even strong baselines benefit: LastShot gains +1.44% (64.80→66.24) and CAML gains +0.88% (96.15→97.03) on the same setting. On tieredImageNet, improvements are similarly consistent across all categories, with MAML gaining +7.36% (51.39→58.75) on 5-way 1-shot.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ConML is not a standalone meta-learner but a plug-in contrastive objective that improves every baseline it is applied to. The largest relative gains are for optimization-based methods: MAML improves by +7.5% (48.75→56.25) and FOMAML by +9.5% (48.12→57.64) on miniImageNet 5-way 1-shot, while Reptile gains +3.6%. Metric-based methods also benefit substantially: MatchNet improves by +4.8% (43.92→48.75) and ProtoNet by +2.1% (48.90→51.03). Amortization-based SCNAPs gains +2.6% (53.14→55.73). For already-strong baselines, gains are smaller but consistent: LastShot improves by +1.44% and CAML by +0.88% on miniImageNet 1-shot. On the cross-domain META-DATASET benchmark (Table 3), ConML brings consistent 1–8% improvements across all 10 source domains for MatchNet, ProtoNet, fo-MAML, fo-Proto-MAML, and P>M>F, confirming problem-agnostic applicability. In the ICL setting (Table 5), ConML reduces relative minimal error to 0.42–0.81× of vanilla ICL and spares 4–11 in-context examples across LR, SLR, DT, and NN tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

ConML extends contrastive learning from the sample-level representation space (as in SimCLR, CPC) to the task-level model space in meta-learning, using task identity as the contrastive signal. It departs from prior task-identity approaches (MELR, LastShot, global label inference) that are either problem-specific (requiring static class pools for few-shot classification) or learner-specific (tied to explicit model vectors in neural processes), by being both problem-agnostic and learner-agnostic. The alignment-and-discrimination framing draws on cognitive science literature on human fast learning, and the theoretical analysis connects the contrastive meta-objective to a generalization error upper bound via multi-task representation learning theory (Maurer et al., 2016).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ConML offers a drop-in training modification that consistently improves every major meta-learning category (optimization-based, metric-based, amortization-based, ICL) with a single hyperparameter setting, suggesting task identity is an underexploited signal in episodic training. The theoretical result (Theorem 1) providing a worst-case generalization guarantee for the contrastive objective is a clean conceptual contribution, though the modified distance function it implies (squared cosine for inter-task distance) was not implemented experimentally. Open questions include whether better subset-sampling strategies, task-aware batch scheduling, and tailored distance metrics per model architecture can unlock further gains, and whether the approach transfers to non-episodic or online meta-learning settings.
