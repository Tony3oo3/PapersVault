## Problem

Existing methods for solving Regularized Unbalanced Optimal Transport (RUOT) problems typically parameterize velocity and growth fields with separate neural networks without enforcing the first-order optimality conditions, resulting in solutions that violate the principle of least action and exhibit unstable convergence. Additionally, the widely used quadratic growth penalty ψ(g)=½g² in the Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao metric has not been examined for its biological implications, despite the fact that the curvature of ψ(g) determines whether proliferation increases or decreases along the transport direction.

## Input / Output

The method consumes unnormalized point-cloud snapshots at K discrete time points (e.g., single-cell RNA-seq measurements or simulated gene-expression distributions), where total mass may vary across time points to reflect cell birth and death. It produces a single scalar potential field λ(x,t) parameterized by one neural network; the velocity field u(x,t)=∇ₓλ and growth rate g(x,t) are then analytically recovered from the optimality conditions αψ′(g)=λ, rather than being learned independently. The learned dynamics can be evaluated by simulating weighted particles forward in time and comparing the resulting empirical measures against the observed snapshots.

## Method

Var-RUOT derives the necessary optimality conditions for the isotropic time-invariant RUOT problem via variational calculus, yielding a Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation that couples a single scalar field λ(x,t) to both the velocity (u=∇λ) and growth rate (αψ′(g)=λ). A single neural network parameterizes λ, and a weighted-particle scheme simulates the corresponding SDE (for positions) and ODE (for weights) to approximate the Fokker-Planck evolution without solving PDEs directly. Training minimizes a three-term loss: a reconstruction loss matching both total mass and W₂ distance at each snapshot, an HJB loss penalizing violations of the PDE constraint along particle trajectories, and an action loss that directly minimizes the transport cost. The paper further proves that the sign of ψ″(g) dictates whether g ascends or descends along the velocity field, and proposes ψ₂(g)=g^{2/15} (with ψ″<0) to encode the biological prior that upstream stem cells exhibit higher proliferation than downstream differentiated cells.

## Results

On the three-gene simulation dataset, Var-RUOT achieves a path action of 1.1105±0.0515, substantially lower than DeepRUOT (1.4058), TIGON (1.2442), and Action Matching (1.5491), while maintaining competitive W₁/W₂ distribution matching (e.g., W₁=0.0452 at t=1 vs. 0.0569 for DeepRUOT). On the EMT dataset, Var-RUOT reduces path action to 0.3544±0.0019 versus 0.4857 for DeepRUOT and 0.4672 for TIGON. Training convergence is dramatically faster: Var-RUOT reaches the OT-loss threshold in 27.6±5.8 epochs (simulation) and 5.2±1.3 epochs (EMT), compared to 172–228 epochs for DeepRUOT variants and 110–228 epochs for TIGON, with far smaller standard deviations indicating superior stability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Var-RUOT consistently achieves the lowest path action among all methods that report this metric: on the three-gene simulation it reaches 1.1105 vs. 1.2442 (TIGON), 1.4058 (DeepRUOT), and 1.5491 (Action Matching); on EMT it reaches 0.3544 vs. 0.4672 (TIGON) and 0.4857 (DeepRUOT); and on Mouse Blood Hematopoiesis it reaches 3.1491 vs. 3.7438 (TIGON) and 5.5887 (DeepRUOT). In terms of distribution matching (W₁/W₂), Var-RUOT is competitive with or slightly better than DeepRUOT and TIGON at most time points, while methods like SF2M, PISDE, MIO Flow, OTCFM, UOTCFM, and WLF do not report path action and generally show larger Wasserstein distances at later time points. The most striking advantage is in training convergence: Var-RUOT requires only ~28 epochs (simulation) and ~5 epochs (EMT) to reach the OT-loss threshold, versus 172–228 epochs for DeepRUOT variants and 110–228 for TIGON, with standard deviations an order of magnitude smaller (e.g., ±5.8 vs. ±223.7 epochs), indicating far greater training stability. The paper also demonstrates that the choice of growth penalty ψ(g) encodes different biological priors: the standard WFR metric (ψ₁=½g²) yields g increasing along the velocity field, while the modified ψ₂=g^{2/15} yields g decreasing along the velocity field, matching the expected stemness-decay pattern in hematopoiesis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the Action Matching framework (Neklyudov et al. 2023, 2024), which enforces HJB optimality conditions for the Schrödinger Bridge, to the full RUOT setting where unbalanced distributions and stochastic diffusion coexist. It builds on the WFR metric (Chizat et al. 2018) for unbalanced OT and the stochastic control formulation of Schrödinger bridges (Chen et al. 2016), but departs from prior deep-learning solvers (DeepRUOT, TIGON) that parameterize velocity and growth with separate networks without enforcing their coupling through optimality conditions. The key conceptual departure is showing that the growth penalty's second derivative ψ″(g) encodes a biological prior about whether proliferation increases or decreases along the trajectory, a connection not previously made explicit in the WFR literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By reducing the RUOT problem to learning a single scalar potential field, Var-RUOT offers a substantially simpler and more stable training procedure (6–8× fewer epochs to convergence), which is practically important for the single-cell trajectory inference community where training reliability is a recurring pain point. The biological-prior insight—that the default quadratic penalty ψ(g)=½g² forces growth to increase along the trajectory, contradicting the expectation that stem cells (upstream) proliferate most—opens a concrete question about how to choose ψ(g) in a principled, data-driven way. Key limitations remain: the method only guarantees local minima, the HJB loss acts as a soft regularizer rather than a hard constraint, the modified metric worsens distributional fit, and the selection of ψ(g) is still manual and domain-dependent.
