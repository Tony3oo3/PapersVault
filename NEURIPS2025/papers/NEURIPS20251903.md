## Problem

Prior work proposed near access-freeness (NAF) as a mathematical guarantee that generative models won't reproduce copyrighted training data, but this paper proves NAF is fundamentally insufficient: NAF models can be 'tainted,' enabling users to reproduce training data verbatim using only non-copyrightable ideas as prompts, with no protection against multiple queries or data-dependent prompts. The paper asks what definition of provable copyright protection would actually shield blameless users from unwitting infringement.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a training algorithm Train, a dataset D, a user algorithm u with auxiliary input aux (typically the non-copyrightable ideas of a work), and a copyright dependency graph G encoding which works stem from which. It produces a probability bound κ on the user's output falling in SubSim(C) (substantially similar to any in-copyright work), conditional on the user being β-blameless in a counterfactual clean-room distribution τ_{-c} where the model is trained on scrub(D,c) rather than D. The DP instantiation additionally requires D to be golden: at most one element of D stems from any given copyrighted work.

## Method

The paper introduces a blameless copy protection framework and instantiates it as clean-room copy protection: a training algorithm is (κ,β)-clean if every user who would copy in a clean-room setting (model trained on scrub(D,c)) with probability ≤ β also copies in the real world with probability ≤ κ. The key technical result proves that (ε,δ)-differentially private training is (κ,β)-clean for golden datasets, with κ ≥ (e^ε · N_D + 1)β + N_D·δ, where N_D is the number of copyrighted works to which D grants access. The proof exploits the fact that goldenness makes scrub(D,c) and D neighboring datasets (differing by at most one element), so DP's multiplicative and additive bounds directly relate the real-world distribution τ to the clean-room distribution τ_{-c}. The paper also establishes that NAF fails the taintedness test by constructing explicit NAF models (via the CP algorithm and a key-value store construction) that regurgitate training data on idea-based prompts, and shows k-NAF does not compose across multiple queries, allowing full dataset reconstruction.

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical experiments or results tables. The headline quantitative result is Theorem 8.2: an (ε,δ)-differentially private training algorithm is (κ,β)-clean for golden datasets, with the bound κ ≥ (e^ε · N_D + 1)β + N_D · δ, where N_D = |C_D| is the number of copyrighted works the dataset grants access to. For example, with ε=5 and N_D=200,000, a user targeting κ=10⁻⁹ (Stephen King-level risk tolerance) needs β≈10⁻¹⁵, while κ=10⁻¹ (Joe Schmoe-level) only needs β≈10⁻⁶. The negative results (Theorems 3.4, 3.5) show that NAF models can regurgitate training data verbatim on idea-based prompts and allow full dataset reconstruction via poly(L, 1/k) queries, even with arbitrarily small k.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper has no empirical baselines or experimental comparisons; it is a definitional and theoretical contribution. Conceptually, it positions itself against two prior frameworks: (1) Near Access-Freeness (NAF) from Vyas, Kakade, and Barak [VKB23], which the paper proves is insufficient—NAF models can be tainted (Corollaries 5.3, 5.4) and do not compose across multiple prompts or handle data-dependent prompts; and (2) Differential Privacy (DP), which the paper shows is sufficient for clean-room copy protection under the golden dataset condition (Theorem 8.2). The paper's clean-room copy protection framework subsumes and generalizes NAF's safe-model concept (the clean-room distribution τ_{-c} uses the same scrubbed-dataset model as NAF's safe_c), while adding the critical user-side blamelessness condition (β-blamelessness) that NAF lacks. The VA3 attack [LSK24] is discussed as empirical evidence of NAF's practical weaknesses, though the paper notes the attack model has a conceptual flaw (the attacker knows the target work c*).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends and corrects the provable copyright protection framework of Vyas, Kakade, and Barak (VKB23), who introduced near access-freeness (NAF) as the first mathematical definition for deployment-time copyright guarantees. The key departure is showing NAF is fundamentally insufficient: NAF models can be 'tainted,' enabling verbatim copying of training data through data-dependent prompts or multi-query reconstruction, even when the NAF bound is arbitrarily small. The paper then introduces a new framework (blameless copy protection) instantiated via clean-room copy protection, and proves that differential privacy (Dwork et al., 2006) implies clean-room copy protection under a 'golden dataset' deduplication condition, thereby formalizing a widely-suspected but previously unproven connection between DP and copyright protection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper provides the first mathematically rigorous framework for provable copyright protection that actually excludes the worst-case failure mode (tainted models), filling a gap that NAF left open. However, practical applicability is constrained: constructing golden datasets large enough for pretraining is described as 'hopeless,' the guarantee's linear dependence on the number of in-copyright works (N_D) is pessimistic, and blamelessness is fundamentally uncheckable even by the user. The most promising near-term application is fine-tuning with differentially private training on golden datasets of tens of thousands of items. Open questions include whether the linear N_D factor can be tightened, whether alternative formalizations of blamelessness (e.g., via cryptographic extraction) yield stronger guarantees, and how the framework interacts with fair use and other legal doctrines.
