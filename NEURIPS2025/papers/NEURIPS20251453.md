## Problem

The SAC-BL algorithm for unsupervised visual anomaly detection controls the false discovery rate (FDR) but its false positive rate (FPR) on anomalous examples has not been rigorously analyzed. This paper shows that FPR convergence to zero requires both infinite test samples and a generalized Gaussian score distribution—conditions too restrictive for real-world benchmarks like MVTec where test sets contain only ~167 images. The central question is how to theoretically reduce FPR under finite-sample conditions while preserving FDR control.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a testing set of RGB images and a separate calibrated set of normal images (used to compute empirical p-values via rank-based estimation). It outputs both an image-level binary anomaly decision and a pixel-level anomaly mask. In practice, the stochastic perturbation Λ is drawn 100 times and the resulting decisions are averaged to produce a stable output, making the method deterministic in its final prediction despite the internal randomization.

## Method

The paper proposes SAC-SBL, which retains the SAC network (a reconstruction network paired with a discriminative network trained on hard synthetic pairs) as the score function but replaces the deterministic BL decision rule with a Stochastic BL (SBL) algorithm. The key mechanism introduces a random variable Λ ~ Uniform(0,1) into the rejection threshold, changing the condition from p_(i)^γ ≤ δαi/n to p_(i)^γ ≤ δαi/(nΛ), which stochastically lowers the threshold and increases the number of rejected null hypotheses. Theoretically, SAC-SBL rejects a superset of hypotheses compared to SAC-BL almost surely (P(R_SAC-BL ⊆ R_SAC-SBL) = 1) while still guaranteeing FDR ≤ α, which directly implies FPR_SAC-BL ≥ FPR_SAC-SBL almost surely. The paper also establishes that under the generalized Gaussian-like distribution family, the original SAC-BL FPR converges to zero in probability as n → ∞, providing an asymptotic baseline that motivates the finite-sample improvement.

## Results

On MVTec AD (15 categories), SAC-SBL reduces the average image-level FPR from 17.45% to 11.79% and improves the image-level F1-score from 83.22% to 88.19%, while maintaining a comparable average image-level TPR (98.91% vs. 99.55%). At the pixel level, the average FPR drops from 10.85% to 9.03% and F1 rises from 96.22% to 97.75%. On VisA (12 categories), the average image-level FPR decreases from 57.42% to 51.92% and F1 improves from 71.95% to 74.95%. The most dramatic single-class improvement is on MVTec 'Pill', where image-level FPR drops from 43.26% to 29.08% at the same 100% TPR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is SAC-BL (Ma et al., 2025b), the original hypothesis-testing framework that this paper extends. SAC-SBL wins on FPR and F1-score across virtually all categories on both MVTec AD and VisA, while matching or slightly sacrificing TPR (e.g., Carpet image-level TPR drops from 100% to 92.14%). On MVTec AD, the average image-level FPR drops by 5.66 percentage points (17.45% → 11.79%) and F1 rises by 4.97 points (83.22% → 88.19%); on VisA, the average image-level FPR drops by 5.50 points (57.42% → 51.92%) and F1 rises by 3.00 points (71.95% → 74.95%). The largest single-class FPR reductions occur on MVTec 'Pill' (−14.18 pp), 'Grid' (−17.55 pp), and 'Carpet' (−11.24 pp), and on VisA 'Pcb2' (−14 pp) and 'Pcb4' (−6 pp). The only category where SAC-SBL does not improve image-level FPR is 'Zipper' (both at 0.84%), and 'Transistor' image-level FPR is unchanged (30.00%), though its pixel-level FPR improves substantially (42.62% → 31.58%). The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 4.5) that FPR_SAC-BL ≥ FPR_SAC-SBL almost surely is consistent with the empirical results: SAC-SBL never has a higher FPR than SAC-BL in any reported category.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Ma et al. (2025b), which introduced the SAC-BL algorithm combining a strong anomaly constraint (SAC) network with a betting-like (BL) decision rule for FDR-controlled anomaly detection. While prior anomaly detection literature has focused predominantly on designing powerful score functions (via distillation, reconstruction, or embedding), this work shifts attention to the decision rule itself, analyzing its false positive rate (FPR) properties and proposing a stochastic variant (SBL) that incorporates randomization of p-values. The theoretical analysis draws on the Donoho–Jin (2004) sparse hypothesis testing framework and the generalized Gaussian-like distribution family from Ingster–Suslina (2003), applying them to the anomaly detection setting for the first time in this context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a genuine gap: FDR control alone does not guarantee useful FPR performance (the trivial rule of rejecting nothing has FDR = 0 but FPR = 1), and the near-almost-sure guarantee that SAC-SBL dominates SAC-BL in FPR while preserving FDR ≤ α is a clean, actionable result for practitioners. However, the asymptotic analysis requires generalized Gaussian-like score distributions and infinite sample sizes—conditions rarely met in practice (e.g., MVTec 'Pill' has only 167 test images)—and the practical FPR reductions, while consistent across 15+ classes, are moderate (average image-level FPR drops from ~17% to ~12% on MVTec). Open questions include whether the randomization strategy generalizes to non-Gaussian score distributions, how the method behaves under heavy-tailed or multimodal score distributions, and whether the 100-repetition Monte Carlo averaging can be replaced by a closed-form or single-draw variant without losing the FDR guarantee.
