## Problem

In linear non-Gaussian structural causal models with cycles, observational data identifies the causal graph only up to a permutation-equivalence class, and existing experiment design approaches for cyclic graphs are non-adaptive, require multi-variable interventions, or rely on general SCM assumptions with conditional independence testing. This paper addresses the gap of designing an adaptive, single-variable intervention strategy that provably near-optimally resolves the remaining causal ambiguity specific to the linear non-Gaussian cyclic setting.

## Input / Output

The method consumes observational data (n-dimensional vectors) to recover the matrix I-W_ICA via ICA, then sequentially collects interventional data from perfect single-variable interventions, each yielding a new ICA-estimated matrix whose comparison with the observational estimate reveals one row of W. The output is the fully identified causal graph (structural matrix W) together with the adaptive sequence of intervention targets selected online. A key constraint is that each experiment intervenes on exactly one variable, and the ICA step requires at most one Gaussian noise component among the n exogenous terms.

## Method

The core combinatorial insight is that distribution-equivalent graphs in a linear non-Gaussian cyclic model correspond bijectively to perfect matchings in a bipartite graph whose left nodes are rows of I-W_ICA and right nodes are column indices, with an edge (r_i, j) existing iff W_ICA[i,j] is nonzero. Each atomic intervention on variable x_i identifies the true matching edge incident to column i, eliminating all graphs incompatible with that edge and shrinking the equivalence class. The experiment design problem is then cast as adaptive stochastic optimization over the space of equivalence classes with a reward function equal to the number of eliminated graphs; the paper proves this reward is adaptive monotone and adaptive submodular under a uniform prior, yielding a (1-1/e) approximation guarantee for the greedy policy. To avoid exponential enumeration of matchings, a sampling-based estimator draws random perfect matchings (via the Jerrum-Sinclair-Vigoda FPRAS or a fast greedy heuristic) and estimates the conditional expected marginal benefit, with a concentration bound of O-tilde(sqrt(1/M)) for M samples.

## Results

The paper evaluates on synthetic Erdős–Rényi random directed graphs and measures the average number of interventions needed for full causal identification. The adaptive greedy policy consistently requires a number of interventions that closely tracks the feedback vertex set (FVS) lower bound—the theoretical minimum—across both exact (full matching enumeration) and sample (greedy heuristic) modes. In a practical setting using FastICA on finite samples, the method retains this near-optimal behavior, and the relative Frobenius-norm error between the estimated and true structural matrix is reported to be low in the vast majority of runs. No single headline numerical value is tabulated in the main text; results are presented as curves in Figures 2a, 2b, and 6 (Appendix E.3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its adaptive strategy against two heuristic baselines—Random (uniformly selecting the next intervention target) and Max Degree (selecting the node with the highest degree in the bipartite graph)—and benchmarks against the intractable Feedback Vertex Set (FVS) lower bound, which represents the minimum number of interventions needed to break all cycles. The adaptive method consistently outperforms both Random and Max Degree across all graph sizes and densities tested, in both the ideal-ICA and finite-sample ICA settings. Crucially, the adaptive method's intervention count tracks the FVS lower bound very closely, indicating near-optimality despite having no knowledge of the true graph structure. The Max Degree baseline, while better than Random, still requires noticeably more interventions than the adaptive policy, particularly as graph size and edge density increase. No comparison is made against the non-adaptive approach of Mokhtarian et al. [23] in the experiments, though the paper notes its method is adaptive and restricted to single-variable interventions, making direct comparison non-trivial.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the ICA-based identifiability results for cyclic LiNGAM (Lacerda et al.) by providing a combinatorial characterization of the observational equivalence class as perfect matchings in a bipartite graph, a representation not previously exploited for experiment design. It departs from the only prior cyclic experiment design work (Mokhtarian et al.) in three key ways: the policy is adaptive rather than non-adaptive, interventions are restricted to single variables rather than multi-variable sets, and the model class is specifically linear non-Gaussian rather than general SCMs. The adaptive submodular optimization framework of Golovin and Krause is applied here to a new domain, with the Jerrum-Sinclair-Vigoda FPRAS providing the theoretical foundation for the sampling-based reward estimator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first adaptive, single-variable experiment design framework for cyclic causal models with a provable (1-1/e) approximation guarantee, filling a gap between the well-developed DAG experiment design literature and the sparse cyclic setting. The bipartite matching characterization offers a reusable combinatorial tool that could inform other problems involving row-permutation ambiguity. Key limitations include the restriction to linear non-Gaussian noise, synthetic Erdős-Rényi graphs in experiments, and the assumption of an ideal or near-ideal ICA oracle; open questions include scaling to larger graphs, robustness under severe estimation noise, and whether the FVS lower bound is tight in practice for structured (non-random) graphs.
