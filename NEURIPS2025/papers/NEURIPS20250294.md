## Problem

The standard temporal MSE loss for time-series forecasting suffers from two structural flaws: it treats each label step as independent, ignoring label autocorrelation and thus biasing the loss away from the true sequence likelihood, and it imposes T separate regression tasks (one per forecast step), which creates gradient conflicts and hinders convergence at long horizons. Prior frequency-domain losses (e.g., FreDF) only partially address the first issue—Fourier components remain correlated at finite horizons—and do not reduce the task count at all.

## Input / Output

Given N history sequences X ∈ R^{N×H} and corresponding label sequences Y ∈ R^{N×T}, the method produces a scalar training loss. The label matrix Y is first standardized (zero mean, unit variance per step) as a prerequisite for exact decorrelation, then projected via a data-adaptive SVD-derived matrix P* ∈ R^{T×K} into K = round(γ·T) latent components. The forecast model's output Ŷ is projected with the same P*, and the loss is computed in this reduced component space rather than the original T-dimensional time domain.

## Method

Time-o1 computes the optimal projection matrix P* by performing SVD on the standardized label matrix Y, retaining only the K right singular vectors corresponding to the largest singular values. This yields latent components Z = YP* that are provably decorrelated (Lemma 3.2) and ordered by variance, so the first component captures the most information and significance decreases monotonically. The training loss aligns the top K components of Ŷ and Y using an L1 norm (chosen over L2 for stability given the large scale differences across components), then fuses this transformed loss with standard MSE via a convex combination L = α·L_ortho,γ + (1−α)·L_MSE. Theoretically, decorrelation eliminates the autocorrelation bias characterized in Theorem 3.1, while retaining only K < T components reduces the effective number of regression tasks, simplifying optimization with negligible information loss since the top components dominate the variance.

## Results

Time-o1 achieves the best MSE and MAE on all 9 long-term forecasting datasets (ETTm1, ETTm2, ETTh1, ETTh2, ECL, Traffic, Weather, PEMS03, PEMS08), using the top-performing baseline model on each dataset as its underlying architecture. Representative improvements include reducing MSE from 0.447 to 0.431 on ETTh1 (Fredformer base), from 0.426 to 0.419 on Traffic (iTransformer base), and from 0.106 to 0.097 on PEMS03 (MICN base). On the M4 short-term forecasting benchmark, Time-o1 (with Fredformer) achieves the best average SMAPE (11.841), MASE (1.585), and OWA (0.851), winning 4 out of 4 sub-categories.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Time-o1 achieves the best MSE and MAE on all 9 long-term forecasting datasets, outperforming every baseline including Fredformer, iTransformer, FreTS, TimesNet, MICN, TiDE, DLinear, FEDformer, Autoformer, and Transformer. The most substantial gains appear on Traffic (MSE 0.419 vs. iTransformer's 0.426, a 1.6% reduction) and PEMS03 (MSE 0.097 vs. MICN's 0.106, an 8.5% reduction), while ETT datasets show more modest but consistent improvements (e.g., ETTh1 MSE 0.431 vs. Fredformer's 0.447). In the loss-function comparison (Table 2), Time-o1 also beats FreDF, Koopman, Dilate, Soft-DTW, DPTA, and canonical DF (TMSE) on both Fredformer and iTransformer backbones, with the largest margin over shape-alignment losses (Dilate, Soft-DTW, DPTA) which offer little gain over TMSE. The ablation confirms both the decorrelation property and the task-reduction effect contribute independently, with their combination yielding the best results (e.g., Weather MAE 0.280 vs. 0.293 for DF). On M4 short-term forecasting, Time-o1 wins all 4 sub-categories (Yearly, Quarterly, Monthly, Others) in SMAPE, MASE, and OWA.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the authors' prior work FreDF [51], which proposed aligning forecast and label sequences in the frequency domain to reduce autocorrelation bias. Time-o1 departs from FreDF by replacing the fixed Fourier projection with a data-adaptive SVD-based projection that guarantees exact decorrelation at finite horizons and simultaneously reduces the number of optimization tasks by retaining only the most significant components. Conceptually, the method repurposes PCA from its traditional role on input features to the label sequence, a distinction the authors emphasize as technically novel. The work also positions itself against shape-alignment losses (Soft-DTW, Dilate, DPTA) and other spectral methods (Koopman), arguing that these either lack theoretical guarantees or fail to address both autocorrelation bias and task-count complexity simultaneously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By providing a theoretically grounded, model-agnostic loss function that can be plugged into any existing forecast architecture, Time-o1 offers a low-cost path to improving a wide range of time-series models without architectural changes. The finding that a simple SVD transform of labels—computed once per batch—yields consistent gains across nine datasets and multiple architectures suggests that loss function design has been underexploited relative to architecture design in this field. Open questions include whether the same decorrelation-and-reduction strategy applies to input sequences (which also exhibit autocorrelation), whether the approach transfers to other sequential prediction tasks (speech, dense image prediction), and whether the per-batch SVD computation remains efficient at very large batch sizes or in streaming settings.
