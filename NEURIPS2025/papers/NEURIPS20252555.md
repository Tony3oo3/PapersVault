## Problem

In non-exemplar class incremental learning (NECIL), frozen feature extractor-based methods avoid catastrophic forgetting but suffer from representation overlap between new and old classes, since the model was only optimized for the initial distribution. Existing remedies—self-supervised learning (SSL) with rotation and ensemble learning—fall short: rotation-based SSL creates easily separable task-agnostic classes that fail to sharpen discriminative features, and naively adding new experts to an ensemble degrades performance because unreliable new experts mislead equally-weighted joint predictions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images at dataset-specific resolutions (32×32 for CIFAR-100, 64×64 for TinyImageNet, 224×224 for ImageNet-Subset) and outputs a single class label via argmax over a weighted ensemble of per-expert Bayesian predictions. During training, each input image is augmented into 7 variants (original, three rotations, two color-channel swaps, one noise-injected copy) to form a 7N-way task-agnostic classification problem. At inference, each expert computes log-likelihoods against stored per-class Gaussian distributions (mean and covariance), assigns −∞ to classes it has never seen, applies a temperature-scaled softmax, and the final prediction is a PWP-weighted sum across all experts.

## Method

CLOVER is an ensemble of K=5 experts sharing a frozen ResNet-18 encoder, where each expert has its own trainable encoder and classifier. The key training innovation is Confusion-Driven Self-Supervised Learning (CDSSL), which extends standard rotation-based SSL by adding color-channel swapping (RGB→GBR, RGB→BRG) and Gaussian noise injection, generating 7N-way task-agnostic classes that are highly confusable with the raw classes (verified via KL divergence and t-SNE), thereby forcing the model to extract more discriminative features and reducing new/old class representation overlap. At inference, a Progressively Weighted Prediction (PWP) strategy assigns each new expert a low initial weight α that increases by β per task until reaching 1/K, preventing unreliable new experts from degrading the ensemble's joint prediction. The Bayesian classifier models each class per expert as a Gaussian, and the final class is the argmax of the PWP-weighted sum of per-expert softmax probabilities.

## Results

On CIFAR-100, CLOVER achieves the best average accuracy of 72.7% (5 tasks), 72.3% (10 tasks), and 71.0% (20 tasks), along with the best last accuracy of 68.0%, 67.5%, and 64.9% respectively, outperforming the strongest baselines (CEAT, SEED, FeCAM) by 1.5–1.6% in average accuracy and up to 3.5% in last accuracy. On TinyImageNet, CLOVER leads in average accuracy for 5 and 10 tasks (60.2% and 59.9%) but is slightly behind FeCAM on 20 tasks (58.5% vs. 59.3%). On ImageNet-Subset, CLOVER achieves the highest last accuracy across all settings (73.2%, 71.5%, 67.5%), while FeCAM edges it out in average accuracy for 5 and 10 tasks (78.3% vs. 77.8%, 78.2% vs. 77.1%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLOVER outperforms all 12 baselines on CIFAR-100 across every task setting, with the largest margins over SEED (the closest ensemble-based competitor) of +1.6% average and +1.7–3.5% last accuracy. On TinyImageNet, CLOVER leads in average accuracy for 5 and 10 tasks (60.2% vs. FeCAM's 59.6% and 59.4%) but is narrowly behind FeCAM on 20 tasks (58.5% vs. 59.3%); however, CLOVER still achieves the best last accuracy in all TinyImageNet settings. On ImageNet-Subset, FeCAM edges out CLOVER in average accuracy for 5 and 10 tasks (78.3% vs. 77.8%, 78.2% vs. 77.1%), but CLOVER surpasses FeCAM in last accuracy by 2.3–3.5% across all settings, demonstrating superior knowledge retention. Compared to the ensemble-based SEED, CLOVER consistently improves average accuracy by 1.2–1.6% and last accuracy by 1.7–3.7% on CIFAR-100, validating the contributions of both CDSSL and PWP. Against prototype-based methods (PASS, SSRE, PRAKA, POLO), CLOVER achieves substantially larger gains (5–15% in average accuracy), highlighting the advantage of the frozen-feature-extractor paradigm combined with confusion-driven training. The ViT-based methods CEAT and FGKSR are competitive on CIFAR-100 but do not match CLOVER's last accuracy, and CLOVER's ResNet-18 backbone achieves comparable or superior results with fewer parameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

CLOVER is built directly on the ensemble architecture and Gaussian-distribution-based inference of SEED (ICLR 2024), extending it with a shared frozen encoder and a progressively weighted prediction strategy to address the performance degradation caused by unreliable new experts. Its self-supervised component extends the rotation-based SSL introduced in PASS (CVPR 2021) by replacing easily distinguishable rotation classes with highly confusable task-agnostic classes generated via color channel swapping and noise injection, arguing that standard SSL transformations (e.g., 90° rotation) alter representation distributions too drastically to force the model to learn truly discriminative features. Conceptually, the paper positions itself within the frozen feature extractor-based NECIL paradigm, targeting the specific failure mode of representation overlap between new and old classes that this paradigm is known to exhibit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The CDSSL insight—that making the self-supervised proxy task harder by introducing confusable rather than easily separable classes yields better discriminative representations for incremental learning—is a practical and well-motivated design choice that could inform SSL strategies beyond NECIL. However, the method's reliance on a data-rich base task, the 7× increase in classification difficulty (7N-way vs. N-way), and the ensemble's parameter overhead limit its applicability in resource-constrained or class-balanced settings. Open questions include whether the confusion-driven principle generalizes to other transformation types or modalities, and whether the parameter and training cost can be reduced without sacrificing the consistent 2–3% gains observed here.
