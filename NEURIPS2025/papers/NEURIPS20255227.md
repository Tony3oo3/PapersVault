## Problem

Existing inference-time scaling methods for LLMs (beam search, MCTS, DVTS) formulate generation as deterministic search guided by a process reward model, but inherit a critical weakness from classical search: early pruning. Because PRMs are noisy and miscalibrated, these methods irreversibly discard trajectories that score poorly in early steps but would ultimately be correct, and once pruned, such paths cannot be recovered. The paper asks whether a probabilistic, sampling-based approach can avoid this brittleness while still leveraging PRM guidance.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt (e.g., a competition math problem or financial QA question) and a fixed compute budget N specifying the number of parallel particles. It produces a weighted set of N complete candidate solutions approximating the posterior over fully-accepted trajectories, from which a final answer is selected by scoring all outputs with an outcome reward model. During generation, the PRM scores each particle's partial sequence at every step, and the LLM generates token-level continuations conditioned on each particle's individual history, with prefix caching keeping total runtime comparable to N independent generations.

## Method

The paper formulates inference-time scaling as posterior inference in a state-space model where the LLM is the transition model and a process reward model (PRM) is the emission model, then applies particle filtering (sequential Monte Carlo) to sample from the posterior over accepted trajectories. At each step, N weighted particles are propagated by sampling the next token from the LLM, their weights are updated multiplicatively by the PRM score, and a new population is drawn via softmax-weighted resampling with replacement. Unlike beam search, which targets the mode of the approximate posterior and prunes deterministically, particle filtering samples from the typical set, maintaining a diverse population that balances exploitation of high-reward paths with stochastic exploration of alternatives. The approach is entirely training-free, requiring only a fixed LLM and a pre-trained PRM, and the paper proves that the resulting weighted samples yield an unbiased estimate of expected accuracy. Extensions include Particle Gibbs for multi-iteration refinement and Parallel Tempering for distributing computation across chains at different temperatures.

## Results

On MATH500 with a budget of 32 generations, Particle Filtering (PF) achieves 87.7% accuracy with Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct, surpassing o1-preview (87.0%), and 84.6% with Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct. On AIME 2024, PF reaches 10/30 with the 7B model and 7/30 with the 1.5B model. Notably, PF with Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct (a general SLM) reaches 79.3% on MATH500, exceeding GPT-4o (76.2%), and the paper reports a 4–16× faster scaling rate compared to the next-best competitor DVTS across model sizes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Particle Filtering consistently outperforms all four baselines across every model and dataset tested. Against Self Consistency, PF gains 18.3 points on MATH500 with Qwen-2.5-1.5B (79.3 vs. 61.0) and 4.6 points with Qwen-2.5-Math-7B (87.7 vs. 84.0). Against BoN/WBoN, PF leads by 11.5 points (79.3 vs. 67.8/69.2) on the general 1.5B model and by 4.7 points (87.7 vs. 82.6/83.0) on the math 7B model. Against Beam Search, PF wins by 3.1 points on Qwen-2.5-1.5B (79.3 vs. 76.2) and by 0.8 points on Qwen-2.5-Math-7B (87.7 vs. 86.9), while also achieving higher AIME scores (6/30 vs. 5/30 and 10/30 vs. 7/30 respectively). Against DVTS, the closest competitor, PF leads by 2.7 points on Qwen-2.5-1.5B (79.3 vs. 76.6) and by 3.1 points on Qwen-2.5-Math-7B (87.7 vs. 84.6); more importantly, PF scales 4–16× faster, reaching DVTS's budget-32 performance at only budget 8 (Llama-3.2-1B) or budget 8 vs. 128 (Llama-3.1-8B).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repositions inference-time scaling (ITS) from a deterministic search problem (beam search, MCTS, DVTS) to a posterior inference problem in a state-space model, where the LLM is the transition model and a process reward model (PRM) is the emission model. It departs from search-based ITS by replacing greedy pruning with particle filtering (sequential Monte Carlo), which maintains a weighted population of candidate trajectories and balances exploration against exploitation via stochastic resampling. Unlike Twisted SMC (TSMC), which requires a learned twist function and proposal model, this approach is entirely training-free, using only a fixed LLM and a fixed PRM. The work also extends the PF framework with Particle Gibbs (multi-iteration) and Parallel Tempering (multi-chain) extensions, drawing on classical MCMC literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 4–16× faster scaling rate over deterministic search means substantially less compute is needed to reach a target accuracy, making inference-time scaling practical for small open-source models (a 1.5B model surpasses GPT-4o with 4 rollouts; a 7B model reaches o1-level with 32). The formal SSM framing opens a principled path for importing the broader probabilistic-inference toolkit (smoothers, variational methods, advanced MCMC) into LLM inference, which the authors identify as key future work. Open questions include optimal joint allocation of particles, iterations, and parallel chains (N, T, M), the development of domain-specific or generalized PRMs, and reducing the latency overhead of stepwise PRM scoring in production inference engines.
