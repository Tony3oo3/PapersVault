## Problem

Reinforcement learning agents struggle with mathematical problems that combine long horizons, extremely sparse rewards, and a highly non-uniform distribution of instance hardness, where some instances require superexentially long solution paths. This paper uses the Andrews-Curtis conjecture on balanced group presentations as a controlled case study to characterize how RL agents handle varying problem difficulty and to identify algorithmic mechanisms that can adapt to the hardness distribution.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255348/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent receives a balanced two-generator group presentation encoded as a 1024-element integer array (two relators of length 512, with generators mapped to ±1, ±2 and zero-padding), and must output a sequence of AC-moves that trivializes the presentation to ⟨x,y | x,y⟩. The state space is capped at relator word length 512, and the episode terminates upon reaching the trivial presentation (length 2). Beyond individual solutions, the method also yields general theorems about infinite families of AC-trivial presentations, such as all MS(1,w) and MS(n, y⁻¹xyx⁻¹).

## Method

The paper frames AC trivialization as a deterministic MDP and systematically compares DQN, A2C, PPO, and AlphaZero across three reward functions (sparse, dense with length penalties, clipped) and two action spaces (AC vs. AC′ moves), finding PPO with residual networks (6 residual blocks of 512 neurons) trained for 1B interactions to be the strongest baseline (solving 457/1190 presentations). The key algorithmic contribution is a two-part proposal: (1) supermoves, which are compositions of elementary AC-moves aggregated into single actions to compress superexponential solution paths into polynomial-length sequences, and (2) adaptive action spaces, where the action set is dynamically expanded at geometric intervals (10^N epochs) by unmasking supermoves extracted from the hardest instances the agent has already solved, keeping the fraction of new actions per stage small (5–15%) to preserve training history. A piecewise-constant horizon schedule (T_max increasing from 200 to 1200) further lets the agent master easier instances before tackling harder ones, and the combined approach solves 495 presentations including 13 new ones beyond all prior methods.

## Results

On the 1190-presentation Miller-Schupp benchmark dataset, the best PPO agent with T_max=200 solves 457 presentations, while BFS and greedy search (GS) solve 278 and 533 respectively. PPO with a piecewise-constant horizon schedule solves 2 additional presentations that all other methods fail on, and PPO augmented with supermoves (dynamic action spaces) solves 495 presentations, including 13 new ones beyond GS and standard PPO. Among RL algorithms compared in Section 5.1, PPO outperforms DQN (303), A2C, and AlphaZero (242) on the AC trivialization task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PPO with T_max=200 (457 solved) outperforms BFS (278) by 179 presentations but falls short of greedy search (533) by 76 presentations, since GS's greedy selection with respect to presentation length ℓ finds shorter paths for easier instances. However, PPO solves 5 presentations that GS cannot, requiring substantial ℓ-increase along the trivialization path that GS's greedy behavior cannot accommodate. Among RL baselines, PPO (353 in Section 5.1) clearly outperforms DQN (303) and AlphaZero (242), with DQN struggling particularly under sparse rewards R₁ and AlphaZero performing poorly without dense intermediate rewards. The supermove-enhanced PPO (495) surpasses all other methods including GS, solving 13 presentations that eluded both greedy search and standard PPO. A2C follows the same performance pattern as PPO, consistent with the theoretical result that A2C is a special case of PPO.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on PPO as its core algorithmic backbone but departs from prior RL work by introducing 'supermoves' and 'adaptive action spaces'—dynamically growing action sets informed by a hardness distribution over problem instances. In the group theory literature, prior approaches to the Andrews-Curtis conjecture relied on BFS, genetic algorithms, and greedy search; this paper is the first to frame AC trivialization as a long-horizon, sparse-reward MDP and to study the intrinsic hardness distribution of presentations. The supermoves concept draws on hierarchical RL and options frameworks but is specifically motivated by the superexponential path lengths known to exist in this problem class, making it a targeted adaptation rather than a general HRL instantiation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that RL agents can make genuine mathematical contributions by resolving potential counterexamples to the Andrews-Curtis conjecture that had been open for over 25 years, including three infinite subfamilies. The proposed supermoves and adaptive action space mechanism, while only preliminarily validated (solving 13 additional presentations), offers a concrete recipe for tackling long-horizon sparse-reward problems where solution lengths grow superexponentially. Key limitations include the small benchmark (1190 presentations), focus on a single conjecture, and the preliminary nature of the algorithmic contributions; whether these ideas generalize to other mathematical search problems or broader sparse-reward domains remains an open question.
