## Problem

Single-neuron representation models learn identity embeddings that are confounded by batch effects—non-biological variations from different animals, stimulus conditions, or recording platforms—causing severe performance degradation when tested on unseen experimental conditions. Prior work has not rigorously evaluated generalization to out-of-domain animals or stimuli, leaving a critical gap in assessing whether learned embeddings truly capture intrinsic neuronal identity rather than experimental artifacts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251329/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes single-neuron activity recordings in multiple formats: calcium fluorescence traces sampled at 4.3 Hz (from 2-photon imaging), spike waveforms, and activity autocorrelation profiles, each segmented into fixed-length windows (e.g., 256 or 512 timesteps). The primary output is a low-dimensional identity embedding per neuron that should encode molecular cell type (excitatory/inhibitory, Lamp5/Pvalb/Vip/Sst) and anatomical location (10 broad brain areas) while being statistically indistinguishable across batch labels (animal ID, stimulus condition). Downstream linear or MLP classifiers are trained on these frozen embeddings for cell-type and region prediction.

## Method

The core contribution is a model-agnostic adversarial training framework that appends a batch discriminator D_φ (a two-hidden-layer MLP with 256 units) to any existing single-neuron encoder f_θ. During training, a Gradient Reversal Layer (GRL) flips the sign of gradients flowing from D_φ back to f_θ, so the encoder is optimized to produce embeddings that maximize the discriminator's batch-prediction loss while simultaneously minimizing a paradigm-specific base loss (cross-entropy for LOLCAT, reconstruction+KL for VAE, contrastive loss for NEMO, or self-supervised reconstruction for NeuPRINT). The joint objective is min_θ max_φ L_base(f_θ) − λ·L_batch(D_φ∘f_θ), with λ selected via reverse cross-validation over 8 log-spaced values. This plug-and-play module is compatible with all three major representation paradigms (Transformer-based implicit, contrastive, and VAE) without modifying their internal architectures. The paper also introduces a rigorous out-of-domain benchmark protocol: train on 3 of 4 stimulus conditions (or 3 of 4 animals) and evaluate on the held-out condition, explicitly quantifying the generalization gap that random-split evaluations mask.

## Results

On the V1-CellType dataset, adversarial training substantially narrows the in-domain-to-out-of-domain accuracy gap across all models. For cross-stimulus cell-type prediction (Table 1), NeuPRINT (Implicit) achieves the highest out-of-domain accuracy of 0.743 with adversarial training (vs. 0.644 without), while LOLCAT (End2End) shows the largest relative recovery from 0.358 to 0.566 (+58.1%). For cross-animal cell-type prediction (Table 2), NeuPRINT reaches 0.739 and LOLCAT recovers to 0.694 with adversarial training. On the IBL Brain-wide Map for cross-animal brain-region prediction (Table 3), NEMO (Contrastive) achieves the best out-of-domain accuracy of 0.496 with adversarial training, with all models clustered between 0.457 and 0.496.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates four representation-learning baselines—NeuPRINT (implicit Transformer), NEMO (multimodal contrastive), VAE (reconstruction-based), and LOLCAT (end-to-end supervised)—each with and without the proposed adversarial batch-invariance training. NeuPRINT is the strongest baseline in-domain (0.787 on V1-CellType) and retains the best out-of-domain accuracy after adversarial training (0.743 cross-stimulus, 0.739 cross-animal), but still gains 15–27% relative accuracy from the adversarial term. LOLCAT is the most vulnerable to batch effects (−52% cross-stimulus, −39% cross-animal without adversarial training) yet benefits the most in relative terms (+58% and +54% respectively), recovering to 0.566 and 0.694. VAE shows intermediate sensitivity and gains of roughly 18–20% relative improvement. NEMO, tested only on the IBL Brain-wide Map, improves from 0.400 to 0.496 (+24%) under cross-animal evaluation. Across all settings, adversarial training consistently reduces the in-domain/out-of-domain gap to under 10% for most models, whereas without it the gap ranges from 19% to 52%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes domain-adversarial training (specifically the Gradient Reversal Layer from Ganin et al., 2016) to the neuroscience domain of single-neuron representation learning, where it had not previously been applied. It sits at the intersection of three established paradigms for neuron identity embedding—implicit Transformer models (NeuPRINT), multimodal contrastive learning (NEMO), and VAE-based reconstruction—and wraps them with a model-agnostic batch-invariance regularizer. The paper also draws a conceptual parallel to adversarial domain translation in single-cell omics (Zhao et al., 2022), positioning in-vivo neural activity as an 'omics' modality subject to analogous batch effects. Its most distinctive contribution relative to prior work is the explicit out-of-domain evaluation protocol (held-out animals or stimuli), which the authors argue was absent from earlier single-neuron representation studies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dramatic performance collapses revealed by the new benchmark (e.g., LOLCAT dropping 52% on cross-stimulus, 39% on cross-animal) expose a significant blind spot in how single-neuron representation models have been evaluated, and the adversarial training recovers much of that gap in a model-agnostic way. This is practically important for building unified neural atlases and for closed-loop experiments that require reliable cell-type identification across labs and species. Key open questions include cross-species generalization (all experiments are mouse-only), whether treating individual trials as distinct batches would further improve robustness, and how this adversarial approach compares to non-adversarial batch-invariance methods from the single-cell omics literature.
