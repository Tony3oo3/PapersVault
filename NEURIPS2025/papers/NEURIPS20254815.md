## Problem

Inference-time computation methods for LLM reasoning (Best-of-N, MCTS, beam search, etc.) are highly sensitive to implementation details—prompt type, temperature, top-p, and reward model choice—yet these factors are rarely standardized or systematically studied. Most existing work remains at the proof-of-concept stage with limited practical adoption, and no comprehensive benchmark exists that compares these methods under controlled, equal-compute conditions across diverse reasoning tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254815/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes a text reasoning problem (arithmetic word problems, advanced math, code prompts, multi-hop QA, factual claims, or logical puzzles) paired with an instruction prompt (IO, CoT, or Reflect CoT) and produces a single selected solution from N=32 candidates. Candidate generation is governed by temperature (τ) and top-p (nucleus sampling) hyperparameters, while selection is performed by a reward model (RLHF, Proof-Critical, LLM-as-Judge process reward) or a voting/self-evaluation strategy. Evaluation reports accuracy (or pass@1 for code) alongside token consumption to enable fair comparison under fixed computation budgets.

## Method

The paper conducts a large-scale empirical ablation (20,000+ A100-80G GPU hours, 1,000+ experiments) that systematically varies each component of the proposer-verifier inference pipeline: instruction prompt type (IO vs. CoT vs. Reflect CoT), sampling temperature (0.6–1.0), top-p (0.6–1.0), and reward/selection mechanism (majority voting, self-process-evaluation, self-result-evaluation, RLHF reward, Proof-Critical reward, LLM-as-Judge process reward). Six inference-time computation methods—Best-of-N, Step-Level Best-of-N, Beam Search, MCTS, Self-Consistency, and Self-Refine—are benchmarked on eight reasoning tasks using LLaMA-3.3 (8B/70B), Qwen-2.5 (7B/72B), and Mistral-7B under a fixed token budget. Key findings include: temperature τ=0.8 and top-p=0.9 yield 2–6% gains over defaults; CoT prompts consistently outperform IO but Reflect CoT gives mixed results; self-evaluation often underperforms random selection; and reward models can cause performance inflation due to generalization failures, particularly on complex tasks like MATH.

## Results

Across eight reasoning tasks, Self-Consistency and Best-of-N achieve the strongest performance among six inference-time computation methods. For Qwen-2.5-7B, Self-Consistency reaches 90.9% on GSM8K, 70.2% on MATH, and 79.9% pass@1 on HumanEval, while Best-of-N achieves 90.5%, 61.2%, and 78.0% respectively. For Llama-3.1-8B, Self-Consistency leads with 84.3% on GSM8K and 54.8% on Bamboogle. Self-Refine consistently underperforms (e.g., 64.5% vs. 84.3% on GSM8K for Llama-3.1-8B), and Beam Search shows the lowest token efficiency with minimal accuracy gains despite higher token consumption.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks six inference-time computation methods against each other on eight reasoning tasks. Self-Consistency and Best-of-N are the strongest performers overall: Self-Consistency edges out Best-of-N on most tasks (e.g., 84.3% vs. 83.1% on GSM8K, 54.8% vs. 48.4% on Bamboogle for Llama-3.1-8B), while Best-of-N leads on PrOntoQA (94.0% vs. 93.4%). Step-Level Best-of-N is competitive and more token-efficient (e.g., 83.0% on GSM8K with only 845 tokens vs. 976 for Best-of-N) but trails on complex tasks like MATH (39.8% vs. 44.6% for Llama-3.1-8B). Beam Search consistently underperforms, achieving the lowest accuracy on most tasks (e.g., 33.1% on Bamboogle, 25.1% on GSM-Hard for Llama-3.1-8B) while consuming more tokens. MCTS shows highly inconsistent results, excelling on some tasks (93.0% PrOntoQA) but collapsing on others (31.0% MATH, 66.2% GSM8K for Qwen-2.5-7B). Self-Refine is the weakest method across nearly all tasks, with particularly poor performance on FEVER (19.1% for Llama-3.1-8B) and PrOntoQA (62.2%), suggesting iterative self-revision without external feedback is largely ineffective.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a systematic empirical study of the proposer-verifier pipeline that underlies most inference-time computation methods (Best-of-N, MCTS, self-consistency, beam search, self-refine), which prior works like Q*, MALT, GenRM, and CPO each deployed with ad-hoc, non-standardized hyperparameter configurations. Rather than proposing a new algorithm, it carves new ground by isolating and ablating previously overlooked 'tricks'—temperature, top-p, prompt type, and reward model choice—under a unified protocol across multiple model families and eight reasoning tasks. It departs from the dominant focus on mathematical reasoning by extending the evaluation to logical reasoning, code generation, fact verification, and commonsense QA, and it explicitly challenges the assumption that more sophisticated search or reward mechanisms are necessary when simple sampling parameter tuning yields comparable or better gains.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first standardized benchmark for inference-time computation with a fixed token budget, giving practitioners a common reference point that was previously absent. Its finding that self-evaluation can perform worse than random selection, and that reward models can cause performance *degradation* (inflation) on harder tasks like MATH, raises important open questions about the reliability of learned verifiers and the generalization limits of process reward models. A key limitation is that the study is confined to models up to ~72B parameters and a fixed set of eight tasks, leaving open whether the observed hyperparameter sensitivities and reward-model failures persist at frontier-scale or in more diverse domains. The non-additivity of combined tricks also suggests that the interaction space between generation and selection strategies remains poorly understood.
