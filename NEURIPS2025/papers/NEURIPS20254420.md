## Problem

The Tree Alternating Optimization (TAO) algorithm for regression trees with linear leaves solves a D×D linear system at every leaf, incurring cubic cost in the input dimensionality that scales exponentially with tree depth. For high-dimensional inputs or deep trees, this makes training prohibitively slow despite the algorithm producing smaller, more accurate trees than greedy methods like CART. The paper asks whether the leaf optimization can be made faster while preserving the exact same solution.

## Input / Output

The method takes numerical feature vectors as input—flattened images (28×28 MNIST/Fashion-MNIST at D=784, resized to 10×10 through 40×40 for D=100–1600) or tabular data (D=21–2500)—and produces continuous regression predictions of dimension E (ranging from 1 scalar to 784 for image-to-image regression). The tree structure is a fixed complete binary tree of depth Δ with sparse oblique hyperplane splits at internal nodes and linear predictors A_i x + b_i at leaves. The algorithm optimizes all node parameters jointly via alternating optimization, with the tree structure implicitly learned through L1 regularization on decision node weights.

## Method

The key modification is in the leaf optimization step: since each leaf receives only a reduced subset of M training instances (M = N/2^Δ for balanced trees), the algorithm checks whether M < D at each leaf. When this holds (the 'deep regime' beyond critical depth Δ* = log₂(N/D)), it applies the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury formula to invert an M×M Gram matrix instead of the original D×D covariance matrix, yielding the exact same ridge regression solution at lower cost. This makes the total leaf-training cost non-monotonic in depth: it increases up to Δ* then decreases, so sufficiently deep trees train faster than shallow ones, and a deep nonlinear tree can be asymptotically faster to train than a single linear regression. The adaptive algorithm selects the cheaper of the two formulations per leaf, and the same SMW trick is also applied to traditional CART and M5 tree induction, yielding even larger speedups (up to 25×) because those methods produce much deeper trees.

## Results

The adaptive algorithm produces identical RMSE to the naive TAO algorithm while achieving speedups that scale with input dimensionality and tree depth: up to 3.4× on Patched Fashion MNIST (D=784, Δ=15), 2.7× on Rotated MNIST, and 25× on Random MNIST (D=2500, Δ=15). For low-dimensional datasets (ailerons D=40, SARCOS D=21), the speedup is negligible (~1×) since the tree never enters the deep regime. In the grid-search setting on Patched Fashion MNIST, the adaptive TAO reduces search time from 6899.9s to 3485.4s (~2×), while the adaptive CART reduces it from 2444.2s to 350.4s (~7×). Under Bayesian HPO (Optuna/Hyperopt, 100 trials), the adaptive algorithm completes the full search in under an hour, whereas the naive algorithm times out at 3 hours having completed only 28% of trials.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its adaptive (SMW-accelerated) algorithm against two baselines: the original naive TAO algorithm [Zharmagambetov & Carreira-Perpiñan, ICML 2020] and CART with linear leaves [Breiman et al., 1984]. Against the naive TAO baseline, the adaptive algorithm always wins on training time while producing identical RMSE (same learned model), with the speedup growing from ~1× at low dimensions (D=21–40) to 3.4× at D=784 and 25× at D=2500, all at depth Δ=15. The speedup is absent in the shallow regime (Δ < Δ*), confirming the theoretical prediction that the SMW formula only helps when each leaf's reduced set is smaller than D. Against CART, the SMW speedup is even more dramatic (7× in grid search on Patched Fashion MNIST) because CART's greedy construction produces much deeper, larger trees that spend more time in the deep regime. In the Bayesian HPO setting, the adaptive method is essential for feasibility: it completes 100 Optuna/Hyperopt trials in under an hour, while the naive method times out at 3 hours with only 28% completion, and 79–91% of sampled trees have depth >20 (well into the deep regime where the speedup applies).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper accelerates the Tree Alternating Optimization (TAO) algorithm for regression trees with linear leaves, originally developed by Carreira-Perpiñan & Tavallali (NeurIPS 2018) and extended to regression by Zharmagambetov & Carreira-Perpiñan (ICML 2020). The key departure is recognizing that TAO's leaf optimization operates on disjoint reduced sets whose size shrinks with tree depth, enabling the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury formula to replace a D×D matrix inversion with an M×M one when M < D. The same trick is also applied to traditional greedy algorithms (CART, M5), and the paper provides a full computational complexity analysis showing a non-monotonic training cost across tree depth.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The non-monotonic complexity result—that training a tree becomes faster as it gets deeper past a critical depth, and that a nonlinear tree model can be asymptotically cheaper to train than a single linear regression—is a genuinely useful theoretical insight for anyone working with tree-based regression. Practically, the speedup (up to 25×) makes Bayesian hyperparameter optimization (Optuna, Hyperopt) computationally feasible for deep oblique trees where the naive algorithm times out, which is a concrete unblocking for model selection workflows. Limitations include sequential-only experiments, a balanced-tree assumption in the complexity analysis, and the absence of released code; the authors note that low-rank leaf mappings and parallel computation are natural next steps.
