## Problem

When pretrained VLMs are adapted to vision-language-action models by grafting randomly initialized continuous action experts (e.g., flow-matching heads), gradients from these untrained modules degrade the backbone's semantic knowledge, causing slower training convergence, impaired language following, and reduced generalization. The paper asks whether and how one can add fast continuous control capabilities to a VLM without corrupting the web-scale knowledge it already encodes.

## Input / Output

The model consumes multi-view RGB images (224×224 px), a continuous proprioceptive state vector, and a natural language instruction, and produces a 50-step continuous action chunk (joint angles or end-effector poses) via a few steps of flow integration at inference time. During training only, the backbone additionally emits FAST-tokenized discrete action tokens (DCT-compressed, byte-pair encoded) as a representation-learning signal, while the 300M-parameter action expert (width 1024, depth 18) generates the continuous output. The two action representations are kept informationally isolated by the attention mask: FAST tokens never attend to continuous action tokens and vice versa.

## Method

The core contribution is a single-stage training recipe called knowledge insulation. The VLM backbone (PaliGemma 2B) is fine-tuned with a standard next-token prediction loss on FAST-discretized action tokens and general VLM data (captioning, VQA, bounding boxes), providing a stable, well-conditioned learning signal that adapts representations to robot control. Simultaneously, a smaller flow-matching action expert is trained on continuous action chunks, but a stop-gradient operator is applied to the key and value projections in the attention layers so that no gradient flows from the expert back into the backbone. This decouples the two learning objectives: the backbone learns good representations from discrete tokens and VLM data, while the expert learns precise continuous control without disrupting pretrained weights. At inference, only the compact action expert runs (enabling ~10 Hz control), making the discrete tokens a training-time-only mechanism. The recipe also co-trains on non-action VLM data to further preserve semantic knowledge, and the combined loss is a weighted sum of cross-entropy (language + FAST tokens) and flow-matching MSE.

## Results

On the LIBERO benchmark, the proposed method (fine-tuned from the generalist model) achieves state-of-the-art success rates of 98.0% on LIBERO-Spatial and 96.0% on LIBERO-90, outperforming all prior methods. On the DROID real-world benchmark, the method scores 0.55±0.09, surpassing π_0 (0.49±0.09) and π_0-FAST (0.45±0.09). In real-world evaluations across multiple robot embodiments, the method consistently achieves the highest task completion rates while training 7.5× faster than π_0 (in terms of steps to reach comparable performance) and enabling fast continuous-action inference at 10 Hz control frequency.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against π_0 [7], the proposed method wins on DROID (0.55 vs. 0.49), on all real-world manipulation tasks (items in drawer, table bussing, shirt folding, mobile manipulation), and trains 7.5× faster in wall-clock time; π_0 also suffers from degraded language following due to gradient interference from the randomly initialized action expert. Against π_0-FAST [38], the method wins on DROID (0.55 vs. 0.45) and LIBERO-10 (85.8% vs. 60.2%), while also being substantially faster at inference (π_0-FAST requires ~750 ms per action chunk vs. the proposed method's few-step flow integration). Against OpenVLA-OFT [25], the method achieves higher success rates on real-world tasks and on LIBERO-90 (96.0% vs. not reported), though OpenVLA-OFT edges ahead on LIBERO-Goal (97.9% vs. 95.6%) and LIBERO-10 (94.5% vs. 85.8%). Against HybridVLA [33], the closest methodological competitor (joint discrete/continuous training without stop-gradient), the proposed method wins on items in drawer and table bussing, with the key difference being the stop-gradient and attention mask design that prevents autoregressive tokens from attending to continuous action tokens. Against Transfusion [59], the method achieves comparable or better real-world performance with faster inference, since Transfusion reuses backbone weights for continuous denoising rather than a separate action expert. On LIBERO-90, the method (96.0%) outperforms both MoDE (95.0%) and Baku (90.0%), establishing a new state-of-the-art.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the π₀ family of VLAs (π₀, π₀-FAST, π₀.₅) and the broader VLA literature (RT-2, OpenVLA, HybridVLA, Transfusion), which have established the paradigm of fine-tuning pretrained VLMs with continuous action experts for robot control. It departs from prior work by identifying a specific failure mode—gradient interference from randomly-initialized action experts that degrades both training convergence and language following—and proposing a single-stage 'knowledge insulation' recipe that combines FAST discrete action tokens as a backbone learning signal with a flow-matching action expert whose gradients are stopped before reaching the VLM. This formalizes and unifies the two-stage procedure of π₀.₅ into a single training loop, while drawing on the mixture-of-experts architecture from Mixture-of-Transformers to separate backbone and expert weights.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck in VLA development: the tension between fast continuous-action inference (requiring a diffusion/flow-matching expert) and preserving the semantic knowledge from web-scale VLM pretraining. The stop-gradient mechanism is simple to implement yet yields a 7.5× reduction in training steps and consistent SOTA improvements across real-world and simulated benchmarks, making it a likely default recipe for future VLA training pipelines. Limitations include a ~20% increase in training compute, imperfect language following (data correlations still cause the model to ignore instructions), and reliance on proprietary robot data for the strongest results. Open questions include whether the approach scales to much larger backbones, whether the specific choice of FAST over naive tokenization as the representation-learning signal matters at scale, and whether co-training with VLM data can be further optimized for open-world generalization.
