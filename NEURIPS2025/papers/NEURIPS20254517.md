## Problem

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) remain brittle on fine-grained visual understanding, frequently hallucinating or missing subtle but semantically meaningful changes such as object presence, count, spatial position, or attribute shifts. The authors attribute this to two intertwined gaps: large-scale training data rarely contains tightly controlled image pairs with minimal semantic differences and aligned captions, and existing training objectives do not explicitly enforce feature-level stability across such small perturbations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254517/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pair of minimally edited RGB images (original and edited, filtered to CLIP cosine similarity > 0.7 for training and > 0.95 for benchmarking) together with a natural-language question asking for the most notable visual difference. During SFT training, the model is prompted to produce a free-form text difference description; during benchmark evaluation, it selects from four multiple-choice options (one correct answer plus three subtle distractors generated by GPT-4o). Each benchmark pair yields nine rephrased question variants, and the full evaluation set comprises 215 items (165 synthetic edit pairs plus 50 real-world pairs from MMVP).

## Method

The paper introduces a controlled data generation pipeline that uses Qwen-2.5-VL-72B for MLLM-guided filtering and edit-instruction planning, Gemini Flash 2.0 for precise localized image editing across 11 fine-grained categories (object, attribute, scene, spatial, action, part, counting, comparison, differentiation, negation, universality), and a four-step caption alignment procedure (original caption completion, edited caption generation, difference description via Qwen3-32B, and human verification) to produce the 50K-sample Micro Edit Dataset (MED). On top of MED, the authors propose a supervised fine-tuning loss (Eq. 7) that feeds the feature-level difference I_θ(x_i) − I_θ(x̂_i) into the text decoder and trains it to emit the corresponding natural-language difference description, implicitly encouraging the vision encoder to produce stable embeddings for unaltered regions while encoding semantic edits as structured feature-space shifts. Fine-tuning is performed via LoRA (rank 8) on the full model (ViT + LLM jointly unfrozen for Qwen and LLaMA-3.2; LLM-only for LLaVA-V1.6), using LLaMA-Factory with cosine LR scheduling. The ablation shows that joint ViT+LLM fine-tuning outperforms vision-encoder-only adaptation, confirming that aligning the language head with updated visual features is critical for reducing hallucinations and improving cross-modal grounding.

## Results

On the Micro Edit Detection (MED) benchmark, the best fine-tuned open-source model (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) achieves 51.61% average accuracy across 11 fine-grained edit categories, surpassing GPT-4o (51.32%) and Doubao-1.5-Vision-Pro (51.81%) while remaining well below human performance (95.21%). Fine-tuning yields consistent gains across all open-source backbones: Qwen2-VL-7B improves from 38.48% to 47.55% (+9.07), LLaVA-V1.6-7B from 31.04% to 40.44% (+9.40), and LLaMA-3.2-11B from 34.71% to 40.92% (+6.21). On standard benchmarks (Table 2), Qwen2.5-VL-7B (Ours) achieves the highest average score (58.52) and MME perception score (1701.87) among open-source models, with notable gains in Count (+9.17), Visual Correspondence (+4.07), and POPE (+1.23).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's best fine-tuned model (Qwen2.5-VL-7B Ours, 51.61%) surpasses GPT-4o (51.32%) by 0.29 points and Claude 3.7 Sonnet (40.44%) by 11.17 points on the MED benchmark, while slightly trailing GPT-4.1 (54.26%) and Doubao-1.5-Vision-Pro (51.81%). Among open-source baselines, the method consistently improves every backbone: Qwen2-VL-7B gains +9.07 (38.48→47.55), LLaVA-V1.6-7B gains +9.40 (31.04→40.44), and LLaMA-3.2-11B gains +6.21 (34.71→40.92). The largest per-category improvements appear in Comparison (+21.06 for Qwen2.5-VL-7B), Count (+22.23), and Differentiation (+25.00 for Qwen2-VL-7B), indicating the method is most effective on relational and compositional reasoning. On standard benchmarks (Table 2), fine-tuned models also improve: Qwen2.5-VL-7B (Ours) reaches 58.52 average and 1701.87 MME, outperforming its base (55.47 / 1685.14) and all other open-source baselines. Notably, the method reduces hallucination as measured by POPE (97.52 for Qwen2.5-VL-7B Ours vs. 96.29 base), though minor drops in Fine-grained Perception (MMStar) are observed, likely due to increased sensitivity to small edits.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of controlled image editing (leveraging Gemini Flash 2.0 for minimal local modifications) and MLLM fine-tuning, repurposing the image-editing pipeline as a data-generation engine rather than an end goal. It extends the hallucination-evaluation line of work (MMVP, POPE, TLDR) by introducing a structured, multi-category formulation of fine-grained visual difference detection with semantically aligned captions, and augments standard SFT with a feature-level consistency objective that prior MLLM training paradigms (CoCa-style contrastive + autoregressive losses) do not explicitly enforce. The CLIP-similarity filtering threshold follows MMVP's protocol, and the overall SFT recipe is implemented via LLaMA-Factory with LoRA, making the method a targeted extension of existing fine-tuning infrastructure rather than a new architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The publicly released 50K-sample MED dataset and 215-item benchmark give the community a concrete tool for diagnosing and tracking fine-grained visual grounding failures that current MLLMs exhibit in safety-critical domains (robotics, medical imaging, industrial QC). A key open question is whether the gains transfer beyond binary image-pair edits to multi-step, temporal, or compositional transformations, which the authors acknowledge as future work. The reliance on a single image-editing model (Gemini Flash 2.0) for data generation and the modest benchmark size (215 items) leave room for questions about data diversity and evaluation robustness, while the finding that LoRA fine-tuning suffices (vs. full-parameter) suggests the required representational shift is relatively shallow—a useful practical signal for resource-constrained practitioners.
