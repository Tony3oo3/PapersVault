## Problem

Conventional first-order quantization methods (UQ, BCQ) approximate real-valued matrices as linear combinations of binary bases, which severely limits representational capacity under ultra-low-bit constraints where each element can take only a handful of discrete values. While matrix factorization approaches (SVD, VQ) capture inter-dimensional correlations, they rely on unquantized real-valued codebooks or factors, undermining hardware efficiency. No prior work has explored representing real-valued matrices through quadratic (product) combinations of binary matrices with independent per-matrix scaling factors, leaving a gap between the expressiveness of factorization and the compactness of pure binary storage.

## Input / Output

The method takes a real-valued matrix W ∈ R^{m×n} (a DNN weight matrix, distance matrix, feature vector set, or image channel) and produces p stacks of binary matrices Y_i ∈ {0,1}^{m×l} and Z_i ∈ {0,1}^{l×n} along with scalar coefficients (r_i, s_i, t_i, u), where the intermediate dimension l = round(mn/(m+n)) ensures the total binary parameter count matches a p-bit first-order quantized model. For PTQ on ViTs, weight matrices are partitioned into groups (e.g., 384×384 for DeiT, 96×96 for Swin) and BQQ is applied independently to each group, yielding a quantized model whose inference requires only additions and bitwise ANDs. The output model is referred to as 'pseudo p-bit' since each stored element is 1-bit but the configuration encodes the same information as a p-bit quantized model.

## Method

BQQ approximates W as a sum of binary matrix products: W ≈ Σ_i (r_i Y_i Z_i + s_i Y_i 1_Z + t_i 1_Y Z_i) + u·1, where the quadratic structure (products of binary matrices) yields a wider output value range than any individual binary matrix, enabling multi-bit expressiveness from purely 1-bit components. The resulting mixed-integer optimization is NP-hard and is solved via a greedy strategy over stack index i, alternating between (a) convex quadratic programming for the scalar coefficients (closed-form via a 4×4 Hessian inversion) and (b) a PUBO formulation for the binary variables, solved with an extended Annealed Mean Field Descent (AMFD) algorithm that minimizes the KL divergence between a mean-field distribution and the canonical distribution at progressively lower temperatures. For PTQ, BQQ is applied in a data-free manner by minimizing weight reconstruction error per group, with an optional lightweight correction step that refines only bias and layer-normalization parameters using a small unlabeled calibration set via knowledge distillation. The key insight is that the quadratic binary representation subsumes BCQ as a special case (when l = max(m,n) and one factor is set to identity) while capturing structural redundancy analogous to low-rank factorization, yet storing only binary elements and a handful of scalars per group.

## Results

On ImageNet top-1 accuracy with ViT models at pseudo 2-bit quantization, BQQ achieves 72.09% on DeiT-B in the data-free setting (first to reach practical accuracy at this compression level) and 77.94% with calibration-based correction, outperforming the best calibration-based baseline COMQ (77.14%) by 0.8% and the best data-free baseline PSAQ-ViT (0.19%) by over 71 percentage points. At 3-bit, c-BQQ reaches 80.81% on DeiT-B (vs. 81.80% full precision), and at 4-bit it reaches 81.47%, approaching full-precision performance. In the matrix compression benchmark, BQQ consistently achieves the best MSE-vs-memory trade-off across five diverse matrix types (random, DeiT weights, TSPLIB distances, SIFT features, ImageNet images), with the largest gains over UQ/BCQ/LVQ on matrices with concentrated singular value spectra.

## Baselines & Comparisons

At 2-bit (the headline setting), c-BQQ outperforms COMQ by 2.2% on DeiT-S (69.41 vs. 67.19) and 0.8% on DeiT-B (77.94 vs. 77.14), while BQQ in the data-free setting surpasses PSAQ-ViT by over 57 percentage points on DeiT-S (58.25 vs. 0.27) and 71.9 points on DeiT-B (72.09 vs. 0.19). Against ERQ and RepQ-ViT at 2-bit, BQQ/c-BQQ leads by 26–72 points, as those methods collapse to near-chance accuracy under extreme compression. At 3-bit, c-BQQ edges out COMQ on DeiT-B (80.81 vs. 80.47) and matches ERQ within 0.1%, while BQQ (data-free) beats PSAQ-ViT by 22.85 points on DeiT-S (75.61 vs. 52.76). At 4-bit, all top methods converge within ~1% of full precision, with c-BQQ achieving the best DeiT-S score (79.12) and matching COMQ/ERQ on DeiT-B. In the matrix compression benchmark, BQQ dominates UQ, BCQ, and E8-LVQ on matrices with skewed singular value spectra (e.g., DeiT weights, TSPLIB distances) but is outperformed by JPEG on ImageNet images; conversely, BQQ outperforms SVD and VQ on matrices with flat spectra (e.g., random Gaussian). On language models (Qwen2.5-1.5B), BQQ achieves higher average downstream accuracy than GPTQ at 2-bit (41.0 vs. 37.0) and 3-bit (55.0 vs. 53.5), though GPTQ retains a slight edge at 4-bit (62.1 vs. 60.5).

## Relation to Prior Work

BQQ extends Binary Coding Quantization (BCQ), which represents real-valued matrices as linear combinations of binary matrices with independent scaling factors, by replacing the linear structure with quadratic (product) combinations of binary matrices. This is a conceptual departure from both first-order scalar quantization (UQ, BCQ) and from matrix factorization methods (SVD, VQ, BMF) that either retain floating-point components or operate only within a single numerical domain. The optimization is solved via an extension of Annealed Mean Field Descent (AMFD) from QUBO to general PUBO, which the authors derive explicitly. Unlike prior PTQ methods that minimize activation or output error, BQQ optimizes purely in weight space, making it a weight-reconstruction-based quantizer with a novel binary representation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that BQQ achieves the first practically usable data-free 2-bit-equivalent PTQ accuracy (72% on DeiT-B) without any PTQ-specific binary optimization, suggesting that the quadratic binary representation itself captures structural redundancy that first-order methods miss. Limitations include the greedy (suboptimal) stack-by-stack optimization, non-trivial quantization time (13–57 minutes depending on model size and bit width), and the absence of a theoretical guarantee that BQQ outperforms first-order quantization under specific conditions. Open questions include whether joint optimization of all binary stacks, output-error-driven binary optimization, and adaptive selection of the intermediate dimension l versus stack count p could yield further gains, and whether the approach scales to large language models where quantization time becomes prohibitive.
