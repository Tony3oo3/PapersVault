## Problem

Autonomous web agents routinely access users' personal information to complete tasks, yet there is no end-to-end benchmark measuring whether they adhere to the data minimization principle in practice. Prior privacy evaluations either probe LLMs in isolation about what is socially appropriate to disclose (which the paper shows overestimates real privacy behavior) or operate in emulated text-only environments, leaving a gap in assessing actual multi-step, multi-modal agent trajectories in realistic web settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254609/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each task provides the agent with a user_instruction (e.g., 'create a reddit post about cat allergies'), a synthetic user_data document (chat transcript, email thread, or notes) containing both task-relevant and task-irrelevant sensitive facts, and a webpage representation in either accessibility-tree text or screenshot-with-Set-of-Marks format. The agent produces a sequence of browser actions (clicks, type commands, navigation) that are executed in self-hosted replicas of Reddit, GitLab, and Shopping. Evaluation yields two scores per run: a binary utility indicator (was the target state achieved?) and a privacy leakage rate computed by a GPT-4o judge that inspects every action string for SENSITIVE_DATA mentions, with 98% agreement validated against human annotators.

## Method

AGENTDAM constructs 246 tasks from 123 human-written data seeds, each specifying a PLOT (high-level storyline for user_data) and a SENSITIVE_DATA list drawn from six categories (personal info, religious/political identity, employment, financial, educational, medical). An LLM expands each seed into diverse realistic user_data at sampling temperature, and the agent must complete the task without leaking SENSITIVE_DATA. Privacy is scored per-action by a GPT-4o judge using chain-of-thought reasoning with a binary rubric that accounts for paraphrasing and anonymization. The paper's key empirical finding is that probing LLMs about privacy yields scores 15–30 points higher than end-to-end agentic evaluation for GPT models (reversed for Llama), demonstrating that reasoning about privacy does not translate to acting on it. The proposed mitigation—a privacy-aware system prompt with CoT demonstrations instructing the agent to use only the minimum necessary data—reduces leakage from 12–46% to under 7% across models, at a modest utility cost of 3–6 points.

## Results

On AGENTDAM (246 tasks across Reddit, GitLab, and Shopping), GPT-4o achieves 45.5% utility and 63.8% privacy without mitigation, improving to 91.5% privacy with a privacy-aware CoT system prompt (at the cost of dropping utility to 41.5%). Llama-3.3-70b and Claude-3.5-Sonnet-CUA already exhibit strong privacy (~88–90%) without any mitigation, reaching 93.9% and 93.5% respectively after mitigation. Probing LLMs directly about whether to disclose sensitive information yields substantially inflated privacy scores for GPT models (e.g., 91.5% for GPT-4o vs. 64.6% end-to-end), while the gap reverses for Llama models (88.2% end-to-end vs. 74.8% probing).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between end-to-end agentic evaluation (AGENTDAM) and the probing paradigm used by ConfAIde [Mireshghallah et al., 2023] and CI-Bench [Cheng et al., 2024], where an LLM is simply asked whether disclosing a piece of sensitive information is appropriate. For GPT-4o, probing yields a privacy score of 0.915 versus only 0.646 in the actual agentic environment—a gap of 0.27—indicating that probing substantially overestimates privacy awareness. The same pattern holds for GPT-4o-mini (0.890 vs. 0.557) and GPT-4-turbo (0.846 vs. 0.732). Interestingly, the relationship inverts for Llama models: Llama-3.2-90B scores 0.882 end-to-end but only 0.748 under probing, suggesting these models are actually safer in practice than their verbalized privacy judgments imply. The authors conclude that neither probing method captures the utility–privacy tradeoff, since no agent trajectory is executed, and that end-to-end benchmarking is necessary for accurate privacy assessment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the WebArena/VisualWebArena infrastructure for realistic web-agent evaluation and extends the line of privacy-in-agents work (ConfAIde, CI-Bench, PrivacyLens, AirGapAgent) by moving from LLM probing and emulated text-only environments to end-to-end, multi-modal, full-stack agentic evaluation. The key departure is from prior probing-based methods that ask an LLM whether disclosing information is appropriate; AGENTDAM instead runs the agent through a real (self-hosted) web environment and measures whether sensitive data actually appears in the agent's action trajectory. It also differs from adversarial-agent-security benchmarks (WASP, AgentDojo) by operating in a benign, non-adversarial setting focused on inadvertent leakage rather than targeted attacks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that current state-of-the-art web agents (GPT-4o, Llama-3, Claude-3.5) leak task-irrelevant sensitive information at rates of 12–46% even in benign environments, and that the standard practice of probing LLMs about privacy substantially overestimates their actual data-minimization behavior (e.g., GPT-4o: 0.915 probing vs. 0.646 end-to-end). The proposed privacy-aware CoT prompting mitigation reduces leakage but plateaus below 94% and degrades task utility, leaving open the question of whether training-based or architectural approaches are needed for reliable inference-time data minimization. As autonomous agents gain access to increasingly sensitive user data (financial, medical, identity), a standardized end-to-end evaluation like AGENTDAM will be essential for responsible deployment, though the current 246-task, three-website scope limits generalizability.
