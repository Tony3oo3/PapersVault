## Problem

Visual embeddings are widely used for retrieval (local similarity), but whether they also support global ranking along continuous ordinal attributes (age, crowd count, aesthetics, head pose) remains unexplored. The paper asks two questions: are modern visual encoders 'rankable' (i.e., does a single linear direction exist that preserves attribute order), and how little supervision is needed to recover that direction?

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images (224×224, 320×320, or 518×518 depending on the encoder) and produces a scalar rank score via dot product with a learned rank axis vector in the embedding space. Unusually, the rank axis can be computed from as few as two extreme images (one low, one high on the target attribute) without any continuous labels, or from a text prompt pair in the zero-shot setting. The output is an ordered ranking of an arbitrary image collection along the chosen attribute.

## Method

The paper defines rankability as the existence of a unit vector v_A such that projecting embeddings onto v_A preserves the ground-truth order of a continuous attribute A. The rank axis is recovered via simple linear regression on (embedding, label) pairs over frozen encoder outputs, or more efficiently as the L2-normalized difference between two extreme-sample embeddings (v_A = (f(x_h) − f(x_l))/‖f(x_h) − f(x_l)‖). Across 7 encoders (ResNet-50, ViT-B/32, ConvNeXtV2-L, DINOv2, and three CLIP variants) and 9 datasets with 7 attributes, Spearman's rank correlation between the projected score and ground-truth labels is measured. The key empirical finding is that linear regression on frozen embeddings nearly matches a 2-layer MLP upper bound, indicating the ordinal structure is predominantly linear. The authors further show that only ~1k labelled samples (or just two extreme images) suffice to recover most of the ranking performance, and that rank axes transfer non-trivially across datasets sharing the same attribute.

## Results

Across 7 encoders and 9 datasets, linear regression on frozen embeddings (the 'rankability' metric, measured by Spearman rank correlation ρ) achieves values close to both the nonlinear MLP upper bound and the finetuned encoder upper bound, while far exceeding the no-train lower bound. For example, on Adience (age), rankability reaches ρ=0.861 versus a no-train baseline of 0.266 and upper bounds of 0.878 (nonlinear) and 0.910 (finetuned). On UCF-QNRF (crowd count), rankability is 0.843 vs. 0.220 no-train. Notably, a rank axis computed from just two extreme samples (one low, one high) achieves ρ≈0.75 on Adience, while zero-shot text prompting lags considerably (e.g., ρ=0.782 vs. 0.917 for linear regression on Adience).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares linear rank-axis regression against three reference points: a no-train lower bound (randomly initialized encoder), a nonlinear MLP upper bound on frozen embeddings, and a finetuned encoder upper bound. For most attributes (age, crowd count, pitch, aesthetics, recency), the linear rankability metric is within 0.01–0.05 of the nonlinear upper bound, indicating that nearly all ordinal information in the embedding is linearly accessible. The gap to the finetuned upper bound is slightly larger (e.g., 0.861 vs. 0.910 on Adience), but the linear method still captures the vast majority of learnable signal. Against dedicated SOTA methods, linear regression on UTKFace achieves MAE 4.25, at par with MiVOLO (MAE 4.23) and only slightly behind a ranking-aware adapter (MAE 3.83). In the extreme-pairs setting, two unlabeled samples outperform or match continuous-label training when the training set is ≤1k samples, but regular training surpasses extreme pairs as data grows. Zero-shot text prompting (single prompt or text-difference) consistently underperforms linear regression by 0.1–0.35 SRCC across attributes, with the largest gap on recency (0.449 vs. 0.790).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the 'steering vector' concept from LLM interpretability (Tigges et al., Rimsky et al.) to visual embeddings, showing that a linear direction between two extreme image embeddings captures an ordinal attribute axis. It extends the linear-probing literature on CLIP geometry, which has focused on binary concept detection, to the ordinal/relational setting, and builds on the text-difference prompting approach of Wang et al. (AAAI 2023) for zero-shot axis recovery. The key departure from prior ordinal-attribute work (Parikh & Grauman, Liang & Grauman, and recent CLIP-based ranking methods) is that the paper studies whether *frozen, unmodified* embeddings already contain this structure, rather than training or fine-tuning for a specific ranking task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: a vector database of images could support arbitrary attribute-based sorting (age, aesthetics, crowd density) using only two reference images, without any task-specific training. This opens a new operational mode for image retrieval systems beyond nearest-neighbour search. However, the findings are purely empirical with no theoretical grounding, the attribute set is limited (7 attributes, 9 datasets), and rankability degrades sharply for some attributes (yaw, roll), leaving open whether the phenomenon is universal or specific to attributes with high variability in pretraining data. A broader theoretical characterization of which attributes admit linear rank axes, and why, remains a key open question.
