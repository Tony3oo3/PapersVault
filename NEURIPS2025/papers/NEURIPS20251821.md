## Problem

Flicker artifacts from AC-powered lighting combined with rolling-shutter exposure produce non-uniform brightness bands that degrade image quality and impair downstream vision tasks, yet no large-scale, realistic dataset exists for multi-frame flicker removal (MFFR). Existing synthetic flicker methods incorrectly model the removal target as complete elimination of flicker illumination rather than adjustment to its effective value, and the non-repeatable nature of dynamic scenes makes it nearly impossible to capture aligned flickering/flicker-free pairs with motion.

## Input / Output

The method takes a burst of 3 flickering RGB frames (concatenated along the channel dimension to form a 9-channel input) sampled at random intervals of 1–3 to simulate varying capture rates, and produces a single flicker-free image corresponding to one of the three input frames. Images are resized rather than cropped during training to preserve the periodic spatial structure of flicker bands along the line-scan direction. Synthetic data uses 512×512 resolution with 50 or 60 Hz network frequency and row scanning frequencies between 100–160 kHz.

## Method

The core contribution is a three-pronged dataset construction pipeline. First, a Retinex-based synthesis method models the flickering image as I_flicker = R ⊙ (L_a + L_f) and redefines the clean target as I_clean = R ⊙ (L_a + L_f̄), where L_f̄ is the effective (time-averaged) flicker illumination rather than zero; it supports full-wave, half-wave, and PWM rectification modes to generate diverse flicker patterns at controllable intensity, area, and frequency. Second, 4,000 real-world static flickering pairs are captured using Canon cameras with short exposures (1/1000–1/2000 s) for flicker frames and long exposures (1/50–1/60 s) for clean references across 369 scenes. Third, a green-screen compositing technique extracts foreground subjects with motion from VideoMatte240K and overlays them onto the real flickering backgrounds, producing 3,690 dynamic pairs that teach the model to distinguish motion-induced pixel changes from flicker. Training follows a two-stage protocol: pre-training on the unlimited synthetic data for initialization, then fine-tuning on the real BurstDeflicker-S and BurstDeflicker-G subsets, with Restormer (modified for 3-frame input) serving as the primary restoration network.

## Results

On the static test set, Restormer trained on BurstDeflicker achieves the best full-reference scores: 30.634 PSNR, 0.918 SSIM, and 0.045 LPIPS, outperforming Burstformer (29.439 PSNR) and HDRTransformer (30.031 PSNR). On the dynamic test set (no-reference metrics), Restormer scores 59.097 MUSIQ, 34.896 PIQE, and 19.324 BRISQUE, the best across all three metrics. Retraining Retinexformer and DeflickerCycleGAN on BurstDeflicker yields substantial gains over their original versions (e.g., Retinexformer PSNR improves from 15.704 to 27.212), demonstrating the dataset's effectiveness independent of architecture.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks four prior methods against three architectures trained on BurstDeflicker. Restormer (the best architecture on their dataset) outperforms Burstformer by 1.195 dB PSNR and HDRTransformer by 0.603 dB PSNR on the static set, while also achieving the lowest PIQE (34.896 vs. 37.014 and 37.292) and BRISQUE (19.324 vs. 20.451 and 21.588) on the dynamic set. The most striking comparison is between original and retrained versions: Retinexformer jumps from 15.704 to 27.212 PSNR (+11.5 dB) and DeflickerCycleGAN from 20.358 to 26.408 PSNR (+6.1 dB) when trained on BurstDeflicker, confirming the dataset's value. Among multi-frame architectures, Restormer consistently leads on all six metrics, while HDRTransformer is competitive on PSNR/SSIM but lags on no-reference dynamic metrics. Single-frame methods (Retinexformer, DeflickerCycleGAN) remain inferior to all three multi-frame baselines, validating the MFFR formulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the flicker removal literature from single-image (SIFR) to multi-frame (MFFR) settings, building on the Retinex decomposition of Land [41] to model the interplay between ambient and flickering illumination. It explicitly departs from Lin et al.'s DeflickerCycleGAN [2] by redefining the restoration target: rather than treating all flicker-induced brightness as harmful and removing it entirely, the paper argues the flicker component should be adjusted to its effective (time-averaged) value. The green-screen compositing strategy for injecting motion into flickering pairs is a novel borrowing from film VFX, addressing the non-repeatability problem that has prevented prior work from producing dynamic paired data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first MFFR benchmark, BurstDeflicker removes a critical bottleneck that has kept flicker removal research stuck on single-frame or static-scene formulations, enabling the field to tackle the practically important case of dynamic scenes under AC lighting. The green-screen compositing technique is a reusable recipe for constructing paired data in any restoration task where motion and degradation must co-occur. Open questions remain: performance degrades under severe sole-source flicker and strong inter-frame misalignment, and the paper acknowledges that purpose-built architectures exploiting flicker's periodic, row-aligned structure could outperform general-purpose restorers like Restormer.
