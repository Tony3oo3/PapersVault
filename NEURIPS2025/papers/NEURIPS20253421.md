## Problem

Gradient-based training data attribution (TDA) methods like influence functions offer theoretical grounding but are computationally prohibitive at scale, while representation-based methods are scalable yet rely on heuristic embeddings that are not optimized for the attribution task or aligned with the target model. Additionally, both families typically estimate group influence by naively summing individual scores, failing to capture interactions among training examples. AirRep aims to close this gap by learning task-specific, model-aligned representations explicitly optimized for attribution quality.

## Input / Output

AirRep takes a test example (a prompt–response pair) and a training subset (a set of text examples) as input. The test example is encoded by a BERT-based sentence encoder (GTE-Small, 30M parameters) into a 384-dimensional embedding, while each training example in the subset is independently encoded and then aggregated via attention-based pooling into a single group embedding. The final output is a scalar influence score computed as the inner product of the test embedding and the pooled group embedding, which is trained to correlate with the actual cross-entropy loss of a target LM fine-tuned on that subset.

## Method

AirRep introduces two key mechanisms: (1) a trainable encoder initialized from GTE-Small with a randomly initialized projection head, which is optimized end-to-end so that the learned representation space is aligned with the target model's attribution behavior rather than generic text similarity; and (2) an attention-based influence pooling layer that computes per-example weights α_i proportional to exp(|Enc(x)ᵀ·Enc(z_i)|) normalized across the subset, enabling selective aggregation that reflects the empirically observed sparsity of influence. The entire model is trained with a weighted pairwise ranking loss over auto-generated supervision: for each target example x, M random training subsets are constructed, a base LM (Qwen2.5-0.5B) is fine-tuned on each subset, and the resulting evaluation losses are normalized to produce ground-truth preference labels. The ranking loss assigns lower weights to pairs with small score differences (below T_min=0.1) and clips large differences (at T_max=5.0) to mitigate label noise from stochastic LM training. This design connects to high-order group influence functions, where sample-wise interaction weights emerge naturally from second-order Taylor expansions of the perturbed objective.

## Results

On the Linear Datamodeling Score (LDS) metric across four unseen instruction-tuning datasets (FLAN, Alpaca, Tulu, SafeRLHF), AirRep achieves an average score of 26.23, substantially outperforming the best gradient-based baseline LoGra (18.45 at 48× storage) and the best representation-based baseline GTE-Small (7.65). AirRep uses only 384-dimensional embeddings (1× storage) while being ~80× more computationally efficient and ~50× more storage-efficient than LoGra at inference time. In data classification, AirRep reaches 86.41% accuracy on FLAN (vs. 85.44% for LoGra at 18432 dims) and 88.20% on Tulu, though it trails GTE-Small (90.60%) on SafeRLHF due to lack of harmful content in its training data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AirRep outperforms all eight baselines on the primary LDS metric across all four evaluation datasets. Against the strongest gradient-based method, LoGra at 48× storage (18.45 avg), AirRep gains +7.78 points while using 48× less storage and ~80× less compute. Against LESS (16.16) and Dsdm (18.02), AirRep leads by +10.07 and +8.21 points respectively. Among representation-based baselines, the gap is even larger: AirRep surpasses GTE-Small (its own initialization) by +18.58 points, TF-IDF by +16.25, RDS by +22.37, and DSIR by +26.25. In data classification (Table 2), AirRep beats LoGra (18432) on FLAN (86.41 vs. 85.44) and Tulu (88.20 vs. 86.00), but loses to GTE-Small on SafeRLHF (87.20 vs. 90.60), which the authors attribute to the absence of harmful content in AirRep's UltraChat training data. In data selection (Figure 4), AirRep matches gradient-based methods and full-data training in F1 score while being orders of magnitude faster, whereas gradient-based methods take longer to score data than to train the model on the full dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

AirRep sits at the intersection of two established TDA families: gradient-based influence estimation (influence functions, LoGra, LESS, TracIn) and representation-based similarity methods (TF-IDF, DSIR, RDS, GTE). Rather than building on a single prior method, it repurposes the representation-based inner-product scoring framework but makes the encoder trainable and task-specific, departing from the heuristic/fixed embeddings used by all prior representation-based approaches. The attention-based pooling mechanism is theoretically motivated by the structure of high-order group influence functions (Basu et al.), which show that sample-wise weighting emerges naturally in second-order and beyond approximations. The training objective draws on the datamodels evaluation framework (Ilyas et al.) and noisy-label reweighting techniques to handle stochastic LM training signals.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: matching or exceeding SOTA gradient-based attribution quality at ~80× lower inference cost and ~50× less storage makes large-scale data attribution (e.g., curating pre-training corpora for LLMs) far more tractable. The generalization from a 0.5B-trained AirRep to 7B target models and across different LM families (Qwen, Llama, GPT-2) suggests the learned representations capture transferable attribution structure. Key limitations include evaluation restricted to the fine-tuning stage (not pre-training), the non-trivial cost of generating training data via M model fine-tunings, and the absence of harmful-content signal in UltraChat training data (hurting SafeRLHF classification). Open questions include whether the approach extends to pre-training attribution, multimodal settings, and whether the attention pooling can be further refined to capture higher-order interactions.
