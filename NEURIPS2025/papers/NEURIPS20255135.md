## Problem

Prior random matrix theory analyses of neural network weight matrices identified large singular value outliers as evidence of learned features, but the role of small singular values—those falling below the Marchenko-Pastur bulk in non-square matrices—remained unexplored. The common assumption in SVD-based pruning is that small singular values are negligible, yet conflicting empirical reports exist about whether removing them helps or hurts performance. This paper asks: where exactly in the singular value spectrum is information encoded in pretrained transformers, and can small singular value outliers carry as much or more information than large ones?

## Input / Output

The method consumes weight matrices from pretrained transformers (BERT 110M, Pythia 506M, Llama-8B 8.03B) and activation data computed on WikiText or BookCorpus to build per-layer, per-matrix-type activation covariance matrices. Outputs include: (1) singular value spectra annotated with Marchenko-Pastur bounds to flag left and right outliers, (2) per-singular-vector overlap scores O_k measuring alignment with activation covariance eigenvectors, and (3) perplexity or task-accuracy degradation curves when entire deciles of singular values are zeroed out across all blocks of a given matrix type. A key structural detail is that square matrices can only produce right-side outliers, while non-square matrices (e.g., Up-Projection, Down-Projection, Gate-Projection) can produce left-side outliers, and the two cases behave fundamentally differently in terms of information content.

## Method

The paper uses the Marchenko-Pastur distribution as a zero-information null hypothesis: agreement with RMT indicates randomness, while deviations indicate learned structure. For each weight matrix, it computes the activation covariance matrix from dataset inputs and measures the maximum dot product O_k between each right singular vector and the covariance eigenvectors, revealing that singular vectors associated with both large and small outliers align strongly with high-variance data directions. Empirically, it zeros out deciles of singular values (reconstructing the matrix with original singular vectors) and measures perplexity or benchmark accuracy to rank the importance of each spectral region. The theoretical contribution is a minimal two-layer linear teacher-student model with non-trivial noise covariance Σ = αI − (α²β/(1+αβ))vvᵀ, which shows that the noise matrix itself develops a small singular value outlier below the MP bulk, thereby stabilizing a small outlier in the full weight matrix W = W₀ + X. The model predicts that the farther the small outlier sits below the bulk, the higher the overlap between its singular vectors and the teacher direction, explaining why small singular values can encode more information than bulk values.

## Results

On GSM8K (3-shot, Llama3-8B, baseline 43.2%), removing the smallest singular values (Decile 1) from the non-square Down-Projection matrix drops accuracy to 2.0%, making it the second most damaging decile after Decile 10 (0.0%), while for the square Attention-Output and Query matrices, Decile 1 removal causes negligible loss (40.0% and 40.3% respectively). On the RULER benchmark (LLaMA 3.1 8B Chat, context 8192), removing Decile 1 from all layers simultaneously causes complete failure (score 0.0) on all five tasks (niah multiV, niah multiQ, vt, cwe, fwe), matching the catastrophic effect of removing Decile 10. On HumanEval (baseline 32.32%), Decile 1 removal from Down-Proj. drops accuracy to 0.0% and from Gate-Proj. to 28.7%, confirming the pattern across benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, its central comparison is internal, contrasting the importance of different singular-value deciles across matrix types. The key finding is a sharp dichotomy: for non-square matrices (Down-Projection, Gate-Projection, Up-Projection), the smallest decile is the second or third most important group, whereas for square matrices (Query, Attention-Output), the smallest decile is among the least important. The paper also reconciles two prior works on small singular values: Hsu et al. [23] (who fine-tune before pruning and find small values critical) and Sharma et al. [41] (who prune without re-fine-tuning and report gains). The BERT fine-tuning experiments (Figure 6) resolve this conflict: removing small singular values before fine-tuning causes only minor or no accuracy loss (recoverable), but removing them after fine-tuning causes statistically significant degradation on RTE and SST2, indicating that fine-tuning and alignment encode information into small singular values that is then irreplaceable. The RULER results on LLaMA 3.1 8B Chat further confirm that the smallest decile is the second most important group across all long-context tasks when removing from all layers simultaneously.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RMT-based spectral analysis of neural network weight matrices established by Martin et al. and Thamm et al., which identified large singular value outliers as signatures of feature learning. The key departure is showing that small singular value outliers in non-square matrices are equally informative, a regime previously dismissed as noise under the standard Eckart-Young low-rank approximation framework. The theoretical model in Section 6 builds on Forner's blue-function computation for low-rank perturbations of random matrices with non-trivial noise covariance, extending the BBP transition analysis to the small-outlier regime. Empirically, the paper also reconciles conflicting findings in the SVD-pruning literature (Hsu et al. vs. Sharma et al.) by showing that the importance of small singular values depends critically on whether fine-tuning has occurred.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the smallest decile of singular values in non-square matrices can be the second most important for model performance directly changes how SVD-based pruning and compression of LLMs should be performed, with immediate practical implications for model deployment. The fine-tuning experiments resolve a genuine debate in the literature by showing that small singular values encode alignment-related information that is recoverable before fine-tuning but critical after it. Limitations include the simplified two-layer linear theoretical model, the absence of the expected overlap pattern in some matrices (Llama's Down-Projection, Attention-Output), and testing on only three model families. Open questions include whether the phenomenon scales predictably with model size, whether the noise covariance structure in real trained networks matches the theoretical model, and how to design pruning algorithms that selectively preserve small outliers while removing bulk singular values.
