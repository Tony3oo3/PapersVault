## Problem

Liquid cooling in high-density HPC data centers relies on static or rule-based controls (e.g., ASHRAE Guideline 36) that cannot adapt to dynamic, skewed workloads, leaving significant energy savings unrealized. While RL has shown promise for air-cooled data centers, its application to large-scale liquid cooling systems is constrained by the absence of high-fidelity, RL-ready testbeds that capture the full thermo-fluidic coupling from cooling towers through CDUs to individual server blade groups.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255325/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The RL agents consume time-series state observations from a Modelica-compiled FMU (a non-linear state-space simulator of the Frontier cooling system), including per-blade-group temperatures and power inputs, cooling tower cell power, water leaving temperatures, and outside-air wet-bulb temperature. The action space is hybrid: continuous controls (coolant supply temperature setpoint and pump flow rate in [-1,1] via tanh) combined with a constrained discrete-continuous valve vector that must lie in [0,1] and sum to exactly 1.0 for mass conservation, generated via a softplus-activated Dirichlet head. The LLM-based agents additionally produce natural-language explanations of each control action as a secondary output.

## Method

LC-Opt wraps a high-fidelity Modelica digital twin of the Frontier supercomputer's liquid cooling system (exported as an FMU) in a Gymnasium interface, with the model itself generated from a JSON specification via the AutoCSM API to support customizable cabinet, CDU, valve, pump, and cooling-tower topologies. For scalable multi-agent control, the paper introduces Centralized Action (CA) execution that decomposes the observation space per cooling tower or per cabinet and performs batched inference, treating CT power as a d-separation variable while retaining all return-water temperatures to capture residual coupling. The Blade Group MDP uses a multi-head actor: one head outputs the scaled temperature setpoint and flow rate (tanh), and a second head outputs a Dirichlet-distributed valve vector (softplus), enabling the agent to learn proportional coolant allocation to blade groups. Trained PPO policies are then distilled via VIPER-weighted experience replay into (a) QLoRA-fine-tuned LLMs that both control and explain actions, and (b) interpretable decision/regression trees, all coordinated through an agentic mesh of specialized LLM agents (Control, Sensor, Maintenance, Configuration, UI, Orchestration) communicating over a central message bus with a Math Toolbox for physical grounding.

## Results

On the Frontier supercomputer digital twin (N=2 towers, m=2 cells, C=5 cabinets, B=3 blade groups), the best RL configuration (Centralized Action with Multi-head policy, Case 7) achieves 95.63% blade-group temperature compliance (D_blade_avg) versus 76.92% for the ASHRAE G36 baseline, while reducing average cooling tower power from 237.31 kW to 206.52 kW (~13% reduction) and IT-level cooling power from 235.28 kW to 197.18 kW (~16% reduction). The multi-head policy also discovers a non-linear relationship between blade-group power input and valve actuation, with Spearman rank correlation of 0.680 (vs. 0.215 for single-head). Scaling to larger configurations (up to N=4 towers, C=25 cabinets), the CA+Multihead policy maintains 92–96% temperature compliance while the G36 baseline drops to 68–83%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is ASHRAE Guideline 36 (G36), implemented as a 'trim and respond' rule-based controller for both CDU coolant supply temperature and cooling tower leaving water temperature. The best RL method (Case 7: Centralized Action + Multi-head policy) decisively outperforms G36 on all three primary metrics: blade temperature compliance improves from 76.92% to 95.63% (+18.7 pp), cooling tower power drops from 237.31 kW to 206.52 kW (−13.0%), and IT-level cooling power falls from 235.28 kW to 197.18 kW (−16.2%). On scalability (Table 4), the RL policy consistently maintains 92–96% compliance across unseen larger configurations while G36 degrades to 68–83%, indicating the rule-based approach fails to adapt cooling allocation to heterogeneous blade-group loads. In the LLM distillation ablation (Figure 10), fine-tuned LLMs (particularly Qwen) distilled from the RL policy surpass both the G36 baseline and the original PPO policy in average blade temperature and cooling tower power, demonstrating effective knowledge transfer. The G36 baseline is competitive only in the narrow case of CT-only RL (Case 2), where aggressive G36 valve actions at the BG level inadvertently increase CT power to 246.46 kW.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Frontier supercomputer's high-fidelity Modelica digital twin (Brewer et al., SC24) by wrapping it in a Gymnasium-compatible RL environment with fine-grained blade-group valve control, CDU setpoint/flow control, and cooling tower supervisory control—capabilities absent from the original ASHRAE G36 rule-based controllers. For policy interpretability, it adapts the VIPER policy extraction framework (Bastani et al., NeurIPS 2018) to PPO-trained agents, distilling into both decision trees and QLoRA-fine-tuned LLMs. The work departs from prior RL-for-data-center-cooling literature, which has focused almost exclusively on air-cooled systems, by formulating the first end-to-end liquid cooling MDP spanning cooling towers through blade groups, and introduces a multi-head actor with a Dirichlet-parameterized valve head to enforce mass-conservation constraints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As AI workloads push server power densities beyond air cooling's limits, this benchmark provides the ML community with a realistic, customizable, and open platform for developing and comparing liquid cooling control strategies—something previously infeasible without access to a multi-million-dollar production supercomputer. The finding that a QLoRA-fine-tuned LLM distilled from an RL policy can match or exceed the original PPO agent while generating natural-language explanations addresses a critical deployment barrier (operator trust) in safety-critical infrastructure. Key open questions include whether the approach generalizes to chip-level thermal modeling for next-gen GPUs, hybrid air+liquid cooling, diverse weather profiles, and whether the agentic LLM architecture scales to the ~10⁴ blade groups of a full Frontier-scale deployment.
