## Problem

The ACCORD framework for ℓ₁-regularized pseudolikelihood-based partial correlation network estimation is inherently scalable compared to graphical lasso, but its only available solver is a first-order proximal gradient method requiring thousands of iterations even on HPC supercomputers. No second-order algorithm with faster convergence exists for this nonsmooth problem, limiting its use to accessible computing environments such as GPU workstations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255473/cropped_page7_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a data matrix X ∈ ℝ^{n×p} and a regularization parameter λ, and produces a sparse (generally non-symmetric) matrix Ω whose off-diagonal sparsity pattern matches that of the precision matrix Θ = Ω_D Ω, from which the partial correlation network is read off. A key structural property is that the ACCORD objective is rowwise separable, so the p-dimensional problem decomposes into p independent p-dimensional subproblems that can be solved in parallel. Each Newton step only requires inverting a submatrix of dimension |Ā_k| × |Ā_k| (the active set size), which is small under sparsity, making the per-iteration cost modest even on a single GPU workstation.

## Method

The paper formulates the KKT conditions of the rowwise-separable ACCORD problem as finding a root of a strongly semismooth function F: ℝ^{2p} → ℝ^{2p}, then derives the Bouligand (B-)derivative of F to construct a B-semismooth Newton iteration. Each Newton step reduces to solving a small symmetric positive definite linear system of size |Ā_k| × |Ā_k| plus a linear complementarity problem (LCP) of even smaller dimension, rather than a full p×p matrix inversion. Globalization is achieved via a damped line search on the merit function θ = ½‖F‖², with a fallback to the proximal gradient step when the active-set rank condition fails. The resulting algorithm (Algorithm 1) is shown to converge globally and locally quadratically, and is equivalent to a primal-dual active set (PDAS) strategy in the generic case where no iterate lands exactly on the soft-thresholding kink. Experiments on simulated data and the TCGA-LIHC dataset (p ≈ 305,000, n = 365) demonstrate convergence in a few dozen iterations versus over a thousand for the proximal gradient baseline, with shorter wall-clock time on a 6-GPU workstation.

## Results

On the TCGA-LIHC dataset (p=305,471 features, n=365 samples), Algorithm 1 (damped B-semismooth Newton) converges within a few dozen iterations, whereas ACCORD-FBS (proximal gradient) requires over 1,000 iterations. At λ=0.45, Algorithm 1 achieves ||F||=0 in 3,352 seconds versus ACCORD-FBS's ||F||=4.035×10² in 5,150 seconds. On simulated data (p=1,000, n=500), the B-semismooth Newton method exhibits locally quadratic convergence, achieving approximately an order-of-magnitude faster reduction in both the ACCORD objective and squared distance to the optimum compared to the linearly converging proximal gradient method.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is ACCORD-FBS, the proximal gradient algorithm from Lee et al. (2025) that solves the same ACCORD optimization problem (3) with a linear convergence rate. Algorithm 1 dominates ACCORD-FBS on every tested configuration: it achieves shorter wall-clock time (e.g., 3,352 s vs. 5,150 s at λ=0.45) and a substantially smaller residual ||F|| (0 vs. 4.035×10² at λ=0.45; 1.260×10¹ vs. 4.709×10³ at λ=0.2). The advantage is most pronounced at the default λ=0.45, where Algorithm 1 fully converges (||F||=0) while ACCORD-FBS still has a residual two orders of magnitude larger after 100 iterations. The per-iteration cost is comparable since both methods are dominated by sparse-dense matrix operations, so the speedup comes almost entirely from the reduced number of iterations (tens vs. thousands) enabled by the locally quadratic convergence of the semismooth Newton step.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the ACCORD framework [19], which reformulated sparse precision matrix estimation via a one-to-one transformation to eliminate the O(p³) matrix inversion bottleneck of the graphical lasso, but whose only available solver was a first-order proximal gradient method with linear convergence. The algorithmic core draws on the semismooth Newton framework of Qi and Sun [31] and the Bouligand-derivative globalization strategy of Pang [28], while the connection to the primal-dual active set (PDAS) strategy [12] and the reduction of each Newton step to a small linear complementarity problem [4] provide the practical implementation pathway. The paper explicitly distinguishes itself from the SSNAL augmented Lagrangian framework [21, 23, 35], noting that SSNAL's outer ALM iteration is only linearly convergent and is designed for n ≫ p settings, whereas here the KKT system is solved directly on the primal with globally damped Newton steps.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial for multi-omics researchers: a 300,000-feature LIHC dataset that required 200 node-hours on a national supercomputer with the proximal gradient method is solved in under an hour on a six-GPU workstation, making partial correlation network estimation accessible outside HPC centers. The trade-off revealed in Table 1—that the Newton step becomes unreliable for dense graphs with small λ, forcing fallback to the slower proximal gradient step—highlights that the method's advantage is most pronounced in the sparse regime that is also the regime of statistical identifiability. Open questions include adaptive λ selection via continuation strategies, extension to non-Gaussian or non-separable loss functions, and whether the rowwise separability exploited here can be generalized to other structured precision matrix problems.
