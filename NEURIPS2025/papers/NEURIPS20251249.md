## Problem

While it is empirically clear that deeper transformers exhibit capabilities shallower ones lack, no prior work established a formal, unconditional, parameter-uniform depth hierarchy for standard softmax transformers. This paper asks: can we prove exactly which languages require which depth, and does the theory predict practical length generalization behavior?

## Input / Output

Theoretically, the method operates on strings over a binary alphabet {a, b} from the family L_k (strings with k alternating blocks of a's and b's), producing a language membership decision. Experimentally, the problem is reframed as next-token prediction: given a prefix of a string in L_k (preceded by <BOS>), the transformer outputs a binary label indicating whether the current prefix is a valid prefix of L_k. The transformer is future-masked, uses no positional encodings, and is trained with cross-entropy loss on multi-class classification of next-token sets.

## Method

The paper establishes a strict depth hierarchy through a three-step argument. First, it proves an exact depth-preserving equivalence between fixed-precision transformers (using a 'sumdiv' operation that avoids rounding attention weights to zero) and the temporal logic TL[#], extending prior partial equivalences. Second, it proves a strict depth hierarchy for TL[#] by adapting the restriction-and-reduction technique of Behle et al. (2009): a cropping lemma finds subintervals in Parikh-vector space where minimal depth-1 subformulas are constant, and a reduction lemma replaces those subformulas with Parikh numerical predicates, iteratively reducing depth while restricting the language via affix restrictions. The contradiction arises because depth-1 formulas are commutative on the middle (insensitive to symbol ordering), while the restricted L_{k+1} language is not. Third, experiments train future-masked transformers of varying depth on L_k next-token prediction, confirming that depth k is necessary and sufficient to solve L_{k+2}, matching the theoretical prediction up to L_12.

## Results

The paper proves a strict depth hierarchy: a depth-k fixed-precision transformer can recognize L_{k+1} (strings with k+1 alternating blocks of a's and b's) but no depth-(k-1) transformer can (Theorem 4.11). For the next-token prediction variant, Corollary 5.2 predicts that depth k is necessary and sufficient for L_{k+2}. Empirically, future-masked transformers without positional encodings achieve ~100% accuracy on L_k when depth ≥ k−2, and near 0% accuracy when depth < k−2, closely matching the theoretical boundary (e.g., depth 3 achieves 100% on L5 but only 37% on L6; depth 5 achieves 100% on L7 but only 49% on L8). The models also length-generalize, maintaining high accuracy on test strings up to double the training length.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against empirical baselines in the traditional sense; its primary contribution is a theoretical depth hierarchy validated by experiments. In terms of prior theoretical depth-separation results for transformers, the paper positions itself against three works: Yang et al. (2024) proved a depth hierarchy for unique-hard attention transformers (unconditional, parameter-uniform, but using a non-standard attention mechanism); Chen et al. (2025) proved a depth-width tradeoff for softmax transformers via communication complexity (unconditional, but parameters depend on sequence length n); and Sanford et al. (2024) proved a logarithmic depth lower bound for k-hop induction heads (conditional on a conjecture, parameters depend on n). This work is the first to establish an unconditional, parameter-uniform depth hierarchy for standard softmax transformers, making it the most directly applicable to length generalization in practice. The empirical validation confirms that the theoretical depth boundary (depth k solves L_{k+2} but not L_{k+3}) is tightly predicted by actual transformer training, with the transition from near-0% to ~100% accuracy occurring within 1–2 layers of the predicted threshold.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the partial equivalence between temporal counting logic TL[#] and transformers established by Yang and Chiang (2024) into an exact, depth-preserving equivalence for fixed-precision softmax transformers, and then leverages a depth-separation technique adapted from Behle et al. (2009) for majority-quantifier logic to prove a strict hierarchy. It departs from prior depth-separation results for transformers (Yang et al. 2024 on unique-hard attention, Chen et al. 2025 via communication complexity, Sanford et al. 2024 conditioned on a conjecture) by being the first to provide an unconditional, parameter-uniform separation for standard softmax transformers. The C-RASP programming language, a variant of RASP (Weiss et al., 2021), serves as the computational bridge, with the paper showing that C-RASP depth, TL[#] formula depth, and transformer layer depth all coincide exactly.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result gives a clean, interpretable account of what additional capability each transformer layer provides: one more layer enables recognizing one more alternating block of symbols, i.e., one more sequential dependency. This has practical implications for length generalization and for understanding why deeper models handle longer-range sequential patterns (e.g., k-gram phoneme recognition). Key limitations include the fixed-precision assumption (which differs subtly from standard real-valued softmax), the absence of positional encodings in experiments, and the restriction to formal language tasks on a binary alphabet. Open questions include whether the depth hierarchy persists with positional encodings at sufficiently long sequences, and whether real-world sequential dependencies in speech or code exhibit the same depth-scaling behavior.
