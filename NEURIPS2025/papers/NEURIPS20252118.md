## Problem

Widely used AI benchmarks contain a non-trivial fraction of invalid questions—ambiguous wording, incorrect answer keys, and automated grading errors—that distort model rankings and undermine evaluation reliability. Manually auditing thousands of questions across specialized domains is prohibitively expensive, and prior work has identified the problem without providing a principled, scalable framework for systematically flagging which questions warrant expert review.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252118/cropped_page5_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a binary M×N response matrix (M LLMs as 'test takers' answering N benchmark questions correctly or incorrectly), collected from the HELM leaderboard, along with the benchmark's question prompts and official answer keys. It produces per-question anomaly scores from three measurement-theoretic signals, which are ranked to prioritize expert review. A secondary LLM-judge pass takes the flagged question, its answer key, and several exemplar LLM responses, and outputs a structured verdict: a binary valid/invalid label, one of three predefined invalid categories (Ambiguous Question, Incorrect Answer Key, Grading Issue), and a justification of at most 15 words.

## Method

The core theoretical contribution shows that the common AI-evaluation assumption that the mean (sum) score is a sufficient statistic for model ability implies unidimensionality of the latent construct, which in turn implies the Rasch model is the data-generating model (Lemma 1, Theorem 1). Under the Rasch model, all inter-item tetrachoric correlations and item-total correlations must be non-negative (Corollaries 1–2), so any item whose empirically estimated statistics fall below zero is flagged as potentially invalid. Three complementary detection signals are computed: the average tetrachoric correlation with all other items, the Mokken item scalability coefficient, and the Pearson item-total correlation. These are combined via a Gaussian-rank transform ensemble to produce a unified anomaly score. To reduce human review burden, a frontier LLM is prompted as a first-pass judge that classifies each flagged question into an invalid category and provides a concise justification, allowing domain experts to verify reasoning rather than perform full inspection from scratch.

## Results

Across nine widely used AI benchmarks, the proposed measurement-theoretic framework achieves up to 84% Precision@50, meaning that among the top 50 questions flagged for expert review, up to 42 are confirmed as truly invalid. On GSM8K, the three proposed signals (tetrachoric correlation, item scalability coefficient, and item-total correlation) significantly outperform both heuristic baselines in sensitivity at shallow inspection depths, and no single method detects all 88 known invalid questions. An LLM-judge first pass (ChatGPT O1) achieves 98% precision in identifying invalid questions among the first 100 GSM8K items, with 93.3% of the 30 invalid questions being grading issues. The full pipeline runs in approximately one minute per benchmark of ~1,000 questions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its three measurement-theoretic signals (and their ensembles) against two heuristic baselines on GSM8K: (1) variance in predictions, the detection method used by Vendrow et al. [2025], and (2) Fleiss' Kappa. Both baselines are substantially outperformed in sensitivity at shallow inspection depths (Figure 1, left), with the proposed methods flagging invalid questions much earlier in the ranked list. However, no single proposed method captures all 88 annotated invalid questions, and ensemble strategies (OR Vote, AND Vote, Majority Vote, Gaussian Rank Mean) each flag different subsets, reflecting the No-Free-Lunch principle in anomaly detection. Notably, the authors identify 16 additional invalid GSM8K questions beyond those reported by Vendrow et al., suggesting the prior annotations represent a biased subset rather than ground truth. The LLM-judge component (not a baseline comparison per se) achieves 98% precision, far exceeding what the statistical signals alone provide for grading-issue detection, which requires inspecting model outputs rather than just the question stem.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts classical psychometric tools—Rasch model theory, tetrachoric correlation, Mokken scalability coefficients, and item-total correlation—to the novel domain of AI benchmark revision. Prior benchmark-quality work (Vendrow et al. 2025, Northcutt et al. 2021, Gema et al. 2025) relied on manual inspection, adversarial filtering, or ensemble disagreement to flag errors, but did not provide a principled statistical framework grounded in measurement theory. The key theoretical contribution is showing that the common practice of reporting mean scores as sufficient statistics for model ability implies the Rasch model, which in turn guarantees non-negative inter-item and item-total correlations, giving a concrete anomaly-detection criterion. An LLM-judge first pass is added as a practical layer to reduce human review effort, complementing the statistical flagging.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that benchmark errors can completely reverse model rankings (e.g., DeepSeek-R1 jumping from third-lowest to second-highest after revision), underscoring that current AI evaluation is built on a fragile foundation. The 84% precision in expert-confirmed flaw detection and the 98% precision of the LLM-judge on GSM8K suggest a scalable, semi-automated pipeline that could shift benchmark maintenance from one-off curation to continuous monitoring. Open questions remain: the method assumes near-unidimensionality (which may fail for multi-skill benchmarks), cultural and content validity issues resist purely statistical signals, and the limited diversity of available LLMs constrains statistical power. Extending the framework to polytomous/free-response formats and incorporating content-validity checks are natural next steps.
