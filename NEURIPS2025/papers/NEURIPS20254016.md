## Problem

Existing convergence guarantees for Repeated Risk Minimization (RRM) in performative prediction were neither proven tight nor accompanied by lower bounds, leaving open whether the stated rates were fundamental limits or artifacts of the analysis. Moreover, all prior methods relied exclusively on the most recent distribution snapshot, restricting the class of solvable problems. This paper establishes the first tight lower bounds for RRM under both the Perdomo et al. and Mofakhami et al. frameworks, and introduces a new algorithm class that provably breaks the RRM lower bound.

## Input / Output

At each retraining iteration t, the method consumes the current model parameters θ^t along with historical distribution snapshots {D(f_θ^i)}_{i=0}^{t-1}, which are combined via an affine mixture D_t = Σ α_i^{(t)} D(f_θ^i) with weights summing to one. The aggregation window τ (e.g., τ=1, 2, 4, t/2, or all) controls how many past snapshots enter the mixture, trading convergence speed against per-iteration memory and compute cost. The output is the updated parameter vector θ^{t+1} obtained by minimizing the expected loss under D_t, converging to a performatively stable classifier f_{θ_PS} where the induced distribution no longer shifts.

## Method

The core algorithmic contribution is the Affine Risk Minimizer (ARM) class, which replaces the single-snapshot RRM update with an optimization over an affine combination of past distribution snapshots; the 2-snapshot variant (averaging the last two distributions) yields the tightest theoretical guarantee, extending the convergence threshold from √εM/γ < 1 (RRM) to √εM/γ < 2/√3 ≈ 1.155. Theoretically, the paper derives a new upper bound for RRM under Pearson χ²-sensitivity that eliminates the norm-equivalency constant C, proves tightness of both the Perdomo and Mofakhami rates via explicit problem constructions, and establishes the first lower bounds for any ARM algorithm using a Nesterov-style dimension-introduction technique with a structured lower-triangular matrix. The analysis relies on strong convexity of the loss with respect to predictions (not parameters), bounded gradient norms, and a convex-compact function space, with the Schauder fixed-point theorem guaranteeing existence of the stable point. Empirically, on a credit-scoring environment with Resample-if-Rejected performativity, increasing the aggregation window from τ=1 to τ=2 nearly halves the loss shift due to performativity, with diminishing but persistent gains for larger windows.

## Results

The paper establishes that standard RRM converges at rate (√εM/γ)^t under Pearson χ²-sensitivity, and proves this bound is tight (Theorem 2). The proposed 2-snapshot Affine Risk Minimizer (ARM) improves the contraction factor to (√3/2 · √εM/γ)^t, extending the convergence regime from √εM/γ < 1 to √εM/γ < 2/√3 ≈ 1.155 (Theorem 4). In Perdomo et al.'s Wasserstein-based framework, the ARM lower bound is (εβ/(2γ))^t, showing RRM's rate (εβ/γ)^t is optimal up to a factor of 2 (Theorem 6). For the Resample-if-Rejected (RIR) mechanism, the paper proves ε-sensitivity of O(δ^{-3/2}), improving upon Mofakhami et al.'s O(δ^{-2}) (Theorem 8).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between standard RRM (using only the latest snapshot, τ=1) and the proposed ARM with 2-snapshot averaging (τ=2). Theoretically, ARM extends the convergence regime: RRM requires √εM/γ < 1, while ARM converges for √εM/γ < 2/√3 ≈ 1.155, meaning ARM handles problems ~15.5% further from the stability boundary. In Perdomo et al.'s framework, the ARM lower bound (εβ/(2γ))^t is a factor of 2 better than RRM's (εβ/γ)^t, and the paper proves this gap is essentially tight. Empirically on the credit-scoring benchmark (Give Me Some Credit dataset with RIR), increasing τ from 1 to 2 yields a near-half reduction in the loss shift ΔR_t, particularly in early iterations, with the improvement persisting beyond 50 iterations. Larger windows (τ=4, t/2, all) continue to reduce loss shift but with diminishing marginal gains, while linearly increasing memory and compute per iteration. Compared to Mofakhami et al. [2023], the new ε-sensitivity bound for RIR improves from O(δ^{-2}) to O(δ^{-3/2}), guaranteeing convergence for a strictly wider class of problems under the same framework.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the performative prediction framework initiated by Perdomo et al. (ICML 2020) and Mofakhami et al. (2023), which established convergence rates for Repeated Risk Minimization (RRM) under Wasserstein-based and Pearson χ²-based sensitivity assumptions, respectively. The key departure is the introduction of Affine Risk Minimizers (ARM), a new algorithm class that aggregates historical distribution snapshots via convex combinations, enabling convergence in a strictly wider problem class than standard RRM. Additionally, the paper provides the first tightness analysis for both prior frameworks, confirming that their convergence rates are fundamental limits under the stated assumptions, and introduces the first lower bound techniques for iterative retraining schemes using Nesterov-style dimensional construction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The tightness results are significant for the performative prediction community: they establish that the existing convergence rates cannot be improved without stronger assumptions, closing a gap that had persisted since the original RRM analysis. The ARM framework, while providing only a modest threshold relaxation (from 1 to ≈1.155 in the χ² framework), demonstrates that historical data exploitation is a viable direction for breaking RRM's fundamental limits. However, the ARM lower bound (Theorem 5/6) shows the improvement is bounded—no affine combination of snapshots can do substantially better—suggesting that more radical departures from the RRM paradigm may be needed for meaningful gains. Open questions include whether non-affine aggregation schemes or adaptive weighting can further extend the convergence region, and whether the theoretical improvements translate to significant practical speedups in high-dimensional or non-convex settings.
