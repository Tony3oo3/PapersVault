## Problem

Handheld grippers used for in-the-wild human demonstration collection lack tactile sensing, despite touch being critical for fine-grained, contact-rich manipulation. Moreover, no large-scale, synchronized visuo-tactile dataset collected in unstructured real-world environments has been available, and existing multimodal learning approaches (e.g., contrastive alignment) tend to suppress the fine-grained geometric signals that tactile sensors provide.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255143/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The system consumes a 224×224 RGB frame from a wrist-mounted GoPro fisheye camera, a 1×24×32 single-channel tactile force map (two 12×32 piezoresistive fingertip arrays stacked vertically, converted to 3-channel via a fixed colormap and split into a 6×8 grid of 4×4 patches), and a proprioceptive vector (end-effector pose, gripper width). The pretrained encoder outputs a 1536-dim fused visuo-tactile embedding; the downstream diffusion policy (16-step DDIM, action horizon 16) outputs a sequence of end-effector actions. Tactile data streams at 23 Hz and video at 60 Hz, synchronized via a QR-code-based hardware-free alignment protocol.

## Method

The paper proposes a two-stage pipeline. In Stage 1, a visuo-tactile encoder is pretrained via masked autoencoding: 60–80% of tactile patches are randomly masked, and a decoder reconstructs the full tactile image conditioned on the partially observed tactile input (processed by a 3-layer CNN to a 768-dim embedding) and the RGB image (processed by a finetuned CLIP ViT-B/16 to a 768-dim [CLS] embedding). The two modality embeddings are fused through two rounds of multi-head cross-attention (tactile attends to vision, then vision attends back to updated tactile), producing a 1536-dim joint representation that is decoded to the tactile image via a two-layer MLP with sigmoid. In Stage 2, the pretrained encoder is frozen-then-finetuned (lr 3×10⁻⁵) and its output is concatenated with proprioception to condition a convolutional U-Net diffusion policy trained by behavior cloning on human demonstrations. The key design choices—masked reconstruction over contrastive learning, and cross-attention over naive concatenation—preserve tactile-specific geometry while enabling the policy to coordinate visual context with local force feedback, yielding measurable gains in sample efficiency and robustness on four fine-grained real-world tasks.

## Results

On four fine-grained real-world manipulation tasks, the full visuo-tactile policy with pretraining achieves the highest whole-task success rates: 85% on Test Tube Collection, 85% on Pencil Insertion, 90% on Fluid Transfer, and 70% on Whiteboard Erasing (20 trials each). The vision-only baseline (Chi et al.) achieves substantially lower rates of 25%, 45%, 55%, and 55% respectively. Ablations show that cross-attention fusion and large-scale visuo-tactile pretraining each contribute meaningfully: removing cross-attention drops Test Tube Collection from 85% to 50%, and removing pretraining drops it from 85% to 70%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary external baseline is the Vision-Only policy from Chi et al. (UMI, 2024), which uses a CLIP embedding of a single RGB image plus proprioception to condition a diffusion policy. The proposed visuo-tactile method with pretraining outperforms this baseline on every task: Test Tube Collection improves from 25% to 85% whole-task success (a 60-point gain), Pencil Insertion from 45% to 85%, Fluid Transfer from 55% to 90%, and Whiteboard Erasing from 55% to 70%. The largest relative gains appear in tasks requiring in-hand state estimation (Test Tube Collection, Pencil Insertion), where vision suffers from occlusion of the object within the gripper. Two internal ablations further isolate contributions: removing cross-attention (simple concatenation of tactile and visual features) degrades Test Tube Collection to 50% and Whiteboard Erasing to 45% (worse than vision-only), while removing pretraining (randomly initialized encoder) yields 70% and 60% respectively, confirming that both the cross-modal fusion architecture and large-scale visuo-tactile pretraining are necessary for the full performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the UMI (Universal Manipulation Interface) line of work by integrating flexible piezoresistive tactile sensors into the portable handheld gripper, building on the sensor architecture from 3D-ViTac while adapting it for scalable, tool-free fabrication. For representation learning, it departs from the contrastive-learning approach common in prior visuo-tactile alignment work (e.g., ViTaMIn, Sparsh) in favor of a masked autoencoding objective with cross-attention, arguing that reconstruction better preserves fine-grained tactile geometry. The downstream policy stage directly adopts Diffusion Policy with a convolutional U-Net backbone, and the vision encoder is initialized from CLIP ViT-B/16, making the overall pipeline a composition of well-established components applied to a new portable, in-the-wild data-collection setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The primary practical contribution is the release of a 2.6-million-pair, 43-task, 12-environment in-the-wild visuo-tactile dataset, which fills a clear gap since prior visuo-tactile datasets were confined to structured lab settings. The paper demonstrates that tactile feedback is not merely complementary but essential for force-sensitive and occlusion-prone manipulation (fluid transfer, test tube insertion), and that cross-modal pretraining yields the largest gains in low-data and low-epoch regimes—practically relevant for real-world deployment where demonstrations are expensive. Limitations include the use of a parallel-jaw gripper (limiting dexterity), evaluation on only four tasks with a single XArm 850 robot, and the absence of error bars or statistical significance testing. Open questions include whether the approach scales to multi-finger dexterous hands and whether the masked-reconstruction objective generalizes to higher-resolution or vision-based tactile sensors.
