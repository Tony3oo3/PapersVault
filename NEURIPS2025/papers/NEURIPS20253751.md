## Problem

Standard LLM-as-a-judge validation aggregates multiple forced-choice human ratings into a single gold label per item, but many rating tasks (toxicity, helpfulness, relevance) admit multiple valid interpretations—a condition the authors call rating indeterminacy. Forced-choice elicitation discards this intra-rater disagreement, and the paper shows this can cause standard validation to select judge systems that perform up to 31% worse on downstream tasks than the true best system.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on discrete-option rating tasks (categorical or ordinal scales, not continuous). For each item, it consumes both forced-choice ratings (single option selected) and multi-label response set ratings (all plausible options selected) from humans and judge LLMs, along with a sensitivity parameter β that characterizes how each rater population resolves indeterminacy. Outputs include per-item multi-label probability vectors, corpus-level human-judge agreement scores under multiple metrics, and downstream task performance measured as decision consistency (for content filtering) or estimation bias (for prevalence estimation).

## Method

The paper introduces a probabilistic rating model with a forced-choice translation matrix F that maps a rater's response set distribution to their forced-choice distribution, capturing how individual raters resolve indeterminacy when constrained to pick one option. Two key theorems establish that (1) the response set distribution is identifiable from forced-choice data if and only if the rating task is fully specified (i.e., no multi-option response sets exist), and (2) forced-choice and response-set-based agreement metrics are not rank-consistent unless a strong monotonicity condition holds—meaning they can produce contradictory judge system rankings. The proposed alternative is a continuous multi-label MSE metric computed against response set distributions, with a practical variant (MSE F̂) that estimates F from a small auxiliary sub-corpus of paired forced-choice and response set ratings when full response set data is unavailable. Experiments across 11 real-world rating tasks and 9 commercial LLMs confirm that standard forced-choice metrics (Hit Rate, Cohen's κ, KL-Divergence) systematically mis-rank judges, while the multi-label MSE approach selects substantially more performant systems.

## Results

Across 11 rating tasks and 9 commercial LLMs, the paper shows that standard forced-choice validation selects judge systems performing up to 31% worse on downstream tasks than those selected via the proposed multi-label response set approach. Specifically, GPT o3-Mini (the true best judge on toxicity) is incorrectly ranked 4th under forced-choice elicitation, while Claude Sonnet 3.5 (ranked 1st under forced-choice) has 31% worse decision consistency with human raters. On SummEval (Relevance), sub-optimal judge selection via forced-choice metrics increases estimation bias by 28% (equivalent to mis-estimating the rate of relevant outputs by an additional 0.28 on a [0,1] scale). The proposed MSE F metric (using oracle forced-choice translation matrix) outperforms all forced-choice alternatives when rating indeterminacy is present (β^H ≥ 0.2), and the fully-estimated MSE F̂ variant (using only 100 paired ratings) performs nearly as well.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares five families of human-judge agreement metrics as 'baselines' for judge system selection: categorical forced-choice (Hit Rate, Cohen's κ, Krippendorff's α, Fleiss κ), distributional forced-choice (KL-Divergence, JS-Divergence, Cross-Entropy), discrete multi-label (Coverage), and continuous multi-label (MSE). Among forced-choice metrics, KL-D(h,j) performs best, but all forced-choice metrics become increasingly unreliable as human rating indeterminacy (β^H) grows beyond ~0.2–0.3. The proposed continuous multi-label MSE F metric selects significantly more performant judge systems than any forced-choice alternative once β^H ≥ 0.2, with the gap widening substantially at higher indeterminacy levels. The fully-estimated MSE F̂ (requiring only a small 100-item auxiliary sub-corpus with paired forced-choice and response set ratings) achieves performance nearly matching the oracle MSE F. Categorical metrics like Hit Rate are suboptimal even at low indeterminacy (β^H = 0) because judge systems differ in how they resolve indeterminacy (β^J ≠ 0), causing rank inversions between forced-choice agreement and downstream task performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-as-a-judge meta-evaluation literature by introducing a formal probabilistic framework that connects forced-choice and multi-label 'response set' rating elicitation through a translation matrix. It departs from prior soft-label aggregation approaches (Elangovan et al. 2024, Uma et al. 2020) that model only inter-rater disagreement, by explicitly capturing intra-rater disagreement arising from rating indeterminacy. The forced-choice selection effects modeled here draw on perceptual psychology literature (Dhar & Simonson 2003, Bogacz et al. 2006) that had not previously been applied to LLM judge validation, and the identifiability and monotonicity theorems provide a theoretical foundation absent from prior empirical studies of judge system reliability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that standard forced-choice validation can select judge systems performing up to 31% worse than the true optimum is a significant practical concern for the rapidly growing field of LLM-based evaluation, with direct implications for content moderation, red-teaming, and benchmarking pipelines. The identifiability theorem establishes a fundamental information-loss result: without fully specifying rating tasks, the response set distribution is unrecoverable from forced-choice data, meaning current validation protocols are structurally limited. Open questions include extending the framework to continuous-scale rating tasks, relaxing the homogeneity assumption on human raters, and understanding how response set elicitation cognitive load affects rating quality at scale.
