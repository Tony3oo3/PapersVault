## Problem

Existing shadow detection methods treat shadows as binary masks or assume uniform opacity within shadow regions, oversimplifying the light-object interactions that produce varying degrees of visibility under both hard and soft shadows. This binary paradigm fails to capture nuanced opacity variations (e.g., penumbra gradients, umbra transparency), leading to imprecise boundary estimation and degraded performance in downstream tasks like shadow removal, editing, and 3D reconstruction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253726/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB shadow image (resized to 400×400 during training) and produces a per-pixel continuous opacity mask M_α with values in [0, 1], where 0 indicates fully transparent (no shadow) and 1 indicates fully opaque shadow. The output is governed by the compositing model I_s = I_f·(1−M_α) + M_α·S, where S is the shadow color. Unlike prior binary outputs, the continuous mask encodes pixel-wise attenuation, enabling downstream models to modulate processing strength proportionally to local shadow depth.

## Method

The framework comprises three modules operating on image pairs created by a Shadow Opacity Augmentation (SOA) step that randomly rescales shadow opacity via a parameter β (training only). A weight-shared EfficientNet-B3 backbone in the Shadow Feature Separation (SFS) module extracts position features F_pos and opacity features F_opa from both the original and augmented images; position features are constrained to be identical across the pair via an MAE loss, while the opacity feature difference is supervised to recover β through an MLP. The Opacity Mask Prediction (OMP) module fuses F_pos and F_opa via a cumulative learning schedule where the blending weight μ decays from 1 to 0 over training (controlled by hyperparameter η=0.5), progressively shifting reliance from position to opacity features before a detection head outputs the final opacity map. Training combines five losses: position consistency, opacity parameter recovery, a region-aware area loss that separately weights shadow and non-shadow pixels, a gradient smoothness loss, and a global MSE reconstruction loss.

## Results

On the ISTD dataset, the proposed method achieves a BER of 1.32%, shadow error of 0.96%, and non-shadow error of 1.67%, outperforming the closest competitor SDCM (BER 1.40%) and FDRNet (BER 1.55%). On the newly introduced FSD dataset with diverse opacity variations, the method achieves BER 1.79%, shadow error 1.94%, and non-shadow error 1.64%, substantially outperforming the best baseline FDRNet (BER 3.01%). For downstream shadow removal, replacing binary masks with the predicted opacity masks in ShadowDiffusion yields PSNR gains of up to 4.3 dB on shadow regions (42.06 vs. 37.78 on SRD).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all 18 baselines across both shadow detection and shadow removal tasks. In shadow detection (Table 2), the closest competitors are SDCM (BER 1.40) and FDRNet (BER 1.55) on ISTD, with the proposed method achieving 1.32 BER; on FSD, FDRNet (BER 3.01) is the strongest baseline, and the proposed method improves by 1.22 BER points. Earlier methods like stacked-CNN (BER 8.60), scGAN (4.70), and ST-CGAN (3.85) are significantly outperformed. In shadow removal (Table 6), the opacity-mask-guided ShadowDiffusion achieves the best shadow-region PSNR on both ISTD+ (39.49) and SRD (42.06), surpassing HomoFormer (38.81/38.81), ShadowDiffusion with DHAN masks (40.12/38.72), and DeS3 (36.49/37.91). The method also outperforms inpainting-based (Inpaint4Shadow, PSNR 33.27) and SAM-based (SAM-helps-shadow, PSNR 30.72) approaches on SRD. Notably, the performance gap widens on FSD compared to ISTD, indicating the opacity-aware formulation is particularly effective for soft shadows and varied opacity levels.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant binary-mask paradigm in shadow detection (DSC, BDRAR, FDRNet, SDCM, etc.) by reformulating the task as continuous opacity prediction, drawing a conceptual line from shadow matte estimation (Chuang et al., Wu et al.) while avoiding its entanglement with color and texture. The SOA augmentation strategy and the position/opacity feature separation (SFS) module are novel architectural choices not present in prior shadow detection or removal pipelines, and the cumulative learning fusion strategy is borrowed from long-tailed recognition (Zhou et al., 2020) and repurposed here for progressive feature integration. The FSD dataset is the first to provide opacity-annotated shadow masks, filling a gap identified in Table 1 where no existing dataset (SRD, ISTD, SOBA, DESOBA, RdSOBA) captures this information.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The opacity mask provides a physically meaningful intermediate representation that directly benefits downstream tasks: the ~5 dB PSNR gain in shadow regions for ShadowDiffusion on SRD demonstrates practical utility, and the 3D reconstruction improvement (RMSE 0.00957 vs 0.01074) validates that opacity gradients encode geometric cues. However, the FSD dataset is entirely synthetic (Blender Cycles), raising questions about generalization to real-world shadows with complex material interactions, and the authors acknowledge failure on multi-shadow overlap scenarios. Open questions include whether the opacity formulation transfers to real-image datasets without synthetic ground truth, and whether instance-level shadow association can resolve the complex-interaction limitation.
