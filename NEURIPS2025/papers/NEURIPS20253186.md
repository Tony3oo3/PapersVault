## Problem

Prior theoretical results established that fixed-depth transformers are confined to TC^0 and cannot express regular language recognition or graph connectivity, but treated depth as a constant, leaving unclear how much bounded depth suffices for practical input lengths and whether minimally growing depth (log n) unlocks new expressivity. This paper addresses both questions: it characterizes the exact context-length bounds for fixed-depth transformers and proves that log-depth universal transformers can solve these problems for arbitrary input lengths, while also comparing depth scaling to width scaling and chain-of-thought steps.

## Input / Output

For regular language recognition, the input is a token sequence w of length n over alphabet Σ (with a special $ terminator), and the output is an accept/reject decision at the final token. For graph connectivity, the input is an n×n adjacency matrix encoded as n² binary tokens, followed by n³ padding tokens and source/target vertices s,t in unary, with the output being a boolean indicating path existence. The universal transformer architecture uses averaging-hard attention (saturated attention), masked pre-norm with learned mask vectors, no positional encodings (relying on a BoS token), and a p-precise scalar datatype with p = c·log n bits to store position pointers and intermediate values in the residual stream.

## Method

The core technical contribution is Lemma 1, showing that a fully uniform 7-layer block can compute integer division and remainder (a_i = b_i·m + c_i) for small integers using only masked pre-norm and causal attention, eliminating the need for non-uniform parameters or special positional encodings. Theorem 1 builds on this to construct a (0,8,9)-universal transformer that recognizes regular languages by implementing a binary-tree prefix-sum over the automaton's transition monoid, where each of the 8 repeated layers performs one level of the tree (retrieving the previous active token's group element, multiplying, updating activeness via c_i mod 2). Theorem 2 constructs a (17,2,1)-universal transformer for graph connectivity by iteratively doubling path-length predicates B_ℓ(i,j) and C_ℓ(i,k,j) across the n² and n³ token regions, with the 2 repeated layers alternating between computing C_ℓ (conjunction of two B_{ℓ-1} values) and B_ℓ (existential quantification over k). Empirically, the paper validates the depth requirement on the A5 state tracking task, finding a strong linear fit (r²=0.93) between depth and log n*, with a slope of ~4.8 layers per log token, closely matching the theoretical coefficient of 4–8.

## Results

The paper proves that Θ(log n)-depth universal transformers can recognize regular languages (Theorem 1) and solve graph connectivity (Theorem 2), two problems inexpressible by fixed-depth transformers under TC⁰ ≠ NC¹. Empirically, on the A₅ state tracking task, the effective context length n* scales logarithmically with depth (d = 4.8 log₂ n − 15.8, r² = 0.93) and exponentially with width (n = 7.2 log₂ w − 41.7, r² = 0.98), closely matching theoretical predictions. Concretely, a depth of 32 layers suffices for strings up to length 10⁷ and graphs with 128 vertices, while depth 80 extends these to 440K strings and 2.1B vertices.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baseline methods but instead compares three internal scaling strategies—depth, width, and chain-of-thought (CoT) steps—for expressing problems beyond TC⁰. Depth scaling is shown to be strictly more efficient: Θ(log n) depth suffices for both regular language recognition and graph connectivity, whereas width must grow superpolynomially (Theorem 3) and CoT steps must grow superlogarithmically (Theorem 4) to achieve the same expressivity. Empirically, the A₅ state tracking experiments confirm this hierarchy: increasing depth from 6 to 24 layers (at fixed width 512) extends the effective context length from ~16 to ~128 tokens with a strong linear fit in log-space (r²=0.93), while increasing width from 128 to 1024 (at fixed depth 6) yields an even stronger but far less efficient exponential relationship (r²=0.98). The empirical depth coefficient of 4.8 layers per log-token sits between the theoretical non-uniform bound of 4 and the universal-transformer bound of 8, indicating learned constructions approach theoretical efficiency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the fixed-depth expressivity analysis of Merrill and Sabharwal (2023a), who showed transformers with constant depth are confined to TC^0 and cannot recognize regular languages or solve graph connectivity, by asking what happens when depth grows minimally as Θ(log n). It builds directly on Liu et al. (2023), who showed a variant of log-depth transformers can recognize regular languages, but departs from that work by proving the result holds for fully uniform (looped) transformers with standard architectural components (residual connections, layer-norm, no special positional encodings), rather than the non-uniform, simplified model they used. The core enabling technique—Lemma 1, showing uniform transformers can compute integer division and remainder—replaces the non-uniform positional encodings and hardcoded layer indices that prior constructions relied on, and the paper also strengthens the CoT upper bound from Merrill and Sabharwal (2024) to show O(log n) CoT steps remain in TC^0.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first quantitative characterization of how transformer depth must scale with input length to express sequential reasoning problems, showing Θ(log n) depth suffices while width must grow superpolynomially and CoT steps must grow superlogarithmically—making depth the most efficient scaling axis for these tasks. The empirical validation (r²=0.93 for depth, r²=0.98 for width on the A₅ state-tracking task) bridges the expressivity-learnability gap and gives practitioners concrete guidance: a 32-layer model handles strings up to ~10⁷ characters, while 80 layers makes the limit practically unbounded. Key open questions remain: whether log-depth transformers can handle P-complete problems (they cannot, unless NC=P), what depth is needed for context-free language recognition (in AC¹), and whether the inductive biases of looped transformers affect the practical depth requirements beyond what expressivity theory predicts.
