## Problem

Certified machine unlearning for nonconvex loss functions has previously required either second-order methods (expensive Hessian computations), white-box training modifications (injecting noise at every step or storing per-sample statistics), or restrictive assumptions. No first-order, black-box algorithm existed that could be applied to a model trained with vanilla gradient descent while providing (ε,δ)-certified unlearning guarantees on general nonconvex objectives.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained model trained with standard (noiseless) gradient descent, the original training dataset, a model checkpoint from K steps before the end of training (either saved during training or reconstructed post hoc via the proximal point method), and an unlearning request specifying a data subset Z to remove. It outputs perturbed model weights (final unlearning iterate plus Gaussian noise) that are (ε,δ)-indistinguishable from a model retrained from scratch on the retained data. The algorithm requires only O(d) storage and gradient evaluations, with no modifications to the original training procedure.

## Method

R2D unlearns by 'rewinding' to an earlier checkpoint θ_{T-K} on the original training trajectory, then performing K gradient descent steps on the loss of the retained dataset before adding Gaussian noise. The key insight is that rewinding reverses the divergence between the full-dataset and retained-dataset training trajectories, bringing the unlearning iterate closer to the retraining iterate and thus reducing the required noise level σ. When no checkpoint was saved, Algorithm 3 reconstructs it post hoc by solving the implicit backward-Euler equation via the proximal point method (exploiting the connection between backward Euler for gradient flow and proximal operators). The noise level σ = 2mG·h(K)·√(2log(1.25/δ))/(Lnε) explicitly encodes the privacy-utility-complexity tradeoff: larger K reduces σ at the cost of more computation, and σ scales inversely with dataset size n. For PL functions, the analysis yields linear convergence of the empirical risk and a generalization bound on the population risk.

## Results

On the Lacuna-100 dataset, R2D at 74% rewind achieves a −0.0995 drop in AUC on the unlearned set (D_unlearn), far exceeding Hessian-Free (−0.0191) and Constrained Newton Step (−0.0007), while retaining competitive test AUC (0.9534). On eICU, R2D achieves the best membership inference attack defense with AUC of 0.5044 (Classic MIA) and 0.4997 (MIA-U), below all certified and non-certified baselines. R2D is up to 88× faster than Hessian-Free during training and requires only O(d) storage versus O(nd) for HF. On Lacuna-100, R2D at 36–37% rewind achieves MIA-U AUC of 0.6779, substantially outperforming HF (0.8460) and CNS (0.8480).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Hessian-Free Unlearning (HF) [37], R2D dominates on every axis: it is black-box (no per-step storage of influence vectors), up to 88× faster in training, and achieves lower MIA AUC on both datasets (e.g., 0.4997 vs. 0.5108 on eICU MIA-U; 0.6779 vs. 0.8460 on Lacuna-100 MIA-U at 36–37% rewind). Against Constrained Newton Step (CNS) [48], R2D wins on MIA defense for eICU (0.5044 vs. 0.5145 Classic MIA) and on the D_unlearn drop for Lacuna-100 (−0.0995 vs. −0.0007), though CNS retains slightly higher D_test AUC on eICU (0.7601 vs. 0.7326) due to its different training procedure. Against Fisher Forgetting [20], R2D achieves substantially better MIA defense on both datasets (e.g., 0.5046 vs. 0.5114 Classic MIA on eICU; 0.6779 vs. 0.8735 MIA-U on Lacuna-100) and a much larger D_unlearn drop on Lacuna-100. Against SCRUB [28], R2D similarly outperforms on MIA defense (0.4997 vs. 0.5056 MIA-U on eICU; 0.6779 vs. 0.8586 MIA-U on Lacuna-100) and on the unlearned-set performance drop, while SCRUB shows essentially no unlearning effect (D_unlearn change of +0.0006 on Lacuna-100). Overall, R2D is the only method that simultaneously provides certified (ε,δ)-unlearning guarantees, black-box applicability, first-order computation, and the strongest empirical unlearning across both datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

R2D is conceptually inspired by Descent-to-Delete (D2D) [34], a first-order certified unlearning algorithm for strongly convex functions that fine-tunes from the final trained model. The key departure is replacing 'descending' from the endpoint with 'rewinding' to an earlier checkpoint, which the authors argue is necessary because nonconvex loss functions lack the unique global minimum that D2D's analysis relies on. The certified unlearning guarantee leverages the Gaussian mechanism from differential privacy [14], while the post-hoc checkpoint computation borrows the proximal point method [33] to invert gradient descent steps. Among prior nonconvex unlearning methods, R2D is distinguished from Constrained Newton Step [48] and Hessian-Free Unlearning [37] by being first-order and black-box, and from Langevin Unlearning [12] by not requiring noise injection at every training step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first practical (first-order, black-box) certified unlearning guarantee for general nonconvex functions, removing the need for Hessian access or modified training procedures that hinder deployment. The explicit privacy-utility-efficiency tradeoff (via the K parameter) and the PL-inequality-based generalization bounds give practitioners a principled way to calibrate unlearning. The user-based experimental framework (removing data associated with specific users rather than entire classes) is a more realistic evaluation setting that prior works have not adopted. Open questions include whether the vanilla-gradient-descent requirement limits applicability to modern training pipelines (Adam, mixed precision, etc.), the instability of the proximal-point checkpoint reconstruction for large K, and whether the MIA-based evaluation is sufficient for the non-i.i.d. unlearning setting the authors identify.
