## Problem

Existing automatic curriculum learning methods rely on static task hierarchies or unidirectional heuristics that cannot dynamically adapt to a learner's evolving capabilities, and traditional self-play frameworks require agent symmetry that is incompatible with the inherently asymmetric teacher-student relationship. The paper asks whether formalizing curriculum design as a zero-sum adversarial game between a task-generating instructor and a problem-solving learner can autonomously discover effective pedagogical sequences without handcrafted difficulty orderings.

## Input / Output

The teacher network consumes the student's behavioral history (recent trajectories, success rates, or returns) and outputs a categorical distribution over a discrete task set T = {T₁,...,Tₙ} via softmax over learnable logits, with tasks structured by a directed acyclic graph encoding prerequisite dependencies. The student policy receives the current environment state (grid observations in Minigrid, RGB frames in CRAFT/Crafter) plus the sampled task specification and produces an action sequence over a fixed horizon H. The joint output is an adaptive curriculum: a time-varying task sampling distribution that shifts toward failed tasks (positive feedback) and away from mastered ones (negative feedback).

## Method

HAP formulates curriculum learning as a minimax optimization min_φ max_θ J(θ,φ), where the student maximizes expected discounted return over teacher-sampled tasks and the teacher minimizes that same return by strategically selecting challenging tasks. The teacher's adversarial gradient is derived via the policy gradient theorem: ∇_φ J_teacher = −E[∇_φ log p_φ(T) · R(τ;T)], which increases selection probability for tasks where the student performs poorly and decreases it for mastered tasks. Training alternates between teacher task sampling, student policy rollouts, and gradient updates for both agents, with three stabilization mechanisms: a cold-start warm-up phase where the student explores independently, entropy regularization on the teacher's distribution to prevent task overload, and probabilistic lower bounds on task selection to mitigate catastrophic forgetting. The framework is modality-agnostic and supports any differentiable student policy (PPO, SAC, etc.), and extends naturally to supervised learning where it operates as a data-level curriculum selecting training samples adversarially.

## Results

HAP achieves the best general (weighted) score across all three RL environments: 0.527 on Minigrid, 0.562 on CRAFT, and 0.723 on Crafter, outperforming the strongest baseline DreamerV3 (0.493, 0.516, 0.697 respectively). The advantage is most pronounced on harder tasks, where HAP scores 0.46 vs. 0.34 (DreamerV3) on Minigrid Middle and 0.31 vs. 0.27 on CRAFT Hard. In supervised curriculum learning (CIFAR-100, RTE), HAP matches or exceeds SOTA baselines under challenging conditions (class imbalance, label noise) with faster convergence. Human studies confirm HAP-generated curricula produce learning trajectories comparable to expert-designed tutorials.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HAP outperforms all standard RL baselines (DQN, A2C, PPO, SAC, TD3) on general scores across all three environments, with the largest margins on harder tasks where several baselines (DQN, A2C, TD3) score near zero. Against the SOTA world-model baseline DreamerV3, HAP wins on general scores in all three environments (0.527 vs. 0.493 Minigrid; 0.562 vs. 0.516 CRAFT; 0.723 vs. 0.697 Crafter), with the gap widening on Middle and Hard difficulty levels. Among curriculum-learning baselines, HAP surpasses both TSCL (Matiisen et al., 2019) and EXP3 (Gajane et al., 2015) on every environment, and also outperforms the manually designed Ordered curriculum by a wide margin (e.g., 0.562 vs. 0.26 general on CRAFT). The one exception is Crafter Easy, where PPO (0.94) slightly edges out HAP (0.91), though HAP recovers on Middle and Hard. In supervised learning (CIFAR-100, RTE), HAP matches ScreenerNet on CIFAR-100-Imbalanced-50 and TTCL on RTE-Noised-0.4 while converging faster, and consistently outperforms data-selection (TTCL, SPL) and loss-reweighting (LRE, MW-Net) baselines under noisy or imbalanced conditions.

## Relation to Prior Work

HAP extends the asymmetric self-play paradigm (Sukhbaatar et al., 2018) by making the role asymmetry fundamental rather than incidental: the teacher's objective is explicitly adversarial (minimizing student success) rather than cooperative (selecting Goldilocks-zone tasks as in TSCL, Matiisen et al. 2019). The minimax formulation borrows structural inspiration from GANs (Goodfellow et al., 2014) but applies it to task distribution selection over a discrete task pool rather than continuous data generation. Unlike prior ACL methods (EXP3, value-disagreement curricula) that use unidirectional heuristics for task difficulty, HAP establishes a closed-loop adversarial feedback where the teacher's policy gradient is directly driven by student performance, creating a co-evolutionary dynamic absent in static or heuristic curricula.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The adversarial framing offers a principled alternative to handcrafted curricula, and the human study provides rare validation that machine-generated curricula can match expert-designed instruction for human learners. However, the framework requires a predefined task pool with known dependencies, limiting applicability to truly open-ended discovery; performance gains over DreamerV3 are modest in the most complex (Crafter) environment, and the gap to human performance on hard tasks remains substantial (47–65%). Open questions include whether the adversarial equilibrium generalizes to continuous task spaces, whether the zero-sum framing is necessary versus a softer adversarial objective, and how the approach scales when task dependencies are not explicitly encoded in a DAG.
