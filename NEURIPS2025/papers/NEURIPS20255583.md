## Problem

The industry-standard Last-Click Mechanism (LCM) for ad attribution is not dominant strategy incentive compatible: platforms can strategically delay their click reports to appear last and capture full conversion credit, especially in the modern redirect-less tracking paradigm where no independent verifier exists. Prior attribution research assumes platforms report truthfully and focuses on statistical modeling of contributions, leaving the strategic manipulation problem unaddressed. This paper asks how to design an attribution mechanism that is both DSIC (truthful reporting is a dominant strategy) and accurate in assigning credit to the true last-click platform.

## Input / Output

The mechanism receives, for each of n platforms, a reported click time r_i on a conversion-aligned timeline (conversion at t=0, so valid reports satisfy r_i ≤ 0), along with the known CDFs F_i of each platform's true click time distribution. It outputs a credit vector x_i(r) ∈ [0,1] per platform with expected total credit bounded by 1. A key structural constraint is that any report with r_i > 0 (post-conversion) is automatically ineligible for credit, and the allocation rule must be non-increasing in each platform's own report to satisfy DSIC.

## Method

The paper proposes the Peer-Validated Mechanism (PVM), whose core insight is that a platform's credit depends solely on the reports of all other platforms, making DSIC automatic since a platform's own report cannot affect its outcome. Concretely, platform i receives credit if and only if the maximum reported time among all peers is below a threshold α_i, where α_i is calibrated so that the probability of all peers' true click times falling below α_i equals the prior probability β_i that platform i is the true last-click platform. The paper proves PVM is the optimal DSIC mechanism in the homogeneous setting (Theorem 5), derives tight accuracy bounds for both homogeneous (e.g., 0.75 for n=2) and heterogeneous (e.g., 19/27 ≈ 0.7037 for n=2) cases, and shows PVM is fair in the sense that expected attribution exactly matches each platform's true last-click probability. In contrast, LCM's worst-case accuracy can approach 0 under heterogeneity and it fails both DSIC and fairness.

## Results

In the homogeneous setting, PVM achieves accuracy of 0.75 (n=2), 0.6151 (n=3), 0.5275 (n=4), and 0.4650 (n=5), compared to LCM's worst-case accuracy of (2−√2)²≈0.3431 (n=2) and an upper bound of 0.1605 (n=5), yielding a 1.8×–2.9× accuracy advantage. In the heterogeneous two-platform case, PVM guarantees accuracy of 19/27≈0.7037, while LCM's accuracy can approach 0. PVM is proven to be the optimal DSIC mechanism in the homogeneous setting and is the only mechanism that is simultaneously DSIC and Fair; LCM satisfies neither property.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the industry-standard Last-Click Mechanism (LCM), which allocates full credit to the platform with the latest reported timestamp. PVM dominates LCM on every metric examined: in homogeneous settings, PVM's accuracy exceeds LCM's worst-case upper bound by a factor of 1.8× to 2.9× (e.g., 0.75 vs. 0.3431 for n=2; 0.4650 vs. 0.1605 for n=5). In heterogeneous settings the gap is even more dramatic—LCM's accuracy and fairness can both approach 0, while PVM retains a guaranteed accuracy of 19/27≈0.7037 (n=2) and a lower bound of (19/27)^⌈log₂n⌉ for general n. On the incentive-compatibility axis, PVM is DSIC by construction (a platform's credit depends only on peers' reports), whereas LCM is provably not DSIC, giving platforms a strict incentive to delay reports. Numerical experiments using distributions fitted from real ad-conversion logs confirm these theoretical gaps: PVM improves accuracy by 0.04–0.30 and fairness by up to 0.13 over LCM across all tested configurations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by importing mechanism design and game-theoretic tools (DSIC, Nash equilibrium analysis) into the advertising attribution problem, a domain where prior work—spanning survival analysis, Shapley-value allocation, Markov chain models, causal inference, and deep learning—has uniformly assumed platforms report click data truthfully. Rather than extending any specific prior attribution method, the authors reframe attribution as a strategic game in which platforms choose reporting delays to maximize credit, then design and analyze mechanisms under this adversarial model. The conceptual lineage draws from incentive-compatible mechanism design in auctions and voting, but the application to temporal credit allocation with unobservable true click times is, per the authors, entirely novel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that the industry-default Last-Click Mechanism is not DSIC and can achieve arbitrarily low accuracy (approaching 0) in heterogeneous settings is a fundamental challenge to a practice underpinning a $790B+ market. PVM's provable optimality in the homogeneous case and its strong fairness guarantee (expected credit exactly matches true last-click probability) provide a principled design template—'exclude your own report'—that could be embedded into ML-based attribution models. Open questions include extending to correlated click-time distributions, improving instance-level (rather than expected) accuracy, joint advertiser-platform optimization, and repeated-game dynamics where platforms may strategically harm peers or manipulate learned priors.
