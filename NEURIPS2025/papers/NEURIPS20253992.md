## Problem

State-of-the-art LTSF models produce inconsistent predictions for the same future timestamps when given slightly lagged input sequences, a phenomenon the paper terms the output alignment problem. This inconsistency undermines real-world reliability (e.g., conflicting electricity budget plans from overlapping forecasts) yet has not been explicitly identified or addressed by prior LTSF research. The paper also notes that existing regression metrics (MSE, MAE) only measure prediction-to-ground-truth distance and cannot capture this inter-prediction inconsistency.

## Input / Output

AliO operates on a set of N consecutive input sequences shifted by a lag parameter l (default N=2, l=1), each producing a prediction of length h. The method extracts the overlapping segments of length h−l from adjacent predictions and computes alignment losses on those segments. The output is a scalar alignment loss (sum of time-domain and frequency-domain components) added to the standard regression loss; no architectural modification to the forecasting model is required.

## Method

AliO is a plug-and-play loss function that aligns overlapping predictions from lagged inputs in both time and frequency domains. In the time domain, it minimizes a distance (MSE or DTW) between overlapping prediction segments, but applies a 'regression pulling' mechanism: at each timestamp, the prediction farther from ground truth is pulled toward the closer one via a stop-gradient on the closer prediction, ensuring the alignment gradient reinforces rather than opposes the regression loss. In the frequency domain, overlapping predictions are FFT-transformed and the same regression-pulling alignment is applied to complex-valued frequency components, which provably reduces both phase and amplitude discrepancies (Theorems 4.1–4.2). The total loss is L_reg + λ_T·L_T + λ_F·L_F, and the paper also introduces TAM, a new metric averaging pairwise MAE across all overlapping prediction pairs, to quantify alignment independently of ground truth.

## Results

Across 7 LTSF models and 7 datasets (ECL, ETTh1, ETTh2, ETTm1, ETTm2, Traffic, Weather), AliO consistently improves the Time Alignment Metric (TAM₂) by 36.8%–70.5% while maintaining or improving MSE by up to 11.5%. On the ILI dataset, which exhibits the poorest initial alignment, AliO achieves up to 17.4% MSE reduction and 58.1% TAM reduction (e.g., PatchTST: MSE 1.813→1.497, TAM 0.110→0.046). Overall, the paper reports up to 58.2% TAM improvement and up to 27.5% MSE improvement across all models and datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AliO is a plug-in loss function rather than a competing architecture, so each baseline model is compared against itself with AliO added to the regression loss. Across all 7 models and 7 main datasets, AliO consistently reduces TAM₂ (improving output alignment) without degrading MSE, and in most cases also improves MSE. The largest TAM gains are observed for CycleNet (up to 70.5%) and DLinear (up to 64.0%), while GPT4TS, iTransformer, and TimesNet show ~45% TAM improvements. On the ILI dataset (Table 2), where initial alignment is poorest, MSE improvements are most pronounced: PatchTST drops from 1.813 to 1.497 (17.4%), TimesNet from 2.176 to 1.963 (9.8%), GPT4TS from 1.898 to 1.755 (7.5%), and DLinear from 2.247 to 2.187 (2.7%). Autoformer benefits substantially on ETTm1 (MSE 0.488→0.448, TAM 0.070→0.053) and Weather (MSE 0.342→0.311, TAM 0.056→0.042). The one case where MSE slightly worsens is iTransformer on Traffic (0.422→0.423), though TAM still improves (0.061→0.044), confirming that the alignment objective does not systematically harm forecasting accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by identifying and formalizing the 'output alignment problem' in LTSF, which the authors argue has been overlooked by all prior LTSF work that focuses exclusively on minimizing prediction-to-ground-truth error. Methodologically, AliO repurposes the stop-gradient operator (commonly used in self-supervised and contrastive learning) as a 'regression pulling' mechanism that directs alignment gradients toward the ground truth rather than allowing both predictions to drift. The frequency-domain alignment via FFT draws on signal-processing intuition but is applied here as a training-time loss rather than a representation-learning objective, distinguishing it from contrastive time-series pretraining methods that operate in embedding space. The TAM metric is positioned as complementary to standard regression metrics (MSE, MAE, DTW), filling a gap in how model consistency is evaluated.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The output alignment problem has direct practical consequences in rolling forecasting scenarios (electricity budgeting, weather planning), where inconsistent predictions for the same timestamps force costly rescheduling and erode user trust. The finding that models with poor initial alignment (high TAM) gain the most in MSE from AliO suggests a previously unexploited link between consistency and accuracy, potentially reframing how LTSF training objectives are designed. Open questions include whether TAM remains meaningful on highly volatile or OOD-affected datasets (the authors acknowledge this limitation), whether alternative distance functions (e.g., Soft-DTW, shape-based losses) could further enhance alignment, and whether the approach extends to multi-step-ahead rolling scenarios with larger lags where overlapping regions shrink.
