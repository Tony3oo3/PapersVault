## Problem

State-of-the-art self-supervised vision encoders (DINO, MAE, DINOv2) produce feature grids downsampled by a factor of 16, which is suboptimal for dense prediction tasks requiring pixel-level spatial detail. Existing upsampling strategies like bilateral filters are computationally expensive and degrade under challenging illumination, while video-based representation learning has failed to yield representations that are simultaneously dense, versatile, and temporally consistent.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255419/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At test time, FlowFeat takes a single RGB image (monocular) and produces a pixel-dense feature map of fixed dimensionality 128 at full input resolution, complementing the low-resolution encoder features. During training, it consumes unlabelled video sequences (YouTube-VOS or Kinetics-400) where optical flow is computed on-the-fly by a frozen pre-trained network (SEA-RAFT). The trainable component is a lightweight DPT decoder appended to a frozen ViT encoder, so the method scales across backbone architectures without retraining the encoder.

## Method

FlowFeat embeds a distribution of plausible apparent motions (motion profiles) into pixel-level features via a student-teacher distillation framework. For each training iteration, two overlapping random crops of a frame are fed to an EMA teacher and a student network; a ridge-regression solution on-the-fly computes the optimal linear operator A* mapping teacher features to the optical flow crop, and this fixed A* is then used to compute an L1 reconstruction loss on the student branch. A focal gradient matching term additionally promotes flow consistency at motion boundaries. Because a different linear operator is estimated per frame pair, the learned representation statistically encodes a distribution of motions rather than a single flow, which accommodates motion stochasticity and provides robustness to inaccurate flow estimates. The entire framework is self-supervised, requiring no manual annotation, and trains efficiently on a single GPU.

## Results

On DAVIS-2017 VOS, FlowFeat-K improves the strongest baseline DINO2-B14 by +4.5% JF (linear probing) and +5.7% JF (local KNN), reaching 66.1/72.1 JF. The gains are most dramatic for weaker backbones: MAE-B16 jumps from 40.8 to 43.8 JF (linear) and from 44.3 to 59.1 JF (KNN), a +14.8% KNN improvement. On COCO-Stuff semantic segmentation, FlowFeat-K boosts DINO2-B14 mIoU from 58.1 to 60.4 and DINO-S16 from 39.6 to 44.2. On NYUv2 depth estimation, FlowFeat-K reduces RMSE from 0.3091 to 0.2791 for DINO2-B14 and from 0.4634 to 0.4422 for DINO-S16.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FlowFeat consistently outperforms all seven baselines across every backbone and metric. Against the image-based encoders, FlowFeat-K yields the largest relative gains for MAE-B16 (+14.8% JF with local KNN) and DINO-B16 (+6.2% JF linear probing), while the strongest baseline DINO2-B14 still improves by +4.5% JF (linear) and +5.7% JF (KNN). Compared to the bilateral upsampling method FeatUp [15], FlowFeat-K adds +4.6% JF (linear) on DINO-S16 and +2.8% JF (linear) on DINO2-S14, and also enables a lightweight post-hoc refinement (FlowFeat++) that FeatUp cannot match. Against the contemporaneous LoftUp [20] (which leverages SAM mask supervision), FlowFeat-K achieves +2.8% JF (linear) and +3.6% JF (KNN) on DINO2-S14 while requiring no mask labels. Video-based models V-JEPA [5] and VideoMAE [48] are substantially outperformed: DINO2-B14+FlowFeat-K exceeds V-JEPA by +17.1% JF (linear) and VideoMAE by +22.8% JF (linear), demonstrating that motion-profile distillation from optical flow is far more effective than cycle-consistency or masked autoencoding for dense pixel-level representations.

## Relation to Prior Work

FlowFeat departs from the dominant paradigm of self-supervised representation learning (DINO, MAE, DINOv2) that derives features from static appearance alone, instead distilling apparent motion patterns from video into pixel-dense features. It builds on the EMA teacher-student framework for on-the-fly computation of a lower-bound linear operator, and draws conceptual inspiration from motion profiles (Shi & Malik, 1998) that model velocity distributions per pixel. Compared to feature upsampling strategies like FeatUp (bilateral filtering) and LoftUp (coordinate-based upsampling with SAM supervision), FlowFeat is label-efficient, more computationally lightweight, and learns complementary motion-derived properties rather than merely recovering spatial detail from low-resolution encoder features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FlowFeat offers a practical, single-GPU-trainable path to pixel-dense features that complement frozen transformer encoders, addressing a persistent gap between encoder resolution and dense prediction requirements. Its robustness to flow quality (working even with unsupervised SMURF) and consistency across five backbones and three tasks make it a broadly applicable plug-in. Key limitations include the frozen-backbone ceiling on high-frequency content, a motion bias toward dynamic foreground objects, and dependence on brightness constancy assumptions that may not hold in non-RGB domains. Open questions include whether the motion bias can be mitigated, how the approach scales to very large models (ViT-H+), and whether the gradient-loss-only training variant (which nearly matches the full loss) reveals a simpler underlying principle.
