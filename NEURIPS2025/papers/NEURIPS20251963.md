## Problem

Differentially private first-order methods like DP-SGD incur high computation and memory costs from per-sample gradient clipping, while existing private zeroth-order alternatives suffer from high-variance gradient estimates and underperform first-order baselines on challenging tasks. Prior zeroth-order DP work has been limited to narrow settings such as prompt tuning or extremely sparse models, leaving a significant utility gap for general pre-training and fine-tuning. This paper asks whether modest amounts of public data can guide zeroth-order gradient estimation to close that gap while preserving the efficiency advantages of zeroth-order methods.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a private training set (sensitive, subject to DP), a public training set with similar distribution (non-sensitive, used only for gradient computation), and an initialized model. Crucially, privatization operates exclusively on scalar function evaluations (loss values) on private data via the Gaussian mechanism, while public data gradients are used directly without any noise. The output is a trained model whose parameters satisfy (ε,δ)-differential privacy with respect to the private dataset, evaluated across vision (CIFAR-10, Tiny-ImageNet) and text (IMDB, MNLI) tasks in both pre-training and fine-tuning regimes.

## Method

PAZO introduces three public-data-assisted zeroth-order optimizers that blend public first-order gradients into the private zeroth-order framework. PAZO-M takes a convex combination of the private two-point zeroth-order estimate and the public batch gradient, with norm alignment via sampling perturbation directions from a sphere of radius d^{1/4}. PAZO-P constrains the random search directions to the low-dimensional subspace spanned by k public gradients (k ≪ d), reducing the effective optimization dimensionality. PAZO-S selects the best public gradient by evaluating clipped loss on private data at each candidate step, then adds a small noisy perturbation to account for the residual gap between public and private subspaces. Under a γ-similarity assumption between public and private full gradients, the theory shows PAZO-M improves over DPZero by a factor of log d, while PAZO-P and PAZO-S achieve d-independent convergence rates, and all three methods are up to 16× faster per iteration than first-order baselines.

## Results

Across four settings (CIFAR-10/NFResNet18 pre-training, Tiny-ImageNet/ViT-S fine-tuning, IMDB/LSTM pre-training, MNLI/RoBERTa-base prompt tuning), all three PAZO variants consistently outperform the vanilla zeroth-order baseline DPZero at every privacy budget ε ∈ {0.1, 0.5, 1, 2, 3}. On CIFAR-10, PAZO-M achieves ~70.3–70.5% test accuracy, while on MNLI, PAZO-P reaches up to 70.9%. In highly private regimes (ε ≤ 1), the best PAZO variant matches or exceeds the best public-data-augmented first-order method (FO+PUB), while being 2–16× faster per training iteration. First-order baselines (DP-SGD, DPMD, GEP, DOPE-SGD) degrade sharply as ε decreases, whereas zeroth-order methods remain comparatively robust.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DPZero [8] (vanilla private zeroth-order), all three PAZO variants achieve consistent accuracy gains across all four datasets and all privacy budgets, confirming that public data guidance meaningfully reduces the high variance of zeroth-order estimates. Against DP-SGD [1] (first-order without public data), PAZO is competitive at moderate ε but clearly superior at small ε (≤1), where DP-SGD's per-sample clipping and noise accumulation cause sharp utility drops. Against the three state-of-the-art public-data-augmented first-order baselines—DPMD [39], GEP [40], and DOPE-SGD [41]—PAZO matches performance at ε ≥ 2 and outperforms them at ε ≤ 1, while requiring no per-sample gradient computation and achieving up to 16× per-iteration speedup. No single PAZO variant dominates across all settings: PAZO-M is strongest on CIFAR-10, PAZO-P excels on MNLI, and PAZO-S is competitive but slightly more sensitive to its perturbation-scale hyperparameter. Overall, the paper demonstrates that zeroth-order methods with public data close the gap to first-order methods in moderate privacy regimes and surpass them in highly private regimes, while retaining the computational advantages of function-query-based optimization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the private zeroth-order optimization framework introduced by DPZero [Zhang et al., 2023] by incorporating public data gradients to guide the stochastic gradient estimation, building on the two-point zeroth-order estimator of Duchi et al. [2015]. It departs from prior private zeroth-order work (which focused almost exclusively on language model prompt tuning) by evaluating across vision and text domains in both pre-training and fine-tuning settings. The public-data-assisted first-order methods (DPMD, GEP, DOPE-SGD) that the paper compares against represent a parallel line of work that also leverages public information, but through first-order gradient manipulation rather than zeroth-order function queries. PAZO is positioned as the first systematic combination of zeroth-order privatization with public gradient guidance, with three distinct mechanisms (mixing, subspace projection, and selection) for integrating the public signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in the 16× per-iteration speedup over optimized first-order DP implementations, which addresses a major deployment bottleneck for DP-SGD in large-scale settings where per-sample gradient clipping is memory-prohibitive. The finding that zeroth-order methods with modest public data can match or exceed first-order methods with public data in highly private regimes (small ε) suggests a fundamentally different privacy/utility operating point that first-order methods cannot reach. Limitations include the γ-similarity assumption between public and private data distributions, conservative theoretical bounds (especially for PAZO-M without bounded loss), and the requirement that public data be available and reasonably aligned. Open questions include whether the approach scales to foundation-model-scale training, how sensitive performance is to distribution shift beyond the tested class-imbalance and semantic-shift scenarios, and whether the convergence bounds can be tightened with sharper clipping analyses.
