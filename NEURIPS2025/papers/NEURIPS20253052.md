## Problem

Spike cameras cannot accurately represent light intensity in high-light regions because multiple spikes fire within a single readout interval but only one can be stored. While multi-level (ML) spike camera addresses this with a spike-firing counter, ultra-HDR scenes demand excessively high counter bit depths, making pixel manufacturing costly. The paper proposes time-encoding (TE) to shift from counting spikes to recording the time at which a fixed spike count (overflow) is reached, thereby extending dynamic range with fewer bits.

## Input / Output

The method takes a TE spike stream S ∈ R^(H×W×T) containing multi-level spike symbols (where higher values encode stronger intensity) and produces a single HDR light intensity image at a designated key moment t0. The stream is segmented into five temporal sub-streams of window radius ω_h centered at t0 and four reference moments. For deep-learning baselines, each input slice contains 61 continuous frames; training uses 96×96 random crops with horizontal/vertical flips, while testing uses 512×384 crops.

## Method

The TE spike camera replaces direct timer-based time encoding with a clock-cycle counter (CCC) that counts cycles of a high-frequency timing signal Sh until the spike-firing counter (SFC) overflows at P_max; the intensity is then decoded as the ratio α/m (total Sh cycles per readout interval divided by recorded cycles), achieving D_TE > D_ML whenever B1+B2 > B. For reconstruction, a multi-scale feature extraction module (MSFEG) uses convolution kernels of sizes 1–7 plus fixed Sobel/Laplacian gradient operators to capture texture and motion cues. A similarity-based pyramid alignment (SPA) module performs coarse-to-fine temporal alignment via a 3-level pyramid with deformable convolutions, a motion bias predictor, and a similarity predictor that gates fusion between upsampled and locally-aligned features. A light intensity-based refinement (LIR) module then applies multi-head self-attention where an intensity-derived feature modulates the value vectors element-wise, biasing spatial fusion toward well-illuminated, texture-rich regions. The network is trained end-to-end with L1 loss under supervised learning (Adam, lr=1e-4, 60 epochs, batch 4).

## Results

On the synthesized HDM-HDR-2014 dataset, the proposed method achieves PSNR-μ of 30.86, SSIM-μ of 0.853, HDR-VDP of 8.055, and HDR-VQM of 0.325, outperforming all TE-based baselines (best competitor BSF_TE: 29.64/0.826/7.825/0.370) and all ML-based baselines (best MambaSpike_ML: 27.30/0.788/7.405/0.443). On the Kalantari13 dataset, the method reaches PSNR-μ of 33.65 and SSIM-μ of 0.943, surpassing the next-best BSF_TE (31.74/0.937). The TE spike camera with 4-bit SFC + 4-bit CCC achieves a higher dynamic range than the 8-bit ML spike camera, as confirmed by consistent gains of 2–6 dB PSNR across all adapted reconstruction methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two groups of baselines. First, five reconstruction methods originally designed for ML spike camera (TFI, TFP, Spk2ImgNet, BSF, MambaSpike from Zhu et al. [67]) are adapted to TE spike streams. The proposed method outperforms all TE-adapted versions: it beats BSF_TE by 1.22 dB PSNR-μ and 0.027 SSIM-μ, Spk2ImgNet_TE by 1.22 dB, and MambaSpike_TE by 1.92 dB on HDM-HDR-2014. The gap widens on Kalantari13, where the proposed method leads BSF_TE by 1.91 dB PSNR-μ. Second, the Event-RGB hybrid method HDRev [55] (Yang et al., CVPR 2023) is compared in the supplementary: the proposed method surpasses HDRev by 8.39 dB PSNR-μ (30.86 vs. 22.47) on HDM-HDR-2014 and by 5.60 dB on Kalantari13 (33.65 vs. 28.05), though HDRev achieves a slightly higher SSIM-μ (0.972 vs. 0.943) on Kalantari13. Overall, the TE spike camera consistently yields 2–6 dB PSNR gains over the ML spike camera across all adapted methods, validating the time-encoding mechanism's dynamic range advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multi-level (ML) spike camera framework of Zhu et al. [67], which introduced a spike-firing counter (SFC) to encode multiple spikes per readout interval as distinct spike symbols. The key conceptual departure is replacing the high-bit-depth SFC (costly in manufacturing) with a time-encoding mechanism: once a fixed overflow count is reached, the camera records the elapsed time via a shared clock cycle counter (CCC) driven by an existing high-frequency timing signal, rather than a dedicated timer. For reconstruction, the paper adapts existing spike-camera reconstruction methods (Spk2ImgNet, BSF, MambaSpike) to TE spike streams and introduces a similarity-based pyramid alignment module built on deformable convolutions [5, 66] and a light-intensity-guided self-attention refinement module inspired by Retinex-based low-light enhancement [2].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The time-encoding sensor design offers a practical hardware solution to the ultra-HDR problem: by splitting the bit budget between a small SFC and a small CCC, the total dynamic range exceeds that of an equivalently-sized ML SFC without requiring expensive high-bit-depth counters per pixel. However, all validation is simulation-based, and the authors explicitly acknowledge that real-world hardware challenges (crosstalk, timing jitter, manufacturing variability) remain unaddressed. Open questions include whether the approach extends to color spike cameras, whether the shared-CCC design introduces spatial artifacts at scale, and whether the reconstruction gap over adapted baselines persists under more realistic noise models and higher resolutions.
