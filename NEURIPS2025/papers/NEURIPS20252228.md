## Problem

Vision-language models consistently underperform on visual tasks compared to their analogous textual counterparts (e.g., counting objects in an image vs. counting words in a list), yet the structural and functional source of this accuracy gap within the model's internal computation has remained unexplained. This paper asks which specific model components differ between modalities and whether those differences are structural (different components used) or functional (same components, different behavior).

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on pairs of analogous prompts—each consisting of a data region (an image or a short text sequence), a shared query, and a generation position—constrained to single-token answers and fixed positional templates for circuit alignment. Visual inputs range from SDXL/Flux-generated object scenes and arithmetic images to CLEVR spatial scenes and Wikimedia entity photos; textual inputs are the corresponding descriptions. Beyond the standard text answer, the method produces circuit component sets (attention heads and MLP neurons at specific positions), faithfulness scores, normalized cross-modality IoU scores, and per-layer cosine-alignment scores between visual and textual token activations.

## Method

The paper discovers task-specific circuits in three VLMs (Qwen2-7B-VL, Pixtral-12B, Gemma-3-12B) using attribution patching with integrated gradients (AP-IG) to score component importance, then selects minimal circuits achieving >80% faithfulness. Each circuit is partitioned into data, query, and generation sub-circuits by token position, and the paper measures structural overlap via bidirectional normalized IoU and functional equivalence via sub-circuit interchange (swapping a sub-circuit between modalities and re-measuring faithfulness). The key finding is that circuits are largely structurally disjoint (~18% shared components) yet functionally equivalent for query and generation positions, while data-position sub-circuits are both structurally and functionally modality-specific. To exploit this, the paper applies back-patching: it records visual token activations from a window of later layers (where they have become textually aligned) and re-injects them into an earlier layer window during a second forward pass, requiring no retraining. This simple test-time intervention closes approximately 32% of the visual-textual accuracy gap on average across tasks and models.

## Results

Back-patching visual token representations from later layers into earlier layers yields an average absolute accuracy improvement of 4.6% on visual prompts across five tasks and three VLMs (Qwen2-7B-VL, Pixtral-12B, Gemma-3-12B), closing approximately 32% of the performance gap between visual and textual variants. Improvements range from +0.6% (Color Ordering on Qwen2) to +17.7% (Sentiment Analysis on Pixtral), with the largest gains on Arithmetic (+9.4% on Qwen2, +6.7% on both Pixtral and Gemma-3). On general VQA benchmarks (VQAv2, RealWorldQA), back-patching also produces consistent gains of 2–5% absolute accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an interpretability paper with a training-free intervention, so there are no external method baselines to compare against. The primary comparison is between each model's baseline visual accuracy (e.g., 70.8% for Counting on Qwen2, 67.5% for Arithmetic on Qwen2) and the back-patched accuracy, with the textual variant accuracy serving as the upper-bound reference (e.g., 79.3% for Counting, 99.2% for Arithmetic on Qwen2). A control experiment applying the same back-patching to textual prompts shows smaller accuracy gains in ~79% of settings, confirming that the visual improvement stems from earlier access to textually-aligned representations rather than merely additional computation. The paper also validates generalization on VQAv2 and RealWorldQA, where back-patching improves accuracy by 2–5% over each model's own baseline without any textual analog available for the control.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends mechanistic interpretability's circuit-discovery methodology (Wang et al., 2022; Nanda, 2022) from single-modality LLMs to cross-modal VLM analysis, using attribution patching with integrated gradients to identify task-specific circuits in both visual and textual variants of the same task. It builds on the semantic hub hypothesis (Wu et al., 2024) showing that visual tokens gradually align with textual embeddings, and repurposes the back-patching technique from LLM multi-hop reasoning (Biran et al., 2024; Lepori et al., 2024) as a training-free intervention for VLMs. The key conceptual departure from prior circuit work in LLMs—which found high component overlap for similar tasks (Merullo et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024a)—is the finding that analogous tasks in different modalities use largely disjoint circuits (only ~18% shared), yet implement equivalent functionality in query and generation positions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first mechanistic circuit-level explanation for the well-documented VLM modality gap, pinpointing it to modality-specific data-token processing rather than query understanding or answer generation. The training-free back-patching intervention closing ~32% of the gap is practically useful and generalizes across three architectures and five task types, with validation on broader VQA benchmarks (VQAv2, RealWorldQA). Open questions include why the remaining ~68% gap persists, whether the approach extends to multi-image or complex visual reasoning tasks, and whether it applies to non-adapter VLM architectures. The finding that visual tokens only become textually aligned in late layers—too late to influence downstream processing—suggests architectural or training-time solutions may be needed for the full gap.
