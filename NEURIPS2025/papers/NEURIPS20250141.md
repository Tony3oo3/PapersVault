## Problem

Existing brain foundation models (BrainLM, BrainJEPA, BrainMass) pre-train almost exclusively on resting-state fMRI, overlooking 11+ tasking cognitive states available in large-scale datasets like UK Biobank and HCP. Simply pooling all cognitive states into a single model compromises the underlying neural heterogeneity between states, yielding only marginal or even negative downstream gains. The paper asks how to exploit the full diversity of cognitive states to build a brain foundation model that is robust across heterogeneous, small-sample downstream datasets.

## Input / Output

The method accepts fMRI preprocessed into regional time series (BOLD) or functional connectivity matrices (M×M Pearson correlation over AAL atlas regions), with M regions and C_in input channels. Each expert produces a fixed-dimension cognition embedding (C_hid=2048), and the cognition adapter fuses top-k selected expert embeddings with randomly initialized task query tokens to output class logits (disease, sex, behavior) or a scalar age prediction. For multimodal evaluation, a pre-trained EEG expert (CBraMod) is added alongside the 12 fMRI experts, so the router can select across modalities.

## Method

BrainMoE pre-trains N=12 experts (one per cognitive state) on stratified subsets of 68,251 fMRI scans from UKB and HCP, using either a reconstruction objective (BrainMass for FC, BrainJEPA for BOLD) or a new supervised cognitive-state classification objective. At inference, a lightweight router scores all experts and selects the top-k, and a Transformer-decoder cognition adapter—comprising multi-head self-attention over the selected expert embeddings plus task queries, multi-head cross-attention that injects information from the raw FC matrix, and FFN layers—produces the final prediction. The key design insight is that stratified pre-training preserves state-specific neural signatures (avoiding the information-bottleneck compromise of a single model), while the cross-attention adapter handles the orthogonal embedding spaces of different experts without requiring them to share a common representation. The framework is architecture-agnostic: any model that outputs a latent vector can serve as an expert, and the adapter's Transformer decoder provides the scalability that a simple MLP head lacks.

## Results

On phenotypic classification (F1), BrainMoE with BrainMass experts (FC reconstruction) achieves 81.23 on ADNI, 70.26 on ABIDE, 62.97 on PPMI, 88.57 on Taowu, 83.10 on SZ, 96.67 on HCPA, and 93.19 on HCPYA, outperforming the single-model BrainMass baseline (MLP, 68k pre-training) by up to +20.57 F1 (Taowu) and +11.92 F1 (HCPYA). With BrainJEPA experts (BOLD reconstruction), BrainMoE achieves 77.11 on ADNI, 54.55 on ABIDE, 60.49 on PPMI, 79.86 on Taowu, 82.86 on SZ, 81.74 on HCPA, and 74.59 on HCPYA, surpassing the BrainJEPA ViT baseline by up to +42.92 F1 (Taowu) and +28.62 F1 (HCPA). On age regression (MSE, years), BrainMoE reduces error from 36.77 to 4.86 on ABIDE and from 5.46 to 3.45 on HCPYA. On the NATVIEW fMRI-EEG multimodal benchmark, BrainMoE (13 experts) achieves 68.73 F1 on 8-task recognition, 65.47 F1 on sex, and 7.99 MSE on age, matching or exceeding both fMRI-only BrainMass and EEG-only CBraMod.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against BrainMass (Yang et al., IEEE TMI 2024), BrainMoE consistently wins across all 7 phenotypic datasets, with the largest gains on small cohorts: +20.57 F1 on Taowu (n=40) and +11.92 F1 on HCPYA, while the single-model BrainMass MLP 68k baseline sometimes degrades (e.g., −2.22 F1 on SZ when scaling from 30k to 68k). Against BrainJEPA (Dong et al., 2024), BrainMoE delivers the most dramatic improvements, particularly on HCPA (+28.62 F1, from 53.12 to 81.74) and HCPYA (+18.49 F1, from 56.10 to 74.59), where the single BrainJEPA ViT model actually loses performance when pre-training data is expanded from 30k to 68k. On the NATVIEW fMRI-EEG multimodal benchmark, BrainMoE (13 experts) essentially ties CBraMod (Wang et al., 2024) on 8-task F1 (68.73 vs. 68.71) and slightly outperforms it on sex (65.47 vs. 65.39 F1) and age regression (7.99 vs. 8.26 MSE), while also surpassing fMRI-only BrainMass on all three metrics. On the NSD visual decoding task (Appendix F), BrainMoE outperforms MindEye2 (Scotti et al., 2024) on every metric: lower train loss (7.994 vs. 9.639), lower test loss (9.405 vs. 11.142), higher cosine similarity (0.840 vs. 0.778), and lower MSE (0.261 vs. 0.301), suggesting the MoE framework generalizes to generative decoding tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the brain foundation model lineage (BrainLM → BrainJEPA/BrainMass) by introducing a Mixture-of-Experts framework where each expert is pre-trained on a specific cognitive state rather than a single resting-state pool. It departs from prior work in two key ways: (1) stratifying 68k fMRI scans across 12 cognitive states for expert specialization, and (2) replacing the standard MLP adapter used in language/vision MoE with a Transformer-decoder-based 'cognition adapter' that uses cross-attention to mix orthogonal cognition embeddings. The paper also introduces a new supervised pre-training objective (cognitive state classification) alongside the existing self-supervised reconstruction objectives of BrainMass (FC) and BrainJEPA (BOLD).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that naively adding 38k+ tasking fMRI scans to existing brain foundation models yields marginal or negative downstream improvements challenges the standard 'more data is better' assumption in neuroimaging pre-training, and motivates the cognitive-state stratification approach. BrainMoE's substantial gains on small downstream cohorts (e.g., +43.78 F1 on Taowu, n=40) suggest practical value for clinical settings with limited sample sizes. Key limitations include 4× computational overhead from 12 experts, evaluation restricted to the AAL atlas and classification/regression tasks, and the fact that BrainJEPA-based experts show less consistent improvement than BrainMass-based ones. Open questions include whether the framework scales to more granular cognitive states, whether the cognition adapter generalizes beyond fMRI/EEG, and whether the approach transfers to longitudinal or interventional neuroimaging.
