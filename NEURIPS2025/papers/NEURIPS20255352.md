## Problem

Existing RLHF alignment methods rely on the Bradley-Terry model, which assumes a ground-truth reward function exists for each prompt-response pair and enforces transitive preferences—assumptions contradicted by evidence from human decision-making and RLHF practice. When general (potentially intransitive) preferences are modeled as a two-player zero-sum game, prior self-play algorithms with standard online mirror descent achieve only an O(T^{-1/2}) duality gap bound and lack last-iterate convergence guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method consumes text prompts and binary pairwise preference signals from a general preference oracle (e.g., a pairwise ranking model), rather than scalar reward scores from a BT reward model. At each iteration, K=8 responses are sampled from the current policy per prompt and compared via a tournament to identify winners and losers; the resulting preference dataset drives a direct loss minimization that requires no explicit estimation of the full win-rate vector r_t(y). The output is the last-iterate policy π_T, deployed as the aligned LLM.

## Method

ONPO integrates optimistic online mirror descent into a self-play framework via a two-step update: π_t is computed by maximizing against a reward predictor m_t = r_{t-1} (the previous iteration's win rate), while the auxiliary policy π'_{t+1} is updated using the actual reward r_t, with KL regularization anchoring each step to the prior policy. The key theoretical insight is that the regret-bounded-by-variation-in-utilities (RVU) property of optimistic OMD, combined with the self-play structure where r_t is the win rate over π_t, causes the stability terms ‖r_t − r_{t-1}‖² to cancel against −‖π_t − π_{t-1}‖², yielding an O(1/T) duality gap bound and linear last-iterate convergence under unique Nash equilibrium. In practice, the algorithm avoids computing r_t(y) directly by reformulating the update as a squared-loss minimization over pairwise preference data (analogous to DPO-style objectives), making it computationally lightweight and implementable with standard gradient descent on a preference dataset.

## Results

On AlpacaEval 2.0 (length-controlled win rate), ONPO achieves 42.8 with Mistral-Instruct-7B and 48.6 with Llama-3-SFT-8B, outperforming the strongest baseline INPO (35.3 and 44.2 respectively) by 21.2% and 9.9% relative improvement. On Arena-Hard, ONPO reaches 29.7 (Mistral) and 36.4 (Llama-3), while on MT-Bench it scores 7.68 and 8.40. ONPO also matches or exceeds much larger models: it outperforms Llama-3-70B-it (34.4 on AlpacaEval) and Mixtral-8x22B-it (30.9) despite being 7–8B parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ONPO is compared against three online alignment baselines: Iterative DPO (Dong et al., 2024), SPPO (Wu et al., 2024), and INPO (Zhang et al., 2024). Against Iterative DPO, ONPO wins decisively on all three benchmarks for both base models (e.g., 42.8 vs 32.0 on AlpacaEval with Mistral; 48.6 vs 28.3 with Llama-3). Against SPPO, ONPO also dominates on AlpacaEval (42.8 vs 33.1 Mistral; 48.6 vs 38.5 Llama-3) and Arena-Hard (29.7 vs 24.5 Mistral; 36.4 vs 32.9 Llama-3), with a marginal edge on MT-Bench. Against the strongest baseline INPO, ONPO achieves a clear 7.5-point gain on AlpacaEval with Mistral (42.8 vs 35.3) and a 4.4-point gain with Llama-3 (48.6 vs 44.2); however, INPO slightly edges ONPO on Arena-Hard with Llama-3 (37.0 vs 36.4), while ONPO leads on MT-Bench for both base models (7.68 vs 7.46 Mistral; 8.40 vs 8.28 Llama-3). Overall, ONPO wins or ties on 7 out of 8 benchmark–base-model combinations, with the sole loss being a 0.6-point margin on Arena-Hard with Llama-3.

## Relation to Prior Work

ONPO extends the self-play framework for general preference alignment introduced by INPO (Zhang et al., 2024) and Nash-MD (Munos et al., 2023) by replacing standard online mirror descent with optimistic OMD, a technique from the learning-in-games literature (Rakhlin & Sridharan, 2013; Syrgkanis et al., 2015). The key departure from prior work is the use of a two-step update with a reward predictor (the previous iteration's win-rate vector), which exploits the self-play structure to cancel stability terms and achieve a faster O(1/T) duality gap. Unlike DPO and IPO, which rely on the Bradley-Terry model, ONPO operates directly on pairwise preference signals without assuming a latent reward function, and unlike Nash-MD, it avoids intractable mixture-policy sampling by requiring only draws from the current policy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The O(1/T) duality gap and last-iterate convergence are meaningful theoretical improvements that justify the optimistic OMD integration, and the resulting loss function is simple enough to implement with standard preference-dataset training—no reward model, no PPO, no mixture-policy sampling. However, the evaluation is confined to single-turn settings with 7B/8B models over only 3–5 iterations, and no error bars are reported, so the empirical gains (notably the 21.2% AlpacaEval improvement) should be interpreted with some caution. The multi-turn extension is outlined but left as future work, and the practical question of whether the optimistic predictor's advantage persists at larger scales or with more iterations remains open.
