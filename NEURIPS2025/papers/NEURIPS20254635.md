## Problem

Standard reward model training in RLHF optimizes the reward gap between chosen and rejected responses without distinguishing how much of that gap is driven by response-only features (e.g., length) versus genuine prompt-conditioned preference. This causes the reward model to overfit to spurious prompt-free biases, resulting in poor generalization when evaluating unseen prompt-response pairs — a critical failure mode for reward models used in iterative alignment pipelines.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a preference dataset of (prompt, chosen response, rejected response) tuples and, during training, computes a per-sample prompt-free reward gap Δr₂ via binary search that requires estimating an expectation over P(X|Y₁,Y₂) using importance sampling (re-weighting prompts by P(y₁,y₂|x)). The primary output is a trained reward model (Sequence Classifier on an LLM backbone), but the key intermediate artifact is the decomposed pair (Δr₁, Δr₂) per sample, which drives the data prioritization schedule. At inference, the reward model takes a single prompt-response pair and returns a scalar reward score.

## Method

The paper decomposes the reward r_θ(x,y) additively into a prompt-free component r₂(y) (invariant to prompt) and a prompt-related component r₁(x,y), then formulates the decomposition as a constrained mutual information problem: the prompt-related preference label Z must be independent of the prompt-free preference W̃ (MI(Z‖W̃)=0), while the prompt-free label Z̃ must not exceed the information in W̃ (MI(Z̃‖W̃)=MI(Z̃‖W)), with H(Z) maximized to prevent trivial constant solutions. Theorem 2 shows the optimal solution satisfies E_{x~P(X|Y₁,Y₂)}[σ(Δr₁*(x,y₁,y₂))]=1/2, and Δr₂* is recovered by binary search (Algorithm 1) over a bounded interval, estimating the required expectation via importance sampling that re-weights prompts by P(y₁,y₂|x). During training (Algorithm 2), each batch is filtered by a dynamic threshold on Δr₂ (maintained via exponential moving average of a binary-clustering boundary): samples with small prompt-free gaps are updated immediately, while those with large Δr₂ are deferred and reinserted with limited retries, thereby encouraging the reward model to increase prompt-related gaps while suppressing prompt-free biases.

## Results

On Reward-Bench with LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct backbone, the proposed method achieves 0.82 average accuracy, outperforming vanilla BT training (0.72) and RRM (0.77). With Mistral-7B-Instruct, the method reaches 0.75 average versus 0.66 (vanilla) and 0.70 (RRM). For induced policy evaluation, the method achieves 44.7/48.3 LCWR/WR on AlpacaEval-2 with Best-of-N(32), surpassing vanilla (35.8/45.1) and RRM (41.1/44.9), and 8.44/8.03 on MT-Bench (T1/T2) with DPO, compared to vanilla (7.83/6.51) and RRM (8.35/7.46).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: vanilla BT reward training and RRM (Robust Reward Model) [23], which also targets generalizable reward learning via causal inference. The proposed method consistently outperforms both baselines across all Reward-Bench sub-categories (Chat, Chat Hard, Safety, Reasoning) and both backbone sizes (8B and 7B), with the largest gains on Safety (+0.14 over vanilla-8B) and Reasoning (+0.11 over vanilla-8B). On downstream policy evaluation, the method achieves the best AlpacaEval-2 scores across all RLHF algorithms (DPO, BoN-4, BoN-32), with the most notable improvement being +8.9 LCWR over vanilla on BoN(32). On MT-Bench, the method leads on both 1st-turn (8.44 vs. 7.83 vanilla, 8.35 RRM) and 2nd-turn (8.03 vs. 6.51 vanilla, 7.46 RRM) scores with DPO. RRM is the stronger baseline, closing the gap to the proposed method in some settings (e.g., Chat Hard on 7B: 0.52 vs. 0.56), but the proposed method maintains a consistent 0.05–0.10 average advantage over RRM across all evaluations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the standard Bradley-Terry reward training paradigm established by InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022), but identifies a fundamental blind spot: the BT objective can be minimized by a reward model that ignores prompts entirely, since the response space is vast enough that each pair is unique. It departs from prior generalizable-reward work (RRM via causal inference, ODIN via explicit bias modeling, data augmentation approaches) by extracting prompt-free and prompt-related reward components through a mutual-information objective that requires no auxiliary models or constructed data. The data-prioritization mechanism draws on curriculum-learning ideas from LLM training (e.g., Rho-1, Prioritized Training) but applies them to a newly defined criterion (prompt-free reward gap) rather than loss-based difficulty.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even SOTA reward models can be dominated by prompt-free (spurious) preferences—where swapping prompts barely changes the reward gap—challenges the implicit assumption that training on prompt-conditioned data automatically yields prompt-sensitive evaluation. The method's practical appeal (no extra models, no data augmentation) makes it a lightweight drop-in for existing RLHF pipelines. However, the reliance on an additive decomposition form is a significant limitation: the authors acknowledge this may not hold for all reward models, and the performance gains, while consistent across backbones and benchmarks, are moderate (e.g., ~5 points on Reward-Bench). Open questions include whether more general decomposition forms (multiplicative, learned) would improve accuracy, and whether the approach extends to non-BT reward models like implicit-reward (DPO) formulations where the reward value is inherently unstable for non-corresponding prompt-response pairs.
