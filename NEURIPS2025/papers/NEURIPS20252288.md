## Problem

Current AI addresses distribution shift almost exclusively by scaling model size and training data, leaving the sensing interface static and incurring unsustainable environmental, economic, and equity costs. Biological sensory systems, by contrast, dynamically adapt at the sensor level (pupil dilation, refocusing, gain control) before neural processing, yet no systematic closed-loop framework exists for integrating such input-level adaptation into embodied AI agents that must sense, reason, and act under real-time constraints.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes raw analog sensor signals (RGB, depth, audio, tactile) together with a continuous space of sensor configuration parameters (exposure, gain, aperture, focal length, modality weights) and a perception-aware quality metric Q_M computed by a downstream model. It produces an optimized sensor configuration o* and the resulting model-friendly digital observation s*, and in embodied settings additionally outputs a physical action a_t. Critically, the method operates upstream of digitization—reshaping analog signals before they are converted to digital observations—so information lost to poor sensor settings is never irreversibly discarded, unlike post-hoc domain adaptation.

## Method

The paper proposes a progressive MDP-based closed-loop adaptive sensing framework that generalizes from single-shot perception (Lens) to continuous sensorimotor interaction. At each timestep, a sensing policy π_sense selects the next sensor configuration by maximizing a perception-aware quality metric Q_M (e.g., max softmax confidence) over candidate configurations, while an action policy π_action selects physical actions conditioned on both the observation and sensing-quality feedback. In the full embodied setting, a composite reward R = R_task + λQ_M (and, for multimodal tasks, modality-specific terms like λ_tact·Q_grip + λ_vis·Q_vis) balances task completion with sensing quality, providing dense intermediate signals under sparse environmental rewards. A modality-weight vector w_t dynamically allocates attention across N sensory channels (pressure, torque, IMU, camera), mirroring human sensorimotor reflexes. The framework is presented as a formal scaffold—covering non-RL single-shot, continuous perception-only, sensorimotor, and multimodal humanoid settings—rather than a single trained model, and the paper positions it as a research agenda with open questions on real-time algorithms, standardized benchmarks, and privacy-preserving on-device deployment.

## Results

This is a position paper without its own experimental results table. The headline quantitative evidence it cites comes from prior work (Lens [8], ImageNet-ES [9]): adaptive sensing improves classification accuracy by up to 47.58 percentage points without any model modification, and a 5M-parameter EfficientNet-B0 with ideal sensor adaptation can surpass the 632M-parameter OpenCLIP-H trained on 160× more data. The paper also reports that Lens maintains high accuracy despite a 50× reduction in model size compared to non-adaptive baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position/survey paper, this work does not present its own method-vs-baseline comparison table. The empirical comparisons it references are drawn from prior studies: Lens [8] outperforms traditional human-oriented auto-exposure methods and fixed-sensor configurations on ImageNet-ES and ImageNet-ES-Diverse, achieving up to 47.58%p accuracy gains. In 6D pose estimation (SenseShift6D [28]), adaptive cross-modal (RGB-D) coordination surpasses single-modal controls (RGB-only or depth-only) and factory-default sensor settings. The paper positions adaptive sensing as complementary to (not a replacement for) model-level domain adaptation, arguing that hardware-level control preserves information lost at digitization that post-hoc methods cannot recover. No head-to-head benchmark against domain generalization or test-time adaptation methods is provided; the authors flag this as an open research direction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper builds directly on Lens (Baek et al., ICLR 2025), the first test-time input adaptation framework that dynamically optimizes camera sensor parameters (exposure, gain) frame-by-frame to produce model-friendly observations for image classification. It departs from prior sensor-aware strategies—active perception (repositioning sensors), sensor-fusion budgeting (scheduling modalities), and post-hoc physics-based sensor simulation—by arguing that the critical intervention point is the analog-to-digital conversion itself, where sensor configuration irreversibly shapes the signal before any model can recover lost information. The paper extends Lens's single-shot, perception-only setting into a formal closed-loop MDP framework covering sequential RL, sensorimotor interaction, and multimodal embodied agents, drawing on biological sensory adaptation (pupil dynamics, cochlear gain control, mechanoreceptor mosaics) as the motivating analogy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that a 5M-parameter EfficientNet-B0 with ideal sensor adaptation can surpass a 632M-parameter OpenCLIP-H trained on 160× more data—challenges the field's default assumption that robustness must be purchased through model and data scale, with direct implications for sustainability, equity, and on-device deployment. However, as a position paper it presents no new empirical results; the cited evidence comes from prior work on image classification, and the proposed closed-loop embodied framework remains entirely formal and unvalidated. Key open questions include: whether perception-aware quality metrics (e.g., max softmax confidence) generalize beyond classification to dense prediction or sequential tasks, how to handle the combinatorial explosion of multi-modal sensor configuration spaces under real-time constraints, and how to distinguish covariate shifts (where adaptive sensing excels) from semantic shifts (where model-level adaptation is still needed).
