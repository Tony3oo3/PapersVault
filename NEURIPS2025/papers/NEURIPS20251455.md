## Problem

Standard Frank-Wolfe (FW) methods for smooth nonconvex minimization treat the objective as a black box, ignoring exploitable structure. While any smooth function admits a difference-of-convex (DC) decomposition, the non-uniqueness of these decompositions has not been leveraged to design projection-free algorithms with improved oracle complexities or stronger stationarity notions. This paper asks whether carefully selecting a DC decomposition can yield algorithms that are both computationally more efficient and geometrically more informative than vanilla nonconvex FW.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a DC decomposition (f, g) of the objective over a convex compact set D, an initial feasible point x₀, and a target accuracy ε. The output is a sequence of iterates {x_t} whose minimum gap_DC over all iterates falls below ε, where gap_DC is a decomposition-dependent stationarity measure that upper-bounds either the projected gradient mapping (PGM) or the proximal point mapping (PPM) depending on the chosen decomposition. The key practical detail is that the decomposition choice determines which oracle calls are amortized: one variant reuses a single gradient across O(1/ε) inner FW steps, while the other requires a fresh gradient at every outer iteration.

## Method

DC-FW integrates the DC Algorithm (DCA) with the FW algorithm: at each outer iteration, DCA linearizes the concave component g around the current iterate to form a convex surrogate, and FW solves this surrogate inexactly via a linear minimization oracle. The central insight is that different DC decompositions of the same objective produce structurally different subproblems and hence different algorithms. The decomposition f = L/2‖x‖², g = L/2‖x‖² − φ yields a nonconvex conditional gradient sliding variant that reuses one gradient over K = O(1/ε) inner FW steps, reducing gradient complexity from O(1/ε²) to O(1/ε) and, over strongly convex sets, improving LMO complexity to O(1/ε^{3/2}). The alternative decomposition f = φ + L/2‖x‖², g = L/2‖x‖² yields an inexact proximal point method whose gap retains curvature information, distinguishing local minima from saddle points and maxima. All variants achieve O(1/ε²) total LMO calls, matching standard nonconvex FW, while the gap_DC measure provides a unified convergence certificate that subsumes both PGM and PPM criteria.

## Results

On the QAPLIB benchmark (134 datasets), DC-FW achieves a lower average assignment error of 0.0085 compared to 0.0112 for standard FW, outperforming FW on 73 of 134 instances (FW wins 43, tie 18). On the partially observed embedding alignment task (300-dim FastText, 10% observation), DC-FW requires only 88 subgradient oracle calls over 10⁴ iterations versus 10,000 for both FW-K and FW-M, while achieving 93.11% of the full-observation Procrustes baseline quality. In the neural network training experiments (Appendix C), DC-FW with L=10 outperforms stochastic FW in both training loss and validation accuracy on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, and maintains stable performance as the constraint radius c increases, whereas FW degrades.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against standard FW (Vogelstein et al., 2015) on QAP, DC-FW achieves a lower average assignment error (0.0085 vs. 0.0112) and wins on 73 of 134 QAPLIB instances, though FW still wins on 43 instances, indicating the advantage is problem-dependent. Against non-convex CGS (Qu et al., 2018), DC-FW (variant 1) wins on 68.70 datasets on average (90% CI [66.17, 71.23]) versus 46.40 for CGS (90% CI [43.76, 49.04]) over 10 Monte-Carlo runs, with all three DC-FW variants outperforming CGS. On the embedding alignment task, DC-FW dramatically reduces subgradient oracle calls (88 vs. 10,000 for FW-K and FW-M) while matching or exceeding their convergence in FW gap; FW-M additionally incurs ~20,000 extra function evaluations for its backtracking line-search. Both DC-FW and FW-M outperform FW-K (Khamaru & Wainwright, 2019) in convergence speed on the embedding task, but DC-FW is substantially cheaper per iteration. In the neural network training experiments (Appendix C), stochastic DC-FW outperforms stochastic FW (Pokutta et al., 2020) in both training and validation accuracy on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, and unlike FW, DC-FW maintains stable performance as the ℓ∞-norm constraint radius c increases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper integrates the DC Algorithm (DCA) of Tao & Souad (1986) with the Frank-Wolfe method, building on the nonconvex FW analysis of Lacoste-Julien (2016) and the DC-FW adaptations of Khamaru & Wainwright (2019) and Millan et al. (2023). The key departure is recognizing that DC decompositions are non-unique and that the choice of decomposition fundamentally changes the resulting algorithm: one natural decomposition recovers a nonconvex conditional gradient sliding variant (Lan & Zhou, 2016; Qu et al., 2018) with improved gradient complexity, while another yields an inexact proximal point method. Unlike prior DC-FW methods that simply replace the FW gradient with ∇f − u, this framework exploits the full DC structure to trade off oracle complexities and stationarity notions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The decomposition-choice insight provides a practical design principle for projection-free nonconvex optimization: by selecting the right DC split, practitioners can reduce gradient calls from O(1/ε²) to O(1/ε) or improve LMO complexity to O(1/ε^{3/2}) over strongly convex sets, which is valuable when gradient or subgradient computation is expensive. The gap_DC measure also unifies and strengthens prior convergence criteria (PGM, PPM), clarifying a relationship that Qu et al. (2018) flagged as open. Open questions include extending the framework to stochastic settings, developing adaptive decompositions that adjust to problem geometry, and understanding whether the decomposition insight generalizes beyond the smooth and weakly-convex cases studied here.
