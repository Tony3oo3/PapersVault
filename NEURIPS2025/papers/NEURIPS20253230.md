## Problem

Existing facial emotion recognition benchmarks are constrained to 6–8 basic emotions, use uncontrolled in-the-wild imagery with occlusions and limited demographic diversity, and lack expert-annotated labels. This paper addresses these gaps by introducing a 40-category fine-grained emotion taxonomy grounded in the Theory of Constructed Emotion, along with large-scale synthetic datasets and expert annotations that enable rigorous evaluation of both human and model performance on nuanced affective states.

## Input / Output

The model takes a single synthetic close-up facial image (neutral background, controlled demographics spanning 14 ethnicities, ages 20–80, and balanced gender) as input and produces 40 continuous emotion intensity scores on a 0–7 scale, one per category, enabling multi-label affective profiles. For real-world transfer evaluation, the 40-dimensional output is mapped to 8 basic emotion categories via a softmax threshold (1.5× uniform) to yield a single discrete label. The annotation protocol for the HQ benchmark uses 4 independent expert raters per image scoring all 40 categories on the same 0–7 continuous scale.

## Method

EMPATHICINSIGHT-FACE uses a deliberately simple architecture: a frozen SigLIP2-400M vision encoder producing 1152-dimensional embeddings, followed by 40 independent lightweight MLP regression heads (one per emotion category) that predict continuous 0–7 scores rather than discrete class labels, preserving intensity gradations and multi-label blends. Training follows a two-stage protocol: pre-training on the 203k-image EMONET-FACE BIG (synthetically annotated via Gemini-2.5-Flash with a hinting strategy for underrepresented emotions) followed by fine-tuning on the 20k-image EMONET-FACE BINARY (human-annotated via a triple-consensus binary protocol). A neutral-calibration step subtracts each head's mean prediction on 1,700 neutral-face images to correct for baseline offset. The 40-category taxonomy itself was constructed by OCR-digitizing the 946-page Handbook of Emotions, extracting candidate emotion nouns with GPT-4, and iteratively clustering and refining them with psychology experts, yielding a constructivist, context-aware label space rather than a biologically universal one.

## Results

On the EMONET-FACE HQ benchmark (2,500 images, 40 emotion categories, 0–7 continuous scale), EMPATHICINSIGHT-FACE LARGE achieves a mean weighted κ_w of 0.1795 with human annotators, making it statistically indistinguishable from the human experts (Δ=0.019, p=0.103) while significantly outperforming all proprietary and VLM baselines (p<0.001). On real-world generalization, the same model reaches 78.29% accuracy on FERD (n=152) and 75.72% on AffectNet (n=31,002) after mapping its 40-category output to 8 basic emotions. In a blind preference study, the model's top-5 predictions were preferred over the median of 4 human expert annotations in 29.23% of 999 images.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EMPATHICINSIGHT-FACE LARGE (κ_w = 0.1795) is statistically indistinguishable from the human expert annotators (range 0.1309–0.2185; Δ = 0.019, p = 0.103) and significantly outperforms every other system (p < 0.001). The proprietary HumeFace API achieves only 0.1087, roughly 40% below the paper's model. Among VLMs, performance is extremely heterogeneous: Gemini 2.5 Pro zero-shot (0.1707) and Gemini 2.0 Flash zero-shot (0.1624) approach human-level agreement, while GPT-4o zero-shot (−0.0022), Nova Pro zero-shot (−0.0030), Pixtral Large zero-shot (−0.0004), and Claude 3.7 Sonnet multi-shot (−0.0002) perform at or below random chance. Only 2 of 9 VLMs (Gemini 2.5 Flash and Gemini 2.0 Flash) reliably produce parseable outputs under both zero-shot and multi-shot prompting; GPT-4o, Nova Pro, Pixtral Large, and Qwen Plus all fail on multi-shot prompts (refusal or unparseable output). On real-world transfer, the model achieves 78.29% on FERD and 75.72% on AffectNet, with particularly strong performance on Happy (100%/99.25%) and Surprise (78.95%/98.70%) but weaker results on Contempt (31.58%/28.75%) and Disgust (78.95%/40.53%), reflecting the difficulty of mapping fine-grained to coarse emotion categories.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of synthetic data generation and fine-grained affective science. Rather than building on a specific prior FER method, it repurposes state-of-the-art text-to-image models (Flux, Midjourney) as a controlled data factory and grounds its 40-category taxonomy in the Theory of Constructed Emotion and the Handbook of Emotions, departing from the 6–8 discrete basic-emotion frameworks (Ekman, Plutchik) that dominate existing datasets like AffectNet and EMOTIC. The modeling side is deliberately minimal—SigLIP2 embeddings fed into per-emotion MLP regression heads—positioning the contribution as data-driven rather than architecture-driven, in contrast to prior work that typically pairs modest datasets with increasingly complex end-to-end networks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

EMONET-FACE provides the first large-scale, expert-annotated, demographically balanced benchmark for fine-grained (40-category, continuous) facial emotion recognition, filling a gap where prior datasets offered at most 8 discrete labels with limited diversity and no expert continuous ratings. The finding that a simple MLP on frozen SigLIP2 embeddings is statistically indistinguishable from human experts on synthetic FER, while general-purpose VLMs (GPT-4o, Claude, Pixtral) perform at or near random with high refusal rates, reframes the question of whether scale and multimodality are necessary for affective perception. Key open questions remain: the sim-to-real gap (78% on FERD, 76% on AffectNet is promising but not conclusive), the restriction to static single-frame expressions, the Western-centric grounding of the taxonomy, and the absence of contextual or multimodal cues that TCE identifies as essential to emotion construction.
