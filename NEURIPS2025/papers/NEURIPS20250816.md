## Problem

Existing data selection methods treat the computational budget as a fixed hyperparameter, selecting subsets based on static criteria (gradients, scores, similarity) without accounting for how the available compute constrains what data is actually useful. Empirical evidence shows that no selection algorithm consistently outperforms random selection across varying budgets, and previously effective strategies rarely remain optimal when compute is explicitly factored in. The paper argues that compute budget is a first-order design decision that determines the appropriate quantity, quality, and distribution of training data, and must be integral to the selection process itself.

## Input / Output

The method takes a training dataset (either as individual examples for CADS-E or partitioned into source groups for CADS-S) and a fixed compute budget C expressed as a total number of forward-backward passes. It produces a learnable selection policy — a probability vector s over examples (Bernoulli) or a sampling-ratio vector over sources (truncated Gaussian) — along with the trained model parameters θ. Unlike methods that output a single fixed subset, CADS outputs a probabilistic policy that can be resampled, and the optimal subset size is itself determined by the budget rather than prescribed in advance. The one-dimensional surrogate l(|m|) is pre-fitted from 8 short training runs and reused throughout optimization.

## Method

CADS formulates compute-constrained data selection as a bilevel optimization: the inner loop trains the model for exactly C steps on a sampled subset, while the outer loop updates the selection policy to minimize validation loss. To avoid expensive Hessian computation for outer-loop gradients, the binary selection mask is replaced by a learnable sampling distribution p(m|s) optimized via a Hessian-free policy gradient estimator (REINFORCE). To eliminate the prohibitive cost of training K independent models per outer step, the inner optimality condition is recast as a penalty term in a single-level objective, where the compute-constrained training loss L_trn(u,m) is approximated by a one-dimensional log-linear surrogate l(|m|) fitted via cubic spline interpolation over 8 subset sizes. Two variants are provided: CADS-E assigns per-example Bernoulli inclusion probabilities for fine-grained control, and CADS-S assigns per-source sampling ratios drawn from an annealed truncated Gaussian for scalable selection over heterogeneous data sources. The resulting single-level objective requires only one forward-backward pass per sampled subset per outer iteration, yielding a 3–20× speedup over conventional bilevel implementations.

## Results

On MNIST (1,000 samples, 20-epoch budget), Bilevel-CADS achieves 92.80% average accuracy across initial subset sizes, outperforming PBCS (91.89%) and random selection (89.83%). On CIFAR-10 with heterogeneous noisy sources, CADS-S reaches 63.08% average accuracy across compute budgets (90K–225K), surpassing the best single source (61.65%) and full-dataset training (51.13%). On DomainNet (0.5M images, 6 domains), CADS-S achieves up to 44.49% accuracy versus 37.96% for uniform sampling—a 14.42% gain at the 20% initial ratio. On 13 instruction-tuning datasets (~130K samples), CADS-S reduces perplexity to 6.95 versus 7.56 for uniform sampling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary bilevel baseline is PBCS [74], which also uses a probabilistic reparameterization but does not account for compute budget constraints. On MNIST, Bilevel-CADS outperforms PBCS by 0.91% on average (92.80% vs. 91.89%), with the gap widening at smaller initial subset sizes (e.g., 2.63% at 200 samples). CADS-E also beats PBCS by 0.36% on average. For the source-level variant CADS-S, the relevant baselines are best-single-source and full-dataset training: on CIFAR-10, CADS-S exceeds best-source by 1.43% on average and full-dataset by 11.95%, demonstrating that budget-aware mixing of heterogeneous sources is far superior to either extreme. On DomainNet, CADS-S improves over uniform sampling by 6.05% on average (44.27% vs. 36.22%), with the largest gain (14.42%) occurring at the most constrained 20% initial ratio. On instruction tuning (GPT-2), CADS-S matches or beats both best-source and full-dataset perplexity across all compute budgets, and outperforms uniform sampling by 0.61 perplexity points on the 13-dataset evaluation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the probabilistic bilevel coreset selection framework of PBCS (Zhou et al., ICML 2022) by introducing an explicit compute-budget constraint into the inner training loop, departing from the classical assumption that the inner problem is solved to convergence. The penalty-based single-level relaxation that eliminates costly inner-loop unrolling is borrowed from first-order bilevel optimization methods (Kwon et al., ICML 2023) and ScaleBiO (Pan et al., 2024), but is adapted here to handle the non-stationary, budget-limited inner solution via a log-linear surrogate for the compute-constrained training loss. Conceptually, the paper positions itself against the broader data-selection literature by arguing that compute budget should be a first-order optimization variable rather than a fixed hyperparameter, a gap highlighted by recent empirical findings that no selection strategy consistently beats random across varying budgets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that the optimal data subset is fundamentally budget-dependent (low budget favors low-frequency/clean data, high budget favors diverse/high-frequency data)—challenges the implicit assumption in most data-selection methods that a single 'good' subset exists independent of training resources. However, the empirical validation is limited to small-scale models (GPT-2, ResNet-18) and modest datasets, and the baselines (random, PBCS, uniform sampling, best-source) are relatively weak compared to recent gradient-based or LLM-based selection methods. Open questions include whether the log-linear surrogate generalizes to larger architectures and non-convex loss landscapes, whether the method scales to billion-parameter models, and whether jointly adapting model size, data scale, and budget (as the authors suggest for future work) yields compounding gains.
