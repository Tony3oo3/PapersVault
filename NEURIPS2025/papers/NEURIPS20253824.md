## Problem

Reinforcement learning for instruction following in language models has been difficult to apply broadly because existing reward signals are either too narrow (verifiable-answer tasks), too arbitrary (monolithic reward models prone to reward hacking), or too implicit (distilling preferences from a larger model that must infer what to grade on). The paper asks how to produce reward signals that are simultaneously automatic, flexible across all quality dimensions, intuitive, and applicable to any instruction without human annotation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253824/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes natural-language instructions (sourced from WildChat) and produces instruction-specific checklists of atomic yes/no requirements, each with an importance weight (0–100). Candidate responses are sampled from a policy model at temperature 1.3 / top-p 0.9, then scored per checklist item by a 72B LLM judge (averaging 25 samples) and, when applicable, by an auto-generated verification program. The final output is a set of DPO preference pairs (top 40% of response pairs by maximum single-criterion score difference) used to finetune a 7B instruction-tuned model.

## Method

RLCF decomposes response grading into a series of instruction-specific yes/no checklist items, generated via a candidate-based procedure: multiple model sizes produce responses of varying quality, and a 72B LM is prompted to enumerate all failure modes as atomic, objective criteria with importance weights. Each response is scored per item by averaging 25 LLM-judge samples and, for discrete constraints (e.g., letter counts, format checks), by executing an auto-generated verification program; the two scores are averaged per item. Per-item scores are combined into a single weighted checklist score, and only the 40% of response pairs with the largest difference on at least one criterion are retained as DPO preference pairs. Two universal regularization criteria (no excessive preamble; match the instruction's tone/context) are appended to every checklist to prevent reward hacking. The key distinction from prior work is that the rubric is dynamic and instruction-specific rather than a fixed global set of dimensions, making the reward signal an 'extreme mixture-of-evaluators' where a unique subset of criteria is selected per instruction.

## Results

RLCF is the only method that improves Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct on all five benchmarks simultaneously. On FollowBench, RLCF achieves a 4-point absolute gain in Hard Satisfaction Rate (71.4→75.3) and an 8.2% relative increase in Constraint Satisfaction Level (3.05→3.30). On InFoBench, RLCF raises the overall requirement-following ratio from 78.1 to 84.1 (+6 points), and on Arena-Hard it increases the win rate over GPT-4 from 51.3 to 54.6 (+3.3 points). On IFEval (prompt), RLCF improves the loose score from 75.0 to 77.3, a 2.8% relative gain.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RLCF outperforms all baselines on IFEval, InFoBench, and FollowBench, the three constrained instruction-following benchmarks. On FollowBench HSR, RLCF (75.3) beats Skywork (69.5), ArmoRM (70.4), UltraFeedback (72.6), and AI Judge (70.3) by 2.7–5.8 points. On the general-purpose benchmarks, RLCF is competitive but not dominant: Skywork achieves a higher AlpacaEval score (44.8 vs. 36.2) and a marginally higher Arena-Hard vanilla score (55.1 vs. 54.6), yet Skywork regresses sharply on IFEval (75.8→68.0 strict) and FollowBench (HSR 69.5 vs. 71.4 baseline). ArmoRM similarly regresses on IFEval (73.8 loose) and shows mixed results on FollowBench. SFT (distillation from Qwen2.5-72B) consistently degrades performance across all benchmarks, confirming that the benefit of RLCF comes from the reward signal rather than additional knowledge. The key differentiator is that RLCF is the only method with zero regressions across all five benchmarks, whereas every alternative shows at least one significant drop.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper plugs instruction-specific checklists into the DPO preference-optimization pipeline, departing from prior AI-feedback approaches that rely on global rubrics (UltraFeedback) or fixed reward models (Skywork, ArmoRM). It extends the verifiable-instruction-following line of work (Dong et al., Pyatkin et al.) by making open-ended, subjective instruction following amenable to RL through decomposed yes/no criteria, and it builds on checklist-based evaluation (Cook et al., Saad-Falcon et al.) by using checklists as training rewards rather than inference-time evaluators. The candidate-based checklist generation—deriving criteria from observed failure modes across quality-graded responses—is a novel data-construction step not present in prior checklist or reward-modeling work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RLCF is the only evaluated method that improves on every benchmark simultaneously, while reward models and prompted judges produce regressions on at least one, suggesting that instruction-specific, decomposed reward signals are more robust than scalar or global-rubric rewards for RL. The finding that high RewardBench accuracy does not translate to effective RLHF supervision (Skywork scores 96.1 on Chat but degrades IFEval/FollowBench) challenges a common assumption in the alignment community. Open questions include whether checklist feedback can drive policy-gradient algorithms (GRPO) rather than just DPO, how to reduce the 4-day scoring cost, and whether the modest 1–1.5% drops on underrepresented domains (math, safety) can be closed by expanding WildChecklists' prompt distribution.
