## Problem

PINN failure modes—where the PDE residual loss converges to near-zero but the solution error remains large—have been attributed to local optima separated from the true solution by steep loss barriers, motivating a large body of architectural and optimization work. This paper challenges that understanding by showing no such barrier exists: the failure mode and the true solution lie in the same loss basin, and the optimizer simply stops prematurely due to insufficient arithmetic precision.

## Input / Output

The method requires no observational data; it consumes only the PDE operator, initial conditions, and boundary conditions evaluated at a fixed 101×101 grid of collocation points in (x, t) space. The output is a continuous numerical solution u(x, t) evaluated at test points, scored by relative MAE and RMSE against either closed-form analytical solutions (convection, reaction, wave) or a high-resolution spectral reference (Allen-Cahn). The sole 'hyperparameter' changed relative to standard practice is the floating-point dtype of all model parameters and computations.

## Method

The paper's core finding is that upgrading a vanilla PINN (3-layer MLP, 512 neurons/layer) from FP32 to FP64 while keeping the L-BFGS optimizer and all other settings identical eliminates failure modes on all four benchmark PDEs. The mechanism is that FP32's machine epsilon (1.19e-7) exceeds L-BFGS's default tolerance_change (1e-7), so as weight norms grow and gradient norms shrink during training, the inner-loop convergence criterion is triggered prematurely, freezing the network in the 'failure phase' of a three-stage dynamic (un-converged → failure → success). The authors validate the Same-Basin Hypothesis through three empirical observations: initialization insensitivity (2000 random seeds all fail in FP32), absence of a loss barrier (switching a converged FP32 model to FP64 and continuing training immediately reduces error), and a single connected loss trough. They further show that harder PDEs (larger convection parameter β) extend the failure phase, making them more sensitive to precision, and that even architectures like PINNsFormer and PINNMamba revert to failure modes when downgraded to TF32, confirming that precision—not inductive bias—is the controlling factor.

## Results

On the four canonical PINN failure-mode PDEs (convection, reaction, wave, Allen-Cahn), vanilla PINN trained in FP64 with L-BFGS achieves rMAE of 0.0059 (convection), 0.0271 (reaction), 0.0080 (wave), and 0.0157 (Allen-Cahn), outperforming all seven baselines on three of four problems. The headline finding is that FP32 vanilla PINN fails catastrophically (rMAE > 0.6 on convection, > 0.97 on reaction and Allen-Cahn), while the identical architecture in FP64 reduces error by 2–3 orders of magnitude. FP64 training incurs only ~10–30% additional per-iteration latency and ~1.5–1.7× GPU memory on an H100.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PINN_FP64 decisively outperforms all baselines on convection (rMAE 0.0059 vs. best baseline PINNMamba at 0.0184, a 3.1× improvement) and wave (0.0080 vs. PINNMamba 0.0193, 2.4× better), and by a wide margin on Allen-Cahn (0.0157 vs. best baseline PirateNet 0.1088, a 6.9× improvement). On the reaction equation, PINN_FP64 (rMAE 0.0271) is outperformed by PINNMamba (0.0092), PINNsFormer (0.0147), and KAN (0.0167), making it the sole problem where the method does not rank first. Notably, several architecture-based baselines that claim to address failure modes (PINNsFormer, KAN, PirateNet) still exhibit near-total failure on convection (rMAE > 0.6) and Allen-Cahn (rMAE > 0.3), while the vanilla MLP in FP64 solves them all. The paper further shows that reducing PINNsFormer or PINNMamba from FP32 to TF32 reproduces failure modes, suggesting their architectural inductive biases do not fundamentally eliminate the precision-induced stall.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the vanilla PINN framework (Raissi et al., 2019) and the L-BFGS quasi-Newton optimizer (Liu & Nocedal, 1989), but fundamentally departs from the dominant 'Loss-Barrier Hypothesis' that has guided PINN failure-mode research since Krishnapriyan et al. (2021). Under that hypothesis, failure modes are local minima separated from the true solution by steep loss barriers, motivating a large body of work on architecture changes (PINNsFormer, PINNMamba, KAN, PirateNet), adaptive sampling (R3), and regional optimization (RoPINN). This paper instead proposes a 'Same-Basin Hypothesis,' arguing that failure modes and the true solution occupy the same loss basin and that the real culprit is L-BFGS's inner-loop convergence criterion (tolerance_change = 1e-7) being smaller than FP32 machine epsilon (≈1.19e-7), causing premature optimization stopping.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If the precision-based explanation is correct, it redirects a substantial body of PINN research: many architecture and sampling innovations designed to 'escape' loss barriers may be addressing the wrong problem, and a trivially simple fix (training in FP64, at only ~10–30% extra cost on H100-class GPUs) could replace them. Open questions include whether the finding generalizes to higher-dimensional PDEs, other optimizers (Adam, Broyden), and whether precision-aware convergence criteria can be designed to avoid the FP64 overhead. The paper's scope is limited to four 1D benchmark PDEs and L-BFGS, so broader validation is needed before the 'FP64 is all you need' claim can be treated as a general principle.
