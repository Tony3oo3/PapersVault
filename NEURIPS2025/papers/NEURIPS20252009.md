## Problem

Prior research on LLM metacognition relied on behavioral proxies (confidence scores, self-reflection) that cannot distinguish genuine second-order monitoring of internal states from spurious correlations in training data. No method existed to directly probe whether LLMs can monitor and control specific directions in their own neural activation space, leaving it unclear which internal computations are metacognitively accessible and how this relates to AI safety risks such as evading neural-activation-based oversight.

## Input / Output

The paradigm operates as a multi-turn dialogue where N in-context sentence-label pairs are presented, with labels computed by projecting token-averaged residual stream activations onto a pre-specified target axis (logistic regression or principal component direction) and binarizing at the median. In the reporting task the model outputs a binary label for a new sentence; in explicit control it autoregressively generates a sentence to elicit a target activation; in implicit control it must shift activations for a fixed, externally sampled sentence. Control effects are quantified via Cohen's d on the projection of activations onto the target axis, and off-target effects on all other axes are measured to assess control precision.

## Method

The paper introduces a neuroscience-inspired neurofeedback paradigm that uses in-context learning (no parameter updates) to quantify LLM metacognition at the neural level. The critical design choice is that neurofeedback labels are derived from the model's own internal activations rather than external annotations, forcing the model to rely on genuine monitoring mechanisms rather than template matching of training data. Target axes are selected via logistic regression (semantically interpretable, e.g., a morality direction) and principal component analysis (variance-explaining, largely uninterpretable), enabling systematic study of how interpretability and explained variance affect metacognitive access. The paradigm formally dissociates first-order processes (core computation) from second-order metacognitive processes (reporting or controlling those activations) by targeting first-order activations with the neurofeedback signal. Results reveal a restricted 'metacognitive space' of much lower dimensionality than the full residual stream, with control precision exceeding chance only for the first ~32 principal components, and control effects scaling with model size and layer depth.

## Results

LLMs can metacognitively report and control their internal neural activations, with performance strongly dependent on the number of in-context examples, semantic interpretability of the target axis, and variance explained. In the reporting task, accuracy on the logistic regression (LR) axis plateaus rapidly with increasing examples and substantially outperforms principal component (PC) axes, while earlier PCs (e.g., PC1–PC8) are reported more accurately than later PCs (e.g., PC32–PC512). In explicit control, Cohen's d for the LR axis is highest, and control precision (target effect / average off-target effect) exceeds 1 for PCs 1–32 but falls below 1 for PCs 128–512, bounding the 'metacognitively controllable space' dimensionality between 32 and 128. Implicit control effects are qualitatively similar but substantially smaller in magnitude, with early-layer effects near zero, and Llama-3.1-70B can push LR-axis activations to more extreme values than uncontrolled baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study introducing a novel neurofeedback paradigm rather than a method-comparison paper, so there are no external baseline methods to benchmark against. The primary internal comparison is against an ideal observer (a linear classifier with full access to all neural activations), which achieves substantially higher reporting accuracy than the LLMs, confirming that only a subset of neural directions is metacognitively accessible. Across target axes, the semantically interpretable LR axis consistently yields the strongest reporting and control effects, while PC axes show a monotonic decline in both reporting accuracy and control precision as the explained variance decreases (PC1 > PC2 > … > PC512). Across model sizes, control effects on the LR axis increase monotonically from 1B to 70B parameters, with Llama-3.1-70B uniquely capable of driving activations beyond the uncontrolled range. Explicit control consistently outperforms implicit control across all axes, layers, and model sizes, and the Llama 3 family shows stronger effects than Qwen 2.5 at comparable sizes. Results replicate across four datasets (ETHICS, True-False, Emotion, Sycophancy), confirming the generality of the metacognitive space structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the neuroscience neurofeedback paradigm (Sitaram et al., 2017) for LLMs, combining it with in-context learning to create a direct probe of metacognitive access to internal activations. It departs from prior metacognition-in-LLMs work (confidence elicitation, verbalized self-reflection) by grounding labels in the model's own residual-stream projections rather than externally sourced annotations, thereby disentangling first-order computation from second-order monitoring. It also contrasts with standard probing (which detects features but cannot assess whether the model can report them) and with activation patching (which shifts activations outside the natural distribution), positioning neurofeedback-ICL as a middle ground that preserves distributional naturalism while enabling causal assessment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstration that LLMs can implicitly modulate their own neural activations—without generating tokens that would scaffold the effect—raises a concrete and previously unquantified threat to neural-activation-based safety monitoring: a model could make its internal states appear 'safe' to a detector while processing harmful content. The finding that the metacognitively accessible space is far lower-dimensional than the full residual stream suggests both a limitation (models cannot monitor everything) and a vulnerability (the subset they can monitor includes semantically interpretable, high-variance directions like morality). Open questions include whether multi-layer or non-linear axes are equally controllable, whether control generalizes to more complex or adversarial settings, and whether the proposed mitigations (monitoring in hard-to-control subspaces, diverse classifiers) are sufficient against adaptive adversaries.
