## Problem

Existing retinal vessel segmentation models struggle to generalize across imaging domains due to appearance variability, limited annotations, and the complex branching morphology of vascular structures. Prior Bayesian segmentation approaches (e.g., BayeSeg) model compact organ shapes effectively but lack sensitivity to the long-range connectivity and high surface-to-volume ratio characteristic of retinal vessels, leading to discontinuous predictions under domain shift. No prior method jointly enforces anatomical graph topology and domain-invariant image representation for vessel segmentation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single-channel retinal fundus image (resized to 256×256) and produces a pixel-wise vessel probability map Z, along with two intermediate decomposed components: a structure-preserved image S (enhanced vascular structure) and a structure-degraded image A (artifacts and noise), where X = S + A. At inference, only the decomposition and segmentation modules run; the deformable graph prior is not needed, keeping computational cost comparable to the BayeSeg baseline (~19.3M parameters, ~89 GFLOPs). The graph atlas is pre-computed from training-set annotations via skeletonization and PCA, making it image-agnostic and transferable across datasets.

## Method

GraphSeg is a variational Bayesian framework that factorizes the observed image into structure-preserved (S) and structure-degraded (A) components, then guides both decomposition and segmentation with a deformable graph prior G = wᵀB + δ, where B are principal graph components from a statistical retinal atlas, w are learned linear weights, and δ is a learned displacement field. To bridge the dimensional gap between sparse graph vertices and pixel-space S, a graph neural network predicts per-vertex Gaussian parameters that are rendered into a dense field via Gaussian splatting, yielding a differentiable S^G. An unsupervised energy function H(S; Z, G, D) enforces geometric similarity between adjacent vascular segments while penalizing dissimilarity at detected landmarks (branching points), driving structural alignment between the image and the graph prior. Variational inference is performed with closed-form Gamma posteriors for precision ρ and landmark intensity d, while remaining variables (S, A, Z, δ, w) are parameterized by deep networks (two 10-layer ResNets for decomposition, two GCNs for the graph prior, and a U-Net decoder for segmentation) trained end-to-end with a combined Dice + ELBO objective.

## Results

On same-dataset evaluation (Table 1), GraphSeg achieves the best Soft Dice across all three benchmarks: 82.91 on CHASE, 85.23 on DRIVE, and 84.29 on HRF, outperforming the next-best FSG-Net by 1.57, 1.04, and 1.78 points respectively. In cross-domain generalization (trained on CHASE, tested on unseen datasets), GraphSeg leads in Soft Dice on all three target domains: 77.16 on DRIVE, 78.16 on HRF, and 72.76 on STARE, surpassing the second-best BayeSeg by 5.48, 7.43, and 4.94 points respectively. On HRF cross-domain, GraphSeg also leads in F1 (76.59) by 6.46 points over BayeSeg (70.13), highlighting the benefit of the graph structural prior under complex noisy backgrounds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GraphSeg outperforms all 11 baselines on Soft Dice and F1 across all three same-dataset benchmarks. Against the closest competitor FSG-Net [11], GraphSeg gains +1.57 Soft Dice on CHASE (82.91 vs 81.34), +1.04 on DRIVE (85.23 vs 84.19), and +1.78 on HRF (84.29 vs 82.51). Against BayeSeg [8] (the direct variational Bayesian predecessor), GraphSeg improves Soft Dice by +2.43 (CHASE), +6.15 (DRIVE), and +2.52 (HRF), with the largest gain on DRIVE where low resolution makes decomposition hardest. In cross-domain evaluation (trained on CHASE), GraphSeg beats BayeSeg by 5.48–7.43 Soft Dice points and 5.30–6.46 F1 points on DRIVE, HRF, and STARE, while all other baselines (FR-Net, FSG-Net, AttUNet, AGNet, ConvUNeXt, DCSAU-Net, R2UNet, SAUNet) drop more severely under domain shift. On DRIVE cross-domain, GraphSeg (77.16 Soft Dice) still exceeds the in-domain U-Net (61.09) by 16 points, whereas FSG-Net drops to 57.51 and AttUNet to 45.27. The ablation confirms both modules contribute: removing the graph prior drops Soft Dice from 77.16 to 71.68, and removing decomposition drops it further to 66.56.

## Relation to Prior Work

GraphSeg extends BayeSeg's variational Bayesian image decomposition framework (which decomposes images into shape and appearance components for generalizable segmentation) by introducing a deformable graph prior specifically tailored to the elongated, branching topology of retinal vessels. The graph prior is constructed from statistical shape analysis of retinal vessel networks (Bal et al.), using principal graph components and factorized graph matching to build an image-agnostic atlas, and is bridged to pixel space via Gaussian splatting (Zhang et al.). This departs from both conventional Bayesian segmentation (which models compact organs with local pixel neighborhoods) and skeleton-based approaches (Tan et al.) that require ground-truth-derived skeletons, by using a learnable displacement field to align a statistical atlas to each image's vascular structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that explicitly modeling vascular topology through a deformable graph prior—rather than relying solely on local pixel statistics or compact-shape priors—yields meaningful cross-domain generalization gains, particularly on noisy/complex backgrounds (HRF: +7.43 Soft Dice over second-best). A key open question is the pre-processing dependency: the graph atlas requires skeletonization and graph construction from segmentation masks, introducing heuristic sensitivity (junction merging, branch pruning) that the authors acknowledge as a limitation. The authors also note the natural extension to semi-supervised settings via the unsupervised energy function, and future work on end-to-end learnable graph extraction could remove the hand-crafted morphological pipeline.
