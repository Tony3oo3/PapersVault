## Problem

Classic homophilic GNNs are widely assumed to fail on heterophilic graphs, yet recent empirical work shows they can be surprisingly strong baselines across varying homophily when properly tuned. The underlying theoretical reason for this universality—and the architectural changes that unlock it—remained unexplained. This paper identifies a fundamental smoothness-generalization dilemma in message passing: increasing hops inevitably raises smoothness (oversmoothing) while the Lipschitz constant must grow to compensate, degrading generalization, and this trade-off is most damaging in high-order homophilic and all heterophilic neighborhoods.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an undirected graph specified by its symmetrically normalized adjacency matrix Â and a node feature matrix X ∈ R^{N×D}, and produces node embeddings that are passed through a classifier to yield node class labels. A key structural detail is that IGNN partitions the k-hop rooted neighborhood tree into k distinct hop-specific neighborhoods N_v^(k) = N_v(A^k), aggregating each independently rather than cascading through layers, so the receptive field at layer k covers all hops 0 through k simultaneously without sequential dependency.

## Method

IGNN is built on three minimal design principles applied to the classic GCN message-passing layer. (1) Separative Neighborhood Transformation (SN) assigns an independent linear transformation W^(k) to each hop, giving each neighborhood its own Lipschitz constant and changing the overall Lipschitz bound from a cascade product ‖∏W^(i)‖₂ to a summation ‖Σ W^(i)W_i‖₂, which grows far more slowly with depth. (2) Inceptive Neighborhood Aggregation (IN) simultaneously aggregates from all k distinct hop neighborhoods rather than sequentially propagating, preserving low-order embeddings and preventing error cascading. (3) Neighborhood Relationship Learning (NR) learns correlations among the multi-hop messages via residual, attentive, or concatenative combination, enabling the network to approximate arbitrary K-order polynomial graph filters with learnable coefficients. Theoretically, IN+NR grant adaptive smoothness beyond fixed low-pass filtering, while SN provides distinct hop-wise generalization and improved overall generalization, jointly resolving the smoothness-generalization dilemma without requiring separate homophilic or heterophilic modules.

## Results

c-IGNN (the full IGNN with all three principles) achieves the best average rank of 1.3 across 10 node-classification datasets, outperforming all 30 baselines including graph transformers. On heterophilic datasets, c-IGNN leads by large margins (e.g., 90.97% on Roman-empire vs. 87.57% for CoGNN; 45.71% on Squirrel-filtered vs. 42.40% for UniFilter). On large-scale datasets, c-IGNN reaches 73.26% on ogbn-arxiv, 82.09% on Pokec, and 82.04% on ogbn-products, surpassing the best transformer (SGFormer at 72.62%/73.24%/76.24%) and all GNN baselines. The ablation confirms that all three principles (SN, IN, NR) are necessary for optimal performance, with c-IGNN achieving A.R. 1.0 in the ablation table versus 2.6–5.7 for partial combinations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

c-IGNN achieves the best average rank (1.3) across all 10 datasets, beating the next-best methods Polynormer and r-IGNN (both A.R. 6.5) by a wide margin. On heterophilic benchmarks, c-IGNN dominates: it outperforms the strongest heterophilic GNN CoGNN by 3.4 points on Roman-empire (90.97 vs. 87.57) and the best transformer Polynormer by 5.6 points on Squirrel-filtered (45.71 vs. 40.13). On homophilic datasets, c-IGNN leads by smaller but consistent margins (e.g., 90.41% on PubMed vs. 90.29% for SIGN; 95.91% on Photo vs. 95.87% for OrderedGNN). A notable finding is that inceptive homoGNNs (SIGN, IncepGCN, MixHop) collectively outperform all non-inceptive heterophilic GNNs in average rank, suggesting the inceptive architecture itself—rather than heterophily-specific design—is the key to universality. On large-scale datasets (Table 4), c-IGNN leads on ogbn-arxiv (73.26%) and ogbn-products (82.04%), while r-IGNN slightly edges it on Pokec (82.74% vs. 82.09%), both substantially surpassing the best transformer SGFormer (72.62%/73.24%/76.24%). The ablation (Table 5) confirms that removing any single principle degrades performance: c-IGNN (A.R. 1.0) > r-IGNN (2.6) > SIGN (3.0) > JKNet-GCN (5.3) > GCN (5.7).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the classic GCN message-passing framework and Oono & Suzuki's information-loss analysis, extending their smoothness characterization by coupling it with Lipschitz-constant-based generalization bounds to derive a unified 'smoothness-generalization dilemma.' It departs from the field's prevailing paradigm of designing separate architectures for homophilic vs. heterophilic graphs by showing that inceptive neighborhood aggregation (already present in SIGN, IncepGCN, MixHop, GPRGNN, and OrderedGNN) is the key structural property that alleviates the dilemma, thereby unifying these methods under a single theoretical lens. The work also provides the theoretical explanation for Luo et al.'s empirical observation that residual-connected GCNs are strong baselines, which previously lacked a principled account.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The smoothness-generalization dilemma offers a principled, unified explanation for why oversmoothing, poor generalization, and heterophily co-occur, replacing the field's tendency to address them in isolation. Practically, it suggests that simple inceptive modifications to classic GNNs can match or exceed specialized heterophilic architectures, reducing the need for task-specific design. Open questions include whether the principles extend to link prediction or graph-level tasks, how they interact with dynamic or heterogeneous graph structures, and whether the inceptive aggregation overhead becomes prohibitive at very large hop counts. The paper also notes that language-derived graphs (e.g., Roman-empire) may benefit from stacked IGNN layers rather than single-layer inceptive aggregation, pointing to graph-type-specific adaptations.
