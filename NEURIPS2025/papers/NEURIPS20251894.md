## Problem

Block coordinate descent (BCD) offers significant memory savings for LLM finetuning by optimizing one parameter block at a time, but vanilla BCD suffers from two fundamental flaws when applied to deep networks: backpropagation computes intermediate derivatives of inactive layers that are never used for updates (wasteful computation), and freezing most blocks narrows the optimization landscape, potentially misguiding the active block's search direction. These limitations explain why BCD has been largely overlooked for neural network training despite its memory advantages.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained LLM (Llama 3.1-8B or 70B) partitioned into D blocks (typically one transformer layer per block) and a finetuning dataset, producing fully updated model parameters. Only the active block's FP32 weights, gradients, and Adam optimizer states are stored in memory (reducing cost from 18N GB to 2N + 16N/D GB), while inactive blocks receive lightweight updates via rank-4/8 LoRA matrices or on-the-fly SGD with negligible additional memory overhead (~1-2.5%). The output is a complete set of finetuned parameters ready for downstream evaluation.

## Method

BREAD (Block cooRdinate dEscent via lAnDscape expansion) augments standard BCD by simultaneously updating inactive blocks during the same backward pass used for the active block. The active block receives K Adam steps as in BAdam, while inactive blocks are unfrozen and updated either with extremely low-rank LoRA correction matrices (rank 4–8, BREAD-LoRA) whose optimizer states accumulate across the entire run, or with on-the-fly SGD where each layer's gradient is applied and immediately discarded (BREAD-SGD). The key mechanism is that the intermediate partial derivatives already computed during backpropagation for the active block's gradient are directly reused to compute the expansion matrices' gradients at negligible extra cost, resolving the wasteful-computation limitation. A rank-1 expansion is theoretically proven to eliminate the landscape-narrowing suboptimality, and an 'efficient backward' variant restricts expansion to only deeper layers (ℓ+1,…,L) to further reduce computation. A BREAD-LoRA+ variant applies a higher learning rate to the U matrix of the LoRA pair for faster convergence.

## Results

On Llama 3.1-8B math finetuning, BREAD-LoRA+ achieves the highest average 0-shot score (43.7) and near-best 4-shot score (45.8), matching or exceeding Adam (41.9 / 46.2) while using only 23.2 GB of peak memory versus Adam's 208.2 GB. On Llama 3.1-70B, BREAD-LoRA attains the best average 4-shot score (66.1) across five math benchmarks, outperforming BAdam (63.8) and LoRA-rank64 (62.0). For instruction tuning on MT-bench, BREAD-LoRA scores 6.77 (GPT-4) and 5.08 (GPT-4o) after SFT, surpassing Adam (6.63 / 5.03), and after DPO, BREAD-LoRA+ achieves the highest GPT-4o score (6.15).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Adam (full-parameter training), BREAD-LoRA+ matches or slightly exceeds Adam on 8B math finetuning (43.7 vs. 41.9 average 0-shot; 45.8 vs. 46.2 average 4-shot) while requiring only 23.2 GB vs. 208.2 GB of memory (~9× reduction). On MT-bench SFT, BREAD-LoRA surpasses Adam (6.77 vs. 6.63 GPT-4; 5.08 vs. 5.03 GPT-4o), though Adam retains a slight edge on DPO with GPT-4 (7.83 vs. 7.68), while BREAD-LoRA+ wins on GPT-4o (6.15 vs. 6.08). Against BAdam (vanilla BCD), all BREAD variants consistently outperform across every benchmark: on 8B, BREAD-LoRA+ gains +2.2 average 0-shot and +1.5 average 4-shot over BAdam; on 70B, BREAD-LoRA gains +2.5 average 0-shot and +2.3 average 4-shot. Against LoRA (rank 80 for 8B, rank 64 for 70B), BREAD-LoRA+ improves by +8.9 average 0-shot on 8B and +4.3 average 0-shot on 70B. Against Galore, BREAD-LoRA+ leads by +7.3 average 0-shot on 8B, with Galore not evaluated on 70B due to lack of a distributed implementation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on BAdam [21], the first method to apply block coordinate descent to LLM finetuning, by identifying two specific pathologies of vanilla BCD in deep networks: wasted intermediate gradient computation during backpropagation and a provably narrowed optimization landscape caused by frozen blocks. The landscape expansion mechanism repurposes low-rank adaptation (LoRA-style) matrices not as the primary training vehicle but as lightweight corrections to inactive blocks, drawing on the broader PEFT literature [13, 20, 24] while serving a fundamentally different algorithmic role. The theoretical framing connects to the classical BCD literature [33, 38, 35] but applies it to the compositional, non-coordinate-friendly structure of transformers, a setting where BCD had been largely unexplored.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: training an 8B model in 24 GB (single consumer GPU) and a 70B model in ~300 GB (8×A100) while matching Adam's downstream performance opens LLM finetuning to resource-constrained researchers. The provable suboptimality result (Proposition 3.1) and its rank-1 resolution (Proposition 4.1) provide a clean theoretical justification for why naive BCD underperforms and why even minimal unfreezing helps. Key limitations include convergence theory restricted to the SGD variant under deterministic gradients, evaluation limited to Llama 3.1 models, and the open question of optimal block-switch frequency K. Whether landscape expansion generalizes beyond LLMs to other deep architectures, and whether the stochastic convergence analysis yields similar guarantees, remain open.
