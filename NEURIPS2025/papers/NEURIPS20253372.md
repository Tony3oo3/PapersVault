## Problem

Existing RAG methods for long-video understanding treat clips as independent documents, disrupting temporal dependencies and introducing irrelevant retrievals that distract LVLMs—even when the correct clip is present, ~40% of failures stem from hard negatives overwhelming the model. Additionally, prior approaches rely heavily on proprietary LLMs for planning and reasoning, limiting flexibility and increasing cost. The paper addresses both the retrieval quality gap (lack of cross-clip semantic structure) and the post-retrieval reasoning gap (inability to filter noise and aggregate information across clips).

## Input / Output

The framework is training-free and model-agnostic, operating on any open-source LVLM (2B–7B). Videos are sampled at 1 FPS and segmented into 64-frame clips; the graph is built offline in a query-independent pass (~20 s per minute of video), so multiple questions on the same video reuse the same graph. Online inference takes only ~4 s per minute of video, combining keyword-based graph retrieval, structured subquery verification, and final multimodal generation. Subtitles are used when available (Whisper for MLVU, provided captions for VideoMME and LongVideoBench).

## Method

Vgent introduces a four-stage graph-based retrieval-reasoning-augmented generation pipeline. First, an offline graph construction step uses an LVLM to extract semantic entities (subjects, actions, scenes) from each clip and its subtitles, then merges semantically equivalent entities across clips via BGE text-embedding similarity (threshold τ=0.7) and connects clips sharing an entity as graph edges. Second, graph-based retrieval extracts keywords from the query and retrieves top-N=20 clips by matching keywords to entity descriptions through the graph structure, preserving cross-clip temporal and semantic links that flat chunk-based RAG loses. Third, a structured reasoning step generates binary or numerical subqueries (e.g., 'Is there a laptop open?') and uses them to verify each retrieved clip, filtering out hard negatives and retaining at most r=5 clips. Fourth, the LVLM aggregates the verified subquery answers together with the refined clips as multimodal context to produce the final answer, explicitly bridging information across temporally separated segments.

## Results

Vgent achieves consistent improvements of 3.0%–5.4% over base LVLMs on MLVU, with the largest gain of +5.4% for LongVU (65.4→70.8) and +4.2% for Qwen2.5-VL 3B (66.2→70.4), the latter surpassing its own 7B counterpart. On VideoMME (with subtitles), Vgent yields an average gain of ~3.2% across all models, with the best improvement of +3.8% for Qwen2.5-VL 7B (71.1→74.3). On LongVideoBench, improvements range from +2.5% to +3.7%. Against the strongest open-source RAG baseline (Video-RAG), Vgent with Qwen2.5-VL 7B achieves 72.1% on MLVU versus 63.4%, an 8.7% absolute gain.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against proprietary LVLMs, Vgent with Qwen2.5-VL 7B reaches 72.1% on MLVU, surpassing GPT-4o (64.6%) by 7.5 points, though it remains below Gemini 1.5 Pro (81.3%) on VideoMME w/ sub. Among open-source RAG baselines, Vgent with Qwen2.5-VL 7B outperforms Video-RAG by 8.7 points on MLVU (72.1 vs. 63.4) and 8.6 points on VideoMME w/ sub (74.3 vs. 65.7); with LLaVA-Video 7B, the margin narrows to 1.2 points on MLVU (72.5 vs. 71.3). Against proprietary RAG/agent methods, Vgent (Qwen2.5-VL 7B, 74.3% VideoMME w/ sub) exceeds DrVideo (71.7%), LLoVi (67.7%), and VideoAgent (44.4%), all of which rely on closed-source APIs. The framework's gains are most pronounced on tasks requiring multi-clip temporal reasoning (Count, Order in MLVU), where improvements reach +23.7 points (InternVL2.5 Count: 34.9→59.2), confirming that graph-based retrieval and structured reasoning specifically address the cross-segment reasoning bottleneck.

## Relation to Prior Work

Vgent sits at the intersection of graph-based RAG (inspired by GraphRAG for text) and video retrieval-augmented generation, departing from prior video RAG methods that treat clips as independent documents (Goldfish, Video-RAG) or rely on proprietary LLMs for multi-turn reasoning (VideoAgent, DrVideo, VideoTree). Its key conceptual departure is combining an offline, query-independent entity graph (preserving cross-clip temporal and semantic dependencies) with a structured post-retrieval verification step, all within a training-free, fully open-source pipeline. The structured reasoning component draws on divide-and-conquer query decomposition, but applies it specifically to filter hard-negative video clips rather than to decompose the final answer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical, plug-and-play improvement for long-video QA with open-source LVLMs, and the finding that ~40% of retrieval failures stem from information overload (correct clip present but model distracted by hard negatives) rather than retrieval inaccuracy reframes where the bottleneck lies. Limitations include the graph relying solely on textual entity descriptions (no visual embeddings), performance being bounded by the base LVLM, and the pipeline adding offline construction cost. Open questions include whether incorporating frame-level visual features into the graph would further improve retrieval, and whether the structured reasoning step generalizes to non-multiple-choice or open-ended video QA settings.
