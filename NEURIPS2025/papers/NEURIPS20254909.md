## Problem

Public model repositories host over 1.5 million models, yet more than 60% lack any documentation, making their capabilities, provenance, and interrelationships effectively unknowable. The ML community has no unified structure to represent the model population as a whole, preventing model forensics, meta-ML analysis, and efficient model discovery. This paper advocates the Model Atlas—a directed acyclic graph of models and their weight transformations—and introduces practical methods to chart its largely uncharted regions.

## Input / Output

The charting algorithm consumes each model's weights (subsampled to ~100 neurons for tractable distance computation), upload timestamps, quantization flags, and any known parent references from model cards. It outputs a predicted DAG of parent-child edges (achieved in O(n²) time, recovering graphs with thousands of nodes in seconds), plus imputed node attributes such as accuracy, license, pipeline_tag, and model_type via graph kNN and hub-based majority voting. The functional-probing variant instead takes a model's responses to a fixed set of m probe inputs as its representation, trading m forward passes per model for symmetry-free downstream learning.

## Method

The core charting algorithm operates within connected components of the atlas, where fine-tuning preserves neuron ordering and thus permutation symmetry is already resolved, allowing standard distance-based methods without equivariant architectures. It combines several structural priors extracted from real-world repositories: quantized models are designated as leaves (99.41% are), exact duplicates are collapsed, and edge direction is inferred from upload timestamps (99.73% of parents precede children). A key mechanism distinguishes 'snake' patterns (sequential checkpoints, where weight distance correlates with temporal distance) from 'fan' patterns (hyperparameter sweeps, where it does not) via a correlation threshold, routing each node to the correct parent type. For attribute imputation, the paper uses graph kNN with path-length distance in the undirected atlas (achieving 0.885 correlation on TruthfulQA) and hub-based majority voting across sibling leaf nodes (improving license prediction by 35% over global baselines). The broader position argues that bypassing weight-space permutation symmetry—rather than building equivariant networks—enables scalable charting with standard architectures and training recipes.

## Results

On atlas structure recovery across three in-the-wild connected components (Qwen2.5-0.5B, Llama-3.2-1B, Stable-Diffusion-2), the proposed method achieves 78.87%, 80.44%, and 85.10% edge accuracy respectively, substantially outperforming the strongest baseline MoTHer (32.81%, 19.32%, 50.51%). For metric imputation on the Mistral-7B component, 5-NN graph-based prediction reduces MSE from 100.2 (baseline) to 23.5 on TruthfulQA and from 95.0 to 22.0 on HellaSwag, with correlation reaching 0.885 and 0.890. Hub-based attribute imputation improves license prediction accuracy by 35 percentage points (0.49→0.85) and pipeline_tag by 19 points (0.60→0.79) over global majority baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against four baselines for atlas structure recovery: random edge assignment, majority vote (assigning all nodes to the highest out-degree parent), Price's preferential attachment model [6], and MoTHer [21] (a weight-dependent method using kurtosis for edge direction that assumes tree structure). The proposed method wins decisively on all three connected components, outperforming MoTHer by 46–66 percentage points (e.g., 80.44% vs. 19.32% on Llama). Price's model [6], while more sophisticated than random or majority vote, remains data-agnostic and fails to connect the correct nodes, scoring only 2–9% accuracy. The ablation (Table 3) confirms each structural prior (quantization-as-leaf, deduplication, temporal consistency, snake/fan classification, merge handling) contributes meaningfully, with removing quantization detection causing the largest single drop (Llama: 80.44→36.59). MoTHer's limitation is its tree assumption and weaker directional prediction, whereas the proposed method handles DAGs natively and leverages real-world repository patterns (timestamps, quantization flags, known parents from model cards).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the emerging field of weight-space learning (treating model weights as data) by proposing a unifying graph structure—the Model Atlas—that subsumes previously separate tasks: model lineage recovery (MoTHer [21], Neural Lineage [63]), model discovery/transferability estimation [8, 23, 29], and model forensics [4, 20, 43]. It departs from the dominant equivariant-network paradigm in weight-space learning by arguing that permutation symmetries can be sidestepped via probing, connected-component-local learning, and structural priors, enabling standard architectures at scale. The charting algorithm builds directly on MoTHer's weight-distance nearest-neighbor approach but generalizes it from trees to DAGs, adding temporal dynamics, quantization detection, and snake/fan pattern classification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete and growing problem—over 60% of Hugging Face models lack documentation, creating a vast population of 'lost models' with unknown provenance, capabilities, and relationships—and provides both a unifying framework and working algorithms to address it. The practical demonstration (63k-model atlas visualization, 78–85% edge-recovery accuracy, kNN-based accuracy prediction at 0.85+ correlation) makes the vision tangible. Key open challenges remain: scaling to web-scale repositories, handling black-box models (APIs like ChatGPT), recovering fine-grained training trajectories, and developing principled probe selection. The bold claim that equivariant methods will not scale to full repositories is provocative but currently supported by computational-cost arguments rather than definitive empirical refutation.
