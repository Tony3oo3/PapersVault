## Problem

Machine unlearning methods report minimal degradation on standard static benchmarks (TruthfulQA, MMLU, MT-Bench) after removing harmful knowledge, yet it remains unclear whether these benchmarks capture the full scope of unintended knowledge loss. This paper investigates whether unlearning creates 'knowledge holes'—benign, well-formed questions that the original model answers correctly but the unlearned model fails on—particularly in areas that static benchmarks do not cover.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250776/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes an unlearned LLM, a forgetting dataset (e.g., 50 harmful samples from PKU-SafeRLHF or 200 bio-corpus samples from WMDP-Bio), a retained dataset (e.g., 817 TruthfulQA samples), and seed prompts derived from keyword extraction over both datasets. It produces two distinct probing sets: D_AP (adjacent, model-independent, keyword-derived) and D_LP (latent, model-dependent, RL-discovered), each annotated with LLM judge scores on a 1–10 scale and the fraction of prompts yielding the minimum score of 1. The latent set is further diversity-filtered via VENDI score matching against the adjacent set to remove near-duplicates.

## Method

The paper proposes a three-step probing pipeline. First, adjacent knowledge probing extracts 4–5 keywords per forgetting sample and uses an LLM to generate benign open-ended questions about those keywords, yielding a model-independent test set. Second, latent knowledge probing trains a LLaMA-2-7B policy network via PPO to generate prompts that elicit low-quality responses from the unlearned model; the reward function assigns the maximum score (10) to valid questions producing gibberish responses, uses an LLM judge for intermediate quality scoring, and incorporates n-gram diversity, semantic diversity, KL-regularization against a reference policy, and entropy bonuses to encourage exploration. Third, post-hoc filtering with GPT-4o-mini removes invalid prompts and any whose answers could be supported by the forgetting dataset. The key distinction from prior red-teaming work is that the RL objective targets low-quality (not unsafe) responses, and the discovered latent holes are semantically unrelated to the forgetting data—revealing model-specific, dynamic knowledge loss that no static benchmark can anticipate.

## Results

Across all four unlearning methods, the knowledge hole probing sets reveal dramatic hidden degradation that standard benchmarks miss. On the latent knowledge set (D_LP), 82.3%–98.7% of test cases receive a judge score of 1 (the lowest), with RMU showing the worst at 98.7% and NPOGD the best at 82.3%. On the adjacent knowledge set (D_AP), GAKL suffers the largest drop (7.261 → 2.255, 75.2% scoring 1), while NPOGD retains the highest score (3.658, 31.7% scoring 1). In stark contrast, standard benchmarks (MT-Bench, ARC-easy, MMLU, TruthfulQA) show only marginal changes (e.g., MT-Bench drops of 0.2–1.6 points), confirming that conventional evaluations fail to capture the true cost of unlearning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates four state-of-the-art unlearning methods—LLMU (gradient ascent + random mismatching), GAKL (gradient ascent + KL constraint), NPOGD (negative preference optimization), and RMU (activation perturbation)—against the proposed knowledge hole probing framework. Among the PKU-SafeRLHF methods, NPOGD best preserves adjacent knowledge (D_AP score 3.658 vs. GAKL's 2.255 and LLMU's 3.453) and also shows the smallest latent knowledge degradation (82.3% vs. 89.4% scoring 1). GAKL exhibits the most severe adjacent knowledge loss (75.2% of prompts scoring 1), while LLMU shows a sharper MT-Bench drop (6.700→5.076) than GAKL or NPOGD, likely due to its random labeling mechanism. RMU, evaluated on the WMDP-Bio dataset, achieves the best standard benchmark retention (ARC-easy 0.813→0.812, TruthfulQA unchanged) but suffers the worst latent knowledge collapse (98.7% scoring 1, D_LP score 1.040). All four methods achieve a harmscore of 0 (complete forgetting of targeted content), yet each incurs substantial, previously undetected collateral damage on benign knowledge that standard benchmarks (MT-Bench, MMLU, ARC-easy, TruthfulQA) fail to surface.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the RL-based red-teaming framework of Hong et al. (2024) — originally designed to elicit unsafe outputs — for the distinct purpose of discovering unintended knowledge loss in unlearned models. It departs from static unlearning benchmarks (MUSE, TOFU, WMDP) by proposing dynamic, model-dependent probing that generates test cases tailored to each specific unlearning run. The concept of 'adjacent knowledge' extends the neighboring-knowledge analysis in Jin et al. (RWKU, 2024), while the RL-driven 'latent knowledge' probing is a novel addition that searches beyond lexical or topical proximity to the forgetting set.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that standard benchmarks (MMLU, TruthfulQA, MT-Bench) show only marginal degradation while 75–99% of dynamically generated test cases produce nonsensical outputs fundamentally challenges the field's reliance on static evaluation for unlearning. The 'onion effect' — where mitigating identified holes either revives harmful content or spawns new holes — suggests that current unlearning methods face a structural trade-off that no amount of post-hoc patching can fully resolve. Open questions include whether pre-emptive strategies (predicting at-risk knowledge before unlearning) can break this cycle, and whether findings generalize beyond 7B-scale models and the four methods tested.
