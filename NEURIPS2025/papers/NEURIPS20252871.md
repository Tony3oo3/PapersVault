## Problem

Achieving completeness (universal approximation over equivariant functions) in equivariant GNNs has traditionally required stacking many layers, increasing body order, or raising the degree of steerable features, all at prohibitive computational cost. The GWL-test framework, which connects completeness to geometric isomorphism, inherits only quasipolynomial-time solutions from the classical WL-test. This paper asks whether complete equivariant GNNs can be constructed in polynomial time with a small, fixed number of layers.

## Input / Output

The method takes a geometric graph G = (H, X; A) consisting of N nodes with 3D coordinates, node features, and an adjacency matrix (optionally with edge features). It produces two outputs: (1) a canonical form Γ(G), a graph-level scalar embedding that uniquely identifies the geometric graph up to E(3) isomorphism, and (2) lth-degree steerable features computed as a weighted sum over a full-rank basis set, where the weights are the canonical-form scalars. The canonical form is computed in O(N²) for asymmetric graphs (via four virtual reference nodes) or O(N⁶) in the general case, and the steerable output is a (2l+1)-dimensional vector per node or graph.

## Method

The core contribution is the 'dynamic method' (Theorem 3.2), which proves that any complete lth-degree steerable function can be written as a sum over a finite full-rank basis set with input-dependent scalar weights, eliminating the need for Clebsch-Gordan tensor products and deep stacking. Completeness is achieved by two components: (a) a canonical form of the geometric graph, constructed via a four-point positioning algorithm that converts 3D coordinates into distance vectors relative to four non-coplanar reference points (generated by FastEGNN virtual nodes for the O(N²) asymmetric case), encoded with DeepSet; and (b) a full-rank steerable basis set, whose existence for asymmetric graphs is guaranteed by a node-coloring theorem (Theorem 3.7) that decouples node/edge contributions. Practically, this is implemented as EGNN_cpl and TFN_cpl variants (global and local), where the canonical form is injected into message-passing weights, enabling a single-layer network to achieve completeness while matching or exceeding multi-layer baselines on physical dynamics and molecular property prediction tasks.

## Results

On the completeness test, all Uni-EGNN variants (EGNN_cpl, TFN_cpl, SchNet_cpl, GVP-GNN_cpl) achieve 100% accuracy on both 2-body and 3-body discrimination tasks, whereas baselines like EGNN, SchNet, and TFN score only 50%. On the 100-body system, EGNN_cpl-local achieves the best overall MSE of 0.73×10⁻², outperforming the strongest baseline Equiformer (0.90×10⁻²) and HEGNN_{l≤6} (0.86×10⁻²). On the tetrahedron center prediction task, EGNN_cpl-global reaches 0.108 MSE for the Monge point with just 1 layer, compared to 1.188 for standard EGNN at the same depth — a ~11× improvement. On the 5-body system, EGNN_cpl-local achieves 0.768×10⁻² MSE with 1 layer versus 4.214×10⁻² for EGNN.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EGNN_cpl-local achieves the best overall MSE (0.73) on the 100-body system, beating Equiformer (0.90) by ~19% and HEGNN_{l≤6} (0.86) by ~15%, while also leading on Benzene (44.90 vs. 33.28 for Equiformer) and most individual molecules. On the tetrahedron task, EGNN_cpl-global with 1 layer (0.108 MSE for Monge point) dramatically outperforms EGNN (1.188), FastEGNN (1.187), HEGNN (1.188), TFN (1.188), and MACE (1.188) at the same depth, and even surpasses Equiformer's 4-layer result (0.116). On the 5-body system, EGNN_cpl-local (0.768 at 1 layer) outperforms EGNN (4.214), FastEGNN (3.983), HEGNN (4.114), TFN (2.411), and MACE (2.403) at 1 layer, and remains competitive with Equiformer (0.805). On the completeness test, all _cpl variants reach 100% accuracy on both 2-body and 3-body tasks, while EGNN, SchNet, and TFN score only 50%, and MACE reaches 100% only at 4-body order. On Water-3D mini, EGNN_cpl-local achieves 2.495×10⁻⁴ MSE at 4 layers, outperforming EGNN (3.338), FastEGNN (3.259), and HEGNN (3.287). The key advantage is that Uni-EGNN achieves completeness and strong performance with fewer layers, reducing computational overhead while matching or exceeding deeper baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant strategy for achieving completeness in equivariant GNNs—stacking layers, increasing body order, or raising feature degree (as in TFN, MACE, and the universality proof of Dym & Maron)—by reframing the problem through a 'dynamic method' that separates the task into a complete scalar function (canonical form) and a full-rank steerable basis set. It builds directly on EGNN and TFN as base architectures, leverages FastEGNN's virtual-node mechanism to generate four non-coplanar reference points, and connects completeness to the geometric isomorphism problem (solvable in polynomial time via four-point positioning), in contrast to the quasipolynomial GWL-test framework. The conceptual lineage also draws on HEGNN's analysis of when high-degree representations degenerate on symmetric graphs, and on the scalarization trend (SO3KRATES, GotenNet) that questions whether tensor products are truly necessary.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean, theoretically grounded recipe for complete equivariant GNNs that requires only a single layer plus a canonical-form computation, potentially reducing the depth and body-order overhead that has made prior complete models expensive. Two important open questions remain: (1) constructing a full-rank basis set for symmetric graphs is unresolved (the authors conjecture scalar methods may be infeasible there), and (2) no empirical validation exists for l≥2 steerable objective functions, leaving the high-degree regime untested. The connection between completeness and polynomial-time geometric isomorphism also opens a path toward more efficient expressiveness guarantees beyond the GWL-test's quasipolynomial bound.
