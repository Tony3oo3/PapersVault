## Problem

While LayerNorm is a fundamental component in transformers for stabilizing training, its distinct impact on memorization versus generalization in Pre-LN versus Post-LN architectures remains poorly understood. Prior work hinted that LN may contribute to overfitting in Pre-LN models, but no systematic analysis existed to disentangle LN's role in learning stability from its role in label memorization across these two architectural variants.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard vision (CIFAR-10, NICO++, UTK-Face) and language (Emotions, News, TweetTopic) classification datasets, with 1% random label noise injected into the training set to induce memorization. Models are trained to 100% training accuracy, and the key experimental manipulation is removing LN's learnable parameters (weight w and bias b) while retaining the normalization operation N(x) intact. Outputs include four behavioral metrics—learning (test) accuracy, memorization score, recovery score, and random prediction score—alongside per-layer learning and memorization gradient norms (‖g_x^learn‖₂ and ‖g_x^mem‖₂) computed across all transformer layers.

## Method

The paper removes LN's learnable parameters during training across 13 transformer models (7 Pre-LN, 6 Post-LN) and 6 datasets, then compares behavioral metrics between the full and parameter-removed variants. The central finding is an architectural asymmetry: in Pre-LN models, LN is essential for stable learning (its removal destabilizes training and exacerbates overfitting), whereas in Post-LN models, LN governs memorization (its removal suppresses noisy-label memorization and recovers genuine labels without harming generalization). The authors further show that early-layer LNs are disproportionately critical in both settings, formalized through train-test accuracy gap inequalities. Theoretically, they prove (Theorem 1) that learning gradient norms dominate memorization gradient norms at every layer, and (Theorems 2–3) that gradient norm upper bounds decay monotonically from early to later layers, explaining why early LNs carry the strongest influence. The divergent Pre/Post-LN behavior is attributed to the ratio ‖g_x^learn‖₂/‖g_x^mem‖₂ being far larger in Pre-LN than Post-LN models, meaning LN removal in Pre-LN disproportionately disrupts the dominant learning signal, while in Post-LN it selectively suppresses the comparable memorization signal.

## Results

Across 13 transformer models (7 Pre-LN, 6 Post-LN) and 6 vision/language datasets, removing LN learnable parameters has fundamentally different effects depending on architecture. In Pre-LN models (e.g., Qwen2, DeiT, GPT-2), LN removal significantly disrupts test accuracy and widens the train-test overfitting gap, with memorization of noisy labels persisting throughout training. In Post-LN models (e.g., ELECTRA, DeBERTa, BERT), LN removal leaves learning accuracy essentially unchanged while substantially reducing memorization scores and increasing genuine-label recovery scores. The effect is strongest for early-layer LNs: removing early LN parameters produces the largest overfitting gap increase in Pre-LN models (Δ_overfit^Pre,early > Δ_overfit^Pre,middle > Δ_overfit^Pre,later) and the largest overfitting gap decrease in Post-LN models (Δ_overfit^Post,early < Δ_overfit^Post,middle < Δ_overfit^Post,later).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an analytical study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so there are no external baselines. The key internal comparison is between Pre-LN and Post-LN architectures under identical LN-parameter-removal conditions: Pre-LN models (Qwen2, GPT-2, GPT-Neo, ViT-B, ViT-S, DeiT, RoBERTa-PreLayerNorm) show learning degradation and persistent memorization, while Post-LN models (BERT, RoBERTa, DistilBERT, DeBERTa, ELECTRA, Longformer) maintain stable learning and suppress memorization. A second comparison isolates early vs. middle vs. later LN layers, showing that early-layer LN removal is consistently the most impactful in both architectures. A third comparison uses gradient norm analysis (‖g_x^learn‖₂ vs. ‖g_x^mem‖₂), revealing that the learning-to-memorization gradient ratio is significantly higher in Pre-LN than Post-LN models (Eq. 5), which theoretically explains why LN removal disrupts learning in Pre-LN but only suppresses memorization in Post-LN. The paper also contrasts its findings with prior work by Xu et al. [2019], which suggested LN contributes to overfitting in Pre-LN models; this paper refines that claim by showing LN in Pre-LN is critical for learning stability rather than directly driving memorization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-studied threads: (1) the role of LayerNorm in transformer training stability (Xiong et al. 2020, Liu et al. 2020) and (2) the localization of memorization to specific transformer components such as attention heads and FFNs (Haviv et al. 2023, Geva et al. 2023, Maini et al. 2023). While Xu et al. (2019) hinted that LN parameters might contribute to overfitting in Pre-LN models, this paper departs from that suggestion by showing the opposite: LN removal in Pre-LN actually destabilizes learning and worsens overfitting. The gradient-norm analysis builds on the singular-value bounding techniques from Xiong et al. (2020) but applies them to a new comparison—learning vs. memorization gradients—rather than training stability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LN learnable parameters serve fundamentally different roles in Pre-LN (learning stability) versus Post-LN (memorization control) architectures provides a principled explanation for why these two configurations behave differently beyond just gradient flow. Practically, the observation that removing early-layer LN parameters in Post-LN models suppresses memorization while preserving generalization suggests a simple, parameter-free intervention for reducing overfitting on noisy data. Limitations include the use of only 1% label noise, the absence of Post-LN vision models (a practical constraint of the architecture), and the somewhat idealized 'recovery' metric. Open questions include whether these findings extend to RMSNorm or GroupNorm, whether the effect persists at LLM scale, and whether the gradient-norm bounds hold under more realistic (non-100%-training-accuracy) stopping criteria.
