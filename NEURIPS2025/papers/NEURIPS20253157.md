## Problem

Conditional diffusion models are mathematically formulated around an idealized denoising process, yet no prior work had rigorously measured or explained the extent to which trained models actually deviate from this idealization. The paper asks whether this deviation is a fundamental inductive bias of conditional diffusion rather than a training artifact, and whether it can be explained by the smoothness properties of neural networks with respect to the conditioning variable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253157/cropped_page2_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained conditional diffusion model (U-Net) and a conditioning variable z — a 2D t-SNE embedding for MNIST/Fashion-MNIST or a 2D observation for maze path planning — and produces a Schedule Deviation score as a function of z and diffusion time s. Unusually, Schedule Deviation is evaluated on the forward diffusion path of the model's own generated distribution (not the reverse process), requiring only access to the learned flow field and an empirical estimate of the IMCF score, without needing the true data distribution or ground-truth score function.

## Method

The paper introduces Schedule Deviation (SD), a metric defined as the L1 norm of the instantaneous difference between the time derivative of a tangent process (evolved under the learned flow from the IMCF initial condition) and the time derivative of the ideal model-consistent flow path. Via the transport equation, SD reduces to an expectation over the IMCF path of the divergence of the velocity difference plus the velocity difference dotted with the IMCF score, making it efficiently estimable. Theoretically, the paper proves that an inductive bias toward smoothness in the conditioning variable z causes the learned flow to perform 'self-guidance' — linearly combining denoising flows from nearby conditioning points — which cannot be expressed as a valid denoising flow and thus necessarily induces schedule deviation. This is formalized via cubic spline interpolation for discrete conditioning support (Theorem 2) and Fourier-convolution smoothing for continuous uniform conditioning (Theorem 3), and empirically validated across MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, Celeb-A, and maze trajectory generation, showing SD predicts Earth Mover's Distance between DDPM and DDIM samples.

## Results

The paper's central empirical finding is that Schedule Deviation (SD) is strongly correlated with the Earth Mover's Distance (1-Wasserstein) between samples generated by DDPM and DDIM across all three datasets (MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, maze paths), as shown in scatter plots (Figure 2) and structural heatmaps (Figures 3–4). SD persists across model capacities (up to 13.3M parameters) and training set sizes (10K–60K samples), and even increases with more training data in some cases. SD varies dramatically across conditioning values that are equally represented in the training set, indicating it is driven by the geometric structure of the conditional distributions rather than data scarcity. The paper does not report a single headline accuracy or FID number; instead, the key quantitative claim is the predictive correlation between SD and inter-sampler EMD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new generative method to be benchmarked against baselines; rather, it introduces Schedule Deviation as a diagnostic metric and evaluates its predictive power against the divergence of existing sampling algorithms. DDPM (Ho et al., 2020) and DDIM (Song et al., 2020a) are the two primary samplers compared: although they are mathematically equivalent in the limit of zero discretization error, the paper shows they produce measurably different sample distributions (quantified by 1-Wasserstein/EMD) precisely at conditioning values where SD is high. The Gradient-Estimation (GE) sampler (Permenter and Yuan, 2023) is additionally evaluated in the appendix, and SD is shown to be predictive of its divergence from DDPM as well. The key outcome is that SD acts as a leading indicator: high SD regions in the conditioning space correspond to regions where DDPM and DDIM disagree, while low SD regions show near-perfect sampler agreement. This positions SD not as a competing method but as an explanatory tool that unifies previously observed task-specific performance gaps between samplers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the stochastic interpolants framework (Albergo et al., 2023) and flow matching (Lipman et al., 2022) to formalize conditional diffusion models, and uses the DDPM denoising objective (Ho et al., 2020) to define the ideal model-consistent flow (IMCF) as a reference. It departs from concurrent work on non-denoising behavior (Vastola, 2025; Bertrand et al., 2025), which measures deviation from the true data denoiser, by instead studying self-consistency: whether a model's flow is consistent with the denoising path of its own generated distribution. The paper also extends the smoothness-bias analysis from unconditional closed-form diffusion models (Scarvelis et al., 2023; Aithal et al., 2024) to the conditional setting, introducing 'self-guidance' as a natural analogue of classifier-free guidance that arises purely from smoothness inductive biases over the conditioning variable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that conditional diffusion models inherently deviate from idealized denoising—and that this cannot be resolved by more data or model capacity—challenges a foundational assumption underlying the equivalence of SDE/ODE samplers and has direct implications for designing future sampling, distillation, and evaluation methods. The Schedule Deviation metric provides a practical diagnostic tool that predicts sampler disagreement (DDPM vs. DDIM) without requiring access to ground-truth scores or training data. Key limitations include the computational cost of computing flow divergence, the relatively small experimental scale (MNIST/Fashion-MNIST with 2D t-SNE conditioning), and the fact that the paper is purely diagnostic—it does not propose a fix for schedule deviation. Open questions include whether non-denoising behavior is beneficial or harmful for sample quality, and how to properly account for this in distillation and few-step sampling methods.
