## Problem

Existing backdoor detection methods rely on statistical analyses (gradient-based, posterior, activation clustering) that are tightly coupled to specific learning paradigms and often require a clean reference model or known attack patterns, making them unreliable across supervised, self-supervised, and autoregressive settings. No prior unified framework can detect backdoors in multimodal large language models such as LLaVA or MiniGPT-4, leaving a critical security gap in outsourced third-party training pipelines.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two independently trained models (from different third-party providers) trained on the same dataset, a small clean subset D_s of 1000 samples for trigger optimization, and a fine-tuning subset D_ft (~10% of clean data) for sensitivity analysis. It outputs a binary verdict (clean or backdoored) per model plus a recovered spatial trigger parameterized as a pattern-mask pair (p ∈ R^{H×W×C}, m ∈ [0,1]^{H×W×C}). The output distribution loss is paradigm-specific: cross-entropy for SL, feature similarity for SSL, and autoregressive reconstruction for AL, so the same trigger-recovery pipeline adapts to each learning objective without architectural changes.

## Method

Lie Detector is a three-stage cross-examination framework that exploits behavioral inconsistencies between two independently trained models. Stage I (Cross-Model Trigger Reverse) jointly optimizes a trigger mask and pattern by minimizing a composite loss combining CKA-based representation divergence (Eq. 6, capturing architecture-invariant feature-space misalignment) and a paradigm-specific output distribution loss (Eq. 5), regularized by L1 sparsity. Stage II (Activation-Based Identification) computes the attack success rate on the recovered trigger and flags models exceeding threshold η. Stage III (Fine-tuning Sensitivity Analysis) fine-tunes the flagged model on a small clean subset and confirms the backdoor only if the ASR drops by more than γ, distinguishing genuine backdoor dependencies from benign adversarial perturbations. The key differentiator is the use of CKA as an architecture- and paradigm-agnostic similarity metric for trigger optimization, combined with the fine-tuning sensitivity test to suppress false positives, enabling a single unified pipeline across SL, SSL, and AL without task-specific redesign.

## Results

On supervised learning (ResNet-18), Lie Detector achieves 100% Detection Success Rate (DSR) with 0–5% False Positive Rate (FPR) across all four attacks (BadNets, Blended, ISSBA, Low-Frequency) on both CIFAR-10 and TinyImageNet, yielding an average 4.4% DSR gain over the next-best method. On self-supervised learning (CLIP), it maintains 90–100% DSR with ≤5% FPR, a 1.7% average gain over baselines. On autoregressive learning (LLaVA), it reaches 90–95% DSR with ≤10% FPR, a 10.6% average gain — e.g., 95% DSR on COCO/TrojanVLM where MM-BD achieves only 15% and DECREE only 60%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Lie Detector outperforms all seven baselines across every attack and dataset. Against the strongest SL baselines, UNICORN (90–100% DSR) and MM-BD (92.5–100% DSR), Lie Detector matches or exceeds DSR while consistently achieving lower FPR (0–5% vs. 5–25%). Early methods (NC, ABS, NAD) collapse under stealthy attacks: NC drops to 10% DSR on CIFAR-10/Low-Frequency with 55% FPR, and ABS reaches only 25% DSR on the same setting. On SSL (CLIP), DECREE leads among baselines (87.5% DSR on Caltech-101/BadNets) but Lie Detector matches it at 100% with 0% FPR, and both outperform MM-BD (75%) and SEER (100% but only on Caltech-101). The largest margin appears on autoregressive learning (LLaVA): Lie Detector achieves 95% DSR on COCO/TrojanVLM versus 15% for MM-BD, 60% for DECREE, and 80% for SEER — a 10.6% average gain over the best baseline. SEER is the only baseline applicable to AL and performs competitively on CLIP (87.5–100% DSR) but still trails Lie Detector by 2.5–5% DSR on several SSL settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant single-model statistical analysis paradigm (Neural Cleanse, ABS, NAD, UNICORN, MM-BD, DECREE) by introducing a cross-examination framework that compares two independently trained models from different providers, exploiting their behavioral inconsistencies rather than relying on a clean reference or predefined attack priors. It repurposes Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA), originally developed for measuring representational similarity in neural networks (Kornblith et al.), as a trigger-optimization loss that captures architecture-agnostic representation-level backdoor signatures. The fine-tuning sensitivity analysis draws on the observation that backdoor dependencies are fragile under clean-data fine-tuning, extending the NAD-style distillation idea into a detection confirmation step rather than a defense. The unified treatment across supervised, self-supervised, and autoregressive paradigms generalizes prior methods that were largely confined to classification or single-paradigm settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The untrusted third-party training setting is increasingly relevant as institutions outsource model training to cloud providers, and this paper provides the first unified detection framework spanning SL, SSL, and AL paradigms, including the first effective backdoor detection in multimodal LLMs (LLaVA, MiniGPT-4) where prior methods fell below 50% DSR. Key limitations include the requirement for two independently trained models (impractical in some settings), vulnerability to collusion attacks (DSR drops to ~70% under strong collusion), and partial degradation under adaptive attacks that explicitly suppress CKA divergence. Open questions include scaling to very large models, handling fully black-box settings without any clean reference, and whether the cross-examination assumption holds in federated or single-provider deployments.
