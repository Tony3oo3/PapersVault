## Problem

Standard RLHF maximizes expected reward, improving average performance but leaving rare, high-impact tail failures—harmful conversations, insecure code—insufficiently controlled. No existing alignment method provides a principled mechanism for users to specify exactly what fraction of outputs must meet a given safety or quality threshold, i.e., to constrain specific quantiles of the reward distribution rather than just its mean.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255548/cropped_page6_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pretrained reference model p₀, one or more scalar reward models (e.g., GPT-2 value heads for harmlessness/helpfulness, GPT-4o for code security/simplicity), and user-specified quantile pairs (τⱼ, κⱼ) that encode lifting the τⱼ-quantile of the aligned distribution to the κⱼ-quantile level of the reference. It produces a finite-dimensional dual multiplier vector λ* (one nonnegative scalar per constraint) and a composite indicator-based reward R(x,y) = Σⱼ λⱼ*·(τⱼ − I{r(x,y) < cⱼ}), which is then fed into a standard PPO solver to yield the aligned model. The Monte Carlo dual-estimation step requires only forward passes under p₀, with runtime scaling as O(nm) where n is the sample count and m the number of constraints, and the same sample set is reusable across different quantile targets.

## Method

The core reformulation converts each quantile constraint Q_{τ,p}(r) ≥ c into the linear inequality E_p[τ − I{r < c}] ≥ 0, turning the overall problem into a convex KL-regularized optimization over the distribution space. By Lagrangian duality, the optimal aligned distribution takes the exponential-family form p(y|x) ∝ p₀(y|x)·exp{Σⱼ λⱼ·ρ_{τⱼ,κⱼ}(r(x,y))}, where the dual multipliers λⱼ ≥ 0 are found by maximizing a concave log-partition function estimated via Monte Carlo sampling from p₀. This reduces the infinite-dimensional distribution optimization to a finite-dimensional concave problem whose dimension equals the number of quantile constraints, solvable by projected gradient ascent. Once λ* is obtained, the composite reward R(x,y) = Σⱼ λⱼ*·ρ_{τⱼ,κⱼ}(r(x,y)) is plugged directly into standard RLHF tooling (PPO via TRL) with inverse temperature β=1, requiring no architectural changes to the training pipeline. The framework generalizes to multiple reward functions simultaneously (e.g., harmlessness + helpfulness, or simplicity + security) and to continuous quantile constraints over all τ ∈ [0,1], with a first-order sensitivity analysis (Theorem 5.1) characterizing how small perturbations in target quantile functions propagate through the dual variables via a linear integral operator V.

## Results

The paper evaluates QA on conversational (OPT-1.3B, HH-RLHF) and code-generation (CodeGen-350M, HumanEval + 200 custom prompts) tasks. In Experiment 1, enforcing nine quantile-lifting constraints on Helpfulness (e.g., 1%→5%, 10%→50%, 95%→99%) shifts the entire Helpfulness QQ curve above the 45-degree line, while Harmlessness shows a measurable tradeoff decline. Baseline metrics (Diversity, Coherence) remain largely stable, and Perplexity improves slightly. In the code-generation appendix, aligning Simplicity (1%/5%/10%→60%, 50%→70%, 90%→99%) lifts lower quantiles while Security also improves marginally without explicit constraints, and vice versa when aligning Security, suggesting synergy rather than strict tradeoff.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary quantitative comparison is against MORL (Barrett & Narayanan 2008; Li et al. 2020; Wu et al. 2024) in Experiment 3 (Figure 3), where MORL uses randomly sampled tradeoff weights λ = s·u with s ~ U(0,6) and u from the probability simplex. At τ = 5%, 10%, 25%, and 75% for both Helpfulness and Harmlessness, the QA-aligned model consistently moves toward the upper-right quadrant (simultaneous improvement in both values relative to the original model), whereas MORL's 20 randomly sampled weight vectors scatter across the plot, frequently sacrificing one objective for the other. The paper also implicitly compares against the unaligned reference model p₀ across all experiments: QA lifts targeted quantiles (e.g., the worst 5% of harmful outputs) while preserving overall distribution shape, something standard RLHF's mean-reward maximization does not guarantee. No head-to-head numerical table is provided; the comparison is visual (QQ plots) and qualitative, with the authors noting that some Helpfulness degradation in Experiment 2 may stem from Monte Carlo approximation error and PPO stochasticity rather than a fundamental limitation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the standard RLHF framework (Christiano et al. 2017; Ouyang et al. 2022) by replacing the scalar expected-reward objective with quantile constraints on the reward distribution, formulated as a convex KL-regularized program. It is closely related to MAP (Wang et al. 2025), which constrains expected values of multiple reward functions, and to MORL approaches (Barrett & Narayanan 2008; Li et al. 2020) that use linear scalarization of reward signals; QA departs from both by operating on distributional quantiles via indicator-based rewards rather than means or weighted sums. The key theoretical departure is showing that the resulting Lagrangian dual is finite-dimensional (one multiplier per quantile constraint) and that the optimal policy retains the exponential-family reweighting form of standard RLHF, making it a drop-in replacement within existing PPO/TRL pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

QA addresses a concrete gap in current alignment practice: standard RLHF leaves rare but catastrophic tail events (harmful outputs, insecure code) insufficiently controlled, and the quantile-constraint formulation gives practitioners a direct, interpretable knob ('99% of outputs must exceed threshold c') rather than an opaque temperature parameter. However, empirical validation is limited to small models (OPT-1.3B, CodeGen-350M) on a single A100, and the paper acknowledges that Monte Carlo variance for aggressive tail targets (e.g., 0.1% quantiles) can be large. Open questions include whether the approach scales to frontier-scale models, how to handle infeasible constraint sets gracefully, and whether the observed Helpfulness–Harmlessness tradeoff can be mitigated through richer constraint specifications.
