## Problem

Offline multi-agent reinforcement learning faces two compounding challenges in many-agent settings: extrapolation error, which grows exponentially with the joint action space, and miscoordination, where agents cannot actively interact to develop compatible policies. Prior offline MARL methods addressed these issues but were typically validated only on small agent populations, leaving open whether they can scale to dozens of agents with long-horizon temporal dependencies.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255467/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Oryx consumes sequences of per-agent local observations (e.g., 3×3 grid views, previous actions) and the actions of previously-ordered agents within the same timestep, processing them through a retention-based encoder over a fixed-length temporal window (default 20 steps). The dual-head decoder simultaneously outputs a categorical action distribution for each agent and a scalar Q-value estimate for the partial joint action, enabling both policy improvement and advantage computation in a single forward pass. The autoregressive factorisation means agent j's output is conditioned on the actions of agents 1 through j−1, with the agent ordering drawn randomly each training step.

## Method

Oryx integrates Sable's retention-based sequence model (which replaces softmax attention with a decaying matrix component for linear-time long-range dependency modelling) with a novel sequential autoregressive ICQ loss derived via the advantage decomposition theorem. The key theoretical result (Theorem 1) shows that the joint-policy ICQ objective decomposes into a sum of per-agent losses, each optimised sequentially over a random agent permutation, with the normalising partition function factorising across agents. A dual-decoder architecture produces both policy logits and Q-values, enabling a counterfactual advantage baseline that removes the (n−1) factor in the gradient variance bound. The critic is updated with implicit importance weights sampled from the behaviour policy (SARSA-like), while the policy is updated with a KL-constrained objective that keeps the learned policy close to the dataset distribution, jointly addressing extrapolation error and miscoordination in a single end-to-end training loop.

## Results

Oryx achieves state-of-the-art performance on more than 80% of 65 evaluated datasets across SMAC (34/43, 79%), Multi-Agent MuJoCo (14/16), and RWARE (6/6, with up to ~20% improvement over prior SOTA). On the Connector many-agent benchmark, Oryx maintains near-expert normalised scores from 23 to 50 agents, while MAICQ degrades to only 25% of expert performance at 50 agents. On the T-Maze long-horizon coordination task, Oryx achieves 0.99±0.01 (replay) and 0.94±0.03 (expert) episode return, while all baselines (I-ICQ, MAICQ) score 0.0. In the architecture ablation (Table 1), Oryx outperforms MAT+ICQ on median, IQM, mean, and optimality gap across both SMAC and RWARE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MAICQ (Yang et al., 2021), Oryx wins decisively: on T-Maze, MAICQ scores 0.0 while Oryx reaches 0.99; on Connector at 50 agents, Oryx achieves near-expert normalised performance whereas MAICQ plateaus at ~25% of expert. Against the prior offline MARL SOTA methods (Matsunaga et al. 2023, Shao et al. 2023, Bui et al. 2025, Li et al. 2025), Oryx matches or surpasses the best reported score on 34/43 SMAC datasets, 14/16 MAMuJoCo datasets, and all 6 RWARE datasets (with up to ~20% absolute improvement on RWARE). In the architecture ablation, Oryx (Sable backbone) outperforms MAT+ICQ on every aggregated metric: e.g., on SMAC the median rises from 0.71 to 0.91 and the optimality gap drops from 0.38 to 0.23. On the smaller Connector scenarios (3–23 agents), Oryx, I-ICQ, MADT, and IQL-CQL perform comparably, but Oryx pulls ahead as agent count increases, while MADT becomes infeasible beyond 23 agents due to memory constraints.

## Relation to Prior Work

Oryx sits at the intersection of two previously separate lines of work: retention-based sequence models for MARL (Sable, MAT) and offline MARL with distributional constraints (ICQ, CQL-based methods, distributional correction). The key conceptual move is deriving a sequential, autoregressive form of ICQ via the advantage decomposition theorem, which had not been done before—prior offline MARL methods (MAICQ, CQL-MARL, ALBERDICE) all used non-autoregressive joint policies, while prior sequence models (MAT, Sable) were exclusively online. The paper also departs from the standard CTDE value-decomposition paradigm (QMixer, FACMAC) by replacing it with a counterfactual advantage computed directly from the autoregressive Q-head, and it replaces MAT's transformer backbone with Sable's more scalable retention mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses the two core failure modes of offline MARL—extrapolation error and miscoordination—in a single framework that scales to 50 agents, a regime where prior methods (MAICQ, CQL variants) degrade sharply. The new Connector datasets (up to 50 agents) and the T-Maze diagnostic environment are useful community resources for stress-testing many-agent offline coordination. However, the evaluation remains confined to cooperative grid-world and MuJoCo benchmarks; the authors acknowledge that real-world industrial settings (logistics, traffic, rail) remain untested, and the method is limited to cooperative (shared-reward) DecPOMDPs. An open question is whether the sequential ICQ update and retention architecture transfer to competitive or mixed-motive settings, and whether hybrid offline-to-online fine-tuning can close the remaining gap to fully online performance.
