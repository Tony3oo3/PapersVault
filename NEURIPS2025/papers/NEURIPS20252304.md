## Problem

Existing multimodal multitask learning approaches assume fixed fusion strategies, complete modality availability, and uniform parameter sharing across all samples. In clinical settings, modality informativeness and task correlation vary substantially per patient—some samples have rich textual notes while others rely on structured scores, and depression-anxiety co-occurrence is not uniform. This paper addresses the gap by learning a per-sample routing policy that dynamically selects both the modality processing pathway and the task-sharing strategy.

## Input / Output

The model consumes two input modalities per sample: structured numerical features (e.g., PHQ-9, GAD-7 questionnaire scores) and unstructured clinician notes. A key design detail is the bidirectional transformation layer: numerical features can be textualized into natural language (via a fine-tuned LLM) and text can be embedded into fixed-dimensional vectors (via BERT/MPNet), enabling four distinct modality paths (text-only, numeric-only, textualized-numeric+text, numeric+text-embedding). For each sample, the model outputs heteroscedastic predictions (mean and log-variance) for both depression and anxiety severity, along with a joint probability distribution over eight modality-task expert combinations that serves as an interpretable routing decision.

## Method

The architecture is a hierarchical mixture-of-experts with eight expert pathways formed by the Cartesian product of four modality configurations (T1, T2, N1, N2) and two task strategies (single-task learning with independent heads, or multitask learning with a shared encoder). A two-stage probabilistic router—first a modality gating network over the four input representations, then a task gating network over STL/MTL for each modality path—assigns a soft probability distribution to each expert, and the final prediction is a weighted mixture over all eight experts. Training is end-to-end with a heteroscedastic loss (each expert predicts both the outcome mean and its input-dependent variance) plus an entropy regularization term to prevent expert collapse. The paper supports both soft routing (all experts evaluated, gradients flow through all paths) and hard routing (single expert selected via Gumbel-Softmax reparameterization), with the latter enabling near-fixed-cost inference while retaining the flexibility learned during soft-routing training.

## Results

On the Mental Health in Healthcare Workers dataset, the soft-routing model achieves the best RMSE of 3.62 on PHQ-9 and 3.34 on GAD-7, outperforming the best fixed modality path (T2: 3.66/3.42) and the heteroscedastic MTL baseline (3.85/3.52). Across three generalization datasets (Yelp, RateMyProfessor, Customer Satisfaction), soft routing achieves R² of 0.24/0.23, 0.29/0.27, and 0.32/0.31 respectively, while removing routing entirely causes a 22–26% relative drop in R². Hard routing incurs only a 1.6–2.3% degradation versus soft routing, confirming near-equivalent performance at 1× computational cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its adaptive routing model against four categories of baselines: (1) fixed modality paths (T1, N1, T2, N2), (2) fixed training objectives (STL, MTL, Heteroscedastic MTL), and (3) routing variants (soft vs. hard). The soft-routing model wins on both PHQ-9 and GAD-7 against every fixed-path and fixed-objective baseline, with the largest margin over text-only T1 (4.60→3.62 on PHQ-9, a 21% RMSE reduction) and over standard STL (4.28→3.62). Among modality paths, T2 (textualized numeric + text) is the strongest single path, but routing still improves upon it by 1.1% on PHQ-9 and 2.3% on GAD-7, indicating that per-sample adaptation adds value even when the best fixed path is known. On the generalization datasets, removing routing entirely (non-routing ensemble) degrades R² by 22–26%, while hard routing loses only 1.6–2.3% relative to soft routing, showing the routing mechanism is the primary source of the performance gain rather than the ensemble structure itself.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of adaptive computation—modality fusion (Sahu & Vechtomova, 2021; Xue & Marculescu, 2023) and task-relationship modeling via MMoE (Ma et al., 2018)—into a single hierarchical routing architecture that selects both the input representation pathway and the supervision structure per sample. The routing mechanism draws on Gumbel-Softmax (Jang et al., 2016) for differentiable discrete expert selection and on routing networks (Rosenbaum et al., 2017) for input-dependent path activation, while the heteroscedastic loss follows Kendall et al. (2018). The key departure from prior MoE work is the two-stage factorization of routing decisions (modality first, then task) and the bidirectional num↔text transformations that create four distinct input representations rather than a single fused embedding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a principled way to handle the heterogeneity that is endemic to clinical data—varying modality availability, differing task correlations across patients, and inconsistent data quality—without requiring separate models for every data configuration. However, the 'real-world' evaluation relies on a hybrid corpus augmented with LLM-generated synthetic encounters, and the generalization experiments use small, non-clinical datasets (Yelp, RateMyProfessor), leaving open whether the routing gains persist on large, untouched clinical cohorts. A key open question is whether the 8× computational overhead of soft routing can be amortized in deployment settings, and whether the routing policy remains stable under distribution shift across sites or clinician populations.
