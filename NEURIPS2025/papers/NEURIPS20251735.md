## Problem

Existing NLP approaches to patent evaluation treat patentability as a coarse prediction task (e.g., forecasting grant outcomes via similarity metrics or classifiers on historical labels), overlooking the step-by-step, claim-level evaluations and written rationales that patent examiners actually produce in office action documents. This makes it difficult to measure where LLMs succeed or fail in the real examination workflow, particularly in assessing novelty (§102) and non-obviousness (§103) against specific prior art.

## Input / Output

Each benchmark instance operates at the granularity of a single patent claim. PAR4PC presents a target claim alongside eight candidate prior-art documents (each with title, abstract, and claims) and requires selecting the one or more documents the examiner actually cited; PI4PC supplies a claim plus five candidate paragraphs from a single cited reference and asks for the paragraph number most pertinent to the comparison; NOC4PC provides the claim together with the cited prior art and the examiner's referenced paragraphs, requiring a three-way classification (rejected under §102, rejected under §103, or allowed). The underlying dataset records also carry full specification text, PDF drawings, and structured JSON parsed from Non-Final Rejection documents containing per-claim rejection status, legal basis codes, cited patent numbers, paragraph indices, figure references, and free-text rationales.

## Method

The paper constructs PANORAMA from 8,143 authentic USPTO examination records (2015–2024) by collecting Non-Final Rejection documents via the USPTO OA Text Retrieval API, extracting cited patents with regular expressions and the patent_client library, and gathering application metadata through the ODP Beta API. Non-Final Rejection documents are parsed into claim-level structured JSON using GPT-4o, extracting rejection status, section codes (§101/§102/§103/§112), cited prior art with specific paragraph and figure references, and the examiner's written rationale; a systematic refinement pass and validation by seven patent professionals (92.5% accuracy, Fleiss's κ = 0.751) ensure quality. Three sequential benchmark tasks—PAR4PC (2,896 instances), PI4PC (3,402 instances), and NOC4PC (2,884 instances)—decompose the examination workflow following Schmitt et al.'s framework, each framed as a multiple-choice or classification problem with gold/silver/negative answer tiers. Baselines evaluate 12 LLMs (proprietary, open-source, and reasoning models) under zero-shot and chain-of-thought prompting, and supervised fine-tuning on the PANORAMA training split is applied to three 7–8B open-source models, yielding substantial gains (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B improving from 13.45 to 77.98 on PAR4PC).

## Results

Across the three PANORAMA benchmark tasks, LLMs show a clear capability gradient. On PAR4PC (prior-art retrieval), the best CoT score is 77.30 (Gemma-3-12B-Instruct) and the best zero-shot is 68.94 (Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct), well above the 5.63 random baseline. On PI4PC (paragraph identification), GPT-4o leads with 63.33 (zero-shot) and 62.62 (CoT), a 56% gain over the 27.10 random baseline. On NOC4PC (novelty/non-obviousness classification), performance is critically weak: the best model (Claude-3.7 Sonnet) reaches only 45.40 with CoT, barely above the 32.33 three-way random baseline, and most models score at or below chance level.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o dominates PI4PC (63.33 zero-shot, 62.62 CoT) but performs near-random on NOC4PC (34.69 zero-shot, 32.19 CoT vs. 32.33 baseline), showing it can locate relevant paragraphs yet cannot judge patentability. Claude-3.7 Sonnet is the strongest on NOC4PC (45.40 CoT), the only model to clearly exceed the random baseline on the hardest task, but trails GPT-4o on PI4PC (60.55 vs. 62.62 CoT). Among open-source models, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct leads PAR4PC zero-shot (68.94) while Gemma-3-12B-Instruct achieves the best CoT score on PAR4PC (77.30); however, both collapse on NOC4PC (26.88 and 32.54 zero-shot respectively). Smaller 7–8B models (Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, EXAONE-3.5-7.8B) are substantially weaker, with Llama-3.1-8B scoring 0.00 on PI4PC CoT and EXAONE-3.5-7.8B scoring 0.00 on PAR4PC zero-shot. The two reasoning models (QWQ-32B, EXAONE-Deep-32B) do not close the gap on NOC4PC (34.73 and 21.23 CoT), confirming that extended reasoning alone does not resolve the patentability-judgment bottleneck. Supervised fine-tuning on PANORAMA yields large gains (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B jumps from 15.71 to 49.39 on NOC4PC), but even post-SFT scores remain well below human-examiner levels.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the patentability assessment framework of Schmitt et al. [40] but departs fundamentally from prior NLP approaches that treated patent examination as an application-level prediction task (grant outcomes, novelty scores). Where previous work (e.g., Ikoma & Mitamura [15], Jiang et al. [18]) operated at the application level or used high-level proxies like similarity metrics, PANORAMA introduces claim-level sequential tasks (PAR4PC, PI4PC, NOC4PC) that decompose the actual examiner workflow into measurable steps. The dataset itself is novel in preserving full decision trails—Non-Final Rejections with structured rationales, cited prior art, and Notices of Allowance—rather than just final outcomes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PANORAMA provides the first standardized, claim-level evaluation framework for LLMs in patent examination, revealing that even top proprietary models (GPT-4o, Claude-3.7) barely exceed random chance on the core novelty/non-obviousness judgment task (NOC4PC). This negative result is important because it shows that current LLMs lack the nuanced comparative reasoning that patent examiners perform, and that supervised fine-tuning on examination rationales yields substantial gains (e.g., +34 points for Llama on NOC4PC), suggesting the bottleneck is task-specific training data rather than model capacity. Key open questions include whether benchmark scores predict real-world examination performance, how to scale beyond USPTO's 8K applications, and how to incorporate rationales for allowed claims to enable CoT training.
