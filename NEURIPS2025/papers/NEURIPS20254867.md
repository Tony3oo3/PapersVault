## Problem

Scaling inference-time compute via multiple chain-of-thought samples improves LLM decision accuracy but can amplify unfair bias by surfacing stereotyped reasoning, and existing fairness interventions (prompting, fine-tuning) are brittle or lack flexible control over the fairness–accuracy trade-off. No prior method provides a generalizable, step-level mechanism to identify and down-weight biased reasoning without altering the model's internal generation process.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254867/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the system takes a decision-making prompt and N (e.g., 32) independently sampled CoT reasoning chains from a base LLM, segmented into atomic reasoning steps. The FRM (a LLaMA-3.2-1B-Instruct model) assigns a real-valued fairness score to each step; chain-level scores are the mean of step scores, and the final decision is produced by a temperature-controlled softmax-weighted vote across all chains. The temperature τ (typically 0.2) governs the trade-off: τ→∞ recovers uniform majority voting, while τ→0 selects only the fairest chain.

## Method

The core contribution is a Fairness Reward Model trained as a process reward model that scores individual reasoning steps for reliance on protected-attribute stereotypes. Training uses weakly supervised binary labels from GPT-4o-mini over 255,000 reasoning steps generated from ~4,400 BBQ questions using four LLaMA models of varying sizes, optimized with binary cross-entropy loss in a PPO-style procedure from a LLaMA-3.2-1B-Instruct checkpoint. At inference, the FRM scores every step in all sampled chains, and a temperature-controlled softmax converts chain-level mean scores into aggregation weights for a weighted vote over candidate answers. The key design choices—step-level (process) granularity rather than chain-level (outcome) supervision, and LLM weak labels rather than ground-truth fairness annotations—enable a single model to transfer across tasks (recidivism, moderation, hiring), protected attributes (race, religion, gender, orientation), and unseen reasoning models (e.g., Mistral) without any additional fine-tuning.

## Results

On COMPAS (recidivism prediction), the FRM reduces the TPR gap from 15.52 (CoT@1) to 7.70 and the FPR gap from 12.40 to 7.60, a 50.4% and 38.7% reduction respectively, while maintaining accuracy (61.16% vs. 59.09% for CoT@1). On Civil Comments, the equalized odds gap for religion-based disparities (exceeding 60 percentage points under baselines) shrinks by more than 10 points, and accuracy actually increases by ~4 percentage points. On Bias in Bios, gender gaps narrow by over 20%, and when applied to Mistral-7B, the FRM reduces gender disparities by ~33% while improving accuracy by more than 10% on COMPAS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The FRM consistently outperforms all three baselines on fairness metrics across all three downstream tasks. Against CoT@1, the FRM reduces TPR and FPR gaps by 39–50% on COMPAS and by up to 40% on Civil Comments, while matching or exceeding accuracy. Against Maj@32 (majority voting over 32 chains), the FRM narrows fairness gaps further (e.g., TPR gap drops from 12.81 to 7.70 on COMPAS) and, notably, majority voting alone can actually worsen disparities (e.g., equalized odds in Civil Comments-Religion), confirming that ensembling more chains does not neutralize bias. Against Fairness Prompting, the FRM achieves comparable or better fairness while avoiding the substantial accuracy loss that prompting incurs; the paper notes that fairness prompting 'produces substantial loss of accuracy.' The FRM also generalizes to an unseen reasoning model (Mistral-7B), where it improves accuracy by >10% on COMPAS while reducing fairness gaps, a result no baseline achieves.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the process reward model (PRM) paradigm from Lightman et al. (ICLR 2024) and the weakly-supervised training recipe of Math-Shepherd (Wang et al., ACL 2024) to a new objective: scoring individual reasoning steps for fairness rather than correctness. It departs from prior fairness interventions—prompting-based approaches (Tamkin et al., 2023) and fine-tuning methods—by intervening only at the aggregation stage, replacing the uniform self-consistency weights of Wang et al. (ICLR 2023) with a temperature-controlled softmax over FRM scores. The key conceptual departure is that no ground-truth fairness label exists for arbitrary reasoning steps, so the authors bootstrap supervision from an LLM judge (GPT-4o-mini) on BBQ questions, a strategy not previously applied to process-level fairness scoring.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstration that a single FRM trained once on BBQ generalizes across three disparate decision domains (recidivism, content moderation, job screening) and to an unseen model family (Mistral) without fine-tuning is practically significant for organizations deploying LLMs in high-stakes settings where bespoke fairness tuning per domain is infeasible. The temperature parameter offers a transparent, post-hoc fairness/accuracy dial that prompting-based methods lack. Open questions include whether the FRM can detect implicit or statistical biases (it is most sensitive to explicit stereotype invocation), how it generalizes beyond English and US-centric contexts, and whether equal step-weighting can be improved to account for causal importance of individual reasoning steps to the final decision.
