## Problem

Conditional hypothesis testing—deciding whether two phenomena share the same input-output relationship at specific covariates—lacks finite-sample guarantees at arbitrary points, and existing methods either detect only global mismatches, require i.i.d. data, or provide guarantees only in the infinite-sample limit. No prior work formalizes guarantees on the covariate rejection region (the set of points where the null is rejected), which is essential for identifying *where* conditional distributions differ rather than merely *whether* they differ globally.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two data sets of transition pairs (covariate, output) that may be sequentially generated (non-i.i.d.), along with a kernel K (possibly operator-valued and non-trace-class), a regularization parameter λ, and a covariate region of interest S. For testing functionals beyond expectations (e.g., full distributions, moments), an additional representation map Φ_F or kernel κ is specified, which embeds the measurement space into a Hilbert space G. The output is a covariate rejection region χ(D₁,D₂) ⊆ S—a subset of the input space where H₀ is rejected—rather than a single global binary decision, enabling pointwise identification of discrepancies. Confidence intervals for conditional expectations are also produced as intermediate quantities.

## Method

The core mechanism transforms time-uniform confidence bounds of kernel ridge regression into a conditional two-sample test: the test statistic is the norm difference ‖f_{D₁,λ₁}(x) − f_{D₂,λ₂}(x)‖ at each covariate x, compared against the sum of KRR confidence radii β₁σ_{D₁,λ₁}(x) + β₂σ_{D₂,λ₂}(x). The key theoretical contribution is a new time-uniform confidence bound for vector-valued KRR (Theorem 4.3) that holds under a novel uniform-block-diagonal (UBD) kernel assumption, which is strictly weaker than the trace-class condition required by prior bounds and accommodates the diagonal kernels K = k·id_G needed for kernel mean embedding-based tests. For general functionals of conditional distributions, the paper reduces the problem to comparing conditional expectations via kernel mean embeddings, so that the same KRR machinery applies with embedded data Y_n = Φ_F(Z_n). In practice, the theoretically-derived but inaccessible constants β_i are replaced by bootstrapped thresholds (naive resampling or wild bootstrap), which the paper shows yield substantially tighter intervals and higher power while preserving the type I level.

## Results

The paper evaluates its conditional test on synthetic and dynamical-systems experiments, reporting empirical type I and II errors as functions of the significance level α, the magnitude of the true difference ξ, and the sampling density θ of the differing region. The test upholds the prescribed type I level α across all configurations, and type II error decreases monotonically with increasing ξ. In the local-sensitivity experiment (where the two conditional expectations differ only on a small region sampled with probability θ), the proposed test achieves substantially higher power than the global baseline for low θ, directly benefiting from its pointwise guarantees. In the process-monitoring experiment on a linear dynamical system, the test reliably detects a perturbation in the dynamics before the sliding window is fully populated with post-perturbation data, confirming robustness to correlated (non-i.i.d.) sampling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the conditional two-sample test of Hu and Lei [5] (JASA 2024), which uses conformal prediction and a weighted rank-sum statistic. On the controlled type I/II error experiments (Figure 2), the proposed KRR-based test achieves comparable type I and II error rates to the Hu–Lei baseline *without* requiring knowledge of the ground-truth noise distribution, whereas the baseline is parameterized by density estimators that use the true distribution. The key advantage emerges in the local-sensitivity setting: when the two conditional expectations differ only on a rarely-sampled region (mixture weight θ), the proposed test maintains high power while the Hu–Lei baseline—computing a single aggregated statistic—loses power, because the global statistic is diluted by the large region where the distributions coincide. For bootstrapping calibration, the paper also compares against the wild-bootstrap scheme of Singh and Vijaykumar [27]; the Singh et al. variant falls between the paper's naive resampling (more aggressive, slightly higher type I error) and its own wild bootstrap (more conservative, lower type I error) in terms of the type I/II error trade-off. Overall, the proposed method wins on local power and on not requiring ground-truth distributional knowledge, while matching the baseline on global type I/II error in well-sampled regions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of kernel learning theory and conditional hypothesis testing. It builds directly on time-uniform confidence bounds for KRR from Abbasi-Yadkori [12] (scalar outputs) and Chowdhury and Gopalan [20] (infinite-dimensional outputs under trace-class kernel assumptions), generalizing both via a new uniform-block-diagonal (UBD) kernel assumption that removes the trace-class requirement. Conceptually, it departs from prior conditional testing work (Zhang et al. [2], Shah and Peters [3], Hu and Lei [5]) by formalizing the covariate rejection region and providing finite-sample, pointwise-in-covariate guarantees rather than global or asymptotic ones, and by explicitly connecting learning-theoretic confidence bounds to test construction. The subgaussianity framework follows Mollenhauer and Schillings [22], and the wild bootstrap scheme adapts Singh and Vijaykumar [27].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes a principled, finite-sample framework for conditional testing that works with non-i.i.d. (e.g., online) data and provides pointwise guarantees at arbitrary covariates—addressing a gap where prior methods either detect only global discrepancies or require i.i.d. assumptions. The UBD kernel assumption is a reusable technical tool that extends confidence bounds to a broad class of non-trace-class kernels, with implications beyond conditional testing (e.g., vector-valued least squares, conditional mean embeddings). Limitations include the need for subgaussianity and RKHS membership assumptions, heuristic (non-guaranteed) bootstrapping for practical thresholds, and validation only on synthetic and low-dimensional examples. Open directions include conditional independence testing, richer functionals, and scaling to high-dimensional or real-world applications.
