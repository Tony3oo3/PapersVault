## Problem

Training sparse neural networks from scratch (Pruning at Initialization) consistently underperforms dense-to-sparse methods, and the root cause has been unclear. This paper identifies that the critical missing ingredient is sign alignment: dense-to-sparse training acquires correct parameter signs early, enabling convergence to flatter minima, while PaI cannot stabilize sign flips. The paper asks whether a reparameterization can provably recover correct signs in a regime complementary to overparameterized dense training.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 224×224 for ImageNet) together with a fixed sparse mask (random or derived from PaI/dense-to-sparse methods) that is applied at initialization and held constant throughout training. During training, each unmasked weight is internally represented as the product m⊙w of two continuous parameters; at inference these are merged back into a single weight θ = m⊙w, so the output is a standard sparse network producing class labels with no architectural change.

## Method

Sign-In reparameterizes every unmasked weight θ as the Hadamard product m⊙w, introducing an inner degree of freedom β = m² − w² that induces a Riemannian gradient flow with a √(θ²+β) scaling factor, enabling parameters to cross zero and flip sign. To counteract the implicit sparsity bias of this reparameterization (which shrinks β toward zero under stochastic noise and weight decay), Sign-In dynamically resets β to a fixed value every p epochs up to a stop epoch T₂, using an analytic rescaling formula that preserves the effective weight. In a single-neuron theoretical setting, the paper proves via Riemannian gradient flow and dynamical systems analysis that Sign-In recovers the correct sign in the (a<0, w>0) initialization case that standard gradient flow cannot solve, and that no continuous reparameterization can replace overparameterization for the (a<0, w<0) case. Combined with moderate overparameterization, Sign-In empirically resolves all four sign quadrants. Across ResNet20/18/50 and DeiT-Small on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet at 80–99% sparsity, Sign-In consistently improves PaI accuracy by 0.5–1.5 points with negligible FLOP overhead (~5% training-time increase).

## Results

Sign-In consistently improves sparse training from scratch across all three benchmarks and sparsity levels. On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-20, Sign-In gains +1.12 pp at 80% sparsity (89.37% vs. 88.25%) and +1.18 pp at 90% sparsity (87.83% vs. 86.25%). On ImageNet with ResNet-50, the gains are +0.25 pp at 80% sparsity (74.12% vs. 73.87%) and +0.63 pp at 90% sparsity (72.19% vs. 71.56%). The method also improves a DeiT-Small vision transformer by over 1 pp (70.62% vs. 69.31% at 50% sparsity on ImageNet), and the FLOP overhead is negligible (e.g., 5.0G vs. 4.99G training FLOPs for ResNet-50 at 80% sparsity).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PaI baselines, Sign-In uniformly wins: on CIFAR-10 at 90% sparsity, Random+Sign-In (87.83%) outperforms SNIP+Sign-In (87.27%), Synflow+Sign-In (86.47%), and NPB+Sign-In (86.88%), and all Sign-In variants beat their un-augmented counterparts by 1–2 pp. Against dense-to-sparse methods, Sign-In provides smaller but consistent gains: AC/DC+Sign-In improves from 74.75% to 74.74% at 90% sparsity (essentially a tie) but from 72.59% to 72.88% at 95% sparsity on ImageNet; RiGL+Sign-In gains +0.57 pp at 90% and +1.18 pp at 95% sparsity; MEST+Sign-In gains +0.22 pp at 90% and +0.57 pp at 95% sparsity. However, Sign-In cannot close the gap to dense-to-sparse baselines overall (e.g., AC/DC baseline 74.68% vs. Random+Sign-In 72.19% at 90% sparsity on ImageNet), and it fails to help masks with highly imbalanced layerwise sparsity (STR, Synflow masks in Table 8). The paper's impossibility theorem (Theorem 5.4) formally establishes that no reparameterization can fully replace the sign-flipping mechanism of dense overparameterized training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the m⊙w reparameterization previously used by continuous sparsification methods (spred, PILoT) to induce implicit sparsity, instead leveraging it as a mechanism to enable parameter sign flips during sparse training from scratch. The theoretical analysis builds directly on the single-neuron testbed and Riemannian gradient flow framework from Gadhikar & Burkholz (2024) and Li et al. (2022), extending them to characterize a new sign-recovery case complementary to overparameterization. Conceptually, the paper departs from the broader PaI literature (SNIP, Synflow, GraSP) by explicitly shifting focus from mask selection to weight optimization given a fixed mask, arguing that sign alignment—not mask quality—is the binding constraint for sparse trainability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies sign alignment as the critical mechanism underlying dense-to-sparse training's success and provides a provable (though partial) remedy via reparameterization, while simultaneously establishing an impossibility result showing no reparameterization can fully replace the sign-flipping capacity of initial dense training. This carves out the precise open problem: closing the PaI-to-dense-to-sparse gap requires an unknown mechanism beyond reparameterization. Practically, the 1–2% improvements across ResNets and DeiT at multiple sparsity levels are consistent but modest, and the method doubles parameter count during training (though negligible at inference). The finding that learned signs from epoch 10 of dense-to-sparse training nearly recover baseline performance when paired with the mask suggests a potential shortcut for practitioners who want to avoid full dense training.
