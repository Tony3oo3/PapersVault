## Problem

Bayesian uncertainty quantification for neural networks faces a fundamental trade-off: methods that are computationally cheap (MC-Dropout, SWAG) provide poor posterior approximations, while theoretically grounded methods (MCMC, full Laplace) are intractable for modern overparameterized networks. The paper asks whether one can efficiently sample from the true posterior of a trained network by exploiting the structure of the overparameterized regime, and whether this posterior has a clean interpretation in terms of the Neural Tangent Kernel.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254983/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method is applied post-hoc to a fully trained network: it takes the trained weights θ̂, the original training dataset, and a hyperparameter γ controlling the perturbation scale. It produces S linearized network predictors, from which the predictive mean and variance at any test point are computed as the sample mean and covariance of the ensemble outputs. The variance is scaled by γ², enabling post-hoc calibration on a validation set without retraining. For classification, the key output metric is the variance of the maximum softmax probability (VMSP) per test point.

## Method

NUQLS linearizes the trained network around its parameters θ̂, then initializes S copies with zero-mean isotropic Gaussian perturbations (scale γ) of θ̂ and trains each via (stochastic) gradient descent to fit the original training data. The key theoretical result (Theorem 3.2) shows that under convexity assumptions on the loss, GD/SGD converges to solutions whose null-space component is a projection of the random initialization onto Null(J_X), making the ensemble a set of draws from a GP posterior with the empirical NTK as kernel. This avoids the need for explicit Hessian computation, prior specification, or covariance structure approximations required by LLA-based methods. Because each linearized network starts near a local minimum, convergence is fast, yielding an order-of-magnitude speedup over deep ensembles while remaining embarrassingly parallel and numerically stable.

## Results

On UCI regression (Table 1), NUQLS achieves the best or tied-best ECE across all datasets (0.002 on Energy, 0.000 on Kin8nm, 0.002 on Protein) while matching or improving RMSE and NLL over DE, LLA, and SWAG. Computationally, NUQLS is dramatically faster: 8.4s vs. 102s (DE) on Energy, 26.6s vs. 74s on Kin8nm, and 81s vs. 1015s on Protein, with the post-hoc UQ step taking only 0.15–1.4s. On image classification (FashionMNIST/CIFAR-10 with MNIST/CIFAR-100 as OoD), NUQLS achieves the best VMSP separation between correctly-predicted ID, incorrectly-predicted ID, and OoD points, outperforming DE, SWAG, LLA*, and MC-Dropout. On ResNet-50 for SVHN and ImageNet, NUQLS also outperforms BDE, SNGP, BatchEnsemble, and SGLD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Deep Ensembles (DE), NUQLS matches or beats RMSE and NLL on all UCI datasets (e.g., 0.047 vs. 0.218 RMSE on Energy) while being 12×–12.5× faster; on image classification, NUQLS achieves better VMSP separation than DE on both FashionMNIST and CIFAR-10. Against LLA, NUQLS achieves comparable RMSE and ECE but slightly better NLL on Energy (−2.400 vs. −2.475) and Kin8nm (−0.796 vs. −0.783), and avoids the need for KFAC covariance approximations on larger datasets. Against SWAG, NUQLS dominates on every metric: e.g., 0.047 vs. 0.058 RMSE and 0.002 vs. 0.080 ECE on Energy, with a 5.4× speedup. On the toy regression (Figure 1), VI underestimates and LA overestimates uncertainty, while NUQLS and LLA both contain the target curve within ±3σ. On image classification (Figure 3), NUQLS outperforms MC-Dropout, SWAG, and LLA* on VMSP for all three test groups (ID-correct, ID-incorrect, OoD). On ResNet-50 (SVHN/ImageNet, Figure 6), NUQLS outperforms BDE, SNGP, BatchEnsemble, and SGLD; in the appendix, NUQLS also beats Sampling-LLA, VaLLA, ELLA, SLU, and PNC on their respective comparison metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

NUQLS extends the sample-then-optimize posterior sampling framework (Matthews et al., 2017) to finite-width neural networks by replacing explicit parameter-space sampling with (stochastic) gradient descent on linearized networks. It departs from the LLA family (Khan et al., 2019; Daxberger et al., 2021) by bypassing the need for a parameter-space prior and Hessian approximation, instead directly generating predictive-distribution samples whose variance arises naturally from the overparameterized null space. The GP connection is made through the empirical (finite-width) NTK rather than the infinite-width analytic NTK used in Bayesian Deep Ensembles (He et al., 2020), and the method is positioned as a post-hoc, embarrassingly parallel alternative to Deep Ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NUQLS offers a practically important efficiency gain—often an order of magnitude faster than Deep Ensembles—while maintaining competitive or superior UQ performance across regression and classification benchmarks, making Bayesian UQ more accessible for large-scale deployment. The clean theoretical identification of GD/SGD on linearized networks as sampling from an NTK-GP posterior provides a novel unifying perspective linking NNs, GPs, and the NTK. Key open questions include extending the theoretical guarantees to non-convex losses like cross-entropy (where the GP interpretation breaks down but empirical performance remains strong) and understanding behavior when the linearization is poor (undertrained networks). The proposed VMSP metric addresses a genuine gap in evaluating UQ quality for classification, though its adoption by the broader community remains to be seen.
