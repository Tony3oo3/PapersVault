## Problem

Conformal prediction provides distribution-free marginal coverage guarantees, but achieving conditional coverage (e.g., per covariate group) is either impossible in the fully conditional sense or requires computationally expensive full-conformal procedures. Split-conformal methods with adaptive thresholds are fast and natural, yet lacked rigorous sample-conditional coverage guarantees—this paper fills that gap by proving that quantile regression on a held-out validation set yields near-optimal conditional coverage with high probability over the calibration data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a scoring function s(x,y) (e.g., absolute prediction error or class scores), a validation dataset of size n, and a feature mapping φ: X → R^d (e.g., group indicators or random projections of model features). It outputs a confidence set Ĉ(x) = {y | s(x,y) ≤ ⟨θ̂, φ(x)⟩} where θ̂ is the empirical minimizer of the α-quantile loss, together with a sample-conditional coverage bound of the form 1 − α − O(1)√(α·d log n / n) that holds simultaneously for all weight functions in a d-dimensional class with probability at least 1 − e^{−t} over the validation draw.

## Method

The core mechanism replaces the constant split-conformal threshold τ̂ with an adaptive threshold ĥ(x) = ⟨θ̂, φ(x)⟩ obtained by minimizing the empirical quantile loss ℓ_α(⟨θ,φ(x)⟩ − S) over the validation set. The paper proves sample-conditional coverage via three escalating technical tools: (i) a Bernstein-type concentration bound for the scalar case tracking α(1−α)/n, (ii) a VC-dimension uniform convergence argument for the vector-valued process φ(x)·1{S > ⟨θ,φ(x)⟩} yielding Theorem 1, and (iii) Talagrand's empirical process inequality combined with localized Rademacher complexities to obtain sharper, variance-adaptive bounds in Theorems 2–3. A key insight is that the first-order optimality condition of the quantile loss directly controls the weighted miscoverage, and the distinctness of scores bounds the number of ties by d, enabling two-sided guarantees. The resulting rate √(d log n / n) is shown minimax-optimal via lower bounds from Areces et al., and experiments on CIFAR-100 (using ResNet features and logistic regression) confirm that the adaptive split-conformal set matches full-conformal coverage on random slices while being ~8000× faster to compute.

## Results

The paper's primary contributions are theoretical: Theorems 2 and 3 establish that the empirical quantile-regression conformal set achieves weighted sample-conditional coverage with error O(1)·√(α·d·log n/n), which is minimax rate-optimal (matching the lower bound of Areces et al. [1] up to log factors). In the exploratory CIFAR-100 experiment (target 90% coverage), the adaptive split-conformal method achieves coverage close to the nominal level on random 20% slices, with only slight marginal undercoverage, while the static-threshold baseline shows substantially more variability across slices. The split-conformal method is approximately 8000× faster to compute than the full-conformal method of Gibbs et al.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the full-conformal method of Gibbs et al. [12], the proposed split-conformal approach achieves comparable coverage on random slices of CIFAR-100 (both near the nominal 90% level), but at roughly 8000× lower computational cost; the full-conformal method slightly overcovers marginally while the split method slightly undercovers. Against the static split-conformal baseline (constant threshold τ̂ from Barber et al. [4]), the adaptive-threshold method shows substantially less variability in coverage across random data slices, indicating better group-conditional behavior. In the synthetic regression experiments (Appendix D.2), both methods achieve similar per-group miscoverage, though the full-conformal method produces slightly tighter prediction intervals. The paper notes that when the feature dimension d is large relative to the validation sample size n, the split-conformal method exhibits a downward coverage bias scaling as √(d/n), whereas the full-conformal method's error scales as O(d/n), making the full method more accurate in high-dimensional regimes at the cost of much higher computation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the split-conformal framework of Barber et al. [4] by replacing the constant threshold with an adaptive threshold from quantile regression, a direction initiated by Jung et al. [17] and Gibbs et al. [12]. While Gibbs et al. established population-level weighted coverage guarantees for their full-conformal procedure, and Jung et al. considered group-conditional variants, this paper provides the first sample-conditional coverage guarantees for the computationally cheaper split-conformal analogue. The technical machinery draws on empirical process theory (Talagrand's inequality, VC-dimension bounds, localized Rademacher complexities) to sharpen the concentration bounds beyond what prior work achieved, and connects to the minimax lower bounds of Areces et al. [1] to establish rate optimality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The minimax rate-optimality result validates the simple quantile-regression split-conformal approach as theoretically optimal for two-sided weighted coverage guarantees, resolving a question about whether the computationally expensive full-conformal method is necessary for conditional guarantees. A key open question is the downward coverage bias (scaling as √(d/n)) that appears when the feature dimension is large relative to the calibration set size; full conformal achieves O(d/n) error, and bridging this gap—possibly via leave-one-out bias correction—remains unresolved. Practically, the 8000× speedup over full conformal while maintaining near-equivalent coverage on random slices makes adaptive-threshold split conformal a compelling default for large-scale deployment, though the experiments are exploratory and do not directly validate the sample-conditional (as opposed to group-conditional) guarantees.
