## Problem

Softmax normalization in attention mechanisms suffers from a chronic failure mode: as context length L grows, attention weights collapse toward 1/L (vanishing attention), eroding the model's ability to distinguish informative from irrelevant tokens. While architectural workarounds (sparse windows, LSH, compressed memories) reduce compute, no principled closed-form explanation existed for why softmax fails in this regime or what the fundamental capacity limit of a single attention head actually is.

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on a frozen pre-trained GPT-2 model, extracting full L×L attention matrices and associated query/key/value tensors for all 144 head-layer pairs from long-form prose (War and Peace, up to L=1024 tokens). The theoretical framework takes token embeddings x_i ∈ R^d, a temperature parameter T, and sequence length L as inputs, and produces three diagnostic quantities: a non-asymptotic upper bound on the Euclidean distance between the top-N context vector and non-selected embeddings, a geometric separability ratio N_s/N under spherical embedding assumptions, and a Jacobian norm bound quantifying gradient sensitivity to logit perturbations.

## Method

The paper frames attention as a capacity-limited selector and derives three non-asymptotic theoretical results. Theorem 1 provides an explicit upper bound on the cumulative distance between the weighted context vector s = Σ α_i x_i (over top-N tokens) and all non-selected embeddings, proving that this distance necessarily collapses when N grows proportionally to L. Theorem 2, under uniform spherical distribution and minimum pairwise separation assumptions, bounds the fraction of geometrically distinguishable selected tokens, showing no more than ~80% can remain inside a selective ball B_r(s) regardless of N. Lemma 2 and its softmax specialisation (Corollary 3) bound the Jacobian norm of the normalisation map, recovering the 1/(4T) instability and explaining why aggressive temperature scaling trades token separability for optimisation difficulty. All three predictions are validated empirically on GPT-2: distance scales linearly in L for fixed small N, separability saturates at 70–85%, and the empirical Jacobian norm tracks the theoretical 1/T curve for T < 0.1.

## Results

On GPT-2 (144 heads/layers) evaluated on War and Peace text, the representation distance grows roughly linearly with sequence length L when N=5, and plateaus once N exceeds ~20 at L=1024. The critical top-N (where empirical and expected distances become statistically indistinguishable via KS test at α=0.01) is approximately 0.06L, meaning fewer than 6% of tokens need to be selected before attention behaves as if weights were uniform. Geometric separability (N_s/N) saturates between 70% and 85% for N≥16, confirming the theoretical upper bound of ~80% distinguishable tokens. The empirical Jacobian norm follows the theoretical 1/(4T) bound for T<0.1 and drops by two orders of magnitude once T≥1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing normalization methods (Sparsemax, Scalable-Softmax, Self-Adjusted Softmax) in a head-to-head experimental comparison; instead, it validates three theoretical predictions against GPT-2's empirical behavior. The empirical distance curves (Fig. 2) confirm Theorem 1's upper bound is safe but conservative, with the true distance tracking the expectation term closely. The geometric separability ratio (Fig. 3) falls within Theorem 2's analytic bounds, with the exponential upper bound tracking empirical maxima tightly while the lower bound remains intentionally conservative. The gradient sensitivity curves (Fig. 4) align with Corollary 3's min{1/(4T), √2} bound for T<0.1, with GPT-2 operating close to the theoretical limit at low temperatures. The paper positions its contributions as complementary to existing alternatives: the distance bound formalizes why sparse or length-aware rules (Sparsemax, Scalable-Softmax) improve long-context performance, and the gradient bound explains why gradient-controlled variants (SA-Softmax) are necessary to avoid Jacobian explosion.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Transformer's softmax attention formulation (Vaswani et al., 2017) and the softmax bottleneck identified by Yang et al. (2018), but departs from both by providing non-asymptotic, closed-form bounds rather than informal or asymptotic characterizations. Where prior work proposed architectural workarounds (sparse windows, LSH, compressed memories) or alternative normalizers (Sparsemax, Scalable-Softmax, SA-Softmax) with empirical justification, this paper derives the theoretical reasons those modifications are necessary from first principles. The geometric separability bound (Theorem 2) introduces a new analytical angle—bounding how many tokens a single head can simultaneously distinguish under spherical embedding assumptions—that has no direct precedent in the attention literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practitioners with concrete diagnostic thresholds (the ~80% geometric separability ceiling, the ~6L critical-N, the 1/(4T) Jacobian bound) that can be monitored during training to detect when an attention head has saturated its capacity. This reframes the design of long-context Transformers from trial-and-error architecture search to principled capacity management. Key open questions include whether the spherical-embedding assumption holds in practice, how multi-head interactions alter the per-head limits, and whether a single normalizer can simultaneously achieve sparsity, length-awareness, and gradient stability. The empirical validation is limited to GPT-2 on a single public-domain text, so generalization to other architectures and domains remains untested.
