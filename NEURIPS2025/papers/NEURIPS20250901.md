## Problem

Existing Process Reward Models (PRMs) are trained on structured, linear final responses and fail to reliably score the messy, branching intermediate thinking trajectories produced by frontier reasoning models like DeepSeek-R1. The paper identifies two root causes: a structural/formatting mismatch between trajectories and polished responses, and the absence of trajectory-response pairs with assigned rewards during PRM training, leading to misaligned data selection and degraded downstream fine-tuning.

## Input / Output

The model takes a problem x, a thinking trajectory s = (s_1, …, s_T) of T intermediate steps, and a final response a = (a_1, …, a_T), and produces a per-step reward vector for the thinking trajectory plus a single trajectory-level scalar. The composite score used for data selection or RL is r̂ = (1/T)Σ r̂_t^step + α·r̂^final, where α balances local step supervision against global strategic quality. Unlike prior PRMs that only score the final response, ReasonFlux-PRM explicitly conditions each step score on the full final response a, using it as a reference signal for the earlier, less-organized thinking steps.

## Method

ReasonFlux-PRM introduces a two-level reward design. At the step level, each thinking step s_t receives a weighted combination of three signals: an alignment score (cosine similarity between Φ(s_t) and Φ(a_t) via a pretrained encoder), a quality score from an LLM-as-a-judge evaluating logical soundness in context, and a coherence score computed via a contrastive mutual-information formulation against N negative samples from unrelated trajectories; the three are fused with softmax-based weighting. At the trajectory level, a template-guided reward extracts a high-level reasoning template from the full trajectory-response using GPT-4o, conditions a policy model on that template to generate N candidate solutions, and uses their average correctness as the reward. The model is trained with a joint MSE objective over both levels (weighted by λ_step and λ_final) on 10k curated trajectory-response pairs from OpenThoughts-114K, initialized from Qwen2.5-1.5B/7B-Instruct. The resulting PRM is then deployed in three settings: offline top-k data selection for SFT, online reward augmentation of GRPO policy optimization (composite reward r_new = (1−β)·r_out + β·r̂), and reward-guided Best-of-N test-time scaling.

## Results

On offline data selection (SFT of Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct), ReasonFlux-PRM-7B achieves 40.0% on AIME24, 33.3% on AIME25, 84.8% on MATH500, and 47.5% on GPQA-Diamond, surpassing the human-curated s1k baseline by 6.7%, 0.0%, 6.0%, and 6.1% respectively. In online RL (GRPO with DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B), ReasonFlux-PRM-7B yields 54.6% on AIME24, 44.2% on AIME25, 94.8% on MATH500, and 51.6% on GPQA-Diamond, improving over the rule-based GRPO baseline by 4.4%, 5.9%, 5.2%, and 4.5%. The paper reports average gains of 12.1% in SFT, 4.5% in RL, and 6.3% in Best-of-N test-time scaling across benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ReasonFlux-PRM-7B outperforms all four PRM baselines and the human-curated s1k dataset on every benchmark in offline data selection. Against the strongest PRM baseline (Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-72B scoring on final responses only), ReasonFlux-PRM-7B gains 3.3% on AIME24, 6.6% on AIME25, 7.0% on MATH500, and 6.6% on GPQA-Diamond. Compared to the human-curated s1k baseline, the improvement is 6.7% on AIME24 and 6.0–6.1% on MATH500 and GPQA-Diamond, while matching on AIME25. In the RL setting (Table 3), ReasonFlux-PRM-7B consistently beats both the rule-based GRPO reward and Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B across both policy models; for example, on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B it improves MATH500 from 89.6% (rule-based) to 94.8% and GPQA-Diamond from 47.1% to 51.6%. The weaker 7B PRMs (Math-Shepherd-PRM-7B, Skywork-PRM-7B) perform at or below random sampling, confirming that existing PRMs are poorly calibrated for trajectory-response data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Process Reward Model (PRM) lineage (Math-Shepherd, Skywork-PRM, Qwen2.5-Math-PRM) by addressing a data-format mismatch: existing PRMs are trained on structured final responses, but frontier reasoning models (DeepSeek-R1, OpenAI-o1) now produce a trajectory-response format with a long unstructured thinking trajectory preceding the final answer. The key methodological departure is a joint reward design combining step-level signals (semantic alignment to the final response, LLM-as-a-judge quality scoring, and contrastive coherence between adjacent steps) with a template-guided trajectory-level reward that verifies whether the high-level strategy generalizes. The paper also integrates the resulting PRM into GRPO-based policy optimization and Best-of-N test-time scaling, building on the GRPO framework from DeepSeekMath and the test-time scaling paradigm from s1.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As trajectory-response distillation from models like DeepSeek-R1 becomes the dominant paradigm for training smaller reasoning models, the inability of existing PRMs to reliably score intermediate thinking steps is a practical bottleneck that this paper directly addresses. The finding that a 7B trajectory-aware PRM outperforms a 72B response-only PRM on data selection is practically significant for resource-constrained settings. Open questions include whether the reward decomposition (alignment + quality + coherence + template-guided trajectory reward) generalizes beyond math/science to open-ended reasoning, whether the GPT-4o-dependent training labels can be replaced with cheaper verifiers, and whether the α and β hyperparameters can be made adaptive rather than fixed.
