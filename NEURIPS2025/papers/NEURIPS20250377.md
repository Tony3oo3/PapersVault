## Problem

Standard CNNs rely on linear convolutions followed by pointwise nonlinearities, whose Taylor expansions couple weights across polynomial orders (the 'tied-weight issue'), limiting their ability to capture multiplicative interactions and higher-order spatial correlations in natural images—especially in shallow or narrow architectures. This paper asks whether explicitly embedding higher-order (Volterra-like) terms directly into the convolution operator, with independent learnable weights per order, can overcome this limitation and better exploit the hierarchical correlation structure of visual scenes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250377/cropped_page3_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard RGB or grayscale images at dataset-specific resolutions (28×28 for MNIST, 32×32 for CIFAR, 224×224 for ImageNet) and outputs a class label via a standard softmax head. The higher-order convolution operates on local pixel patches within each receptive field, computing multiplicative interactions between neighboring pixels; for the synthetic texture experiments, inputs are 32×32 binary images encoding specific N-point correlation structures. No data augmentation is used for the small-dataset benchmarks, while ImageNet and CUB-200-2011 use standard augmentation (random crops, flips, color jittering).

## Method

The core contribution is a learnable higher-order convolution (HoConv) layer that extends the classical linear convolution with a Volterra-like polynomial expansion up to the 3rd or 4th order, where each order has fully independent weight tensors. Concretely, the input patch is flattened to a vector x; the 1st order performs standard convolution, while the 2nd and 3rd orders compute one and two successive outer products of x with itself, each dotted with its own weight tensor. Feature maps from all orders are summed before a standard ReLU, and a normalization factor s = 1/√(nV) (where nV is the number of unique parameters for that order) balances higher-order contributions. Kernel symmetry reduces parameter counts (e.g., 4.42× for 3rd order with 3×3 kernels). The HoConv layer replaces standard convolutions in early network layers—only the first stage of ResNet-18 for ImageNet—since deeper placement degrades performance. Training uses standard backpropagation with AdamW (small datasets) or SGD with momentum (ImageNet), and the approach is validated through perturbation analysis, RSA, and FLOPs-controlled comparisons against deeper CNN baselines.

## Results

On the main benchmarks (50 runs each), HoCNN with 3rd-order 3×3 kernels consistently outperforms the standard CNN baseline: CIFAR-10 (72.87% vs 69.76%, +3.11%), CIFAR-100 (40.42% vs 36.92%, +3.50%), FashionMNIST (90.95% vs 90.48%), and MNIST (99.30% vs 99.13%). On ImageNet (5 runs), HoResNet-18 achieves 69.47% top-1 accuracy versus ResNet-18's 68.62% (+0.85%). The largest gain appears on fine-grained CUB-200-2011, where HoResNet-18 reaches 72.31% versus ResNet-18's 68.24% (+4.07%), suggesting higher-order correlations are especially valuable for subtle visual discrimination.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the standard ResNet-18 baseline [He et al., CVPR 2016], HoResNet-18 wins on every dataset tested: +0.85% on ImageNet (69.47% vs 68.62%), +1.17% on Imagenette (89.30% vs 88.13%), and a substantial +4.07% on fine-grained CUB-200-2011 (72.31% vs 68.24%). On the smaller benchmarks, the simpler HoCNN (3rd-order 3×3 kernels) beats the equivalent CNN by +3.11% on CIFAR-10 and +3.50% on CIFAR-100, while using slightly fewer parameters (80,262 vs 82,706). Against the biologically-inspired VOneNet [Dapello et al., NeurIPS 2020], HoResNet-18 achieves higher Imagenette accuracy (89.30% vs 88.02%) with fewer parameters (11.17M vs 14.45M), indicating that higher-order convolutions capture visual structure more efficiently than V1-inspired front-end features. The paper also shows that a deeper CNN with 25% more parameters (108,130) still underperforms the HoCNN (71.20% vs 72.35% on CIFAR-10), and a FLOPs-matched deep CNN (7.09M FLOPs) trails the HoCNN (6.81M FLOPs) by 1.21%, confirming the gains are not merely due to extra compute.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Volterra convolution framework introduced by Zoumpourlis et al. (2017) and Roheda et al. (2024) by implementing higher-order terms (up to 4th order) directly within the convolution operator with independent weights per order, rather than the 2nd-order-only or low-rank-approximation approaches of prior work. It draws a direct conceptual link to Koenderink and van Doorn's (2018) analysis of natural image statistics, arguing that the observed optimal 3rd/4th-order performance reflects the sparsity hierarchy of pixel correlations in natural scenes. The biological motivation connects to Drosophila motion-detection circuits sensitive to 3-point correlations and to the VOneNet line of biologically-inspired CNNs, positioning higher-order convolution as a mechanistic implementation of non-pointwise nonlinearities observed in biological visual processing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, biologically-grounded explanation for why higher-order local interactions improve classification, with the optimal order matching the empirical distribution of pixel correlations in natural images. However, the computational overhead (5× for 2nd order, 18× for 3rd order FLOPs) and the finding that HoCNNs are more vulnerable to higher-order statistical perturbations (while more robust to common corruptions) represent a meaningful trade-off that limits immediate practical adoption. Open questions include whether low-rank tensor decompositions can reduce the cost, how higher-order convolutions interact with attention mechanisms in hybrid architectures, and whether the gains generalize to dense prediction tasks beyond classification.
