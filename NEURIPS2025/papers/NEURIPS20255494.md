## Problem

Multivariate distributions do not form a vector space, rendering standard functional data analysis tools (FPCA, regression, modes of variation) inapplicable. While invertible transformations to Hilbert space exist for univariate densities (e.g., log quantile density), no analogous invertible mapping was available for the multivariate case. Existing multivariate approaches—kernel embeddings, tangent-space PCA on the Bures-Wasserstein manifold, and Bayes Hilbert space methods—are either non-invertible, restricted to Gaussian distributions, or lack convergence guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method takes finite random samples (e.g., 200 bivariate observations per distribution) from which kernel density estimates are first computed, then applies the RLQD transform to map each estimated density into L²(Θ×S). Outputs include principal component scores for each distribution, transformation modes of variation (families of valid multivariate densities parameterized by α ∈ ℝ), and K-component truncated density approximations reconstructed via a regularized inverse Radon transform. The regularization parameter τ controls the trade-off between reconstruction fidelity and numerical stability of the inverse.

## Method

The core contribution is the Radon Log Quantile Density (RLQD) transformation Ψ_Q, which composes the Radon transform (slicing a p-dimensional density into univariate projections along every direction θ ∈ Θ) with the univariate log quantile density transform applied slice-by-slice, yielding an invertible map from multivariate densities to L²(Θ×S). The inverse applies the LQD inverse to each slice and then reconstructs the multivariate density via a regularized filtered back-projection with frequency cutoff τ, followed by normalization to ensure a valid density. Standard FDA machinery—Karhunen-Loève expansion, FPCA, and transformation modes of variation—is then performed entirely in the Hilbert space, with all results mapped back to interpretable densities through the regularized inverse. The paper establishes convergence rates for the estimated modes of variation and truncated density estimators under smoothness and eigenvalue-decay assumptions, showing O_p(τ^p n^{-1/2}) rates for fixed K and parametric rates under exponential eigenvalue decay.

## Results

On the BLSA blood pressure data, the proposed RLQD method with K=2 components explains 87% of the variance in sliced Wasserstein distance, whereas standard FPCA requires at least 3 components (first two explaining 70% and 11% respectively) to reach the 85% cumulative variance threshold. In Monte Carlo experiments (bivariate normals and Gaussian mixtures), RLQD consistently achieves the highest fraction of variance explained across all component counts K=1,2,3, with the advantage becoming particularly pronounced beyond the first two modes where FPCA plateaus and TPCA yields diminishing returns. On the temperature dataset, five RLQD components explain over 85% of sliced Wasserstein variance (first component: 0.41, second: 0.18).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares RLQD against two baselines: (1) standard FPCA applied directly to estimated densities as L² elements (ignoring density constraints, then truncating and renormalizing), and (2) TPCA performing PCA in the tangent space of the Bures-Wasserstein manifold at a reference density, mapping back via the exponential map. RLQD outperforms both baselines on the fraction of variance explained (sliced Wasserstein metric) across all component counts in both simulation settings (bivariate normals and Gaussian mixtures). The gap is most pronounced at K≥3, where FPCA's explained variance plateaus and TPCA shows diminishing returns, while RLQD continues to capture meaningful higher-order variation. On the real BLSA blood pressure data, RLQD achieves 87% variance explained with just 2 components versus FPCA's 81% at K=2 and >85% only at K=3. The paper also notes that FPCA modes of variation resemble Gaussian-shaped perturbations, which is restrictive for the skewed blood pressure data (confirmed by Mardia's test, p<0.001), whereas RLQD captures more flexible non-Gaussian modes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the univariate log quantile density (LQD) transformation of Petersen and Müller (2016) to multivariate distributions by composing it with the Radon transform, creating the RLQD transformation that maps multivariate densities into a Hilbert space. It builds on the regularized inverse Radon transform and sliced Wasserstein framework from Chen and Müller (2023) to ensure invertibility of the mapping back to the density space. The approach departs from prior multivariate distributional FPCA methods—Wasserstein geodesic PCA, tangent-space PCA on the Bures-Wasserstein manifold, and Bayes Hilbert space decompositions—by providing an explicitly invertible transformation with formal convergence guarantees, addressing the critical limitation that those methods either lack a global inverse or are restricted to Gaussian distributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework to combine an invertible multivariate density transformation with classical FDA tools (FPCA, modes of variation) and provide asymptotic convergence rates in the sliced Wasserstein metric, filling a gap the authors identify as 'far less is known' for the multivariate case. The practical demonstration on blood pressure and temperature data shows that the method captures non-Gaussian structure (skewness, changing covariance) more faithfully than Gaussian-shaped FPCA perturbations, requiring fewer components for equivalent variance explanation. Key limitations include the curse of dimensionality in the preliminary density estimation step, the need to tune the regularization parameter τ, and the restriction to compact convex supports. Open questions include extension to higher dimensions, integration with distributional regression, and whether the Radon slicing is the optimal choice among possible multivariate slicing schemes.
