## Problem

RL-trained LLMs on QA tasks develop imprecise, redundant search behaviors because standard reward signals (e.g., a single binary reward on the final answer) provide poor temporal credit assignment, leaving intuition and verification underdeveloped. The paper asks whether decomposing the QA task into structured sub-stages with stage-specific rewards can explicitly train these distinct cognitive capabilities and yield more efficient, accurate reasoning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253437/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a math question as text input and produces a structured multi-stage response: a concise initial answer (≤1000 tokens), a Yes/No verification judgment (≤2000 tokens), a refined answer if verification fails (≤6000 tokens), and a distilled summary (≤1000 tokens). At inference, the episode terminates early after verification if the fast answer is confirmed correct, and the Fast Thinking mode can be deployed standalone for simpler tasks, requiring fewer than 1000 tokens total.

## Method

The Thinker task decomposes standard QA into four stages inspired by Dual Process Theory: Fast Thinking (intuition under a strict token budget), Verification (class-balanced binary reward for correct/incorrect judgment), Slow Thinking (refinement with a generous budget), and Summarization (distilling the slow reasoning into a concise trace consistent with the Fast Thinking prompt, enforced via a log-probability term under the original question). Each stage receives a reward restricted to itself, with a discount factor of 0 between stages and 1 within, enabling precise per-stage credit assignment. Training uses PPO on the Open-Reasoner-Zero codebase with 129K math QA pairs. The Summarization step creates a virtuous loop: by re-presenting the original question and rewarding summaries that are both correct and high-probability under the Fast Thinking prompt, it reinforces concise reasoning patterns that directly improve future Fast Thinking intuition.

## Results

On the full Thinker task (inference mode), Qwen2.5-1.5B achieves 27.33% average Pass@1 accuracy across eight math benchmarks, up from 25.62% for the standard QA baseline (6.7% relative gain). For DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, the Thinker task yields 50.98% average accuracy versus 45.90% for the QA baseline (11.1% relative gain). Notably, the Fast Thinking mode alone (≤1000 tokens) achieves 25.18% for Q1.5B and 41.05% for R1.5B, approaching full-model performance at a fraction of the token cost. On the 7B model (R7B), the Thinker task improves average accuracy from 54.41% to 59.09% (8.6% relative gain).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is standard single-turn QA training with PPO (8000-token budget). The Thinker task outperforms this baseline on nearly every benchmark for both model sizes: for Q1.5B, gains range from +4.63 pp (MATH500) to +3.70 pp (AMC23); for R1.5B, gains are larger, e.g., +11.78 pp on AMC23 and +11.72 pp on GPQA Diamond. Against Open-Reasoner-Zero (ORZ) [3], Thinker leads on all four overlapping benchmarks (e.g., 64.45 vs. 58.00 on MATH500, 6.25 vs. 3.50 on AIME24). Against SimpleRL [2], Thinker similarly wins on all five overlapping benchmarks (e.g., 64.45 vs. 59.00 on MATH500, 39.06 vs. 35.00 on AMC23). In the token-efficiency comparison (Table 2), Thinker surpasses all four concurrent methods—ThinkPrune [19], Concise Reasoning [20], SR-FLOW [21], and AdaptThink [22]—on accuracy across MATH500, AIME24, and AMC23 while using a competitive or lower token count (e.g., 88.5% at 2501 tokens on MATH500 vs. ThinkPrune's 83.2% at 1938 tokens). The Thinker-Fast mode, restricted to 1000 tokens, achieves 80.9% on MATH500 and 66.9% on AMC23, demonstrating that the structured training transfers efficiency even when only the fast stage is deployed at inference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the RL-for-LLM-reasoning line (DeepSeek-R1, Open-Reasoner-Zero, SimpleRL) that trains models on standard single-turn QA with a binary final-answer reward, and on the observation that such training produces inefficient, over-reflective chain-of-thought. Rather than modifying the reward function or RL algorithm within a single response (as ThinkPrune, Concise Reasoning, SR-Flow, and AdaptThink do), the paper restructures the RL *environment* itself into a four-stage task inspired by Kahneman's Dual Process Theory, assigning a distinct reward signal to each stage to achieve finer temporal credit assignment. It also relates to the Thinker MDP (Chung et al., 2023) as another instance of environment augmentation, and to prompt-based self-correction methods (Self-Refine, Reflexion) which elicit similar verify-and-refine behavior at inference time without RL fine-tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Thinker task offers a practical deployment lever: the Fast Thinking mode (≤1000 tokens) approaches full-baseline accuracy at roughly one-eighth the token budget, enabling a quality-vs-cost trade-off at inference. The conceptual framing—treating intuition and deliberation as separately trainable capabilities with a virtuous feedback loop—provides a clean design principle for future RL environments beyond math. However, gains are modest (6.7–11.1% relative), experiments are limited to 1.5B/7B models on mathematical reasoning only, and the Summarization ablation shows its benefit is confined to Fast Thinking rather than overall accuracy, leaving open whether the four-stage structure generalizes to other domains or scales to frontier models.
