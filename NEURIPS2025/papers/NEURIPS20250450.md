## Problem

Pre-LN Transformers, the dominant architecture in modern LLMs (LLaMA, Qwen, DeepSeek), suffer from exponential growth of activation variance across layers, causing the residual shortcut to increasingly overpower sub-layer outputs and limiting the learning capacity of deeper layers. This leads to slow convergence, underutilized deep layers, and poor parameter efficiency, yet existing remedies (DeepNorm, Sandwich-LN, LNS) either fix scaling heuristically or introduce architectural complexity without addressing the underlying gradient dynamics.

## Input / Output

GPAS operates as a drop-in modification within standard LLaMA-style transformer blocks during pretraining on tokenized text (C4 corpus, T5 tokenizer). It does not alter the input/output interface—models still consume text token sequences and produce next-token predictions—but inserts a per-layer learnable scalar gate α_l into the residual connection after each attention or FFN sub-layer. During supervised fine-tuning, the gates are frozen and the model is evaluated on seven 0-shot reasoning benchmarks (MMLU, BoolQ, PIQA, SIQA, HellaSwag, WinoGrande, ARC, OBQA).

## Method

GPAS modifies the residual output after each sub-layer as x_{l+1} = x'_{l+1} − SiLU(α_l)·sg(x'_{l+1}), where sg(·) is the stop-gradient operator that acts as identity in the forward pass but blocks gradient flow in the backward pass. This scales down activations by a factor (1 − SiLU(α_l)) to reduce variance accumulation, while the stop-gradient ensures the Jacobian ∂x_{l+1}/∂x'_{l+1} = I, eliminating the exponential product of scaling factors that would otherwise cause gradient vanishing in early layers. The per-layer gate α_l is initialized to zero (making the first forward/backward pass identical to the baseline) and learned during training, with SiLU encouraging positive values for variance reduction while permitting negative values when the network needs to scale up (e.g., in the first layer where embedding variance is low). Theoretical analysis (Theorem 1) proves that GPAS yields a strictly non-constant lower bound on gradient norms (preventing vanishing) while exponentially suppressing the upper bound compared to the O(L) growth of standard Pre-LN. The method generalizes as a plugin to DeepNorm (applied to the scaled shortcut), Sandwich-LN, Mix-LN, and LNS, with consistent perplexity and downstream accuracy gains across model sizes from 71M to 7B parameters.

## Results

GPAS consistently reduces pretraining perplexity across all tested architectures and model scales (71M–1B). At the 1B scale, LNS+GPAS achieves the best perplexity of 14.87 (vs. 15.61 for LNS alone, −0.74), while Pre+GPAS reaches 16.11 (vs. 16.53 for Pre-LN, −0.42). The most dramatic gain is DeepNorm+GPAS at 1B, which drops from a diverged perplexity of ~1400 to 16.01. On downstream 0-shot benchmarks after SFT, LNS+GPAS attains the highest average accuracy of 44.19%, outperforming all baselines by 1.5–13+ points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPAS wins against every baseline it is paired with, across all model sizes. Against Pre-LN (Xiong et al., ICML 2020), GPAS reduces 1B perplexity from 16.53 to 16.11 (−0.42) and 350M from 20.71 to 19.77 (−0.94). Against LNS (Sun et al., 2025), the strongest baseline at 1B, GPAS still yields a meaningful −0.74 PPL improvement (15.61→14.87) and the largest gain at 71M (−1.90). Against Sandwich-LN (Ding et al., NeurIPS 2021) and Mix-LN (Li et al., 2024), GPAS provides consistent 0.4–0.9 PPL reductions at 1B. The most striking comparison is DeepNorm (Wang et al., TPAMI 2024): vanilla DeepNorm diverges at 1B (PPL ~1400), while DeepNorm+GPAS stabilizes training and reaches 16.01, suggesting its fixed residual scaling is suboptimal and GPAS's learnable per-layer gates compensate effectively. Post-LN (Vaswani et al., NeurIPS 2017) also diverges at larger scales and was not directly augmented with GPAS; the authors instead used DeepNorm as the Post-LN starting point. On downstream 0-shot benchmarks (Table 3), LNS+GPAS achieves the best average accuracy (44.19%), beating LNS (42.63%), Mix+GPAS (42.83%), and Pre+GPAS (42.55%), with the largest single-task gain on OBQA (+4.60 over LNS).

## Relation to Prior Work

GPAS is positioned as a lightweight, architecture-agnostic plugin that addresses the well-documented exponential activation variance growth in Pre-LN Transformers (Xiong et al., 2020). Unlike DeepNorm's fixed residual scaling, LNS's depth-dependent normalization scaling, or Sandwich-LN's dual normalization, GPAS introduces a learnable per-layer gate with a stop-gradient operator that decouples forward activation scaling from backward gradient flow. This stop-gradient mechanism is the key conceptual departure: prior methods either scale both forward and backward passes (causing gradient decay) or avoid scaling altogether, whereas GPAS selectively suppresses activation magnitude while preserving gradient magnitude, enabling the network to learn layer-specific mixing ratios between shortcut and sub-layer outputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GPAS offers a simple, drop-in modification that consistently improves convergence and downstream performance across five normalization schemes and five model scales, making it practically attractive for LLM pretraining pipelines. However, the paper is limited to models up to 1B parameters (with partial 7B results), and the learnable gates introduce training instability (gradient spikes around step 10K) that gradient clipping only partially mitigates at a small performance cost. Open questions include whether GPAS scales to 70B+ models, whether predefined gate schedules with theoretical guarantees can replace learnable gates for better stability, and how the method interacts with other training techniques like mixture-of-experts or long-context training.
