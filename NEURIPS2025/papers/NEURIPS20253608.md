## Problem

Existing theoretical guarantees for RLHF either assume direct reward observations or restrict to the Bradley-Terry preference model, leaving the general preference model (which models preferences directly without latent rewards) without tight performance bounds. Moreover, prior algorithm designs rely on optimism or pessimism—constructing confidence bounds or solving optimization problems within confidence sets—which are computationally expensive and difficult to implement in practice. This paper asks whether the KL-regularized structure of RLHF can make the seemingly naive approach of greedy sampling (using empirical estimates directly) provably efficient.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives a context (prompt) drawn from a distribution d₀, samples two actions (one from the learned policy, one from the reference policy), and observes a binary preference label indicating which action is preferred. Under the general preference model, it estimates a preference function P(x,a¹,a²) over action pairs; under the BT model, it estimates a reward function R(x,a). The output is a stochastic policy constrained to the optimal policy class, where every action probability is bounded within a factor of exp(η) of the reference policy—ensuring the policy is always stochastic and shares the same support as the reference.

## Method

The key insight is that KL-regularization confines every candidate optimal policy to a bounded likelihood-ratio regime around the reference policy (Lemma 1: π_f(a|x)/π₀(a|x) ∈ [exp(-η), exp(η)]), a structural property that previous works overlooked. This boundedness allows the per-step regret to be controlled by the MLE estimation error alone, without needing optimistic or pessimistic corrections. The algorithm (Algorithm 1 online, Algorithm 2 offline) simply performs maximum likelihood estimation on the observed preference data within a function class, then directly constructs the optimal policy from the empirical estimate—the NE policy under the general preference model or the Gibbs policy under the BT model. This yields O(log T) regret online and O(1/ε) sample complexity offline under single-policy coverage, matching or improving prior bounds (which required optimism/pessimism) while eliminating the need for confidence-bound construction. The analysis leverages a value decomposition (Lemma 2) that converts the suboptimality gap into a squared estimation error term, with the bounded likelihood ratio enabling all intermediate policies to be converted back to the reference policy.

## Results

Under the general preference model (GP), greedy sampling achieves O(log T) regret in the online setting (Theorem 1) and O(ε⁻¹) sample complexity in the offline setting (Theorem 3), improving upon the O(√T) and O(ε⁻²) bounds of Ye et al. (2024b). Under the Bradley-Terry (BT) model, greedy sampling matches the O(log T) regret (Theorem 2) and O(ε⁻¹) sample complexity (Theorem 4) previously obtained only via optimism/pessimism. Empirically, greedy sampling shows statistically comparable convergence to optimism-based baselines, with slightly higher cumulative regret at larger T, consistent with the additional exp(η) multiplicative constant in the theoretical bounds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Ye et al. (2024b) (GP model, optimism via tournament-style enhancer policy), this paper achieves a strict order-wise improvement: O(log T) regret vs. O(√T) online, and O(ε⁻¹) vs. O(ε⁻²) sample complexity offline, while eliminating the need for the computationally heavy enhancer policy construction. Against Xiong et al. (2023) and Zhao et al. (2025a) (BT model, closed-form optimism with bonus coefficient β), greedy sampling matches the O(log T) regret bound but without solving the confidence-bound optimization; empirically (Figure 2), greedy sampling (β=0) tracks closely with β=0.3 and β=0.5 optimism variants, with only a marginally higher cumulative regret at large T. The paper notes this small gap is explained by the exp(η) factor in the greedy sampling bounds versus the tighter constants achievable with optimism.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the KL-regularized contextual bandit framework and value decomposition from Zhao et al. (2024), and the game-theoretical Nash equilibrium formulation of the general preference model from Munos et al. (2023). It departs from the dominant algorithmic paradigm in RLHF theory—where prior works (Zhao et al. 2025a,b; Ye et al. 2024b; Xiong et al. 2023) all rely on optimism (online) or pessimism (offline) to handle uncertainty—by showing that the KL-regularization's structural property (bounded likelihood ratio between any candidate optimal policy and the reference policy) makes direct greedy sampling from empirical estimates provably sufficient. This eliminates the need for confidence-bound construction or optimization within confidence sets, which are computationally expensive in general function classes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result provides the first O(log T) regret and O(ε⁻¹) sample complexity guarantees for the general preference model in RLHF, substantially improving over prior O(√T) and O(ε⁻²) bounds, while also matching BT-model bounds without the computational overhead of optimism/pessimism. The key structural insight—that KL-regularization confines the optimal policy class to a bounded likelihood-ratio neighborhood of the reference policy—opens a new analytical angle for RLHF that prior work largely overlooked. Limitations include the restriction to single-step (contextual bandit) settings, finite function classes, and an exp(η) multiplicative factor in the bounds; extending to multi-turn RL and tightening the η-dependency are natural open directions. Large-scale empirical validation beyond the small linear experiments would strengthen the practical case for adopting greedy sampling in LLM post-training pipelines.
