## Problem

Global Covariance Pooling (GCP) faces a fundamental trade-off: Newton-Schulz-based iSQRT-COV provides stable early gradients but progressively over-flattens large covariance eigenvalues in later training, capping final accuracy especially at large model scales and batch sizes; SVD-based methods preserve spectral fidelity but suffer from gradient explosions when eigenvalues are close. No existing method unifies early numerical stability with late-stage eigenvalue preservation without resorting to thresholding, polynomial truncation, or mid-training switching protocols.

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images and processes them through conventional CNN or Transformer backbones (ResNet-50/101, ViT-B/16, Swin-T), where the GCP layer replaces the final Global Average Pooling layer. Feature channels are first projected to d=256 via a 1×1 convolution, then a batch-wise covariance matrix is computed and its square root is approximated via K=8 Halley iterations (initialized at X₀=10⁻³I) in float64 precision. The output is a standard class label from the downstream classification head, with the GCP layer contributing a second-order (covariance) representation rather than a first-order mean.

## Method

Halley-SVD replaces the Newton-Schulz iteration in iSQRT-COV with a higher-order Halley matrix iteration for computing the covariance square root: X_{k+1} = X_k [I + ½(I − X_k⁻¹ΣX_k⁻¹)]. The key mechanism is that the second-order correction term in Halley's update applies a gentler downward pull on large eigenvalues compared to Newton-Schulz's aggressive whitening, preventing the over-flattening that stalls iSQRT-COV at scale. The method avoids all explicit 1/(λ_i−λ_j) denominators (unlike SVD backpropagation) and requires no thresholds, polynomial degrees, or hybrid switching schedules. The backward pass is handled by automatic differentiation through the unrolled iterative chain, yielding stable gradients without custom Jacobian implementations. Theoretically, the paper proves (Theorem 1) that Halley's iteration preserves the top eigenvalue closer to √λ₁ than Newton-Schulz for sufficiently large k, and provides a perturbation stability result (Proposition 3) showing the approximation tracks the evolving per-batch covariance throughout training.

## Results

On ImageNet with large batch sizes, Halley-SVD achieves 78.5% Top-1 on ResNet-101 (BS=2048) and 82.9% Top-1 on ViT-B/16 (BS=4096), outperforming iSQRT-COV by 0.8–0.9% Top-1 in these large-scale settings. At moderate batch sizes (256/1024), Halley-SVD ties or marginally exceeds iSQRT-COV (e.g., 77.3% vs. 77.2% on ResNet-50, BS=256). On fine-grained transfer (CUB-200, Stanford Dogs, Stanford Cars), Halley-SVD pre-training yields 88.43% average accuracy, surpassing iSQRT-COV (87.20%) by ~1.2% and SVD-Padé (88.20%) by 0.23%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against iSQRT-COV (Newton-Schulz), Halley-SVD ties at moderate batch sizes (e.g., 77.3% vs. 77.2% Top-1 on ResNet-50, BS=256) but pulls ahead by 0.8–0.9% Top-1 at large batch sizes (ResNet-101 BS=2048: 78.5% vs. 77.7%; ViT-B/16 BS=4096: 82.9% vs. 82.0%; Swin-T BS=4096: 82.3% vs. 81.4%), confirming that iSQRT-COV's over-flattening becomes a bottleneck as scale grows. Against SVD-Padé (E2E), Halley-SVD matches or marginally exceeds it across all settings (e.g., 78.5% vs. 78.1% on ResNet-101 BS=2048; 82.9% vs. 82.4% on ViT-B/16 BS=4096), while avoiding the rational-approximation degree and threshold heuristics that SVD-Padé requires. On fine-grained transfer, Halley-SVD beats iSQRT-COV by ~1.2% average (88.43% vs. 87.20%) and SVD-Padé by 0.23% (88.43% vs. 88.20%), indicating that its more balanced eigenvalue spectrum yields richer discriminative features for subtle inter-class distinctions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established GCP paradigms: the Newton-Schulz-based iSQRT-COV (Li et al., CVPR 2018), which trades spectral fidelity for gradient smoothness, and exact SVD-based MPN-COV (Li et al., ICCV 2017), which preserves eigenvalue structure but suffers from 1/(λi−λj) gradient explosions. It also relates to SVD-Padé (Song et al., ICCV 2021), which stabilizes SVD gradients via rational approximations but requires polynomial-degree hyperparameters. The paper's departure is to replace the Newton-Schulz iteration with Halley's higher-order method, arguing that the second-order correction term naturally tempers the aggressive eigenvalue compression that causes iSQRT-COV's late-stage saturation, while still avoiding explicit eigenvalue-difference denominators.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The over-flattening diagnosis provides a concrete mechanistic explanation for why iSQRT-COV plateaus on deep networks and large batches, which is practically useful for GCP practitioners choosing pooling strategies at scale. However, the gains are modest (0.8–0.9% Top-1 over iSQRT-COV at large batch sizes) and the method introduces matrix inversions (X_k^{-1}) that add computational overhead relative to pure matrix-multiply iterations. Open questions include whether the Halley iteration's advantage persists at even larger scales (e.g., ViT-L, batch >8k), how the iteration count K interacts with the evolving covariance across mini-batches, and whether the theoretical guarantees (Theorem 1) hold under the non-stationary Σ encountered in real training. The paper's scope remains confined to GCP; it does not address whether similar over-flattening phenomena affect other iterative matrix-function approximations in deep learning.
