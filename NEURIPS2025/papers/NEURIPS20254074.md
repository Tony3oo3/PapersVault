## Problem

AI's ability to compress, distill, and interpolate information at scale is collapsing the already-fragile incentives for information production (Arrow's information paradox), creating a structural pathway toward extreme capital concentration that the authors term a 'capital singularity.' No existing institutional mechanism allows the diffuse population of information producers—journalists, researchers, creative professionals, data contributors—to collectively negotiate terms, compensation, and usage constraints with the concentrated AI operators who aggregate and exploit their work.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the analytical input is the existing landscape of information economics (non-rivalry, Arrow's paradox), data-as-labor and data-leverage scholarship, antitrust and data-protection law, and observed AI industry practices (scraping, model distillation, winner-take-all dynamics). The output is a multi-stakeholder action roadmap (Table 1) specifying concrete recommendations for six actor classes—information-producing industries, regulators, ML researchers, HCI communities, AI safety advocates, and large technology companies—along with a coalition-building strategy that sequences legal, technical, and social interventions.

## Method

The paper constructs its argument by synthesizing classical information economics (Arrow's paradox, non-rivalry of information), the data-as-labor and data-leverage literature, collective action theory, and historical precedents of information-market interventions (IP regimes, government R&D funding, antitrust relaxation in network industries). It identifies the specific mechanism by which AI reduces informational frictions (making extraction and exploitation of public information near-free) while simultaneously increasing the economic importance of capital as the binding constraint, thereby tilting returns toward consolidated actors. The proposed intervention—collective bargaining in the information economy (CBI)—creates deliberate market frictions through trusted data intermediary organizations that pool information producers' bargaining power, negotiate licensing terms and compensation, and titrate data access to AI operators. Concrete technical enablers proposed include federated data management with escrow and provenance tracking, data valuation and attribution methods (influence functions, Data Shapley) to reduce information asymmetries in bargaining, and HCI paradigms for individual data-pooling decisions, all coordinated with regulatory safe-harbor guidance to protect collective ventures from antitrust challenge.

## Results

This is a position paper and does not report quantitative experimental results. The paper's central contribution is a normative argument that collective bargaining in the information economy (CBI) is necessary to prevent AI-driven power concentration and 'capital singularity.' The key actionable output is a six-stakeholder agenda (Table 1) spanning data joint ventures, antitrust safe harbors, data valuation research, human-data UX, AI safety advocacy, and tech company engagement. No datasets, benchmarks, or performance metrics are evaluated.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare methods empirically but qualitatively positions CBI against two alternative approaches in Section 1.4. First, top-down state regulation (e.g., AI-specific laws, data-dependence taxes, public ownership) is argued to be insufficient because it is limited in responsiveness and cross-sovereign coordination, and is vulnerable to political capture by well-funded AI labs. Second, a heavy reliance on open AI for power decentralization is argued to fail because frontier models retain a performance advantage over open-source models and because the ability to profit from open models still depends on capital access. The paper claims CBI uniquely addresses both limitations by creating multiple sites of durable market power (trusted data intermediaries) that preserve diverse incentives for information production while constraining the most concentrated capital pools, without requiring a single sovereign to legislate every data-use scenario.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes several established literatures—information economics (Arrow's information paradox, Grossman-Stiglitz), data-as-labor and data-leverage frameworks (Lanier & Weyl, Vincent et al.), data-sharing consortium design (Castro Fernandez, Hardjono & Pentland), and collective action theory (Oliver & Marwell, Doellgast & Benassi)—into a unified policy proposal for 'collective bargaining in the information economy' (CBI). It departs from prior AI-governance work (Kulveit et al., Korinek, Verdegem) that typically focuses on top-down regulation or open-source decentralization by instead proposing a bottom-up, multi-stakeholder bargaining mechanism modeled on labor union structures. The 'capital singularity' framing extends the power-concentration literature by explicitly linking information market failure to non-linear capital consolidation in the AI era.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, multi-stakeholder roadmap (Table 1) that bridges technical (data valuation, federated data management), legal (antitrust safe harbors), and social (guild/syndicate formation) interventions, making it a useful coordination document for researchers, policymakers, and industry actors working on AI governance. Key open questions include whether large-scale collective action among diffuse information producers is practically feasible, whether existing data-valuation techniques (Shapley values, influence functions) are mature enough to support real-time bargaining, and how antitrust law will actually respond to sector-wide data joint ventures. As a position paper, it offers no empirical validation of its core claims about market dynamics, leaving the causal chain from CBI to reduced power concentration as a theoretical argument rather than a demonstrated result.
