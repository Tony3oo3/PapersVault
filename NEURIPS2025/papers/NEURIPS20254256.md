## Problem

Geospatial prediction (estimating socio-economic indicators from urban regions) and geographic information retrieval (ranking POIs for spatial keyword queries) have been studied as isolated tasks requiring fundamentally different features and separate models. This fragmentation imposes redundant computational costs and prevents the complementary strengths of each task from enhancing the other, despite the natural synergy between fine-grained textual matching and holistic urban context.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254256/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes spatial keyword queries with GPS coordinates, POI text descriptions with geographic coordinates, and urban region boundaries (constructed via Delaunay triangulation). Text is decomposed using multiple n-gram and dictionary-based tokenizers (1-gram, 2-gram, Jieba) and encoded into fixed-length Bloom filter bit vectors (m=8192, k=2 SHA-256 hashes), yielding sparse representations with only 2–3% density. Outputs include a ranked POI list scored by a combined text-similarity and logarithmic geographic-distance relevance function, a 64-dimensional urban region embedding, and regression estimates for population density and house price.

## Method

UrbanSparse employs a two-view learning architecture: an Individual View that encodes query and object text into Bloom filters and evaluates each bit's importance via a shared dense codebook followed by non-linear compression and an evaluation layer (preserving term-matching capability), and a Collective View that applies dual 2-layer GCNs on a region graph and a K-hop context graph to maximize mutual information between intra-region and surrounding bit distributions, filtering out uninformative terms. A two-phase training strategy first warms up the shared codebook on prediction tasks for 2–3 epochs, then alternates prediction and retrieval batches to balance holistic region features with fine-grained query-object matches. Row- and column-selection optimizations exploit Bloom filter sparsity to skip zero-bit computations in the codebook and evaluation layers, reducing training time by 65.97% and inference memory by 86.49% compared to BERT-based baselines.

## Results

On POI retrieval, UrbanSparse achieves NDCG@1 of 0.4991 (Beijing) and 0.5289 (Shanghai), surpassing the strongest baseline DPR-D (0.4132 / 0.4554) by 20.76% and 16.71% respectively. On population density prediction, UrbanSparse attains R² of 0.75 (Beijing) and 0.78 (Shanghai), improving MAE by 25.16% and 22.90% over the best baseline CityFM. In retrieval, UrbanSparse also reduces training time by 65.97% and inference memory by 86.49% compared to BERT-based methods, while achieving ~3.6× higher query throughput (476 QPS vs. 133 QPS for DPR-D).

## Baselines & Comparisons

UrbanSparse outperforms all 12 baselines across both cities. In prediction, it beats the strongest baseline CityFM (R²=0.66/0.67) by 13.6%/16.4% in R² for population density, and surpasses HGI (R²=0.59/0.46) by a wide margin; graph contrastive methods DGI and MVGRL perform poorly (R²≈0.17–0.47) due to loss of fine-grained text features. In retrieval, UrbanSparse exceeds DPR-D (the strongest BERT-based baseline) by 20.76% in NDCG@1 on Beijing and 16.71% on Shanghai, and outperforms DrW by 31.8%/28.7% in NDCG@1. Notably, the classical BM25-D significantly outperforms BERT-D and OpenAI-D, confirming that term-matching is critical for geographic retrieval; UrbanSparse preserves this via Bloom filters while adding learned relevance. On the large-scale GeoGLUE benchmark (2.85M POIs), UrbanSparse matches DPR-D within 0.4% on NDCG@5 while training 2.4× faster (54 vs. 131 min), and slightly edges out MGeo on Recall@10 (0.7621 vs. N/A) and NDCG@5 (0.6344 vs. N/A). The ablation variant without the Collective View (w/o Collective) drops to NDCG@1 of 0.4990/0.5315, confirming that prediction-task signals contribute ~1–2% retrieval gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

UrbanSparse builds on the graph contrastive learning lineage of DGI and MVGRL, adapting their mutual information maximization to a two-graph (region + context) setting inspired by Tobler's Second Law of Geography, as also explored in Zhou et al.'s road network work. Its key departure is from the dominant PLM-based paradigm (BERT, DPR, DrW) that underpins both geospatial prediction and retrieval: the paper argues that city-specific terms (addresses, local business names) are poorly captured by pre-trained semantic embeddings and are better served by fine-grained lexical matching via Bloom filters. The two-view architecture—sparse Bloom filter encoding for retrieval and dense codebook + GCN contrastive learning for prediction—repurposes classical information-retrieval primitives (BOW, term matching) within a modern self-supervised graph learning framework, bridging a gap that prior work in either domain had not addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework to jointly optimize geospatial prediction and retrieval, demonstrating that the two tasks are complementary rather than conflicting: retrieval labels improve prediction by a few percent, and prediction context improves retrieval by ~5–20%. The efficiency story (66% faster training, 86% less inference memory, 3.6× higher QPS) makes it practically relevant for large-scale urban data where BERT-based methods are prohibitive. However, generalization is untested beyond two Chinese cities with Meituan POI data, the prediction tasks are limited to known-to-known area estimation (no spatio-temporal forecasting), and the GeoGLUE scalability test shows only marginal gains over DPR-D, suggesting the advantage may narrow at scale. Open questions include whether the Bloom filter approach transfers to multilingual or low-resource urban contexts, and whether fairness concerns from POI-based training can be mitigated.
