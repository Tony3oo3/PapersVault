## Problem

Standard Transformer decoders in ASR systems perform redundant computation: analysis of attention patterns reveals that early decoder layers build text-only context (making their cross-attention modules ineffective) while later layers merge acoustic and textual information for final predictions. This unexploited separation of roles, combined with strictly one-token-at-a-time autoregressive generation, results in unnecessary inference latency without a principled architectural redesign to address it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255716/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes raw speech audio as input, which is processed by a shared audio encoder (e.g., Conformer or E-Branchformer) into contextualized acoustic features H of length T. The BLOCKDECODER then autoregressively generates text tokens in blocks of K tokens per step (rather than one token per step), conditioned on both H and a text-only context vector C. For the SLU task, the decoded output is further parsed to extract intent labels and entity tags. The block-based generation means the MERGER conditions on the same precomputed encoder representations across all K positions within a block, enabling efficient reuse.

## Method

BLOCKDECODER replaces the standard decoder with two specialized modules: a text encoder (N_T self-attention-only layers) that builds rich text-only context C from the token sequence, and a lightweight MERGER (N_M layers with self-attention plus two cross-attention modules attending to C and H respectively) that autoregressively generates K tokens per block. The key insight driving this design is that cross-attention is under-utilized in early decoder layers while self-attention becomes strongly diagonal in later layers, so separating global context building from late audio-text integration eliminates redundant computation. The MERGER's block-based generation (predicting K future tokens from each position using the same encoder contexts) enables three inference strategies—naive, iterative, and full block averaging—that trade off latency for accuracy. With the majority of layers allocated to the text encoder and only a small number to the MERGER, the architecture achieves ~2× RTF speedup with no WER degradation across multiple datasets, languages, and tasks.

## Results

On Librispeech-100h with E-Branchformer encoder, BLOCKDECODER (iterative block decoding) achieves 6.19/16.94 WER (Test Clean/Other) versus 6.39/17.03 for the standard Transformer decoder, while reducing RTF from 1.52 to 0.67 (~2.3× speedup). On the full Librispeech 960h dataset, BLOCKDECODER matches the strongest baseline E-Branchformer at 2.1/4.4 WER (vs. 2.1/4.6) with a 2.6× RTF improvement (7.73→2.98). Across five non-English languages (Chinese, Czech, Italian, Japanese, Tamil) and the SLURP SLU task, BLOCKDECODER consistently matches or slightly outperforms the baseline WER/CER while maintaining 1.5–2.4× RTF gains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is against the standard Transformer decoder with the same encoder (Conformer or E-Branchformer): BLOCKDECODER matches or slightly improves WER (e.g., 6.19 vs. 6.39 on Librispeech-100h Test Clean with E-Branchformer) while achieving 1.7–2.4× RTF speedup. Against other ASR systems on Librispeech 960h (Table 2), BLOCKDECODER (2.1/4.4 WER) outperforms Branchformer (2.4/5.5), Transformer Transducer (2.4/5.6), ContextNet (2.1/4.6), QuartzNet (3.9/11.3), and the earlier hybrid Conformer (2.9/7.0) and Transformer (2.9/7.0) baselines. Against CTC-only models (Appendix C), BLOCKDECODER (6.19/16.94 WER on Librispeech-100h) substantially outperforms vanilla CTC-only (10.05/23.87) and InterCTC (8.80/21.29), confirming that the autoregressive decoder component remains essential for accuracy. A simplified variant that merely removes cross-attention from early decoder layers (decoder_efficient) achieves similar RTF (0.73) but degrades WER (6.51/17.26 vs. 6.19/16.94), validating the full BLOCKDECODER design. On the SLU task (SLURP), BLOCKDECODER matches the baseline on intent accuracy (87.1% vs. 87.2%) and F1 (0.78 vs. 0.78) while halving RTF (3.11→1.31).

## Relation to Prior Work

BLOCKDECODER builds directly on the hybrid CTC/attention framework [Watanabe et al., 2017], replacing the standard Transformer decoder with a two-module design: a text-only encoder and a lightweight MERGER. The architectural motivation comes from a systematic analysis of attention patterns in existing decoders—specifically that cross-attention is underutilized in early layers and self-attention becomes strongly diagonal in later layers—a phenomenon previously noted in audio encoders but never analyzed in the decoder. Conceptually, the separation of text context building from audio-text integration echoes the predictor/joiner split in RNN-Transducer [He et al., 2019], but with multi-layer attention in the merger rather than a simple feedforward joiner. The block-based autoregressive generation also connects to recent block-transformer and speculative decoding work, though BLOCKDECODER's block structure is baked into the architecture rather than being a decoding-time trick.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~2x inference speedup without WER degradation across multiple languages, datasets, and encoder architectures makes this a practically significant contribution for ASR deployment, particularly in latency-sensitive or resource-constrained settings. The modular design (independent text encoder and MERGER layer counts) offers a clean knob for the accuracy-latency tradeoff, and the paper notes that the text encoder can be swapped for a pretrained LLM, opening a path toward ASR-LLM integration. Limitations include minimal GPU RTF gains (bookkeeping dominates over decoder compute) and the observation that full block averaging, while slightly improving WER, negates the speed advantage. Open questions include adaptation to streaming settings, tasks with shorter encoder outputs, and whether the attention-pattern insight generalizes beyond ASR to other encoder-decoder tasks like summarization.
