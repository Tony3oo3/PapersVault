## Problem

Human preference data used for LLM alignment is often noisy or inconsistent, yet no standardized benchmark exists to systematically compare the 13+ proposed automated cleaning methods in terms of their actual impact on alignment quality and their generalizability across datasets, model architectures, and optimization algorithms. This paper asks: which cleaning strategies consistently produce better-aligned models, and under what conditions do their benefits hold?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254149/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes preference triplets (prompt, chosen response, rejected response) from four datasets (Anthropic-HH, UltraFeedback, PKU-SafeRLHF, HelpSteer2) and applies each of 13 cleaning methods to produce a cleaned dataset. Evaluation then trains a base LLM via SFT followed by a preference optimization algorithm (DPO, CPO, SLiC, KTO, AOT, IPO, rDPO, or ORPO) on both cleaned and original data, and compares the resulting models using two metrics: win-tie rate (measured by GPT-4o as judge over 200 test samples with positional-bias mitigation) and average gold reward (scored by a held-out URM-LLaMa-3.1-8B reward model). Generalizability is further assessed by repeating the pipeline across five base models (Llama3-8B, Llama3.2-1B, Qwen2.5-7B, Mistral-7B, phi-2) and eight optimization algorithms.

## Method

PrefCleanBench unifies 13 data cleaning approaches under a three-paradigm taxonomy—LLM-as-a-judge (GPT-4o re-annotation), reward model scoring (reward gap or multi-model voting), and heuristic criteria (prompt complexity/diversity via InsTag, instruction-following difficulty via IFD)—each with removal (R) and label-flipping (F) variants. The key protocol insight is that both the identification strategy and the corrective treatment independently affect alignment: the paper shows removal consistently outperforms flipping because unreliability often stems from low-quality prompts rather than mislabeled responses, making label correction counterproductive. For identification, multi-judge voting (VoteMaj-R, using six diverse reward models from RewardBench) outperforms single-judge methods because it better flags cases where both responses are low-quality (e.g., both harmful), which a single LLM judge forced to pick a winner would retain. The benchmark further reveals that optimal filtering ratios cluster around 20–30%, and that the interaction between cleaning method and optimization algorithm is non-trivial (e.g., VoteMaj-R pairs better with AOT/ORPO, while Tag-Cmp pairs better with KTO/rDPO).

## Results

Using Llama3-8B with DPO as the main setting, VoteMaj-R (majority-vote reward model filtering) achieves the highest average gold reward across all four datasets: 7.287 (Anthropic-HH), 4.253 (UltraFeedback), 8.478 (PKU-SafeRLHF), and 6.834 (HelpSteer2), compared to vanilla baselines of 6.001, 4.109, 6.318, and 6.509 respectively. Tag-Cmp (InsTag complexity filtering) achieves the highest win-tie rate on Anthropic-HH (0.760) and PKU-SafeRLHF (0.780). Removing unreliable data (R variants) consistently outperforms flipping labels (F variants) across nearly all settings, and the optimal filtering ratio for methods requiring an explicit threshold (RwGap-R, IFD-Gap-R) falls between 20%–30%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 13 cleaning methods spanning three paradigms against a vanilla (no-cleaning) baseline. VoteMaj-R (RM majority voting from Yeh et al. [19]) is the strongest overall method, achieving the highest average gold reward on all four datasets (e.g., 8.478 vs. 6.318 vanilla on PKU-SafeRLHF, a +34% improvement) and the highest or near-highest win-tie rate. Tag-Cmp (InsTag complexity from Lu et al. [73]) is the second-best, leading in win-tie rate on Anthropic-HH (0.760) and PKU-SafeRLHF (0.780) but underperforming VoteMaj-R on average reward. RwGap-R (reward gap from Wang et al. [62]) is competitive on win-tie rate (0.680 on Anthropic-HH) but lags VoteMaj-R on average reward (6.333 vs. 7.287). IFD-Gap-R (from Li et al. [74]) shows strong win-tie rates (0.730–0.770) but lower average rewards than VoteMaj-R. LLM-Judge methods (single GPT-4o judge) underperform all multi-judge and heuristic approaches, particularly on HelpSteer2 where LLM-Judge-R drops to 0.470 win-tie and 5.657 avg. reward. Across all methods, the removal (R) treatment consistently beats the label-flipping (F) treatment, with VoteMaj-R outperforming VoteMaj-F by 0.277 in avg. reward on PKU-SafeRLHF (8.478 vs. 3.179).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not build on a single prior method but rather unifies and systematically compares 13 existing preference data cleaning approaches spanning three paradigms: LLM-as-a-judge (GPT-4o scoring), reward-model-based (RwGap from Wang et al., RM voting from Yeh et al.), and heuristic-driven (InsTag, IFD). While individual cleaning methods have been proposed in isolation, the authors identify a critical gap: no standardized protocol exists for fairly comparing these methods across datasets, model architectures, and optimization algorithms. PrefCleanBench carves new ground by providing the first unified benchmarking framework that evaluates all 13 methods under controlled, reproducible conditions, positioning data preprocessing as a first-class component of the alignment pipeline rather than an afterthought.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides practitioners with clear, actionable guidance: removing unreliable data consistently outperforms flipping labels, and multi-judge voting (VoteMaj-R) generalizes better across models and algorithms than single-judge or heuristic approaches. A key limitation is the absence of ground-truth labels for data quality, forcing indirect evaluation through downstream alignment performance rather than direct precision/recall of noise detection. Open questions include developing cost-effective noise identification with human oversight, creating curated benchmarks with partially verified labels for direct cleaning-accuracy evaluation, and understanding the non-trivial interaction between cleaning strategies and specific optimization algorithms (e.g., AOT/ORPO favoring VoteMaj-R while KTO/rDPO favor Tag-Cmp).
