## Problem

Computing convex conjugates (Legendre-Fenchel transforms) in high dimensions is intractable for classical grid-based methods, which scale exponentially with dimension. Existing neural network approaches to convex conjugation have been studied primarily as tools for optimal transport and require solving complicated optimization or max-min problems. This paper introduces a general-purpose deep learning framework that directly approximates convex conjugates via gradient-based training, without requiring the conjugate in closed form or solving auxiliary optimization problems.

## Input / Output

The method takes a differentiable convex function f: C → ℝ (with accessible gradient ∇f) defined on an open convex set C ⊆ ℝ^d, and produces a neural network g_θ: D → ℝ approximating the convex conjugate f* on the dual image D = ∇f(C). A key feature is that the same residual evaluated on a held-out test set yields an unbiased Monte Carlo estimate of the L² approximation error ‖g_θ − f*‖²_{L²(D,ν)} even when f* has no closed form. When instantiated with KANs, the output can be a recovered symbolic expression (e.g., polynomial, exp, log) rather than a black-box network.

## Method

The core insight is the implicit Fenchel-Young identity f*(∇f(x)) = ⟨x, ∇f(x)⟩ − f(x), which makes the training targets exactly computable from f and ∇f alone, eliminating the need for f* in closed form. DLT trains a neural network g_θ by minimizing the empirical squared residual Σ_{x∈X_train}(g_θ(∇f(x)) + f(x) − ⟨x, ∇f(x)⟩)², where the residual equals (g_θ(y) − f*(y))² on the gradient manifold. The approximator can be an MLP, ResNet, ICNN (which guarantees convex output), or KAN (which enables symbolic regression to recover exact closed-form conjugates). When the gradient map ∇f heavily distorts the primal domain, a learned generalized inverse h_θ of ∇f is used to sample training points so that their images under ∇f follow a desired distribution on D. As a byproduct, the test-set residual provides a posteriori L² error certificates with confidence intervals via the central limit theorem, applicable to any candidate approximator regardless of how it was trained.

## Results

DLT with a ResNet(128,128) achieves RMSE of 2.14e-02 (d=2) to 1.32e-01 (d=10) on the Negative Log function, while Lucet's grid method (N=10) degrades from 3.65e-01 (d=2) to 2.93e+01 (d=8) and becomes infeasible at d=10 due to O(N^d) memory. For the quadratic-over-linear function with no closed-form conjugate, DLT achieves L² errors from 6.38e-04 (d=2) to 3.68e-02 (d=100) in under 40 minutes. When the conjugate is known analytically, DLT matches direct supervised learning to within statistical noise (e.g., ResNet RMSE 2.43e-5 vs. 3.00e-5 for the quadratic at d=10), confirming the implicit formulation introduces no accuracy penalty.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Lucet's Linear Time Legendre Transform (the state-of-the-art classical grid method), DLT wins decisively in accuracy and scalability: at d=8, DLT achieves RMSE 1.33e-01 versus Lucet's 2.93e+01 (a 200× improvement) for the Negative Log function, while using 1/1400th the memory (1.1 MB vs. 1530 MB) and 75× less solve time. At d=10, Lucet is entirely infeasible due to O(N^d) storage, whereas DLT completes in ~32 seconds with constant memory. Against DGM (Deep Galerkin Method) for Hamilton-Jacobi PDEs, Time-DLT outperforms DGM at larger times: at d=10, t=2.0, Time-DLT achieves L² error 2.72e-02 versus DGM's 1.64e-01 (a 6× improvement), with the error ratio ρ growing from 0.95 at t=0.1 to 8.64 at t=2.0, indicating DGM's PDE-residual minimization does not guarantee small solution error away from the initial condition.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Fenchel-Young identity f*(∇f(x)) = ⟨x, ∇f(x)⟩ − f(x) as a direct training target for neural networks, departing from prior ML approaches to convex conjugation (Dam et al. 2019, Makkuva et al. 2020, Korotin et al. 2021, Amos 2023) that either learn an inverse gradient map or solve max-min/optimal-transport formulations. It also extends the classical grid-based literature (Lucet 1997, Brenier 1989) and the softmax/Gaussian-convolution approach (Peyré 2020, Blondel & Roulet 2024) by replacing the exponential-in-dimension grid with a parametric neural approximator, while adding a novel a posteriori L² error certificate derived from the push-forward measure. The use of KANs for symbolic regression to recover exact closed-form conjugates is a new application of that architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DLT provides a practical, scalable tool for high-dimensional convex conjugation with built-in error certification, which is valuable for downstream tasks in optimal control, optimal transport, and Hamilton-Jacobi PDEs where the conjugate is needed but unavailable in closed form. The a posteriori error estimate (valid for any candidate approximator, not just DLT) is a broadly useful diagnostic. Key limitations remain: the method requires f to be differentiable and convex, and it only approximates f* on the gradient image D = ∇f(C) rather than the full domain of f*, leaving the extension to non-smooth or non-convex settings open. The HJ PDE application, while promising, currently relies on an explicit g* for one of the two conjugates in the Hopf formula, so full end-to-end numerical treatment of both conjugates in high dimensions remains untested.
