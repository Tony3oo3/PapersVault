## Problem

Training a single general whole-body control policy for humanoid robots on diverse motion datasets (e.g., AMASS) suffers from conflicting gradients and mismatched data distributions, because aggressive motions like jumping demand high-torque control while conservative motions prioritize balance. Existing frameworks like ASAP excel at single-motion sim-to-real transfer but cannot be directly applied to general motion tracking without degrading performance across motion types.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254941/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy consumes a reference motion sequence (retargeted joint angles and root pose from AMASS via SMPL), proprioceptive state (23-DoF joint positions/velocities, base linear/angular velocity, base quaternion orientation), and the last action, producing target joint positions for a 23-DoF PD controller at 50 Hz inference with 200 Hz low-level control. The teacher policy observes 5 timesteps of privileged information including global keypoint positions, while the student policy uses 10 timesteps of local-coordinate observations. The delta action model additionally receives the tracking policy's actions alongside full proprioception to output corrective action offsets.

## Method

BumbleBee follows a four-stage expert-to-generalist pipeline. First, a Transformer-based autoencoder trained with contrastive alignment (InfoNCE + L2) between motion latents (3D joint positions, root translation/velocity, foot contact states, foot velocities) and text latents (BERT-encoded HumanML3D descriptions) produces embeddings that are K-means clustered into 6 behaviorally coherent groups (jump, walk-slow, walk-fast, stand-up, stand-mid, stand-low). Second, a general tracking policy trained on the full dataset is fine-tuned per cluster to yield specialized expert policies. Third, each expert is deployed on a real Unitree G1 to collect trajectories, from which cluster-specific delta action models are trained to compensate sim-to-real discrepancies; experts are then iteratively fine-tuned within the delta-modified simulation. Finally, all experts are distilled into a single Transformer-based generalist via DAgger with KL-divergence loss, where the Transformer backbone provides the capacity to fuse multi-expert knowledge that a three-layer MLP cannot accommodate.

## Results

On the filtered AMASS dataset, BumbleBee (BB) achieves a success rate of 89.58% in IsaacGym and 66.84% in MuJoCo, outperforming all baselines in both simulators. In MuJoCo (the more realistic evaluation environment), BB also attains the lowest Mean Per Joint Position Error (0.2356 rad) and Mean Per Keypoint Position Error (294.27 mm), compared to the next-best Exbody2 (0.3576 rad and 272.42 mm respectively). The performance gap is most pronounced in MuJoCo, where BB's success rate exceeds the runner-up by 16.65 percentage points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BB is compared against three SOTA whole-body control methods: OmniH2O, Exbody2, and Hover, all adapted to the Unitree G1 robot with the same training dataset. In IsaacGym, BB leads all three baselines in success rate (89.58% vs. 86.63% for Exbody2, 85.65% for OmniH2O, and 63.21% for Hover) and MPJPE (0.1907 vs. 0.2630–0.2937), though the margins are modest. In MuJoCo, the advantage is far more decisive: BB achieves 66.84% SR, surpassing Exbody2 (50.19%) by 16.65 points and OmniH2O (15.64%) by over 50 points, while also posting the best MPJPE (0.2356 vs. 0.3428–0.4601). Exbody2 achieves the lowest MPKPE in MuJoCo (272.42 mm vs. BB's 294.27 mm), but its substantially lower success rate makes this metric less meaningful. An internal ablation (General Init, trained without expert specialization) confirms that the expert-to-generalist pipeline is responsible for the MuJoCo gains, as it only reaches 33.01% SR.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ASAP framework's delta action model for sim-to-real transfer by specializing it per motion cluster rather than using a single unified model, and adopts DAgger for multi-expert knowledge distillation into a generalist controller. Conceptually, it draws on the mixture-of-experts paradigm from LLMs, applying it to humanoid whole-body control: motions are clustered via an autoencoder that jointly encodes kinematic features (joint positions, foot velocities, contact states) and text descriptions (via BERT), then specialized expert policies are trained per cluster before distillation. This departs from prior general WBC methods (OmniH2O, Exbody2, Hover) that train a single policy on the full AMASS dataset, which the authors argue causes gradient conflicts due to mismatched data distributions across motion types.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The expert-to-generalist paradigm offers a practical recipe for handling the data-distribution conflict that plagues multi-task humanoid control, and the cluster-specific delta action models provide a more principled sim-to-real bridge than a single unified compensator. The MuJoCo success rate gap (66.84% vs. 50.19% for the next-best baseline) suggests the approach meaningfully improves robustness in more realistic dynamics. However, the pipeline's complexity (autoencoder training, per-cluster expert training, iterative real-world data collection, distillation) limits scalability, and the absence of high-precision localization sensors (GPS/VIO) constrains real-world deployment. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond the Unitree G1, how it scales to larger motion libraries, and whether the Transformer-based generalist can be replaced by lighter architectures without losing the cross-expert knowledge fusion benefit.
