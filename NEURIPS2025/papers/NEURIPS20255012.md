## Problem

Existing ride-hailing dispatch methods face a fundamental trade-off: reinforcement learning captures long-term dynamics but requires extensive training data and struggles to enforce hard operational constraints, while decomposed optimization frameworks rely on manually designed high-level objectives that are blind to low-level routing dynamics, producing myopic assignments. No prior work integrates LLMs with mathematical optimization solvers in a dynamic, closed-loop setting for sequential decision-making, leaving a gap between semantic adaptability and constraint-enforced feasibility.

## Input / Output

At each timestep, the system consumes real-time vehicle position vectors, pending passenger OD requests with request timestamps, and historical congestion/assignment context, all embedded into a structured prompt alongside a geospatial zone graph and OD matrix. The LLM outputs a semantic objective function in solver-compatible form for the high-level assignment MILP, while the Gurobi solver produces binary passenger-to-taxi assignments and per-vehicle pickup/dropoff visiting sequences. Unusually, the LLM's output is not a decision but an objective function that is itself evolved over simulation epochs via harmony search, with fitness evaluated by end-to-end simulation performance rather than a fixed reward.

## Method

The framework hierarchically decomposes each decision step into a high-level assignment MILP (assigning passengers to taxis) and a low-level routing MILP (determining pickup/dropoff sequences per taxi), with the LLM replacing handcrafted high-level objectives by generating them adaptively from real-time state. A harmony search algorithm with three operators—random inference (exploration), heuristic improvement (exploitation via parent refinement), and innovative generation (exploitation via parent re-interpretation)—iteratively evolves the LLM prompt population, using simulation-based fitness scores as selection pressure. The system operates in closed-loop: at every timestep, the LLM is queried with the current environment state to produce an updated objective, which the Gurobi solver then solves to enforce spatiotemporal feasibility. This training-free design eliminates the data requirements of RL while the solver integration ensures constraint satisfaction that LLM-only methods (FunSearch, EoH) cannot guarantee, achieving ~16% average waiting-time reduction over state-of-the-art baselines on New York and Chicago taxi data.

## Results

On the New York (Manhattan) taxi dataset, the proposed LLM+Optimizer hybrid achieves an average passenger waiting time of 2.59 min across 9 scenarios, outperforming the best baseline (FunSearch, 4.38 min avg.) by ~41% and Full RL (4.52 min avg.) by ~43%. In the largest-scale scenario (P200_C100_T1200), the method reduces waiting time to 4.01 min versus 6.82 min for Full RL and 10.27 min for FunSearch. On the Chicago dataset, the method achieves 12.03 min average waiting time, a ~18% improvement over the best baseline (FunSearch, 14.70 min avg.), with the gap widening to 29% in Scenario 9 (15.37 vs. 21.74 min).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all six baselines in 7 of 9 Manhattan scenarios, losing only to FunSearch in the two smallest-scale cases (S1: 1.55 vs. 0.77 min; S3: 2.50 vs. 1.55 min). Against the strongest RL baseline (Full RL [6]), the gap widens dramatically with scale: from a 0.28 min margin in S1 to a 2.81 min (41%) margin in S9. FunSearch [15], the best LLM-only baseline, degrades sharply under high demand (10.27 min in S9 vs. 4.01 min for Ours), revealing its lack of per-step dynamic feedback and constraint enforcement. EoH [16] shows moderate performance in mid-scale scenarios but trails Ours by 2.5–5.0 min in large-scale settings. Manual composite objectives (Distance×Temporal×Utilization) and the two-stage MILP baselines (Simonetto [2], Rossi [24]) are consistently outperformed by 2–20 min, confirming that handcrafted high-level objectives fail to capture low-level routing dynamics. On the Chicago dataset, Ours wins 8 of 9 scenarios, with the largest margin over Full RL reaching 4.66 min in S9 (15.37 vs. 20.03 min).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the EoH/FunSearch line of work (LLM-driven heuristic generation for static combinatorial optimization) into the dynamic, sequential decision-making domain of ride-hailing dispatch. The key conceptual departure is the hierarchical decomposition: LLM generates high-level assignment objectives as semantic heuristics, while a MILP solver (Gurobi) enforces spatiotemporal constraints at the low-level routing stage—something absent in prior LLM-for-OR work that either produces full solutions or static formulations. The evolutionary refinement mechanism adapts harmony search (a classical metaheuristic) to operate over LLM prompt space rather than numerical parameter vectors, with three novel operators (random inference, heuristic improvement, innovative generation) guided by solver feasibility feedback. Unlike RL-based dispatch methods that require extensive interaction data, this framework is training-free, and unlike decomposed optimization with handcrafted objectives, it adaptively evolves the high-level objective to account for downstream routing dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that LLMs can serve as effective meta-objective designers in dynamic optimization loops, bridging the gap between semantic reasoning and mathematical rigor without the data hunger of RL. The ~16% average improvement (up to 40% in large-scale scenarios) over strong baselines including Full RL and FunSearch validates the closed-loop LLM-optimizer interaction, particularly as problem scale increases where static objectives and RL policies degrade. Key limitations include reliance on simulation without fine-grained traffic dynamics (congestion, signals), the LLM operating only at the prompt level without fine-tuning, and the computational cost of per-timestep LLM queries that may challenge real-time deployment. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes to other dynamic dispatch domains (logistics, drone delivery), whether co-adaptation between LLM and optimizer (e.g., RL fine-tuning the LLM using solver feedback) could further close the gap, and how the approach scales to city-wide deployments with thousands of vehicles.
