## Problem

Existing synthetic tampered text image datasets focus on visible attributes (scene variety, scale, language diversity) but fail to model the 'invisible distribution'—the complex, high-dimensional combinations of tampering parameters (operation sequences, post-processing steps, and their specific configurations) that characterize real-world forgeries. This distribution gap causes models trained on synthetic data to generalize poorly to real-world text image forgery localization (T-IFL) scenarios, where forgers apply multi-step, scenario-dependent editing pipelines that are visually imperceptible yet critically shape forgery trace diversity.

## Input / Output

The FSTS framework takes original text images (documents, receipts, screenshots, certificates) and synthesizes tampered versions by applying parameterized operations across five tampering types (copy-move, splicing, removal, insertion, replacement), with each operation guided by specific parameter configurations (e.g., blur radius, noise level, content-aware fill settings) sampled from a learned distribution. The resulting tampered images are cropped into 512×512 patches, and ground-truth pixel-level binary masks are generated by thresholding the difference between tampered and original images. The downstream T-IFL models consume these 512×512 RGB patches and output pixel-level forgery localization masks.

## Method

FSTS (Fourier Series-based Tampering Synthesis) is a structured, interpretable framework that models the invisible distribution of real-world tampering parameters using a hierarchical Fourier-series-inspired decomposition. First, a structured collection pipeline recruits 67 participants to perform 16,750 tampering instances in Photoshop, with operation histories automatically recorded via multi-format logs (video, PSD files, editing logs) to recover fine-grained parameter vectors. Second, analysis reveals recurring behavioral patterns at both individual and population levels, enabling each individual's tampering distribution to be approximated as a sparse weighted combination of representative basis operation-parameter configurations (analogous to Fourier basis functions), with the population distribution constructed by aggregating individual contributions. Third, synthetic tampered images are generated by sampling operation-parameter configurations and their frequencies from the modeled distribution and applying them to original text images via an automated Photoshop scripting pipeline, producing 294,182 samples that better reflect real-world forgery trace diversity than rule-based or generative-model-based alternatives.

## Results

Under Protocol 2 (synthetic training → real-world testing), models trained on FSTS-T consistently outperform those trained on DocT-T across all five real-world benchmarks (FSTS-1.5k, AFAC, CertificatePS, STFD, FindIt). The average F1 gain across all seven methods exceeds 14%, with the largest gains seen in TruFor (+27.9% F1), DTD (+24.9% F1), and DFCN (+22.5% F1). Under Protocol 4 (synthetic pretraining + real fine-tuning), FSTS-T pretraining yields positive gains over direct real-data training for most methods (e.g., DFCN: F1 0.400 vs 0.324 on FindIt; TruFor: F1 0.498 vs 0.459), while DocT-T pretraining often produces negative gains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates seven detection/localization methods (five N-IFL: RRU-Net, DFCN, PSCC-Net, MVSS-Net, TruFor; two T-IFL: DTD, STFL-Net) under identical training/testing splits, comparing DocT-T vs. FSTS-T as the training source. FSTS-T wins on the average real-world F1 for all seven methods, with gains ranging from +14.3% (RRU-Net) to +27.9% (TruFor). The largest absolute improvements appear on FSTS-1.5k (the held-out real set), where FSTS-T-trained models achieve F1 scores of 0.54–0.68 versus 0.08–0.23 for DocT-T. PSCC-Net is the one method where FSTS-T does not uniformly dominate: it slightly underperforms DocT-T on AFAC (F1 0.099 vs 0.091) and STFD (F1 0.209 vs 0.261), likely due to its limited ability to extract features from low-texture text images. Under Protocol 4 (pretraining + fine-tuning on CertificatePS), FSTS-T pretraining yields consistent positive gains over direct real-data training for DFCN (+7.6% F1 on FindIt), TruFor (+3.8% F1), and STFL-Net (+3.4% F1), whereas DocT-T pretraining produces negative or negligible gains for most methods, confirming FSTS-T's superiority as a pretraining source.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of two established lines of work: text image forgery localization (T-IFL) methods (STFL-Net, DTD) and synthetic tampered data generation (DocTamper's rule-based pipeline, GAN/diffusion-based text editing). It departs from prior synthetic approaches by arguing they model only 'visible' attributes (scene variety, scale, language) while ignoring the 'invisible' high-dimensional distribution of tampering parameters (operation sequences, post-processing choices, parameter values). The Fourier series analogy is repurposed as an interpretive framework: tampering parameter configurations serve as basis functions, and their learned frequency weights approximate the real-world tampering distribution, enabling structured sampling rather than random or rule-based generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the largest publicly available T-IFL dataset (294K images) and a principled synthesis framework that consistently improves cross-domain generalization (average F1 gain >14% over DocTamper across 7 methods and 5 real-world benchmarks). The visible-vs-invisible distribution distinction offers a useful diagnostic lens for evaluating synthetic data quality in forgery detection more broadly. Key limitations include reliance on Photoshop-based tampering (excluding AI-generated forgeries), the strong assumption that 67 participants capture sufficient behavioral diversity, and the simplification t_k ≈ t̂_k that reduces distribution matching to coefficient alignment. Open questions include whether the framework extends to generative-AI forgeries and whether the Fourier-series decomposition generalizes beyond the five studied tampering types.
