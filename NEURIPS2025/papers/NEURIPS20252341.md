## Problem

Mechanistic interpretability research typically verifies circuit necessity via ablation but rarely establishes sufficiency, because circuits are tested against an empty baseline rather than a minimal, interpretable subnetwork. This paper asks what the simplest nontrivial next-token prediction is and whether a minimal, identifiable subnetwork responsible for it exists in fully trained models, providing a foundation upon which more complex circuits can be studied compositionally.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252341/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a fully trained Transformer language model (Pythia or GPT-2, up to 1B parameters) with frozen weights and English text sequences (128 tokens, batch size 32) from OSCAR. It produces a sparse binary mask over all non-embedding, non-layernorm parameters; when applied, the masked subnetwork reproduces bigram predictions P(w_i | w_{i-1}) with surprisal correlations r > 0.95. Notably, the subnetworks plateau at roughly 10M active parameters regardless of full model size, meaning they constitute less than 0.2% of parameters in the largest models tested.

## Method

The paper applies continuous sparsification to find bigram subnetworks: it optimizes one real-valued parameter per model weight through a sigmoid (with annealed temperature) to produce a binary mask, minimizing a loss that combines cross-entropy against a cached bigram distribution with an L1 sparsity penalty. The resulting subnetworks are concentrated in the first Transformer MLP layer across all checkpoints, and they recreate the first-layer rotation that aligns residual-stream activations with next-token rather than current-token representations. Ablating the bigram subnetwork degrades language modeling loss comparably to ablating an optimally pruned subnetwork of similar size, while ablating a random subnetwork of the same size has negligible effect. The bigram subnetworks also overlap 15× more than chance with subnetworks trained to optimally prune the model, confirming they identify genuinely critical parameters rather than arbitrary sufficient subsets.

## Results

Bigram subnetworks achieve bigram surprisal correlations of r > 0.95 across all models tested (Pythia 70M–1B, GPT-2 small–large), while the corresponding full models only reach r = 0.58–0.74. These subnetworks plateau at roughly 10M active non-embedding parameters regardless of model size, meaning they constitute as little as 0.17% of parameters in Pythia 1B. Ablating the bigram subnetwork degrades language modeling loss to a degree comparable to ablating an optimally-pruned subnetwork of similar size, while ablating a random subnetwork of the same size has almost no effect. Parameter overlap between bigram and optimal subnetworks is 15.3× greater than chance in Pythia 1B (38.0% of the bigram subnetwork is contained in the optimal subnetwork).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but instead contrasts bigram subnetworks against three internal baselines. First, against the full model: bigram subnetworks outperform the full model on bigram surprisal correlation by 0.25–0.40 r points (e.g., 0.987 vs. 0.632 for Pythia 1B), confirming that the full model has moved beyond bigram predictions while the subnetwork retains them. Second, against a random subnetwork of identical size and parameter-block distribution: the bigram subnetwork recreates the first-layer rotation from current to next token space far more strongly than the random subnetwork, and ablating it causes a large loss increase while ablating the random subnetwork has almost no effect on language modeling loss. Third, against optimally-pruned subnetworks (trained to minimize cross-entropy with the original model rather than the bigram distribution): the bigram subnetwork overlaps 15.3× more with the optimal subnetwork than chance in Pythia 1B, and ablating the bigram subnetwork produces a similar or larger performance drop than ablating the (slightly larger) optimal subnetwork, indicating the bigram subnetwork captures the most critical parameters for next-token prediction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the continuous sparsification framework (Savarese et al., 2020) and its application to circuit discovery (Lepori et al., 2023b, who found subject-verb agreement subnetworks in BERT-small) to much larger autoregressive models (up to 1B parameters) and a more general target function: naive bigram prediction. It also draws on the n-gram overfitting literature (Chang & Bergen, 2022; Choshen et al., 2022) and the residual stream / logit lens hypothesis (Nostalgebraist, 2020; Belrose et al., 2023), which it quantifies via ridge-regression-based rotation analysis. The key positioning departure is that prior circuit work typically establishes necessity via ablation but not sufficiency over a well-understood minimal baseline; this paper explicitly provides both by showing the bigram subnetwork is sufficient in isolation and necessary via ablation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, minimal (~10M parameter) starting point from which mechanistic interpretability researchers can build up more complex circuits and evaluate their sufficiency, addressing a gap the authors identify in prior work that relied on empty circuits as baselines. The scale-independence of bigram subnetwork size (roughly 10M parameters whether the full model is 70M or 1B) is a useful structural insight, and the quantification of the first-layer rotation toward next-token space gives a measurable target for interpretability probes. Limitations include English-only models up to 1B parameters, the non-uniqueness of the identified subnetworks, and the open question of whether models truly make 'intermediate bigram predictions' in early layers or whether the bigram subnetwork has been exapted for other early-layer computations.
