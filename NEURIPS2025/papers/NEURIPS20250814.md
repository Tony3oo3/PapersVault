## Problem

Supervised learning depends on high-quality labels, but human annotation is expensive and hard to scale, while LLM-generated labels still fall short of human-level accuracy. Existing LLM-based annotation pipelines are largely confined to NLP, require white-box model access or additional training of dedicated error detectors, and do not address how to optimally train downstream models on the resulting mixed-quality data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250814/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes unlabeled multimodal data—RGB images, text passages, or image-question pairs—and produces a corrected training dataset where the majority of labels come from an MLLM annotator and a budget-constrained subset (controlled by human budget B) are verified or corrected by humans. The criticizer outputs a continuous per-sample error probability that drives budget-aware sampling (normalization, exponential weighting, or thresholding) to select which samples receive human review. Downstream, the ACT loss blends machine-annotated and human-corrected losses with importance-weighted correction terms, yielding a training signal that approximates full human supervision without requiring perfect labels.

## Method

ACT is a three-step, training-free pipeline: (1) an MLLM annotator (e.g., GPT-4o with CoT) labels all unlabeled samples; (2) a second MLLM criticizer estimates the error probability ε̂ᵢ for each annotation, with cross-criticism (a different model as criticizer) consistently outperforming self-criticism; (3) a budget-aware sampling rule selects the top-B highest-risk samples for human correction. The key theoretical contribution is the ACT loss (Eq. 8), an adaptation of active M-estimation that is an unbiased estimator of the ground-truth loss, whose variance is minimized when the criticizer accurately identifies errors. The paper proves a probabilistic upper bound on the parameter gap (Theorem 5.2) showing that exponential weighting and thresholding sampling rules—unlike the normalization rule used in prior work—keep the transformed-error lower bound q close to 1, yielding downstream performance within 2% of full human annotation while saving up to 90% of human cost.

## Results

On the six main benchmark datasets, ACT with the thresholding loss (ACT thre. loss) achieves test accuracy within 2% of full human annotation on 5 out of 6 datasets: 87.95% on CIFAR-10 (gap 0.71%), 89.16% on Fashion-MNIST (gap 3.28%), 86.00% on Stanford Cars (gap 1.69%), 81.41% on Emotion (gap 0.38%), 68.21% on Irony (gap 1.69%), and 67.02% on VQA-RAD (gap 0.08%). The corresponding human budgets range from 9.56% (Cars) to 33.79% (Irony), saving 66–90% of human annotation costs. In contrast, training solely on machine-annotated data yields up to 10.15% lower accuracy (Fashion-MNIST), and the normalization-based ACT loss performs poorly under small budgets (e.g., 11.54% on Cars vs. 87.88% for full human annotation).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is CDI [Gligorić et al., NAACL 2025], which uses a trained XGBoost model to detect LLM annotation errors. With the same ideal human budget, ACT with the thresholding loss consistently outperforms CDI across all six datasets: e.g., 87.95% vs. 84.91% on CIFAR-10 (+3.04%), 89.16% vs. 87.64% on Fashion-MNIST (+1.52%), and 67.02% vs. 67.44% on VQA-RAD (−0.42%, the only loss). The advantage is most pronounced on CIFAR-10 and VQA-RAD, where CDI's normalization-based loss degrades severely (72.22% and 62.24% respectively) while ACT's thresholding loss remains robust. ACT also outperforms pseudo labeling (83.67%, gap 4.99%) and active pseudo labeling (85.39%, gap 3.27%) on CIFAR-10 by 4.28% and 2.56% in human-performance gap, respectively, confirming that the criticizer-guided sampling strategy is more effective than uncertainty-based or confidence-based selection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-as-annotator paradigm from NLP-only settings to multimodal tasks (image classification, text classification, VQA) by leveraging MLLMs as both annotators and criticizers. It builds most directly on CDI (Gligorić et al., NAACL 2025), which uses a trained XGBoost model for error detection, by replacing that detector with a training-free MLLM criticizer and proposing new budget-aware sampling rules (exponential weighting, thresholding) that outperform CDI's normalization-based approach. The theoretical foundation draws on active M-estimation (Zrnic & Candès, ICML 2024), which the authors adapt to the budget-constrained annotation setting and prove parameter-gap bounds for. The LLM-as-a-Judge literature informs the criticizer design, and the observation that cross-criticism outperforms self-criticism aligns with known self-preference biases in LLM evaluators.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ACT pipeline offers a practical, training-free recipe for reducing human annotation costs by up to 90% while keeping downstream performance within 2% of fully human-annotated baselines, with theoretical guarantees (Theorem 5.2) explaining why the proposed sampling rules succeed where normalization fails under small budgets. A key open question is whether the approach extends to open-ended generation tasks (summarization, free-form QA) where 'error' is not binary, which the authors discuss but do not empirically validate. The finding that the standard normalization rule from active M-estimation degrades severely under small budgets (76% gap on Cars) challenges a default assumption in the literature and suggests practitioners should revisit their sampling choices. Limitations include focus on classification tasks, reliance on current MLLM capabilities, and the need for a small human-annotated test set to select annotator-criticizer pairs.
