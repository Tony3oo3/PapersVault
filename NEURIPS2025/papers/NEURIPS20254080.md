## Problem

Traditional best-arm identification (BAI) requires practitioners to pre-specify either a fixed sampling budget or a fixed confidence level, but in many real-world applications (e.g., A/B testing, drug trials) neither is naturally known in advance. This paper introduces a risk-based formalism that explicitly balances the performance penalty (probability of misidentification or simple regret) against the cumulative sampling cost, revealing a phase transition where it becomes optimal to guess without sampling when gaps are sufficiently small relative to the cost.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes sequential reward samples from σ-sub-Gaussian distributions bounded in [0,B], along with the known per-pull cost c and the sub-Gaussian parameter σ. Unlike fixed-budget or fixed-confidence BAI, the total number of pulls is not predetermined; instead, the per-arm budget N*(k) is a function of the current surviving set size k, and the confidence parameter δ is calibrated to the cost c rather than set as a free hyperparameter. The output is a recommended arm and the realized stopping time τ, with the risk R = E[penalty] + c·E[τ] as the optimization objective.

## Method

DBCARE (Dynamically Budgeted Cost-Adapted Risk-minimizing Elimination) is an elimination-style algorithm that proceeds in epochs, pulling every surviving arm once per epoch and comparing empirical means against Hoeffding confidence intervals to eliminate suboptimal arms. The key design choice is a dynamic per-arm budget N*(k) that increases as the surviving set shrinks: N*(k)=(kec)^(−1) for the misidentification risk and N*(k)=(3/2e)σ^(2/3)((k−1)c)^(−2/3) for the simple regret risk, allowing the algorithm to concentrate resources on the remaining candidates while capping total pulls. The confidence parameter δ is set proportional to c (e.g., δ=c(1+2c log(K)N*(2))^(−1)), tying the statistical guarantee directly to the economic cost. The algorithm terminates early when a single arm survives or when the budget is exhausted, in which case it returns the arm with the largest empirical mean. Theoretical analysis via change-of-measure arguments establishes matching lower and upper bounds (up to polylog factors) for both risk measures, with the lower bounds exhibiting a phase transition at H·(σ²c)^(−1) where the optimal strategy shifts from sampling to guessing.

## Results

The main theoretical result is that DBCARE matches the problem's lower bound up to polylogarithmic factors for both risk measures: for R_MI, the upper bound is O(log(K)·log(K log(K)/(ec²))·LB_MI(H) + (K+1)c, and for R_SR (when complexity is moderate), O(polylog(B,K,σ,c⁻¹)·LB_SR(H)). A key finding is a phase transition: when the suboptimality gaps are sufficiently small relative to the cost (H > (σ²c)⁻¹ for R_MI), the optimal strategy is to guess an arm without any pulls, and DBCARE adapts to this regime. Empirically, DBCARE achieves uniformly low risk across all gap values in both Gaussian and Bernoulli simulations, while fixed-budget and fixed-confidence baselines each have a region of gaps where they perform sub-optimally.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DBCARE is compared against Sequential Halving (fixed budget, T=10 and T=500) and a fixed-confidence elimination procedure (δ=0.1 and δ=0.01) in both 2-arm simulations and a 5-arm drug discovery experiment. In the simulations, Sequential Halving with T=10 performs poorly at moderate-to-large gaps (insufficient budget to separate arms), while T=500 over-samples at small gaps (incurring unnecessary cost). Fixed-confidence methods with both δ values perform poorly at small gaps where the cost of achieving the target confidence outweighs the benefit. DBCARE, by contrast, exhibits uniformly good performance across all gap values, adapting its sampling budget to the problem difficulty. On the secukinumab rheumatoid arthritis dataset (5 arms, binary and leveled efficacy outcomes), DBCARE is not uniformly outperformed by any baseline across the range of cost parameters c ∈ [10⁻³, 10⁻⁵], whereas each fixed-budget or fixed-confidence method has a range of c values where it is clearly suboptimal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing a risk-based formalism for BAI that explicitly combines a performance penalty (probability of misidentification or simple regret) with a per-sample cost, departing from both the fixed-budget and fixed-confidence paradigms that dominate the literature. Algorithmically, DBCARE repurposes racing-style elimination (from Maron & Moore, Paulson) and Hoeffding confidence intervals (from fixed-confidence BAI à la Kaufmann et al. and Jamieson et al.), but with a dynamically adapted per-arm budget N*(|S|) that depends on the cost parameter and surviving set size rather than a pre-specified horizon or failure probability. The lower bound proofs build on change-of-measure arguments from Kaufmann et al. [30] and a convexity argument over the expected stopping time, while the conceptual framing draws on Bayesian sequential testing (Wald, Arrow et al.) where the cost of continued testing is balanced against decision error.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work addresses a genuine practical gap: in A/B testing, drug discovery, and hyperparameter optimization, practitioners rarely have a natural fixed budget or confidence level, yet must decide when to stop sampling. The phase transition result—that below a cost-dependent threshold it is provably optimal to skip sampling entirely and guess—is a clean conceptual departure from fixed-confidence BAI where the lower bound always grows with Δ^{-2}. Open questions include whether the additive gap in the simple regret upper bound for large H is truly unavoidable, whether the lower bounds can be tightened for heterogeneous gap structures, and whether the polylog factors separating upper and lower bounds can be closed. The framework also naturally extends to settings with heterogeneous per-arm costs or multi-fidelity sampling, which the authors note as distinct from prior cost-aware BAI work.
