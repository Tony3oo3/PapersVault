## Problem

Existing neural encoding models align ANN representations with fMRI BOLD responses but lack a principled metric to quantify the degree of BOLD response nonlinearity at the voxel level. The common strategy of comparing R² between linear and nonlinear encoders fails when deep visual features already embed nonlinear transformations, yielding near-identical predictive performance and poor regional discriminability. This leaves the spatial distribution and hierarchical organization of cortical nonlinearity insufficiently characterized.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 768-dimensional CLIP-ViT image representations (z-score normalized) and 7T fMRI BOLD beta maps (1.8 mm resolution, z-score normalized) from the Natural Scenes Dataset. It produces voxel-wise JNE scores (a single scalar per voxel representing Jacobian dispersion across 1,000 test samples), predicted BOLD responses from the encoding model, and JNE-SS matrices (N×M) capturing per-sample, per-voxel nonlinearity. Jacobian matrices are 768×M per sample, computed via forward-mode analytical differentiation for a >99.9% speedup over standard backward-mode autograd.

## Method

JNE quantifies nonlinearity by measuring the statistical dispersion of per-sample Jacobian matrices (local input-output linear mappings) of a trained nonlinear neural encoding model. The 4-step pipeline computes: (1) the mean Jacobian across all test samples as a baseline linear mapping, (2) per-sample L1-norm deviations from this mean along the feature dimension, (3) the cross-sample mean of these deviations, and (4) the voxel-wise standard deviation of deviations, yielding the JNE score. The encoding model is a 4-layer fully connected network with skip connections (ReLU added for the nonlinear variant), trained with MSE loss and Adam on NSD data. The key theoretical insight is that an ideal linear system has input-invariant Jacobians (JNE=0), while nonlinear mechanisms produce sample-dependent Jacobian shifts whose dispersion monotonically increases with nonlinearity strength. JNE-SS further decomposes the squared deviation term to yield per-sample nonlinearity scores, enabling stimulus-selective analysis via t-SNE/K-means clustering of image categories.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that linear and nonlinear neural encoding models achieve highly similar R² on the Natural Scenes Dataset (lowest Pearson correlation between their R² sequences is r=0.82 in V1), showing that R²-based nonlinearity inference is unreliable when deep features already embed nonlinearities. JNE reveals a clear hierarchical progression of BOLD nonlinearity: primary visual cortex (V1) shows the lowest JNE values, intermediate cortex (V2–V4) shows moderate values, and higher-order cortex (EBA, PPA, RSC, OPA, FFA) shows the highest, with all pairwise differences statistically significant (P<0.05). JNE-SS further shows stimulus-selective nonlinearity: PPA and RSC exhibit stronger nonlinear responses to outdoor scenes, while FFA and EBA respond more nonlinearly to faces and people. Robustness checks confirm JNE is stable across input scales (uniform ±100 to ±10000), distributions (uniform vs. normal), and sample sizes (r≥0.99 for N≥100), with all JNE variants (L1/L2, z-scored/unz-scored) correlating at r≥0.92 on real fMRI data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark JNE against external nonlinearity metrics; instead, it validates the metric through internal comparisons. The primary 'baseline' it challenges is the common strategy of inferring nonlinearity from R² differences between linear and nonlinear encoding models: the paper shows both models achieve near-identical predictive performance (explaining up to 74% of response variance, with some voxels exceeding 80% of the noise ceiling), making R²-based inference unreliable. JNE, by contrast, provides spatially discriminative nonlinearity scores that reveal the hierarchical cortical gradient invisible to R² comparisons. Among JNE variants, the original L1 formulation correlates at r=1.00 with L2 and r≥0.92 with z-score-normalized versions, confirming that the metric's spatial patterns are robust to norm choice and scale normalization. The simulation experiments further validate JNE against ground-truth second-derivative curves of activation functions (ReLU, Leaky ReLU, GELU, Swish), showing JNE=0 for purely linear systems and monotonic increase with nonlinearity strength.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the established neural encoding framework that aligns ANN representations (here CLIP-ViT features) with fMRI BOLD responses via linear or nonlinear regression models, as developed in prior work on visual and language encoding. It departs from the common strategy of inferring BOLD nonlinearity by comparing R² between linear and nonlinear encoders, which the authors demonstrate is unreliable when deep features already embed nonlinear transformations. The core novelty is repurposing Jacobian dispersion—a concept from sensitivity analysis in ML—as a principled interpretability metric (JNE) for quantifying input-dependent nonlinearity in the encoding mapping, rather than relying on performance gaps between model families.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that R² comparison between linear and nonlinear encoders cannot reliably reveal voxel-level nonlinearity when driven by deep features challenges a widely used heuristic in the neural encoding literature, motivating more mechanistic interpretability tools. JNE provides a concrete, computable metric that reveals a hierarchical nonlinearity gradient across the visual cortex consistent with known cortical organization, and sample-specific selectivity patterns aligned with functional specialization. Key limitations include JNE's dependence on the specific encoding architecture and stimulus distribution, its conflation of hemodynamic and neural nonlinearity inherent to BOLD, and the absence of an absolute threshold for classifying voxels as linear vs. nonlinear. Open questions include robustness across architectures (CNNs, Transformers), training paradigms, and whether finer-grained feature-level analyses can pinpoint what drives region-specific nonlinear responses.
