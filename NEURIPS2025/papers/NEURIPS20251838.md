## Problem

Transformers trained on algorithmic tasks exhibit abrupt learning—extended loss plateaus followed by sudden convergence—but no unifying account exists for what the model is doing internally during the plateau. Prior work has studied abrupt learning in isolated settings (in-context learning, parity, grammar) with task-specific assumptions, leaving open whether common mechanistic patterns underlie these phase transitions across diverse tasks.

## Input / Output

The primary experiments train 1-layer, 1-head linear-attention Transformers on token sequences of length 16 over a vocabulary of 18 symbols (e.g., moving-window-sum, prefix-sum, multi-digit addition), producing next-token predictions alongside diagnostic metrics: an Attention Progress Measure (APM) quantifying alignment with the task-optimal attention pattern, a repetition frequency counting consecutive identical output tokens, and pairwise cosine similarity of hidden states. For LLM validation, early Pythia and OLMo-2 checkpoints generate 8 tokens via greedy decoding on ARC-Easy/Challenge and GSM8K prompts, with hidden-state geometry measured at each generated position.

## Method

The paper identifies three co-occurring implicit biases during the loss plateau: (1) partial solutions where the model correctly predicts intuitively simpler output tokens (e.g., copying the first element in MWS) while failing on complex parts, (2) a strong repetition bias where outputs degenerate into repeated tokens, and (3) representation collapse where hidden states across positions become nearly parallel (cosine similarity >0.9). The key mechanistic finding is that slow learning of the optimal attention map is the bottleneck: APM increases gradually during the plateau before the sudden loss drop, and the attention layer is where representation collapse originates. Training-time interventions—multiplicatively biasing attention scores toward (c>1) or away from (c<1) the optimal configuration—directly alter plateau duration and the severity of both repetition bias and collapse. Fixing the attention map to its optimal value at initialization eliminates the plateau entirely, while fixing MLP or embeddings does not, confirming attention as the critical component. These phenomena are validated in early pretraining checkpoints of Pythia (14M–2.8B) and OLMo-2 (7B), where cosine similarity spikes above 0.9 within the first few hundred steps before declining.

## Results

During the loss plateau on the moving-window-sum task, the model's output repetition frequency rapidly increases to ~0.8 within the first ~50 steps, while the average pairwise cosine similarity of hidden states (excluding the first output position) surges to ~0.95, indicating severe representation collapse. The Attention Progress Measure (APM) increases gradually from near 0 to ~0.8, with nontrivial progress already accumulated during the plateau before the sudden loss drop. Biasing the attention map by a factor of c=10 toward the optimal configuration reduces the peak cosine similarity from ~0.95 to ~0.6 and accelerates convergence, while biasing away (c=0.2, 0.5) prolongs the plateau and deepens collapse. In LLM validation, Pythia models (14M–2.8B) show cosine similarity jumping from 0.4–0.65 at initialization to >0.9 within a few training steps, and OLMo-2 7B exhibits ~0.93 cosine similarity at step 150 that drops to ~0.43 by step 600.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical analysis paper without a single proposed method to benchmark against baselines; instead, the 'comparisons' are training-time interventions and cross-configuration validations. Biasing the attention map toward the optimal pattern (c=10) from the start of training reduces peak representation cosine similarity from ~0.95 to ~0.6 and eliminates the extended plateau, while biasing away (c=0.2 or 0.5) prolongs the plateau and keeps repetition frequency high throughout. Fixing the attention weights (W_K, W_Q) to their optimal values at initialization eliminates the plateau entirely, whereas fixing the MLP or embeddings to optimal values does not qualitatively change the dynamics, confirming attention as the key bottleneck. The phenomena are robust across model configurations: they persist with softmax attention (with a longer plateau), multi-layer and multi-head architectures, varying embedding dimensions, and alternative optimizers (SGD, Muon). Finally, the core findings generalize beyond toy settings: both Pythia (14M–2.8B) and OLMo-2 7B exhibit the same early-phase representation collapse (cosine similarity >0.9) and repetition bias during pre-training, validating that these are general characteristics of Transformer training dynamics rather than artifacts of the controlled small-scale setup.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by providing a unifying empirical account of loss-plateau dynamics across diverse algorithmic tasks, where prior work has studied abrupt learning in isolation for specific settings (in-context learning, parity, grammar, matrix completion) without a common mechanistic framework. It repurposes the 'search' analogy from saddle-to-saddle dynamics [1] and hidden-progress analyses [3] to the Transformer attention-learning context, and connects to the rank-collapse literature [2] while explicitly distinguishing its post-initialization, shallow-model representation collapse. The attention-map intervention methodology draws on circuit-formation studies for induction heads [44] and attention transfer for ViTs [29], but applies it to diagnose plateau duration rather than to enable transfer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The validation that repetition bias and representation collapse appear in early pre-training checkpoints of Pythia and OLMo elevates these findings from toy-setting curiosities to potentially general characteristics of Transformer training, offering practitioners diagnostic signals (cosine similarity, output entropy) for detecting when a model is stuck in a degenerate plateau state. Key open questions remain: why representation collapse arises mechanistically, whether the hypothesized 'search' over attention positions has a rigorous theoretical grounding, and how task complexity maps to plateau duration. Limitations include the absence of error bars, reliance primarily on 1–2 layer linear-attention models for mechanistic analysis, and LLM validation restricted to very early checkpoints on a narrow set of evaluation prompts.
