## Problem

Standard deep learning training uniformly samples all data across every epoch, treating each sample equally regardless of its current learning value. While substantial effort has gone into reducing model size (pruning, quantization, efficient architectures), the training-data side of the computational cost equation remains largely unexplored. This paper asks whether progressively dropping subsets of training data across epochs can reduce effective training cost without sacrificing—and potentially improving—generalization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255411/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a standard labeled training set (e.g., 32×32 RGB images for CIFAR, 224×224 for ImageNet) along with a user-specified confidence threshold τ (for difficulty-based variants) or decay factor α (for scalar variants), and a model architecture. It produces a trained model whose effective epochs (total backpropagation passes divided by dataset size) are reduced to as little as 12.4% of baseline. Crucially, the method performs a forward pass on all samples each epoch but applies backpropagation only to the retained subset, keeping the data loader and pipeline structure unchanged.

## Method

Progressive Data Dropout (PDD) incrementally shrinks the training set across epochs, always restoring the full dataset in the final epoch as a 'revision' pass. Three variants are explored: (1) DBPD drops samples whose predicted correct-class probability exceeds a threshold τ after each epoch, focusing early training on hard examples; (2) SRD randomly retains α^t·N samples per epoch via geometric decay, requiring no per-sample evaluation; (3) SMRD uses the same per-epoch retention schedule as DBPD but drops samples at random rather than by confidence. The key empirical finding is that SMRD—structured random dropout—consistently outperforms both difficulty-based and pure scalar dropout, suggesting the benefit arises from the scheduled reduction pattern and stochasticity rather than from identifying which specific samples are hard. The approach is architecture- and optimizer-agnostic, requiring only a modification to the training loop's backpropagation mask, and reduces effective epochs by up to 89.5% while improving accuracy by up to 4.82%.

## Results

On CIFAR-100 (trained from scratch), SMRD 0.3 achieves up to 4.86% accuracy gain over baseline (e.g., ResNet-50: 64.67% vs 59.81%) while reducing effective epochs from 200 to as few as 28.6 (a 6.99× reduction). On ImageNet, SMRD 0.3 improves ResNet-50 top-1 accuracy from 75.04% to 79.18% (4.14% gain) with only 46.4 effective epochs vs 100. In self-supervised MAE pretraining, SRD 0.98 reduces pretraining effective epochs from 800 to 49.95 (16× reduction) while retaining competitive downstream accuracy (77.89% ImageNet top-1 vs 83.10% baseline). Overall, the method reduces effective epochs to as little as 12.4% of baseline with accuracy gains of up to 4.82%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the direct comparison (Table 4, CIFAR-100 fine-tuned from ImageNet, 200 epochs), SMRD outperforms all five baselines on both accuracy and effective epochs simultaneously. Against DataDiet (ELN and Forget), SMRD achieves higher accuracy (e.g., 81.5% vs 78.6%/79.1% for ResNet-50) with dramatically fewer EE (33 vs 141/142). Against IES (Instance-dependent Early Stopping), SMRD wins on all three architectures (81.5/33 vs 79.5/157 for ResNet-50; 87.5/51 vs 85.8/155 for EfficientFormer-L1). Against InfoBatch, SMRD achieves comparable or higher accuracy (84.4% vs 84.0% for EfficientNet) while using only 34 EE versus 141. Early Stopping is the weakest baseline, losing by 4–9% accuracy across all models. The key differentiator is that SMRD achieves its gains with 3–5× fewer effective epochs than any competing method, making it both more accurate and substantially more efficient.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the single-epoch data dropout concept of Wang et al. (2018) into a progressive, multi-epoch strategy inspired by cognitive science (desirable difficulties, spaced revision) and the regularization logic of activation dropout. It departs from curriculum learning and hard example mining by operating as an 'anti-curriculum' that *removes* data over time rather than reordering or reweighting it, and from data-pruning methods (DataDiet, SVP, influence-based selection) by requiring no pre-processing, proxy models, or expensive influence estimation—everything happens inline within the training loop. The closest conceptual ancestor is the data-level analog of dropout, but the key departure is the progressive schedule and the final full-dataset 'revision' epoch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

A 10× reduction in effective epochs with simultaneous accuracy gains (up to +4.82%) is practically significant for resource-constrained settings and sustainability, and the method's architecture-agnostic, drop-in nature makes it a strong candidate for wide adoption as a default training practice. Open questions include: whether the SMRD schedule can be fully replaced by the proposed mathematical approximations (logarithmic decay comes close but doesn't match), how the method behaves under strong augmentation or in fine-tuning regimes where gains shrink, and whether the random-over-difficulty finding generalizes to segmentation, multi-modal, or fairness-critical settings where dropping samples could bias learning toward majority classes.
