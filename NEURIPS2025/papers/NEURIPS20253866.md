## Problem

Sparse-view 3D Gaussian Splatting produces appearance artifacts (color outliers not belonging to the scene) in novel views, yet prior work has focused almost exclusively on geometry regularization. This paper identifies the overlooked root cause: Gaussians become overly entangled (co-adapted) to aggressively fit limited training pixels, neglecting the true underlying appearance distribution. The paper formalizes this phenomenon, quantifies it with a new metric, and proposes lightweight strategies to suppress it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253866/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes sparse RGB images with known camera poses (3 views for LLFF/DTU, 8 for Blender; downsampled by factors of 8, 4, and 2 respectively) and produces novel view renderings plus a 3D Gaussian point cloud. Additionally, it outputs a Co-Adaptation Score (CA) computed by randomly dropping 50% of Gaussians, rendering the target view K times, and measuring the average per-pixel color variance within the intersection of visible regions (accumulated alpha > 0.8) across all renderings.

## Method

The paper introduces the Co-Adaptation Score (CA), which quantifies Gaussian entanglement by measuring pixel-wise variance across multiple dropout renderings of the same viewpoint, with a theoretical derivation (Appendix A.1) showing CA is proportional to the sum of squared color-opacity products across contributing Gaussians. To mitigate co-adaptation, two plug-and-play training strategies are proposed: (1) random Gaussian dropout, where each Gaussian is independently dropped with probability p during training and opacities are scaled by (1−p) at test time, forcing nearby Gaussians along a ray to acquire similar color and opacity characteristics; and (2) multiplicative opacity noise injection (opacity ← opacity·(1+ε), ε~N(0,σ²)), which softly perturbs each Gaussian's blending weight without the instability of position noise or the blurring of scale noise. Both strategies are validated as drop-in additions to five existing sparse-view 3DGS methods (3DGS, DNGaussian, FSGS, CoR-GS, Binocular3DGS) across LLFF, DTU, and Blender, consistently reducing CA scores and improving PSNR/SSIM/LPIPS in novel views.

## Results

On LLFF (3 views), applying dropout to Binocular3DGS improves PSNR from 21.44 to 22.12 dB and reduces Test CA from 0.001951 to 0.000978. On DTU (3 views), the same strategy lifts PSNR from 20.71 to 21.03 dB. Across all five baselines on both datasets, dropout consistently yields the largest PSNR gains (e.g., +0.84 dB for 3DGS on LLFF, +0.95 dB for DNGaussian on DTU) and the greatest CA reductions, while opacity noise provides slightly smaller but still substantial improvements. Combining both strategies does not further improve quality beyond either alone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed dropout and opacity noise strategies are evaluated as plug-and-play add-ons to five existing sparse-view 3DGS methods: 3DGS, DNGaussian, FSGS, CoR-GS, and Binocular3DGS. Dropout consistently outperforms opacity noise in rendering quality across nearly all settings, achieving higher PSNR and lower LPIPS (e.g., on LLFF, 3DGS+dropout reaches 20.20 dB vs. 19.91 dB for 3DGS+opacity noise; DNGaussian+dropout reaches 19.43 vs. 19.15). The strongest absolute gains appear on weaker baselines: 3DGS gains +0.84 dB PSNR on LLFF and DNGaussian gains +0.95 dB on DTU with dropout. Even the strongest baseline, Binocular3DGS, still benefits meaningfully (+0.68 dB PSNR on LLFF, +0.32 dB on DTU with dropout), confirming that co-adaptation persists even in geometry-aware methods. Opacity noise achieves the lowest CA scores (e.g., 0.000762 vs. 0.000978 for dropout on Binocular3DGS LLFF Test CA) but with marginally lower PSNR, suggesting it suppresses entanglement more aggressively at a small quality cost. Combining both strategies yields no further improvement over either alone, indicating they target the same underlying co-adaptation mechanism.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sparse-view 3DGS literature, which has predominantly focused on geometry regularization (monocular depth priors in DNGaussian, FSGS; keypoint-guided initialization in Binocular3DGS; co-regularization in CoR-GS), by identifying appearance artifacts as a distinct failure mode and attributing them to co-adaptation among Gaussians. It draws a direct conceptual lineage from Hinton et al.'s dropout for neural networks, formalizing an analogous entanglement phenomenon in the alpha-compositing rendering pipeline. Two concurrent works (DropoutGS, DropGaussian) also apply dropout to sparse-view 3DGS but attribute its benefits to empirical factors (reduced overfitting, improved gradient flow); this paper provides a principled mechanistic explanation via the Co-Adaptation Score and a theoretical derivation linking CA to color-opacity coupling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The CA metric offers a practical diagnostic tool for understanding when and why 3DGS representations become fragile, and the two plug-and-play strategies (Gaussian dropout, opacity noise) are simple enough to be adopted across existing sparse-view pipelines without architectural changes. A notable limitation is that CA is not strictly monotonic with rendering quality—excessively suppressing co-adaptation can degrade results—so the metric is a guide rather than an optimization target. Open questions include whether the co-adaptation framework generalizes to other explicit 3D representations (meshes, SDFs), whether the strategies remain beneficial in dense-view or dynamic-scene settings, and whether the observed non-monotonicity between CA and quality reveals a fundamental trade-off between representational flexibility and generalization.
