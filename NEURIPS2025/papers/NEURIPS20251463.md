## Problem

Multimodal models saturate perception benchmarks (80–90% on CLEVR, MMBench, etc.) yet fail to reason about what objects are shared across distinct real-world scenes, with the best model reaching only 35% on a new contamination-free benchmark. Existing multi-image benchmarks rely on web-sourced data that overlaps with training corpora, inflating scores and masking the true gap between single-image perception and cross-scene reasoning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251463/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each example provides two scene images (real or synthetic, 3–7 objects in Common-0 Bench; 8–16 in Common-0 Complex) captured from different camera viewpoints, alongside a text list of 8 candidate objects alphabetized as A–H. Models must output a comma-separated subset of letters identifying all objects present in both images; images are resized to a minimum 384 px while preserving aspect ratio, and for single-image-trained models the two images are concatenated side-by-side before feeding. A parallel single-image binary probe ('Is <object> in this image?') isolates perception from reasoning.

## Method

The paper introduces Common-0 Bench (10.5k pairs, 45% real / 55% synthetic) and Common-0 Complex (12k fully synthetic pairs), both built exclusively from novel images to eliminate training-data contamination. Real images were photographed by the authors with smartphones across diverse backgrounds and viewpoints; synthetic images were rendered in Unreal Engine 5.4 using 129 assets from the Aria Digital Twin Catalog, randomly placed at 16 positions with constrained sizes to prevent overlap. Evaluation uses strict-set-match accuracy and a hallucination rate (false-positive objects / total choices), with bootstrapped standard errors and Pearson-correlation analyses of object similarity. Thirteen models spanning LLaVA-OneVision, DeepSeek-VL2, Qwen2.5-VL, LLaMA-V-o1, Llama 4, PerceptionLM, QVQ-72B, and GPT-4o are benchmarked, revealing that multi-image-trained models achieve ~3× higher accuracy, scale yields only marginal gains, and chain-of-thought reasoning has a mixed effect on cross-scene performance.

## Results

On Common-0 Bench (multi-image reasoning across scenes), the best-performing models GPT-4o and Llama 4 Instruct Scout achieve only ~35% accuracy, while most other models fall below 25%. In stark contrast, single-image object perception accuracy ranges from 65% to 85% across the same models, revealing a massive perception-to-reasoning gap. On the harder Common-0 Complex subset (8–16 objects per scene), no model exceeds ~1% accuracy, with hallucination rates reaching 76% for 1+ objects. Models trained with multi-image inputs achieve roughly 3× higher multi-image accuracy than single-image-trained models of comparable scale.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This benchmark paper evaluates 13 models across 7 families rather than proposing a new method, so the key comparison is between model architectures and training paradigms. GPT-4o (35.11%) and Llama 4 Instruct Scout (35.12%) are the clear leaders on multi-image reasoning, outperforming all other models by at least 11 percentage points; both benefit from native multi-image training. Among single-image-trained models, Qwen 32B (24.16%) is the strongest, but still trails the multi-image-trained leaders by ~11 points. Reasoning/CoT models (LLaMA-V-o1, QVQ-72B) do not consistently outperform their non-reasoning counterparts—LLaMA-V-o1 11B scores only 8.9% versus Llama 3.2 Instruct 11B's 5.07%, a modest gain, while QVQ-72B scores just 0.03% on Common-0 Complex. Scale within a family yields modest gains (Qwen 7B→32B: 13.3%→24.2%), but the jump from single-image to multi-image training is far more impactful (e.g., Llama 3.2 11B at 5.07% vs. Llama 4 Scout at 35.12%). All models exhibit severe hallucination: at least one object is hallucinated 53% of the time on average, and this rate increases when objects in the scene are visually similar (Pearson r ≈ −0.3 to −0.4, statistically significant for 10 of 13 models).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multi-image semantic correspondence task from MMIU (Meng et al., 2024) by focusing specifically on 'what's in common?' reasoning across scenes at much larger scale (10.5k vs. 1.2k examples) and with entirely contamination-free images. It departs from prior multi-image benchmarks (NLVR2, GQA, SEED-Bench-2) that rely on web-sourced data and have saturated at 68–88% accuracy, and from hallucination benchmarks (POPE, HallusionBench) that operate in single-image settings. The cognitive science framing—drawing on the 'magical number seven' and scene-memory tests used in Alzheimer's screening—distinguishes the task design from purely geometric or chart-based abstract reasoning benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper exposes a fundamental and previously underappreciated gap: models that saturate perception leaderboards (80–90%) drop to 35% (best) on cross-scene reasoning and below 1% on complex scenes, revealing that current VLMs cannot reliably compare objects across images. The finding that hallucination increases when similar objects are present suggests models exploit co-occurrence priors from training rather than performing genuine visual comparison, pointing to a need for new training paradigms beyond standard reward-based CoT. Open questions include whether scaled multi-image training can close the gap, whether open-ended (non-multiple-choice) evaluation changes the picture, and whether the contamination-free synthetic data approach generalizes to other benchmark designs.
