## Problem

A prevailing hypothesis attributes generalization in flow matching to the stochasticity of the conditional training target (i.e., regressing on a single random x_1 rather than the full optimal velocity field). This paper challenges that claim by showing that in realistic high-dimensional settings, the stochastic and closed-form targets are nearly identical, and that generalization instead arises from the network's inability to approximate the optimal velocity field, particularly at early flow times.

## Input / Output

The model takes a Gaussian noise vector x_0 ~ N(0, Id) in R^d (where d = 3·32² for CIFAR-10 or d = 3·64² for CelebA) and produces a synthetic RGB image by integrating the learned velocity field ODE over t ∈ [0,1]. During training, the key input is the pair (x_0, x_1) where x_1 is a data sample, and the regression target is either the single conditional velocity (x_1 − x_0) in vanilla CFM or a weighted average over M data samples in EFM. Evaluation uses FID (Inception-V3 and DINOv2 embeddings) and LPIPS distance to the training set as a memorization metric.

## Method

The paper derives the closed-form optimal velocity field û*(x,t) as a softmax-weighted sum of all n conditional velocities, then shows empirically that in high dimensions this weighted sum collapses to a single direction very early (t < 0.2), rendering the stochastic target effectively deterministic. Building on this, the authors propose Empirical Flow Matching (EFM, Algorithm 2), which regresses the U-Net velocity field against a Monte Carlo estimate û*_M of the closed-form field using M samples, with the crucial trick of always including x_1 in the batch to guarantee unbiasedness (Proposition 2). The estimator is shown to have lower variance than the standard conditional target. Experiments on CIFAR-10 and CelebA 64×64 with a 34M-parameter U-Net demonstrate that EFM with M ∈ {128, 256, 1000} matches or slightly improves FID over vanilla CFM and OTCFM, confirming that removing target stochasticity does not harm—and can help—generalization.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 and CelebA 64×64, Empirical Flow Matching (EFM) with M∈{128, 256, 1000} samples achieves FID scores that match or modestly improve upon vanilla CFM and OTCFM across both Inception and DINOv2 embeddings (Figure 4). On MNIST, EFM with M=1024 reaches a test FID of 1.84 at epoch 25, compared to 2.28 for vanilla CFM (M=1). On FMNIST, EFM with M=512 achieves a test FID of 16.85 at epoch 200 versus 19.67 for CFM. The paper also demonstrates that generalization (measured by LPIPS distance to training data) occurs predominantly at early flow-matching times (t < 0.2), and that following the closed-form optimal velocity field û* for t ≥ 0.4 causes the model to memorize training samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares EFM against two baselines: vanilla Conditional Flow Matching (CFM, Lipman et al. 2023) and Optimal Transport CFM (OTCFM, Tong et al. 2024). On CIFAR-10 and CelebA 64×64 (Figure 4), EFM with increasing M (128→1000) consistently achieves equal or slightly lower FID than both CFM and OTCFM under both Inception and DINOv2 embeddings, with the most deterministic target (M=1000) yielding the best scores. On MNIST, EFM M=1024 improves test FID from 2.28 (CFM) to 1.84 at epoch 25, a ~19% relative reduction. On FMNIST, EFM M=512 improves test FID from 19.67 (CFM) to 16.85 at epoch 200, a ~14% relative reduction. The key finding is that removing target stochasticity does not degrade generalization—contrary to the 'generalization through variance' hypothesis—and in fact provides modest but consistent gains, with the computational overhead of EFM being under 3% for M=128 and ~17% for M=1000 relative to standard CFM training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the conditional flow matching framework of Lipman et al. (2023) and the closed-form optimal velocity field identified in several recent works (Kamb & Ganguli 2025, Biroli et al. 2024, Gao & Li 2024). It departs most sharply from Vastola (2025), who argued that generalization in diffusion/flow matching arises from the stochasticity of the regression target ('generalization through variance'), by showing that in high-dimensional settings the stochastic and closed-form targets are nearly identical. The Empirical Flow Matching (EFM) algorithm extends Ryzhakov et al. (2024), who also proposed regressing against the closed-form velocity, by introducing a key trick—systematically including the generating sample x₁ in the Monte Carlo batch—that yields an unbiased estimator with lower variance than the standard conditional target.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By ruling out target stochasticity as the primary driver of generalization in realistic high-dimensional regimes, the paper redirects attention toward the interaction between network capacity, the complexity of the optimal velocity field, and architectural inductive biases—factors that remain only partially understood. The practical EFM algorithm offers a modest but consistent FID improvement with minimal computational overhead (~2% per 100 samples), making it a low-cost drop-in for existing flow matching pipelines. Key open questions include how the identified temporal regimes (generalization concentrated at t < 0.2) interact with the inductive biases of convolutional architectures, and whether the findings extend to non-Gaussian source distributions or conditional generation settings.
