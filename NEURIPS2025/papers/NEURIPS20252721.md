## Problem

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) faces a fundamental tension: LoRA is parameter-efficient but lacks the capacity for complex tasks, while Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) adapters add capacity at the cost of more under-utilized parameters. Existing MoE-PEFT methods only address 'which experts to activate' but ignore 'how activated experts should be connected,' leaving the structural relationships among experts entirely unexploited as a source of model flexibility.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252721/cropped_page3_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The adapter takes a d-dimensional token embedding (e.g., d=4096) and operates on frozen pre-trained LLM weights, producing a d-dimensional residual embedding that is added back to the base model's output at each transformer layer. The adapter can be inserted into both FFN and attention modules. Internally, the token propagates through a depth-L tree of low-rank residual experts (each of rank r_ℓ ≪ d), with intermediate hidden dimensions d_ℓ growing as the sum of activated expert ranks, keeping the total parameter count comparable to a single LoRA of rank d_L.

## Method

S'MoRE decomposes expert weights into hierarchical low-rank residuals of varying orders and arranges them in a multi-layer tree structure. A hierarchical router selects experts top-down: at each layer, it computes conditional probabilities for children given activated ancestors, building a depth-L residual tree per token. The token then propagates bottom-up through the tree, where each layer aggregates its activated children via a GNN-like operation (weighted sum of non-linearly activated residuals plus a skip connection to the raw token). The non-linear activation σ in this aggregation is critical: it enables S'MoRE to generate distinct outputs for all non-isomorphic tree structures, analogous to the Weisfeiler-Lehman graph isomorphism test. Theoretically, this multi-layer structural design boosts the model's 'structural flexibility' (number of distinct outputs achievable) by an exponential factor over single-layer MoE baselines like MixLoRA, while maintaining parameter and computation efficiency equivalent to vanilla LoRA.

## Results

On LLaMA 3-8B with a dense gate, S'MoRE(2-2) achieves 86.83% average accuracy across five reasoning benchmarks (ARC-c, ARC-e, CSQA, OBQA, Winogrande), outperforming MixLoRA(8) (85.82%) and HydraLoRA(4) (85.75%) with fewer trainable parameters (0.170B vs. 0.193B and 0.086B respectively). On GSM8K, S'MoRE(4-4) reaches 65.20% accuracy (dense gate) and 63.91% (switch gate), surpassing the best baseline by 2.7–4.1 points. On HumanEval, S'MoRE(2-2) with a switch gate achieves 45.73% Pass@1, exceeding MixLoRA(4) (42.68%) and HydraLoRA(8) (44.51%). On Gemma 2-9B, S'MoRE(4-4) attains 77.35% average accuracy/Pass@1, a 1.5-point gain over MixLoRA(4) (75.86%) with comparable parameter counts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

S'MoRE consistently outperforms all three baselines across every gate type and model scale. Against LoRA, S'MoRE gains approximately 2–3 percentage points in average accuracy (e.g., 61.30% vs. 59.15% on LLaMA 3.2-1B dense gate; 86.83% vs. 84.56% on LLaMA 3-8B dense gate), confirming that a single low-rank matrix is insufficient for complex tasks. Against MixLoRA (the single-layer MoE baseline), S'MoRE's multi-layer structure yields a 1–1.5 point improvement (e.g., 61.30% vs. 60.25% on 1B; 86.83% vs. 85.82% on 8B) while using fewer or comparable parameters, demonstrating that the structural interconnection of experts adds capacity without extra parameter cost. Against HydraLoRA, S'MoRE achieves 1–2 points higher accuracy (e.g., 61.30% vs. 59.63% on 1B; 86.83% vs. 85.75% on 8B), and critically, HydraLoRA's accuracy plateaus or degrades when parameters increase beyond ~0.3B, whereas S'MoRE continues to improve, indicating that structural flexibility enables better parameter utilization at scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

S'MoRE extends the single-layer Mixture-of-LoRA-Experts (MoLRE) family—exemplified by MixLoRA and HydraLoRA—into a multi-layer tree structure where low-rank residual experts at different orders propagate and aggregate across layers. The key conceptual departure from prior MoE-PEFT work is shifting the question from 'which experts to activate' to 'how should activated experts be connected,' formalized through a hierarchical router that builds per-token residual trees. The theoretical analysis draws on Graph Neural Network literature, specifically the Weisfeiler-Lehman color-refinement test, to prove that the multi-layer propagation with non-linear activation achieves exponential structural flexibility over flat MoE baselines under the same parameter budget.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The structural flexibility metric provides a principled way to quantify MoE expressivity beyond simple expert count, and the GNN/WL-test connection offers a clean theoretical justification for why multi-layer expert arrangement outperforms flat designs. However, the practical accuracy gains over MixLoRA are modest (typically 1–2 points), and the theoretical exponential advantage does not translate into exponential accuracy improvements. Open questions include whether the structural advantage persists at larger model scales (beyond 9B), whether it applies to pre-training or continued pre-training settings, and whether the ideal-router assumption in the theory holds under practical training dynamics.
