## Problem

Existing Noise-as-Watermark (NaW) methods for diffusion models face a fundamental tension: achieving strong watermark robustness requires heavily constraining the initial Gaussian noise sampling, which degrades generation diversity and user experience, while preserving full noise diversity makes the watermark too fragile under common distortions like JPEG compression or cropping. No prior method simultaneously delivers high traceability accuracy, competitive detection rates, and near-unchanged generation diversity.

## Input / Output

For embedding, the method takes a text prompt, a fixed master key, a 256-bit user identity watermark, and a randomly generated 16-bit session key, and produces a 512×512 watermarked image by modifying only the initial noise vector in the diffusion model's latent space (4 channels for SD v2.1, 16 channels for SD v3.5M). For extraction, the watermarked image is inverted via 10-step DDIM with an empty prompt and guidance scale 1 to recover the noise vector, from which the session key is first decoded (enabling detection via L1-norm thresholding) and then the full 256-bit identity watermark is recovered for traceability. The noise vector is partitioned channel-wise into two segments: the first encodes the session key, the second encodes the watermark bits.

## Method

T2SMark introduces Tail-Truncated Sampling (TTS), which divides each Gaussian dimension into three zones: a negative tail for bit-0, a positive tail for bit-1, and a central undecided region sampled uniformly to preserve the overall noise distribution. A secret key seeds a PRNG to generate pseudorandom vectors with orthogonal, non-overlapping supports that define hyperplane normals; bits are encoded by assigning signs to tail-sampled components along these normals, while central components retain full randomness. A two-stage encryption hierarchy further randomizes the noise: a fixed master key encrypts a random session key in the first segment, and that session key then encrypts the actual watermark bits in the second segment, so the full noise vector varies across generations even for the same user. At extraction, the reconstructed noise is projected onto the same hyperplane normals, with bit recovery from projection signs and detection from the L1 norm of the first-stage projection. A theoretical analysis under AWGN shows that even a small truncation threshold strictly reduces bit-error probability by increasing the effective signal-to-noise ratio, and the optimal threshold (τ≈0.674) is selected empirically to balance robustness against over-constraining the sampling distribution.

## Results

On SD v2.1, T2SMark achieves a detection TPR of 1.000 (clean) / 0.998 (adversarial) and a traceability bit accuracy of 1.0000 / 0.9754, matching the best detection performance of Gaussian Shading while surpassing it in traceability. Generation diversity (LPIPS) is 0.7069, nearly identical to the unwatermarked baseline (0.7072) and PRCW (0.7074), whereas GS drops to 0.6446. For visual quality, T2SMark yields the lowest t-statistics for both CLIP score (0.5081) and FID (0.5490), confirming no statistically significant degradation versus the clean model. On the DiT-based SD v3.5M, T2SMark again leads in adversarial bit accuracy (0.9768) and diversity (0.6102) while maintaining near-perfect detection (TPR 1.000/0.985).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the two traditional post-processing baselines (dwtDct and dwtDctSvd from Cox et al.), T2SMark dominates on both detection and traceability under adversarial distortions (e.g., adversarial TPR 0.998 vs. 0.173/0.471; adversarial bit accuracy 0.9754 vs. 0.5744/0.6800). RivaGAN and Stable Signature, the learning-based and fine-tuning approaches, are similarly outperformed: T2SMark's adversarial TPR (0.998) exceeds RivaGAN (0.436) and StableSig (0.418) by a wide margin, and its adversarial bit accuracy (0.9754) surpasses both (0.8666 and 0.7462 respectively). Among inversion-based methods, T2SMark ties GS on detection TPR (both 1.000/0.998) but leads in adversarial traceability (0.9754 vs. 0.9548) and diversity (0.7069 vs. 0.6446), while also showing far lower quality-degradation t-values (FID t=0.55 vs. 3.08). PRCW achieves marginally higher diversity (0.7074 vs. 0.7069) but is decisively outperformed on robustness: its adversarial TPR drops to 0.294 and adversarial bit accuracy to 0.5024, making it impractical for real-world deployment. TRW, a single-bit scheme, has no traceability capability and shows lower adversarial TPR (0.907) and higher FID degradation (t=6.93) than T2SMark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Noise-as-Watermark (NaW) paradigm for diffusion models, which embeds watermarks in the initial Gaussian noise and recovers them via DDIM inversion. It departs from Gaussian Shading (GS), which achieves robustness through fixed repetition codes at the cost of diversity, and PRC-Watermark (PRCW), which prioritizes undetectability via pseudorandom error-correcting codes but is fragile under common distortions. The key conceptual departure is Tail-Truncated Sampling (TTS), which embeds bits only in the reliable tail regions of the Gaussian distribution while randomly sampling the central zone, combined with a two-stage session-key encryption hierarchy to randomize the full noise vector. The method is positioned within the inversion-based family, distinct from post-processing and fine-tuning approaches, and is validated on both U-Net (SD v2.1) and DiT (SD v3.5M) backbones.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

T2SMark addresses a central practical tension in AI content provenance: watermark robustness for traceability versus generation diversity for user experience. By achieving near-perfect traceability (97.5% adversarial bit accuracy) while preserving diversity within 0.001 of the unwatermarked baseline, it offers a deployable solution for service providers needing both copyright protection and user satisfaction. Key limitations remain: vulnerability to Gaussian noise (shared across all inversion-based NaW methods), dependence on ODE-based samplers for inversion, susceptibility to geometric distortions, and a forgery attack vector where recovered noise can be reused to generate new watermarked images. Open questions include whether the TTS principle can be extended to handle geometric transforms and whether the two-stage key hierarchy can be made resistant to the subtle distributional anomalies that enable limited detectability.
