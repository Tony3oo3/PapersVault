## Problem

While deep neural networks are known to memorize training data, the analogous phenomenon in graph neural networks remains largely unexplored due to the difficulty of defining per-sample memorization when all nodes in a graph are interdependent. This paper asks: what factors drive GNN memorization in semi-supervised node classification, how does it relate to graph structure and training dynamics, and what privacy risks does it create?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250988/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework operates on a single graph with node features and an adjacency matrix, where only a subset of node labels is available (semi-supervised setting). To make leave-one-out evaluation tractable, the labeled training set is partitioned into three disjoint subsets (shared S_S, candidate S_C, independent S_I), and two models are trained on S_S∪S_C versus S_S∪S_I. Outputs include a per-node memorization score (difference in prediction confidence between the two models), an aggregate memorization rate, a node-level Label Disagreement Score, NTK alignment values tracked over training, and membership inference attack AUC scores for privacy risk quantification.

## Method

NCMemo defines a memorization score for each candidate node as the expected difference in correct-label prediction probability between a model trained with that node's label and one trained without it, following Feldman's leave-one-out formulation adapted to the semi-supervised node classification paradigm. The paper then analyzes memorization through three lenses: (i) graph-level, establishing an inverse relationship between memorization rate and homophily via NTK alignment matrices (kernel-graph alignment A(Θ_t, A) and kernel-target alignment A(Θ_t, Θ*)); (ii) training dynamics, showing via a geometric triangle-inequality argument that GNNs' implicit bias toward aligning their NTK with the adjacency matrix forces poor kernel-target alignment in low-homophily graphs, compelling the model to memorize labels to minimize training loss; and (iii) node-level, introducing the Label Disagreement Score (LDS) that measures local label-feature inconsistency in k-NN feature space to identify which specific nodes are most susceptible. Finally, feature-similarity-based graph rewiring is proposed as a mitigation that increases effective homophily, reducing both memorization and MIA vulnerability without degrading accuracy.

## Results

The paper establishes a strong inverse relationship between memorization rate and graph homophily, with Pearson correlations of r = −0.98 (kernel-target alignment) and r = −0.88 (kernel-graph alignment) on syn-cora, and r = −0.79 / r = −0.58 on real-world datasets. Memorized nodes exhibit significantly higher Label Disagreement Scores (LDS) than non-memorized nodes across all tested datasets (e.g., Cora: 0.8016 vs. 0.5754; Chameleon: 0.8433 vs. 0.7362, p < 0.01). For privacy risk, the LiRA membership-inference AUC reaches 0.936 on the most heterophilic syn-cora graph (h = 0.0) versus 0.538 on the most homophilic (h = 1.0), and graph rewiring reduces this AUC by over 20% for low-homophily graphs while preserving model accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an analysis/framework paper rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so it does not pit a single proposed method against competing approaches. Instead, it compares memorization behavior across five GNN backbones (GCN, GraphSAGE, GATv2, SGC, Graph Transformer) and across homophilic (Cora, Citeseer, Pubmed) versus heterophilic (Cornell, Texas, Wisconsin, Chameleon, Squirrel, Actor) datasets, consistently finding higher memorization in heterophilic settings. The paper also compares original versus feature-similarity-rewired graphs as a mitigation: rewiring increases effective homophily, reduces memorization rate, and drops the LiRA MIA AUC by more than 20% on the most heterophilic syn-cora graph without degrading classification accuracy. In the graph-classification extension (Table 3), adding Gaussian feature noise (σ up to 0.9) to candidate graphs induces memorization (MUTAG MR rises from 0% to 46.2%), confirming the framework's sensitivity to atypical samples. The NTK alignment analysis (Propositions 2–3) contrasts low- vs. high-homophily regimes, showing that kernel-graph alignment A(Θ_t, A) increases during training regardless of homophily, while kernel-target alignment A(Θ_t, Θ*) remains poor for low-homophily graphs, directly linking the GNN's structural bias to memorization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the leave-one-out memorization framework from DNNs (Feldman, 2020) to the fundamentally different setting of GNNs, where node interdependence makes per-sample memorization ill-defined. It adopts the efficient three-way partition evaluation from Wang et al. (2024) and the NTK alignment analysis from Yang et al. (2024) to provide a mechanistic explanation for why memorization emerges, while distinguishing its phenomenon from structural overfitting (Bechler-Speicher et al., 2024). The graph rewiring mitigation draws on Rubio-Madrigal et al. (2025), repurposing a feature-similarity-based edge modification strategy originally designed for performance improvement as a privacy-preserving intervention.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic framework for quantifying memorization in GNNs, filling a notable gap between well-studied DNN memorization and the largely unexplored GNN setting. The inverse homophily-memorization relationship and its NTK-alignment-based explanation offer actionable insights for practitioners deploying GNNs on sensitive data, as memorized nodes are shown to be significantly more vulnerable to membership inference attacks. Key limitations include the reliance on synthetic data for primary mechanistic claims, the preliminary nature of the graph rewiring mitigation (which may lose information on real-world graphs), and the restriction to semi-supervised node classification; open questions include extending the analysis to link prediction, understanding over-squashing/over-smoothing effects on memorization, and generalizing to attention-based architectures like GATs and Graph Transformers.
