## Problem

The ML value chain systematically strips economic equity from data generators while refining technical signal, with an analysis of 73 public data deals showing creator royalties rounding to zero and 57 of 73 deals lacking any revenue disclosure. Three structural faults—missing provenance, asymmetric bargaining power, and static one-shot pricing—interact multiplicatively to concentrate capital and cut generators out of the value chain. No existing framework simultaneously addresses all three dimensions to enable a sustainable, efficient data economy that benefits generators, aggregators, and model monetizers alike.

## Input / Output

The EDVEX framework consumes candidate datasets (or representative subsamples), task descriptions specifying model architecture and training regime, and existing model state to produce ranked task-optimized data bundles with per-dataset utility estimates, auditable lineage records encoding multi-creator provenance, and utility-driven price signals. The sandbox protocol operates on small-scale proxy models and data subsamples, extrapolating via empirical scaling laws to estimate full-corpus utility before any acquisition commitment. Provenance metadata must efficiently encode combinations from potentially millions of creators while capturing workflow-specific transformation details (filtering, hyperparameters, architecture choices) without revealing proprietary pipeline internals.

## Method

EDVEX is a three-layer framework: (1) a task-data matching layer that functions as a dataset recommendation system, using a sandbox-first protocol where candidate datasets are evaluated on small proxy models and representative subsamples, with performance gains extrapolated via empirical scaling laws to estimate marginal utility for the target task; (2) a lineage tracking layer that automatically logs data sources and usage through integration with existing ML frameworks (e.g., PyTorch), requiring efficient encoding schemes for multi-creator provenance across complex iterative pipelines; and (3) a valuation layer that uses sandbox-derived utility estimates as the basis for price discovery—via bidding, utility-correlated pricing, or revenue-sharing arrangements—with Shapley values determining equitable revenue distribution among contributors within dynamically formed, task-optimized data unions. The empirical foundation is a systematic analysis of 73 publicly disclosed data deals (2016–2025) documenting the scale of the inequality, including the concentration of transactions among a few buyers and the near-absence of generator revenue sharing. The framework is designed so that dynamic data unions formed around specific task requirements enhance smaller contributors' bargaining power against large aggregators, breaking the multiplicative feedback cycle of provenance loss, weak bargaining, and one-shot buy-outs.

## Results

Across 73 publicly disclosed data deals (2016–2025), total reported revenue is $677.3M, yet creator royalties publicly disclosed amount to only ~$2.5K (HarperCollins, $2,500/book). Of the 73 deals, 57 (78%) do not disclose any revenue figure, and only 6 mention any revenue-sharing arrangement with data generators. More than 40% of known transactions were conducted by OpenAI/Microsoft, Google, or Perplexity on the buying side, and 4 deals are under active litigation rather than negotiated at arm's length.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper without traditional method-vs-baseline comparisons; the 'compares_to' set is empty. Instead, the paper benchmarks current market practice (opaque bilateral deals, lump-sum buy-outs, click-wrap ToS) against the proposed EDVEX framework's three technical primitives (task-data matching, lineage tracking, utility-driven valuation). The empirical evidence shows that existing data marketplaces (AWS Data Exchange, Databricks Marketplace, Web3 protocols like Ocean Protocol and Bittensor) and regulatory instruments (GDPR, EU Data Act) each address only a subset of the three identified structural faults—provenance, bargaining asymmetry, and static pricing—whereas EDVEX aims to address all three simultaneously. The paper also contrasts its dynamic, task-optimized data unions with static organizational models (data cooperatives, data trusts, data unions) and argues that the latter lack the task-dependent adaptability needed for ML-specific value discovery.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes three distinct technical threads—empirical scaling laws for sandbox-based data utility estimation (Hestness et al., Kaplan et al., Hoffmann et al.), cooperative-game-theoretic data valuation via Shapley values (Ghorbani & Zou, 2019), and optimal-transport-based data selection (Kang et al., 2023)—into a unified EDVEX framework for equitable data exchange. It extends the conceptual lineage of Data Dignity (Lanier), Data Dividends, and data cooperatives/unions (MIDATA, Freedman) by proposing dynamic, task-optimized data unions rather than static membership collectives, and departs from traditional data marketplaces (one-time fees, query-based pricing) by grounding price discovery in measured task-specific utility. The paper explicitly positions itself as translating normative proposals into actionable technical primitives, while acknowledging that regulatory frameworks (GDPR, EU Data Act) and Web3 decentralized marketplaces address overlapping but insufficient subsets of the problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The empirical documentation that creator royalties across 73 public deals round to near-zero (a single $2.5K payment) while aggregators capture the bulk of $677.3M in disclosed revenue provides a concrete, citable baseline for the structural inequality argument, and the three-fault taxonomy (provenance, bargaining power, price discovery) gives the community a shared vocabulary for diagnosing and addressing the problem. However, as a position paper with no implementation or pilot, all three EDVEX layers remain open research problems—scalable provenance encoding, incentive-compatible sandbox evaluation at millions-of-datasets scale, and gaming-resistant valuation are non-trivial and unsolved. The paper's evidence base is limited to publicly disclosed, largely U.S.-centric deals, and the rapid evolution of the market (ongoing litigation, shifting ToS) means the snapshot may already be partially outdated, underscoring the need for longitudinal tracking alongside technical solutions.
