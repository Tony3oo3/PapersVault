## Problem

Existing LLM-as-a-Judge methods produce single-point judgments, collapsing the rich distribution of human opinions into one label and discarding signals about consensus, disagreement, and uncertainty. This information loss reduces evaluation reliability, particularly in high-stakes domains. The paper asks: can we explicitly train LLMs to output probability distributions that faithfully match the full human evaluation distribution rather than a single majority-vote label?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251008/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes text pairs (premise+hypothesis, source+summary, or two model responses) and produces a normalized probability vector over discrete judgment categories (e.g., 3 NLI labels, 5 Likert scores, or 3 preference options). Predictions are extracted by reading the logits of specific judgment tokens, applying softmax, and aggregating synonymous token probabilities into canonical labels. Training requires multiple independent human annotations per sample (typically 5) to form the empirical target distribution, which is the key data requirement distinguishing this from standard single-label fine-tuning.

## Method

The framework aligns the LLM's output distribution with the human evaluation distribution via a hybrid loss: a KL divergence term (primary) that penalizes distributional mismatch, plus an auxiliary cross-entropy term (weighted by α) that anchors training to the majority-vote label for stability. To counter overfitting to noisy empirical distributions from limited annotators, the method introduces adversarial training on the target label distribution itself: at each step, PGD searches for the worst-case perturbation within an ℓ2 ball intersected with the probability simplex, and the model is trained to minimize loss against this adversarially shifted target. Crucially, the PGD inner loop perturbs the label vector (a C-dimensional simplex point) rather than the model input, so the LLM's output is treated as a fixed constant during the ascent steps, avoiding costly backpropagation through the language model and incurring only ~21% training overhead.

## Results

On the four main benchmarks, the proposed distribution alignment method (Qwen2.5-7B) reduces KL divergence to 0.23 on SNLI and MNLI, 0.53 on SummEval, and 0.68 on MT-Bench, while maintaining or improving top-1 accuracy (93.3%, 89.8%, 45.9%, 65.4% respectively). The same pattern holds for LLaMA3.1-8B, with KL divergences of 0.28, 0.24, 0.51, and 0.74. All improvements over the single-point baseline are statistically significant (p<0.05) on at least one metric per dataset. The method also outperforms raw GPT-4o on KL divergence for SNLI (0.23 vs. 1.75), MNLI (0.23 vs. 1.16), and SummEval (0.53 vs. 2.82).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the single-point alignment baseline (Ouyang et al., 2022), the distribution alignment method consistently wins on KL divergence across all four datasets and both open-source backbones: for Qwen2.5, KL drops from 0.60→0.23 (SNLI), 0.64→0.23 (MNLI), 0.73→0.53 (SummEval), and 0.82→0.68 (MT-Bench); for LLaMA3.1, from 0.69→0.28, 0.67→0.24, 0.67→0.51, and 0.81→0.74 respectively. Accuracy is either matched or slightly improved (e.g., 92.7%→93.3% on SNLI for Qwen2.5, 45.7%→47.3% on SummEval for LLaMA3.1). Against the raw Qwen2.5-7B model (Yang et al., 2024), the fine-tuned distribution method reduces KL by 6–9× (e.g., 2.08→0.23 on SNLI) while boosting accuracy by 10+ points. Against the raw LLaMA3.1-8B model (Grattafiori et al., 2024), the improvement is even more dramatic on SummEval (3.60→0.51 KL) and MT-Bench (1.58→0.74 KL). Notably, the fine-tuned Qwen2.5 distribution method also outperforms the raw GPT-4o on KL divergence for SNLI, MNLI, and SummEval, though GPT-4o retains an edge on MT-Bench accuracy (68.5% vs. 65.4%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the distributed NLI framework of Zhou et al. (2022), which first proposed learning to predict full human opinion distributions rather than single labels, by transplanting the idea into the modern LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm and adding a novel adversarial training mechanism that perturbs the target label distribution itself (via PGD on the simplex ∩ ℓ₂-ball) rather than model inputs as in TRADES. It also departs from distributional reward models in RLHF (Siththaranjan et al. 2024, Dorka 2024), which require architectural modifications to output mean-variance or quantile parameters, by instead extracting standard LLM logits over judgment tokens and aligning them with empirical human distributions via a hybrid KL + cross-entropy loss that mirrors the soft/hard-target philosophy of knowledge distillation. The single-point alignment baseline it compares against is the standard supervised fine-tuning recipe from InstructGPT (Ouyang et al. 2022), making the contribution a methodological upgrade on top of an already-well-established evaluation task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work addresses a real information-loss problem: collapsing multi-annotator human judgments into a single majority-vote label discards signals about consensus, controversy, and uncertainty that matter in high-stakes domains (medical diagnosis, policy-making). The practical finding that a balanced annotation strategy (3 judgments per sample) outperforms both breadth-only and depth-only strategies under a fixed annotation budget gives practitioners a concrete, cost-aware recipe for collecting evaluation data. Key open questions remain: the explainability gap (generated rationales correspond to a single sampled judgment, not the full distribution), the scarcity of multi-annotated datasets in most NLP benchmarks, and whether the adversarial label-perturbation idea transfers to continuous or open-vocabulary judgment spaces beyond the discrete categories tested here.
