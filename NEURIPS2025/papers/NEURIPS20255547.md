## Problem

Agentic benchmarks, which evaluate AI agents on complex real-world tasks with unstructured outputs and dynamic environments, frequently suffer from design flaws that cause under- or overestimation of agent performance by up to 100% in relative terms. Despite growing adoption by top AI providers, no systematic, actionable framework existed to assess whether an agentic benchmark's evaluation results faithfully reflect the target capability, leaving critical validity gaps in both task design and outcome grading unaddressed.

## Input / Output

The checklist consumes an agentic benchmark's full specification—task descriptions, environment setup, tool definitions, evaluation harness code, and ground-truth annotations—and produces binary (0/1) scores for each applicable checklist item across three dimensions: task validity (T.1–T.10), outcome validity (O.a.1–O.i.1, grouped by outcome type and evaluation method), and benchmark reporting (R.1–R.14). For each identified violation, the authors design targeted experiments (e.g., trivial agents, shortcut probes, fuzzing with varied configurations) to quantify the resulting performance misestimation in absolute or relative terms.

## Method

The paper formalizes two necessary validity conditions for rigorous agentic evaluation—task validity (the target capability is equivalent to task success) and outcome validity (task success is equivalent to a positive evaluation result)—and translates them into the Agentic Benchmark Checklist (ABC), a set of 30+ actionable checks organized by the source of potential failure: tool/environment/implementation flaws for task validity, and evaluation-method-specific pitfalls (string matching, unit/fuzz/E2E testing, state matching, answer parsing, quality metrics) for outcome validity. The checklist was synthesized from the authors' benchmark-building experience, a survey of 17 widely used agentic benchmarks, prior issue reports, and domain-specific best practices from software testing. Applied to ten open-source benchmarks spanning software engineering, cybersecurity, environment interaction, and general assistance, ABC revealed that seven violated task validity, seven violated outcome validity, and all ten had reporting limitations, with concrete issues including a 38% free pass rate in τ-bench, a 100% score achievable in SWE-Lancer without solving tasks, and a 31% overestimation in KernelBench due to inadequate fuzzing. As a case study, applying ABC to CVE-Bench during development reduced performance overestimation by 33% in absolute terms, confirmed by cybersecurity experts.

## Results

Applying ABC to ten widely used agentic benchmarks reveals that 7 violate task validity, 7 violate outcome validity, and all 10 have limitations in result reporting. The most severe issues include: τ-bench overestimates performance by 38% (a trivial empty-response agent passes intentionally impossible tasks) and by an additional 40% (agents can dump entire databases to pass substring checks); SWE-Lancer allows agents to score 100% without solving any tasks by overwriting test files; KernelBench overestimates kernel correctness by 31% due to inadequate fuzz testing; and OSWorld underestimates performance by 28% due to broken HTML selectors. When ABC is applied to improve CVE-Bench during development, it reduces performance overestimation by 33% in absolute terms.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method to compare against baselines; instead, it assesses existing agentic benchmarks using the ABC checklist. The closest to a before/after comparison is the CVE-Bench case study: before applying ABC, time-based SQL injection evaluation overestimated agent performance by 32.5% (agents could insert SLEEP anywhere in a query), and the outbound server check was trivially bypassed via the shared Docker network, inflating success rates by 10%. After ABC-guided fixes, total overestimation dropped by 33% in absolute terms, confirmed by cybersecurity experts. Across the ten assessed benchmarks, the paper shows that no benchmark satisfies all ABC criteria: 80% fail to acknowledge design weaknesses in their reporting, and none satisfies every reporting criterion (R.1–R.14). The findings demonstrate that current agentic benchmarks systematically over- or under-estimate agent capabilities by up to 100% in relative terms, with SWE-bench-Verified's 24% incorrect top-50 leaderboard positions and τ-bench's 38% trivial-agent success rate being the most consequential examples.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the benchmark-quality assessment line of work (Cao et al. 2025, Reuel et al. 2024) from traditional multiple-choice and generative tasks to the agentic setting, where evaluation involves complex environment setup and unstructured outputs. Prior analyses identified issues in individual benchmarks (SWE-bench test insufficiency, KernelBench fuzzing gaps, WebArena rate limits, BIRD annotation noise) but none provided a systematic, actionable checklist. The paper also draws on established software-testing practices (unit testing, fuzz testing, E2E testing) to ground its outcome-validity checks, and formalizes two validity criteria—task validity and outcome validity—that are specific to agentic evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Because major AI providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, xAI) now report model capabilities primarily through agentic benchmarks, systematic evaluation flaws of the magnitude found here (up to 100% relative error) directly distort the public record of AI progress. The finding that 7 of 10 assessed benchmarks violate both task and outcome validity, and that all 10 have reporting gaps, suggests the field needs a shared quality standard before agentic leaderboards can be trusted. Open questions include whether the checklist is exhaustive as agent capabilities evolve, how to handle benchmarks that depend on dynamic external resources, and whether the necessary-but-not-sufficient framing leaves room for undetected failure modes.
