## Problem

In multi-objective learning, achieving good trade-offs across K competing tasks may require a model class G with much larger capacity than the classes H_k needed to solve individual tasks, inflating the labeled sample complexity to Θ(Kd_G/ε²). It was unclear whether access to unlabeled data could reduce this cost, since prior semi-supervised results for single-objective learning require the marginals to carry label information. This paper asks: given each task is solvable in H_k, how much labeled and unlabeled data is needed to recover the Pareto set in a larger class G, and for which losses can unlabeled data help?

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes n_k labeled pairs (X_i^k, Y_i^k) and N_k unlabeled covariates X̃_i^k for each of K tasks, along with the hypothesis classes {H_k} (each containing a Bayes-optimal solution) and a larger class G ⊇ ∪H_k, plus a scalarization family S. It outputs a family of models {ĝ_s : s ∈ S} ⊆ G, one per scalarization, each minimizing the estimated s-trade-off. A key structural detail is that the second optimization step (over G) uses only unlabeled data to compute empirical risk discrepancies d̂_k(f; ĥ_k) = (1/N_k)Σ ℓ_k(ĥ_k(X̃_i^k), f(X̃_i^k)), so the labeled data enters only through the first step of estimating the individual task models.

## Method

The paper establishes a minimax lower bound showing that for non-Bregman losses (e.g., zero-one), the label complexity Θ(Kd_G/ε²) is unavoidable even with infinite unlabeled data and known Bayes-optimal classifiers, because such losses lack the risk decomposition needed to separate task-specific noise from model discrepancy. For Bregman losses, the key property is that the excess risk decomposes as E_k(f) = d_k(f; f_k*) = E[ℓ_k(f_k*(X), f(X))], which depends only on the marginal P_X^k and can be estimated from unlabeled data. The pseudo-labeling algorithm (PL-MOL) first learns empirical risk minimizers ĥ_k ∈ H_k from labeled data, then minimizes the scalarized empirical discrepancy ŝ(d̂_1(·;ĥ_1), …, d̂_K(·;ĥ_K)) over G using unlabeled data. Uniform convergence bounds (Theorem 1) show the labeled sample complexity depends only on {H_k} while G appears solely in the unlabeled complexity, and localized Rademacher bounds (Theorem 2) yield faster, distribution-specific rates under strong convexity and smoothness assumptions, with the algorithm adapting to the local geometry of the Pareto set within G.

## Results

The paper establishes that for Bregman losses, the pseudo-labeling algorithm (PL-MOL) reduces labeled sample complexity from Θ(Kd_G/ε²) (supervised) to Õ(Kd_H/ε⁴) (Theorem 1) or Õ(Kd_H/ε) (Theorem 2, localized), while the complexity of G appears only in the unlabeled sample complexity Õ(Kd_G/ε²). A hardness result (Proposition 1) shows this improvement is impossible for zero-one loss: even with infinite unlabeled data and access to Bayes-optimal classifiers, the label complexity remains Ω(Kd_G/ε²). In the Lipschitz regression example (Corollary 1), PL-MOL achieves the minimax rate (L_H/n_k)^(2/3) + (L_G/N_k)^(2/3), and the toy experiment (Fig. 2) confirms that PL-MOL reaches small excess s-trade-off with only 2^5 labeled samples when N=2^12 unlabeled samples are available, whereas ERM-MOL requires ~2^12 labeled samples for comparable performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is ERM-MOL (Algorithm 2), the supervised S-MOL method from Sükenik & Lampert [59], which minimizes the empirical s-trade-off directly on labeled data. PL-MOL wins decisively in the regime d_G ≫ d_H: while ERM-MOL always pays the full statistical cost of the large class G (requiring ~Kd_G/ε² labeled samples per task), PL-MOL's labeled complexity depends only on the smaller task-specific classes H_k. In the toy Lipschitz regression experiment (Fig. 2), with L_H=0.2 and large L_G, PL-MOL achieves a small excess s-trade-off with only 32 labeled samples (n=2^5) when 4096 unlabeled samples (N=2^12) are available, whereas ERM-MOL needs labeled sample sizes on the order of 2^12 to reach comparable performance. Conversely, when labeled data is abundant (n=2^12) but unlabeled data is limited, only PL-MOL continues to improve with additional unlabeled samples, confirming that the benefit is specifically in absorbing the complexity of G into the unlabeled budget. The paper also shows that for non-Bregman losses (zero-one, hinge), no amount of unlabeled data can improve upon the supervised rate, establishing a sharp boundary on when semi-supervision helps.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the supervised S-MOL framework of Sükenik & Lampert [59] and Cortes et al. [19] to the semi-supervised setting, where the learner additionally has access to unlabeled data from each task's marginal distribution. The key technical enabler is the Bregman risk decomposition from Banerjee et al. [7], which allows excess risks to be estimated from unlabeled data via pseudo-labeling. The localized analysis builds on the local Rademacher complexity framework of Bartlett, Bousquet & Mendelson [9], and the variational characterization of Pareto minimizers draws on convex analysis tools from Zeidler [69]. The paper departs from prior semi-supervised learning theory by showing that the mechanism by which unlabeled data helps is fundamentally different: no clusterability or manifold assumptions on the marginal are needed, since unlabeled data helps the learner determine relative instance importance across tasks rather than recovering label information.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean dichotomy: for non-Bregman losses (e.g., zero-one), even infinite unlabeled data plus known Bayes classifiers cannot reduce label complexity, while for Bregman losses (square, cross-entropy), a simple pseudo-labeling algorithm fully absorbs the statistical cost of the larger model class G into the unlabeled budget. This gives practitioners a clear criterion for when semi-supervised multi-objective learning is worthwhile. Open questions include whether the norm-equivalence assumption (Assumption 4) is truly necessary for adaptive rates, whether the framework extends to non-linear scalarizations, and how it applies to generative models. The tension between uniform control over all scalarizations and local adaptivity remains unresolved.
