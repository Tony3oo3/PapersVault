## Problem

Existing vision-based intrusion detection methods perform only simple binary detection (object present/absent in a restricted area) without comprehensive scene understanding such as summarizing, localizing, or describing intrusion events. No benchmark or dataset exists to evaluate whether Multimodal Large Language Models can perform this richer, multi-faceted intrusion scene understanding, leaving the capability of current MLLMs in this domain entirely unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253658/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a single RGB image (1024×2048, from Cityscapes or BDD-100K) paired with a structured text question prompt and produces task-specific outputs across six subtasks: binary intrusion/non-intrusion judgments, fine-grained person intrusion category labels, summary analyses counting intrusion and non-intrusion behaviors, object bounding-box coordinates for the most dangerous intruders, intrusion type identification, and free-text scene descriptions. Five subtasks are evaluated as multiple-choice (Accuracy) and one open-ended subtask (ISD) is scored with BLEU-4, with a randomization strategy applied to answer options to prevent positional bias.

## Method

The paper introduces an automatic VQA generation pipeline that leverages existing Cityscapes detection and segmentation annotations to compute overlapping pixel points (threshold=20) between objects and the area-of-interest mask, producing ground-truth intrusion/non-intrusion labels that are written back into XML files and combined with images to generate 3000 evaluation and 8925 training VQA pairs across six subtasks. For improving MLLM performance, a three-stage LoRA-based supervised fine-tuning framework is proposed: Stage 1 (I-VIP) uses a descriptive system prompt to instill coarse-grained intrusion perception; Stage 2 (I-COT) introduces a five-step chain-of-thought reasoning template to teach the model to explain why an event constitutes intrusion; Stage 3 (I-VQA) fine-tunes on 2975 structured VQA pairs to sharpen fine-grained, task-specific understanding. The progressive perception→reasoning→understanding ordering is validated by training loss curves showing distinct learning phases, and the framework lifts Qwen2.5-VL-3B from 50.79% to 72.15% average accuracy, surpassing all proprietary MLLMs tested.

## Results

On the MLLM-ISU benchmark (Cityscape-based, 3000 VQA pairs across six subtasks), the best zero-shot MLLM is Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct with 50.79% average accuracy, followed by DeepSeek-VL2-small (50.15%) and GPT-4o (47.12%). Human performance reaches 71.39%, indicating a substantial gap. After applying the proposed three-stage post-training framework (I-VIP → I-COT → I-VQA), Qwen2.5-VL-3B improves to 72.15% and Qwen2.5-VL-7B to 78.37%, both surpassing human-level performance. On the more diverse MLLM-ISU-BDD benchmark (BDD-100K-based), the best model is DeepSeek-VL2-tiny at 56.08%, still well below the 73.90% human score.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct (50.79%) outperforms all proprietary MLLMs, beating GPT-4o (47.12%) by 3.67 points, Gemini-1.5-pro (42.17%) by 8.62 points, and Claude-3.7-sonnet (41.27%) by 9.52 points on average accuracy. Among open-source models, DeepSeek-VL2-small (50.15%) is the closest competitor, while the larger Qwen2.5-VL-7B (42.05%) paradoxically underperforms its 3B counterpart by 8.74 points, attributed to overthinking on simple binary-classification subtasks. On the harder BDD-100K-based benchmark, DeepSeek-VL2-tiny leads at 56.08%, with Qwen2.5-VL-3B second at 53.08%, and GPT-4o dropping to 48.32%. After the proposed three-stage post-training, Qwen2.5-VL-7B jumps from 42.05% to 78.37%, surpassing even the human baseline (71.39%), while Qwen2.5-VL-3B reaches 72.15%. Notably, all models struggle severely on the open-ended Intrusion Scene Descriptions subtask (BLEU-4 scores below 20 for most models), and the ISA subtask remains the weakest across all models (best zero-shot: 37.00% by Gemini-1.5-pro).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two previously separate research threads: (1) domain-specific intrusion detection architectures (PIDNet, MF-ID, MMID, Ada-iD) that perform single-task detection/segmentation on Cityscapes-derived datasets, and (2) the growing family of MLLM evaluation benchmarks (MMAD, FAVOR-Bench, OpenEMMA, EMMA) that probe multimodal understanding on structured VQA tasks. Rather than building on a specific prior method, the paper repurposes the annotation infrastructure of prior intrusion detection works (detection boxes + segmentation masks from Cityscapes) into an automatic VQA generation pipeline, and applies the MLLM benchmarking paradigm to a domain where no such evaluation existed. The three-stage post-training framework (I-VIP → I-COT → I-VQA) follows the general progressive fine-tuning paradigm seen in instruction tuning literature but is specialized with intrusion-domain prompts and chain-of-thought templates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even frontier proprietary models (GPT-4o at 47.12%) and the best open-source models (~50.8%) fall well short of human performance (71.39%) on intrusion scene understanding, exposing a concrete gap in current MLLM capabilities for safety-critical visual reasoning. The three-stage LoRA fine-tuning framework closes much of this gap (3B: 50.79% → 72.15%), suggesting that domain-specific post-training is a practical path to deploying MLLMs in intrusion monitoring. Key open questions include: whether the 'overthinking' phenomenon (larger models underperforming smaller ones on simple binary judgments) generalizes beyond Qwen2.5-VL, whether the framework transfers to truly novel scenes without retraining, and whether the narrow training data (~9K pairs) limits robustness. The paper's scope is limited to two base datasets and two trained model families, and no error bars or multi-seed results are reported.
