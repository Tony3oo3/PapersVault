## Problem

Existing data fusion methods for causal inference require either no unmeasured confounding (NUC) in observational data or study exchangeability between RCT and observational cohorts to point-identify treatment effects. When both assumptions fail simultaneously—a common practical scenario—classical estimators break down and no principled framework exists to quantify how severe the violations must be to overturn a causal conclusion. This paper addresses that gap by developing a partial identification framework with interpretable sensitivity parameters for the simultaneous-violation setting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255038/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two datasets—RCT units (S=1) and observational units (S=0)—each providing treatment T, outcome Y, and covariates X, along with user-specified sensitivity parameters ρ ≥ 0 (relative magnitude of unmeasured confounding in the observational cohort) and γ ≥ 0 (relative magnitude of study exchangeability violation). It produces lower and upper bounds on the ATE and CATE as functions of (ρ, γ), along with confidence intervals via the efficient influence function. The primary visualization is a breakdown frontier plot over the (ρ, γ) plane, partitioned into four regions: conclusive, tentative, inconclusive, and incompatible (where the parameter values contradict observed cross-study discrepancies).

## Method

The core mechanism introduces two ratio-based sensitivity parameters: ρ bounds the relative deviation between observed and counterfactual outcome means within the observational cohort (quantifying NUC violation), and γ bounds the relative deviation between potential outcome means across the RCT and observational populations (quantifying exchangeability violation). For each covariate-treatment profile, the paper constructs two upper bounds on the unidentifiable observational potential outcome—one from RCT data scaled by (1+γ) and one from observational data inflated by (1+ρ)—then takes their minimum (and maximum of the corresponding lower bounds) to obtain sharp conditional potential outcome bounds. Because the resulting min/max operators are non-differentiable, the authors replace them with the Boltzmann operator (a softmax-weighted average controlled by a temperature parameter α) to obtain smooth, pathwise-differentiable approximations that converge to the sharp bounds as α→∞. From the smoothed functional, they derive the efficient influence function and construct doubly robust, cross-fitted estimators that achieve √n-consistency under standard nuisance convergence rates. Finally, they operationalize the Masten-Poirier breakdown frontier by sweeping over a grid of (ρ, γ) values, estimating bounds and confidence intervals at each point, and classifying each cell into conclusive, tentative, inconclusive, or incompatible regions.

## Results

On the Project STAR dataset (11,601 RCT + 1,780 observational students), the breakdown frontier analysis shows that the positive ATE of small class sizes remains conclusively supported as long as study exchangeability violations (γ) stay below 5%, even under substantial unmeasured confounding (ρ). Given test scores range from 486–745 (mean 618), this implies study selection bias would need to shift outcomes by over 30 points on average to overturn the conclusion. Subgroup analysis reveals the positive effect is more robust for students who enrolled in kindergarten at age six or older compared to those who enrolled before age six, consistent with developmental psychology findings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not perform head-to-head quantitative comparisons against existing data fusion estimators, as its contribution is a new partial identification framework rather than a point-estimation method. Existing approaches (e.g., Parikh et al. 2023, Yang et al. 2023, Lin et al. 2025) require assuming either no unmeasured confounding (A5) or study exchangeability (A6) holds, and cannot produce valid bounds when both fail simultaneously. The proposed framework fills this gap by providing interpretable two-parameter sensitivity bounds (ρ, γ) with doubly robust estimators, enabling researchers to quantify how severe violations must be to overturn causal conclusions. The simulation study (Figure 2) validates the framework's behavior: the conclusive region expands with larger treatment effects and shrinks with greater unobserved confounding, while the incompatible region grows with confounding magnitude. The Project STAR application demonstrates that prior findings of unmeasured confounding (von Hippel & Wagner 2018; Justman 2018) are captured by the incompatible region at small (ρ, γ) values, while the positive ATE conclusion remains robust across a wide range of simultaneous violations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the data fusion literature for causal inference (Parikh et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2025), which typically assumes either no unmeasured confounding (NUC) in observational data or study exchangeability between RCT and observational cohorts, to the case where both assumptions are simultaneously violated. It draws on the Masten-Poirier (2020) breakdown frontier framework for visualizing sensitivity, the Chernozhukov et al. (2018) DML/cross-fitting machinery for doubly robust estimation, and the smooth-approximation strategy from Levis et al. (2025) and Ben-Michael (2025) to handle the non-differentiable max/min operators in the partial identification bounds. The key departure is introducing two interpretable sensitivity parameters (ρ for confounding, γ for exchangeability violation) that jointly bound treatment effects, whereas prior sensitivity analyses in data fusion address only one assumption at a time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a practical gap: when both NUC and study exchangeability fail (a common real-world scenario), existing methods cannot even bound the treatment effect, yet researchers still need to know whether conclusions are robust. The Project STAR application demonstrates that the positive class-size effect survives substantial simultaneous violations, strengthening confidence in a landmark educational policy finding. Open questions include how to calibrate ρ and γ from domain knowledge, whether sharper bounds are achievable by incorporating known outcome support, and how to extend the framework to multiple datasets, continuous treatments, or dynamic regimes. The incompatibility test for (ρ, γ) pairs also does not yet account for nuisance estimation uncertainty, leaving room for methodological refinement.
