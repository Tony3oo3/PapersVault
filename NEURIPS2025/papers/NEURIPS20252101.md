## Problem

Behavioral cloning only supervises an agent at states the expert visited, so when the agent makes a mistake and drifts out of the demonstration support, it lacks the ability to recover. This paper asks whether an agent can learn to recover from its own mistakes at test time—matching expert outcomes in unseen situations—using only the same expert demonstrations a standard BC pipeline would consume, without additional human corrections.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252101/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent receives RGB images from a wrist-mounted camera and a front-facing camera (downscaled to 64×64 for world-model training) along with a 7-dimensional proprioceptive state (end-effector position, orientation, and gripper position). At test time, the base Diffusion Policy first generates a nominal 8-step action plan; SAILOR then outputs a corrected 8-step plan by adding a learned residual Δ to each step, where each action is a 7-dim vector in [−1, 1]. Only the first action of the corrected plan is executed before re-planning in an MPC loop.

## Method

SAILOR learns three components from a mixture of expert demonstrations and base-policy rollouts: a Dreamer-style RSSM world model (encoder, latent dynamics, decoder), a reward model trained via a moment-matching discriminator between expert and learner latent states, and an ensemble of critic networks for truncated-horizon bootstrapping. At inference, MPPI searches over residual action chunks in the latent world model, scoring N=256 imagined rollouts with the RM and critic over J=6 iterations, then executes the first step of the best corrected plan. Training proceeds in three phases: (1) warm-start, where ~20% of the interaction budget collects base-policy rollouts to pre-train WM/RM/V on hybrid data; (2) online fine-tuning, where the full SAILOR stack collects rollouts and periodically re-fits all components on the growing hybrid buffer; and (3) expert iteration, where the search outputs are relabeled and distilled back into the base policy via BC, recycling test-time compute. The key distinction from prior L2S or residual RL work is that SAILOR infers the entire search process—world model, reward model, and planner—solely from expert demonstrations and self-generated rollouts, requiring no ground-truth simulator, no explicit reward function, and no human corrections beyond the initial BC dataset.

## Results

Across 12 visual manipulation tasks from RoboMimic, RoboSuite, and ManiSkill3, SAILOR consistently outperforms Diffusion Policy (DP) trained via behavioral cloning on the same demonstrations, with particularly large gaps in the low-data regime (e.g., 5–20 demos). Scaling DP's training data by 5–10× (up to 200 demonstrations) still fails to close the performance gap, as DP plateaus after ~100 demos. SAILOR is also significantly more interaction-efficient than the model-free inverse RL baseline DPPO-IRL, which cannot match SAILOR even with 5× the environment interaction budget. All results report Success Rate (SR) over 50 rollouts with mean ± standard error across 3 seeds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SAILOR is compared against two baselines. First, Diffusion Policy (DP) trained via behavioral cloning on the same expert demonstrations: SAILOR wins on all 12 tasks at every dataset scale tested, with the gap being largest in the low-data regime (e.g., 1–10 demos for Door, 5 demos for Lift/PokeCube). Critically, scaling DP's demonstrations by 5–10× (up to 200 demos) does not close the gap, as DP performance plateaus around 100 demos. Second, DPPO-IRL, a model-free inverse RL method that directly updates DP parameters using a learned reward model: SAILOR is substantially more interaction-efficient, outperforming DPPO-IRL even when the latter is granted 5× the environment interaction budget. This highlights the value of the learned world model, which allows SAILOR to spawn counterfactual trajectories from each observation rather than treating each real-world step as a single data point.

## Relation to Prior Work

SAILOR operationalizes the Learning to Search (L2S) paradigm of Ratliff et al. (2009) for visual robot manipulation by combining a Dreamer-style RSSM world model, a moment-matching reward model trained on hybrid expert/learner data, and MPPI-based residual planning, all initialized from a Diffusion Policy base. It departs from prior L2S work (e.g., TD-MPC2, AlphaGo-style search) by requiring no ground-truth simulator or reward function at test time, and from residual RL by replacing a learned residual policy with explicit test-time search. The expert iteration distillation phase connects to dual policy iteration (Sun et al., 2018) and STaR-style bootstrapping in LLMs, while the hybrid world model training follows the agnostic system identification line of Ross & Bagnell (2012) and Ren et al. (2024b).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 5–10× more demonstrations for behavioral cloning still cannot close the performance gap with SAILOR challenges the dominant 'scale the data' narrative in robot learning and suggests that learning verifiers (reward models) rather than just generators (policies) may be a more data-efficient path, especially as task horizons grow. The demonstrated robustness to reward hacking (no degradation with 8× planning samples) is encouraging for test-time scaling in embodied settings, though the evidence is limited to 12 simulation tasks. Open questions include whether the framework transfers to foundation VLA policies, real-robot deployment with compounding model error, and whether the interaction-efficiency advantage persists in more stochastic or partially observable environments.
