## Problem

Existing dynamical systems reconstruction (DSR) methods require purpose-training on each specific system and cannot generalize zero-shot to novel dynamics. Time series foundation models like Chronos, while offering zero-shot forecasting, fail at DSR because they treat dimensions independently, are optimized for short-term prediction rather than long-term invariant statistics, and suffer from context parroting that masks true chaotic dynamics. No prior model could reconstruct a novel DS's attractor geometry and long-term temporal statistics from a short context signal alone, without any retraining.

## Input / Output

The model takes a context time series C of arbitrary length T_C (flexible by design, not fixed) and arbitrary observation dimensionality N, producing forward-iterated predictions of arbitrary length. For univariate empirical data, the input is first lifted via delay embedding (using autocorrelation-estimated lags) or positional encoding (sinusoidal phases tied to the dominant autocorrelation period) to approximate the underlying higher-dimensional DS. At test time, no fine-tuning occurs: the model simply forward-iterates from the last context time step, with the context guiding expert selection throughout the entire forecast horizon.

## Method

DynaMix is a mixture-of-experts architecture with J=10 AL-RNN experts (each ~10k parameters total), where the gating network selects and weights experts at every time step based on both the current latent state and the provided context. The gating uses a state attention mechanism that computes softmax weights over context timesteps by measuring the distance between projected latent states (via a learnable matrix D plus exploration noise) and actual context observations, combined with CNN-extracted temporal features of the context, all fed through a small MLP to produce expert weights. Training employs sparse teacher forcing (STF), a control-theoretic technique that replaces forward-iterated latent states with data-inferred states at fixed intervals τ=10, preventing gradient explosion on chaotic systems while still allowing the model to explore future trajectories during training. The entire model is pre-trained on just 34 diverse 3D chaotic/cyclic systems (~0.6M sequences) with a standard MSE loss plus a regularization term encouraging wider state-space exploration, and requires no retraining or fine-tuning at test time.

## Results

On the 54-system Gilpin chaos benchmark, DynaMix achieves significantly lower D_stsp (geometrical disagreement) and D_H (temporal disagreement) than all tested TS foundation models (Chronos, Chronos-2, Panda, Mamba4Cast, TimesFM, TTM), while matching the best short-term MASE scores. On empirical time series (Table 1), DynaMix attains the best D_stsp on 7 of 8 datasets (e.g., 0.02 on partially observed Lorenz-63, 0.17 on human fMRI, 0.22 on ETTh1) and the best D_H on 6 of 8, despite having never seen any empirical data in training. The model uses only ~10k parameters and achieves inference times orders of magnitude faster than Chronos (which has millions of parameters), while also outperforming custom-trained DSR models (AL-RNN, Neural ODEs, reservoir computers) that benefit from in-domain fine-tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DynaMix decisively outperforms all six TS foundation models (Chronos, Chronos-2, Panda, Mamba4Cast, TimesFM, TTM) on the 54-system DSR benchmark in both D_stsp and D_H, with the gap being largest for Chronos (which suffers from context parroting, yielding near-zero Lyapunov exponents ~0.02 vs. ground truth ~0.87 for Lorenz-63). On empirical time series, DynaMix achieves the best D_stsp on 7/8 datasets and best D_H on 6/8, with particularly large margins on human fMRI (0.17 vs. 5.78 for Chronos) and EEG (0.79 vs. 9.45 for Chronos); the sole exception is Traffic, where Chronos-t5-base leads on D_stsp (0.40 vs. 0.81) and D_H (0.10 vs. 0.21). On short-term MAE, DynaMix is competitive but not uniformly best—TimesFM and Chronos win on Traffic (3.16/3.66 vs. 6.93) and ETTh1 (3.04/3.22 vs. 4.92)—yet DynaMix still wins on fMRI (0.45 vs. 0.85) and EEG (1.07 vs. 1.10). Against custom-trained DSR models (AL-RNN with STF, Neural ODEs, reservoir computers) that enjoy the advantage of in-domain fine-tuning, DynaMix keeps pace or occasionally outperforms them despite being strictly zero-shot. Notably, Panda included variations of the test-set DS in its training corpus, biasing the evaluation in its favor, yet DynaMix still outperforms it on DSR metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

DynaMix sits at the intersection of two previously separate research threads: (1) DSR methods (AL-RNN, reservoir computing, neural ODEs, Koopman operators) that faithfully reconstruct long-term dynamics but require per-system training, and (2) TS foundation models (Chronos, Mamba4Cast, TimesFM) that offer zero-shot forecasting but are optimized for short-term prediction and treat dimensions independently. The paper departs from both by combining the AL-RNN expert architecture (Brenner et al., 2024) with sparse teacher forcing (Mikhaeil et al., 2022; Brenner et al., 2022) inside a mixture-of-experts wrapper with context-conditioned gating, producing the first architecture that performs true zero-shot DSR without any fine-tuning. Prior meta-learning approaches toward DSR foundation models (e.g., Nzoyem et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2021) still require parameter adaptation, which DynaMix eliminates entirely.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that TS foundation models with millions of parameters fundamentally fail at DSR due to univariate treatment, short-term optimization, and context parroting, while a ~10k-parameter model trained on only 34 synthetic 3D chaotic systems outperforms them on long-term statistics and often on short-term forecasts for real-world data (EEG, fMRI, traffic, weather) it never saw in training. This challenges the prevailing assumption that large-scale pre-training on diverse empirical data is necessary for competitive time series forecasting, suggesting DS-theoretic inductive biases may be more important than scale. Key limitations include the narrow training corpus (34 systems, mostly 3D), restriction to stationary time series, and sensitivity to embedding quality; open questions include extending to non-stationary/multiscale data, irregular sampling, and whether scaling the training corpus to include empirical series would further close the gap on short-term forecasting benchmarks.
