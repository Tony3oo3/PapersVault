## Problem

Steinke et al. proved that one-run auditing (ORA) of differential privacy is valid, but left open how close its lower bounds actually are to the true privacy parameter and what algorithmic properties determine that gap. This paper shows that ORA is fundamentally limited by three distinct gaps—non-worst-case privacy for elements, non-worst-case outputs, and interference between simultaneously audited elements—and that for important algorithms like DP-SGD, these gaps can make ORA's bounds far from tight even under optimal strategies.

## Input / Output

The auditor receives oracle access to a randomized algorithm M, a pair vector Z specifying two candidate elements for each of n dataset positions, and a guesser G that maps algorithm outputs to a vector in {-1, 0, 1}^n where 0 denotes abstention. The output is a pair of counts (v, r)—correct guesses and taken guesses—which are converted into a Clopper-Pearson lower bound ε'_β(v,r) on the true privacy level and an uncorrected estimation p⁻¹(v/r). The key intermediate quantity is the distributional privacy loss ℓ_{M,Z,i}(o), a log-likelihood ratio that captures all uncertainty about the dataset when only a single output is observed.

## Method

The paper introduces a hierarchy of relaxed privacy notions—AE-AC-DDP, AC-DDP, and DDP—that each capture one of the three efficacy gaps, and proves (Theorem 5.2) that the optimal ORA efficacy equals AE-AC-DDP, with each successive relaxation corresponding to one gap. Asymptotic tightness is then characterized (Theorem 5.3) as the condition that an unlimited number of elements experience near-worst-case distributional privacy loss. To mitigate the interference gap in practice, the paper proposes two approaches: assigning multiple auditing elements per coordinate (showing an optimal tradeoff between more guesses and more interference), and Adaptive ORA (AORA), a new variant where the guesser conditions on previously revealed bits, which provably and empirically improves auditing of DP-SGD by converting interference into useful information for subsequent guesses.

## Results

The paper provides a complete theoretical characterization of ORA's efficacy, showing that the optimal efficacy is bounded by AE-AC-DDP (Theorem 5.2), which is strictly below the true privacy level p(ε(M)) due to three gaps: non-worst-case element privacy, non-worst-case outputs, and interference. Empirically, on DP-SGD (d=1000, T=100, ε=2, δ=10⁻⁵), ORA with 1 element per coordinate yields a mean 95% lower bound of 0.49 (±0.01) on ε, improving to 0.62 (±0.01) with 8 elements per coordinate. The proposed Adaptive ORA (AORA) outperforms ORA across all settings, and while ORA's bounds degrade beyond 5 elements per coordinate due to interference, AORA's bounds continue to improve with additional elements.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the original One-Run Auditing (ORA) method of Steinke et al. (NeurIPS 2023), which the paper both extends theoretically and improves upon empirically. On DP-SGD, AORA consistently produces higher 95% lower bounds on ε than ORA at every number of elements per coordinate (Figure 3). The advantage is most pronounced when multiple elements share a coordinate: ORA's bounds peak around 5 elements per coordinate and then decline as interference overwhelms the non-adaptive guesser, while AORA's bounds monotonically improve because the adaptive guesser uses previously revealed bits to cancel out interference. The paper also contrasts ORA against classic (multi-run) auditing, showing that classic auditing is asymptotically tight for all algorithms while ORA is not—e.g., for the XOR algorithm ORA's optimal efficacy is exactly 1/2 (random guessing) regardless of n, whereas classic auditing can recover the true privacy level. The theoretical contribution formalizes exactly when ORA fails (Theorem 5.3: ORA is tight iff infinitely many elements have distributional privacy loss approaching ε(M)), a condition that DP-SGD only partially satisfies due to the interference gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the one-run auditing (ORA) framework of Steinke et al. (NeurIPS 2023), who proved ORA's validity and demonstrated empirical results on DP-SGD but left open the question of how precisely ORA can recover true privacy parameters. The authors formalize three fundamental efficacy gaps—non-worst-case privacy for elements, non-worst-case outputs, and interference between elements—using a hierarchy of relaxed privacy notions (DDP, AC-DDP, AE-AC-DDP) that precisely decompose the gap between ORA's achievable efficacy and the true DP level. The work also connects to the broader privacy auditing literature (Jagielski et al., Nasr et al., Malek Esmaeili et al.) and a contemporaneous f-DP-based method by Xiang et al. that independently identifies the interference gap, though this paper provides the complete characterization and mitigation strategies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The three-gap characterization gives practitioners a clear diagnostic framework for interpreting ORA results: a low bound may reflect genuine privacy loss, or it may reflect an inherent limitation of one-run auditing for the specific algorithm being audited. The finding that the interference gap dominates for DP-SGD, and that assigning multiple elements per coordinate (contrary to the standard Dirac canary approach) can improve auditing, has direct practical implications for auditing deployed DP-SGD systems. However, the experimental validation is limited to synthetic gradient canaries and single-step DP-SGD; the authors explicitly leave comprehensive multi-step DP-SGD auditing and realistic ML settings as future work, and the AORA method's benefits in those settings remain untested.
