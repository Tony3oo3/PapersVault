## Problem

State entropy regularization has empirically improved exploration and sample efficiency in RL, but its theoretical connection to robustness has not been formally characterized. The paper asks what type of robustness state entropy provides, how it differs from the well-studied policy entropy regularization, and where its guarantees break down.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250708/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on finite MDPs (state space S, action space A, transition kernel P, reward r) and produces a policy π that is robust to structured perturbations. State entropy is estimated via a k-nearest-neighbor estimator over sampled trajectories, making the number of rollouts per evaluation a critical practical parameter—too few rollouts can cause the entropy estimate to favor deterministic, suboptimal behavior rather than diverse exploration. The robustness guarantees are expressed in terms of explicit uncertainty sets over rewards (LSE-constrained) and transition kernels (geometric-mean-constrained on state distributions).

## Method

The paper proves that maximizing the state-entropy-regularized objective exactly solves a reward-robust RL problem where the adversary's perturbation budget is governed by a log-sum-exp constraint on the reward difference, with the worst-case reward depending on the global state occupancy distribution d_P^π rather than local action probabilities. This contrasts with policy entropy, whose adversarial reward depends only on π(·|s) per state, making it robust to local perturbations but fragile to spatially structured ones. The regularization temperature α monotonically controls the uncertainty set, interpolating between ℓ∞-like (α→0) and ℓ₁-like (α→∞) constraints. Under kernel uncertainty, state entropy yields a non-trivial lower bound via an importance-sampling reduction to reward robustness, and adding policy entropy strictly weakens this bound. The paper also establishes that no entropy-based regularizer can fully solve kernel-robust RL, that entropy regularization can arbitrarily degrade CVaR performance, and that robustness gains are sensitive to the rollout budget used for entropy estimation.

## Results

The paper's primary contribution is theoretical: state entropy regularization exactly solves a reward-robust RL problem (Theorem 3.1) and yields a non-trivial lower bound under kernel uncertainty (Theorem 3.3), while policy entropy only provides protection against locally-informed adversaries. Empirically, on MiniGrid, MuJoCo Pusher, and MuJoCo Ant, state-entropy-regularized agents significantly outperform both unregularized and policy-entropy-regularized baselines under spatially correlated perturbations (e.g., large walls blocking optimal paths), while policy entropy retains a slight edge under uniform small-scale perturbations. The robustness gains of state entropy are sensitive to the rollout budget: with only 4 rollouts for entropy estimation, state entropy can underperform policy entropy, though nominal performance remains unchanged. Increasing the regularization temperature monotonically improves robust performance until it plateaus, consistent with the theoretical interpolation between ℓ∞- and ℓ₁-type uncertainty sets (Theorem 3.2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against policy entropy regularization (Eysenbach & Levine, ICLR 2022), state entropy wins decisively on spatially correlated perturbations: in MiniGrid with large wall segments blocking the maze, in Pusher with obstacles along the optimal trajectory, and in Ant with a vertical wall, state-entropy agents maintain substantially higher success rates while policy-entropy agents fail catastrophically. Under uniform small-scale perturbations (scattered obstacle tiles in MiniGrid), policy entropy shows a slight advantage, but adding state entropy does not degrade this effect. Against the PRMDP/NRMDP robust baselines (Chen et al., WSDM 2019), state entropy significantly outperforms on the Pusher obstacle task (Figure 5a), though the authors note this is specific to spatially correlated perturbations rather than a general dominance. Theoretically, state entropy provides robustness against globally-informed adversaries (depending on the full state occupancy d^π), while policy entropy only protects against locally-informed adversaries (depending on π(·|s) per state), and adding policy entropy to state entropy weakens the kernel-robustness lower bound (Appendix A.4.1).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the regularization-robustness duality established for policy entropy by Eysenbach & Levine (2022) to state entropy regularization, providing the first formal characterization of what type of robustness state entropy induces. It builds on the state entropy maximization literature initiated by Hazan et al. (2018) and made practical by Seo et al. (2021) via k-NN entropy estimation, but reframes the contribution through the lens of robust MDPs rather than exploration. The analysis also connects to Brekelmans et al. (2022) on policy regularizers as implicit adversaries, generalizing their convex-conjugate framework to the state-occupancy setting and explicitly contrasting local (policy) versus global (state) adversarial capabilities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practitioners with a clear theoretical criterion for choosing between state and policy entropy regularization: state entropy protects against spatially correlated, globally structured perturbations (e.g., obstacles blocking optimal paths), while policy entropy handles diffuse, local noise. Two important negative results temper the optimism: no entropy regularization can solve general kernel-robust RL problems, and entropy can arbitrarily degrade risk-averse (CVaR) performance. A practically critical open question is the rollout sensitivity of state entropy estimation—its robustness benefits vanish in low-sample regimes, and the paper does not yet offer a principled way to determine the minimum rollout budget needed for a given environment.
