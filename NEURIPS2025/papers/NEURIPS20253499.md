## Problem

Anomaly detection in chemical processes is critical for preventing catastrophic failures, yet the field has lacked publicly available real-world data for method development and evaluation. The long-standing Tennessee Eastman Process benchmark relies on simulated data from a hypothetical process that cannot capture the noise, operational variability, and failure complexity of actual chemical plants, while real industrial data remains proprietary. NoBOOM addresses this gap by releasing the first collection of real-world multivariate time-series datasets from operating chemical distillation plants, including data from a major industrial partner (BASF SE).

## Input / Output

Each dataset provides multivariate time-series sensor readings (18–244 channels measuring temperature, pressure, flow rate, and liquid level) sampled at 1 Hz for batch plants or 30 s for continuous plants, spanning 986 to 1.8 million time steps. Beyond standard binary anomaly labels, induced anomalies carry a three-phase annotation distinguishing cause injection (no visible effect), visible anomaly recognized by operator, and post-removal recovery, enabling evaluation of detection timing relative to human operators. The industrial dataset uses anonymized, order-randomized sensor names and omits explicit units and sampling frequencies for confidentiality, while lab datasets include full ontology-based metadata linking each anomaly to its physical cause and affected plant components.

## Method

The core contribution is a benchmark suite of six datasets spanning batch and continuous distillation at laboratory, pilot, and full industrial scale, with anomalies inspired by documented industrial accident failure modes (e.g., cooling failures, sensor drift, pipeline clogging, foaming). The evaluation protocol centers on the ALARM score, which provably satisfies industrial requirements by rewarding early detection, penalizing false alarms with a tunable tolerance parameter, and respecting temporal ordering of predictions—properties that standard point-wise or point-adjusted metrics lack. Supplementary metrics (event-wise recall, average alarm frequency, early/late detection frequency) provide operational insight into whether a detector outperforms or lags behind a human operator. To verify non-triviality, the authors evaluate a range of baselines (local-mean deviation, k-means, isolation forest, LSTM autoencoders, LSTM predictors) across all datasets, showing consistently sub-optimal performance that confirms the benchmark presents a meaningful challenge for TSAD methods.

## Results

The NoBOOM benchmark demonstrates non-triviality by showing that simple baselines (local mean deviation and first-order difference methods from Wu & Keogh [35]) achieve consistently sub-optimal ALARM scores across all six datasets, ranging from 0.05 (industrial) to 0.80 (batch abm). Event-wise recall similarly spans 0.05–0.85, with the industrial dataset (ind) being the hardest (ALARM 0.05, recall 0.05) and the batch abm dataset the easiest (ALARM 0.80, recall 0.85). Average Alarm Frequency (AAF) values of 1.13–2.55 and Early Detection Frequency (EDF) values of 0.00–1.00 further confirm that no simple method reliably detects anomalies early or with low false-alarm rates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates the simple baselines introduced by Wu & Keogh [35] (thresholding on local mean deviation, with and without first-order differencing) across all six NoBOOM datasets. These baselines fail to achieve near-perfect detection on any dataset: the best ALARM score is 0.80 on batch abm, while the industrial dataset (ind) yields only 0.05, indicating that the real-world multi-stage process is far more challenging than the controlled laboratory settings. On the continuous water dataset (wat), the baselines detect anomalies only in phase 3 (LDF = 1.00), meaning they alarm only after the operator has already identified and removed the fault cause—rendering them practically useless for early intervention. The batch abm dataset is the sole exception where baselines perform reasonably (ALARM 0.80, recall 0.85), but even there EDF is 0.00, so no early detection is achieved. Overall, the results confirm that NoBOOM presents a genuinely non-trivial challenge that cannot be solved by trivial, training-free methods, motivating the development of more sophisticated ML-based anomaly detection approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the long-standing reliance on the Tennessee Eastman Process (TEP), a simulated hypothetical process that has served as the de facto benchmark for chemical process anomaly detection for over two decades. It builds directly on the ALARM score from Wagner et al. [34] as its primary evaluation metric, which was designed to satisfy industrial safety requirements that standard point-wise or permutation-invariant TSAD metrics fail to capture. The benchmark also addresses known flaws in existing multivariate TSAD datasets (SWaT, WADI, SMAP, SMD) identified by Wu & Keogh [35] and others, specifically the use of oversimplified signals and trivial anomalies that do not reflect real industrial behavior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NoBOOM is the first publicly available collection of real-world chemical process data with labeled anomalies, including long-term industrial data from BASF SE, which bridges a critical gap between academic TSAD research and industrial deployment. The three-phase labeling scheme (cause injection, active anomaly, recovery) enables evaluation of early-detection capability relative to human operators, a dimension absent from prior benchmarks. Key limitations include the relatively high anomaly density compared to real production, the inability to simulate extreme failures like explosions for safety reasons, and the lack of detailed annotations on the industrial dataset due to confidentiality. Open questions include whether methods validated on these datasets transfer to other chemical unit operations and whether the ALARM score's false-alarm tolerance parameter should be adapted per application.
