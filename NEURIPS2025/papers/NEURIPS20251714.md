## Problem

Diffusion- and flow-based robot policies apply a uniform inference budget at every control step, wasting computation on simple subtasks (e.g., free-space arm movements) while potentially underperforming on precision-critical phases (e.g., sub-millimeter placements). Unlike LLMs that adaptively allocate reasoning steps to query difficulty, existing generative controllers lack any mechanism to adjust their solver, step count, or integration mode at test time without retraining.

## Input / Output

The system consumes RGB-D observations at each control cycle. The difficulty classifier (a 32×32×3 CNN or a 224×224 VLM) outputs one of six discrete state categories (Initial, Near, Grabbing, Stochastic, Continuous, End), which is mapped to an inference configuration triple ⟨step count ∈ {1,5,10,50,100}, solver ∈ {Euler, Heun, RK4}, integration ∈ {ODE, SDE}⟩. The SIP policy then generates a full robot action sequence conditioned on the observation and the selected configuration, with the number of integration steps directly controlling the computational cost per control cycle.

## Method

DA-SIP unifies diffusion and flow policies under the stochastic interpolant (SI) framework, where a single trained policy network can perform both ODE and SDE integration with interchangeable interpolants (Linear, VP, GVP) and solvers (Euler, Heun, RK4) at inference time without retraining. A difficulty classifier—implemented as a lightweight ResNet-18 CNN (~20 ms), a few-shot VLM (~500–1000 ms), or a LoRA fine-tuned Qwen2.5-VL-7B (~300–400 ms)—predicts the current state's difficulty from RGB-D input, trained on ~20,000 human-annotated states across six categories. The predicted difficulty level is mapped via an empirically determined schedule to a specific inference configuration triple, enabling the system to use 1-step Euler ODE for free-space motion while reserving 100-step Heun SDE with Tweedie correction for continuous pushing. This adaptive allocation achieves 2.6–4.4× total computation reduction while matching the success rate of fixed maximum-compute baselines across six manipulation benchmarks.

## Results

DA-SIP achieves an average 3.3× reduction in total computation time with the lightweight CNN classifier (2.7× with few-shot VLM, 2.3× with fine-tuned VLM) while maintaining success rates within 1.3–4.7% of the fixed maximum-computation baseline. The largest single-task speedup is 5.55× on Push-T (few-shot VLM) and 4.43× on Transport (CNN). Performance is essentially preserved on simple tasks (Lift, Can at 100%/99%) and degrades by at most 3% on Transport with the CNN classifier, while the fine-tuned VLM actually *improves* success rate by 8% on Tool Hang over the max-compute baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is SIP with a fixed maximum-computation budget (100 steps, Heun, SDE) applied uniformly at every control step. DA-SIP matches or exceeds this baseline's success rate on 5 of 6 tasks: it ties on Lift (100%), Can (99%), and Push-T (87% vs. 86%), and *surpasses* it on Tool Hang (33% vs. 25%, +8 pp with both VLM classifiers) and Square (92% vs. 90%, +2 pp with CNN). The only notable degradation is Transport with the few-shot VLM (62% vs. 80%, −18 pp), though the CNN (−3 pp) and fine-tuned VLM (−1 pp) largely close this gap. Against Diffusion Policy (DDPM/DDIM) in Appendix B, the SI Policy backbone outperforms on Push-T (92.6% vs. 80.7%/81.0%), Block Push (22.7% vs. 21.0%), and Square (95.2% vs. 94.4%/96.2%), but underperforms on Tool Hang (38.1% vs. 53.4% DDPM at 50 steps), indicating that the stochastic interpolant formulation is not universally superior to diffusion for all task types.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper grounds its generative policy in the stochastic interpolant (SI) framework of Albergo et al. (2023) and the SiT codebase of Ma et al. (2024), which unify flow-matching (ODE) and diffusion (SDE) under a single continuous-time formulation. It departs from prior adaptive-inference work in robot control—AdaFlow (Hu et al., 2024) adapts only to action-space variance, and RF-Policy (Hu et al., 2024) adjusts step count—by extending adaptation to the full inference triplet (step count, solver type, ODE/SDE mode) selected by a learned difficulty classifier. Conceptually, the approach mirrors how LLMs allocate more reasoning steps to harder queries (Wei et al., 2022), but applied to the visual manipulation domain with a lightweight CNN or VLM as the difficulty oracle.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 2.6–4.4× compute reduction while matching maximum-compute success rates is practically significant for real-time robotic deployment where inference latency is a hard constraint. The finding that no single SI configuration dominates across tasks—and that excessive steps can actively hurt exploratory manipulation—challenges the default assumption that more inference budget is always better. Key limitations include evaluation exclusively in simulation (no closed-loop real-robot results), weak VLM classifier accuracy (25–53% vs. 81–94% for CNN), and the difficulty-to-configuration mapping being hand-designed rather than learned end-to-end. Open questions include whether this adaptive scheme transfers to large VLA foundation models and whether the difficulty classifier can be replaced by an uncertainty signal from the policy itself.
