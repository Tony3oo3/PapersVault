## Problem

Despite widespread empirical success of teacher-based data filtering in CLIP-style multimodal contrastive learning, no theoretical characterization existed for how filtering improves the learned representation as a function of the clean data fraction η. The paper asks: can one prove a benefit of filtering over unfiltered contrastive learning, and what is the exact dependence on η in each regime?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250081/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on n paired samples (x, x̃) drawn from a linear bimodal generative model where a fraction η of pairs share the same latent variable z (clean) and the remaining 1−η have independently drawn latents (corrupted), with Gaussian noise of diagonal covariance. A scalar filtering threshold θ is the sole hyperparameter. The output consists of a filtered subset of samples and the learned linear embedding matrices G ∈ R^{r×d} and G̃ ∈ R^{r×d̃}, whose right singular subspaces approximate the ground-truth signal subspaces U and Û, measured via chordal (Frobenius-norm sin-Θ) distance.

## Method

The paper analyzes a Train-Filter-Train procedure (Algorithm 1): a teacher is trained on the first n/2 samples via a ρ-regularized linear contrastive loss (which admits a closed-form SVD solution), the teacher's inner-product scores xᵀG_TᵀG̃_T x̃ are used to threshold and retain samples from the second n/2, and a student is trained on the filtered set. The key insight is that the oracle score S(x,x̃; UÛᵀ) decomposes into a signal term zᵀz̃ plus zero-mean noise, yielding a mixture distribution D = ηD₁ + (1−η)D₀ where D₁ (clean) has mean r and D₀ (corrupted) has mean 0 with comparable variance, making threshold-based separation effective. The main theorem (Theorem 1) shows the error scales as 1/√(ηn) for large η (threshold θ = r/2) and as 1/√n independent of η for small η (threshold θ = 0), both strictly improving on the unfiltered rate of 1/(η√n) established by matching upper and lower bounds. The proof combines operator-norm concentration of the filtered cross-covariance S_n(θ), Davis-Kahan subspace perturbation bounds, and careful analysis of conditional expectations and tail probabilities of the Gaussian score distribution.

## Results

The paper establishes that unfiltered contrastive learning achieves a tight error rate of Θ(1/(η√n)) (Corollary 1 upper bound, Proposition 1 lower bound), while teacher-based filtering (Theorem 1) improves this to O(1/√(ηn)) in the large-η regime and O(1/√n) (independent of η) in the small-η regime, with the regime switch occurring at η ≈ 1/r². The synthetic experiment (Figure 3a, d=10, d̃=8, r=4, γ=γ̃=10⁴, n=10M) validates both the 1/η dependence of unfiltered learning and the improved 1/√η / η-independent dependence of filtering across η values from 1 to 10⁻³. The real-data validation (Figure 3b, from Fang et al.) confirms that the qualitative trend of reduced sensitivity to η holds for actual image-text pairs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the unfiltered contrastive learning baseline from Nakada et al. [24], the paper proves a strict improvement: the error dependence on the clean fraction η improves from 1/η to 1/√η (large η) or to η-independent (small η), meaning filtering provides a provable √η or even constant-factor gain in error. This is validated empirically in Figure 3a, where the unfiltered curve follows a 1/η slope while the filtered curve is visibly flatter. Against the empirical data-filtering results of Fang et al. [10] (Data Filtering Networks), the paper's theory explains *why* filtering helps: the oracle score distribution (Figure 2) has a clean component D₁ with mean r and a corrupted component D₀ with mean 0, so thresholding retains high-signal samples from the right tail of D₀ that still carry useful information about U. Figure 3b reproduces Fang et al.'s real-data finding that downstream accuracy degrades more steeply without filtering as η decreases, confirming the theoretical prediction qualitatively. The paper does not directly compare against other filtering methods (e.g., heuristic or norm-based filtering) in a head-to-head empirical table, as the contribution is theoretical rather than algorithmic.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical analysis of multimodal contrastive learning established by Nakada et al. [24] and Ji et al. [14] to the setting of stochastically corrupted data, where a fraction η of image-text pairs have mismatched modalities. While the empirical success of teacher-based filtering (Fang et al. [10], Wang et al. [35]) is well-documented in the CLIP training pipeline, the paper notes that no prior theoretical work had characterized the error rate improvement from filtering. The analysis inherits the linear contrastive learning framework and spiked covariance model from [24, 14], and uses Davis-Kahan-type perturbation results to translate operator-norm concentration of the cross-covariance matrix into subspace recovery guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first rigorous justification for why teacher-based filtering improves representation quality in CLIP-style training, moving the practice from empirical heuristic to theoretically grounded procedure. The counterintuitive finding that error becomes independent of η in the small-η regime (better than the oracle 1/√η rate) suggests filtering is most beneficial precisely when data quality is worst. Key limitations include the linear model assumption, diagonal Gaussian noise, and stochastic (non-adversarial) corruption; open questions include optimal threshold selection, whether single-pass filtering can match the two-step Train-Filter-Train gains, and extension to non-linear encoders and general noise covariances.
