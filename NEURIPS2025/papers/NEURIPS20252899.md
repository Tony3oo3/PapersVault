## Problem

Stochastic gradient descent convergence is well-understood for fully specified objectives, but many important learning problems (causal inference, semiparametric estimation, distributionally robust learning) involve losses that depend on an unknown nuisance parameter g₀ that must be estimated. The convergence behavior of SGD when the loss is evaluated at a misspecified nuisance estimate ĝ — and the precise role of Neyman orthogonality in controlling the resulting bias — had no non-asymptotic theoretical guarantees in the stochastic (online) setting.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a stream of i.i.d. observations Z₁,…,Zₙ from distribution P, a fixed nuisance estimate ĝ ∈ G (computed independently on auxiliary data, where G is a possibly infinite-dimensional Banach/Hilbert space), and a scalar learning rate η. It produces a sequence of finite-dimensional parameter estimates θ⁽ⁿ⁾ ∈ Θ ⊆ ℝᵈ along with explicit non-asymptotic bounds on E[‖θ⁽ⁿ⁾ − θ⋆‖₂²] that decompose into a decaying initialization term, a bias term depending on ‖ĝ − g₀‖_G (squared or fourth power), and a variance term proportional to η. The nuisance estimate is held fixed across all n iterations in the main theorems, though an interleaved two-stream variant is also analyzed.

## Method

The paper establishes that standard SGD with the score oracle S_θ(θ,ĝ;Z) converges linearly to a neighborhood of θ⋆ whose radius scales as ‖ĝ−g₀‖²_G in general, but improves to ‖ĝ−g₀‖⁴_G when the loss satisfies Neyman orthogonality (i.e., the cross-derivative D_g D_θ L vanishes at the optimum). To extend this benefit to non-orthogonal losses, the authors introduce Orthogonalized SGD (OSGD), which replaces the raw score with an approximately orthogonalized oracle Ŝ_no(θ,g;z) = S_θ(θ,g;z) − Γ̂∇_g ℓ(θ,g;z), where Γ̂ is an estimated orthogonalizing operator defined as the least-squares linear projection of the θ-score onto the g-gradient (analogous to a control variate). The OSGD convergence rate interpolates between the nuisance-sensitive ‖ĝ−g₀‖² and nuisance-insensitive ‖ĝ−g₀‖⁴ regimes depending on the estimation error ‖Γ̂−Γ₀‖_Fro: when Γ̂ is accurate to order O(‖ĝ−g₀‖_G), the full fourth-power rate is recovered. The orthogonalizing operator Γ₀ is characterized in closed form as H_θgᵀ H_gg⁻¹ for likelihood-type losses, and the paper provides explicit constructions for partially linear models, CATE, and conditional relative risk examples.

## Results

The paper establishes non-asymptotic convergence guarantees for SGD under nuisance misspecification. Under Neyman orthogonality (Asm. 4), SGD converges linearly to a ball of radius O(‖ĝ−g₀‖⁴_G + η) around the true optimum θ_★ (Theorem 1, Part 2). Without orthogonality, the convergence radius degrades to O(‖ĝ−g₀‖²_G + η) (Theorem 1, Part 1). The proposed OSGD algorithm (Theorem 3) interpolates between these regimes, achieving a bias term of O(‖ĝ−g₀‖⁴_G + ‖ĝ−g₀‖²_G·‖Γ̂−Γ₀‖²_Fro), recovering the nuisance-insensitive rate when the orthogonalizing operator is estimated at rate O(‖ĝ−g₀‖_G).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines; comparisons are made against existing convergence results in the DML/OSL and SGD literature. Compared to Foster and Syrgkanis [2023] and Liu et al. [2022], which provide excess risk bounds for full-batch empirical risk minimization with a fixed training set, Theorem 1 here accounts for the interplay between bias from ĝ and per-iterate progress in SGD, showing the bias does not accumulate over n steps even with a constant learning rate. Compared to the biased SGD literature (Demidovich et al. [2023]), where the convergence radius depends on the average squared bias of the gradient oracle and may not scale with η, the present results naturally leverage Neyman orthogonality to achieve a fourth-power dependence on the nuisance error. In the idealized case of a known nuisance (g = g₀), the rate O((1−μη/2)^n + η) matches the classical non-asymptotic SGD convergence rates of Bach and Moulines [2011] and Cutler et al. [2023], though with a slightly more restrictive learning rate requirement due to the second-moment growth assumption. The OSGD algorithm (Theorem 3) is novel and has no direct prior counterpart; it provides the first convergence guarantee for an iteratively orthogonalized stochastic gradient procedure, interpolating between the nuisance-sensitive and nuisance-insensitive regimes based on the quality of the estimated orthogonalizing operator Γ̂.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-developed threads: stochastic gradient convergence analysis (Bach & Moulines, Cutler et al.) and the double/debiased machine learning (DML) / orthogonal statistical learning (OSL) framework (Chernozhukov et al. 2018a, Foster & Syrgkanis 2023). Prior DML/OSL work provides non-asymptotic excess-risk bounds for full-batch empirical risk minimization with a misspecified nuisance, but leaves open the question of how SGD iterates behave under the same misspecification. The paper fills this gap and further introduces OSGD, which operationalizes the orthogonalizing operator (previously derived by hand via semiparametric theory) as a learnable correction to the gradient oracle, drawing on the least-squares / Riesz regression interpretation from Chernozhukov et al. and Luedtke (2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that Neyman orthogonality upgrades the nuisance-bias dependence from ‖ĝ−g₀‖² to ‖ĝ−g₀‖⁴ in the SGD setting is a natural but previously unproven extension of the full-batch OSL literature, and it gives practitioners a concrete theoretical justification for using orthogonal losses in large-scale stochastic training pipelines. The OSGD algorithm is a practical contribution: it shows that even when a loss is not analytically orthogonal, an estimated orthogonalizing operator can interpolate between the nuisance-sensitive and nuisance-insensitive regimes, reducing the need for case-by-case hand derivations. Open questions include extensions to SGD variants (momentum, Adam), hypothesis testing, reinforcement learning, and the two-stream setting where the nuisance is learned online alongside the target. The analysis is confined to convex, strongly-convex objectives and single-sample gradients, so applicability to non-convex deep learning losses remains unaddressed.
