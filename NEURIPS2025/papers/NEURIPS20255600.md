## Problem

Jointly trained multimodal models frequently underperform ensembles of unimodal models due to modality competition, where one modality rapidly minimizes training error and suppresses learning of the others. Existing balancing strategies either rely on unimodal performance as a proxy for contribution (missing correlated information) or use input-space perturbations that increase computational cost and sensitivity to noise. The paper asks how to efficiently regularize this competition so that all modalities are adequately optimized during joint training.

## Input / Output

The method consumes multimodal inputs (video clips, audio waveforms, text, or optical flow) that are first encoded by modality-specific backbones (ResNet-18, Wav2Vec2, HuBERT, ViViT, Swin-TF) into fixed-dimensional latent representations. A fusion network then combines these latents to produce a class label or sentiment score. Unusually, the regularizer operates entirely in latent space: within-batch permutations of the encoded representations generate perturbed pairs that are passed through the same fusion head, and a small reconstruction head maps labels back to the joint latent space, so no additional input-space augmentations or extra encoder forward passes are required.

## Method

MCR decomposes the joint mutual information I(X₁;X₂;Y) into task-relevant unique CMI terms, shared information, and task-irrelevant shared information, and assigns a dedicated loss to each: (1) L_MIPD bounds each CMI via the Jensen-Shannon divergence between the fusion output on the true pair and on a within-batch permuted pair, (2) L_Con is a supervised contrastive loss that maximizes a lower bound on shared MI between modalities, and (3) L_CEB is a conditional-entropy-bottleneck reconstruction loss that penalizes task-irrelevant alignment by predicting the joint latent from the label. The key differentiator is a game-theoretic gradient scheme: each encoder's update on L_MIPD includes a signed term (k ∈ {−1, 0, 1}) on the other modality's MIPD gradient, so the default 'greedy' strategy (k = −1) lets an underutilized encoder simultaneously boost its own contribution and suppress the dominant one, preventing the zero-sum collapse seen in standard joint training. Latent-space permutation replaces input-space noise or masking, eliminating extra forward passes and task-specific augmentation design.

## Results

MCR achieves the highest accuracy across nearly all dataset-model combinations in Table 1. Headline numbers include 76.1% on CREMA-D (ResNet, V-A), 85.7% on CREMA-D (Conformer, V-A), 73.4% on AVE (ResNet, V-A), 55.2% on UCF101 (ResNet, V-A), 76.5% on MOSI (V-A-T), 81.1% on MOSEI (V-A-T), and 65.0% on Something-Something V2 (Swin-TF, V-OF). The sole exception is AVE with the Conformer model, where D&R reaches 91.0% versus MCR's 88.8%. MCR consistently outperforms both simple baselines (Ensemble, Joint Training, Uni-Pre Finetuned) and all prior multimodal balancing methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MCR outperforms all nine compared baselines on the vast majority of dataset-model pairs. Against the strongest prior methods, MCR leads by 4.2 points over Uni-Pre Finetuned on CREMA-D ResNet (76.1 vs. 73.3), by 3.0 points over AGM on MOSI V-A-T (76.5 vs. 73.9), and by 3.4 points over Ensemble on UCF (55.2 vs. 52.8). The gradient-modulation baselines (OGM, AGM, MLB) and the Pareto-based MMPareto all trail MCR by 1–5 points across datasets, while the learning-rate-based MSLR and the cosine-based MMCosine underperform even simple Joint Training on several datasets. The single exception is AVE with the Conformer backbone, where D&R achieves 91.0% versus MCR's 88.8%, a 2.2-point gap. On Something-Something V2, MCR's 65.0% surpasses the next-best Ensemble (64.6%) and Uni-Pre Frozen (64.0%), with a much larger margin over prior methods like ReconBoost (56.1%) and AGM (56.6%). Overall, MCR is the only method that consistently beats both simple baselines and all prior multimodal balancing strategies across all six datasets and both model scales (ResNet/Transformer and large pretrained Conformer/Swin-TF).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Conditional Entropy Bottleneck (Fischer, 2020) and Supervised Contrastive Learning (Khosla et al., 2020) as two of its three loss components, but departs fundamentally from prior multimodal balancing methods (MSLR, OGM, AGM, MLB, MMPareto, D&R) that measure modality contributions via unimodal performance or input-space perturbations. Instead, MCR frames modality interaction as a game-theoretic competition over conditional mutual information, using latent-space permutations as a computationally efficient CMI estimator and a JSD-based surrogate (MIPD) to bound each modality's unique task-relevant contribution. The approach is conceptually distinct from gradient-modulation or learning-rate-adjustment strategies, positioning itself as an information-theoretic regularizer rather than an optimizer-level correction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that most prior multimodal balancing methods fail to beat simple baselines like ensembles or unimodal models, and MCR is the only method that consistently surpasses all of them across six datasets and multiple architectures. However, the error analysis reveals a clear limitation: MCR excels at routing information to the correct modality but does not promote synergistic integration when all unimodal models fail, leaving an open question about how to explicitly encourage cross-modal synergy. The finding that the CEB component only benefits SSL-pretrained models (not from-scratch training) further narrows the method's applicability, and the moderate 1–3% gains, while consistent, do not constitute a paradigm shift in multimodal fusion.
