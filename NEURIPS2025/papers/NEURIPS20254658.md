## Problem

In multimodal intention recognition, models tend to over-rely on dominant modalities while underutilizing weaker but potentially critical signals, limiting robustness and generalization. Existing imbalance-mitigation strategies operate at either the sample level (e.g., modality dropout) or the model level (e.g., gradient modulation) but fail to account for the multi-level nature of modality imbalance, which manifests simultaneously in per-sample feature usage and in the structural encoding of modalities by the network.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-extracted sequential features from three modalities—text (768-dim), visual (256-dim on MIntRec, 20-dim on MOSI), and acoustic (768-dim on MIntRec, 5-dim on MOSI)—which are first temporally aligned into a shared sequence length before fusion. ARL operates as a lightweight plug-and-play calibration module layered on top of existing multimodal architectures (MAG-BERT, MulT, TCL-MAP) without modifying their fusion mechanism, and produces intent class labels (20-class or 2-class on MIntRec) or 7-class sentiment labels on MOSI via a standard softmax head.

## Method

ARL introduces a dual-path calibration mechanism. The first path, Contribution-Inverse Sample Calibration (CISC), uses a Leave-One-Out (LOO) valuation that computes per-sample modality contributions by measuring cosine similarity between the full fused representation and representations with each modality zeroed out (O(M) forward passes vs. O(2^M) for Shapley values); modalities whose contribution exceeds a threshold τ are hard-masked to force reliance on weaker signals. The second path, Weighted Encoder Calibration (WEC), is invoked every T epochs and adjusts modality-specific encoder parameters by blending current weights toward their initial values, with the blend ratio governed by a tanh function of the train–validation purity-score gap (stability) and the average LOO contribution (importance). The two paths operate at complementary timescales—CISC per-batch at the input level, WEC periodically at the architectural level—creating an iterative loop that simultaneously breaks per-sample dominance and prevents long-term encoder bias.

## Results

On MIntRec (twenty-class), ARL improves the strongest baseline TCL-MAP from 73.62% to 75.28% accuracy and from 73.31% to 75.16% F1, while on the binary task it lifts accuracy from 89.66% to 90.56%. On MOSI (7-class sentiment), ARL boosts TCL-MAP accuracy from 42.13% to 43.73% and MulT from 42.42% to 43.44%. In the extended comparison against dedicated imbalanced-learning methods (Table 9a), ARL (74.38% ACC) outperforms OGM (73.31%), OPM (73.24%), InfoReg (73.55%), and Curriculum Dropout (67.15%) on MIntRec twenty-class.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ARL is evaluated as a plug-in module on three base architectures (MAG-BERT, MulT, TCL-MAP) and consistently improves all of them across both MIntRec and MOSI. Against dedicated imbalanced-learning baselines on MIntRec twenty-class, ARL (74.38% ACC) beats OGM (73.31%), OPM (73.24%), InfoReg (73.55%), and Curriculum Dropout (67.15%), with the largest margin over Curriculum Dropout (+7.23%). On MOSI, ARL improves every base model: TCL-MAP gains +1.60 ACC and +2.06 F1, MulT gains +1.02 ACC and +1.26 F1, and MAG-BERT gains +0.87 ACC. In the plug-and-play validation (Table 9b), ARL applied to DMD lifts ACC7 from 45.6 to 46.3 and F1 from 86.0 to 86.7, surpassing CAGC (44.8 ACC7, 85.6 F1) without architectural modification. Under missing-modality stress tests on MOSI, ARL yields the largest gains when audio is removed ({a,t} setting: +1.57 ACC7 for MAG-BERT), confirming its robustness advantage over the unmodified baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself within the modality imbalance literature for multimodal learning, which includes gradient modulation (OGM/OPM), modality dropout, and Shapley-value-based contribution estimation. It departs from prior work by addressing imbalance simultaneously at two levels—sample-level feature masking (CISC) and architectural-level encoder weight adjustment (WEC)—whereas existing methods typically target only one. The Leave-One-Out valuation serves as a linear-complexity substitute for the exponential Shapley approach, and the overall framework is designed as a plug-and-play module atop existing multimodal architectures (MAG-BERT, MulT, TCL-MAP) rather than a new end-to-end model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dual-path calibration offers a practical, drop-in solution for modality imbalance in MIR and related tasks, with the LOO mechanism making per-sample modality valuation computationally feasible where Shapley methods are not. However, the gains are modest (1–2% over strong baselines), the approach inherits limitations of the base model's fusion design, and the added forward passes for LOO and the periodic WEC updates increase training cost. Open questions include whether the dual-path strategy generalizes beyond three modalities, whether the hard-masking threshold τ can be learned rather than tuned, and whether the purity-based stability metric in WEC remains informative for larger or more heterogeneous datasets.
