## Problem

Remote physiological measurement (RPM) for in-vehicle driver monitoring is critically constrained by the absence of comprehensive public datasets that simultaneously cover multiple contactless sensing modalities, diverse biometric ground truths, and realistic driving conditions. Existing resources are typically single-modality (mostly RGB), collected in controlled indoor settings, and provide limited physiological labels, making it impossible to benchmark or develop robust multimodal in-vehicle RPM systems.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251230/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The dataset provides three contactless sensing streams per driver: RGB face video (1024×576, cropped to 128×128 at 30 fps), NIR face video (1920×1080, cropped to 128×128 at 30 fps), and mmWave radar tensors (N_f=200 frames × 2 complex × 8 range × 16 angle × 8 Doppler bins, 20 fps, 10-second segments after static-reflection removal and subject localization). Ground truth includes six synchronized contact signals (ECG, BVP, RESP at 1000 Hz; HR, RR derived via NeuroKit2; SpO2 at 1 Hz), all resampled to 30 Hz for vision modalities and 20 Hz for mmWave. A derived STMap representation (T × 16 × 3) is also provided as a lightweight alternative input for certain baselines.

## Method

PhysDrive is a dataset and benchmark contribution rather than a single algorithm. The data collection protocol uses a mixed between-subject/within-subject design: 48 drivers are split into 12 groups (3 vehicle types × 4 natural lighting conditions), and each driver completes 6 driving segments (~30 min, 16.5 km) varying in action state (stationary vs. talking) and road condition (flat-unobstructed, flat-congested, bumpy-congested). All sensors are hosted on a single laptop with ordered start/stop procedures ensuring <1 s temporal drift. The benchmark evaluates 20+ baselines spanning traditional signal processing (CHROM, POS, GREEN, ICA), unsupervised/self-supervised DL (SiNC, Contrast-Phys+), supervised single-task DL (PhysNet, DeepPhys, PhysFormer, RhythmFormer, FactorizePhys, etc.), multi-task learning (MTTS-CAN, BigSmall, PhysMLE), mmWave-specific models (VitaNet, mmFormer, IQ-MVED), and RGB-mmWave fusion (Fusion-Vital), under intra-dataset cross-subject, cross-dataset (PURE, UBFC-rPPG, SCAMP, iBVP, MMPD), and cross-scenario protocols.

## Results

On the intra-dataset cross-subject evaluation (48 drivers, 80/10/10 split), mmWave methods substantially outperform vision-based approaches for HR estimation: mmFormer achieves MAE 3.65 bpm and Pearson r 0.97, while the best RGB method (PhysNet) reaches MAE 6.29 bpm and P 0.61, and the best NIR method (PhysNet) only MAE 10.69 bpm and P 0.12. For multi-task HR+RR estimation, mmFormer leads with RR MAE 1.49 and P 0.83, whereas the best RGB MTL method (PhysMLE) achieves RR MAE 2.21 and P 0.16. Cross-dataset evaluation reveals a significant domain gap: models trained on indoor datasets (PURE, UBFC-rPPG) drop to P < 0.25 on PhysDrive, and models trained on full PhysDrive generalize poorly to cleaner lab data (e.g., RhythmFormer P drops from 0.73 on UBFC when trained on a PhysDrive subset to 0.12 when trained on full PhysDrive).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 22 methods across three sensing modalities on PhysDrive. For HR estimation, mmWave methods (mmFormer: MAE 3.65, P 0.97; VitaNet: MAE 4.94, P 0.94) decisively outperform all vision-based approaches, with mmFormer exceeding the best RGB method (PhysNet, P 0.61) by ~54% in correlation. Among RGB methods, supervised DL models (PhysNet, RhythmNet, RhythmFormer) significantly beat traditional signal-processing baselines (CHROM, POS, GREEN, ICA) by 5–7 bpm in MAE, while unsupervised methods (SiNC, Contrast-Phys+) perform comparably to traditional methods (P < 0.10). For multi-task estimation, STMap-based MTL methods (PhysMLE, BaseNet) outperform direct-video-input MTL methods (MTTS-CAN, BigSmall) by ~25% in RR Pearson correlation. Cross-dataset results show that RhythmNet trained on PURE (MAE 9.30, P 0.14) and FactorizePhys trained on PURE (MAE 13.36, P 0.13) both struggle on PhysDrive, confirming the domain gap between controlled indoor and real driving conditions. Notably, training on a simplified PhysDrive subset (noon + stationary) dramatically improves cross-dataset generalization (RhythmFormer P jumps from 0.08 to 0.73 on UBFC), suggesting curriculum-style training as a promising direction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not propose a new sensing or learning method but instead carves new ground in the dataset and benchmark landscape for remote physiological measurement (RPM). Prior RPM datasets (PURE, UBFC-rPPG, COHFACE, MMPD) are predominantly indoor, single-modality (RGB), and limited in physiological labels; the only driving-specific datasets (MR-NIRP, Wu et al.) are small-scale with a single label, and the only public mmWave datasets (EquiPleth, 4TU.ResearchD) are indoor. PhysDrive repurposes the full existing zoo of rPPG and radar baselines (PhysNet, DeepPhys, RhythmFormer, VitaNet, mmFormer, STMap-based MTL methods, etc.) as a comprehensive benchmark, positioning itself as the first public resource that simultaneously provides RGB, NIR, and mmWave data with six synchronized ground truths under real-world driving conditions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PhysDrive fills a critical gap by providing the first large-scale, multimodal, in-vehicle RPM benchmark, enabling systematic comparison across sensing modalities and driving factors (lighting, motion, road surface, vehicle type) that were previously untestable in a unified protocol. A notable open question it surfaces is whether ECG-derived HR is actually a suitable ground truth for training and evaluating video-based rPPG methods, given the observed consistency gap between ECG and BVP-derived HR under dynamic lighting. Key limitations include the East-Asian-only participant pool, limited SpO2 variability, and a 1-second cross-modal synchronization tolerance, all of which constrain the dataset's immediate applicability to diverse populations and transient cardiac event analysis. The paper also highlights the need for temporal-robustness training for mmWave models and curriculum learning strategies to bridge the domain gap between noisy in-vehicle data and cleaner laboratory settings.
