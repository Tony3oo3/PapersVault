## Problem

Fully finetuning large pretrained models is prohibitively expensive in memory and compute, primarily because Adam-family optimizers maintain per-parameter momentum states. Prior sparse-update methods (e.g., MicroAdam, GaLore) rely on gradient information to select which parameters to update, incurring additional memory for error feedback or SVD computation. The paper identifies an unexploited empirical pattern—large gradients consistently correspond to small-magnitude weights during finetuning—and asks whether weight magnitude alone can serve as a gradient-free, more effective selection criterion that also mitigates catastrophic forgetting.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pretrained model's weight matrices (any architecture: Transformer, CNN) and a downstream finetuning dataset, producing updated parameters where only a per-layer subset of bottom-k smallest-magnitude weights is modified. The sparse mask is computed by flattening each weight matrix, chunking it, and applying bottom-k selection within each chunk—requiring no gradient computation. The density k can be dynamically scheduled over training, and the mask is refreshed every m steps rather than every step, preserving Adam's momentum dynamics for the selected subset while reducing memory to O(kS) instead of O(S).

## Method

NANOAdam is an Adam-like optimizer that updates only the bottom-k parameters by absolute weight magnitude in each layer, determined without any gradient information. A mask interval m controls how often the selection is recomputed (small weights remain small, so frequent updates are unnecessary), and a density scheduler linearly decays k over training to progressively reduce the number of updated parameters. Crucially, no error-feedback mechanism is used—the method deliberately pursues a distinct optimization path rather than approximating full-gradient updates. The theoretical justification comes from a two-layer teacher-student analysis (Theorem 2.2) showing that updating the smallest neuron in the feature-learning regime converges to the target representation while preserving all large pretrained neurons, thereby preventing catastrophic forgetting. Empirically, the hyperbolic gradient–weight correlation is stronger in overparameterized models and in finetuning (vs. training from scratch), explaining why the strategy is most effective for adapting large pretrained networks to related downstream tasks.

## Results

On the GLUE benchmark, NANOADAM achieves the highest average score across all three model scales: 85.65 (BERT-Base), 87.48 (BERT-Large), and 87.92 (OPT-1.3B), outperforming the next-best baseline (AdamW-8bit) by 0.42, 0.71, and 0.72 points respectively. NANOADAM also uses the least memory (3.58 GB for BERT-Base, 11.60 GB for OPT-1.3B) compared to AdamW (3.94 GB, 18.16 GB). On the catastrophic forgetting evaluation (CIFAR-10 → Flowers102), NANOADAM reduces forgetting from 68.14% (AdamW) to 12.55% on ResNet101 and from 68.05% to 28.36% on ResNet18, while simultaneously achieving the best average accuracy (87.20% and 75.79% respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

NANOADAM outperforms all four baselines on average GLUE accuracy across all three model sizes, with the largest margin over GaLore (up to +2.04 on BERT-Large) and a consistent 0.4–0.7 point lead over AdamW-8bit. On memory, NANOADAM uses the least optimizer-state memory at every scale (e.g., 11.60 GB vs. 18.16 GB for AdamW on OPT-1.3B), while maintaining training time comparable to AdamW and AdamW-8bit—unlike MicroAdam and GaLore, which incur significantly higher runtime on larger models. On the catastrophic forgetting benchmark (CIFAR-10 → Flowers102), NANOADAM dramatically reduces forgetting: on ResNet101 it drops from 68.14% (AdamW) and 82.98% (MicroAdam) to just 12.55%, while also achieving the highest Flowers102 accuracy (81.52% vs. 52.97% and 14.89%). On ViT-Large, forgetting is already low for all methods, but NANOADAM still achieves the best average accuracy (98.95%) with the smallest parameter shift (ℓ₂ = 0.68) despite using the largest learning rate (1e-3 to 2e-3 vs. 1e-4 for AdamW). The key differentiator is that NANOADAM selects parameters by weight magnitude rather than gradient magnitude, enabling larger learning rates without divergence and preserving pretrained features that gradient-based methods (MicroAdam, GaLore) tend to overwrite.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of memory-efficient sparse optimizers for finetuning (MicroAdam, GaLore, AdamW-8bit, LDAdam) by replacing gradient-based parameter selection with a gradient-free criterion: updating only the smallest-magnitude weights. It builds on the static small-weight mask of Liao et al. [19] and the observation in BlockLLM [27] that small weights are updated more frequently, but departs by making the mask dynamic, per-layer, and theoretically grounded via a two-layer teacher-student analysis showing that small-weight updates preserve pretrained representations while learning new task-specific features. The paper explicitly reconciles two previously separate selection principles—large gradients and small weights—showing they are correlated during finetuning but select distinct parameter subsets, and argues the small-weight criterion is the more robust and efficient choice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NANOADAM offers a practically attractive recipe: no gradient computation needed for mask selection, no error feedback, and only optimizer states stored for a sparse subset, yielding meaningful memory savings that scale with model size. The catastrophic forgetting results on ResNet101 (83.77% vs. 29.59% CIFAR-10 accuracy after Task 2) are striking and suggest a simple selection criterion can substantially mitigate forgetting without auxiliary regularization. However, the method's effectiveness depends on knowledge transferability between pretraining and finetuning tasks and on model overparameterization; when tasks are poorly aligned or models are small, full-update optimizers may still be preferable. Open questions include how the approach behaves at the scale of 7B+ LLMs, whether the density schedule can be made task-adaptive, and whether the two-layer theoretical guarantees extend to deep, multi-layer architectures.
