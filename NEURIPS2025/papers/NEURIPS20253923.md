## Problem

Temporal interactions in continuous-time temporal graphs exhibit natural heterogeneity: interaction intervals follow a power-law distribution with high right-skewness, and a few node pairs account for most events. This heterogeneity causes two underexplored failures in existing temporal graph neural networks: sinusoidal time encoding functions become harder to learn under skewed temporal differences, and node embeddings computed from only recent interactions forget the historical interaction patterns of infrequently interacting node pairs, degrading link prediction accuracy for those pairs.

## Input / Output

Given a continuous-time temporal graph (a chronologically ordered sequence of timestamped interaction events), a target node pair (u, v), and a target time τ, TAMI outputs a link prediction probability p_uv. A key structural detail is that LHA maintains a per-node-pair buffer of the most recent k edge embeddings (each of dimension d_r), which is updated incrementally whenever a new interaction between u and v occurs; for node pairs with no prior interaction history, the buffer is initialized to zero. The temporal node embeddings h_u and h_v are produced by any existing TGNN operating on the m most recent neighbors, while the link predictor consumes the concatenation [h_u; h_v; h_uv] where h_uv summarizes the pair-specific interaction history.

## Method

TAMI is a plug-and-play framework with two modules that can be integrated into any existing temporal GNN. The Log Time Encoding (LTE) module replaces the standard sinusoidal encoding z(t)=cos(Δt×ω) with z_l(t)=cos(ln(1+Δt)×ω), applying a logarithmic transformation to rescale the highly right-skewed temporal differences into a more balanced distribution; Proposition 1 proves that for Pareto-distributed Δt, this reduces skewness to exactly 2, making the learnable frequency parameters ω easier to optimize. The Link History Aggregation (LHA) module maintains the most recent k edge embeddings for each target node pair, computing the current edge embedding as a convex combination r_uv^τ = γ·MLP([h_u;h_v]) + (1−γ)·r_uv^{t_1} that blends the current node states with the most recent historical edge embedding, thereby preventing forgetting of past interactions. The final link probability is p_uv = MLP([h_u; h_v; h_uv]), where h_uv is obtained by aggregating the k historical edge embeddings (using a most-recent aggregator by default). The framework requires no architectural changes to the underlying TGNN—only the time encoding is swapped and LHA is appended at the prediction stage—enabling seamless integration with GraphMixer, DyGFormer, TGAT, TGN, JODIE, and CAWN.

## Results

On 13 classic temporal graph datasets under random negative sampling (the default setting), TAMI consistently improves both integrated TGNNs: GraphMixer w/TAMI achieves up to 16.64% AP improvement over vanilla GraphMixer (e.g., 75.56→88.13 on LastFM), while DyGFormer w/TAMI achieves up to 2.66% improvement (e.g., 92.46→98.94 on Enron). On the TGB benchmark, DyGFormer w/TAMI achieves an 87.05% MRR improvement on tgbl-review (0.224→0.419), making it the second-best model on that dataset and the top model on tgbl-wiki and tgbl-coin. TAMI also reduces total training time by up to 76.7% and GPU memory usage by up to 53.22%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TAMI (GraphMixer) outperforms all 7 standalone baselines (JODIE, DyRep, TGAT, TGN, CAWN, EdgeBank, TCL) on 11 out of 13 datasets under random negative sampling, with the largest margin over EdgeBank (e.g., 90.97 vs. 83.53 on Enron) and TCL (e.g., 88.13 vs. 67.27 on LastFM). TAMI (DyGFormer) dominates all baselines on every dataset, achieving the highest AP on all 13 (e.g., 98.94 on Enron vs. 86.53 for TGN, the next-best baseline). Compared to their vanilla counterparts, GraphMixer w/TAMI wins on all 13 datasets (up to +16.64% on LastFM), and DyGFormer w/TAMI also wins on all 13 (up to +2.66% on Enron). On the TGB benchmark, DyGFormer w/TAMI surpasses the vanilla DyGFormer by 87.05% MRR on tgbl-review (0.224→0.419), 2.13% on tgbl-wiki, and 5.59% on tgbl-coin, reaching rank 1 on two of three TGB datasets. The improvements are most pronounced on datasets with high interaction-interval skewness (e.g., Social Evo with skewness 18.63, Contact with 10.96), confirming that TAMI's heterogeneity-taming mechanism is most beneficial where temporal heterogeneity is strongest.

## Relation to Prior Work

TAMI builds directly on the periodic (sinusoidal) time encoding functions introduced by Xu et al. (NeurIPS 2019) and the two strongest contemporary temporal graph neural networks, GraphMixer (ICLR 2023) and DyGFormer (NeurIPS 2023), which it uses as its underlying architectures. The key conceptual departure is identifying heterogeneity—specifically the power-law, right-skewed distribution of interaction intervals—as a systematic source of degradation that prior TGNNs have overlooked. Rather than proposing a new architecture, TAMI introduces two lightweight, architecture-agnostic modules (logarithmic time encoding and per-pair link history aggregation) that can be plugged into any existing TGNN, positioning itself as a general-purpose enhancement layer rather than a competing model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic diagnosis of why temporal link prediction degrades for infrequently interacting node pairs, attributing it to skewness in temporal differences and the forgetting of pair-specific history in neighbor-based aggregation. The practical impact is notable: a simple log transform plus a small history buffer yields consistent gains across 16 datasets and multiple architectures, with up to 87% MRR improvement on TGB-review and 76.7% training-time reduction. Limitations include the theoretical guarantee (Proposition 1) assuming a Pareto distribution that real data only approximately follows, the modest gains for already-strong models like DyGFormer on the 13 classic datasets (often <2%), and the additional memory footprint of LHA. Open questions include whether other transformations (beyond log) might be better calibrated to specific skewness profiles, and whether LHA's most-recent aggregator is optimal versus attention-based alternatives.
