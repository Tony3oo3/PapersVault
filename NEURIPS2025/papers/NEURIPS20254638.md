## Problem

Transformer-based LLMs consistently fail on global reasoning tasks (graph reachability, majority voting, function composition) that require integrating information across the entire input, yet no principled framework explains *why* these failures occur. The paper hypothesizes that the root cause is a limited effective bandwidth for information flow across residual streams, and introduces a formal computational model to quantify and predict which problems exceed this bandwidth.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254638/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The BAPO model consumes a token sequence x₁…xₙ split at an arbitrary (worst-case) index k into a prefix and suffix. The prefix oracle f sees only the prefix and outputs a bits; the attention function g selects up to b individual prefix tokens based on the suffix; and the suffix oracle h receives the a-bit summary, the b attended tokens (with positions), the suffix tokens, and the split index to produce a single output token. For decision problems this is a yes/no answer; for problems like UNIQUE or SETDIFF it is a specific token from the vocabulary. The model must work for all possible prefix-suffix splits, reflecting causal attention's constraint that prefix streams cannot depend on future tokens.

## Method

The paper introduces the bounded attention prefix oracle (BAPO), a two-parameter computational model (a bits of prefix bandwidth, b tokens of attention bandwidth) that isolates the information-flow bottleneck in causal-attention transformers. Theoretically, they prove that problems like INDEX, EQUALITY, and DISJOINTNESS are BAPO-easy (solvable with constant bandwidth), while REACHABILITY, MAJORITY, and MATCH3ₙ are BAPO-hard (requiring super-constant bandwidth), with explicit lower bounds trading off between the two bandwidth parameters. They further prove that adding chain-of-thought (iteratively applying the BAPO to its own output) makes constant-bandwidth BAPOs Turing-complete, reducing any decidable problem to a (2,3)-BAPO-CoT. Empirically, they validate the framework by showing GPT-4o, Claude, and Gemini succeed on BAPO-easy tasks and fail on even small BAPO-hard instances, while reasoning models (o3, Gemini 2.5 Flash) that use thousands of CoT tokens overcome these failures.

## Results

The BAPO model predicts that LLMs (GPT-4o, Claude, Gemini) should succeed on BAPO-easy problems (INDEX, EQUALITY, DISJOINTNESS, MATCH2) and fail on BAPO-hard problems (REACHABILITY, MAJORITY, MATCH3). Empirically, this prediction holds: on BAPO-hard tasks, no LLM maintains accuracy above random guessing (50%) at input length n=200, while on BAPO-easy tasks, most models perform consistently well across all tested lengths. Adding chain-of-thought (CoT) with a 250-word soft limit modestly improves non-reasoning models on BAPO-hard problems for smaller n, but without a CoT budget limit, o3 and Gemini 2.5 Flash succeed on BAPO-hard problems using over 10k reasoning tokens in some cases. In real-world tasks, LLMs solve BAPO-easy components (finding a negative review) with much greater accuracy than BAPO-hard components (majority sentiment, variable tracking).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against traditional ML baselines; instead, its central comparison is between BAPO-easy and BAPO-hard problems as predictors of LLM performance. Across three model families (GPT-4o, Claude, Gemini), BAPO-easy problems (INDEX, EQUALITY, DISJOINTNESS, MATCH2) show consistently high accuracy across all input lengths, while BAPO-hard problems (REACHABILITY, MAJORITY, MATCH3) show accuracy dropping to near the 50% random-guessing baseline by n=200 for all models tested. The CoT comparison reveals that non-reasoning models with a 250-word CoT budget show only modest improvement on BAPO-hard tasks, whereas reasoning models (o3, Gemini 2.5 Flash) without a CoT limit achieve near-perfect accuracy on BAPO-hard problems, using over 10k reasoning tokens in some cases. In real-world settings, the same pattern holds: LLMs solve the BAPO-easy component of a review-aggregation task (finding one negative review among positives, analogous to INDEX) with high accuracy, but struggle with the BAPO-hard component (determining majority sentiment, analogous to MAJORITY) and with variable-tracking in code (a special case of REACHABILITY). The theoretical contribution also contrasts with prior expressivity results: while circuit-based analyses place MAJORITY in TC⁰ (suggesting it should be trivial for transformers), the BAPO model correctly predicts that LLMs struggle with it due to bandwidth constraints rather than representational limitations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of transformer expressivity work (Hahn, Bhattamishra et al., Sanford et al.) that characterizes what transformers *can* represent, instead introducing the BAPO model to characterize what they *cannot* solve in practice due to bandwidth limits on cross-stream communication. It builds directly on Merrill and Sabharwal's result that CoT makes transformers Turing-complete, strengthening it to show CoT makes constant-bandwidth BAPOs Turing-complete. The model also draws on communication complexity (one-way communication, EQUALITY/DISJOINTNESS/INDEX) and prior evidence of attention capacity limits (Edelman et al.), but reframes these through the lens of causal attention's constraint that prefix processing must be suffix-independent.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The BAPO framework gives practitioners a principled, testable criterion for anticipating LLM failures on global reasoning tasks and choosing mitigations (CoT, tool-calling, retrieval) before deployment. A key open question is what causes the small effective bandwidth in practice—whether it is architectural, a training artifact, or a fundamental tradeoff with generalization—and whether scaling or new architectures can increase it. The lower bounds are acknowledged as loose (e.g., REACHABILITY), and the model does not capture all failure modes (tokenization errors, positional encoding failures), so BAPO-easiness is necessary but not sufficient for LLM success.
