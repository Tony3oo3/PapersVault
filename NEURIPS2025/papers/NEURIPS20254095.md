## Problem

Diffusion models generalize well beyond their training data, but the mechanism by which they preserve low-dimensional manifold structure remains unexplained. While density-domain smoothing (e.g., KDE) smears mass off the manifold, the paper identifies that the score matching formulation inherently smooths in the log-density domain, and asks whether and how this log-domain smoothing is geometry-adaptive—i.e., whether it preserves manifold structure and how the smoothing kernel controls which manifold the model interpolates along.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254095/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a finite dataset {x_i}_{i=1}^N and a smoothing kernel k (e.g., isotropic Gaussian with variance σ²) applied to the empirical score function, along with diffusion parameters (noise schedule α, horizon T, and early-stopping time ε). It produces generated samples by simulating the reverse SDE with the smoothed score s^k(t,x) = k * ∇log p̂_t(x), and the analysis is validated in both VAE latent space (32-dim, MNIST digit-4 manifold) and pixel space (32×32 MNIST, 64×64 synthetic bump images). The early-stopping parameter ε plays a critical role: smaller ε preserves fine geometric detail while larger ε promotes interpolation along the manifold.

## Method

The core insight is that convolving the empirical score with a kernel commutes with the gradient (k * ∇log p̂_t = ∇(k * log p̂_t)), meaning score smoothing is equivalent to smoothing in the log-density domain rather than the density domain. In the log-domain, zero-density regions map to −∞, so smoothing cannot create probability mass off the manifold, unlike KDE which smears density everywhere the kernel overlaps the manifold. The paper proves geometry-adaptivity in two settings: in the linear/affine case (Proposition 3.1), log-domain smoothing exactly equals manifold-adapted smoothing that restricts the kernel to tangent directions; for curved manifolds (Theorem 3.6), the Rényi divergence between the generic-kernel and manifold-adapted smoothed densities is bounded by (K/τ)·max{d*+1, (c_μ²N)^(−1/d*)ε^(−1)}, where K quantifies the kernel's normal-direction noise and τ is the manifold reach. A second theorem (Theorem 4.1) formalizes 'geometric bias': by optimizing the bound over permissible manifolds, one can identify which interpolating manifold the kernel's directional properties favor, explaining how different smoothing scales or anisotropic kernels select different geometries from the same training data.

## Results

The main theoretical contribution (Theorem 3.6) shows that the Rényi divergence between the log-domain smoothed density and its manifold-adapted counterpart is bounded by (K/τ)·max{d*+1, (c_μ²N)^(−1/d*)·ε^(−1)}, where K measures normal-direction kernel variance, τ is the manifold reach, and ε is the early-stopping time. In experiments on MNIST (32-dim VAE latent space and 32×32 pixel space) and a synthetic 64×64 bump-image manifold, score-smoothed diffusion models maintain samples on the data manifold as smoothing increases, while KDE samples rapidly deviate off-manifold. In pixel-space MNIST, score-function smoothing yields a consistent FID improvement over KDE, with the manifold-adapted kernel mitigating the blurring (barycentre effect) that causes steep FID increases under isotropic Gaussian score smoothing at larger bandwidths.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is kernel density estimation (KDE), which performs smoothing in the density-domain rather than the log-domain. Across all experimental settings (MNIST latent space, synthetic bump images, MNIST pixel space), score-smoothed diffusion models consistently outperform KDE in preserving manifold structure: as smoothing bandwidth increases, KDE samples move substantially off-manifold (distance-to-manifold grows identically with distance-to-data), while diffusion samples remain on or near the manifold and even become closer to unseen training points. In the pixel-space MNIST experiment, isotropic Gaussian score smoothing produces barycentres of training datapoints (visible as increasing blurring and a steep FID rise at larger smoothing), whereas the manifold-adapted score-smoothing kernel mitigates this blurring and avoids the FID degradation. The manifold-adapted kernel also produces samples with lower anisotropy than isotropic Gaussian smoothing on the synthetic bump manifold, confirming that kernel geometry directly controls the interpolating manifold. Overall, log-domain (score) smoothing wins on all manifold-preservation and visual-quality metrics, while KDE loses as bandwidth grows.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the score matching framework (Hyvärinen, 2005) and SDE-based diffusion models (Song et al., 2021) to provide a theoretical mechanism for the manifold hypothesis conjecture in generative modeling. It extends a small but growing line of work on score smoothing (Scarvelis et al., 2025; Chen, 2025; Gabriel et al., 2025) by proving that log-domain smoothing—arising naturally from score matching combined with kernel smoothing—is approximately equivalent to manifold-adapted smoothing, using Rényi divergence bounds under manifold reach assumptions. The paper departs from prior manifold-hypothesis analyses (Pidstrigach, 2022; De Bortoli, 2022; Azangulov et al., 2024) by shifting focus from convergence of the diffusion process to the geometric properties of the implicit regularisation induced by the learning objective itself, and introduces the notion of 'geometric bias' to explain how the smoothing kernel selects among multiple plausible interpolating manifolds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first rigorous theoretical evidence that the score matching formulation of diffusion models inherently produces geometry-adaptive smoothing, offering a concrete mechanism for the long-standing conjecture that diffusion models succeed by adapting to low-dimensional data structure. The geometric bias framework opens a new axis of analysis: rather than asking whether a model recovers 'the' manifold, it asks how inductive biases (kernel shape, scale, anisotropy) steer the model toward particular interpolating geometries. Key open questions include deriving formal generalisation error bounds, extending the analysis to location-dependent kernels and high-curvature manifolds where the K/τ assumption breaks down, and understanding how neural architecture biases (convolutions, attention) interact with the log-domain smoothing mechanism in practice.
