## Problem

Reinforcement learning agents achieve superhuman task performance but exhibit unnatural behaviors—unnatural shaking, spinning, and non-human smoothness—that make them easily distinguishable from humans, raising interpretability and trustworthiness concerns. While human-likeness has been explored in NLP, it remains largely unaddressed in deep RL, where the core challenge is designing agents that not only complete tasks but also behave in a recognizably human manner.

## Input / Output

The method takes a robot environment state (e.g., Shadow Hand joint positions and object state in Adroit tasks) as input and produces a macro action—a sequence of H consecutive primitive actions (H=9 in the main experiments)—decoded from a single codebook index. During the VQVAE training phase, the input is a (state, macro action) pair extracted via a sliding window over 25 human demonstration trajectories per task; during RL, the policy outputs K discrete logits (K=8 or 16) over the learned codebook, and the VQVAE decoder reconstructs the full H-step action sequence from the selected latent embedding and current state.

## Method

MAQ formulates human-likeness as a constrained trajectory optimization problem using receding-horizon control: at each decision step, the agent selects an H-step action segment from a finite set of human-like macro actions rather than optimizing over the full continuous action space. A Conditional-VQVAE is trained on offline human demonstrations to distill these macro actions into a discrete codebook of K entries, where each entry encodes a human-like action sequence conditioned on the current state. The RL policy (IQL, SAC, or RLPD) is then trained to select codebook indices, effectively transforming the MDP into an SMDP whose action space is restricted to human-learned behaviors. This design ensures the agent can only produce action sequences that resemble human demonstrations while still maximizing cumulative reward, and the framework integrates seamlessly into existing RL algorithms by simply replacing the primitive action head with a discrete codebook-selection head followed by the VQVAE decoder.

## Results

On the D4RL Adroit benchmark (Door, Hammer, Pen, Relocate), MAQ substantially improves trajectory similarity across all four tasks. The average DTW_s (state similarity) increases from −0.49 (SAC) to 0.56 (MAQ+SAC), from 0.21 (IQL) to 0.63 (MAQ+IQL), and from 0.10 (RLPD) to 0.56 (MAQ+RLPD), while maintaining or improving task success rates. In the Turing Test, MAQ+RLPD achieves a 39% win rate (evaluators fooled into thinking the agent is human), compared to 24% for RLPD, 19% for SAC, and 13% for IQL. In the human-likeness ranking, MAQ+RLPD reaches a 71% win rate, nearly matching the human demonstration's 74%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MAQ consistently outperforms all four baselines on trajectory similarity metrics. Compared to SAC (which uses no human demonstrations), the improvement is most dramatic: average DTW_s jumps from −0.49 to 0.56 (MAQ+SAC), and Door DTW_s from −0.39 to 0.80. Against IQL, MAQ+IQL raises Door DTW_s from 0.43 to 0.84 and average DTW_a from 0.52 to 0.82. Against RLPD, MAQ+RLPD improves Door DTW_s from −0.06 to 0.76 and average DTW_a from 0.34 to 0.78. Against Behavioral Cloning, MAQ agents achieve substantially higher success rates (e.g., 0.93 vs. 0.02 on Door) while also matching or exceeding BC's similarity scores. The one notable trade-off is on the Hammer task, where vanilla RLPD achieves 100% success (by completing the task in fewer steps) while MAQ+RLPD reaches 56%—the paper attributes this to MAQ's preference for slower, more natural movements within the 200-step episode limit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on two main threads: (1) prior human-like RL work that either imposes handcrafted behavioral penalties (Fujii et al., Ho et al.) or relies on imitation learning (behavioral cloning, inverse RL), and (2) the use of conditional VQVAE for discretizing action spaces in robotic control (Luo et al., 2023). MAQ departs from both by formulating human-likeness as a constrained trajectory optimization problem over a learned discrete codebook of macro actions, combining receding-horizon control with SMDP-style action quantization. Unlike imitation learning, the RL agent still optimizes for reward but is restricted to a human-like behavioral manifold; unlike penalty-based methods, no handcrafted behavior costs are needed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MAQ provides a modular, plug-and-play framework for human-like RL that integrates with off-the-shelf algorithms (IQL, SAC, RLPD) without architectural changes, and the Turing Test evaluation (MAQ+RLPD at 71% vs. human at 74% in pairwise ranking) offers a compelling validation that quantized macro actions can make agents nearly indistinguishable from humans. Key limitations include reliance on human demonstrations (quality and availability), evaluation restricted to four Adroit manipulation tasks, and the tradeoff between task speed and human-likeness (e.g., MAQ+RLPD at 56% success vs. RLPD at 100% on Hammer at h=200). Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to high-dimensional or continuous-action domains beyond Shadow Hand, how it scales with demonstration quality and quantity, and whether the learned codebook captures intent-level human behavior or merely kinematic patterns.
