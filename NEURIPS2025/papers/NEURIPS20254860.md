## Problem

Existing time series forecasting benchmarks (ProbTS, TFB, BasicTS, etc.) evaluate models on real-world data where multiple temporal components (trend, seasonality, dependencies) are intertwined, making it impossible to attribute performance to specific modeling capabilities. Furthermore, these frameworks provide no theoretical performance boundaries, so it is unclear whether a model's accuracy reflects genuine pattern learning or coincidental correlation exploitation. This paper addresses both gaps by proposing a synthetic data-driven evaluation paradigm that isolates confounding factors and establishes mathematical optima for each pattern type.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254860/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a forecasting model (12 fine-tuning architectures spanning Transformer, MLP, CNN, RNN, and KAN families, plus 3 zero-shot foundation models) and applies it to programmatically generated synthetic time series with input length 96 and forecast horizons of {10, 24, 48, 96, 192}. Outputs are structured as MSE/MAE values per pattern type, explicitly compared against analytically derived theoretical optima (e.g., zero error for deterministic trends, variance of noise for white noise), yielding capability maps, noise-tolerance thresholds, anomaly-degradation ratios, and cross-variable dependency detection scores rather than a single aggregate accuracy number.

## Method

SynTSBench generates controlled synthetic datasets across six categories: 11 parametric trend functions, 10 periodic patterns derived from Fourier superposition, ARMA processes with short/long-range dependencies, multivariate systems with lagged/conditional/nonlinear relationships, noise-corrupted signals at SNR levels from −10 dB to clean, and anomaly-injected series (point, pulse, mean-shift, trend-shift at varying densities). The key mechanism is that each synthetic pattern has a known closed-form optimal prediction, enabling direct quantification of the gap between model output and mathematical optimum. Robustness is assessed via two complementary metrics—MSE_Obs (prediction vs. noisy observation) and MSE_True (prediction vs. clean signal)—which reveal whether a model is denoising or merely fitting noise. Cross-variable capability is tested by generating pairs where one variable is a lagged or transformed version of another, so that channel-dependent architectures should show asymmetric performance between the two variables. The entire pipeline is driven by a single sliding-window protocol with 7:1:2 train/validation/test splits, ensuring all 15 evaluated models are compared under identical conditions.

## Results

Across 11 trend patterns, MLP-based architectures dominate: PaiFilter achieves the best MSE on 5/11 patterns (e.g., 1.52e-07 on Exponential-Trend vs. 8.45 for Autoformer), while DLinear leads on 7/10 periodic signals (e.g., 3.85e-11 MSE on Sin(f=0.005)). Under extreme noise (SNR=−10 dB), TimesNet achieves the lowest MSE_True (0.0432) among all models, with PaiFilter (0.0300) and TexFilter (0.0318) close behind, whereas N-HiTS degrades most severely (1.2221). For cross-variable lag relationships (48-step lag), channel-dependent models (TimesNet, TSMixer, TimeMixer) reduce MSE by ~50% on the lagged variable versus the source, while channel-independent models (PaiFilter, PatchTST) show no such advantage. No single model approaches the theoretical optimum (MSE=0) across all pattern types simultaneously.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PaiFilter and TexFilter (FilterNet family [22]) emerge as the strongest trend forecasters, with PaiFilter winning 5/11 trend patterns and both achieving near-optimal MSE (1e-06 to 1e-12 range) on most functional trends, substantially outperforming transformer-based models like Autoformer (MSE 8.45 on Exponential vs. 1.52e-07) and PatchTST (5.42e-02). DLinear [5] is the clear leader on periodic signals, achieving best MSE on 7/10 patterns (e.g., 3.85e-11 on single sine, 9.22e-11 on Double-Sin), far ahead of TimesNet (3.98e-04) and iTransformer (1.14e-03) on the same signals. Under noise, TimesNet [11] and the FilterNet models are most robust (MSE_True ≈ 0.03 at SNR=−10 dB), while N-HiTS [24] and Autoformer [4] degrade most severely (MSE_True 1.22 and 0.065 respectively). For cross-variable dependencies, TSMixer [8] and TimesNet [11] consistently achieve the lowest MSE across all five multivariate scenarios (e.g., 0.7527 MSE on 48-step lag vs. 1.09 for Autoformer), while channel-independent models (PaiFilter, PatchTST [6]) show no benefit from the lagged variable. ARIMA [1] retains a slight edge over all deep learning models on ARMA processes (MSE 6.09 vs. 6.47 for DLinear on ARMA(1,1)), and zero-shot foundation models (Chronos [25], TimeMoE [26], Moirai [27]) underperform fine-tuned models across all evaluated dimensions.

## Relation to Prior Work

SynTSBench positions itself as a methodological departure from existing time series forecasting benchmarks (ProbTS, TFB, BasicTS, TSlib, M3/M4, Monash) by introducing three capabilities none of them jointly provide: programmable synthetic feature isolation, controlled robustness testing, and theoretical performance boundary establishment. Rather than building on a specific prior architecture, it repurposes classical signal processing (Fourier series, Wold decomposition) and stochastic process theory (ARMA, random walks) as the foundation for generating evaluation data with known optimal solutions. The framework treats the 15+ evaluated models (spanning transformers, MLPs, CNNs, RNNs, and KAN-based architectures) as subjects of study rather than as building blocks, making it a diagnostic tool rather than a methodological contribution to forecasting itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The quantified gaps between model predictions and theoretical optima provide, for the first time, a principled basis for model selection in industrial forecasting scenarios—practitioners can now match architectures to the dominant temporal characteristics of their target data rather than relying on aggregate leaderboard scores. The robustness findings (N-HiTS's catastrophic anomaly sensitivity, TSMixer's 342% error inflation, transformer architectures' noise degradation) reveal architectural vulnerabilities that aggregate benchmark scores mask. Open questions include whether synthetic pattern findings transfer faithfully to real-world data with uncontrolled confounders, whether the evaluated pattern space adequately captures the full complexity of industrial time series, and whether the absence of error bars (acknowledged in the paper's own checklist) limits the statistical confidence in the capability rankings.
