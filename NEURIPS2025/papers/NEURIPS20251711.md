## Problem

Characterizing how interacting subsystems (e.g., brain areas) control one another requires nonlinear control-theoretic analysis, but existing methods are predominantly linear and cannot capture context-dependent modulation of control. The central technical obstacle is estimating the Jacobian of the dynamics from time-series data alone, since good function approximation does not guarantee accurate derivative estimation, and the time-derivative function is typically unobserved.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251711/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes only observed state trajectories x(t₀+kΔt) sampled at a fixed interval, with no access to the underlying vector field f. It produces a neural network Ĵ^θ(x) that maps any state to its Jacobian matrix, from which trajectory predictions, time-varying reachability Gramians (quantifying pairwise control ease between subsystems), and ILQR control inputs are derived. Tested systems range from 2-dimensional (Van der Pol) to 64-dimensional (Lorenz 96) and a 128-dimensional task-trained RNN, with robustness to 1–10% Gaussian observation noise.

## Method

JacobianODE directly parameterizes the Jacobian J(x) with a four-layer MLP rather than learning the dynamics f(x) and differentiating. The key mechanism is a Jacobian-parameterized ODE formulation (Proposition 1): f(x(t)) is expressed as a double integral of J along paths between observed states, so all gradients backpropagate through the Jacobian network without ever representing f explicitly. Training combines three components: (1) a trajectory reconstruction loss computed via path integration of Ĵ followed by RK4 ODE stepping, (2) a self-supervised loop-closure loss that enforces each row of Ĵ is a conservative vector field (∮J ds = 0 over closed loops formed from random data-point pairs), and (3) generalized teacher forcing to prevent divergence in chaotic regimes. For control analysis, the learned Jacobian is decomposed into within-subsystem (diagonal) and inter-subsystem (off-diagonal) blocks, and time-varying reachability Gramians are computed via an ODE to quantify how readily one subsystem drives another toward novel states.

## Results

JacobianODE achieves the lowest mean Frobenius norm error on Jacobian estimation across all six benchmark systems and all three noise levels (1%, 5%, 10%). On the 64-dimensional Lorenz 96 system at 1% noise, JacobianODE achieves 30.9±0.5 error versus 45.5±0.1 for NeuralODE and 66.39 for weighted linear regression. On the 128-dimensional task-trained RNN at 1% noise, JacobianODE achieves 188.5±7.1 versus 294.0±0.03 for NeuralODE and 301.46 for weighted linear regression. In the ILQR control task, JacobianODE achieves accuracy nearing ground truth, while both baselines perform substantially worse.

## Baselines & Comparisons

JacobianODE outperforms both baselines on Jacobian estimation (Frobenius norm error) across virtually all systems and noise levels. Against NeuralODE (Chen et al., 2018), which learns the vector field f directly and computes the Jacobian via automatic differentiation, JacobianODE achieves roughly 30–50% lower error on chaotic systems (e.g., 3.3 vs 8.7 on Lorenz at 1% noise; 30.9 vs 45.5 on 64D Lorenz 96) and ~36% lower error on the task-trained RNN (188.5 vs 294.0). The gap widens substantially at higher noise levels, where NeuralODE degrades sharply (e.g., 26.0 vs 5.1 on Lorenz at 5% noise) while JacobianODE remains stable. Against weighted linear regression (Deyle et al., 2016; Ahamed et al., 2020), JacobianODE wins by a large margin in every setting (e.g., 30.9 vs 66.39 on 64D Lorenz 96; 188.5 vs 301.5 on the RNN). On the downstream ILQR control task, JacobianODE achieves accuracy nearing ground truth, while both NeuralODE and weighted linear regression produce substantially higher trajectory MSE, confirming that accurate Jacobian estimation (not just trajectory prediction) is what enables effective control.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends path-integral-based Jacobian estimation (Lorraine & Hossain, Beik-Mohammadi et al.) from general function learning to dynamical systems, where the time derivative is unobserved. It departs from the standard NeuralODE approach (learn f, then autodiff for J) by directly parameterizing the Jacobian as a neural network, motivated by the well-known gap between function approximation and derivative accuracy. The loop-closure loss builds on Iyer et al.'s self-supervised conservative-field constraint, and the control-theoretic analysis extends linear reachability Gramian methods (Liu et al., Bassett et al.) to nonlinear systems via Jacobian linearization, filling a gap the authors identify between communication-subspace methods and control-theoretic characterizations of interareal interactions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first data-driven pipeline that goes from raw trajectories to nonlinear control-theoretic characterizations of subsystem interactions, demonstrated end-to-end on a task-trained RNN where the visual area's reachability over the cognitive area increases with learning. Key open questions include scaling to partially observed dynamics (latent Jacobian estimation), whether global (contraction-theoretic) rather than local controllability can be recovered data-drivenly, and performance at the scale of thousands of neurons. The finding that moderate observation noise (5%) can improve Jacobian estimation by encouraging exploration of perturbation directions is a practical insight for noisy biological recordings.
