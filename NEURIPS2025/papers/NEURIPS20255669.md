## Problem

Parallel-in-time sequence models such as Mamba and S4D use diagonal state-transition matrices that are provably not maximally expressive and fail at state-tracking tasks. Replacing them with dense matrices restores expressivity but increases computational cost from O(d_h²) to O(d_h³), making them infeasible at scale. The paper asks whether structured matrices can retain the maximal expressivity of dense matrices while remaining computationally efficient.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a driving path ω constructed from the input: for language and permutation tasks, ω_t = (1, x_t) (time-augmented token embeddings); for time-series, ω_t = X_t directly. The recurrence produces a hidden state sequence h_t ∈ R^{d_h} at each time step, which is passed through a linear readout to yield a scalar or class label. A key structural detail is that the state-transition matrices A_θ^i depend linearly on the input path increments, and the full sequence of hidden states is computed via a parallel associative scan in O(log n) steps rather than sequentially.

## Method

SLiCEs replace the dense state-transition matrices of a Linear Neural CDE with structured variants—block-diagonal, sparse, Walsh-Hadamard (H·diag(Δ)), or diagonal-plus-low-rank—while preserving linear input dependence. The core theoretical result, proven via rough path signature methods, is that block-diagonal, sparse, and Walsh-Hadamard SLiCEs achieve maximal probabilistic expressivity: with high probability under Gaussian initialisation, a linear readout of the hidden state can approximate any continuous function on a compact path set. The block-diagonal structure is empirically and computationally preferred because it is closed under matrix multiplication, so the parallel associative scan preserves the structure at each composition step (O(d_h·Σb_j²) per composition) rather than collapsing to dense O(d_h³). In practice, the matrix exponential in the flow is approximated by its first-order Taylor expansion, and a hybrid Log-ODE + parallel scan strategy is used for time-series to avoid materialising intermediate matrices in GPU memory.

## Results

On the formal language benchmark (4 regular-language tasks, train length 3–40, eval length 40–256), the best SLiCE variants (D-DE-SLiCE and DPLR-SLiCE) achieve 85.1% average validation accuracy, outperforming the strongest parallel baseline Gated DeltaProduct[-1,1] (80.7%) and Mamba (58.9%). On the UEA-MTSCA time-series classification archive (6 datasets), BD-SLiCE achieves 64.0% average test accuracy—matching Log-NCDE (64.3%)—while reducing average training time per 1,000 steps from 1,321.7 s to 68.1 s (≈20× speedup). On the A₅ state-tracking benchmark, BD-SLiCE, DPLR-SLiCE, and S-SLiCE each require only a single layer to exceed 90% validation accuracy at all sequence lengths (3–20) and sustain high test accuracy at sequences 8× longer than the training range.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among parallel-in-time models, BD-SLiCE, D-DE-SLiCE, and DPLR-SLiCE all outperform every baseline on the formal language benchmark: the best baseline Gated DeltaProduct[-1,1] reaches 80.7% average, while the top SLiCE variants reach 85.1%. Mamba (58.9%) and S4D (41.3%) are substantially weaker, confirming the paper's theoretical result that diagonal state-transition matrices are not maximally expressive. On the UEA-MTSCA time-series archive, BD-SLiCE (64.0%) matches Log-NCDE (64.3%) within 0.3% while cutting per-step training time by ~20× (68.1 s vs. 1,321.7 s per 1,000 steps), and outperforms LRU (61.7%), S5 (61.8%), S6 (62.0%), NCDE (60.2%), NRDE (60.6%), and Mamba (58.6%). On the A₅ length-generalisation task, BD-SLiCE, DPLR-SLiCE, and S-SLiCE sustain high accuracy at sequences 8× the training length, whereas Gated DeltaProduct and D-DE-SLiCE generalise only to ~2× training length, and Mamba/WH-SLiCE fail to reach high accuracy even at training lengths. The recurrent LSTM achieves near-perfect scores (100%) on all formal language tasks but cannot be parallelised, highlighting the expressivity–parallelism trade-off that SLiCEs address.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies and extends the LNCDE framework of Cirone et al. [22] (which showed dense matrices are maximally expressive while diagonal matrices are not) by proving that block-diagonal, sparse, and Walsh-Hadamard structured matrices also achieve maximal probabilistic expressivity. It subsumes previously separate architectures—DeltaNet's DPLR structure [85], input-dependent block-diagonal LRNNs [33], and Mamba's diagonal SSM [39]—under a single SLiCE framework, and introduces two novel structures (sparse and Walsh-Hadamard) with matching theoretical guarantees. The Log-ODE method from Walker et al. [98] is incorporated as a practical acceleration technique, and the NCDE lineage [53] provides the continuous-time foundation that gives SLiCEs robustness to irregular sampling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: block-diagonal SLiCEs match Log-NCDE accuracy on six UEA time-series datasets while cutting per-step training time by ~20×, and they are the only maximally expressive structure that simultaneously reduces parameter count, recurrent cost, and parallel scan cost relative to dense LNCDEs. Key open questions include scaling to multi-billion-parameter regimes (current implementations struggle with GPU kernel efficiency for many small independent systems), characterising expressivity at finite hidden dimensions, and developing true path-to-path models that can be stacked. A theoretical characterisation of the general conditions under which a structured matrix achieves maximal probabilistic expressivity would guide the search for further efficient architectures.
