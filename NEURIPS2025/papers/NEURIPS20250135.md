## Problem

Under Title VII's less discriminatory alternative (LDA) requirement, the claimant must prove that a less discriminatory yet comparably performant model exists, yet they typically lack the compute, data, and model access to train one. No existing method allows a resource-constrained auditor to determine whether an LDA exists without training a model of comparable scale to the contested system, creating a fundamental resource-information asymmetry that effectively shields AI systems from scrutiny.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250135/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The claimant provides a small subsample of training data (D⁻ ≪ D⁺), test data, and estimates of the contested model's parameter count N⁺ and training set size D⁺, along with a compute budget. The method outputs a fitted loss-fairness Pareto frontier curve for the contested model's scale and a quantitative determination of whether the contested model lies at least δ-far from that frontier (implying an LDA is feasible). Notably, the claimant never trains a large model; all empirical fitting is done on small models, and the PF for the contested scale is obtained purely by extrapolation via the scaling law.

## Method

The paper derives a closed-form upper bound on the loss-fairness Pareto frontier (Theorem 4.2) under a DGP where A ~ Ber(π), X ⊥ A, and Y|X,A ~ Ber(σ(g(X) − ζA)), with loss measured by binary cross-entropy and fairness by demographic parity. The bound has the form L(Δ) ≤ B(𝓕,D) + f(Δ; c, c′, c″), where f is a fixed functional shape determined by three constants depending only on the test distribution p, and B is a single additive constant that scales as Θ(1/N^α + 1/D^β) per known neural scaling laws. The claimant fits the seven constants (C₁–C₇) by training as few as 7 small models via linear scalarization (varying the weight λ on the demographic-parity regularizer), tracing the lower convex hull of (loss, fairness-gap) points as the empirical PF, and curve-fitting. The proof technique transforms the constrained minimization (minimize loss subject to fairness gap = Δ) into an unconstrained problem by constructing an artificial training distribution q that 'tilts' p to induce the target fairness gap, then approximates Pareto-optimal classifiers with Bayes-optimal estimators on q under a model-misspecification symmetry assumption (Assumption 4.1). Experiments on synthetic data with X not independent of A confirm the scaling law holds even when the theoretical DGP conditions are relaxed.

## Results

On synthetic data (10,000 samples, 20-dim Gaussian features, 2-layer MLP with hidden size 32), the closed-form PF from Theorem 4.2 closely matches empirical PFs traced via linear scalarization (100 λ values, 3 trials each). The fitted constants are c=0.0176, c′=0.92, c″=0.1424, with bias −0.285 and loss scaling law 55(N^{−0.7}+D^{−0.5}). The y-intercepts confirm that B(𝓕,D) scales proportionally to N^{−α}+D^{−β} plus an additive constant, and the effect of scaling via B is linearly additive as predicted. The approach requires training as few as 7 small models to fit the PF, then extrapolates to the contested (large) model without training it.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not perform head-to-head comparisons against other Pareto-frontier-finding methods. Instead, it validates its theoretical closed-form PF (solid curves) against empirical PFs obtained via linear scalarization (dashed curves) across multiple model sizes N, as shown in Figure 3 and Appendix J. The empirical PFs are generated by training models with varying fairness regularization weights λ ∈ [−3, 5] and tracing the lower convex hull of (loss, fairness gap) points. The theoretical curve mimics the empirical data well, though imperfectly, with the authors noting that (i) the empirical PF is inherently conservative, (ii) Theorem 4.2 provides an upper bound, and (iii) multiple constant fits can match the same empirical PF. The paper positions its contribution as complementary to existing PF-tracing methods (e.g., [21, 47, 53, 56]): those methods find the PF for a given model size, while the scaling law extrapolates the PF from small models to large ones, eliminating the need to train the contested model at all.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three literatures: (1) performance-fairness Pareto frontier (PF) tracing methods (Feldman et al. 2015, Navon et al. 2020, Ruchte & Grabocka 2021), which typically require training many models of comparable size to the contested system; (2) neural scaling laws (Hoffmann et al. 2022, Bahri et al. 2024), whose Θ(1/N^α + 1/D^β) loss-scaling form is borrowed to parameterize the PF's model-size dependence; and (3) the legal LDA requirement operationalized by Gillis et al. (2024) and Laufer et al. (2024), which cast LDA search as an optimization problem but remain computationally intractable for large, unstructured-input models. The key departure is the use of a 'duality' technique—approximating Pareto-optimal classifiers via Bayes-optimal estimators on artificially tilted training distributions—to derive the first closed-form upper bound on a loss-fairness PF, which then functions as a scaling law enabling extrapolation from small to large models without ever training the latter.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete and recurring barrier in AI accountability: the resource-information asymmetry that prevents claimants from meeting the LDA evidentiary burden under Title VII and analogous statutes. The closed-form PF bound is a genuinely new theoretical object, and the scaling-law framing means the method is plug-and-play—improving automatically as better PF-tracing techniques are developed. However, significant open questions remain: the result is specific to demographic parity and BCE loss under a particular DGP, experiments are limited to small synthetic datasets with 20-dimensional Gaussian features, and the upper-bound nature means the method is conservative. Whether courts would accept a scaling-law-based PF estimate as sufficient evidence of LDA existence, and whether the DGP and symmetry assumptions (Assumption 4.1) hold for real-world unstructured data, are critical unresolved questions.
