## Problem

Despite LLMs' strong performance on static tasks like math and coding, their general learning ability—how they acquire new knowledge, internalize abstract rules, and adapt from accumulated experience—remains largely unexplored. Existing benchmarks measure zero- or few-shot task performance but lack a principled framework for probing how models actually learn, generalize, and update behavior across cognitively distinct learning modes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255760/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

In the LearnArena benchmark, the evaluated model (Player-1) operates within a two-player game loop where it simultaneously receives three input streams: (1) instructor feedback from a fixed Player-0 (Qwen2.5-32B) who reviews three self-selected prior game experiences and returns improvement suggestions, (2) a static natural-language summary of game rules and strategies generated by Qwen2.5-32B, and (3) its own selected past trajectories used as in-context examples. Output is the average win rate over 20 independent matches per environment across eight games (Checkers, Stratego, Tic Tac Toe, Truth and Deception, SpellingBee, SpiteAndMalice, Tak, WordChains). In the math experiments, inputs are instructor-generated solutions (passive) or solutions plus one clarification Q&A turn (interactive), and outputs are normalized accuracy on eight benchmarks from GSM8K to MMLU-Math.

## Method

The paper proposes a cognitively grounded framework that decomposes general learning into three complementary dimensions: Learning from Instructor (LfI, explicit guided interaction), Learning from Concept (LfC, internalizing static abstract rules), and Learning from Experience (LfE, adapting from accumulated interaction history). Each dimension is operationalized through targeted experimental paradigms: LfI uses tutor-learner training on MagpieMath comparing passive consumption versus interactive clarification; LfC injects conceptual rule summaries into TextArena competitive games and logic/planning tasks; LfE conditions models on self-selected prior game trajectories or scaled in-context examples. The key unifying contribution is LearnArena, a benchmark that embeds all three dimensions simultaneously within a single two-player game protocol—Player-1 must absorb instructor feedback, apply conceptual rule knowledge, and leverage its own experiential memory in every round—making success require integrated learning rather than isolated skill. Empirical findings reveal that interaction consistently improves instruction-based learning, conceptual understanding is scale-emergent (small models are harmed by conceptual input while large models benefit), and LLMs exhibit a sharp performance drop beyond ~900 in-context examples, confirming they are effective few-shot but not many-shot learners.

## Results

On the LearnArena benchmark (8 competitive environments), GPT-4o achieves the highest average win rate of 0.77, followed by Qwen3-14B (0.60), GPT-4o-mini (0.52), and Qwen2.5-32B (0.51). In the Learning from Instructor experiments, Interactive Clarification consistently outperforms Passive Consumption across all 8 math benchmarks, with Qwen2.5-32B gaining +25.7% normalized accuracy on NumGLUE versus +18.2% under passive supervision. In the Learning from Experience setting, all models exhibit a non-monotonic trend with in-context examples: performance peaks at a moderate number of shots and drops sharply beyond ~900 examples, confirming that LLMs are effective few-shot but not many-shot learners.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper validates LearnArena's distinctiveness by computing Spearman rank correlations against nine existing benchmarks. LearnArena shows only moderate correlation with AlpacaEval (ρ=0.66) and MMLU (ρ=0.66), lower correlation with HellaSwag (ρ=0.53), WizardLM (ρ=0.44), and Self-Instruct (ρ=0.43), and near-zero correlation with ARC, Winogrande, and GSM8K, confirming it captures a distinct learning-ability signal not measured by static task benchmarks. Within LearnArena itself, GPT-4o dominates all other models by a wide margin (0.77 avg. vs. 0.60 for the best open-source Qwen3-14B), while Qwen3-14B outperforms all Qwen2.5 variants and GPT-4o-mini, suggesting architectural and training improvements beyond raw scale are critical. In the LfI experiments, interactive clarification beats passive consumption across all model families, but the smallest model (Phi-3-mini) shows only marginal gains, and a weak instructor (Qwen2.5-1.5B) actually degrades interactive learning below passive baselines on GSM8K, MATH, and SAT. In LfC, concept injection degrades performance for Qwen2.5-1.5B across all TextArena environments but consistently helps 14B and 32B models, establishing conceptual understanding as scale-emergent. In LfE, experience-based adaptation shows the same scale-emergent pattern: Qwen2.5-1.5B win rates drop (e.g., Checkers 0.30→0.11) with experience, while Qwen2.5-32B improves substantially (Stratego 0.6→0.8, TruthAndDeception 0.6→0.9).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the TextArena competitive game framework [28] as its evaluation substrate, but departs from it by layering a cognitively grounded tripartite decomposition (Learning from Instructor, Concept, and Experience) drawn from educational psychology (RITL, experiential learning theory, rule-based cognition). It positions itself against the dominant LLM evaluation paradigm (MMLU, BIG-Bench, HELM) that measures static zero/few-shot task performance, and against scattered in-context learning studies that probe adaptation in isolation without a unifying theoretical lens. The key conceptual departure is treating 'learning ability' as a first-class, multi-dimensional evaluation target rather than a byproduct of task-specific benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic, cognitively motivated framework for diagnosing *how* LLMs learn, not just *what* they can do, and the LearnArena benchmark operationalizes this in a unified game-based setting. Practically, the findings that weak instructors degrade interactive learning and that many-shot ICL degrades beyond ~900 examples offer concrete guidance for agent design and data curation. Key limitations include the narrow domain coverage (math + board games), the fact that 'learning' here is in-context conditioning rather than parameter updates, and the reliance on a single fixed opponent (Qwen2.5-32B) for most experiments. Open questions include whether the many-shot bottleneck can be overcome architecturally, whether the framework generalizes to non-game domains, and whether true online adaptation (beyond ICL) can be meaningfully evaluated.
