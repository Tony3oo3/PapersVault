## Problem

Large-scale networked MARL faces two simultaneous challenges: the joint state-action space grows exponentially with the number of agents, making standard RL intractable, and real-world systems experience environment shifts that require efficient cross-domain adaptation. Prior work on networked MARL (e.g., Qu et al.) achieved scalability via neighborhood truncation but did not address domain generalization, while causal representation learning for RL generalization was limited to single-agent settings. No existing methodology provides provable guarantees for policies that are simultaneously scalable and generalizable in networked MARL.

## Input / Output

Each agent consumes only its κ-hop neighborhood states and actions, but further reduced to the ACR subset identified by causal masks (typically a small fraction of the full neighborhood), plus a compact domain-factor vector ω estimated from a few trajectories. The state and action spaces are tabular (discrete), and the causal masks are binary structural indicators recovered from observed trajectories. The output is a set of localized, domain-conditioned policies π_i(a_i | s_{N_i}^∘, ω_{N_i}^∘) and tabular Q-function estimates over the compact ACR space, with the adapted policy for a new domain requiring only T_a trajectories to estimate the new domain factor before deployment.

## Method

GSAC integrates four phases: (1) local causal discovery where each agent recovers its binary causal mask and estimates the latent domain factor from trajectories; (2) ACR construction, which recursively traces causal edges backward from each agent's reward to identify the minimal set of state and domain-factor components within the κ-hop neighborhood, yielding compact representations whose approximation error decays as O(γ^{κ+1}); (3) meta actor-critic training across M source domains, where a tabular TD critic is updated per-agent over the ACR space and a policy-gradient actor is updated with stepsize η/√(k+1), both conditioned on the compact domain factor; (4) fast adaptation, where a new domain factor is estimated from a few trajectories and the meta-policy is deployed immediately without further training. The key insight is that the same causal structure that enables dimensionality reduction for scalability also isolates domain-specific variation into a low-dimensional factor, enabling provable generalization with O(1/√T_a) adaptation gap.

## Results

GSAC is evaluated on wireless communication networks of varying scale (grid sizes 3, 4, 5 corresponding to 16, 25, and 36 agents) and a traffic control benchmark. The primary metric is cumulative return during few-shot adaptation (Episodes 1–30) in an unseen target domain (ω_target = 0.65) after meta-training on M = 3 source domains (ω ∈ {0.2, 0.5, 0.8}). GSAC consistently achieves the highest adaptation return across all grid sizes, demonstrating both scalability (stable performance as agent count grows from 16 to 36) and rapid generalization from only T_a adaptation trajectories. Results are presented as adaptation curves (Figure 2, Figure 6) rather than a single numerical summary table.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GSAC is compared against three baselines: SAC-MTL (multi-task learning with one-hot domain encoding, following [37]), SAC-FT (fine-tuning a single policy in the target domain), and SAC-LFS (learning from scratch in the target domain). GSAC wins on all three comparisons across every grid size, with the largest gap over SAC-LFS, which suffers the slowest convergence and lowest overall return due to the absence of any meta-training or domain conditioning. SAC-MTL shows moderate early-phase performance and improves steadily over episodes, but its lack of explicit domain-factor conditioning leads to slower adaptation than GSAC in the critical few-shot window (Episodes 1–30). SAC-FT performs worse than SAC-MTL in the early phase because it must adjust policy parameters directly in the target domain, incurring higher sample complexity. The paper's key finding is that conditioning the policy on a compact, causally-identified domain factor (rather than a one-hot or no domain signal) is what enables GSAC's rapid adaptation advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines: (1) scalable networked MARL exploiting local interactions and exponential Q-function decay (Qu et al. 2022, Lin et al. 2021, Qu et al. 2020), and (2) causal representation learning for domain generalization in single-agent RL (AdaRL [10], causal world models [6], counterfactual data augmentation [22]). The key departure is that no prior work simultaneously addresses both scalability and provable domain generalization in networked MARL; GSAC bridges this gap by using recovered causal masks to construct Approximately Compact Representations (ACRs) that reduce input dimensionality while preserving exponential approximation guarantees, then conditions a meta actor-critic on compact domain factors for few-shot adaptation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ACR framework is a clean theoretical contribution of potential independent interest: it shows that causal structure can be exploited to reduce the effective state dimensionality for both value functions and policies while maintaining provable approximation bounds, and that domain factors can be estimated from O(√T_e) trajectories. However, the empirical validation is limited to small tabular benchmarks (16–36 agents in wireless communication and traffic control), and the framework assumes full observability, known rewards, and a compact domain factor space. Open questions include whether ACRs remain useful with function approximation in continuous spaces, how the faithfulness assumption holds in practice for large-scale networks, and whether the approach extends to partially observable settings.
