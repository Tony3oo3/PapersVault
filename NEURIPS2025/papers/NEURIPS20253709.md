## Problem

Frame-based networks applied sequentially to video produce temporally inconsistent (flickering) outputs, a problem amplified by time-varying corruptions such as noise, compression artifacts, and adverse weather. Existing solutions are either task-specific video architectures requiring expensive training on large video datasets, or black-box temporal consistency methods that operate only on model inputs/outputs and cannot capture feature-level stability. The paper seeks a general, lightweight mechanism that stabilizes any pre-trained frame-based model without modifying its parameters.

## Input / Output

The method consumes sequential RGB video frames (optionally corrupted) and produces task-specific outputs—denoised images, enhanced images, relative depth maps, or semantic segmentation masks—depending on the frozen base network. Processing is strictly causal: each stabilized output at time t depends only on frames up to t, making it suitable for streaming video. Stabilizers are inserted at multiple intermediate feature layers and the final output, operating on 2D feature maps at the native resolution of each layer (e.g., 256×256 crops for denoising, 512×512 for depth, full-frame for enhancement).

## Method

The core contribution is a class of lightweight stabilization adapters built on an exponential moving average (EMA) operation, where a learned controller network predicts per-pixel, per-layer decay values β from spatiotemporal context (current and previous frames plus feature maps). A spatial fusion extension replaces the scalar decay with a softmax-normalized kernel over a local neighborhood, enabling the stabilizer to track inter-frame motion by translating features from previous frames. Training uses a unified accuracy-stability-robustness loss that jointly penalizes prediction error against ground truth and frame-to-frame prediction variation under input corruptions; the paper proves an oracle bound (λ < 1/2 ensures the ground truth remains the global minimizer) and a collapse bound (λ > τ−1 causes prediction collapse). Only the adapter parameters are trained via BPTT on short video snippets (τ=8) while the base network remains frozen, keeping the approach resource-efficient and broadly applicable across architectures.

## Results

On corruption robustness (Table 1), the spatial-fusion stabilizer reduces instability by 50–85% across all five corruptions while simultaneously improving or preserving accuracy. For example, under patch drop, enhancement PSNR improves from 17.43 to 31.39 dB and instability drops from 164.6 to 30.36; under impulse noise, denoising PSNR rises from 24.65 to 32.04 dB and instability falls from 62.10 to 19.73. On adverse weather (Table 2), stabilizing NAFNet improves rain PSNR from 21.43 to 28.63 dB (instability 151.76→57.88) and snow PSNR from 18.62 to 31.34 dB (instability 262.48→59.31); joint fine-tuning with stabilization further lifts rain PSNR to 32.61 dB. On semantic segmentation (VIPER), stabilizers reduce categorical instability from 0.079 to 0.059 while improving mIoU from 0.406 to 0.411.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external video-stabilization methods; instead it compares its learned stabilizer variants against the unstabilized base model and simpler internal baselines (hand-tuned fixed EMA, per-channel learned EMA, controlled EMA, and spatial-fusion EMA). The controlled and spatial-fusion stabilizers consistently outperform all fixed and simple-learned variants: for image enhancement, spatial fusion achieves roughly 2 dB higher PSNR than the base model while reducing frame-to-frame variation by ~35%, whereas fixed EMA stabilizers trade quality for stability with no net gain. For denoising, the simple fixed EMA actually worsens both PSNR and instability (because it over-smooths the i.i.d. noise residual), while the controlled stabilizer improves both metrics simultaneously at λ≤0.4. The only notable regression is denoising PSNR under frame drop (33.42→27.34 dB), which the authors attribute to PSNR's inability to capture the perceptual jarring of dropped frames. On adversarial robustness (Appendix F.5), the stabilizer-augmented ResNet-50 reaches 88.8% accuracy versus 77.0% for the fine-tuned baseline, an 11.8-point absolute gain under I-FGSM attacks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of blind video temporal consistency work (Bonneel et al., Lai et al., Lei et al.) by moving beyond input-output black-box smoothing to model stability in both feature and output spaces, and by unifying temporal stability with corruption robustness under a single accuracy-stability-robustness loss. It departs from task-specific video architectures (for colorization, stylization, depth, segmentation) by proposing lightweight, modular EMA-based adapters with learned controllers that can be attached to any frozen pre-trained image model, drawing conceptual inspiration from selective state space models (Mamba) for the controller design. The corruption robustness formalism builds directly on Hendrycks and Dietterich's benchmarking framework, extended to time series and arbitrary metrics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: any pre-trained image model (denoiser, enhancer, depth estimator, segmenter) can be made temporally stable and corruption-robust with minimal additional training and no architectural modification, which is valuable for deploying existing models on streaming video. The clean theoretical bounds (oracle bound λ<1/2, collapse bound λ>τ-1) give practitioners principled guidance for tuning the stability-accuracy tradeoff, and the negative result showing that naive feature-space smoothing worsens denoising (by over-smoothing the i.i.d. noise residual) is a useful caution. Open questions include whether the norm-based theoretical guarantees extend to the non-norm losses (e.g., cross-entropy) that the authors find empirically effective, the sim-to-real gap observed for depth estimation, and whether spatial fusion failures on long sequences under extreme noise can be fully resolved by longer training sequences.
