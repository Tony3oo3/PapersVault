## Problem

Existing embodied navigation benchmarks either focus on passive 3D perception or rely on task-specific supervision with final success rates, providing limited insight into whether MLLMs genuinely understand navigation behavior. No prior benchmark jointly evaluates instruction comprehension, sequential decision-making, difficulty stratification, and real-world transferability under zero-shot settings for generalist MLLMs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253188/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For execution, the MLLM receives a panoramic RGB observation, a natural language instruction, and a discrete set of candidate navigable viewpoints (typically 5), and outputs a single selected next viewpoint; this discrete viewpoint-selection abstraction avoids continuous control confounds and enables fair zero-shot comparison. For comprehension, inputs are panoramic trajectory sequences paired with multiple-choice questions (5 candidates each), and outputs are the selected answer index. The real-world pipeline additionally consumes RGB-D camera input and produces low-level robot motion commands via a waypoint predictor and controller.

## Method

NavBench decomposes evaluation into two complementary components: Navigation Comprehension (3,200 QA pairs across three cognitively grounded tasks—Global Instruction Alignment with four distractor perturbation strategies, Temporal Progress Estimation over segmented sub-instructions, and Local Observation-Action Reasoning with future-observation and future-action prediction variants) and Navigation Execution (432 episodes across 72 Matterport3D scenes with step-by-step viewpoint selection). A key design choice is a fine-grained difficulty classification into easy/medium/hard levels, computed from composite scores over spatial complexity (path length, turn angles, elevation, area), cognitive complexity (instruction length, verb count, spatial terms, landmarks, subordinate clauses), and execution complexity (steps, turns, floor changes, decision points), validated by human raters. A deployment pipeline bridges simulation and physical embodiment by chaining a waypoint predictor, an MLLM decision module, and a low-level controller on a dual-arm mobile robot. All evaluation is conducted zero-shot, with no task-specific fine-tuning, to probe the generalization of off-the-shelf MLLMs.

## Results

On NavBench, GPT-4o achieves the best execution performance with 41.33% average (67.36% SR on Easy, 41.67% SR on Medium, 27.78% SR on Hard), while o4-mini leads in comprehension at 59.66% average accuracy. Among open-source models, Qwen2.5-VL-7B is the strongest, reaching 45.26% comprehension and 21.77% execution average, approaching GPT-4o-mini (46.42% / 27.99%). Temporal Progress Estimation is a consistent bottleneck across all models, with GPT-4o scoring only 42.90% and most open-source models below 32%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among open-source models, Qwen2.5-VL-7B clearly outperforms the others on both comprehension (45.26% vs. 33.97% for InternVL2.5-2B, 32.26% for LLaVA-OneVision-7B, and 14.75% for Llama3.2-Vision-11B) and execution (21.77% vs. 13.02%, 15.86%, and 15.04% respectively). InternVL2.5-2B achieves the highest Global Instruction Alignment score among open-source models (67.25%), surpassing even GPT-4o (51.33%), but collapses on Progress Estimation (23.40%) and Local reasoning (11.25%), dragging its overall comprehension to 33.97%. LLaVA-OneVision-7B and Llama3.2-Vision-11B show comparable execution performance (~15–16% average) but differ sharply in comprehension, with Llama3.2-Vision-11B scoring only 14.75% on comprehension average, suggesting a disconnect between its execution and understanding. Against proprietary models, the best open-source model (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) trails GPT-4o by roughly 20 points on execution average (21.77% vs. 41.33%) and 8 points on comprehension, though it narrows the gap on Easy episodes (41.67% vs. 67.36% SR). Gemini-2.0-flash is notable for achieving 46.53% SR on Medium difficulty—higher than GPT-4o's 41.67%—while o4-mini leads comprehension (59.66%) but underperforms GPT-4o on execution (28.98% vs. 41.33%), indicating that strong instruction understanding does not always translate to effective step-by-step navigation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of MLLM evaluation and embodied navigation. Traditional VLN benchmarks (R2R, REVERIE, ObjectNav) were designed for supervised, task-specific agents and reduce evaluation to final success rates, while recent spatial-reasoning benchmarks (SpatialBench, VSI-Bench) assess passive 3D perception without decision-making. NavBench departs from both by jointly probing comprehension (instruction alignment, progress tracking, local action reasoning) and step-by-step execution under zero-shot settings, and by introducing a three-dimensional difficulty stratification (spatial, cognitive, execution complexity) absent from prior navigation benchmarks. It also extends the evaluation beyond simulation by providing a real-world robot deployment pipeline, a dimension no prior MLLM navigation benchmark addresses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that temporal reasoning—specifically progress estimation along multi-step instructions—is a persistent bottleneck across all evaluated models, including GPT-4o (42.90%), suggesting a fundamental gap in current MLLMs' ability to track sequential state. The finding that comprehension and execution scores are closely correlated implies that improving diagnostic understanding may transfer to better execution, offering a practical pathway for model developers. Open questions include whether the temporal weakness is specific to the panoramic, viewpoint-selection formulation or generalizes to continuous control, and whether the difficulty stratification holds when applied to outdoor or multi-floor real-world environments. The small real-world validation (10 cases per model) limits confidence in the simulation-to-reality transfer claim.
