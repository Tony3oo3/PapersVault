## Problem

AI-assisted psychiatric diagnosis is hindered by the absence of objective biomarkers, heavy reliance on subjective self-reported and clinician-estimated scales, significant symptom overlap across disorders, and strict privacy constraints that limit access to clinical data. No specialized multi-agent framework existed for mood disorder diagnosis that could handle these intertwined challenges simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250925/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each case provides narrative medical records (which may be absent for first-time visitors) plus item-level responses from 13 clinical scales (8 self-reported, 5 clinician-rated). Rather than consuming raw total scores, the framework decomposes scales into individual item responses and selects only the 16 questions with the highest Pearson correlation to mood disorder presence, reorganized into five symptom groups (depressive mood, suicidal ideation, energy/interest loss, anxiety, insomnia). The output is a binary mood disorder classification accompanied by structured diagnostic reasoning, produced through a multi-agent debate that yields a final judgment with supporting evidence.

## Method

MoodAngels combines granular-scale analysis with a retrieval-augmented multi-agent architecture. Granular-scale analysis decomposes 13 clinical scales into item-level responses, selects the top 5% most mood-relevant questions via Pearson correlation, and evaluates intra-group consistency to detect inter-scale conflicts (e.g., self-reported depression contradicted by clinician observations). Three diagnostic agents—Angel.R (no case reference), Angel.D (retrieved cases displayed as context), and Angel.C (comparative analysis of retrieved cases)—perform independent diagnoses using BGE-M3 dense-vector retrieval over a knowledge base of structured DSM-5 criteria and 2,804 anonymized clinical cases. A Judge Agent then synthesizes the three opinions; disagreements trigger a structured debate between a Positive and Negative agent until consensus is reached. Complementing the diagnostic framework, MoodSyn generates 1,173 synthetic psychiatric cases via a TabSyn-based pipeline (VAE + conditional diffusion with adaptive tokenization and dynamic loss weighting) that preserves multivariate clinical correlations while providing privacy guarantees beyond traditional anonymization.

## Results

On 561 real-world clinical cases, the best MoodAngels variant (Angel.R with DeepSeek-V3) achieves 92.7% accuracy, 86.3% recall, 0.841 MCC, and 0.920 Macro F1. The headline result is that even the simplest agent (Angel.R with GPT-4o) reaches 92.0% accuracy, a 12.3-point improvement over GPT-4o used in-context (79.7%). The full multi-agent system (multi-Angels with GPT-4o) further improves recall to 88.1% while maintaining 92.5% accuracy. On the 140-case MoodSyn synthetic test set, multi-Angels with DeepSeek-V3 achieves 82.1% accuracy and 0.642 MCC, outperforming all in-context baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All MoodAngels variants substantially outperform every in-context LLM baseline on real-world data. Angel.R with GPT-4o (92.0% ACC) beats GPT-4o in-context (79.7% ACC) by 12.3 points, and Angel.R with DeepSeek-V3 (92.7% ACC) beats DeepSeek-V3 in-context (84.7% ACC) by 8.0 points. The smallest gap is against DeepSeek-V3, the strongest baseline, where even the weakest agent variant (Angel.C with GPT-4o, 91.4% ACC) still leads by 6.7 points. On the synthetic MoodSyn test set, the advantage narrows: multi-Angels with DeepSeek-V3 (82.1% ACC) matches DeepSeek-V3 in-context (82.1% ACC) on accuracy but leads on recall (82.4% vs. 76.1%) and MCC (0.642 vs. 0.664, where DeepSeek-V3 actually edges ahead slightly). The multi-agent debate mechanism provides the largest recall gains (88.1% for multi-Angels GPT-4o vs. 84.0–84.8% for single agents), suggesting it is most beneficial for hard cases with conflicting signals.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of LLM-based medical diagnosis agents (Agent Hospital [7], multi-specialist consultation [8]) into the psychiatric domain, where the concrete biological test results and structured lab data those systems rely on are largely unavailable. It departs from social-media-based depression detection [16–18] by operating on clinical scale data and medical records rather than user-generated text, and from general multi-agent debate approaches [14] by designing three agents with deliberately different levels of reliance on retrieved historical cases to accommodate psychiatric uncertainty. The synthetic data component builds directly on the TabSyn framework [20] (VAE + diffusion for mixed-type tabular synthesis), adapting it to preserve clinically meaningful correlations between individual scale items and their aggregate scores.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The MoodSyn dataset (1,173 cases) addresses a genuine bottleneck in computational psychiatry research—clinical data cannot be publicly shared—while the granular-scale analysis and multi-agent debate framework offer a template for handling the subjective, overlapping-symptom nature of psychiatric assessment. However, the framework is limited to binary mood-disorder classification from a single hospital's data, does not perform subcategory diagnosis, and cannot replicate the full hour-long clinical interview process. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to other psychiatric conditions (anxiety, psychosis, personality disorders), whether the Pearson-correlation-based question selection transfers across populations, and whether the multi-agent debate overhead is justified in real-time clinical settings.
