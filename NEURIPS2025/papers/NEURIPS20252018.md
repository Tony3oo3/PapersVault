## Problem

Standard regression requires exact target labels, but in many real-world settings only interval bounds (lower and upper) on the target are available due to expensive measurements, sensor discretization, or domain knowledge. Prior work on interval-target regression (Cheng et al., 2023a) required both realizability and a small ambiguity degree assumption—conditions that are frequently violated in practice (e.g., a simple ε-ball around the true target already breaks the ambiguity condition). The paper asks whether meaningful non-asymptotic generalization guarantees can be established under far weaker assumptions, and whether better learning objectives can exploit the structure of the hypothesis class.

## Input / Output

Each training sample consists of a feature vector x paired with an interval [l_i, u_i] rather than a single scalar target; the true label y_i is guaranteed to lie within [l_i, u_i] but is never observed. Intervals may be deterministic (one fixed interval per x) or probabilistic (drawn from a distribution D_I, potentially yielding multiple intervals per x). The model outputs a single scalar regression prediction f(x), and the loss is evaluated against the unknown true y, not against the interval endpoints directly.

## Method

The paper proposes two complementary learning objectives. First, a projection loss π_ℓ(f(x), l, u) = min_{ỹ∈[l,u]} ℓ(f(x), ỹ) replaces the discontinuous 0-1 indicator, and the key theoretical insight is that for an m-Lipschitz hypothesis class, the effective interval constraining f(x) is strictly smaller than [l,u] because smoothness propagates bounds from neighboring points—yielding non-asymptotic generalization bounds that hold without the small-ambiguity-degree assumption. Second, a minmax objective minimizes the worst-case loss over labels within the interval; a constrained variant restricts worst-case labels to outputs of hypotheses already lying inside all intervals (F̃_0), which is provably tighter than the unconstrained version. Practically, the constrained minmax is solved via a regularization trick (Minmax-reg) or by generating pseudo-labels from multiple projection-loss-trained models (PL-Max, PL-Mean). All experiments use Lipschitz MLPs with spectral normalization layers to enforce a tunable Lipschitz constant m, which the theory identifies as a critical hyperparameter balancing interval-shrinkage benefit against model capacity.

## Results

On 18 tabular regression benchmarks with interval targets, Lipschitz MLPs (LipMLP) outperform standard MLPs on 14 of 18 datasets in test MAE. The largest gains appear on Superconduct (0.540 vs 1.459 MAE, a 63% reduction), YProp 4 (2.360 vs 3.828, 38% reduction), and Sulfur (10.681 vs 14.421, 26% reduction). In the UCI experiments, the projection objective and PL(Mean) generally perform best under uniform interval settings, while naive minmax excels when the target is near the interval center. The optimal Lipschitz constant balances hypothesis smoothness against capacity, with the best value typically near the one estimated from the training data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between Lipschitz-constrained MLPs (spectral normalization) and standard MLPs, both trained with the projection loss on interval targets. LipMLP wins on 14 of 18 datasets, with the most dramatic improvements on Superconduct (0.540 vs 1.459 MAE), YProp 4 (2.360 vs 3.828), and Sulfur (10.681 vs 14.421), while MLP wins on GPU (25.123 vs 29.817) and Allstate Claims (86.542 vs 86.547, a negligible margin). Among learning objectives, the projection loss and PL(Mean) are generally best under uniform interval settings, whereas the naive minmax objective (equivalent to midpoint regression for L1 loss) performs best when the true target is known to be near the interval center. The paper also shows that tuning the Lipschitz constant m as a hyperparameter is critical: too small an m over-constrains the hypothesis class (inflating OPT), while too large an m forfeits the interval-reduction benefit, with the optimal value typically near the Lipschitz constant estimated from the training data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the interval-target regression framework introduced by Cheng et al. (ICML 2023), which required both realizability and a small ambiguity degree assumption for convergence guarantees. The key departure is replacing the ambiguity degree condition with a Lipschitz smoothness assumption on the hypothesis class, yielding non-asymptotic generalization bounds that hold in the agnostic setting. The minmax formulation further departs from prior work by restricting worst-case labels to outputs of hypotheses within the class (rather than arbitrary points in the interval), drawing on the partial-label learning literature (Cour et al. 2011, Lv et al. 2020) but adapting it to the continuous regression setting where the ambiguity degree concept is less natural.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation is strong: many real-world regression tasks (sensor readings at discrete intervals, expensive medical measurements, bond pricing) naturally produce interval targets rather than point labels, and this paper provides the first theory that works without the often-violated small ambiguity degree assumption. The finding that Lipschitz-constrained MLPs outperform standard MLPs on 14 of 18 tabular regression benchmarks offers a simple, broadly applicable regularization technique. Open questions include extending to noisy settings where targets may fall outside intervals, non-i.i.d. data (e.g., time series), and whether stronger structural assumptions on interval distributions could yield sharper bounds than the uniform convergence approach used here.
