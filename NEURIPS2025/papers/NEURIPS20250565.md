## Problem

Self-supervised learning (SSL) models trained for longer periods consistently show improved classification performance but degraded dense prediction performance (e.g., semantic segmentation), a phenomenon the authors term Self-supervised Dense Degradation (SDD). This inconsistency between image-level and dense-level performance is prevalent across sixteen state-of-the-art SSL methods yet has no dedicated diagnostic metric, since existing transferability estimators target image-level tasks and are negatively correlated with dense performance. The paper addresses the gap of predicting and mitigating dense performance degradation without access to downstream labels or data.

## Input / Output

The DSE metric consumes only a small batch of 2048 unlabeled training images (~0.16% of ImageNet-1k) passed through a frozen SSL encoder to obtain dense (patch-level) representations, requiring no downstream data, labels, or segmentation annotations. It outputs a single scalar DSE score combining class-separability and effective-dimensionality statistics. In the model-selection setting, the output is a ranked set of top-T (T=3) checkpoint candidates; in the regularization setting, the DSE value is backpropagated as a differentiable penalty added to the original SSL loss with a small coefficient (β=0.001).

## Method

The DSE metric is derived from a theoretical error-rate decomposition for a nearest-neighbor classifier on dense representations, showing that downstream error is small when (1) the intra-class radius (estimated via singular values of k-means-centered cluster matrices) is smaller than the inter-class distance, and (2) the effective dimensionality of representations is large. Concretely, DSE = M_inter − M_intra + λ·M_dim, where M_intra is the mean normalized trace of per-cluster centered representation matrices, M_inter is the mean minimum distance from each representation to the nearest foreign cluster center, and M_dim is the effective rank (exponential of negative entropy of normalized singular values) of a matrix of independently sampled dense features. Two mitigation strategies are proposed: an off-the-shelf checkpoint selection algorithm that identifies local maxima of DSE across saved checkpoints and retains the top-T candidates, and an online regularization that adds −β·DSE to the original SSL loss, making the representation structure explicitly optimized during training.

## Results

The DSE metric achieves an average Kendall's τ of 0.57 with downstream dense segmentation performance across 16 SSL methods and 4 benchmarks, significantly outperforming all existing estimators. DSE-based model selection improves mIoU by 3.0% on average (e.g., MoCo v3 on COCO-Stuff: 15.1→30.9, DINO on PASCAL VOC: 45.8→56.3) with only 0.025 GPU hours of overhead (~97× faster than supervised oracle selection). DSE regularization further reverses the dense degradation trend, restoring performance to near-best levels for methods like iBOT and I-JEPA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All three existing estimators (α-ReQ, RankMe, Lidar) are designed for image-level tasks and are actually *negatively* correlated with dense performance (average Kendall's τ of -0.02, -0.08, and -0.30 respectively), because they track image-level quality which continues improving while dense performance degrades under SDD. Even when adapted to operate on dense representations (denoted with †), they achieve only 0.14, 0.24, and 0.33 average τ, far below DSE's 0.57. DSE's advantage stems from jointly measuring class separability (inter-class distance minus intra-class radius) and effective dimensionality, whereas the baselines capture only dimensionality-related properties. In terms of practical model selection, DSE achieves +3.0 mIoU improvement with 0.025 GPU hours, matching the supervised oracle's +3.6 mIoU while being ~97× cheaper (vs. 2.43 GPU hours for supervised evaluation).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical error-rate decomposition framework from Huang et al. [34] (NN classifier analysis for contrastive SSL) to the dense representation setting, and incorporates the effective rank measure from RankMe [24] as a component of its proposed DSE metric. It departs from prior transferability estimation work (α-ReQ, RankMe, Lidar) by showing these image-level metrics are not merely weak but actively *negatively* correlated with dense performance, motivating a fundamentally different metric that jointly captures class separability and effective dimensionality. The identification of SDD as a distinct phenomenon—separate from overfitting or distribution shift—represents a conceptual departure from the standard SSL narrative that longer training monotonically improves all downstream tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SDD has immediate practical consequences: any practitioner training SSL models for segmentation, depth estimation, or other dense tasks is likely using a suboptimal final checkpoint, with losses up to 22% mIoU in extreme cases. The DSE metric offers a near-zero-cost diagnostic (2048 images, ~0.01× the cost of supervised evaluation) that can be dropped into any existing SSL pipeline. Open questions include whether the sub-Gaussian concentration assumptions in the theory hold for real ViT/ResNet features, whether DSE generalizes to 3D or video dense tasks, and whether the moderate Kendall's τ (~0.57) can be improved—particularly for methods like DINO and Mugs where correlation is near zero. The paper also leaves open whether SDD can be prevented at the loss-design level rather than merely detected and mitigated post-hoc.
