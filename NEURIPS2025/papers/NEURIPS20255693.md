## Problem

VQVAEs consistently compress their latent representations into a surprisingly low-dimensional subspace (typically 4–10 dimensions) regardless of the background embedding dimension, a phenomenon the authors term dimensional collapse. Unlike the well-studied codebook collapse (underutilization of entries), this geometric underutilization limits expressiveness and cannot be resolved by encoder-side rank regularization, which degrades reconstruction quality. The paper identifies the structural cause—quantization's inherent bias toward high-variance directions—and proposes an architectural remedy.

## Input / Output

The model takes RGB images (CIFAR-10 at 32×32, CelebA at 64×64, ImageNet at 256×256) and produces a reconstructed image along with discrete latent codes. The encoder output z ∈ ℝ^d is partitioned into N subspaces of dimension d_s (typically 8), each independently quantized against its own codebook of K entries; the final latent is the concatenation of N quantized sub-vectors, giving a total codebook capacity of N×K codes across N parallel quantizers.

## Method

DCVQ (Divide-and-Conquer VQ) addresses dimensional collapse by partitioning the encoder output into N equally sized low-dimensional subspaces (d_s = 8, near the empirically observed optimal effective dimension d*), quantizing each subspace independently with its own codebook, and concatenating the quantized results. The key insight is that VQVAEs exhibit a U-shaped performance curve with an optimum at low effective dimension, so rather than fighting this preference with rank regularization (which consistently degrades loss), DCVQ embraces it: each small subspace operates near its individual optimum, while the direct-sum structure guarantees that total effective rank scales linearly with N. This breaks the intrinsic low-dimensional bottleneck without violating the quantizer's structural bias, achieving both higher effective dimensionality and lower reconstruction loss compared to vanilla VQVAE and RQVAE baselines.

## Results

DCVQ achieves lower validation reconstruction loss at significantly higher effective dimensions than vanilla VQVAEs across CelebA and CIFAR-10 (both CNN and ViT, f=16), shifting the U-shaped performance curve toward higher dimensionality and lower loss (Figure 9). On ImageNet-256, DCVQ-8d consistently outperforms RQVAE in reconstruction FID (rFID) when the number of quantizers N > 16, while also achieving substantially higher effective dimensionality (Figure 10). In contrast, rank regularization methods (e.g., Spectrum Hinge at weight 100) increase effective dimension from 9 to 111 on CIFAR-10 but degrade validation loss from 0.081 to 0.129, confirming that naively enforcing high rank is counterproductive. The vanilla VQVAE optimum sits at an effective dimension of roughly 4–10, regardless of background dimension up to 256.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against vanilla VQVAE, DCVQ wins on both metrics simultaneously: it achieves lower validation reconstruction loss while operating at substantially higher effective dimensions across all tested settings (CelebA and CIFAR-10, CNN and ViT backbones, f=16, codebook size 512). Vanilla VQVAEs are confined to a narrow low-dimensional regime (effective dim ≈ 4–10), whereas DCVQ variants (e.g., 4×8, 8×8, 16×8) span a much broader range of effective dimensions with strictly better loss. Against RQVAE on ImageNet-256 (codebook size 16,384, CNN backbone), DCVQ-8d achieves significantly higher effective dimensionality than RQ at every quantizer count and consistently outperforms RQ in rFID once N > 16; RQ's reconstruction quality quickly saturates with additional quantizers, while DCVQ continues to improve, especially with the smaller subspace dimension d_s = 8. The paper also demonstrates that encoder-side rank regularizers (KoLeo, Barlow Twins, VICReg, Spectrum Hinge, Rank Hinge) all fail to improve post-quantization performance, with stronger regularization consistently increasing effective dimension but worsening reconstruction loss (e.g., Barlow Twins at weight 0.01 raises effective dim from 10 to 78 on CIFAR-10 but increases loss from 0.081 to 0.105).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the VQVAE framework (van den Oord et al., 2017) and its widely adopted variants (VQGAN, RQVAE), but shifts the diagnostic lens from the well-studied problem of codebook collapse (underutilized entries) to a previously overlooked geometric phenomenon: dimensional collapse, where representations compress into 4–10 effective dimensions regardless of background dimensionality. The structural explanation draws on the classical k-means/PCA connection (Ding & He, 2004; Zha et al., 2001), reframing quantization as inherently biased toward high-variance subspaces. While multi-codebook methods like RQVAE, XQ-GAN, and ImageFolder also partition latent space, they do so to serve autoregressive generation or reduce sequence length; DCVQ instead targets the low-dimensional preference itself, using parallel subspaces to scale capacity while respecting each subspace's natural operating regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that VQVAEs universally operate in a 4–10 dimensional subspace—and that the intuitive remedy of rank regularization consistently degrades performance—challenges a common assumption that higher-dimensional latents should monotonically improve expressiveness. DCVQ offers a simple, drop-in architectural fix with negligible computational overhead, making it immediately adoptable across the large ecosystem of VQ-based models (image synthesis, audio codecs, protein structure modeling). Key open questions include whether the U-shaped performance curve has a formal theoretical characterization, whether the optimal subspace dimension (d_s ≈ 8) is universal or dataset-dependent, and whether DCVQ extends to non-visual modalities where the quantization bias may interact differently with data structure.
