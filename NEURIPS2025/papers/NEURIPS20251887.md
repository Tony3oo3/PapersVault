## Problem

Knowledge graph enrichment from scientific literature is bottlenecked by the gap between rapidly growing unstructured text and structured representations: manual curation is unsustainable at scale, conventional NLP methods fail on domain-specific terminology and context-dependent relationships, and single-pass LLM extraction suffers from hallucination, schema inconsistency, and lack of conflict resolution. No existing framework systematically addresses the full pipeline from raw publication to verified, schema-aligned KG integration with built-in contradiction detection.

## Input / Output

KARMA consumes raw PDF publications (1,200 PubMed articles across genomics, proteomics, and metabolomics) alongside an existing knowledge graph with a predefined entity/relation schema. The PDFs are ingested, normalized, and segmented before any extraction occurs. The output is an augmented KG containing new entity-relation triplets, each annotated with confidence, clarity, and relevance scores, plus newly discovered entities normalized to canonical ontology identifiers (e.g., UMLS, MeSH). Triplets failing the multi-layer evaluation threshold are discarded or queued for expert review rather than integrated.

## Method

KARMA decomposes KG enrichment into a pipeline of nine specialized LLM-based agents orchestrated by a Central Controller: Ingestion, Reader, Summarizer, Entity Extraction, Relationship Extraction, Schema Alignment, Conflict Resolution, and three Evaluator sub-agents (confidence, clarity, relevance). The key architectural insight is cross-agent verification: Relationship Extraction Agents validate candidates against Schema Alignment outputs, Conflict Resolution Agents resolve contradictions via LLM-based debate between new and existing triplets, and Evaluator Agents aggregate weighted verification signals through a logistic function before threshold-based integration. Entity normalization maps textual mentions to canonical KG entities via embedding-space distance minimization, flagging novel entities beyond a distance threshold. The entire system is prompt-driven with no fine-tuning; domain adaptability is achieved through specialized prompt templates per agent and per domain, and the modular design allows swapping LLM backbones (evaluated with GLM-4, GPT-4o, and DeepSeek-v3) without redesigning the pipeline.

## Results

Across 1,200 PubMed articles spanning genomics (720), proteomics (360), and metabolomics (120), KARMA with DeepSeek-v3 identifies up to 38,230 new entities in genomics (53.1/article) while achieving 83.1% LLM-verified correctness (R_LC) and reducing conflict edges by 18.6% (R_CR = 0.186). DeepSeek-v3 leads on 17 of 24 metrics (71%) across all three domains, with the largest advantage in coverage gain (Δ_Cov), outpacing GPT-4o by 3.9× in genomics and 2.3× in metabolomics. GPT-4o achieves the peak correctness scores (R_LC = 0.880 genomics, 0.740 proteomics) but yields 41% lower connectivity gains than DeepSeek-v3, reflecting a precision-over-recall trade-off.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four configurations: a Single-Agent baseline (all triples extracted in one LLM call), and KARMA powered by GLM-4 (9B), GPT-4o, or DeepSeek-v3 (37B MoE). The multi-agent KARMA framework consistently outperforms the Single-Agent baseline across all quality indicators: in genomics, R_LC improves from 0.493 to 0.831 (DeepSeek-v3) and human evaluation from 0.320 to 0.625, while the Single-Agent baseline cannot compute confidence/clarity/relevance scores or conflict ratios at all. Among the three LLM backbones, DeepSeek-v3 dominates on coverage (Δ_Cov) and connectivity (Δ_Con) in every domain, achieving 38,230 new entities in genomics versus 9,795 for GPT-4o and 4,969 for GLM-4. GPT-4o wins on LLM-based correctness (R_LC) in genomics (0.880 vs. 0.831) and proteomics (0.740 vs. 0.772 is actually DeepSeek-v3 winning; GPT-4o wins in genomics only), but sacrifices connectivity gains by ~41% relative to DeepSeek-v3. GLM-4, despite being the smallest model, achieves the best clarity in metabolomics (M_Cla = 0.790) and the best QA coherence in proteomics (C_QA = 0.617), demonstrating domain-specific strengths from its biomedical tuning. Overall, DeepSeek-v3 achieves the best aggregate performance (17/24 metrics), while GPT-4o and GLM-4 each win niche metrics, illustrating that backbone choice involves a coverage-vs-precision trade-off that KARMA's multi-agent design partially mitigates through cross-agent verification.

## Relation to Prior Work

KARMA sits at the intersection of two active research threads: LLM-powered knowledge graph construction and multi-agent LLM orchestration. It departs from prior single-agent LLM extraction pipelines (which suffer from hallucination, schema drift, and quadratic scaling) by decomposing the enrichment task into nine specialized agents with cross-agent verification, drawing architecturally on multi-agent frameworks like AutoGen and Magentic-One. Unlike earlier neural KG construction methods (BioBERT, SapBERT) that require thousands of labeled examples per relation type and cannot generalize beyond a fixed schema, KARMA uses domain-adaptive prompting to handle open-schema extraction while retaining ontology-guided normalization. The paper explicitly positions itself against the unresolved tension it identifies: rules scale poorly, neural methods need supervision, and LLMs enable open schema but lack systematic verification—KARMA's multi-agent verification loop is its answer to that gap.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine scalability bottleneck in biomedical knowledge management, demonstrating that a multi-agent LLM pipeline can extract tens of thousands of new entities from 1,200 PubMed articles across three sub-domains with 83% LLM-verified correctness. However, the evaluation relies heavily on LLM-as-judge metrics rather than gold-standard annotations, and the authors acknowledge that domain experts must ultimately validate critical claims before clinical use. Open questions include whether the 9-agent architecture is optimal (the ablation shows the Summarizer Agent's contribution is domain-dependent), how the system handles rapidly evolving ontologies, and whether the approach generalizes beyond the three biomedical sub-domains tested. The modular design suggests practical extensibility, but the lack of a public benchmark with human-annotated ground truth limits the strength of the empirical claims.
