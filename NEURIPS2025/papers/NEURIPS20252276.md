## Problem

Most performance diagnostics for large language models rely on supervised validation or task-specific probes, leaving the geometric structure of contextual embedding spaces largely unexplored as a source of unsupervised insight. This paper asks whether structural changes in the embedding space—specifically, shifts in local intrinsic dimension—can reveal training dynamics such as grokking, overfitting, and convergence without requiring labeled validation data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a text corpus (tokenized by the model's tokenizer), a contextual language model (MLM or ALM), and three hyperparameters: a sequence sub-sample size M, a token sub-sample size N, and a local neighborhood size L. After a two-step sampling (M sequences, then N de-duplicated token vectors) and a forward pass to obtain contextual embeddings at a chosen layer, it produces a vector of N non-negative real numbers—one local TwoNN dimension estimate per token—which is then aggregated to a mean per dataset split. The estimates are computed in the ambient Euclidean space of the hidden layer (typically 768–4096 dimensions), and the method is layer-agnostic, though the final hidden layer is the default.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a localized adaptation of the TwoNN intrinsic dimension estimator (Facco et al., 2017): rather than computing a single global dimension for the entire point cloud, the paper computes the TwoNN estimate within the L-nearest-neighbor neighborhood of each token embedding, yielding a per-token local intrinsic dimension (LID). This reveals that contextual embedding spaces are not confined to a single manifold of uniform dimensionality but consist of multiple regions with varying local geometry. The mean of these local estimates across a dataset split serves as a stable, unsupervised diagnostic signal: sustained drops in mean LID predict improved generalization (as in grokking on modular arithmetic), an initial drop followed by a rise signals overfitting (as in emotion recognition), and stabilization of the mean LID indicates exhaustion of training capacity (as in dialogue state tracking). The approach requires no labels, operates on the training data alone, and is applicable across model architectures (RoBERTa, GPT-2, BERT, Llama, Phi) and training paradigms (masked LM, causal LM, LoRA fine-tuning).

## Results

Across four experimental settings, the mean local intrinsic dimension (LID) of contextual token embeddings serves as a reliable unsupervised diagnostic signal. In fine-tuning experiments, the standardized mean difference in LID between base and fine-tuned RoBERTa is 1.19 on the fine-tuning dataset (MultiWOZ) but only 0.08 (Reddit) and 0.1 (Wikipedia) on out-of-distribution corpora, confirming dataset-specific geometric compression. In the dialogue state tracking task (TripPy-R on MultiWOZ), the Spearman rank correlation between mean LID and joint-goal accuracy reaches −0.982 (train), −0.958 (val), and −0.905 (test), and LID stabilization coincides with JGA convergence around 25,000 batches. In the grokking experiment (addition mod 197), a drop in training-set mean LID precedes and predicts the onset of validation accuracy gains, with negative Spearman correlations for grokking runs (≥20% training data) versus positive correlations (0.880/0.922) for non-grokking runs. In the emotion recognition task (EmoWOZ), mean LID drops from ≈9.94 to ≈7.25 after one epoch then rises to ≈8, tracking overfitting with Spearman correlations of −0.952 (train loss), +0.952 (val loss), and +0.976 (test loss).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing dimension-estimation methods or task-specific baselines; instead, it positions the mean LID as an alternative to standard supervised metrics (loss, accuracy, F1) for monitoring training dynamics. The most direct comparison is with the differentiable validation loss in the dialogue state tracking task (Section 4.3): the validation loss reaches its minimum after 7,500 batches, prematurely suggesting convergence, while the mean LID continues decreasing and only stabilizes near 25,000 batches—matching the true JGA convergence point. In the grokking experiment, training accuracy and loss alone cannot distinguish which hyperparameter configurations will eventually generalize; the mean LID drop on the training set is the sole early indicator of upcoming validation gains. In the overfitting experiment (EmoWOZ), the validation loss and weighted F1 are tightly aligned and serve as valid stopping signals, but the macro F1 continues improving after weighted F1 declines; the LID trajectory (drop then rise) captures this trade-off between majority and minority class performance that the loss curve alone obscures. Overall, the LID signal is shown to be complementary to, and in several cases more informative than, standard supervised metrics for detecting convergence, overfitting, and generalization transitions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper localizes the TwoNN intrinsic dimension estimator (Facco et al., 2017) to per-neighborhood estimates and applies it to contextual token embeddings across entire datasets, rather than to single sequences, parameter spaces, or non-contextual embedding layers as in prior work (Tulchinskii et al., 2023; Aghajanyan et al., 2021; Viswanathan et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2025a). It departs from topology-based model analyses (Kushnareva et al., 2021; Durrani et al., 2022) by working at the token level with an intrinsic, model-agnostic measure rather than sequence-level topological descriptors tied to a fixed corpus. The key conceptual shift is repurposing a geometric descriptor—previously used for static characterization of embedding spaces—as a dynamic, unsupervised training diagnostic signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practitioners with a label-free geometric signal for monitoring LLM training: drops in mean local intrinsic dimension predict generalization gains, while rises flag overfitting or memorization, all computable from training data alone. This is particularly valuable in low-resource or label-scarce settings where validation sets are limited. Key open questions include establishing a causal (rather than correlational) link between LID reduction and generalization, developing differentiable surrogates for use as training objectives, and understanding whether the observed 'sweet spot' in LID generalizes across architectures and task types. The computational cost of neighborhood construction and the reliance on Poisson-point-process assumptions of TwoNN remain practical limitations.
