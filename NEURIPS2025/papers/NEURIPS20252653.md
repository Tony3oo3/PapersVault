## Problem

Existing LLM distillation methods require the teacher and student to share an equivalent tokenizer, severely restricting the set of usable teacher-student pairs. Prior cross-tokenizer approaches (DSKD, MinED, ULD) rely on heuristics to map teacher signals into the student's vocabulary and can only operate in a hybrid distillation setup, never enabling pure distillation across fundamentally different tokenizers such as subword-to-byte.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a text sequence along with a frozen teacher model (with its tokenizer) and a trainable student model (with a potentially different tokenizer, e.g., subword vs. byte-level). It produces a trained student model whose text-level likelihoods match the teacher's, despite the two models operating over entirely different token vocabularies and granularities. The alignment is computed on-the-fly per training example by detokenizing both token sequences and matching the text spans they encode, requiring no pre-computed token-to-token mappings.

## Method

ALM tokenizes each training example with both the teacher and student tokenizers, then identifies aligned token chunks that encode identical text spans. For each aligned chunk pair, it computes the chunk-level probability (the product of next-token probabilities over the chunk) under both models. Because the number of possible multi-token outcomes is infinite, the method approximates the f-divergence between the two chunk distributions by binarising it into a two-point comparison {p, 1−p}, which preserves the key property that the loss is minimised iff the two distributions match. An optional outcome-chunk debiasing step multiplies the chunk probability by the marginal probability of a pre-token-boundary byte following the chunk, removing tokenization bias that would otherwise make chunks incomparable. A temperature parameter τ rescales chunk probabilities to prevent longer chunks from contributing vanishingly small gradients, and an auxiliary hidden-state alignment loss (with a learned projection) enriches the sparse 32-bit-per-chunk signal. Loss components are combined via GradMag, which directly equalises per-task last-layer gradient magnitudes rather than optimising toward them.

## Results

On tokenizer transfer (Use Case 1), ALM achieves 55.1 average across 7 benchmarks for Gemma2 2B IT → Qwen2 (vs. 51.6 for SFT, 51.6 for DSKD, 53.0 for MinED) and 50.6 for Gemma2 2B IT → Byte-level (vs. 46.5 SFT, 47.4 DSKD, 47.1 MinED), the first effective subword-to-byte transfer. In large-to-small math distillation (Use Case 2), ALM+SFT reaches 70.2 on GSM8K and 36.4 on MATH (avg 53.3), outperforming SFT (51.7), DSKD (50.3), and MinED (49.8) while achieving 71% of the 8B teacher's performance at 4× fewer parameters. For zero-shot tokenizer transfer (Use Case 3), ALM improves BoolQ from 67.5 to 74.0 on Mistral v2 and from 68.0 to 74.3 on Llama3 over the SFT-trained hypernetwork baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ALM consistently outperforms both DSKD (Zhang et al., 2024b) and MinED (Wan et al., 2024) across all three use cases. Against DSKD, ALM gains 3.5 points on Gemma2→Qwen2 (55.1 vs 51.6), 3.2 points on Gemma2→Byte (50.6 vs 47.4), 7.5 points on Llama3→Qwen2 (58.6 vs 51.1), and 3.3 points on Llama3→Byte (52.0 vs 48.7); in math distillation, ALM+SFT beats DSKD by 3.0 points (53.3 vs 50.3 avg). Against MinED, ALM leads by 2.1 points on Gemma2→Qwen2 (55.1 vs 53.0), 3.5 points on Gemma2→Byte (50.6 vs 47.1), 1.3 points on Llama3→Qwen2 (58.6 vs 57.3), and 3.5 points in math distillation (53.3 vs 49.8), though it trails MinED by 0.4 points on Llama3→Byte (52.0 vs 52.4). The advantage is most pronounced in the byte-level transfer setting, where prior methods fail to consistently beat SFT while ALM achieves substantial and consistent improvements. ALM also requires no expensive pre-computation (unlike MinED's Levenshtein distance matrix) and avoids the cross-attention overhead of DSKD, making it more efficient in both FLOPs and memory.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the cross-tokenizer distillation line initiated by DSKD (Zhang et al., 2024b), MinED (Wan et al., 2024), and ULD (Boizard et al., 2025), which all rely on heuristics to map teacher signals into the student's token space and thus only work for similar tokenizers. ALM departs from these by formulating a principled binarized f-divergence over aligned token chunks, enabling pure distillation across fundamentally different tokenizers (e.g., subword → byte) for the first time. The outcome chunk debiasing mechanism builds directly on Pimentel & Meister (2024), and the zero-shot tokenizer transfer application extends the hypernetwork framework of Minixhofer et al. (2024) by replacing its next-token prediction objective with ALM.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ALM unlocks a substantially larger space of valid teacher-student pairs for distillation, enabling practical applications like rapid subword-to-byte transfer, cross-tokenizer ensembling, and improved zero-shot tokenizer transfer hypernetworks. Key limitations include the small training budget (~0.6B tokens), a single instantiation of the chunk-level divergence (binary rather than categorical), and restriction to supervised KD, leaving open whether the method scales to larger token budgets, on-policy distillation, or more complex chunk selection strategies. The finding that pure distillation outperforms hybrid distillation in self-distillation settings challenges the common assumption that anchoring to ground-truth data is always beneficial.
