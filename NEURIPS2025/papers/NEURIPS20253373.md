## Problem

Existing system identification methods (PAC-NeRF, GIC, etc.) estimate physical parameters only when objects interact with planar boundaries such as flat ground, failing in realistic scenarios involving arbitrarily shaped rigid bodies. Meanwhile, MPM simulators that accurately handle complex colliders (e.g., CPIC) are not differentiable, making them unsuitable for gradient-based parameter optimization. This paper addresses both gaps simultaneously by making complex-collider collision handling differentiable and integrating it into a system identification pipeline.

## Input / Output

The method takes multi-view video of a continuum object (segmented via SAM2 or video matting) colliding with a static rigid body, plus the collider geometry as either a mesh or a 2D Gaussian Splatting reconstruction, and initial guesses for physical parameters. It outputs optimized physical parameters (viscosity, bulk modulus, yield stress, plasticity viscosity, or friction angle depending on material type), physically plausible particle trajectories, and rendered novel views. Synthetic experiments use 11 cameras and 16-timestep rollouts; the real-world dough experiment uses 5 cameras over 3 seconds post-collision.

## Method

AS-DiffMPM extends differentiable MPM by incorporating CPIC-style particle-wise collision handling into the P2G and G2P stages. A Collision Grid (affinity flag, unsigned distance, side tag, and normal per grid node) is projected from the rigid body collider onto the MPM Eulerian grid; material particles inherit collision properties via B-spline interpolation, and particle-grid compatibility (matching side tags) determines whether a particle contributes to or receives velocity from a grid node. Incompatible particles bypass the G-OP stage entirely and instead receive velocity corrections directly during G2P (e.g., projecting velocity onto the surface for slippery contact), enabling accurate handling of sharp corners and complex surfaces that grid-level velocity assignment cannot resolve. The framework provides a generalized collider interface supporting both mesh faces and 2D Gaussian planar disks as primitives, and is plug-and-play with multiple rendering backends (DVGO, 2DGS, MDyn-3DGS), where gradients flow through the photometric loss via rendering → primitive-particle mapping → differentiable MPM to optimize physical parameters end-to-end.

## Results

In system identification from particle trajectories (Table 1), AS-DiffMPM achieves the lowest absolute error for most material-collider combinations, e.g., log₁₀(κ) = 0.22±0.24 for Non-Newtonian/Box (vs. 34.61±46.52 for RP-DiffMPM and 13.08±10.66 for GOP-DiffMPM), and θ_fric = 0.06±0.07 for Granular/Bunny (vs. 1.91±0.42 and 0.11±0.15). In system identification from visual observations (Table 2), MDyn-3DGS combined with AS-DiffMPM yields the best physical parameter estimates for Newtonian fluids (e.g., log₁₀(κ) = 22.38±17.32 for Bunny vs. 99.16±68.65 for 2DGS and 53.34±64.94 for DVGO), while 2DGS achieves the lowest error for granular media (θ_fric = 0.50±0.29 for Bunny vs. 3.33±0.62 for MDyn-3DGS). The real-world dough experiment estimates μ=57,033.1, κ=139,650.4, τ_Y=7,919.1, η=25.1, with PSNR degrading from 33.2 dB at 1 s to 25.3 dB at 3 s post-collision.

## Baselines & Comparisons

For the core collision-handling comparison (Table 1), AS-DiffMPM consistently outperforms RP-DiffMPM across nearly all material-collider pairs, with particularly large margins on Non-Newtonian materials (e.g., log₁₀(κ) drops from 34.61 to 0.22 for Box). Against GOP-DiffMPM, AS-DiffMPM wins on most Non-Newtonian and Granular cases but occasionally trails on simple Newtonian/Box settings where the planar SDF boundary is numerically stable. In the visual-observation setting (Table 2), MDyn-3DGS [8] combined with AS-DiffMPM achieves the lowest physical parameter errors for Newtonian and Non-Newtonian fluids across all three colliders (e.g., log₁₀(κ) = 22.38±17.32 for Bunny vs. 99.16±68.65 for 2DGS [11] and 53.34±64.94 for DVGO [5]), benefiting from its dynamic reconstruction prior. However, 2DGS [11] reverses the trend for granular media, achieving θ_fric = 0.50±0.29 (Bunny) and 0.36±0.13 (Armadillo) versus 3.33±0.62 and 2.89±0.78 for MDyn-3DGS, likely because its explicit point-based representation better captures the discrete particle interactions in granular flow. DVGO [5] is consistently the weakest rendering backend for physical parameter estimation, with errors roughly 2–5× higher than the best method across all fluid types.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the PAC-NeRF/GIC line of system identification (differentiable MPM + rendering for physical parameter estimation from video) by replacing the planar-only collision handling with a differentiable version of Compatible Particle-in-Cell (CPIC), which was previously available only in non-differentiable MPM simulators. The key conceptual bridge is making CPIC's particle-wise collision resolution (originally designed for accurate rigid-body coupling in production simulators) amenable to gradient-based optimization, then wrapping it in a plug-and-play interface that accepts both mesh and 2D Gaussian collider representations. The rendering side is deliberately orthogonal, interfacing with DVGO, 2DGS, and MDyn-3DGS to show the physics module is renderer-agnostic.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By enabling system identification with arbitrarily shaped rigid bodies, the paper removes a significant simplifying assumption (planar ground contact) that limited prior work to toy scenarios, moving the field closer to realistic robotics and graphics applications where objects interact with complex environments. The real-world dough experiment demonstrates practical viability but also exposes a key limitation: reconstruction quality degrades over time as Gaussians deviate from their initial positions under large deformation, pointing to the need for time-dependent Gaussian attributes. The restriction to static colliders (with moving-body coupling only shown qualitatively) and the high variance in non-Newtonian results suggest the problem remains far from solved, particularly for viscoelastic materials interacting with dynamic obstacles.
