## Problem

Dense encoding models using word embeddings or ANN features reliably predict brain responses to naturalistic stimuli, but their regression weights are fundamentally non-identifiable: when latent semantic features outnumber embedding dimensions, superposition entangles distinct concepts along correlated directions, making it impossible to determine which specific concepts a voxel actually encodes. This ambiguity precludes principled interpretation of voxel selectivity and blocks neuroscientific analyses that require disentangling individual concept representations.

## Input / Output

The method takes story transcript words (from The Moth Radio Hour) represented as 300-dimensional GloVe embeddings and fMRI BOLD responses from participants listening to naturalistic audio stories. The dense embeddings are lifted into a 1,000-dimensional sparse, non-negative space via learned concept atoms, and the model outputs predicted BOLD responses (evaluated by R²), interpretable voxel weights where each of the 1,000 dimensions corresponds to a distinct semantic concept, and concept-level cortical flatmaps. Voxel weights are postprocessed by averaging across FIR delays, normalizing to unit length, and scaling by cross-validated prediction accuracy to reflect both tuning direction and model reliability.

## Method

The Sparse Concept Encoding Model applies non-negative sparse dictionary learning to GloVe word embeddings, learning a 1,000-atom overcomplete dictionary that decomposes each word into a sparse combination of interpretable concept atoms, effectively lifting the 300-dimensional dense space into a 1,000-dimensional axis-aligned space where each dimension is an independent semantic concept. A Vector Norm Reparameterization (VNR) preprocessing step nonlinearly rescales word vector norms (expanding low-norm, high-frequency words while compressing high-norm words) to improve atom separability and semantic coherence. The sparse concept activations then replace dense embeddings as regressors in a standard voxelwise banded ridge regression encoding model with a finite impulse response hemodynamic model. The key theoretical contribution is a formal proof of non-identifiability in dense spaces (via the Gram matrix of concept directions) and a Bayesian reinterpretation showing that the dense model implicitly imposes a non-spherical Gaussian prior coupling correlated concept directions, whereas the sparse model uses an isotropic prior that decouples them. This axis-aligned structure enables direct readout of conceptual selectivity: positive weights indicate activation and negative weights indicate suppression along each concept axis.

## Results

The Sparse Concept Encoding Model matches the Dense Encoding Model in prediction accuracy (mean difference −0.00047 ± 0.00071, paired t(6) = −1.77, p = 0.13) while substantially improving interpretability. Inter-subject consistency of concept-level cortical maps rises from 0.09 ± 0.04 (Dense) to 0.26 ± 0.04 (Sparse) across seven participants. In the time/space/number case study, IoU scores of 0.34 ± 0.05 (time–space), 0.32 ± 0.07 (time–number), 0.32 ± 0.07 (space–number), and 0.18 ± 0.05 (three-way) reveal bilateral overlap in IPS and IFG, supporting the shared magnitude system hypothesis. Sparse feature vectors show near-zero pairwise correlations (|r| = 0.00 ± 0.10; 85% < 0.10) versus moderate positive correlations in the original GloVe space (0.16 ± 0.14; 87% > 0), confirming effective disentanglement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the standard Dense Encoding Model using 300-dimensional GloVe word embeddings with banded ridge regression (the Huth et al. 2016 paradigm). On prediction accuracy, the Sparse Concept Encoding Model is statistically indistinguishable from the Dense Model (mean difference −0.00047 ± 0.00071, p = 0.13 across 7 subjects), confirming that the sparse transformation preserves predictive power. On interpretability, the Sparse Model decisively outperforms: inter-subject consistency of concept-level cortical maps more than doubles (0.26 vs. 0.09 cosine similarity), and heuristic concept maps from the Dense Model are described as 'less spatially coherent, less bilaterally symmetric, and more variable across individuals' (Appendix E, Figs. 8–9). The Sparse Model also enables analyses infeasible with the Dense baseline—specifically, principled disentanglement of overlapping time/space/number representations and structured comparison of cortical tuning across 20 concept atoms—because each concept occupies an independent axis rather than a correlated direction in dense space.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-developed lines of work: (1) the encoding model paradigm for fMRI, initiated by Huth et al. (2016) and formalized in the voxelwise framework of Dupré la Tour et al. (2025), which predicts brain activity from dense word embeddings or LLM activations; and (2) sparse dictionary learning, originally proposed by Olshausen and Field (1996) for visual cortex and recently applied to word embeddings by Zhang et al. (2021) and to LLM activations in mechanistic interpretability (Bricken et al., 2023). The key conceptual departure is the paper's formal demonstration that regression weights in dense encoding models are non-identifiable due to superposition, drawing on the superposition hypothesis from the interpretability literature (Elhage et al., 2022) and the linear-structure theory of word embeddings (Arora et al., 2016, 2018). Rather than treating dense embedding axes as interpretable, the paper lifts the feature space into an overcomplete, axis-aligned basis of learned concept atoms, making voxel weights directly readable as concept selectivities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled (non-heuristic) method for generating concept-level cortical maps from encoding models, enabling analyses that were previously infeasible—such as disentangling overlapping representations of time, space, and number, and revealing structured similarity geometry among distributed conceptual maps. The non-identifiability theorem formalizes a concern that the field has informally grappled with, giving a clear mathematical reason why the standard practice of projecting voxel weights onto semantic directions is unreliable. Key limitations include reliance on static (non-contextualized) GloVe embeddings, a single fMRI dataset with only seven subjects, and heuristic labeling of concept atoms; extending the framework to contextualized LLM representations and other recording modalities (ECoG, MEG) is identified as important future work. The framework's practical impact is likely to be significant within the neuroimaging community, where interpretability of encoding models is a central bottleneck for advancing cognitive neuroscience.
