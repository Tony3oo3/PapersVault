## Problem

Standard RL algorithms struggle with combinatorial MDPs (C-MDPs) because the exponentially large action spaces render action selection intractable and prevent neural networks from exploiting combinatorial structure, leading to poor generalization and unstable learning. Existing structured approaches either require offline expert demonstrations (imitation learning) or treat the combinatorial optimizer as a black-box action mask within unstructured RL, both of which limit performance in dynamic, multi-stage settings. The paper asks whether one can train a CO-augmented actor end-to-end from collected experience alone, without expert supervision, while maintaining stability and scalability.

## Input / Output

The method takes an environment state s (which may be a 96×96×3 RGB map image, a set of tabular job/arc features, or a graph of customer requests) and produces a combinatorial action a ∈ A(s) — e.g., a path, a job permutation, a set of vehicle routes, or an item assortment. Critically, the neural network does not output the action directly; it outputs a latent score vector θ ∈ R^{d(s)}, which is then mapped to a feasible action by a problem-specific CO-layer (Dijkstra's algorithm, an LP solver, or a ranking operator). The score vector is never observed in the environment, and the action space is state-dependent and combinatorially structured, so the output is always a valid feasible solution rather than a raw vector.

## Method

SRL replaces the standard neural actor with a COAML-pipeline: a statistical model φ_w maps the state to a score vector θ, and a CO-layer f solves max_{a∈A(s)} ⟨θ, a⟩ to select a feasible action. To enable end-to-end gradient-based training through the piecewise-constant CO-layer, SRL introduces three distinct Gaussian perturbations of θ (with standard deviations σ_f, σ_b, and ε) and minimizes a Fenchel-Young loss L_Ω(θ; â) between the score vector and a softmax-weighted target action â. The target action â is constructed online by perturbing θ, passing m perturbed scores through the CO-layer, evaluating the resulting candidate actions with a critic, and computing a temperature-controlled softmax over their Q-values — eliminating the need for expert demonstrations. The paper further provides a geometric interpretation of the actor update as a sampling-based primal-dual algorithm in the dual of the moment polytope, where the dual update corresponds to minimizing the Fenchel-Young loss and the primal update is approximated by m critic evaluations rather than an intractable combinatorial optimization over the critic.

## Results

On the three dynamic environments, SRL outperforms unstructured PPO by 16% (DVSP), 77% (DAP), and 92% (GSPP), while surpassing Structured Imitation Learning (SIL) by 8% (DAP) and 78% (GSPP), even exceeding the online optimum by 79% in GSPP. On static benchmarks (WSPP, SMSP, SVSP), SRL matches SIL performance without requiring expert demonstrations. PPO exhibits up to 80× higher validation-reward variance than SRL and 40× higher than SIL, while SRL converges at a comparable rate to SIL (within ~10–150 episodes) and substantially faster than PPO (~200 episodes to plateau).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SIL (structured imitation learning), SRL matches performance on static tasks and the DVSP, but clearly wins on the DAP (+8%) and GSPP (+78%), where SIL's reliance on expert trajectories limits exploration and caps performance at the expert level. SIL converges slightly faster in DAP (~150 episodes earlier) and GSPP (~10 episodes earlier), reflecting its offline access to expert demonstrations, but at the cost of being unable to escape suboptimal states. Against PPO (unstructured RL), SRL dominates across all three dynamic environments by 16–92%, with PPO struggling to even reach greedy-policy performance. PPO also shows dramatically higher variance (up to 80× that of SRL) and slower convergence (~200 episodes vs. ~50–100 for SRL), confirming the instability of unstructured policy gradients in combinatorial action spaces. The trade-off is computational: SRL requires ~30 min per environment versus 3–12 min for SIL and 3–10 min for PPO, due to up to 61× more CO-layer invocations per update.

## Relation to Prior Work

SRL extends the COAML-pipeline framework (Parmentier 2022; Dalle et al. 2022) by replacing the imitation-learning training paradigm with an end-to-end reinforcement learning scheme. It builds directly on Fenchel-Young losses (Blondel et al. 2020; Berthet et al. 2020) for differentiating through combinatorial optimization layers, and on the primal-dual algorithm for contextual stochastic combinatorial optimization (Bouvier et al. 2025), which it adapts to the RL setting via a sampling step that avoids intractable critic-dependent optimization. The key departure from prior work is that SRL trains the CO-layer-augmented actor purely from collected experience without expert demonstrations, whereas Structured Imitation Learning (Baty et al. 2024; Jungel et al. 2024) requires offline expert solutions, and unstructured RL (PPO, SAC) treats the CO-layer as an environment component, inducing piecewise-constant rewards that destabilize gradients.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SRL addresses a practical gap in industrial decision-making: many real-world C-MDPs (routing, scheduling, assortment) lack expert demonstrations and are too combinatorially complex for unstructured RL, yet prior structured approaches either require expert data or sacrifice stability. The geometric interpretation as a sampling-based primal-dual algorithm in the dual of the moment polytope provides a unifying theoretical lens connecting structured prediction, convex optimization, and RL. Key limitations include the computational overhead (up to 61× more CO-layer calls per update than PPO), which scales with CO-layer complexity and may be prohibitive for very large or time-critical problems. Open questions include whether the approach extends to multi-agent settings, whether the sampling-based primal update can be made more efficient, and whether suitable entropy regularization schemes for combinatorial action spaces (needed for SAC-style methods) can be developed.
