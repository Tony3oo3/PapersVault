## Problem

AlphaZero's self-play is inefficient because only ~40% of positions benefit from MCTS search, runner-up decisions are discarded without exploration, and value labels from single game results exhibit high variance. Prior work on improving training efficiency either focuses on early-game exploration (missing mid-to-late game uncertainty) or on modifying the learning target, leaving the exploration of high-uncertainty positions across the full game unaddressed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20251361/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes Go board states (9x9 or 19x19) along with MCTS policy distributions and search values at each position, plus accumulated game results from prior variations. It produces policy labels (MCTS root visit-count distributions) and value labels computed as the average of all game results passing through a position across multiple branched variations, rather than a single game outcome. The branching process reuses runner-up moves already present in the initial MCTS search, avoiding additional simulations or larger networks.

## Method

The core mechanism is uncertainty-guided branching during self-play, guided by a Label Change Rate (LCR) metric defined as θ = 2w(1−w) under a Bernoulli model of game outcomes, which quantifies the probability that two independent replays from a position yield different results. A Beta-Bernoulli Bayesian framework refines the LCR estimate by combining the prior (derived from the MCTS search value) with empirical label-change observations from completed variations. At each branching step, positions exceeding an LCR threshold are weighted by their time step to favor mid-to-late game turning points, and a new variation is initiated by resampling a runner-up move from the MCTS policy at the selected position. With V=10 variations per game, the value label at each position is ensembled across all variations that pass through it, reducing label variance by a factor of n while simultaneously exploring previously overlooked decisions. This yields 58.5% sample-efficiency improvement on 9x9 Go (early training) and 47.3% on 19x19 Go (late training) over the unbranched baseline.

## Results

On 9x9 Go (trained from scratch), the proposed method reaches the baseline's performance at 40M and 80M samples using only 29.6M and 50.5M samples, yielding 34.9% and 58.5% sample efficiency improvements, respectively, with an average Elo lead of 99.3. On 19x19 Go (pretrained with 100M samples), the method matches the baseline at 5M and 10M samples with only 3.9M and 6.8M samples, giving 29.0% and 47.3% improvements and a consistent 75 Elo lead. In the design-space ablation (9x9 Go, 12M positions), V=10 variations achieves a 69% win rate over the V=1 baseline, while random branching drops to a 25.5% win rate against the proposed method. The LCR uncertainty estimator validates against empirical measurements with an RMSE of 0.023.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the original AlphaZero self-play (V=1, no branching), which the proposed uncertainty-guided branching consistently outperforms across both 9x9 and 19x19 Go. In 9x9 Go, the method requires only 59.6% of the training samples to reach equivalent strength (up to 58.5% savings in the late stage), maintaining a 99.3 Elo advantage at every sample count. In 19x19 Go, the savings are 29.0–47.3% with a 75 Elo lead, confirming the method's benefit persists even in the late training phase where the network is already strong. A secondary comparison against uniform random branching (same V=10 budget) shows the proposed LCR-guided selection is critical: random branching achieves only a 35% win rate against the baseline and a 25.5% win rate against the proposed method, demonstrating that the value ensemble alone is insufficient without targeted high-uncertainty position selection. The LCR-guided method also outperforms alternative uncertainty metrics (MinV, MinMaxP) in end-to-end training, with MinV showing similar early-stage gains but degrading toward baseline performance in later stages.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends AlphaZero's self-play loop by introducing uncertainty-guided branching, departing from the dominant line of work that improves training efficiency through modified learning targets (path consistency, contrastive learning, value target changes) or experience replay. It also contrasts with prior uncertainty-in-RL approaches that rely on neural network ensembles or world models, instead deriving a closed-form analytical metric (Label Change Rate) directly from MCTS search values. The branching strategy builds on the observation that runner-up MCTS decisions frequently become the improved choice under deeper search, and on prior work on revisiting positions (KataGo's fork option, targeted search control), but generalizes from early-game-only exploration to targeting high-uncertainty positions across the entire game via a Bayesian-refined LCR threshold.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 58.5% and 47.3% sample efficiency gains in 9x9 and 19x19 Go respectively are practically significant for anyone training AlphaZero-style systems, as self-play dominates training cost. The LCR metric offers a lightweight, analytically grounded alternative to learned uncertainty estimators, requiring no additional network inference. Key limitations include the Bernoulli assumption (restricting applicability to binary/discrete outcomes), evaluation limited to Go, and the requirement for a simulator or state-restoration mechanism. Open questions include extending LCR to continuous reward settings, applying it to multi-head value networks, and validating the approach in non-Go domains.
