## Problem

Contrastive learning methods like SimCLR and CLIP achieve strong representation quality, yet the theoretical reason why the simple principle of attracting positive pairs and repelling negatives works remains underexplored. This paper addresses that gap by casting contrastive learning as linear dimensionality reduction for Gaussian Mixture Models, where the key challenge is formalizing noisy augmentations (as opposed to idealized same-component resampling) and proving that contrastive objectives recover the Fisher-optimal subspace without requiring class labels.

## Input / Output

The method takes unlabeled data points drawn from a SharedGMM (shared-covariance Gaussian mixture) along with their augmentations, which are stochastic draws from an Augmentation-enabled Distribution biased toward the same component with probability δ. For real-data experiments, inputs are 256-dimensional CIFAR-100 image vectors or 2048-dimensional ResNet-50 features from ImageNet, where same-class images serve as augmentations. The output is a linear projection matrix A ∈ R^{d×r} whose column space is characterized as a subset of (and under conditions, equal to) the Fisher subspace, rather than a specific matrix; downstream clustering via K-Means in the projected space is used to evaluate quality via ARI and AMI.

## Method

The core technical contribution is the Augmentation-enabled Distribution (AeD), which models each augmentation as a random draw from the GMM that is biased toward the original point's component with probability δ, capturing realistic noisy augmentations. The main proof for InfoNCE proceeds in two steps: first, a reflection argument shows that any direction in the optimal projection B = AA^T outside the Fisher subspace can be reflected through a hyperplane orthogonal to all means without changing the loss, contradicting strict convexity; second, a derivative argument shows that when δ = 1, every direction within the Fisher subspace must have positive weight in B, yielding exact recovery. For SimSiam, a simplified L2-regularized variant is analyzed, recovering the Fisher subspace when the regularization coefficient ξ is below a threshold involving the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of the inter-component covariance. The multi-modal CLIP-GMM extension shows that the CLIP InfoNCE loss learns projections whose column spaces are subsets of the per-modality Fisher subspaces, filtering out noise directions that do not contribute to inter-component separation.

## Results

On ImageNet (20 classes, ResNet-50 features), InfoNCE achieves ARI of 0.982 and AMI of 0.981 at 10-dimensional projection, and ARI of 0.996 / AMI of 0.996 at 19 dimensions, consistently outperforming the fully supervised LDA baseline (ARI 0.670 / AMI 0.839 at 10 dim; ARI 0.947 / AMI 0.950 at 19 dim). On CIFAR-100 (20 classes, raw pixel features), InfoNCE and LDA are closely matched: LDA achieves slightly higher ARI (0.064 vs 0.054 at 15 dim) while InfoNCE achieves higher AMI (0.128 vs 0.124 at 15 dim). Both methods dramatically outperform PCA, SimSiam, and random projections across all mapping dimensions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The central comparison is against LDA [9], the fully supervised baseline that requires class labels. On ImageNet, InfoNCE decisively outperforms LDA at every mapping dimension (e.g., ARI 0.982 vs 0.670 at 10 dim; ARI 0.996 vs 0.947 at 19 dim), demonstrating that noisy augmentations suffice to match or exceed label-based supervision. On CIFAR-100, the two methods are nearly tied: LDA edges out InfoNCE on ARI (0.064 vs 0.054 at 15 dim) while InfoNCE leads on AMI (0.128 vs 0.124), suggesting contrastive learning preserves global class structure slightly better. Against SimSiam [7] (non-contrastive, no negatives), InfoNCE consistently wins by a wide margin on both datasets (e.g., ARI 0.982 vs 0.561 on ImageNet at 10 dim), confirming the importance of the negative/regularization term. Against PCA (SVD-based spectral methods [24]), InfoNCE is dramatically superior, particularly at low dimensions where PCA's variance-maximizing objective captures noise directions orthogonal to the Fisher subspace (e.g., ARI 0.982 vs 0.398 on ImageNet at 10 dim).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical analysis of contrastive learning initiated by Arora et al. [2] and HaoChen et al. [12], which established connections between contrastive objectives and spectral clustering, by specifically characterizing the projection subspace learned for Gaussian Mixture Models. It departs from prior work by formalizing noisy augmentations through the Augmentation-enabled Distribution (AeD), going beyond the idealized same-component sampling assumed in [3], and by benchmarking against the supervised Fisher/LDA subspace rather than merely spectral methods. The multi-modal extension to CLIP-GMM builds on the CLIP architecture [20] and the SimSiam objective [7], analyzing what joint subspace the InfoNCE loss recovers when the two modalities follow different GMMs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical justification for why contrastive learning works: even with noisy augmentations (δ < 1), InfoNCE provably recovers the Fisher-optimal subspace, matching fully supervised LDA without requiring labels. Key limitations include the restriction to linear projections and shared-covariance GMMs, the unproven gap for 0 < δ < 1 in the single-modal case, and the weaker 'subset of Fisher subspace' guarantee in the multi-modal CLIP setting. Open questions the authors highlight include extending the analysis to non-linear projectors (the practical case) and to data distributions beyond GMMs, which would be needed to explain the strong empirical performance of contrastive methods on real images.
