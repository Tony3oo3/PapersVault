## Problem

LLM-based agents deployed in real-time environments such as high-frequency trading and competitive gaming face a fundamental latency-quality trade-off that has been largely unexplored: faster responses capture fleeting opportunities, but overly aggressive compression degrades decision quality. Existing evaluation benchmarks assume high latency tolerance, and static quantization or fixed model-size choices offer only discrete operating points that cannot adapt to the task-specific balance between speed and accuracy.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained transformer model (Qwen2.5, 1.5B–14B), a calibration dataset (Wikitext-2), and a user-specified compression ratio γ ∈ [0,1], and produces a per-layer precision assignment (FP4 or FP8 for each linear layer) that is then deployed for real-time inference. In the trading benchmark, the agent receives 1-second-interval market observations and must emit a buy/sell decision whose reward depends on both correctness and arrival time relative to competitors; in the gaming benchmark, the agent receives a game state and must produce an action within ~200 ms (the in-game frame rate) to avoid positional penalties.

## Method

FPX is an adaptive mixed-precision inference framework that selectively applies FP4 quantization to the most compression-tolerant linear layers while preserving FP8 for sensitive ones, enabling continuous fine-grained control over the latency-quality trade-off. During a one-time offline calibration on Wikitext-2, FPX computes a relative L2 error ε_l for each linear layer by comparing its FP16 and FP4 outputs; given a target compression ratio γ, it assigns FP4 to the γL layers with the smallest ε_l and FP8 to the remainder. Because transformer linear layers exhibit approximately uniform latency savings when switching from FP8 to FP4, the optimization decouples latency impact from precision assignment and focuses entirely on minimizing quality loss. This layer-level, error-guided selection replaces the coarse-grained choice of a single model size or uniform bitwidth, allowing the same model to operate at multiple points along the Pareto frontier and be tuned per task (e.g., γ=0.2 for 14B trading vs. γ=0.3 for 3B gaming).

## Results

On HFTBench, the 14B model with FPX (avg 7.2-bit) achieves the highest daily yield of 26.52% at 713 ms latency, outperforming the same model in FP8 (23.14% at 801 ms) and FP16 (17.20% at 1302 ms). On Street Fighter, the 3B model with FPX (avg 6.8-bit) attains an ELO score of 5.99 at 195 ms, surpassing 3B-FP8 (2.19 at 222 ms) and 3B-FP16 (0.25 at 349 ms). Across both benchmarks, FPX consistently finds a superior latency–reward operating point compared to any fixed-precision configuration, with the optimal compression ratio (γ) varying by task (γ=0.2 for HFTBench-14B, γ=0.3 for Street Fighter-3B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares FPX against three fixed-precision baselines—FP16, FP8 (Micikevicius et al., 2022), and FP4—at multiple model sizes (1.5B–14B Qwen2.5). On HFTBench, FPX applied to the 14B model (compressing 20% of layers to FP4) beats the FP8 baseline by 3.38 percentage points in daily yield (26.52% vs. 23.14%) while also reducing latency by 88 ms (713 vs. 801 ms), and beats FP16 by 9.32 points in yield. On Street Fighter, 3B+FPX (ELO 5.99) outperforms 3B-FP8 (ELO 2.19) by 3.80 points and 3B-FP16 (ELO 0.25) by 5.74 points, while also being faster (195 ms vs. 222 ms and 349 ms). Notably, simply reducing model size to gain latency (e.g., 7B or 1.5B) does not help: the 7B models lose money on HFTBench regardless of precision, and the fastest 1.5B-FP8 model scores negative ELO on Street Fighter due to insufficient decision quality. The key advantage of FPX over the FP8 baseline is its ability to provide a continuous, fine-grained latency–quality knob (via the compression ratio γ) rather than a single discrete operating point, enabling task-specific optimization that fixed-precision schemes cannot match.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes hardware-supported mixed-precision quantization (FP8/FP4) from a throughput-optimization context into a fine-grained latency-control mechanism for real-time agent decision-making. Prior quantization work (SVDQuant, GPTQ, mixed-precision schemes by Tang et al. and Pandey et al.) targets static accuracy preservation or serving throughput, while LLM agent research (ReAct, Reflexion, FinMem, FinAgents) assumes high latency tolerance. The paper carves new ground by formulating the latency-quality trade-off as an explicit optimization objective in dynamic environments and introducing the first benchmarks (HFTBench, StreetFighter) where reward is a joint function of decision quality and response speed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The non-monotonic latency-reward relationship—where performance peaks at an intermediate operating point and degrades in both directions—challenges the common assumption that either 'faster is always better' or 'bigger is always better' for deployed agents. The task-specific nature of the optimal trade-off (γ=0.2 for 14B trading vs. γ=0.3 for 3B gaming) implies that one-size-fits-all quantization or model selection is suboptimal for real-time deployments. Key limitations include evaluation on a single model family (Qwen2.5), a single GPU (RTX 5090), and small-scale experiments (40 game matches, 2 stocks on 1 day), leaving open whether findings generalize across architectures, hardware, and more complex environments. Token-level or sub-layer precision control, broader model evaluation, and integration with serving systems are natural next steps.
