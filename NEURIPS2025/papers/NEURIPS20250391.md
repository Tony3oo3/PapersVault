## Problem

Sparse Autoencoders are widely used to extract interpretable concepts from neural network representations, but it remains unclear whether they uncover all concepts a model relies on or are inherently biased toward specific concept geometries. Different SAE architectures (ReLU, TopK, JumpReLU) achieve similar reconstruction fidelity yet discover qualitatively different concepts, and recent negative results—algorithmic instability, lack of causality—suggest a fundamental mismatch between SAE inductive biases and the true structure of concepts in model activations. No prior work provides a unified theoretical account of what each SAE architecture implicitly assumes about concept organization, nor a principled method for designing SAEs that match the actual geometry of the data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes activation vectors from model residual streams: for language experiments, 128-dimensional activations from the middle layer of a 2-layer nanoGPT trained on English PCFG strings (max 128 tokens); for vision experiments, 261 tokens per image (spatial, CLS, and register tokens) from DINOv2-base on Imagenette (10 classes, 1.5k images each, ~200M total tokens). The output is an overcomplete set of sparse latent codes (128–512 latents depending on experiment) and corresponding dictionary atoms/prototypes that decompose the input representations, with each latent ideally corresponding to a single interpretable concept. SpaDE additionally produces a distance-weighted sparse code where the number of active latents adapts per input, unlike fixed-sparsity alternatives.

## Method

The paper formulates all SAEs as solutions to a bilevel optimization problem where the encoder nonlinearity is an orthogonal projection onto an architecture-specific constraint set (positive orthant for ReLU, k-sparse subspaces for TopK, hypercube corners for JumpReLU), establishing a duality between the SAE's receptive field geometry and its implicit assumptions about concept structure. Building on this, SpaDE (Sparsemax Distance Encoder) replaces the linear encoder transform with squared Euclidean distances to learned prototypes and projects onto the probability simplex via Sparsemax, yielding adaptive sparsity (different concepts activate different numbers of latents) and nonlinear receptive fields shaped by all prototype locations. The outer optimization uses K-Deep Simplex with a distance-weighted L1 regularizer that encourages active prototypes to move closer to data, increasing representation sparsity. The inner optimization is a one-sided sparsity-regularized optimal transport problem. This design explicitly accommodates two properties of real-world concepts—nonlinear separability and heterogeneous intrinsic dimensionality—that ReLU/JumpReLU (requiring linear separability) and TopK (requiring uniform dimensionality) cannot capture.

## Results

On the 2D separability experiment, SpaDE achieves F1 = 1.0 for both linearly and nonlinearly separable concepts, while ReLU and JumpReLU are bounded at F1 ≤ 0.5 for the nonlinearly separable concept. In the 128D heterogeneity experiment, TopK only achieves <20% normalized MSE when its fixed sparsity k exceeds the intrinsic dimension of each concept (e.g., k > 6 for d=6, k > 126 for d=126), whereas SpaDE tracks the intrinsic dimension nearly perfectly along the y=x curve. On the formal-language (PCFG) and DINOv2/Imagenette experiments, SpaDE attains a perfect F1 = 1.0 for its most monosemantic latents across all classes/parts-of-speech, while ReLU and JumpReLU show class-dependent F1 peaks and TopK requires different K values per concept.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ReLU SAE [Bricken et al., 2023], SpaDE decisively wins on nonlinearly separable concepts: ReLU's half-space receptive fields cap F1 at 0.5 for nonlinear concepts, while SpaDE reaches F1 = 1.0; ReLU also exhibits significant cross-concept latent co-occurrence that SpaDE eliminates. JumpReLU SAE [Rajamanoharan et al., 2024] shares the same half-space receptive field structure as ReLU and thus shows identical failure modes on nonlinear separability, with SpaDE again achieving perfect F1 where JumpReLU does not. Against TopK SAE [Gao et al., 2024], SpaDE wins on concept heterogeneity: TopK's fixed sparsity k forces uniform latent activation across all concepts, yielding >20% normalized MSE whenever k < concept dimensionality, whereas SpaDE's sparsemax projection onto the probability simplex adapts sparsity per concept (e.g., 6 active latents for a 6-D concept, 126 for a 126-D concept). On the DINOv2/Imagenette vision benchmark, SpaDE identifies interpretable fine-grained concepts (foreground/background, object parts like hands, fins, windows) that the other three SAEs fail to isolate, and shows the lowest latent co-occurrence across classes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the sparse autoencoder (SAE) interpretability literature—ReLU SAE [12], TopK SAE [13], and JumpReLU SAE [14]—by recasting all of them as solutions to a unified bilevel optimization problem where the encoder nonlinearity is an orthogonal projection onto an architecture-specific constraint set. It draws on sparsemax [44] and K-Deep Simplex [45] to construct SpaDE, a new SAE whose encoder projects onto the probability simplex using Euclidean distances to learned prototypes. Conceptually, the paper departs from prior SAE work by making explicit the implicit data assumptions (linear separability, uniform concept dimensionality) that each architecture bakes in, and by connecting this to the receptive-field formalism from neuroscience and the identifiability results from disentangled representation learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled explanation for a well-known but poorly understood phenomenon in the interpretability community: SAEs trained on identical data with different architectures (or even different seeds) recover qualitatively different and sometimes contradictory concepts. By identifying nonlinear separability and concept heterogeneity as the two key data properties that existing SAEs fail to accommodate, it offers a concrete design criterion for future SAEs and reframes the field's search for a 'universal' SAE as a geometry-matching problem. Open questions remain: SpaDE assumes Euclidean-distance separability of concepts, the experiments are limited to synthetic clusters, a small formal-language transformer, and a 10-class ImageNet subset, and the behavior on overlapping (non-mutually-exclusive) concepts is left for future work. Whether the framework extends to transcoders or scales to frontier LLMs is untested.
