## Problem

Despite strong aggregate metrics, mainstream time series forecasting models exhibit a long-tail distribution of per-instance errors, where a subset of instances—driven by distribution shifts, missing data, or rare numerical patterns—suffers significantly degraded forecasts. No prior work addresses this instance-level failure mode in a model-agnostic, post-processing manner that can be plugged into arbitrary forecasting architectures.

## Input / Output

PIR consumes the intermediate forecasts (N×L_out) produced by any pretrained backbone model, along with the original multivariate input series, available exogenous variables (timestamps or textual descriptions), and a retrieval database constructed exclusively from training-set input-target pairs to prevent data leakage. It outputs a revised forecast via a residual correction (y_pred = ȳ + α·y_local + β·y_global) and a per-instance uncertainty scalar δ that gates the revision strength. The retrieval database can be extended to multi-source datasets since it depends only on raw time series.

## Method

PIR is a model-agnostic post-processing plugin with three coupled components trained end-to-end under a multi-task objective L = L_pr + λ·L_ue. First, a two-layer FC network with channel embeddings estimates per-instance uncertainty by regressing the backbone's MSE, serving as a data-driven proxy that captures both data and model uncertainty without requiring token-level distributions. Second, the local revision module projects covariate forecasts and exogenous variables into a shared hidden space and processes them through a Transformer to capture lead-lag dependencies and prior conditions within the local window. Third, the global revision module retrieves top-K similar historical series via instance-normalized cosine similarity and produces a weighted-sum reference forecast, explicitly targeting long-tail patterns underrepresented in training. The final prediction blends the backbone output with both revisions using learned weights α (from uncertainty) and β (from uncertainty plus retrieval similarities), ensuring the framework adaptively scales its correction based on predicted confidence.

## Results

PIR achieves an average MSE reduction of 8.99% over PatchTST, 25.87% over SparseTSF, 3.47% over iTransformer, and 2.34% over TimeMixer across 48 experimental settings (12 datasets × 4 models). The largest gains appear on traffic and solar datasets: e.g., SparseTSF on PEMS07 drops from MSE 0.352 to 0.149 (−57.7%), and PatchTST on PEMS04 drops from 0.206 to 0.140 (−32.0%). Gains are smaller for channel-dependent backbones (iTransformer, TimeMixer) that already exploit cross-channel dependencies, and a few minor degradations (≤0.7%) occur on ETTm2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PIR is evaluated as a plug-in on four mainstream backbones: PatchTST (channel-independent, patch-based), SparseTSF (channel-independent, sparse MLP), iTransformer (channel-dependent, inverted attention), and TimeMixer (channel-dependent, multiscale mixing). PIR improves all four on the majority of 48 settings, with the most dramatic gains on SparseTSF (avg. −25.87% MSE), where the weak baseline leaves substantial room for revision—e.g., PEMS07 MSE drops from 0.352 to 0.149. PatchTST benefits by an average of −8.99% MSE, with standout improvements on PEMS04 (−32.0%) and PEMS07 (−30.3%). Channel-dependent models show smaller gains (iTransformer −3.47%, TimeMixer −2.34%) because they already model cross-channel dependencies, reducing the marginal value of PIR's local revision. The only consistent degradation is on ETTm2 (≤0.7% MSE increase for PatchTST and TimeMixer), suggesting PIR's revision is most beneficial when instance-level variance is pronounced (high-dimensional traffic/solar data) rather than on low-variance transformer-load series.

## Relation to Prior Work

PIR repurposes two established ideas—retrieval-augmented forecasting (RATD, RATSF, RAFT) and exogenous-variable conditioning (TFT, TiDE, NBEATSx, TimeXer)—into a unified post-hoc, model-agnostic revision pipeline rather than integrating them into the backbone architecture. The failure-identification component draws on uncertainty estimation for regression, but sidesteps the lack of token-level distributions by directly predicting per-instance MSE as a proxy for uncertainty. Conceptually, the paper carves new ground by framing 'post-forecasting identification and revision' as a distinct problem: rather than improving the forecasting model itself, it treats the backbone's output as a potentially biased intermediate result to be corrected using contextual signals from both local (covariates, timestamps) and global (retrieved historical series) perspectives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper highlights a practically important gap—average metrics can mask systematic per-instance failures caused by long-tail numerical patterns, distribution shifts, or missing data—and offers a drop-in solution that works across channel-dependent and channel-independent architectures. The particularly large gains on SparseTSF (up to ~57% MSE reduction on PEMS07) and the PEMS traffic datasets suggest the approach is most valuable when the backbone is capacity-limited or the data is highly heterogeneous. Key limitations remain: the framework only addresses input-side instance variation (not target-side data quality issues like noise or outliers in ground truth), and the identification/revision modules use relatively simple architectures (two-layer MLP, single Transformer block), leaving room for stronger inductive biases. An open question is whether the retrieval-based global revision generalizes well when the training distribution shifts substantially from the test distribution, since the retrieval database is frozen at training time.
