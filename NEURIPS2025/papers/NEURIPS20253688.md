## Problem

Chain-of-thought monitoring is a proposed AI safety mechanism for reasoning models, but its effectiveness depends on CoTs being legible and faithful to the model's actual reasoning. While prior work identified faithfulness gaps and condensed reasoning patterns, no systematic evaluation existed for whether outcome-based RL training causes models to produce CoTs that are outright illegible—mixing nonsensical phrases, random words, and non-English characters—making monitoring unreliable precisely when it is most needed.

## Input / Output

The evaluation feeds 198 multiple-choice scientific questions from GPQA-Diamond (with answer choices removed to increase difficulty) to 14 models across six families, sampling at temperature 1 by default. Each model's full CoT is segmented into ~5000-character chunks and individually scored by GPT-4o on a 1–9 legibility scale (1 = fully legible, 9 = completely inscrutable), with Claude 3.5 Sonnet used as a cross-check autograder (Cohen's κ = 0.782). Beyond the legibility scores, the paper produces accuracy deltas from prefill-truncation experiments and per-question Pearson correlations between normalized illegibility and answer correctness across 100 resamples per question.

## Method

The paper is an empirical study with three complementary experimental designs. First, it systematically scores CoT legibility across 14 models (DeepSeek R1, R1-Zero, V3, QwQ, Qwen3-235B, Kimi K2, and five Claude variants) to isolate whether illegibility is a consequence of outcome-based RL rather than model size or base architecture. Second, prefill experiments truncate QwQ's CoT at the point where legibility drops below a threshold (score ≥ 6) and force the model to produce its final answer from only the legible prefix, measuring the resulting accuracy drop (53% decrease in fully-correct answers). Third, 100-sample resampling per question examines the within-question correlation between normalized CoT illegibility and correctness, controlling for the confound that longer CoTs are both more illegible and more likely to be correct. The analysis further decomposes illegibility by position in the CoT, question difficulty (classified via Claude 3.7 Sonnet accuracy), and sampling temperature to characterize the structure and causes of the phenomenon.

## Results

Across 14 models evaluated on GPQA-Diamond, reasoning models trained with outcome-based RL produce substantially more illegible CoTs than non-RL or Claude models: R1 scores 4.30±2.13, R1-Zero 4.59±2.84, and QwQ 3.78±1.20 (on a 1–9 scale where higher is more illegible), while all Claude models score between 1.55 and 1.84. R1 outputs CoTs with illegibility ≥5 in 45% of cases and ≥7 in 29.4% of cases. Prefill experiments on QwQ show that truncating the CoT at the point it becomes illegible (score ≥6) increases the wrong-answer rate from 54.1% to 73.6% and drops the definitely-correct rate from 24.6% to 11.5% (a 53% decrease), indicating illegible portions contribute to performance. Per-question resampling (100 samples per question) yields a near-zero mean correlation between illegibility and correctness (r = 0.061), suggesting illegible reasoning is not categorically more effective than legible reasoning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sharpest contrast is between R1/R1-Zero (4.30/4.59) and their non-RL base model V3, which shows almost no illegible reasoning, strongly implicating outcome-based RL as the cause. Claude models (1.55–1.84) are dramatically more legible than R1, R1-Zero, and QwQ despite also using outcome-based RL, suggesting training-pipeline choices (e.g., late-stage CoT SFT) can mitigate the effect. QwQ (32B, 3.78) is less illegible than R1 (671B, 4.30) despite being ~20× smaller, ruling out a simple coherence-capacity explanation. R1-Distill-Qwen-32B/14B (3.22/3.30) show modest illegibility without RL training, and Qwen3-235B (2.81) is lower still, consistent with its two-stage SFT on curated CoT data. Kimi K2 (1.24–1.48) is the most legible of all models tested, though it was not trained to produce explicit reasoning traces, making it a less direct comparison. Overall, the paper's finding is that within the same training paradigm, larger models tend to produce more illegible CoTs, and that the illegibility gap between RL-trained and non-RL models is the dominant factor.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CoT faithfulness and monitoring literature (Turpin et al. 2023, Chen et al. 2025, Korbak et al. 2025) by examining a distinct dimension: not whether reasoning is faithful, but whether it is comprehensible at all. It builds on scattered qualitative observations of illegible or condensed reasoning in R1-Zero (DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025), OpenAI o1/o3 (Baker et al. 2025, Schoen et al. 2025), and GPT-5 (METR 2025), but is the first to systematically quantify legibility across 14 models and connect it to performance via prefill and resampling experiments. The discussion draws on the vestigial reasoning hypothesis (Biddulph 2025) and heavy-tailed token importance findings (Wang et al. 2025) to propose a mechanistic account for why outcome-based RL produces illegible CoTs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that CoT legibility degrades with question difficulty and model size directly undermines a key AI safety assumption: that monitoring a model's reasoning trace provides reliable oversight, especially for the hardest and most consequential tasks. The near-zero correlation between illegibility and correctness, combined with the 53% accuracy drop when illegible portions are removed, suggests a nuanced picture where illegible tokens are causally useful but not a superior reasoning language—pointing toward the vestigial reasoning hypothesis as the most plausible mechanism. Open questions include whether the legibility-preserving techniques used by Claude models generalize, whether white-box methods can identify which specific tokens within illegible passages are functionally important, and whether this phenomenon worsens as models tackle longer-horizon or multi-step agentic tasks beyond single-turn Q&A.
