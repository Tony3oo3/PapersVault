## Problem

Diffusion-based image restoration faces a fundamental perception-distortion trade-off: standard posterior sampling yields perceptually sharp but artifact-prone outputs, while ensemble averaging minimizes distortion at the cost of blurriness. Existing remedies typically require additional model training, specialized architectures, or external verifier networks, limiting their generality and applicability to real-world degradations. This paper asks whether inference-time ensemble guidance alone—without retraining, external verifiers, or explicit degradation models—can simultaneously improve both fidelity and perceptual quality.

## Input / Output

The method takes a degraded observation (e.g., a 128×128 low-resolution image for 4× super-resolution, or a center-masked 512×512 image for inpainting) together with a pre-trained conditional latent diffusion model, and produces a single restored 512×512 RGB image. Internally it operates on an N-particle ensemble (typically N=10) in the 64×64×8 latent space, applying steering at 1×1 spatial patches (8-dimensional vectors) to sidestep the curse of dimensionality. The final output is the ensemble member closest to the ensemble mean in latent space, decoded by the autoencoder.

## Method

KDS is a training-free, plug-and-play inference-time framework that steers an N-particle diffusion ensemble toward shared high-density modes via patch-wise kernel density estimation. At each denoising step, the model predicts the clean latent for every particle; KDS then computes a Gaussian-kernel mean-shift vector for each 1×1 patch using the corresponding patches from all N particles as the local ensemble, and updates the patch by a fraction δ_t of this shift. The refined predictions are fed back into the standard sampler (DDIM or DPM-Solver++) to compute the next noisy latent, effectively blending the original score with a consensus score derived from the ensemble. A time-dependent steering schedule (δ_t = 0 for the first 30% of steps, 0.3 thereafter) ensures stability during early high-noise phases. Because KDS operates entirely on predicted clean latents and requires no degradation model, external verifier, or retraining, it integrates as a drop-in module with any conditional LDM backbone (LDM-SR, DiffBIR, SeeSR) and any standard sampler, including those using classifier-free guidance.

## Results

On the RealSR dataset with the DiffBIR backbone, KDS (N=10) achieves PSNR 24.39, SSIM 0.669, LPIPS 0.339, DISTS 0.244, and CLIPIQA 0.692, improving over the unguided DiffBIR baseline (PSNR 23.21, LPIPS 0.370) by +1.18 dB PSNR and −0.031 LPIPS. On DIV2K with LDM-SR, KDS lifts PSNR from 22.05 to 22.37 and reduces FID from 20.89 to 20.78. For inpainting on ImageNet, KDS with DDIM improves PSNR from 21.03 to 21.35 and LPIPS from 0.140 to 0.131. All improvements are statistically significant (P < 0.05).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against HDS [66], KDS wins on 4 of 5 metrics (SSIM 0.669 vs 0.646, LPIPS 0.339 vs 0.386, DISTS 0.244 vs 0.274, CLIPIQA 0.692 vs 0.647) while HDS achieves a slightly higher PSNR (24.56 vs 24.39); the paper attributes this to HDS's unimodal Gaussian assumption being violated by multi-modal IR posteriors, causing it to sacrifice perceptual quality for distortion. Against DGS [67], KDS wins on all 5 metrics (PSNR 24.39 vs 24.05, SSIM 0.669 vs 0.651, LPIPS 0.339 vs 0.355, DISTS 0.244 vs 0.246, CLIPIQA 0.692 vs 0.687), with the gap most pronounced in LPIPS (−0.016) and SSIM (+0.018). KDS also substantially outperforms Best-of-N selection using non-reference metrics (LIQE or CLIPIQA) at the same N=10 compute budget: KDS achieves PSNR 24.39 and LPIPS 0.339 versus 23.72/0.361 for BoN(LIQE) and 23.01/0.382 for BoN(CLIPIQA), demonstrating that ensemble consensus steering is more effective than post-hoc metric-based selection. The paper further notes that combining KDS with BoN (KDS+BoN) yields PSNR 23.69 and LPIPS 0.359, still below KDS alone, confirming that KDS's in-sampling guidance is the primary driver of improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

KDS repurposes the classical Mean Shift / KDE mode-seeking algorithm (Fukunaga & Hostetler 1975; Cheng 1995) as an inference-time steering mechanism for latent diffusion samplers, applied to the predicted clean latent ensemble rather than raw data. It positions itself against two existing inference-time scaling paradigms: (i) external-verifier-guided resampling (CLIP-score, classifier-based) and (ii) SMC/likelihood-reweighting methods that require explicit degradation models. Compared to the closest mode-seeking competitors HDS (assumes unimodal Gaussian) and DGS (sharpens a single sample's marginal), KDS is non-parametric over the ensemble and makes no shape assumption on the posterior, while a patch-wise decomposition addresses the curse of dimensionality in high-dimensional latent spaces.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As a training-free, plug-and-play module that requires no external verifiers or degradation-model knowledge, KDS offers a broadly applicable lever for improving the perception-distortion trade-off across any conditional diffusion restoration pipeline. The main limitation is linear compute scaling with particle count N, and the fixed steering schedule (δ_t = 0 for t/T < 0.3) is a simple heuristic that may not be optimal for all degradation types. Open questions include adaptive ensemble sizing, extension to 3D spatio-temporal patches for video restoration, and whether the patch-wise KDE can be replaced by learned or hierarchical density estimators to reduce the particle count needed for reliable mode-seeking.
