## Problem

Existing driving world models produce high-quality short-horizon videos but degrade rapidly on long rollouts, particularly during turning maneuvers where new content must be generated. A key open question is whether this failure is inherent to continuous-space (diffusion/flow-matching) modeling or whether discrete token-based (LLM-style) approaches are fundamentally better suited for long-horizon driving prediction. The paper addresses both gaps by establishing a fair side-by-side comparison and proposing targeted trajectory-based evaluation metrics that expose shortcomings invisible to standard FVD/JEDi scores.

## Input / Output

The model consumes 5 context frames at 5 Hz from a single front camera (512×288 or 256×256 resolution, 16× spatial compression in latent space) and autoregressively generates the next frame in latent space using a sliding-window context that discards the oldest frame at each step. Optionally, steering angle and speed are injected via FiLM conditioning for ego-motion control. Outputs are decoded video frames and, via the VGGT inverse-dynamics model, estimated ego-vehicle trajectories used for trajectory-level precision/recall evaluation.

## Method

The core contribution is a hybrid factorized tokenizer with separate DINOv2-distilled semantic and detail encoders, each quantized by its own codebook, fine-tuned with a 50% probability of bypassing the VQ bottleneck so a single tokenizer serves both discrete and continuous objectives. On top of this shared tokenizer, two next-frame autoregressive world models are trained from scratch: a continuous flow-matching model (STDiT with per-frame causal attention, 30-step ODE sampling) and a discrete MaskGIT-based masked generative model (confidence-based iterative unmasking, plus a 30M-parameter U-Net refinement module to reduce flickering). The continuous model uses context dropout (50%) and context noise augmentation (50%) for robustness, while the discrete model adds a codebook-similarity KL regularizer to preserve pairwise token structure. Trained on only 280 hours of raw front-camera video (BDD100K + OpenDV) with 469M parameters, the flow-matching variant achieves state-of-the-art FVD on nuPlan, Waymo, and the newly curated nuPlan-turns benchmark, outperforming Vista, GEM, DrivingWorld, and Cosmos by a large margin on long-horizon rollouts up to 20 seconds.

## Results

On 6-second rollouts at 5 Hz, Orbis achieves FVD of 134.06 on nuPlan, 167.57 on Waymo, and 239.20 on nuPlan-turns, outperforming all baselines by a large margin (next best: Cosmos at 248.39 on nuPlan-turns). On the JEDi metric, Orbis scores 0.14 (nuPlan), 0.33 (Waymo), and 0.16 (nuPlan-turns), again leading on two of three benchmarks. On the proposed trajectory-based evaluation (nuPlan-turns), Orbis achieves the best Fréchet precision (0.47) and recall (0.56), as well as the best ADE precision (0.41) and recall (0.51), confirming superior ego-motion realism. The model uses only 469M parameters and 280 hours of training video, substantially less than competing approaches.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Orbis outperforms all four baselines on FVD across every benchmark, with the largest gaps on nuPlan (134.06 vs. 291.80 for Cosmos, the closest competitor) and Waymo (167.57 vs. 278.19). On JEDi, Orbis leads on nuPlan (0.14 vs. 0.37 for Vista) and nuPlan-turns (0.16 vs. 0.31 for GEM), but trails Cosmos slightly on Waymo (0.33 vs. 0.31). Vista and GEM, both built on fine-tuned Stable Video Diffusion, show the most severe degradation on turning events, with FVD exceeding 350 on nuPlan-turns. DrivingWorld, which is trained on nuPlan itself and receives ego-motion as an additional input, still lags Orbis by a wide margin (FVD 334.89 vs. 239.20 on nuPlan-turns; JEDi 1.50 vs. 0.16). On the proposed trajectory-based metrics, Orbis achieves the best precision and recall on both Fréchet distance and ADE, indicating more realistic and diverse ego-motion generation than any baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

Orbis builds on the driving world model lineage (Vista, GEM, DrivingWorld, GAIA-1, Cosmos) but departs from the dominant diffusion-based formulation by introducing a hybrid discrete-continuous tokenizer that enables a controlled, side-by-side comparison between flow matching (continuous) and MaskGIT (discrete) objectives under identical architectural and data conditions. The factorized semantic/detail token design draws on recent VQ-VAE extensions (DINOv2 distillation, product quantization, EQ-VAE regularization), while the world model itself follows the spatial-temporal transformer (STDiT) architecture with flow matching as the generative objective. Unlike prior driving world models that rely on BEV annotations, depth maps, or multi-camera inputs, Orbis is trained exclusively on raw front-camera video, positioning it as a more scalable and generalizable baseline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central finding—that continuous flow matching substantially outperforms discrete MaskGIT for long-horizon driving prediction—challenges the narrative (exemplified by GAIA-1) that discrete tokens are inherently better suited to long rollouts, and provides practitioners with a clear design recommendation. The dramatic efficiency gain (469M parameters, 280 hours of video vs. multi-billion-parameter models trained on thousands of hours) makes high-quality driving world modeling accessible to smaller labs. Open questions remain: why do large SVD-based pretrained video diffusion models still fail on long rollouts despite the continuous-space advantage shown here, whether the model can be effectively used for downstream planning or policy learning, and how to improve compliance with traffic rules and fine-grained content (traffic lights, signs) at scale.
