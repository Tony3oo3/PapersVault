## Problem

Autoregressive sequence models for exchangeable data have predominantly relied on one-step (i.i.d.) generation, which conflates epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty and leads to suboptimal decision-making in tasks like bandits and active learning. Additionally, recent Transformer architectures that claim to enforce exchangeability via specialized masking schemes actually only guarantee conditional permutation invariance, a strictly weaker property that does not ensure the conditionally identically distributed (c.i.d.) property required for valid probabilistic inference.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20255165/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a sequence of observed (x, y) context pairs and a target input x, producing a Gaussian predictive distribution N(μ, σ²) for the next observation. In multi-step inference, the model autoregressively generates an entire future sequence by conditioning each new sample on all previously generated samples, rather than sampling i.i.d. from a single-step posterior. In the bandit setting, x is fixed to 0 (no covariate context); in active learning, x is drawn from a pool of 100 non-overlapping clusters with heteroscedastic noise.

## Method

The paper makes two core contributions. First, it proves (Theorem 2) that one-step inference loses mutual information equal to Σ I(y_i; y_{t+1:i-1} | y_{1:t}) relative to multi-step inference, and shows (Theorem 3) that Thompson sampling with one-step predictions incurs O(T) Bayesian regret in certain bandit scenarios, whereas multi-step generation achieves the optimal O(√T) rate. Second, it formalizes conditional permutation invariance (Property 1) as the actual guarantee of existing exchangeable-sequence masking schemes (Müller et al., Nguyen & Grover, Ye & Namkoong) and constructs a counterexample (Example 3) showing Property 1 does not imply the c.i.d. property (Property 2), hence full exchangeability is not achieved. Empirically, the C-permutation-invariant architecture (where all context points attend to each other) is shown to underperform standard causal masking by ~10% out-of-horizon, require up to 20% more training data, and incur O(T³) inference cost versus O(T²) with KV caching for the causal variant.

## Results

For inference strategy, multi-step autoregressive generation achieves up to 60% lower Bayesian regret than one-step inference in two-armed bandit settings (Thompson sampling, T=100 steps, 1000 runs) and requires approximately 10× fewer labeled samples to reach the same predictive performance in active learning (uncertainty sampling, 50 steps, 50000 runs). For architecture, the standard causal masking outperforms the conditionally permutation-invariant (C-permutation) masking by ~10% on out-of-training-horizon multi-step log-loss and demonstrates up to 20% better training/data efficiency, while also reducing inference compute from O(T³) to O(T²) via KV caching. In-training horizon performance is comparable between the two architectures, and the C-permutation variant requires 17 hours vs. 4 hours of A100 training time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two axes: inference strategy (one-step vs. multi-step) and architecture (C-permutation invariant masking from Müller et al. [22], Nguyen & Grover [23], Ye & Namkoong [30] vs. standard causal masking). On the inference axis, multi-step generation decisively wins: it reduces bandit regret by up to 60% and cuts active learning sample complexity by ~10×, confirming the theoretical information-loss bound (Theorem 2). On the architecture axis, the standard causal masking outperforms the C-permutation invariant masking on out-of-training-horizon performance (~10% lower log-loss) and training/data efficiency (up to 20% better), while also enabling O(T²) inference via KV caching versus O(T³) for the C-permutation variant. In-training horizon performance is comparable, but the C-permutation architecture's additional computational overhead (17 hr vs. 4 hr training on A100) and inability to leverage KV caching make it strictly worse in practice. The paper's key finding is that the masking schemes proposed by Müller et al. [22], Nguyen & Grover [23], and Ye & Namkoong [30]—which were designed to enforce exchangeability—actually only guarantee the weaker conditional permutation invariance (Property 1) and fail to satisfy the c.i.d. property (Property 2), yet they still underperform the simpler causal baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the line of Transformer architectures for exchangeable sequence modeling initiated by Müller et al. (ICLR 2022) and extended by Nguyen & Grover (ICML 2022) and Ye & Namkoong (2024), which introduced masking schemes intended to enforce exchangeability. The key departure is twofold: (1) it formally demonstrates that these masking schemes only achieve conditional permutation invariance (Property 1) rather than full exchangeability, which additionally requires the c.i.d./martingale property (Property 2); and (2) it shifts the inferential focus from the one-step predictive distributions emphasized in prior work to multi-step autoregressive generation, which is necessary for distinguishing epistemic from aleatoric uncertainty in decision-making contexts. The theoretical grounding draws on De Finetti's predictive view of Bayesian inference and the Bayesian statistics literature on martingale posteriors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper corrects a widespread misconception in the exchangeable sequence modeling literature: the masking schemes in Müller et al., Nguyen & Grover, and Ye & Namkoong do not guarantee exchangeability, and empirically perform worse than standard causal masking while incurring higher inference cost (O(T³) vs O(T²) with KV caching). For practitioners building decision-making systems (bandits, active learning) on top of sequence models, the multi-step vs. one-step distinction is critical—Theorem 3 shows one-step inference can degrade Thompson sampling from O(√T) to O(T) regret. A key open question the paper explicitly flags is the absence of any Transformer architecture that simultaneously achieves full exchangeability and computational efficiency for multi-step inference, leaving the architectural design space for exchangeable sequence modeling largely unresolved.
