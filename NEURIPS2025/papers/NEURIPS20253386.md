## Problem

Dynamic coreset selection methods for deep learning suffer from a previously underexplored issue: the representativeness of the selected subset degrades over time as the model overfits to the stale coreset, causing the subset gradient to diverge from the full-data gradient. Existing remedies either update at fixed intervals regardless of quality or rely on expensive Hessian-based checks (e.g., CREST), undermining the core efficiency advantage of coreset methods. The paper asks whether a lightweight, scalable signal can detect representativeness loss and trigger timely reselection without full-batch or second-order computations.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a standard RGB image training set (CIFAR-10/100 at 32×32, ImageNet at 224×224), the current model parameters at each check epoch, and a coreset size budget (5%, 10%, or 20% of the full dataset). It outputs a weighted coreset subset (with per-sample non-negative weights from the OMP solver) used for one epoch of BatchSGD, plus the updated model parameters. Unusually, the selection trigger is not a fixed schedule but a runtime-computed SNR statistic over only the coreset's per-sample gradients, which costs roughly one order of magnitude less than the full-dataset gradient it approximates (≈1.45 s vs. ≈14.39 s on CIFAR-10).

## Method

ERACS introduces the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) of coreset gradients—defined as the ratio of the mean coreset gradient norm to its standard deviation—as a scalable surrogate for the intractable full-data gradient discrepancy. A concentration inequality (Theorem 1) shows that under sub-Gaussian gradient assumptions, the relative gradient approximation error is upper-bounded by O(1/(√m · SNR)), so a declining SNR certifies representativeness loss. A linear-regression analysis further shows that as the model overfits the coreset, the mean gradient vanishes while the variance floor from irreducible noise persists, driving SNR toward zero. At runtime, ERACS computes the coreset SNR every C epochs; if it falls below a threshold τ_snr, the GradMatch OMP solver is invoked to reselect a new weighted subset, otherwise training continues with the current coreset. This design avoids both Hessian evaluations (as in CREST) and full-batch gradient computations, keeping per-check overhead minimal while maintaining tight gradient alignment throughout training.

## Results

Under a 10% data budget with ResNet-18, ERACS achieves 91.37% test accuracy on CIFAR-10, 69.67% on CIFAR-100, and 63.91% on ImageNet, consistently outperforming all gradient-based coreset baselines. The largest gain is on CIFAR-100, where ERACS improves over GradMatch by 5.4% (69.67% vs. 64.26%) and over CRAIG by over 14%. On ImageNet, ERACS surpasses GradMatch by 4.75% and CREST by 2.27%. ERACS also maintains a 7.4× training speedup over full-data training on CIFAR-100 while preserving over 0.9 relative accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ERACS outperforms all four gradient-based coreset baselines (CRAIG, GLISTER, GradMatch, CREST) across every dataset and budget setting. Against the closest competitor GradMatch, ERACS gains 1.96% on CIFAR-10 (10%), 5.41% on CIFAR-100 (10%), and 4.75% on ImageNet (10%), with the advantage growing as the task becomes harder. CREST, which uses Hessian-based curvature estimation for reselection, is the second-strongest baseline but still trails ERACS by 1.57% on CIFAR-100 and 2.27% on ImageNet at 10% budget, while incurring substantially higher computational cost. CRAIG and GLISTER are significantly weaker, with GLISTER underperforming even random sampling on CIFAR-100 (43.94% vs. 63.17% at 10% budget), suggesting that bilevel optimization and submodular selection struggle with the non-stationary dynamics of deep training. ERACS achieves these gains with negligible additional overhead, matching the training speed of GradMatch and CREST while maintaining tighter gradient alignment throughout training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on GradMatch's gradient-matching objective and OMP solver as the underlying selection mechanism, but introduces a fundamentally different layer of control: a self-awareness mechanism that monitors coreset quality during training. While prior dynamic coreset methods (CRAIG, GLISTER, CREST) focus on designing more accurate selection criteria or update schedules, this work argues that the critical missing piece is the ability to detect when the current coreset has become stale. The closest prior work, CREST, addresses representativeness degradation via Hessian-preconditioned gradient checks, but at prohibitive computational cost; ERACS replaces this with a lightweight SNR-based surrogate grounded in a concentration inequality linking SNR to gradient approximation error.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of coreset representativeness degradation as a distinct, measurable phenomenon that plagues existing gradient-matching methods is a useful diagnostic for the field, and the SNR-based trigger offers a practical, low-overhead solution (1.45s vs. 14.39s for full-gradient computation). However, the approach is limited to classification tasks with ResNet-18, and the authors acknowledge that SNR may not generalize to non-Gaussian gradient distributions or non-classification settings. Open questions include whether adaptive thresholding, alternative quality proxies, or extensions to streaming/continual learning settings can further improve the framework's robustness at scale.
