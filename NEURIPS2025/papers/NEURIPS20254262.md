## Problem

Despite the empirical success of data augmentation-based contrastive learning (e.g., SimCLR), a unified theoretical framework explaining why minimizing contrastive losses yields useful representations has been missing. The concept of approximate sufficient statistics, introduced for CLIP, was restricted to a single KL-divergence form and did not account for the additional challenges posed by random data augmentations in SimCLR-style training. This paper fills both gaps by generalizing approximate sufficiency to arbitrary f-divergences and establishing how augmentation-induced error interacts with sufficiency to govern downstream performance.

## Input / Output

The method consumes unlabeled samples x from a distribution P_X and a set of random transformations G (e.g., random cropping, color distortion, dropout), producing two augmented views z^(1)=g^(1)(x) and z^(2)=g^(2)(x) per sample. The encoder f maps these augmented views to a p-dimensional representation in R^p, which is then used to fit a downstream predictor h (linear regression or softmax classifier). A key structural detail is that the encoder is trained exclusively on augmented views but evaluated on either original samples or further-augmented samples, so the augmentation error ε_G (the change in target function or label distribution under g) enters the performance bound alongside the sufficiency term.

## Method

The core contribution is a three-part theoretical framework. First, the paper defines approximate sufficient statistics in three equivalent forms—Information Loss Sufficiency (ILS), Variational Form Sufficiency (VFS), and Conditional Bregman Sufficiency (CBS)—for general f-divergences, proving their equivalence (Lemma 1). Second, it shows that the SimCLR/InfoNCE loss is a finite-batch approximation of the KL-contrastive loss R_kl(S), so minimizing it via ERM yields an encoder whose KL-sufficiency is bounded by a generalization error (depending on the (2,∞)-covering number of the encoder class) plus an approximation error (Theorem 1). Third, it proves that downstream regression and classification risks are upper-bounded by c·(√Suff_kl(f) + ε_G) (Theorems 2–3), and extends the analysis to χ²-contrastive learning with a corresponding unbiased finite-sample estimator (Theorem 4). Concrete end-to-end guarantees are provided for linear regression with a linear encoder (Theorem 6) and topic classification with a dropout augmentation (Theorem 7).

## Results

The paper is primarily theoretical, with the main contributions being sufficiency bounds (Theorems 1, 4) and downstream performance guarantees (Theorems 2, 3, 6, 7) for contrastive-learning-trained encoders. The key theoretical result is that the downstream risk is bounded by c·(√Suff_f(f) + ε_G), where Suff_f(f) is the f-sufficiency of the encoder and ε_G is the augmentation-induced error. In the synthetic experiment (Figure 1), both KL (InfoNCE) and χ²-contrastive learning produce representations whose downstream linear regression excess risk is substantially lower than direct linear regression at small sample sizes (m = 150, 500), with the two contrastive methods achieving comparable performance to each other.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three approaches on a synthetic linear regression task: (1) direct linear regression on the raw d=100-dimensional features, (2) linear regression on representations from KL/InfoNCE contrastive learning, and (3) linear regression on representations from χ²-contrastive learning. At small downstream sample sizes (m = 150, 500), both contrastive-pretrained representations achieve substantially lower excess risk than direct linear regression, confirming that the learned low-dimensional (s=10) representations capture task-relevant information. The KL and χ² methods perform comparably to each other across all sample sizes. As m grows large, direct linear regression's excess risk converges to zero (since it has access to all d features), while the contrastive-pretrained methods converge to non-zero constants — consistent with the theoretical prediction that their residual error is governed by the non-zero sufficiency of the encoder and the augmentation error ε_G. No head-to-head comparison against other contrastive losses (e.g., SigLIP, N-pair) is provided in the main text.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the approximate sufficient statistics framework introduced by Oko et al. [28] for CLIP, generalizing it from a single KL-divergence-based form to three provably equivalent forms (information loss, variational, and conditional Bregman) and to arbitrary f-divergences. It then applies this generalized framework to data augmentation-based contrastive learning (SimCLR [5]), a setting that introduces the additional challenge of random transformations not present in CLIP's paired-modality setup. The key conceptual departure from prior theoretical work on contrastive learning is framing loss minimization as the search for near-sufficient statistics, with downstream performance governed jointly by sufficiency and augmentation-induced error.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a unified theoretical lens explaining why contrastive losses work and why different f-divergences (KL/InfoNCE vs. chi-squared) yield comparable downstream performance, which is validated by synthetic experiments. The augmentation error term ε_G identifies a fundamental limitation specific to data augmentation-based methods that is absent in CLIP-style analysis, clarifying when contrastive representations will or will not transfer well. Open questions include extending the framework to minimal sufficient statistics (removing redundancy), understanding how optimization dynamics shape the learned representation beyond the ERM idealization, and whether the theory applies to other self-supervised paradigms beyond contrastive learning.
