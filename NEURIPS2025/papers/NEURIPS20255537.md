## Problem

The state-of-the-art EMG-based typing system (emg2qwerty baseline) misrecognizes 51.8% of characters zero-shot on unseen users, with errors driven by mismatched cross-user signal statistics, fragile reliance on high-order feature dependencies, and the absence of architectural inductive biases reflecting the bilateral nature of typing. The paper asks whether simple, computationally inexpensive modifications to normalization, augmentation, and architecture can close much of this generalization gap without requiring additional training data.

## Input / Output

The model consumes bilateral wrist sEMG signals (16 electrodes per hand at 2 kHz) converted to log-power spectrograms, with the 33 frequency bins aggregated into 6 coarser log-spaced bands (Reduced Spectral Granularity). Inputs are processed in 16-second windows and normalized causally per feature via Rolling Time Normalization. The output is a typed character sequence produced by CTC decoding with backspace-aware beam search (beam width 50) re-ranked by a 6-gram character language model, yielding a sequence of keystrokes including backspace corrections.

## Method

SplashNet combines three targeted modifications with a bilateral architecture. First, Rolling Time Normalization (RTN) replaces batch normalization with a causal, per-feature z-score that updates mean and variance online via running sums, aligning input distributions across users without calibration. Second, Aggressive Channel Masking (ACM) zeros out at least half of the 16 electrodes in most training batches and removes broad spectral chunks from the rest, forcing the network to rely on small, cross-user-consistent feature subsets. Third, the Split-and-Share architecture processes left- and right-hand signals through separate but weight-shared encoder streams (Rotation-Invariant MLP + TDSConv stack), converging only at a final linear layer with a shared vocabulary softmax, enforcing hand-specific locality while halving parameter count. The compact SplashNet-mini uses 1/4 the parameters and 0.6× the FLOPs of the baseline, reducing zero-shot CER from 51.8% to 36.4% and finetuned CER from 7.0% to 5.9%; an upscaled SplashNet variant (1/2 parameters, 1.15× FLOPs) further lowers these to 35.7% and 5.5%.

## Results

On the emg2qwerty benchmark, SplashNet reduces zero-shot character error rate (CER) from 51.78% (Sivakumar et al. baseline) to 35.67%, a 31% relative improvement, while using only half the parameters (2.58M vs. 5.29M) and 1.15× the FLOPs. After user-specific fine-tuning, CER drops from 6.95% to 5.51% (21% relative improvement). The compact SplashNet-mini variant achieves 36.41% zero-shot and 5.87% finetuned CER using only 1.38M parameters (¼ of baseline) and 0.6× the FLOPs. On the emg2pose hand-pose estimation benchmark, RTN+ACM yields marginal improvements over the baseline (7.60° vs. 7.65° mean angular error for user generalization).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Sivakumar et al. (2024) emg2qwerty baseline, SplashNet wins decisively on both evaluation protocols: zero-shot CER improves from 51.78% to 35.67% (−16.1 pp, 31% relative) and finetuned CER from 6.95% to 5.51% (−1.44 pp, 21% relative), while SplashNet-mini matches the Joint-Hand +RTN+ACM configuration (36.41% vs. 36.42%) at only ¼ the parameters and 0.6× the FLOPs. The Split-and-Share architecture is the key differentiator: the Split-only model (37.37% zero-shot) already matches the full Joint-Hand baseline at half the parameters, and weight sharing (SplashNet-mini) recovers the last fraction of accuracy without adding FLOPs. On the emg2pose hand-pose estimation benchmark (Salter et al., 2024), the comparison is essentially a tie: RTN+ACM achieves 7.5951° mean angular error versus 7.6549° for the baseline under user generalization, and 11.1243° vs. 11.2892° under stage generalization—differences that are small and not highlighted as significant by the authors, who note the architectural mismatch (learned featurizer vs. spectrogram features) limits the transferability of their methods to that setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the emg2qwerty baseline of Sivakumar et al. (14), which established the first large-scale benchmark for wrist-EMG keystroke decoding using a joint-hand CNN encoder with batch normalization and mild SpecAugment-style masking. The three proposed modifications—Rolling Time Normalization (replacing batch-norm with causal per-session z-scoring), Aggressive Channel Masking (escalating SpecAugment's masking to zero out >50% of electrodes), and a Split-and-Share bilateral encoder—each draw on established ideas from BCI normalization, data augmentation, and multi-stream weight-sharing architectures in ASR and sign language recognition, but their combination and application to EMG typing is novel. The paper explicitly positions itself as a complementary, compute-efficient alternative to the data-scaling path pursued by CTRL-Labs, arguing that principled inductive biases can close a large fraction of the generalization gap without additional users.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 31% relative zero-shot CER reduction (51.8%→35.7%) and 21% finetuned reduction (7.0%→5.5%) while cutting parameters to 1/4–1/2 of the baseline make on-device wrist-EMG inference substantially more feasible, addressing a key deployment bottleneck for AR/VR and assistive interfaces. The finding that a simple causal z-score (RTN) dramatically outperforms batch normalization for cross-user EMG generalization is a practically important and somewhat counterintuitive result that could transfer to other EMG/BCI decoding pipelines. Open questions include whether the final cross-hand merge at the output layer can be eliminated for fully independent per-wrist inference, whether the priors transfer to populations with motor or limb differences, and whether hybrid architectures with limited structured cross-hand interaction can outperform both the Split-and-Share and Joint-Hand extremes.
