## Problem

The fundamental learning-theoretic complexity of tuning continuous hyperparameters for deep neural networks is poorly understood, despite the widespread use of heuristic methods like Bayesian optimization and random search. The core difficulty is that the utility function as a function of the hyperparameter is highly volatile and defined implicitly through an optimization over model parameters, making it far less structured than the underlying parameter-dependent function and resistant to standard piecewise-structure analyses used in prior data-driven algorithm design work.

## Input / Output

The method takes a sample of complete training datasets (each problem instance is an entire task dataset, not a single example) drawn from an unknown distribution, a continuous hyperparameter interval [α_min, α_max], and a neural network whose parameter-dependent dual function f_x(α, w) admits piecewise polynomial structure with bounded-degree polynomial boundaries. It produces a learned hyperparameter value with an explicit sample complexity guarantee of the form O(√((log N + d·log(ΔM) + log(1/δ))/m)), along with pseudo-dimension bounds for the utility function class expressed in terms of the number of pieces N, boundary degree Δ, number of boundaries M, and parameter dimensionality d.

## Method

The key technical insight is that even though the dual utility function u*_x(α) = max_w f_x(α, w) is not itself piecewise polynomial, it has a bounded number of discontinuities and local extrema when f_x(α, w) is piecewise polynomial. The proof partitions the hyperparameter domain into intervals where u*_x(α) equals the pointwise maximum of f_x along a fixed set of monotonic curves (identified via Lagrange multiplier conditions on the boundary intersections), then bounds the number of local extrema along these curves using Bezout's theorem, Warren's theorem, and properties of smooth 1-manifolds from differential geometry. Bounded discontinuities and local maxima are connected to bounded oscillations of the dual function, which via prior results of Balcan et al. implies bounded pseudo-dimension and hence uniform convergence guarantees. The framework handles both piecewise constant (0-1 loss) and piecewise polynomial dual functions, extends to approximate ERM oracles with an additive ξ error term, and is instantiated for tuning activation function interpolation parameters (DARTS-style) and graph kernel parameters in graph convolutional networks.

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no experimental results or empirical benchmarks. The headline contributions are sample complexity bounds for data-driven hyperparameter tuning. The main result (Theorem 4.1) shows that when the parameter-dependent dual function is piecewise polynomial with N pieces and M boundary functions of degree Δ, the sample complexity is O(√((log N + d·log(ΔM) + log(1/δ))/m)). For the concrete application of tuning activation function interpolation (Theorem 5.1), the bound becomes O(√((L²W·log Δ + LW·log(Tpk) + log(1/δ))/m)), where L is the number of layers, W the total parameters, T the number of examples per task, p the max breakpoints, and k the total nodes. The piecewise constant case (Theorem 3.2) yields the simpler O(√(log(N/δ)/m)) bound.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper provides no empirical comparisons or baseline experiments; it is entirely theoretical. The paper positions itself against prior hyperparameter tuning methods (Bayesian optimization, random search, Hyperband) by noting that those approaches either lack formal guarantees or require strong assumptions (e.g., Hyperband assumes finitely many arms or a distribution over hyperparameters). The paper's contribution is the first learning-theoretic sample complexity guarantee for tuning a continuous hyperparameter in deep networks under a data-driven (multi-task) setting, where the utility function is defined implicitly through an optimization over network weights. The theoretical framework builds on Balcan et al. [12] for connecting oscillations to pseudo-dimension, and extends the piecewise polynomial structure analysis of Bartlett et al. [34] to the hyperparameter-dependent setting. No head-to-head empirical comparison is provided or expected for this type of theoretical contribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the data-driven algorithm design framework of Balcan et al. [12] and Gupta & Roughgarden [10] to the setting where the hyperparameter tuning process involves an optimization over model parameters w with no closed-form solution. Prior data-driven algorithm design either avoids parameters entirely (e.g., tuning clustering or branch-and-bound hyperparameters) or assumes the optimal parameter has a closed analytical form (e.g., ElasticNet tuning). The key technical departure is showing that the dual utility function u*_x(α) = max_w f_x(α,w), despite being defined via an intractable optimization, still has bounded oscillations when f_x is piecewise polynomial — a result requiring tools from algebraic geometry, differential geometry, and constrained optimization that go well beyond the piecewise polynomial/rational structure arguments used in prior work. The paper also extends the piecewise polynomial structure results of Bartlett et al. [34] from the parameter-only setting to the joint (hyperparameter, parameter) setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to provide learning-theoretic sample complexity guarantees for tuning continuous model hyperparameters in deep networks under a data-driven multi-task formulation, filling a gap the authors identify between the practical success of methods like Bayesian optimization and the absence of formal guarantees. The main limitation is the restriction to a single hyperparameter; the authors explicitly flag extending to multiple hyperparameters (α ∈ R^n) as a major open question, noting that the bounded-oscillations structure they exploit for 1D hyperparameters has no obvious high-dimensional analogue. The regularity assumptions (ELICQ, non-degeneracy) are mild but non-trivial to verify in practice, and the bounds depend on the number of pieces N and polynomial degrees, which can be large for deep networks. The framework is general enough to apply beyond neural networks to any algorithm with a tunable hyperparameter and learned parameters, potentially broadening its impact.
