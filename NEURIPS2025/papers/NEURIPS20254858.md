## Problem

Credal sets (sets of probability distributions) offer a principled way to represent epistemic uncertainty, and relative likelihood provides a theoretically grounded criterion for selecting plausible models. However, adapting this relative-likelihood-based credal construction from simple model classes (e.g., logistic regression) to complex predictors like neural networks raises unresolved practical challenges: how to approximate the α-cut of plausible models in a high-dimensional hypothesis space, how to ensure sufficient diversity among ensemble members, and how to control the coverage–efficiency trade-off of the resulting credal predictions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard supervised classification inputs: 768-dimensional text embeddings (ChaosNLI), 32×32 RGB images (CIFAR-10, QualityMRI), or MRI scans. Rather than producing a single probability vector, the output is a credal set represented as per-class lower and upper probability bounds, from which an epistemic uncertainty score (difference between max and min Shannon entropy over the set) can be derived. Notably, training uses hard labels sampled from ground-truth annotation distributions rather than soft labels, so the method does not require access to true conditional probabilities during training.

## Method

CreRL constructs credal predictions by approximating the α-cut of models whose relative likelihood (ratio to the MLE likelihood) exceeds a threshold α. An ensemble of M neural networks is trained with early stopping so that each member h_i reaches a target relative likelihood τ_i = α + i·Δτ, ensuring the ensemble spans the full range of plausible models from α up to 1. A key diversification mechanism, ToBias initialization, sets a large constant (β=100) on one output bias per member so that initial predictions sit at distinct vertices of the probability simplex, representing full epistemic uncertainty that shrinks as training proceeds. At inference, the credal set for a query x is the per-class interval [p̲(y_k|x), p̄(y_k|x)] computed across all ensemble members. The hyperparameter α directly controls the coverage–efficiency trade-off: lower α admits more models (higher coverage, lower efficiency), while higher α restricts to near-MLE models (lower coverage, higher efficiency).

## Results

On the OoD detection task (CIFAR-10 as in-distribution), CreRL with α=1.0 achieves AUROC of 0.948 (SVHN), 0.918 (Places365), 0.916 (CIFAR-100), 0.957 (Fashion-MNIST), and 0.889 (ImageNet), performing at or near the best across all five OoD datasets. On the coverage–efficiency Pareto front (Figure 4), CreRL uniquely spans the high-coverage region (up to ~0.95 on ChaosNLI) while maintaining competitive efficiency, whereas CreEns is confined to low coverage (<0.5) and CreNet collapses to near-point predictions. The α parameter provides a continuous trade-off: lowering α from 1.0 to 0.2 increases coverage but degrades OoD AUROC (e.g., SVHN drops from 0.948 to 0.582).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CreRL (α=1.0) outperforms CreBNN on all five OoD datasets by 3–5 AUROC points (e.g., 0.948 vs. 0.907 on SVHN), confirming that the relative-likelihood ensemble captures epistemic uncertainty more effectively than imprecise Bayesian neural networks. Against CreNet, CreRL wins on four of five datasets (SVHN 0.948 vs. 0.943, CIFAR-100 0.916 vs. 0.912, FMNIST 0.957 vs. 0.951, ImageNet 0.889 vs. 0.884) and ties on Places365 (0.918), reflecting that CreNet's loss function drives lower and upper probabilities to converge, yielding near-point predictions with poor coverage. Compared to CreWra, CreRL matches or exceeds it on Places365 (0.918 vs. 0.916), CIFAR-100 (0.916 vs. 0.916), and FMNIST (0.957 vs. 0.952), but trails slightly on SVHN (0.948 vs. 0.957) and ImageNet (0.889 vs. 0.890). Against CreEns₀.₀, CreRL wins on four of five datasets (Places 0.918 vs. 0.913, CIFAR-100 0.916 vs. 0.914, FMNIST 0.957 vs. 0.949, ImageNet 0.889 vs. 0.888) but loses narrowly on SVHN (0.948 vs. 0.955). On the coverage–efficiency Pareto front, CreRL is the only method that reaches the high-coverage regime (>0.8) while retaining competitive efficiency, whereas CreEns is restricted to low coverage, CreNet degenerates to a point, and CreWra/CreBNN offer only a single fixed operating point.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the relative likelihood (normalized likelihood) framework for constructing credal sets, originally developed for simple model classes like logistic regression (Senge et al., 2014; Antonucci et al., 2012a), to complex neural network predictors. The key methodological departure is the ToBias initialization strategy and the early-stopping-at-specific-likelihood-thresholds training procedure, which together approximate the α-cut of plausible models in high-dimensional hypothesis spaces. The paper also positions itself as the first systematic benchmark comparing multiple credal prediction approaches (CreWra, CreEns, CreBNN, CreNet) under a unified coverage-efficiency Pareto framework, a comparison that had not previously been conducted.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The α parameter provides an intuitive, statistically grounded knob for controlling the coverage-efficiency trade-off in credal predictions without requiring Bayesian priors or resampling, which is practically appealing for practitioners. However, the method is currently limited to neural networks, relies on interval-based credal sets that are larger than the convex hull would be, and requires access to ground-truth distributions for coverage evaluation (available only in a few datasets). Open questions include extending the framework to other model classes, quantifying how many ensemble members are needed for faithful α-cut approximation, and whether convex-hull-based sets would improve efficiency without sacrificing coverage.
