## Problem

Selecting task-relevant data from large general instruction datasets is critical for improving LLM performance on specific targets, but existing distribution alignment methods rely on data-centric embeddings (BM25, BGE, LLM2Vec) that fail to capture how the model internally processes samples. Even when using raw neuron activations as a model-centric alternative, neuron polysemanticity—where a single neuron responds to multiple unrelated concepts—produces spurious similarity between unrelated samples, undermining selection quality.

## Input / Output

The method takes a large source instruction dataset (e.g., OPENHERMES-2.5 with ~1M samples or LESS with ~270K samples), a small set of target task representative examples (M << N, typically 81–285 samples), a pretrained LLM from which activations are extracted at the penultimate layer, and a pre-trained sparse autoencoder (SAE) with sparsity parameter K=192. It produces a selected subset of k samples (e.g., 5% of the source) for instruction tuning, with intermediate outputs being high-dimensional sparse monosemantic activation embeddings (d' >> d) and per-sample generalized Jaccard similarity scores against an aggregated task prototype. Token-level activations are averaged (not summed) to prevent length bias from dominating selection.

## Method

MONA represents each sample by the neuronal activation pattern it triggers in a pretrained LLM, then applies a sparse autoencoder with TopK sparsification to disentangle polysemantic activations into sparse, monosemantic units where each dimension corresponds to a distinct semantic feature. Token-level monosemantic vectors are averaged into a sample-level embedding, and all target examples are aggregated into a single task prototype to reduce computational complexity from O(|D_tgt|·|D_src|) to O(|D_src|). A generalized Jaccard similarity metric—chosen over cosine or Euclidean because it is more reliable in high-dimensional sparse spaces—is computed between each source sample and the task prototype, and the top-k samples by similarity are selected for fine-tuning. The key differentiator is the combination of SAE-based monosemantic decomposition (eliminating polysemanticity-induced spurious similarity) with the Jaccard metric, both of which are validated through ablations showing consistent gains over raw activations and alternative similarity measures.

## Results

On LLaMA3.1-8B with 5% data selection from OPENHERMES-2.5, MONA achieves an average score of 55.75 across MMLU, GSM8K, BBH, MBPP, and GPQA, surpassing full-data fine-tuning (54.23) and the best baseline DSIR (54.74). On the LESS source dataset, MONA reaches 67.20 average (MMLU, BBH, TydiQA), again exceeding full-data tuning (67.19) and all baselines. On OLMo-7B, MONA achieves 34.71 average on LESS, outperforming the best baseline LLM2Vec (33.52) and full-data tuning (33.87). On the larger LLaMA2-13B model, MONA attains 46.65 average, beating the strongest baseline BM25 (46.18) and full-data tuning (45.64).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MONA consistently outperforms all influence-based baselines: on LLaMA3.1-8B/OPENHERMES-2.5, it beats LESS (55.75 vs. 54.70 avg) and MATES (55.75 vs. 51.90 avg), with the largest gap on GSM8K (67.93 vs. 66.87 for LESS, 54.28 for MATES). Among distribution-alignment methods, MONA surpasses the strongest baselines DSIR (55.75 vs. 54.74) and DLRDS-BGE (55.75 vs. 54.66) on OPENHERMES-2.5, and DLRDS-LLaMA3-8B (67.20 vs. 65.19) and LLM2Vec (67.20 vs. 64.07) on LESS. On OLMo-7B, MONA leads all baselines by 1.2–3.7 points in average score, with the largest margin over DLRDS-BGE (34.71 vs. 31.38 on LESS). In the data-synthesis comparison (Appendix D.4), MONA's data selection (72.60 TydiQA) dramatically outperforms GPT-4o Self-Instruct (46.99), a 25.6-point gap. Finally, MoE-based monosemantic embeddings (Appendix D.6) provide only a modest improvement over full-data tuning, trailing SAE-based MONA by a noticeable margin, confirming that SAE disentanglement is critical for effective task-specific selection.

## Relation to Prior Work

MONA builds on the sparse autoencoder (SAE) literature for monosemantic feature extraction (Huben et al. 2024, Templeton et al. 2024, Gao et al. 2025), repurposing SAEs not for interpretability analysis but as an embedding mechanism for data selection. It departs from prior distribution-alignment methods (BM25, DSIR, DLRDS, LLM2Vec) by shifting from data-centric representations (text features or separate encoder embeddings) to model-centric representations — the actual neuronal activations of the target LLM, disentangled into monosemantic units. This positions MONA as a bridge between the mechanistic interpretability community's monosemanticity tools and the data curation community's distribution-matching objectives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MONA offers a more interpretable and semantically grounded alternative to data selection, where selection decisions can be traced to specific monosemantic features rather than opaque embedding similarities. The consistent (if modest) gains across multiple models, datasets, and tasks suggest the model-centric representation captures task-relevant computation that data-centric embeddings miss. Key open questions include whether the approach extends to pre-training data selection, multimodal settings, and whether the reliance on pre-trained SAEs (currently only available for a few LLaMA variants) limits broader applicability. The finding that 10% selection underperforms 5% also raises unresolved questions about optimal selection ratios.
