## Problem

Training agents from offline data to generalize to unseen procedurally generated environments remains a significant challenge, as model-free offline RL methods consistently fail to transfer to novel task variations. While world models can generate diverse training data, naively sampling from them produces ineffective training trajectories and requires manually tuned hyperparameters like episode length. The paper addresses how to efficiently leverage world models to create useful, self-adapting training curricula that improve generalization without additional environment interaction.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a mixed offline dataset of 1M transitions per game (1/3 expert, 1/3 medium, 1/3 random trajectories) collected across 200 procedurally generated levels, with 64×64×3 RGB observations and discrete action spaces (up to 18 actions). The world model is trained on this data and then frozen; the agent trains exclusively on autoregressive imagined rollouts of randomly sampled lengths (5–22 steps) starting from initial states drawn from the dataset. The output is a trained actor-critic policy evaluated on held-out levels (201+) that were never seen during data collection or world model training.

## Method

IMAC combines a diffusion world model (2D U-Net with EDM formulation, frame-stacking observation conditioning, and adaptive group normalization for action/diffusion-time conditioning, sampled with 5 Euler denoising steps) with Prioritized Level Replay (PLR) to create an emergent curriculum over imagined environments. A separate CNN-LSTM reward/termination predictor with a 10-head ensemble provides uncertainty estimates during long-horizon imagination. The actor-critic agent (A2C with λ-returns) is trained entirely within frozen world model rollouts, where PLR prioritizes initial states by their positive TD-error scores, naturally focusing computation on the most informative scenarios. Rather than fixing or randomizing episode lengths, the prioritization mechanism discovers an emergent curriculum that progressively shifts toward longer, more challenging horizons as the agent improves, without any manual difficulty programming or environment-specific knowledge.

## Results

On the Procgen benchmark (7 environments, 200 training levels, evaluated on held-out levels 201+), IMAC achieves mean returns of 6.20 (CoinRun), 5.15 (Ninja), 5.78 (Jumper), 5.41 (Maze), 3.87 (CaveFlyer), 2.91 (Heist), and 5.61 (Miner), consistently outperforming all model-free offline RL baselines. The headline improvements over the best model-free baseline per environment range from 17% (CoinRun) to 48% (Jumper), with an average improvement of approximately 29%. In the world model ablation (Table 2), IMAC further improves over the fixed-horizon world model baseline by up to 56% on Jumper and 38% on Maze, and over the random-horizon baseline by up to 40% on Jumper.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IMAC outperforms all six baselines across all seven Procgen environments. Against the strongest model-free baselines, DT leads on Maze (4.32) and Jumper (3.84), while BC leads on CoinRun (5.31) and Miner (4.68); IMAC surpasses these by 25% on Maze (5.41 vs 4.32), 51% on Jumper (5.78 vs 3.84), 17% on CoinRun (6.20 vs 5.31), and 20% on Miner (5.61 vs 4.68). The Q-learning-based offline methods (CQL, IQL, BCQ) perform substantially worse, with CQL and IQL dropping below 2.0 on Maze, Heist, and Miner, indicating severe generalization failure on held-out levels. BCT is the closest competitor on Ninja (4.33 vs IMAC's 5.15, a 19% gap) and Heist (1.5 vs 2.91, a 94% gap). The world model ablation further shows that the PLR-based autocurriculum contributes an additional 11–41% improvement over simply using random-horizon rollouts from the same frozen diffusion world model, confirming that the prioritization mechanism—not just the world model itself—drives the generalization gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper combines two previously separate research threads: diffusion-based world models for RL (following DIAMOND/Alonso et al. and the EDM formulation) and Unsupervised Environment Design via Prioritized Level Replay (PLR, Jiang et al.). While DIAMOND demonstrated that diffusion world models improve agent performance in single Atari environments, and PLR showed that prioritizing levels by learning potential creates effective curricula in online procedural settings, no prior work applied UED principles within a frozen world model trained on offline data. The key departure is treating the world model as a procedural generator of agent experience and using PLR to select which imagined rollouts to train on, creating an 'imagined autocurriculum' that operates entirely without environment access or direct level control.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that strong generalization to unseen procedurally generated levels is achievable using only imagined trajectories from a world model trained on a limited offline dataset (200 levels), opening a path toward leveraging larger foundation world models for generalist agents. However, the approach is computationally expensive (~180 GPU days per game), relies on a fixed 2D U-Net architecture that may not scale to more complex or higher-dimensional settings, and the world model is frozen during agent training, limiting adaptability. Open questions include whether the PLR-based prioritization transfers to continuous control, whether more advanced world model architectures (3D DiT, transformer-based) benefit similarly, and how the method performs when the offline dataset is even more heterogeneous or when the world model itself is imperfect.
