## Problem

Standard alignment methods like DPO and RLHF assume a single universal reward function, but human preferences are inherently heterogeneous across user types. This paper formalizes preference heterogeneity and shows that DPO implicitly maximizes Borda count rather than the user-weighted average reward, leading to undesirable behaviors such as mediocrity promotion and sensitivity to the sampling distribution of preference data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252350/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes query-response preference pairs (x, y_w, y_l) along with a reference policy π_ref, and optionally annotator information at varying levels: anonymous labels (impossible to learn from), paired preferences from the same annotator (enabling first-order correction), or full feedback from every user type on each data point (enabling a consistent loss). The output is an aligned language model policy π that approximates the optimal policy maximizing the expected reward across user types with KL regularization. In experiments, Llama-3-8B and Qwen-2.5-7B are fine-tuned via LoRA on the HH-RLHF dataset with length-based heterogeneous rewards.

## Method

The paper establishes that only affine reward aggregation yields a well-defined alignment problem, with the average reward as the natural choice, and proves that DPO's induced reward is ordinally consistent with Borda count rather than the average reward. For minimal annotator information, it derives a first-order corrected DPO by Taylor-expanding the heterogeneous likelihood around the average reward, adding a variance correction term estimated from a joint likelihood model trained on paired preferences from the same annotator. For maximum annotator information (every user type labels every data point), it proposes a temperature-adjusted DPO loss that scales the log-policy-ratio by |U| and uses only unanimous-agreement samples, achieving consistent estimation of the optimal policy. A key impossibility result shows that no consistent direct loss can exploit disagreement samples, revealing a fundamental tension between consistency and sample efficiency in direct alignment.

## Results

On the semi-synthetic HH-RLHF benchmark with Llama-3-8B, explicitly modeling preference heterogeneity improves ordinal agreement with the ground-truth average reward from 89.6% to 93.9% for reward learning (+4.3 pp) and from 67.4% to 71.7% for direct alignment (+4.3 pp). The same trend holds with Qwen-2.5-7B, where modeling heterogeneity raises direct-alignment accuracy from 63.7% to 69.8% (+6.1 pp). In the synthetic environment, DPO's policy is ordinally consistent with Borda count (preferring δ≈0) while the optimal policy favors δ∈{−10, 10}; the first-order corrected DPO at α=1 already recovers the correct preference ordering, and the consistent loss from Prop. 6.1 largely recovers π*.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole empirical baseline is standard DPO (and its reward-learning counterpart, BT-based reward modeling) applied to an anonymous preference dataset where each sample is labeled by a randomly sampled user type. The proposed consistent loss (Prop. 6.1), which uses full annotator information from all user types, outperforms DPO by 4.3 percentage points in both reward learning and direct alignment on Llama-3-8B, and by 6.1 points on Qwen-2.5-7B. Theoretically, the paper shows DPO's induced reward is ordinally consistent with the Normalized Borda Count rather than the user-weighted expected reward, meaning DPO can promote mediocre options and is sensitive to the sampling distribution of alternatives even in the infinite-data limit. The first-order corrected DPO (Sec. 5) provides an intermediate option that requires only paired preferences from the same annotator and improves upon vanilla DPO in the synthetic setting, though it sacrifices full consistency. The consistent loss, while guaranteed to recover π*, is sample-inefficient because it discards all samples with any inter-type disagreement (Theorem 6.2 proves no consistent direct loss can use those samples).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024) to a heterogeneous-preference setting where multiple user types each have their own reward function, building directly on Siththaranjan et al.'s (2023) observation that RLHF aggregates preferences via Borda count. It formalizes the heterogeneity using the Luce-Shepard model from mathematical psychology and draws on social choice theory (Arrow's theorem, distortion results) to characterize which reward aggregations yield well-defined alignment problems. The paper departs from prior work by proving impossibility results for anonymous data, proposing a first-order Taylor-corrected DPO, and introducing a consistent (but sample-inefficient) loss for the full-information setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a fundamental tension: no sample-efficient consistent direct loss exists when preferences are heterogeneous, meaning practitioners must either accept approximate methods (first-order corrected DPO) or forgo direct optimization in favor of training individualized reward models. The finding that DPO implicitly optimizes Borda count—with its known pathologies like sensitivity to data sampling distribution and mediocrity promotion—challenges the assumption that DPO is a neutral, reward-agnostic method. Open questions include whether unsupervised methods can identify annotator types from anonymous data, what data-collection structures aid identifiability, and whether the impossibility results extend beyond the BT model.
