## Problem

When users exhibit inconsistent preferences—spending time on content that offers little long-term value—revealed preferences mislead engagement-driven algorithms, yet it remains unclear what it takes for the rational part of a user (system 2) to steer the algorithm toward her true interests. Prior work either assumes consistent preferences or models the platform as the Stackelberg leader, leaving the user-side burden of alignment unquantified in a multi-user, multi-step setting.

## Input / Output

The model operates on a finite set of user types θ, a two-item content space S = {a, b} in the special case, per-type engagement rates α_θ(s) (governing expected engagement length), reward functions r_θ(s), discount factors γ_H (user) and γ_A (algorithm), and a common-knowledge prior λ over types. The algorithm observes only binary engagement indicators (not durations), and the output is a fully characterized Stackelberg equilibrium: per-type engagement probabilities f_θ(s), the algorithm's optimal policy π* (a linear classifier on the posterior), a critical effective horizon τ_H = 1/(1−γ_H) per type, a steerable set F_θ defining feasible equilibrium strategies, and the user's undiscounted regret over T steps.

## Method

The paper models the interaction as a multi-leader, single-follower extensive-form Stackelberg game where users (system 2) commit to engagement strategies and the algorithm best-responds via Bellman updates over a binary engagement history. The key structural result (Theorem 4.1) shows the algorithm's Q-value is piecewise linear in the posterior λ, so its optimal policy reduces to a linear classifier with per-type weights h_θ that depend only on that type's own strategy. User equilibria are then characterized through steerable sets F_θ defined by linear (or bilinear, with signaling) constraints, yielding a critical horizon τ_H below which the user cannot steer the algorithm and instead gets aligned to its engagement objective. In the costly-signaling extension, users can pay an observable cost c to decouple type communication from content consumption, which expands the steerable set and reduces the required effective horizon by a factor proportional to r_θ(a)/r_θ(b) · α_θ(a)/α_θ(b) − c/r_θ(b).

## Results

The paper establishes that a type-1 user (with inconsistent preferences) achieves constant regret in equilibrium if and only if her effective horizon satisfies τ_H = 1/(1−γ_H) > r_θ(b)/(r_θ(b)−r_θ(a)), meaning she must optimize over a sufficiently long future window to steer the algorithm away from tempting content. When costly signaling (e.g., an extra click of cost c) is available, the required horizon is reduced by r_θ(a)/r_θ(b)·α_θ(a)/α_θ(b) − c/r_θ(b), a strictly positive quantity for small c. The algorithm's best response is characterized as a linear classifier on the posterior over user types, with the steerable set F_θ nonempty if and only if λ_θ ≥ α_θ(b)·m_θ^AE, where m_θ^AE is the classifier margin from other users.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or experimental comparisons. The primary internal comparison is between the no-signaling setting (Section 5) and the costly-signaling setting (Section 6). Without signaling, a type-1 user who cannot steer the algorithm (empty F_θ) must either fully disengage from tempting content (f_θ(a)=0) if sufficiently foresighted, or fully engage (f_θ(a)=1) if myopic, incurring linear regret. With costly signaling, the same user can partially engage (f_θ(a)→α_θ(a)/α_θ(b)) while paying the cost (u_θ(a)=1), decoupling type communication from reward consumption and lowering the critical horizon. The paper also contrasts its multi-leader, single-follower Stackelberg formulation with prior work where the platform leads (Haupt et al. [20], Cen et al. [21,22]), noting that placing users as leaders fundamentally changes the alignment burden analysis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the engagement-optimization framework of Kleinberg et al. [6] (system 1/system 2 split) into a multi-leader, single-follower Stackelberg game where users, rather than the platform, commit to strategies first. It departs from prior strategic-user analyses (Haupt et al. [20], Cen et al. [21,22]) that treat the platform as the Stackelberg leader and the user as the follower, instead centering the user's perspective and asking what it takes for the user to steer the algorithm. The costly-signaling mechanism draws on Hartline and Roughgarden's [18] money-burning results from mechanism design, repurposing them as a platform-design lever to reduce the user's alignment burden.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper introduces 'burden of alignment'—a critical optimization horizon—as a concrete, quantifiable measure of how hard it is for a user to align an engagement-driven algorithm, revealing that even a game leader can be effectively 'led' by the follower when foresight is insufficient. The finding that a small, observable cost (e.g., an extra click) can substantially shrink this horizon offers a practical design principle for platforms. Limitations include the two-item special case, full-information assumptions, and the absence of content creators; open questions include whether the critical-horizon structure persists in richer content spaces, with learning algorithms, or under heterogeneous user populations, and whether the costly-signaling remedy generalizes beyond the simplified setting.
