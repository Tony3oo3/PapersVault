## Problem

Existing continuous-time approximations of the Heavy-Ball (HB) momentum method, such as the rescaled gradient flow (RGF) and the O(η²) model of Ghosh et al., have discretization errors that are too large to distinguish HB from vanilla gradient descent in the continuous limit. This gap prevents a faithful continuous-time characterization of momentum-specific phenomena like implicit regularization and implicit bias, since RGF predicts HB and GD converge to similar points, contradicting observed behavior.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254476/cropped_page8_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes the discrete HB update rule parameterized by an objective function L, a constant momentum factor μ ∈ (0,1), and a step size η, and produces a piece-wise continuous first-order ODE (HB Flow, HBF) whose right-hand side changes at each iteration boundary t_k = kη. The counter terms γ_k are expressed as a power series in η whose coefficients are determined recursively; truncating the series at order α−2 yields a continuous model with discretization error O(η^α) for any user-chosen α ≥ 1. As a downstream output, the paper also derives an explicit implicit-bias potential function for diagonal linear networks that depends on the time-evolved initialization scale κ(∞) and an additional correction term φ_j(∞) absent from the gradient-flow case.

## Method

The core construction is a piece-wise continuous ODE β̇ = −G_k(β) − η γ_k(β) on each interval [t_k, t_{k+1}), where G_k = μ G_{k−1} + ∇L mirrors the discrete momentum recursion and γ_k is a counter term designed to cancel higher-order discretization error. The key technical step is deriving a functional integral equation (I_k⁺ + μ I_k⁻ = γ_k − μ γ_{k−1}) by subtracting the discrete HB update from a Taylor expansion of the ODE solution, then solving it by expanding both sides in powers of η and matching coefficients order-by-order. The coefficients γ_k^{(σ)} are computed recursively via differential operators L_β^{(j,σ)} = γ_j^{(σ−1)}·∇ applied to previous-order terms, with the full expression involving sums over combinatorial index sets S_{m,σ}. A critical composite quantity ψ = η/(1−μ)² governs the convergence of the series, requiring η ≪ (1−μ)² for the approximation to remain valid. The paper explicitly works out HBF for α = 2 (recovering Ghosh et al.'s result) and α = 3 (a new third-order model involving third derivatives of L), and applies the framework to reveal that HB implicitly regularizes directional smoothness and shifts the implicit bias of diagonal linear networks toward sparser solutions compared to gradient flow.

## Results

The paper's main contribution is theoretical: Theorem 2.1 establishes that the proposed HB Flow (HBF) achieves a discretization error of O(η^α) for any user-specified order α ≥ 1, where η is the step size. Concretely, HBF with α=2 (Eq. 19) recovers the O(η²) result of Ghosh et al. (2023), while HBF with α=3 (Eq. 22) is a new result involving third-order derivatives of the loss. Numerically, in a 2D toy model (Fig. 2), the HBF α=3 trajectory tracks the discrete HB dynamics more closely than both RGF and HBF α=2, and its discretization error norm ‖ε_k‖² is the lowest after several steps. On CIFAR-10 (MLP, 5k subset, full-batch, η=0.1, μ=0.9), HB exhibits smaller directional smoothness D^HB < D^GD ≈ 2/η = 20, confirming the implicit regularization predicted by HBF.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares HBF against two prior continuous-time approximations of HB. Against RGF (Kovachki & Stuart, 2020), which has only O(η) discretization error, HBF strictly improves: in the 2D trajectory experiment (Fig. 2a), RGF closely follows GD and fails to capture HB's distinct convergence point, while HBF α=2 and α=3 track HB much more faithfully. Against Ghosh et al. (2023), which achieves O(η²) error, HBF matches exactly at α=2 (Eq. 19 reproduces their coefficient (1+μ)/(1−μ)³) and extends the analysis to arbitrary order α, with the α=3 case (Eq. 22) being entirely new. The discretization error plot (Fig. 2b) confirms that HBF α=3 achieves the lowest ‖ε_k‖² after a few steps, outperforming both RGF and HBF α=2. The paper also contrasts with GD continuous approximations (Barrett & Dherin 2022; Miyagawa 2023; Rosca et al. 2023), showing that setting μ=0 in HBF recovers the GD results, while the HB-specific terms (involving (1+μ)/(1−μ)³ and higher powers) capture momentum effects invisible to RGF.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the backward-error-analysis framework for continuous-time approximations of gradient descent (Barrett & Dherin 2022; Miyagawa 2023; Rosca et al. 2023) to the Heavy-Ball momentum method, going beyond the rescaled gradient flow (Kovachki & Stuart 2020) and the second-order approximation of Ghosh et al. (2023). The key methodological departure is a piece-wise continuous first-order ODE with recursively defined counter terms that cancel discretization error to arbitrary order of the step size, rather than the second-order ODE formulations used in prior momentum analyses (Shi et al. 2022; Su et al. 2016; Wilson et al. 2016). For the implicit bias application, it builds on the diagonal linear network literature (Woodworth et al. 2020; Azulay et al. 2021) and the recent second-order ODE analysis of Papazov et al. (2024), but uses a first-order ODE that also recovers the GD case by setting μ=0.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The arbitrary-order discretization error control provides a principled and extensible continuous-time tool for studying momentum methods, filling a gap where prior HB approximations could not distinguish HB from GD in the continuous limit. The discovery that HB implicitly regularizes directional smoothness (preferring less oscillatory dynamics) and that its implicit bias for diagonal linear networks differs from GD's (via initialization rescaling and symmetry breaking) are new conceptual insights not visible in the RGF. However, the framework does not cover adaptive methods like Adam, the implicit bias analysis is limited to diagonal linear networks, and the effectiveness condition η/(1−μ)² ≪ 1 restricts the practical regime. Open questions include generalization to stochastic settings, deeper architectures, and whether the directional smoothness effect persists at scale.
