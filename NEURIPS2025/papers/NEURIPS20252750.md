## Problem

Applying RLHF to modern flow-based video generation models faces three intertwined gaps: existing preference datasets were collected on pre-Sora-era, low-resolution T2V models and no longer reflect what users prefer in current high-fidelity outputs; the design space of VLM-based video reward models (annotation paradigms, tie handling, token positioning) remains largely unexplored; and naively porting Diffusion-DPO to rectified flow introduces a timestep-dependent KL coefficient that causes reward hacking and visual artifacts rather than genuine alignment.

## Input / Output

The reward model (VideoReward) consumes a text prompt paired with a video sampled at 2 fps and approximately 448×448 resolution (preserving aspect ratio), and outputs three continuous scores for Visual Quality, Motion Quality, and Text Alignment via dedicated [VQ], [MQ], and [TA] tokens in the Qwen2-VL-2B backbone. The alignment pipeline takes a text prompt as input and produces a generated video; during Flow-NRG inference, a lightweight time-dependent reward model operating in latent space provides per-step gradient signals without requiring backpropagation through the full VAE decoder. The preference dataset consists of 182k prompt-video triplets (each triplet pairing a prompt with two videos from different T2V models) annotated with pairwise preferences and 1–5 Likert scores across the three dimensions.

## Method

The paper introduces three alignment algorithms under a unified KL-regularized reward-maximization objective for rectified flow models. Flow-DPO derives a DPO-style loss by substituting the noise-velocity relation ‖ε−ε_pred‖² = (1−t)²‖v−v_pred‖² into the Diffusion-DPO objective, but critically replaces the resulting timestep-dependent coefficient β_t = β(1−t)² with a constant β, which prevents the model from overfitting at high-noise levels and eliminates reward hacking. Flow-RWR formulates reward-weighted velocity regression, weighting the flow-matching loss by exp(r(x₀,y)). Flow-NRG is an inference-time technique that shifts the predicted velocity field by −w·t/(1−t)·∇r(x_t,y) at each denoising step, where ∇r is computed by a lightweight time-dependent reward model trained directly on noised latents using the Bradley-Terry loss, enabling users to assign arbitrary weights to VQ/MQ/TA objectives without retraining. The reward model itself uses a Bradley-Terry-with-Ties (BTT) loss and a token-positioning strategy that places context-agnostic [VQ] and [MQ] tokens after the video but before the prompt, while the context-aware [TA] token follows the full prompt, preventing cross-dimensional score leakage.

## Results

On VideoGen-RewardBench (modern T2V models), VideoReward achieves 61.26% ties-included and 73.59% ties-excluded overall pairwise accuracy, substantially outperforming all baselines. On GenAI-Bench (pre-Sora-era models), it reaches 49.41% ties-included / 72.89% ties-excluded, showing strong cross-era generalization despite being trained on a disjoint dataset. For video alignment, Flow-DPO with a constant KL coefficient achieves 83.41 VBench total score and 93.42% / 69.08% / 75.43% win rates on VQ / MQ / TA (VideoGen-Eval), outperforming SFT, Flow-RWR, and the timestep-dependent β variant of Flow-DPO across all dimensions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On VideoGen-RewardBench (modern T2V models), VideoReward decisively outperforms all three baselines: it achieves 61.26% ties-included overall accuracy versus 41.80% for VideoScore, 39.08% for LiFT, and 56.77% for VisionReward. The gap is most pronounced on the Visual Quality dimension (59.68% vs. 47.41–47.53% for the other three), indicating that existing reward models fail to assess the improved fidelity of modern video outputs. On GenAI-Bench (pre-Sora-era models), the comparison is tighter: VisionReward (51.56%) and VideoScore (49.03%) are competitive with VideoReward (49.41%), while LiFT lags significantly at 37.06%. This suggests VideoReward's advantage is specifically in handling modern, higher-quality video generations. For alignment, Flow-DPO with constant β dominates all internal baselines: it achieves 75.43% TA win rate versus 53.43% for Flow-RWR and 52.84% for SFT, while the timestep-dependent β variant of Flow-DPO suffers severe reward hacking (28.14% TA win rate) despite high VQ/MQ scores.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RLHF pipeline (reward model → preference optimization) from diffusion-based video generation to rectified flow models, building directly on Diffusion-DPO (Wallace et al.) and RWR (Lee et al.) while adapting them to velocity-field prediction. It departs from prior video reward models (VideoScore, LiFT, VisionReward) that were trained on pre-Sora-era, low-resolution clips, instead constructing a 182k preference dataset from 12 modern T2V systems. The key conceptual departure is showing that the timestep-dependent KL coefficient β_t = β(1-t)² derived from Diffusion-DPO actively harms alignment in rectified flow, and that a constant β resolves this—contradicting the 'correct' mathematical derivation. The reward guidance (Flow-NRG) draws on the Guided Flows framework (Zheng et al.) but applies it to a noise-aware latent reward model rather than pixel-space backpropagation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 182k multi-dimensional preference dataset and VideoGen-RewardBench (26.5k pairs) fill a critical gap: prior reward models were calibrated to artifacts that modern T2V models have already eliminated, making them unreliable for aligning current systems. Flow-NRG's inference-time reward guidance is practically significant because it lets users trade off visual quality, motion quality, and text alignment at generation time without retraining—addressing a real deployment need. Open questions include whether the constant-β finding generalizes beyond the single 1B-parameter internal model tested, whether reward hacking can be fundamentally mitigated (the authors acknowledge it persists), and whether the pipeline extends to image-to-video or other conditional generation tasks.
