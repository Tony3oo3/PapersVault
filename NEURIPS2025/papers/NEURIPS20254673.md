## Problem

Feel-Good Thompson Sampling (FG-TS) achieves minimax-optimal regret in linear contextual bandits under exact posteriors, but its behavior with the approximate posteriors produced by practical MCMC samplers has never been systematically studied. The interaction between posterior approximation error and the optimism bonus is critical: a mismatch can either be benign or trigger a feedback loop that severely degrades performance, yet no prior work has characterized when each regime occurs.

## Input / Output

At each round the algorithm receives a d-dimensional context vector (or a set of arm-specific context vectors for multi-arm settings) and a finite action set, then outputs a single arm selection. Performance is measured by cumulative regret R_T over the full horizon and simple regret over the last 500 steps, serving as a proxy for final policy quality. Settings range from 20- and 40-dimensional linear bandits with Gaussian rewards, to 20-dimensional logistic bandits with Bernoulli rewards, to neural bandits on tabular UCI datasets (up to 54 features) and vision benchmarks (MNIST_784, CIFAR-10 at 32×32), with horizons of 5,000–15,000 rounds.

## Method

The paper constructs a unified benchmark that plugs FG-TS and its smoothed variant (SFG-TS) into six MCMC samplers—Langevin MC, MALA, HMC, and their preconditioned, SVRG, and underdamped variants—across linear, logistic, and neural contextual bandits. The feel-good likelihood modifies the standard TS posterior by subtracting a bonus term λ·min(b, f_θ(x)) (or its smooth approximation), biasing the sampler toward high-reward models; the smoothed version replaces the hard min with a softplus to ensure differentiability for gradient-based MCMC. Ablations sweep the bonus scale λ ∈ {0, 0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0}, inverse temperature β ∈ {1, 10³}, preconditioning on/off, and damping coefficient γ, revealing that larger bonuses improve regret only when posterior samples are accurate (small d, low noise) and actively harm performance when stochastic-gradient or discretization noise dominates. The paper also provides convergence proofs for preconditioned SFG-LMC and regret bounds of Õ(d√T) for the preconditioned variants, and introduces an HMC-FG-TS algorithm with leapfrog integration as a novel optimal-regret sampler for the smoothed objective.

## Results

In the linear bandit (d=20, β=10³), MALATS achieves the lowest cumulative regret at 61.3±26.6, outperforming LMCTS (62.6±9.5) and LinUCB (73.0±13.8); at d=40, MALATS reaches 100.6±10.0 vs. 129.1±16.1 for LMCTS. In the logistic bandit (d=20), exact-Gaussian methods lead: LinUCB (176.9±41.9) and LinTS (179.9±53.2) outperform all MCMC variants, with FGLMCTS closest at 184.8±38.0. In neural bandits (Table 2), vanilla LMCTS and greedy methods (Neural-ε-Greedy, NeuralUCB) consistently match or outperform FG/SFG variants, with the only FG advantage appearing on MagicTelescope where SFGLMCTS reduces regret by ~27 over LMCTS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LinUCB (Chu et al., 2011), MALATS wins in linear settings (61.3 vs. 73.0 at d=20; 100.6 vs. 126.3 at d=40) but loses in the logistic setting (194.0 vs. 176.9). Against LinTS (Agrawal & Goyal, 2013), the same pattern holds: MALATS is superior in linear (61.3 vs. 114.7) but inferior in logistic (194.0 vs. 179.9). Against LMCTS (Xu et al., 2022b), MALATS provides a modest improvement in linear (61.3 vs. 62.6) due to its Metropolis correction eliminating discretization bias, while in logistic both are competitive (194.0 vs. 202.7). Against NeuralUCB (Zhou et al., 2020) and Neural-ε-Greedy (Riquelme et al., 2018) in neural bandits, the FG/SFG variants lose: Neural-ε-Greedy achieves the lowest simple regret on 4 of 6 tasks, and NeuralUCB attains the lowest cumulative regret on Adult (2444.4 vs. 2456.6 for LMCTS) and Mushroom (145.6 vs. 324.6). The key negative finding is that FG-TS with λ=0.5 degrades performance relative to vanilla TS across nearly all settings, with the exception of a small gain on MagicTelescope (SFGLMCTS: 2193.2 vs. LMCTS: 2220.0).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the MCMC-based Thompson Sampling framework of Xu et al. (2022b) by incorporating the Feel-Good bonus from Zhang (2022) and its smoothed variant from Huix et al. (2023), then systematically benchmarking the resulting algorithms (FGLMCTS, SFGMALATS, FGHMCTS, etc.) across linear, logistic, and neural contextual bandits. Prior theoretical analyses of FG-TS assumed exact posteriors for simplicity; this work is the first to study what happens when those posteriors are approximated by finite-step MCMC samplers (LMC, MALA, HMC, preconditioned and SVRG variants). The paper also introduces a novel optimal-regret HMC algorithm for SFG-TS and provides convergence/regret proofs for preconditioned variants, bridging the gap between the theoretical guarantees in Huix et al. (2023) and practical MCMC implementations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical takeaway is that FG-TS's theoretical minimax-optimal regret advantage in linear bandits does not transfer to neural contextual bandits, where vanilla TS via LMC or MALA remains more competitive—a finding that should temper enthusiasm for FG-TS in modern deep-learning-based bandit applications. The paper also documents a dramatic failure mode where SVRG-based samplers degenerate at higher dimensions (regret ~19,000 vs. ~130), highlighting the fragility of combining variance reduction with MCMC in online settings. Open questions include whether geometry-aware TS variants (Luo and Bayati, 2025) or noise-adaptive methods can recover FG-TS's advantages in non-Gaussian posteriors, and whether the observed run-to-run sensitivity can be mitigated by adaptive MCMC step schedules.
