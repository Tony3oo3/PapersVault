## Problem

Purely data-driven system identification methods (e.g., NODEs) often produce physically implausible forecasts, fail to extrapolate, and exhibit instabilities that make them unsafe for practical deployment. While physics-guided architectures based on port-Hamiltonian systems encode energy conservation and dissipation, they do not guarantee global Lyapunov stability of the learned dynamics, leaving the basin of attraction unconstrained and allowing unbounded trajectories. This paper closes that gap by constructing a PHS-based neural network whose architecture inherently enforces global asymptotic stability without requiring projection-based corrections.

## Input / Output

The model is trained on time-series observations of a state vector x(t) ∈ ℝⁿ and an input signal u(t) ∈ ℝᵐ, using either derivative fitting (matching state derivatives) or trajectory fitting (matching integrated trajectories). In the surrogate modeling experiments, full-order temperature and heat-source fields are first projected onto 40-dimensional latent spaces via proper orthogonal decomposition, and the equilibrium position x* is either fixed from physical knowledge or learned as a trainable parameter. The output is a learned ODE of the form ẋ = [J(x) − R(x)]∂H/∂x + G(x)u(t) that can be integrated with a numerical solver (Tsit5) to produce predicted state trajectories or, after decoding, full-order temperature field predictions.

## Method

The sPHNN parameterizes each port-Hamiltonian component with a dedicated neural network: the Hamiltonian H is a fully input convex neural network (FICNN) with a normalization term that enforces H(x*)=0, ∇H(x*)=0, and a regularization term ensuring positive definiteness of the Hessian at x*, collectively guaranteeing a unique strict global minimum. The structure matrix J is constrained to be skew-symmetric via a linear reshaping of the FFNN output, the dissipation matrix R is made positive semi-definite through a Cholesky factorization R=LLᵀ of a lower-triangular FFNN output, and the input matrix G is a rectangular FFNN output. The key theoretical result (Theorem 3.1) proves that convexity of H together with the minimum conditions makes H a valid Lyapunov function, yielding global asymptotic stability whenever R ≻ 0, all without any projection step. Training uses standard supervised learning (MSE loss, ADAM optimizer) with either derivative or trajectory fitting; the single global basin of attraction eliminates the exploding-gradient issues that plague unconstrained NODEs during trajectory-based training.

## Results

On the thermal food processing surrogate (n_A=3 augmented dimensions, n_D=2 training trajectories), sPHNN achieves a median test RMSE of 1.170 at 5% noise, 2.595 at 15%, and 3.177 at 25%, consistently outperforming all baselines. The unconstrained PHNN is dramatically worse, with test RMSE of 28.233 at 5% noise (over 24× higher than sPHNN), while NODE achieves 3.577 and bPHNN 1.572. On the cascaded tanks dataset, sPHNN, sPHNN-LM, and bPHNN achieve comparable RMSE, while NODE and PHNN perform worse on average and exhibit poor physical extrapolation (diverging or oscillatory predictions). For the additive manufacturing surrogate, sPHNN's peak test RMSE stays below 30 K and decreases toward equilibrium, whereas NODE and PHNN errors increase rapidly due to instabilities in the learned dynamics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against NODE (Chen et al., 2018), sPHNN wins decisively across all experiments: on the thermal food processing surrogate, sPHNN's test RMSE is 3.177 vs. NODE's 4.362 at 25% noise, and the gap widens with augmented dimensions where NODE predictions become unstable. On the cascaded tanks, NODE shows higher RMSE and poor extrapolation (diverging water levels), while sPHNN guarantees convergence to the physically correct zero equilibrium. Against sNODE (Kolter & Manek, 2019), sPHNN outperforms on the spinning rigid body (sNODE shows unphysical energy increases and high variance), and sNODE could not be trained with trajectory fitting at all due to discontinuities from its projection-based stability enforcement. The paper also compares against PHNN (unconstrained Hamiltonian) and bPHNN (bounded but not globally stable): PHNN is catastrophically worse (test RMSE ~24–28 vs. ~1–3 for sPHNN on the food processing task), while bPHNN is competitive but consistently outperformed by sPHNN, especially in the scarce-data regime (n_D=1) and at higher noise levels (5.231 vs. 3.177 at 25% noise). The sPHNN-LM variant (learnable equilibrium) matches sPHNN when sufficient data is available but degrades in the scarce-data setting, confirming that providing the equilibrium as a prior reduces data requirements.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes three existing threads: port-Hamiltonian system (PHS) theory for physics-based dynamical modeling, Lyapunov stability theory for guaranteed convergence, and fully input convex neural networks (FICNNs) for enforcing convexity. It departs from prior PHS-based neural networks (PHNN, bPHNN) by adding a convexity constraint on the Hamiltonian that yields global asymptotic stability, and from projection-based stable NODEs (Kolter & Manek, 2019) by achieving stability through architectural constraints rather than post-hoc projection, thereby avoiding the discontinuities that hinder trajectory-based training. The OnsagerNet framework (Yu et al., 2021) is the closest prior work in spirit, but sPHNN differs by supporting arbitrary time-dependent inputs and providing a cleaner convexity-based stability guarantee.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The projection-free global stability guarantee via convex Hamiltonian offers a practical and interpretable inductive bias for learning stable dynamics from sparse data, with demonstrated benefits in surrogate modeling, digital twins, and reduced-order modeling across thermal, fluid, and mechanical systems. A key limitation is that the convexity constraint restricts the model to globally stable (single-equilibrium) dynamics, precluding multistable systems; the authors note future work on allowing multiple minima while retaining stability. The finding that augmented state dimensions—which destabilize unconstrained NODEs and PHNNs—become a net benefit under the sPHNN constraint is practically important for modeling complex multiphysics processes where the true state dimensionality is unknown.
