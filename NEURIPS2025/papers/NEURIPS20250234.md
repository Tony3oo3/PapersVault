## Problem

Existing RLHF algorithms for LLM alignment are highly sensitive to misspecification in the preference model (e.g., the Bradley-Terry assumption), the reward function, or the reference policy, leading to degraded fine-tuning. No prior method is simultaneously robust to misspecification in both the preference model and the reference policy, leaving a gap in statistically reliable alignment under realistic modeling errors.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250234/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes preference data as i.i.d. tuples (X, Y^(1), Y^(2), Z) where X is a prompt, Y^(1) and Y^(2) are paired responses sampled from a reference policy, and Z is a binary preference label. It additionally requires an estimated reference policy π̂_ref and an estimated preference model ĝ (which may be a BT-based reward model or a general preference model). The output is a fine-tuned LLM policy π̂ that maximizes total preference over the reference policy, along with a doubly robust preference evaluation score p̂_DR(π) for any candidate policy. Each data tuple is exploited twice in the estimating function—as (X, Y^(1), Y^(2), Z) and (X, Y^(2), Y^(1), 1−Z)—to reduce variance in the pairwise comparison setting.

## Method

DRPO constructs a doubly robust preference estimator by combining a Direct Method (DM) term that plugs in the estimated preference model ĝ with an Importance Sampling (IS) augmentation term weighted by the ratio π(Y^(a)|X)/π̂_ref(Y^(a)|X) and the residual Z − ĝ(X, Y^(1), Y^(2)). This augmentation corrects bias from preference-model misspecification, yielding consistency when either ĝ or π̂_ref is correctly specified (double robustness) and semi-parametric efficiency when both are approximately correct. The optimization step solves for the policy maximizing the DR preference estimate minus a KL-divergence penalty against π̂_ref, with practical refinements including IS-ratio clipping, a pseudo-objective for Monte Carlo sampling, and a group-relative KL measure for variance reduction. Theoretically, the resulting policy achieves a suboptimality gap bounded by the product of the reference-policy and preference-model estimation errors, which is strictly smaller than the first-order dependence in PPO- and DPO-based algorithms.

## Results

On the TL;DR summarization task, DRPO-BT achieves win rates of 98.5% vs IPO, 90.0% vs CPO, and 72.5% vs Dr.DPO (Table 2), demonstrating substantial gains over all DPO variants under a misspecified reference policy. On the Anthropic HH human dialogue task, DRPO-GPM attains an 86.38% length-controlled win rate vs SFT, comparable to the strongest robust DPO variants (Dr.DPO: 92.16%, rDPO: 86.89%) while outperforming IPO (78.29%), RSO (80.62%), and CPO (73.59%). On the synthetic IMDb preference evaluation task, the DR estimator with both models correctly specified achieves near-zero MSE at 1500 samples, while the both-misspecified variant plateaus at a significantly higher MSE, empirically confirming the double robustness property (Corollary 3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DRPO dominates IPO [56] with a 98.5% win rate on TL;DR and 86.38% vs 78.29% LC win rate on HH, confirming that the doubly robust correction over the shared objective is critical. Against CPO [158], DRPO wins 90% on TL;DR and 86.38% vs 73.59% on HH. On TL;DR (where the reference policy is misspecified), DRPO beats Dr.DPO [54] (72.5%), rDPO [53] (65.0%), cDPO [157] (63.5%), and RSO [48] (69.5%), all by comfortable margins. On HH (where the BT preference model is likely misspecified), DRPO-GPM is competitive with the noise-robust variants Dr.DPO (92.16%) and rDPO (86.89%) but trails them slightly in LC win rate; the authors note that adopting the same noise-aware preference model would likely close this gap. Against PPO [20] and DPO [26], DRPO-BT achieves a 57% win rate on HH and substantially outperforms both on TL;DR (Figure 4), with the advantage attributed to robustness against reward and reference-policy misspecification. ORPO [159] is the weakest baseline, losing to DRPO on both datasets (57.5% TL;DR, 75.92% vs 86.38% HH).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the doubly robust estimation framework from causal inference and off-policy evaluation (Dudik et al., 2014) into the RLHF/LLM alignment setting, adapting it to the pairwise preference comparison structure where each data tuple is used twice. Conceptually, it sits closest to IPO (Azar et al., 2024), sharing the same population-level objective of maximizing total preference over the reference policy, but departs by making the estimator robust to reference policy misspecification. It also generalizes beyond the Bradley-Terry assumption that underpins PPO and DPO, while remaining more robust to preference model misspecification than GPM-based approaches. The paper explicitly positions itself as bridging two previously disconnected literatures: doubly robust methods in statistics/econometrics and preference-based LLM alignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The product-of-errors structure in the suboptimality bound (Theorem 7) is a meaningful theoretical improvement: estimation errors in the reference policy and preference model only compound multiplicatively rather than additively, providing a principled robustness guarantee that PPO and DPO lack. Practically, this offers a drop-in robustness layer for existing RLHF pipelines where the reference policy or preference model is imperfect—a common real-world scenario. Open questions include how DRPO performs when both models are simultaneously misspecified (the product term still grows), whether the approach scales to very large models where the reference policy is inherently approximate, and how to integrate noise-aware preference models (as used by rDPO/Dr.DPO) to handle the pairwise label noise present in datasets like HH.
