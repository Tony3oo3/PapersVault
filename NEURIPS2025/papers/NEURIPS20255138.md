## Problem

Dropout-style algorithms such as DropEdge, DropNode, DropAgg, and DropGNN have been widely adopted to alleviate over-smoothing in deep GNNs, but their impact on over-squashing—the loss of information between distant nodes—remains unexplored. Prior evaluations were restricted to short-range (homophilic) tasks, masking the fact that these methods provably reduce sensitivity to distant nodes and degrade performance on long-range tasks, which is the intended use case of deep MPNNs.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a directed graph G=(V,E) with adjacency matrix A and node feature matrix X ∈ R^{N×H^(0)}, producing node representations Z^(L) ∈ R^{N×H^(L)} for node classification or graph-level representations for graph classification. The key mechanism acts on the propagation matrix (asymmetrically or symmetrically normalized adjacency with self-loops), where per-node dropping probabilities are computed from in-degrees before each message-passing layer. ## Method

The paper first proves that in an L-layer linear GCN, DropEdge-variants reduce the expected sensitivity between nodes L-hops away by computing the expected propagation matrix under random edge masks, showing that cross-edge weights decrease while self-loop weights increase, leading to exponential decay of sensitivity with distance. To address this, the authors introduce DropSens, a sensitivity-aware variant of DropEdge that assigns a per-node dropping probability q_i (dependent only on the receiving node's in-degree d_i) solved from a closed-form equation that enforces a fixed information preservation ratio c over cross-edges. This allows DropSens to drop the same number of edges as DropEdge while explicitly controlling the fraction of information lost, thereby maintaining sensitivity to distant nodes. The approach is derived specifically for GCN-style architectures where edge weights are simple functions of node degree, and the paper demonstrates that extending it to GIN (constant weights) or GAT (feature-dependent attention) is nontrivial. Empirically, DropSens with GCN outperforms state-of-the-art graph-rewiring methods (SDRF, DIGL, FoSR, GTR) on both node and graph classification long-range benchmarks.

## Results

On node classification, DropSens with GCN achieves 85.17% on Cora, 73.81% on CiteSeer, 83.76% on PubMed, 53.45% on Chameleon, and 39.72% on Squirrel, outperforming all graph-rewiring baselines (SDRF, DIGL, +FA) on 5 of 6 datasets. On graph classification, DropSens reaches 80.16% on Reddit and 64.16% on Collab, surpassing DIGL (76.04%, 54.50%) and FoSR (70.33%, 33.84%). The core empirical finding is that existing dropout methods are detrimental on long-range tasks: 85% of heterophilic node-classification cases and 61% of graph-classification cases show worse performance than the NoDrop baseline, while 57% of homophilic cases improve.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DropSens with GCN outperforms all graph-rewiring baselines on node classification: it beats SDRF by +10.72 pts on Chameleon (53.45 vs 42.73), +2.67 on Squirrel (39.72 vs 37.05), and +2.41 on Cora (85.17 vs 82.76), though it trails SDRF on Actor (22.89 vs 28.42). On graph classification, DropSens dominates on Reddit (80.16 vs DIGL 76.04, FoSR 70.33) and Collab (64.16 vs DIGL 54.50, FoSR 33.84), but is outperformed by DIGL on Enzymes (28.42 vs 35.72) and IMDb (49.34 vs 64.39). Among the dropout baselines, DropAgg is the most harmful on heterophilic datasets (e.g., −15.18 pts on Chameleon, −13.74 on Squirrel with GCN), while DropNode has the mildest negative effect. DropEdge, Dropout, and DropGNN all show consistent degradation on heterophilic node classification (46/54 ≈ 85% of cases worse than NoDrop). DropMessage is the least harmful dropout variant, showing positive gains on some heterophilic GIN settings (+4.24 on Chameleon, +2.44 on Squirrel). The paper also notes that DropSens is specifically designed for GCN's degree-based normalization and does not transfer well to GIN, where it fails to be competitive.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of two well-studied but previously disconnected lines of work: random edge/feature-dropping methods (DropEdge, DropNode, DropAgg, DropGNN, DropMessage) designed to alleviate over-smoothing in deep GNNs, and graph-rewiring techniques (SDRF, DIGL, FoSR, GTR) designed to mitigate over-squashing. The key departure is identifying that the former family has been evaluated almost exclusively on short-range, homophilic benchmarks, leaving their impact on long-range interactions unexamined. The paper extends the sensitivity analysis framework of Topping et al. [81] and Black et al. [8] to the random edge-dropping setting, providing the first theoretical characterization of how these methods affect the effective receptive field, and introduces DropSens as a sensitivity-aware variant derived from the GCN message-passing scheme.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a foundational assumption in the deep GNN literature: that successfully training a deep GNN (by addressing over-smoothing) implies it can model long-range interactions. The finding that standard regularization methods systematically degrade performance on heterophilic/long-range tasks (85% of cases) has broad practical implications for anyone training deep GNNs beyond citation networks. Key limitations include the theory being restricted to linear GCNs with asymmetric normalization, and DropSens being architecture-specific (GCN-only), which limits immediate generalizability. Open questions include developing reliable measures of information mixing in ground-truth data to better classify tasks as short- vs. long-range, and understanding whether over-squashing matters for graph-level aggregation tasks where the readout module partially mixes distant information.
