## Problem

Pause tokens (filler symbols like '...') consistently improve Transformer performance on language and mathematical tasks, yet their theoretical effect remained unexplained. This paper provides the first formal separation result, proving that adding pause tokens to constant-depth, logarithmic-width Transformers strictly increases their computational expressivity, and characterizes exactly which circuit complexity classes they unlock.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20253001/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The theoretical framework takes a binary string x of length n, optionally padded with b(n) pause tokens (polynomial or quasi-polynomial in n), and produces a single Boolean output. Arithmetic is performed in fixed-point representation with saturation (clamping to ±B_p), at either constant precision p=O(1) or logarithmic precision p=O(log n). In the empirical setting, a 2-layer, 4-head GPT-2-style Transformer is trained on bit sequences of length 20–300 to predict parity, with hint labels (threshold values or sub-parities) provided at intermediate positions to guide learning.

## Method

The core contribution is a circuit-complexity equivalence: constant-precision Transformers with polynomial pause tokens (TF[1,L,P]) are exactly AC^0, and logarithmic-precision Transformers with pause tokens (TF[L,L,P]) are exactly TC^0. The simulation mechanism uses pause tokens as intermediate computational units representing circuit gates and edges, with positional encodings encoding the circuit's connectivity structure; each pair of Transformer layers simulates one circuit layer, where the first attention layer copies previously computed values to argument positions and the second computes gate outputs via attention-based summation and feedforward thresholding. A strict separation is proven for the constant-precision case: TF[1,L,0] ⊊ TF[1,L,P] (non-uniformly) and TF_l[1,L,0] ⊊ TF_l[1,L,Q] (uniformly, fixed depth), leveraging the non-uniform size hierarchy and fixed-depth uniform size hierarchy for AC^0. Empirically, the paper demonstrates that a 2-layer causally masked Transformer can learn parity only when supplied with pause tokens and parallel threshold hints, while without pause tokens accuracy degrades to chance by 100 bits, confirming the theoretical bottleneck imposed by causal masking on global information aggregation.

## Results

The paper's main contributions are theoretical: it proves TF[1,L,P] = AC^0 (constant-precision Transformers with polynomial pause tokens are exactly equivalent to AC^0 circuits) and TF[L,L,P] = TC^0 (log-precision Transformers with pause tokens match TC^0). It further establishes strict separations: TF[1,L,0] ⊊ TF[1,L,P] (non-uniform) and TF_l[1,L,0] ⊊ TF_l[1,L,Q] (uniform, fixed-depth). Empirically, a 2-layer 4-head GPT-2-style Transformer trained on parity over bit sequences of length 20–300 shows that causal masking without pause tokens drops to chance accuracy by ~100 bits, while removing the causal mask sustains near-perfect accuracy to ~200 bits, and adding n pause tokens under causal masking provides a further non-trivial gain beyond simply removing the mask.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods; instead it compares internal architectural configurations. In the empirical parity experiment, three settings are contrasted: (1) causal masking with no pause tokens, (2) no causal masking with no pause tokens, and (3) causal masking with n pause tokens. Setting (1) fails to learn parity beyond ~100 bits, setting (2) succeeds up to ~200 bits, and setting (3) outperforms setting (2) at longer lengths, indicating that pause tokens provide benefits beyond merely enabling global attention. Theoretically, the key comparison is between Transformers with and without pause tokens: the paper proves that without pause tokens, constant-precision Transformers compute only a strict subset of AC^0 (bounded by a fixed polynomial circuit size), while with polynomial pause tokens they recover all of AC^0. In the log-precision regime, a strict separation between TF[L,L,0] and TF[L,L,P] remains open due to the lack of superpolynomial TC^0 lower bounds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the circuit-complexity analysis of Transformer expressivity established by Li et al. (2024) and Merrill & Sabharwal (2023), who characterized constant-precision Transformers as a strict subset of AC^0 and log-precision Transformers as contained in TC^0, respectively. The key departure is introducing pause tokens as a variable in the expressivity equation: the authors prove that polynomial pause tokens close the gap, yielding exact equality with AC^0 (constant precision) and TC^0 (log precision), and that this strictly increases expressivity in the constant-precision regime. The paper also positions pause tokens as a parallel-width mechanism complementary to chain-of-thought's sequential-depth mechanism, drawing on Li et al.'s CoT results to contrast the two.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first rigorous theoretical explanation for the empirically observed performance gains from pause tokens, grounding them in circuit complexity rather than heuristic arguments. The clean separation (TF[1,L,0] ⊊ TF[1,L,P]) clarifies that a Transformer's computational power depends not just on depth and width but on how computation is distributed across the token sequence. Key open questions remain: the log-precision separation is blocked by the lack of superpolynomial TC^0 lower bounds, the expressivity-learnability gap is unaddressed (pause tokens require specific training signals to be useful), and the practical utility is limited since the theoretical gains require polynomially many pause tokens. The quantization implications (constant precision → AC^0, log precision → TC^0) are relevant as low-bit LLMs become more common.
