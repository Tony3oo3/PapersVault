## Problem

Existing LLM-based peer review systems replicate traditional workflows—assigning absolute scores to individual papers and aggregating via a meta-reviewer—rather than exploiting LLMs' scalability to fundamentally redesign evaluation. The paper asks whether replacing absolute scoring with large-scale pairwise comparisons, aggregated through a probabilistic ranking model, can more accurately identify high-impact papers while avoiding the calibration inconsistencies and rating compression that plague conventional approaches.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20250394/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each LLM agent (GPT-4o mini, temperature 0) receives a randomly sampled pair of papers, with each paper represented by its title, abstract, figure/table captions, and main text extracted via ScienceBeam. The agent acts as an area chair and outputs a binary preference judgment (which paper is better) along with brief reasoning, structured as JSON. These pairwise judgments are then aggregated into Bradley-Terry coefficients that yield a global ranking of all submissions, from which acceptance decisions are derived at the conference acceptance rate.

## Method

The system operates in three stages: (1) M LLM agents are each assigned a random pair of papers and produce a binary preference, yielding an observation set of (i, j, y_ij) triplets; (2) the Bradley-Terry model is fitted via maximum likelihood estimation, where each paper's latent quality score β_i determines the probability of beating any other paper through a logistic function of score differences; (3) papers are ranked by their estimated BT coefficients. The key insight is that relative judgments between two items are more reliable than absolute scores, and the Bradley-Terry model provides stable statistical estimation even from sparse comparisons—less than 2% of all possible pairs suffice to recover a high-quality ranking. The paper demonstrates a clear scaling law: average citation counts of accepted papers increase steadily as the number of agents grows from 10³ to 10⁵, plateauing near human-level performance, while the rating-based baseline (three GPT reviewers + meta-reviewer) shows minimal improvement over random due to rating compression around 6–7.

## Results

On ICLR 2024 (7,158 papers), the GPT ranking system achieves 20.00 average citations for accepted papers (without area control) and 18.97 (with area control), compared to 19.36 for human decisions, 11.41 for the GPT rating system baseline, and 8.56 for random acceptance. The system's consistency with human peer review is 41.0% for ICLR 2024 (42.2% for ICLR 2023), on par with the 48.0% human-human agreement observed in the NeurIPS 2021 consistency experiment. Across ICLR 2023, ICLR 2024, NeurIPS 2023, EMNLP 2023, and CoRL 2023, higher-ranked papers consistently receive significantly more citations (p < 0.001 in most cases), confirming discriminative capability comparable to human review.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The GPT ranking system (pairwise comparisons + Bradley-Terry aggregation) substantially outperforms the GPT rating system baseline (Lu et al. [27], Jin et al. [18]), which uses three independent GPT reviewers plus a meta-reviewer to produce a 1–10 score: 20.00 vs. 11.41 average citations for accepted papers on ICLR 2024, a 75% improvement. The rating baseline's limitation is that GPT-generated scores cluster tightly around 6–7, severely limiting discriminative power and yielding only marginal gains over random acceptance (8.56). The GPT ranking system also slightly exceeds human peer review (19.36 avg citations) at full scale, while maintaining a 41% overlap with human acceptances—comparable to the 48% inter-committee agreement in the NeurIPS 2021 consistency experiment. However, the ranking system exhibits systematic biases absent in human review: it over-accepts applied areas (robotics 0.56 vs. 0.32; societal considerations 0.51 vs. 0.30) while under-accepting theoretical areas (learning theory 0.12 vs. 0.50), selects papers with measurably lower topic novelty (smaller embedding nearest-neighbor distances), and concentrates acceptances among fewer institutions (higher Gini coefficients, e.g., 37.2% of top ICLR 2024 picks from 10 institutions vs. 26.7% for humans).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the classical Bradley-Terry model (1952) for ranking from pairwise comparisons, but departs fundamentally from prior LLM-based peer review work (AgentReview, AI Scientist, CycleReview, DeepReview) that replicates the traditional individual-scoring-then-aggregation pipeline. The key conceptual shift is replacing absolute LLM ratings with relative pairwise judgments, motivated by the well-known finding that evaluators are more reliable at comparative than absolute assessments. It also extends the peer-review reform literature on pairwise comparisons (Su, Wang & Shah) by fully replacing human ratings with LLM agents rather than using author-provided rankings to calibrate noisy human scores.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The near-2× gap in average citations between the pairwise ranking system (20.0) and the rating-based baseline (11.4) provides strong evidence that the evaluation *format* (relative vs. absolute) matters as much as the evaluator, with direct implications for designing scalable LLM-assisted review pipelines. However, the paper reveals critical emergent biases—reduced topic novelty, higher institutional concentration (elevated Gini coefficients), and a systematic preference for applied over theoretical work—that must be addressed before any deployment. Open questions include whether these biases can be mitigated through prompt engineering or aggregation design, whether the approach generalizes beyond ML to other disciplines, and how to effectively integrate human-AI collaboration in a hybrid review process.
