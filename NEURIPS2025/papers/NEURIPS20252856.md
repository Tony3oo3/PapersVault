## Problem

Standard end-to-end reasoning models learn statistical heuristics from training data but fail to generalize to problems with more constraints or larger structure than those seen during training. Existing combinatorial optimization solvers (GNNs, diffusion solvers, RL-based methods) are trained on fixed-size instances and degrade sharply out-of-distribution. This paper asks whether reasoning can be made compositional: can a model learn energy landscapes on small, tractable subproblems and then combine them at test time to solve arbitrarily larger instances?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20252856/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model is trained on individual subproblem units — a single chessboard row (length-8 binary vector), a single 3-literal clause (3-bit assignment + clause sign), a single graph edge (28-dim one-hot color pair), or a single hint embedding (1536-dim vector) — and at test time the same model is reused across all subproblems of a full instance. The energy function E_θ(x, y) maps a problem condition x and a candidate solution y to a scalar, and the output is a continuous vector decoded greedily into a discrete assignment (queen positions, truth values, node colors, or letter placements). The number of composed subproblems at test time is unconstrained, enabling generalization to instances far larger than any training example.

## Method

The approach trains a small MLP energy function on each subproblem using a combined diffusion loss (matching the EBM gradient to a Gaussian noise schedule, avoiding backpropagation through T steps) and a contrastive loss that pushes valid and invalid noise-corrupted samples apart to sharpen the energy minima. At inference, the full problem's energy landscape is constructed by summing the energy functions of all subproblems (e.g., all rows + columns + diagonals for N-Queens, all clauses for 3-SAT, all edges for graph coloring). To optimize this composed landscape, the paper introduces Parallel Energy Minimization (PEM), a particle-based sampler that maintains P parallel trajectories, resamples them via softmax weights derived from their current energies, injects scheduled Gaussian noise to escape local minima, and then takes a gradient step on the composed energy. An optional refinement stage fine-tunes the composed model on complete instances to correct spurious minima introduced by composition.

## Results

On the 3-SAT problem, the proposed EBM+PEM approach solves 91/100 instances on the similar distribution (20 variables, 91 clauses) and 43/100 on the larger distribution (50 variables, 218 clauses), outperforming the best neural SAT solver NSNet (58 and 37 respectively) and the best combinatorial optimization model FastT2T (23 and 20). On the 8-Queens problem, the method generates 97/100 valid solutions versus 41 for the best baseline (FastT2T with guided sampling). On graph coloring, the approach achieves the lowest number of conflicting edges across most distributions, e.g., 8.60±4.82 on small Erdős-Rényi graphs vs. 46.80±20.47 for GCN, and maintains strong performance on larger graphs where GNN baselines degrade significantly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On 3-SAT, the proposed method decisively outperforms NSNet (the strongest neural SAT solver using belief propagation), solving 91 vs. 58 instances on the similar distribution and 43 vs. 37 on the larger distribution, while also achieving higher satisfied-clause ratios (0.9985 vs. 0.9856). Against combinatorial optimization baselines, FastT2T (the best CO model) solves only 23 and 20 instances respectively, and DIFUSCO, GCN, and DGL all solve 0–10 instances. On 8-Queens, EBM+PEM generates 97 valid solutions out of 100, nearly doubling the best baseline FastT2T with guided sampling (41) and substantially outperforming LWD (22), GFlowNets (14), and DIFUSCO (17). On graph coloring, the method achieves the fewest conflicting edges on most distributions (e.g., 8.60 on small Erdős-Rényi vs. 46.80 for GCN and 25.00 for XLVIN), and critically maintains performance on larger graphs where GNN baselines degrade sharply (e.g., 29.20 vs. 151.60 for GCN on large Erdős-Rényi). On crosswords, the approach achieves 80.4% letter success rate, slightly exceeding Tree of Thoughts (78.0%) while far surpassing Chain-of-Thought (40.6%) and standard IO (38.7%), though ToT edges ahead on word-level success (60.0% vs. 50.5%). Against state-of-the-art reasoning LLMs on graph coloring (Gemini 2.5 Pro, DeepSeek R1, Qwen 235B), the method achieves the lowest conflicting edges on larger graphs (45.81 vs. 78.00 for DeepSeek R1), where LLM performance deteriorates significantly with scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the energy-based reasoning framework established by Du et al. (2022, 2023, 2024), which cast reasoning as energy minimization over solution spaces. The key conceptual departure is compositional decomposition: rather than training an EBM on complete problem instances (as in prior end-to-end EBM reasoning), the authors train on minimal subproblems (a single chessboard row, a single 3-SAT clause, a single graph edge) and compose their energy landscapes at inference time to solve arbitrarily larger instances. The Parallel Energy Minimization (PEM) sampler is a novel adaptation of Sequential Monte Carlo resampling to the EBM optimization setting, replacing standard MCMC methods (ULA, MALA, HMC) that struggle with the multimodal landscapes created by composition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The compositional energy landscape approach offers a principled path to generalization in combinatorial reasoning: by training on tractable subproblems and combining objectives at test time, the model can solve problems with far more constraints than it has ever seen, with a tunable compute budget via particle count. This addresses a core limitation of end-to-end neural solvers that memorize heuristics from training distributions. Open questions include whether the Gaussian initialization assumption limits applicability to non-continuous solution spaces, whether the approach scales to problems with thousands of interacting constraints, and whether the refinement step (Eq. 7) can be replaced by purely compositional training to reduce dependence on ground-truth solutions at inference time.
