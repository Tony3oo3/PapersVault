## Problem

Existing astronomical super-resolution datasets suffer from three critical flaws: they violate the physical flux-conservation principle of telescopes (using naive interpolation instead of photon-preserving downsampling), they contain only single-object crops rather than full star fields, and they are too small (1,597–17,000 images) for reliable training and evaluation. This paper addresses all three gaps simultaneously by introducing a large-scale, flux-consistent star field benchmark and a model that explicitly enforces photometric fidelity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20254765/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a low-resolution star field image (downsampled ×2 or ×4 from HST WFC/ACS F814W observations with Gaussian or Gaussian+Airy PSF blurring) and produces a corresponding high-resolution star field image. Unlike prior ASR work that processes single-object crops, STAR images are full field-level patches averaging ~30 celestial objects each, including cross-object interactions, weak lensing arcs, and diffuse interstellar medium. The FISR model additionally consumes a real-time-computed flux map derived from the input via object detection and elliptical photometry, which serves as an explicit guidance signal alongside the raw image.

## Method

FISR extends the PromptIR transformer architecture with two flux-aware components. The Flux Guidance Generation (FGG) module detects celestial objects in the input, computes per-object flux via photometry, and renders a flux map using rotatable Gaussian kernels scaled by each object's flux value; this map is then encoded into multi-scale guidance features. The Flux Guidance Controller (FGC) injects these guidance features into the encoder's multi-scale representations by modulating PromptIR's learnable prompts through Hadamard products with both the encoded features and the flux guidance, effectively making flux a visual prompt that steers attention toward astrophysically relevant structures. At the optimization level, a Flux Consistency Loss (FCL) weights the pixel-wise L1 reconstruction loss by the ground-truth flux map, so that photometrically critical regions (bright stars, overlapping sources) receive stronger supervision. The combined design ensures that flux information is both explicitly extracted from the input and constrained in the output, achieving 24.84% lower Flux Error than the best prior method while also improving PSNR and SSIM.

## Results

On the STAR benchmark, FISR achieves the best PSNR at both ×2 (37.88 dB) and ×4 (35.18 dB) super-resolution, outperforming the strongest baseline HAT by 0.63 dB and 0.26 dB respectively. The proposed Flux Error (FE) metric shows FISR's most dramatic advantage: 0.5739 at ×2, a 24.84% reduction over HAT (0.7636) and 29.2% over SwinIR (0.813). At ×4, FISR also achieves the lowest FE (1.0125) while maintaining competitive SSIM (0.7266, marginally below HAT's 0.7276). On the cross-dataset AstroSR evaluation, FISR re-trained on that data achieves 24.02 dB PSNR and 33.23 FE, surpassing all original AstroSR baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FISR outperforms all seven baselines on PSNR at both ×2 and ×4, with the largest margins over EDSR (+2.50 dB at ×2) and RealESRGAN (+1.04 dB at ×2). On the novel Flux Error metric, FISR's advantage is most pronounced: it reduces FE by 24.84% over HAT, 29.2% over SwinIR, 37.9% over RCAN, 60.8% over EDSR, and 92.2% over RealESRGAN at ×2, confirming that GAN-based methods (RealESRGAN) are particularly poor at preserving astrophysical flux. At ×4, FISR maintains its PSNR lead over HAT (+0.26 dB) and FE lead (+1.2%), though it trails HAT by 0.001 on SSIM. The ablation (Table 3) confirms that the Flux Guidance modules alone (without flux cues) actually degrade FE, while adding the flux map and FCL together yields the full gain. On the cross-dataset AstroSR evaluation (Table 12), FISR re-trained on that data achieves 24.02 dB PSNR and 33.23 FE, beating the original SRGAN baseline (23.00 dB, 42.31 FE) and RCAN (23.61 dB, 61.39 FE), demonstrating architectural transferability beyond the STAR dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends natural image super-resolution methods (SwinIR, HAT, PromptIR) to the astronomical domain, departing from prior astronomical SR datasets (AstroSR, DiffLense, ZJ Luo et al.) that are object-centric, flux-inconsistent, and limited in scale. The FISR model is architecturally grounded in PromptIR's prompt-based restoration framework, but introduces a Flux Guidance Generation module and Flux Guidance Controller that inject physically-derived flux maps as visual prompts, alongside a Flux Consistency Loss that weights pixel-wise supervision by object flux. The key conceptual departure from prior work is treating flux conservation—a fundamental telescope imaging principle—as a first-class constraint in both data generation (flux-consistent downsampling via celestial receptive-field overlap) and model optimization, rather than relying on simple interpolation that violates this property.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

STAR addresses a genuine gap: prior astronomical SR datasets (1,597–17,000 pairs) used naive interpolation that breaks flux conservation, making them physically unreliable for photometry and stellar population studies. The 54,738-pair field-level benchmark with 15× more objects per image and 60% additional cosmic information (ISM, weak lensing) opens the door to training SR models that capture cross-object interactions previously invisible in object-crop settings. However, the work is limited to a single telescope (HST WFC/ACS), single filter (F814W), and single instrument, raising open questions about generalization to JWST, LSST, or multi-band observations. The Flux Error metric and FCL loss are lightweight and architecture-agnostic (Table 4 shows broad applicability), suggesting they could be adopted by other astronomical imaging pipelines beyond SR.
