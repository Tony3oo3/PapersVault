## Problem

Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting is widely assumed to improve inductive reasoning in large language models, yet no controlled diagnostic tasks exist to isolate inductive rule induction from other capabilities, and no theoretical account explains why CoT can degrade performance on hidden-rule inference. This paper identifies that Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) consistently underperform non-reasoning LLMs on special-rule induction, and provides both a formal error-propagation theory and targeted interventions to explain and fix this degradation.

## Input / Output

Each task instance provides a single gameplay transcript (10–12 chess moves, 12 poker hands, 12 dice rolls, or 12 blackjack hands) governed by a composite of normal rules (NRs) and hidden special rules (SRs) that are never revealed to the model. The model must output a concise natural-language description of the induced rule, which is then scored by GPT-4o as a semantic-alignment judge using 3-query majority voting against the ground-truth rule. Transcripts are structured per game (e.g., board size, piece positions, move sequences for chess; hole cards, community cards, and winner for Texas Hold'em) but contain no rule annotations or symbolic summaries.

## Method

The paper introduces four controlled game-based diagnostic tasks (chess, Texas Hold'em, dice, blackjack) with 225+ rule combinations per game, each pairing standard normal rules with manually designed hidden special rules. The core theoretical contribution models CoT as a belief-update recursion m_k = m_{k-1} + γ_k g_k where evidence g_k = α_k(y* − m_{k-1}) + ε_k, decomposing reasoning error into three sources: breakdown error (misaligned sub-task decomposition, α_k ≈ 0 or < 0), solving error (stochastic answer noise ε_k), and summary error (halting at a suboptimal depth N ≠ N*). Theorem 4.1 proves the expected squared error E(N) is U-shaped with a unique minimizer N*, establishing a fundamental bias-variance trade-off in reasoning depth. To mitigate each failure mode without retraining, the paper applies three interventions: structured decomposition templates that constrain sub-question posing, worked examples that anchor solving to non-numeric structural patterns (following Kuo et al.), and a 1000-token budget to prevent over-generation past N*.

## Results

On Normal Rules (NRs), all eight models exceed 90% inductive accuracy across all four games, confirming strong surface-pattern recognition. On Special Rules (SRs), non-reasoning models (e.g., GPT-4o at 56.67% on Chess SR1, DeepSeek-V3 at 48%) consistently outperform their reasoning counterparts (GPT-o3 at 21.33%, DeepSeek-R1 at 34%), with reasoning models dropping below 25% on several SRs. Solving Errors dominate failure analysis, accounting for over 80% of all incorrect inferences, with Math Overuse being the single largest subtype (up to 60.4% in Dice Games). The combined intervention (structured decomposition + non-numeric worked examples + 1000-token budget) yields 20–40% absolute gains on SR accuracy in Chess and Dice, and in most cases surpasses non-reasoning baselines on both SRs and NRs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between four non-reasoning LLMs (GPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3, Qwen2.5-Max, Grok-2) and four Large Reasoning Models (GPT-o3, DeepSeek-R1, QwQ, Grok-3-Mini) on the same inductive rule-inference tasks. Non-reasoning models win decisively on Special Rules: GPT-4o achieves 56.67% on Chess SR1 versus 21.33% for GPT-o3 and 34% for DeepSeek-R1, a gap of 15–35 percentage points that persists across all four games. On Normal Rules, both groups perform comparably (90–100%), confirming the gap is specific to hidden-rule induction. Among the three intervention strategies, the combined approach (decomposition templates + non-numeric worked examples + 1000-token budget) outperforms any single intervention on SRs by 20–40% in Chess and Dice, and in most settings also surpasses the best non-reasoning baseline. A controlled CoT-prompting ablation on GPT-4o (Table 6) shows that simply adding 'think step by step' yields negligible or slightly negative changes (±1–3%), indicating that the degradation is specific to trained reasoning architectures rather than generic CoT instructions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CoT reasoning literature by providing a formal theoretical framework (belief-update with alignment and noise) that explains *why* reasoning can degrade performance, building on empirical observations by Wu et al. [14] of non-monotonic CoT length effects and on mechanistic analyses by Cui et al. [18] and Li et al. [17]. For interventions, it repurposes worked-example guidance from Kuo et al. [41] and token-budget constraints from Han et al. [43] and Wang et al. [42], but applies them in a novel error-guided manner targeting three distinct failure modes. The evaluation departs from existing inductive reasoning benchmarks (WILT, MIRAGE, KoR-Bench, LogicVista) by requiring models to infer the rule itself from gameplay transcripts rather than apply a hidden rule to new test inputs, positioning the task closer to naturalistic human induction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LRMs systematically underperform non-reasoning LLMs on inductive tasks with hidden rules challenges the near-universal assumption that CoT reasoning is a free performance gain, and the three-mode error taxonomy (decomposition, solving, summarization) offers a diagnostic lens applicable beyond games. However, the scope is limited: four human-designed games with relatively simple hidden rules, eight models, and a GPT-4o judge (validated but single-model). Open questions include whether the U-shaped error curve and the interventions generalize to open-ended inductive settings, whether the scalar alignment model captures the full complexity of LLM reasoning drift, and whether the 1000-token budget is optimal or merely a heuristic proxy for the theoretical N*.
