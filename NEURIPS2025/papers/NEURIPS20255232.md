## Problem

A two-decade-old open question in agnostic active learning asked whether every concept class benefits from active over passive learning in the non-realizable case. All prior upper bounds on query complexity carried an additional distribution- or class-dependent factor c(β) (e.g., the disagreement coefficient) that could be as large as 1/β, erasing the β-factor improvement over passive learning. This paper proves the known lower bound Ω(β²/ε²(d + log(1/δ))) is always sharp, establishing that the β-factor improvement is universally achievable with no restrictions on the concept class or distribution.

## Input / Output

The AVID algorithm receives a pool of m unlabeled examples X₁,…,Xₘ (with m = Θ̃(dβ/ε² + d/ε), the same order as passive sample complexity), error and confidence parameters ε, δ, and sequential access to a label oracle. It adaptively determines how many unlabeled examples to use without knowing β. The output is a classifier ĥ that may be an improper learner (a shallow decision list of concepts from C rather than a single element of C), along with a total number of label queries of order β²/ε²(d + log(1/δ)) + (s ∧ 1/ε)d.

## Method

The AVID (Adaptive Variance Isolation by Disagreements) algorithm iterates N = O(log(1/ε)) rounds at geometrically decreasing resolutions ε_k = C^{1-k}. In each round, it adaptively partitions the instance space into a high-variance region Δ_{i_k} (a union of pairwise disagreement regions) and its complement, then allocates a disproportionately larger number of queries to Δ_{i_k} (≈ d·P_X(Δ)/ε_k²) versus the complement (≈ d/ε_k), exploiting the uniform Bernstein inequality for the low-diameter complement. The key innovation bounding P_X(Δ_{i_k}) = O(β) is comparing each surviving concept not to the best in V_{k-1} but to a more complex decision-list function ĥ_k from an expanded class V_{k-1}^{(4)} that separately optimizes predictions in each disagreement region; this ensures each addition to Δ chops off a proportional fraction of the best-in-class error. A While loop within each round identifies pairs f,g ∈ V_k with P_X({f≠g}\Δ_i) > ε_{k+2} and adds their disagreement regions to Δ until the diameter of V_k outside Δ drops below ε_{k+1}. An early-stopping check (Step 4) verifies ĥ_k is not so much better than all of V_{k-1} that h⋆ might have been pruned, ensuring the inductive argument for bounding Δ remains valid.

## Results

The paper proves that for every concept class C with VC dimension d, the optimal agnostic active learning query complexity satisfies QC_a(ε,δ;β,C) = O(β²/ε² · (d + log(1/δ))) + Õ(d/ε), matching the known lower bound Ω(β²/ε² · (d + log(1/δ))) up to log factors. This yields a β-factor improvement over the passive learning sample complexity M_p(ε,δ;β,C) = Θ̃(d·β/ε² + d/ε) in the far-from-realizable regime (√ε ≤ β ≪ 1). Theorem 3 further refines the lower-order term to Õ((s ∧ 1/ε)·d) using the star number s, which is nearly optimal for every concept class.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's AVID algorithm removes the c(β) complexity factor (e.g., disagreement coefficient θ(β)) that appeared in all prior general upper bounds of the form c(β)·d·β²/ε². Previously, for classes with infinite star number (e.g., linear classifiers in R^k, k≥2), c(β) ≥ 1/β in the worst case, making the upper bound no better than passive learning's d·β/ε². The AVID algorithm achieves d·β²/ε² without any such factor, matching the Kääriäinen (2006) / Beygelzimer et al. (2009) lower bound and proving the β-factor improvement is universally achievable. In the intermediate regime (ε ≪ β < √ε), the query complexity Õ(d/ε) improves over passive learning's Ω(d·β/ε²) by a factor of Õ(ε/β). The algorithm is also adaptive to β (requires no prior knowledge of the best-in-class error rate) and uses only Õ(d·β/ε² + d/ε) unlabeled examples, matching the passive sample complexity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the agnostic active learning framework initiated by Balcan, Beygelzimer, and Langford (A² algorithm, 2005–2009) and the matching lower bound of Kääriäinen (2006), and extends the disagreement-based query principles of Hanneke (2007b, 2014) and the star number complexity measure of Hanneke and Yang (2015). The key departure is the introduction of the AVID (Adaptive Variance Isolation by Disagreements) principle, which adaptively partitions the instance space to isolate high-variance regions and allocates queries disproportionately to them, thereby eliminating the c(β) factor (e.g., disagreement coefficient) that appeared in every prior general upper bound and could negate the improvement over passive learning. The analysis also draws on empirical localization (Koltchinskii, 2006; Bartlett et al., 2005) and the uniform Bernstein inequality, but applies them in a novel two-region decomposition that separates the challenging Δ region from the low-diameter complement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This result resolves a 20-year open question by proving that every concept class, without exception, benefits from active learning in the non-realizable regime, with a clean β-factor improvement over passive learning that requires no distributional or class-specific restrictions. The practical implication is that active learning is universally justified in noisy settings, removing the need to verify conditions like bounded disagreement coefficient before deploying active learning strategies. Open questions include whether the bound is achievable by a proper learner (the AVID algorithm is improper), whether the remaining factor of d in the lower-order term can be removed, and whether the log²(1/ε) dependence for finite-star-number classes can be tightened to log(1/ε).
