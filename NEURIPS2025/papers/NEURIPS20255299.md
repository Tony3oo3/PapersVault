## Problem

In iterative synthetic data bootstrapping (generate → reward-filter → fine-tune), practitioners must decide how much data to generate and select at each iteration under a fixed computational budget, yet no principled allocation strategy existed. Prior theoretical work (Ferbach et al., 2024) only proved convergence under infinite-sample assumptions, offering no finite-budget guidance. This paper is the first to characterize the optimal per-iteration data schedule that maximizes final model performance.

## Input / Output

The method takes an initial generative model (a DPM or LLM), a reward model mapping samples to [0,1], and a fixed total budget C that jointly constrains generation cost (c_g·N_t) and training cost (c_t·n_t) across T iterations. The policy π specifies n_t (selected samples per iteration); N_t is then determined stochastically by the reward-based selection rate. The output is a fine-tuned model evaluated by expected reward—PSNR for image denoising and answer accuracy for math reasoning—on held-out validation sets.

## Method

The core contribution is a theoretical framework proving that exponential growth in per-iteration data size is the optimal budget allocation. In a Gaussian toy setting with exponential reward, they analytically derive n_t ∝ (1+σ²/κ²)^t via Cauchy-Schwarz, showing early updates' error contribution decays exponentially so later iterations require exponentially more samples. In general settings with mild regularity assumptions, they prove: (i) constant policies fail to converge with probability ≥1/4 due to persistent gradient noise, (ii) any increasing policy converges to optimal reward, (iii) a specific exponential policy achieves O((1+ζ)^{-2T}) convergence, and (iv) in the worst case, exponential policies cost no more than any polynomial or constant policy to reach a target ε. Empirical validation on DPM-based MNIST denoising and Llama-3-8B GSM-Symbolic math reasoning confirms exponential and polynomial growth consistently outperform constant policies, with exponential policies showing the most stable improvement.

## Results

On image denoising (MNIST, PSNR), the exponential growth policy achieves 44.84 dB at s=10 and 40.14 dB at s=20, outperforming the best constant policy (44.18 / 39.91) and matching or exceeding the best linear policy (44.64 / 40.26). On math reasoning (Llama-3-8B-Base), the exponential policy raises accuracy on GSM_p1 from 35% to 47% and on GSM_p2 from 16% to 21%, while achieving 45.66% on GSM_symbolic. Across both tasks, constant policies plateau and eventually degrade due to overfitting, while exponential and linear (polynomial) policies show steady improvement with increasing budget.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three policy families—constant, linear (polynomial), and exponential growth—across two tasks. On image denoising at s=10, the exponential policy (44.84 PSNR) beats the best constant policy (44.18) by 0.66 dB and the best linear policy (44.64) by 0.20 dB; at s=20, the best linear policy (40.26) slightly edges out the exponential (40.14), while both clearly surpass the best constant (39.91). On math reasoning, the exponential policy achieves the highest accuracy across all three difficulty levels (GSM_symbolic 45.66%, GSM_p1 47.26%, GSM_p2 20.65%), with the text noting it shows the 'steadiest improvement' and that linear policies fail to match it when the training budget is large. Constant policies match exponential only in early training stages before plateauing and degrading due to overfitting, consistent with Theorem 4.1's prediction of a persistent reward gap of order n₀^{-1/2}. The pre-trained model serves as the lower-bound baseline, with iterative learning under any increasing policy yielding substantial gains (e.g., +3.9 dB PSNR on denoising, +12 points on GSM_p1).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the iterative synthetic data bootstrapping paradigm popularized by STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) and its successors by introducing a principled budget allocation layer across iterations. The closest prior theoretical work is Ferbach et al. (2024), which proves convergence under an infinite-sample assumption but offers no finite-sample guidance; this paper fills that gap by deriving optimal sample-size schedules under a fixed computational budget. The authors position their contribution as the first to formally analyze how the per-iteration data volume should scale, moving beyond the ad hoc constant-batch-size choices common in practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that constant policies provably fail to converge (Theorem 4.1) directly challenges the default practice of using a fixed number of synthetic samples per iteration, and the exponential growth recommendation gives practitioners a concrete, theoretically grounded schedule. Limitations include reliance on a well-specified reward model, focus on supervised fine-tuning rather than RLHF, and theoretical assumptions (local reducibility, loss-reward coupling) that may not hold in all practical settings. Open questions include extending the framework to RLHF-based iterative training, handling noisy or imperfect verifiers, and validating the exponential schedule at much larger scales where generation cost dominates.
